1. Introduction and Objectives
Thousands of radio frequency (RF) linear accelerators are produced every year around the world in
response to the ever-growing demand in medicine, security, industry and research [1]. However, only a
small fraction of these linacs are developed and deployed for high gradient operations. The main limiting
factor is economic: higher gradient linacs are more difficult to manufacture and require much higher peak
power microwave sources as the RF peak power requirements scale quadratically with the accelerating
gradient. There are many other practical shortcomings of high gradient operations, such as higher dark
currents, high sensitivity to the environment, longer warm up and down times. In general, in every
operational consideration, the user of the high gradient linac system needs to maintain a constant delicate
balance between pushing the linac performance while minimizing the risks of damaging the equipment,
including not just the linacs themselves, but also the RF power and network components.
As significant, as all these risks and costs are, they are more than compensated by the functional benefits
of high gradient systems in some application specific scenarios. One obvious example is in the design
studies for the future discovery science colliders [2], where pushing the gradient beyond state-of-the-art is
the only way to reach TeV energy range within a practical footprint of such machine. Other situations
involve legacy accelerators upgrades, where the higher energy accelerators needed to fit into the existing
building, vault, or laboratory, or be transported by a standard cargo container, or manipulated by a smaller
gantry arm. In these examples, a low energy linac could still do the job in theory, but the cost of civil
engineering and operational complexity of dealing with a larger footprint systems are such that despite all
the risks, high gradient systems are economically viable and advantageous. Finally, there are applications
where the use of high gradient structures is mandated for being uniquely capable of solving the beam
dynamics problems (i.e. emittance compensation in the injectors and injector beamlines, certain types of
deflecting and crab cavities, or high shunt impedance linacs operating in a transient mode). Combined,
these scenarios represent a relatively small but overtime consistent demand for high gradient normal
conducting radio frequency (NCRF) structures across the disciplines. Correspondingly, to meet such
demand, there is a limited yet consistent production capacity for high gradient structures that currently
exists and has been tenaciously sustained for the last several decades by a very niche and small
community of experts, thinly spread between small businesses, universities, and national labs.
Sustaining this current production capacity is not trivial in the absence of large projects; however, to date
the industrial companies, national laboratories and academic institutions involved have managed to
develop and maintain skills, equipment, and engineering culture necessary for small scale manufacturing
activities, prototyping and baseline R&D programs. For the most institutions involved, this is only
possible through diversification of revenue streams among multiple synergetic activities of which only a
fraction is directly relevant to NCRF production. The diversification enables retainment of the expertise
and a critical mass of skilled technicians, machinists, and engineers necessary to perform high gradient
NCRF work, even when demand for such work does not justify the size of the enterprise in and of itself.
This craft industry business model has organically evolved to support the current market size and
community needs, but it does not necessarily provide a scalable framework if there are larger projects on
the horizon, and if the demand for high gradient NCRF structures grows considerably beyond its current
level. This study, supported by DOE ARDAP program, examines one potential innovative approach to
achieve such scalability, by transitioning from the use of high-temperature-brazed soft copper accelerating
structures to the electron beam welding (EBW) manufactured NCRF structures made with hard Cu alloys.

2. Conventional approach to NCRF linacs manufacturing and motivation to
 consider EBW for high gradient structures
Fabrication of conventional, brazed NCRF accelerating structures is a well-established process developed
over many decades, and it involves in various iterations: a procurement of accelerator grade copper [3],
multiple machining steps, multiple inspection steps, clamped cold testing, chemical cleaning, brazing and
post-brazing, vacuum testing, heat load testing, and eventually high power testing of the completed linac
system.
It is important to note that machining often involves multiple different machining setups as well as the
potential for using different machining equipment for each setup, and the relative contribution of the setup
and tooling on the linac cost and lead time could be a very significant factor, especially for the first
article, or a one-off structure. Another relevant and often underappreciated cost consideration is the
degree to which it is necessary to perform thorough mechanical inspection of the machined parts
throughout the fabrication cycle.
In terms of chemical cleaning needed for NCRF structures, there are some differences in processes used,
with some manufacturers only performing ultrasonic cleaning in a mild detergent, while others doing a
combination of ultrasonic cleaning as well as 1-2 µm of material removal via isotropic chemical etching.
This difference is based on the target gradients and the machining technologies utilized, as lower gradient
linacs do not require pristine surface quality and ultra-precise machining technologies typically do not
impart high levels of foreign material contamination.
Brazing is done either in a hydrogen or vacuum atmosphere, again with some differences in technology
used depending on the manufacturer and application. Roughly speaking, hydrogen brazing is historically
more common and there are arguments to be made regarding improved cleanliness. Unfortunately, due to
the safety challenges associated with hydrogen gas, it is increasingly difficult to source hydrogen brazing
technology. Alternatively, vacuum furnace technology has continuously improved over the decades and
the resulting high gradient performance between vacuum and hydrogen brazed components is often
indistinguishable.
Before we introduce the argument to replace brazing of a disk-type structures with the fusion-based
joining methods such as electron beam welding of a split-type structure [4], it is important to summarize
multiple technical benefits of brazing, even besides the obvious fact that brazing disk-type linacs has been
an industry standard for many decades and there is an obvious benefit in relying on a considerable
expertise and practical experience accumulated in this field:
 1. First, unlike in case of the welding, there is very little thermal deformation of the final assembly
 during brazing, as the parts are heated and cooled uniformly. During brazing, a multi-meter-long
 linac structure can be stacked vertically with self-locating registration features such that the
 central cell axis can be very precisely aligned.
 2. In addition, brazing also has the advantage that multiple hermitic joints can be formed during a
 single brazing operation, and economies of scale can be realized when brazing multiple structures
 in the same furnace. For example, the roughly 100+ cells used in the Spring8 and SwissFEL linac
 structures could be joined in a single brazing cycle, assuming availability of a sufficiently tall
 vertical brazing oven.
 3. Furthermore, brazing, albeit imperfectly, can generate internal joints which are not accessible
 from the outside of the components, with a common example being the formation of integrated
 cooling channels within each cell. Brazing also generates joints with minimal virtual vacuum
 leaks as the bond line can be placed close-to or at the vacuum volume boundary.

 4. Finally, brazing also has the advantage of being able to join dissimilar materials, including copper
 to stainless steel as well as ceramics to metals. We note that while dissimilar welding is also
 possible, it often involves a significant added complexity to the process.
 5. Last but not the least, brazing is an inherently clean process, which also serves to clean the
 structure from any surface contaminants.

Despite these numerous advantages, the striking disadvantage of brazed accelerator structures is in the
significant reduction of material hardness, generally decreasing the yield strength from 350 MPa to
60 MPa and reducing the surface hardness from 100 HV to 40 HV. It has been shown that at high gradient
operations, the structures made of hard copper alloys with high electrical conductivity exceed the
performance of the structures made with copper which has been softened by the thermal cycle of the
brazing process [5]. Consequently, a transition from brazing to an alternative cold-joining technique
fabrication method could lead to an increase in high gradient performance of fabricated structures.
Furthermore, it is not inconceivable that once fully developed, the novel split structure manufacturing
approach – which is the key ingredient for enabling cold joining techniques – will offer a considerable
advantage in cost and speed of manufacturing due to reduced number of machining steps.
***
The main phenomenon which limits the practical gradients achievable in the RF linear accelerators is the
RF breakdown (RFBD), which is a microscopically localized excessive power deposition event randomly
occurring when high intensity RF fields interact in-vacuum with the inner surfaces of the copper
accelerating structures. High magnitude RFBDs can abruptly and significantly alter RF dynamics of a
structure, degrade vacuum and generate ablation, dislocation, and surface cratering at the vicinity of the
RFBD event [6,7]. Not only RFBD events interrupt the performance of the accelerator system and limit
its reliability, but they can also permanently damage the structure, reduce the lifetime of many RF
network components (i.e., windows), and damage other neighboring beamline elements sensitive to the
vacuum breaks (i.e. cathodes).
The RFBD probability (number of breakdowns per total number of rf pulses) at a given gradient is one of
the main quantitative parameters characterizing the high gradient linac performance. Due to the statistical
nature of RFBD phenomena, the operational gradient is defined as a gradient at which the breakdown rate
is below a certain acceptable limit. For many US and international discovery science accelerator facilities
and light sources, the RFBD rate requirements can be very demanding, such as upon commissioning not
to exceed 10−6 events per pulse per meter [8].
A typical operational gradient for a standing-wave S-band structure in a medical linear accelerator is 20
MV/m, with surface electric fields up to 130 MV/m and pulse lengths of several microseconds [9]. The
operating gradient for the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center’s linac was about 18 MV/m. In contrast, the
proposed high gradient CLIC accelerating structure has a target loaded gradient of 100 MV/m with a
breakdown rate of the order of 10-7 (per 1 meter structure) at a pulse length of 240 ns [2]. There are many
instances where much higher accelerating gradients have been achieved in experiments, as in a recent
development at Argonne Wakefield Accelerator Facility (AWA), where ~ 300 MV/m gradients have been
demonstrated in a single cell X-band test structure driven by an ultrashort (~ 6 ns, > 0.5 GW) wakefield
pulse [10,11]. While these results are quite galvanizing for the high gradient science community, the
approach is not yet fully applicable for industrially produced accelerating structures composed of 10s to
100s individual cells.
It is in the context of RFBD that the benefits of hard copper alloys have been shown to be of particular
significance. Using a purpose-built test fixture shown in Fig. 1 below, high gradient X-band tests

Thus, the observed benefits of hard alloys prompted the investigation into the cold joining methods for
copper parts. Of the four possible approaches: adhesives, mechanical fastening, soldering/brazing and
fusion welding; welding has the most promise. By definition, welding locally heats the weld joint to its
melting temperature then dissipates that localized heat throughout the bulk component. The implication is
that the copper near the weld will be soft, while the RF surfaces far from the weld will remain cold during
joining and thus retain the hardness imparted into the material during forging.
Electron beam welding (EBW) is a particularly attractive welding technology for fabricating hard-copper
accelerating structures [13] because of the stable power coupling from the electron beam to copper; the
clean, oxygen-free vacuum processing environment as well as the high-power density need to achieve
good weld penetration with minimal heat input. High power density, proper weld joint design and
optimized weld parameters minimize the volume of the softened ‘heat affected zone’(HAZ) as well as the
thermally-induced deformation of the structure.
It is also important to note that EBW is used routinely to fabricate superconducting RF (SRF) accelerating
structures, but this is an entirely different and unrelated process to the topic of this paper. In fact, the
similarity is in the name only, SRF and NCRF structures have fundamental differences between the
structure design, fabrication strategies, operating frequencies, materials, surface resistance, mechanical
precision and post-processing, which makes the two processes very dissimilar.

3. The split-structure NCRF linacs, and initial EBW studies
One enabling recent development that prompted the community interest towards NCRF EBW is the
emergence of the NCRF split-structure topology. There has been growing interest in manufacturing linacs
from either halves or quadrants, using the so-called split-cell’ approach, with examples of both split
configurations shown in Fig. 2. Besides potential cost savings in production of industrial linacs, which
can be particularly relevant in case of the X-band linacs [4,13], where machining a half requires less time

and labor than precise fabrication of individual cells, there are multiple important RF design advantages
which can be implemented in a split-structure geometry.
From an electromagnetic design perspective, a key feature of split-cell structures is that surface currents
do not flow across any bonding junctions, which is not only advantageous from the manufacturability
point of view, but also improves reliability by removing potential RFBD nodes from the RF volume. In
addition, the split-cell structures are manufactured using only multi-axis milling and significantly reduce
the part count and greatly simplify the assembly process, offering potentially significant cost savings.
However, issues such as longitudinal alignment between parts, as well as straightness of beamline bore,
can be significant.
Another advantage of split-cell structures is the ability to implement so-called ‘distributed coupling’ [14].
In this topology, internal waveguides run alongside the accelerator cavities, and power is coupled directly
to each cell. In comparison to conventional side-coupled or aperture coupled accelerating structures, the
distributed coupling approach enables cavity shapes to be optimized solely for high gradients, maximizes
shunt impedance and results in a more localized power reflection during breakdown events compared to
traditionally coupled structures.

Fig. 2: Split-cell structure fabricated from vertical milling (Left) X-band Structure halves fabricated at
CERN [15] (Center and Right) Structure quadrants fabricated by KEK [16].

Because no RF currents cross any of the bonding junctions, split-cell structures are well suited for
welding, which can be tungsten inertial gas (TIG), laser or EBW. Fundamentally, the challenge with
welded NCRF structures is that the welding source joins the parts together by only accessing the outer
surface of the structure. Furthermore, small weld features with minimal contact between faying surfaces
are desirable because this limits the necessary heat input for hermetic joints as well as minimizes thermal
deformation. Yet, for the very same reason (joints being few and far away from the RF facing surface) the
welded structure has the potential to overcome problems of copper softening characteristic to the braze
cycle, and thus to produce structures with much superior performance at high gradients.
While there has been very limited practical development on EBW of NCRF accelerating structures,
CERN has a long history of applying EBW as one of the steps in fabrication process of various copper RF
structures [17]. Fig. 3 (left) demonstrates EBW application for CERN’s Linac4 drift tube linac. Despite
the extremely tight tolerances required, EBW was chosen because of the integrated permanent magnet
quadrupole within the tube, which would have otherwise demagnetized during high temperature vacuum
brazing. Another example of copper EBW includes high power RF windows (Fig. 3 center), which
generally include a TiN coating. The EBW technique studies have also been conducted at KEK with the
X-band structure welded from quadrants as shown in Fig. 3 (right) [18].

One important feature of KEK design is the use of waveguide couplers attached to the ends of the
structure. Presumably, these waveguide couplers are vacuum brazed using conventional approaches and
then welded onto the ends of the structure, resulting in a hybrid welded/brazed accelerating structure.

Similarly, it was proposed at SLAC to develop a welding manufacturing strategy for X-band split-cell
structures based on a combination of electron beam welding, brazing and mechanical fastening, with the
structure design and weld joint geometry shown in Fig. 4, including numerous stainless-steel, knife edge
vacuum flanges. Similar to the KEK structure design, the SLAC design utilizes a series of linear welds to
join the two halves of the structure, as shown in Fig. 4-center. Attaching the vacuum flanges is more
complex, as it requires a separate setup for the radial welds of the beam port flanges as well as XY CNC-
controlled plug welds of the side-mounted vacuum pumping ports. Because the welding electron beam
must have the direct line of sight to the weld joint, the vacuum flanges must be welded onto the stems
after joining the stem to the body, otherwise the flange will shadow the weld joint. These additional welds
require multiple pump-down cycles and setups, incurring significant costs and increasing the likelihood of
structure contamination with vacuum pump hydrocarbons or weld vapor. Furthermore, the four
waveguide WR90 vacuum flanges are mechanically fastened to the structure body. The technical
feasibility of this approach is yet unknown, as the waveguide stainless steel bolts must fasten into the
copper structure, potentially causing deformation of the delicate internal features.

demonstrate feasibility of the approach, it is important to note that both SLAC and KEK EBW initial
studies addressed the two most fundamental challenges of welding compared to brazing: (1) the beam
must be able to ‘see’ the weld joint, and (2) the welded joints must be formed serially.

4. Case study of the C3 NCRF structure for EBW manufacturability
To perform a business case analysis outlined in this project, it is important to start with a specific example
of the welded structure. While a complete mature design for welded, split-cell NCRF accelerating
structures has yet to be demonstrated; we will utilize a C-band, quadrant type, distributed-coupling cell
stack proposed as a part of the cryocooled copper colider (C3) initiative [22]. The C3 concept utilizes
liquid nitrogen cooling of conventional copper to improve surface conductivity which increases the
structure shut impedance by nearly a factor of 3 [23]. This specific choice of the structure is partially
motivated by the fact that if successfully implemented, such NCRF structure can become a standard
building block in multiple accelerator facilities and systems, so that having a significant and consistent
demand for a large number of the identical structures, while still unlikely, could become technologically
and economically feasible.
In its current baseline design reiteration, the C3 accelerating structure is 1.1m long, built with 4 quadrants,
and contains 40 RF cells with re-entrant nose-cone type features. A CAD models of this structure as
quadrants, post-electron beam weld conditions and final conditions are shown in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5: A conceptual design for welded, distributed coupling C3 accelerating structure: exploded view of four
primary RF quadrants forming the AS (Top-left); EB welded structure with waveguides, pumping and a
beampipe (Top-right); final structure with TIG weld locations indicated. Total mass of structure is 182 lbs.
with each quadrant weighing 41 lbs. (Bottom).

While the above design is preliminary, it captures some of the primary design concepts outlined by KEK,
SLAC and others. The structure can be joined using either one of the two welding processes: direct

vacuum brazed as copper/stainless sub-assemblies. A bill of materials of all the sub-components is listed

The manufacturing strategy is based on a series of copper EB welds which are illustrated in Fig. 5-top
right. Namely EB weld #1 is four, lengthwise linear welds which join the quadrants together while two
EB welds #2 close out front and back of the quadrant structure. Next EB weld #3 is composed of four
plug-type welds joining the brazed copper/stainless steel waveguide stem to the upper section of the
structure. Finally, ten plug-type EB welds #4 are joint brazed copper/stainless steel vacuum stems around
the perimeter of the structure, which serve either as beam or vacuum ports. In the following process, a
series of manual stainless-steel to stainless TIG welds are performed, joining the waveguide flange to the
waveguide stem (TIG#1) or the vacuum flange to the vacuum stem (TIG#2).

This two-step “EB then TIG” welding approach addresses a significant geometric challenge associated
with the structure, which is that it is difficult for the electron beam to weld beneath the shadow of a
flange. This approach addresses this issue by EB-welding brazed copper/stainless steel stems to various
ports on the structure. The flanges can then be TIG welded using stainless-steel to stainless-steel welding.
The EB welding process will take a full 8 hours, with a pump down beginning the day before. Special
consideration regarding handling, assembly and EB welding setup will be needed to account for the
weight of the structure (250kg) and to consolidate the number of setups within the EBW machine.

With the device installed onto an axially rotating remotely controllable fixture, it may be possible to
reduce the number of setups to 3 total, one for all four longitudinal welds, four coupler welds and eight
side stems and a setup each for the end joints and stems. However, designing and proofing out such a
fixture have not been performed and would encompass a significant, but potentially highly valuable
engineering effort. In terms of structure’s design, we assume that the external cooling channels are
mechanically fastened to the structure, similar to the idea proposed by Dolgashev et al and shown in Fig.
4, although it may be possible to mill cooling channels into the structure which could subsequently be
sealed with EBW covers.

As form and finish tolerances are responsible for a significant portion of the machining costs, we have
utilized similar tolerances to those used for the brazed distributed coupling C-band GRIT RF structure
[24]. The flatness and perpendicularity of the quadrant mating features must be within ±50 µm over the
length of the structure, with the relevant surfaces shown in Fig. 6-right. The RF surfaces require a form
locational tolerance of ±50 µm with respect to alignment features/datums as well as a form tolerance of
±25 µm. The surface finish requirements of the RF surfaces is Ra = 0.4 µm or an N5 surface finish.
Achieving this number for the under-cut feature associated with the structure’s nose cone (Fig. 6-center)
is challenging. Finally, the deep, off-axis tuning pin holes require special considerations regarding setup
and milling machine configuration, with an example shown in Fig. 6-left.

5. EBW equipment considerations
There are two major configurations of electron beam guns in EBW: internally mounted on a motion
control system (Fig. 7 left) and externally mounted in a fixed position (Fig. 7 right). There are multiple
tradeoffs to be considered here. The major benefit of the internal gun is related to joint access in large
assemblies without repositioning the workpiece. Internal guns often simplify fixturing and weld assembly
design, particularly when it is possible to weld underneath the shadow of a flange using a tilt on the gun
itself. However, the internal gun is limited to low voltage, and is also limited in size, whereas the external
gun has no limit on column length or voltage. The longer column length enables integration of more
focusing lenses and advanced electro-optical controls for beam manipulation. The smaller size of the
internal gun also limits the additional vacuum that can be added to the column thus increasing its risk for
contamination of the gun which can lead to defects in the welds.

Fig. 7: (Left) Internally mounted EB gun [27]; (Right) EBW chamber with gun mounted on top [28].

High accelerating voltage guns (>60kV) have smaller focal diameters and longer focal distances than low
accelerating voltage guns. High voltage systems can also generate greater net power with greater weld
penetration. The smaller focal diameter is not necessarily beneficial for most joint types on copper
accelerators, unless an advanced beam manipulation is used. The longer focal distance has three major
benefits: longer depth of focus which increases the allowable position tolerance, improved joint access,
and reduced contamination from materials vaporizing off the weld pool. The latter is an issue in internal
guns, which can cause the tungsten emitter to short circuit via an arc event. This type of failure can cause
significant damage to the welder as well as the part being welded; however, with proper preventative
maintenance and a skilled operator and engineering team, this can be avoided. The higher voltage gun can
achieve much deeper weld penetration than the low voltage gun, but both are able to fabricate all welds
needed in a copper accelerator. High voltage welders are generally more expensive than low voltage
systems because of the increased power supply costs, more complex electrical isolation needs within the
gun as well as thicker- chamber walls to shield X-ray generated by the process. A high power, high
voltage gun can be used for complex beam manipulation which has been shown to reduce porosity
defects, but further research will be needed for implantation of this type of advanced control system in
copper accelerator manufacturing.
Single spot beam deflection is commonly used in electron beam welding to increase the size of the weld
pool. Multi-pool technology based on the use of low-inductance crossed dipole deflection magnets
enables preheating prior to welding and cosmetic smoothing of the crown of the weld after the deep
penetration weld is completed. A preheat may reduce defect formation in the high conductivity copper but
the preheat may also have limited penetration into the component. Multi-process technology shown in
Fig. 8 is similar to the multi-pool technology with the preheat leading the weld on both sides. Having a
preheat leading the weld, or on the sides of the weld could control the defect formation such as cracking,
or slow down cooling time of the weld pool to enable the escape of trapped gas and thus limit porosity.
However, there is a need for more research to determine if the added complexity of the system provides
sufficient benefit.

Process monitoring equipment that can be used in electron beam welding includes in-situ measurements
(e.g. beam measurement) that occur before welding, and in-operando measurements (e.g. optical or
thermal imaging) that occur during welding. Electron beam processes operate in vacuum, which can
damage or decrease the effectiveness of equipment through pressure differentials, lack of convective
cooling, or vapor deposition. In-addition, the X-rays produced during the process can damage or decrease
the effectiveness of sensors. This requires custom modifications of sensors to operate with electron beam
welding.

Technologies for online electron-optical (ELO), light optical, and thermal observation allow the
monitoring of the melt pool in real time and the recording of the process providing traceability for quality
control. Each of these monitoring methods are integrated into the system with closed loop control. On-
line ELO observation uses the back scattered electrons created by the electron beam and does not suffer
under the vapor deposition of the metal vapor on optical systems. Temperature monitoring capabilities can
be used to track consistency of build properties. These monitoring technologies support rapid
development of process parameters and modification of these parameters to maintain quality.

In summary, EBW is a large and growing branch of manufacturing with rich experience and large variety
of configurations available. However, adopting and optimizing this technology for the specific need of
manufacturing high gradient NCRF structures likely requires a considerable learning curve and empirical
process optimization to select an optimal configuration in the EBW machine topography, voltage and
other operating conditions, beam manipulation and monitoring, diagnostics and controls, weld types
optimization for multiple variants and geometries of joints, interlocks and safety features, etc.

6. Establishing EBW production facility
Capital expenditures (CAPEX) associated with standing up a fully functional production shop capable to
produce welded linacs at a rate of 2 per month in full production mode – would be significant. Assuming
that each quadrant would take 40-80 hours of machine time, the most complex highest value piece parts in
the linac could easily be completed with one dedicated machine and a two-shift operation. Another
dedicated machine would be required to perform post weld machining operations although even with an
allowance of 80 hours of machine time for all post-weld operations, a single-shift month seems sufficient.
The machine tool’s cost for the required class of CNN machine (i.e., GM-2-5AX 5-axis gantry mill by
Haas) would be on the order of $0.5M-$1M with additional $1M for multiple lower grade machine tools
to manufacture less precise ancillary components, $1M for high precision coordinate measuring machines
(CMM), $1M for facilities improvements such as floor space demising, equipment foundation
ruggedization, climate control and material handling, $0.25M for ancillary expenses such as CAD/CAM,
basic support equipment including saws, grinders, manual inspection tools, and $0.1M per machine for
basic infrastructure (compressed air, coolant handling, electrical, office equipment). The above estimates
assume an existing suitable facility floor space of approximately 8-10k sq. ft. The above considerations
apply onto fabrication of parts, and additional space would be required for chemical cleaning, EBW tools
(1-2 each), and the final RF cold test bench and hot testing cell. It is also prudent to mention that
equipment downtime can significantly affect production schedules and thus some degree of redundancy
would be recommended, with schedule risk tolerance being the determining factor when considering the
amount of redundancy required. Summing up all of the above, would place a total in the range of $10-
12M of CAPEX to stand up a greenfield shop capable of handling the ~2 linacs/month production rate.
On the other hand, EBW equipment and expertise can be acquired as an ‘add-on’ to the existing linac
company (consider companies such as RadiaBeam [30], Tibaray [31], or Euclid [32], with a build-in
expertise and already existing manufacturing capabilities in the accelerator technology space). In that
case significant CAPEX savings could be realized and the upfront costs reduced to $3-5M range. For the
purposes of this business case study, we should always differentiate these two options such as,

7. Operating the EBW production facility
In Table 2 we summarized a ramp up, CAPEX, and other preproduction costs, and now we will consider
operational costs for EBW NCRF linac shop in a stable recurrent production mode. However, it is
important first to define a “Unit” of production. RF structures can be of different length, and also, they
can operate in various RF bands, and can be of a varying degree of complexity (i.e., a 5𝜋/6 magnetically
coupled low beta ion accelerating structure [33] and a high gradient 𝜋-mode electron linac structure [34]
have both been developed by RadiaBeam, operate at 2856 MHz S-band frequency, and achieved 50
MeV/m accelerating gradient, but the ion linac structure had a much more complex geometry).
However interestingly enough, based on the authors’ experience with the conventionally manufactured
NCRF structures, once the NRE, development and prototyping phases are completed, the cost of
manufacturing per unit length is not much different over the large range of structure variants. Longer
structures require less manufacturing steps per unit length but are more difficult in handling. High
frequency structures (i.e. X-band) require very high dimensional tolerances and mirror surface finish, but
are small, easy to handle, and consume much less materials. Structures with more complex, or exotic
geometry are always more expensive, but not very common. Thus, it is not unreasonable to define a
production unit as 1 meter of the NCRF structure. It can be a more typical 1-meter long S-band or C-
band linac, or it can be two 50-cm long X-band structures, or a half of the 2-meter long high gradient
structure, as long as we count 1 meter of linac structure as 1 unit, the notion of average unit cost can be
applied to a broad range of products.

8. Conclusions
In this project a business case was considered to incorporate EBW joining process into manufacturing
NCRF high gradient accelerator structures. The technical benefit of EBW process is that it keeps the
structure relatively cold, enabling the use of hard copper alloys which have shown much superior RF
breakdown statistics compared to soft (brazed) copper strictures. EBW implementation can also
eventually reduce the cost and take full advantage of the novel split structure manufacturing approach to
NCRF cavities, where the structure is built not out of individual cells but out of halves or quarters.
However, there are technical and business risks to consider before undertaking such approach. The EBW
RF structure adjoining process was already tested with some experimental structures, with some initial
positive results; however, we are still very far from optimizing the process in an industrial production
setting. A lengthy trial-and-error campaign awaits the first business willing to take that road, with no
guarantees of a successful outcome.
Even if the proposed process is proven to be technically feasible, the business case is still not very clear.
RadiaBeam has been making linacs and other high gradient RF structures for over a decade, and the
demand has been mostly flat for many years, resulting in sales of about a dozen of units (meters) per year,
of which only a half are high gradient structures. The financial models developed for EBW linac shop
require a minimal demand of 20-30 units per year to support a sustainable business, while maintaining a
reasonable unit prices.
Finally, even if the demand is sufficiently high to allow for profitability, a business must make a large
investment (in particularly for Case A) in a technically challenging process, years before profitability is
reached, all while anticipating a very limited upside once it is reached. Even when the revenue starts
coming in, the business will continue operating at a loss for a while, as the EBW process development
will continue over the first few years, and the initial volume of sales will likely be insufficient to cover
operating expenses.

Thus, if the investment into the EBW NCRF accelerating cavities production was left to the market forces
alone, it hardly qualifies as the most attractive business opportunity, unless there is a drastic increase in
the anticipated demand. It is, however, possible that it would be in the public interest to incubate domestic
manufacturers, which can implement and sustain such capability. Some relevant steps that the finding
agencies can undertake to facilitate this goal would include: (1) supporting the EBW process development
and possibly some of CAPEX costs with grants, (2) inducing the NCRF structures demand over the 3-5
years incubation period, either through policies, or even by stockpiling linacs before the actual need
arises, and (3) encouraging other grant recipients to adjust their procurement policies and to better support
domestic industrial manufacturers and innovators. Even with all this help, the Case B – which is
implementing EBW under the realm of the existing accelerator business, is much less risky, less costly,
and more sensible than Case A (a new single purpose company to manufacture EBW linacs).
This of course may change. If by the emergence of novel industrial applications, or through bold and new
scientific initiatives, the community experiences a dramatic increase in the demand for high gradient
NCRF structures, the industrial business community will certainly be able rise to the occasion and
respond with the increased supply and innovations in manufacturing. In such scenario of a protracted and
growing demand, the Case A companies will likely be more competitive in pricing and production
volumes, and thus be able to capture larger shares of the market. Until that time, however, the
investigation of EBW linacs manufacturing should be mostly confined to research institutions and
accelerator companies with the access to federal R&D funding to support the EBW NCRF cavities
manufacturing studies and process optimization.
