Stochastic Wright’s Equation: Existence of Invariant Measures
M. van den Bosch, O. W. van Gaans, S. M. Verduyn Lunel

Abstract
Wright’s delay differential equation is one of the prime examples of a fully nonlinear equation without an explicit solution and whose dynamics can be understood by analytic means. In this paper, we introduce stochastic perturbations by adding Brownian noise with a bounded Lipschitz noise coefficient to a transformed version of Wright’s equation. The transformation considered plays an important role in the deterministic theory as well. We demonstrate that this stochastically perturbed equation has (at least) two invariant measures: a trivial measure concentrated at − 1 -1 and a nontrivial measure on ( − 1 , ∞ ) (-1,\infty) . The crucial and most challenging step of the proof is showing that every solution is bounded away from − 1 -1 in probability. In addition, a major part of our analysis is devoted to deriving detailed estimates for Itô processes with a negative drift.

Introduction

The aim of this paper is to introduce noise to Wright’s delay differential equation

y^{\prime}(t)=-ry(t-1)(1+y(t)),\quad t\geq 0,

(1.1)

where the constant
r>0
is positive and fixed throughout the paper. Since additive noise typically destroys all dynamical properties of the underlying nonlinear system, we are interested in a class of stochastic perturbations that preserve most of the dynamical properties.

Wright’s equation originates from a study in prime number theory
[
37
]
. The equation cannot be solved explicitly, but it has been possible to establish much of its rich behavior rigorously. Thus, it has become one of the leading examples of nonlinear delay differential equations
[
10
,
15
,
25
]
. Wright’s equation has also been encountered as an ecological evolution model, which in this context is actually referred to as Hutchinson equation
[
16
,
24
,
33
]
. This equation, or rather the corresponding equation for
y(t)+1
, is a delayed version of the logistic equation where current growth responds to past population pressure. This delayed negative feedback may represent, for example, resource depletion with recovery time or the accumulation of toxins whose effects are only felt after a delay.
Finally, due to its status in the deterministic theory, it may be of general interest to add noise to Wright’s equation. The more so as it is a fully nonlinear equation, which cannot be treated by theory for so-called semi-linear equations
[
8
]
.

Let us start by considering a stochastic term with an a priori arbitrary coefficient,

\mathrm{d}y(t)=-ry(t-1)(1+y(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

(1.2)

where the process
W=(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
satisfying the usual conditions. We use the notation
y_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, for the segments of a function
y\colon[-1,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which are defined by

y_{t}(\theta)=y(t+\theta),\quad\theta\in[-1,0].

(1.3)

We will consider
\sigma\colon C[-1,0]\to{\mathbb{R}}
to be locally Lipschitz, where we endow the space of real valued continuous functions
C[-1,0]
with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
This is because we will be relying on Theorem
1.5
, an existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with locally Lipschitz coefficients; see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. 2.2]
or
[
30
, Thm. V.38]
.

A reasonable noise coefficient

What are reasonable assumptions on
\sigma
in light of the properties of the deterministic equation? An important feature of the deterministic dynamics is that a solution which approaches
-1
from above will slow down its decay and never cross
-1
. How should the noise coefficient
\sigma
be defined so that the stochastic system preserves this behavior? One approach is to directly impose the condition that
\sigma
vanishes when
y(t)\to-1
, preventing stochastic fluctuations from pushing the solution across the boundary.

Another approach is to first take the transformation
x=\log(1+y)
of variables, which is important in Wright’s analysis of the deterministic problem. Put

x(t)=\log(1+y(t)).

(1.4)

Due to Itô’s formula
[
20
, Thm. 3.3]
, the process
x=(x(t))_{t\geq 0}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{1+y(t)}(-ry(t-1))(1+y(t))\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{(1+y(t))^{2}}\sigma(y_{t})^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{1+y(t)}\sigma(y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t)

\displaystyle=\left(-ry(t-1)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma(y_{t})^{2}}{(1+y(t))^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\sigma(y_{t})}{1+y(t)}\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(1.5)

It is clear that the coefficients of this equation only have no singularities if
\sigma(y_{t})
contains a factor
1+y(t)
. Therefore we will assume that
u\mapsto\sigma(u)/(1+u(0))
extends to a locally Lipschitz map on
C[-1,0]
, or, equivalently, that

\sigma(u)=(1+u(0))h(u)\quad\mbox{ for all }u\in C[-1,0],

(1.6)

where
h\colon C[-1,0]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a locally Lipschitz map. Equation (
1.2
) then becomes

\mathrm{d}y(t)=-ry(t-1)(1+y(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+(1+y(t))h(y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(1.7)

One could (at least formally) restate (
1.7
) as

\mathrm{d}y(t)=-r(1+y(t))\Big(y(t-1)\,\mathrm{d}t+h(y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t)\Big),

(1.8)

which means that the noise actually acts on the delayed feedback. Equation (
1.7
) is the
stochastic Wright equation
that we will consider.

Let us now address the
global
existence and uniqueness of (
1.7
).

Proposition 1.1
.

Assume that the locally Lipschitz coefficient
h
is bounded, that is, there exists a constant
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

|h(u)|\leq\beta\mbox{ for all }u\in C[-1,0].

(1.9)

For every
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\varphi
in
C[-1,0]
with
\varphi(0)>-1
a.s., there exists a unique global solution
y
of (
1.7
) with
y_{0}=\varphi
and

y(t)>-1\mbox{ for every }t\geq 0\mbox{ a.s..}

(1.10)

Proof.

By Theorem
1.5
, there exists a unique solution
y=(y(t))_{-1\leq t<\tau_{y}}
with maximal existence time
\tau_{y}
such that
y_{0}=\varphi
.
Define the stopping time

\tau:=\sup\{t\in[0,\tau_{y})\colon\,y(t)>-1\}.

(1.11)

By Itô’s formula
[
20
, Thm. 3.3]
we have a.s. for
t\in[0,\tau)
that

\log(1+y(t))=\log(1+y(0))+\int_{0}^{t}\left[-ry(s-1)+\frac{1}{2}h(y_{s})^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}h(y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W(s).

(1.12)

Denote

H(s)(\omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h(y_{s}(\omega)),&s<\tau(\omega),\\
0&s\geq\tau(\omega).\end{array}\right.

(1.13)

Since
h
is bounded and continuous and
t\mapsto y_{t}
is continuous on
[0,\tau_{y})
, we have that
H
is stochastically integrable with respect to
W
,
\int_{0}^{\cdot}H(s)\,dW(s)
has a.s. continuous paths, and

\int_{0}^{t}H(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)=\int_{0}^{t}h(y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\mbox{ for all }t\in[0,\tau(\omega))\mbox{ a.s..}

(1.14)

Consider
\omega\in\Omega
with
y(0)(\omega)>-1
,
t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}H(s)\,dW(s)(\omega)
continuous on
[0,\infty)
, and
t\mapsto y(t)(\omega)
continuous on
[-1,\tau(\omega))
. Notice that
\tau(\omega)>0
and suppose
\tau(\omega)<\infty
. Then the function
t\mapsto y(t-1)(\omega)
is continuous on
[0,\tau(\omega)]
and
t\mapsto h(y_{t})
is bounded and continuous on
[0,\tau(\omega))
. Hence, equation (
1.12
) implies that
t\mapsto y(t)(\omega)
is bounded above and bounded away from
-1
on
[0,\tau(\omega))
. This contradicts the fact that
y(\tau(\omega))=-1
or
\tau(\omega)=\tau_{y}(\omega)
, since
\lim_{t\uparrow\tau_{y}(\omega)}|y(t)(\omega)|=\infty
.
∎

Under condition (
1.6
), the equation for
x(t)=\log(1+y(t))
becomes the
stochastic transformed Wright’s equation

\mathrm{d}x(t)=-r(e^{x(t-1)}-1)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}b(x_{t})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+b(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

(1.15)

where

b(u)=h(\theta\mapsto e^{u(\theta)}-1),\quad u\in C[-1,0].

(1.16)

Almost our entire analysis concerns (
1.15
) rather than (
1.7
). The proof of Proposition
1.1
can easily be modified to prove the following existence result for equations like (
1.15
).

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz and let
a,b\colon C[-1,0]\to\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz and bounded. For every
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\varphi
in
C[-1,0]
there exists a unique global solution
z
of

\mathrm{d}z(t)=f(z(t-1))\,\mathrm{d}t+a(z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

(1.17)

with initial condition
z_{0}=\varphi
.

As in the deterministic theory, it turns out that the predominant dynamics of Wright’s equation with a suitable noise term can be established rigorously.
The way described above to add a “reasonable” noise term to the deterministic Wright equation may be useful more generally. If the dynamics of a deterministic equation have a natural barrier that is not crossed, the barrier may be transformed to
-\infty
by a logarithmic transformation. The noise coefficient in the transformed equation may then be chosen (bounded) locally Lipschitz (e.g., constant), which yields the according form of the original noise coefficient. This approach has also been explored for population models in
[
5
]
, which includes the Mackey–Glass equations
[
27
]
and Nicholson’s blowflies equation
[
14
]
, where population sizes are naturally above zero.

Main results

In what ways are the dynamics of the stochastic Wright’s equation (
1.7
) similar to those of the deterministic equation (
1.1
)? Without noise, this delay equation is known to exhibit subtle dynamical properties; see, e.g., the classical work
[
37
]
, the more recent contributions
[
1
,
2
,
18
,
35
]
, and the extensions developed in
[
9
,
21
,
26
]
. In this paper, we focus on robust properties that have a chance to survive the presence of noise. For instance, the property that solutions starting above
-1
remain above
-1
has already been established in Proposition
1.1
.

The constant solutions
-1
and
0
are equilibrium states of system (
1.1
). Furthermore, Wright’s equation undergoes a supercritical Hopf bifurcation at
r=\pi/2
. In Wright’s seminal paper, he showed that if
r\leq\tfrac{3}{2}
, then any solution
y
satisfying
y(t)>-1
converges to
0
as
t\to\infty

[
37
, Thm. 3]
. It was subsequently conjectured that this is true for all
r<\pi/2
, a statement known as Wright’s conjecture, which has recently been proved in
[
2
]
.
For
r>\pi/2
, it was shown by Wright that there are solutions
y
with
y(0)>-1
that do not converge to zero
[
37
, Thm. 4]
. More recently, Jones’ conjecture has been established, showing that for any
r>\pi/2
the system admits a unique slowly oscillating periodic solution (up to time translation)
[
18
]
. These periodic orbits are likely to be globally attracting, yet the existence of asymptotically stable rapidly oscillating periodic solutions—andthus having co-existing attractors (multistability)—has not yet been fully ruled out; see
[
18
,
35
]
.

In this paper, we establish the following main results. In fact, in Section
4
we prove a theorem for a more general class of equations of which the present setting is a special case.
Turning to the stochastic system, one finds for small
r>0
that the stochastic Wright’s equation (
1.7
) admits (at least) two stationary distributions; these may be viewed as stochastic analogues of the equilibria
-1
and
0
. For sufficiently large
r
, we argue below Corollary
1.4
that one of the invariant measures we obtain corresponds to a stochastic version of the periodic orbit.
Finally, the notions of boundedness in probability, tightness, invariant measures, and stationary distributions are recalled in the terminology section in this introduction below.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider the stochastic delay equation

\mathrm{d}x(t)=-r({e^{x(t-1)}-1})\,\mathrm{d}t-a(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t)

(1.18)

with
a,b\colon C[-1,0]\to\mathbb{R}
locally Lipschitz. Assume there are
positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
such that

0\leq a(u)\leq\alpha\quad\mbox{and}\quad b(u)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\qquad\mbox{for all }u\in C[-1,0].

(1.19)

For every
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\varphi
in
C[-1,0]
there exists a unique global solution
x
of (
1.18
) with
x_{0}=\varphi
and
(x(t))_{t\geq-1}
is bounded above in probability. If
\alpha<r
, then
(x(t))_{t\geq-1}
is also bounded below in probability and the segment process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight.

Moreover, if
\alpha<r
, then there exists an infinite-dimensional invariant measure for (
1.18
).

Corollary 1.4
.

Consider the stochastic delay equation (
1.7
),

\mathrm{d}y(t)=-ry(t-1)(1+y(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+(1+y(t))h(y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

(1.20)

with
h\colon C[-1,0]\to\mathbb{R}
locally Lipschitz. Assume there is a positive constant
\sigma
such that

h(u)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}<2r\qquad\mbox{for all }u\in C[-1,0].

(1.21)

For every
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\varphi\in C[-1,0]
with
\varphi(0)>-1
a.s. there exists a unique global solution
y
of (
1.7
) with
y_{0}=\varphi
, the process
(y(t))_{t\geq-1}
is bounded in probability, and the segment process
(y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight.

Moreover, there exist (at least) two stationary distributions for (
1.7
): the Dirac measure at
-1
and an invariant measure
\nu
with
\nu[(-1,\infty)]=1
, both of which are one-dimensional
projections of infinite-dimensional invariant measures on
C[-1,0].

Proof.

By Proposition
1.1
we have a unique global solution
y
with
y(t)>-1
for all
t\geq 0
. After the transformation of variables
x(t)=\log(1+y(t))
in (
1.7
) we arrive at the stochastic delay equation (
1.15
) with
b
as in (
1.16
). The assumptions on
h
imply that
b
is locally Lipschitz and

0\leq\frac{1}{2}b(x_{t})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}<r.

(1.22)

It follows from Theorem
1.3
by setting
a=\frac{1}{2}b^{2}
that the solution
(x(t))_{t\geq-1}
of (
1.15
) is bounded in probability, the segment process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in
C[-1,0]
, and that (
1.15
) has an invariant measure
\mu
.
Therefore, the image measure
\mu_{0}
of
\mu
under the map
u\mapsto u(0)\colon C[-1,0]\to\mathbb{R}
is a stationary distribution for (
1.15
) and, thus, the image measure
\nu
of
\mu_{0}
under the map

x\mapsto e^{x}-1:\mathbb{R}\to(-1,\infty)

(1.23)

is a stationary distribution for (
1.7
) with
\nu[(-1,\infty)]=1
.
∎

In the earlier work
[
23
]
, the existence of invariant measures has been established for (
1.7
), the non-transformed version of a stochastic Wright’s equation. A lower bound is then rather straightforward, since solutions are clearly bounded from below by
-1
. However, an application of the Krylov–Bogoliubov theorem—or particularly Theorem
1.6
—does not guarantee that the resulting invariant measure
\nu
is non-trivial, as it may degenerate to the Dirac measure at
-1
. By establishing boundedness in probability from below for the log-transformed equation (
1.18
), we ensure that trajectories remain bounded away from
-1
in probability, which in turn guarantees the existence of a
non-trivial invariant measure
, i.e., one distinct from the Dirac measure at
-1
.

Recall that, in deterministic systems, the existence of steady states and periodic orbits implies that of an invariant measure
[
11
]
. We observe from the numerics in Section
5
that
\nu
and its infinite-dimensional counterpart are physical measures
[
11
]
that capture the long-term statistical behavior of the non-trivial solutions. Corollary
1.4
may therefore be viewed as a first stochastic analogue of the deterministic dynamics, where the non-trivial invariant measure extends the role of the stable fixed point for
r<\pi/2

and that of stable periodic orbits for
r>\pi/2
.
Furthermore, we conjecture that the measure
\nu
in Corollary
1.4
is unique in the presence of noise
(\sigma_{0}>0)
. For the deterministic equation (
\sigma_{0}=0
), however,
\nu
may either be the Dirac measure at
0
, corresponding to the unstable equilibrium at zero, or the invariant distribution corresponding to the slowly oscillating periodic solution when
r>\pi/2
; see Section
5
for more details on this matter.

For a further discussion on our methods and results, as well as an outlook, we refer to
[
5
]
where we studied the existence of invariant measures for the stochastic Mackey–Glass equations.

Terminology

Let us elaborate some of terminology used in the introduction above. Throughout the paper, we fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
together with a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the usual conditions; see Section
2.1
for the definitions. We canonically extend the filtration by setting
\mathcal{F}_{s}=\mathcal{F}_{0}
for
-1\leq s<0
.
As we have mentioned earlier, we will rely on the following existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with locally Lipschitz coefficients.

Theorem 1.5

(Theorem 2.2 of
[
4
]
or Theorem V.38 of
[
30
]
)
.

Let
a,b\colon C[-1,0]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be locally Lipschitz functionals and consider the stochastic delay differential equation

\mathrm{d}y(t)=a(y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(1.24)

For every
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\varphi
in
C[-1,0]
there exists a stopping time
\tau_{y}
with values in
(0,\infty]
and an adapted process
(y(t))_{-1\leq t<\tau_{y}}
with almost surely continuous paths such that (
1.24
) holds for all
t\in[0,\tau_{y})
a.s. and
y(t)=\varphi(t)
for all
t\in[-1,0]
a.s..

Moreover, the stopping time
\tau_{y}
can be chosen m maximal, meaning that

\lim_{t\uparrow\tau_{y}}|y(t)|=\infty\mbox{ if }\tau_{y}<\infty,\mbox{ a.s..}

(1.25)

If
(\tilde{y}(t))_{-1\leq t\leq\tau_{\tilde{y}}}
is another process satisfying the same conditions, then
\tau_{y}=\tau_{\tilde{y}}
and
y(t)=\tilde{y}(t)
for all
t\in[0,\tau_{y})
a.s..

A stochastic process
(y(t))_{-1\leq t<\tau}
for a stopping time
\tau
can be trivially extended to a stochastic process
\hat{y}(t)=y(t)\mathbbm{1}_{t<\tau}
, for all
t\geq-1
, and is said to be
adapted
if
y(t)\mathbbm{1}_{t<\tau}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for all
t\geq-1.

The process
(y(t))_{-1\leq t<\tau_{y}}
in Theorem
1.5
is called a
solution
of (
1.24
) and
\tau_{y}
is called its
(maximal) existence time
. If
\tau_{y}=\infty
a.s., then
y
is said to be a
global
solution. For almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, the function
t\mapsto y(t)(\omega)
is continuous on
[-1,\tau_{y}(\omega))
, hence uniformly continuous on every closed subinterval of
[-1,\tau_{y}(\omega))
, so that
t\mapsto y_{t}\colon[0,\tau_{y}(\omega))\to C[-1,0]
is continuous. In other words, the segment process
(y_{t})_{0\leq t<\tau_{y}}
has a.s. continuous paths.

The statement in Theorem
1.5
that equation (
1.24
) holds for all
t\in[0,\tau_{y})
means that

y(t)=\varphi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}a(y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\quad\mbox{for all }t\in[0,\tau_{y})\mbox{ a.s.},

(1.26)

which, more precisely, means that there exists a sequence of finite stopping times
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\tau_{n}\leq\tau_{n+1}
a.s. for all
n
,
\tau_{n}\to\tau_{y}
a.s. as
n\to\infty
, and

y(t\wedge\tau_{n})=\varphi(0)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}a(y_{s}^{\tau_{n}})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}b(y_{s}^{\tau_{n}})\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\quad\mbox{for all }t\geq 0\mbox{ a.s.},

(1.27)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In here,
(t\wedge\tau_{n})(\omega)=\min\{t,\tau_{n}(\omega)\}
and
(y_{s}^{\tau_{n}})(\omega)=\theta\mapsto y(s\wedge\tau_{n}(\omega)+\theta)(\omega)
.

A solution
y
of (
1.24
) with maximal existence time
\tau_{y}=\infty\ \mbox{a.s.}
is called
stationary
if the distribution of
y(t)
equals the distribution of
y(0)
for all
t\geq 0
. The distribution of
y(0)
is then called a
stationary distribution
of equation (
1.24
). This measure is a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
. If, for each
t\geq 0
, the segment
y_{t}
has the same distribution as the initial value
\varphi
, then we call this measure
\nu
on
C[-1,0]
an
invariant measure
for (
1.24
). The image measure of
\nu
under the map

u\mapsto u(0):C[-1,0]\to{\mathbb{R}}

(1.28)

is then a stationary distribution. Usually, the terms “stationary distribution” and “invariant measure” are used interchangeably, and it should be understood from the context whether the scalar process or the segment process is considered.

A family
(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}
of real valued random variables is
bounded above (below) in probability
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
R_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for all
\alpha\in I
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z_{\alpha}\leq R_{\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\qquad\Bigl({\mathbb{P}}\left(Z_{\alpha}\leq R_{\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\Bigr).

(1.29)

The family is bounded in probability if it is both bounded above and below in probability.
A family
(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}
of random variables with values in a topological space
E
is called
tight
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact set
K_{\varepsilon}\subseteq E
such that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{\alpha}\in K_{\varepsilon})\geq 1-\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\in I
. Note that in the case
E={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have that
(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}
is tight if and only if it is bounded in probability.

Finally, observe that if
(x(t))_{t\geq-1}
is a stochastic process with almost surely continuous paths, then for every
-1\leq t_{0}<t_{1}
we have that the process
(x(t))_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}
is bounded in probability. Indeed, the sets
S_{n}:=\{\omega\in\Omega\mid-n\leq x(t)\leq n\mbox{ for all }t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\}
are nested and their union is a subset of
\Omega
of probability
1
, hence for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N
such that
\mathbb{P}(S_{N})\geq 1-\varepsilon
.

Approach and organization

It turns out that proving Theorem
1.3
is all about showing that solutions of (
1.18
) are bounded in probability—the challenging part is demonstrating the bound from below in probability, which is related to being strictly bounded away from
-1
in probability for the original equations. The other assertions then follow by known results for more general equations. Indeed, one can exploit the structure of the equation and use Doob’s maximal inequality or the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality to obtain that the process
(\|x_{t}\|)_{t\geq 0}
is also bounded in probability. Kolmogorov’s tightness criterion and, once again, the structure of the equation then yields that the segment process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in
C[-1,0]
. As a result of the Krylov–Bogoliubov theorem, existence of an invariant measure follows.

These steps above are given in detail in
[
4
, Prop. 3.5, Prop 4.6, and Cor. C.2]
for more general equations. Corollary 4.5 in
[
4
]
summarizes the ensuing result that we will be using, and reads as follows.

Theorem 1.6

(Corollary 4.5 in
[
4
]
)
.

Consider the stochastic differential equation (
1.24
), where
a,b\colon C[-1,0]\to{\mathbb{R}}
are locally Lipschitz functionals. Assume there are positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
such that

a(u)\leq\alpha\quad\mbox{and}\quad b(u)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\qquad\mbox{for all }u\in C[-1,0].

(1.30)

If there exists a global solution

which is bounded in probability, then the segment process

is tight in

. If, in addition, all solutions exist globally, then there exists an invariant measure for (

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
and Corollary

Section

2.1
Filtration and stopping times

A
filtration
of
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
with index set
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a collection of
\sigma
-algebras
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\in T}
in
\Omega
such that
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
for all
s,t\in T
with
s\leq t
. A filtration
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\in T}
is said to satisfy the
usual conditions
if
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is
{\mathbb{P}}
-complete (i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{0}
contains all
{\mathbb{P}}
-null sets, which requires that
\mathcal{F}
is
{\mathbb{P}}
-complete) and

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\bigcap_{s\in T,s>t}\mathcal{F}_{s}\qquad\hbox{ for all }t\in T.

(2.1)

A stochastic process
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
is called
adapted
to the filtration
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\in T}
when
X(t)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for all
t\in T.
A
stopping time
for
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\in T}
is a
[0,\infty]
-valued random variable
\tau
such that

\{\tau\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}\qquad\hbox{for every }t\in T.

(2.2)

For a stopping time
\tau
together with a filtration
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\in T}
, we define

\mathcal{F}_{\tau}:=\left\{A\in\mathcal{F}\mid A\cap\{\tau\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}\ \hbox{ for all }t\in T\right\}.

(2.3)

Note that a deterministic time is a stopping time. If
({S(t)})_{t\in T}
is an increasing family of stopping times for
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
such that
t\mapsto S(t)
is continuous a.s., then

\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}_{S(t)},\qquad t\in T,

(2.4)

defines a filtration of
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
satisfying the usual conditions; see
[
32
, II.7.3 and IV.30]
.

2.2
Brownian motion and its maximum process

A
Brownian motion
on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
is an adapted stochastic process
({B(t)})_{t\geq 0}
when
B(0)=0
a.s.,
t\mapsto B(t)
is continuous a.s., and the increments

B(t_{3})-B(t_{2})

(2.5)

are normally distributed, with mean
0
and variance
t_{3}-t_{2}
, and are independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
for all times
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq t_{3}
.
For a proof of the next lemma, we refer to
[
32
, Thm. I.12.1]
or
[
30
, Thm. I.32]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
({B(t)})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
and let
\tau
be a stopping time for
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
with
\tau<\infty
a.s.. Then
({\widetilde{B}(t)})_{t\geq 0}
, defined by

\widetilde{B}(t):=B(\tau+t)-B(\tau),\qquad t\geq 0,

(2.6)

is a Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{\tau+t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
.

The distribution of the maximum process of a Brownian motion is known exactly, and we refer to, e.g., Remark (i) below Corollary I.13.3 of
[
32
]
. The probability density function is as follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
({B(t)})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
and define

B^{\ast}(t):=\max_{0\leq s\leq t}B(s),\qquad t\geq 0.

(2.7)

For every
t>0
, the maximum process
B^{\ast}(t)
has the probability density function

f_{t}(x)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\pi t}}\exp\left({-\dfrac{x^{2}}{2t}}\right),&\qquad x\geq 0,\\
0,&\qquad x<0.\end{cases}

(2.8)

In particular, we will be needing the following estimate.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
({B(t)})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
and
B^{\ast}(t)
defined by (
2.7
). For every
t>0
and
c\geq 0
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(B^{\ast}(t)\geq c\right)\leq\exp\left({-\frac{c^{2}}{2t}}\right).

(2.9)

Proof.

Due to Lemma
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(B^{\ast}(t)\geq c\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi t}}\exp\left({-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi t}}\exp\left({-\frac{(x+c)^{2}}{2t}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi t}}\exp\left({-\frac{x^{2}+2xc+c^{2}}{2t}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi t}}\exp\left({-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}\right)\mathrm{d}x\,\exp\left({-\frac{c^{2}}{2t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left({-\frac{c^{2}}{2t}}\right),

(2.10)

which proves the inequality.
∎

2.3
Time change

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions. Let us consider time changes of stochastic integrals. Let
({W(t)})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
and suppose
(b(t))_{t\geq 0}
is an adapted measurable process satisfying

{\mathbb{E}}\,\int_{0}^{t}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty\qquad\hbox{for all }t\geq 0.

(2.11)

Assume that there exist positive constants
\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
with

\beta_{0}^{2}\leq b(s)^{2}\leq\beta_{1}^{2}\qquad\hbox{a.s.\ for all }s\geq 0.

(2.12)

The stochastic integral

M(t)=\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\qquad t\geq 0,

(2.13)

is then a continuous martingale with quadratic variation process (see
[
32
, IV.30.8]
)

T(t):=[M]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad t\geq 0.

(2.14)

Note that
({T(t)})_{t\geq 0}
is adapted,
T(0)=0
a.s.,
t\mapsto T(t)
is strictly increasing and continuous a.s., and
T(t)
goes to infinity a.s. as
t
tends to infinity.

Let us proceed by defining
({S(s)})_{s\geq 0}
to be the inverse of
({T(t)})_{t\geq 0}
, i.e.,

T(S(t))=t\quad\hbox{and}\quad S(T(t))=t\qquad\hbox{a.s. for all }t\geq 0.

(2.15)

For each
s\geq 0
,
S(s)
is a stopping time for
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
, since

\{S(s)\leq t\}=\{s\leq T(t)\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}\qquad\hbox{for every }t\geq 0,

(2.16)

as
({T(t)})_{t\geq 0}
is adapted.
Define

\mathcal{G}_{s}:=\mathcal{F}_{S(s)},\qquad s\geq 0.

(2.17)

Then
({\mathcal{G}_{s}})_{s\geq 0}
is a filtration on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
, again satisfying the usual conditions.

The following time change result plays a crucial role in our estimates and is a fundamental result in stochastic calculus; see, e.g.,
[
32
, IV.34.1]
or
[
20
, Thm. 4.6]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz)
.

Consider the setting above and define

B(t):=\int_{0}^{S(t)}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\qquad t\geq 0.

(2.18)

Then
({B(t)})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\left(\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
and

\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)=B(T(t))\qquad\hbox{a.s. for all }t\geq 0.

(2.19)

We also need the following lemma of which a proof can be found in
[
32
, IV.30]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
({S(s)})_{s\geq 0}
be the inverse of
({T(t)})_{t\geq 0}
given by (
2.14
).

(a)

If
\tau
is a stopping time for
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
, then
T(\tau)
is a stopping time for
({\mathcal{G}_{s}})_{s\geq 0}
.

(b)

If
\tau
is a stopping time for
({\mathcal{G}_{s}})_{s\geq 0}
, then
S(\tau)
is a stopping time for
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
.

2.4
Martingales

In this subsection, we collect some basic properties of continuous martingales that are used in the sequel. The next two lemmas can be found in
[
32
, II.70.1]
and
[
32
, IV.37.11]
, respectively.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\left(Z(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
be a non-negative continuous martingale and
c>0
. For any
t\geq 0
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}Z(\theta)\geq c\right)\leq\frac{1}{c}\,{\mathbb{E}}Z(0).

(2.20)

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(b(t))_{t\geq 0}
be an adapted measurable process such that

{\mathbb{E}}\,\int_{0}^{t}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty\qquad\hbox{for every }t\geq 0.

(2.21)

The process
\left(M(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
defined by

M(t)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s},\qquad t\geq 0,

(2.22)

is a continuous martingale.

Below we state important inequalities that will be used extensively in this paper. The power of the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality lies in the values other than
p\neq 2.
Indeed,
p=2
is a special case with
c_{2}=1
and
C_{2}=4
, following quite directly from Doob’s maximal inequality.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous martingale on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t},{\mathbb{P}})
.

(a)

(Doob’s maximal inequality.)
For any
p,q>1
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
, we have

\left({\mathbb{E}}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|M(s)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq q\Bigl({\mathbb{E}}\,|M(t)|^{p}\Bigr)^{1/p}.

(2.23)

(b)

If
(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded above in probability, then

\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}M(s)\right)_{t\geq 0}

(2.24)

is bounded above in probability.

(c)

(Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality.)
For every
p\geq 1
, we have

c_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[[M]_{t}^{p/2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|M(s)|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[[M]_{t}^{p/2}\right],\quad t\geq 0,

(2.25)

where
([M]_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the quadratic variation process of
M
and with
c_{p}
and
C_{p}
constants independent of
M
and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
.

(d)

For
p\geq 1
, any standard Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
,
0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, and any locally bounded adapted process
(X(t))_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}}
one has

{\mathbb{E}}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}X(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right|^{p}\leq C_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}X(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p/2},

(2.26)

where
C_{p}
is as in
(c)
.

Proof.

For (a) and (c), we refer to
[
30
, Thm. I.20]
and
[
19
, Thm. 26.12]
, respectively. For (b), let
R>0
and use property (a) to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}M(s)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{(R,\infty)}\Bigl(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}M(s)\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}{\mathbb{E}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[R,\infty)}(M(s))^{2}

\displaystyle=4\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}{\mathbb{P}}\left(M(s)\geq R\right),\qquad\mbox{for all }t\geq 0.

(2.27)

So if
(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded above in probability, then so is
(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}M(s))_{t\geq 0}.

Finally, for (d), let
X^{\prime}(t)=X(t)
for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and
X^{\prime}(t)=0
for
t\in[0,T]\setminus[t_{0},t_{1}]
, because then (c) yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}X(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\int_{0}^{t}X^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right|\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\,\left(\int_{0}^{T}X^{\prime}(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}X(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p/2},

(2.28)

proving the final assertion.
∎

3
Estimates for Itô processes with negative drift

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions together with a Brownian motion
W=({W(t)})_{t\geq 0}
.
We provide some estimates for the Itô process

Y(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\qquad t\geq 0,

(3.1)

where
(a(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(b(t))_{t\geq 0}
are measurable and adapted processes such that

\int_{0}^{t}{|a(s)|}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty\mbox{ a.s.}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}{b(s)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty,

(3.2)

for all
t\geq 0
. In this setting, the Itô process in (
3.1
) is well-defined and its second term is a continuous martingale satisfying the Itô isometry

\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}{|b(s)|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(3.3)

The first two estimates rely on standard techniques and serve to illustrate how negative drift leads to exponential bounds on tail probabilities.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process given by (
3.1
).
If there exists a constant
\beta>0
such that we have

\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\geq\beta\int_{0}^{t}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\qquad\mbox{for all }t\geq 0\mbox{ a.s.},

(3.4)

then for every
t\geq 0
and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}Y(\theta)\geq R\right)\leq\exp\left(-2\beta R\right).

(3.5)

Proof.

According to Lemma
2.7
,

\left(\exp\left(2\beta\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)-2\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\right)_{t\geq 0}

(3.6)

is a non-negative continuous martingale. This implies, with the aid of Lemma
2.6
, that for
t\geq 0
and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}Y(\theta)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}\exp\left(2\beta\int_{0}^{\theta}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)-2\beta\int_{0}^{\theta}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\geq e^{2\beta R}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}\exp\left(2\beta\int_{0}^{\theta}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)-2\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\theta}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\geq e^{2\beta R}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2\beta R}\,{\mathbb{E}}e^{0}=e^{-2\beta R},

(3.7)

where we have exploited (
3.4
) to estimate
\exp\left(-2\beta\left(\int_{0}^{\theta}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\beta\int_{0}^{\theta}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\right)\geq 1.

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, for every
t\geq 0
and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}\left(-\alpha\theta+\sigma W(\theta)\right)\geq R\right)\leq\exp\left({-\frac{2\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}R}\right).

(3.8)

Proof.

With
\beta=\alpha\sigma^{-2}
we have
\alpha t\geq\beta\sigma^{2}t
for all
t\geq 0
and Lemma
3.1
yields the result.
∎

Finding good probability estimates for the
reverse time supremum

\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}\left(Y(t)-Y(\theta)\right)

(3.9)

is rather involved and the subject of the next lemmas. In short, the proof of the first lemma requires a negative drift—no matter how small—to ensure an upper bound as in (
3.11
). This bound tends to infinity as
\alpha\downarrow 0.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process given by (
3.1
). Assume that there exist positive constants
\alpha
,
\beta_{0}
, and
\beta_{1}
such that

a(s)\geq\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad 0<\beta_{0}^{2}\leq b(s)^{2}\leq\beta_{1}^{2}\quad a.s.\,for\,all\,s\geq 0.

(3.10)

Then for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
R\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)+\frac{2\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)}.

(3.11)

Proof.

Define

T(t):=\int_{0}^{t}b(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\geq 0.

(3.12)

Almost surely, the map
t\mapsto T(t)
is continuous and strictly increasing,
T(0)=0,
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T(t)=\infty.
Introduce the family of
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
defined by

S(t)=\inf\left\{s\geq 0;\,T(s)>t\right\},

(3.13)

where
\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Put

\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}_{S(t)},\,t\geq 0,

and consider the stochastic process
B=(B(t))_{t\geq 0}
, defined by

B(t):=\int_{0}^{S(s)}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.14)

Thanks to the Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz time change result for local martingales, see Theorem
2.4
, we obtain that
B
is a standard Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{G},\mathbb{P}),
where
\mathbb{G}:=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

For
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
R\geq 0
fixed, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}-\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}\int_{\theta}^{l}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)-\alpha(l-\theta)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}B(T(l))-B(T(\theta))-\alpha(l-\theta)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k-1\leq\theta\leq k}B(T(l))-B(T(\theta))-\alpha(l-\theta)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k-1\leq\theta\leq k}B(T(l))-B(T(\theta))\geq R+\alpha(l-k)\right).

(3.15)

For
k\in\{1,\ldots,l\},
the time
k-1
is a deterministic stopping time, hence Lemma
2.5
yields that
T(k-1)
is a
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time. Indeed, for all
u\geq 0,
we have

\{T(k-1)\leq u\}=\{k-1\leq S(u)\}\in\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{S(u)}
. According to Lemma
2.1
, it follows that

B_{k}(t):=B(T(k-1)+t)-B(T(k-1)),\quad t\geq 0

(3.16)

is a standard Brownian motion with respect to the filtration
\left(\mathcal{G}_{T(k-1)+t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.

Moreover, for any
0\leq s\leq t
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,l\},
it holds that

T(t)-T(s)\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(t-s)
,
hence

T(l)-T(k-1)\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1),

(3.17)

and for every
\theta\in[k-1,k]
, we have

T(\theta)-T(k-1)\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(\theta-k+1)\leq\beta_{1}^{2}.

(3.18)

Consequently, we obtain the following inequality:

\displaystyle\sup_{k-1\leq\theta\leq k}

\displaystyle B(T(l))-B(T(\theta))

\displaystyle\leq B(T(l))-B(T(k-1))+\sup_{k-1\leq\theta\leq k}-\big(B(T(\theta))-B(T(k-1))\big)

\displaystyle=B_{k}(T(l)-T(k-1))+\sup_{k-1\leq\theta\leq k}\big(-B_{k}(T(\theta)-T(k-1))\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}B_{k}(\theta)+\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\beta_{1}^{2}}\big(-B_{k}(\theta)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}B_{k}(\theta)+\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}\big(-B_{k}(\theta)\big).

(3.19)

Note that
-B_{k}=(-B_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion, since
B_{k}
is a standard Brownian motion.
Due to Corollary
2.3
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}B_{k}(\theta)\geq c\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}\left(-B_{k}(\theta)\right)\geq c\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2t}\right),

(3.20)

for any
t>0
and
c\geq 0.

Combining all the results above yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-\int_{\theta}^{l}

\displaystyle a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k-1\leq\theta\leq k}B(T(l))-B(T(\theta))\geq\alpha(l-k)+R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}B_{k}(\theta)+\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}\left(-B_{k}(\theta)\right)\geq\alpha(l-k)+R\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}2\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}B_{k}(\theta)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}(l-k)+\frac{1}{2}R\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}(l-k)+\frac{1}{2}R\right)^{2}}{2\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}\right).

(3.21)

The assertion now follows from the estimation

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{l}\exp

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}(l-k)+\frac{1}{2}R\right)^{2}}{2\beta_{1}^{2}(l-k+1)}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\exp\left(-\frac{(\alpha k+R)^{2}}{8\beta_{1}^{2}(k+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}k^{2}+2\alpha kR+R^{2}}{8\beta_{1}^{2}(k+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\frac{k^{2}}{k+1}-\frac{2\alpha R}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\frac{k}{k+1}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{8\beta_{1}^{2}(k+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16\beta_{1}^{2}}k-\frac{\alpha R}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R}{8\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16\beta_{1}^{2}}\right)\right)^{-1},

(3.22)

where we have used that
k/(k+1)\geq 1/2
holds for
k\geq 1
.
∎

The condition in Lemma
3.3
that
b
be nonzero can be disposed of at the cost of larger constants in the estimate. The following slight generalisation in this spirit is useful in the sequel.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process given by (
3.1
).
Assume that there exist positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
such that

a(s)\geq\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\hbox{a.s. for all }s\geq 0.

(3.23)

Then for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
R\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{64\beta^{2}}\right)+\frac{4\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R}{64\beta^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\beta^{2}}\right)}.

(3.24)

Proof.

Let
c>\beta
be arbitrary, yet fixed. Introduce the stochastic processes
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
, where

Y_{1}(t):=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(b(s)+c)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\quad t\geq 0,

(3.25)

and

Y_{2}(t):=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(b(s)-c)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.26)

Note that
Y(t)=Y_{1}(t)+Y_{2}(t)

holds for all
t\geq 0
.

In addition, the stochastic processes
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
3.3
with
\alpha
and
\beta^{2}_{1}
replaced by
\alpha/2
and
(\beta+c)^{2}/4
, respectively, since

\beta+c\geq b(s)+c\geq-\beta+c>0\quad\text{ and }\quad-(\beta+c)\leq b(s)-c<\beta-c<0.

(3.27)

Taking
c=(\sqrt{8}-1)\beta>\beta
specifically, gives us
(\beta+c)^{2}/4=2\beta^{2}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}\left(Y_{1}(l)-Y_{1}(\theta)\right)+\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}\left(Y_{2}(l)-Y_{2}(\theta)\right)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}\left(Y_{1}(l)-Y_{1}(\theta)\right)\geq R/2\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}\left(Y_{2}(l)-Y_{2}(\theta)\right)\geq R/2\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{64\beta^{2}}\right)+\frac{4\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R}{64\beta^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\beta^{2}}\right)}.

(3.28)

This completes the proof.
∎

An important feature of the estimates in Lemma
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
is that they do not depend on the length of the interval
[0,l]
over which the supremum is taken. It is easier to obtain estimates for a supremum over an interval with a fixed length, for which one does not a negative drift. Such estimates are known even in a setting of Banach space valued stochastic integrals; see
[
29
, Corollary 4.4]
. We present an estimate in the next lemma which is sufficient for our purposes and we give a proof using the same techniques as the previous proofs in this section. See
[
5
, Lemma 4.3]
for an alternative proof and a more detailed discussion.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
W=(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Brownian motion. Assume there exists a positive constant
\beta
such that

b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\text{a.s. for all $s\geq 0$}.

(3.29)

Let
t_{0}\geq 0
and
T>0
be fixed. Then for every
R\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{16\beta^{2}T}\right).

(3.30)

If, in addition, there exists a positive constant
\beta_{0}
such that

0<\beta_{0}^{2}\leq b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\text{a.s. for all $s\geq 0$},

(3.31)

then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\beta^{2}T}\right).

(3.32)

Proof.

As before, first consider the case that there exists positive constants
\beta
and
\beta_{0}
such that

0<\beta_{0}^{2}\leq b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\hbox{a.s. for all }s\geq 0.

(3.33)

Define the continuous martingale

M(t):=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+{t}}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\quad t\geq 0,

(3.34)

which is with respect to the filtration
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}:=(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, given by

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}+t},\,t\geq 0.

Subsequently, consider the time change

T(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+t}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\geq 0.

(3.35)

The map
t\mapsto T(t)
is continuous and strictly increasing a.s.,
T(0)=0,
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T(t)=\infty.

Let
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}
-stopping times defined by

S(t)=\inf\left\{s\geq 0;\,T(s)>t\right\}.

Set

\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{S(t)}
,
t\geq 0.

Thanks to the Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz theorem, see Theorem
2.4
, we know that there exists a standard Brownian motion
B
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{G},\mathbb{P}),
where
\mathbb{G}:=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, such that

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)=B(T(t)),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.36)

For every
t\in\left[t_{0},t_{0}+T\right]
we have

T(t)\geq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad T(t)\leq\beta^{2}T,

(3.37)

holding
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.
Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}B(T(t))\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\beta^{2}T}B(t)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{2\beta^{2}T}\right),

(3.38)

by Corollary
2.3
.

We can reduce the general case to the case where (
3.33
) holds. This is achieved along similar lines as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
. In essence, we can choose
c>\beta
such that
0<c\pm b(s)\leq 2\sqrt{2}\beta
and apply (
3.38
) twice with
\beta^{2}
replaced by
8\beta^{2}
.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
Y(t)
be given by (
3.1
). Assume there exist positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
such that

a(s)\geq\alpha\quad\hbox{and}\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\hbox{a.s. for all }s\geq 0.

(3.39)

Then the process

\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}\left(Y(t)-Y(\theta)\right)\right)_{t\geq 0}

(3.40)

is bounded above in probability.

Proof.

Let
R>0
and let
t\geq 0
. Let
l
be the greatest integer below
t
. For
t_{0}\in[0,l]
we have that the expression

Y(t)-Y(t_{0})=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)

(3.41)

is at most

-\int_{t_{0}}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{0}}^{l}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)+\int_{l}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),

(3.42)

and for
t_{0}\in[l,t]
we have that (
3.41
) is at most

\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\leq\sup_{\theta\in[(t-1)^{+},t]}\int_{\theta}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),

(3.43)

where
(t-1)^{+}=t-1
if
t\geq 1
and
(t-1)^{+}=0
if
t<1
.
As
l\in[(t-1)^{+},t]
, the first case yields for every
t_{0}\in[0,l]
that (
3.41
) is at most

\sup_{\theta\in[0,l]}\left(-\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right)+\sup_{\theta\in[(t-1)^{+},t]}\int_{\theta}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s).

(3.44)

The second case yields that (
3.41
) is also below (
3.44
) for every
t_{0}\in[l,t]
.
Combining both cases we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{t_{0}\in[0,t]}Y(t)-Y(t_{0})\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{\theta\in[0,l]}\left(-\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right)+\sup_{\theta\in[(t-1)^{+},t]}\int_{\theta}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{\theta\in[0,l]}\left(-\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right)\geq R/2\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{\theta\in[(t-1)^{+},t]}\int_{\theta}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R/2\right),

(3.45)

which is below a given
\varepsilon>0
for all
t\geq 0
, provided
R
is chosen big enough, due to Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
.
∎

In the next section, we will also require three estimates on sums of probabilities involving the process
Y
, stated in the Lemmas
3.8
,
3.9
, and
3.10
below. The first two of them are the most delicate ones, yet make use of the following straightforward calculation.

Lemma 3.7
.

For every
p,q\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
0<p<q
we have

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(pl+q)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{p(q-p)}.

(3.46)

Proof.

For
c>1
we have

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(l+c)^{2}}\leq\int_{c-1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{(c-1)},

(3.47)

yielding

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(pl+q)^{2}}=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(l+q/p)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{p^{2}(q/p-1)}=\frac{1}{p(q-p)},

(3.48)

proving the estimate.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
Y(t)
be given by (
3.1
) and let
p
and
C
be given positive constants. Assume there exist positive constants
\alpha
,
\lambda
, and
\sigma
such that

a(s)\geq\alpha\quad\hbox{and}\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\quad\hbox{a.s. for all }s\geq 0

(3.49)

and

\alpha>32\lambda\sigma^{2}.

(3.50)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
q>0
such that for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq m-l}\left(Y(m-l)-Y(\theta)\right)\geq R_{l}\right)<\varepsilon,

(3.51)

where

R_{l}=-C+\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\log\left(pl+q\right).

(3.52)

Proof.

We prove the lemma for
\lambda=1/2
. The general case follows by rescaling
Y
.
According to Lemma
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq m-l}\left(Y(m-l)-Y(\theta)\right)\geq R_{l}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4\sum_{l=0}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{R_{l}^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)+\frac{4}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R_{l}}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4\sum_{l=0}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{C^{2}+\log(pl+q)\left(\log(pl+q)-2C\right)}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{4\exp\left(\frac{\alpha C}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha\log(pl+q)}{64\sigma^{2}}\right).

(3.53)

Next choose
q
so large that
\log q-2C\geq 128\sigma^{2}
and proceed the estimation by

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq m-l}\left(Y(m-l)-Y(\theta)\right)\geq R_{l}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4\left(1+\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\alpha C}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)}\right)\sum_{l=0}^{m}\exp\left(-2\log(pl+q)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=4\left(1+\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\alpha C}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)}\right)\sum_{l=0}^{m}\frac{1}{(pl+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4\left(1+\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\alpha C}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)}\right)\frac{1}{p(q-p)},

(3.54)

where we have used Lemma
3.7
. Summarizing this shows that

\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq m-l}\left(Y(m-l)-Y(\theta)\right)\geq R_{l}\right)\leq 4\left(1+\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\alpha C}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)}\right)\frac{1}{p(q-p)},

(3.55)

which tends to zero as
q\to\infty
.
∎

Now that we have obtained estimates for
Y(t)
at integer time points
t=l
,
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the next step is to relate the process
Y(t)
to
Y(l)
. In the lemma below, we allow for positive drift as
a(t)
may attain negative values.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
Y(t)
be given by (
3.1
) and
p
,
\lambda
, and
C
positive constants. Assume that there exist constants
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

-a(s)\leq\alpha\quad\hbox{and}\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\quad\hbox{a.s. for all }s\geq 0.

(3.56)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
q>0
such that for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{m-l-1\leq t\leq m-l+1}\left(Y(t)-Y(m-l-1)\right)\geq R_{l}\right)<\varepsilon,

(3.57)

where

R_{l}=-C+\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\log(pl+q).

(3.58)

Proof.

We prove the lemma for
\lambda=1/2
.
The general case again follows by rescaling
Y
.
Clearly, we may assume in the proof that
\alpha>0
. Due to Lemma
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l-1\leq t\leq m-l+1}-\int_{m-l-1}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{m-l-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R_{l}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l-1\leq t\leq m-l+1}\int_{m-l-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R_{l}-2\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(R_{l}-2\alpha)^{2}}{16\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\exp\left(-\frac{(-C-2\alpha+\log(pl+q))^{2}}{16\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(C+2\alpha)^{2}+\log(pl+q)\left(\log(pl+q)-2C-4\alpha\right)}{16\sigma^{2}}\right).

(3.59)

Choose
q
so large that
\log q>32\sigma^{2}+2C+4\alpha
holds, sum the inequality (
3
) from
l=0
to
m
, and use Lemma
3.7
to arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l-1\leq t\leq m-l+1}-\int_{m-l-1}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{m-l-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R_{l}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 2\sum_{l=0}^{m}\exp\left(-2\log(pl+q)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2\sum_{l=0}^{m}\frac{1}{(pl+q)^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{p(q-p)},

(3.60)

which tends to
0
as
q\to\infty
.
∎

In view of the proof of the main theorem in Section
4
, we encounter a process
Y(t)
which has a positive drift term. We may hence apply Lemmas
3.9
and
3.10
to it. Carefully observe the minus sign within (
3.62
) in front of the difference
Y(m+1)-Y(t)
. To remain in the spirit of this section, we reformulate Lemma

in terms of processes

with negative drift, which is done in Corollary

. After all,

is an Itô process with negative drift if and only if

is one with positive drift.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
Y(t)
be given by (
3.1
) and
p>0
a positive constant. Assume that there exists a constant
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

a(s)\leq 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\quad\hbox{a.s. for all }s\geq 0.

(3.61)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
q>0
such that for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}-\left(Y(m+1)-Y(t)\right)\geq pl+q\right)<\varepsilon.

(3.62)

Proof.

Due to Lemma
3.5
, we have for
q>0
the inequality

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}-\left(Y(m+1)-Y(t)\right)\geq pl+q\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}\int_{t}^{m+1}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{m+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq pl+q\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}-\int_{t}^{m+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq pl+q\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}-\int_{m-l}^{m+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)+\int_{m-l}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq pl+q\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(-\int_{m-l}^{m+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq\frac{1}{2}(pl+q)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}\int_{m-l}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq\frac{1}{2}(pl+q)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{(pl+q)^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}(l+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=4\exp\left(-\frac{q^{2}+2pql+p^{2}l^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}(l+1)}\right).

(3.63)

Summation from
l=0
to
m
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}-\left(Y(m+1)-Y(t)\right)\geq pl+q\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{q^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{m}4\exp\left(-\frac{2pql}{64\sigma^{2}(l+1)}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{p^{2}l^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}(l+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{q^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{m}4\exp\left(-\frac{pq}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{p^{2}l}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{q^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)+4\exp\left(-\frac{pq}{64\sigma^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{p^{2}}{128\sigma^{2}}\right)},

(3.64)

which tends to
0
as
q\to\infty
.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
Y(t)
be given by (
3.1
) and
p>0
a positive constant. Assume that there exists a constant
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

a(s)\geq 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\quad\hbox{a.s. for all }s\geq 0.

(3.65)

For every

\sum_{l=0}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\,\left(\sup_{m-l\leq t\leq m-l+1}\left(Y(m+1)-Y(t)\right)\geq pl+q\right)<\varepsilon.

(3.66)

Proof of main theorem

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
for the stochastic transformed Wright equation (
1.18
),

\mathrm{d}x(t)=-r\left(e^{x(t-1)}-1\right)\,\mathrm{d}t-a(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(4.1)

It turns out that our proof actually applies to a class of equations, which includes (
1.18
).
Let
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function such that there exist
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma>0
and
\lambda>0
such that

0\leq G(x)\leq\gamma_{0}+\gamma e^{\lambda x}\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}

(4.2)

and

\lim_{x\to-\infty}G(x)=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}G(x)=\infty.

(4.3)

The stochastic transformed Wright equation (
1.18
) is of the form

\mathrm{d}z(t)=-G(z(t-1))\,\mathrm{d}t+(r-a(z_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t+b(z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

(4.4)

where
G(x)=re^{x}
,
0\leq a(u)\leq\alpha
, and
b(u)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}
for all
u\in C[-1,0]
, for some positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Due to Proposition
1.2
, we have that for every
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\varphi
in
C[-1,0]
, there exists a unique global solution
z
of (
4.4
) with
z_{0}=\varphi
.

Our estimates in this section hold for processes satisfying the even more general equation

\mathrm{d}z(t)=-G(z(t-1))\,\mathrm{d}t+a(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+b(t)\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\qquad t\geq 0,

(4.5)

where
(a(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(b(t))_{t\geq 0}
are measurable and adapted processes satisfying
\alpha_{0}\leq a(t)\leq\alpha_{1}
, and
b(t)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
for some positive constants
\alpha_{0}
,
\alpha_{1}
, and
\sigma
. We deal with system (
4.5
) in Lemmas
4.2
–
4.4
. This is followed by Theorem
4.5
, where we consider the system (
4.4
) and exploit the previous lemmas.

But first, we start with a result on a general class of integral delay equations, which allows us to estimate solutions from above by increments of the driving process. It is similar to
[
5
, Lem. 5.3]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous and assume that there exist constants
x_{0},\delta,c\geq 0
such that

\begin{array}[]{rll}&f(x,y)\geq\delta&\mbox{ for all }x,y\in[x_{0},\infty),\mbox{ and}\\
&f(x,y)\geq-c&\mbox{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}

(4.6)

Let
t_{0}\geq 0
and suppose
d,u\colon[t_{0},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous functions with
d(t)\leq\delta
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. If
z\colon[t_{0}-1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous with
z(t_{0})\leq x_{0}
and satisfies

z(t)=z(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left[-f(z(s-1),z(s))+d(s)\right]\mathrm{d}s+u(t)-u(t_{0}),\quad t\geq t_{0},

(4.7)

then for every
t\geq t_{0}
there exists
a^{t}\in[t_{0},t]
such that

z(t)\leq\max\{x_{0},x_{0}+c+\delta+u(t)-u(a^{t})\}.

(4.8)

Proof.

Fix
t\in[t_{0},\infty)
. We have to deal with the case
z(t)>x_{0}
. Let

a^{t}=\sup\{s\in[t_{0},t)\colon\,z(s)\leq x_{0}\}.

(4.9)

By continuity of
z
we have
z(a^{t})\leq x_{0}
. If
t-a^{t}<1
, then

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z(a^{t})-\int_{a^{t}}^{t}f(z(s-1),z(s))\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{a^{t}}^{t}d(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+u(t)-u(a^{t})

\displaystyle\leq x_{0}+c+\delta+u(t)-u(a^{t}).

(4.10)

Otherwise, we have
t-a^{t}\geq 1
. Then
z(s)\geq x_{0}
for all
s\in[a^{t},t]
, so
f(z(s-1),z(s))\geq\delta\geq d(s)
for all
s\in[a^{t}+1,t]
, hence

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z(a^{t})-\int_{a^{t}}^{a^{t}+1}f(z(s-1),z(s))\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{a^{t}}^{a^{t}+1}d(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{a^{t}+1}^{t}\left[f(z(s-1),z(s))-d(s)\right]\mathrm{d}s+u(t)-u(a^{t})

\displaystyle\leq x_{0}+c+\delta+u(t)-u(a^{t}),

(4.11)

which shows the upper bound (
4.8
).
∎

The main use of Lemma
4.1
is in Lemma
4.4
, where we show that solutions of (
4.5
) are bounded below in probability. It also yields boundedness above in probability, as we show next. Note that there are several alternative ways of showing that solutions are bounded above in probability; see, e.g.,
[
5
, Prop. 3.5]
which uses an idea of
[
36
]
. We do not need a lower bound on
a(t)
here.

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider the equation (
4.5
), where
a(t)\leq\alpha_{1}
and
b(t)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
, for some positive constants
\alpha_{1}
and
\sigma
. Then every solution
z
is bounded above in probability.

Proof.

Define

U(t)=-2\alpha_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\\
=-\int_{0}^{t}[2\alpha_{1}-a(s)]\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),

(4.12)

for
t\geq 0.
Since
\lim_{x\to\infty}G(x)=\infty
, there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
G(x)\geq r+2\alpha_{1}
for all
x\geq x_{0}
. Due to Lemma
4.1
with
f(x,y)=G(x)-r
,
\delta=2\alpha_{1}
, and
c=r
, we have

z(t)\leq\min\left\{x_{0},x_{0}+r+2\alpha_{1}+\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}(U(t)-U(\theta))\right\},

(4.13)

for every
t\geq 0
. By Corollary
3.6
, we find that the process
(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq t}(U(t)-U(\theta)))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded above in probability and, therefore,
z=(z(t))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded above in probability.
∎

Once a solution
z
of (
4.5
) is bounded in probability at integer times, the next lemma says it is bounded in probability at all times. Theorem
1.6
then yields that the process
(\|z_{t}\|)_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability as well. Since our proof of the lemma shows that conclusion without extra effort, we will not invoke Theorem
1.6
at this point.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function satisfying (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). Consider the stochastic delay equation
(
4.5
).
Assume that there are positive constants
\alpha_{1}
and
\sigma
such that

0\leq a(t)\leq\alpha_{1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b(t)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\qquad\hbox{for all }t\geq 0.

(4.14)

If
z
is a solution of (
4.5
) such that
(z(m))_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in probability, then
(\|z_{t}\|)_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Take
R>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(|z(n)|\leq R\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

(4.15)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[n-1,n+1]
, we have

z(n+1)=z(t)-\int_{t}^{n+1}G\left(z(s-1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{n+1}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{n+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s).

(4.16)

This implies

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z(n+1)+\int_{t}^{n+1}G\left(z(s-1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{n+1}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{n+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s).

\displaystyle\geq z(n+1)-2\alpha_{1}-\int_{t}^{n+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)

\displaystyle=z(n)-2\alpha_{1}-\int_{n-1}^{n+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)+\int_{n-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)

\displaystyle\geq z(n+1)-2\alpha_{1}-2\sup_{\theta\in[n-1,n+1]}\left|{\int_{n-1}^{\theta}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}\right|.

(4.17)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z(n-1)-\int_{n-1}^{t}G\left(z(s-1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{n-1}^{t}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{n-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)

\displaystyle\leq z(n-1)+2\alpha_{1}+\sup_{\theta\in[n-1,n+1]}\left|\int_{n-1}^{\theta}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right|,

(4.18)

so that

\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}|z(t)|\leq\max\{|z(n-1)|,|z(n+1)|\}+2\alpha_{1}+2\sup_{\theta\in[n-1,n+1]}\left|\int_{n-1}^{\theta}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right|.

(4.19)

Since

\left(\int_{n-1}^{n-1+t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right)_{t\geq 0}

(4.20)

is a martingale, Doob’s maximal inequality in Lemma
2.8
yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}\left|{\int_{n-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,2]}\left|{\int_{n-1}^{n-1+t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{n-1}^{n+1}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right)^{2}=4\int_{n-1}^{n+1}\mathbb{E}\,b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 8\sigma^{2}.

(4.21)

This shows

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}\left|{\int_{n-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}\right|\right)^{2}\leq 8\sigma^{2},

(4.22)

and hence, by the Markov inequality, we obtain that

\left(\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}\left|{\int_{n-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}\right|\right)_{n\geq 1}\quad\mbox{is bounded in probability}.

(4.23)

Take
S>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}\left|{\int_{n-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}\right|\geq S\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\quad\mbox{for every }n\geq 1.

(4.24)

Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}|z(t)|\leq R+2\alpha_{1}+2S\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\geq\mathbb{P}\left(|z(n-1)|\leq R,\ |z(n+1)|\leq R,\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}\int_{n-1}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\leq S\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(4.25)

Thus,
(\sup_{t\in[n-1,n+1]}|z(t)|)_{n\geq 1}
is bounded in probability.

Finally, for every
t\geq 0
there is a unique
n(t)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t\in[n(t),n(t)+1)
and then

\sup_{\theta\in[-1,0]}|z(t+\theta)|\leq\sup_{s\in[n(t)-1,n(t)+1]}|z(s)|.

(4.26)

This shows that
(\|z_{t}\|)_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability as well.
∎

The most involved part of our entire analysis is showing that solutions of (
4.5
) are bounded below in probability. The next lemma presents the core of the proof.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function satisfying (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). Consider the stochastic delay equation
(
4.5
).
Assume that there are positive constants
\alpha_{0}
,
\alpha_{1}
, and
\sigma
such that

\alpha_{0}\leq a(t)\leq\alpha_{1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b(t)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\qquad\hbox{for all }t\geq 0.

(4.27)

For every solution
z=(z(t))_{t\geq-1}
, the process

\left(z(m)\right)_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\quad\mbox{is bounded below in probability.}

(4.28)

Proof.

First assume there is a constant
L>0
such that
|z(\theta)|\leq L
a.s. for all
\theta\in[-1,0]
.
Choose
\eta\in(0,\alpha_{0})
and choose
R>0
so large that

-L\geq-R\quad\mbox{and}\quad G(x)<\tfrac{\eta}{2}\mbox{ for all }x\in(-\infty,-R).

(4.29)

Choose
\beta>32\lambda\sigma^{2}
and put

A_{0}=\max\left\{x_{0},x_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}+\beta,L\right\},

(4.30)

where
x_{0}
is such that
G(x)>\alpha_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}+\beta
for all
x>x_{0}
. Define for
t\geq 0
the processes

\displaystyle U(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta t-\int_{0}^{t}\tfrac{1}{2}b(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),

(4.31)

\displaystyle V(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(a(s)-\eta\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s).

(4.32)

Note that using the processes
U
and
V
we can rewrite equation (
4.5
) for
z
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}z(t)

\displaystyle=-G(z(t-1))\,\mathrm{d}t+\eta\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}V(t),\qquad t\geq 0,

(4.33)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}z(t)

\displaystyle=-\left(G(z(t-1))-a(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}b(t)^{2}-\beta\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}U(t),\qquad t\geq 0.

(4.34)

The aim is to apply Lemma
3.8
, Lemma
3.9
and Lemma
3.10
to suitable processes
Y
.

For the process
U
, we have

U(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\beta+\tfrac{1}{2}b(s)^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)=-\int_{0}^{t}a_{U}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),

(4.35)

where

a_{U}(s)=\beta+\tfrac{1}{2}b(s)^{2}.

(4.36)

So, from the assumptions,

a_{U}(s)\geq\beta,\quad\sigma_{0}^{2}\leq b(s)^{2}\leq\sigma^{2},\quad\mbox{and }\beta>32\lambda\sigma^{2}.

(4.37)

This shows that the process
Y:=U
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.8
.
Similarly for the process
V
, we have

V(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\eta-a(s)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\\
=-\int_{0}^{t}a_{V}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s),

(4.38)

where

a_{V}(s)=\eta-a(s).

(4.39)

Since

-a_{V}(s)=a(s)-\eta\leq\alpha_{1}-\eta\quad\mbox{and}\quad a_{V}(s)\leq\eta-\alpha_{0}<0,

(4.40)

the process
Y:=V
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.9
and Lemma
3.10
. Note that, in contrast to

Let
\varepsilon>0
. It follows from Lemma
3.8
, Lemma
3.9
and Lemma
3.10
that we can choose
C>0
so large such that, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the processes
U
and
V
satisfies the following estimates

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\,\sup_{t\in[m,m+1]}\left(V(t)-V(m)\right)\geq C-R-\gamma_{0}-\eta\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{6},

(4.41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\,\sup_{t\in[0,1]}(V(t)-V(0))\geq D(m-1,C)\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{6},

(4.42)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\,\sup_{t\in[0,1]}-\left(V(m)-V(t)\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{4}(C-R-\gamma_{0})+\tfrac{\gamma}{4}(m-1)\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{6},

(4.43)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m-2}\mathbb{P}\left(\,\sup_{t\in[0,m-2-l]}\left(U(m-2-l)-U(t)\right)\geq D(l,C)\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{6},

(4.44)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\,\sup_{t\in[m-2-l,m-l]}\left(V(t)-V(m-2-l)\right)\geq D(l,C)\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{6},

(4.45)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\,\sup_{t\in[m-1-l,m-l]}-\left(V(m)-V(t)\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(C-R-\gamma_{0})+\tfrac{\gamma}{4}l\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{6},

(4.46)

where

D(l,C):=-\tfrac{1}{2}A_{0}-\eta-\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\log 2+\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(C-R-\gamma_{0})+\tfrac{\eta}{2\gamma}l\right).

(4.47)

Goal.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and define

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in\Omega\mid z(m)(\omega)\leq-C\},

(4.48)

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in\Omega_{0}\mid\exists\,t\in[m,m+1]\hbox{ such that }z(t)(\omega)\geq-R\},

(4.49)

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in\Omega_{0}\mid z(t)(\omega)\leq-R\hbox{ for all }t\in[m,m+1]\}.

(4.50)

We wish to show that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})<\varepsilon
. Note that
\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
and hence

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})\leq\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})+\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2}).

(4.51)

So it suffices to provide estimates for
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{j})
,
j=1,2
.

Estimate on
\Omega_{1}
.

Pointwise on
\Omega_{1}
, let
t^{*}\in[m,m+1]
such that

. Then it follows from (

z(t^{*})=z(m)-\int_{m}^{t^{*}}G\left(z(s-1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\eta(t^{*}-m)+V(t^{*})-V(m),

(4.52)

so

\displaystyle V(t^{*})-V(m)

\displaystyle=z(t^{*})-z(m)-\eta(t^{*}-m)+\int_{m}^{t^{*}}G\left(z(s-1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq-R+C-\gamma_{0}-\eta,

(4.53)

hence

\sup_{t\in[m,m+1]}\left(V(t)-V(m)\right)\geq-R+C-\gamma_{0}-\eta,

(4.54)

so that

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})\leq\mathbb{P}\,\left(\,\sup_{t\in[m,m+1]}\left(V(t)-V(m)\right)\geq C-R-\gamma_{0}-\eta\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{6}.

(4.55)

Estimate on
\Omega_{2}
.

Next we estimate
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2})
and we will prove that

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2})\leq\frac{5\varepsilon}{6}.

(4.56)

Define for
l\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
, the subsets
\Omega_{2,l}
of
\Omega_{2}
by

\Omega_{2,l}=\{\omega\in\Omega_{2}\mid z(t)\leq-R\hbox{ on }[l+1,m+1),\ \exists\,t\in[l,l+1]:z(t)(\omega)>-R\}.

(4.57)

By assumption,
z(0)\geq-L\geq-R
. So
\Omega_{2}=\bigcup_{l=0}^{m-1}\Omega_{2,l}

and hence

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2})\leq\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2,l})

(4.58)

and in order to estimate
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2})
it suffices to estimate
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2,l})
for
1\leq l\leq m-1
.

Estimate on
\Omega_{2,l}
.

Pointwise on
\Omega_{2,l}
, define for
l\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
,

t_{l}^{*}=\inf\left\{t\in[l,l+1]\mid z(s)\leq-R\hbox{ for all }s\in[t,m+1]\right\}

(4.59)

and note that

t_{l}^{*}\in[l,l+1]\quad\hbox{and}\quad z(t_{l}^{*})\geq-R\quad\hbox{on }\Omega_{2,l}.

(4.60)

Fix
l\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
for a moment. Pointwise on
\Omega_{2,l}
we have

\displaystyle z(m)

\displaystyle=z(t_{l}^{*}+1)-\int_{t_{l}^{*}+1}^{m}G\left(z(s-1)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\eta(m-t_{l}^{*}-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+V(m)-V(t_{l}^{*}+1).

(4.61)

Let us explain in some detail how we obtain numerical approximations of the invariant measures present; see
[
5
]
for an extensive discussion. Define for
0\leq t_{0}\ll T<\infty
the quantities

\nu_{T}(A):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T}\mathbb{P}(x(t)\in A)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\mu_{T}(B):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T}\mathbb{P}(x_{t}\in B)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(5.3)

for measurable sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and
B\subset C[-1,0].
In here,
\nu_{T}
and
\mu_{T}
are probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
and
C[-1,0]
, respectively.
The Krylov–Bogoliubov theorem
[
4
, App. C]
allows us to conclude that
(\mu_{T})_{T\geq 0}
has a convergent subsequence, whose limit we denote by
\mu
. In many applications, the limit
\mu=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mu_{T}
simply exists (this is something we do not prove), which is hence an invariant measure. Note that the existence of this (subsequential) limit
\nu
implies, for example, the existence of a stationary distribution
\mu=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mu_{T}
satisfying
\mu(A)=\nu(\{\varphi\in C[-1,0]:\varphi(0)\in A\})
. Since this construction depends on the choice of the initial condition
z_{0}=\varphi
, different initial data may in principle lead to different invariant measures
\mu
and
\nu
.

As a side remark, recall that properties such as support, ergodicity, or uniqueness of invariant measures obtained through the Krylov–Bogoliubov procedure are generally not known a priori. As explained in
[
5
]
for the stochastic Mackey–Glass equations, a strength of Theorem
1.3
lies within the numerical implementation: it provides a rigorous framework for verifying that the structures observed in, for instance, Figures
1
and
2
are genuine dynamical features rather than numerical artefacts. In particular, when numerical simulations reveal
physical finite-dimensional invariant measures, our theory indicates that these are projections of an underlying invariant measure that is infinite-dimensional.

In view of the above, one obtains a numerical approximation of a stationary distribution
\nu
using discrete sums:

\nu(A)\approx\frac{1}{(M+1)N}\sum_{k=0}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x_{i}(t_{k})),\qquad\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\in A,\\
0&x\not\in A,\end{cases}

(5.4)

where
x_{i}(t_{k})
denotes the
i
-th trajectory at time
t_{k}=\alpha+k\Delta t
, on a uniform grid of length
T=M\Delta t
with step size
\Delta t
, and where
N
denotes the number of trajectories. This discrete formulation directly corresponds to creating a histogram as in Figures
1
and
2
. The phase portraits within these figures are a numerical approximation of the push-forward measure
\nu^{\prime}
of
\mu
under the evaluation map
\varphi\to(\varphi(-1),\varphi(0)),
i.e.,
\nu^{\prime}(A^{\prime})=

\mu(\{\varphi\in C[-1,0]:(\varphi(-1),\varphi(0))\in A^{\prime}\})
for
A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.
Here,
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are finite-dimensional projections of the infinite-dimensional object
\mu.

To ensure that, e.g., (
5.4
) provides a good approximation of the stationary distribution,
N
and
M
should initially be chosen sufficiently large, and the resulting histogram should remain stable under variations in
N
and
M
. By doing so, we tacitly exploit the fact that the limit
\nu=\lim_{T\to\infty}\nu_{T}
exists (and thus not only as a subsequential limit).
We also observe that a single trajectory (
N=1
) suffices to approximate the invariant measure, indicating the measure is ergodic.

Finally, we believe that the invariant measures for equation (
5.2
) are unique whenever
\sigma\neq 0
, because noise has a regularizing effect.
For the deterministic equation (
5.1
), notice that we have a unique invariant measure fully concentrated at zero for
r<\pi/2
and multiple invariant measures for
r>\pi/2
: one corresponding to the now unstable zero equilibrium state, one corresponding to the unique slowly oscillating periodic orbit, and taking convex combinations of the previous two. This follows from the recently established results
[
2
,
18
]
. Theoretically speaking, there may even be values

for which rapidly oscillating periodic solutions exist
[
18
, Sec. 7]
, implying the existence of another invariant measure (no matter its stability). Nevertheless, the presence of noise is expected to destroy unstable invariant structures and, in the case of co-existing attractors, induce metastable switching between basins of attraction. As a consequence, one may indeed expect the stochastic system (
1.7
) to admit a unique non-trivial invariant measure.
