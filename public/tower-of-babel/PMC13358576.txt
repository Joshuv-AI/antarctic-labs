Machine learning-based preoperative prediction of spread through air spaces in clinical stage IA non-small cell lung cancer: a single-center study integrating clinical and CT imaging characteristics
Authors: Xuanyu Zhu, Jinhao Kou, Heng Tao, Xiaolong Liu, Zhihui Yang, Jing Luo, Ciren Yundan, Yi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpread through air spaces (STAS) is a key adverse pathologic feature in clinically early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and is associated with higher recurrence risk, which can undermine the oncologic safety of sublobar resection. Because STAS is typically confirmed only on postoperative specimens and cannot be reliably determined preoperatively, this study aimed to develop and validate machine learning models integrating preoperative clinical and computed tomography (CT) imaging characteristics to predict STAS in clinical stage IA NSCLC to support surgical procedure selection.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 445 patients and randomly assigned them to a training cohort (n=312) and a validation cohort (n=133) at a 7:3 ratio. Candidate predictors were selected using minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) combined with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and inter-feature correlations were assessed with Spearman rank correlation. We developed and compared seven machine-learning models: Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), and naïve Bayes (NB). Model performance was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with AUCs compared using DeLong’s test. Confusion-matrix-derived metrics (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1 score, among others) were also reported. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Brier score, and clinical utility was examined via decision curve analysis (DCA). The final model was interpreted with Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to quantify feature importance.ResultsSTAS was presented in 26.9% of the training cohort and 30.8% of the validation cohort. mRMR and LASSO yielded highly concordant feature selection results, and nine variables were ultimately retained for model development. Among the seven machine-learning algorithms, XGBoost achieved the best overall performance. The AUC was 0.830 in the training cohort, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.781–0.878, and 0.780 in the validation cohort, with a 95% CI of 0.702–0.859. Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed risks, and XGBoost showed the lowest Brier score in the validation cohort (0.171). DCA indicated a favorable net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. SHAP interpretation highlighted pure-solid nodule, spiculation, nodule volume, pleural retraction, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) as the most influential predictors.ConclusionsThe XGBoost model based on preoperative clinical and CT imaging characteristics achieved good discrimination and calibration for predicting STAS in clinical stage IA NSCLC, with potential clinical utility for preoperative risk stratification and surgical decision-making.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study developed and internally validated a machine learning model integrating preoperative clinical and computed tomography (CT) imaging characteristics to predict spread through air spaces (STAS) in patients with clinical stage IA non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).

• Among seven machine learning algorithms, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) showed the best overall performance, with good discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit.

• SHAP analysis identified pure-solid nodule, spiculation, nodule volume, pleural retraction, and carcinoembryonic antigen as the major contributors to STAS risk prediction.

What is known and what is new?

• STAS is an adverse pathological feature associated with recurrence and poor prognosis in early-stage NSCLC, but it is usually confirmed only after surgical resection. Previous studies have explored radiological and clinicopathological predictors of STAS, but reliable preoperative risk stratification remains challenging.

• This study integrated routinely available preoperative clinical and CT imaging characteristics and compared multiple machine learning algorithms for STAS prediction. The XGBoost model provided better overall performance than conventional logistic regression and offered interpretable feature-level explanations using Shapley Additive exPlanations.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This model may provide supplementary preoperative information for individualized surgical assessment, especially when balancing sublobar resection and lobectomy.

• The model should not be used as a stand-alone determinant of surgical procedure selection, and multicenter external validation is required before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related death, imposing a substantial global burden in terms of both incident cases and mortality (1,2). For resectable, clinically early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), lobectomy has long been regarded as the standard anatomical resection (3).

In recent years, with the widespread implementation of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening, an increasing number of small, peripheral, early-stage lesions have been detected. In this context, sublobar resection (segmentectomy or wedge resection) has gradually emerged as an acceptable alternative for carefully selected, low-risk patients and surgical decision-making has increasingly shifted from routine lobectomy to individualized, risk-adapted strategies aimed at preserving lung parenchyma (4-7).

Although multiple randomized trials have demonstrated that, in selected patients with early stage, sublobar resection provides disease-free/recurrence-free survival (DFS/RFS) and overall survival (OS) comparable to lobectomy, recurrence risk among patients with early-stage NSCLC remains markedly heterogeneous and is closely associated with tumor aggressiveness (8). Because tumor-node-metastasis (TNM)staging and conventional imaging alone are often insufficient for precise preoperative risk stratification, surgical decision-making continues to require a careful balance between lung parenchyma preservation and oncologic safety, particularly with respect to recurrence risk and local control (5-7).

Spread through air spaces (STAS) is considered a key pathological risk factor affecting prognosis in early-stage NSCLC. It is characterized by the presence of tumor cells within alveolar air spaces beyond the edge of the main tumor—appearing as tumor cell clusters, micropapillary tufts, solid tumor nests, or discohesive single cells—and has been associated with an increased risk of recurrence and worse survival outcomes (9,10). Available evidence suggests that, among patients with STAS-positive stage I NSCLC, sublobar resection, compared with lobectomy, may be associated with inferior RFS and OS (11-13). Accordingly, accurate preoperative identification of STAS risk is a key prerequisite for determining whether lung parenchyma–sparing surgery can be performed with oncologic safety.

However, definitive identification of STAS typically relies on postoperative pathological evaluation of the entire resection specimen. In contrast, small biopsy samples obtained via bronchoscopy or percutaneous procedures, as well as intraoperative frozen sections, are generally not used as routine preoperative or intraoperative diagnostic approaches for STAS because of inherent limitations in sampling extent, section quality, and detection reproducibility (14-16). Moreover, STAS cannot be reliably identified on routine preoperative imaging, and clinical staging and conventional imaging alone are often insufficient to achieve robust preoperative risk stratification for STAS.

In recent years, machine learning has garnered growing attention for its capacity to learn high-dimensional representations and to capture nonlinear relationships and higher-order interactions between imaging and clinical features, providing a methodological foundation for mining latent phenotypic information from preoperative CT and offering a potential framework for preoperative risk assessment (17-20). Accordingly, we sought to integrate preoperatively available clinical data and radiologic features to develop a reproducible preoperative STAS prediction model for patients with clinical stage IA (cStage IA) NSCLC. We then evaluated the model in the validation cohort to assess its stability and generalizability, and to explore its potential clinical utility in guiding procedure selection between lobectomy and sublobar resection. This study was designed as a clinically oriented machine learning prediction study rather than an algorithm-development study, with the goal of integrating routinely available preoperative variables to support individualized STAS risk stratification. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0429/rc).

Collection of clinical and histopathologic characteristics

Clinical and histopathologic characteristics were obtained from the hospital electronic medical record system. Clinical characteristics included sex, age, surgical procedure, and preoperative serum tumor markers, such as cytokeratin 19 (CK19), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA). Surgical procedure was recorded for baseline description only and was not included in feature selection or model development. In addition, preoperative seven serum tumor-associated autoantibodies (7-TAABs) were also collected, including anti-p53, anti-PGP9.5, anti-SOX2, anti-GAGE7, anti-GBU4-5, anti-MAGEA1, and anti-CAGE. Histopathologic characteristics included histologic subtype, differentiation grade, pathologic stage, the status of STAS, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), perineural invasion (PNI), and visceral pleural invasion (VPI).

Assessment of CT imaging characteristics

All patients underwent preoperative chest CT examinations with two types of CT machines, including SOMATOM Perspective 64 row and UIH uCT760 128 row CT machines. For the Somatom scanner, the acquisition parameters were as follows: tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 100–300 mA, pitch 1.0, reconstructed slice thickness 1.0 mm, and matrix size 512×512. For the UIH scanner, the acquisition parameters were as follows: tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 80–300 mA, pitch 0.8, reconstructed slice thickness 1.0 mm, and matrix size 512×512. The lung window (window width: 1,500 HU, window level: −500 HU) and mediastinal window (window width: 400 HU, window level: 40 HU) settings were fixed. All patients received breath-hold training before scanning and were instructed to hold their breath during image acquisition. The scan range extended from the lung apices to 2 cm below the diaphragm, covering the bilateral axillae and chest wall.

CT-derived quantitative metrics were automatically measured using an artificial intelligence system and were subsequently reviewed by an assessment panel comprising two experienced radiologists and one senior thoracic surgeon. These quantitative characteristics included maximum tumor diameter (for clinical T staging), nodule volume, maximum CT attenuation (HU max), and the solid volume ratio (SVR). CT semantic characteristics were independently evaluated by the panel members blinded to the pathologic findings and STAS status; discrepancies were resolved by consensus discussion. The assessed semantic characteristics included tumor location, affiliated lobe, lobulation, vacuole, spiculation, pleural retraction, air bronchogram, vascular convergence, bronchial cut-off, and spinous process. These CT semantic characteristics have been previously reported in Fleischner Society Glossary of Terms for Thoracic Imaging (2024), and their specific definitions are provided in Table S1 (21).

Histopathologic evaluation

All resection specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 12–48 h and processed according to standard protocols for tissue sampling, paraffin embedding, sectioning, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, followed by evaluation under light microscopy. All histologic slides were independently assessed by a junior pathologist and a senior pathologist according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lung tumors (22). If discrepancies arose, a consensus diagnosis was reached through joint slide review and discussion.

STAS was defined as tumor cells spreading within alveolar air spaces beyond the edge of the primary tumor in the form of micropapillary clusters, solid nests, or single cells. When necessary, immunohistochemical staining was performed to distinguish alveolar macrophages from true STAS tumor cells (Figure 2).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (n) and percentage (%) and compared between groups using the Chi-squared test. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, for normally distributed variables, homogeneity of variance was further evaluated using Levene’s test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test when variances were equal and Welch’s t-test when variances were unequal. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (P25, P75) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A two-sided α level of 0.05 was applied, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patients were randomly split into training and validation cohorts at a 7:3 ratio. All feature selection procedures were performed exclusively within the training cohort. Feature selection was performed to reduce feature redundancy and noise, limit potential overfitting in this single-center cohort, improve model interpretability, and ensure that all algorithms were compared using the same set of input variables. Candidate predictors were prescreened using maximum relevance minimum redundancy (mRMR), followed by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty parameter; variables selected at λ.min were used as model inputs. Pairwise correlations among retained predictors were assessed using Spearman correlation.

For the selected variables, seven machine learning models were developed separately. Model training and parameter settings were performed within the training cohort, and the validation cohort was used only for final performance evaluation. The final models were fixed before being applied to the validation cohort to reduce the risk of data leakage and optimistic performance estimation. Model discrimination was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); AUCs were compared using the DeLong test. Threshold-dependent performance was summarized using confusion matrix-derived metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1 score, among others. Calibration was evaluated using calibration curves and the Brier score. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA) by estimating net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. After identifying the best-performing machine learning model, a multivariable logistic regression model was additionally constructed using the same final selected variables as a conventional statistical reference model. The logistic regression model was trained in the training cohort and evaluated in the validation cohort, and its performance was compared with that of the best-performing machine learning model using ROC analysis, calibration assessment, DCA, and confusion matrix-derived metrics. The optimal model was further interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to quantify global feature importance and to illustrate patient-level contributions.

All statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using R (version 4.4.2), with the following packages: comparegroups, glmnet, mRMRe, rpart, randomForest, xgboost, kknn, lightgbm, riskregression, ROCR, and Dcurves.

Results

Patient cohorts and baseline characteristics

A total of 445 patients were included in this study, including 320 patients with STAS negativity and 125 patients with STAS positivity. The overall cohort was split into a training cohort (n=312) and a validation cohort (n=133) at a 7:3 ratio. There were 84 STAS-positive cases in the training cohort and 41 in the validation cohort, yielding comparable STAS-positive rates between the two cohorts (26.9% vs. 30.8%). As shown in Table 1, the training and validation cohorts were well balanced with respect to major clinical, histopathologic, and CT imaging characteristics, with no statistically significant differences observed between the two cohorts for any variable (all P>0.05).

These histopathologic variables were not included in subsequent model development.

Association between STAS and clinicoradiologic characteristics

As shown in Table 2, in the training cohort, STAS positivity was more frequently observed in male patients and in those with Anti-GAGE7 positivity and CEA positivity (all P<0.05). STAS-positive patients also presented with a more advanced clinical T category (with a higher proportion of cT1c) and underwent lobectomy more often (both P<0.01). Regarding CT imaging characteristics, the STAS-positive group had a higher HU max, larger nodule volume, higher SVR, and more frequently exhibited the lobulation, spiculation, pleural retraction, and bronchial cut-off (all P<0.05).

In the validation cohort, several key preoperative factors showed distributional differences broadly consistent with the patterns observed in the training cohort. Association between STAS and histopathologic characteristics

In the training cohort, STAS positivity was more common in invasive adenocarcinoma (P<0.001) and in tumors with poor differentiation (P<0.001), and it was accompanied by higher rates of LVI, PNI and VPI (all P<0.05). STAS-positive patients also tended to present with more advanced postoperative pathologic T and N categories (both P<0.001), indicating a greater burden of local invasion and pathologic upstaging. In the validation cohort, the associations of histologic subtype, differentiation grade, and LVI with STAS were consistently observed (all P<0.001), and differences in postoperative pathologic T and N categories remained significant (both P<0.05).

Variable selection and correlation analysis

To obtain robust candidate predictors while reducing redundancy and the risk of overfitting, we applied two complementary variable-selection approaches. First, all candidate variables were prescreened using the mRMR algorithm, retaining the top ten variables with high relevance to the outcome and low redundancy among predictors. We then fitted the LASSO regression model and determined the optimal penalty parameter (λ) using ten-fold cross-validation (Figure 3). The model corresponding to λ.min was selected, yielding nine variables: sex, anti-GAGE7, CEA, SVR, nodule volume, HU max, spiculation, pleural retraction, and the bronchial cut-off.

Notably, the two different variable-selection approaches yielded highly consistent results: all nine variables selected by LASSO were also ranked among the top ten candidates identified by mRMR, suggesting good reproducibility of key predictors across different selection frameworks. Based on this concordance, the nine variables were retained as input features for subsequent machine learning models. Spearman correlation analysis among the nine model-input variables showed no strong pairwise correlations (maximum |r|≈0.40), indicating low redundancy among predictors (Figure 4).

Model development and performance comparison

Using the nine selected variables, we developed seven machine learning models and compared their performance: Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), and Naive Bayes (NB).

For discrimination, the AUCs in the training cohort ranged from 0.691 to 0.848. DT achieved the highest AUC (0.848; 95% CI: 0.799–0.898), followed by XGBoost (0.830; 95% CI: 0.781–0.878) and RF (0.821; 95% CI: 0.770–0.872). In the validation cohort, AUCs ranged from 0.586 to 0.780, with XGBoost demonstrating the highest AUC (0.780; 95% CI: 0.702–0.859), followed by DT (0.734; 95% CI: 0.644–0.824). RF, SVM, and NB showed comparable performance (AUC approximately 0.670–0.680), whereas KNN and LGBM performed relatively worse. These findings indicate that the apparent discriminative advantage of DT in the training cohort attenuated substantially in the validation cohort, and its stability and generalizability were inferior to those of XGBoost (Figure 5). To assess the statistical significance of between-model differences in AUC, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the DeLong test. In the training cohort, most comparisons between high-AUC models (DT, RF, and XGBoost) and lower-AUC models (LGBM, SVM, and NB) were statistically significant (P<0.05). In the validation cohort, between-model differences generally narrowed; however, XGBoost still showed significantly better discrimination than RF, SVM, KNN, and LGBM (all P<0.05). In addition, DCA demonstrated that XGBoost provided a net benefit above the reference strategies (treat-all and treat-none) and was generally not inferior to the other models across most threshold probabilities (Figure 6). DT also yielded net benefit within certain threshold ranges, although the range and stability were less favorable than those of XGBoost.

Comparison between XGBoost and the conventional logistic regression model

To further evaluate the potential incremental value of XGBoost over conventional statistical modeling, we constructed a multivariable logistic regression model using the same final selected predictors and compared it with XGBoost in the validation cohort. The logistic regression model achieved an AUC of 0.674 (95% CI: 0.579–0.769) in the validation cohort (Figure 9). The calibration curve and DCA suggested that the logistic regression model provided a certain degree of probabilistic prediction ability and clinical net benefit, although its overall performance remained inferior to that of XGBoost. Further comparison showed that XGBoost outperformed the logistic regression model in the validation cohort, with higher sensitivity (0.732 vs. 0.683), specificity (0.641 vs. 0.587), positive predictive value (PPV) (0.476 vs. 0.424), NPV (0.843 vs. 0.806), F1 score (0.577 vs. 0.523), and balanced accuracy (0.687 vs. 0.635). In addition, XGBoost achieved a lower Brier score than the logistic regression model (0.171 vs. 0.212), indicating better overall accuracy of probabilistic prediction (Table 4). These findings support the superior overall predictive performance of XGBoost compared with conventional logistic regression in the validation cohort.

Model interpretation and SHAP analysis

Based on the global SHAP interpretation of the optimal XGBoost model, the predictive signal was jointly driven by imaging-based invasive phenotypes, lesion burden, and tumor biology-related markers. Pure-solid nodule (SVR =100%), spiculation, nodule volume, pleural retraction, and CEA were the dominant contributors, indicating that the model primarily relied on variables closely associated with invasiveness and malignant potential for overall risk discrimination (Figure 10). The beeswarm plot further illustrated the relationships between feature values and prediction direction: pure-solid nodules, spiculation, pleural retraction, and elevated CEA were generally associated with positive SHAP contributions, shifting the model output toward a higher-risk range. Nodule volume also showed predominantly positive contributions but with a more dispersed distribution, suggesting potential nonlinear effects and feature interactions.

Individual-level SHAP waterfall plots further supported these findings. In the high-risk case, a pure-solid nodule, CEA positivity, larger nodule volume, and the presence of spiculation and pleural retraction jointly contributed positively, shifting the prediction upward from the baseline to the high-risk range. In contrast, in the low-risk case, although some features showed positive contributions, a lower solid component proportion, smaller nodule volume, and CEA negativity provided the dominant negative contributions, pulling the overall output downward and maintaining a low-risk prediction.

In summary, the model’s prediction was not driven by any single feature but reflected the direction and magnitude of multiple feature contributions within an individual. Risk increased when adverse imaging findings co-occurred with biologic high-risk signals, whereas low-risk values of key high-weight features could maintain a low-risk prediction despite localized unfavorable factors.

Discussion

STAS, an important invasive growth pattern in lung cancer, is closely associated with a higher risk of postoperative recurrence and can substantially compromise RFS and OS. However, the identification of STAS largely relies on postoperative pathologic evaluation. If high-risk features can be assessed preoperatively, more precise risk stratification and individualized surgical planning may be achieved, enabling maximal preservation of lung function while maintaining oncologic safety (23,24). In this study, we developed and validated a machine-learning model incorporating CT imaging characteristics and tumor biology-related markers to preoperatively predict the risk of STAS in patients with cStage IA NSCLC. Using mRMR and LASSO regression, we identified nine variables contributing to STAS prediction and subsequently constructed seven machine-learning models. Overall performance evaluation showed that XGBoost demonstrated favorable discrimination in both the training and validation cohorts, achieved the lowest Brier score with the best calibration in the validation cohort, and provided stable net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. Finally, SHAP was used to visualize model interpretability, thereby enhancing model transparency.

Regarding histopathologic characteristics, we observed that STAS was associated with LVI, PNI, and VPI, as well as poorer tumor differentiation, and was more frequently accompanied by more advanced postoperative T and N categories, suggesting a higher tumor burden and greater local invasiveness with increased propensity for recurrence and metastasis. Prior studies have also corroborated these findings (25-27).

Our results indicated that SVR was the most important independent risk factor for STAS, with a markedly increased risk observed in pure-solid nodules, which is consistent with previous studies (28-31). In this study, the STAS positivity rate was 47% among patients with pure-solid nodules (SVR =100%), 24% among those with 50%< SVR <100%, and only 13% among those with SVR <50%. As the proportion of the solid component increased, tumor invasiveness appeared to intensify, accompanied by a stepwise increase in STAS positivity. Consistently, in the interpretability analysis, SVR emerged as the top-ranked contributor, highlighting its indispensable role in driving STAS prediction. HU max, an indicator reflecting the degree of tumor consolidation, was broadly concordant with SVR in capturing tumor invasiveness toward surrounding tissues. Notably, prior studies have primarily focused on tumor maximum diameter and solid component–related metrics in relation to STAS, whereas evidence regarding total nodule volume remains limited and inconsistently reported (32,33). Our study identified total nodule volume as an important positive contributor, suggesting that it may serve as a complementary quantitative metric reflecting lesion burden.

In addition, spiculation and pleural retraction were important predictors of STAS positivity, which is closely aligned with the underlying pathobiology of tumor invasion (34,35). Spiculation reflects tumor infiltration into the surrounding stroma with concomitant desmoplastic reaction, resulting in radiating fibrous strands and potential pathways of outward extension (21). Pleura retraction, in turn, reflects invasion toward adjacent structures in the setting of peritumoral fibrosis, manifesting as linear or band-like opacities tethering the lesion to the pleura (21,36). As tumors progress, increasing infiltrative growth and reactive proliferation within and around the lesion make spiculation and pleural retraction more likely to occur, thereby indicating a higher risk of STAS.

Multiple studies have reported that STAS is more common in male patients, which is consistent with our findings (37,38). However, this association should be interpreted with caution, as it may be influenced by residual confounding, particularly smoking exposure. In addition, sex differences may indirectly affect STAS occurrence through variation in histologic subtype distribution and radiologic tumor burden. Serum CEA plays an important role in the diagnosis and prognostic assessment of lung cancer. CEA positivity may indicate more advanced disease and a substantially increased metastatic potential (34). Prior studies have suggested that elevated preoperative CEA levels are associated with STAS status and are frequently incorporated into preoperative prediction models, which is consistent with our findings (37,39,40). In contrast, anti-GAGE7 has been primarily investigated as a lung cancer-related autoantibody, with multiple multicenter studies focusing on its diagnostic performance and utility for early risk identification. Evidence directly linking anti-GAGE7 to STAS remains limited. Our findings suggest that anti-GAGE7 may reflect a more aggressive tumor biology; however, this association warrants further validation in larger cohorts with appropriate adjustment for potential confounders (41,42).

Given the limitations of “black-box” machine-learning models in clinical practice, we introduced SHAP to interpret the XGBoost model and to quantify each feature’s contribution to STAS risk prediction at both the global and individual levels. The SHAP summary plot indicated that pure-solid status (SVR =100%), spiculation, nodule volume, pleural retraction, and CEA were the most influential features, suggesting that invasive imaging phenotypes, lesion burden, and tumor biology-related signals jointly drove the model’s decisions. Further patient-level visualizations (e.g., waterfall and force plots) illustrated how individual predictors increased or decreased the predicted risk across different patients, thereby improving model transparency and supporting preoperative risk communication and procedure selection.

Although the machine-learning model developed in this study demonstrated favorable predictive performance and discrimination, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study and lacked an independent external validation cohort, which may introduce case-mix bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. To mitigate center effects and improve model generalizability, future work should incorporate multicenter data, implement cross-center calibration and stratified performance assessment, and involve data science specialists to independently verify the technical robustness, reproducibility, and clinical applicability of the proposed model. Second, CT semantic characteristics are semi-subjective variables based on established imaging definitions and rely heavily on radiologists’ visual recognition and experiential judgment, which inherently limits standardization. Future studies will establish standardized CT acquisition and feature-assessment workflows, perform multi-reader agreement analyses (kappa/ICC), prioritize features with high reproducibility, and consider incorporating semi-automated or automated quantitative metrics to reduce subjectivity. In addition, residual confounding in retrospective studies may introduce bias; prospective studies could mitigate confounding through standardized variable collection and approaches such as propensity score methods, thereby improving model robustness and real-world usability. Moreover, although mRMR and LASSO were used to reduce feature redundancy and improve model interpretability, we did not systematically compare model performance with and without this prescreening strategy. Future studies should further evaluate how different feature-selection strategies affect model performance and generalizability. Although the model performed well, the modeling and deployment process remains relatively complex. Future work should explore simpler, more practical approaches without compromising performance, such as risk scores based on a small set of key variables or lightweight models, to enhance interpretability and scalability. Finally, integrating additional biomarkers may better capture tumor biology and further improve predictive accuracy and robustness.

Conclusions

This study developed and internally validated an explainable machine learning model integrating preoperative clinical and CT imaging characteristics to estimate STAS risk in patients with cStage IA NSCLC. Among the seven algorithms, XGBoost showed the best overall performance in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit, and SHAP analysis enhanced model interpretability. These findings suggest that the model may support preoperative STAS risk stratification and risk enrichment, providing supplementary information for individualized surgical assessment while balancing lung parenchyma preservation and oncologic safety. However, it should not be regarded as a stand-alone determinant of procedure selection, and further multicenter external validation is warranted before clinical implementation.
