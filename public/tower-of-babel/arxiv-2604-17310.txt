Interpolating Discrete Diffusion Models with Controllable Resampling
Marcel Kollovieh, Sirine Ayadi, Stephan Günnemann

Abstract
Discrete diffusion models form a powerful class of generative models across diverse domains, including text and graphs. However, existing approaches face fundamental limitations. Masked diffusion models suffer from irreversible errors due to early unmasking, while uniform diffusion models, despite enabling self-correction, often yield low-quality samples due to their strong reliance on intermediate latent states. We introduce IDDM , an Interpolating Discrete Diffusion Model, that improves diffusion by reducing dependence on intermediate latent states. Central to IDDM is a controllable resampling mechanism that partially resets probability mass to the marginal distribution, mitigating error accumulation and enabling more effective token corrections. IDDM specifies a generative process whose transitions interpolate between staying at the current state, resampling from a prior, and flipping toward the target state, while enforcing marginal consistency and fully decoupling training from inference. We benchmark our model against state-of-the-art discrete diffusion models across molecular graph generation as well as text generation tasks, demonstrating competitive performance.

Introduction

Discrete diffusion models
(
Austin et al. 2021
)
have emerged as a powerful approach for generating structures across domains such as proteins
(
Gruver et al. 2023
;
Nisonoff et al. 2024
)
, DNA sequences
(
Sarkar et al. 2024
)
, and molecules
(
Vignac et al. 2022
;
Campbell et al. 2022
)
, and are starting to catch up with autoregressive models in language generation
(
Lou et al. 2023
;
Sahoo et al. 2025
;
Schiff et al. 2024
)
. As with their continuous counterparts, the core idea is to define Markov transitions that gradually corrupt the data, and then learn a model to denoise it by parametrizing the reverse process.
Common forward processes include uniform transitions over the state space or masking, also known as absorbing-state diffusion, in which the state either remains unchanged or transitions to a mask token
(
Austin et al. 2021
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
)
.

While these frameworks work analogously to the continuous case, they introduce considerable limitations.
Masked diffusion models, for example, do not support refinement: once a token is unmasked, it remains fixed throughout the entire generation, restricting the model to correct earlier mistakes and leading to error accumulation
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Boget 2025
)
. Uniform transitions, on the other hand, allow flips back to the prior, but they can still suffer from error accumulation
(
Boget 2025
)
, and are typically outperformed by absorbing-state diffusion approaches
(
Austin et al. 2021
;
Lou et al. 2023
)
.

In this work, we propose
Interpolating Discrete Diffusion Model
(
IDDM
), a generative model for discrete data that derives transition probabilities from a different perspective. Instead of defining a Markovian forward transition to reach a terminal distribution, we directly define the reverse, i.e., the generative distribution. To this end, our transitions make use of three different actions:
(i)
staying in the current state,
(ii)
transitioning to the goal state, and
(iii)
resampling from the prior distribution, see
Figure

1
.

Figure 1
:
Graphical model of IDDM. In each step, our model consists of three actions:
(i)
stay in the current state,
(ii)
flip to the goal state, and
(iii)
sample from the prior distribution. The model can be trained without the need to define a forward process.

By constraining the marginal distribution to match an interpolation from prior to the data distribution, we obtain a family of posterior transitions. The posteriors interpolate between a fully local update without random flips and a state-independent update that discards the current state, enabling us to trade off preservation of the current knowledge and stochasticity.
To demonstrate IDDM’s capability, we evaluate it on molecule generation and language tasks.

\mathbf{q}_{1}
. In each denoising step
\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}
, our model constructs the posterior
p_{\lambda}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Increasing
\lambda
increases the stochasticity and state transitions by shifting probability mass from the current to the predicted and random states, as visualized in the bar plot.

Contributions.

Our contributions can be summarized as:

•

Interpolating reverse dynamics.
We propose the
Interpolating Discrete Diffusion Model (IDDM)
, a simple discrete diffusion model consisting of three different actions, i.e., stay in the current state, transition to the goal state, resample from the prior.

•

Marginal-consistent posterior family.
By enforcing that the marginals adhere to an interpolation between the prior and the data distribution, we derive a family of posterior transitions that trades off preserving the current state against injecting stochasticity.

•

Forward-process interpretation.
We show that the resulting transitions correspond, in general, to a non-Markovian forward process.

•

Empirical evaluation.
We demonstrate that our model achieves competitive performance on well-established benchmarks for molecule generation and language modeling.

Discrete Diffusion Models

Notations.

For clarity and ease of presentation, we use
\mathbf{x}
to denote a single discrete random variable taking values in
\{1,\dots,K\}
.
In sequence modeling,
\mathbf{x}
represents a single token, while in graph modeling, it can represent a single node or a single edge in the graph. We denote the corresponding one-hot encoding by
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\in\Delta^{K-1}
, where
\Delta^{K-1}
is the simplex over
K
categories. We denote by
\Cat(\cdot;\mathbf{y})
the categorical distribution with probability vector
\mathbf{y}\in\Delta^{K-1}
.

Diffusion models learn a Markov chain that involves a forward process to perturb the data
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbf{q}_{0}(\mathbf{x})
to increasingly noisy latent variables
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, for
t\in[0,1]
, and a trained reverse process.
The forward process
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s})
is expressed under the Markovian assumption, and the induced marginal for every latent variable
\mathbf{x}_{t}
can be defined as an interpolation between the clean data and a noisy prior
\mathbf{q}_{1}

(
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
:

p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})\;=\;\Cat(\mathbf{x}_{t};(1\;-\;\alpha_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}\;+\;\alpha_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})\;,

(1)

where
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]
is a strictly decreasing function in
t
, and unless stated otherwise,
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is represented in one-hot form.
The parametrized reverse process
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}),s<t
is trained to optimize a variational lower bound on the log-likelihood:

\displaystyle\log\

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x}_{t(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t(T)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{t(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t(T)})}{p(\mathbf{x}_{t(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t(T)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right],

(2)

where
t(i)=i/T
,
T
is the number of diffusion steps, and the reverse Markov process

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{t(1:T)})=

\displaystyle\qquad p(\mathbf{x}_{t(T)})\,\prod_{i=2}^{T}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t(i-1)}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t(i)})\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t(1)}),

(3)

gradually denoises the latent states
towards the data distribution. There exist two popular variants for interpolating discrete diffusion models: uniform diffusion models with a stationary distribution
\mathbf{q}_{1}=\bm{1}/K
, where
\bm{1}
is a
K-
dimensional column vector of all ones, and masked diffusion models, also known as absorbing state diffusion, where the space is augmented with a special mask token, and the prior distribution is set to
\mathbf{q}_{1}=\mathbf{m}
. Marginal distributions over node and edge attributes as noise have proven to be more suitable for graph generation.

Standard discrete diffusion models tightly couple the reverse process to a fixed forward corruption mechanism. The forward process is defined as a Markov chain
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s})
, and the reverse transitions can be determined via Bayes’ rule:

p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s})p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})}\;,

where
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s})=p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})
follows from the Markov property. As a result, the reverse dynamics are largely determined once the forward kernel and the noise schedule are fixed. This can limit the extent to which reverse updates can be modified independently of the noising process and, consequently, can negatively impact the sample quality. For example, in absorbing-state diffusion, once a token is unmasked, it remains fixed for the remainder of the generative process. This can lead to systematic error accumulation as early mistakes cannot be recovered. Uniform diffusion models, on the other hand, inherently enable self-correction since the latent states
\mathbf{x}_{t}
can be continuously updated throughout the reverse process. However, this mechanism can also introduce errors, as correctly predicted tokens can be randomly resampled from the uniform prior.

In this section, we propose
Interpolating Discrete Diffusion Model (IDDM)
, a simple generative model for discrete data that enables controlled resampling during inference and improves token correction. We provide an overview of IDDM in
Figure

2
.
Our goal is to learn the target distribution
\mathbf{q}_{0}
. Similar to diffusion models, we define a sequence of latent variables
\mathbf{x}_{t}
that interpolate between the data at
t=0
and a prior distribution at
t=1
. However, unlike diffusion models, we do not explicitly define forward, i.e., noising process
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})
, for
s<t
. Instead, we define the reverse-time generative process directly.

We start by defining our posterior transition probability with three actions: (i)
stay
at the current state,
(ii)
flip
to the goal state
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
, or (iii)
resample
from the prior
\mathbf{q}_{1}
. More specifically:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(w_{\mathrm{stay}}\mathbf{x}_{t}+w_{\mathrm{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}+w_{\mathrm{flip}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big),

(4)

with
w_{\mathrm{stay}},w_{\mathrm{flip}},w_{\mathrm{prior}}\geq 0
and
w_{\mathrm{stay}}+w_{\mathrm{flip}}+w_{\mathrm{prior}}=1
.

Marginal distribution.

We constrain the marginal distribution to interpolate between the prior distribution and the goal state:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{t};\,(1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big),

(5)

with
\gamma_{t}\in[0,1]
,
\gamma_{1}=0
and
\gamma_{0}=1
.
This choice enables direct sampling of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
at arbitrary
t
without simulating intermediate states.

Parametrization of transition weights.

To enforce
p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x})=\Cat((1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{s}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})
for all
s<t
, our weights must satisfy the following constraints:

\displaystyle(1)\,w_{\mathrm{stay}}(1-\gamma_{t})+w_{\mathrm{prior}}=1-\gamma_{s},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(2)\,w_{\mathrm{stay}}\gamma_{t}+w_{\mathrm{flip}}=\gamma_{s}.

(6)

We use this to introduce a forgetting parameter

\lambda\in[0,1]
that interpolates between a purely local update (
\lambda=0
) and a global reset to the marginal at time
s
(
\lambda=1
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]
satisfy
\gamma_{0}=1
and
\gamma_{1}=0
, and assume
\gamma
is monotone decreasing.
For any
0<t\leq 1
and
0\leq s<t
, define

\displaystyle\gamma_{s\mid t}:=\frac{1-\gamma_{s}}{1-\gamma_{t}}\in[0,1].

(7)

Then, for every
\lambda\in[0,1]
, the weights

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{stay}}

\displaystyle:=(1-\lambda)\,\gamma_{s\mid t},

(8)

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{prior}}

\displaystyle:=\lambda\,(1-\gamma_{s}),

(9)

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{flip}}

\displaystyle:=(1-\lambda)\,(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})+\lambda\,\gamma_{s}

(10)

satisfy the marginal constraints in equation
6
.
Therefore, the reverse transition can be written equivalently as

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}

(11)

\displaystyle+\Big(1-\big((1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\big)\Big)\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big)

and its marginal satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}),\quad\text{for all }s<t,

(12)

where
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})=\Cat(\mathbf{x}_{t};(1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})
.

We provide a proof for
Theorem

3.1

in
Appendix

A
.

Special cases.

The parameter
\lambda
acts as a forget mechanism controlling how much probability mass is taken from the current state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, and how much is resampled independently from the marginal.
Figure

3
visualizes the transition distributions
p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})
for increasing values of
\lambda
.
At
\lambda=0
, our model reduces to two choices, i.e., either remain in the current state, or flip to the data token. More specifically,

\displaystyle p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};\gamma_{s\mid t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+\Big(1-\gamma_{s\mid t}\Big)\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big).

(13)

This dynamics defines an absorbing diffusion process directed toward the data token
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
. In particular, when
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
, the transition distribution collapses to
p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x})=\Cat\!\big(\mathbf{x}_{s};\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\big)
indicating that
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
is absorbing and every other state
\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
remains non-absorbing, with a nonzero probability of transitioning to
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
.
This behavior is reflected in
Figure

3
, where probability mass is concentrated along the diagonal and the column corresponding to the data point
\mathbf{x}
. For
\lambda=1
, the posterior reduces to:

\displaystyle p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{s}\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big)

(14)

\displaystyle=p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}).

In this case, the latent variables
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\mathbf{x}_{s}
are conditionally independent given
\mathbf{x}
, i.e., the state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is entirely discarded, and the transitions reset to the marginal distribution. This is illustrated in
Figure

3
where all rows collapse to the same distribution, reflecting complete forgetting of the current state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
.

Remark on the forward process.

Our approach does not require instantiating a Markovian noising process
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s})
, for
s<t
, following standard diffusion methods. Instead, we directly sample the noisy latents from the marginal distribution in equation
5
.
For completeness, however, we derive the underlying forward transition step of IDDM via Bayes’ rule,
p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})\propto p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}).
This induces a non-Markovian forward kernel of the form:

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{t};\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\mathbf{m}_{t}+(1-\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})\mathbf{x}_{s}\Big)\;,

(15)

where
\mathbf{m}_{t}=p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})
and
\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\in[0,1]
is a state-dependent resampling probability. This transition admits a simple interpretation: with probability
\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}
, the latent
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is resampled from the marginal distribution, otherwise the state retains the previous state
\mathbf{x}_{s}
with probability
1-\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}
.
We provide a full derivation in
Appendix

B
. Crucially, our marginal is independent of the resampling scheduler
\lambda
, allowing decoupled training and sampling.

3.1
Generative Distribution and optimization

As the true sample
\mathbf{x}
is not known during inference, we train a neural network as a surrogate. We define the generative distribution as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\lambda)\big[\gamma_{s\mid t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})\mathbf{x}_{\theta}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda\big[(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{s}\mathbf{x}_{\theta}\big]\Big),

(16)

where
\mathbf{x}_{\theta}\coloneqq f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
is a neural network prediction. As with standard diffusion models, we aim to optimize the log-likelihood
\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})
. However, due to the intractability of the marginalization, we derive the evidence lower bound (ELBO):

\displaystyle\log p_{\lambda}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\geq

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t(T)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\frac{p_{\lambda}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{t(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t(T)})}{p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t(T)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t(1)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\!\left[\log p_{\lambda}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t(1)})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\;\sum_{i=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\!\left[\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}^{(i)}\right]\;

\displaystyle\qquad-\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{q}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(p(\mathbf{x}_{t(T)}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\big\|\,p(\mathbf{x}_{t(T)})\Big)\bigg]\;,

(17)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}^{(i)}=\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t(i-1)}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})\,\big\lVert\,p_{\lambda}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t(i-1)}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t(i)})\right).

The ELBO is maximized when the KL divergences between the two distributions are minimized, i.e., when the neural network
f_{\theta}
predicts
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
. As the ELBOs share the same optimum across all
\lambda
when the model perfectly predicts
\mathbf{x}
, we only need to train one model, allowing us to change
\lambda
only during inference.
We provide the training and sampling algorithms of IDDM in
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
, respectively.

Expected total number of state transitions.

In the following, we analyze the expected number of state transitions induced by IDDM, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{t(k-1)}\neq\mathbf{x}_{t(k)}
, for
k\in\{1,\cdots,T\}
, along a generation trajectory of
T
steps. Let

C_{k}=\mathbf{1}\Big[\mathbf{x}_{t(k-1)}\neq\mathbf{x}_{t(k)}\Big]

denote the indicator of a state change at step
k
. The expected total number of transitions is then given by:

\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[N_{T}\big]=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{T}C_{k}\Bigg]=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\Big[p_{\lambda}(C_{k}=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t(k)})\Big].

In
Appendix

C
, we show that this expectation admits the form
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[N_{T}\big]=A\lambda+B
, where
A,B\geq 0
. This implies that the expected number of state transitions grows linearly with the resampling coefficient
\lambda
.
In practice, however, the model
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
does not perfectly preserve the marginal, therefore the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{t(k)}
can drift with
\lambda
. We investigate this effect in
Section

4.4
.

Extension to graphs and sequences.

So far, we have considered a single-token data point
\mathbf{x}
. IDDM can be readily applied to arbitrary discrete domains and accommodates arbitrary prior distributions.
In this paper, we apply IDDM to both graph and sequence generation tasks. We represent a graph
G=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{E})
with
N
nodes, where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K_{1}}
is a matrix of one-hot encoded representations of each node
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{K_{1}}
, and
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times K_{2}}
groups the one-hot encoding of each edge
\mathbf{e}_{ij}\in\{0,1\}^{K_{2}}
. Here,
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
denote the number of possible node and edge attributes, respectively. We adopt marginal prior distributions over nodes and edges, as they have proved effective in prior graph diffusion approaches
(
Vignac et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2024
)
. For sequence generation, a sequence is represented as
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times K}
, where
L
is the sequence length and
K
is the vocabulary size. In this work, we mainly focus on uniform priors, as we find that they consistently yield lower performance than absorbing-state diffusion models in language modeling
(
Austin et al. 2021
;
Lou et al. 2023
)
.

5
Related Work

Discrete Diffusion models.

Diffusion models were first introduced for continuous data
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
)
, and have demonstrated strong generative performance across a range of domains, including images
(
Nichol & Dhariwal 2021
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
, audio
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
, and video
(
Ho et al. 2022
)
.
The extension of diffusion models to discrete data was first explored by
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
, which adopted a binomial diffusion process for binary data. Building on this,
Hoogeboom et al. 2021
generalized the framework to categorical random variables using transition matrices with uniform corruption probabilities.
Austin et al. 2021
propose more structured categorical corruption processes for certain data types, such as absorbing-state diffusion for sequences, and discretized Gaussian kernels for ordinal data.

Recent works
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
examine a special case of the corruption process in
Austin et al. 2021
, reformulating it as an interpolation between clean data and a prior distribution. In particular,
Sahoo et al. 2024
focus on masked diffusion models, and derive a simplified expression of the ELBO, showing that it corresponds to a weighted average of cross-entropy losses.
Schiff et al. 2024
extend this to uniform noise models and provide a continuous-time NELBO leading to performance gains.
Sahoo et al. 2025
propose a hybrid model that combines a discrete diffusion process with a Gaussian diffusion process defined directly over one-hot token representations rather than continuous embeddings. Alternative perspectives on discrete diffusion leverage continuous-time Markov chains (CTMC)
(
Campbell et al. 2022
;
Campbell et al. 2024
)
or concrete score matching
(
Lou et al. 2023
)
.

Graph generation.

Graph generation approaches can be categorized into two classes: autoregressive approaches, which generate nodes and edges sequentially, and one-shot approaches, which rely on diffusion-based methods.
Diffusion models leverage the order-agnostic nature of denoising processes to maintain permutation equivariance, and many state-of-the-art approaches further incorporate marginal distributions over node and edge attributes as priors
(
Vignac et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2024
)
.
Recent advances in graph generation have demonstrated that both discrete diffusion models, implemented in either discrete-time settings
(
Vignac et al. 2022
)
or continuous-time formulations
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
, as well as discrete flow-matching approaches
(
Qin et al. 2024
)
, achieve strong performance in different domains, including molecular and synthetic graph generation.
Boget 2025
propose a diffusion model for graphs by assuming that the intermediate noisy states only depend
on the clean data, thereby removing the dependence on partially denoised states. This is closely related to IDDM’s extreme case where the resampling coefficient is equal to 1.

Text generation.

For language modeling, autoregressive transformers
(
Radford et al. 2019
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
have achieved impressive results. However, their sequential token sampling leads to slow inference and limited controllability. To address this, various works explore discrete diffusion models for language generation. In particular, absorbing-state diffusion models
(
Shi et al. 2024
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
have demonstrated strong empirical results; however, they suffer from a key limitation: once a token is unmasked, it cannot be revised, which can cause error accumulation and degrade sample quality. Several predictor-corrector samplers have been proposed
(
Campbell et al. 2022
;
Gat et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
to mitigate this issue, but at the cost of increased computation. In contrast, uniform diffusion models
(
Austin et al. 2021
;
Schiff et al. 2024
)
naturally exhibit a self-correcting property absent in autoregressive and masked diffusion approaches. However, they do not achieve state-of-the-art performance. We note that our IDDM approach naturally enables resampling from arbitrary priors, thereby allowing refinement of tokens for both absorbing-state and uniform diffusion.

6
Discussion

Limitations.

We evaluate our model on graph and text generation. However, our proposed framework applies directly to any other discrete domain, including protein generation
(
Campbell et al. 2024
)
and DNA sequences
(
Stark et al. 2024
)
. Further, our experiments are limited to uniform and marginal priors. Our generative model, however, readily extends to masking-based priors, thereby enabling iterative token refinement.

Future work.

Beyond extending to different domains and prior distributions, future work could investigate the use of guidance and other conditioning techniques. Finally, it would be promising to explore alternative training techniques based on variance reduction or distillation
(
Sahoo et al. 2025
)
.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a discrete diffusion model whose reverse process is expressed by three actions:
(i)
staying in the current state,
(ii)
transitioning to the goal state, and
(iii)
resampling from the prior distribution. By defining a marginal distribution, we derive a family of posteriors that interpolates between a local and a state-independent update, enabling us to directly control the transition frequency, while only training a single model. Further, we show that our reverse process cannot be recovered from a standard Markov noising process, as it corresponds to a non-Markovian forward process. Finally, we demonstrate that IDDM achieves competitive performance on graph and language generation tasks.

Broader Impact

In this work, we propose a generative model for discrete data and apply it to molecule and text generation. As discrete generative models can be applied in various fields, we see potential positive and negative societal impacts.
Generative modeling can accelerate scientific discovery and enhance natural language generation capabilities. However, there are potential societal consequences, specifically those related to the generation of harmful text or designing unsafe drugs.

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

3.1

Theorem A.1
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]
satisfy
\gamma_{0}=1
and
\gamma_{1}=0
, and assume
\gamma
is monotone decreasing.
For any
0<t\leq 1
and
0\leq s<t
, define

\displaystyle\gamma_{s\mid t}:=\frac{1-\gamma_{s}}{1-\gamma_{t}}\in[0,1].

(18)

Then for every
\lambda\in[0,1]
, the weights

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{stay}}

\displaystyle:=(1-\lambda)\,\gamma_{s\mid t},

(19)

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{prior}}

\displaystyle:=\lambda\,(1-\gamma_{s}),

(20)

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{flip}}

\displaystyle:=(1-\lambda)\,(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})+\lambda\,\gamma_{s}

(21)

satisfy the marginal constraints equation
6
.
Therefore, the reverse transition can be written equivalently as

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}

(22)

\displaystyle+\Big(1-\big((1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\big)\Big)\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big)

and its marginal satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}),\qquad\text{for all }s<t,

(23)

where
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})=\Cat(\mathbf{x}_{t};(1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})
.

Proof.

We provide a proof by induction.

As a starting point, we have
p(\mathbf{x}_{t_{1}}\mid\mathbf{x})=\Cat((1-\gamma_{t_{1}})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t_{1}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})
by definition.
For the inductive hypothesis, we assume that for some
t\in[0,1]
, the marginal satisfies
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})=\Cat((1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})
.
The goal is to show that
p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x})=\Cat((1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{s}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})
, for
s<t
.

Let
\mathbf{m}_{t}=(1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
.
By marginalizing out
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and using the linearity of the expectation, we have:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\Cat(\mathbf{x}_{s};w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{x}_{t}+w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{e}_{x})\,\Cat(\mathbf{x}_{s};\mathbf{m}_{t})

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})}[w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{x}_{t}+w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{e}_{x}]\Big)

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{m}_{t}+w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big)

(24)

Now we simplify the expressions using the coefficients in equation
19
and equation
21
:

\displaystyle w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\,\gamma_{s\mid t}\bigl((1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{x}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\frac{1-\gamma_{s}}{1-\gamma_{t}}(1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{x}

(25)

\displaystyle w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=\bigl((1-\lambda)(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})+\lambda\gamma_{s}\bigr)\mathbf{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})\mathbf{e}_{x}+\lambda\gamma_{s}\mathbf{e}_{x}

(26)

Substituting equation
25
and equation
26
into equation
24
yields:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{m}_{t}+w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\lambda)(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{x}+\lambda(\bm{1}-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+(1-\lambda)(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})\mathbf{e}_{x}+\lambda\gamma_{s}\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\big((1-\lambda)(1-\gamma_{s\mid t}(1-\gamma_{t}))+\lambda\gamma_{s}\big)\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\big((1-\lambda)(1-\frac{1-\gamma_{s}}{1-\gamma_{t}}(1-\gamma_{t}))+\lambda\gamma_{s}\big)\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\big((1-\lambda)\gamma_{s}+\lambda\gamma_{s}\big)\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Cat\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{s}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big)

This completes the induction.
∎

Appendix B
Derivation of the forward transition
p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})

In the following, we derive the forward transition step
p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})
.
Recall our reverse process defined for
\lambda\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}

(27)

\displaystyle+\Big(1-\big((1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\big)\Big)\mathbf{e}_{x}\Big)

(28)

\displaystyle=\Cat\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_{s};w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{x}_{t}+w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big)

(29)

Let
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}
be single tokens. We write the categorical parameters in scalar form.
The reverse kernel implies that, for any
i,j\in\{1,\dots K\}
,

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=i\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=j,\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})=w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{1}[i=j]+w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}(i)+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{1}[i=\mathbf{x}].

Let
\mathbf{m}_{t}(j)=p(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{m}_{t}(\cdot)=(1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\cdot)+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{1}[.=\mathbf{x}]
.

We first derive the unnormalized forward probabilities:
We fix
\mathbf{x}_{s}=i
. Then, for each
j
, we have:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{x}_{s}=i,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\propto p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=i\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=j,\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{x})

(30)

\displaystyle\propto\big(w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{1}[i=j]+w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}(i)+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{1}[i=\mathbf{x}]\big)\mathbf{m}_{t}(j)

(31)

\displaystyle\propto\big(w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{1}[i=j]+D_{i}\big)\mathbf{m}_{t}(j),

(32)

where
D_{i}=w_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{q}_{1}(i)+w_{\text{flip}}\mathbf{1}[i=\mathbf{x}]
.

The normalizing constant is
p(\mathbf{x}_{s}=i\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{m}_{s}(i)
. We can also derive it as follows:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{s}=i\mid\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime}}p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=i\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=j^{\prime},\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{x})

(33)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime}}(w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{1}[i=j^{\prime}]+D_{i})\mathbf{m}_{t}(j^{\prime})

(34)

\displaystyle=w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{m}_{t}(i)+D_{i}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\mathbf{m}_{t}(j^{\prime})

(35)

\displaystyle=w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{m}_{t}(i)+D_{i}\;,

(36)

since
\sum_{j^{\prime}}\mathbf{m}_{t}(j^{\prime})=1
. The normalized forward transition of equation
32
is:

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{x}_{s}=i,\mathbf{x})=\frac{\big(w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{1}[i=j]+D_{i}\big)\mathbf{m}_{t}(j)}{\mathbf{m}_{s}(i)}.

(37)

If
i=j
, then:

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}=i\mid\mathbf{x}_{s}=i,\mathbf{x})=\frac{(w_{\text{stay}}+D_{i})\mathbf{m}_{t}(i)}{\mathbf{m}_{s}(i)}.

For
i\neq j
:

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{x}_{s}=i,\mathbf{x})=\frac{D_{i}\mathbf{m}_{t}(j)}{\mathbf{m}_{s}(i)}.

To further simplify equation
37
, we define the coefficient:

\alpha_{i}=\frac{D_{i}}{\mathbf{m}_{s}(i)}=\frac{D_{i}}{w_{\text{stay}}\mathbf{m}_{t}(i)+D_{i}}\in[0,1]\;.

(38)

Then, we can rewrite the distribution over
\mathbf{x}_{t}
as a mixture:

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{x}_{s}=i,\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{i}\mathbf{m}_{t}(j)+(1-\alpha_{i})\mathbf{1}[j=i]\;.

(39)

The final forward transition can be simplified to:

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})=\Cat(\mathbf{x}_{t};\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\mathbf{m}_{t}+(1-\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})\;,

(40)

which means that in the forward transition,
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{s}
with probability
(1-\alpha_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})
, otherwise we sample from the marginal
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim\mathbf{m}_{t}=p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})
.
The underlying forward process of IDDM is therefore non-Markovian as it requires access to the clean state
\mathbf{x}
.

Instantiating the forward transitions for the special cases.

For
\lambda=0
:

The coefficients in the reverse process are simplified to:

w_{\text{stay}}=\gamma_{s\mid t},\quad w_{\text{prior}}=0,\quad\text{and }w_{\text{flip}}=1-\gamma_{s\mid t}.

Therefore,
D_{i}=(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})\mathbf{1}[i=x]
.
The forward transition is:

•

Case 1:
if
\mathbf{x}_{s}\neq\mathbf{x}
i.e.,
(i\neq x)
, then
D_{i}=0
and the mixing coefficients
\alpha_{i}=0
according to equation
38
. If we plug in these terms to equation
40
, we get

p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})=\Cat(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}})\;.

•

Case 2:
if
\mathbf{x}_{s}=\mathbf{x}
, then
D_{i}=(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})
, and the coefficient
\alpha
in equation
40
simplifies to

\alpha=\frac{1-\gamma_{s\mid t}}{(1-\gamma_{s\mid t}+\gamma_{s\mid t}\mathbf{m}_{t})}\;.

For
\lambda=1
:

The coefficients of the generative process are

w_{\text{stay}}=0,\quad w_{\text{prior}}=(1-\gamma_{s}),\quad\text{and }w_{\text{flip}}=\gamma_{s}

.
Therefore, the Bayes expression in equation
37
collapses to:

p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{x}_{s}=i,\mathbf{x})=\frac{D_{i}\mathbf{m}_{t}(j)}{D_{i}}=\mathbf{m}_{t}(j)\;.

This implies that the forward transition is
conditionally independent of
\mathbf{x}_{s}
given
\mathbf{x}
and corresponds directly to the marginal

p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x})=\Cat\Big((1-\gamma_{t})\mathbf{q}_{1}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big).

Appendix C
Number of expected transitions

We can analytically derive the expected number of transitions, i.e., number of transitions where
\mathbf{x}_{s}\neq\mathbf{x}_{t}
.
First, we recall the update probability:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=j\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=i,\mathbf{x})=(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}\mathbf{1}[i=j]+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(j)+w_{\mathrm{flip}}\mathbf{1}[j=\mathbf{x}].

(41)

A transition occurs when
j\neq i
. We define the indicator variable
C=\mathbf{1}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x}_{s}]
. Now we want to derive the probability of a transition:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(C=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=i,\mathbf{x})=1-p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=i\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=i,\mathbf{x}).

(42)

Next, we condition the probability
p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=j\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=i,\mathbf{x})
on two events:

Case 1:
\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x}

In this case, the last term drops out out and the transition simplifies further:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),

(43)

and therefore:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(C=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=1-(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}-\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),

(45)

\displaystyle=1-\gamma_{s\mid t}+\lambda\gamma_{s\mid t}-\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

(46)

\displaystyle=A+\lambda\left(\frac{1-\gamma_{s}}{1-\gamma_{t}}-(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)

(47)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{A}_{\geq 0}+\lambda\underbrace{\left((1-\gamma_{s})\left(\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{t}}-\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)\right)}_{\geq 0}

(48)

Case 2:
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}

Here, we plug in the value of
w_{\mathrm{flip}}
:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}+\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x})+(1-\lambda)\,(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})+\lambda\,\gamma_{s}

(49)

And therefore:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(C=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=1-(1-\lambda)\gamma_{s\mid t}-\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x})-(1-\lambda)\,(1-\gamma_{s\mid t})-\lambda\,\gamma_{s}

(50)

\displaystyle=1-(1-\lambda)-\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x})-\lambda\,\gamma_{s}

(51)

\displaystyle=\lambda-\lambda(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x})-\lambda\,\gamma_{s}

(52)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(1-(1-\gamma_{s})\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x})-\gamma_{s}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle=\lambda\underbrace{\left((1-\gamma_{s})(1-\mathbf{q}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\right)}_{\geq 0}

(54)

Finally, we write down the number of total expected transitions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}C_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[C_{t}\right]

(56)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[C_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]p(\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x})+\mathbb{E}\left[C_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x}\right]p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x})

(57)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{T}p_{\lambda}(C_{t}=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x})+p_{\lambda}(C_{t}=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\neq\mathbf{x}),

(58)

which is a mixture of two functions that are linear with respect to
\lambda
. As the mixture parameters are independent of
\lambda
, the total expected value is linear.

D.4
Evaluation metrics

D.4.1
Text Generation

Perplexity.

Perplexity is computed as the exponential of the negative average token-level log-likelihood over the evaluation data. We report the perplexities on the test splits following
Sahoo et al. 2024
.

Generative perplexity.

We report generative perplexity for unconditionally generated sequences, following
(
Sahoo et al. 2025
)
. We measure it using GPT-2 large
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
. We further report sample entropy to assess diversity.

D.4.2
Graph Generation

We evaluate the graph generation with standard molecule metrics. We report the validity, uniqueness, i.e., proportion of generated molecules with different SMILES string, and novelty, i.e., proportion of generated molecules not in the training set. Furthermore, we report the Frechet ChemNetDistance (FCD)
(
Preuer et al. 2018
)
, which measures the embedding similarity of the generated molecules with respect to the training set. Finally, we report the Scaffold similarity and SNN.
