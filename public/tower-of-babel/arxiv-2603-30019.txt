A McKean–Pontrygin maximum principle for entropic-regularized optimal transport
Sebastian Reich

Abstract
. This note outlines a mean-field approach to dynamic optimal transport problems based on the recently proposed McKean–Pontryagin maximum principle. Key aspects of the proposed methodology include i) avoidance of sampling over stochastic paths, ii) a fully variational approach leading to constrained Hamiltonian equations of motion, and iii) a unified treatment of deterministic and stochastic optimal transport problems. We also discuss connections to well-known dynamic formulations in terms of forward-backward stochastic differential equations and extensions beyond classical entropic-regularized transport problems.



1.
Problem statement

We wish to bridge two given distributions
\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{T}
in a variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
along the controlled stochastic differential equation

(1)

{\rm d}\tilde{X}_{t}=U_{t}{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2}\Sigma^{1/2}{\rm d}\tilde{B}_{t},

subject to minimizing the cost

(2)

\mathcal{J}(U)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{X}_{t}}\|U_{t}\|^{2}_{R}\,{\rm d}t

in the control
U=U_{[0,T]}
,
U_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
,
d_{u}=d_{x}
, and under constraints
\tilde{X}_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
and
\tilde{X}_{t}\sim\pi_{T}
. Here,
\|u\|_{R}
denotes the weighted norm defined by

(3)

\|u\|^{2}_{R}=u^{\rm T}R^{-1}u=R^{-1}:uu^{\rm T}

for given symmetric positive definite matrix
R
. The problem reduces to the classical optimal transport problem for
\Sigma=0
and
R=I
and to the Schrödinger bridge problem for
\Sigma=R=I

[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

The goal of this note is to formulate a mean-field formulation of the Schrödinger bridge problem based on an extension of the classical Pontryagin maximum principle
[
4
]
. More precisely, the proposed formulation extends the McKean–Pontryagin maximum principle, first introduced in
[
5
]
, to the general coupling problem (
1
)–(
2
).

We note that the subsequent formulations can be easily extended to stochastic differential equations of the form

(4)

{\rm d}\tilde{X}_{t}=b(\tilde{X}_{t}){\rm d}t+GU_{t}{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2}\Sigma^{1/2}{\rm d}\tilde{B}_{t}

for given
b(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
G\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}\times d_{u}}
. More specifically, consider controlled underdampled Langevin dynamics, which leads to
X_{t}=(Q_{t}^{\rm T},V_{t}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
,

(5)

b(q,v)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v\\
0\end{array}\right),\quad G=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
I\end{array}\right),\quad\Sigma=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&\tilde{\sigma}I\end{array}\right).

One can also extend the variational formulation to multiplicative noise
\Sigma(x)
and mean-field formulations such as the Kalman–Wasserstein gradient flow
[
6
]
, which corresponds to
R=\Sigma=C(\rho_{t})
with
C(\rho_{t})
the covariance matrix of
X_{t}\sim\rho_{t}
.

2.
McKean–Pontryagin maximum principle

We apply the McKean–Pontryagin variational formulation from
[
5
]
with states
X_{t}(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and co-states
P_{t}(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
. The distribution of
X_{t}(a)
is denoted by
\rho_{t}
. Here,
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
are labels that remain constant and serve as independent variables. The proposed variational formulation will ensure that
X_{t}
and
\tilde{X}_{t}
agree in law for all
t\in[0,T]
.

In a first step, we introduce initial and final conditions in a weak form; that is,

(6)

X_{0}(a)=b_{0},\qquad X_{T}(a)=b_{T}

for suitable boundary values
b_{0}=b_{0}(a)
and
b_{T}=b_{T}(a)
such that

(7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}f(X_{0}(a))\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}f(x)\pi_{0}(x){\rm d}x=\pi_{0}[f]

and

(8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}f(X_{T}(a))\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}f(x)\pi_{T}(x){\rm d}x=\pi_{T}[f]

for all suitable test functions
f(x)
. This formulation takes into account that there is a relabeling symmetry, which preserves the prescribed marginal densities
\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{T}
.

Next, following
[
5
]
, we state the action functional
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(X,P,U,\beta,\psi)
defined by

(9a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=

\displaystyle\,\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\left(\langle P_{t},\dot{X}_{t}-U_{t}\rangle-\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t}))+\frac{1}{2}\|U_{t}\|_{R}^{2}\right)\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a\,{\rm d}t

(9b)

\displaystyle\,-\,\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\langle P_{t}-\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}),\beta_{t}\rangle\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a\,{\rm d}t

(9c)

\displaystyle\,-\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\psi_{T}(X_{T})\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a+\pi_{T}[\psi_{T}]+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\psi_{0}(X_{0})\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a-\pi_{0}[\psi_{0}]

with inner product
\langle a,b\rangle=a^{\rm T}b
. We introduce the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(X,P,U,\beta,\psi)

(10)

\mathcal{H}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\left(\langle P,U\rangle+\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi(X)-\frac{1}{2}\|U\|^{2}_{R}+\langle\beta,P-\nabla_{x}\psi(X)\rangle\right)\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a

and note that

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=

\displaystyle\,\int_{0}^{T}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\langle P_{t},\dot{X}_{t}\rangle\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a-\mathcal{H}(X_{t},P_{t},U_{t},\beta_{t},\psi_{t})\right\}{\rm d}t

(11b)

\displaystyle\,-\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\psi_{T}(X_{T})\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a+\pi_{T}[\psi_{T}]+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\psi_{0}(X_{0})\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a-\pi_{0}[\psi_{0}].

We note that
\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi(x)
in (
10
) acts as a state-dependent running cost similar to stochastic optimal control
[
7
]
; the main difference being that the cost is defined implicitly through the relation
P_{t}=\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t})
. As will be verified in the following,
\psi_{t}(x)
solves the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation
[
7
]
.

Taking variations of (
9
) with respect to
P_{t}
,
X_{t}
,
\beta_{t}
,
\psi_{t}
, and
U_{t}
, the evolution equations

(12a)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta P_{t}}=U_{t}+\beta_{t},

(12b)

\displaystyle-\dot{P}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta X_{t}}=\nabla_{x}(\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t}))-D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})\,\beta_{t}

emerge subject to the following constraints:

(13a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\beta_{t}}=P_{t}-\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}),

(13b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\psi_{t}}=\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\rho_{t}(X_{t})\left\{\beta_{t}+\Sigma\nabla_{x}\log\rho_{t}(X_{t})\right\}\right),

(13c)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta U_{t}}=P_{t}-R^{-1}U_{t}.

Here, it has been used in (
13
b) that

(14a)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t}(a))\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(x)\,\rho_{t}(x){\rm d}x

(14b)

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{t}(x)\,\Sigma\nabla_{x}\log\rho_{t}(x))\,\psi_{t}(x)\,{\rm d}x

and

(15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\langle\beta_{t}(X_{t}(a)),\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}(a))\rangle\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{t}(x)\,\beta_{t}(x))\,\psi_{t}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

The boundary conditions are given by

(16)

\rho_{0}=\pi_{0},\qquad\rho_{T}=\pi_{T},\qquad P_{0}=\nabla_{x}\psi_{0},\qquad P_{T}=\nabla_{x}\psi_{T},

which arise from variations with respect to
X_{0}
,
\psi_{0}
and
X_{T}
,
\psi_{T}
, respectively. Here we have assumed for simplicity that
\rho_{0}(x)=\pi_{0}(x)>0
everywhere. The potentials
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{T}
are unknown and arise from the boundary constraints on the density
\rho_{t}
. We also find that
\beta_{t}
is not uniquely determined by (
13
b); but a natural choice is

(17)

\beta_{t}=-\Sigma\nabla_{x}\log\rho_{t}(X_{t}).

The gauge freedom in the choice of
\beta_{t}
is a consequence of the relabelling symmetry of our McKean–Pontryagin formulation. We will demonstrate below that, in fact,
\beta_{t}
can be arbitrarily chosen while still providing the optimal control law
u_{t}(x)=R\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(x)

[
5
]
.

It follows from the Lagrangian variational principle
[
8
]
that the Hamiltonian (
10
) is preserved and takes the constant value

(18)

\mathcal{E}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|U_{t}\|_{R}^{2}+\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})\right)\rho_{0}(a)\,{\rm d}a

along solutions of (
12
).

The optimal transport formulation of Benamou–Brenier is recovered for
\Sigma\to 0
and
R=I

[
3
]
. Furthermore, for
\Sigma=R=I
, we have found a (deterministic) mean-field formulation of the dynamic Schrödinger bridge problem. Indeed, the law,
\rho_{t}(x)
, of
X_{t}
satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation

(19)

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\mathcal{L}_{u_{t}}^{\dagger}\rho_{t},

while
\psi_{t}(x)
satisfies the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

(20)

-\partial_{t}\psi_{t}=\mathcal{L}_{u_{t}}\psi_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\|u_{t}\|_{R}^{2}

with generator
\mathcal{L}_{u_{t}}f=\langle\nabla_{x}f,u_{t}\rangle+\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}f
and control law
u_{t}(x)=R\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(x)

[
7
]
. More precisely, it follows from

(21)

\dot{P}_{t}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t})=\partial_{t}\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t})+D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})\dot{X}_{t}

together with the evolution equations (
12
) for
X_{t}
and
P_{t}
that

\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}
satisfies

(22)

-\partial_{t}\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t})=D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})U_{t}+\nabla_{x}(\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})),

which implies (
20
). Furthermore, this calculation demonstrates that the time evolution of
\psi_{t}(x)
is independent of the specific choice of
\beta_{t}
as already observed in
[
5
]
.

Note that the potentials
\psi_{t}
are directly related to the Schrödinger potentials
\phi_{t}
and
\hat{\phi}_{t}

[
3
]
via

(23)

\hat{\phi}_{t}=e^{\psi_{t}},\qquad\rho_{t}=\phi_{t}\hat{\phi}_{t}.

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Remark 1
.

It is possible to replace the potential
\psi_{t}
and
\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}
in the variational principle by a vector-valued function
\Psi_{t}
leading to the modified Hamiltonian

(24)

\mathcal{H}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\left(\langle P,U\rangle+\Sigma:D_{x}\Psi(X)-\frac{1}{2}\|U\|^{2}_{R}+\langle\beta,P-\Psi(X)\rangle\right)\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a.

Taking variations with respect to
\Psi_{t}
then leads to the constraint

(25)

0=\beta_{t}+\Sigma\nabla_{x}\log\rho_{t}(X_{t}).

Furthermore, a regularizing penality term

(26)

\mathcal{J}(\Psi)=\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\|P-\Psi(X)\|^{2}\,\rho_{0}(a){\rm d}a,

\gamma>0
, can be added to the Hamiltonian (
24
) without changing the resulting equations of motion
[
9
]
. In this context, we briefly describe a connection to adjoint matching
[
10
,
3
]
. Since
U_{t}=R\,\Psi_{t}(X_{t})
, the constraint (
13
a) or, equivalently, minimizing (
26
)
can be viewed as matching the adjoint
P_{t}
and the control
U_{t}
. We again mention the observation from
[
5
]
that the resulting control law
u_{t}(x)=R\,\Psi_{t}(x)
is independent of the specific choice of
\beta_{t}
as long as
\Psi_{T}
is specified appropriately. Finally, if the assumption is dropped that
\Psi_{t}
is itself the gradient of a potential
\psi_{t}
, ideal barotropic fluid dynamics
[
11
]
can also be treated within this framework by setting
\Sigma\equiv 0
and adding the internal energy
e(\rho_{t})
as a cost function to the Hamiltonian (
24
). It would be of interest to discuss the effect of a non-zero
\Sigma
in the context of barotropic fluid dynamics.

Example 1
.

Consider the specific case of a given target distribution
\pi_{0}
, which one wishes to transform into a standard Gaussian at time
T>0
; that is,
\pi_{T}={\rm N}(0,I)
. Then, one can consider the following iterative procedure to find the optimal control
U_{t}
, which follows the idea of iterative proportional fitting
[
3
]
. A natural initial guess is
U_{t}=0
,
which formally follows from
P_{t}=0
for
t\in[0,T]
. The resulting distribution of
X_{t}
at time
T
is denoted by
\rho_{T}^{(0)}
, suggesting the following corrected boundary condition

(27)

\psi_{T}^{(1)}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}-\log\rho_{T}^{(0)}(x)=\log\pi_{T}(x)-\log\rho_{T}^{(0)}(x)

and
P_{T}(a)=\nabla\psi_{T}^{(1)}(X_{T}(a))
.
Upon induction, one derives

(28)

\psi_{T}^{(K)}(x)=K\,\log\pi_{T}(x)-\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\log\rho_{T}^{(k)}(x).

and
P_{T}(a)=\nabla\psi_{T}^{(K)}(X_{T}(a))
. The process converges as
\nabla_{x}\log\rho_{T}^{(k)}\to\nabla_{x}\log\pi_{T}
.

3.
A non-variational Lagrangian approach

The following closely related approach has been proposed in
[
12
]
. In the setting of this note, the formulation is given by

(29a)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}=

\displaystyle\,U_{t}-\Sigma\nabla_{x}\log\rho_{t}(X_{t}),

(29b)

\displaystyle-\dot{Y}_{t}=

\displaystyle\,-\frac{1}{2}\|U_{t}\|^{2}_{R}+\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})+\langle\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}),\Sigma\nabla_{x}\log\rho_{t}(X_{t})\rangle,

(29c)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\,Y_{t}-\psi_{t}(X_{t}),

(29d)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\,R^{-1}U_{t}-\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}).

Since

(30)

\dot{Y}_{t}=\partial_{t}\psi_{t}(X_{t})+\langle\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}),\dot{X}_{t}\rangle,

equation (
29
b) constitutes a Lagrangian formulation of (
20
) along solutions of (
29
a). However, the formulation does not satisfy a Hamiltonian variational principle
[
8
]
.

4.
Stochastic McKean–Pontryagin maximum principle

The time evolution of
\psi_{t}(x)
does not depend on the specific choice of
\beta_{t}
. This fact has already been exploited in the numerical implementations proposed in
[
5
]
. Here we use this freedom to build a link to forward-backward SDE formulations
[
7
]
of stochastic optimal control in the context of the Schrödinger bridge problem. Hence, let us formally set

(31)

\beta_{t}=\sqrt{2}\Sigma^{1/2}\dot{B}_{t}

with
B_{t}
denoting
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. Stochastic integrals are interpreted in Stratonovitch form
[
13
]
. Then (
12
)-(
13
) give rise to the pathwise formulation

(32a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\,U_{t}\,{\rm d}t+{\rm d}\beta_{t},

(32b)

\displaystyle-{\rm d}P_{t}

\displaystyle=\,\nabla_{x}(\Sigma:D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t}))\,{\rm d}t-D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})\circ{\rm d}\beta_{t},

(32c)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\,P_{t}-\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}),

(32d)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\,P_{t}-R^{-1}U_{t}

with
X_{t}=X_{t}(a,B_{[0,T]})
and
P_{t}=P_{t}(a,B_{[0,T]})
. However, while delivering the optimal control law
u_{t}(x)=R\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(x)
, this stochastic formulation is not optimal from a variational perspective.

Furthermore, replacing the Stratonovitch integral in (
32
) by its Itô interpretation, one obtains a set of evolution equations formally equivalent to the forward-backward stochastic differential equations associated with stochastic optimal control
[
7
]
; that is,

(33a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\,U_{t}\,{\rm d}t+{\rm d}\beta_{t},

(33b)

\displaystyle-{\rm d}P_{t}

\displaystyle=\,-D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})\,{\rm d}\beta_{t},

(33c)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\,P_{t}-\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(X_{t}),

(33d)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\,P_{t}-R^{-1}U_{t}.

It is important to note that
\psi_{t}(x)
is a deterministic function despite the fact that both
P_{t}
and
X_{t}
depend on
B_{[0,T]}
. A related formal equivalence has also been observed in the context of (
29
)

[
12
]
.

5.
Closing remarks

An extension to stochastic differential equations (
4
) subject to given marginal distributions fits naturally within the proposed framework and provides an alternative to approaches based on forward-backward stochastic differential equations
[
7
]
. It is possible to consider diffusion matrices
\Sigma
and drift terms
b
, which depend on the law,
\rho_{t}
, of
X_{t}
.

It would also be of interest to treat diffusion matrix
\Sigma
as an additional control term with, for example, reward

(34)

-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\Sigma\|^{2}_{\rm F}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\Sigma:\Sigma,

\gamma>0
. A formal application of Pontryagin’s maximum principle to an appropriately extended Hamiltonian (
10
) then leads to the optimal choice

(35)

\Sigma(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma}D_{x}^{2}\psi_{x}(x)

and (
18
) becomes

(36a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|U_{t}\|_{R}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t}):D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(X_{t})\right)\rho_{0}\,{\rm d}a

(36b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|R\nabla_{x}\psi_{t}(x)\|_{R}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|D_{x}^{2}\psi_{t}(x)\|_{\rm F}^{2}\right)\rho_{t}(x)\,{\rm d}x,

which places a smoothness penalty on
\psi_{t}
. Note that our mean-field approach does not require the symmetric matrix
\Sigma
to be positive definite; however, the connection to a stochastic differential equation of the form (
1
) is no longer straightforward.

Discrete-time interacting particle approximations can be obtained by discretizing the action (
9
) in label space and time
[
14
]
. See also
[
5
]
for numerical approximations of
\beta_{t}
and
\psi_{t}
. The mean-field formulation (
12
)-(
13
), based on (
9
), is expected to lead to less noisy approximations than its stochastic counterpart (
32
). A detailed numerical comparison of different methodologies based on mean-field ordinary differential equations and forward-backward stochastic differential equations for optimal transport problems is left for future work.
