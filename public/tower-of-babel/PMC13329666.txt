Preserving predictive information under biologically plausible compression
Authors: Sylvia C L Durian, Kyle Bojanek, Olivier Marre, Stephanie E Palmer, Lilianne Mujica-Parodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinal ganglion cells show high convergence onto their downstream projections, which poses a problem for information transfer: how can information be preserved through a synaptic layer that has significantly more inputs than outputs? Lossy compression suggests many efficient, yet computation-agnostic, methods for reading out input stimuli or activity patterns. Focusing on prediction as a ubiquitous computation in the brain, we compare compressions that explicitly retain predictive information to common neural compression frameworks that do not. We find evidence that compressing retinal inputs to perform optimal predictive computations allows putative downstream neurons to predict the future near-optimally across natural scenes. Other sensory systems also exhibit compression in their processing hierarchies, and we hope that our framework will be useful in cases where it is not yet known how information about a specific computation is maintained under compression.Keywords: prediction, information theory, natural scenes, population coding, downstream readout

Producing successful behavior, such as escaping predators, requires the visual system to overcome significant sensory processing delays by predicting the future state of the world. Neurons in the eye capture some of the most predictive features of visual information, but this information must be accessible to downstream areas that receive synaptically compressed retinal inputs. We tested how carefully retinal activity must be compressed to preserve predictive information in natural scenes. Biologically plausible compressions that are agnostic to prediction can preserve substantial future information, but only compression optimized for this task extracts generalizable motifs that allow for prediction in any natural scene. This suggests that neural connections are organized to optimally compress inputs to downstream neurons for the task of prediction.

Introduction

Several sensory pathways exhibit both compression and expansion at different computational stages, as shown in neural recordings from successive regions in a processing hierarchy (1–4), and to help computational flow through layers of deep neural networks (5, 6). Whereas divergence is crucial for pattern separation (7, 8) and parallel processing (9), convergence supports information integration (10) and can help restructure information into a more efficient representation (11, 12). Convergence in particular poses a problem for information transfer, since it makes it difficult to retain all the input information. Previous work has used a compressed-sensing framework (13–15) to suggest that input stimuli or input neural activity patterns can be preserved by using random projections (16, 17), which was validated in an idealized network (18, 19). However, the job of neural systems is to drive behavior, not to reconstruct inputs, and not all input features are important for behavior. Therefore, the efficient way to coarse-grain, or compress, would be to keep only the bits that matter for behavior. We explore this possibility using an information bottleneck approach (20–22), which is an established framework for studying compression achieved by throwing away task-irrelevant information.

Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), the output cells of the retina, are particularly suited to studying how information about biologically relevant computations can be preserved under coarse-graining. RGC axons form the optic nerve, the sole channel carrying information about the visual world to the brain (23), which then synaptically converges onto several retinal projection areas. For instance, RGCs provide converging inputs to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus at an average rate of 10–1 in mice (24), although the number of inputs that significantly affect postsynaptic activity may be closer to 3 (24, 25). Another key projection area is the optic tectum (OT in amphibians, fish, reptiles, etc.; superior colliculus in mammals), which receives input at a rate of roughly 6–1 in mice (26, 27).

Moreover, a large body of previous work has identified many important computations in the retina which downstream areas must be able to read out from their compressed inputs. For example, RGCs show a variety of nonlinear processing characteristics associated with motion prediction (28–31), which is necessary to avoid predators, catch prey, and navigate complex environments. In contrast to theories of predictive coding in which differences from an expected signal are encoded to efficiently transfer information (32–34), here we study bottom-up encoding of the predictive features of spatiotemporal structure in sensory stimuli. RGC population activity has internal temporal correlations which provide a near-optimal representation of future information about the external sensory world (22), indicating that prediction is an essential computation in the retina. The OT has a central role in making rapid eye and head movements in response to visual stimuli (35, 36) and is involved in fast innate behaviors like escaping and freezing in mice and amphibians such as salamanders and toads (37–40), suggesting that it relies on predictive information from the retina in order to drive successful behavior. We aim to understand how downstream areas like OT are able to perform predictive computations using the compressed information they receive from RGCs.

Information bottleneck theory and the animal’s behavioral goals guide us to hypothesize that downstream areas carry a near-optimal but compressed representation of the future of their natural environment. We compare optimized and prediction-agnostic coarse-grainings of RGC inputs to the full, uncompressed predictive information to determine how much information can be preserved under compression without taking readout of the most likely future input into account. If a prediction-agnostic coarse-graining can reach near-optimal readout, downstream areas might not waste energy and resources to actively optimize their RGC inputs. However, regardless of how compression is achieved, the amount of information received by a downstream neuron also depends on which RGCs it receives input from. Downstream circuits decode the visual scene from action potentials generated by a diversity of RGC types (41, 42), which encode different visual features (43). The retina’s output channels relay these different features to the brain, but our understanding of this process is complicated by the fact that some RGC types simultaneously code for multiple features in species such as mice and salamanders (44–46). This can be beneficial: activity from across multiple direction-selective ganglion cell (DSGC) subtypes in mice enhances decoding of visual features and can help resolve ambiguities in single-cell responses (45, 47, 48). The direction-selectivity of DSGCs is largely attributed to GABAergic input from starburst amacrine cells (45, 49), illustrating how differing inputs to RGCs leads to their encoding of different visual features. This motivates us to explore how RGC input composition affects coarse-grained outputs. We hypothesize that RGCs with convergent inputs can be efficiently coarse-grained together to reduce redundancy and optimally preserve predictive information in natural scenes.

We use methods from information theory to investigate optimal downstream readout of predictive information and show that common neural coarse-graining functions that do not explicitly take the future into account can preserve a surprisingly high proportion of the optimal predictive information. However, an efficient coarse-graining should ideally be able to predict the future in any natural scene (50–52), and we demonstrate that only a coarse-graining function explicitly optimized for prediction can also retain near-optimal predictive information across scenes. This generalization suggests that downstream mechanisms may optimize their compressed RGC inputs specifically for the task of prediction. Finally, efficient compression depends not only on the coarse-graining algorithm but also on which input cells are combined, and we determine that coarse-graining RGCs with shared bipolar inputs results in significantly more predictive information than combining random RGCs. Our results suggest a biologically plausible way for downstream areas to efficiently encode the compressed predictive information they receive from the retina.

Results

We use dense extracellular recordings from larval tiger salamander RGCs that were made while presenting the retina with naturalistic movies (53) (Fig. 1A). In this dataset, several movies were chosen to represent the wide variety of scenes that a salamander might encounter during its lifetime (Fig. 1B). These include trees moving in the wind, grasses rustling, fish swimming, and woodland underbrush as viewed by a moving camera to imitate the animal’s self-motion. Movies were presented in pseudorandom order and a white noise checkerboard stimulus was presented at the beginning and end to probe the classical receptive fields. Salamanders are an intriguing model species because they hatch underwater and move onto land as juveniles, then alternate between aquatic and terrestrial lifestyles as adults (51, 52). However, their retinal structure remains largely the same across these stages (52, 54). This allows us to study a wide diversity of natural scenes using larval population responses.

Computation-agnostic compression preserves considerable predictive information

To test how we can extract compressed predictive information in practice, we compare coarse-grainings that are agnostic to the future to an optimized predictive coarse-graining. We model an optimal downstream neuron with a single-layer perceptron coarse-graining (PCG) (56), which is a linear weighting of inputs followed by a sigmoid nonlinearity (Fig. 1D top). This is a biologically feasible, single-step computation. The inputs to the PCG represent inputs from the dendrites, the weights represent synapses, and the output represents axonal output. The output of the coarse-graining is the conditional probability that the meta neuron spikes, given the input. Previous work has shown that PCG preserves reasonable predictive information in retinal data (22, 28), and that optimal PCG readouts are learnable via spike timing dependent plasticity (STDP) rules (55). We compare PCG to a model downstream neuron that is biologically feasible but does not take the future into account, which we call correlation coarse-graining (CCG). Inputs are paired greedily based on their pairwise correlation, then summed and normalized at each step, again outputting a spiking probability (Fig. 1D middle). This could in principal be achieved by STDP driven by spike timing correlations (57, 58), which strengthens connections between correlated neurons, and onto shared downstream targets, over time forming cell assemblies that act like pooled units similar to our CCG meta neurons. Meta neurons constructed in this way form a kind of real-space renormalization scheme that has been shown to capture output computations in visual areas (59, 60). In addition, applying CCG to hippocampal populations results in meta neurons with well-developed place fields (61), indicating that CCG can preserve biologically relevant information. We consider CCG as averaging with a Hebbian flavor (58), which we contrast with a Winner Take All (WTA) coarse-graining (Fig. 1D bottom). This coarse-graining is also prediction-agnostic and could emerge from a combination of lateral inhibition and excitatory–inhibitory interactions, as observed in cortex and early visual circuits (62–64). As with the other coarse-grainings, synaptic plasticity allows for experience tuning: inhibitory synapses can undergo activity-dependent plasticity (65), strengthening competitive, WTA-like suppression. WTA is an aggressive form of redundancy reduction, while CCG reduces pairwise redundancy but aims to preserve shared dynamics. Unlike PCG, which applies a highly nonlinear transformation to the data, both WTA and CCG rely only on first- and second-order statistics. Although we do not test every possible prediction-agnostic coarse-graining function, these two are both biologically realistic and are established computations used by the brain (62, 66–68).

One key difference between these coarse-graining schemes is that the PCG weights are optimized for a particular computation, such as prediction, while the others are not. In particular, we expect WTA to encode predictive information poorly—unless the highest-firing neuron happens to encode signals that are themselves predictive. While this might seem unlikely, neurons with high average activity often reflect dominant features in the stimulus. If this feature evolves slowly or with structured dynamics, its current state can be informative about its future, allowing WTA to preserve some predictive information. Similarly, we would expect CCG to preserve some predictive information if the underlying dominant stimulus feature is predictive, because activity patterns that are repeated across neurons likely reflect that feature. Because CCG pools information across neurons, it may provide a more robust representation than WTA, especially in the presence of noise.

We maximize predictive information in PCG while constraining its mean firing rate to match that of the corresponding CCG meta neuron (ie the CCG meta neuron with the same input cells) to ensure that the output rate is biologically plausible. Relaxing this constraint initially allows PCG to expand its dynamic range and preserve more predictive information (Fig. 2A), but this increase is achieved by effectively bypassing the coarsening constraint. As the firing rate increases further, PCG responses apparently become increasingly nonselective, causing response distributions conditioned on different futures to converge and actually reduce predictive information. We choose a small convergence rate of N=4 cells to 1 meta neuron, since we are interested in modeling downstream areas involved in short-timescale prediction, such as OT, which has a similarly small convergence rate (26, 27). With only 1 output meta neuron, future information is relatively constant as a function of the number of inputs N (Fig. 2C), which validates our choice of small N. The plateau in information suggests that a downstream neuron might not gain much predictive information by further local coarsening; instead, a small pool size may be optimal.

By design, PCG maintains more future information than the agnostic compressions (Fig. 2D–F) but surprisingly, both CCG and WTA retain a high amount of predictive information. This result is along the same lines as recent findings showing that random projections preserve input information in cortex (16, 19). However, here we compared these coarse-grainings to optimal to find that they are both able to maintain a high proportion of the available predictive information. The success of CCG and WTA may stem from the fact that natural scenes are highly temporally structured, and therefore have predictable dominant features (28, 50, 55, 69). Importantly, the relative performance of these coarse-grainings depends on how predictive information is distributed across the input population. Predictive information may be concentrated in a small subset of strongly informative neurons, in which case a competitive, WTA-like operation can be effective, or distributed redundantly across many neurons, in which case CCG is advantageous. Different movies may emphasize these regimes to different degrees, reflecting differences in their motion and correlation structure. We return to this question two sections later, where we examine how input structure influences predictive information. These findings point to the possibility that downstream areas may not need to optimize their inputs in order to effectively use predictive information from RGCs to drive behavior.

Optimized coarse-graining generalizes

Salamanders and other organisms that experience a wide variety of natural scenes during their lifetimes (50–52) must be able to perform predictive computations in many environments. Much like orientation tuning of visual cortical neurons is invariant to changes in contrast (70, 71), efficient coding of predictive information should be scene invariant. Metabolic (72) and temporal constraints (73, 74) make it more efficient to use a coarse-graining algorithm which is flexible enough to successfully predict the future in any environment. One way that neural circuits reuse the same biological hardware to perform different computations is by changing synaptic properties. In the retina, for example, changes in luminance result in changes in synaptic rectification that control how RGCs perform computations in dim versus bright light (75). This can provide a stimulus-dependent change in the inputs to the downstream, coarse-grained meta neuron. While the explicit predictive power of PCG did not offer an overwhelming advantage over CCG and WTA in a given scene, it is the only coarse-graining function we consider that can flexibly handle gross synaptic changes, and therefore might be the only one that can extract fully generalizable predictive information. To investigate this, we construct a pooled dataset by concatenating the four recordings of population spike trains in response to the four movies. We train our three coarse-grainings on the pooled data, then test them on an example movie (fish; Fig. 3B). PCG meta neurons trained on the pooled data have comparable prediction performance to PCG meta neurons trained on the test movie, while CCG and WTA meta neurons trained on the pooled data fail to generalize to the test data. We quantify this in the proportion of future information kept by the coarse-grained meta neurons, which we normalize by the future information retained in the relevant test movie by the corresponding coarse-graining (Fig. 3C). CCG and WTA might seem more efficient because they are more simple, but they do not allow for scene invariant prediction, whereas PCG may be flexible enough to predict its next inputs in any natural scene. We test generalization in PCG more carefully by training on individual movies; this allows us to determine whether PCG can generalize to a movie it has never seen before, and to rule out the possibility that its high generalization performance in Fig. 3C was due to learning the similar scene statistics in the tree and grasses movies, for example, rather than learning something more general about natural scene statistics. We find that indeed, PCGs trained on individual movies retain a high proportion of future information in other movies, and more than rate-matched random PCGs Fig. 3E. This indicates that retinal projection areas may be able to solve a generalizable prediction problem for natural scene statistics, if they can wire up converging downstream connections in a way that retains predictive information. Related work from our lab has shown that a bottom-up coarse-graining of the retina identifies the slow features of natural scenes as both the most important and most general across scenes (76, 77). Because physical objects have momentum, slow features are also the most predictable. Therefore, our result is a product of the retina’s selection of specific predictive features in the natural movies, as well as the fact that natural scenes have predictive-generalizable features because they share some common scene statistics.

Cells with shared inputs maintain more predictive information

In practice, the predictive information available to a downstream neuron depends on which RGCs it receives input from, regardless of the specific coarse-graining function used by downstream circuits. How do we identify which groups of cells the brain might coarse-grain together? Classical efficient coding theories (11, 12) suggest that neurons with overlapping information can be efficiently coarse-grained by reducing redundancy. This is clearly illustrated by the case of CCG, where performing CCG on two perfectly correlated cells would perfectly preserve the original activity. A biologically plausible source of redundancy is shared input: if multiple RGCs receive input from the same bipolar cells (Fig. 4A), their responses will naturally share overlapping information. In particular, bipolar cells that drive RGCs most strongly—perhaps because they synapse onto more RGCs—will induce more correlated activity across the RGC population. This raises the possibility that the brain exploits shared input structure by coarse-graining together RGCs with common bipolar inputs. Such a strategy would reduce redundancy, but might also reduce the amount of information preserved by the meta neuron, since it pools together less overall information. As a result, prediction by a meta neuron constructed from these RGCs might be worse—unless the RGCs with shared inputs carry especially high predictive information. We argue that this is likely the case: the same bipolar cells that drive many RGCs (and thus induce redundancy) may also carry the most predictive information because it is such an ethologically relevant signal. Therefore, we hypothesize that coarse-graining RGCs together based on shared bipolar input not only reduces redundancy but may also preserve the most predictive features of the retinal activity.

We identify putative shared bipolar inputs using spike-triggered independent component analysis (ST-ICA; Fig. 4B) (50, 78). ST-ICA assumes that the spike-triggered ensemble can be modeled as the product of an unknown mixing matrix and the source signals—here, the upstream input. The resulting outputs are spatial subunits, whose activations we compute with the checkerboard stimulus. We classify subunits as sharing input if their centers are in the same square of the checkerboard and the correlation between the activations is at least 0.5 (Fig. 4C). Using a similar method, subunits have been experimentally shown to map to bipolar cell inputs (79). To test how meta neuron input affects output information, we compare meta neurons constructed from RGCs with shared bipolar inputs to ones with distinct inputs. If Syn>0, the group is synergistic, and if Syn<0, the group shares redundant information. Interpreting synergy/redundancy in the context of decoding requires some care (81, 82), but here we simply note that groups of RGCs with shared bipolar inputs are more redundant than ones with distinct inputs (Fig. 4D). This confirms that coarse-graining based on shared input is a form of redundancy reduction. Although shared inputs introduce redundancy that could reduce the total predictive information pooled by the group, this reduction appears to be offset by the high predictive content of the inputs themselves. We find that the distribution of predictive information carried by groups of cells who share at least one subunit is significantly greater than the information carried by other groups (Mann–Whitney U test) (Fig. 4D, E). Moreover, this difference is maintained after coarse-graining using PCG, CCG, and WTA (Fig. 4E) and in different movies and at different prediction timescales (Fig. 4F). This suggests that the brain has to coarse-grain RGCs together somewhat “carefully” (ie not randomly) to preserve predictive information. Previous work from our lab shows that a downstream neuron can learn a PCG-like compression of predictive information from its inputs via STDP rules (55), even though STDP does not detect predictive information directly. Bipolar cells carrying strong, temporally structured signals that are locked to stimulus features drive RGC spikes that also influence the downstream neuron. As a result, these RGC spikes tend to be predictive of downstream responses and are preferentially strengthened, effectively grouping RGCs that are predictive of the same downstream responses. Therefore, our results support the idea that bipolar cells carrying strong predictive signals may drive functional RGC groupings, allowing downstream circuits to efficiently extract predictive information from the retina.

Discussion

Generating successful behavior in response to changing sensory inputs, in the face of sensory and motor delays, requires the visual system to make predictions about the future state of the world. Previous work has shown that predictive computations start at the earliest stages of visual processing, in the retina (22, 28–31). In order for an organism to make use of the predictive information available in the retina, downstream areas must read it out. However, the output cells of the retina (RGCs) show convergence onto many of their downstream areas, limiting the amount of information that can be transferred.

Efficient coding and information bottleneck principles suggest that the near-optimal representation of predictive information available in RGCs (22) is efficiently compressed for downstream readout. Clearly, a coarse-graining function optimized for prediction can achieve this, but it is not yet known whether it is necessary. We compare optimized and prediction-agnostic coarse-grainings of RGC activity to determine whether predictive information can be retained even under agnostic compression. Similarly to previous work in hippocampus (61) and cortex (16, 19), we find that computation-agnostic compression retains substantial information, but we elaborate on previous work by specifying that the information retained is actually about the relevant computation (prediction), and by quantifying how much information is lost by not actively optimizing. We show that in this case, computation-agnostic compression is not overwhelmingly less than optimal. However, efficient coding of predictive information should ideally hold over all natural scenes. We find that an optimized coarse-graining for prediction generalizes across natural scenes, while the prediction-agnostic coarse-grainings do not.

These results suggest that predictive information in the retina is not confined to a subset of specialized neurons, but is encoded in dominant, shared patterns of activity across many groups of cells. Because RGC responses to visual scenes tend to co-vary in structured ways, large-scale fluctuations in population activity already carry information about how responses will evolve over short timescales. As a result, coarse-grainings that preserve strong instantaneous activity patterns, such as CCG and WTA, can retain a substantial fraction of predictive information even without being explicitly optimized for prediction. However, both of these primarily preserve stimulus-dependent population statistics, which vary across scenes. In contrast, PCG learns which specific patterns of activity predict future responses, capturing more specifically how population activity changes over time. These temporal changes reflect the dynamics of RGC population responses to visual input and tend to be more stable across movies.

Our coarse-graining methods represent two extremes of how downstream neurons may encode predictive information from their RGC inputs. The reality is likely somewhere in between: downstream neurons may not sit at the theoretical optimum for prediction, but because predicting the future is so evolutionarily advantageous, they are probably able to flexibly switch between different natural scenes. Our results provide some insight into how this is possible. A coarse-graining scheme which can reuse the same circuit structure while minimally changing synaptic weights could help quickly adapt the system for prediction across a wide variety of scenes by extracting generalizable predictive encoding motifs. In addition, RGCs may already carry so much predictive information that small tweaks to the encoding can still preserve a significant fraction of their predictive power. Together, these considerations suggest that predictive performance may depend on parameters in a “sloppy” way, with widely varying parameters yielding close to optimal predictive information. Several biological systems display this kind of parameter variation (83), and it has been argued that in many cases, performing close to physical limits does not require parameters to be implausibly fine-tuned. This kind of shallowness of the predictive information landscape would allow for resilience to wiring damage, or to evolutionary tweaks that slowly move the system toward expanded computational goals.

Sophisticated machine learning algorithms implemented by deep networks are often needed for good generalization across tasks (84–86), so it is striking that a single-layer perceptron can generalize so well in this context. Further work on the origin of this ability to generalize, as in other work from our lab (76, 77), could help make progress on the design of better transfer learning algorithms.

We also explore how RGC input composition affects coarse-grained outputs and demonstrate that the amount of future information per spike in a meta neuron is largely invariant to its number of inputs (Fig. 2C). Although anatomical convergence in early visual pathways is on the order of 6–10:1 (24, 26, 27), we do not find an optimum in this range. Instead, the relative stability in our model is consistent with physiological evidence that only a smaller subset of inputs (∼3) may strongly influence postsynaptic activity (24, 25) and suggests that biological convergence ratios are likely shaped by constraints beyond predictive information alone. In addition, we show that coarse-graining biologically reasonable inputs yields substantially more predictive information than randomly combined inputs. In the PCG coarse-graining we use, where optimal readouts are learnable via STDP rules (55), this indicates that synaptic weights could be tuned such that cells with convergent inputs are synaptically summed for near-optimal prediction in all natural scenes.

In the amphibian visual system, the OT is regarded as the main center for visuomotor behavior and object recognition (87, 88) and has evolutionary pressure to use predictive information to make fast motor responses to visual inputs (36–39). Therefore, our results could be experimentally tested by tracing RGC inputs to OT neurons, then recording from single neurons in OT to determine how much information they carry about the future of their RGC inputs. This could be compared to the amount of future information carried by different coarse-grainings, to possibly rule out coarse-grainings that do not preserve a comparable amount. The caveat to this is that recording from salamander OT is difficult and not often done, but our results could instead be experimentally tested across species. We do not think what we found is specific to salamanders; our qualitative results about different coarse-grainings should hold in mouse superior colliculus as well, which is involved in making fast defensive and prey behaviors in response to visual inputs (37, 89–92).

Another compelling future research direction is to use this framework to study different computations and even different sensory systems that exhibit compression. In the retina, prediction is clearly not the only relevant computation (93, 94); scene identity recognition is especially important for species who travel through significantly different environments, such as salamanders. It would be interesting to optimize PCG on different combinations of prediction and scene identity information to investigate whether it is possible to represent multiple kinds of compressed information near-optimally. This suggests that PCG may be a powerful tool for studying biologically relevant computations under compression.

Materials and methods

Multielectrode recordings

Voltage traces from the RGC layer of a larval tiger salamander retina were recorded following the methods described in Ref. (95). Briefly, the retina from a larval tiger salamander was isolated in darkness and a 252 channel multielectrode array was used to record from the retina as images were projected onto the photoreceptor layer. The procedures used to collect these data were approved by the Princeton University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; Protocol No. 1828) and have been described previously (50). Spikes were sorted using a mostly automated spike sorting algorithm, binned at 60 Hz. This technique captured a highly overlapping neural population of 93 cells that fully covered a region of visual space. The stimuli were 20 s movies of trees, grasses, water, fish, and optic flow, which were repeated 83, 80, 84, 91, and 85 times, respectively. We omitted the water movie from our analysis because responses to this movie were extremely sparse. The movies were played in a pseudo-random order, and all natural scenes except for the tree stimulus were displayed at 60 Hz. The tree stimulus was shown at 30 Hz to match the slower recording speed of that camera. We included this stimulus to facilitate comparison with previous recordings in the retina in response to this particular natural movie (22, 96, 97). A 30 Hz white noise checkerboard stimulus was played for 30 min prior to and after the natural scene stimuli. This dataset is publicly available from Dryad (53). To construct the pooled dataset, we grouped the spiking responses to all repeats of the same movie together, then added these long spiking responses from different movies together.

Since the spiking recordings were binned at the frame rate of the movies, 60 Hz (into 16.67 ms time windows), this calculation tells us how many bits, on average, the activity of a group of cells in one 16.67 ms window shares with the activity of the same group in a future 16.67 ms window. Predictive information carried by a meta neuron (created via any coarse-graining function) was calculated as the mutual information shared by the meta neuron’s activity and its future input activity: I(Mt;σ→t+Δt). Similarly to the full information, we normalized this by the meta neuron’s firing rate to report information in bits/spike. Because we used a small input population size and assumed that Mt and σ→t+Δt are conditionally independent given σ→t, we calculated information from Eqs. 1 and 2 instead of sampling meta neuron activity to estimate the entropies. To find the error in the mutual information estimates, we followed the methods in (98): data were subsampled via bootstrapping for 50 of the data, with 50 bootstrap samples taken. Errors were estimated as the SD of the sampled information, divided by 2.

Coarse-graining functions

Our coarse-graining functions compressed N cells into one meta neuron by transforming a binary activity vector with 2N states into an activity vector with only two states. The output of PCG was the conditional probability that the meta neuron spiked given a certain pattern of current binary activity, which was modeled as a weighted sum of the inputs passed through a sigmoid nonlinearity

where w→ represents the weights and c represents the threshold parameter of the nonlinearity. Since meta neurons only have two states, we have fully characterized P(Mt|σ→t). The other components of Eq. 2, P(σ→t+Δt|σ→t) and P(σ→t), were counted directly from the data.

To construct a CCG meta neuron, inputs were summed together based on their pairwise correlation: the first two maximally correlated cells were summed together, then the next two maximally correlated cells were summed, and so on. These were normalized by their maximal values, and then the process was repeated until there was only one trace left. Note that the process could be stopped earlier to obtain a population of meta neurons. The trace was bounded between 0 and 1, and we took it to represent the spiking probability of the meta neuron. We used this to simulate the meta neuron’s activity, and for each input pattern σ→t, we counted the number of times the meta neuron elicited an output spike to calculate P(Mt=1|σ→t). To calculate CCG on the pooled dataset, the pairwise correlations were computed across the entire pooled recording, without conditioning on separate movies.

The output of WTA was simply the trace of the input cell with the highest firing rate. Again, we counted the number of times the meta neuron spiked in response to each input pattern σ→t to calculate P(Mt=1|σ→t).

Optimization methods

We maximized Eq. 1 for PCG over the weights and threshold parameter, subject to the constraint that its firing probability was equal to the CCG meta neuron with the same input cells. The firing probability of PCG is

which makes clear how its firing probability depends on the weights and threshold parameter. To perform soft constrained optimization, we used the fmincon function in MATLAB. We initialized the parameters with 100 random seeds, bounded the weights between [0, 100], and bounded the threshold between [0, 200]. We did not specify the gradient of the objective function (‘‘GradObj’’ = off) and we used the sequential quadratic programming (SQP) optimization algorithm. After optimization, if the output parameters did not yield a PCG firing rate within tolerance (0.1 Hz) of the CCG firing rate, we manually adjusted the sigmoid threshold parameter until the PCG firing rate was within tolerance. Some hand curation, such as simulated annealing and restarting, was used to achieve convergence for some cell groups.

Random PCG meta neurons were constructed for each movie by randomly drawing weights from the distribution of optimal PCG weights found in the relevant natural movie, then adjusting the sigmoid threshold parameter so that its firing probability was within tolerance of the firing probability of the corresponding optimal PCG meta neuron with the same inputs. When testing a PCG meta neuron on a different movie from which it was trained, we used the training weights but adjusted the threshold parameter so that the firing rate probability was within tolerance of the corresponding PCG meta neuron optimized on the test movie.

Spike-triggered ICA

Spike-triggered ICA (ST-ICA) was calculated following methods developed in Ref. (50). Briefly, we computed the spike triggered average for each cell, then decomposed the resulting spatiotemporal filter into spatial and temporal filters. We then convolved the checkerboard stimulus with the temporal filter before applying ICA. The resulting output sources were spatial subunits, which we identified as the same following methods developed in Ref. (99).

Drawing input cells

Seventy-three input cell sets of size N=4 were drawn from the population of 93 total recorded cells for Figs. 2–4. These were a combination of random cells, nonoverlapping sets made by sorting and grouping cells by their individual predictive information, and subsamples of the top 20 most individually predictive cells. The “shared input” sets (n=19) in Fig. 3 were classified as the sets where at least two of the four input cells shared at least 1 ST-ICA spatial subunit. Difference in the distributions between shared input sets and other sets was assessed with a Mann–Whitney U test: for each combination of coarse-graining (full, PCG, CCG, and WTA), movies, and prediction horizons (Δt=16 ms and Δt=100 ms), we used the median as the measure of central tendencies of the shared input and other groups. We performed the U test with the ranksum function in MATLAB. For each combination, the distributions in the shared input and other groups differed significantly (P<0.001 two-tailed).
