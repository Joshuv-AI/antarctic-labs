Is it time for a systematic and objective assessment of knee laxity? The impact of objective knee laxity after TKA on patient-reported outcomes: a comprehensive review
Authors: Hannes Vermue, Victor Klincke, Lenka Stroobant, Thomas Tampere, Jan Victor, Nele Arnout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeOver the past decade, robot assistance has emerged as an innovative and rapidly advancing tool in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Despite technological advancements, the evaluation of the soft-tissue envelope during TKA often relies on subjective and rudimentary assessments by the surgeon. This literature review explores the relationship between objective intraoperative and postoperative laxity measurements in the coronal and sagittal planes and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following TKA.MethodsA literature review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase to identify studies on intra- or postoperative laxity measurements in TKA and their correlation with PROMs. Inclusion criteria required studies on TKA patients with quantifiable coronal and/or sagittal laxity measurements and postoperative PROMs linked to these measurements.ResultsThirty-four studies met inclusion criteria: ten on sagittal laxity, twenty-seven on coronal laxity, and three on both. Follow-up ranged from 1 month to over 8 years. Sagittal laxity thresholds of 5–10 mm under applied loads of 89–133 N were associated with better functional outcomes, whereas excessive (> 10 mm) or overly tight (< 5 mm) laxity correlated with reduced satisfaction. Medial laxity in the coronal plane was linked to lower PROMs, while the impact of lateral laxity on PROMs was less conclusive.ConclusionsObjective laxity parameters correlate with PROMs, particularly a stable medial compartment throughout the range of motion, while the role of lateral laxity remains unclear. Systematic use of objective soft-tissue evaluations may enhance PROMs. While fixed-force assessments are standardized, personalized approaches with variable forces tailored to individual patients might be needed for optimal outcomes.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a widely utilized and highly effective surgical intervention for addressing end-stage knee osteoarthritis. While TKA is generally successful, a significant portion of patients still remains dissatisfied after TKA, which has been attributed to a multitude of pre-, intra- and postoperative factors [1, 2]. Among these, knee stability, determined by the laxity between tibia and femur, plays a crucial role in determining post-operative outcomes [2]. During TKA surgeons aim to achieve a ‘balanced’ joint, which resembles a soft-tissue envelope under physiological tension. This involves an interplay between ensuring adequate stability while avoiding excessive laxity. Excessive laxity may lead to a subjective feeling of instability, which is strongly correlated with inferior patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) [3]. Conversely, tightness of the soft-tissue envelope can result in stiffness, which is also associated with less favorable outcomes. Surgeons often rely on the stable medial side of the knee joint, with the medial collateral ligament under physiological tension, while accepting a variable degree of lateral laxity. This approach is in line with the natural biomechanics of the knee, which exhibits more lateral laxity in flexion [4].

Over the last couple of years, a lot of emphasis has been put on implant positioning, with the advent of robot-assistance and new alignment philosophies. Despite the perception of high-tech assistance by robotic systems, joint laxity is still assessed by the surgeon, applying manual stress on the joint. No need to say that this is not an accurate nor reproducible measurement. For instance, Sohmiya et al. demonstrated that collecting knee joint laxity intraoperatively differed between surgeons, especially in flexion [5]. The recent development of intra-operative objective laxity measurement tools, opens the door to finding personalized laxity targets and accommodate implant position accordingly [6, 7]. Despite the lack of studies with large cohorts analyzing the link between PROMs and stability, there is some evidence suggesting that medial-lateral balance and anteroposterior (AP) stability may significantly influence PROMs, yet the ideal ranges for laxity in the coronal and sagittal planes have not been conclusively determined [2, 8].

This review aims to synthesize the available evidence regarding the link between objective sagittal and coronal laxity, and PROMs and clinical outcomes following primary TKA. Therefore, we asked two questions: (1) How is objective sagittal laxity linked to postoperative PROMs and clinical outcomes after TKA? (2) How is objective coronal laxity linked to postoperative PROMs and clinical outcomes after TKA?

Materials and methods

This review was conducted used Pubmed/MEDLINE and Embase databases used to conduct a keyword search for articles from inception up to October 15th, 2024. Due to assumed heterogeneity between studies and the lack of clear control/intervention groups, no systematic review could be performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [9]. As such, the authors opted for a more comprehensive style approach. The following keywords were combined to conduct the literature search: “Oxford Knee Score” (OKS), “OKS”, “Knee Society Score” (KSS), “KSS”, “Hospital for Special Surgery Knee Rating Scale” (HSS), “HSS”, “Total Knee Function Questionnaire”, “Japanese Knee Osteoarthritis Measure” (JKOM), “JKOM”, “University of California and Los Angeles Score” (UCLA), “UCLA Activity Score”, “Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score” (KOOS), “KOOS”, “Self-Report”, “Patient Outcome Assessment”, “Patient reported outcome”, “PROM”, “Postural Balance”, “Laxity”, “Stability”, “Instability”, “Joint Instability”, “Range of Motion, Articular”, “TKA”, “Knee Joint Replacement” and “Arthroplasty, Replacement, Knee”. All citations were screened by two authors (*blinded*), after which the full-text articles were reviewed for eligibility. In case of disagreement, a third reviewer (*blinded*) participated to reach agreement through discussion.

The Risk of Bias (RoB) assessment for the included studies was conducted using the non-comparative part of MINORS (Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies) [10] on each study. This validated tool evaluates methodological quality based on 8 criteria. Each criterion was scored from 0 to 2 (0 = not reported, 1 = inadequately reported, 2 = adequately reported), with a maximum score of 16. Results

Study selection and study characteristics

The literature search strategy resulted in a total amount of 6873 articles. 3871 Articles were retrieved from MEDLINE and 3002 from Embase. After removal of duplicates, 4530 articles were screened by title and abstract. As a result, 4,460 studies were excluded, mainly because they did not focus on TKA. Subsequently, the remaining 70 articles were screened by their full text for all inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 34 references were found fit for inclusion [2, 3, 11–42]. The selection flowchart can be found in Fig. 1. Most studies were published in 2020 or later. The oldest included study was from 2003, the most recent from 2024.

Demographics

The demographic characteristics of the included studies can be found in Table 2. There was great variability in the size of the included patient cohorts in each study, ranging from 20 to 769. The total number of knees included was 3752, the total number of patients was 3605, consisting of 874 men and 2731 women. The mean age at time of surgery was also widespread ranging from 63.3 to 79 years old. The mean length of follow-up varied widely from 1 month to 8 years.

Laxity and clinical outcomes

Ten of the included studies reported on the relationship between sagittal laxity and PROMs, twenty-seven studies reported on the relationship between coronal laxity and PROMS. Results of these measurements, their correlation to PROMs and possible cut-off values or ideal laxity windows for sagittal and coronal laxity can be found in Tables 3 and 4, respectively.

Regarding the sagittal plane, several authors have mentioned cut-off values of less than 5–10 mm of total anteroposterior laxity would provide highest PROMs [18, 24, 31]. As well, several studies have demonstrated positive correlations between total range of motion and total anteroposterior laxity [21, 32, 41].

Regarding the coronal plane, most common association found in the studies was medial laxity throughout range of motion induced lower PROMs [11, 15, 16, 19, 23, 29, 30, 33, 35–37, 40]. While some surgeons advocate for a degree of gap asymmetry, with the lateral side being looser than the medial side, particularly in flexion [3, 13, 19, 22, 30, 35, 37, 38], other studies argue that increased lateral laxity is unwarranted, regardless of the implant type [20, 28, 33, 40]. Several studies reported cut-off values which could be used to improve PROMs postoperatively [2, 11, 25, 28, 29, 37].

Discussion

This review found that achieving precise laxity thresholds in TKA could be paramount to obtain postoperative stability and knee function. While aiming for native knee laxity seems reasonable, TKA induces significant changes to the native knee joint through a series of surgical interventions, including exposure of the joint, resection of the menisci, partial or complete resection of some ligaments, and potential iatrogenic capsular damage. Each of these modifications can alter knee stability and kinematics, making it essential to understand how stability is maintained in TKA.

One of the primary stabilizing factors in TKA is the surface geometry of the implant [43]. The polyethylene insert’s posterior lip plays a critical role in providing anterior stability, while the anterior lip contributes to posterior stability. Medial congruent and ultra-congruent designs enhance conformity on the medial side and across both the medial and lateral compartments, respectively, with the goal of restoring native knee kinematics. Similarly, the medial pivot (MP) design uses a fully congruent ball-in-socket medial compartment while allowing lateral translation. In addition to surface geometry, other constrains exist to provide stability. posterior-stabilized (PS) designs incorporate a post-cam mechanism to replace the function of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL). A constrained condylar knee design provides increased stability compared to PS designs by incorporating a higher, more robust central post to manage moderate ligament deficiencies. The most constrained version of TKA uses a rotating hinge which accommodates for higher degrees of ligament laxity. As such, the intrinsic stability of the implant directly influences the optimal level of ligament tension required to achieve excellent knee function. However, quantifying ligament laxity remains a challenge, for example phenomena such as the pivot shift test continue to defy consistent measurement [44]. Moreover, patient-specific factors such as constitutional ligament laxity, which may have a genetic basis, further complicate the surgeon’s task [45]. Since implant design plays a key role in knee laxity, understanding how different designs impact postoperative stability and patient satisfaction is essential.

Sagittal stability is particularly important in dynamic activities such as walking, stair climbing and pivoting [46]. Multiple studies have underscored the need for optimal anterior-posterior laxity ranges to mimic native knee laxity while avoiding postoperative instability. Most studies converge on sagittal laxity thresholds between 5 and 10 mm under applied loads of 89–133 N, depending on the degree of knee flexion. These values depend on the surface geometry of the implant. In Cruciate Retaining (CR) TKA, moderate PCL laxity (2–4 mm) achieved the highest post-op satisfaction [2]. However, in general, values exceeding the threshold of 10 mm seem to induce instability, which was further highlighted by Jones et al. and Seon et al. [18, 31]. In MP designs, total AP laxity over 7 mm was found to be an independent risk factor for subjective instability according to Mochizuki et al. [24]. While increasing anterior laxity was negatively correlated with KSS function in the study by Kato et al., a positive correlation between anterior translation and the Forgotten Joint Score was found as well [21], which might stress the complex interplay between tightness and excessive laxity. In PS designs, only the study by Watanabe et al. found a positive correlation between anterior translation at 30° of flexion and total range of motion [41]. No studies on PS designs resulted in significant correlations between PROMs and objective AP laxity parameters.

Several authors have postulated coronal laxity after TKA should mimic native knee laxity with a stable medial side and more loose lateral side, especially in flexion. Most studies identified medial stability to be one of the predominant factors to achieve increased patient function [11, 15, 16, 19, 23, 29, 30, 33, 35–37, 40]. However, no studies have investigated whether excessive medial tightness could contribute to stiffness or persistent pain.

The role of lateral laxity remains unclear. While, on average, lateral laxity is physiological in the native knee it’s impact post-TKA is debated, especially since all studies included in this review have used a mechanical alignment philosophy, which intrinsically leaves more lateral laxity in the typical varus osteoarthritis in case no medial release is performed. Still, some studies reported a positive influence of lateral laxity on PROMs [3, 8, 19, 22, 30, 35–38], whereas others described detrimental effects of excessive lateral laxity, and still others advocated for equal medial and lateral balance [20, 28, 33, 40]. No clear correlation was seen between implant type and the relationship between coronal laxity and PROMs. Similar to sagittal laxity, specific cut-off values have been published for coronal laxity as well to optimize patient outcomes. For instance, Randall et al. and Wakelin et al. described specific targets for laxity in extension, mid-flexion, and flexion [2, 29]. Combining these cut-off values could potentially lead to further improvements in patient outcomes.

Given the current trend toward personalization in TKA, it is plausible that soft tissue management also requires a personalized approach, including individualized force application for each patient. Just as alignment phenotypes have been described preoperatively, distinct laxity phenotypes may also exist, warranting further investigation with objective measurements.

Several limitations were noted during the process of this study. First, it was deemed impossible to perform meta-analysis on these study results, as there was significant heterogenicity in implant designs, the force used and the degrees of flexion during the laxity measurement. More significantly, the major amount of different PROMs reported made it hard to standardize findings. As well, significant differences in study quality have been seen. Lastly, little is known about the evolution of knee laxity in the long run after total knee arthroplasty. It has been described that the medial and lateral laxity increased with 1 mm only 30 min after the first intraoperative measurement [47]. In contrast, other authors have found that coronal laxity does not alter between 6 months and more than 6 years post-op [48].

Conclusion

This systematic review found that objective laxity parameters are indeed correlated with PROMs after total knee arthroplasty, particularly with a stable medial compartment throughout the full range of motion. However, the role of lateral laxity remains unclear. Systematic use of objective soft-tissue evaluations thus has the potential to further enhance PROMs. While a fixed force is currently used to assess the soft-tissue envelope, a personalized approach, with variable forces tailored to individual patients, might be required to achieve optimal outcomes.
