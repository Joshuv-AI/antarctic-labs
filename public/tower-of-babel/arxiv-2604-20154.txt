Maximum Likelihood Reconstruction for Multi-Look Digital Holography with Markov-Modeled Speckle Correlation
Xi Chen, Arian Maleki, Shirin Jalali

Abstract
Multi-look acquisition is a widely used strategy for reducing speckle noise in coherent imaging systems such as digital holography. By acquiring multiple measurements, speckle can be suppressed through averaging or joint reconstruction, typically under the assumption that speckle realizations across looks are statistically independent. In practice, however, hardware constraints limit measurement diversity, leading to inter-look correlation that degrades the performance of conventional methods. In this work, we study the reconstruction of speckle-free reflectivity from complex-valued multi-look measurements in the presence of correlated speckle. We model the inter-look dependence using a first-order Markov process and derive the corresponding likelihood under a first-order Markov approximation, resulting in a constrained maximum likelihood estimation problem. To solve this problem, we develop an efficient projected gradient descent framework that combines gradient-based updates with implicit regularization via deep image priors, and leverages Monte Carlo approximation and matrix-free operators for scalable computation. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed approach remains robust under strong inter-look correlation, achieving performance close to the ideal independent-look scenario and consistently outperforming methods that ignore such dependencies. These results highlight the importance of explicitly modeling inter-look correlation and provide a practical framework for multi-look holographic reconstruction under realistic acquisition conditions. Our code is available at: https://github.com/Computational-Imaging-RU/MLE-Holography-Markov .

1
Introduction

Speckle noise is one of the primary challenges in coherent imaging systems, such as holographic displays
[
32
,
25
,
24
]
and wavefront imaging through scattering media
[
30
]
, as well as digital holography
[
4
,
19
]
, severely degrading the perceived image quality. In such systems, speckle arises from the interference of coherent waves undergoing random phase variations, which can be induced by both surface roughness and propagation through scattering media. These effects lead to granular intensity fluctuations in the recorded image. Over the decades, several optical and computational approaches for mitigating the impact of speckle noise have been developed
[
4
]
. One of the most effective methods is the so-called multi-look acquisition, which acquires multiple sets of measurements and generates the reconstructed image by properly averaging or jointly processing these measurements. If the speckle realizations are independent across the looks, this method leads to a reduction in the impact of speckle noise, and an improvement in reconstructed image quality.

Multi-look measurement is typically achieved by introducing controlled diversity in the optical field, such that each measurement corresponds to an independent (or weakly correlated) speckle pattern while preserving the same reflectivity information. For instance, in holographic displays, temporal multiplexing
[
10
]
generates a sequence of phase patterns over time, while polarization multiplexing
[
18
]
exploits orthogonal polarization states to produce statistically independent speckle realizations. These approaches have been empirically validated as effective in reducing speckle noise
[
1
,
23
,
32
,
25
,
17
,
24
]
in holographic systems.

While ideally one requires independent speckle noise realizations across looks, in practice, achieving full independence remains challenging
[
4
]
. The diversity introduced by spatial light modulators, phase masks, or diffusers can be fundamentally limited by finite spatial resolution, phase quantization, and hardware imperfections
[
31
]
. As a result, the speckle realizations across different looks are not always guaranteed to be independent, which limits the performance gains promised by the multi-look strategy.
Modeling of dynamic speckle has been studied from multiple perspectives in the literature. For instance, in
[
5
,
6
]
, the authors consider deterministic geometric motion models and analytically characterize how such motion affects speckle correlation, enabling explicit decorrelation of dynamic speckle. In a different line of work, the temporal statistics of speckle are treated as informative signals, where temporal correlation is estimated to quantify motion dynamics, such as flow or diffusion
[
2
,
35
]
.

In this work, we propose a stochastic model that captures the dependencies between speckle realizations across looks. Based on this model, we formulate a constrained maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) problem, which results in a computationally challenging non-convex optimization. Focusing on digital holography, we develop an efficient Monte Carlo–based algorithm to solve the resulting MLE problem. Simulations demonstrate that the proposed approach improves reconstruction performance over an MLE baseline that ignores inter-look dependencies under correlated measurements, while maintaining scalability to high-resolution imaging systems through efficient, matrix-free implementations.

2
Digital holography: Problem setting

We begin by reviewing the mathematical model of the multi-look digital holography system. We then formulate the corresponding MLE problem and discuss its algorithmic solutions, along with the associated challenges and limitations of existing approaches. Based on this analysis, we conclude by summarizing the contributions of this work.

2.1
Mathematical Model

A multi-look coherent imaging system is mathematically described as follows: for
\ell=1,\ldots,L
,

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{\ell}=A\mathbf{g}_{\ell}+{\bf z}_{\ell},

(1)

where
{\bf y}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is the measurement vector,
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
denotes the sensing matrix, and
{\bf z}_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{z}^{2}I_{m})
is the additive sensor noise. Let
X=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits({\bf x})
denote the diagonal matrix formed from the underlying speckle-free reflectivity
{\bf x}
. Given
{\bf x}
, ideally, for
\ell=1,\ldots,L
, the vectors
\mathbf{g}_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},X)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
are independent and represent the complex field after random phase interference, where the randomness models fully developed speckle arising from coherent interference
[
34
,
7
]
.

The model described above applies to general coherent imaging systems. In this paper, however, we focus on digital holography, which corresponds to a specific form of the forward model and will be important for our subsequent algorithmic developments. In digital holography, based on the Fresnel model, the sensing matrix can be written as

\displaystyle A=\mathcal{F}^{-1}M\mathcal{F},

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
denotes the discrete Fourier transform operator. By Fourier optics
[
13
]
, the Fourier transform is applied to the 2D image. When acting on the vectorized image
{\bf x}
, the operator
\mathcal{F}
can be written as the Kronecker product of row and column Fourier matrices, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{r}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{c}^{H}.

The matrix
M=\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{vec}(P))\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
represents the aperture mask in the spatial frequency domain, where
P\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}
denotes the 2D aperture function.

The complex optical field
{\bf y}_{\ell}
is obtained either directly via off-axis digital holography or through wavefront reconstruction using phase retrieval algorithms
[
16
]
. In off-axis digital holography, the interference between the object and a tilted reference beam shifts the signal of interest away from the zero-frequency component in the Fourier domain. This enables the separation of the desired complex field through appropriate demodulation and filtering operations, typically by isolating the first-order diffraction term via bandpass filtering and then applying an inverse Fourier transform to recover the complex-valued field. We then formulate a constrained MLE problem of reconstructing
{\bf x}
from the measurements
{\bf y}
with known sensing matrix
A
.

2.2
Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Given the complex-valued measurements
{\bf y}_{1},\ldots,{\bf y}_{L}
and the sensing matrix
A
, the goal of the recovery algorithm is to estimate the underlying reflectivity
{\bf x}
. Note that although the forward operator
A
in digital holography is typically square, the presence of an aperture mask with limited transparency generally renders it rank-deficient, thereby making the resulting inverse problem ill-posed
[
21
,
8
]
.

Given the independence and Gaussianity of
{\bf z}_{\ell}
and
\bm{g}_{\ell}
, with fixed
{\bf x}
and
A
, the measurement vectors
{\bf y}_{1},\ldots,{\bf y}_{L}
are independently distributed as zero-mean Gaussian random vectors with covariance
\Sigma({\bf x})
defined as

\displaystyle\Sigma({\bf x})=AXA^{H}+\sigma_{z}^{2}I_{m}.

(3)

Given
{\bf x}
, the negative log-likelihood of
{\bf y}_{\ell}
,
-\log p({\bf y}_{\ell}|A,X)
, denoted as
f_{\ell}({\bf x})
, up to additive constants independent of
{\bf x}
, can be written as

f_{\ell}({\bf x})=\log\det\Sigma({\bf x})+{\bf y}_{\ell}^{H}\Sigma^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf y}_{\ell},

where
{\bf y}_{\ell}^{H}
is the Hermitian transpose of
{\bf y}_{\ell}
. Given the assumption on the independence of the measurements, the normalized negative log-likelihood of all
L
looks can be written as
f({\bf x})={\frac{1}{L}}\sum_{\ell}f_{\ell}({\bf x})
. That is,

\displaystyle f({\bf x})=\log\det\Sigma({\bf x})+\frac{1}{L}\sum^{L}_{\ell=1}{\bf y}_{\ell}^{H}\Sigma^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf y}_{\ell}.

(4)

It is known that properly modeling and exploiting the underlying source structure plays a key role in solving the noisy and ill-posed inverse problems, and can substantially improve reconstruction performance. So, instead of optimizing over all
{\bf x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}
, we assume that
{\bf x}
belongs to a class of images
\mathcal{X}
that captures prior knowledge of the desired signal. This leads to the constrained MLE formulation

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf x}}=\argmin_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{X}}f({\bf x}).

(5)

The choice of
\mathcal{X}
plays a central role in regularizing the reconstruction and mitigating the effects of speckle noise and limited measurements.

Prior work
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
3
]
models speckle statistically as in (
1
), and recovers the reflectivity
{\bf x}
from the complex field
{\bf y}
via maximum likelihood estimation, often within a Plug-and-Play (PnP) framework
[
29
]
to incorporate learned image priors such as DnCNN
[
33
]
. Due to the intractability of directly optimizing the likelihood, these approaches rely on variational approximations and EM-based optimization
[
11
]
. In addition, simplifying assumptions on the sensing matrix (e.g.,
A^{H}A=I
) are often introduced. While effective in practice, these approximations can lead to suboptimal reconstruction performance.

More recently,
[
34
,
7
]
theoretically characterize the performance of MLE and show that accurate recovery is possible despite the ill-posedness of the problem. To address the computational burden of matrix inversion in the MLE objective,
[
7
]
employs the Newton-Schulz iterative algorithm
[
15
,
27
]
, enabling direct optimization of the likelihood. For digital holography,
[
9
,
8
]
propose a Monte Carlo-based approach for matrix diagonal approximation, combined with conjugate gradient methods, which significantly accelerates gradient computation and enables scalable, high-resolution reconstruction with exact likelihood modeling and accurate aperture representation.

These works typically assume that the acquired multi-look measurements correspond to independent speckle noise realizations. In practice, however, existing decorrelation methods are imperfect, and non-negligible correlation across looks often persists. This raises a fundamental question: can we explicitly model the statistical dependence across multiple measurements and leverage it to develop an improved MLE-based algorithm?

2.3
Contributions

In this paper we focus on addressing the question raised at the end of Section
2.2
. The main contributions of this work are as follows:

\bullet

We model the dependence across speckle realizations in multi-look digital holography using a first-order Markov process, and incorporate this structure into the likelihood formulation alongside the Fresnel imaging model.

\bullet

We formulate the corresponding constrained maximum likelihood estimation problem that explicitly accounts for inter-look correlation.

\bullet

We develop an efficient algorithm to solve the resulting optimization, including the estimation of the correlation coefficients across looks, while preserving accurate aperture modeling and enabling scalability to high-resolution settings.

We then demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method through simulation results.

3
Method

We begin by developing a stochastic model for the inter-look speckle correlation in multi-look coherent imaging. We then derive the corresponding likelihood function based on a first-order approximation of this correlation structure. Focusing on digital holography, we address the computational challenges introduced by the resulting objective function by proposing an efficient algorithm that combines Monte Carlo approximation with the conjugate gradient method.

3.1
Markov Model of Speckle Correlation in Multi-Look

As discussed earlier, in practical acquisition setups, speckle realizations across multiple looks are not strictly independent. To mathematically characterize this inter-look correlation and study its impact on reconstruction performance, we model the speckle field across looks as a first-order Markov process.

Consider a sequence of
L
looks as defined in (
1
). We assume that the speckle corresponding to the first look is fully developed
[
14
]
, i.e.,
\bm{g}_{1}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},X)
. For
\ell=2,\ldots,L
, to capture the dependence between successive speckle realizations, we let
\bm{g}_{\ell}
evolve according to

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{\ell}=\alpha\bm{g}_{\ell-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\,{\bf u}_{\ell},

(6)

where, for a fixed
{\bf x}
, the random vectors
{\bf u}_{1},\ldots,{\bf u}_{L}
are independent of
\bm{g}_{1}
and are independently and identically distributed as
{\bf u}_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},X)
. Here,
\alpha\in[0,1]
denotes the correlation coefficient between consecutive looks. The case
\alpha=0
corresponds to fully independent speckle realizations. This construction ensures that
\bm{g}_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\bf 0},X)
,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{g}_{\ell}]={\bf x}
for all
\ell
, while introducing controlled correlation across looks. Note that, for
\ell=2,\ldots,L
,

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{\ell}=\alpha^{\ell-1}\bm{g}_{1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2(\ell-1)}}\,\tilde{{\bf u}},

(7)

where
\tilde{{\bf u}}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\bf 0},X)
is independent of
\bm{g}_{1}
.

3.2
Likelihood of Correlated Speckle

Recall that, given the speckle correlation in (
6
), the multi-look measurement process considering the sensing matrix
A
is given by
{\bf y}_{1}=A\bm{g}_{1}+{\bf z}_{1}
, and, for
\ell=2,\ldots,L
,

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{\ell}=A\bm{g}_{\ell}+{\bf z}_{\ell}=\alpha A\bm{g}_{\ell-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\,A{\bf u}_{\ell}+{\bf z}_{\ell},

(8)

where
{\bf u}_{2},\ldots,{\bf u}_{L}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{CN}({\bf 0},X)
and
{\bf z}_{1},\ldots,{\bf z}_{L}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{CN}({\bf 0},\sigma_{z}^{2}I)
.

Unlike the case of independent speckle, the measurements
{\bf y}_{1},\ldots,{\bf y}_{L}
are statistically dependent given
{\bf x}
. Moreover, due to the presence of additive noise, the sequence
\{{\bf y}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
does not form a first-order Markov process. As a result, directly characterizing the full likelihood function is intractable.

To address this challenge, we instead adopt a first-order approximation of the joint distribution. Specifically, rather than expressing the likelihood as

\displaystyle p({\bf y}_{1},\ldots,{\bf y}_{L}\mid A,X)=p({\bf y}_{1}\mid A,X)\prod_{\ell=2}^{L}p({\bf y}_{\ell}\mid{\bf y}_{\ell-1},\ldots,{\bf y}_{1},A,X),

(9)

we approximate it using a first-order Markov model as

\displaystyle p({\bf y}_{1}\mid A,X)\prod_{\ell=2}^{L}p({\bf y}_{\ell}\mid{\bf y}_{\ell-1},A,X).

(10)

Note that in the absence of additive noise, this approximation becomes exact.

For
\ell\geq 2
,
({\bf y}_{\ell-1},{\bf y}_{\ell})
follows a joint complex Gaussian distribution given by

\begin{bmatrix}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}\\
{\bf y}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}_{\ell-1}\\
\bm{\mu}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}_{(\ell-1)(\ell-1)}&\bm{\Sigma}_{(\ell-1)\ell}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell(\ell-1)}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell\ell}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(11)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{\ell}=\bm{\mu}_{\ell-1}=\mathbf{0},

(12)

and

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{(\ell-1)(\ell-1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell\ell}=B+\sigma_{z}^{2}I_{n},

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{(\ell-1)\ell}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell(\ell-1)}=\alpha B,

(13)

with

B\triangleq AXA^{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}{\bf a}_{i}{\bf a}_{i}^{H}.

(14)

Therefore, the conditional distribution of
{\bf y}_{\ell}
given
{\bf y}_{\ell-1}
is also a complex
Gaussian. That is,

{\bf y}_{\ell}\mid{\bf y}_{\ell-1}\sim\mathcal{CN}\!\left(\bm{\mu}_{\ell|\ell-1},\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell|\ell-1}\right),

where the conditional mean and covariance can be characterized as

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{\ell|\ell-1}=\bm{\mu}_{\ell}+\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell(\ell-1)}\bm{\Sigma}_{(\ell-1)(\ell-1)}^{-1}({\bf y}_{\ell-1}-\bm{\mu}_{\ell-1})=\alpha B(B+\sigma^{2}_{z}I_{n})^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1},

(15)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell|\ell-1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell\ell}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell(\ell-1)}\bm{\Sigma}_{(\ell-1)(\ell-1)}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{(\ell-1)\ell}=(B+\sigma^{2}_{z}I_{n})-\alpha^{2}B(B+\sigma^{2}_{z}I_{n})^{-1}B.

(16)

Ignoring the constants with respect to
{\bf x}
, the resulting approximate negative log-likelihood is

\displaystyle\log\det(B+\sigma^{2}_{z}I_{n})+{\bf y}_{1}^{H}(B+\sigma^{2}_{z}I_{n})^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}+\sum^{L}_{\ell=2}\Big[\log\det\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell|\ell-1}+({\bf y}_{\ell}-\bm{\mu}_{\ell|\ell-1})^{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\ell|\ell-1}^{-1}({\bf y}_{\ell}-\bm{\mu}_{\ell|\ell-1})\Big].

(17)

We define the resulting objective function for estimating
{\bf x}
as

\displaystyle f_{L}({\bf x})=

\displaystyle\log\det S+{\bf y}_{1}^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\Big[\log\det M+{\bf r}_{\ell}^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}\Big],

(18)

where
B
is defined in (
14
), and

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\triangleq B+\sigma_{z}^{2}I_{n},

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\triangleq S-\alpha^{2}BS^{-1}B,

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\triangleq{\bf y}_{\ell}-\alpha BS^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}.

(19)

We next develop an efficient algorithm to minimize the resulting non-convex objective, with a focus on scalable gradient computation and effective incorporation of source structure.

3.3
Constrained MLE-based Reconstruction

We aim to solve the following constrained MLE problem, while assuming that
{\bf x}
belongs to a class of images
\mathcal{X}
that captures prior knowledge of the desired signal,

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf x}}=\argmin_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{X}}f_{L}({\bf x}),

(20)

where
f_{L}
is defined in (
18
).
To solve the nonconvex constrained MLE problem in (
20
), we adopt the projected gradient descent (PGD) algorithm. Starting from an initial estimate
{\bf x}_{0}
, the iterations are given by

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{t}-\mu\nabla f_{L}({\bf x}_{t}),

(21)

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}({\bf s}_{t+1}),

(22)

where
\mu>0
denotes the step size,
\nabla f({\bf x}_{t})
is the gradient of the negative log-likelihood at iteration
t
, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot)
denotes a projection onto the constraint set
\mathcal{X}
. The gradient descent step enforces data fidelity through the likelihood function, while the projection step incorporates prior information by restricting the iterate to the admissible set
\mathcal{X}
.

In practice,
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot)
can be implemented using a codebook or untrained neural networks such as implicit neural representations (INRs)
[
26
]
or deep image prior (DIP)
[
28
]
, which parameterize images in the class
\mathcal{X}
. In this work, we adopt DIP as the projection operator. Specifically, DIP is represented by a neural network
g_{\theta}(\cdot)
, parameterized by
\theta
, that takes a fixed random input.

The projection is carried out by optimizing
\theta
at each iteration
t
:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{t}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\theta}\|g_{\theta}(\cdot)-{\bf s}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t+1}=g_{\hat{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot).

Next, we describe how to efficiently compute
\nabla f_{L}({\bf x}_{t})
, addressing the computational challenges associated with matrix inversion. The gradient of
f_{L}({\bf x}_{t})
in (
18
) with respect to
{\bf x}_{t}
at each iteration
t
of PGD is given by (see
Appendix A
for derivation details)

\displaystyle\nabla f_{L}({\bf x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}A)-|A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}|^{2}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\Bigg[\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}M_{t}^{-1}A)-|A^{H}M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{t,\ell}|^{2}

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\bigg(2\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\Big\{\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}B_{t}M_{t}^{-1}A)-A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}B_{t}M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{t,\ell}\odot\overline{A^{H}M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{t,\ell}}\Big\}

\displaystyle-\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}B_{t}M_{t}^{-1}B_{t}S_{t}^{-1}A)+|A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}B_{t}M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{t,\ell}|^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle+2\alpha\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\Big\{A^{H}B_{t}S_{t}^{-2}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}\odot\overline{A^{H}M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{t,\ell}}-A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}\odot\overline{A^{H}M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{t,\ell}}\Big\}\Bigg].

(23)

Note that
B({\bf x}_{t}),S({\bf x}_{t}),M({\bf x}_{t}),{\bf r}_{\ell}({\bf x}_{t})
as functions of
{\bf x}_{t}
change through the PGD iteration, where we simplify the notation as
B_{t},S_{t},M_{t},{\bf r}_{t,\ell}
. We can see that the gradient vector
\nabla f({\bf x}_{t})
involves multiple matrix-vector and matrix-matrix multiplication, each of which requires one or more matrix inversion. The direct calculation of the gradient is particularly intensive, especially when signal dimension
n
and the number of looks acquired
L
are large, in the context of an iterative algorithm. In particular, repeated evaluations of
S_{t}^{-1}
and
M_{t}^{-1}
dominate the computational cost.

3.4
Efficient Calculation of the Gradient

To efficiently compute the gradient in (
3.3
), we decompose the required terms into two categories: vector-valued terms and diagonal matrix terms. The vector terms include
A^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}
,
A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}
,
A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}
,
A^{H}BS^{-2}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}
,
A^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}
, while the diagonal terms include
\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}A)
,
\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}M^{-1}A)
,
\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}A)
,
\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}BS^{-1}A)
.

Direct computation of these quantities is computationally prohibitive, as it requires forming large matrices and computing their inverses. To address this, we first employ the conjugate gradient method in conjunction with the Fourier structure of
A
to avoid explicit matrix formation and inversion. We then develop a Monte Carlo–based randomized approach to approximate the diagonal terms efficiently, where conjugate gradient is again used to avoid direct matrix inversion.

3.4.1
Conjugate gradient for vector terms involving matrix inversion

We first show how to compute
A^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}
and
A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}
, two representative vector terms, without explicitly forming
S^{-1}
and
M^{-1}
. These computations provide the necessary building blocks for evaluating other similar vector terms.

To compute
A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}
in (
3.3
), define
{\bf h}_{t}=S_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}
. Note that
{\bf h}_{t}
is the solution to the following least-squares problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf h}}\|S_{t}{\bf h}-{\bf y}_{1}\|^{2}.

(24)

Therefore, if we can efficiently solve (
24
), we obtain the desired quantity
A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}
by applying
A^{H}
to
{\bf h}_{t}
.

To solve (
24
) efficiently, we employ the conjugate gradient method
[
12
]
, which leverages the fact that
S_{t}
is Hermitian positive definite. The details are provided in Algorithm
1
.

Running Algorithm
1
requires computing expressions of the form
S_{t}\mathbf{p}_{j}
and
S_{t}{\bf h}_{0}
. Owing to the special structure of the forward operator, these operations can be performed efficiently using the Fourier transform. Specifically, for a 2D image
\mathbf{X}
with vectorized form
{\bf x}=\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{X})
,

A{\bf x}=\operatorname{vec}(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})),

where
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})=\operatorname{IFFT2}\;(P\odot\operatorname{FFT2}(\mathbf{X}))
.
Here,
\operatorname{FFT2}
denotes the two-dimensional Fourier transform, and
P
represents the 2D aperture mask defined earlier. Similarly, the matrix-vector product
S_{t}{\bf h}
can be computed by applying the operator
S({\bf x}_{t})
to the 2D representation of
{\bf h}
, denoted by
\mathbf{H}
, as

S({\bf x}_{t}){\bf h}=\operatorname{vec}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\odot\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{H})\right)+\sigma_{z}^{2}\mathbf{H}\right).

Here,
\mathbf{X}_{t}
denotes the 2D image corresponding to the estimate
{\bf x}_{t}
at iteration
t
. By operating directly in the 2D domain, these computations can be performed efficiently without explicit formation of large matrices. Furthermore, by exploiting the structure of the Fourier optics operator in (
2
) and leveraging fast Fourier transform (FFT) implementations within the conjugate gradient method, the overall reconstruction is significantly accelerated and scales efficiently to high-resolution images.

Next, to compute
A^{H}M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}
, first we need to obtain
{\bf r}_{t,\ell}={\bf y}_{\ell}-\alpha B_{t}S_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}
, in which the
S_{t}^{-1}
is not needed as in (
24
). Assuming we have access to
M_{t}
, we can avoid
M_{t}^{-1}
by using conjugate gradient to solve the following least-squares

\displaystyle\operatorname*{argmin}_{\bm{g}}\|M_{t}\bm{g}-{\bf r}_{\ell}\|^{2},

(25)

where the solution
\hat{\bm{g}}_{t}=M_{t}^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}
. However, even
M_{t}
requires the inversion
S_{t}^{-1}
, which we avoid computing and saving explicitly. To achieve this, we apply a nested CG to avoid computing
S_{t}^{-1}
when applying
M_{t}
in CG.

Given an arbitrary vector
{\bf s}
, when it is multiplied with
M_{t}=S_{t}-\alpha^{2}B_{t}S_{t}^{-1}B_{t}
in conjugate gradient, we solve the least-squares to avoid
S_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{argmin}_{{\bf q}}\|S_{t}{\bf q}-B{\bf s}\|^{2}.

(26)

where the solution
\hat{{\bf q}}_{t}=S_{t}^{-1}B{\bf s}
can be obtained with the nested CG. Having
\hat{{\bf q}}_{t}
, the product
M_{t}{\bf s}
is expressed as
S_{t}{\bf s}-\alpha^{2}B_{t}\hat{{\bf q}}_{t}
. The remaining direct vector terms in (

A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}
and

Next, we employ a method based on randomized linear algebra to estimate the diagonal entries. In particular, we proposed an efficient approach for estimating
\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}A)
, and the other matrix diagonals in (
3.3

and define

by
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{t}=S_{t}^{-1}A{\bf v}

As before,
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{t}
can be computed as the solution to the following least-squares as in Algorithm
1

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{t}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\mathbf{c}}\bigl\|S_{t}\mathbf{c}-A{\bf v}\bigr\|^{2}.

(27)

Note that, since the entries of
{\bf v}
are zero-mean and independent,

\displaystyle\E\left[A^{H}\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\odot{\bf v}\right]=\E\left[(A^{H}S_{t}^{-1}A{\bf v})\odot{\bf v}\right]

(28)

is equal to the diagonal entries of
A^{H}S^{-1}_{t}A
. This observation implies that
A^{H}\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\odot{\bf v}
provides an unbiased estimator of the desired diagonal entries.

To exploit this property and efficiently estimate the diagonal entries, we adopt a Monte Carlo approach. Specifically, we independently generate
{\bf v}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf v}^{(K)}
, each distributed as
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{n})
, and compute
\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(k)}_{t}
, for
k=1,\ldots,K
, by solving the corresponding least-squares problems. The desired diagonal entries are then estimated as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}A^{H}\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(k)}_{t}\odot{\bf v}^{(k)}_{t}.

(29)

In practice, for sufficiently large

, this approximation yields an accurate estimate, as demonstrated in
[
8
]
. The algorithm is summarized as in Algorithm

B

S

This framework enables direct computation of the likelihood gradient without forming large matrices and performing expensive matrix–vector multiplications. It also avoids the memory overhead associated with storing such matrices. The resulting gradient descent step is efficient and accurate while maintaining scalability to high-dimensional imaging systems.

3.5
Estimation of Correlation Coefficient
\alpha

The proposed MLE-based optimization requires knowledge of the correlation coefficient
\alpha
. However, in practice,
\alpha
is unknown and needs to be estimated based on the measurements. From the measurement model (
8
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bf y}_{\ell}{\bf y}^{H}_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=AXA^{H}+\sigma^{2}_{z}I,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bf y}_{\ell-1}{\bf y}^{H}_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\alpha AXA^{H}.

(30)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf y}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(AXA^{H})+n\sigma^{2}_{z},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bf y}_{\ell-1}^{H}{\bf y}_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(AXA^{H}).

(31)

Let
\gamma=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(AXA^{H})
. This observation suggests that, given
{\bf y}_{1},\ldots,{\bf y}_{L}
, we can estimate
\gamma
and
\alpha
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}=\frac{1}{nL}\sum^{L}_{\ell=1}\sum^{n}_{i=1}y_{\ell,i}\overline{y_{\ell,i}}.

(32)

In this approximation, we ignore the contribution of
\sigma_{z}^{2}
. As shown later in our simulation results, this simplification has a limited impact on estimation accuracy. Since

\alpha=\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\bf y}_{\ell-1}^{H}{\bf y}_{\ell}]}{\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(AXA^{H})},

it can estimated with
\hat{\gamma}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\hat{\gamma}}\left(\frac{1}{n(L-1)}\sum^{L}_{\ell=2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}y_{\ell-1,i}\overline{y_{\ell,i}}\right).

(33)

The estimation accuracy is evaluated in Section
4.1
.

4.3
Reconstruction Efficiency of the Algorithm

We now evaluate the computational efficiency of the PGD. The gradient computation involves solving least-squares problems using the conjugate gradient (CG), where the number of CG convergence iterations directly impacts the runtime of each PGD iteration.

Neglecting lower-order operations (e.g., inner products
{\bf w}_{j}^{H}{\bf w}_{j}
and vector updates), the dominant computational cost arises from repeated applications of the operator
S
(e.g.,
S_{t}\mathbf{p}_{j}
) within the CG iterations. Since both direct and nested linear solves (
M_{t}
) rely on CG procedures involving
S
, the overall computational complexity is effectively characterized by the total number of
S
-operator applications.

In Figure
2
, we report the number of CG iterations required to approximate
S^{-1}
at each PGD iteration, which corresponds to the number of times the
S
-operator is applied. The number of the Monte-Carlo samples is
K=50
, the number of looks is
L=10
. We consider different apertures in the forward imaging process, including circular apertures with varying transparency ratios and annular apertures.

The
S
-operator consists of Fourier transform operations, which are efficiently implemented using FFT. For a
256\times 256
image, a single application of the
S
-operator takes approximately
2\times 10^{-4}
seconds. So, every iteration of the PGD algorithm takes approximately
20
to
40
seconds. The PGD algorithm takes
50
to
100
iterations to converge.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the reconstruction of speckle-free reflectivity from complex-valued multi-look measurements in digital holography, where the common assumption of independent speckle realizations across looks may be violated due to hardware constraints. We model the inter-look dependence using a first-order Markov process and derive the corresponding negative log-likelihood, leading to a constrained maximum likelihood estimation problem. To solve this problem efficiently, we adopt a PGD framework with implicit regularization via DIP. The computational challenges in evaluating the gradient are addressed using Monte Carlo–based diagonal approximation and conjugate gradient methods to avoid explicit matrix inversion, together with matrix-free implementations enabled by the Fourier optics structure. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed approach remains robust under strong inter-look correlation, achieving performance close to the ideal independent-look scenario and outperforming methods that ignore such dependencies.

As future work, we plan to apply the proposed framework to real digital holography data, where the measurement process may deviate from the assumed model. In particular, real-world data may exhibit model mismatches, such as imperfect calibration, non-Gaussian noise, or more complex correlation structures. Extending the proposed method to handle such discrepancies and validating its performance on experimental datasets will be an important direction for further study.

Derivation of Gradient

We first compute the gradient with respect to
B
, and then use chain rule to obtain the gradient w.r.t
{\bf x}
. By matrix identities and symmetry
[
22
]
,

\displaystyle d\log\det S

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(S^{-1}dB),

(34)

\displaystyle d\big({\bf y}^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}\big)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{tr}(S^{-1}{\bf y}{\bf y}^{H}S^{-1}dB).

(35)

Next we derive the gradient of
f_{L}
with respect to
B
, first for the term corresponding to
\ell=1
, and then for the other terms that correspond to
\ell\geq 2
.

For the first term, i.e.,
\log\det S+{\bf y}_{1}^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}
, we have

G^{(1)}=\frac{\partial}{\partial B}(\log\det S+{\bf y}_{1}^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1})=S^{-1}-S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}{\bf y}_{1}^{H}S^{-1}.

(36)

For the terms corresponding to
\ell>1
, fixing
{\bf r}_{\ell}
, the differential of
M
can be written as

dM=dB-\alpha^{2}\Big(dB\,S^{-1}B+BS^{-1}\,dB-BS^{-1}\,dB\,S^{-1}B\Big).

(37)

Therefore, the contribution through
M
from
\log\det M+{\bf r}_{\ell}^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}

\displaystyle d(\log\det M+{\bf r}_{\ell}^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(W_{\ell}\,dM)

(38)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\bigg(\Big[W_{\ell}-\alpha^{2}(S^{-1}BW_{\ell}+W_{\ell}BS^{-1})+\alpha^{2}S^{-1}BW_{\ell}BS^{-1}\Big]dB\bigg).

(39)

where

\displaystyle W_{\ell}\triangleq M^{-1}-M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}(M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell})^{H}

(40)

On the other hand, for a fixed
M
, the differential of
{\bf r}_{\ell}
can be written as

d{\bf r}_{\ell}=\alpha(BS^{-1}-I)dB\,S^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}.

(41)

Therefore, given that
B
is Hermitian, the contribution through
{\bf r}_{\ell}
from the quadratic term
{\bf r}_{\ell}^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}
yields

\displaystyle d({\bf r}_{\ell}^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=2\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\{(M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell})^{H}d{\bf r}_{\ell}\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\alpha(C_{\ell}dB+(C_{\ell}dB)^{H})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\alpha(C_{\ell}+C_{\ell}^{H})dB\right),

(42)

where

\displaystyle C_{\ell}\triangleq(BS^{-1}-I)\,S^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}(M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell})^{H}.

(43)

Finally, we compute the gradient with respect to
{\bf x}
. Note that

dB=A\,\mathrm{diag}(d{\bf x})\,A^{H}.

(44)

Therefore, since

\nabla f_{L}({\bf x})=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(A^{H}\frac{\partial f_{L}}{\partial B}A\right),\qquad\frac{\partial f_{L}}{\partial x_{i}}=a_{i}^{H}\frac{\partial f_{L}}{\partial B}a_{i},

(45)

using the chain rule, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{L}}{\partial B}=S^{-1}-S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}{\bf y}_{1}^{H}S^{-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\bigg(W_{\ell}-\alpha^{2}(S^{-1}BW_{\ell}+W_{\ell}BS^{-1})+\alpha^{2}S^{-1}BW_{\ell}BS^{-1}+\alpha(C_{\ell}+C_{\ell}^{H}),\bigg).

(46)

where
W_{\ell}
and
C_{\ell}
are defined as

\displaystyle W_{\ell}

\displaystyle\triangleq M^{-1}-M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}(M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell})^{H},

\displaystyle C_{\ell}

\displaystyle\triangleq(BS^{-1}-I)\,S^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}(M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell})^{H}.

Therefore, the gradient
\nabla f_{L}({\bf x})
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\nabla f_{L}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}A)-|A^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}|^{2}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\Bigg[\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}M^{-1}A)-|A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}|^{2}

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\bigg(\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}A)-\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}{\bf r}_{\ell}^{H}M^{-1}A)+\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}M^{-1}BS^{-1}A)

\displaystyle-\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}{\bf r}_{\ell}^{H}M^{-1}BS^{-1}A)-\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}BS^{-1}A)+|A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}|^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle+2\alpha\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\Big\{\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}BS^{-2}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}{\bf r}^{H}_{\ell}M^{-1}A)-\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}{\bf r}^{H}_{\ell}M^{-1}A)\Big\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}A)-|A^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{1}|^{2}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\Bigg[\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}M^{-1}A)-|A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}|^{2}

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\bigg(2\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\Big\{\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}A)-A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}\odot\overline{A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}}\Big\}

\displaystyle-\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}BS^{-1}A)+|A^{H}S^{-1}BM^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}|^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle+2\alpha\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\Big\{A^{H}BS^{-2}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}\odot\overline{A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}}-A^{H}S^{-1}{\bf y}_{\ell-1}\odot\overline{A^{H}M^{-1}{\bf r}_{\ell}}\Big\}\Bigg].
