Hemiptera diversity using the mtDNA COI barcode in almond orchards in the Northern San Joaquin Valley of California
Authors: Phillip Callahan, Jhalendra Rijal, Sudan Gyawaly, Fabian Gonzalez, Andrea Joyce, Hannah Burrack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlmonds (Prunus dulcis) (Mill.) are an economically important crop in California, with an estimated value of $8.6 billion dollars annually. The aim of this study was to identify the hemipteran pest and beneficial species in conventionally managed almond orchards when the developing almond crop was vulnerable to feeding damage. Insects were collected in 9 orchards by multiple methods and then Hemiptera were sorted to morphospecies. The DNA barcode of the mitochondrial DNA COI gene was sequenced for each specimen. For each species, the haplotype diversity was determined. A high diversity of Hemiptera was collected, including 26 species from 12 families. Pest species included Halyomorpha halys Stål (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae), Chinavia hilaris (Say) (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae), Lygus hesperus Knight, and Leptoglossus zonatus (Dallas) (Hemiptera: Coreidae); beneficials included Brochymena quadripustulata (Fabricius) (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae), Orius spp. Wolff (Hemiptera: Anthocoridae), Nabis spp. Latreille (Hemiptera: Nabidae) and Geocoris atricolor Montandon (Hemiptera: Geocoridae). Several orchards had high species diversity (13 species), others had only one species recovered. Haplotype diversity was low in most pest species except for L. hesperus (0.6) and was high in 2 beneficial groups, Nabis spp. and Orius tristicolor (White) (0.6 to 0.76). Hemipteran pests were detected through the time when developing almonds were susceptible to feeding damage, while beneficial Hemiptera were more abundant later. Correct identification of insect species is an integral component to Integrated Pest Management (IPM) to trace the origin of invasive species, uncover cryptic species, and to develop a relationship between species and crop damage.Keywords: Pentatomidae, Lygus hesperus, Halyomorpha halys, biodiversity, haplotype diversity

Introduction

Hemiptera is a megadiverse order of insects containing over 100,000 species (Engel 2018). Hemipterans are some of the most economically damaging pests to crops because of their high population growth potential and ability to vector plant viruses and transmit fungal and bacterial pathogens (Koch et al. 2016, Perilla-Henao and Casteel 2016, Cortez-Madrigal et al. 2022). These insects use their piercing-and-sucking mouthparts to feed directly on plant parts such as fruits and seeds. Numerous Hemiptera are considered pests in agricultural crops, such as Lygus Hahn spp. (Hemiptera: Miridae) on strawberry and cotton, citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri Kuwayama)(Hemiptera: Psyllidae) on orange and other citrus production, while emerging pests such as spotted lanternfly (Lycorma delicatula)(White)(Hemiptera: Fulgoridae) also threaten high-value commodities (Hagler et al. 2018, Grafton-Cardwell 2020, Jones et al. 2022). A crop vulnerable to hemipteran damage is almonds (Prunus dulcis)(Mill.), which are California’s top exported agricultural commodity with an annual international market value of $4.6 billion (CDFA 2022).

Several of the pest species of Hemiptera that occur in almonds and pistachios include Chinavia hilaris (Say)(Hemiptera: Pentatomidae), Leptoglossus zonatus (Dallas) (Hemiptera: Coreidae), and Halyomorpha halys Stål (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae), whose feeding leads to kernel necrosis, downgraded product, and nut abortion (Ingels and Daane 2018). Feeding from leaffooted bugs (Leptoglossus spp. Guérin-Méneville) and stink bugs (Pentatomidae) results in gummosis, a clear sap liquid at the feeding site on the nut hull (Barman et al. 2017, Joyce et al. 2019, Rijal et al. 2021). In almonds, H. halys feeding damage can begin in early-season; feeding between fruit set and shell hardening tends to be the most destructive, which in California almonds is typically from April to May (Rijal et al. 2021). In many agricultural crops, Lygus spp. Hahn (Hemiptera: Miridae) are often highly damaging (Hagler et al. 2018). Although Lygus hesperus Knight can be present in almond orchards, its impact on almonds is considered minimal despite occasional damage reports (Rijal et al. 2021). Beneficial Hemiptera can also occur in orchards, including Nabis spp. Latreille (Hemiptera: Nabidae), Orius spp. Wolff (Hemiptera: Anthocoridae) and Geocoris spp. Fallen (Hemiptera: Geocoridae)(Miliczky and Horton 2005, Hagler 2011, Wang et al. 2021, Saleh 2022).

Successful implementation of Integrated Pest Management (IPM) programs relies on accurate species identification (Antwi and Rondon 2019). DNA barcoding is a process in which molecular tools are used to extract a genetic sequence from an organism and can be used with global databases such as GenBank’s BLAST feature to identify species (Altschul et al. 1990). DNA barcoding has played a significant role in identifying insect species and other organisms, tracing the origin of introduced species, uncovering the presence of cryptic species, and examining genetic variability, including in Hemiptera (Hebert et al. 2004, Park et al. 2011, Joyce et al. 2017, Antwi and Rondon 2019). The identification of Hemiptera can aid in the development of IPM strategies that contribute to sustainable crop production. Species that are closely related can exhibit different biological features that are useful for pest management such as relative resistance to insecticides, host preference and range, attraction by natural enemies, and ability to cause plant disorders or transmit plant pathogens (Brown 2000, Jiu et al. 2017).

The goal of this study was to conduct a survey of the hemipteran species present within almond orchards in California’s San Joaquin Valley, throughout the time the developing almond crop is most vulnerable to Hemiptera feeding damage in April and May. Insects were photographed and used for visual identification of morphospecies, and then samples were sequenced for molecular identification. A comparison was made between the dates of the hemipteran insect collection and whether the species was a pest or beneficial insect.

Materials and Methods

Insect Collections

Insects were collected from 9 almond orchards from the northern San Joaquin Valley from April to May 2021 during the time when developing almonds were susceptible to Hemiptera feeding (Rijal et al. 2021) (Table 1, Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1). Collections were done by sweep sampling orchard floor vegetation for 8 to 10 sweeps. In addition, H. halys adults were also collected opportunistically, and by beat tray sampling of 6 border trees in each orchard. The number of collection visits at each of the 9 orchard sites varied (Table 1), with a total of 27 collection visits made for sampling when developing almonds were susceptible to Hemiptera feeding damage (April to May). At each almond orchard, collected insects were placed in brown paper bags and transported to the laboratory at the University of California Cooperative Extension, Stanislaus County, Modesto, California. Insects were examined under a dissecting microscope, and Hemiptera were sorted from other families and given a tentative species identification. The assignment of insect samples to a particular species was based on external morphological features for those species described in the literature (Mueller et al. 2003, Barman et al. 2017, Rijal and Gyawaly 2018). Reference photographs were recorded for each of the insects for later comparison with DNA molecular work, which was performed at the University of California, Merced, CA.

DNA Extraction and Polymerase Chain Reaction

The thorax of each hemipteran was removed from the insect body using forceps, scalpel and surgical scissors which were sterilized with molecular grade ethanol between specimens. The intact thorax was then placed inside an Eppendorf tube and used for extraction of genomic DNA. The Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen Inc., Valencia, CA) was used for DNA extraction, following the manufacturer’s protocol for tissue extraction and using a 1 h incubation period at 65 °C (Qiagen 2006). The DNA quantity was measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). The quantity of DNA in samples averaged 5 ng/μl. Specimens of male and female adults and nymphs were used for individual DNA extraction. The universal primers used where: LCO1490 (5′-GGTCAACAAA TCATAAAGATATTGG-3′) and the reverse primer HCO2198 (5′-TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCA AAAAATCA-3′) (Folmer et al. 1994). The DNA quantity was measured and concentrated in instances when the concentration was below 5 ng/μl. A polymerase chain reaction (PCR) mix for each sample consisted of the following: 40 µl sterile ultra-pure water; 5 µl Taq 10× buffer; 1 µl dNTPs; 1 µl forward primer; 1 µl reverse primer, and 1 µl Taq polymerase (Takara, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). For each reaction, 6 µl of template DNA was added to each vial and the contents were vortexed and spun down. The PCR program was the following: an initial 1 min warm-up at 95 ° C; then 40 cycles of a program consisting of 92 ° C for 30 s, 43 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s (Barman et al. 2017). After 40 cycles, the final extension was at 68 °C for 10 min followed by a hold at 4 °C. The PCR products were verified by visualizing samples on a 1.5% agarose gel and then cleaned up by the USB Exo-sap-it (Affymetrix Inc., Santa Clara, CA) PCR cleanup kit. Each forward and reverse sequence reaction was prepared using 1 µM primer and analyzed on an Applied Biosystems 3730xl genetic sequencer using BigDye Terminator v.3.1 Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) at the UC Berkeley DNA Sequencing Facility.

Sequence Analysis

Resulting forward and reverse sequences were edited, aligned, and used to produce consensus sequences using Geneious 7 (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand) (Kearse et al. 2012). Each consensus sequence obtained from the mtDNA COI barcode (1490/2198) primer was used to conduct a BLAST search in GenBank (Altschul et al. 1990). The sequence with the highest percentage match (all over 98% match, except Tepa sp. Rolston and McDonald) was used for identification. Sequences were submitted to GenBank for GenBank accession numbers.

Genetic Distance and Haplotype Trees

Haplotype diversity was determined for all species sequenced using DNAsp (Librado and Rozas 2009). PopArt was used to visualize the genetic distances between individuals of H. halys and Lygus spp. (Leigh and Bryant 2015). For H. halys, additional specimens were obtained from Barcode of Life Data Systems (BOLD) to include in the haplotype network (Ratnasingham and Hebert 2007) (described in Phylogenetic Trees section below).

Phylogenetic Trees and Haplotype Networks

Sequences from all species were used to produce a maximum likelihood tree in Mega. The Find Best DNA/Protein Models (ML) feature was first run to determine the appropriate model to run. Subsequently, each tree was run for 500 iterations. In addition, the sequences of H. halys and Lygus spp. were used to produce additional haplotype networks to visualize genetic distance among individuals. For the H. halys haplotype network, other BOLD accessions were included from the United States and from Asia (Ratnasingham and Hebert 2007, Yan et al. 2021). Fifteen H. halys collected in this study from California were compared to fifteen H. halys sequences collected in China (BMSB018-20 through BMSB025-20 and BMSB031-20 through BMSB037-20).

Landscape Surrounding Collection Sites and Hemipteran Diversity

The surrounding landscape around each collection site was examined using Google Earth. The landscape on each side of the orchard included almond orchards, vegetable crops, homes/residential areas, processing plants, canals, and a railroad. The descriptions of surrounding areas were used to make a descriptive assessment of the area. In a few cases, multiple categories were present on the border of a farm. Simmons (site 7) had a mustard cover crop, while other sites did not have cover crops but had variable densities of orchard floor vegetation. The landscape types were compared qualitatively for almond fields with the highest and lowest hemipteran diversity.

Hemipteran Species in Relation to Almond Phenology

The early stages of the almond fruit development typically occur in late March through May and is when the almond is considered vulnerable to Hemiptera feeding damage and major hemipteran pests become active in the orchard. Hemipteran pests can insert their stylet mouthparts into the soft almond to feed, resulting in nut abortion (March to April) as well as necrotic kernels (April-June). Samples collected that produced a sequence in this study had their collection date plotted across the critical 8-wk (April to May) period of almond development when almonds are most susceptible to feeding damage. Species were separated into 4 groups based on the literature; those considered pests in almonds, beneficial Hemiptera, species considered pests on other crops (not considered pests on almonds), and species of unknown pest status. This allowed the examination of species which were sequenced in relationship to almond development.

Hemipteran Damage in Almonds at Collection Sites

At each collection site, the almond damage by Hemiptera was determined at the end of the growing season at harvest. Almond samples were collected from each orchard from the Nonpareil variety. Twelve samples (with 50 nuts per sample) were collected from a border row in each orchard. Nuts were hand cracked after drying 7 to 10 days, and hemipteran feeding damage indicated by gummy and necrotic kernels was recorded and converted into mean percent damage.

Results

Sequences Produced

The sample collections resulted in a total of 106 Hemipterans sequenced, consisting of 26 species from 12 hemipteran families (Table 2). Approximately half of the 232 Hemiptera samples collected yielded a high quality sequence (Supplementary Table S1). The 2 most diverse families were Miridae with 6 species (4 Lygus spp.) and Pentatomidae with 6 species (Table 2). The most sequences produced were from H. halys (15 specimens), followed by Graminella nigrinota DeLong and Mohr (12 specimens) and Lygus elisus Van Duzee (9 specimens). Other species produced less sequences, such as Geocoris atricolor Montandon and Myzus persicae Sulzer each with 1 individual (Table 2).

Haplotype Diversity and Phylogenetic Trees

The species with the most haplotypes included a non-almond pest, Graminella nigrinota (7 haplotypes), 2 pest species (Closterotomus norwegicus)(Gemlin)(Hemiptera: Miridae) and L. hesperus with 3 haplotypes each and one beneficial species, Orius tristicolor with 3 haplotypes (Table 3). Haplotype diversity (genetic diversity) was highest (0.6 to 0.89) for 6 species, L. hesperus, C. norwegicus, G. nigrinota, Nabis spp., O. tristicolor, and Spanagonicus albofasciatus (Reuter)(Hemiptera: Miridae), and moderate (0.3 to 0.5) for 4 species Lygaeus kalmii Stål (Hemiptera: Lygaeidae), L. elisus, Euschistus conspersus Uhler (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae), and Muirodelphax spp. Wagner (Hemiptera: Delphastidae). The insects considered pests typically had a lower number of haplotypes and lower genetic diversity, including C. hilaris, L. zonatus, and H. halys (Tables 2 and 3). Lygus spp. were an exception to this pattern, as they can be considered a pest yet have high genetic diversity (Table 4). Eleven of the species had just one specimen sequenced (Tables 2 and 3).

Hemipteran Species and Almond Phenology

The different species of Hemiptera collected and sequenced were related to dates of almond development from April to May, when the developing almond is considered vulnerable to Hemipteran feeding. In the last week of April and early May, more almond pests such as H. halys, C. hilaris, and L. hesperus were detected (Table 4). Six species considered beneficial, G. atricolor, Nabis alternatus Parshley (Hemiptera: Nabidae), Nabis americoferus Carayon (Hemiptera: Nabidae), B.quadripustulata, Orius harpocrates Herring (Hemiptera: Anthocoridae) and O. tristicolor were collected mostly during May (Table 4). More than half of these were late in the almond development, occurring during the last week of May 2021 (Table 4). Hemiptera which are pests in other crops but not thought to be pests in almonds were collected from mid-April through May, the most numerous of these being L. elisus (Table 4). Several Hemiptera which are not thought to be pests were sequenced as well, including G. nigrinota and Muirodelphax spp., which had 12 and 9 individuals sequenced, respectively (Table 4). Other species had just a few specimens collected and sequenced (Table 4).

Hemipteran Damage in Almonds at Collection Sites

The damage from hemipteran feeding on nonpareil almonds was determined at each collection site. Letteau (site 5) had the highest percent damage (3.33 ± 0.93), followed by almonds from the Pauline (site 6) (2.5 ± 0.65) and then Chowchilla North (site 2) (1.17 ± 0.52) (Table 5). Damage was not assessed at Chowchilla South (site 3). Low damage levels were recorded at Escalon (site 4), Valley Home (site 8), and Bloss (site 1) (0.17 ± 0.17), and Warnerville (site 9) (0.66 ± 0.22) (Table 5).

Discussion

Hemiptera collected from commercial almond orchards throughout the time the developing almond was susceptible to Hemiptera feeding damage were collected for morphological and molecular species identification. The mitochondrial DNA COI barcode was used to identify species, and results were examined in relationship to which species were collected during almond development (April and May) and their known or suspected pest status.

Accurate hemipteran species identification can be improved by using both DNA barcoding and visual assessment of specimens using morphological traits. When used together, both techniques achieve high levels of accuracy in identification (Antwi and Rondon 2019). Using the mitochondrial DNA COI barcode, 26 Hemiptera species from twelve hemipteran families were identified in this study. Several known pests of almonds were detected as well as known beneficial Hemiptera. Additionally, Hemiptera considered pests in other crops but not considered pests in almonds were collected. One of the more numerous insects was H. halys, an invasive Hemiptera with a broad host range. Several Lygus spp. were abundant, including L. hesperus.

Several recent studies similarly found a high level of hemipteran diversity when sampling crops. Another Hemiptera diversity study in hazelnut orchards found 27 species of Hemipterans in Turkey (Kaçar and Dursun 2022). In India, 18 species of Hemiptera were detected in almond orchards (Chougar et al. 2024). In collections from the San Joaquin Valley from diverse crops including alfalfa, almonds, vineyards, olive orchards, and pastures, Hemiptera was the most abundant order collected (Sisterson et al. 2020). Since numerous species of Hemiptera can co-occur in a field crop, it remains important to understand which species are numerous during vulnerable stages of the developing crop.

Differences in hemipteran diversity occurred between the 9 collection sites in this study. The site with the most Hemiptera diversity was Escalon (site 4), with 8 families, followed by the Valley Home and Simmons sites (sites 8 and 7), each with 7 families sequenced. These sites had more collection effort, which likely uncovered more species. In contrast, Chowchilla North and Pauline (sites 2 and 6) only had one species (one sequence) produced. Most sites had collections once per month, however Escalon had more collection visits overall (6) while Pauline only had 1, which contributed to the abundance of insects collected (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). Another factor which could influence the diversity is the ecology of land adjacent to collection sites. The type and density of vegetation present on the orchard floor might have a beneficial effect on diversity, and this could be a contributing factor to the higher level of diversity observed at Simmons (site 7) which had a mustard cover crop. Diversity of surrounding crops has been recorded to influence insect abundance (Sisterson et al. 2020). Based on sweep net sampling, this study reported many hemipterans, mainly from small plant families, except in one cover crop planted orchard where other pentatomids were found. All samples of Hemiptera collected in this study were obtained using sweep netting. Multiple species of pentatomid pests, including C. hilaris, E. conspersus, Thyanta spp., and Chlorochroa uhleri Stål (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae) have similarly been collected from almond orchards using multiple sampling methods including beating tray, sweep nets, traps, and visual sampling methods (Barman et al. 2017, Rijal et al. 2021). All orchards in the present study were cultivated under conventional methods and most likely used a range of different broad-spectrum insecticides that would influence hemipteran diversity (Rijal et al. 2021).

When comparing Hemiptera diversity with insect damage levels, Escalon (site 4) and Valley Home (site 8) had higher hemipteran diversity and lower damage levels, while sites with lower diversity (Pauline-site 6, North Chowchilla-site 2, and Letteau-site 5) had higher hemipteran damage levels. Letteau (site 5) had the highest damage and 3 specimens of H. halys recovered in April which shows this pest was present while almonds were vulnerable to damage. Two L. hesperus specimens were sequenced from Chowchilla North (site 2), which had a moderate level of damage. Although L. hesperus is an occasional pest of almonds, the damage might have been contributed by other stink bugs.

The 2 most diverse families in the present study were Miridae and Pentatomidae, each with 6 species. The insects considered pests typically had a lower number of haplotypes and lower genetic diversity, except for L. hesperus. Lygus spp. are native to North America, and the 4 species of Lygus (L. hesperus, L. elisus, L. lineolaris, and L. keltoni) collected in this study were also collected previously in the San Joaquin Valley (Mueller et al. 2003, Mueller et al. 2005, EPPO 2015). Lygus spp. have been found to have a high number of haplotypes (Zhou et al. 2012, Zhang et al. 2017). Species of Lygus spp. have been found to exhibit low intraspecific genetic distances (∼1%) compared to other Hemiptera (eg Chinavia, Thyanta) (Barman et al. 2017, Antwi and Rondon 2019). Lygus hesperus is a pest on over 100 plants including fruit, field, and vegetable crops, and it has a geographic range that spans across North America from southern Mexico to the southwestern provinces of Canada. Lygus hesperus has recently been detected in Saudi Arabia and is expected to become an established pest, and genetic identification of the strain or species introduced is important for pest management (Sayed et al. 2020). Numerous L. elisus were collected in this study; it is widespread in Canada and the Western United States including California, Arizona, Oregon, Washington, New Mexico, and Utah (Clancy 1968, Timlick et al. 1993). Its important wild hosts are winter mustard, sowbane, Russian thistle, and burrow weed (Clancy 1968). Lygus elisus is not considered a pest on almonds, it is notable how similar L. elisus and L. herperus look (Fig. 5). It may be worthwhile to determine if L. elisus has pest potential on almonds, since it was present during almond development. Lygus keltoni attacks canola and alfalfa in western Canada and it has been found in the United States, but is not a reported pest on almonds (Cárcamo et al. 2006).

Halyomorpha halys sequenced from this study only contained 2 haplotypes despite being one of the most numerous species sequenced (15 sequences). This invasive Hemiptera was retrieved from 4 of the 9 sites. When H. halys from the present study were compared to 15 samples from China, 13 of the 15 Californian samples shared a haplotype with Chinese specimens, while 2 of the Californian samples formed a single unique haplotype group. The match of the California genotype to Chinese genotypes indicates a region where natural enemies could be investigated. In addition, understanding the climate of the geographic range in China where the abundant California haplotype occurs could help determine where to search for natural enemies to be effective in California. The abundance of haplotypes from China shows that there is more genetic diversity from the country of origin (China) than within the introduced population present in the Central Valley of California (Fig. 3). Halyomorpha halys collected in the Western United States also contained haplotypes similar to Chinese specimens indicating the likely introduction from Asia (Valentin et al. 2017). Parasitoids of H. halys native to East Asia, including Trissolcus japonicus Ashmead (Hymenoptera: Scelionidae) typically provide parasitism rates above 50% (Chen et al. 2021). Comparing genetics of invasive pests to those in their region of origin is a valuable step in creating biological control programs.

Several generalist hemipteran predators were found within almond orchards in this study. Orius spp. were detected, and there were multiple haplotypes with high haplotype diversity. Orius spp. are very effective predators and can provide biological pest control in a variety of cropping systems including orchards (Brust and Yurchak 2021). Geocoris spp. were also detected, although in lower numbers; they are among the most important and numerous predatory insects in many agricultural systems in the United States (Hagler 2011). Geocoris is susceptible to broad spectrum insecticides and thrives in systems that conserve natural enemy populations; use of conventional insecticides at collection sites in this study could contribute to the observed low abundance in this study (Hagler 2011). This study uncovered at least 5 haplotypes of Nabis spp., while at least 9 species of Nabis occur in California (University of California Statewide Integrated Pest Management Program [UCIPM] 2017). They feed on various true bugs such as Lygus spp. and aphids along with other arthropod pests (UCIPM 2017). Brochymena quadripustulata was detected, and it is a beneficial pentatomid that is commonly found in almonds and other tree crops. They feed on caterpillars, sawfly and leaf beetle larvae, and other relatively large, slow-moving, soft-bodied insects (Larivière 1994, UCIPM 2020).

There were 4 Lygus spp. sequenced which were present during the almond kernel development. Generally, smaller hemipteran insects such as Lygus spp. have shorter mouthparts than the larger Hemiptera such as Halyomorpha, Chinavia and Leptoglossus. Chinavia hilaris has been found to have a rostrum 2.8 times longer than that of Lygus spp. (Esquivel 2015). Lygus spp. are not believed to feed on almonds as the kernel hardens, and except for L. hesperus, are not an economically significant pest of almonds (Daane et al. 2019, Rijal et al. 2021). At times, almonds are reported to have significant kernel damage yet only modest numbers of large-bodied Hemiptera such as Chinavia or Leptoglossus are found in orchards. Feeding by large Hemiptera is thought to allow secondary pathogens to enter the developing almond and result in brown spot on the kernel. The enzyme complex, however, can vary from species to species, which can be a factor contributing to the extent of crop damage regardless of insect size (Rijal et al. 2021). Feeding on almonds by Lygus spp. could be further investigated. Cage studies of different Lygus species could examine the ability of Lygus feeding to damage developing kernels. In addition, hemipteran insects captured in almond orchards could have their gut contents analyzed using a DNA metabarcoding approach to determine which plant species they were feeding on, to determine whether the most abundant species are feeding on the crop.

The use of integrating morphology and molecular markers in accurately identifying hemipteran insects could be more useful than each method individually. One of the most important first steps to successfully control or implement IPM programs is accurate species identification. Species that are closely related can exhibit different features that are relevant to pest management, such as relative resistance to insecticides, host preference, range, susceptibility to natural enemies, and ability to cause plant disorders or transmit plant pathogens (Barman et al. 2017, Antwi and Rondon 2019). As pest control efforts globally continue to move from conventional methods of applying broad-spectrum insecticides toward bio-based pest control approaches, it is increasingly important to identify which species are in a crop field. The implementation of Hemiptera identification using DNA barcodes can aid in developing IPM strategies that ultimately lead to sustainable agricultural practices.
