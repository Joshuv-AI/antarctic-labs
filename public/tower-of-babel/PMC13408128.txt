TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 establish integrated control over processing of most abundant human non-coding RNAs
Authors: Monireh Panah, Rui Che, Bhoomi Mirani, Xiaotong Chen, Hong Luo, Andrei Alexandrov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough abundance of the metastasis-associated long non-coding RNA MALAT1 depends on the maturation of its triple-helix-containing 3′ end via non-canonical processing, the control of this step has remained unknown. Using iterative genome-wide screening, we identified TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 that together control the 3′-end processing of MALAT1, enabling a reduction in its levels. We further show that these factors form part of a previously unrecognized RNase MRP and P control (RMPPc) pathway that, in addition to MALAT1, establishes integrated control over the processing of MEN-β, internal transcribed spacers in pre-rRNA, and 5′-leader sequences in pre-tRNA, thereby impacting fundamental processes prerequisite for translation. We demonstrate that this far-reaching impact is achieved through a single control point: inclusion of the initiating ATG-containing exon 2 in RPP14, an essential component of both RNase MRP and RNase P, thereby controlling both catalytic RNAs—MRP RNA and H1 RNA. Finally, we show that upstream of this primary control point, the RMPPc pathway forms a remarkably interlinked four-pronged feedback circuit that confers stability on post-transcriptional processing of over 90% of the total RNA content in human cells.Subject terms: High-throughput screening, Long non-coding RNAs

Introduction

Abundant, vertebrate-specific nuclear long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs)—Metastasis Associated Lung Adenocarcinoma Transcript 1 (MALAT1)1–4 and Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia β (MEN-β, also called NEAT1_2)5—have been implicated in a number of human cancers6–8. Overexpression of the lncRNA MALAT1 promotes tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis, and correlates with poor survival in such prevalent cancers as lung, pancreatic, colorectal, and cervical cancer, in which overexpression of MALAT1 is associated with a dramatic, nearly 60% decrease in 5-year survival9–14. Similarly, expression of lncRNA MEN-β is increased in cancerous tissue compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissue, correlating with stage and tumor size7,8,15–17. Conversely, downregulation of MALAT1 decreases tumor growth and metastasis while promoting cell differentiation18,19, whereas knockdown of MEN-β leads to significant inhibition of growth and metastasis, and induces apoptosis in certain cancer types8. Consequently, downregulation of MALAT1 and MEN-β levels represents a promising cancer treatment strategy4,6,19–23.

One of the distinctive features contributing to the high levels of MALAT1 and MEN-β is their structured, triple-helical Elements for Nuclear Expression (ENEs) (Fig. 1a)2,3,5, which protect MALAT1 and MEN-β from the nuclear degradation machinery2,3. Due to this protective effect of the ENE, a number of strategies have been pursued to downregulate MALAT1 by targeting its ENE structure, including the use of small molecules22–26 and antisense oligonucleotides10,27–31, aiming to suppress MALAT1-promoted growth and metastasis.

A second distinctive feature of mature MALAT1 and MEN-β is that their 3′ ends are formed by Ribonuclease P (RNase P) cleavage upstream of their ENE-adjacent tRNA-like structures, mascRNA and menRNA (shown for MALAT1 in Fig. 1a), rather than by canonical cleavage and polyadenylation5,32. We have recently shown that inhibition of RNase P cleavage of MALAT1 markedly reduces its steady-state levels in cervical cancer HeLa cells33, suggesting that targeting MALAT1 3′-end processing could complement ENE-targeting strategies for downregulating this metastasis-promoting lncRNA.

To identify cellular machinery acting on the MALAT1 3′-end, we previously developed a forward genetic Mirror approach that relies on nuclear export of a fragment of non-polyadenylated nuclear RNA of interest. Using this approach, we identified components of RNase P, the RNA exosome, including DIS3, EXOSC10, and exosome-associated C1D, the Nuclear Exosome Targeting (NEXT) complex, and DDX5933. Separately, we developed a sensitivity-enhanced iterative screening strategy that employs “omission” CRISPR libraries, which deliberately lack guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting known pathway components. Demonstrating efficient forward genetic discovery of previously unknown factors, this strategy identified previously unknown protein components of human RNase MRP, Rmp24, and Rmp6434.

Here, we report that using a genome-wide screen combining the Mirror33 and omission34 approaches, we identified a previously unrecognized RNase MRP and RNase P control (RMPPc) pathway, which controls 3′-end processing of lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β, enabling a reduction in their levels. We further show that beyond MALAT1 and MEN-β, this feedback-safeguarded pathway regulates processing of internal transcribed spacers in pre-rRNA and 5′-leader sequences in pre-tRNA, collectively exerting global control over processing of the most abundant human RNAs required for translation.

Results

Design of the genome-wide sensitivity-enhanced forward genetic screen to identify genes that control 3′-end processing of MALAT1 and MEN-β

We recently showed for MALAT133 and now show for MEN-β that inhibiting their RNase P-mediated 3′-end processing markedly reduces the steady-state levels of these lncRNAs in HeLa cervical cancer cells (shown by knockouts of the RNase P-specific components RPP21 and RPPH1 in Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), suggesting that interfering with this step could assist in downregulating these metastasis-associated lncRNAs.

To identify genes that control 3′-end processing of MALAT1, we employed, in a single genome-wide screen, two approaches we developed previously: (1) the Mirror approach33, and (2) the omission screening approach34. The Mirror approach relies on the nuclear export of a fluorescent reporter carrying the MALAT1 triple-helical ENE stability element and the tRNA-like mascRNA element (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1c)33. Inhibition of MALAT1-mascRNA cleavage redirects 3′-end formation to the downstream BGH polyadenylation site of the reporter (Fig. 1c), thereby increasing the stability of the reporter partially destabilized by the C8351G single-nucleotide substitution, enabling FACS-based identification of genes involved in MALAT1 3′-end processing33. Since Mirror relies on cell fluorescence rather than cell viability or growth rate, it efficiently identifies pathway-affecting sgRNAs regardless of whether they target essential or non-essential human genes33. The omission screening approach34 enhances the sensitivity of this screen by preventing saturation effects caused by reidentification of known MALAT1 3′-end processing components33 through deliberate omission of sgRNAs targeting them from the library (Fig. 1d). To further enhance screening sensitivity, the screening employed iterative rounds of library enrichment35 (Fig. 1d). We performed this study in HeLa cells because MALAT1 steady-state levels strongly associate with tumor aggressiveness in cervical cancers11.

Iterative omission screening identified two genes as required for MALAT1 3′-end processing: TRNAU1AP and PRPF39

We conducted four rounds of genome-wide iterative FACS-based enrichment of the omission sgRNA library in the “Red” Mirror reporter cell line (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1c), during which cells harboring sgRNAs that increased fluorescence of the GFP Mirror reporter by perturbing its 3′-end processing (Fig. 1c) were enriched in the blue sorting gate (Fig. 1e). At the end of each round, a new enriched lentiviral sgRNA library was constructed for the subsequent round of screening (Fig. 1d, e). After the third screening round, deep sequencing revealed three genes with no previously known roles in MALAT1 3′-end processing: TRNAU1AP, PRPF39, and WDR61 (Fig. 1f), with multiple sgRNAs enriched for each gene (Supplementary Fig. 1d and Supplementary Data 1). Similar to knockouts of the RNase P-specific components RPP21 and RPPH1, individual knockouts of TRNAU1AP, PRPF39, and WDR61 increased Mirror reporter fluorescence (Figs. 1g and 2n, and Supplementary Fig. 1e). Accordingly, knockouts of TRNAU1AP, PRPF39, and WDR61 reduced the steady-state levels of endogenous lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β (Fig. 1h). Furthermore, knockouts of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 impaired 3′-end processing of endogenous MALAT1 and MEN-β (Fig. 1i), thereby establishing that TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 are required for this process.

TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 control 3′-end processing of MALAT1 and MEN-β by regulating the levels of H1 RNA, the catalytic component of RNase P

Since knockouts of RNase P-specific components RPPH1 and RPP21 (i) inhibit 3′-end processing of endogenous MALAT1 and MEN-β and (ii) reduce their levels33 (Fig. 1b), and since knockouts of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 exhibit both of these defects (Fig. 1h, i), we analyzed the effects of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 on the steady-state levels of H1 RNA, the catalytic component of human RNase P. Indeed, as shown by Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) tracks and quantified using RT-qPCR in Fig. 2a, knockouts of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 reduced H1 RNA levels 5.5- and 8.1-fold, respectively, suggesting that these factors affect 3′-end processing and the levels of the non-coding RNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β by controlling H1 RNA levels.

TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 control the levels of H1 RNA by regulating inclusion of the ATG-containing exon in a single protein component of RNase P, Rpp14

To determine the mechanism underlying the control of the steady-state levels of H1 RNA by TRNAU1AP and PRPF39, we analyzed RNA-seq of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts. As shown by IGV tracks and quantified using RT-qPCR (Fig. 2b), knockouts of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 reduce inclusion of exon 2 in RPP14 mRNA by 11.2- and 22.8-fold, respectively. Critically, this exon contains the ATG start codon of RPP14 (Fig. 2b), essential for translation of this mRNA into functional Rpp14 protein. In the absence of exon 2, this transcript is predicted to yield amino acid sequences unrelated to Rpp14, one of which encoding a different protein, 3-hydroxyacyl-thioester dehydratase 2 (Htd2)36.

Ruling out sgRNA off-target effects, the introduction of plasmid-expressed TRNAU1AP into TRNAU1AP knockout cells rescues both the inclusion of the ATG-containing exon 2 in RPP14 mRNA (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 2b) and the H1 RNA levels (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Consistently, the introduction of plasmid-expressed PRPF39 into PRPF39 knockout cells rescues both the inclusion of the ATG-containing exon 2 in RPP14 mRNA (Fig. 2h) and H1 RNA levels (Fig. 2i).

Similar to the effects of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts on H1 RNA in Fig. 2a, the knockout of RPP14, which is an essential core protein component of human RNase P37, leads to a dramatic 9.1-fold reduction in H1 RNA steady-state levels (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2a). This effect mirrors the severe reduction in steady-state levels of H1 RNA observed upon knockout of other core human RNase P components, including RPP20, RPP30, RPP40, and POP5 (Supplementary Fig. 2d). It is also consistent with the previously reported reduction in the steady-state levels of H1 RNA upon depletion of core RNase P components in yeast, including the RPP14 homolog POP8, potentially due to destabilization of the core ribonucleoprotein complex38. However, knockout of the RPP21, a peripheral component of the RNase P complex in both human and yeast39–41, has only a minimal effect on the steady-state levels of H1 RNA (Fig. 2c), despite both RPP21 and RPP14 knockouts causing similarly severe defects in RNase P-mediated 3′-end processing of MALAT1 and MEN-β (Fig. 2d, e).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 control inclusion of the ATG-containing exon in a single core protein component of RNase P, Rpp14, which, in turn, controls steady-state levels of the catalytic RNA component of RNase P, H1 RNA, consistent with the requirement for the core protein components of RNase P to maintain H1 RNA levels, thereby uncovering the post-transcriptional mechanism underlying TRNAU1AP-PRPF39-mediated control of RNase P activity.

Exogenous RPP14 rescues the defects of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts, including the loss of H1 RNA and impaired 3′-end processing of MALAT1 and MEN-β

To determine whether TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 regulate MALAT1 and MEN-β 3′-end processing and levels through a single control point, i.e., by controlling RNase P activity through the inclusion of the ATG-containing exon 2 in RPP14—which, in turn, regulates H1 RNA levels—we examined whether re-introduction of the correctly spliced ATG-containing RPP14 would rescue the defects caused by TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts. Remarkably, plasmid-expressed intronless, ATG-containing RPP14 fully restored H1 RNA levels in both TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockout cells (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 2b), demonstrating that the TRNAU1AP- and PRPF39-mediated loss of Rpp14 alone is responsible for the severe decrease in H1 RNA steady-state levels in these knockouts. Furthermore, the plasmid-expressed intronless, ATG-containing RPP14 rescued the MALAT1 and MEN-β 3′-end processing defects in TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockout cells (Fig. 2k, l and Supplementary Fig. 2b), establishing RPP14 as the central component of the previously unrecognized post-transcriptional mechanism through which human cells regulate H1 RNA abundance and, consequently, RNase P activity.

Through the same RPP14-dependent mechanism, TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 control tRNA and ribosome biogenesis

In humans, RNase P processes the 5′-leader sequences of pre-tRNAs40,42,43 and cleaves tRNA-like structures5,32,44, whereas ribonuclease MRP (RNase MRP) processes precursor rRNA (pre-rRNA) at a specific site, ITS1 site 234,45–48.

Consistent with the loss of ATG-containing exon 2 in RPP14 mRNA and the resulting loss of H1 RNA (Fig. 2a, b), and MALAT1 and MEN-β RNase P-processing defects observed in TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockout cells (Fig. 1i), knockouts of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 increase levels of pre-tRNAs with unprocessed 5′-leader sequences (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

Since Rpp14 is not only one of the 10 essential protein components of human RNase P37, but also one of the eleven essential protein components of RNase MRP (Fig. 2m)34, we examined the effects of TRNAU1AP, PRPF39, and RPP14 knockouts on the steady-state levels of MRP RNA, the catalytic component of RNase MRP, and the efficiency of RNase MRP-catalyzed cleavage of ITS1 site 2 in pre-rRNA.

Indeed, in addition to reducing the steady-state levels of the catalytic component of RNase P, H1 RNA, 5.5- and 8.1-fold (Fig. 2a), TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts reduce the steady-state levels of MRP RNA 2.9- and 3.3-fold, respectively (Fig. 2o). Ruling out sgRNA off-target effects, introducing plasmid-expressed TRNAU1AP into TRNAU1AP knockout cells and plasmid-expressed PRPF39 into PRPF39 knockout cells successfully restores MRP RNA levels (Fig. 2p, q and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Furthermore, similar to the effects of knockouts of other shared core components of RNase P/MRP, RPP20, RPP30, RPP40, and POP5, on steady-state levels of H1 RNA and MRP RNA (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e), knockout of RPP14 results in a significant 4.5-fold reduction in MRP RNA levels (Fig. 2o and Supplementary Fig. 2a), demonstrating that the levels of not only H1 RNA, but also MRP RNA depend on RPP14.

Analogous to the rescue of the levels of H1 RNA (Fig. 2j), introduction of plasmid-expressed intronless, ATG-containing RPP14 fully restores MRP RNA levels in TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockout cells (Fig. 2r and Supplementary Fig. 2b), demonstrating that TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 control the levels of both catalytic RNA components of RNase P and RNase MRP through a single control point—by regulating the levels of Rpp14 through the inclusion of its ATG-containing exon (Fig. 2b).

Finally, consistent with the ITS1 site 2 pre-rRNA processing defect in RPP14 knockout (Fig. 2s), and with the loss of catalytic MRP RNA resulting from the loss of Rpp14 in TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts (Fig. 2o), TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts show a defect in ITS1 site 2 cleavage of pre-rRNA (Fig. 2t). In line with the pre-tRNA and pre-rRNA processing defects, TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts impair translation (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and cell growth (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 cross- and self-regulate their mRNA levels via four interconnected negative feedback loops

As shown by IGV RNA-seq tracks (Fig. 3a, d), the mRNAs of both TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 contain exons harboring premature termination codons (poison exons), located between exons 3 and 4 in TRNAU1AP (Fig. 3a) and between exons 8 and 9 in PRPF39 (Fig. 3d). In TRNAU1AP and PRPF39, inclusion of these poison exons results in mRNAs predicted to be degraded by the nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) and to encode truncated proteins.

We found that TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 regulate the inclusion of each other’s poison exons: knockout of PRPF39 results in a 3.2-fold decrease in the inclusion of the poison exon in the mRNA of TRNAU1AP (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4a); conversely, knockout of TRNAU1AP results in a 5.7-fold decrease in the inclusion of the poison exon in the mRNA of PRPF39 (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Demonstrating that the effects of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 knockouts on the inclusion of each other’s poison exons are largely independent of (i) splicing of the other introns in their pre-mRNAs, (ii) promoter-associated effects, and (iii) 5′ and 3′ UTR regulation, the minimal minigene splicing reporters (Fig. 3g) containing the introns flanking the poison exon and two neighboring exons replicated these cross-regulatory effects (Fig. 3h, i and Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Consistent with escaping NMD, the PRPF39 knockout-mediated exclusion of the poison exon in the mRNA of TRNAU1AP (Fig. 3a, b) resulted in a 2.5-fold increase in endogenous TRNAU1AP transcript levels (Fig. 3c). Conversely, the TRNAU1AP knockout-mediated exclusion of the poison exon in the mRNA of PRPF39 (Fig. 3d, e) resulted in a 1.8-fold increase in endogenous PRPF39 transcript levels (Fig. 3f). In addition to these two cross-regulatory feedback loops (labeled as negative feedback loops 1 and 2 in Fig. 4), each, TRNAU1AP and PRPF39, regulate the inclusion of poison exons in their own mRNAs (indicated as negative feedback loops 3 and 4 in Fig. 4): knockout of PRPF39 results in the exclusion of the poison exon in the minigene of PRPF39 (Fig. 3i), whereas knockout of TRNAU1AP results in the exclusion of the poison exon in the minigene of TRNAU1AP (Fig. 3h).

Model of the RNase MRP and RNase P control (RMPPc) pathway.

Two jointly acting regulators, TRNAU1AP and PRPF39, establish coordinated, feedback-safeguarded post-transcriptional control of non-canonical 3′-end processing of the lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β, as well as processing of internal transcribed spacers in pre-rRNA and 5′-leader sequences in pre-tRNA. Through a single control point, the inclusion of the ATG-containing exon in RPP14, TRNAU1AP, and PRPF39 control RPP14, one of the core components of the two major human ribonucleases, RNase P and RNase MRP. Loss of Rpp14 results in loss of H1 RNA and MRP RNA, which are the catalytic RNA components of RNase P and RNase MRP, respectively. Loss of the catalytic components of RNase P and RNase MRP results in a decrease in non-canonical processing of 3′-end tRNA-like structures of lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β, as well as defective processing of 5′-leader sequences in pre-tRNA and internal transcribed spacers in pre-rRNA. Furthermore, TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 are themselves subject to cross- and self-regulatory feedback via poison exons arranged in a four-pronged circuit (labeled 1 through 4). Positioned upstream of fundamental cellular processes, the RMPPc pathway establishes integrated control and safeguards processing of >90% of the total RNA content in human cells.

In contrast to PRPF39 and TRNAU1AP knockouts, plasmid-driven overexpression of intronless PRPF39 and TRNAU1AP reduces endogenous PRPF39 and TRNAU1AP mRNA levels (compare Fig. 3c, f with 3j, k). Consistent with the joint requirement of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 for poison exon inclusion in their mRNAs, the largest reduction in endogenous PRPF39 and TRNAU1AP mRNA levels occurs when both intronless constructs are overexpressed (Fig. 3j, k).

Trnau1ap and Prpf39 interact with each other and the splicing machinery through distinct regions

The joint requirement of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 for inclusion of (i) poison exons in their mRNAs and (ii) the ATG-containing exon in RPP14 mRNA suggests a potential physical interaction between Trnau1ap and Prpf39. Consistently, AlphaFold49 predicts that, similar to murine Prpf3950, human Prpf39 forms a homodimer. Additionally, it predicts that the human Prpf39 homodimer directly interacts with C-terminal residues 248-287 of human Trnau1ap, such that α-helix 2 (aa 248-271) of Trnau1ap is buried within the Prpf39 dimer interface (Fig. 3l).

Indeed, our FLAG purification of the full-length Trnau1ap co-purifies Prpf39 (Fig. 3m and Supplementary Fig. 4b), whereas a parallel FLAG purification of Trnau1ap lacking either α-helix 2 or both α-helices 1 and 2 (Fig. 3l) fails to co-purify detectable amounts of Prpf39 (Fig. 3m), consistent with the predicted critical role of the α-helix 2 for this interaction.

The co-purification of Prpf39 with Trnau1ap, their joint requirement for inclusion of poison exons in their mRNAs and of exon 2 in RPP14 mRNA, together with the known role of Prpf39 as a human alternative splicing factor that recruits U1 snRNP51, suggests that Trnau1ap may also interact with components of the splicing machinery. Indeed, spliceosomal U1, U4, and U5B snRNAs are all enriched in preparations of FLAG-purified full-length Trnau1ap (Fig. 3n). However, removal of the α-helix 2 of Trnau1ap (Fig. 3l), which is critical for Trnau1ap-Prpf39 interaction (Fig. 3m, compare lanes 1 and 3), only marginally decreases enrichment of U1, U4, and U5B snRNAs in FLAG-purified preparations of Trnau1ap lacking α-helix 2 (Fig. 3n). In contrast, removal of both α-helices 1 and 2 (Fig. 3l) abolishes this interaction (Fig. 3n), demonstrating distinct requirements for Trnau1ap interaction with Prpf39 and these snRNAs, and suggesting that Trnau1ap interaction with the splicing machinery is largely independent of its interaction with Prpf39, and instead depends on the α-helix 1 of Trnau1ap (Fig. 3l–n).

Discussion

We identified, using genome-wide screening, a previously unrecognized RNase MRP and RNase P control (RMPPc) pathway (Fig. 4) that establishes—by converging on a single control point—integrated control of post-transcriptional processing of the lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β, internal transcribed spacers in pre-rRNA, and 5′-leader sequences in pre-tRNA, thereby orchestrating central control over several fundamental cellular processes.

We show that this broad control is established by TRNAU1AP and PRPF39, which jointly control the levels of H1 RNA and MRP RNA by regulating inclusion of the ATG-containing exon in one out of the nine protein components shared by RNase P and RNase MRP, Rpp14. Consistent with this role of Rpp14 in co-regulation of these two major human RNases, the S. cerevisiae ortholog of human Rpp14, Pop8p, is the only one of the eleven yeast RNase P and RNase MRP protein components that requires splicing, and its single conserved intron occupies the same position in the aligned protein sequences. We further demonstrate that two major components of the RMPPc pathway, TRNAU1AP and PRPF39, are themselves subject to elaborate regulation via poison exons that establish their cross- and self-regulatory feedback in a remarkable arrangement of a four-pronged circuit (labeled 1 through 4 in Fig. 4). Lacking known additional inputs, the closest analogy of this arrangement would be a Zener diode that produces a stable, fluctuation-resistant reference signal in electronic circuits. It is plausible that in human cells, such a circuit, positioned upstream of fundamental cellular processes required for translation, would safeguard their coordinated, continuous, and stable operation.

Recently, as part of a large-scale study evaluating the ability of RNA-binding proteins to activate exon inclusion, TRNAU1AP was shown to act as an alternative splicing factor regulating poison exon inclusion in PRPF3952, thereby independently supporting one of the four feedback loops in the RMPPc pathway that we identified (labeled loop “2” in the RMPPc pathway schematic in Fig. 4). However, (i) the RMPPc pathway itself (Fig. 4), including (ii) the joint requirement of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 for inclusion of the initiating exon 2 in RPP14, thereby concurrently controlling the levels of the catalytic RNAs (iii) H1 RNA of RNase P and MRP RNA of RNase MRP, and thus processing of their cellular substrates, including (iv) lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β as well as (v) pre-tRNA and pre-rRNA, as well as (vi) three of the four feedback loops in the RMPPc pathway that we identified (labeled loops 1, 3, and 4 in the RMPPc pathway schematic in Fig. 4), (vii) the interaction between Trnau1ap and Prpf39, and (viii) the determinants critical for their association with each other and with the splicing machinery, were not known previously.

Supporting the joint requirement of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 for inclusion of poison exons in their mRNAs (Fig. 3a–k) and for inclusion of the ATG-containing exon 2 in RPP14 mRNA (Fig. 2b, f, h), our co-purification shows that Trnau1ap and Prpf39 interact with each other and spliceosomal components (Fig. 3m, n and Supplementary Fig. 4b), which is consistent with high-throughput affinity capture-MS and large-scale two-hybrid studies53–57 as well as the AlphaFold prediction of the Trnau1ap-Prpf39 direct physical interaction (Fig. 3l). We further show that the region of Trnau1ap required for Prpf39 binding is not the main determinant of its association with spliceosomal snRNAs (Fig. 3l–n), supporting a model in which Trnau1ap engages Prpf39 and the splicing machinery through separate regions.

It is plausible that, in addition to the processing of abundant RNAs—rRNA, tRNAs, and lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β—which together represent the bulk of the RNA content in human cells58, the RMPPc pathway may regulate additional downstream targets. For example, knockouts of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 exhibit exclusion of exon 32 in Replication Timing Regulatory Factor 1 (RIF1)59 (Supplementary Fig. 5a), whose longer isoform has a related yet distinct function in protecting DNA damage sites60. Another example is the retention of introns flanking exon 22 in SKI3 (SKIC3), the largest subunit of the cytoplasmic SKI complex, which eliminates a significant fraction of the functional transcript (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Since one of the functions of the SKI complex is to extract mRNA from stalled ribosomes and cooperate with Pelota-Hbs1l and Abce1 for ribosome disassembly and recycling61–64, TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 may thereby coordinate this activity with translation-associated processes, such as pre-tRNA and pre-rRNA processing. Remarkably, in line with the recently reported synthetic cancer vulnerability resulting from deficiencies in SKI complex components and Pelota-Hbs1l65,66, the effects of TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 on SKIC3 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 5b) may explain the known negative genetic interaction between TRNAU1AP and PELO57,67 (Supplementary Fig. 5c).

Collectively, we demonstrate that—through a single control point, the inclusion of the ATG-containing exon in RPP14—TRNAU1AP and PRPF39 establish coordinated feedback-safeguarded post-transcriptional control of the levels of the two catalytic RNAs, H1 RNA and MRP RNA, and thus the processing of cellular RNA substrates by the two major human ribonucleases, RNase MRP and RNase P. Identified by our genome-wide screening, this previously unrecognized RNase MRP and RNase P control (RMPPc) pathway regulates non-canonical 3′-end processing and thus the levels of the lncRNAs MALAT1 and MEN-β, post-transcriptional processing of internal transcribed spacers in pre-rRNA and 5′-leader sequences in pre-tRNA, thereby establishing integrated control of processing over 90% of the total RNA content in human cells.

Methods

Construction of plasmids

LentiCRISPR plasmids expressing individual guide RNAs. The 140-bp PCR products encoding individual guide RNAs (sgRNAs) and flanking sequences were amplified using primers RandomF and RandomR from synthetic 60-nucleotide DNA templates; sequences of primers and templates are listed in Supplementary Data 2. The resulting PCR products were Gibson-cloned into the BsmBI-linearized blasticidin-resistant variant (pAVA2834; Addgene #254887) of lentiCRISPR vector68, and the sequences of the resulting plasmids were verified by whole-plasmid sequencing.

LentiCRISPR plasmids expressing dual sgRNAs. The 332-bp PCR products encoding, in succession, a 20-nucleotide sequence of sgRNA1, the Cas9 scaffold, the 7SK promoter, and a 20-nucleotide sequence of sgRNA2 were amplified using sgRNA-encoding primers sgRNA1F and sgRNA2R and the plasmid pAVA3129 as template; sequences of primers and sgRNAs are listed in Supplementary Data 2; the template plasmid is available at Addgene (#239329)33. The resulting PCR products were Gibson-cloned into the BsmBI-linearized blasticidin-resistant variant (pAVA2834) of lentiCRISPR vector68, and the sequences of the resulting plasmids were verified by whole-plasmid sequencing.

Plasmids expressing poison exon-containing minigenes. For the TRNAU1AP minigene, genomic regions spanning exon 3, intron 3 (containing the poison exon), and exon 4 of TRNAU1AP were PCR-amplified from human genomic DNA using primers EFS-flank_tRNAU_ex3_F and tRNAU_ex4_hbb3UTR-flank_R (Supplementary Data 2). For the PRPF39 minigene, genomic regions spanning exons 8, intron 8 (containing the poison exon), and exon 9 of PRPF39 were PCR-amplified from human genomic DNA using primers EFS-flank_PRPF_ex8_F and PRPF_ex9_hbb3UTR-flank_R (Supplementary Data 2). The resulting PCR products were cloned into the Eco53kI- and HindIII-linearized vector pAVA3939 (Addgene #239345)33 using Gibson Assembly.

Plasmids expressing cMyc-tagged Prpf39 and FLAG-tagged Trnau1ap variants. For the 6×cMyc-tagged Prpf39 construct, the 6×cMyc sequence was PCR-amplified from pAVA156369 using primers pcDNA3_6MYC_F and 6MYC_PRPF39_R, and the PRPF39 open reading frame (ORF) was PCR-amplified from pAVA3914 (GenScript #OHu02625D) using primers PRPF39_F and PRPF39_pcDNA3_R (Supplementary Data 2). The resulting PCR products were cloned into the BamHI- and XhoI-linearized vector pcDNA3 using Gibson Assembly. For the TRNAU1AP(Δ184-287)-FLAG construct, the TRNAU1AP fragment lacking amino acids 184-287 was PCR-amplified from pAVA3913 (TRNAU1AP-ORF-FLAG, GenScript #OHu13496D) using primers TR1P-F and TR1P-R1, and the FLAG tag and bGH poly(A) sequence was PCR-amplified from pAVA3913 using primers TR1P-F1 and TR1P-R (Supplementary Data 2). The resulting fragments were assembled into the HindIII- and DraIII-linearized pAVA3913 vector using three-piece Gibson Assembly. For the TRNAU1AP(Δ248-287)-FLAG construct, the TRNAU1AP fragment lacking amino acids 248-287 was PCR-amplified from pAVA3913 using primers TR1P-F and TR1P-R2, and the FLAG tag and bGH poly(A) sequence was PCR-amplified from pAVA3913 using primers TR1P-F2 and TR1P-R (Supplementary Data 2). The resulting fragments were assembled into the HindIII- and DraIII-linearized pAVA3913 vector by three-piece Gibson Assembly.

The sequences of the resulting plasmids pAVA3932 (Addgene #254878, TRNAU1AP minigene: exon3-intron3 including poison exon-exon4), pAVA3936 (Addgene #254879, PRPF39 minigene: exon8-intron8 including poison exon-exon9), pAVA3920 (Addgene #254882, CMV-6cMyc-PRPF39), pAVA4173 (Addgene #254880, CMV-TRNAU1AP(Δ184-287)-FLAG), and pAVA4176 (Addgene #254881, CMV-TRNAU1AP(Δ248-287)-FLAG) were verified by whole-plasmid sequencing and are provided in Supplementary Data 3. All plasmids generated in this study will be available from Addgene.

Mammalian cell lines

The “Red” Mirror reporter cell line33 was maintained at 37 °C in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, 150 µg/mL hygromycin, and 80 ng/mL puromycin. The “Green” Mirror reporter cell line33 was maintained at 37 °C in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, 300 µg/mL hygromycin, and 160 ng/mL puromycin. HEK293T cells were maintained at 37 °C in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and verified negative for mycoplasma using PCR-based assay.

Construction of the genome-wide lentiviral CRISPR sgRNA omission library

The customized lentiviral genome-wide sgRNA omission library comprising 90,260 sgRNAs targeting 20,884 human genes (Supplementary Data 1), was designed to exclude guide RNAs targeting 30 genes, including RNase P components (POP1, RPP38, RPP29, POP5, RPP25, RPP25L, RPP20, RPP14, RPP21, RPP30, and RPP40); RNA exosome components and cofactors (EXOSC1, EXOSC2, EXOSC3, EXOSC4, EXOSC5, EXOSC6, EXOSC7, EXOSC8, EXOSC9, EXOSC10, DIS3, DIS3L, C1D, and DIS3L2); the NEXT complex components (RBM7, SKIV2L2, and ZCCHC8); and DDX59 and BRF233. A pooled library of sgRNA-encoding 78-mer DNA oligonucleotides was synthesized by CustomArray, Inc. and used as template for PCR with primers RandomF and RandomR (Supplementary Data 2) for 17 cycles (98 °C for 20 s, 63 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 3 min), followed by 72 °C for 10 min. The resulting pool of 140-mer DNA duplexes was Gibson-cloned into the BsmBI-linearized blasticidin-resistant variant (pAVA2834) of lentiCRISPR vector68 and electroporated into E. cloni 10G electrocompetent cells, yielding on average over 1000 transformants per sgRNA. The plasmid library was extracted directly from colonies grown on LB agar plates, without liquid culture growth, using the ZymoPURE II Plasmid Gigaprep Kit. The purified library was Illumina-sequenced to confirm uniform sgRNA representation; data are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Production of lentiviruses and transduction of human cell lines

Lentivirus production. HEK293T cells were seeded at approximately 60% confluency 16 h before transfection. Cells were co-transfected with the packaging plasmid pCMV-dR8.91 and the envelope plasmids pMD2.G, together with an sgRNA-expressing LentiCRISPR plasmid or the customized lentiviral genome-wide sgRNA omission library using the Mirus TransIT-293 Transfection Reagent. Twenty-four hours later, the medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 30% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection and clarified by centrifugation at 200 × g for 6 min. The following lentiviral plasmids were used to generate gene knockouts: pAVA3291 (Addgene #254883) for TRNAU1AP knockout, pAVA3245 (Addgene #254885) for PRPF39 knockout, pAVA3321 (Addgene #254886) for WDR61 knockout, pAVA3585 (Addgene #239262)34 for RPPH1 knockout, pAVA332534 for RPP21 knockout, pAVA3507 (Addgene #239317)33 for RPP14 knockout, and pAVA3773 (Addgene #239310)33 for negative control (sgRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 2).

Transduction of Mirror cells. Mirror cells were seeded in 15-cm dishes at 40% confluency in 20 mL DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, and 27 µg/mL polybrene, and transduced with 22 mL of lentiviral supernatant. Twenty-four hours later, the medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Seventy-two hours post-transduction, cells were selected with 3.0 µg/mL blasticidin for 4 days.

Iterative forward genetic screening of the lentiviral CRISPR sgRNA omission library

Thirteen days post-transduction with the customized lentiviral genome-wide sgRNA omission library, 3.2 × 108 cells of the “Red” Mirror reporter cell line33 were sorted using Bio-Rad S3e cell sorter utilizing ProSort™ V1.6 software to isolate cells with increased green fluorescence (blue FACS sorting gate in Fig. 1e). The FACS-isolated cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min, and their genomic DNA was purified using phenol extraction. Guide RNA-encoding sequences were PCR-amplified from the extracted genomic DNA in two steps33: first, a linear amplification was performed using the Herculase II DNA polymerase with a single primer RandomF (Supplementary Data 2) for 13 cycles (96 °C for 20 s, 63 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 90 s); then, conventional PCR was performed using two primers, RandomR and RandomF (Supplementary Data 2), for 35 cycles (96 °C for 20 s, 63 °C for 1 min and 72 °C for 45 s), followed by 72 °C for 10 min. The resulting amplified sgRNA pool was Gibson-cloned into the BsmBI-linearized, blasticidin-resistant variant (pAVA2834) of lentiCRISPR vector68 to generate an enriched lentiviral sgRNA library for subsequent rounds of forward genetic screening. After the third enrichment round, three technical replicates of the screening-enriched sgRNA pool and the original customized genome-wide sgRNA omission library were Illumina-sequenced and data were processed as previously described34.

The enrichment factor was calculated for each individual sgRNA as the ratio of its average abundance after versus before the screen (Supplementary Data 1).

Analysis of endogenous substrate processing by RNase P and RNase MRP in knockout cells

FACS-isolated knockout cells were incubated in 6-well plates for 3 h, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove cell debris, lysed in TRI Reagent, and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was isolated using TRI Reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions, treated with RQ1 RNase-Free DNase for 15 min at 37 °C, phenol-purified, and ethanol-precipitated. cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System according to manufacturer’s instructions and diluted 20-fold for downstream qPCR analysis using Bio-Rad CFX Opus 384 Real-Time PCR System and CFX Maestro software. The levels of RNase P-unprocessed endogenous lncRNAs MALAT1-mascRNA and MEN-β-menRNA were quantified using nested qPCR across the RNase P cleavage site as described previously34. Briefly, an initial 20-cycle (95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 30 s) pre-amplification step was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix with primers JD08-F and JD08-R for RNase P-unprocessed MALAT1-mascRNA and RMen08-F and RMen08-R for RNase P-unprocessed MEN-β-menRNA. Pre-amplification products were diluted 50-fold and used as templates for the following qPCR with primers JD07-F and JD07-R for RNase P-unprocessed MALAT1-mascRNA and RMen07-F and RMen07-R for RNase P-unprocessed MEN-β-menRNA using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix with the following cycling parameters: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 30 s. The levels of RNase P-unprocessed pre-tRNAs and RNase MRP-unprocessed pre-rRNA at ITS1 site 2 were quantified using qPCR with primers TRNAG15_F and TRNAG15_R for RNase P-unprocessed TRNAG15, TRNAH4_F and TRNAH4_R for RNase P-unprocessed TRNAH4, and ITS1_2_F1 and ITS_1_2_R2 for RNase MRP-unprocessed pre-rRNA. In all of the above experiments, 18S rRNA primers H.18SrRNA_F and H.18SrRNA_R were used for normalization, except in Supplementary Fig. 2d, e, in which 28S rRNA primers H.28S-F and H.28S-R were used. Schematics of primer locations for RNase P-unprocessed endogenous lncRNAs MALAT1-mascRNA and MEN-β-menRNA are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b; primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Translation assay

Protein synthesis was measured using the SUnSET puromycin incorporation assay70. TRNAU1AP knockout, PRPF39 knockout, and negative control cells were generated using CRISPR-Cas9, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2b. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown overnight to ~80% confluency. Puromycin was then added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL for 40 min to allow its incorporation into nascent polypeptides. For translation shut-off control, the sample was pretreated with cycloheximide for 10 min at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL before puromycin addition. The cells were subsequently lysed by addition of 2x Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad #1610737) and incubating in boiling water for 5 min. Puromycin incorporation was analyzed by Western blot.

Competitive cell growth assay

TRNAU1AP knockout, PRPF39 knockout, and negative control HeLa cells were generated using CRISPR-Cas9, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2b. To determine growth rate differences, TRNAU1AP KO, PRPF39 KO, and negative control HeLa cells were each mixed at a 1:1 ratio with the HeLa cell line AVAM52635. Since the co-cultured cell populations occupy distinct positions on 2D fluorescence flow cytometry plots, their growth and relative abundance were simultaneously monitored daily by flow cytometry by analyzing 200,000 cells for each sample.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Relative RNA levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method in RT-qPCRs. Bar graphs show the means of 3 biological × at least 3 technical replicates for TRNAU1AP, PRPF39, and WDR61 knockouts and their controls; 2 biological × at least 3 technical replicates for gene rescue and overexpression experiments; at least 1 biological × 4 technical replicates for RPP14, RPP21, and RPPH1 knockouts, with error bars representing standard deviations (SD). Statistical significances were tested using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s HSD post hoc test by GraphPad Prism (macOS version 10.4.1). Three technical replicates of the sgRNA pool were Illumina-sequenced to analyze sgRNA enrichment after the third screening round.

RNA sequencing and genome mapping

TRI-reagent-extracted total RNA was diluted to 50 ng/μl and submitted to Innomics (San Jose, CA) for cDNA library preparation without poly(A) selection and 100-bp paired-end sequencing according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Sixty million reads per sample were mapped to the human genome assembly GRCh38.p14 (GCA_000001405.29) using STAR71 (version 2.7.10b) with coordinate-sorted BAM output and standard attributes. Alignments were indexed using SAMtools72 (version 1.18) and visualized using IGV73 (version 2.16.1).

Western blotting

After FACS sorting, cells were allowed to attach to plates for 3 h in DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS, washed with cold PBS, and then lysed using 2× Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad) supplemented with 10 mM DTT, followed by incubation in boiling water for 5 min. Whole-cell lysates were resolved on 4–15% Criterion Precast Gels and transferred to an Odyssey 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membrane in Tris-glycine buffer with 20% methanol using a Bio-Rad 560BR Criterion blotter according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The membrane was blocked in 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Data 4: TRNAU1AP, 15053-1-AP, 1:1000; PRPF39 PA5-21627, 1:1000; GAPDH, HRP-60004, 1:4000; Puromycin, MABE343, 1:10000; FLAG, F3165, 1:3000; and cMyc, MA1-81357, 1:2000) overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibody (1:5000) at room temperature for 2 h, washed again three times with TBST buffer, developed with ECL kit, and imaged using Syngene G:Box iChemi XR5 system and GeneSys V1.5.6.0 software.

Gene rescue and overexpression

Gene rescue. FACS-isolated cell populations expressing Cas9 and gene-targeting sgRNAs (pAVA3291 for TRNAU1AP knockout, pAVA3245 for PRPF39 knockout, and pAVA3773 for Control(−)) were seeded in 6-well plates at 30% confluence. After 10 h of incubation at 37 °C, cells were transfected with TransIT-293 Transfection Reagent and 3 µg of sgRNA-resistant intronless protein-expressing plasmids: pAVA3913 (TRNAU1AP, GenScript #OHu13496D), pAVA3914 (PRPF39, GenScript #OHu02625D), pAVA3915 (RPP14, GenScript #OHu15516D), or pAVA1221 (vector control), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2b. 24 h post-transfection, the transfection media was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed using TRI Reagent and stored at −80 °C.

Gene overexpression. 12 h before transfection, HEK 293 T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 30% confluency. The cells were transfected using TransIT-293 Transfection Reagent with TRNAU1AP (pAVA3913) and PRPF39 (pAVA3914) overexpression plasmids and pBluescript (pAVA1221) plasmid as transfection carrier DNA. 24 h post-transfection, the media was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed in TRI Reagent and stored at −80 °C.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and RNA immunoprecipitation qPCR (RIP-qPCR)

10 h before transfection, HEK293T cells were seeded on 10-cm plates at 50% confluency in DMEM media supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Each plate was co-transfected with 9 μg of pAVA3913 [CMV-TRNAU1AP-FLAG] and 9 μg of pAVA3920 [CMV-6cMyc-PRPF39]; 9 μg of pAVA4173 [CMV-TRNAU1AP(Δ184-287)-FLAG] and 9 μg of pAVA3920 [CMV-6cMyc-PRPF39]; 9 μg of pAVA4176 [CMV-TRNAU1AP(Δ248-287)-FLAG] and 9 μg of pAVA3920 [CMV-6cMyc-PRPF39]; 9 μg of pAVA3913 [CMV-TRNAU1AP-FLAG] and 9 μg of pAVA1575 [CMV-6cMyc-METTL1]; 9 μg of pAVA1596 [CMV-FLAG-METTL1] and 9 μg of pAVA3920 [CMV-6cMyc-PRPF39]; or 18 μg of pBluescript (pAVA1221) as a negative control using the TransIT-293 Transfection Reagent. 24 h post-transfection, the transfection media was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. 36 h post-transfection, cells were trypsinized and transferred into 15-cm plates containing DMEM media supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. 60 h post-transfection, cells were washed twice with PBS, pelleted by centrifugation at 200 g for 6 min, and placed on ice. Cell pellets were resuspended in 400 μl of pre-chilled buffer A [10 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 2 mM MgCl2, 0.1 M KCl, 1 mM DTT], placed on ice for 15 min and then passed eight times through a 25-gauge needle using a 1 mL syringe. Cell nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 30 s and resuspended in 200 μl of buffer B [0.2 M Hepes (pH 7.6), 25% (w/v) glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.42 M NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.04% (v/v) NP-40, and RiboLock RNase Inhibitor and protease inhibitors (Roche) at the manufacturer-recommended concentrations]. The lysates were rotated at 4 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 5 min. 170 μl of the clarified nuclear extract was transferred to a new tube and combined with 1180 μl of buffer C [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 25% (w/v) glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.04% (v/v) NP-40, and protease inhibitors (Roche) at the manufacturer-recommended concentration]. Following 5 min of incubation on ice, the lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 5 min. 60 μl of the clarified lysates were combined with 1 mL of TRI Reagent and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA purification as input controls for RIP-qPCR. Another 60 μl of the clarified lysates were combined with 2x Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610737), and incubated in boiling water for 5 min, and stored at −20 °C as input controls for Western blots. For immunoprecipitations, 30 μl of anti-FLAG M2 affinity gel beads (Sigma, #A2220) were resuspended in 100 μl of Wash Buffer [42.67 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 25% (w/v) Glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, 52.9 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.04% (v/v) NP-40, and protease inhibitors (Roche)], combined with 1200 μl of the clarified nuclear extract, rotated at 4 °C for 1 h, and washed eight times with 800 μl of Wash Buffer, each time pelleting the beads at 5000 × g for 1 min and transferring them to a new tube. FLAG-tagged proteins were eluted by combining 30 μl of washed beads with 100 μl of 1 μg/μl 3xFLAG peptide (Sigma, F4799) resuspended in Wash Buffer for 1 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 g for 1 min. Then 45 μl of each FLAG-tagged protein-containing supernatant was combined with 1 mL of TRI Reagent and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA isolation and RT-qPCR analysis, or combined with 2x Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610737), incubated in boiling water for 5 min, and stored at −20 °C for subsequent Western blot analysis.
