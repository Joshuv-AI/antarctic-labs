Gestational weight gain and child nutritional outcomes in East Coast Peninsular Malaysia: protocol for the 10-year prospective GROW-MY cohort study
Authors: Arif Sabta Aji, Abdul Jalil Rohana, Nur Nadia Mohamed, Soo Kah Leng, Adriyan Pramono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChildhood overweight and obesity are growing public health concerns in Malaysia. Increasing evidence suggests that early-life factors, including maternal gestational weight gain (GWG) and nutritional status, contribute to long-term child health outcomes. The Gestational Weight Gain and Child Nutritional Outcomes in East Coast Malaysia study is a 10-year prospective cohort established to investigate the intergenerational effects of maternal GWG on the nutritional and metabolic health of their respective offspring living in the East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia.Method and analysisThis study follows up a cohort of mother-child pairs (n=499) initially recruited during pregnancy in 2014 from maternal and child health clinics in Kota Bharu, Kelantan and Kuala Terengganu, Terengganu, Malaysia. At the 10-year follow-up scheduled for 2026, anthropometric assessments, body composition and dietary intake will be collected from both mothers and their children. GWG classifications will be made in accordance with the Institute of Medicine guidelines. Multivariate regression models will be used to evaluate the relationship between GWG and childhood outcomes, including body mass index-for-age, mothers’ nutritional status and other metabolic risk indicators.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval of this study was obtained from the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR-12-1256-13141). Apart from that, human ethical approval (FWA Reg. No: 00007718; IRB Reg. No: 00004494) was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee Universiti Sains Malaysia. Findings will be disseminated through academic and professional conferences and peer-reviewed literature. Additionally, findings and analyses will be disseminated to the Ministry of Health (MoH) through regular presentations. The investigators also plan a public-facing podcast of the study findings and related topics to disseminate results to participants, the MoH and the general public.

Introduction

Childhood obesity is a rapidly escalating public health challenge across Southeast Asia, including Malaysia. National surveys indicate that the obesity rate in children under 18 years old rose from 9.5% in 2006 to 12.9% in 2015.1 By 2024, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children and adolescents aged 5–17 years was 14.4% and 13.6%, respectively.2 However, a study showed that the prevalence of overweight and obesity was 15.0% and 12.0%, respectively, among children aged 10–17 years.3 Compared with other states, such as Kelantan, the prevalence of overweight and obesity was significantly higher by a margin of 13.9%.4 Early-life exposures, particularly maternal health and nutrition during pregnancy, are increasingly recognised as key determinants of childhood obesity risk, underpinning the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis.5 The most recent study in Malaysia found that intergenerational weight gain is a worrisome trend. Using the 10-year National Health Morbidity Survey Database for Malaysian adults, a multivariate regression model was performed to determine factors associated with overweight mother/overweight child pairs, with normal weight mother/normal weight child pairs as the reference group. The prevalence of overweight mother/overweight child pairs increased from 15.3% to 21.7%, while the prevalence of normal weight mother/normal weight child pairs decreased from 28.4% to 23.8% between 2006 and 2015. Older maternal age and higher levels of primary and secondary education were positively associated with overweight mother/overweight child pairs. Conversely, older child age, Chinese ethnicity, large household size and low-income households were inversely associated with overweight mother/overweight child pairs.6 These data suggest that early-life determinants play a pivotal role in shaping future nutritional risk, especially in regions undergoing nutritional transition, such as Malaysia, where both undernutrition and overnutrition coexist.

Comparatively, Malaysia ranks among the highest in Southeast Asia for childhood overweight prevalence. A recent review of obesity in Southeast Asian countries reports an estimated overweight and obesity prevalence among children aged 5–19 years of 29.8% in Malaysia, higher than in Thailand (22.0%), Indonesia (15.0%) and the Philippines (13.0%).7 Urbanisation, increasing consumption of ultra-processed foods and sedentary behaviours have all been implicated in this upward trend. These risk factors are compounded by early-life exposures such as excessive maternal weight gain and suboptimal antenatal nutrition, which may predispose children to adiposity and metabolic disorders later in life. Addressing intergenerational risk through maternal health has thus become a global and regional priority.

Maternal gestational weight gain (GWG) has emerged as a key modifiable factor influencing childhood weight status. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that excessive GWG significantly increases the odds of childhood overweight, independent of pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and other confounders.8 9 The 2009 Institute of Medicine (IOM) guidelines have been widely adopted for monitoring GWG, though few studies have assessed their predictive validity in Southeast Asian populations. In addition to GWG, maternal nutritional status—including micronutrient adequacy and dietary patterns—can influence fetal programming, adiposity rebound and insulin sensitivity during childhood.10 11 These findings are consistent with the DOHaD framework, which links early exposures to long-term non-communicable disease (NCD) risk.12

Despite growing awareness of the importance of antenatal determinants, there remains a paucity of long-term cohort data in Malaysia and the broader Southeast Asian region. Most studies are cross-sectional or short-term follow-ups in infancy or toddlerhood.13–15 Very few have followed children into middle childhood—a critical period when excess weight tends to persist into adolescence and adulthood. Furthermore, evidence from coastal and rural populations, such as those in Kelantan and Terengganu, is scarce, despite differences in dietary patterns, healthcare access and socioeconomic status compared with urban centres. This evidence gap constrains the development of region-specific public health strategies.

To address this gap, the GWG and child nutritional outcomes in the East Coast Peninsular Malaysia: protocol for the 10-year prospective Gestational Weight Gain and Child Nutritional Outcomes in East Coast Malaysia (GROW-MY) cohort study was established as a prospective birth cohort. This study primarily aims to assess the long-term association between maternal GWG and child nutritional outcomes at 10 years of age. Second, this study aims to evaluate the influence of intergenerational exposures on child overweight/obesity, mothers’ nutritional status and metabolic risk indicators. By integrating baseline pregnancy data with follow-up data collected when the children are approximately 10 years old, this study seeks to generate context-specific evidence to inform maternal and child health policies and interventions in Malaysia and other Southeast Asian settings.

Methods and analysis

Conceptual framework

Our conceptual framework considers maternal nutritional status, GWG, dietary intake, physical activity and maternal factors, including socioeconomic and demographic characteristics, pre-pregnancy BMI and medical and obstetric history, as potential determinants of maternal and pregnancy nutritional outcomes. These factors also modify the growth trajectories of both mother-child pairs, potentially influencing their metabolic indicator parameters and nutritional status (figure 1). Maternal health has long been recognised as a cornerstone of children’s and families’ well-being.16 Globally, there is increasing awareness that adverse maternal health conditions, including pre-pregnancy obesity, excessive GWG and GDM, contribute to the persistence of health disparities.17 Despite this recognition, maternal health is seldom the central focus of longitudinal research, with most prospective pregnancy and birth cohort studies primarily designed to investigate child health outcomes. Within this framework, maternal behaviours are often regarded merely as exposures that may influence child health and lifelong well-being. In contrast, the present study was designed to specifically examine pre-pregnancy BMI, nutrient intake, dietary diversity and physical activity among pregnant women and to assess whether these maternal factors are associated with intergenerational health outcomes, particularly focusing on child nutritional status. While there is growing interest in the complex social, biological and environmental factors influencing maternal health, these factors have received limited attention in life-course research.

This study used a prospective cohort design, enrolling pregnant women during mid-pregnancy and following both mothers and their children for 10 years post delivery. It captured comprehensive data on the onset, severity, chronicity and recurrence of common maternal physical and psychological health issues, aspects that are often overlooked in other pregnancy and birth cohort studies. Initially, the study aimed to investigate GWG and related lifestyle factors, including physical activity and dietary patterns during pregnancy. The primary outcomes of this follow-up after 10 years include associations between maternal nutritional status, GWG and other determinants with maternal and child health and nutritional outcomes from birth to age 10 years in Kelantan and Kuala Terengganu, on the East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia.

Study design

The GROW-MY cohort study is a prospective cohort that recruits participants in early pregnancy and follows outcomes through birth, with continued follow-up of children at 10 years after delivery. Briefly, the GROW-MY cohort study was conducted in Kota Bharu and Kuala Terengganu districts, densely populated coastal areas in Peninsular Malaysia.

Baseline recruitment of pregnant women was conducted in 2014 and their offsprings were born in 2015; the GROW-MY cohort study includes 2500 households and 8540 people. A graphical representation of the study timeline including recruitment process and participants follow-up is provided in figure 2. The site includes four health clinics in the Kota Bharu district and six health clinics in the Kuala Terengganu district. Table 1 lists the health clinics involved in this study. For the present 10-year follow-up, eligible participants are mother-child pairs from the baseline pregnancy cohort who can be successfully traced, meet the follow-up eligibility criteria and provide written informed consent. Participant tracing, database availability, contact verification and preparation for follow-up activities began in October–December 2025. The main follow-up data collection is scheduled from June to December 2026.

This prospective cohort study used a dataset of pregnant women collected in 2014–2015. The primary data collection in 2014 was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC) from the Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR 121256 13141) and the Human Research Ethics Committee Universiti Sains Malaysia (FWA Reg. No:0007718, IRB Reg No: 00004494). Using the database employed in 2014, the baseline of the GROW-MY study assessed the pattern of sociodemographic data, medical and obstetric history from the antenatal record, pre-pregnancy weight, nutrient intake and physical activity among pregnant women in trimesters two and three. The follow-up study will collect data on mothers’ sociodemographic factors, pregnancy and childbirth history, anthropometry, BMI, body composition, metabolic indicators, GWG status, dietary intake and physical activity. As GWG influences children’s nutritional status, data on health and nutritional status will be obtained from the respective child delivered in 2014.18–20 The child’s nutritional status, in particular BMI-for-age, weight-for-age, height-for-age and waist circumference, will be determined through anthropometric measurement, dietary intake, eating behaviour, gestational age at birth, newborn anthropometry history and medical history will be collected. In brief, the present study is designed to specifically examine pre-pregnancy BMI, dietary intake, dietary diversity and physical activity among mothers and to assess these maternal factors and their association with intergenerational nutrition and health outcomes in their children at 10 years old.

Study population

The estimated sample size of the baseline cohort was calculated based on the Peduzzi formula,21 expressed as n=10 k/p, where k is the number of covariates in the regression model and p is the proportion of subjects experiencing the outcome of interest. Based on the recent estimates,2 the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity among children was 28%. Assuming 12 predictor variables, the total number of participants was 411 mothers and after adding a potential 20% loss to follow-up, the minimum required sample size was 514 participants. A total of 600 participants were enrolled in the baseline cohort study. Of these, 499 mother-child pairs from the baseline are eligible after checking database availability and verifying contact in the study dataset. Then, eligible participants will be traced and invited to participate in the 10-year follow-up assessment. The follow-up analysis will therefore include all eligible mother-child pairs who can be successfully contacted, meet the follow-up eligibility criteria and provide written informed consent.

Based on the available baseline pregnancy cohort, the expected analysable sample will be up to 499 mother-child pairs, depending on tracing success, consent and completeness of key exposure and outcome data. As this is an existing cohort follow-up, no additional participants will be recruited to replace those who are lost to follow-up or decline participation. This limitation will be taken into account in the interpretation of the findings.

During the baseline study, pregnant women who consented to participate in the maternal study and resided in the districts of Kuala Terengganu, Terengganu and Kota Bharu, Kelantan, on the East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia, were of reproductive age (18–49 years for baseline eligibility) and pregnant, with gestational weeks between 13 weeks and 27 weeks, who sought antenatal check-up at health clinics in both study locations. Then, ongoing recruitment will be conducted to reach a total of 499 same-cohort mother-infant pairs. The inclusion criteria for the second follow-up will be mothers and their children who registered as study participants at baseline. However, mothers and children who have physical disabilities, mental health illnesses, are unable to understand either Malay or English and move out from both study locations during the follow-up visit, with no feasible follow-up, will not be recruited. Mothers who are not willing to be involved and do not consent to participate in the GROW-MY study will be excluded as study participants.

Follow-up schedule

Eligible mother-child pairs will be contacted by the research team to update contact information, confirm current residence and schedule follow-up interviews and measurements. Follow-up assessments will be conducted at the participant’s home or a nearby health facility, depending on the mother-child pair’s convenience and logistical feasibility. Home visits will be arranged once the participant’s updated location has been confirmed. To reduce potential loss to follow-up, the study team will use updated contact information, multiple contact modes, flexible scheduling and home visits where feasible. Mother-child pairs will be excluded from the follow-up if the child or mother has died, if they have permanently migrated outside the study area and cannot be feasibly assessed, if they cannot be contacted after repeated tracing attempts or if they decline or are unable to provide informed consent. The follow-up will target eligible children from the baseline pregnancy who are approximately 10 years old at the time of follow-up assessment. Data collection

Data are collected by trained enumerators (nutritionists), with assistance from midwives using available data from the health clinics listed above. The study questionnaire consisted of sections as described: (a) demography and socioeconomic variables—mother’s age, education, occupation, household income and number of household members. (b) Medical, health and obstetric history: NCD history, previous obstetric history, previous number of pregnancies, previous miscarriage and stillbirth, previous pregnancy-associated medical condition, smoking history, Hb level, result of modified oral glucose tolerance test and diagnosis yellow coding. (c) Child medical and health history: previous disease history, Hb level, previous allergy history, disability status, result of modified oral glucose tolerance test and diagnosis yellow coding. (d) Parental anthropometric measurement: weight, height, waist and hip circumference, mid-upper arm circumference, BMI and body composition (body fat %). (e) Child anthropometric measurement: child weight, height, nutrient intake and eating behaviour. The details of the variables involved in the GROW-MY study are presented in table 2.

The following anthropometric assessments of the mother will be conducted during the 10-year follow-up visit. Body height will be measured using a Seca 213 stadiometer (Seca, Hamburg, Germany). Height will be measured three times, and the average will be used to minimise measurement error. The device complies with Annex VI of Directive 93/42/EEC on medical devices. Body weight and body fat (%) will be assessed using a bioelectrical impedance analysis device (Tanita BC-420MA, Tanita, Tokyo, Japan). Measurements will be taken with participants standing barefoot on the analyser, following standardised procedures. The device will estimate body fat percentage using electrical impedance, incorporating inputs for age, sex, height and weight. All measurements will be conducted by trained personnel under standardised conditions. Participants will stand barefoot on the analyser, with the upper limbs along the torso, slightly away from it and the eyes directed straight ahead. The person performing the test will enter the participant’s age and body height into the analyser software (Health Monitor GMON V.3.4.1, Tanita Japan Pro, Tokyo, Japan). Waist circumference will be assessed at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest, recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm with participants standing. Central obesity will be defined based on waist circumference and it will be classified according to WHO criteria as waist circumference ≥80 cm for women.22 The measurements will be conducted in the morning by the same researcher, with the subject in a normal, healthy state. Maternal BMI will be categorised according to the WHO guidelines as group 1—underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), group 2—normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), group 3—overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m2) and obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m2).23

The GWG will be calculated as the difference between early-pregnancy weight and the last recorded maternal weight. Early-pregnancy weight will be defined as measured weight at the first antenatal visit at ≤13 weeks of gestation; if unavailable, self-reported pre-pregnancy weight will be used and documented accordingly. GWG will first be analysed as a continuous variable. For categorical analysis, GWG will be classified according to the IOM recommendations, using pre-pregnancy BMI category and the gestational age at the last maternal weight measurement.24 The adequacy of GWG will not be determined solely from total weight gain at delivery. Instead, the observed weight gain will be compared with the expected IOM range for the corresponding gestational age at the final antenatal weight measurement to reduce misclassification particularly among women whose last weight was recorded before term or not close to delivery. This approach follows recommendations that gestational age at the last weight measurement should be incorporated when applying IOM GWG categories.25

Child nutritional status will be assessed using WHO age-specific and sex-specific growth indicators, including height-for-age z-scores, BMI-for-age z-scores and weight-for-age z-scores where age-appropriate. The z-scores will be calculated by first computing the traditional BMI (weight/height (kg/m2)), and subsequently transforming the values according to the WHO Child Growth Standard for 2–19 years, based on age, gender, height and weight. Height-for-age z-scores will be used to assess linear growth and stunting, while weight-for-age z-scores will be considered only where applicable according to WHO growth reference recommendations. These indicators will be calculated from measured height and weight using WHO growth reference standards based on the child’s age and sex.26 Birth information, including birth weight, gestational age at birth, birth length and head circumference, will be extracted from available delivery records, child health records or baseline study records. These data will be checked for completeness, consistency, missing values and implausible entries before analysis, and variables with substantial missing or unreliable data will be excluded. These variables will be used to characterise early-life growth status and incorporated into the analysis when data are sufficiently complete. Birth weight will also be classified into standard categories: low birth weight (<2500 g), normal birth weight (2500–3999 g) and macrosomia (>4000 g).26 Gestational age at birth will be used to classify preterm birth (<37 completed weeks) and term birth (≥37 completed weeks).27 Birth size will be classified using small-for-gestational-age (SGA), appropriate-for-gestational-age (AGA) and large-for-gestational-age (LGA) categories, rather than birth weight alone, because these classifications account for both gestational age at birth and infant sex. Birth weight, gestational age at birth and infant sex will be extracted from available delivery, baseline or child health records. Using the INTERGROWTH-21st newborn size standards, SGA will be defined as birth weight below the 10th percentile for gestational age and sex, AGA as birth weight between the 10th and 90th percentiles and LGA as birth weight above the 90th percentile.28 Anthropometric data assessment will be conducted directly through 10-year measurements. However, optional data from routine historical measurements of child anthropometry in home-based/clinic settings and from child health records will complement the dataset and describe growth patterns. Furthermore, this study will explore BMI-for-age trajectories as a secondary analysis where available.

Mother’s physical activity will be assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire and classified according to WHO guidelines.29 Total physical activity will be expressed in metabolic equivalent (MET)-min per week by summing activity across work, transport and leisure domains. Participants will then be categorised into three levels: low, moderate and high physical activity. Moderate activity is defined as ≥600 MET-min/week, while high activity is defined as ≥3000 MET-min/week or meeting specific combinations of vigorous and moderate activities as per WHO recommendations.

At the 10-year follow-up, the sociodemographic characteristics will include age in years, ethnic group, gender, marital status, currently residing with whom, educational background, employment status, present occupation, total monthly income, monthly income category and current health problem. Educational attainment was collected at baseline and will be analysed into four categories: no education, primary, secondary and tertiary, which align with the Malaysian education system.30 No education refers to individuals who have never attended any school or educational institutions that provide formal education. The primary level of education represented not completing primary school or completed standard six. Secondary education is defined as completed form 3 or form 5. Tertiary education was attained on completing form 6, a certificate, a diploma or a degree and above. The questionnaire for this study will include a description of the occupation of persons in the participant’s household. To accurately code occupations according to the Ministry of Human Resources, the Malaysia Standard Classification of Occupations is used.31

Data quality

To ensure high-quality follow-up data collection, questionnaires were pilot-tested before follow-up fieldwork, and data collectors received training. Data collectors were recruited based on predefined competency criteria and underwent structured training, including pre-training and post-training assessments to confirm their readiness. Field activities were closely supervised through weekly on-site mentoring sessions and regular team meetings, providing continuous support. As part of quality assurance, supervisors conducted home visits to a randomly selected 5% of participating households to validate data collectors’ performance. Anthropometric data cleaning will follow WHO/UNICEF recommendations for identifying and handling biologically implausible values, including flagging implausible BMI-for-age z-scores and re-verifying where feasible.32 The electronic data capture system incorporates built-in quality control features, including single-field validation checks, inter-field consistency checks and inter-form logic controls. Real-time data entry errors will trigger automated pop-up alerts, prompting immediate correction before record submission. After fieldwork, data analysts team systematically reviewed the dataset to identify any inconsistencies or quality issues. Detected errors will be documented, communicated to data collectors for resolution and subsequently corrected within the final dataset. However, drop-out and missing data are expected during the 10-year follow-up study.

Data analysis

All data obtained from completed proformas and questionnaires will be entered into SPSS V.30.0. All data collection forms will be given serial numbers. After data entry, each data collection form will be assigned a unique serial number to support data tracking and verification. After data entry, the dataset will be checked for completeness, duplicate entries, coding errors, out-of-range values, inconsistent responses, missing data and biologically implausible anthropometric values. Data cleaning will include cross-checking entries against the original forms, verifying unusual values where possible and documenting all corrections before statistical analysis. All data will be tabulated for descriptive statistics. All categorical variables will be summarised as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). The numerical variables will be described as mean and SD or median and IQR, depending on the distribution of the data assumed.

A prespecified approach will be used to examine pre-pregnancy BMI as an effect modifier by including interaction terms in regression models and conducting stratified analyses. If cell sizes are insufficient for the four BMI groups, the groups will be redefined into two strata (<25 kg/m² vs ≥25 kg/m²).33 This study will also monitor the availability of WHO gestational weight-gain charts or standards as an alternative classification framework and consider their use in additional analyses or interpretation.27 The primary outcome of this study remains child nutritional status at approximately 10 years of age, particularly BMI-for-age and overweight/obesity status. Maternal anthropometry at follow-up, including BMI, waist circumference and body composition, will be treated as secondary maternal outcomes and used as maternal follow-up characteristics or covariates rather than as separate study outcomes. Mothers who are pregnant during the 10-year follow-up visit will be excluded from analyses that use current maternal nutritional status or anthropometric indicators as outcomes or covariates, because pregnancy would directly affect maternal weight, BMI, waist circumference and body composition.

BMI-for-age trajectories will be explored as a secondary analysis among children with sufficient repeated historical anthropometric measurements available from routine child health records. BMI-for-age z-scores will be calculated for each available time point using WHO growth reference standards.34 Individual growth patterns will be described graphically and summarised using repeated-measures or trajectory-based approaches. If the number or timing of historical measurements is insufficient for formal trajectory modelling, these data will be used descriptively to support the interpretation of the 10-year BMI-for-age outcomes.

The main analysis will examine the association between GWG during the pregnancy and child nutritional outcomes at the 10-year follow-up. Child nutritional outcomes will include BMI-for-age z-score as a continuous outcome and overweight/obesity status as a binary outcome. Linear regression will be used for continuous outcomes and logistic regression for binary outcomes. GWG will be analysed first as a continuous exposure and then as a categorical exposure based on the IOM, using adequate GWG as the reference category.

Selection of covariates will be guided by prior evidence and a directed acyclic graph (DAG), rather than by statistical significance in bivariate analyses. The DAG will be constructed to identify the minimum sufficient adjustment set for estimating the association between GWG and child nutritional outcomes while avoiding inappropriate adjustment for mediators or colliders. The analysis will not focus on identifying independent predictors of GWG. Instead, GWG will be treated as the main exposure, and models will estimate its association with child nutritional outcomes after adjustment for the DAG-informed minimal set of confounders. Crude and adjusted estimates will be reported to show the influence of confounder adjustment.

The secondary analyses will include other child growth indicators, including height-for-age z-score and weight-for-age z-score where age-appropriate. BMI-for-age trajectories will be explored among children with sufficient repeated historical anthropometric data. Effect modification by pre-pregnancy BMI will be examined using interaction terms and stratified analyses. If the sample size is insufficient for four BMI categories, stratified analyses will use two categories: <25 kg/m² and ≥25 kg/m². In addition to GWG, other maternal variables, including dietary intake, physical activity and related maternal anthropometric factors such as maternal weight change measured during pregnancy, will be examined as secondary exposures. These variables will be analysed in separate regression models, independent of the primary GWG analysis, to assess their associations with child nutritional outcomes at the 10-year follow-up and adjustment for GWG will be included as a potential mediator. Each exposure will be modelled individually in relation to child outcomes (eg, BMI-for-age z-score and overweight/obesity), using a multivariate regression model as appropriate. Models will be adjusted using the same DAG-informed minimal sufficient adjustment set, ensuring consistency in confounder control across analyses. GWG will not be included in these models where it is considered a potential mediator of the relationship between maternal behaviours and child outcomes to avoid overadjustment. Multinomial logistic regression will be used when child nutritional outcomes contain more than two categories, such as underweight, normal weight, overweight and obesity. Associations will be reported as regression coefficients, ORs or relative risk ratios, as appropriate, with 95% CIs. Statistical significance will be assessed using two-sided tests, with a p value of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics and dissemination

The baseline study (2014–2015) and the 10-year follow-up have approvals from the MREC, Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR-12-1256-13141) and the Human Research Ethics Committee, Universiti Sains Malaysia (FWA Reg. No: 00007718; IRB Reg. No: 00004494). Any protocol amendments will be submitted to the relevant ethics committees prior to implementation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating mothers at baseline. For the follow-up visit, written informed consent will be obtained again from mothers, and written parental/guardian consent will be obtained for each child; age-appropriate child assent will also be sought.

Participants will be assigned unique study identifiers. Identifiable information will be stored separately from research data in password-protected files accessible only to the study investigators. Analyses and reporting will use de-identified datasets. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, scientific and professional conferences and briefings to the Ministry of Health (MoH) via regular presentations. We will also share participant-friendly summaries (including a planned public-facing podcast) to provide feedback results to participants and the wider community.

Findings will be disseminated through academic and professional conferences and peer-reviewed literature. Additionally, findings and analyses will be disseminated to the MoH through regular presentations. The investigators also plan a public-facing podcast of the study findings and related topics to disseminate results to participants, the MoH and the general public.
