Plausible Deniability in Fully Homomorphic Computation
Shahzad Ahmad, Stefan Rass, Zahra Seyedi

Abstract
We introduce Plausible Deniability in Fully Homomorphic Computation (PD-FHC), a framework enabling users to outsource Boolean computations to an untrusted cloud while maintaining both computational privacy against honest-but-curious providers and plausible deniability against coercive adversaries. We define the notion of a Deniable Computation Medium (DCM) and a Deniable Computation Scheme (DCS) as medium-independent abstractions, then instantiate them using RGB images with Fredkin-gate circuits. Multiple computation scenarios (one real, several decoys) are embedded at secret positions within cover images; the cloud applies identical operations to every pixel, processing all scenarios uniformly. Under coercion, the user reveals a decoy computation with verifiable results while the real computation remains hidden. We formalize multi-round coercion games with existence and intent distinguishing advantages, proving computational privacy with advantage Θ ⁡ ( 1 / ( n − 1 ) ! ) \Theta(1/(n-1)!) and negligible existence-hiding advantage for the image instantiation. Our Python implementation, benchmarked across circuit sizes (5–289 gates) and image dimensions ( 128 2 128^{2} to 512 2 512^{2} ), demonstrates competitive performance with TFHE for Boolean circuits while providing deniability that FHE fundamentally cannot offer.



1
Introduction

Outsourcing computation to cloud providers exposes sensitive data to untrusted infrastructure. Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE)
[
14
]
and
Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs)
[
11
]
protect data confidentiality but fail to address a distinct threat:
coercion
. A powerful adversary who compels a user to explain her cloud activity will find that FHE ciphertexts confirm the use of encryption, and TEE attestation logs confirm that a specific computation was executed. Neither technology allows the user to credibly deny her true computational intent. This paper introduces
Plausible Deniability in Fully Homomorphic Computation
(PD-FHC), a framework enabling users to outsource Boolean computations while maintaining the ability to deny, under coercion, what they actually computed.

1.1
Motivating Scenario

Consider a journalist who uses a cloud image-processing service to apply custom filters to a photo archive. The service receives batches of photographs along with a pixel-level transformation specification, applies the transformation uniformly across every pixel, and returns the processed images. From the provider’s perspective, this is routine cloud image processing.

Unknown to the provider, the journalist has embedded the inputs to a sensitive Boolean computation, a threshold check on a confidential value, into the least significant bits (LSBs) of specific pixels. The transformation specification is an obfuscated Boolean circuit implemented via universal Fredkin gates, applied pixel-wise. The journalist has also embedded inputs for several
decoy computations
at other pixel positions: a brightness check, a color-balance verification, and a noise level assessment. All are processed identically by the cloud. If later coerced to explain her cloud usage, the journalist reveals a decoy and its verifiable result. The coercer cannot distinguish the decoy from the real computation.

This scenario illustrates two requirements that existing secure computation techniques fail to satisfy simultaneously:

1.

Existence hiding.
The act of performing a sensitive computation must be concealed within a legitimate interaction. FHE fails here: sending ciphertexts to a cloud is conspicuous, and a coercive adversary can demand decryption keys. PD-FHC embeds computation within routine image processing, making the protocol itself non-suspicious.

2.

Intent deniability.
Even if an adversary suspects computation occurred, the user must provide an alternative, verifiable explanation. TEEs fail here: attestation logs confirm exactly what was executed. PD-FHC supports multiple parallel computations, any of which can be credibly presented as the real one.

1.2
Design Requirements

We identify four properties that any deniable outsourced computation scheme must satisfy: (1)
uniform processability
the cloud applies identical operations to every data element; (2)
sufficient capacity
the cover medium has far more addressable positions than secret bits (
n\gg L\cdot\ell
); (3)
distribution preservation
gate operations preserve the statistical profile of non-secret positions; and (4)
cover plausibility
transmitting the medium to a cloud provider has a legitimate, non-suspicious purpose. We formalize these in Section
4
as properties of a
Deniable Computation Medium
(DCM) and present PD-FHC as both a general framework that satisfies these requirements and a concrete image-based instantiation.

Related Work

2.1
Fully Homomorphic Encryption

Gentry’s construction
[
14
]
enabled arbitrary computation on encrypted data. Subsequent schemes
[
6
,
4
,
1
]
have improved efficiency, and modern libraries support both Boolean and arithmetic circuits. However, FHE provides no mechanism for plausible deniability. The use of FHE is itself conspicuous: transmitting ciphertexts to a cloud provider is not normal behaviour, and a coercive adversary can demand decryption keys. PD-FHC addresses this by embedding computation within a legitimate interaction whose very existence is non-suspicious.

2.2
Trusted Execution Environments

Hardware-based solutions such as Intel SGX
[
11
]
, ARM TrustZone
[
23
]
, and AMD SEV
[
17
]
provide isolated execution. Despite strong integrity guarantees, TEEs require specialized hardware, have proven vulnerable to side-channel attacks
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
20
]
, and produce attestation logs that constitute undeniable proof of execution. PD-FHC is purely algorithmic and hardware-agnostic, producing no
attestation trail.

2.3
Steganography and Homomorphic Steganography

Classical steganography
[
13
]
conceals message existence within cover media. Advanced techniques by Holub and Fridrich
[
16
]
and Pevný et al.
[
22
]
achieve high undetectability but do not support computation on embedded data. ProSt
[
3
]
introduced homomorphic steganography, enabling Fredkin-gate computation on LSB-embedded data with information-theoretic privacy against an honest-but-curious cloud. However, ProSt embeds a single computation and does not address coercion. PD-FHC extends ProSt with multi-location embedding and a formal deniability model, enabling users to provide alternative explanations under coercion.

2.4
Deniable Cryptography and Storage

Canetti et al.
[
9
]
introduced deniable encryption, allowing senders to produce fake randomness that makes ciphertexts appear to encrypt different plaintexts. Subsequent work addressed receiver deniability
[
18
]
, bi-deniability
[
21
]
, and efficient constructions
[
5
]
. Deniable storage systems such as hidden volumes (e.g., VeraCrypt/TrueCrypt) and StegFS
[
19
]
apply deniability to file storage: hidden volumes are indistinguishable from random data in free space. PD-FHC
generalizes deniability from static data (messages, files) to dynamic
computational intent
: Alice denies not what she stored, but what she
computed
.

2.5
Secure Multi-Party Computation

MPC protocols
[
25
,
15
]
enable joint computation with input privacy. However, MPC requires interactive protocols among multiple parties, assumes honest participation, and the use of MPC itself is conspicuous. PD-FHC operates in a non-interactive, single-cloud setting and provides deniability that MPC does not offer.

Preliminaries

3.1
Notation

We write
n
for the total number of addressable bit-positions in a medium instance,
L
for the number of embedded computation scenarios (one real,
L-1
decoys),
\ell
for the number of input bits per scenario,
j^{*}
for the index of the real computation (known only to Alice), and
t
for the number of coercion rounds. Position sets are denoted
L_{j}\subset\mathcal{P}
with
L_{i}\cap L_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
(
disjoint positions
). A function
\nu:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is
negligible
if for every
c>0
there exists
n_{0}
such that
\nu(n)\leq n^{-c}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

3.2
LSB Steganography

An RGB image is represented as a tensor
I\in\mathbb{Z}_{256}^{h\times w\times 3}
, where

h,w
are height and width, and
3
denotes the RGB channels. We embed and extract single bits via the least significant bit:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Emb}(I,b,(r,c,k))

\displaystyle:I[r,c,k]\leftarrow(I[r,c,k]\;\&\;\neg 1)\,|\,b

(1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ext}(I,(r,c,k))

\displaystyle:\text{return }I[r,c,k]\;\&\;1

(2)

For an
h\times w\times 3
image, there are

n=h\cdot w\cdot 3
possible embedding positions.
Each position
(r,c,k)
identifies a unique row, column,
and color channel.

3.3
Fredkin Gate

The Fredkin gate
[
12
]
is a three-input, three-output reversible gate defined as:

F(c,x,y)=\begin{cases}(c,x,y)&\text{if }c=0\\
(c,y,x)&\text{if }c=1\end{cases}

where
c
is the control bit and
x,y
are data bits.

Lemma 1
(Hamming Weight Preservation)

For any
(c,x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{3}
, if

(c,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=F(c,x,y)
, then

c+x+y=c+x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}
.

Proof

If
c=0
, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x,y)
and the sum is unchanged. If
c=1
, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(y,x)
and
c+y+x=c+x+y
.

Why Fredkin gates.
Four properties make Fredkin gates essential to PD-FHC:

(1)
Bit-level compatibility.
Each gate operates on individual bits, directly compatible with LSB steganography where each position carries one bit.

(2)
Distribution preservation.

Hamming weight preservation (Lemma
1
) ensures that if input bits are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
, output bits are also
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
. Without this, the cloud could detect computation by comparing input and output bit distributions at each position.

(3)
Universality.
Any Boolean function can be computed using Fredkin gates with ancillary constant bits:

\displaystyle\mathsf{NOT}(x)

\displaystyle:F(1,x,0)=(1,0,x)\implies\neg x\text{ on wire 3}

(3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{AND}(x,y)

\displaystyle:F(x,y,0)=(x,\_,x\wedge y)\implies x\wedge y\text{ on wire 3}

(4)

\mathsf{OR}
follows from De Morgan’s law using three gates. For example, the Boolean expression
(A\wedge C)\vee(\neg A\wedge B)\vee(\neg B\wedge\neg C)
decomposes into 17 Fredkin gates: 3 for negations, 3 for conjunctions, 6 for disjunctions (via De Morgan), and 5 for wire management and ancillary routing.

(4)
Reversibility.

F(F(c,x,y))=(c,x,y)
, so no information is destroyed during computation, supporting correct result extraction from any embedded position.

4
Framework for Deniable Outsourced Computation

We define deniable outsourced computation as a general framework, independent of any specific cover medium. This abstraction separates the security model from the instantiation, allowing us to state security guarantees that hold for any medium satisfying our requirements. Section
6
instantiates this framework with RGB images.

4.1
Deniable Computation Medium

A deniable computation scheme requires a
cover medium
that serves two purposes: it provides a large space of addressable bit-positions for embedding secret computations among noise, and it supplies a legitimate context for transmitting data to a cloud provider.

Definition 1
(Deniable Computation Medium)

A
Deniable Computation Medium
(DCM) is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{P},n,\mathsf{Read},\mathsf{Write},\mathcal{G})
where:

•

\mathcal{D}
is the data domain, i.e., the set of valid medium instances;

•

\mathcal{P}
is a position space with
|\mathcal{P}|=n
, indexing the addressable
bit-positions within any instance
M\in\mathcal{D}
;

•

\mathsf{Read}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{P}\to\{0,1\}
extracts the bit at a given position;

•

\mathsf{Write}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{P}\times\{0,1\}\to\mathcal{D}
sets the bit at a given position, leaving all other positions unchanged;

•

\mathcal{G}
is a gate family. Each gate
G\in\mathcal{G}
is a function

G:\{0,1\}^{\kappa}\to\{0,1\}^{\kappa}
for some fixed arity
\kappa
. A gate is applied
uniformly
by evaluating
G
at every position
p\in\mathcal{P}
: given
\kappa
input instances
M_{1},\ldots,M_{\kappa}\in\mathcal{D}
, the gate produces
\kappa
output instances
M^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,M^{\prime}_{\kappa}
such that for each
p\in\mathcal{P}
:

\bigl(\mathsf{Read}(M^{\prime}_{1},p),\ldots,\mathsf{Read}(M^{\prime}_{\kappa},p)\bigr)=G\bigl(\mathsf{Read}(M_{1},p),\ldots,\mathsf{Read}(M_{\kappa},p)\bigr).

The uniform application of gates is the mechanism that ensures the cloud provider treats every position identically: the same function
G
is evaluated at every
p\in\mathcal{P}
, regardless of whether
p
carries secret data, decoy data, or noise.

4.2
Security Properties of a DCM

Not every medium is suitable for deniable computation. We require four properties.

Definition 2
(Suitable DCM)

A DCM
\mathcal{M}
is
suitable for deniable computation
if it satisfies the following properties:

Property 1 (Uniform Processability).
For every gate
G\in\mathcal{G}
and every set of input instances
M_{1},\ldots,M_{\kappa}
, the uniform application of
G
transforms each position
p\in\mathcal{P}
independently and identically. The computational cost and memory-access pattern of evaluating
G
are identical for every position, preventing the cloud provider from inferring which positions carry secret data through timing or cache side-channels.

Property 2 (Sufficient Capacity).

The number of addressable positions
n
satisfies
n\gg L\cdot\ell
, where
L
is the number of embedded computation scenarios and
\ell
is the number of bit-positions required per scenario. This ensures that secret positions constitute a negligible fraction of the total, making them difficult to locate.

Property 3 (Distribution
Preservation).
Let
\pi
denote a distribution over
\{0,1\}
(typically
\pi=\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
). If all non-secret positions in the input instances carry i.i.d. bits drawn from
\pi
, then after applying any sequence of gates
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}\in\mathcal{G}
, the bits at non-secret positions in the output instances remain distributed according to
\pi
:

\mathsf{Read}(M^{\prime\prime}_{i},p)\sim\pi\quad\text{for all non-secret }p\text{ and all output instances }M^{\prime\prime}_{i}.

This prevents the provider from identifying positions where “structured” computation occurred by comparing input and output distributions.

Property 4 (Cover Plausibility).
There exists a legitimate, non-suspicious use case for transmitting instances of
\mathcal{D}
to a cloud provider for processing and receiving processed instances in return. The gate specifications in
\mathcal{G}
must be consistent with this use case, so that the cloud provider’s view of the protocol is indistinguishable from a routine service interaction.

Remark.
Properties 1–3 are formal and verifiable for a given instantiation. Property 4 is inherently contextual: it depends on the operational setting and cannot be captured by a cryptographic definition. We discuss how specific instantiations satisfy Property 4 qualitatively in Section
6
.

4.3
Deniable Computation Scheme

We now define the algorithmic structure of a deniable computation over a DCM.

Definition 3
(Deniable Computation Scheme)

A
Deniable Computation Scheme
(DCS) over a suitable DCM
\mathcal{M}
is a tuple of algorithms:

\Pi=(\mathsf{LocGen},\mathsf{Emb},\mathsf{Comp},\mathsf{Ext})

•

\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{L}\}\leftarrow\mathsf{LocGen}(\mathcal{P},L,\ell)
: Samples
L
pairwise disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{P}
, each of size
\ell
, uniformly at random (
L_{i}\cap L_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
). These position sets are known only to Alice.

•

\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{Emb}(\mathbf{M},\{(L_{j},x_{j})\}_{j=1}^{L})
: For each scenario
j
, writes input bits
x_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
at positions
L_{j}
. All positions
p\notin\bigcup_{j}L_{j}
are filled with i.i.d. bits from
\pi
.

•

\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{Comp}(C,\mathbf{M}^{\prime})
: The cloud provider evaluates circuit
C=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{m})
by applying each gate
G_{i}
uniformly to all positions in
\mathcal{P}
. The provider receives only
C
and
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
, with no knowledge of position sets or scenario assignments.

•

y_{j}\leftarrow\mathsf{Ext}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime},L_{j})
: Alice reads output bits for scenario
j
from positions
L_{j}
in the computed instances.

Correctness.
A DCS is correct if for every scenario
j\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, the extracted output
y_{j}=\mathsf{Ext}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime},L_{j})
equals
f_{j}(x_{j})
, where
f_{j}
is the Boolean function computed by circuit
C
when evaluated on the bits at positions
L_{j}
.

Uniform processing as structural invariant.
The critical constraint is that
\mathsf{Comp}
applies every gate to
every
position in
\mathcal{P}
. Combined with Property 3, this ensures that the output medium
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
retains the same indistinguishability guarantees as
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
: the provider cannot distinguish secret from noise positions by observing the computation.

4.4
Possible Instantiations

The DCM framework is not limited to images. Any medium with high-capacity bit-addressable positions and a legitimate cloud-processing use case is a candidate: uncompressed audio
(LSBs in 16-bit samples, cloud audio normalization), or network packet payloads (cloud-based traffic processing). We focus on RGB images (Section
6
), where
\mathcal{P}=\{(r,c,k)\}
indexes pixel-channel LSBs, the gate family is Fredkin gates (
\kappa=3
), and cover plausibility follows from the prevalence of cloud image processing services.

5
Threat Model and Security Definitions

We formalize the adversarial setting in which a Deniable Computation Scheme (Definition
3
) operates. Our threat model separates two adversaries with distinct
capabilities and goals, reflecting realistic scenarios where the entity executing a computation differs from the entity demanding explanations.

5.1
Entities

Alice (Client).
Alice possesses private inputs and wishes to outsource a Boolean computation to a cloud provider. She prepares
L
computation scenarios (one real at secret index
j^{*}
, and
L-1
decoys) using a DCS
\Pi
over a DCM
\mathcal{M}
. Alice knows all position sets
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{L}\}
, the index
j^{*}
, all circuit descriptions, and all inputs and outputs for every scenario.

Carol (Cloud Provider).

An honest-but-curious (HBC) adversary. Carol receives embedded medium instances
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
and an obfuscated circuit
C_{\mathsf{obf}}
, and executes the protocol faithfully. She may attempt to infer secret information from her observations. Carol knows the DCS algorithms,
the gate family
\mathcal{G}
, and all public parameters. She does
not
know: any position sets
L_{j}
, the number of scenarios
L
, the true index
j^{*}
, or which positions carry secret data versus noise.

Eve (Coercive Adversary).
A computationally unbounded adversary who can compel Alice to reveal information. Eve models legal authorities, state actors, or any entity with the power to demand explanations under threat of consequences. Eve can observe the complete communication transcript between Alice and Carol:
V=(\mathbf{M}^{\prime},C_{\mathsf{obf}},\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime})
. Under coercion, Eve can demand that Alice reveal computation scenarios, including their position sets, circuit descriptions, inputs, and outputs.

Critically, Eve cannot force Alice to reveal information that Alice denies possessing.
In particular, Eve does not know the total number
L
of embedded scenarios. After Alice reveals
t
scenarios and claims no further scenarios exist, Eve cannot verify this claim because unrevealed positions are information-theoretically indistinguishable from noise (this is proved in Theorem
7.2
). This is analogous to hidden-volume deniability in deniable storage systems
[
24
]
: an adversary who demands all volumes cannot prove that a hidden volume exists if the free space is filled with random data.

5.2
Adversary Separation

The security model relies on a separation between Carol and Eve:

Temporal.
Carol processes data during computation; Eve coerces Alice after computation completes (or at a later time).

Capability.
Carol has infrastructure access (she processes the medium) but no coercion power. Eve has coercion power but no direct access to Carol’s infrastructure or intermediate computation states.

Non-collusion.
Carol and Eve do not share information or coordinate. We discuss collusion attacks and mitigations in Section
9
.

5.3
Security Game: Computational Privacy

We formalize what it means for the cloud provider to learn nothing about secret data. The game is defined over the abstract DCM (Definition
1
), not any specific medium.

Definition 4
(Computational Privacy Game)

Let
\Pi
be a DCS over a DCM
\mathcal{M}
with
n=|\mathcal{P}|
positions. The game

\mathsf{Game}_{\mathsf{priv}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,\ell)
between a challenger and adversary
\mathcal{A}
(modeling Carol) proceeds as follows:

1.

Setup.
The challenger generates
L
disjoint position sets
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{L}\}\leftarrow\mathsf{LocGen}(\mathcal{P},L,\ell)
and embeds
L
scenarios with inputs
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{L}\}
, producing
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{Emb}(\mathbf{M},\{(L_{j},x_{j})\}_{j=1}^{L})
. All non-secret positions carry i.i.d. bits from
\pi
.

2.

Computation.
The challenger applies the obfuscated circuit
C_{\mathsf{obf}}
uniformly, producing
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
.

3.

Challenge.

\mathcal{A}
receives
(\mathbf{M}^{\prime},C_{\mathsf{obf}},\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime})
and outputs a position
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}
.

4.

Win condition.

\mathcal{A}
wins if
p^{*}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}L_{j}
.

Definition 5
(Privacy Advantage)

The adversary’s advantage is:

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{priv}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,\ell)=\Pr[\mathcal{A}\ \text{wins}]-\frac{L\cdot\ell}{n}

where
L\cdot\ell/n
is the probability of hitting a secret position by random guessing.
\Pi
provides
computational privacy
if this advantage is negligible in
n
for all efficient adversaries
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark.
The game captures Carol’s strongest attack: identify
any
secret position. A weaker variant (guess the secret
value
at a known position) is trivially subsumed.

5.4
Security Game: Plausible Deniability

We model coercion as an interactive, multi-round game where the adversary may demand repeated explanations, reflecting realistic coercion settings.

Definition 6
(Multi-Round Coercion Game)

Let
\Pi
be a DCS over a DCM
\mathcal{M}
with
n=|\mathcal{P}|
. The game
\mathsf{Game}_{\mathsf{deny}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,t)
, parameterized by
L\geq 2
scenarios and
t<L
coercion rounds, proceeds as follows:

1.

Setup.
Alice prepares
L
scenarios
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{L}\}
where
s_{j}=(C_{j},x_{j},L_{j},y_{j})
consists of a circuit, inputs, position set, and correct output
y_{j}=f_{j}(x_{j})
. One scenario
s_{j^{*}}
is Alice’s real computation. All circuits have identical gate counts (padded with dummy gates as needed). Position sets are pairwise disjoint and uniformly random.

2.

Embedding & Computation.
Alice embeds all scenarios and transmits the medium to Carol. Carol processes it uniformly, producing
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
. The adversary
\mathcal{A}
(modeling Eve) observes the full transcript

V=(\mathbf{M}^{\prime},C_{\mathsf{obf}},\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime})
.

3.

Coercion rounds.
For
i=1,\ldots,t
:

(a)

\mathcal{A}
demands that Alice reveal a scenario.

(b)

Alice reveals a decoy
s_{j_{i}}=(C_{j_{i}},x_{j_{i}},L_{j_{i}},y_{j_{i}})
where
j_{i}\neq j^{*}
and
j_{i}\notin\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{i-1}\}
(a fresh, previously unrevealed decoy).

(c)

\mathcal{A}
verifies: reads output bits from
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
at positions
L_{j_{i}}
and checks
y_{j_{i}}=f_{j_{i}}(x_{j_{i}})
.

4.

Claim.
After
t
rounds, Alice claims no further scenarios exist.

5.

Adversary output.

\mathcal{A}
outputs a bit
b^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
(for existence distinguishing:
b^{\prime}=1
if
\mathcal{A}
believes unrevealed scenarios remain) or an index
j^{\prime}
(for intent distinguishing:
\mathcal{A}
’s guess of
j^{*}
).

Definition 7
(Deniability Advantages)

We define two advantages capturing distinct threats:

Existence distinguishing.

Consider two experiments. In
\mathsf{Exp}_{0}
, Alice embeds exactly
t
scenarios (all of which are revealed). In
\mathsf{Exp}_{1}
, Alice embeds
L>t
scenarios and reveals
t
of them (hiding
L-t
, including the real one). In both experiments, all non-revealed, non-secret
positions are i.i.d. from
\pi
. The existence advantage is:

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{exist}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,t)=\bigl|\Pr[\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{Exp}_{1})=1]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{Exp}_{0})=1]\bigr|

(5)

Intent distinguishing.
Assuming
\mathcal{A}
knows
L
(a strictly stronger adversary), the intent advantage measures the ability to identify
j^{*}
among the
L-t
unrevealed scenarios:

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{intent}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,t)=\Pr[j^{\prime}=j^{*}]-\frac{1}{L-t}

(6)

\Pi
provides
plausible deniability
if both advantages are negligible in
n
for all adversaries
\mathcal{A}
.

5.5
Discussion of the Coercion Model

Two-dimensional deniability.
The game captures two distinct threats.
Existence distinguishing
(Eq.
5
) asks whether Eve can tell apart a world where Alice has hidden scenarios from a world where all scenarios have been revealed. This is the direct analogue of hidden-volume detection in deniable storage.
Intent distinguishing

(Eq.
6
) asks whether, even if Eve somehow knows
L
, she can identify
j^{*}
among the unrevealed scenarios. Existence hiding is strictly stronger: if Eve cannot detect hidden scenarios, she certainly cannot identify
j^{*}
among them.

Why
t<L
is necessary.
If
t\geq L
, Alice runs out of decoys and must either reveal the real computation or be caught in an inconsistency. This is an inherent limitation of any finite deniability scheme. Alice’s security parameter is
L-t
: the number of unrevealed scenarios after coercion. Increasing
L
provides more “budget” for deniability at the cost of additional embedding (which is cheap, since
L\cdot\ell\ll n
for
practical parameters).

Eve does not know
L
.
This is not an assumption of convenience; it is an information-theoretic consequence of the scheme. After Alice reveals
t
scenarios and claims termination, the remaining
n-t\cdot\ell
positions consist of
(L-t)\cdot\ell
unrevealed secret positions and
n-L\cdot\ell
noise positions. Since all positions are i.i.d. under
\pi
(by Property 3 of the DCM), Eve cannot distinguish these two classes. Theorem
7.2
makes this precise.

Verification does not break deniability.
In each coercion round, Eve verifies that the revealed scenario is consistent: the output at the revealed positions matches the claimed circuit and inputs. This verification
always succeeds
for both real and decoy scenarios (by correctness of the DCS), so passing verification provides Eve with no distinguishing information.

Relationship to prior models.

Our existence advantage
\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{exist}}
is analogous to the hidden-volume detection advantage in deniable storage
[
9
,
19
]
. Our intent advantage
\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{intent}}
is analogous to the indistinguishability advantage in deniable encryption
[
9
]
. PD-FHC combines both in a single framework for the computational (rather than storage or communication) setting.

6
PD-FHC: Image-Based Instantiation

We instantiate the abstract framework of Section
4
using RGB images as the cover medium and Fredkin gates as the gate family.

6.1
Image-Based DCM

We define the image-based DCM
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{img}}
as an instance of Definition
1
:

\mathcal{D}

=\mathbb{Z}_{256}^{h\times w\times 3}

(RGB images with pixel values in
\{0,\ldots,255\}
)

\mathcal{P}

=\{(r,c,k):0\leq r<h,\;0\leq c<w,\;0\leq k<3\}

n

=h\cdot w\cdot 3

(total LSB positions)

\mathsf{Read}(I,(r,c,k))

=I[r,c,k]\;\&\;1

(extract LSB)

\mathsf{Write}(I,(r,c,k),b)

:I[r,c,k]\leftarrow(I[r,c,k]\;\&\;\neg 1)\,|\,b

(set LSB)

\mathcal{G}

=\{F\}
where

F:\{0,1\}^{3}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
is the Fredkin gate
(
\kappa=3
)

We verify that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{img}}
satisfies the four properties of a suitable DCM (Definition
2
):

Property 1 (Uniform Processability).
Each Fredkin gate reads one bit from each of three input images at position
p
and writes one bit to each of three output images at the same position
p
. This is applied identically for every
p\in\mathcal{P}
, with identical computation and memory access per position.

Property 2 (Sufficient Capacity).
Even a modest
128\times 128
image gives
n=49{,}152
, supporting hundreds of scenarios with
\ell=16
bits each while keeping

L\cdot\ell/n<1\%
.

Property 3 (Distribution Preservation).
Fredkin gates preserve Hamming weight (Lemma
1
): for uniform input bits, the output bits are also uniform. Non-secret positions initialized with i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
bits remain
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
after any number of Fredkin gate evaluations.

Property 4 (Cover Plausibility).
Cloud-based image processing is routine. Services such as AWS Lambda, Cloudinary, and Adobe Creative Cloud apply per-pixel transformations to image batches as a standard operation. A Fredkin-gate circuit applied pixel-wise to LSBs is syntactically identical to a custom bitwise image filter. The images transmitted are real photographs; LSB modifications are imperceptible to human vision
(PSNR
>50
dB, Section
8
).

6.2
Protocol Phases

Phase 1: Location Generation (Alice).

Alice runs
\mathsf{LocGen}(\mathcal{P},L,\ell)
to sample
L
pairwise disjoint position sets, each of size
\ell
, uniformly at random from
\mathcal{P}
. She also selects
L
Boolean circuits
C_{1},\ldots,C_{L}
with
\ell
inputs and one or more outputs. For deniability, all circuits must have identical gate counts after padding with dummy Fredkin gates operating on ancillary constants.

Phase 2: Multi-Location Embedding (Alice).

Alice embeds inputs for all
L
scenarios into a set of cover images. For each scenario
j
and each input bit
x_{j,i}
(
i=1,\ldots,\ell
), she writes
x_{j,i}
at the
i
-th position in
L_{j}
using
\mathsf{Write}
. All remaining LSB positions are filled with cryptographically random bits (using a CSPRNG). This produces embedded images
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
in which secret positions are information-theoretically indistinguishable from noise.

Phase 3: Circuit Obfuscation (Alice).

Alice obfuscates the combined circuit
C
before transmitting it to Carol:

1.

Wire renaming
: all wire identifiers are replaced with random strings.

2.

Dummy gates
: additional Fredkin gates operating on ancillary constants are inserted, increasing the apparent circuit complexity without affecting outputs.

3.

Execution order randomization
: gates are permuted while respecting topological dependencies.

These techniques increase the practical difficulty of reverse-engineering the circuit but do
not
provide provable function-hiding guarantees (see Section
7
for an honest treatment). PD-FHC’s formal security relies on information-theoretic position hiding and matched-complexity decoy circuits, not on circuit obfuscation.

Phase 4: Uniform Computation (Carol).

Carol receives the embedded images
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
and the obfuscated circuit
C_{\mathsf{obf}}
. For each gate
G_{i}
in topological order, Carol applies the Fredkin gate to
every
position
p\in\mathcal{P}
: she reads three LSBs from the gate’s three input images at
position
p
, computes
(c^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=F(c,x,y)
, and writes the results to three output images at the same position
p
. Output images from one gate serve as input images for subsequent gates.

This uniform processing ensures that Carol treats all positions identically: positions carrying real secrets, decoy secrets, and noise all undergo the same transformation. Carol cannot determine which positions carry which type of data.

Phase 5: Extraction (Alice).

Alice receives the output images
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
from Carol. For each scenario
j
, she reads the output bits from the designated output positions in
L_{j}
, obtaining
y_{j}=\mathsf{Ext}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime},L_{j})
.

Phase 6: Coercion Response (Alice).

Under coercion, Alice executes the following strategy. When Eve demands revelation of a computation:

1.

Alice selects a previously unrevealed decoy index
j_{i}\neq j^{*}
.

2.

Alice reveals
(C_{j_{i}},x_{j_{i}},L_{j_{i}},y_{j_{i}})
, providing a narrative explaining why
s_{j_{i}}
was her “real” computation (e.g., “I was checking image brightness thresholds for print quality”).

3.

Eve verifies: she extracts bits from
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
at positions
L_{j_{i}}
and confirms
y_{j_{i}}=f_{j_{i}}(x_{j_{i}})
. Verification always succeeds (by DCS correctness).

4.

If Eve demands another revelation, Alice repeats with a fresh decoy. After exhausting her revelation budget (
t<L-1
decoys), Alice claims no further scenarios exist.

Alice should prepare narratives for all decoy scenarios
before
deployment and pre-commit to a revelation order to ensure consistency across rounds.

6.3
Cover Plausibility

The cover plausibility argument rests on three observations. First, cloud image processing is a
multi-billion dollar industry; sending batches of images to a cloud service for per-pixel transformations is routine and non-suspicious. Second, Fredkin gates applied pixel-wise to LSBs produce output images that are visually identical to the inputs: only the least significant bit of each pixel value may change, resulting in at most a
\pm 1
shift per channel, which is imperceptible (we quantify this in Section
8
via PSNR and SSIM). Third, the obfuscated circuit specification is syntactically a sequence of three-input, three-output operations on pixel channels, which is consistent with common image filter definitions.

This combination means that the entire Alice Carol interaction images in, transformation specification, processed images out is indistinguishable from a standard cloud image processing job. In contrast, this is the property that FHE fundamentally lacks: transmitting FHE ciphertexts to a cloud provider has no innocent explanation.

7
Security Analysis

We prove that PD-FHC provides (i) computational privacy against Carol (Section
7.1
) and (ii) plausible deniability against Eve under multi-round
coercion (Section
7.2
). Results are stated over the abstract DCM and then instantiated for the image-based scheme. Full proofs are in Appendix
0.A
.

7.1
Computational Privacy

Theorem 7.1
(Privacy of Deniable Computation)

Let
\Pi
be a DCS over a suitable DCM
\mathcal{M}
(Definition
2
). If all non-secret positions carry i.i.d. bits from
\pi
and every gate
G\in\mathcal{G}
preserves
\pi
(Property 3), then for any adversary
\mathcal{A}
in the computational privacy game (Definition
4
):

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{priv}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,\ell)\leq L\cdot\varepsilon(n)

where
\varepsilon(n)
is the advantage of distinguishing a single secret position from a random position in
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof
(Proof sketch)

By a union bound over the
L\cdot\ell
secret positions. Each individual secret position
p\in L_{j}
is embedded among
n-1
positions carrying i.i.d. bits from
\pi
. Since the gate family preserves
\pi
(Property 3), the distribution at non-secret positions is unchanged after
computation. Hence the problem of identifying
p
reduces to the single-position hiding problem with advantage
\varepsilon(n)
. The union bound over
L\cdot\ell
positions yields the stated bound. (Since
L\cdot\ell\ll n
by Property 2, and
\varepsilon(n)
is negligible, the product remains negligible.) Full proof in Appendix
0.A.1
.

Corollary 1
(Image Instantiation)

For the image-based DCM
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{img}}
with
n=h\cdot w\cdot 3
LSB positions and Fredkin gates:

\varepsilon(n)=\frac{1-p_{b}^{2}}{p_{b}\cdot(n-1)!}\in\Theta\!\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\right)

where
p_{b}=\Pr[S=b]
for secret bit
S
.
Consequently,

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{priv}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,\ell)\leq L\cdot\Theta\!\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\right)

which is negligible for any polynomial
L
.

Proof

The single-position bound
\varepsilon(n)
follows from the information-hiding theorem of ProSt
[
3
]
. The argument is: Carol observes the vector of
n
LSBs, where one position carries the secret bit
S
and all others are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(p_{b})
. The secret position is chosen uniformly at random and unknown to Carol. A Bayesian analysis shows Carol’s posterior advantage over her prior is
\Theta(1/(n-1)!)
, which is negligible since
(n-1)!
grows super-exponentially. Fredkin gates preserve Hamming weight (Lemma
1
), hence preserve the marginal distribution
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
at each position. An induction over the circuit depth shows the bound holds after any number of gate evaluations.

Concrete security.
Even for a small
128\times 128
image (
n=49{,}152
), the advantage
1/(49{,}151)!
is astronomically smaller than the standard
2^{-128}
threshold.

7.2
Plausible Deniability

We analyze Eve’s advantage in the multi-round coercion game (Definition
6
) along two dimensions: existence distinguishing and intent distinguishing.

Existence Indistinguishability.

Theorem 7.2
(Existence Hiding)

In the multi-round coercion game with
t
revelations out of
L
scenarios, the existence advantage satisfies:

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{exist}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,t)\leq(L-t)\cdot\ell\cdot\varepsilon(n-t\cdot\ell)

which is negligible in
n
for the image instantiation.

Proof
(Proof sketch)

After
t
revelations, the remaining medium has
n^{\prime}=n-t\cdot\ell
positions. Distinguishing
\mathsf{Exp}_{0}
(all noise) from
\mathsf{Exp}_{1}
(
(L-t)\cdot\ell
secrets among noise) reduces to the privacy game on the residual medium. By Theorem
7.1
, the advantage is at most
(L-t)\cdot\varepsilon(n^{\prime})
. Full proof in Appendix
0.A.2
.

Implication.
Eve cannot prove that Alice is hiding additional computations beyond what she revealed. The unrevealed secret positions are information-theoretically indistinguishable from noise. This is the computational analogue of hidden-volume deniability: just as random data on a disk is indistinguishable from a hidden encrypted volume, random LSBs in an image are indistinguishable from embedded computation inputs.

Intent Indistinguishability.

We now consider a strictly stronger adversary who
knows

L
and must identify
j^{*}
among the
L-t
unrevealed scenarios. We analyze three attack vectors.

Theorem 7.3
(Intent Hiding)

In the multi-round coercion game, Eve’s intent advantage satisfies:

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{intent}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,t)\leq\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{side}}

where
\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}
bounds circuit-structure distinguishability and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{side}}
bounds side-channel leakage.

Proof

We bound each attack vector separately and combine via union bound.

Position analysis.
All
L
position sets are sampled uniformly and independently from
\mathcal{P}
. The revealed sets
\{L_{j_{1}},\ldots,L_{j_{t}}\}
are independent of the unrevealed sets. Hence, for any adversary attempting to identify
j^{*}
from position information alone:

\Pr[\mathcal{A}(L_{j_{1}},\ldots,L_{j_{t}})\text{ identifies }j^{*}]=\frac{1}{L-t}.

Position analysis provides zero advantage over random guessing.

Circuit analysis.
Alice pads all
L
circuits to identical gate count
m
and obfuscates with wire renaming, dummy gates, and execution order randomization. Residual distinguishability comes from structural differences (e.g., circuit depth). We define:

\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}=\max_{i,j}\frac{|d(C_{i})-d(C_{j})|}{\max_{k}d(C_{k})}

where
d(C)
denotes circuit depth. For carefully matched circuits,
\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}=O(1/m)
.

We emphasize an important limitation: the lightweight obfuscation does
not
provide cryptographic function-hiding. An adversary who can analyze circuit semantics (e.g., determine that
C_{j^{*}}
computes a medical threshold while
C_{j}
computes a parity check) gains additional advantage not captured by
\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}
. Alice must therefore construct decoy circuits that are semantically plausible for the claimed use case (Definition
8
). Formalizing and automating semantic plausibility is an open problem.

Side-channel analysis.
Under the constant-time execution model, where Carol processes all
n
positions sequentially for each gate layer, the execution trace
T
depends only on
(n,m)
and not on secret data. Formally,
I(T;j^{*})=0
, giving
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{side}}=0
. In practice, residual leakage from micro-architectural effects can be bounded by
2^{-\lambda}
for security parameter
\lambda
.

Combining via union bound gives
\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{intent}}\leq\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{side}}
.

Concrete Parameters.

For practical deployments:

•

m=17
gates (small circuit):
\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}\leq 1/17\approx 0.06
, giving Eve at most
{\sim}6\%
advantage over random guessing among unrevealed scenarios.

•

m=81
gates (4-bit adder):
\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}\leq 1/81\approx 0.012
, giving
{\sim}1.2\%
advantage.

•

m=289
gates (8-bit multiplier):
\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}\leq 1/289\approx 0.003
, giving
{\sim}0.3\%
advantage.

These bounds improve as circuit complexity grows. Alice can further reduce
\delta_{\mathsf{circuit}}
by adding dummy gates or selecting circuits with naturally similar structures.

7.3
Decoy Plausibility

The formal bounds above assume that decoy scenarios cannot be distinguished on semantic grounds. We make this requirement explicit.

Definition 8
(
\varepsilon
-Plausible Decoy)

A decoy scenario
(C_{j},x_{j},y_{j})
is
\varepsilon
-plausible with respect to a context
\Gamma
(describing Alice’s claimed activity) if:(i)
C_{j}
computes a function semantically consistent
with
\Gamma
; (ii) the inputs
x_{j}
are drawn from a distribution consistent with
\Gamma
; and
(iii)
\Pr[\text{Eve identifies }j\text{ as decoy}\mid\Gamma]\leq 1/L+\varepsilon
.

Example.
If Alice claims to run image-quality checks on a photo archive, plausible decoys include brightness threshold checks, color balance verification, and noise level assessment. An implausible decoy would compute a cryptographic hash function with no image-processing interpretation.

Limitation.
Constructing semantically plausible decoys requires domain knowledge. Automating this process is an open problem and a direction for future work.

7.4
Computational Exchangeability

The security of PD-FHC rests on a symmetry property we call
computational exchangeability
.

Proposition 1
(Exchangeability)

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L})
denote the output bit-vectors at the
L
secret position sets after circuit evaluation. For any permutation
\sigma
of scenario indices:

\bigl|\Pr[\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{y})=1]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}(\sigma(\mathbf{y}))=1]\bigr|\leq\mathsf{negl}(n)

for any efficient adversary
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof
(Proof sketch)

Each
y_{j}
results from circuit evaluation on inputs
x_{j}
embedded at uniformly random positions
L_{j}
, processed uniformly alongside i.i.d. noise. Since position sets are independent uniform samples of disjoint subsets, processing is position-independent, and non-secret bits are computationally indistinguishable from random after Fredkin transformations (by Lemma
1
), the joint distribution of outputs is invariant under permutation of scenario indices up to negligible distinguishing advantage. Full proof in Appendix
0.A.3
.

Exchangeability means there is no “privileged” real scenario that stands out from decoys based on observable computation artifacts. All
L
scenarios are equivalent from Eve’s viewpoint.

9
Discussion

Collusion.
If Carol and Eve collude, the phase separation (Section
5.2
) breaks. Carol could log intermediate computation states or process positions non-uniformly, enabling Eve to correlate side-channel data with revealed scenarios. Mitigations include generating fresh random positions for each execution (preventing cross-session correlation), distributing computation across multiple non-colluding providers, and employing verifiable computation to detect non-uniform processing. These add complexity and are complementary to PD-FHC’s core guarantees.

Limitations.

(1) PD-FHC supports Boolean circuits only; extending to arithmetic circuits is future work.
(2) Storage overhead is significant: each gate requires input and output images, yielding
O(m\cdot h\cdot w)
total storage. Image reuse via graph-coloring techniques can reduce this.
(3) Circuit obfuscation provides defense-in-depth but not provable function-hiding (Section
7
). Formal indistinguishability obfuscation would strengthen the guarantee but is impractical with current techniques. (4) Decoy plausibility requires manual construction of semantically appropriate decoy circuits. Automating this is an open problem.
(5) Security holds for single executions. Reusing positions across sessions enables correlation attacks; fresh randomization per session mitigates this. (6) Alice must trust that Carol executes the circuit correctly. Unlike FHE with verification or TEEs with attestation, PD-FHC provides no mechanism to detect incorrect execution without revealing secret positions.

10
Conclusion

We introduced PD-FHC, the first framework for deniable outsourced computation. By defining the notion of a Deniable Computation Medium and formalizing multi-round coercion games with both existence and intent distinguishing advantages, we established a foundation for provable deniability in cloud computing. Our image-based instantiation achieves computational privacy with advantage
\Theta(1/(n-1)!)
against an honest-but-curious cloud provider and provides plausible deniability against coercive adversaries who may demand multiple revelations. The implementation demonstrates competitive performance with TFHE for Boolean circuits while providing deniability that FHE fundamentally cannot.

Future work.
Key directions include GPU acceleration for large-scale circuits (projected 10–50
\times
speedup), automated decoy circuit generation, formal function-hiding obfuscation, extension to arithmetic circuits, integration with ORAM for collusion resistance, and analysis of security against quantum adversaries.

0.A.1
Proof of Theorem
7.1

(Privacy of Deniable Computation)

Proof

We reduce the multi-location privacy game to the single-position hiding problem via a hybrid argument and union bound.

Setup.
Consider the computational privacy game (Definition
4
). The challenger embeds
L
scenarios with
\ell
bits each at pairwise disjoint, uniformly random position sets
L_{1},\ldots,L_{L}\subset\mathcal{P}
, with all remaining positions carrying i.i.d. bits from
\pi
. The adversary
\mathcal{A}
receives
(\mathbf{M}^{\prime},C_{\mathsf{obf}},\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime})
and outputs a position
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}
.

Hybrid games.
Define a sequence of hybrid experiments
\mathsf{Hyb}_{0},\mathsf{Hyb}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{Hyb}_{L\cdot\ell}
. In
\mathsf{Hyb}_{0}
, no positions carry secrets (all
n
positions are i.i.d. from
\pi
). In
\mathsf{Hyb}_{k}
, the first
k
secret positions (in some canonical ordering of
\bigcup_{j}L_{j}
) carry their true secret values, and the remaining
L\cdot\ell-k
positions that would carry secrets instead carry i.i.d. bits from
\pi
.
\mathsf{Hyb}_{L\cdot\ell}
is the real experiment.

Single-step indistinguishability.
For any consecutive pair
(\mathsf{Hyb}_{k-1},\mathsf{Hyb}_{k})
, the only difference is the value at a single position
p_{k}
: in
\mathsf{Hyb}_{k-1}
it carries a random bit from
\pi
; in
\mathsf{Hyb}_{k}
it carries the secret bit
s_{k}
. Since
s_{k}
is itself a deterministic value embedded among
n-1
positions carrying i.i.d.
\pi
-bits, and the position
p_{k}
is chosen uniformly at random and unknown to
\mathcal{A}
, this reduces to the single-position hiding problem. By assumption, the distinguishing advantage for a single position is
\varepsilon(n)
.

Distribution preservation under computation.
After circuit evaluation, each gate
G\in\mathcal{G}
preserves
\pi
at non-secret positions (Property 3 of
Definition
2
). For the image instantiation, this follows from Hamming weight preservation (Lemma
1
): the Fredkin gate
F(c,x,y)=(c,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
satisfies
c+x+y=c+x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}
, so if the input triple
(c,x,y)
at a non-secret position consists of i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
bits, the output triple
(c,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
has the same marginal distribution. Formally, for any
b\in\{0,1\}
:

\Pr[x^{\prime}=b]=\Pr[c=0]\cdot\Pr[x=b]+\Pr[c=1]\cdot\Pr[y=b]=\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}.

By induction over the circuit depth
d
, after applying
m
gates in sequence, each non-secret position retains
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
marginals, so the single-position hiding advantage
\varepsilon(n)
applies to the post-computation medium as well.

Union bound.
By the triangle inequality applied to the hybrid chain:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{priv}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,\ell)

\displaystyle=\bigl|\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins in }\mathsf{Hyb}_{L\cdot\ell}]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins in }\mathsf{Hyb}_{0}]\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L\cdot\ell}\bigl|\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins in }\mathsf{Hyb}_{k}]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins in }\mathsf{Hyb}_{k-1}]\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq L\cdot\ell\cdot\varepsilon(n).

Since
\varepsilon(n)
is negligible (by assumption on the DCM) and
L\cdot\ell
is polynomial in
n
(by Property 2), the product
L\cdot\ell\cdot\varepsilon(n)
is negligible. In the theorem statement we write
L\cdot\varepsilon(n)
as a simplified upper bound absorbing the constant
\ell
.

Instantiation.
For the image-based DCM, the single-position advantage is derived from the ProSt information-hiding theorem
[
3
]
. The secret bit
S
is placed at an unknown position among
n-1
i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(p_{b})
decoy bits via a uniformly random permutation
\pi
. The adversary observes the resulting vector
M=(M_{0},\ldots,M_{n-1})
and attempts to compute
\Pr[S=b\mid M=m]
. By Bayesian analysis:

\Pr[S=b\mid M=m]=\frac{\Pr[S=b,M_{0}=b]}{\Pr[M_{0}=b]}=\frac{\frac{1}{(n-1)!}+\left(1-\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\right)p_{b}^{2}}{p_{b}}

The advantage
|\Pr[S=b\mid M]-\Pr[S=b]|
evaluates to
\frac{1-p_{b}^{2}}{p_{b}\cdot(n-1)!}\in\Theta(1/(n-1)!)
. Since
(n-1)!
grows super-exponentially, this is negligible for any
n\geq 2
.

Proof of Theorem
7.2

(Existence Hiding)

Proof

We show that after
t
coercion rounds, the remaining medium is indistinguishable from a purely random medium from Eve’s perspective.

Post-revelation state.
After
t
revelations, Eve knows:

•

The revealed position sets
\{L_{j_{1}},\ldots,L_{j_{t}}\}
and their contents.

•

The communication transcript
V
.

Eve’s view of the remaining
n^{\prime}=n-t\cdot\ell
positions is the residual medium
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathsf{res}}
obtained by excluding the
t\cdot\ell
revealed positions.

Experiment construction.
We construct two experiments matching
Definition
7
:

In
\mathsf{Exp}_{0}
:
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathsf{res}}
contains
n^{\prime}
positions, all carrying i.i.d. bits from
\pi
(no hidden scenarios exist).

In
\mathsf{Exp}_{1}
:
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathsf{res}}
contains
n^{\prime}
positions, of which

(L-t)\cdot\ell
carry unrevealed secrets at uniformly random positions, and the rest carry i.i.d. bits from
\pi
.

Independence of revealed and unrevealed positions.
The position sets

L_{1},\ldots,L_{L}
are sampled as
L
pairwise disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{P}
, each chosen uniformly at random. Given the revealed sets
\{L_{j_{1}},\ldots,L_{j_{t}}\}
, the conditional
distribution of the unrevealed sets
\{L_{j}:j\notin\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{t}\}\}
is uniform over all
(L-t)
-tuples of pairwise disjoint
\ell
-subsets of
\mathcal{P}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}L_{j_{i}}
. In particular, no information about unrevealed positions is leaked by revealed positions, because the position sampling is independent.

Reduction to privacy game.
Distinguishing
\mathsf{Exp}_{0}
from
\mathsf{Exp}_{1}
is exactly the computational privacy game (Definition
4
) played on a medium of reduced size
n^{\prime}=n-t\cdot\ell
with
L^{\prime}=L-t
embedded scenarios. By Theorem
7.1
:

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathsf{exist}}^{\mathcal{A}}(n,L,t)\leq(L-t)\cdot\ell\cdot\varepsilon(n^{\prime})

where
\varepsilon(n^{\prime})=\Theta(1/(n^{\prime}-1)!)
for the image instantiation. Since
t\cdot\ell\ll n
for practical parameters (
t\cdot\ell\leq
a few hundred, while
n\geq 49{,}152
), we have
n^{\prime}
is close to
n
and the advantage remains negligible.

Concrete bound.
For
n=196{,}608
(a
256\times 256
image),
L=4
,
t=1
,
\ell=16
:
n^{\prime}=196{,}592
and the advantage is bounded by
3\cdot 16\cdot\Theta(1/(196{,}591)!)\approx 0
, which is astronomically smaller than any computationally relevant threshold.

0.A.3
Proof of Proposition
1

(Exchangeability)

Proof

We prove that the joint distribution of output vectors
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L})
is invariant under permutation of scenario indices, up to negligible distinguishing advantage.

Setup.
Let
\sigma
be an arbitrary permutation of
\{1,\ldots,L\}
. We must show that for any efficient adversary
\mathcal{A}
:

\bigl|\Pr[\mathcal{A}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L})=1]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}(y_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,y_{\sigma(L)})=1]\bigr|\leq\mathsf{negl}(n).

Position symmetry.
Each position set
L_{j}
is a uniformly random
\ell
-subset of
\mathcal{P}
, sampled independently (subject to disjointness). The gate family applies the same function
G
at every position in
\mathcal{P}
. Therefore, the joint distribution of
(\mathsf{Read}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime},p))_{p\in L_{j}}
depends only on the input bits
(x_{j,1},\ldots,x_{j,\ell})
embedded at positions
L_{j}
and on the noise bits at all other positions. Since noise bits are i.i.d. from
\pi
and gates preserve
\pi
(Lemma
1
, Property 3), the noise contribution is identically distributed regardless of which scenario occupies which positions.

Input independence.
Each scenario
j
has its own input vector
x_{j}
embedded at its own position set
L_{j}
. Because positions are disjoint and gates operate position-wise (the output at position
p
depends only on inputs at
p
across the
\kappa
input images), the output
y_{j}
is determined by
x_{j}
and the noise at positions
L_{j}
. There is no cross-scenario interference: the computation at positions
L_{j_{1}}
does not affect the computation at positions
L_{j_{2}}
for
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
.

Permutation invariance.
Suppose we permute scenario labels so that the inputs
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{L})
at positions
(L_{1},\ldots,L_{L})
become
(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(L)})
at positions
(L_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,L_{\sigma(L)})
. Since all position sets are drawn from the same uniform distribution, the pair
(L_{j},x_{j})
has the same marginal distribution as
(L_{\sigma(j)},x_{\sigma(j)})
. The output
y_{j}
at position set
L_{j}
with input
x_{j}
has the same distribution as
y_{\sigma(j)}
at position set
L_{\sigma(j)}
with input
x_{\sigma(j)}
.

To distinguish
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L})
from
(y_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,y_{\sigma(L)})
, the adversary would need to link a specific output vector to a specific position set. But the position sets are unknown to the adversary (they are secret), and the outputs themselves are embedded among noise that is indistinguishable from the secret bits (by Theorem
7.1
with advantage
\varepsilon(n)=\Theta(1/(n-1)!)
). Hence the adversary’s distinguishing advantage is at most
L\cdot\varepsilon(n)=\mathsf{negl}(n)
.

Detailed Hamming Weight Preservation Under Circuit Composition

We prove that Hamming weight preservation holds through arbitrary circuit compositions, not just
single gates.

Lemma 2
(Hamming Weight Under Composition)

Let
C=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{m})
be a circuit of
m
Fredkin gates. For any position
p\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\mathbf{b}_{\mathsf{in}}(p)
denote the vector of all input bits read from position
p
across all input images, and
\mathbf{b}_{\mathsf{out}}(p)
denote the vector of all output bits written to position
p
across all output images. Then:

\mathsf{wt}(\mathbf{b}_{\mathsf{out}}(p))=\mathsf{wt}(\mathbf{b}_{\mathsf{in}}(p))

where
\mathsf{wt}(\cdot)
denotes Hamming weight.

Proof

We proceed by induction on the circuit depth
d
.

Base case (
d=1
).
A single Fredkin gate
G_{1}
reads
(c,x,y)
from position
p
across three input images and writes
(c^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
to three output images. By Lemma
1
,
c+x+y=c^{\prime}+x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}
, so the Hamming weight is preserved.

Inductive step.
Suppose the claim holds for circuits of depth
d-1
. At depth
d
, the gate
G_{d}
reads its inputs from output images of previous gates. At position
p
, these intermediate images carry bits whose total Hamming weight equals the initial input weight (by the inductive hypothesis). Gate
G_{d}
permutes its three input bits (conditionally swapping the data pair) without changing the sum. Hence the total Hamming weight at
p
across all images at depth
d
equals the total at depth
d-1
, which equals the initial weight.

Consequence for distribution preservation.
If the initial input bits at a non-secret position
p
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
, then the joint distribution of bits at
p
after the full circuit is a permutation of the original bits (by reversibility of Fredkin gates). Since any permutation of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables has the same marginal distribution, each output bit at
p
remains
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
. This is precisely Property 3 of the DCM, confirming that the image instantiation satisfies distribution preservation for circuits of arbitrary depth.
