Linerixibat: First Approval
Authors: Sheridan M Hoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLinerixibat (LYNAVOY®) is an orally administered reversible ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT) inhibitor developed by GSK for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus. In March 2026, linerixibat received its first approval for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus associated with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) in adult patients in the USA. It is the first US FDA-approved therapy for this indication. Subsequently, linerixibat was approved in May 2026 in the UK for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus in adult patients with PBC. A regulatory review of linerixibat is currently underway in Canada, China and the EU for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus associated with PBC. Introduction

Disturbances in the formation and/or flow of bile result in cholestasis [1]. Hepatobiliary disorders with cholestatic features [e.g. primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)] are commonly accompanied by pruritus [1], with bile acids, interleukin (IL)-31 and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) among the agents believed to play a causative role [2]. While primary skin lesions are not present in patients experiencing cholestatic pruritus, intense scratching can result in secondary skin lesions (e.g. excoriations, prurigo nodularis and scars); these lesions may be difficult to distinguish from dermatological or other conditions [1]. Chronic cholestatic pruritus can impair patients’ ability to function, their quality of life and their sleep, resulting in fatigue, depression and potentially suicidal ideation [1, 3]. Over half to approximately nine-tenths of patients with PBC experience cholestatic pruritus, with at least one quarter to approximately one half experiencing moderate to severe, or clinically relevant cholestatic pruritus [4–7].

Ileal bile acid transporters (IBATs) are involved in the reabsorption of bile acids from the small intestine (where they have been converted from bile acid intermediates to bile acids by small intestinal flora) to the bloodstream, from where they are conveyed back to the hepatobiliary system [2]. A number of IBAT inhibitors have been evaluated for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus [2]. Linerixibat (LYNAVOY®) is an orally administered reversible IBAT inhibitor [8]. Discovered and developed by GSK, linerixibat received its first approval on 17 March 2026 for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus associated with PBC in adult patients in the USA [8–10]. It is the first US FDA-approved therapy for this indication [9]. Subsequently, linerixibat was approved on 1 May 2026 in the UK for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus in adult patients with PBC [11, 12].

Linerixibat is available as a 40 mg tablet [8, 11]. The recommended dosage is 40 mg, administered orally, twice daily. The tablets should be swallowed whole and taken approximately 30 min before any food or beverage (other than water) [8, 11]. A missed dose of linerixibat should be taken as soon as possible [8, 11], with the US prescribing information specifying that a missed dose may be administered up to 6 h after the scheduled dose and at least 30 min before the next meal, then the original dosing schedule should be resumed [8]. If a dose is missed by > 6 h, it should be skipped and the original dosing schedule resumed thereafter [8]. A double dose should not be taken to make up for a missed dose [8, 11]. Linerixibat should be administered ≥ 4 h before or 4 h after taking a bile acid binding resin [8, 11]. In the USA, the use of linerixibat should be avoided in patients with decompensated cirrhosis or those with prior or active hepatic decompensation events (e.g. ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal hemorrhage) [8]. In the UK, linerixibat is not recommended in patients with severe hepatic impairment [11]. Prior to treatment initiation, the hepatic status of the patient and whether they have decompensated cirrhosis (including Child-Pugh Class C) or have had a prior decompensation event must be known [11].

In the USA, warnings and precautions regarding linerixibat are elevations in liver tests, diarrhoea and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency [8]. Liver tests [alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin, direct bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase (ALP)] should be obtained before commencing therapy with linerixibat and monitored per standard clinical practice for PBC patients [8]. In the UK, warnings and precautions regarding linerixibat are elevations in transaminases and the efficacy of obeticholic acid (OCA) [11]. ALT and AST levels should be obtained prior to initiating treatment with linerixibat, with transaminases monitored at a frequency aligned with standard clinical practice for PBC patients. Concomitant linerixibat and OCA administration may reduce the efficacy of OCA; thus, monitoring the biochemical response (e.g. ALP and bilirubin levels) during OCA therapy is recommended when linerixibat treatment is initiated [11].

Linerixibat is undergoing regulatory review in Canada, China and the EU for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus associated with PBC, and has been granted Orphan Drug Designation in Japan for pruritus in PBC [13] and in the EU and the USA for the treatment of PBC [9, 14, 15].

Company Agreements

In April 2026, a licence agreement between GSK and Alfasigma S.p.A. was completed [16]. Under the terms of this agreement, Alfasigma acquired worldwide exclusive rights to develop, manufacture and commercialise linerixibat [16].

Scientific Summary

Pharmacodynamics

The pathophysiology of pruritus in patients with PBC is not completely understood [8, 11]. Linerixibat is a reversible IBAT inhibitor. By decreasing bile acid reabsorption in the terminal ileum, it increases faecal bile acid excretion [8, 11].

In a phase I study (NCT02801981) in healthy Japanese adult men [17], and in phase II [NCT01899703 [18]; NCT02966834 (GLIMMER) [19]] and III (NCT04950127; GLISTEN) [20] studies in adults with cholestatic pruritus and PBC (Sect. 2.4), linerixibat reduced potential mediators of cholestatic pruritus, including total serum bile acid (TBSA) levels [17–20], serum autotaxin (a rate-liming enzyme in LPA production and measured as a surrogate of LPA) activity [18–20] and serum IL-31 levels [20]. In terms of biomarkers of bile acid synthesis and reabsorption, reductions in serum fibroblast growth factor-19 (FGF-19) levels [18–20] and serum conjugated bile acid (particularly taurocholate, glycocholate and taurochenodeoxycholate) levels [18], and increases in serum 7α-hydroxy-4-cholesten-3-one (C4; a key intermediate in the synthesis of bile acids) levels [17–20] were observed.

Based on results from a k-PD model [which described the effect of linerixibat on worst daily itch score over time and was developed using data from a multinational, phase II dose-ranging study (NCT02966834; GLIMMER)] and a post hoc longitudinal proportional odds model (which described the probability of patient-reported diarrhoea severity over time), a 40 mg twice daily dosage of linerixibat was selected for the confirmatory phase III GLISTEN (NCT04950127) study [Sect. 2.3] [21].

Pharmacokinetics

Following oral administration, linerixibat absorption is minimal; the absolute oral bioavailability of linerixibat is 0.05% [8, 11]. In healthy individuals treated twice daily with linerixibat 3–90 mg (0.08–2.25-fold the approved recommended dose), plasma concentrations of linerixibat were generally below the limit of quantification (1000 pg/mL) [8]. In patients with cholestatic pruritus and PBC, the mean maximum concentration of linerixibat following multiple 40 mg twice daily dosages was 922 pg/mL [8, 11]. Taking linerixibat with food has no clinically relevant impact on the systemic exposure of the drug; however, in order to permit linerixibat to enter the gastrointestinal lumen prior to bile acid release (gall bladder contraction), the drug should be administered before any food or drink (other than water) [8, 11].

In vitro, linerixibat is 71.0–76.4% bound to plasma proteins [8, 11]. Metabolism of linerixibat is minimal in humans. The elimination of linerixibat after the administration of oral radiolabeled linerixibat occurred mainly in the faeces (≈ 97%), with ≈ 0.04% of the dose eliminated via the urine. Almost all (> 99%) faecal radioactivity was determined to be unchanged and unabsorbed linerixibat [8, 11]. The mean half-life of linerixibat was 6.76 h following oral administration of a single 90 mg (2.25-fold the approved recommended dose) dose in healthy adults [8].

The changes in TBSA levels observed with linerixibat were not affected to a clinically relevant extent by age, body weight, race (White, Black, or Asian) or sex [8]. Mean area under the linerixibat concentration–time curve from 0 to infinity and maximum linerixibat concentration values were approximately 13-fold and 9.4-fold higher in patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh B) than matched healthy patients. The pharmacokinetics of linerixibat have not been studied in patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C) and minimal data are available from patients with cirrhosis (Child-Pugh A) [8].

Drug Interactions

Although in vitro studies have shown linerixibat to be an inhibitor of CYP3A4, and suggest that there is potential for linerixibat to inhibit OATP1B1 and OATP1B3, such interactions are unlikely owing to the low fraction of linerixibat absorbed [8]. As bile acid sequestrants cholestyramine and colesevelam bound ≈ 78–95% of linerixibat in vitro, they may inhibit the effects of linerixibat on IBAT. The concomitant administration of linerixibat (administered at dosages ranging from 20 mg once daily to 90 mg twice daily) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) for 12 weeks in adults with PBC had no effect on plasma UDCA concentrations but reduced the concentrations of UDCA conjugates glyco-UDCA and tauro-UDCA by 69% and 90%. However, no clinically relevant increases in ALP levels were seen [8].

Therapeutic Trials

Phase III GLISTEN Study

In the randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multinational, phase III GLISTEN (NCT04950127) study in adults (aged 18–80 years) with proven PBC and moderate­to-severe pruritus, treatment with oral linerixibat 40 mg twice daily resulted in significant improvements in pruritus and pruritus-related sleep interference over 24 weeks compared with placebo [3]. GLISTEN was comprised of two parts. Part A consisted of a 24-week period in which patients received either linerixibat or placebo. In part B, patients either continued to receive the same treatment or crossed over to the other treatment group (with timing blinded to the patient) for a further 8 weeks. The Worst Itch Numerical Rating Scale (WI-NRS) [scores range from 0 (no itching) to 10 (worst imaginable itching)] was used to calculate the worst daily itch score (defined as the worst of two scores recorded in a day), the weekly itch score (defined as the average of the worst daily scores over 7 days) and the monthly itch score (defined as the worst weekly itch score for the month). A numerical rating scale (NRS) [scores range from 0 (did not interfere) to 10 (completely interfered)] was used to calculate pruritus-related sleep interference. The weekly sleep score was defined as the average of seven daily sleep scores and the monthly sleep score was defined as the worst weekly sleep score of the month [3].

In part A of GLISTEN, the least-squares mean (LSM) change from baseline over 24 weeks in the monthly itch score (primary endpoint) was significantly greater in patients receiving linerixibat than those receiving placebo [− 2.86 vs − 2.15; adjusted mean difference − 0.72 (95% CI − 1.15 to − 0.28); p = 0.0013] [3]. Moreover, the LSM change from baseline at week 2 in the weekly itch score (key secondary endpoint) [− 1.78 vs − 1.07; adjusted mean difference − 0.71 (95% CI − 1.07 to − 0.34); p = 0.0002] and the LSM change from baseline over 24 weeks in monthly sleep score (key secondary endpoint) [− 2.77 vs − 2.24; adjusted mean difference − 0.53 (95% CI − 0.98 to − 0.07); p = 0.024] were significantly greater in linerixibat recipients than placebo recipients. Notably, the change in pruritus was highly correlated with the change in sleep interference (r = 0.87). The proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 2-point reduction from baseline in monthly itch score at week 24 (key secondary endpoint) did not significantly differ between linerixibat and placebo [68% vs 64%; adjusted difference 4% (95% CI − 9 to 17)]; thus, as per the hierarchical testing procedure, all subsequent p-values were considered nominal. A nominally significant difference with linerixibat versus placebo was seen for the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 3-point reduction [56% vs 43%; adjusted difference 13% (95% CI 0 to 27); p = 0.043] but not a ≥ 4-point reduction [41% vs 29%; adjusted difference 12% (95% CI 0–24)] from baseline in monthly itch score at week 24 (key secondary endpoints). In part B of GLISTEN, patients who switched to linerixibat experienced improvements in pruritus while those who switched to placebo experienced a worsening in pruritus. No notable change was experienced by those patients who continued with the treatment assigned in part A [3].

At enrolment, moderate-to-severe pruritus was defined as a monthly itch score of ≥ 4 with no weekly itch score of < 3 for any other week in the month prior to randomization [3]. Moderate and severe pruritus, respectively, were defined as a monthly itch score of ≥ 4 to < 7, and ≥ 7. Among the key exclusion criteria were the presence of other liver diseases (e.g. primary sclerosing cholangitis, alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis), the presence of dermatological diseases associated with pruritus, total bilirubin > 2 × the upper limit of normal (ULN), ALT > 6 × ULN and hepatic decompensation. Randomization was stratified by pruritus severity (moderate or severe) and concomitant pruritus treatment (bile acid binding resins, other treatments, or none). Concomitant medications that may affect pruritus and those for PBC were required to be stable for 8 weeks prior to the start of and during the study. The use of an IBAT inhibitor other than linerixibat, or OCA was not permitted. Patients were instructed to take one tablet of the study medication twice a day (i.e. once in the morning and once in the evening; ≈ 12 h apart) before food or drink. Treatment was to be administered at approximately the same time each day. No dose modification was permitted. The key secondary endpoints were assessed in a hierarchical manner. At baseline of part A, baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were generally similar between the linerixibat and placebo groups (n = 119 and 119) [3]. In the linerixibat and placebo groups, respectively, the mean monthly itch score was 7.33 and 7.36 and the mean monthly sleep score was 6.29 and 6.33; 57% and 60% of patients had severe pruritus, 51% and 53% had an ALP level of < 1.67 × ULN, 96% and 98% were receiving concomitant UDCA and 19% and 25% were receiving concomitant fibrates [3, 8]. At the end of part B, patients were either followed-up for 2 weeks or enrolled in the linerixibat long-term safety and tolerability (LLSAT) study (NCT04167358) [3].

Phase II Studies

In adults with proven PBC and moderate-to-severe pruritus (≥ 4 on 0–10 NRS) participating in a randomized, placebo-controlled, multinational, phase IIb dose-ranging study (NCT02966834; GLIMMER) [19], there were no significant differences between any of the oral linerixibat groups and the placebo group in terms of the primary endpoint [LSM change from baseline in mean worst daily itch (MWDI) score at week 16] and no evidence of a dose-response relationship in the intent to treat (ITT) population. However, a dose-dependent reduction in this endpoint was seen in the per protocol analysis [19]. GLIMMER consisted of a 4-week single-blind period followed by a 12-week double-blind period and then another 4-week single-blind period. Patients received placebo during the two single-blind periods. At the end of the initial single-blind period (i.e. baseline), 147 patients with a MWDI NRS score of ≥ 3 were randomized to receive linerixibat (20 mg once daily, 90 mg once daily, 180 mg once daily, 40 mg twice daily or 90 mg twice daily; n = 16, 20, 22, 22 and 21, respectively) or placebo (n = 36) [19]. A 0–10 NRS [scores range from 0 (no itching) to 10 (worst imaginable itching)] was used to calculate the worst daily itch score (defined as the worst of two scores recorded in a day), the MWDI score (also known as the weekly score; defined as the average of the worst daily itch scores over 7 days) and the monthly itch score (defined as the worst MWDI score over 4 weeks) [19, 22]. In the ITT population, the LSM change from baseline at week 16 in MWDI score for all the linerixibat groups was ≥ 2 points [19]. However, the differences versus placebo were not significant, potentially due to a high placebo response. A post hoc analysis of data from the per-protocol population indicated a dose-response relationship between total daily linerixibat dose and LSM change from baseline at week 16 in MWDI score (p = 0.0542). Moreover, a post hoc analysis of data from the ITT population indicated significant differences between linerixibat 180 mg once daily (p = 0.0424), 40 mg twice daily (p = 0.0105) and 90 mg twice daily (p = 0.0370) and placebo in the LSM change from baseline in monthly itch score at week 16 [19]. The therapeutic response benefit of linerixibat over placebo was consistent across a subgroup of 38 Japanese patients [23]. GLIMMER had an adaptive design, which permitted the doses to be adjusted based on interim data at week 12 [19]. This resulted in recruitment to the linerixibat 20 mg once daily group ceasing and the introduction of a linerixibat 40 mg twice daily group.

In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre, phase IIa two-period crossover study (NCT01899703) [18], oral linerixibat significantly improved pruritus in 21 adults with proven or likely PBC and ongoing pruritus who were receiving concomitant UDCA. After linerixibat therapy, the LSM percentage changes from baseline in itch scores measured using a 0–10 NRS, the PBC-40 itch domain and the 5-D itch scale (all secondary endpoints) were − 57% (p < 0.0001), − 31% (p < 0.0001) and − 35% (p < 0.0001), respectively. The differences in these values were significantly greater with linerixibat than placebo in the NRS (− 23%; p = 0.037), PBC-40 itch domain (− 14%; p = 0.034) and 5-D itch scale (− 20%; p = 0.0045). Patients eligible for NCT01899703 were receiving stable doses of UDCA for > 8 weeks at the time of screening and had serum ALP levels of ≤ 10-fold the ULN. Following a 14-day open placebo run-in period, patients were randomized to receive oral linerixibat or placebo twice daily for 14 days and then crossed over to the alternate therapy for 14 days; there was no washout period between the treatment periods. Patients in the active treatment periods commenced linerixibat therapy at a dose of 45 mg twice daily for 3 days after which the dose was increased to 90 mg twice daily for days 4–14 [18].

Adverse Events

In adults with proven PBC and moderate-to-severe pruritus participating in part A of GLISTEN [3], adverse events (AEs) were reported in 92% of 119 oral linerixibat 40 mg twice daily recipients and 74% of 118 placebo recipients following 24 weeks’ therapy. As expected, the use of linerixibat was associated with diarrhoea, which occurred in 61% of patients (vs 18% of placebo recipients). Abdominal pain (18% vs 3% of patients), nausea (10% vs 9%), increased ALT levels (9% vs 3%), headache (8% vs 3%), increased AST levels (8% vs < 1%) and dyspepsia (8% vs < 1%) were the other most frequently reported AEs (incidence ≥ 8% in either group and occurring more frequently in the linerixibat group than the placebo group) [3].

Diarrhoea was reported as an adverse event of special interest (AESI) in 70 (59%) and 22 (19%) patients in the linerixibat and placebo groups [3]. However, most of these events were mild [occurring in 43 (61%) of 70 and 14 (64%) of 22 patients) or grade 1 [44 (63%) and 15 (68%)] in intensity. They resolved in 86% of 70 patients receiving linerixibat and 95% of 22 patients receiving placebo, with 44% and 23% of patients taking antidiarrhoeal medication. None of the diarrhoea events in either group were reported as serious or resulted in hospital admission. Elevated ALT levels reported as a liver event (i.e. they met liver monitoring or stopping criteria) were also considered AESIs and occurred in 9% and 3% of patients in the respective groups. Serious AEs were reported in 12% of linerixibat recipients and 3% of placebo recipients. They were reported in one patient each, and no discernible patterns were observed. Most of the serious AEs were assessed (by site investigators) as unrelated to the treatment. AEs leading to treatment discontinuation occurred in 14% and 5% of patients in the linerixibat and placebo groups, with diarrhoea, abdominal pain, increased ALT levels and increased AST levels the most common reasons for treatment discontinuation. Specifically, gastrointestinal AEs resulted in 7% and 1% of patients in the respective groups discontinuing therapy. AEs resulting in study withdrawal occurred in 3% and 2% of patients in the linerixibat and placebo groups. No deaths were reported during GLISTEN [3].

Ongoing Clinical Trials

A noncomparative multinational phase III study (NCT04167358; LLSAT) is currently underway to evaluate the long-term safety and tolerability of linerixibat in adults with cholestatic pruritus associated with PBC who participated in a previous linerixibat study [GLIMMER (LLSAT group 1) or GLISTEN (LLSAT group 2)].

Current Status

Linerixibat received its first approval on 17 March 2026 for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus associated with PBC in adult patients in the USA [8–10]. Subsequently, linerixibat was approved on 1 May 2026 in the UK for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus in adult patients with PBC [11, 12].
