Executive Summary
The topic area of smart charge management spans many different technologies and applications.
Throughout the course of this project, ABB and its partners have focused on advanced research applica-
tions for smart charge management, via technologies implemented across cloud and edge locations. In
the research phase of the project, applications such as dynamic pricing, bus charge planning, and fast and
slow demand response control were developed and implemented in a lab environment. On the hardware
side, the edge platform developed during this project was designed to provide localized and bulk grid
services by aggregating telemetry at a charging site and allowing control actions informed by utility smart
charge management signals such as hourly and day ahead forecasting.

Selected grid service functions such as peak load shaving, demand charge mitigation, and voltage support
were developed and demonstrated by the team members at the site or edge level where electric vehicle
supply equipment and other charging site devices will be connected to the grid. In support of this func-
tionality, the team members utilized and implemented dynamic DC charge point balancing, smart sequen-
tial charging, and smart energy profiles to coordinate individual and aggregate load at charging sites in
smaller scale, hardware in the loop demonstrations. Modeling and simulation work has been used to
develop advanced features such as price and load forecasting engines, reinforcement learning based
charging site control, and to support larger scale simulations of EV charging conditions across diverse sites.

In the demonstration phase of the project, members of the ABB, RMP, and USU teams participated in
workshops to clearly map out the solution architecture for the project including the telecommunication
requirements and integration plans for Open Automated Demand Response (OpenADR) 2.0b and Open
Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) 1.6. The project team focused on leveraging an OpenADR 2.0b aggregator
model to minimize the integration points and to meet typical utility cyber security requirements, as well
as an OCPP cloud server approach, which is typical for the EV industry.

As part of the demonstration phase, the team installed more than 200 Level 2 and DC fast chargers at
workplace and multi-unit dwelling sites to test and validate the developed applications. The RMP NTO
site (North Temple Office) is the RMP Headquarters, while the Packsize and Giv Group complexes are sites
with all public charging available. USU also installed more than 40 new EV chargers at their facility. The
tables below summarize the installations.

Project Objectives
ABB (Prime Contractor), in collaboration with its partners at the Utah State University (USU), Idaho Na-
tional Laboratory, Rocky Mountain Power (RMP), and Electric Power Engineers (EPE), have performed
research, development, and wide scale demonstration of a scalable and resilient Electrification Mosaic
(eMosaic) platform for Smart Charge Management (SCM) for Electric Vehicle Infrastructure. Work was
completed under DE EE0009194, titled “eMosaic Electrification Mosaic Platform for Grid Informed Smart
Charging Management”, funded by the US Department of Energy. The project members developed algo-
rithms that provide localized and bulk grid services and that reduce and stabilize costs all the way down
the supply chain to the PEV owner through SCM. This platform aggregates telemetry from multiple data
sources as pieces of the larger picture including personal, private fleet or transportation EVs, fast chargers
and other supply equipment, weather service information, and geographically distributed charging sites
such as public lots, garage and retail, and private or shared usage depots. ABB and the project team
designed, tested, and improved a charging management system at local/edge and cloud levels. The ulti-
mate objective of the project was to convincingly demonstrate that the developed secure eMosaic plat-
form can be readily and favorably adopted by diverse utilities and site owners at scale. This was achieved
through a demonstration plan with field deployment at several physical sites across 4 states and additional
scalable simulation from high fidelity charging models.

Objectives
The objective of the project was to research, develop, and demonstrate a scalable and resilient Electrifi-
cation Mosaic (eMosaic) platform to provide localized and bulk grid services and smart charge manage-
ment for a variety of charging destinations and load types.

Project Introduction
ABB and its partners have developed a control platform with edge and cloud components to enable con-
trol functions operating at both levels across single and multiple sites. The edge platform or secure gate-
way has been designed to provide localized and bulk grid services by aggregating telemetry at a charging
site and allowing control actions informed by utility smart charge management signals such as hourly and
day ahead forecasting. Selected grid service functions such as peak load shaving, demand charge mitiga-
tion, and voltage support have been developed and demonstrated by the team members at the site or
edge level where electric vehicle supply equipment and other charging site devices will be connected to
the grid. The team members have utilized and implemented dynamic DC charge point balancing, smart
sequential charging, and smart energy profiles to coordinate individual and aggregate load at charging
sites in smaller scale hardware in the loop demonstrations. Modeling and simulation work has been used
to develop advanced features such as price and load forecasting engines, reinforcement learning based
charging site control, and to support larger scale simulations of EV charging conditions across diverse sites.
ABB and its team members have taken a “security by design” approach to build and test cyber security
features in the eMosaic platform from the ground up to allow utilities to identify potential cyber events
based on present and forecasted usage. Project deliverables have been demonstrated in a wide scale
system demonstration focused on diverse charging sites identified during the project.

Details of Project Activities
The main deliverables for the eMosaic project consisted of the demonstration of the four use cases of
interest, as well as the implementation of the OpenADR [1] server to facilitate two of these use cases. The
use case implementations spanned from installed edge devices all the way up to algorithms deployed in
the cloud. Use Case 1 focused on a dynamic pricing algorithm implemented in the cloud that utilized data
from EV chargers installed at the USU EVR lab. Use Case 2 involved a bus charge planning optimization
algorithm, working in concert with UTA to monitor the state of charge of electric buses and other data to
take the necessary actions to charge the buses as required. Use Case 3 utilized the OpenADR [1] server to
facilitate direct load control commands to reduce the grid demand by disabling EV chargers across a work-
place and public charging sites. Use Case 4 focused on a “slow demand response” algorithm implemen-
tation where the grid demand determined the availability of EV chargers in the future, resulting in reduced
load demand over time. All of the use cases were significantly influenced by user behavior during charging
of EVs, resulting in closed loop feedback which provided vital lessons learned for the end of this project
and future projects going forward.

Use Case 1 – Dynamic Pricing

Use Case 1 demonstrates a dynamic pricing framework designed to guide EV user behavior and reduce
stress on the local power grid. Built around a reservation-based charging system, this approach lets drivers
schedule charging sessions through a custom app that displays dynamic price signals reflecting grid status
and electricity market conditions. The framework uses a reinforcement learning (RL) based price modula-
tion engine, which can influence user behavior by offering lower prices during times of lower demand and
higher prices when grid conditions are constrained. Through these behavioral nudges, the system aims to
align user charging patterns with grid needs, optimize cost efficiency, and help mitigate congestion im-
pacts.

 Figure 1: Optimization Sequence Diagram

The architecture features three integrated components:

 • Reinforcement Learning Agent (eMosaic Cloud): Modifies base energy prices using grid condi-
 tion feedback.
 • Reservation App: Developed with React, Express.js, and PostgreSQL, this app allows drivers to
 reserve time slots, choose between maximum and intelligent (cost optimized) charging, and
 view dynamic price forecasts (enhanced with LSTM based predictions and wholesale pricing
 data from CAISO).
 • Grid Simulation and Control Layer: Includes an OpenDSS [2] based grid model to simulate con-
 gestion and determine power caps, leveraging OpenADR [1] and OCPP [3] for site level and
 charger level power modulation.

 Figure 2: Reservation App Demonstration

The system allows real time telemetry, session forecasting, and price backed scheduling. The RL agent
operates in the cloud while local price responsive algorithms automatically adjust Level 2 (L2) charging
sessions, especially for long dwell scenarios. This is deployed at the Utah State University Electric Vehicle
and Roadway (EVR) facility, which features two ABB 175 kW DC fast chargers (DCFCs) and multiple L2
chargers.

A major technical achievement of Use Case 1 was the development of a fully interoperable, reservation
based EV charging system capable of operating across diverse hardware and communication protocols.
The architecture required extensive custom development to bridge layers of site wide energy manage-
ment and individual charger control, combining Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) [3] for individual
charger communication and OpenADR [1] for site level energy coordination. To ensure safe and reliable
communication across these channels, a dedicated firewall and communication security mechanism was
implemented, including careful handling of asynchronous control messages and fail safe fallbacks. This
security framework was essential for enabling real time price responsive control without exposing the
system to external cyber risks.

A key technical advancement involved augmenting existing chargers with extended OCPP [3] features that
were not natively implemented on the hardware side. These included support for dynamic charging pro-
files, intelligent power curtailment modes, and enhanced reservation state handling. This additional logic
was needed to support fine grained power modulation down to the individual connectors with intermit-
tent power fluctuations derived from changes in solar availability or price conditions.

The price forecasting component was anchored by a Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) model rather than
a reinforcement learning engine. This LSTM price predictor was trained using over four years of historical
California ISO (CAISO) data—including local node pricing, demand curves, and weather conditions specific
to the EVR area. The model demonstrated high predictive accuracy on holdout data representative of the
local feed, achieving low error and strong generalization performance, as discussed in recent technical
publications. This model provided users with dynamic, forward looking price signals within the reservation
app, empowering them to schedule charging sessions in alignment with both personal convenience and
grid friendly price windows.

Beyond these communication and forecasting advances, the system achieved operational milestones
through active coordination with the Utah State University Electric Vehicle and Roadway (EVR) Energy
Management System (EMS). By integrating telemetry with an operating envelope derived from OpenDSS
[2] based grid simulations, the system successfully limited feeder level voltage violations and reduced the
occurrence of line overloads in the local Green Canyon network. The approach proved resilient under
synthetic stress tests and real world traffic conditions, confirming the system’s ability to maintain power
quality even during peak aggregate load. Furthermore, session level load shaping on Level 2 chargers
proved highly effective in smoothing demand curves, minimizing transformer stress, and enabling better
alignment with renewable generation patterns. Collectively, these achievements highlight the robustness,
interoperability, and forward thinking architecture of the Use Case 1 deployment.

Use Case 2 – Bus Charge Planning

At the outset of the project, the team focused on optimizing the charging schedules for three New Flyer
buses operating in Salt Lake City, UT, while accounting for uncontrolled loads from the Traction Power
Substation (TPSS). Early findings demonstrated that the optimization process effectively creates charging
schedules that deliver significant energy and cost savings, often requiring only minor adjustments to
achieve optimal performance.

The team initially proposed leveraging machine learning techniques to tackle the problem and identified
comprehensive data collection as a critical component for success. However, challenges were identified
in modeling and actively managing the complex behaviors at the UTA Intermodal Hub. In particular, de-
fining and modeling the states and associated rewards within the reinforcement learning framework,
while adhering to both hard and soft constraints, proved to be a key challenge. These obstacles were
addressed by decomposing the problem into simplified steps, starting with the most crucial aspects. The
strategy was to add additional variables into the optimization model, thereby enhancing both the accu-
racy and the realism of the modeled site behavior over time.

The objective of the project was to maximize grid usage through sustained and even energy consumption,
lowering peaks and raising troughs, while also leveraging the supply/demand/price forecast generated by
ABB through reinforcement learning for general grid utilization. Key actors in this endeavor included fixed
scheduled loads, such as battery electric buses whose schedules the team controlled; schedulable loads
like fleet vehicles (the “uber” agent) with complex tasks that could be addressed in various ways; external
loads, including the train (TPSS) and EV charging stations, for which historical usage and dispersion data
were available; and distributed energy resources (DERs), such as solar panels and storage systems, which
provided short term load balancing.

The proposed planners used a two-tier architecture designed to address different time horizons and res-
olutions by pushing decisions “to the edge” – setting overall goals and limits while allowing independent
decision makers to act using higher fidelity models. The peak load scheduling tier was designed to develop
a 24-hour plan that updates hourly. This schedule was proposed to use both expected and historical data
as well as incorporate the supply/demand/price forecast, thereby balancing both spatial and temporal
considerations. The peak load scheduling tier was designed to account for fixed and schedulable loads by
integrating historical information for external loads and was proposed to use integer programming and
metaheuristic approaches for its optimization. The demand cost scheduling tier, on the other hand, was
designed to generate a 1-to-2-hour plan that updates every minute to reflect real time peak load utiliza-
tion and vehicle charge constraints.

The flow of information is as follows: the pricing mechanism feeds directly into the peak load scheduling
tier, whose plan is then passed to the demand cost scheduling tier along with historical data and current
interface feedback. The demand plan is subsequently forwarded to the hardware such as buses and
chargers. The planner repeats this planning cycle at the fastest possible rate. Data requirements for peak
planning include the initial state of charge (SOC), SOC model inputs like temperature and terrain effects,
fixed schedules incorporating station and depot availability, as well as historical/forecasted data, TPSS
information, weather conditions, and the to date maximum power usage used for calculating the P 15.
For the more rapid planning levels, the system also requires real time data such as expected SOC, battery
temperature, additional SOC model inputs, expected arrival times, and current load usage from buses,
fleets, EVs, and TPSS.

This section outlines the efforts of scheduling the charging sessions of electric buses in a way that reduces
costs and increases grid utilization. Several technical achievements were made in the form of various
planners, the process of collecting data, and various theoretical and real life results. The most notable
approaches to planning include the bin packing approach, the network flow approach, the receding hori-
zon planner, and the rule based planner.

The Bin Packing Approach
The Bin Packing Approach is a strategy to minimize monthly energy costs for battery electric bus (BEB)
fleets, addressing challenges associated with time varying, uncontrolled loads on the power grid. This
strategy aims to optimize charging schedules while maintaining adherence to fixed transit schedules.
To minimize the monthly energy costs for BEB fleets, the authors propose casting the charging optimiza-
tion problem as a constrained bin packing problem solved using a mixed integer linear program (MILP).
Key aspects of the approach include (1) Incorporating time of use (TOU) tariffs and demand charges into
a unified cost function, (2) Balancing energy demands from BEBs with uncontrolled grid loads to extend
infrastructure lifespan, and (3) Delivering precise charging schedules while reducing computational com-
plexity.

The Network Flow Approach
The Network Flow approach introduces a scheduling framework to optimize battery charging for electric
bus fleets, addressing the challenges posed by route timing constraints and limited battery charges. The
method aims to minimize charging costs by balancing the use of slow and fast chargers.
The proposed solution formulates the scheduling problem as a network flow MILP. Key features include
(1) The use of a directed graph to model bus schedules and available charging times, (2) Integration of
TOU costs into charging plans to minimize expenses, and (3) Simultaneous optimization of charging sched-
ules and determination of the number of chargers required to meet battery state of charge thresholds.

The scheduling framework reduces charging costs by up to 20.9% compared to a threshold based plan
and by up to 12.3% compared to an optimal strategy that excludes TOU costs. By balancing slow and fast
charger usage, the method ensures cost efficiency while meeting stringent route schedules. Randomly
generated test cases with thirty buses demonstrated the practicality of the framework in real world fleet
management. The figure below shows an example of the charge vs time for each bus under two frame-
works: a strategy similar to the operators (left) and the TOU strategy (right). This example scenario used

Data Collection for Charge and Discharge Models
To develop accurate charge and discharge models, the team collected SOC and GPS data over several
months and interpolated these datasets to match their timesteps. The data for each bus on each day,
referred to as a block day, was stored in terms of block days. Given that block days with insufficient SOC
samples tended to register zero charging and discharging rates, these days were excluded from calcula-
tions. The team established that approximately 720 samples per day (or one sample every two minutes
on average) were required for reliable interpolation.

Additionally, since the SOC data was highly discretized and noisy, with occasional anomalous drops fol-
lowed by rapid recoveries that do not occur in reality, a median filter with a 10-minute window was ap-
plied to smooth out significant irregularities. Similarly, the GPS data also needed to meet the cutoff of
approximately 720 samples per block day to be deemed valid. The team matched the GPS data to specific
routes manually by converting the data into KML files and visually verifying it against route descriptions
from UTA’s KML files or website. This process was time consuming, as nearly 500 KML files from a month’s
worth of raw data for the Ogden routes had to be inspected, with many cases showing buses either miss-
ing data or remaining at the depot all day.

A script was developed to determine the general location of the bus on any given route. To account for
route specific variations in battery discharge, block days were grouped by route, and the team determined
bus positions by drawing a 250 yard radius around stations and depots (recording entries as arrivals) and
a 125 yard radius around terminals, with any bus outside these zones assumed to be on route. By inter-
polating the SOC data with these determined locations, the team successfully calculated depot and station
charging rates alongside discharge rates from the different routes.

The Receding Horizon Planner
The receding horizon planner is a receding horizon control strategy [6] for scheduling BEB charging, ad-
dressing challenges like operational stochasticity, timing constraints, and complex utility cost structures.
The method aims to reduce costs while ensuring reliable fleet operations.

The receding horizon approach enhances network flow based charge scheduling methods by (1) Including
both TOU consumption and demand costs in optimization, (2) Developing a discrete, time invariant,
piecewise linear model for nonlinear, variable rate charging, (3) Employing a hierarchical two stage plan-
ner, with a static long term plan supplemented by a receding horizon dynamic planner that reacts to real
time feedback, and (4) Using Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate performance against other planning
methods.

The receding horizon approach offers up to 52% cost savings over simpler methods and demonstrates
significant robustness compared to static, open loop strategies. Integration of variable rate charging im-
proves cost effectiveness and aligns closely with real world charging behaviors. Empirical results from
Utah Transit Authority deployment scenarios show the method’s scalability and effectiveness under sto-
chastic conditions.

This work is particularly suitable for transit agencies adopting BEBs, offering cost optimization and resili-
ence to uncertainties such as traffic delays and unpredictable discharges. Challenges include computa-
tional complexity and the need for further exploration of long-term feasibility across multiple days of
operation.

OpenADR Implementation

Key to provision of grid services by EV chargers is communication between the utility or grid operator and
the chargers themselves. In order to provide demand response, this project chose to use the Open Auto-
mated Demand Response (OpenADR) protocol due to its heavy adoption in the utility sector, built in se-
curity features, and ability to scale. On this project, use case 3 implements load dispatch events and use
case 4 implements load limit events, which are each defined event types within OpenADR.
OpenADR communication is handled by two types of nodes: virtual top nodes (VTNs) and virtual end
nodes (VENs) [1].

VTNs create demand response events and send them over OpenADR to the VENs. Upon receiving an
event, a VEN tells the VTN whether it will opt in or opt out, then implements the event on its on site
hardware if it opts in. Additionally, the VEN can send reports back to the VTN which contain various meas-
urements from hardware onsite [1].

To use OpenADR [1] to control EV charging, the team developed VTN software that generates events for
use cases 3 and 4. These events are received and implemented by a VEN which in turn controls the
chargers.

The VTN designed for this project has two main roles: event generation and data collection. For each use
case, the VTN will create a different type of event. These events can vary in duration and magnitude. Data
is collected from the VENs over OpenADR [1], and this data is collected in a database on the VTN device.
The database contains tables describing the VENs, report types, and report data.

To implement the goals of this project using OpenADR, USU developed its own OpenADR software, called
aspiredr. The open source OpenLEADR [7] package is used for the base OpenADR communication func-
tionality, which is used to create lightweight VTNs and VENs and initiate connection over the internet.
USU has made several key developments on top of the OpenLEADR software.

First, USU developed a database to accompany each VTN in order to store reports provided by each VEN.
The reports stored in each database provide historical data from each of the VENs. This data can be used
in real time to properly size demand response events to send to each VEN, or can be used for data analysis
and visualization after the fact.

Second, USU developed methods for generating events on a VTN to send to various VENs. The event gen-
erator is given certain parameters, like the frequency of event creation, and lead time. It is also given a
range of values to choose from. As a result, events are transmitted to the VEN(s) at a fixed frequency and
lead time, but the VTN picks a random event magnitude from within the provided range. This allows for
validation of the performance of each VEN at controlling chargers across a wide range of power levels.

OpenADR [1] can operate in a pull mode, in which each VEN polls the VTN to pull any new events that
might have been created, or in push mode, in which the VTN pushes events to each VEN as soon as they
are created. The open source OpenLEADR [7] software upon which USU based their OpenADR implemen-
tation only uses pull mode to date. However, PacifiCorp IT described pull mode as challenging for their
firewall, as they do not want so many polls from external VTNs. As a result, USU and PacifiCorp had to
transition OpenLEADR to use push mode. This was achieved using the OpenLEADR push mode python
package on GitHub [8]. However, maintenance for this package ended in 2023. USU, PacifiCorp and EPE
had to perform significant work to get the push mode to work. This involved creating a fork of the

 26

OpenLEADR push mode python package, removing the post logic of the VEN from the __init__ function
and placing it in its own asynchronous function, and integrating it with both OpenLEADR [7] and aspiredr.
After making these changes, USU and EPE successfully demonstrated the use of push mode over the open
internet between EPE’s VTN and USU’s VEN in Utah.

As part of this project, VTNs were deployed at USU, EPE, and PacifiCorp. The USU VTN was used to test
OpenADR [1] report and event handling with VENs at USU. By deploying the VTN and VEN both at USU, it
was possible to begin testing of the OpenADR [1] implementation without having to wait for firewall issues
to be resolved. After it was demonstrated that USU’s VENs could successfully register with USU’s VTN,
receive and implement events, and send reports, testing began with a VTN at EPE. This step required some
changes to EPE’s firewall. Specifically, a loopback rule was implemented between the internal VTN port
and the public facing port to keep the connection open once established. This takeaway proved valuable
in the implementation of the PacifiCorp VTN as well.

The role of each VEN differs depending on the use case. The Use Case 3 section of this report describes
how the VEN passes along load control events from OpenADR [1] to USU’s API to curtail EVSE charging
power. Similarly, the Use Case 4 section of this report describes how OpenADR [1] load limit events are
passed along to ABB’s optimizer to enforce the limits on the chargers.

Implementation of this use case has two primary communication legs. First, an OpenADR VTN generates
the event and communicates it to the VEN over the internet. This event contains details about when to
start and stop the curtailment, and by how many kilowatts. The OpenADR event makes no prescriptions
about which chargers to curtail to achieve this goal. The VEN then writes the details of the event to an API
endpoint, including the start time, end time, and desired kilowatts. The VEN can also send reports to the
VTN detailing measured values at the site [1].

Following receipt of the charging event at the VEN, event information is relayed to a controller which
works with granular charger state to determine the best optimization which meets requirements of the
curtailment event within boundaries set by the controller administrator. After the best curtailment is de-
termined on the macro level, charging events are created for and sent to individual chargers based on the
current load. After the initial curtailment event, the charger states and site loads are regularly reassessed,
and new profiles are generated which reflect any changes in state.

Charger state and control levers are managed using OCPP [3]. Chargers connect to an OCPP server, which
controls chargers and aggregates information, including charging events and state. Information is kept in
a database, which the controller reads from to make its calculations. Commands to the chargers also make
use of a secured API layer which enables the sending of profiles and commands directly to the chargers
on an individual basis. It is through this mechanism that the Use Case 3 controller can perform its calcula-
tions in response to the event generated by the VTN.

Testing was first performed using a VTN and VEN at USU in order to control a single DC fast charger at
USU’s EVR. Figure 13 shows the results of this testing. The blue line shows the charging power as reported
by the VEN to the VTN. The shaded red areas are when the direct load control events take place. When
the event begins, the charging power is curtailed by the amount prescribed in the OpenADR event [1].
This is a reduction below the pre-event baseline power. At the end of the event, this limit is released,
allowing the charger to return to the maximum power allowed by the vehicle and charger. When another
event begins, the power is reduced by a new amount from a new baseline.

Use Case 4 – Load Limit Setting

Operating the electrical grid with reliability and cost effectiveness involves multiple layers, typically
mapped to different timescales. While Use Case 3 addresses stability at a fast response, short duration
timescale, requiring precise control to reduce load by an exact desired amount, Use Case 4 focuses on
longer timescales, spanning several hours, where the precision of control actions is less critical for imme-
diate stability but still vital for overall grid management. Together, these two use cases highlight important
facets of demand response. Similar to Use Case 3, this scenario involves the utility sending an OpenADR
[1] signal to an eMosaic VEN. However, unlike Use Case 3, which focuses on active charging sessions, this
utility request applies to the total charging capacity. This means that even if the current load is low, the
utility can be assured that their total load will not exceed a pre-defined safe limit, especially if many charg-
ing sessions begin simultaneously during the event. This Use Case, termed slow demand response, enables
grid operators to proactively limit future load in anticipation of known events like maintenance downtime
or high wholesale prices. The eMosaic VEN, acting as the site controller, will receive these future load limit
events via OpenADR [1]. With knowledge of user reservations, vehicle types, and their starting and final
States of Charge (SOCs), the Model Predictive Control (MPC) optimizer within the eMosaic cloud can then
intelligently optimize the charging environment. This optimization involves setting appropriate power lim-
its for individual charging vehicles, ensuring the total charging remains below the utility defined limit, and
carefully staggering charging sessions over the event's duration (which can range from one to four hours)
to prevent excessive overlap while respecting users' charging needs. To achieve this, the MPC requires
information about both ongoing and future charging sessions, further integrating with a public facing res-
ervations app to enhance its predictive capabilities.

Summary and Conclusions
Lessons Learned Summary
There were several lessons learned throughout the project by the different teams, which have been out-
lined in this report. User behavior and technology acceptance was the key lesson learned, showing the
importance of introducing the technologies early to workplace and multi-unit dwelling users, and provid-
ing solutions with the greatest ease of use to facilitate acceptance. The Human-Machine Interface (HMI)
and other user interfaces are the key to gaining faster user acceptance with any technology. Other lessons
learned included providing adequate technical resources for any public-facing app or website that is de-
ployed for EV charging applications, providing feedback loops for frequent communications for deploy-
ment projects when dealing with end customers (e.g., RMP, UTA), and identifying key personnel for net-
working/cybersecurity configuration early on.

Future Research Activities
Through the course of the project, several areas were identified as potential topics for future research
related to smart charge management. The identified areas are shown in the list below.
• Technology implementation, acceptance, and user behavior
 • Driver: EVSE HMI research
 • Ease of use is the key for the HMIs
• Intelligent/automatic power sharing between chargers (workplace or multi-unit dwelling)
 • Workplace – consider longer charging periods and when the user will end charging
 • ISO 15118 implementation in multi-unit dwelling L2 chargers
• System/technology cost targets – keeping costs reasonable will be the key to proliferation of the
 technology in the future
• Cybersecurity considerations – always must be considered as this is a growing area of concern
• Demand response accuracy
 • Accuracy is currently limited by EVSE setting electrical current at discrete integer levels.
 • EX: VEN dispatches 10 kW curtailment to 3 chargers (3.33 kW per charger)
 • Best case: 3 kW is curtailed from each charger (3 * 0.33 kW = 0.99 kW of error)
 • Error propagates as EVSE and/or VEN count grows
• Computational complexity and battery health management for medium and heavy-duty (bus) DCFC
 depot charging optimization

Conclusions
The topic area of smart charge management spans many different technologies and applications.
Throughout the course of this project, ABB and its partners have focused on advanced research applica-
tions for smart charge management, via technologies implemented across cloud and edge locations. In
the research phase of the project, applications such as dynamic pricing, bus charge planning, and fast and
slow demand response control were developed and implemented in a lab environment. In the demon-
stration phase of the project, the team installed more than 200 Level 2 and DC fast chargers at workplace
and multi-unit dwelling sites to test and validate the developed applications. The result of the project was
a comprehensive cloud-edge platform with intelligence smart charge management applications, with suc-
cessful field demonstrations and valuable lessons learned for future projects.
