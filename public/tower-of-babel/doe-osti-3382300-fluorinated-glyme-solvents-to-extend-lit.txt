EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 This project investigated a number of partially fluorinated glymes (PFGs) as electrolyte
cosolvents to improve the performance of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries.
 A major issue in Li-S cells is the electrochemical reaction of sulfur in the cathode to form
lithium polysulfides (LPS) that dissolve in the electrolyte. Those LPS are electrochemically and
chemically reactive at the lithium anode, resulting in lithium sulfide deposition on the anode and
also electrochemical reaction at both the anode and cathode, leading to a “polysulfide shuttle” and
reduced coulombic efficiency (CE) and self-discharge of the cell. PFGs reduce the solubility of
LPS while maintaining good solubility of lithium salts such as LiTFSI. By adjusting the amount
of PFG as cosolvent in the electrolyte, we showed that the solubility of LPS in the electrolyte can
be tuned. (It is not desirable to completely eliminate LPS in the electrolyte, as they facilitate
electrochemical reaction of the electrically insulating S8 and Li2S within the cathode by shuttling
charge between them and the conductive carbon.)
 Another issue in Li-S cells is degradation of the Li anode over many cycles of stripping
(discharge) and plating (charge). We showed that PFGs have a beneficial effect on the physical
morphology of the Li anode, SEI formation, and the CE of a Li-Li cell.
 Among the many PFGs tested, we found the best performance from PFGs designated PFG2
and PFG5, and these two PFGs were thoroughly studied. A systematic coin-cell study of
electrolyte solvents of 90:10, 80:20, or 70:30 DME:PFG (DME = 1,2-dimethoxyethane) revealed
some systematic trends: a higher percentage of PFG solvent led to substantially longer cycle life,
but at the same time reduced specific capacity (mAh/g(S)) and cell capacity at higher rates. These
studies used LiFSI as the electrolyte salt, as it was found to extend cycle life compared to LiTFSI.
Finally, the addition of a small amount of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) to the electrolyte was found to be
beneficial. The overall optimal electrolyte solution was found to be 0.6 M LiFSI + 0.5 M LiNO3
in 75:5:20 DME/DOL/PFG (either PFG2 or PFG5).
 Multilayer pouch cells were fabricated with a nominal capacity of 3.3 Ah. We discovered
that the type of external compression fixture was critical to the proper performance of the cells,
and that Li-S cells did not function well in a constant-gap fixture and required a constant-pressure
fixture.
 The overall best-performing 3.3 Ah pouch cells used a cathode constructed from the
Navitas Gen 2 sulfur cathode active material and an aqueous binder system, a Li-Mg (90:10) alloy
anode, a simple porous polyethylene separator, and the optimal electrolyte described above: 0.6 M
LiFSI + 0.5 M LiNO3 in 75:5:20 DME/DOL/PFG (either PFG2 or PFG5). These cells cycled at
C/5 with a specific capacity of ~800 mAh/g(S) for over 120 cycles. Partly because the Li-Mg alloy
available was thicker than optimal (100 μm Li-Mg on each side of 10 μm Cu), these cells did not
have the highest gravimetric energy density among the ones we fabricated and tested. Similar cells
with a 50 μm Li on Cu anode had a gravimetric energy density of 215 Wh/kg.

Introduction
 Navitas Advanced Solutions Group, in collaboration with Argonne National Laboratory
(ANL), proposed to improve the performance of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries through the
addition of fluorinated glyme solvents to the battery electrolyte. An example of the chemical
structure of one such “partially fluorinated glyme”, PFG1, is given in Figure 1.

Figure 1. Molecular structure of partially fluorinated glyme PFG1.
 These PFGs are meant to serve several purposes in Li-S batteries. The first and most
important function of the PFG is to control the solubility of lithium polysulfides (Li 2S8, Li2S6,
Li2S4) in the electrolyte, as those species can migrate between the cathode and anode and undergo
electrochemical reactions at each electrode, allowing self-discharge of the battery and significantly
reducing coulombic efficiency. This is the “polysulfide shuttle effect” and is a major impediment
to the realization of Li-S batteries that are efficient and maintain a long cycle life and calendar life.
 While there are many solvents in which lithium polysulfides (Li2Sx) are insoluble, the
solvents used in the Li-S battery electrolyte must maintain solubility of the other ionic components
of the electrolyte solution, including the lithium salt such as LiTFSI and critical additives such as
lithium nitrate (LiNO3). We found that certain PFGs met this requirement of dissolving LiTFSI
(or other salts) while limiting the solubility of Li2Sx.
 The PFGs as electrolyte solvents or cosolvents have additional benefits in Li-S batteries.
One is that the fluorinated functionalities will be reactive with the lithium metal anode, producing
LiF and other species and contributing to the SEI on the anode. The SEI formation and re-
formation and repair is critical in any lithium metal battery, and particularly in Li-S batteries where
there are electrochemically reactive Li2Sx species in the electrolyte. Another positive aspect of the
PFGs is that, with sufficient fluorination, they will reduce the flammability of the electrolyte.
 Several (~15) PFGs and fluorinated ethers were evaluated as electrolyte cosolvents in Li-
S cells (coin cells, double-layer pouch (DLP) cells, and multilayer pouch (MLP) cells) produced
at Navitas and ANL. The results will be discussed in detail below. While the focus of this project
was the optimization of the electrolyte by the addition of PFGs, the other components of the cell,
in particular the sulfur cathode, evolved during the project. Therefore the multiple sulfur cathode
active materials and cathodes (including binder system) developed at Navitas will be discussed
first below.

Cathode Development
 Cathode development is not one of the official tasks of this project, but during the period
of performance of this grant, the Navitas sulfur cathode active material (CAM) was continuously
improved under support from other sources. They were incorporated into the cells made in this
project. While these changes in cathode during the project complicated the comparison among
different electrolyte formulations, the first priority was to fabricate high-performing cells, and the
improved cathodes were a critical factor in improving cell performance. Once the optimal cathode
was settled, a systematic study of electrolyte formulations were studied with that cathode (and
separator) to find the optimal cell.
 Navitas Gen 0 CAM. Navitas has patented1,2 sulfur CAMs wherein a porous form of a
metal nitride such as titanium nitride (TiN) was synthesized and then sulfur was melt-infused into
that porous metal nitride. This is analogous to the well-known method reported by Nazar in which
sulfur is melt-infused into a porous conducting carbon.3 The metal nitrides in the Navitas CAM
are electrically conductive and therefore function well as a support for the sulfur/lithium sulfide
while conducting electrons to the current collector. In addition, the metal nitride absorbs the
lithium polysulfides more strongly than carbon does, and thereby helps reduce lithium polysulfide
dissolution in the electrolyte and the resulting polysulfide shuttle effect. Finally, the metal nitride
can catalyze the sulfur-polysulfide redox reaction at the cathode. These effects are illustrated
schematically in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Polysulfide adsorption and redox catalysis by the metal nitride in the Navitas sulfur
CAM.
 Navitas Gen 1 CAM. A modified CAM was developed that took advantage of the
advantageous properties of the metal nitride illustrated in Figure 2, and also included high surface
area carbon. The Gen 1 CAM was fabricated by melt-infusing sulfur into a mixture of metal nitride
and a high surface area conductive carbon.
 One limitation of the Gen 1 CAM was imposed by the very high surface area of the
conductive carbon, which made the use of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as a binder very
difficult because N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) is the required solvent for slurries containing
PVDF binder. NMP has a high boiling point of 202°C and is adsorbed strongly on the carbon,
making its evaporation on our pilot-line coater difficult. That is particularly true for a sulfur-based

 5

cathode, where high temperatures on the coater can lead to sulfur sublimation out of the cathode.
For that reason, the sulfur CAM purchased from Arkema, described below, was studied.
 “Arkema CAM”. Navitas purchased a sulfur CAM produced by Arkema, which consisted
of 87.5% sulfur and 12.5% carbon nanotubes (CNTs) by mass. This material could be coated onto
a aluminum current collector foil on our pilot line coater with a PVDF binder in NMP solvent.
Because PVDF was the favored binder early in the project, this Arkema CAM was used in several
cathodes and cells early in the project.
 Navitas Gen 2 CAM. A CAM similar to the Gen 1 CAM was created by melt-infusing
sulfur into a mixture of metal nitride a different carbon support. This Gen 2 CAM was better
performing than the Gen 1 CAM in both specific capacity (mAh/g(S)) and cycle life. It could be
coated in a cathode with PVDF binder because the significantly lower surface area of this other
carbon, although the evaporation of NMP on the coater did often lead to a small amount of
sublimation of sulfur, and the harsh drying conditions would generally cause some cracking of the
cathode composite during drying (visible in SEM images).
 This Gen 2 CAM became the standard active material for the cathodes in the latter part of
this project. It was produced in 600 g batches at Navitas, which included the melt-infusion step,
planetary ball-milling, and sieving. One 600 g batch is shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3. A batch of Gen 2 sulfur CAM produced at Navitas.
 While it is possible to coat a cathode from the Gen 2 CAM with a PVDF/NMP binder, a
number of water-based binders were examined, as water has several advantages over NMP as a
slurry solvent. One advantage is the non-toxicity of water. Another significant advantage is the
lower boiling point of water compared to NMP (100°C vs 202°C), allowing much more gentle
electrode drying conditions when the cathode is coated. Of the aqueous binders tested, a
combination of an acrylonitrile-based copolymer latex (LA) and a slurry thickening agent (STA)
was found to be optimal. The chemical structure of acrylonitrile is shown in Figure 4. An example
of a slurry thickening agent (STA) is carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), with the structure shown in
Figure 5. While the STA partly acts as a binder, its main function is a slurry thickener. This allows
a slurry of the appropriate viscosity for coating to be created with a lower solids content. That
leads to a coated cathode with high porosity, which is critical for the cathode in Li-S batteries.

 The Gen 2 CAM combined with the aqueous binder system (along with additional
conductive carbon in the coating slurry) became our standard sulfur cathode. As shown in Figure

evidence of sulfur sublimation from the cathode, as evaporation of water is fairly mild. Coatings

Electrolyte and General Cell Development
 The focus of this project was the use of fluorinated glyme solvents in the electrolyte of
Li-S batteries to extend cycle life and generally improve performance. This section of the report
will focus on electrolyte development, including experiments that shed light on how the electrolyte
affects other components of the cell (e.g., the lithium anode). In addition, some
development/optimization of other components of the cell will be noted as overall cell performance
is improved.
 The first coin cells tested examined cathodes using the Gen 1 CAM and three different
cathode binders:
 Binder 1 – PVDF
 Binder 2 – PEO (polyethylene oxide)
 Binder 3 – HPC (hydroxypropyl cellulose)
 The electrolyte used was a standard “baseline” electrolyte solution of 1 M LiTFSI
(structure shown in Figure 7) in a 1:1 by volume mixture of the solvents 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and
dimethoxyethane (DME) (structures shown in Figure 8). This electrolyte had no added LiNO3. The
coin cells were tested at a simple C/10 charge and discharge rate. The cells with the PEO binder
cathode were the best performing, with their specific capacity (in mAh/g of sulfur in the cathode)
and coulombic efficiency (CE) shown in Figure 9. The specific capacity begins reasonably high,
at 900 mAh/g(S). (The theoretical specific capacity of sulfur is 1672 mAh/g, but sulfur cathodes
in Li-S batteries rarely approach that theoretical value.) However, the specific capacity fades
relatively quickly, and is below 600 mAh/g(S) by cycle 40. In addition, the CE of the cells are
below 90%, but this is unsurprising for cells that do not contain LiNO3 in the electrolyte. Without
LiNO3 a poor SEI is formed on the Li anode that does not effectively block polysulfides in the
electrolyte from interacting electrochemically with the Li anode (i.e., the polysulfide shuttle
occurs) and a poor CE results. Finally, it should be noted that the loading of this cathode is low, at
~1 mg(S)/cm2. It is significantly easier to obtain higher specific capacity (mAh/g(S)) in Li-S cells
with low sulfur loading on the cathode. Later in the project we use higher loading cathodes, 3.5-
4.0 mg(S)/cm2, that are necessary to obtain good energy density in the cells.

 First tests with PFG1. The first partially fluorinated glyme (PFG) was tested next. It was
1,2-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxy)ethane, which we have designated as PFG1, and has the structure
shown in Figure 1. PFG1 is a glyme because it contains two ethereal oxygens separated by two
methylene groups. DME is similarly a glyme, also known as monoglyme. So in these first
experiments, PFG1 was tested as a replacement for DME in the baseline electrolyte. In other
words, 1 M LiTFSI was dissolved in a 1:1 volume ratio of DOL + PFG1 (with no added LiNO3).
These coin cells used the cathode with PVDF binder. The comparison was made with baseline
electrolyte, baseline electrolyte with 3 wt% LiNO3, and the new PFG1-containing electrolyte, with
results shown in Figure 11. The cells with the PFG1 in the electrolyte are the best, by far, in
maintaining their specific capacity. That may be due to the PFG1 suppressing the solubility of the
lithium polysulfides, thus slowing the loss of sulfur from the cathode. In addition, the PFG1-
containing electrolyte has a CE that is significantly better than baseline electrolyte, ~86-90% CE
vs ~70-75% CE. That is an indication that PFG1 is contributing to the formation of a favorable
SEI on the lithium anode, though it is not as effective as the electrolyte containing LiNO 3. The
combination of both a PFG and LiNO3 in the electrolyte may be the most effective at forming an
SEI and maintaining good CE for long-term cycling.

 Solubility of LiTFSI in PFGs and other solvents. One requirement of the fluorinated
glyme and ether cosolvents is that they maintain good solubility of the lithium salt of the
electrolyte, e.g. LiTFSI. For that reason, solubility tests were performed on LiTFSI in the various
electrolyte solvents and cosolvents. The results of some semiquantitative tests are shown in
photographs in Figure 18. For the photos, an amount of LiTFSI was added to the solvent that would
give a concentration of either 1 M or 6 M if all of the salt dissolved. At 1 M, DME, DOL, and
PFG2 completely dissolve the salt, showing that the solubility is > 1 M. TTE, on the other hand,
dissolves very little of the LiTFSI. In addition, the PFG1 solvent leaves most of the LiTFSI
undissolved, indicating a solubility significantly less than 1 M. In the “6 M” solutions, we see that
PFG2 completely dissolves the LiTFSI, emphasizing the large difference in
coordinating/solubilizing ability of PFG1 and PFG2. DOL and DME do not quite dissolve all of

dissolve the LiTFSI or other lithium salts.

 Solubility of Li2S6 in PFGs and other solvents. A major component of the role of PFGs
and fluorinated ethers in this project is the reduction of the solubility of lithium polysulfides in the
battery electrolyte. The solubility of Li2S6 in a number of solvent mixtures was therefore tested.
Figure 19 shows images of Li2S6 placed in solvents such that the concentration would be 0.6 M if
all of the solid Li2S6 dissolved. The solubility is high in DME and DOL and low in PFG2 and TTE.
Table 2 lists the quantified solubilities of Li2S6 in solvents and solvent mixtures. The solubility
order of Li2S6 can be summarized as DME > DOL >> PFG2 > TTE. Thus PFG2 has the desirable
effect of decreasing lithium polysulfide solubility while maintaining LiTFSI solubility.

 A qualitative test of the solubility of Li2S8 in a number of solvents and solvent mixtures

PFG2, respectively, show that the solubility inhibition is PFG1 > PFG5 > PFG2. It will thus be
possible to tune the solubility of lithium polysulfides in the battery electrolyte by choosing the
identity of the PFG used and its percentage of the composition of the solvent along with DOL
and/or DME.
 Two of the milestones in this project concerned the reduction of lithium polysulfide
solubility in the electrolyte. Those were Milestone 6.1. Polysulfide dissolution reduction >70% of
baseline electrolyte, and Milestone 6.2. Polysulfide dissolution reduction >90% of baseline
electrolyte. It is now evident that it is trivially easy to achieve those milestones. In Figure 19, for
example, Li2S6 has negligible solubility in PFG2. It would be even lower in PFG1 (as the results
in Figure 20 demonstrate). Those milestones have been achieved.
 However, much more important for functional cells is tuning the degree of lithium
polysulfide solubility. If too much lithium polysulfides dissolve in the electrolyte, there will be a
large polysulfide shuttling effect, where the lithium polysulfides diffuse to the anode and are
reduced there, reducing coulombic efficiency and effectively acting as a slow short-circuit in the
cell. But some polysulfide solubility is necessary to act as a catalyst for S8 reduction and Li2S
oxidation at the cathode, since they are insoluble in the electrolyte and electrically insulating and
cannot be efficiently electrochemically active on their own. It is possible to tune the lithium
polysulfide solubility by adjusting the proportion of PFG in the electrolyte such that the cathode
reactions can be effectively catalyzed while the polysulfide shuttle to the anode does not have
significant detrimental effects on the cell performance. The solubility studies above guided us in
the search for the best-performing solvent/PFG mixtures for the electrolyte.

 19F NMR and Raman studies. 19F NMR and Raman studies of LiTFSI solutions lend

further insight into the solvating power of the various fluorinated and nonfluorinated solvents. In
Figure 22 the 19F NMR spectra of 1 M solutions of LiTFSI in DME, DME/DOL (1:1), DOL,
DOL/:PFG2 (8:2), PFG2, and DOL/TTE (8:2) are shown. The 19F chemical shifts are furthest
downfield for DME, indicating the Li+ and TFSI- are present mainly as solvent-separated ion pairs,
which implies that DME is the solvent that most strongly coordinates Li+. The DOL-TTE mixture
shows the furthest upfield shifts, indicative of Li+-TFSI- ion pairs and aggregates, indicating that
TTE is the weakest coordinating solvent toward Li+ among the solvents studied. Overall, the order
of coordinating/solvating power (and therefore generally the order of solubility of LiTFSI) is:
 DME >> DOL ~ PFG2 > TTE
 Table 4 lists the Donor Numbers and 19F chemical shifts of solutions of LiTFSI in DME,
DOL, PFG1, and PFG2. From these chemical shifts we see the agreement with the trend above for
donor ability of the solvents, DME >> DOL ~ PFG2. In addition, we see that PFG1 is a
significantly weaker donor than PFG2. That is consistent with the very high solubility of LiTFSI
in PFG2, in contrast with its low solubility in PFG1, as illustrated in Figure 18.
 Raman spectra of 1 M solutions of LiTFSI in DME and DOL give a related but somewhat
different picture of the solvation. The main difference is the presence of solvent-separated ion pairs
(SSIPs), which are present in both the DME and DME/DOL (1:1) solutions, but absent in neat
DOL. This indicates that DOL is not coordinating enough toward Li+ to create SSIPs, but the
presence of DME, even at 50% concentration, will coordinate Li+ to create SSIPs. In neat DOL,
there is an increase in concentration of multi-ion aggregates in place of the SSIPs.

candidate PFGs, PFG1, PFG2, and PFG5 were tested. As shown in Figure 24, PFG1 is not
flammable, while PFG2 and PFG5 are flammable. While this is informative, we did not limit
ourselves to the use of only nonflammable PFGs in this project, as optimal cell performance is
ultimately the main goal.

were not particularly promising, and the cells did not perform well at higher rates. An example is
shown in Figure 38. Here the electrolyte is 0.6 M LiTFSI + 0.5 M LiNO3 in DME:DOL:PFG5
(45:45:10). The rate capability was tested from C/20 up to 1C, followed by 3 cycles of C/10
recovery, followed by cycling at C/3. One cell dropped to very low capacity at C/5 and the other
two dropped to low capacity at C/2.25.
 That large step change in capacity is caused by the cells during discharge reaching the
discharge cutoff voltage before the “second plateau” of the discharge curve. A normal discharge
curve can be seen in Figure 29, right, in the purple discharge curve. Generally about ¼ of the
discharge capacity happens between 2.4 to 2.1 V, and the remaining ¾ is discharged in a relatively
flat plateau at ~2.1 V. That first ¼ is generally understood to be the conversion of S8 to soluble
polysulfides such as Li2S4, and then the second plateau is the conversion of soluble polysulfides
to insoluble Li2S2 and Li2S on the cathode. Sometimes due to the slow kinetics of nucleation of
Li2S2 and/or Li2S in the cathode, there is an “overpotential” (here a dip to a lower potential) at the
start of the second plateau. Examples of this are shown in a charge-discharge curve in Figure 39.
In this cell the endpoint for a discharge half-cycle was set to 1.8 V. As labeled in the Figure, in the
first discharge shown the voltage does not reach 1.8 V at the start of the second plateau, and the
discharge continues through the second plateau and the normal capacity of that cell. However, in
the third discharge shown in Figure 39, the overpotential at the beginning of the second plateau
causes the voltage to reach 1.8 V and the battery cycle reverses to charging, and the capacity of
the second plateau is not used. This behavior is the cause of the large changes in capacity seen in

 28

Figure 38. The low capacity cycles happen at high C-rates when the kinetics of nucleation are slow
compared to the rate of discharge.

 The color-coded cells in Tables 5 and 6 make some trends clearly evident. First, at higher
rates the specific capacity drops, as expected. There is sometimes a dramatic shift from high to
low capacity, as in the 20% and 30% PFG5 entries in Table 5, where the capacity has a precipitous
drop between C/5 and C/2.25. That is due to the discharge not continuing through the “second
plateau” of the discharge curve, the effect illustrated in Figure 39 above.
 A more general overall trend in both Tables is that higher concentrations of fluorinated
cosolvents decrease the solubility of the lithium polysulfides and thereby decrease the specific
capacity at all rates, but particularly at higher rates. A contrasting phenomenon is that higher
concentrations of fluorinated cosolvents increase cycle life. This can be seen in Table 6 for the
PFG5 cells, where the 10%, 20%, and 30% PFG5 cells have a C/3 cycle life of 245, 356, and 460
cycles. While this trend does not hold perfectly across every set of cells, it is generally true that
higher concentrations of fluorinated cosolvents increase cycle life.
 It is thus not possible to choose one optimal solvent system from those in Tables 5 and 6,
but there is instead a tradeoff between high capacity and rate capability (with low fluorinated
cosolvent concentration) on the one hand, and long cycle life (with high fluorinated cosolvent
concentration) on the other hand, with the choice of solvent system dependent on the desired
performance parameters. Overall the optimum for overall performance is ~20% of one of the PFGs
as a cosolvent.

 5% DOL-containing vs DOL-free solvent systems. As noted above, DOL will polymerize
in the presence of LiFSI. For that reason, when we chose LiFSI as our standard lithium salt, our
systematic test of electrolyte solvent systems (in Tables 5 and 6) included solvent systems with
DME and various proportions of a PFG, with no DOL present. When DOL is a large fraction of
the solvent and is polymerized, it will form a gel with reduced Li+ ion conductivity and reduced
cell performance. However, when DOL is present at a lower volume fraction like 5%, it will still
be polymerized by LiFSI but the poly-DOL will simply remain dissolved in the other solvents and
the electrolyte will remain a homogeneous liquid solution. And DOL may have beneficial effects
on the SEI formed at the anode and CEI at the cathode. We therefore decided to additionally
investigate electrolyte systems with 5% DOL.

Conclusions
 In this project a number of fluorinated or partially fluorinated glymes (PFGs) were
investigated as cosolvents in Li-S battery electrolytes to improve the cycle life and other
performance parameters of Li-S batteries. The solubilizing properties of the PFGs was studied,
including their ability to dissolve lithium salts like LiTFSI and also lithium polysulfides, and come
PFGs had the desirable property that they dissolved LiTFSI very well but limited solubility of
lithium polysulfides. Solutions of LiFSI in DME/PFG mixtures had high ionic conductivity of 8
mS/cm or more.
 Studies of Li-Li cells by the Aurbach method showed that PFGs as electrolyte cosolvents
improved CE of the cells. SEM images of Li anodes after cycling revealed that the presence of
PFG in the electrolyte led to more compact, less porous plating of lithium, which is less likely to
form dead (disconnected) lithium over the life of the cell.
 PFG2 and PFG5 were found to be the most promising PFGs, and a systematic study of
those two PFGs as cosolvents with DME were studied in coin cells. The solvent mixtures were
DME-PFG in 90:10, 80:20, or 70:30 ratios, the electrolyte salts were 0.6 M LiFSI + 0.5 M LiNO3,
and the anode was either lithium or a 90:10 Li:Mg alloy. That study revealed a consistent pattern
that more PFG led to a longer cell cycle life but with a decreased specific capacity and decreased
capability at high rates. The overall performance of PFG2 and PFG5 was fairly similar, and
performance of the Li:Mg alloy anode was slightly better than the simple Li anode.
 Many 3.3 Ah pouch cells were fabricated and tested (more than the 10x 2 Ah cells in
Milestone 8.5). One important practical conclusion from these studies is that a simple constant-
gap external pressure fixture (left in Figure 46) does not work well for our Li-S cells, and leads to
low and slowly increasing specific capacity, as in Figure 52. While such a constant-gap fixture can

 43

work well for regular lithium ion batteries, the significant expansion and contraction of both the
anode and cathode during cycling of a Li-S cell require a constant-pressure fixture (right in Figure
46) to function properly.
 The fully optimized pouch cell system is either RF VTO 029 or RF VTO 030, which had
similar performance (cycling results given in Figure 53). Cell VTO 029 contained PFG5 and cell
VTO 030 contained PFG2, but were otherwise identical. So the overall optimal system is the
Navitas Gen 2 sulfur cathode (with aqueous binder system), a lithium-magnesium (90:10) alloy
anode, a simple polyethylene separator, and an electrolyte of 0.6 M LiFSI + 0.5 M LiNO3 in
75:5:20 DME/DOL/PFG (either PFG2 or PFG5). These cells cycled steadily at C/5 with a specific
capacity of ~800 mAh/g(S) for over 120 cycles. The gravimetric energy density of another cell,
VTO 027, was somewhat higher at 215 Wh/kg, partly due to the thinner 50 μm Li anode that was
not available for the 100 μm Li-Mg anode. Nevertheless, the Li-Mg anode of cells VTO 029 and
030 led to a longer cycle life and was judged to be the optimal anode and system overall.
 While our Li-S cells did not reach the ultimate goals of 400 Wh/kg and 600 cycles, 3 Ah
pouch cells with reasonable energy density (215 Wh/kg) and cycle life (120) were fabricated with
the challenging Li-S cell chemistry. An optimal electrolyte formulation was identified as 0.6 M
LiFSI + 0.5 M LiNO3 in 75:5:20 DME/DOL/PFG.
