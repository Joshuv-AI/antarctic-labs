Perspectives and challenges in implementing nursing-specific mini-CEX and DOPS: a descriptive qualitative study of clinical nursing educators and novice nurses in a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Shengxiao Nie, Shengmiao Ma, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the dual perspectives and challenges of clinical nursing educators and novice nurses regarding the implementation of nursing-specific mini-clinical evaluation exercise (mini-CEX) and direct observation of procedural skills (DOPS) in novice nurses’ training within a single tertiary hospital in China.DesignA descriptive qualitative research design was used. Participants were selected using a purposive sampling method, and semistructured interviews were conducted to collect data, which were then analysed following the six-step thematic analysis process.SettingThe study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in Beijing, China.ParticipantsA total of 24 participants were recruited from November to December 2023, comprising 13 clinical nursing educators and 11 novice nurses.ResultsFour themes emerged: (1) novice nurse growth: true reflection of novice nurses’ clinical nursing abilities through direct observation; tailored learning pathways for individual growth; immediate feedback and self-reflection-driven engagement for individualised learning advancement; visual analytics for identifying targeted improvement areas and tracking progress across clinical domains; (2) educator teaching quality: practical contents and flexible methods of evaluation; real-time teaching adaptation and refined teaching plans; educator-led multidimensional nursing competencies cultivation; (3) obstacles in implementing evaluation tools: time-consuming processes in assessment and manual documentation; insufficient and delayed feedback due to heavy clinical workload; interruption of implementation by significantly changed clinical situation and (4) factors affecting implementation efficacy: subjective differences in scoring and inconsistent evaluation standards; lack of specialty in assessment tools; teaching-related factors; novice nurses’ personal factors; patient-related factors; feedback-related factors.ConclusionsEducators and novice nurses perceived that the nursing-specific mini-CEX and DOPS have many advantages but raise concerns about time-consuming assessments, inadequate or delayed feedback, disruptions to implementation due to significant clinical changes, inconsistent standards and a lack of specialised assessment tools as well as multiple factors affecting its efficacy. Future research should focus on efficient strategies for optimal use.Keywords: Education, Medical; Nursing research; EDUCATION & TRAINING (see Medical Education & Training)

Introduction

Medical education has undergone significant changes over the decades. The approach to teaching and learning has shifted towards emphasising competencies and specific learning goals. However, traditional assessments (eg, written exams) assess only selected clinical knowledge and may not accurately reflect students’ clinical abilities. Evaluating students’ performance in clinical skills requires observing them in actual or simulated clinical scenarios.1 Direct observation of clinical skills is a key assessment strategy in competency-based medical education.2 It allows for assessing knowledge and skills during patient care.3 The feedback from this interaction significantly enhances students’ performance and skills.4

Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (mini-CEX) is a clinical skills assessment method developed by the American Board of Internal Medicine for graduate medical education.5 It involves direct observation of trainee-patient interactions to evaluate trainees’ clinical skills, attitudes and behaviours. Postassessment, trainees receive constructive feedback on their performance and areas for improvement.5 6 Due to varying competencies/practices (especially nurses’ caring role), we have revised the items and developed a nursing-specific version of mini-CEX. The final mini-CEX included seven dimensions: patient interviews, physical examinations, empathy, professionalism, clinical judgement, counselling and overall efficiency. After educator training, we implemented mini-CEX in novice nurse training. Educators scored novice nurses on a 9-point scale (1–3: unsatisfactory, 4–6: satisfactory, 7–9: excellent) for competent performance via bedside observation.

Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS), developed by the Royal College of Physicians, is a valuable tool for assessing procedural skills.7 8 DOPS involves direct observation of technical operations on patients with immediate feedback. To enhance the applicability of nursing, we also revised the DOPS form. The revised DOPS form includes eight aspects: knowledge, patient consent, technical competence, smooth techniques, seeking assistance, communication, humanitarianism and overall performance. Each aspect uses a 0–5 scale (0=0%, 5=100%). Both mini-CEX and DOPS forms include self-evaluation and evaluator comments as open questions.

Combining mini-CEX and DOPS can complement each other’s shortcomings and contribute to improving clinical skills. Several studies have demonstrated that this approach benefits nursing students’ clinical skills. For instance, Jasemi et al.’s randomised controlled trial (RCT) (108 nursing students) showed that DOPS and mini-CEX effectively improved learning ability and clinical skills and ultimately led to better patient care.9 Jafarpoor et al.’s quasi-experimental study (63 nursing students) showed that students evaluated by DOPS and mini-CEX had higher performance scores and satisfaction levels.10 Other studies reported similar findings.11 12

However, there is limited research on the perspectives of these tools in various medical specialties, including nursing. Some studies have explored trainees’ experiences and views. Cohen’s study collated the experiences of UK dermatology trainees and found the assessments time-consuming, difficult to organise and inducing stress and artificiality.13 Kipen et al. found that trainees viewed mini-CEX as a complex, interrelated cyclical process influenced by the supervisor at all stages.14 A qualitative synthesis of 12 articles identified multiple mini-CEX/DOPS implementation factors, including time and tool usability and user influences (supervisor/trainee knowledge, attitudes and perceptions).15

Existing studies have provided valuable insights into the objective impacts of mini-CEX and DOPS on clinical competence, but differ from subjective perceptions of supervisors or trainees. At our institute, nursing-specific mini-CEX and DOPS have been integral to novice training. In prejob training, specialised training will be organised on mini-CEX and DOPS for educators and novice nurses to ensure their mastery of usage, evaluation frequency and form norms. To understand implementation holistically, we conducted this descriptive qualitative study interviewing both educators and novice nurses to capture their perspectives, concerns, and expectations regarding the application of mini-CEX combined with DOPS to improve teaching and address literature gaps on isolated groups.

Methods

Design

This is a descriptive qualitative study.

Setting and participants

Participants were recruited via purposive sampling from a tertiary-level hospital in Beijing, China. This hospital serves as an affiliated teaching facility of a medical university and a modern public general hospital.

Inclusion criteria for educators: (1) 5 years’ experience in clinical nursing education or more; (2) college diploma or higher; (3) current teaching role and (4) using the mini-CEX combined with DOPS for novice training. Inclusion criteria for novice nurses: (1) college diploma or higher; (2) 6–12 months’ experience in clinical practice and (3) active engagement in clinical nursing duties during the study period.

Develop an outline for the interview

The semistructured interview outline was developed through a rigorous and iterative process to ensure its relevance, comprehensiveness, and alignment with the research objectives. Initially, an extensive literature review was conducted to identify key themes and gaps in clinical nursing formative evaluation. This was supplemented by a preliminary interview with a small group of clinical nursing educators to gather preliminary insights and refine the focus of the study.

To further enhance the validity and applicability of the interview outline, an expert panel was convened. The panel comprised one chief nurse with expertise in clinical nursing education, three associate chief nurses, and one charge nurse for novice nurse training programmes. The panel members reviewed the interview outline draft and engaged in a structured discussion to evaluate the clarity, coverage and logical flow of the questions. Based on their feedback, the outline was revised to clarify ambiguities, align with the study’s aims, and elicit rich data.

The interview outline for clinical nursing educators focused on four key areas: (1) effect of nursing-specific mini-CEX and DOPS; (2) feedback mechanisms, outcomes and factors; (3) implementation challenges of mini-CEX and DOPS; (4) overall satisfaction, recognition and suggestions.

The interview outline for novice nurses covered: (1) advantages and limitations of mini-CEX and DOPS; (2) feedback effectiveness and factors; (3) adherence to evaluation frequency and (4) overall satisfaction, recognition and recommendations.

The interview guides for clinical nursing educators and novice nurses were provided as onlinesupplemental material files 1 2, respectively.

Data collection

Data were collected from June to December 2023 through in-depth interviews. The interviewees were sent the semistructured interview guide by WeChat for preparation. Then, the researcher and the interviewee would reach an agreement on the time and place (usually in the conference room or the department office) for the interview. Written informed consent was obtained before the interview. The consent form detailed the study’s purpose, methods, potential risks, benefits, confidentiality measures and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences. The interviews were conducted face-to-face and tape-recorded with consent. The recordings were transcribed verbatim, while omitting names and identifiers to preserve anonymity within 24 hours after the interview. The interviewee’s expressions and actions were also recorded. Interviews lasted 20–40 min (average: 30.83±9.91 min).

The sample size for semistructured interviews was determined using the principle of data saturation. That is, when researchers find that subsequent interviews no longer yield new information or insights, they consider the information saturated and stop further data collection. In this study, saturation was monitored continuously throughout data collection and analysis. Saturation was considered achieved when two consecutive interviews yielded no new significant statements or codes, as determined jointly by the principal investigator and a second researcher. In our study, no new themes emerged after the 22nd interview. Two additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation, after which recruitment ceased.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was selected as the methodology for analysing qualitative data obtained from semistructured interviews, following a structured six-step process: Phase 1: familiarising oneself with data; phase 2: initial coding; phase 3: identification of potential themes; phase 4: refinement of themes; phase 5: definition and naming of themes and phase 6: report production (Braun and Clarke).16 The first author transcribed all interviews verbatim and read the transcripts multiple times to achieve data familiarisation and then created initial codes. These codes were categorised into subthemes and grouped into main themes. Next, the second author independently reviewed the themes, suggesting adjustments like merging/creating themes. The second and third authors refined themes through iterative discussions. Finally, the first author synthesised findings into a report, and all authors reviewed the final themes for coherence and representativeness.

Rigour and trustworthiness

To ensure interview quality, the first author, a nursing MD with qualitative research expertise, conducted all interviews. The interviews were conducted in a secluded setting without observers to ensure data accuracy. Additionally, the interviews were audio-recorded, and ambiguous responses were immediately clarified. All translated documents were confirmed by participants. To enhance credibility and confirmability, the second author independently reviewed the themes and suggested adjustments, followed by iterative discussions with the third author to refine the themes. We maintained an audit trail of research decisions, conducted peer debriefing through regular team meetings, and kept reflexive notes. Regarding researcher reflexivity, the first and third authors were responsible for the training of all novice nurses at the study hospital and served as supervisors for all participants. To minimise potential social desirability bias, participants were assured before each interview that their responses would not affect their evaluations and that honest feedback was encouraged.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

This study included 24 participants: 13 clinical nursing educators (E1–E13) and 11 novice nurses (N1–N11). The educators were all female, with a mean age of 35.77±3.59 years (range: 31–42 years) and a mean years of clinical experience of 12.23±4.75 years (range: 7–20 years). Regarding areas of practice: three worked in medical wards, four in surgical wards, three in the intensive care unit (ICU), two in the emergency department and one in the obstetrics ward. Novice nurses had a mean age of 23.82±1.54 years (range: 22–27 years), with 10 females (90.91%) and 1 male (9.09%). Regarding areas of practice: five worked in medical wards, four in surgical wards, one in the ICU and one in the emergency department. The participants’ demographics are summarised in table 1.

Themes and subthemes Identified from the Interviews

Theme 1: novice nurse growth

Subtheme 1: true reflection of novice nurses’ clinical nursing abilities through direct observation

All clinical educators believed that mini-CEX and DOPS provide a more accurate representation of novice nurses’ clinical nursing capabilities. These tools enable direct observation of novice nurses’ real-time performance in clinical settings, not just exam performance.

Currently, written examinations are the predominant method for assessing novice nurses’ clinical proficiency. However, many factors, such as stress, affect the scores. Moreover, some nurses have strong theoretical knowledge but weak clinical skills despite high written scores. For instance, they may demonstrate poor proficiency in basic procedures, such as venipuncture. Consequently, written examination scores fail to capture their actual clinical competence comprehensively . (E2)

Subtheme 2: tailored learning pathways for individual growth

Nursing managers specified the implementation frequency of mini-CEX and DOPS. Early training focuses on basic procedural skills and clinical practical abilities, while the focus of later training is on enhancing novice nurses’ comprehensive capabilities in providing all-round care for patients, facilitating their advancement towards higher-level professional development. Therefore, during the first 1–6 months after entry, novice nurses are required to complete ≥1 mini-CEX per 2 months and ≥2 DOPS per month. During months 7–12, they are required to carry out ≥1 mini-CEX and ≥1 DOPS per month.

Formative evaluation plays a critical guiding role in my professional growth. In early training, when I was not yet responsible for independent patient management, the educator emphasized the need to rapidly master foundational nursing skills and develop clinical competencies, including patient communication techniques, humanistic care principles, and clinical judgment abilities. Consequently, I consciously prioritized practical application and skill accumulation in these areas during daily clinical activities. With experience, I took on supervised direct care, raising competency expectations. Through a structured feedback mechanism, my educator identified my professional development gaps and provided targeted improvement strategies. This iterative process not only facilitated timely adjustments to my learning trajectory but also enabled stepwise enhancement of clinical capabilities, creating a virtuous cycle of “practice-feedback-refinement” that accelerated my professional maturation. (N3)

Subtheme 3: Immediate feedback and self-reflection-driven engagement for individualised learning advancement

Immediate feedback optimises learning via active, learner-centric engagement, shifting from passive to active participation. During feedback sessions, novice nurses are actively involved in self-reflection, identifying strengths and areas for improvement before receiving educator input. Educators then add their perspectives. This active participation will make the teacher’s guidance more effective and impressive.

Novice nurses, not educators, are the main body of the teaching process. Mini-CEX and DOPS require novice nurses to participate in the feedback. First, self-reflection on performance is more impactful than external feedback. This helps identify issues and focus on future errors. Without self-reflection, errors may recur despite educator feedback. Besides, feedback is one-to-one guidance. Finally, Involvement in plan development promotes future improvement. (E12)

Mini-CEX and DOPS have cultivated a habit of continuous self-reflection that significantly enhances my performance during surgical procedures. I now reflect not only postoperatively but also intraoperatively. For instance, if I falter at a step initially, I prioritize refinement in that phase later. Previously, I often overlooked clinical details, but my educator’s nuanced feedback—like highlighting subtle areas—has been invaluable to my learning. (N4)

Subtheme 4: visual analytics for identifying targeted improvement areas and tracking progress across clinical domains

A radar chart is a graphical method of displaying multivariate data in the form of a two-dimensional chart of three or more quantitative variables represented on axes starting from the same point.17 Post-feedback, educators are required to fill out radar charts showing the competency scores of novice nurses across various dimensions. These visualisations serve a dual purpose: helping nurses identify strengths and weaknesses. Also, as part of the mini-CEX and DOPS forms, the radar charts highlight specific areas for improvement.

The radar chart shows which aspects they do well and which they need improvement in. (E1)

She will have a more accurate understanding of herself with each evaluation. She knows her progress, shortcomings, and next focus. (E10)

Theme 2: educator teaching quality

Subtheme 1: practical contents and flexible methods of evaluation

Clinical educators value the flexibility of mini-CEX and DOPS, which enable the identification of learning gaps during patient care. By integrating assessments into daily workflows without advance preparation, educators can dynamically adjust teaching strategies to address emerging clinical challenges, enhancing teaching quality. One educator said, 'It is very convenient to conduct Mini-CEX and DOPS—no advance preparation needed. We can evaluate novice nurses anytime, patient-side. Besides, patients often present multiple issues, allowing for flexible and practical assessment content. Take a patient with a fever as an example, cause, assessment, and treatment can be evaluation content.' (E2)

Subtheme 2: real-time teaching adaptation and refining clinical nursing teaching plans

Mini-CEX and DOPS can help educators identify problems in the teaching process and improve their teaching plans in time. Through direct observation, teachers can accurately assess novice nurses’ understanding/mastering stage. Based on observations, educators can promptly adjust their teaching plan. As quoted by one educator, 'the areas where novice nurses perform poorly are also those where teaching needs strengthening. In the evaluation process, you can find learning and teaching issues for loop correction. This correction process is a spur to teaching.' (E8)

Subtheme 3: educator-led multi-dimensional nursing competencies cultivation

Mini-CEX and DOPS do not focus on improving a single ability but on core competence, thus indicating the cultivation direction for educators as assessment methods. As one educator quoted, 'Formative evaluation sets training goals for novice nurses and educators. That is very good. To be qualified, nurses need diverse abilities beyond rote memorization—they must apply knowledge clinically. For me, whatever I want them to develop into, I will cultivate in whichever aspect. (E1)

Theme 3: obstacles in implementation of evaluation tools

Subtheme 1: time-consuming processes in assessment and manual documentation

The evaluation and manual documentation procedures were identified as time-intensive tasks. This time-consuming nature is linked to factors such as the number of novice nurses and patients’ conditions. Four educators specifically highlighted the negative impact of time-consuming aspects on the implementation of mini-CEX and DOPS.

I work in the emergency room. There are several novice nurses in the emergency room. It will take a whole day to finish the formative evaluation of all the novice nurses. A teacher cannot do this alone for one day. However, summative evaluation can assess all novice nurses simultaneously and takes less time. (E1)

The ICU is particularly busy with frequent patient condition changes. Although observation takes~10 min and feedback~5 min, formative evaluation remains difficult due to patient, time, and workload constraints. Besides, manual documentation also takes several minutes. We often finish manuals after work hours. Mini-CEX and DOPS will increase one’s workload. (E3)

Subtheme 2: insufficient and delayed feedback due to heavy clinical workload

Five novice nurses perceived that feedback was occasionally inadequate or delayed, primarily attributed to the heavy clinical workload. As novice nurses stated,

Heavy clinical workload occasionally prevents dedicated time allocation for structured feedback. Although my educator tried alternative feedback (eg, WeChat or phone calls), these methods were less effective than face-to-face interactions. (N1)

I work in the operating room. I am often so busy that there is no time for feedback. If feedback is given later, the educator’s memory of the specific situation becomes hazy, and my recollection also fades. With high surgery volume, I may mix up other surgeries’ memories. Such delayed feedback is common and significantly harms learning outcomes. For example, I find it difficult to grasp the key points of surgical techniques and to thoroughly understand surgical procedures, hindering my professional growth. (N4)

Subtheme 3: interruption of implementation by significantly changed clinical situation

In the dynamic healthcare environment, drastically changing clinical scenarios can profoundly impact educators and novice nurses involved in evaluation. These complex clinical situations often bring high unpredictability.

The clinical situation changes significantly. Novice nurses cannot prepare in advance because they do not know what patient they will encounter or how conditions may change during the evaluation. Evaluation may be interrupted. For instance, unexpected critical emergencies may arise during assessment. Educators, also frontline healthcare providers, must prioritize urgent patient care over evaluation. This shift disrupts the flow of mini-CEX and DOPS, making comprehensive evaluation challenging. (E1)

Theme 4: factors affecting implementation efficacy

Subtheme 1: subjective differences in scoring and inconsistent evaluation standards

Ten participants believed the rating scores were somewhat subjective, stemming from varying educators and standards. Some comments were as follows:

Mini-CEX and DOPS have shortcomings. In evaluation, unclear criteria for differentiating score ranges (e.g., 4-6 vs. 7-9) make scoring accuracy hard, increasing subjectivity. (E5)

Novice nurses evaluated by different educators may have varied results due to varying standards, with some educators being more lenient. (N8)

Subtheme 2: lack of specialty in assessment tools

Four participants stated that both the mini-CEX and DOPS form are universal. However, there are significant differences across departments, which is quite challenging for educators. Some comments were as follows:

Many factors, such as respiratory tract infections, wound infections, urinary tract infections, and catheter-related infections, may cause cough or fever in ICU patients. However, for patients in general departments, it may be caused only by colds. Although the symptoms are the same, the reasons are quite different, but they cannot be reflected in the form. (N4)

Departments differ in features. There are differences in communication and other aspects between the ward and the outpatient department. There are also differences between surgical and medical departments. Medical patients suffer from chronic diseases with a long course, but the prognosis may not be good. Communication with such patients is challenging. Most surgical patients are discharged happily after surgery, so it may be relatively more straightforward to communicate with these patients. The situation is different in pediatrics because some children cannot express themselves well. These may cause errors in the evaluation results. (N5)

Subtheme 3: teaching-related factors

Teaching-related factors, including teaching proficiency, attention level, relationship with novice nurses, and patient care experience, significantly influence the evaluation of novice nurses. Some comments were as follows:

Evaluation effectiveness depends on proper implementation. If educators lack the awareness to implement it diligently, the evaluation effectiveness will inevitably be significantly affected. Moreover, educator competence varies. Some identify problems quickly; others do not, directly affecting the implementation effectiveness of the evaluation process. Additionally, the relationship with educators can also influence the evaluation results. Close ties may lead to higher scores from favoritism. (N3)

If I prioritize humanistic literacy and clinical communication skills, I will focus on this novice nurse’s communication and humanistic qualities. In contrast, another educator might focus on other areas where the novice nurse excels. This divergence in focus can influence the novice nurses’ growth. (E7)

Another factor is whether my educator and I have cared for this patient. For instance, I have cared for this patient, but my educator has not. In that case, she may not be well-informed about the patient’s condition. Under such circumstances, her evaluation of me may not be entirely accurate. (N7)

Subtheme 4: novice nurses’ personal factors

Novice nurses’ personal factors, such as psychological state and self-requirements, affect evaluation results. Some comments were as follows:

If novice nurses are overly nervous during assessment, it affects their performance. (N7)

If individuals set high standards for themselves and yearn for the educator’s approval, they will give special attention to the problems the educator has previously pointed out, thereby performing better. (N8)

Subtheme 5: patient-related factors

Patient-specific characteristics, including but not limited to their willingness to engage, are influential in evaluation settings. As one novice nurse stated, interaction with patients is crucial in evaluation. For example, when communicating with her, she may be extremely uncooperative, which can seriously impact evaluation. (N7)

Subtheme 6: feedback-related factors

Environment and feedback timing can significantly influence effectiveness.

In daily feedback, key factors affecting feedback effectiveness include time pressure or sudden interruptions from other tasks. Short or incomplete feedback fails to achieve desired results. Conversely, sufficient time allows educators to provide more comprehensive and detailed feedback. (N6)

After evaluation, the educator should provide me with improvement suggestions and mutually discuss privately. If the educator points out my shortcomings immediately after evaluation in front of the patient, I will feel quite embarrassed. This may, in turn, dampen my enthusiasm for learning and even make me feel apprehensive about future evaluations. Moreover, the timing of feedback is also of great importance. Whether the educator offers guidance promptly after the evaluation holds significant weight; if feedback is delayed for a while, the details of the areas where I did not perform well will likely be overlooked. (N7)

Discussion

Our study found that clinical educators and novice nurses highly appreciate mini-CEX and DOPS, consistent with previous research demonstrating that these tools enhance clinical competency and support teaching plan refinement.9 18 19 Unlike prior research that typically reported only one group’s views in isolation, we captured the dual perspectives of both educators and novice nurses and found that the benefits of formative assessment are recognised equally by both groups. Different from traditional summative assessments, mini-CEX and DOPS facilitate a shift from passive to active learning for novice nurses, helping them to identify the gaps in their knowledge, promote self-reflection, and motivate them to learn.4 20 This shift from passive to active learning aligns with self-determination theory, which emphasises that competence, autonomy and relatedness enhance intrinsic motivation.21 Learners benefit more from specific feedback than numerical scores, challenging to provide in unobserved student–patient encounters.22 23 Repeated evaluations ensure teaching plans alignment with student development traits. In addition, manual documentation allows educators to share feedback on novice nurses’ strengths and weaknesses. This allows more targeted guidance in subsequent training sessions. Finally, mini-CEX and DOPS require no additional preparation and can be conducted if the patient’s condition allows, making these tools feasible for routine clinical training.

We found time-consuming processes and a heavy clinical workload as the primary obstacles to implementing mini-CEX and DOPS. Similar to a previous study, a national survey of 216 nursing educators revealed that time constraints were the primary barrier (43.5%) to implementing effective formative assessment.24 This indicates that the time-pressure issue is not unique to our specific clinical context but represents a systemic challenge. The educational impact of mini-CEX and DOPS relies on having enough time for direct observation and feedback conversations. Even if supervisors are competent and motivated to use these tools, without sufficient time, they will not have the desired educational effect.15 What is more significant is that this time-pressure issue is not merely logistical but fundamentally threatens the pedagogical value of formative assessment, as rushed or omitted feedback defeats the core purpose of these tools. Furthermore, if there are many novice nurses, the educator’s workload would increase significantly. This highlights a structural tension between service delivery and educational responsibilities—a tension that is particularly acute in high-volume clinical settings like Chinese tertiary hospitals. The first step in improving the educational impact of mini-CEX and DOPS is to ensure appropriate implementation. Therefore, we recommend promoting the value of teaching and feedback, as supported by literature,25 and emphasising the importance of mini-CEX and DOPS for all stakeholders. Additionally, creating a culture that encourages interest in medical education and in taking responsibility for teaching and providing feedback is essential. To foster supervisors’ interest in medical education and enable them to actively engage in teaching and provide feedback, a corresponding career path might be beneficial.25 Finally, reducing educators’ workloads and implementing incentives to boost motivation when conducting mini-CEX and DOPS should be considered.

We identified several factors affecting the efficacy of mini-CEX and DOPS implementation. Similar to previous research, concerns have been raised regarding the subjective differences in scoring and inconsistent evaluation standards, which can compromise usefulness.126,28 This issue reveals a core challenge of competency-based assessment: balancing the need for standardisation to ensure consistency among raters with the need for contextualisation to allow flexibility across clinical situations. Therefore, providing uniform instructions to educators is recommended. For instance, specialists can develop grading standards and demonstration videos. Clinical educators can then watch these videos and simulate scoring. Subsequently, experts can guide educators in evaluating items and corresponding behaviours, detail each scoring item, and ensure mastery of mini-CEX and DOPS scoring methods. Additionally, scoring standards can be marked on the mini-CEX and DOPS form to ensure clear performance-score matching. Finally, we suggest a “cross” evaluation in which different educators evaluate one student to reduce subjectivity.

Our study revealed that the lack of specialty in assessment tools posed another significant challenge for clinical nursing educators. Although mini-CEX and DOPS have been implemented in various departments, including the emergency department, ICU, dialysis unit, medical and surgical ward, we must recognise that patient conditions vary across departments, leading to substantial differences in assessment focus. Our findings demonstrate that mini-CEX and DOPS are universal assessment tools but lack specificity. Specialised mini-CEX versions have been developed for occupational therapy29 and pharmacists.30 Thus, we propose department-specific tools to enhance the precision and effectiveness of clinical nursing education assessments.

The study also finds that teaching-related factors (including proficiency, attention, nurse relationships and patient care experience), novice nurses’ factors (psychological states, motivations), patient-related factors (cooperation) and feedback-related factors (environment, timing) affect the implementation effectiveness of mini-CEX and DOPS. While prior research by Lörwald et al. focused on user-related dimensions such as supervisors’ and trainees’ tool knowledge and attitudes,15 we identify novel dynamics: inconsistent criteria from educator differences, performance fluctuations from novice emotions, process disruptions from patient non-cooperation and reduced feedback efficacy due to time constraints or inappropriate settings. Crucially, our study identifies and integrates patient-related factors (eg, non-cooperation) and the hierarchical educator-novice relationship into this framework. These dimensions are highlighted by recent research as critical considerations. For instance, Tomas et al. found that the relationship between the students and supervisors could introduce subjectivity in the assessments.31 By examining these factors, we stress the need for standardised educator training to reduce bias, psychological support for novice motivation, proactive patient engagement and structured feedback protocols. These are crucial for optimising the implementation of mini-CEX/DOPS and advancing educational quality.

The positive perceptions and implementation challenges reported here must be interpreted within the specific context of a single tertiary hospital in China. Work culture in Chinese tertiary hospitals involves high patient volumes and heavy clinical workloads, which were identified in our study as primary barriers to implementation; in healthcare systems with lower patient-to-nurse ratios, the challenges of time constraints and delayed feedback might be less pronounced. Nursing training structures also vary internationally. In many hospitals in China, novice nurses undergo a standardised 3-year rotational training programme that provides a supportive framework for implementing mini-CEX and DOPS, whereas in systems where postgraduate training is less centralised or clinical supervision is structured differently, the same tools might encounter different barriers or require adaptation. Healthcare financing and staffing models influence the availability of dedicated educators. In our setting, educators had dual responsibilities for patient care and teaching without reduced clinical workloads, but in systems that explicitly resource teaching time, implementation efficacy might improve. Finally, feedback culture differs: in hierarchical or high-pressure clinical environments, facilitating open feedback conversations may be more challenging. Despite these contextual differences, several findings are transferable to other settings. The core principles of mini-CEX and DOPS—direct observation, immediate feedback and repeated evaluation cycles—are universally applicable. The barriers we identified likely resonate with clinical educators in other high-demand healthcare environments, and the strategies we proposed may be adaptable to other contexts.

Strengths and limitations

Our study offers several notable strengths. A key strength is its dual-perspective approach, which combines the views of clinical educators and novice nurses. It also covers multiple factors: tools, teaching, novices, patients and feedback. This multidimensional analysis enables systematic in-depth examination of factors affecting mini-CEX/DOPS effectiveness, providing a rich basis for subsequent improvements.

However, there are limitations. First, as a single-institutional study with limited clinical educators and novice nurses, the findings primarily reflect local perspectives and organisational culture and may not generalise to other settings with different cultural norms. Second, while examining multiple factors, some intangible elements, like the subtle influence of organisational culture and the long-term impact on novice nurses’ career development, were underconsidered. Third, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses, as the hierarchical relationship between clinical nursing educators (who held supervisory roles) and novice nurses could have led the latter to provide answers they perceived as expected rather than their true opinions. Additionally, patient-related factors focused on cooperation, overlooking aspects like underlying health conditions and cultural backgrounds.

Implications for practice

This study’s findings on implementing nursing-specific mini-CEX and DOPS offer crucial guidance for clinical practice. For novice development, direct observation should be prioritised for accurate clinical assessment, enabling tailored learning design. Immediate feedback and self-reflection can drive personalised learning, while visual analytics help identify areas for improvement and track progress. For educators, it is necessary to adjust teaching strategies in real-time and optimise teaching plans based on assessment results. Educators should also lead in cultivating multidimensional nursing competencies in novice nurses.

To overcome implementation obstacles, healthcare institutions need to address excessive time spent on documentation. Information technology can streamline processes, improve efficiency and ensure sufficient time for feedback. To address untimely feedback, feedback time should be reasonably arranged, and an effective feedback mechanism should be established. To improve implementation efficacy, standardising scoring and evaluation criteria through assessor training is essential to reduce subjectivity. Developing department-specific mini-CEX and DOPS can address the lack of specialty. Moreover, it is necessary to comprehensively consider teaching, novice nurses, patients and feedback factors to advance high-quality professional nursing assessment.

Conclusions

This study explored perspectives and challenges associated with the implementation of nursing-specific mini-CEX and DOPS. These tools benefit novices by accelerating growth and competence and educators by improving teaching quality. However, challenges such as time-consuming assessments, inadequate/delayed feedback, clinical disruptions, inconsistent standards, gaps in specialised tools and efficacy factors exist. Future research should focus on efficient strategies and standardised criteria for optimal use.
