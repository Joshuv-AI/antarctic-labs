Multi-omics analysis identifies key genes and functional loci affecting teat number in American Large White and Landrace pigs and their application in optimizing genomic selection models
Authors: Jinfeng Ma, Xinjie Ai, Yun Hao, Yanzhen Yin, Qian Liu, Jianghui Yu, Jin Zhou, Fusheng Wang, Liang Shuai, Yikun Jin, Ruihua Huang, Qingbo Zhao, Pinghua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTeat number is a crucial economic trait in pigs. It directly affects the ability of sows to lactate, which in turn influences the survival and health of piglets. The teat number of French Large White pigs is close to 16, while the teat number of American Large White and Landrace pigs is about 14. In order to improve the teat number of American Landrace and Large White pigs through molecular approaches and precise breeding techniques, we genotyped 2,131 American Landrace and 4,564 American Large White with teat number phenotype using a 50 K SNP chip. Then, the SNP-chip data was imputed to the level of whole-genome sequencing (iWGS). Based on iWGS data, we conducted GWAS to identify novel, significant SNPs associated with teat number and to incorporate them into genomic selection.ResultsIn Landrace pigs, significant SNPs for TTN mapped to SSC2, SSC7, SSC8, and SSC14; the SSC8 and SSC14 effects are novel. LTN mapped to SSC7, RTN to SSC7 and SSC8. The lead SSC7 SNP explained 2.60% of TTN phenotypic variance. In Large White pigs, significant SNPs were detected on SSC7 and SSC10 for TTN; SSC7, SSC10, and SSC12 for LTN; and SSC7 and SSC10 for RTN. The most significant locus on SSC7 accounted for 2.99% of the phenotypic variance in TTN. Additionally, a multi-population meta-analysis detected significant novel SNPs for LTN on SSC1 and SSC8. By utilizing Bayesian fine mapping, the most precise QTL confidence interval on SSC7 for both TTN and RTN in Large White pigs was reduced to 40 kb. By integrating functional gene annotation with RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data from Erhualian and Bamaxiang pigs mammary placodes at embryonic day 26, we prioritized PTPN13, TRPV3, ZDHHC13, and BRD2 as novel candidate genes for teat number. We then incorporated the significant SNPs to GBLUP and benchmarked genomic-selection accuracy. In both breeds, fitting the top SNP as fixed maximized prediction for TTN and RTN, whereas treating all significant loci as an additional random effect optimized LTN.ConclusionsOur findings provide a theoretical basis for dissecting new key genes affecting teat number and for advancing molecular breeding of teat number in pigs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13050-w.

Background

Teat number is a critical trait in pigs because it influences their reproductive performance. Milk secreted from the teats provides nutrients and antibodies to piglets, thereby supporting their growth and determining their health at weaning [1]. Mean teat number varies both within and between breeds. For example, Dutch Large White pigs average 15.79 teats [2], and Korean Landrace pigs 15.55 [3]. In contrast, American Large White pigs have only 14.68 teats [4], whereas the American Landrace pigs examined here averaged 14.43 teats. Consequently, selective breeding is required to increase teat number. Teat number is generally considered a moderately to highly heritable trait; nevertheless, studies across diverse breeds report wide ranges of heritability estimates. Such fluctuation is mainly attributed to genetic background differences among populations and to environmental influences.

Currently, the Pig QTL database contains 2,396 QTLs associated with teat number. The most intensively studied region is located near the VRTN gene on SSC7. Numerous studies have shown that VRTN is significantly associated with teat number [5–8]. Fan demonstrated that two completely linked sites within VRTN significantly affect thoracic-vertebrae number [9]. Subsequently, Yang detected an insertion mutation in VRTN that influences teat number in Duroc, Landrace and Erhualian pigs [10]. Zhuang speculated that the impact of VRTN on teat number may reflect high linkage disequilibrium with the true causal mutation in this QTL region [11]. Furthermore, both Hong and Yang identified ABCD4, located adjacent to VRTN, as a candidate gene for total teat number [12, 13]. Ma identified multiple candidate genes associated with high teat number in Large White pigs by employing selection signature detection methods [14]. Nevertheless, the major genes and causal mutations on SSC7 that underlie teat number variation in American Large White and Landrace pigs remain elusive, and it is still unknown whether additional novel QTLs influence teat number in American Landrace and Large White pigs. Given that GWAS alone is insufficient to pinpoint the key genes regulating teat number, integrating fine-mapping with transcriptome data markedly improves the reliability of functional-gene discovery [15]. Liu integrated GWAS, meta-analysis, Bayesian fine-mapping and transcriptome data and proposed that OLFML2A regulates mammary placodes and thus influences teat number in Meishan and Erhualian pigs, making it the most probable causal gene underlying teat-number variation in these populations [16]. Using ATAC-seq and RNA-seq on mammary placodes collected at embryonic day 26 from Erhualian and Meishan pigs, Liu identified three key candidate genes that regulate teat number [17]. Regarding genomic selection, Lopes demonstrated that incorporating GWAS-significant loci as fixed effects in the model significantly improves the prediction accuracy of teat number [18]. Raymond found that incorporating GWAS-significant loci as an additional random effect in the GBLUP model enhances prediction accuracy [19].

In this study, leveraging whole-genome data from 4,564 American Large White and 2,131 Landrace pigs, we first identify QTLs influencing teat number. Bayesian fine-mapping, coupled with mammary-gland RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data, was then used to prioritize the most likely causal genes. Finally, the significant variants were incorporated into a GBLUP framework to enhance the prediction accuracy of genomic selection for this trait.

Materials and methods

Animals and phenotypic data

In this study, we collected ear tissue samples from 4,564 American Large White pigs and 2,131 Landrace pigs from the core farm of Jiangxi Jia Da Group Co., Ltd., where all were raised under uniform conditions. No animals were sacrificed or anesthetized in this study. We recorded the number of teats on the left and right sides of the piglets, with the combined total designated as the Total Teat Number (TTN).

Genotyping and quality control

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Megi Universal Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit. The extracted DNA was then quantified and assessed for quality using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, with acceptable samples meeting an OD260/280 range of 1.7 to 2.1 and a concentration of at least 50 ng/μL. Genotyping was performed using two high-throughput chips: the Compass Porcine 50 K Plus Breeding Beadchip (Tianjin, China) and the GenoBaits® Porcine 50 K Panel comprising 57,466 and 52,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), respectively. All SNPs positions were mapped to the pig reference genome, Sscrofa 11.1. Quality control of the genotyping data was performed using PLINK software (v1.9) [20] We excluded SNPs with call rates lower than 90%, as were as those with genotype-missing rates higher than 0.1 or a minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 0.05. For further analysis, only SNPs located on autosomal chromosomes were retained. Following quality control, the genotyping data from 3,464 Large White pigs (39,049 SNPs) and 1,483 Landrace pigs (38,216 SNPs) collected using the Compass Porcine 50 K Plus Breeding Beadchip were utilized for subsequent analysis. Additionally, data from 1,254 Large White pigs, comprising 42,890 SNPs, and 786 Landrace pigs, with 42,848 SNPs, obtained using the GenoBaits® Porcine 50 K Panel, were incorporated into the analysis.

Genotype imputation

The reference panel uses whole-genome resequencing data from 1,662 pigs, including data from 1,602 pigs representing various breeds within the PigGTEX project [22] and an additional 60 Suhuai pigs [23]. Haplotype phasing for the reference panel [5] and the imputation of SNP-chip data from each of the two platforms to whole-genome resolution [24] were performed separately using Beagle software v5.2 with the default parameters. SNPs were filtered out if their dosage R-squared (DR2) value was below 0.9 or their minor allele frequency (MAF) was lower than 0.05. After merging the imputed data from the two chips and conducting a rigorous quality control process, we retained a total of 4,246,754 SNPs from 2,131 Landrace pigs and 5,361,654 SNPs from 4,564 Large White pigs for further analysis.

Genome-wide association analysis

For the imputed SNP data of each population, GWAS analyses were performed for the LTN, RTN, and TTN using the single-marker associations of the LDAK software v5.2 [25]. Using the Bayesian framework CAVIARBF [28], we established the confidence intervals for QTLs identified from iWGS data. We designated all SNPs that showed a significant association (p < 0.01) within a 1-megabase region upstream and downstream of the lead SNP as candidate variant sets [29]. By combining the marginal association statistics obtained from GWAS with the linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns of the candidate variants, we pinpointed the smallest set of variants that included the causal variant with a 95% posterior probability.

Identify functional candidate genes for teat number

We integrated multiple databases to perform systematic functional annotation of genes residing within the QTL intervals identified by GWAS and Bayesian fine-mapping, conducted comprehensive literature searches via PubMed, and corroborated the findings with the PheWAS module of the PigGTEx portal. We further re-analyzed RNA-seq and ATAC-seq datasets of embryonic day 26 mammary placodes from high-teat Erhualian (n = 12 RNA-seq; n = 3 ATAC-seq) and low-teat Bamaxiang (n = 3 RNA-seq; n = 3 ATAC-seq) pigs [17] Read counts for candidate genes were converted to TPM to quantify expression, and chromatin accessibility in the surrounding regions was visualized with IGV.

The principal component analysis (PCA) results revealed a clear separation between the Large White and Landrace pig populations, with a slight separation observed within individuals of each breed (Figure S1).

Using iWGS data, separate GWAS analyses were performed on Large White and Landrace pigs for the traits of LTN, RTN, and TTN. The Manhattan plots for these traits are presented in Fig. 1, while the corresponding quantile–quantile (Q-Q) plots are shown in Figure S2. The genomic inflation factors (λ) ranged from 0.910 to 0.997. Based on iWGS data, we identified significant SNPs associated with teat number in Landrace pigs (Table S1). SNPs linked to LTN were identified on SSC7. Significant SNPs for RTN were detected on SSC7 and SSC8; For TTN, significant SNPs were located on SSC2, SSC7, SSC8, and SSC14. The most significant SNP for RTN was rs1108211168, located in the ABCD4 gene region, which explained 2.07% of the phenotypic variance (Table 3). Bayes fine mapping of teat number

We performed Bayesian fine mapping (BFM) using iWGS data to precisely locate the QTLs affecting the teat number trait. For the LTN in Landrace pigs, the 95% confidence interval of the QTL mapped by BFM was 97.08–97.62 Mb on SSC7. For RTN, the 95% confidence intervals of QTLs mapped by using BFM were 97.17–97.62 Mb on SSC7 and 132.12–132.66 Mb on SSC8, respectively. Additionally, the 95% confidence intervals of QTLs for TTN on SSC2, SSC7, SSC8, and SSC14 were 40.02–40.49 Mb, 97.17–97.62 Mb, 132.12–132.66 Mb, and 55.20–57.19 Mb, respectively (Figure S3, Table S4). Comparison with the Pig QTL Database revealed that SSC8:132.12–132.66 Mb and SSC14:55.20–57.19 Mb are novel QTL detected in this study.

For Large White pigs, the 95% confidence intervals of QTLs for left teat number were mapped to SSC7: 25.01 ~ 26.18 Mb, SSC7: 97.58–97.76 Mb, SSC10: 47.10–47.99 Mb, and SSC12: 49.40–50.40 Mb. The 95% confidence intervals of QTLs for right teat number were SSC7: 97.58–97.62 Mb and SSC10: 46.21–47.54 Mb. For total teat number, the 95% confidence intervals of QTLs were SSC7: 97.58–97.62 Mb and SSC10: 46.69–47.77 Mb (Figure S4, Table S4). Compared with the Pig QTL Database, the QTL on SSC7: 25.01–26.18 Mb was further refined to a narrower interval. Moreover, the QTL on chromosome 7 was narrowed down to SSC7:97.58–97.62 Mb, reducing the interval to 40 kb, which encompasses the ABCD4 and VRTN genes.

In the meta-analysis, the 95% confidence intervals of QTLs for left teat number were mapped to SSC1: 36.96–38.03 Mb, SSC7: 25.01 ~ 26.18 Mb, SSC7: 97.58–97.62 Mb, SSC8: 132.12–132.66 Mb, SSC10: 47.10–48.77 Mb, and SSC12: 49.40–50.56 Mb. Similarly, the 95% confidence intervals of QTLs for right teat number were SSC7: 97.58–97.62 Mb, SSC8: 132.12–132.66 Mb, and SSC10: 46.53–47.57 Mb. Additionally, the 95% confidence intervals of QTLs for total teat number were SSC7: 97.58–97.62 Mb, SSC8: 132.12–132.66 Mb, and SSC10: 46.53–48.14 Mb (Fig. 3, Table S4). Discussion

In this study, the heritability of large white pigs was estimated to be 0.15 for left teat number (LTN), 0.25 for right teat number (RTN), and 0.25 for total teat number (TTN). For Landrace pig samples, the heritability estimates obtained were 0.16 for LTN, 0.08 for RTN, and 0.13 for TTN. Our findings are in line with the heritability estimates for French Large White pigs reported by Lin: 0.17 ± 0.07 for TTN, 0.14 ± 0.06 for LTN, and 0.17 ± 0.07 for RTN [30]. Additionally, Fang reported a TTN heritability of 0.10 ± 0.03 in Danish Large White pigs [31]. Teat number is generally considered to have moderate heritability. Notably, the heritability of RTN in Landrace pigs (0.08) was considerably lower, suggesting that right teat number is under weaker genetic control in this breed. The differences in heritability values measured in this study and other reports may be attributed to breed, genetic background, or population size.

In American Large White and Landrace pigs, we conducted a GWAS and identified novel QTL for total teat number on SSC8 and SSC14 in Landrace. To increase power, we meta-analysed the GWAS summary statistics across both populations and detected additional genomic regions associated with left teat number on SSC1 and SSC8. To check whether the top SNPs associated with the teat number in this study match any previously identified QTLs, we compared the significant SNPs from this study with those listed in the Pig QTL database based on their locations. None of the five newly identified SNPs associated with teat number were found within any previously reported QTLs, as highlighted in bold in Tables 3 and 4. Estimating confidence intervals by SNP-based GWAS is limited as this method separately tests each SNP and does not consider the effect of linkage disequilibrium (LD) among nearby SNPs. To address this limitation, we applied Bayesian Fine-Mapping (BFM) using imputed whole-genome sequencing (iWGS) data. By integrating GWAS summary statistics with LD information, BFM elevates both the efficiency of QTL detection and the precision in identifying causal mutations [32]. This study used GWAS analysis to identify QTL intervals associated with the traits of left, right, and total teat numbers in Large White and Landrace pigs. For Landrace pigs, the QTL interval affecting TTN on SSC2 was refined to SSC2: 40.02–40.49 Mb. Comparison with the Pig QTL database revealed new QTLs associated with TTN on SSC8 (SSC8: 132.12–132.66 Mb) and SSC14 (SSC14: 55.20–57.19 Mb). Notably, the SSC8 QTL interval was also identified in the meta-analysis. In Large White pigs, the QTL interval associated with TTN on SSC7: 25.01–26.18 Mb was further narrowed; the meta-analysis additionally identified two novel QTLs at SSC1: 36.96–38.03 Mb and SSC8: 115.38–117.34 Mb.

To prioritize causal candidates, we performed an in-depth literature review of all genes within the teat-number QTL intervals refined by Bayesian fine-mapping. VRTN, ABCD4, BBOF1, COQ6, FAM161B, LIN52, LTBP2, and PTGR2 on SSC7, FRMD4A on SSC10, and UBE2G1 on SSC12 have all been previously reported as candidate genes for teat number [3, 5, 6, 33–35]. Zhuang suggested that its effect may reflect high linkage disequilibrium with the actual causal variant in the QTL, indicating that VRTN may not be the sole or primary gene influencing teat formation [11]. Guo further discovered that ABCD4 significantly inhibits the proliferation of mammary epithelial cells and induces apoptosis [36]. They proposed that ABCD4 could be a key gene in the regulation of mammary gland development during pregnancy and lactation, potentially through its effects on PRLR expression. Additionally, through GWAS, we identified four novel candidate genes associated with teat number: ZDHHC13 on SSC2, BRD2 on SSC7, PTPN13 on SSC8, and TRPV3 on SSC12.

Studies indicate that embryonic day 26 constitutes the critical window for porcine teat determination, during which the mammary line gives rise to mammary placodes that subsequently form mammary buds [17]. Leveraging ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data from Erhualian and Bamaxiang placodes, we profiled candidate genes and observed significantly higher chromatin accessibility and expression of TRPV3 and ZDHHC13 in Erhualian pigs than in Bamaxiang pigs; moreover, PheWAS analysis linked TRPV3 to teat number. Unfortunately, the ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data used in this study originated from Chinese indigenous pig breeds, whereas our study population comprises Western commercial pig breeds, which exhibit significant genomic and regulatory differences. Given the extreme scarcity of multi-omics data on porcine mammary placode development, this dataset is currently the only available high-quality reference, justifying its use in our analysis. Considering the potential for false-positive or false-negative functional inferences due to breed differences, the candidate gene functional conclusions drawn here should be considered exploratory and require independent validation in commercial breeds.

ZDHHC13 regulates the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling pathway and is crucial for the specifying germ layers during embryogenesis. During the period of differentiation and maintenance of the ectodermal and mesodermal lineages, ZDHHC13 depletion led to the neuralization of the embryonic ectoderm and the dorsalization of the mesoderm [37]. In addition, the BRD2 protein can specifically localize to the nuclei of mouse mammary epithelial (alveolar) cells. The mammary epithelial cells proliferate during pregnancy and exhibit higher levels of BRD2 mRNA expression than the fully expanded, secreting alveolar cells in the lactating gland. It is hypothesized that BRD2 may function as a coactivator for genes initiating apoptosis in mammary epithelial cells [38]. PTPN13 is located at the junction of cell migration and cell death in epithelial tissues, influencing cell growth on extracellular matrix components, invasiveness, and adhesiveness. Also, PTPN13 regulates the development and aggressiveness of breast tumors [39–41]. TRPV3 encodes the Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 3 protein, a non-selective cation channel that plays an essential role in epidermal regeneration via the EGFR signaling pathway. Activation of EGFR by the TRPV3 gene triggers the downstream PI3K/AKT pathway, enhancing skin homeostasis and regeneration through promoting cell proliferation, facilitation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and inhibition of apoptosis [42–44].

In Large White and Landrace populations, we leveraged imputed whole-genome sequence data and five-fold cross-validation to systematically evaluate the predictive performance of alternative genomic selection models for teat number by calculating prediction accuracy and bias. Studies demonstrate that imputation markedly enhances the capture of causal variants or markers in tight linkage with them, resulting in substantially improved genomic prediction accuracy [45]. Lopes incorporated GWAS-identified large-effect loci as fixed effects into GBLUP and found that this markedly improves the model’s predictive accuracy [18]. Raymond found that incorporating GWAS-significant loci as an additional random effect in the GBLUP model enhances prediction accuracy [19]. We further incorporated the most significant GWAS SNP or the lead SNP from each QTL region as fixed covariates into the GBLUP model. Both strategies improved prediction accuracy. The second strategy, which treats all lead SNP as fixed effects within GWAS-defined QTL regions, yielded the highest gain for TTN and RTN, whereas treating all significant SNPs as an additional random effect achieved the highest accuracy for LTN.

Conclusion

Integrating genome-wide association analysis with Bayesian fine mapping, we uncovered novel loci governing teat number in pigs. In Landrace pigs, two distinct QTLs emerged on SSC8 and SSC14. In Large White pigs, two QTLs on SSC7 were sharply delimited. Meta-analysis revealed two novel QTLs. PTPN13, TRPV3, ZDHHC13, and BRD2 have been identified as new functional candidate genes for teat number. In Large White and Landrace pigs, genomic improvement of TTN and RTN is best achieved by fitting the most significant SNP from each GWAS-defined QTL region as a fixed effect in GBLUP, whereas LTN is optimally modeled by including all significant loci as an additional random effect in GBLUP; this should therefore be adopted as the preferred strategy in practical genomic selection breeding.
