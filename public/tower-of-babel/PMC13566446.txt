PROS1 Cys475Tyr mutation associated with a type III protein S deficiency pattern
Authors: José A Mejias-Figueroa, Charlotte M Story, Michael B Streiff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 35-year-old woman presented with a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and was diagnosed with protein S deficiency. She was treated with parenteral anticoagulation before switching to a direct oral anticoagulant. She presented to our practice 5 years later, where testing was repeated, demonstrating a type III deficiency pattern and a variant of uncertain significance in PROS1: c.1424G>A; p.Cys475Tyr. This cysteine residue participates in a disulfide bond in the sex hormone-binding globulin-like domain, which interacts with C4b-binding protein and is involved in several antithrombotic roles of protein S. This case report and the biochemical context support its pathogenicity.Keywords: case report, PROS1 gene, protein S deficiency, thrombophilia, venous thromboembolism

Introduction

Protein S, named after Seattle, where it was discovered, is a vitamin K-dependent glycoprotein synthesized by the liver and serves as a critical regulator of hemostasis [1]. Protein S has several antithrombotic functions: serving as a cofactor for activated protein C (APC)-mediated inactivation of coagulation factors Va and VIIIa, promoting Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor inhibition of factor Xa, and directly binding to and inhibiting factors IXa and Xa [1–4]. Inherited protein S deficiency is an autosomal dominant thrombophilia most commonly caused by heterozygous variants in the PROS1 gene [5]. Protein S deficiency caused by homozygous and compound heterozygous defects has been described in newborns with severe purpura fulminans [6]. The PROS1 gene is located on chromosome 3 and spans over 80 kb with 15 exons. As of June 2025, 589 variants have been identified, primarily missense mutations, with 81 variants classified as pathogenic in ClinVar [7]. We report a rare PROS1 variant and its association with clinical protein S deficiency. The patient provided written informed consent and had the opportunity to review this manuscript before submission for publication.

Case

A 35-year-old woman of Korean descent presented to an outside hospital with right leg swelling, redness, and pain. She was found to have an extensive right common femoral vein and right popliteal vein deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and was started on parenteral anticoagulation. She had been on an estrogen-containing oral contraceptive (OCP) for more than 5 years and was taking no other medications. She had no recent illness, trauma, hospitalization, surgery, or significant travel. She had no personal history of recurrent pregnancy loss and no family history of thrombosis. The medical team sent a hypercoagulable workup, which included antithrombin, protein C activity, and protein S activity testing. Initial thrombophilia testing was normal except for low protein S activity (Table 1). Genetic testing for PROS1 was sent, and she was found to be heterozygous for a variant of uncertain significance. Her OCP was stopped, and she was switched to edoxaban 60 mg daily at discharge. After 6 months of anticoagulation, her physician reduced her edoxaban dose to 30 mg daily due to weight below 60 kg with a plan to continue indefinitely. She did well for 5 years, without recurrent thrombosis.

In 2023, she presented to our practice for management of anticoagulation during pregnancy. She was transitioned to prophylaxis-dose enoxaparin prepregnancy, continued during pregnancy, and her child was born full term without complications. She remained on prophylaxis-dose enoxaparin postpregnancy while breast-feeding. One year following delivery, her protein S testing was repeated due to confounders present during initial testing (acute thrombosis, recent estrogen OCP therapy). The medical record was incomplete, so in order to fully investigate the possible protein S deficiency, Protein S activity, as well as free and total protein S were collected simultaneously. A type III protein S deficiency pattern was demonstrated (Table 2). After breastfeeding, she was switched to apixaban with plan to continue indefinite anticoagulation. The patient received genetic counseling, and family testing was deferred due to a lack of family history and unclear actionability of results.

Discussion

Protein S is a vitamin K-dependent glycoprotein that serves as a crucial regulator of hemostasis. Normally, approximately 40% is found in free active form, and 60% is bound to C4b-binding protein (C4BP) [1]. Inherited protein S deficiency is an autosomal dominant thrombophilia caused by heterozygous variants in the PROS1 gene and is sub-classified into three types [1]. Accurate diagnosis of protein S deficiency is challenging due to the many preanalytical variables that can influence the protein S assays. Testing during acute thrombosis, pregnancy, use of estrogen-containing medications, liver disease, acute inflammation, use of vitamin K antagonists, and vitamin K deficiency can also yield falsely low values [8]. Importantly, a recent population-level study demonstrated that a genetic mutation in PROS1 is found in less than 10% of the general population with low protein S levels, indicating that many acquired, environmental, and trans-acting genetic factors may lead to low protein S in the absence of true PROS1 gene mutation [9]. In this case, the patient's initial testing occurred during an acute thrombotic episode while on an estrogen-containing OCP, both of which can lead to low protein S levels. Repeat testing postpartum, without these confounders, demonstrated a persistent type III protein S deficiency pattern. Notably, if the patient's subsequent testing did not include a Free Protein S antigen and Protein S Activity, it would not have been possible to detect this pattern. Genetic testing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation from a Cysteine to a Tyrosine at codon 475 in the PROS1 gene, a highly conserved region [3].

This specific mutation has been previously published, as described by Chan et al.in 2019 [10], who reported two patients with the same mutation, both of whom had DVTs (functional assays were not reported). The first study to report a mutation in this specific codon described a Cys475Arg mutation that led to a type I deficiency pattern in a patient with DVT and pulmonary embolism [11]. Prior studies have shown that the Cys475 in the PROS1 gene forms a disulfide bridge with Cys449 and is essential for maintaining the proper tertiary structure of protein S and the sex-hormone-binding globulin (SHBG)-like domain [5]. Protein S has two Laminin G domains (LG1 and LG2) comprising the SHBG-like domain [12]. The Cys475 and Cys449 disulfide bond participates in the positional relationship between these two LG domains [13]. A cysteine to tyrosine change in this codon should change the structural integrity of the SHBG-like domain, possibly leading to increased binding affinity to C4BP. An increased affinity between the SHBG-like domain and C4BP would explain the type III deficiency pattern observed. Other pathogenic mutations in this codon have been reported previously, primarily Cys475Phe, which is designated as pathogenic in ClinVar [5,10,13–15]. Another study has suggested that mutations in the SHBG-like domains increase the risk of thrombosis when compared to other domains [16].

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report a rare missense PROS1 gene variant that affects a highly conserved cysteine residue, which in this case is associated with type III protein S deficiency pattern and clinically significant venous thrombosis. This specific mutation has been reported only once previously in two patients with DVT; however, it has not been associated with a particular type of Protein S deficiency pattern before. Additionally, the biochemical context strongly suggests its pathogenicity. Our patient was successfully treated with low-molecular-weight heparin during pregnancy and the postpartum period. She remains on apixaban indefinitely for anticoagulation and has not had recurrent thrombosis. Recent studies have raised concern that severe forms of Protein S deficiency (activity <20%) may be at risk for direct oral anticoagulant failure [17]. If this patient's mutation only affects the finding to C4BP rather than impeding other Protein S functions, this may explain the successful use of edoxaban and apixaban in this case. Further studies of Protein S variants, particularly those involving the Cys475 codon, are needed to fully characterize the structural and functional implications of mutations at this site.
