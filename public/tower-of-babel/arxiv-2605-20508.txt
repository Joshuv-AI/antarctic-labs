Compensator-based inference for signal detection under unknown background
Aritra Banerjee, Sara Algeri

Abstract
The problem of detecting new signals in the presence of an unknown background is ubiquitous in scientific discoveries and is especially prominent in the physical sciences. Most solutions proposed thus far to address the problem focus on estimating the background distribution and using that estimate to infer the signal. By studying the geometry of the problem, this article demonstrates that estimating the background distribution is somewhat unnecessary for inferring the signal intensity. Instead, it suffices to estimate a single parameter, referred to as the compensator , to account for the incomplete knowledge on the background, substantially simplifying the problem’s complexity and enabling proper uncertainty propagation. Such a compensator is shown to govern the conservativeness of the inference, both in the proposed setup and in likelihood-based approaches.

Introduction

The discovery of new physics, whether it involves finding a new particle or detecting a new astrophysical phenomenon, often consists of searching for a signal of interest in the data recorded by detectors. While the data at hand are not guaranteed to contain such a signal, they typically collect signals from a wide variety of nuisance sources, known as
the background
. Hence, the problem of signal detection depends heavily on the knowledge available about the latter.

The true data distribution,
F
, with density
f
, can be expressed as a convex combination of the distribution of the signal of interest,
F_{s}
, with density
f_{s}
, and a background distribution
F_{b}
with density
f_{b}
, i.e.,

f(x;\eta)=\eta f_{s}(x)+(1-\eta)f_{b}(x);\hskip 5.0ptx\in\mathcal{X};\hskip 5.0pt\eta\in[0,1)

(1)

where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the search region, over which the recorded observations lie, and is assumed to be a compact set;
\eta
is the true proportion of signal events, i.e., the relative amount of data generated by the signal of interest. To exclude the case in which the experimental data contain no background events, which is unrealistic in practical applications, it is sensible to assume
\eta
to be bounded away from one.

The signal detection problem can be formulated as a statistical test for the hypotheses:

H_{0,\eta}:\eta=0\quad\text{vs}\quad H_{1,\eta}:\eta>0

(2)

in which null hypothesis,
H_{0,\eta}
, corresponds to the situation in which the observed data consists only of background events. Thus, rejecting this hypothesis would provide statistical evidence for the presence of the signal of interest.

In many instances, rather than recording a continuous stream of ‘unbinned’ data, the measurements obtained consist of counts observed over a large number of bins
[
5
, Cf.]
. In these settings, the data-generating process is usually modeled as a Poisson process with intensity function proportional to the density in (
1
)
[
17
,
15
, e.g.]
.
Thus, while the testing problem in (
2
) is also relevant in this setup, we focus here on the case where the data are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). An extension to the binned-data regime is pursued in
[
9
]
.

Usually, the form of the signal density,
f_{s}
, is assumed to be known. This assumption is realistic because physical theories extensively investigate the behavior of the source we aim to detect and can reliably model its distribution. Extensions to situations in which
f_{s}
is known only up to some free parameters are also possible and are discussed in Section
6
.
The background density,
f_{b}
, is often unknown as the multitude of sources contributing to it and their interactions are not well understood. This renders testing (
2
) especially challenging. In particular, overestimating the background in the region where the signal is expected to occur yields overly conservative inference. More critically, underestimating the background in the signal region may lead to false discoveries.

Most solutions proposed in the literature to address the problem rely on estimating
f_{b}
before proceeding to test (
2
). Such an estimate is obtained by considering a labeled dataset assumed not to contain any signal events, hereinafter referred to as the ‘background-only data’, and then used in place of
f_{b}
in (
1
) to test (
2
) via a likelihood ratio test (LRT). The latter is conducted on the unlabeled dataset of interest, hereinafter referred to as the ‘physics data’, generated by the mixture model in (
1
) and, therefore, may (if
\eta>0
) or may not (if
\eta=0
) contain the signal of interest.
For example,
[
4
]
describes a method that models the ratio of the true and postulated background densities semiparametrically using an orthonormal expansion estimated from background-only data.

[
23
]
employs such an approach, along with a correction for multiple testing, to detect one or more line emissions (the signal) in high-resolution X-ray spectra under high background.
Other methods use auxiliary events expected to behave similarly, but not exactly, as the background distribution of interest. A naive background estimate based on such data is then adequately corrected by estimating the density ratio between the true and auxiliary background distribution or via optimal transport
[
17
]
on data collected on ‘control regions’, i.e., regions of the support known not to contain signal events and acting as the background-only sample. The resulting estimate is then extrapolated to the ‘signal region’ in which the signal, if present, is expected to lie.
None of these methods, however, accounts for the uncertainties affecting the estimate of the background distribution when testing for the presence of the signal.

The methods proposed in
[
18
]
and
[
1
]
, introduced, respectively, by members of the dark matter community and the ATLAS experiment at CERN, focus on the construction of an estimate of
f_{b}
in which a signal-like component is introduced in order to ‘safeguard’ against false discoveries. However, as demonstrated in
[
19
]
with a rather compelling example (see also Sec
2.1
), this approach does not guarantee conservative inference and can still lead to false discoveries.
Moreover, such methods fail to address the issue of model misspecification under
H_{0,\eta}
, which naturally arises due to the signal-like component introduced in the background model. As a result, the usual
\bar{\chi}^{2}_{01}
approximation of the null distribution of the LRT statistic, arising when
\eta
is tested at the boundary of its parameter space, no longer applies, making the outcome of a signal search based on such a test unreliable.

An approach that uses only the physics dataset for testing (
2
) is the ‘discrete profiling method’ introduced by
[
15
]
as a part of the CMS experiment
[
11
]
at CERN. Given a predetermined pool of candidate background models, a discrete index is assigned to each of its elements, and for each of them, a profile negative log-likelihood curve is obtained with respect to the signal strength parameter. An envelope is then constructed around the corresponding minima with the goal of approximating the overall profile curve. The minimizer of this curve is reported as the final estimate of the signal strength. While avoiding the drawbacks of the ‘safeguard’-style methods, this method is also not exempt from other limitations. For example, when considering candidate background models with varying numbers of parameters, a ‘
p
-value correction’ is used to adjust for model complexity. The validity of such a correction, however, is only based on a heuristic argument. Moreover, it is unclear how to select the set of candidate background models from the infinite number of choices available.

This paper proposes a shift of perspective. In particular, it is shown that, when a background-only dataset is available, valid inference on
\eta
can be obtained even when the user only has access to a misspecified description of
f_{b}
. In particular, by studying the geometry of the problem, we demonstrate that estimating
f_{b}
is somewhat unnecessary. Instead, it is sufficient to estimate a single, identifiable parameter, hereafter referred to as
the compensator
(formally defined in Section
2
), to account for the incomplete knowledge of the background. This significantly reduces the complexity of the investigation as consistently estimating the compensator is much easier than consistently estimating
f_{b}
. Moreover, the uncertainties associated with the estimation of the compensator can be naturally accounted for when performing inference on
\eta
.
The compensator parameter is also shown to arise in LRT-based signal search methods and determines their degree of conservativeness under model misspecification.

In the absence of background-only data, the compensator is no longer identifiable. Nevertheless, our geometric study lays the groundwork for a sensitivity analysis that allows us to evaluate the degree of conservativeness of the proposed inferential approach and prevent false discoveries.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we describe the geometry of the problem at hand and discuss the shortcomings affecting LRT-based approaches. Section
3
introduces the proposed estimation and testing framework for
\eta
when a background-only sample is available to the user. Section
4
describes the proposed sensitivity analysis for the case in which a background-only dataset is not available. Section
5
demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed methodology using a realistic simulation of Fermi-Large Area Telescope data. Extensions are discussed in Section
6
. Technical proofs, additional figures are provided in the Appendix. The data and codes are collected in the Supplementary Material.

2
On the geometry of the problem

As discussed in Section
1
, a description of the background density
f_{b}
may only be approximately known or completely unknown. Begin by specifying a postulated background density
g
with distribution function
G
. Assume the latter shares the same support as
F_{s}
and
F_{b}
and
\frac{f_{s}}{g},\frac{f_{b}}{g}\in L^{2}(G)
.
The density
g
is not required to provide a close approximation of the true background density
f_{b}
: at least for the case in which a background-only sample is available to the user, valid inference on
\eta
can be obtained even when
g
is substantially different from
f_{b}
. The robustness of the method with respect to the choice of
g
is demonstrated in Sections
3
and
5
through practical examples.

To ease the intuition, begin with the case in which
g
is fully specified. The case where
g
depends on unknown parameters is discussed in Section
3
.
Consider an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{T}
for
L^{2}(G)
such that
\mathcal{T}=\{1,S^{\dagger},T_{1},T_{2},\dots\}
in which

\begin{split}S^{\dagger}=\frac{S}{\|S\|_{G}},&\quad S=\frac{f_{s}}{g}-1,\quad\|S\|_{G}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S^{2}(x)dG(x),\end{split}

(3)

and the remaining elements
\{T_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
are constructed by taking any orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(G)
, namely
\mathcal{T}^{*}
,
and orthonormalizing its elements with respect to
S^{\dagger}
via Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization so that

\begin{split}\langle T_{j},T_{j^{\prime}}\rangle_{G}&=\int_{\mathcal{X}}T_{j}(x)T_{j^{\prime}}(x)dG(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{j=j^{\prime}\}};\quad\langle T_{j},1\rangle_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}T_{j}(x)dG(x)=0\\
\langle T_{j},S^{\dagger}\rangle_{G}&=\int_{\mathcal{X}}T_{j}(x)S^{\dagger}(x)dG(x)=0;\quad\langle S^{\dagger},1\rangle_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S^{\dagger}(x)dG(x)=0\\
\end{split}

where
\mathds{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
is the indicator function. It is possible to show (see Appendix
B.1
) that if
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is complete then so is
\mathcal{T}
. Hence, we can expand any function in
L^{2}(G)
using
\mathcal{T}
.
In particular,
f_{b}
can be expressed as:

f_{b}(x)=g(x)\biggl[1+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\zeta_{j}T_{j}(x)+\delta S^{\dagger}(x)\biggl];

(4)

where the term in the square brackets is an orthonormal expansion of the density ratio
\frac{f_{b}}{g}
. The coefficients of this expansion specify as:

\zeta_{j}=\left\langle\frac{f_{b}}{g},T_{j}\right\rangle_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}T_{j}(x)dF_{b}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\delta=\left\langle\frac{f_{b}}{g},S^{\dagger}\right\rangle_{G}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S^{\dagger}(x)dF_{b}(x),

(5)

and we implicitly assume that the
\zeta_{j}
coefficients are suitably constrained to ensure (
4
) is non-negative.
The function
S^{\dagger}
is a normalized version of the score function for the density
f
in (
1
) evaluated at
\eta=0
, when
f_{b}\equiv g
. It describes the direction from which deviations of the postulated background toward the signal model occur. By ensuring its inclusion into the basis
\mathcal{T}
, such a function allows us to explicitly oversee how such deviations affect our inference not only on
f_{b}
but, most importantly, on
\eta
.

In particular, by plugging in (
4
) into (
1
) and rearranging the terms, we can express
f
as:

f(x)=g(x)\left[1+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\tau_{j}T_{j}(x)+\theta S^{\dagger}(x)\right]

(6)

where,

\tau_{j}=(1-\eta)\zeta_{j}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}T_{j}(x)dF(x),\hskip 10.0pt\theta=\eta\|S\|_{G}+(1-\eta)\delta=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S^{\dagger}(x)dF(x);

(7)

consequently,

\eta=\frac{\theta-\delta}{\|S\|_{G}-\delta}.

(8)

In (
6
), all
\tau_{j}
parameters and
\theta
are identifiable and from (
8
) it follows that, when
\delta=0
, the process of inferring
\eta
is equivalent to that of inferring
\theta
. Whereas, when
\delta\neq 0
,
\theta
can still be employed to infer
\eta
. The parameter
\delta
, however, now intervenes by compensating for the differences between
g
and
f_{b}
. For this reason, as anticipated in Section
1
, we refer to such a parameter as
the compensator
.

\frac{f_{s}}{g}-1

The densities
f^{\perp}
and
f_{b}^{\perp}
are, respectively, the projections of
f
and
f_{b}
onto
Span(\{T_{j}\}_{j\geq 1})
. Note that
\delta
is zero when either
f_{b}\equiv f^{\perp}_{b}
, i.e., the ratio
\frac{f_{b}}{g}
is completely spanned by the
T_{j}
’s or
f_{b}\equiv g
. In all the other cases, the compensator
\delta
captures the sign and the magnitude of the deviation between
f_{b}
and
g
in the direction of
f_{s}
.

While
\delta
is not identifiable under (
6
), it is under (
4
). It follows that, as discussed in Section
3
, when a sample from
F_{b}
is available,
\delta
can be consistently estimated via (
4
) on such a sample and, by the virtue of (
8
), the resulting estimate enables us to both estimate
\eta
consistently and test (
2
) while ensuring the desired probability of type I error.

On the other hand, in the absence of a background-only sample from
F_{b}
, if
\delta<0
, it is still possible to perform valid, yet conservative, inference on
\eta
by relying on the estimable quantity
\frac{\theta}{\|S\|_{G}}<\eta
. Caution must be taken, however, to avoid situations in which
\delta
is positive, which results in
\frac{\theta}{\|S\|_{G}}>\eta
, leading to overestimating the signal proportion and an inflation of the probability of type I error. The relationship between
\frac{\theta}{\|S\|_{G}}
and
\eta
can also be examined visually by comparing
\theta

and the position of
\eta\|S\|_{G}
in Figure
1
. In particular, as
\delta
gets closer to zero,
our bias in estimating
\eta
with
\frac{\theta}{\|S\|_{G}}
decreases. A sufficient condition that ensures the negativity of
\delta
in bump-hunting problems is identified in Section
4
; whereas, Section
2.1
demonstrates the role of
\delta
when relying on tests based on the LRT.

2.1
Anti-conservativeness of LRT-based methods

As anticipated in Section
1
, the first step of most likelihood-based approaches is to estimate
f_{b}
on a background-only sample. Denote such an estimate with
\tilde{g}
. In order to test (
2
) using the LRT,
\tilde{g}
is plugged into (

) in place of
f_{b}
; thus, instead of estimating
\eta
via the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) based on (

), such a parameter is estimated by the MLE of
{\tilde{\eta}}
in the model:

\tilde{f}(x;\tilde{\eta})=\tilde{\eta}f_{s}(x)+(1-\tilde{\eta})\tilde{g}(x)\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{\eta}\in[0,1).

(9)

(a)
\widehat{\tilde{\eta}}<0,\widehat{\tilde{\eta}}_{c}=0

(b)
\widehat{\tilde{\eta}}=\widehat{\tilde{\eta}}_{c}>0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}}\log\tilde{f}(x;{\tilde{\eta}})dF_{b}(x)\right|_{{\tilde{\eta}}=0}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{f_{s}(x)}{\tilde{g}(x)}-1\right)dF_{b}(x),

. As shown on the left panel of the same figure,
\tilde{g}
exhibits a visible bump for
\varepsilon=0.01
(red dot-dashed line) while for
\varepsilon=0.005
(orange two-dashed line) the signal-like term is rather faint. The corresponding LRT distributions (the orange two-dashed line and the red dot-dashed line on the right panel) show clear deviation from the
\bar{\chi}^{2}_{01}
distribution (black solid line).
For comparison, the simulated distribution of the LRT for
\tilde{g}(\cdot;\varepsilon=0)\equiv q
(blue dashed line) is also plotted and, as expected, exhibits significant deviation from
\bar{\chi}^{2}_{01}
.
For
\varepsilon=0.01
,
\tilde{\delta}
is negative, and the LRT has a distribution that is stochastically smaller (red dot-dashed line) than the
\bar{\chi}^{2}_{01}
distribution, leading to overly conservative inference. More importantly, even though
\tilde{g}(\cdot;\varepsilon=0)
and
\tilde{g}(\cdot;\varepsilon=0.005)
appear to well approximate
f_{b}
, the corresponding LRT statistics have distributions stochastically larger than the
\bar{\chi}^{2}_{01}
distribution. In these cases, the compensator
\tilde{\delta}
is positive, and inference based on the
\bar{\chi}^{2}_{01}
approximation unavoidably leads to false discoveries.

3
Inference when a background-only sample is given

Although LRT-based approaches can lead to anti-conservative inference, when the user has access to both background-only and physics data, valid inference on
\eta
can be performed on the basis of the framework introduced in Section
2
.

Begin with the case in which the proposal background
g
is fully specified. Formally, the background-only data are realizations of
m
i.i.d. random variables
Y_{1},Y_{2},\cdots,Y_{m}\sim F_{b}
and recall that the physics data consists of i.i.d. observations of
n
random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}\sim F
. When both datasets are available, the parameters
\theta
and
\delta
are estimable. In particular, by replacing
F
and
F_{b}
in (
5
) and (
7
) with their empirical counterparts

\mathbb{F}^{(n)}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{i}\leq x\}}}{n}
and
\mathbb{F}_{b}^{(m)}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}_{\{Y_{i}\leq x\}}}{m}
,
we obtain:

\widehat{\theta}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S^{\dagger}(x)d\mathbb{F}^{(n)}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}S^{\dagger}(X_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\delta}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S^{\dagger}(x)d\mathbb{F}_{b}^{(m)}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}S^{\dagger}(Y_{i}).

(18)

Plugging in
\widehat{\theta}
and
\widehat{\delta}
in place of
\theta
and
\delta
into (
8
) leads to the following estimator of
\eta
:

\widehat{\eta}=\frac{\widehat{\theta}-\widehat{\delta}}{\|S\|_{G}-\widehat{\delta}}.

(19)

To account for the uncertainties associated with estimating
\delta
on the background-only sample, while inferring
\eta
on the physics sample, it is sensible to assume that

\frac{n}{m+n}\to\pi\in(0,1)\quad\text{as $m,n\to\infty$}.

(20)

Such an assumption reflects the realistic scenario in which the size of the two samples is comparably large, as opposed to the case in which
\pi
is assumed to be zero to avoid dealing with any propagation of the uncertainties on the quantities estimated on the background-only data.

When the above assumption holds, the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\eta}
is given by the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

Under (
20
),

\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}(\widehat{\eta}-\eta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})

(21)

where

\sigma_{\eta}^{2}=(1-\pi)\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{(\|S\|_{G}-\delta)^{2}}+\pi\frac{\sigma_{{\delta}}^{2}(\theta-\|S\|_{G})^{2}}{(\|S\|_{G}-\delta)^{4}}

with
\sigma_{{\theta}}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}({S^{\dagger}}(x))^{2}dF(x)-\theta^{2}
and

\sigma_{{\delta}}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(S^{\dagger}(x))^{2}dF_{b}(x)-\delta^{2}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.2
.

Proposition
1
offers all the elements needed to test the hypotheses in (
2
). Specifically, from the definitions of
\widehat{\theta}
and
\widehat{\delta}
in (
18
), it directly follows that,
\widehat{\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta
and

\widehat{\delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\delta
. Moreover, it is easy to show that,

\widehat{\sigma}_{\theta}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({S^{\dagger}}(X_{i})\right)^{2}-\widehat{\theta}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left({S^{\dagger}}(Y_{i})\right)^{2}-\widehat{\delta}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma_{\delta}^{2};

hence,

\widehat{\sigma}_{\eta}^{2}=\frac{(1-\widehat{\pi})\widehat{\sigma}_{\theta}^{2}}{(\|S\|_{G}-\widehat{\delta})^{2}}+\frac{\widehat{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta}^{2}(\widehat{\theta}-\|S\|_{G})^{2}}{(\|S\|_{G}-\widehat{\delta})^{4}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma_{\eta}^{2}

where
\widehat{\pi}=\frac{n}{m+n}
. Therefore, a statistic for testing (
2
) is given by:

\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}\cdot\frac{\widehat{\eta}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\eta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\quad\text{under }H_{0,\eta}.

(22)

Letting
\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{obs}
be its value observed on the data, an asymptotic
p
-value can then be computed as
1-\Phi(\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{obs})
where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal CDF.

Notice that the above test does not depend on the choice of the basis
\mathcal{T}^{*}
introduced in Section
2
. The latter is a mathematical construction that enables us to unveil the existence and role of the compensator
\delta
, but is unnecessary for inferring
\eta
.
Moreover, it is worth emphasizing that, since the true background distribution is unknown, tests for (
2
) cannot be conducted via simulations. Hence, testing procedures based on (valid) asymptotics are especially valuable for the problem at hand.

Let us now consider the case in which the proposal background density
g
depends on an unknown parameter
\beta\in\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Denote such density with
g_{\beta}
and let its distribution be
G_{\beta}
. Notice that, in this setting, the function
S
, and all related quantities, also depend on
\beta
through
g_{\beta}
. Let
S_{\beta}
,
\|S_{\beta}\|_{G_{\beta}}^{2}
,
S^{\dagger}_{\beta}
,
\theta_{\beta}
, and
\delta_{\beta}
be the counterparts of
S
,
\|S\|_{G}^{2}
,
S^{\dagger}
,
\theta
, and
\delta
, respectively, defined as in (
3
), (
5
), and (
7
), with
g_{\beta}
in place of
g
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
be the MLE of
\beta
obtained on the background-only sample, i.e.,

\widehat{\beta}_{m}=\arg\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log g_{\beta}(Y_{i});

(23)

the parameters
\theta_{\beta}
,
\delta_{\beta}
, and
\eta
can be estimated as in (
18
)-(
19
), but with
g_{\widehat{\beta}}
in place of
g
, i.e.,

\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{\beta}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S^{\dagger}_{\widehat{\beta}}(X_{i}),\quad\widehat{\delta}_{\widehat{\beta}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}S^{\dagger}_{\widehat{\beta}}(Y_{i}),\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\eta}_{\widehat{\beta}}=\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{\beta}}-\widehat{\delta}_{\widehat{\beta}}}{\|S_{\widehat{\beta}}\|_{G_{\widehat{\beta}}}-\widehat{\delta}_{\widehat{\beta}}}.

(24)

Let
\beta^{*}
be the minimizer of the KL divergence between
F_{b}
and
G_{\beta}
, then,
\widehat{\beta}_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\beta^{*}
if the following conditions hold (cf.
[
22
, p .46]
):

(A1)

For any
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
, the maps
x\mapsto g_{\beta}(x)
and

x\mapsto\frac{\partial^{i}g_{\beta}(x)}{\partial\beta^{i}}
for
i=1,2
are continuous.

(A2)

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the maps
\beta\mapsto g_{\beta}(x)

and
\beta\mapsto\frac{\partial^{i}g_{\beta}(x)}{\partial\beta^{i}}
for
i=1,2
are continuous.

(A3)

The parameter space
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is compact.

(A4)

The point
\beta^{*}
is in the interior of the parameter space
\mathcal{B}
.

Let

\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}({S^{\dagger}}_{\beta^{*}}(x)^{2})dF(x)-\theta_{\beta^{*}}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{\beta^{*},\delta}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}({S^{\dagger}}_{\beta^{*}}(x)^{2})dF_{b}(x)-\delta_{\beta^{*}}^{2}.

The asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\eta}_{\widehat{\beta}}
is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

If the assumptions
(A1)
-
(A4)
hold, then under (
20
),

\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}(\widehat{\eta}_{\widehat{\beta}}-\eta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\beta^{*},\eta}^{2})\hskip 5.0pt

(25)

where
\sigma_{\beta^{*},\eta}^{2}=(1-\pi)\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}A_{\beta^{*}}^{2}+\pi\Bigg[\sigma_{\beta^{*},\delta}^{2}B_{\beta^{*}}^{2}+(\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}^{T}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}V_{\beta^{*}}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}\Gamma_{\beta^{*}})+2B_{\beta^{*}}\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}^{T}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}C_{\beta^{*}}\Bigg]
, with

A_{\beta^{*}},B_{\beta^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Gamma_{\beta^{*}},C_{\beta^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
J_{\beta^{*}}
,
V_{\beta^{*}}
are
p\times p
matrices, depending on
\beta^{*}
.

The proof of Proposition
2
, along with the explicit expressions for
A_{\beta^{*}}
,
B_{\beta^{*}}
,
J_{\beta^{*}}
,
V_{\beta^{*}}
,
\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}
and
C_{\beta^{*}}
, is given in Appendix
B.3
.
Notice that the asymptotic distributions of
\widehat{\eta}_{\widehat{\beta}}
differ from that of
\widehat{\eta}
in Proposition
2
in the variance, which accounts for the uncertainty associated with estimating
\beta
on the background-only sample.

Similar to the case in which
g
is fully specified, it is possible to show (see Appendix
B.4
) that there exists a consistent estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{\widehat{\beta},\eta}^{2}
for
\sigma_{\beta^{*},\eta}^{2}
.

Thus, when

) is:

\mathcal{Z}_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}\frac{\widehat{\eta}_{\widehat{\beta}}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\widehat{\beta},\eta}}

(26)

and, letting
\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{obs}
be its value observed, an asymptotic

-value is, once again,

1-\Phi(\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{obs})
.

Let us now investigate, with an example, the statistical properties of the test statistics in
(

) and (

), their robustness with respect to the choice of

, and the effect of estimating the latter parametrically.

Consider a physics sample generated from (

) with
f_{b}
and
f_{s}
as in (

)-(

), and choose
\eta=0.01,0.02
and 0.03. The sample size is varied so that
n=50,250,500,1000
, and
2000
. For each physics sample of size

considered, a background-only sample of size
m=2n
is generated from (

).
Letting
5\%
be the nominal significance level, the probability of type I error and the power of the proposed procedure are simulated for three different choices of the proposal background distribution with varying degrees of departure from the true background density (see Figure
5
, left panel).

First, consider a Pareto type I (or power-law) density with unknown slope, i.e.,

g_{\beta}(x)\propto x^{-(\beta+1)},\quad x\in[1,2]

(27)

with
\beta
estimated via MLE on the background-only data. Since the analysis is conducted considering different sample sizes, to visualize the corresponding density, on the left panel of Figure
5
, we report the graph of
g_{\beta^{*}}
with
\beta^{*}=3.92
(blue dashed line); the latter corresponds to the minimizer of the KL divergence between (

) and
f_{b}
in (
16
) (solid black line). Next, we consider
g_{\beta}
as in (
27
) but with
\beta=2
(brown dot-dashed line). Although such a density also decays smoothly, it deviates substantially from

. Finally, we choose

to be the uniform density (red dotted line) that is entirely different from the true background.

The simulated probability of type I error for each choice of

is shown on the right panel of Figure

. The power curves for varying sample sizes and for different values of
\eta
are given in Figure

. In all cases, the simulation has been conducted using
100,000
Monte Carlo replicates. Figure

shows that, regardless of the choice of

(or

), the simulated type I error probabilities are close to the desired significance level 0.05 and never exceed it.

(or

g_{\beta}
to achieve a reasonable approximation of

, the gain in power is negligible and the resulting inference is practically equivalent to an inferential analysis based on a potentially severely misspecified postulated background model
g
. This result not only provides strong evidence for the robustness of the proposed inferential strategy with respect to the choice of

, but also demonstrates that estimating the compensator suffices in accounting for any departure of
g
(or

) from

, without affecting the resulting inference on

and, therefore, minimizes its dependence on the scientist’s guess of
g
.

4
Inference without background-only data

Let us now consider the case in which a background-only sample is not available to the user. As we shall soon see, in this setup, the ‘closeness’ of
g
to
f_{b}
plays a more prominent role. It is therefore sensible to focus on the parametric form of such a density, i.e.,
g_{\beta}
.

Since a background-only sample is not available, neither
\delta_{\beta}
nor
\eta
are estimable. Nevertheless, from (
8
), we have that

\theta_{0,\beta}=\frac{\theta_{\beta}}{\|S_{\beta}\|_{G_{\beta}}}\leq\eta

(28)

if
\delta_{\beta}\leq 0
. In particular, inferring
\theta_{0,\beta}
is equivalent to inferring
\eta
, when
\delta_{\beta}=0
; whereas, testing and estimating
\theta_{0,\beta}
, in lieu of
\eta
, leads to conservative, yet valid, inference whenever
\delta_{\beta}<0
.
Therefore, to avoid anti-conservative results, we can shift the focus of our inference to
\theta_{0,\beta}
and identify suitable conditions under which
\delta_{\beta}\leq 0
. In what follows, we introduce one such condition for one-dimensional bump-hunting problems with smoothly decaying backgrounds, which are commonly encountered in physics and astronomy
[
13
,
6
,
23
, e.g.,]
.

Fig 7:
Left panel: Example of the data generating density
f
(orange long-dashed line) for a bump-hunting scenario, the corresponding true background density
f_{b}
(black solid line) as in (
16
), the baseline background model
q
(blue dashed line) and the proposal background
g_{\beta}
(purple dot-dashed line) with a bump added across the signal region (green shaded region). Right panel: Example of a sensitivity analysis with
g_{\beta}
as in (
30
) for different values of
\lambda
.

Let the search region,
\mathcal{X}
, be a bounded interval on the real line.
In bump-hunting problems, the presence of the signal over such a region is characterized by a peak in the data-generating density
f
(orange long-dashed line in Figure
7
, left panel). In such cases, the signal distribution
F_{s}
is unimodal, with mode
\mu_{s}
, relatively low dispersion, and typically modeled by a Gaussian or other bell-like symmetric distributions. As a result,
F_{s}
places most of its mass over a ‘localized’ signal region
M_{\epsilon}=[\mu_{s}-d_{\epsilon},\mu_{s}+d_{\epsilon}]\subset\mathcal{X}
(green shaded region in Figure
7
) such that
F_{s}(M_{\epsilon})=1-\epsilon
for some small
\epsilon>0
. Observe that,

\delta_{\beta}\propto\int_{\mathcal{X}}S_{\beta}(x)dG_{\beta}(x)=\Big\langle\frac{f_{s}}{g_{\beta}},\frac{f_{b}}{g_{\beta}}\Big\rangle_{G_{\beta}}-1,

(29)

Therefore,
\delta_{\beta}\leq 0
if the inner product in the above expression is at most one. Notice that:

\Big\langle\frac{f_{s}}{g_{\beta}},\frac{f_{b}}{g_{\beta}}\Big\rangle_{G_{\beta}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{f_{s}(x)}{g_{\beta}(x)}\frac{f_{b}(x)}{g_{\beta}(x)}dG_{\beta}(x)\leq\sup_{x\notin M_{\epsilon}}\frac{f_{b}(x)}{g_{\beta}(x)}\epsilon+\sup_{x\in M_{\epsilon}}\frac{f_{b}(x)}{g_{\beta}(x)}(1-\epsilon);

hence, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, the first term on the right-hand side of the above inequality can be ignored; whereas, the structure of the second term implies that
\delta_{\beta}\leq 0
whenever
\sup_{x\in M_{\epsilon}}\frac{f_{b}(x)}{g_{\beta}(x)}\leq 1
. This last condition is satisfied whenever
g_{\beta}\geq f_{b}
for all
x\in M_{\epsilon}
.

There are many possible ways to construct a density
g_{\beta}
that satisfies such a condition. The left panel of Figure
7
illustrates one possible route based on the following heuristic: start with a baseline density,
q_{\alpha}
(blue dashed line), possibly depending on some unknown parameter
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
, and let
g_{\beta}
be a mixture inclusive of both
q_{\alpha}
and a ‘dominating component’, such as a diffused bump, enabling us to bound
f_{b}
over
M_{\epsilon}
(purple dot-dashed line). The size of such a dominating term cannot be excessively large; if it were, it could either lead to overly conservative inference, or may overly inflate
\sup_{x\notin M_{\epsilon}}\frac{f_{b}(x)}{g_{\beta}(x)}
, thereby failing to provide a negative
\delta_{\beta}
altogether. Since
f_{b}
is unknown, the baseline density
q_{\alpha}
should provide a reasonable approximation of
f_{b}
to ensure that the size of the dominating term being injected can indeed be calibrated to bound
f_{b}
over
M_{\epsilon}
. In essence,
q_{\alpha}
and the size of the dominating term should be such that
\delta_{\beta}
approaches zero from below.

Let
\beta=(\alpha,\lambda)
in which
\alpha
characterizes the shape of
q_{\alpha}
and
\lambda
controls the size of the dominating term introduced in
g_{\beta}
. Choose the latter so that:

g_{\beta}(x)=(1-2\lambda)q_{\alpha}(x)+\lambda\Big[\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x;\mu_{1},\sigma_{0})+\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x;\mu_{2},\sigma_{0})\Big];

(30)

with
\quad\beta=(\alpha,\lambda)\in\mathcal{A}\times[0,1/2)
, and
\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot;\mu,\sigma)
denoting the Gaussian density with mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
truncated on
\mathcal{X}
. By placing two Gaussian densities centered at
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, respectively, both with scale parameter
\sigma_{0}
, the parametric form in (
30
) allows us to inject a dominating component that visually appears as a diffused bump on top of
q_{\alpha}
. As shown through the numerical example and the real data analysis that follows, to ensure
f_{b}
is dominated by (
30
) over
M_{\epsilon}
for at least some values of
\lambda
, it is recommended to choose
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
within
M_{\epsilon}
, but close to its boundaries
\mu_{s}-d_{\epsilon}
and
\mu_{s}+d_{\epsilon}
, whereas
\sigma_{0}
should be substantially larger than that of the width of the expected signal described by
f_{s}
.
By varying
\lambda
, we can control the contribution of the dominating component.
Specifically, once
\alpha
is estimated on the physics data via its MLE,
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
, a sensitivity analysis for different values of
\lambda
can then be performed to allow the user to visually assess, through plots such as the one presented on the right panel of Figure
7
, which values of
\lambda
are expected to yield dominance of
f_{b}
over
M_{\epsilon}
.

Let
\lambda^{*}
be the chosen value for
\lambda
through such a sensitivity analysis and let
\beta^{*}=(\alpha^{*},\lambda^{*})
, in which
\alpha^{*}
denotes the minimizer of the KL divergence between the
q_{\alpha}
and
f
. If
(A1)
-
(A4)
hold for
\{q_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\}
, then
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\alpha^{*}
, and a conservative counterpart of the test in (
2
) is:

H_{0,\theta_{0}}:\theta_{0,\beta^{*}}=0\quad\text{vs}\quad H_{1,\theta_{0}}:\theta_{0,\beta^{*}}>0.

(31)

Let

\widehat{\theta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}}=\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{\beta}}}{\|S_{\widehat{\beta}}\|_{G_{\widehat{\beta}}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{0,\widehat{\beta}}(X_{i})

be the estimator of
\theta_{0,\beta^{*}}

with
\widehat{\beta}_{n}=(\widehat{\alpha}_{n},\lambda^{*})
. The test above can then be conducted on the basis of the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

If
(A1)
-
(A4)
hold for
\{q_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\}
, then

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}}-\theta_{0,\beta^{*}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta_{0}}^{2}\Big),\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$,}

(32)

where
\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta_{0}}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}}{\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|_{G_{\beta^{*}}}^{2}}+D_{\alpha^{*}}^{T}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha^{*}}^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{\alpha^{*}}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha^{*}}^{-1}D_{\alpha^{*}}+2D_{\alpha^{*}}^{T}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha^{*}}^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\alpha^{*}}
and
\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(S^{\dagger}_{\beta^{*}}(x)\big)^{2}dF(x)-\theta_{\beta^{*}}^{2}
, with
D_{\alpha^{*}},\mathcal{C}_{\alpha^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha^{*}},\mathcal{V}_{\alpha^{*}}
are
(p-1)\times(p-1)
matrices depending on
\alpha^{*}
.

The proof of Proposition
3
, along with the expressions for
D_{\alpha^{*}},\mathcal{C}_{\alpha^{*}},\mathcal{J}_{\alpha^{*}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha^{*}}
, is given in Appendix
B.5
. The quantities
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha^{*}},\mathcal{J}_{\alpha^{*}}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha^{*}}
are analogous to
C_{\beta^{*}}
,
J_{\beta^{*}}
, and
V_{\beta^{*}}
in Proposition
2
, except the former quantities involve
q_{\alpha}
instead of
g_{\beta}
and the expectations are taken under
F
and rather than
F_{b}
.

Given a consistent estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{\widehat{\beta},{\theta}_{0}}^{2}
for
\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta_{0}}^{2}
(see Appendix
B.6
), a test statistic for (
31
) is:

\mathcal{Z}_{3}=\frac{\sqrt{n}\widehat{\theta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\widehat{\beta},{\theta}_{0}}}

(33)

and, from Proposition
3
and Slutsky’s theorem,
\mathcal{Z}_{3}
converges to the standard normal distribution under
H_{0,\theta_{0}}
, as
n\to\infty
. The asymptotic
p
-value for the above test is then given by
1-\Phi(\mathcal{Z}_{3}^{obs})
, with
\mathcal{Z}_{3}^{obs}
being the observed value of
\mathcal{Z}_{3}
on the physics sample.

Let us now investigate the statistical properties of the test statistic in (

) with an example. Choose
f_{b}
and

as in (

) and (

), respectively. The type I error probability and the power at a 5% significance level are obtained via a Monte Carlo simulation involving 100,000 replicates. For each such replicate, physics samples of size

As shown in Figure

6
Summary and extensions

The main contribution of this article is that of unveiling the existence of the compensator
\delta
: a single parameter that, when inferring the true signal intensity, collects all the necessary information needed to account for the discrepancy between the true background distribution and the one postulated by scientists.

When a (labeled) background-only sample is available to the practitioners, the compensator is estimable, allowing for accurate inference on the signal intensity that is robust to the choice of the postulated background.

Moreover, the tests described in Section
3
can be extended to the situation in which the signal distribution
F_{s}
depends on an unknown parameter,
\gamma
, possibly multidimensional, with compact support. The main statistical problem here is that
\gamma
is unidentifiable under
H_{0}
in (
2
).
Nonetheless, since the asymptotic results of Propositions
1
-
2
still hold for each value of
\gamma
fixed, one can proceed as described in
[
6
]
and base the inference on a stochastic process,
\{\mathcal{Z}_{k}(\gamma)\}
, with elements given by (
22
) and (
26
) for
k=1,2
, respectively, for each value of
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
fixed. In particular, a ‘global’ test statistic for (
2
) when
\gamma
is unknown can be specified as

T_{k}=\sup_{\gamma}\{\mathcal{Z}_{k}(\gamma)\},

for
k=1,2
. Being
T_{k}
asymptotically distributed as the supremum of a standard Gaussian process, its limiting null distribution can be approximated by means of tube-formulae or the so-called Euler Characteristic Heuristic
[
2
, e.g.,]
. The latter, in particular, can be easily implemented in R via the R package TOHM
[
3
]
.

The proposed method does not directly apply when the functional form of
F_{s}
is unknown. In this setting, model-independent procedures
[
10
, e.g.,]
are recommended.

When a background-only sample is unavailable to users, the compensator is no longer estimable. At least for one-dimensional bump-hunting problems, the compensator enables us to establish a sufficient condition on the choice of the postulated background,
g_{\beta}
, that guarantees conservative, yet valid inference. Section
4
describes how to perform a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the validity of such a condition for different choices of
g_{\beta}
and assess the degree of conservativeness of the resulting inference. In the multidimensional setting, such a sensitivity analysis can still be performed ‘one-dimension-at-a-time’, but extensions to the case in which
F_{s}
depends on the unknown parameter
\gamma
may require a more delicate treatment as the choice of
g_{\beta}
may vary for different values of
\gamma
.

B.1
Proof for completeness of
\mathcal{T}

Let
\mathcal{T}^{*}=\{1,T^{*}_{1},T^{*}_{2},\cdots\}
be a complete orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(G)
. To prove the completeness of
\mathcal{T}=\{1,S^{\dagger},T_{1},T_{2},\cdots\}
, recall the following result:

Result 1
.

[
14
, p. 16]
An orthonormal set of functions
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
forms a complete basis for
L^{2}(G)
if and only if, for some
h\in L^{2}(G)
,
\langle u_{\ell},h\rangle_{G}=0
, for all
\ell\geq 0
,
\implies

h=0
.

Let
h\in L^{2}(G)
be such that:

\langle h,1\rangle_{G}=0;\quad\langle h,S^{\dagger}\rangle_{G}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\langle h,T_{j}\rangle_{G}=0\quad\text{for all $j\geq 1$}.

(37)

By construction, the functions
T_{j}\in\mathcal{T}
,
j\geq 1
, specify as:

\displaystyle T_{1}^{\circ}=

\displaystyle T^{*}_{1}-\langle T^{*}_{1},S^{\dagger}\rangle_{G}S^{\dagger};\quad T_{1}=\frac{T_{1}^{\circ}}{\|T_{1}^{\circ}\|_{G}}

(38)

\displaystyle T^{\circ}_{j}=

\displaystyle T^{*}_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\langle T_{k},T^{*}_{j}\rangle_{G}T_{k}-\langle S^{\dagger},T^{*}_{j}\rangle_{G}S^{\dagger};\quad T_{j}=\frac{T_{j}^{\circ}}{\|T_{j}^{\circ}\|_{G}}

Therefore, from (
37
)-(
38
), it follows that,
\langle h,1\rangle_{G}=0=\langle h,T^{*}_{j}\rangle_{G}
, for all
j\geq 1
and since
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is complete and orthonormal,
h=0
, which completes the proof.

\square

B.2
Proof of Proposition
1

Consider
\nu=(\theta_{0},\delta_{0})
in which, letting
S_{0}(x)=\frac{S^{\dagger}(x)}{\|S\|_{G}}=\frac{S(x)}{\|S\|_{G}^{2}}
,

\theta_{0}=\frac{\theta}{\|S\|_{G}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S_{0}(x)dF(x);\quad\delta_{0}=\frac{\delta}{\|S\|_{G}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S_{0}(x)dF_{b}(x)

(39)

The empirical estimator of
\nu
is
\widehat{\nu}=(\widehat{\theta}_{0},\widehat{\delta}_{0})

\widehat{\theta}_{0}=\frac{\widehat{\theta}}{\|S\|_{G}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{0}(X_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\delta}_{0}=\frac{\widehat{\delta}}{\|S\|_{G}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}S_{0}(Y_{i}).

(40)

Under (20), by the law of large numbers, we have
\widehat{\nu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\nu
,
and by the central limit theorem:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}_{\theta_{0}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\sqrt{m}(\widehat{\delta}_{0}-\delta_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}_{\delta_{0}}\right),

(41)

where
\sigma_{\theta_{0}}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{\|S\|_{G}^{2}}
and

\sigma_{\delta_{0}}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\delta}^{2}}{\|S\|_{G}^{2}}
.

The limiting distributions in (
41
) are valid if the second-order moments of
\widehat{\theta}_{0}
and
\widehat{\delta}_{0}
exist. This can be easily shown as the function
S_{0}
—being continuous on a compact set—is bounded.
Let us now consider the function

T(x,y)=\frac{x-y}{1-y}.

(42)

such that
\eta=T(\nu)
and
\widehat{\eta}=T(\widehat{\nu})
. We denote the gradient vector of
T
by
\partial T
, with elements
\partial_{i}T
, for
i=1,2
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\partial_{1}T(x,y)=\frac{1}{1-y}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{2}T(x,y)=\frac{x-1}{(1-y)^{2}}.

(43)

The mean value theorem gives:

\displaystyle\widehat{\eta}-\eta=

\displaystyle T(\hat{\nu})-T(\nu)=(\widehat{\nu}-\nu)^{T}\partial T(\tilde{\nu})

\displaystyle\implies\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}\left(\widehat{\eta}-\eta\right)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})\sqrt{\frac{m}{m+n}}\partial_{1}T(\tilde{\nu})+\sqrt{m}(\widehat{\delta}_{0}-\delta_{0})\sqrt{\frac{n}{m+n}}\partial_{2}T(\tilde{\nu})

where
\tilde{\nu}
is a point on the line segment joining
\nu
and
\widehat{\nu}
. Since
T
is continuously differentiable and
\widehat{\nu}
is consistent for
\nu
, then
\partial T(\tilde{\nu})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\partial T(\nu)
. Moreover, given that
\widehat{\theta}_{0}
and
\widehat{\delta}_{0}
are independent, from (
41
) and Slutsky’s theorem we obtain (21) with

\displaystyle\sigma_{\eta}^{2}=

\displaystyle(1-\pi)\partial_{1}T(\nu)^{2}\sigma_{\theta_{0}}^{2}+\pi\partial_{2}T(\nu)^{2}\sigma_{\delta_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\pi)\sigma_{\theta_{0}}^{2}}{(1-\delta_{0})^{2}}+\frac{\pi\sigma_{\delta_{0}}^{2}(\theta_{0}-1)^{2}}{(1-\delta_{0})^{4}}=\frac{(1-\pi)\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{(\|S\|_{G}-\delta)^{2}}+\frac{\pi(\theta-\|S\|_{G})^{2}\sigma_{\delta}^{2}}{(\|S\|_{G}-\delta)^{4}}.

\square

B.3

\sqrt{\frac{m}{m+n}}\partial_{1}T(\tilde{\nu})\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(S_{0,\beta^{*}}(X_{i})-\theta_{0,\beta^{*}}\right)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,(1-\pi)\left(\partial_{1}T(\nu_{\beta^{*}})\right)^{2}\sigma_{0,\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}\right)

(54)

where

\sigma_{0,\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(S_{0,\beta^{*}}(x)\big)^{2}dF(x)-\theta_{0,\beta^{*}}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}}{\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|^{2}_{G_{\beta^{*}}}}.

The asymptotic distribution of the last two summands in (
53
) is:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{m+n}}\partial_{2}T(\tilde{\nu})\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(S_{0,\beta^{*}}(Y_{i})-\delta_{0,\beta^{*}}\right)\right]+

(55)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{m+n}}\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}^{T}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\beta^{*}}(Y_{i})\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\pi\Lambda_{\beta^{*}}\right)

where

\Lambda_{\beta^{*}}=\left(\partial_{2}T(\nu_{\beta^{*}})\right)^{2}\sigma_{0,\beta^{*},\delta}^{2}+\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}^{T}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}V_{\beta^{*}}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}+\frac{2\partial_{2}T(\nu_{\beta^{*}})}{\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|_{G_{\beta^{*}}}}\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}^{T}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}C_{\beta^{*}}

with

\displaystyle\sigma_{0,\beta^{*},\delta}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(S_{0,\beta}(x))^{2}dF_{b}(x)-\delta_{0,\beta^{*}}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\beta^{*},\delta}^{2}}{\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|^{2}_{G_{\beta^{*}}}},

\displaystyle V_{\beta^{*}}

\displaystyle=Var_{F_{b}}\Big(\Big[\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\beta(Y)}\Big]_{\beta=\beta^{*}}\Big)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bigg[\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\beta}(y)\bigg]_{\beta=\beta^{*}}\bigg[\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\beta}(y)\bigg]_{\beta=\beta^{*}}^{T}dF_{b}(y),

and

C_{\beta^{*}}=Cov_{F_{b}}\left(S^{\dagger}_{\beta^{*}}(Y),\Big[\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\beta(Y)}\Big]_{\beta=\beta^{*}}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}S^{\dagger}_{\beta^{*}}(y)\bigg[\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\beta}(y)\bigg]_{\beta=\beta^{*}}dF_{b}(y).

Combining the above expressions, we obtain (
25
)
and

\sigma_{\beta^{*},\eta}^{2}=(1-\pi)A_{\beta^{*}}^{2}\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}+\pi\Bigg[B_{\beta^{*}}^{2}\sigma_{\beta^{*},\delta}^{2}+\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}^{T}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}V_{\beta^{*}}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}+2B_{\beta^{*}}\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}^{T}J_{\beta^{*}}^{-1}C_{\beta^{*}}\Bigg]

with
A_{\beta^{*}}=\frac{1}{(\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|_{G_{\beta^{*}}}-\delta_{\beta^{*}})}
and
B_{\beta^{*}}=\frac{(\theta_{\beta^{*}}-\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|_{G_{\beta^{*}}})}{(\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|_{G_{\beta^{*}}}-\delta_{\beta^{*}})^{2}}
.

\square

B.4
Estimator for
\sigma_{\beta^{*},\eta}^{2}

To derive a consistent estimator for
\sigma_{\beta^{*},\eta}^{2}
start by constructing consistent estimators for the quantities
A_{\beta^{*}}
,
B_{\beta^{*}}
,
J_{\beta^{*}}
,
V_{\beta^{*}}
,
\Gamma_{\beta^{*}}
, and
C_{\beta^{*}}
defined in the previous section.

From Appendix
B.3
, we have
\widehat{\theta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta_{0,\beta^{*}}
and
\widehat{\delta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\delta_{0,\beta^{*}}
. By consistency of
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
and the dominated convergence theorem, we have
\|S_{\widehat{\beta}}\|_{G_{\widehat{\beta}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\|S_{\beta^{*}}\|_{G_{\beta^{*}}}

\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta_{\beta^{*}}
and

\widehat{A}_{\widehat{\beta}}=\frac{1}{\|S_{\widehat{\beta}}\|_{G_{\widehat{\beta}}}-\widehat{\delta}_{\widehat{\beta}}},\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{B}_{\widehat{\beta}}=\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{\beta}}-\|S_{\widehat{\beta}}\|_{G_{\widehat{\beta}}}}{(\|S_{\widehat{\beta}}\|_{G_{\widehat{\beta}}}-\widehat{\delta}_{\widehat{\beta}})^{2}}

and

By consistency of
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{\beta}}
,
\widehat{\delta}_{\widehat{\beta}}

\widehat{\sigma}_{\widehat{\beta},\theta}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(S^{\dagger}_{\widehat{\beta}}(X_{i})\right)^{2}-\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{\beta}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma_{\beta^{*},\theta}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\sigma}_{\widehat{\beta},\delta}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(S^{\dagger}_{\widehat{\beta}}(Y_{i})\right)^{2}-\widehat{\delta}_{\widehat{\beta}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma_{\beta^{*},\delta}^{2}.

Similar arguments yield the following consistent estimators for
\Gamma_{\beta^{*}},J_{\beta^{*}},V_{\beta^{*}}
, and

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}_{\widehat{\beta}}=\left[\frac{1}{(1-\widehat{\delta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}})}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\partial_{\beta}S_{0,\beta}(X_{i})\Big]_{\beta=\widehat{\beta}}\right)+\frac{(\widehat{\theta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}}-1)}{(1-\widehat{\delta}_{0,\widehat{\beta}})^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\partial_{\beta}S_{0,\beta}(Y_{i})\Big]_{\beta=\widehat{\beta}}\right)\right];

\displaystyle\widehat{J}_{\widehat{\beta}}=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\partial_{\beta}^{2}\log g_{\beta}(Y_{i})\Big]_{\beta=\widehat{\beta}};\qquad\widehat{V}_{\widehat{\beta}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigg[\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\beta}(Y_{i})\bigg]_{\beta=\widehat{\beta}}\bigg[\partial_{\beta}\log g_{\widehat{\beta}}(Y_{i})\bigg]_{\beta=\widehat{\beta}}^{T};

\square
