Solid-state spin dynamics of a molecular qubit of Ce3+
Authors: José Serrano-Guarinos, Zahra Hosseinzadeh, Abinash Swain, David Aguilà, Leoní A. Barrios, Yulia Nelyubina, Jordi Ribas-Ariño, Olivier Roubeau, Valentin Novikov, Guillem Aromí
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A Ce3+ ion is the qubit of a molecular coordination compound with a [ErCeEr] core that was reported as a platform for the implementation of the three qubit (TQC) repetition code for quantum error correction, by exploiting its interaction with two Er3+ ancillary qubits. We report here a detailed study of the spin dynamics of this Ce3+ qubit by embedding it within [LuCeLu] derivatives of a molecular analogue of the error protected qubit. In these novel molecules the magnetic influence of the Er3+ ancillary qubits is suppressed by replacing them with diamagnetic Lu3+ ions. The pure site-selective heterometallic nature of these molecules is demonstrated and their magnetization dynamics is studied with SQUID measurements. Most notably, the spin dynamics of the qubits are investigated with X-band pulsed EPR in the solid state and their quantum coherence thus quantified. This is achieved by doping molecules of the qubit within an isostructural diamagnetic crystal lattice made of the [LuLaLu] analogue. The solid solution is accessed by co-crystallization of two different [LnLn′Ln] molecules. Their required stability in solution is here unambiguously demonstrated by paramagnetic 1H NMR and mass spectrometry. The influence of the ligand scaffold around the [LuCeLu] metal core on the quantum coherence of the Ce3+ center is discussed.

Introduction

Spins within molecules constitute a promising platform for the physical realization of qubits and qugates of quantum technologies. This implementation benefits from the huge versatility of chemical design to achieve atomistic control on many essential properties and perfect reproducibility.1–5 One of the major advantages is the ability to synthesize multiqubit molecular quantum processors for the realization of complex operations.6–10 The biggest challenge of this approach is the addressability and interfacing (writing and reading information). Proposals for exploiting the coupling of molecules to superconducting resonators have been reported,11,12 and experimental realizations of this coupling have been performed on molecular ensembles diluted within crystal lattices.13 One key aspect is ensuring that the essential properties of the molecular qubit or qugate as determined in the bulk or in solution are maintained on the surface where these molecules are deposited within the hybrid devices.14–16 Another essential feature that requires improvement when using molecules is the coherence of the quantum superpositions that encode the qubit and qugate states, which should be long enough to allow the processing of this information. Coherence may be lost in two different ways. One is through spin-lattice relaxation (governed by a relaxation time termed T1)17 which is linked to the coupling between the electronic spin and the various phonons either from the lattice or from the vibrations of the molecule bearing the spin,18 causing the return of the spin polarization of a superposition to the equilibrium. The other one affects the phase of the quantum superposition and mainly occurs through the interaction of the spin qubit with neighboring spins (both nuclear and electronic), causing magnetic fluctuations. Its characteristic coherence time is called T2 (or Tm if other decoherence sources are also considered), and it is never longer than T1. In recent years, much progress has been made towards understanding the factors affecting T2 (or Tm) and how to increase it.19–29 The above considerations impact directly on the main challenge in implementing quantum computing, which is the mitigation of quantum errors.30 Current solutions imply the use of very large numbers of qubits, thus constituting a bottleneck. Alternatively, the area of chemical design has produced very game-changing propositions for addressing this problem, which consist of encoding quantum error correction protocols within multiqubit individual molecules.7,31 In this context, we have discovered a synthetic method for accessing heterolanthanide dinuclear32–34 and trinuclear35 [LnLn′] and [LnLn′Ln] molecules exhibiting very high topological selectivity of the metal ions. We have exploited this tool to put forward two- and three-qubit quantum gates by using the ground state crystal field stabilized magnetic doublets of the Ln ions as addressable qubits.10,36–38 In particular the [ErCeEr] molecule [Er2Ce(LA)2(LB)2(H2O)2(py)]NO3 (H2LA, 2,6-bis[(3-oxo-3-naphthalene-2yl)-propionyl]pyridine, py = pyridine;39 H2LB, 6-(3-oxo-3-(naphthalene-2-yl)-propanoyl)-picolinic acid;35Fig. 1) was shown to fulfil the conditions to carry out, using resonant pulses, the three-qubit phase-flip repetition code (TQC) for the quantum error correction (QEC) of the central qubit Ce3+, exploiting the Er3+ ions as ancillary qubits.40 The phase memory time (Tm) of the various EPR transitions of the [ErCeEr] molecule were determined in frozen solution using pulse Hahn–echo sequences at the X-band. At 6 K, Tm values in the 0.3 to 0.5 µs range were determined. We were now interested in studying the spin dynamics of the qubit of the molecular [ErCeEr] quantum processor prototype, without the influence of the ancillary Er3+ components, and its dependance with small variations of the ligand scaffold. For this we have prepared the molecule [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LB)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (1, dmf = dimethylformamide), which is a close version of the [LuCeLu] molecule used previously to obtain the magnetic structure of the Ce3+ qubit, now with different terminal ligands (H2O and dmf instead of py and H2O) as a result of the improved synthetic method presented here to obtain the [LnLn′Ln] molecules. In order to assess the possible influence of small changes to the molecular scaffold on the coherence of Ce3+, the analogous molecule [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LC)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (2) has also been obtained, bearing a slightly different bridging ligand, H2LC (6-(3-oxo-3-phenylpropanoyl)-picolinic acid; Fig. 1). The very strong site-selectivity of the heterometallic complexes was established by mass spectrometry (MS). In this work, in addition, the stability and purity of the molecular complexes was shown unambiguously by combining 1D and 2D 1H NMR spectra, which were fully assigned. The spin dynamics of Ce3+ within 1 and 2 in the solid state was investigated in solution and the solid state. In the latter case, in order to keep the Ce3+ qubits separated from each other, we studied them within the solid solutions [Lu2La(1−x)Cex(LA)2(LB)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (1x) and [Lu2La(1−x)Cex(LA)2(LC)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (2x), respectively (with 0 < x ≪ 1). From this investigation we have discovered that the presence of the Er3+ ions is not detrimental to the coherence of the Ce3+ qubit in solution. However, the Ce3+ coherence in the [LuCeLu] compounds improves significantly in the solid state. The differences of Tm between 1 and 2 are discussed in terms of the coupling with ligand protons and local vibration modes.

Ligands 2,6-bis-((3-oxo-3-naphthalene-2-yl)propionyl)-pyridine (H2LA), 6-(3-oxo-3-(naphthalene-2-yl)-propanoyl)-picolinic acid (H2LB) and 6-(3-oxo-3-phenylpropanoyl)-picolinic acid (H2LC).

Results and discussion

Synthesis

While ligand H2LA was obtained following a method previously published by our group,39 ligand H2LB was prepared with a procedure modified from the reported one.35 It consists of the direct crossed Claisen condensation of equimolar amounts of 2-acetonaphtone with dimethyl-2,6-pyridinedicarboxylate, followed by the saponification of the unreacted ester (see SI). The workup allows the separation from the product of both, the unreacted starting diester or any potential bis-β-diketone. This avoids the preparation of the intermediate 6-(methoxycarbonyl)picolinic acid, thus increasing the overall yield while reducing the number of steps and solvent consumption. The same method was used to prepare H2LC, here for the first time (SI). The 1H-NMR spectra of H2LB and H2LC in CDCl3 suggest their purity and revealed both components of the keto–enol equilibrium, with each compound present predominantly (97%) in the enol form.

Compounds [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LB)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (1) and [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LC)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (2) were obtained as polycrystalline solids from the direct reaction of stoichiometric amounts of Ce(NO3)3, Lu(NO3)3, H2LA and either H2LB or H2LC in the presence of an excess of triethylamine in DMF, followed by the slow diffusion of an antisolvent into the solution (Et2O, MTBE, iPr2O or AcOEt). This procedure, slightly different from the original one,35 avoids the use of pyridine as well as the need for CuCl2 as crystal modulator, while leading to significantly increased yields and to the drastic reduction of crystallization times (SI). The use of Et3N solves the lack of pyridine as the acceptor of the protons from H2LA and H2LB (or H2LC), as shown in eqn (1). Compound 2 is obtained in a lower yield than 1, presumably due to its higher solubility.1Ce(NO3)3 + 2Lu(NO3)3 + 2H2LA + 2H2LX + 8Et3N + 2DMF + H2O → [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LX)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 + 8Et3NHNO3 (X = B or C)

The identity and molecular structure of compounds 1 and 2 were determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD, see below). The homogeneity of the bulk products was confirmed, when possible, by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD, see SI and Fig. S1) and microanalysis, which revealed the absorption of water molecules from the atmosphere by both compounds. Importantly, the expected molar ratio of metal ions was determined by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) measurements (see SI). Notably, the stability and purity of the [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LX)2]+ (X = B or C) core of 1 and 2 in solution was established by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) and paramagnetic 1H NMR spectroscopy (see below), showing that this new and more convenient method produces pure and homogeneous [LuCeLu] compounds.

To study the solid-state spin dynamics of Ce3+ in the [LuCeLu] complexes it is necessary to keep them sufficiently separated within a matrix, thus minimizing their mutual spin–spin interactions (see below). This was achieved by doping with the [LuCeLu] complexes isostructural crystal lattices of the corresponding diamagnetic [LuLaLu] species. Thus, the solid solutions [Lu2La(1−x)Cex(LA)2(LB)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (1x) and [Lu2La(1−x)Cex(LA)2(LC)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (2x) were obtained for concentrations ranging x = 0.001 to 0.05 (0.1 to 5%), by repeating the procedures to form 1 or 2, using the appropriate mixtures of La(NO3)3 and Ce(NO3)3 for each composition. This was possible thanks to the high selectivity of the [LnLn′Ln] scaffold, which allowed the co-crystallization of mixtures of pure [LuCeLu] and [LuLaLu] complexes in form of homogeneous solid solutions in the desired La/Ce ratio, with no scrambling of metals between central and peripheral positions. The integrity of both [LuCeLu] and [LuLaLu] moieties within the various 1x and 2x compounds was corroborated with MS measurements, whereas their exact metal composition for selected concentrations was established with ICP determinations (see SI). The various 1x molecular alloys conserve the crystal lattice of the parent 1 compound, as shown by PXRD (Fig. S1). These measurements could not be realized for compound 2 due to the very poor diffraction of the various 2x composites because of their lack of crystallinity. Nevertheless, a behaviour equivalent to 1 was substantiated by the remainder of the physical characterization described above, in addition to FTIR (Fig. S2 and S3).

Molecular structures

Complexes [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LB)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (1) and [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LC)2(H2O)(dmf)2]NO3 (2) crystallise in the tetragonal space groups P4̄and I41/a, respectively (Tables S2 and S3). The asymmetric unit for both compounds contains their respective unit formula plus a severely disordered DMF molecule. Both complexes feature a nearly linear [LuCeLu] metal array (Fig. 2), with intermetallic Lu⋯Ce distances (in the Lu1/Lu2 format) of 3.9406(12)/3.9591(11) and 3.9201(8)/3.9398(10) Å for 1 and 2, respectively and Lu⋯Lu separations of 7.8726(13) and 7.8451(13). The Lu–Ce–Lu angles are 170.52(3) and 173.03(4)°, respectively. The molecular assembly is similar to that of previously reported [LnLn′Ln] systems;35,41 the metal centres are bridged together by two µ3-LA2− ligands and two µ-LB2− or µ-LC2− ligands, which in turn, all chelate the metals with β-diketone (O,O) and 2,6-dipicolinate (O, N, O) units, thereby creating two different coordination environments. These lead to a central N2O8 pocket that is bigger than the lateral NO6 ones. In addition to the donor atoms described, each Lu atom exhibits one coordinated DMF molecule (completing coordination number, CN, of 8), whereas the central Ce atom reaches CN = 11 with one water ligand. The program SHAPE42 was used to calculate the ideal polyhedron that best represents the coordination environment of the metallic centres (Table S4). The Ce3+ coordination environment deviates significantly from any ideal polyhedron, the closest one being a capped pentagonal prism (C5v) for 1 or a capped pentagonal antiprism (C5v) for 2. The Lu3+ coordination environments resemble a triangular dodecahedron (D2d) in 1, while for 2, one Lu3+ is closest to this geometry while the other one is best described as a bi-augmented trigonal prism (C2v). Bond distances to both metals were compared through their average Ln–O separations involving LA2− and LB2− (or LC2−). The averages are 2.621(1) and 2.620(1) Å for Ce in 1 and 2, respectively and 2.298(1)/2.311(1) and 2.290(1)/2.284(1) (in the Lu1/Lu2 format) for Lu. The values given for Lu are for the major component of the disorder exhibited by a large portion of the organic ligands (see SI).

The carbon atoms of ligands LA2− are darker gray, whereas these of LB2− and LC2− are lighter gray. Ce is yellow, Lu is green, O is red, N is purple and C is gray. H atoms are not shown. Raman spectroscopy

Raman spectra were acquired with a 633 nm laser excitation at room temperature on fine polycrystalline powders of 1 and 2 at three different sample locations (Fig. S4 and S5) and the spectra were averaged to avoid orientation effects. The results for both compounds are quasi superimposable, especially at the higher energy region (>650 cm−1) where most internal molecular vibrations are found. Of the lower energy signals, the most intense ones (197, 397 and 429 cm−1) are associated with Ln–O stretches43 and are also identical for both compounds, as is the band at 520 cm−1, ascribed to a deformation of the diketonate chelate ring.43 Shoulders equally observed for 1 and 2 at 89 and 132 cm−1, as well as the weak bands at 159/168 and 271/265 cm−1 in 1/2 could correspond to external vibration modes (i.e. collective molecular vibrations). Therefore, from these results both molecules differ only in very low intensity bands attributed to collective phonon modes while featuring essentially the same internal molecular vibrations.

Stability in solution

The stability in solution of the heterometallic [LnLn′Ln] complexes is an essential requirement for the preparation of molecular alloys with them, since they are obtained as co-crystals of their individual components. This stability was established for compounds 1 and 2, since the study of [LuLaLu](1−x)[LuCeLu]x lattices is central for this work. Strong evidence for the robustness of their [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LX)2]+ (X = B or C) core in solution was obtained by MALDI-TOF MS performed in DMSO/MeOH (1 : 100), which revealed a single dominant signal for this moiety, and the absence of any other metal compositions (Fig. S6 and S7). This method has been crucial in the past to confirm the size-driven topological selectivity of the metal distribution within related [LnLn′Ln] molecules.35,37,40,41,44,45 The results are consistent with the values obtained from ICP-AES metal analyses of the bulk, yielding a molar ratio of 1 : 2 for Ce : Lu for both compounds. The same behavior was observed for the molecules integrating the various 1x and 2x solid solutions (both through MS, Fig. S6–S9, and ICP).

The purity and solution stability of complexes 1 and 2 were also confirmed by paramagnetic 1H NMR spectroscopy. The spectrum of complex 1 in d6-DMSO (Fig. S12) exhibits many signals with very similar intensities, consistent with the 30 unique signals expected for this complex considering its idealized C2 symmetry (Fig. 3) and the dissociation of the terminal ligands. This observation suggests that the asymmetry introduced into the originally symmetric ligand H2LA upon its incorporation into the [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LB)2(H2O)(dmf)2]+ unit persists in solution on the NMR timescale. Due to the relatively weak magnetic anisotropy of the Ce3+ ion, most signals appear in the region typical for aromatic protons in a diamagnetic environment. However, several peaks display slight paramagnetic shifts (several ppm in both directions), which become evident upon comparison with the signals of the diamagnetic [LuLaLu] analogue (Fig. S13).

1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, DMSO-d6, RT) of 1 with a selected zoomed range at the top right inset. The top left inset is a scheme of the symmetrically unique fragment of the molecule bound to the [LuCeLu] metal core, assuming the dissociation of the terminal ligands. The indicated assignments of all the peaks have been achieved through a combined analysis of 1D 1H and 13C spectra, together with 2D 1H–1H COSY, 1H–13C HSQC, and 1H–13C HMBC experiments.

Because the assignment of paramagnetic spectra is inherently nontrivial, we employed 2D NMR spectroscopy to facilitate signal assignment. Although 2D spectra of paramagnetic compounds are often less informative due to short nuclear relaxation times, as we have demonstrated previously for paramagnetic systems with extended aromatic ligands,46,47 2D NMR can still be used to assign most resonances. In this case, the assignment was achieved through a combined analysis of 1D 1H and 13C spectra, together with 2D 1H–1H COSY, 1H–13C HSQC, and 1H–13C HMBC experiments (Fig. 3 and S12–S17).

Importantly, direct observation of the asymmetry of the LA2− ligand in 1 confirms the chemical stability of the complex in DMSO solution and the absence of lanthanide ion scrambling. Similarly, the 1H NMR spectrum of complex 2 shows 26 signals, in agreement with the number of signals expected for this molecule (Fig. S18–S20).

Beyond confirming the persistence of the molecular [Lu2Ce(LA)2(LX)2]+ scaffold in solution, these results demonstrate that 1H NMR can serve as a powerful tool for the characterization in solution of other members of this extended family of [LnLn′Ln] molecules.

Qubit state definition: ab initio calculations, static magnetic properties and CW-EPR

A first assessment of the ability of Ce3+ in 1 and 2 to provide good definitions of a qubit was obtained from CASSCF ab initio calculations (see SI for details). These calculations indicate that the Ce3+ centers reside in the lowest-energy 2F5/2 level, with ground states largely dominated by the MJ = ±5/2 component (>80%, Tables S5 and S6), and exhibit pronounced axial magnetic anisotropy (Fig. S21). Thus, the principal components of the g tensor (gxx, gyy, gzz) were computed as (0.51, 0.71, 3.44) and (0.45, 0.71, 3.52) for 1 and 2, respectively. The ab initio calculations yield energy separations between the ground and first excited states of 187.5 K (130.26 cm−1) and 211.6 K (147.05 cm−1) for 1 and 2, respectively. The Stevens operator parameters extracted from these results48 are given in Table S7. At low temperatures, this portrays the Ce3+ centers in these compounds as well-defined pseudo-spin doublets, significantly isolated from their first excited states, as required for their implementation as spin-based qubits.

From the experimental perspective, variable temperature magnetization measurements were first conducted on the bulk for both, 1 and 2. The temperature dependence of the magnetic susceptibility χ is shown for both compounds in Fig. 4a as the product χT. In both cases, χT increases with temperature, from 0.357/0.492 cm3 mol−1 K at 2 K to 0.495/0.541 cm3 mol−1 K at 300 K (in the 1/2 format), a tendency that can be ascribed to the population of excited doublets of the Ce3+ ion. The data can be reasonably well reproduced using a simplified version of the Van-Vleck susceptibility49 that takes into account two spin doublets and estimates the energy gap that separates them, Δ/kB, producing two sizeable gaps, in line with the picture given by the ab initio calculations. The fact that the experimentally deduced energies are considerably lower (105 and 16 K for 1 and 2, respectively) than the calculated ones is likely because the samples experience exchange of DMF for H2O in the atmosphere (see microanalysis at the SI), which by modifying the second coordination sphere may have changed considerably the magnetic anisotropy of the rare earth ion, as previously observed.50 These low-lying doublets provide in any case suitable 2-level spin systems to encode a qubit at liquid He temperatures. Departure of the data from this simplified model with further decrease of χT at temperatures below 12/4 K for 1/2 may result from the anisotropy of the ground state, although the effect of very weak intermolecular antiferromagnetic interactions cannot be discarded.

The significant difference observed in χT ranges among the two compounds also likely results from different anisotropy at the Ce3+ ions, despite only very subtle differences in their environment, as observed in their respective structures and supported by their similar Raman spectra (see above). To characterize experimentally the Ce3+ ground state in both compounds, X-band CW-EPR spectra were acquired at 10 K on polycrystalline samples (Fig. 4b). These are satisfactorily reproduced using Easyspin,51 considering an effective spin S = 1/2 with axially anisotropic gCe = (1.55, 1.55, 2.78)/(1.30, 1.30, 3.90), respectively for compounds 1/2. Although the occurrence of in-plane anisotropy cannot be discarded, its introduction does not significantly improve the simulation, in part due to the broad and weak nature of the signals observed. Importantly, the experimental field dependence of the bulk magnetization, determined in the range 2–10 K (Fig. S22), is in excellent agreement with the one calculated using the parameters derived from the simulation of CW-EPR solid-state spectra (Fig. S22), thereby supporting the validity of the latter. While this simulation predicts predominant axial anisotropy, the discrepancies with the ab initio results are attributed again to the fact that the structure used for the calculations should not be exactly the same as the one prevailing during the measurements. The corresponding spectra from diluted samples 1x and 2x (not shown) are very noisy, thus not bringing any additional information. Presumably, the decreased broadening expected upon dilution of the Ce3+ ions does not compensate for the concomitant loss of intensity. Attempts to record the spectra in solution did not produce any signal, presumably because of the added strain caused by the detachment of the terminal ligands (leading to increased flexibility).

Spin dynamics: AC magnetic susceptibility and pulsed EPR

The spin dynamics of the Ce3+ ions in 1 and 2 was first studied in the bulk by measuring the frequency dependence of the AC susceptibility at 2 K under increasing applied DC fields (Fig. S23 and S24). While the zero-field data for both compounds show no out-of-phase component of χ, the application of a DC field does induce the emergence of such a signal. Measurements were then also conducted at increasing temperatures under an applied field of 0.1 (1 and 2) and 0.3 (2) T (Fig. S25–S27). In all cases, the characteristic relaxation time τ was determined by fitting the generalized Debye model expressions for the real and imaginary components of the susceptibility to the data (Tables S8 and S9).52,53 A first striking observation is that, across all temperatures and fields, the relaxation of the Ce3+ spin in 2 is about half as fast as in 1 (Fig. 5), despite very similar chemical environment and solid-state structures. This allowed determining the variation of τ with the field for the former, up to 2 T, evidencing a close to B−4 dependence at high fields. This is indicative of a dominant direct relaxation mechanism in this range of fields. Meanwhile, the temperature dependence of the relaxation time at relatively low fields (0.1 or 0.3 T) seems dominated by a Raman relaxation process. The data are well reproduced with aTn power laws with n = −5.53/−4.70/−4.73 for 1 at 0.1 and 2 at 0.1 T and 0.3 T, respectively.

The spin dynamics of a [ErCeEr] molecule, which had been presented as a processor to realize a TQC,40 had been previously studied in frozen solution by pulsed X-band EPR. The Tm values determined for various transition resonances relevant to the realization of the quantum circuit were approximately 0.4 µs. We now decided to study the central qubit of this processor, in the same molecular environment, isolated from the influence of the ancillary qubits (i.e. with the Er3+ ions now substituted by Lu3+ centres), as we find in the [LuCeLu] complexes 1 and 2. Standard X- and Q-band Hahn–echo sequences on MeOH or EtOH : MeOH (better glassing) solutions did not generate any detectable echo signal, contrary to the case of the [ErCeEr] compound. In the latter case, the possibility that the signal is due to products resulting from dissociation processes is very unlikely, since numerous studies on [LnLn′Ln] molecules (including this work) corroborate their robustness in solution.40,41,45,54–56 Thus, the ability to determine Tm values in frozen solution for the [ErCeEr] analogue reflects on the fact that the spin transitions of the latter, which involve states from both Er3+ and Ce3+, exhibit slower spin dynamics than a transition exclusively within the latter metal ion. Thus, the presence of Er3+ brings robustness to the quantum coherence of the system. The focus was therefore placed on studying the spin dynamics of the isolated Ce3+ qubit in the solid state. The detrimental effects of the dipolar spin–spin interactions among qubits were avoided by doping the diamagnetic lattices of the analogous [LuLaLu] molecules with the [LuCeLu] species. Optimal data were obtained for the 0.5% concentration, thus all the analysis is performed on this composition for both compounds.

Echo-detected field-swept (EDFS) EPR spectra for both compounds show a similar anisotropic pattern (Fig. 6, S28 and S29), as suggested by CW EPR (Fig. 4). Despite the similar coordination motifs around the Ce3+ ion, the CW and EDFS spectra of the two compounds are clearly different. Complex 2 exhibits significantly broader and more asymmetric spectral features, as well as a pronounced high-field tail, whereas 1 shows a narrower and more defined resonance (Fig. 6). These differences indicate a larger distribution of local magnetic environments in 2, most likely arising from increased g-strain and static structural disorder, even though the overall ligand field symmetry remains comparable in both systems. The simulation of these spectra required some modifications to the anisotropic g tensor in comparison to the simulation of the CW EPR spectra, and in particular the introduction of in-plane anisotropy, yielding gCe = (1.25, 1.70, 2.18)/(1.25, 1.85, 3.95) respectively for 10.005/20.005. These relatively small variations can reasonably be ascribed to the nature of the complexes as dopants of a diamagnetic analogue, considering the high sensitivity of g parameters, as revealed when comparing 1 and 2 (see above). As expected, the values of g obtained from this simulation are much closer to these obtained from the simulation of the CW EPR spectra than to the ab initio values.

Nutation experiments were performed on solid-state samples of 10.005 and 20.005 at optimal magnetic fields of 4000 and 3336 G, correspondingly. The echo decay as a function of nutation pulse length shows Rabi oscillations (Fig. S30 and S31), with frequencies proportional to microwave field B1, confirming the possibility of coherent control of the qubit state.

The phase memory time Tm was measured at different magnetic fields at 5 K, which revealed clear differences in the spin dynamics of Ce3+ in 10.005 and 20.005. In both complexes, a moderate dependence of Tm on the magnetic field is observed (Fig. 6). For 1, the phase memory time is approximately constant across the entire field range, remaining close to the microsecond regime at lower fields and gradually decreasing at higher fields. In turn, 2 exhibits systematically shorter coherence times, on the order of a few hundred nanoseconds, with only a relatively weak variation across the field range. Overall, the difference between both complexes is primarily a uniform reduction of Tm in compound 2, rather than a qualitative difference in field dependence. These coherence times are very similar to those recently reported for an isolated octacoordinated Ce3+ center within a molecule with a close to D4h coordination geometry, measured at half the concentration used here.57 These are the only molecular species for which the phase memory time of Ce3+ has been determined (in addition to the [ErCeEr] complex).40 Their quantum coherence remains shorter than the values observed for inorganic lattices with low nuclear-spin such as CaWO3, Y2SiO5 or YAG (Y3Al5O12), doped with Ce3+.58–60

The field dependence of the spin–lattice relaxation time, T1, shows a different behavior (Fig. 6, S33 and S34). While complex 1 displays nearly field-independent T1 values, lying in the 10−4 s range, compound 2 exhibits both, shorter T1 values and a noticeably stronger field dependence. This is the result of the differences in spin-lattice relaxation mechanisms underscored by AC magnetization studies (see above). However, in both compounds T1 remains more than two orders of magnitude larger than Tm across the entire field range studied. This clearly indicates that the phase memory times are not limited by spin–lattice relaxation processes, but by pure dephasing mechanisms.

The systematically shorter Tm observed for compound 2 is therefore attributed to a larger distribution of local magnetic environments, as suggested by its broader and more asymmetric CW and EDFS spectra. Such spectral broadening is consistent with increased g-strain and static structural disorder, which enhances spectral diffusion within the surrounding proton nuclear spin bath and leads to faster decoherence. Differences in crystallographic disorder have been detected (see above), which may indeed become more pronounced following the desorption of solvate molecules and absorption of water upon exposure to the atmosphere. Eventually, Raman spectroscopy only unveils very small differences in collective vibrations, which could be partially responsible for the differences in Tm.

Conclusions

The spin dynamics of the Ce3+ qubit of a [ErCeEr] complex, previously reported as a platform to implement quantum error correction,40 can be studied isolated from the Er3+ ancilla by incorporating it within analogous [LuCeLu] molecules. The quantum coherence in the solid state can be determined thanks to the ability of customizing the separation among [LuCeLu] complexes within crystalline [LuLaLu](1−x)[LuCeLu]x molecular alloys of any desired concentration, where [LuLaLu] is a diamagnetic analogue. These lattices are accessible by co-crystallization of their molecular components because the latter are stable in solution. This is ascertained through MS and paramagnetic 1H NMR experiments on the [LuCeLu] complexes, which confirm their idealized symmetry and allow the assignment of most of their protons. Small variations to the ligand periphery in these complexes do not vary the overall molecular core structure but lead to small changes to the Ce3+ coordination geometry causing differences to its magnetic anisotropy, as suggested by ab initio calculations. This also affects the phase memory time (Tm) and spin-lattice relaxation time (T1). This study shows that pure heterometallic [LnLn′Ln] molecules may be studied as versatile quantum processors in solution and the solid state with a view to incorporating them within hybrid quantum devices.
