The greedy 3-sumfree sequence S 1 , g , g + 1 S_{1,g,g+1}
Orion Shtrezi

Abstract
For every integer g ≥ 2 g\geq 2 , we determine exactly which integers occur in the greedy 3-sumfree sequence that starts with 1 1 , g g , and g + 1 g+1 . This gives a direct proof of a conjecture of Bosma, Bruin, Fokkink, Grube, Reuijl, and Tromp. We also give an explicit description of the eventual periodicity, including both the preperiod and the repeating block.



1
Introduction

Let
x<y<z
be positive integers. The greedy 3-sumfree sequence
S_{x,y,z}
is the increasing sequence that begins with
x,y,z
and then continues by the rule that each new term is the least positive integer exceeding the preceding term that is not a sum of three distinct earlier terms.

Bosma et al.
[
1
, Theorem 15 and Conjecture 16]
proved explicit descriptions of
S_{1,2,3}
,
S_{1,3,4}
, and
S_{1,4,5}
with the automatic theorem prover Walnut, and they conjectured a uniform formula for
S_{1,g,g+1}
for every
g\geq 2
, which they verified mechanically for
2\leq g\leq 10
. The cases
S_{1,2,3}
and
S_{1,3,4}
appear in the OEIS
[
2
]
as
A026471
and
A026475
, respectively; a note in entry
A026471
records an earlier proof of the case
g=2
by Matthew Akeran. In this note we prove the full conjecture by a direct interval-sumset argument.

For integers
u
and
v
, we write

[u,v]=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:u\leq n\leq v\},

with the convention that
[u,v]=\varnothing
when
u>v
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
g\geq 2
be an integer. Then the greedy 3-sumfree sequence
S_{1,g,g+1}
is characterized by

\displaystyle z\in S_{1,g,g+1}

\displaystyle\iff z\in\{1,2g+1,6g+1\}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{or }z\bmod(10g+3)\in[g,2g]\cup[6g+2,7g+1].

In particular, after the first
g+4
terms, the sequence modulo
10g+3
is periodic with period length
2g+1
.

Section
2
fixes notation and reduces Theorem
1
to two propositions, which are proved in Sections
3
and
4
; the induction that completes the proof is carried out at the end of Section
4
.

2
Notation and reduction

For subsets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we write

X+Y=\{x+y:x\in X,\ y\in Y\}.

For a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{2}X

\displaystyle=\{x+y:x,y\in X,\ x<y\},

\displaystyle\widehat{3}X

\displaystyle=\{x+y+z:x,y,z\in X,\ x<y<z\}.

We use the elementary identities

\widehat{2}[u,v]=[2u+1,2v-1],\qquad\widehat{3}[u,v]=[3u+3,3v-3],

(2)

whenever the intervals on the left are nonempty.

Set

m=10g+3,\qquad e=2g+1,\qquad f=6g+1,

and define

I_{k}=km+[g,2g],\qquad J_{k}=km+[6g+2,7g+1]\qquad(k\geq 0).

Then the right-hand side of (
1
) is the set

A_{g}=\{1,e,f\}\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 0}(I_{k}\cup J_{k}).

Since
g\geq 2
, the residues of
1
,
e
, and
f
modulo
m
lie outside
[g,2g]\cup[6g+2,7g+1]
, so the sets
\{1\}
,
\{e\}
,
\{f\}
,
I_{0},I_{1},\ldots
, and
J_{0},J_{1},\ldots
are pairwise disjoint and partition
A_{g}
.

We call an integer
forced
if it is a sum of three distinct elements of
A_{g}
. Since all elements of
A_{g}
are positive, any representation

n=a+b+c

with distinct
a,b,c\in A_{g}
automatically satisfies
a,b,c<n
. Thus Theorem
1
follows once we prove the following two propositions.

Proposition 2
.

Every integer
n>g+1
with
n\notin A_{g}
is forced.

Proposition 3
.

No element of
A_{g}
is forced.

3
Representing the omitted integers

Proof of Proposition
2
.

For the initial block, let

B=[g,2g+1],\qquad D=[6g+1,7g+1],

so that
B=I_{0}\cup\{e\}
and
D=\{f\}\cup J_{0}
are subsets of
A_{g}
.
The omitted integers above
g+1
in block
0
are

[2g+2,6g]\cup[7g+2,m].

By (
2
) we have

1+\widehat{2}B=[2g+2,4g+2],\qquad\widehat{3}B=[3g+3,6g],

so these two intervals cover
[2g+2,6g]
. Likewise,

1+B+D=[7g+2,9g+3],\qquad(7g+1)+\widehat{2}B=[9g+2,11g+2].

Since
m=10g+3\leq 11g+2
, these two intervals cover
[7g+2,m]
.

Now let
k\geq 1
. The omitted integers in block
k
are exactly

km+[1,g-1],\qquad km+[2g+1,6g+1],\qquad km+[7g+2,m].

For the first gap we have

((k-1)m+7g+1)+e+[g+2,2g]=km+[1,g-1].

For the middle gap we have

1+B+I_{k}=km+[2g+1,4g+2],\qquad(km+2g)+\widehat{2}B=km+[4g+1,6g+1].

For the last gap we have

1+D+I_{k}=km+[7g+2,9g+2],\qquad(km+7g+1)+\widehat{2}B=km+[9g+2,11g+2].

Again
m=10g+3\leq 11g+2
, so these intervals cover all of
km+[7g+2,m]
.

Each displayed identity gives a representation by three distinct elements of
A_{g}
. The operators
\widehat{2}
and
\widehat{3}
enforce distinctness inside
B
, and the remaining singled-out summand lies outside the relevant copy of
B
. In the mixed sums
1+B+D
,
1+B+I_{k}
, and
1+D+I_{k}
, the three summands come from pairwise disjoint sets, and the same holds for the representation of the first gap, whose summands come from
J_{k-1}
,
\{e\}
, and
[g+2,2g]\subseteq I_{0}
. Therefore every omitted integer
n>g+1
is forced.
∎

4
Excluding the admitted integers

Define the periodic complement-pattern set

C=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\bigl(km+[0,g-1]\bigr)\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\bigl(km+[2g+1,6g+1]\bigr)\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\bigl(km+[7g+2,m]\bigr).

By construction,

A_{g}\cap C=\{1,e,f\}.

(3)

Lemma 4
.

Every sum of three distinct elements of
A_{g}
lies in
C
.

Proof.

We examine each feasible block-membership pattern for three distinct elements of
A_{g}
. By the partition of
A_{g}
noted in Section
2
, the patterns below are exhaustive, and summands taken from different parts of the partition are automatically distinct. Inside the interval blocks
I_{k}
and
J_{k}
we ignore distinctness, since doing so can only enlarge the corresponding sumsets.

For the three exceptional terms we have

1+e+f=8g+3\in C.

For two exceptional terms and one periodic term, we obtain

\displaystyle 1+e+I_{p}

\displaystyle=pm+[3g+2,4g+2],

\displaystyle 1+e+J_{p}

\displaystyle=pm+[8g+4,9g+3],

\displaystyle 1+f+I_{p}

\displaystyle=pm+[7g+2,8g+2],

\displaystyle 1+f+J_{p}

\displaystyle=(p+1)m+[2g+1,3g],

\displaystyle e+f+I_{p}

\displaystyle=pm+[9g+2,10g+2],

\displaystyle e+f+J_{p}

\displaystyle=(p+1)m+[4g+1,5g].

For one exceptional term and two periodic terms, we obtain

\displaystyle 1+I_{p}+I_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q)m+[2g+1,4g+1],

\displaystyle 1+I_{p}+J_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q)m+[7g+3,9g+2],

\displaystyle 1+J_{p}+J_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q+1)m+[2g+2,4g],

\displaystyle e+I_{p}+I_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q)m+[4g+1,6g+1],

\displaystyle e+I_{p}+J_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q)m+[9g+3,m]\cup(p+q+1)m+[1,g-1],

\displaystyle e+J_{p}+J_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q+1)m+[4g+2,6g],

\displaystyle f+I_{p}+I_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q)m+[8g+1,10g+1],

\displaystyle f+I_{p}+J_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q+1)m+[3g,5g-1],

\displaystyle f+J_{p}+J_{q}

\displaystyle=(p+q+1)m+[8g+2,10g].

Finally, for three periodic terms, let
K=p+q+r
. Then

\displaystyle I_{p}+I_{q}+I_{r}

\displaystyle=Km+[3g,6g],

\displaystyle I_{p}+I_{q}+J_{r}

\displaystyle=Km+[8g+2,m]\cup(K+1)m+[1,g-2],

\displaystyle I_{p}+J_{q}+J_{r}

\displaystyle=(K+1)m+[3g+1,6g-1],

\displaystyle J_{p}+J_{q}+J_{r}

\displaystyle=(K+1)m+[8g+3,\min\{11g,m\}]\cup(K+2)m+[1,g-3].

Each right-hand side is contained in
C
. Therefore every sum of three distinct elements of
A_{g}
lies in
C
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

By Lemma
4
and (
3
), the only elements of
A_{g}
that could be forced are
1
,
e
, and
f
.

The smallest sum of three distinct elements of
A_{g}
is

1+g+(g+1)=2g+2,

so neither
1
nor
e=2g+1
can be forced.

It remains to exclude
f=6g+1
. The elements of
A_{g}
below
f
are exactly

1,\ g,\ g+1,\ \ldots,\ 2g,\ e.

The largest sum of three distinct such elements is

e+2g+(2g-1)=(2g+1)+2g+(2g-1)=6g<f.

Therefore
f
is not forced either.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We prove by induction on
n
that

n\in S_{1,g,g+1}\iff n\in A_{g}.

The claim is clear for
1\leq n\leq g+1
, since both sets contain exactly the integers
1
,
g
, and
g+1
in this range.

Now let
n>g+1
, and assume that the claim holds for every positive integer smaller than
n
. If
n\notin A_{g}
, then Proposition
2
gives a representation

n=a+b+c

with distinct
a,b,c\in A_{g}
. Since
a,b,c<n
, the induction hypothesis gives
a,b,c\in S_{1,g,g+1}
. Hence the greedy rule excludes
n
.

If
n\in A_{g}
, then Proposition
3
shows that
n
is not a sum of three distinct elements of
A_{g}
. By the induction hypothesis, the earlier terms of
S_{1,g,g+1}
are exactly the elements of
A_{g}
below
n
. Therefore
n
is not a sum of three distinct earlier terms of
S_{1,g,g+1}
, so the greedy rule admits
n
.

This completes the induction, and therefore
S_{1,g,g+1}=A_{g}
. The description (
1
) follows immediately.

After the first
g+4
terms

1,\ g,\ g+1,\ \ldots,\ 2g,\ 2g+1,\ 6g+1,

the sequence of residues repeats as

6g+2,\ 6g+3,\ \ldots,\ 7g+1,\ g,\ g+1,\ \ldots,\ 2g

modulo
m=10g+3
. This repeating block has length
2g+1
.
∎
