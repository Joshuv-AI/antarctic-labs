The evolution of cellular therapies in sarcoma: Breakthroughs, challenges, and future directions
Authors: Chalothorn Wannaphut, Prapassorn Thirasastr, John Livingston, Dejka Araujo, Anthony Conley, Emily Keung, Christina Roland, Neeta Somaiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCellular therapies representa promising area of immunotherapy for advanced cancers,therapies including T cell receptor (TCR) therapies, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapies, and natural killer (NK) cell therapies.Sarcomas pose unique challenges due to their molecular complexity and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). TCRtherapiesargeting antigens likeNY-ESO-1, MAGE-A4, and PRAME have shown efficacy, highlighted by the FDA’s accelerated approval of afamitresgene autoleucel targeting MAGE-A4 for synovial sarcoma.CAR-T cell therapies targeting HER2, GD2, and B7-H3, along with TIL, and NK cell therapies are also under investigation.However, many are in early stages of clinical development, and their effectiveness may vary by sarcoma subtype. Overcoming challenges such as immunosuppressive TME, antigen escape, lack of T-cell persistence, and off-target toxicities is crucial to improving outcomes for sarcoma patients. This review summarizes the evolution of cellular therapies,ongoing research, challenges, and future directions in this field.KEYWORDS: Cellular therapy, sarcoma, T cell receptor therapy, CAR-T cell therapy, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy

Introduction

Sarcoma is a rare and heterogeneous group of malignancies originating from mesenchymal tissues, encompassing both bone and soft tissue tumors. These cancers account for approximately 1% of all malignancies in adult individuals.1 Importantly, sarcomas are not a single disease but rather a collection of over 75 malignant histologic subtypes that differ substantially in biology, clinical behavior, and therapeutic vulnerability. These subtypes vary in antigen expression profiles, tumor mutational burden (TMB), degree and composition of immune infiltration, and responsiveness to immunotherapy; distinctions that carry direct implications for immune-based treatments. For localized disease, surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, and adjuvant or neoadjuvant therapy with radiation and/or chemotherapy is used in the high-risk cases, although such therapies are used at various rates for different sarcoma subtypes.2–4 Despite the use of these multimodal approaches, recurrence rates for large high-grade tumors are high. Outcomes for patients with recurrent, refractory, or advanced disease remain poor, and the chemotherapy regimens commonly used to treat such diseases (e.g., doxorubicin/ifosfamide and gemcitabine/docetaxel) have a high toxicity burden.5 The rarity and heterogeneity of sarcoma complicate the therapeutic landscape and highlight the necessity for rationally developed, biomarker-matched novel treatments.

As a result, immunotherapeutic strategies, including tumor vaccines, adoptive cell therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, are being actively explored to improve survival and achieve long-term disease control. While immune checkpoint inhibitors have revolutionized cancer treatment, only certain sarcoma subtypes, such as alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), benefit from these therapies. This limited effectiveness is likely due to the predominantly immune-desert tumor microenvironment (TME), which contains immunosuppressive infiltrating cell types.6–8 Given these limitations, adoptive cell therapy presents a promising approach by delivering tumor-reactive and tumor-targeted lymphocytes directly into the bloodstream, bypassing barriers to immune activation and expansion. This strategy has the potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy for this challenging disease.

Immune-based targeting of cancer was first documented in the late 19th century when Dr. William Coley observed tumor regression in a patient with sarcoma following a bacterial infection.9 The exploration of cellular therapies gained momentum in the early 2000 s, with initial studies focusing on adoptive cell therapy, particularly on using tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) for melanoma.10,11 The first genetically engineered T-cell therapy approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) was tisagenlecleucel, a CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell designed to treat refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia.12 This approval occurred in 2017, marking a milestone in cellular therapies. Multiple CAR-T products have been approved by the FDA for hematologic malignancies, including lymphomas and multiple myeloma. In contrast, regulatory approvals for adoptive cellular therapies in solid tumors remain limited. Nonetheless, advances in engineering lymphocytes to recognize intracellular tumor antigens and drive tumor regression have expanded the potential of adoptive cell therapy across a broader range of cancers. The recent FDA accelerated approval of afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel) for SS highlights the growing feasibility of engineered T cell therapies for solid tumors, offering hope for patients with few therapeutic options. Herein, we summarize the current cellular therapies for sarcoma, with a particular focus on adult sarcomas. We highlight key clinical data, ongoing trials, and future directions in this rapidly evolving field.

Material and method

Literature search strategy

A narrative literature review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov to identify relevant preclinical studies, translational research, and clinical trials related to adoptive cellular therapies in sarcoma. Searches included combinations of the terms “sarcoma,” “cellular therapy,” “T cell receptor T-cell,” “TCR-T,” “CAR-T,” “tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte,” “TIL,” and “natural killer cell therapy.” In addition, English-language studies, prospective clinical trials, landmark translational studies, and recent publications relevant to adult sarcoma populations were included.

Modalities of adoptive cellular therapies

Cellular therapies are categorized by the type of immune cells used and how they are modified (Figure 1). In TIL therapy, natural tumor-infiltrating T cells are isolated, expanded ex vivo, and reinfused to enhance the immune response against cancer cells.13 In CAR-T cell therapy, T cells are genetically modified to express synthetic receptors with extracellular antibody-like binding domains that recognize specific antigens on cancer cell surfaces.14 In contrast, T cell receptor (TCR) therapy, or TCR-T, utilizes native or minimally engineered native TCRs to recognize intracellular antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules on the surface of cancer cells (Table 1).15,16 Lastly, in natural killer (NK) cell therapy, the number and function of NK cells are enhanced by selection, differentiation, and ex vivo expansion or genetic engineering with CARs, using either autologous or allogeneic (off-the-shelf) NK cells. Each of these therapeutic approaches uniquely enhances anti-cancer immunity. Ongoing research aims to optimize their efficacy across a range of cancer types.

TCR-T therapy

TCR-T therapy involves engineering T cells to express specific TCRs, allowing them to recognize and target tumor antigens presented by MHC molecules, also called human leukocyte antigens (HLA) in humans, on the surface of cancer cells (Figure 2).

General principles of antigen recognition by the adaptive immune system (A), using T cell receptor (TCR) therapy (B), and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy (C). (A) native T cells recognize peptides presented on MHC class I and II molecules through T cell receptors (TCRs) with coreceptors CD8 and CD4. MHC class I molecules, present on all nucleated cells, display cytosolic peptides, while MHC class II molecules, found on antigen-presenting cells, present processed extracellular peptides. CD8+ cytotoxic T cells are essential for defending against infections and cancer. Upon recognizing an antigen presented on MHC class I, naïve cytotoxic T cells upregulate co-stimulatory receptors such as CD28 and inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 to prevent excessive activation. They eliminate cancer cells by releasing perforin, which forms pores in target cell membranes, and granzymes, which trigger apoptosis.17 (B, C) TCR-T cells interact with the specific MHC-peptide complex on a cancer cell. These TCRs are currently generated by modifying the αβ chains of native TCRs ex vivo. After specific MHC-antigen recognition, TCR-T cells signal through the native CD3 signaling complex, whereas CAR-T cells rely on synthetic receptors to recognize surface antigens with built-in signaling domains.

Endogenous TCRs naturally occur but are restricted by immunological tolerance, which protects against self-antigen responses. However, this limitation reduces T-cell repertoire diversity and the function of tumor-reactive immune cells. Hence, engineered TCR-Ts are synthetically introduced to allow T cells to specifically target tumor antigens, often with higher affinity; however, such affinity is restricted by MHC type.

Native T cells recognize peptides presented on MHC class I and II molecules through TCRs with coreceptors CD8 and CD4, respectively. MHC class I molecules, present on all nucleated cells including tumor cells, display cytosolic (intracellular) peptides that have been processed by the proteasome. In contrast, MHC class II molecules, found on antigen-presenting cells, present processed extracellular peptides. Historically, TCR-T therapy has primarily focused on CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, as they are the main cytotoxic effectors of the adaptive immune system and are essential for defending against infections and cancer. Additionally, MHC class I molecules, recognized by CD8+ T cells, present peptides from the entire intracellular proteome, including tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), neoantigens, and cancer germline antigens; this allows CD8+ T cell TCRs access to a broad range of targetable epitopes.18 More recently, TCR-T therapy products have included both autologous CD4+ and CD8+ T cell products that are transduced with engineered MHC class I-restricted TCRs, as previous studies have demonstrated that these CD4+ T cells can also exhibit cytotoxic effects against tumor cells.19 Since MHC class I molecules have confined binding grooves, they typically present 8–11 amino acid peptide fragments from the intracellular proteins, which are degraded by the proteasome into short peptides before being loaded onto the MHC via Transporter associated with Antigen Processing (TAP) within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER).20 TCRs make contact with both the peptide and the HLA molecule via complementary-determining regions (CDRs) that consist of germline-encoded CDR1 and CDR2 loops, which mediate HLA restriction, and somatically rearranged CDR3 loops that determine peptide specificity through direct contact. In TCR-T therapy, engineered αβ TCR chains are designed to recognize a specific peptide-HLA complex (pHLA) rather than the entire protein.21 As a result, TCR-T therapy is inherently specific to a particular peptide epitope of the target protein presented by a particular HLA allele, conferring dual specificity that defines both the precision and the limitation of the approach. Compared to CAR-T cells, which target surface antigens, TCR-T therapy can access a broader range of targetable cancer antigens by recognizing intracellular antigens.22,23

The TCR-T therapy process involves selecting suitable patients based on the patient’s HLA type and tumor antigen expression, collecting T cells for ex vivo modification, and reintroducing them into the patient.

To be eligible for TCR-T treatment, a patient must have a specific antigen-restricting HLA allele subtype that can present the peptide specific to the engineered TCR-T, and a tumor that tests positive for the selected antigen. Tumor antigen expression is confirmed using an analytically validated immunohistochemistry assay (Table 2). The HLA requirement for most clinically developed TCRs is typically restricted to HLA-A*02 alleles, particularly HLA-A*02:01.35 From real-world screening studies, around 44.8% of the population has HLA-A*02 status; 49.6% in Caucasians, 49.7% in Native Americans, 46.9% in Hispanics, 36% in Asian/Pacific Islanders, and 34.6% in African Americans.36 Including A * 02:02, A * 02:03, and A * 02:06 increased eligibility among Hispanic and Latino (A * 02:06), African American (A * 02:02), and Asian (A * 02:03 and A * 02:06) individuals.37 This HLA restriction may contribute to racial and ethnic disparities in eligibility, and current strategies to expand eligibility include developing TCR-Ts targeting alternative HLA alleles and pan-HLA approaches.

TCR-T has been developed to target cancers expressing specific TAAs, enabling selective targeting of malignant cells. To minimize off-target toxicity, the selected TCR-T target antigen should be absent or minimally expressed on normal tissues. A major class of TAAs developed for TCR-T therapy is cancer testis antigens, such as NY-ESO-1, MAGE-A4, and PRAME. Besides immune-privileged sites such as the testis (which do not express classical MHC class I molecules), cancer testis antigens are not typically expressed in normal adult tissues; rather, they are mostly expressed in various tumors.38 This restricted expression pattern minimizes the risk of damaging healthy tissues, making cancer testis antigens attractive targets for TCR-T therapy.39

Targeting neoantigens, which are tumor-specific peptides generated by somatic mutations, is a potential future TCR-T targeting strategy. Unlike TAAs that may be expressed in normal tissues, neoantigens are exclusively present on tumor cells, reducing the risk of off-target toxicity, making targeting neoantigens an attractive approach for personalized immunotherapy, particularly for patients with sarcomas that harbor unique recurrent mutations or gene fusions.40,41

To obtain autologous T cells from the patient, successful leukapheresis is required. TCR-T manufacturing involves transducing ex vivo native T-cells with a lentiviral vector encoding the affinity-enhanced TCR-T, expanding, and cryopreserving.15,21,33

The structure of TCRs of the engineered TCR-T consists of 2 primary chains, an edited alpha chain and an edited beta chain, which are engineered to confer a desired specificity. These chains combine to bind the antigen-MHC complex, providing both peptide-specific and MHC molecule–specific selectivity.42 TCR mispairing can occur when the therapeutic exogenous α/β TCR chains pair with endogenous α/β TCR chains, forming mixed dimers. This unintended pairing can reduce efficacy by lowering the concentration of antigen-specific TCRs and promote the formation of self-reactive TCRs, leading to autoimmune conditions such as graft-versus-host reaction.43,44 To mitigate this issue, several strategies have been developed, including TCR constant regions engineering (murinization and addition of cysteine disulfide bonds), single-chain TCR designs, and knockout of endogenous TCR genes.45–48 The HLA restriction and TCR mispairing challenge is essentially specific to TCR-T and does not apply to CAR-T and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy.

Before the engineered TCR-T cells are infused, patients typically undergo a lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimen, most commonly fludarabine plus cyclophosphamide.49 Lymphodepletion serves to reduce endogenous lymphocyte populations and regulatory immune elements, thereby creating a more permissive immunologic environment that enhances the expansion, engraftment, and persistence of the infused TCR-T cells.50 Bridging anti-cancer therapy is permissible between leukapheresis and lymphodepletion, as production time for engineered TCR-T products can vary.

When reintroduced to the patient, these engineered TCR-T cells target cells displaying the selected antigen presented on the selected MHC molecules. Once a TCR-T cell interacts with the specific MHC-peptide complex on a cancer cell, this engagement activates the intracellular CD3 signaling complex, initiating downstream pathways via immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs), leading to T cell activation, cytokine production, and cytotoxic responses. These signals drive the TCR-T cells to proliferate and exert cytotoxic effects, specifically against cells presenting the target antigen.21,51,52 (Figure 2). The signaling cascades through CD3 and ITAM, although shared with endogenous T cells, the magnitude and duration of signaling in TCR-T can be modified through engineering, which, weighing against cross-reactivity, is crucial. The persistence of TCR-T cells in the patient’s body is also essential for sustained anti-tumor activity; some of these TCR-T cells differentiate into memory T cells, allowing for prolonged immune surveillance and response to any residual cancer cells that present the target antigen.

In conclusion, TCR-T therapy involves modifying patients’ T cells ex vivo by introducing antigen-specific, MHC class I-restricted αβ-chain TCR constructs, which, upon recognition of processed intracellular antigens presented by specific MHC class I molecules, signal through the native CD3 signaling complex. This innovation leads to enhanced efficacy of the patient’s T cells in recognition and potency, as well as target restriction. In solid tumors, TCR-T therapy has to overcome challenges such as TCR mismatch, toxicity from on- and off-target antigen recognition, the risk of cytokine storms, and the complexities of the tumor microenvironment. Addressing these issues is essential for optimizing the efficacy and safety of TCR-T therapy in sarcoma.

CAR-T cell therapy

In CAR-T cell therapy, T cells are genetically modified to express CARs, enabling T cells to recognize and attack specific cancer cells. First, a patient’s T cells are collected and engineered to express a CAR, which consists of an extracellular binding domain, a hinge and transmembrane region, and intracellular signaling domains (Figure 2). The hinge and transmembrane region connect the binding domain to the intracellular signaling domain, enhancing stability and flexibility to allow effective engagement with the target antigen. The extracellular domain, often an antibody-derived single-chain variable fragment, binds to a specific antigen on the cancer cell surface, initiating targeted activity without relying on a specific MHC.53 The intracellular portion of the CAR includes the CD3 zeta chain, which provides the primary activation signal upon antigen binding, as well as at least 1 costimulatory domain (in second- and later-generation CARs), such as CD28 or 4–1BB. These costimulatory domains support T cell expansion, persistence, and survival after activation. This design allows CAR-T cells to specifically target and respond to TAAs, improving their capacity to kill cancer cells efficiently.54 An ideal target for CAR-T is a surface antigen that is highly and uniformly expressed on tumor cells but absent or minimally expressed on normal tissues, maximizing efficacy while reducing the risk of off-tumor toxicity.

After infusion into the patient, CAR-T cells circulate through the body and recognize cancer cells expressing the target antigen. Then, the single-chain variable fragment binds to the antigen, initiating a series of intracellular signals, beginning with activation through the CD3 zeta chain. This activation triggers downstream signaling pathways, leading to the release of cytotoxic granules and cytokines.

Additionally, CAR-T cells secrete cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-2, further stimulating and recruiting immune cells, amplifying the immune response against the tumor.55 Through this cytokine-mediated stimulation and recruitment, CAR-T cells establish an immune synapse with the target cell, enabling efficient and precise cytotoxic action. The persistence of CAR-T cells in the patient’s body is a critical factor for long-term therapeutic efficacy. After their activation and proliferation, some CAR-T cells may differentiate into memory T cells, which remain in the patient’s system for extended periods, providing continued surveillance against cancer recurrence.56 Incorporation of costimulatory signals such as CD28 and 4–1BB in second- and later-generation CAR-T cells amplifies T cell activation and supports the expansion and persistence of a patient’s CAR-T cells.57 This led to a high response rate for the treatment of certain hematologic malignancies, such as B-cell leukemia and multiple myeloma, eventually resulting in FDA approval.58 Activated CAR-T cells subsequently eliminate tumor cells through well-established cytotoxic pathways and cytokine release, resulting in tumor cell death.51

The major barriers limiting durable responses to CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors especially sarcoma, include impaired tumor infiltration of immune cells, a highly immunosuppressive TME, CAR-T cell exhaustion and limited persistence, and heterogeneous expression of TAAs.59,60 The TME can be enriched with immunosuppressive cell populations including regulatory T cells, M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, which secrete cytokines such as IL-10, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and IL-4 that suppress antitumor immune responses and promote CAR-T dysfunction. Additionally, TAAs are overexpressed in tumors but can also be expressed at low levels in normal tissues, increasing the risk of on-target/off-tumor toxicity. Variability in TAA expression between and within tumors further contributes to antigen escape and limits the durability of CAR-T responses.

Several CAR-T cell therapies have been evaluated in solid tumors, with a wide range of extracellular antigens targeted in early-phase clinical studies. Examples of such therapies include disialoganglioside-directed CAR-T cells for neuroblastomas and gliomas.61,62; HER2-targeted CAR-T cells for HER2-expressing sarcomas and other solid tumors; mesothelin-directed CAR-T cells for mesothelioma, pancreatic, and ovarian cancers.63; and GPC3-targeted CAR-T cells for hepatocellular carcinoma; however, data on durable objective responses have remained limited.64 A systematic review of 794 registered trials on CAR-T therapy found that 182 (23%) trials involved solid tumors, with the most commonly targeted extracellular proteins being mesothelin (13%), members of the epidermal growth factor receptor family (8%), and GD2 (8%), as well as more diverse targets such as CLDN18.2, CEA, MUC1, PSCA, and guanylate cyclase-C.65 These findings highlight both the breadth of antigen exploration and the ongoing challenge of achieving sustained clinical responses in solid tumors.

TIL therapy

TIL therapy is an adoptive cell therapy approach that targets cancer cells using a patient’s own T cells, which are naturally present in the TME. TILs are a subset of T cells that migrate to tumor sites, likely in response to TAAs, which are uniquely expressed by cancer cells and identified as foreign by the immune system. These cells are isolated directly from the patient’s tumor, expanded in vitro, and reintroduced to the patient to enhance the patient’s immune system’s response against the cancer.66

During TIL therapy, first, a portion of the patient’s tumor is acquired through surgery (preferred) or biopsy to collect TILs (Figure 1). Next, these TILs are cultured in the laboratory and expanded with IL-2, a cytokine that promotes T cell growth and survival. This expansion allows for the selection and proliferation of TILs with the highest reactivity to tumor antigens, resulting in a large population of T cells primed to target the patient’s cancer. Through this process, billions of TILs can be produced for therapeutic use.66

Prior to TIL infusion, the patient undergoes a lymphodepletion regimen that includes chemotherapy. This preparatory step removes existing lymphocytes, including regulatory T cells, which may inhibit the activity of TILs and may reduce competition for resources, such as IL-2, in the patient’s body.50 The infusion of the expanded TILs is followed by several doses of IL-2 to support the survival and function of the TILs.10

Within the body, TILs seek out and bind to cancer cells that express the specific tumor antigens recognized by their TCRs. Unlike TCR-T and CAR-T, TILs do not target a single antigen but a variety of tumor-specific antigens. Upon encountering these antigens, TILs become activated and initiate a cytotoxic response. This activation involves TCR signaling, leading to the release of cytotoxic granules that contain perforin and granzymes. These granules create pores in the cell membrane and activate apoptotic pathways.67 Additionally, TILs secrete cytokines such as IFN-γ, which recruit and activate other immune cells, enhancing the immune response within the TME.68

TIL therapy has demonstrated its greatest clinical success in metastatic melanoma, where the high mutational burden and abundance of tumor-reactive lymphocytes have resulted in durable clinical responses.69

Patient selection for TIL therapy does not rely on tumor antigen screening or HLA matching; however, it is heavily dependent on the presence of adequate tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in the tumor sample. Consequently, tumors with insufficient numbers of tumor-reactive TILs may be less suitable for this approach.70

NK cell therapy

NK cell therapy is an immunotherapeutic approach that uses the cytotoxic properties of NK cells to target and eliminate cancer cells. As a part of the innate immune system, NK cells can recognize and kill abnormal cells, including tumor cells, without prior sensitization. This ability is controlled by a balance of activation and inhibitory receptors on the surface of NK cells. NK cells are triggered to activate and initiate cytotoxic activities when they detect cells with low levels of MHC class I molecules (i.e., missing self), which are often downregulated on cancer cells to allow cancer cells to escape T cell surveillance.71

NK cell therapy typically involves isolating NK cells from either the patient (autologous) or a healthy donor (allogeneic). Once isolated, these NK cells are expanded and activated in vitro, often through stimulation with cytokines such as IL-2 or IL-15, to enhance their cytotoxic properties and proliferative capacity. In addition to unmodified NK cells, NK cell therapy now includes target-specific NK products such as cytokine-activated NK cells, NK cell engagers, and genetically engineered NK cells, including TCR-NK and CAR-NK cells that express receptors directed against defined tumor antigens.72 Unlike T cells, NK cells do not require HLA-restricted antigen presentation for target recognition; instead, their activity is regulated by a balance of activation and inhibitory receptors, with HLA class I interactions contributing to NK cell education and self-tolerance.73,74

Once infused into the patient, NK cells seek out target cells displaying stress-induced ligands such as MIC-A or MIC-B, or cells deficient in MHC class I molecules through cell surface inhibitory KIR and NKG2A receptors.75 NK cell activation is mediated by activating receptors such as NKG2D, NKp30, NKp44, and NKp46, which bind to ligands such as MICA, MICB, and ULBP, which are expressed on tumor cells. Additionally, evidence suggests that NK cells can also evoke antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity through CD16, also known as FcγRIIIa, binding to the Fc region of specific antibodies.75,76 Activation through these pathways induces NK cell degranulation, resulting in the delivery of perforin and granzymes into target cells, thereby initiating apoptotic signaling cascades and tumor cell death.77

In addition to direct cytotoxicity, NK cells secrete cytokines, including IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). These cytokines enhance the immune response by promoting antigen presentation and activating other immune cells within the TME.78 The release of cytokines by NK cells supports a broader immune response, assisting in the recruitment and activation of both innate and adaptive immune cells.79

CAR-NK cell therapy is a promising concept and has shown promising preclinical results; it is currently under active investigation in early-phase trials for solid tumors, with no FDA-approved indications for any cancer. Multiple early-phase clinical trials are evaluating this approach across a range of solid tumors, targeting antigens including HER2 (solid tumors; NCT075108022, NCT07589517, NCT07641023), ROBO1 (pancreatic and other solid tumors; NCT 03931720, NCT03941457), NKG2D (colorectal cancer and other solid tumors; NCT05213195, NCT05776355, NCT07021534), and CD70 (renal cell carcinoma and osteosarcoma; NNCT07551349, NCT07072234). CAR-NK cell therapy has advantages over CAR-T cell therapy, including innate cytotoxicity, a better safety profile, and the feasibility of scalable, off-the-shelf manufacturing from umbilical cord blood. This method reduces the risk of graft-versus-host disease and simplifies collection.80 However, CAR-NK cells have limited efficacy against solid tumors, although they generally present a lower risk of severe side effects, such as cytokine release syndrome.81 Their effectiveness is limited by issues like short persistence, poor trafficking, and a challenging tumor microenvironment. Strategies like combination therapy with cytokine support and genetic engineering are being explored to improve their therapeutic impact.82

Compared to the CAR-NK cell approach, TCR-NK cell therapy is currently largely in the preclinical development phase and has recently been shown to be promising in preclinical models, with activity against HLA-class I-deficient tumor cells that were resistant to T-cell-based therapy.83 There is only 1 early-phase clinical trial exploring the safety and antitumor activity of NY – ESO-1 TCR/IL-15 N in SS and MRCL (Table 4).

Published clinical data for adoptive cell therapy in sarcoma

Engineered TCRs

NY-ESO-1–targeting TCRs

The development of TCR engineering for sarcoma treatment has progressed through multiple phases, beginning with Robbins et al. in 2011,24 who conducted the first clinical study in patients with refractory SS undergoing NY-ESO-1–specific TCR-T therapy, which uses an engineered TCR to recognize the peptide SLLMWITQC, corresponding to residues 157 to 165 of NY-ESO-1 (i.e., NY-ESO-1:157–165), plus high-dose IL-2 after a nonmyeloablative chemotherapy regimen. This study marked the first clinical application of genetically engineered T cells targeting NY-ESO-1, a cancer testis antigen expressed in approximately 80% of SS, 88% of myxoid round cell liposarcoma (MRCL), and 35% of myxofibrosarcomas on examination by immunohistochemistry; this antigen is absent in most normal tissues.84,85

The trial reported a 61% overall response rate (ORR) in patients with SS (n = 18), with one patient achieving a complete response (CR) that was ongoing 1 y after T-cell infusion. The partial responses were sustained for 3 to 18 months, with no on-target off-tumor toxicities observed.24,25 An updated analysis of the same patient cohort found a 3-y survival rate of 38% and a 5-y survival rate of 14%.25 Vaccination with the recombinant APIVOX-ESO virus in addition to TCR-T did improve response rates, although the cohort comprised only five patients. Notably, a combined analysis with a melanoma cohort revealed that responses were associated with a higher number of T cells administered and IFNγ ELISPOTs generated by T cells after NY-ESO-1 peptide-pulsed stimulation.

In 2018, D’Angelo et al. assessed the efficacy of autologous T cells engineered to express the NY-ESO-1 .c259 TCR, which target NY-ESO-1–specific HLA-A*02–restricted peptide SLLMWITQC; these T cells, termed NY-ESO-1 SPEAR T-cells (GSK3377794), were administered without IL-2 in patients with metastatic SS.50 The eligibility criteria included NY-ESO-1 expression, confirmed by immunohistochemistry (≥50% of tumor cells express 2+ or 3+) and HLA-A*02–positivity. The trial observed a 50% ORR (n = 6/12), including 1 CR, and a median duration of response of 30.9 weeks. The reported median overall survival (OS) was 120 weeks, and the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 15 weeks. Common side effects included lymphopenia (100%), leukopenia (92%), neutropenia (83%), anemia (83%), hypophosphatemia (75%), and thrombocytopenia (67%), with cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurring in six patients (three patients with grade 3 CRS and three patients with grade 1 CRS).26

In an analysis of an additional three cohorts (cohorts 2 through 4), all of which received the same TCR treatment but different lymphodepletion regimens, Ramachandran et al. reported that one of the cohorts (cohort 2) had low antigen expression by immunohistochemistry (defined as more than 1+ in >1% of tumor cells but less than 2+/3+ in ≥50% of tumor cells).27 These findings underscore the crucial role of antigen expression, the dose of the lymphodepletion regimen, and the presence of fludarabine in the regimen, as demonstrated by cohort 3, which received only cyclophosphamide (1.8 g/m.2/day twice daily), was terminated due to lack of tumor response. The response rate (n = 4/15) for cohort 4, which received a lower-dose lymphodepletion regimen of fludarabine 30 mg/m.2/day three times daily + cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m.2/day three times daily, was lower than those for cohorts 1 and 2 (n = 6/12 and n = 4/10, respectively), which received a higher-doses lymphodepletion regimen of (fludarabine 30 mg/m.2/day four times daily + cyclophosphamide 1800 mg/m.2/day twice daily).

Among 42 treated patients, SPEAR T-cell persistence was assessed by qPCR in peripheral blood and by RNA in situ hybridization in available post-treatment tumor biopsies. Circulating SPEAR T cells were detected in post-infusion samples from all patients and persisted for at least 6 months in all responders. Furthermore, SPEAR T cells were detected within the TME as late as 28 months post-infusion in one patient, and circulating SPEAR T cells were detected at 12 months in another, demonstrating the potential for durable persistence of engineered T cells.

In 2025, D’Angelo et al. published the first peer-reviewed prospective clinical efficacy results for letetresgene autoleucel (letecel), the NY-ESO-1-specific SPEAR T cell, in patients with advanced/metastatic myxoid/round cell liposarcoma (MRCL).28 Based on the results of this pilot study (NCT02992743), the FDA granted letecel a breakthrough therapy designation for MRCL on January 16, 2025. In this multicenter study, 20 patients received letecel following reduced-dose (cohort 1) or standard-dose (cohort 2) lymphodepletion. The investigator-assessed ORR was 20% (95% CI, 2.5–55.6) in cohort 1 and 40% (95% CI, 12.2–73.8) in cohort 2, with a median duration of response of 5.3 months and 7.5 months for cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. Median PFS was 5.4 and 8.7 months for cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. Toxic effects were consistent with those of T-cell therapy and lymphodepletion, including CRS and cytopenia. Post hoc correlational analyses demonstrated robust T cell expansion and measurable persistence, supporting the mechanistic activity of letecel in MRCL. To our knowledge, this study was the first to demonstrate the clinical activity of letecel in HLA-A*02–positive, NY-ESO-1–expressing MRCL in a multicenter prospective trial, further validating NY-ESO-1 as a compelling therapeutic target for treatment of this histologic subtype. All trials for SS and MRCL with letecel are included in the ongoing IGNYTE-ESO master protocol (NCT03967223) for long-term follow-up.29

Another TCR-T cell that targets the same NY-ESO-1157–165 epitope in complex with HLA-A*02:01 (TAEST16001) was initially investigated in HLA-A*02:01–positive patients with advanced soft tissue sarcoma from 2020 to 2021. This phase I trial enrolled a total of 12 patients who had SS (n = 10/12), MRCL (n = 1/12), or dedifferentiated liposarcoma (n = 1/12). The positive NY-ESO-1 expression cutoff was more than 20%, as determined by immunohistochemistry. The protocol involved a lower-dose lymphodepletion regimen followed by a median of 3.24 × 10.9 TAEST16001 cells plus low-dose IL-2 (500,000 IU twice daily for 14 d). An efficacy analysis revealed an ORR of 41.7% and a median PFS of 7.2 months (95% CI, 2.5–11.8), while the median OS was immature at the time of data cutoff. Although most common grade 3 or higher adverse events were likely caused by lymphodepletion, 2 patients experienced CRS.30 The efficacy of TAEST16001 is being further investigated in an ongoing phase IIa trial by Liu et al., which demonstrated an ORR of 50% (n = 4/8) as of January 2024, reported at the ASCO meeting in 2024.86

MAGE-A4–targeting TCR

In 2017, Hong et al. initiated a phase I study evaluating afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel, formerly ADP-A2M4), a TCR targeting MAGE-A4, in HLA-A*02–positive patients with MAGE-A4 RNA or protein expression in one or more tumor samples across nine tumor types, including advanced heavily pre-treated SS (42.1%, n = 16/38). The study reported an ORR of 43.8% (n = 7/16), a stable disease (SD) of 50% (n = 8/16), and a median PFS of 20.4 weeks in the SS cohort.33 All responding patients had high tumor MAGE-A4 expression (H-score ≥ 200, on a scale of 0–300), and were treated with afami-cel doses > 9.5 × 10.9 T-cells, except one patient who received 4.5 × 10.9 cells. The two treatment-related deaths were among the patients who received the highest lymphodepletion dose regimen (cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m.2/day × 3 d + fludarabine 30 mg/m.2/day × 4 d) and who were > 70 y old, and one of these two patients had SS. One death was a consequence of aplastic anemia, which was suspected to be caused by treatment with 3,600 mg/m.2 cyclophosphamide because MAGE-A4 expression was absent in a bone marrow sample, and the other death was from an ischemic cerebrovascular event. After these deaths, the high-dose cyclophosphamide lymphodepletion regimen was discontinued. The lower-dose lymphodepletion chemotherapy regimen of fludarabine 30 mg/m.2 for 4 d and cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m.2 for 3 d was selected for the subsequent phase II SPEARHEAD-1 trial. This choice was made for its better toxicity profile while maintaining similar serum IL-15 levels, which are important for promoting the survival and memory formation of adoptive effector T cells.15,33,87 The median post-LD-to-pre-LD ratio of IL-15 was 18.25 for the high LD group and 12.87 for the low LD group (p = .13).

In the subsequent SPEARHEAD-1 phase II trial, D’Angelo et al. evaluated afami-cel in patients with advanced SS (n = 44) or MRCL (n = 8) who expressed HLA-A*02 and MAGE-A4. The study reported an ORR of 39% and 25% in patients with SS and MRCL, respectively. In the SS cohort, the median PFS by independent review was 3.8 months (95% CI, 2.8–6.4) and the median OS was 15.4 months (95% CI, 10.9–not estimable). CRS was observed in 71% of patients (n = 37/52), including one grade 3 CRS event, and no treatment-related deaths were reported.15

In August 2024, the U.S. FDA granted accelerated approval to afami-cel, commercially known as Tecelra.R, for the treatment of adults with unresectable or metastatic SS, marking the first FDA-approved TCR-T therapy for cancer. Eligibility criteria include prior chemotherapy, HLA-A*02–positivity, and MAGE-A4 tumor expression confirmed by an FDA-approved immunohistochemistry companion diagnostic. These developments reflect a landmark milestone for TCR-based therapies, offering a novel therapeutic option for patients with advanced sarcomas.15

PRAME–targeting TCR

PRAME, an intracellular cancer testis antigen, functions by inhibiting apoptosis and retinoic acid receptor signal transduction, thereby causing tumorigenesis.88 It is diffusely positive as designated by 4+ staining on immunohistochemistry in 55% of SS, 82% of MRCL, 40% of angiosarcomas, 67% of pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcomas, 57% of embryonal rhabdomyosarcomas, 72% of intimal sarcomas, 100% of thoracic SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated tumors, and 70% of melanomas.89 The phase I study of IMA203, a PRAME-specific TCR-T therapy, demonstrated a confirmed ORR of 28.9% and an ORR (confirmed plus unconfirmed) of 52.5% across PRAME-positive, HLA-A*02:01–positive solid tumors. Tumors with high PRAME expression responded to treatment more frequently than those with low PRAME expression, and durable responses were observed, with a maximum duration of response of 23 months. The IMA203 therapy had a favorable safety profile with low rates of severe CRS (4.9%) and no severe neurotoxicity.34 In data presented at the 2026 American Society of Clinical Oncology Annual Meeting, Araujo et al. reported that PRAME-directed TCR-T therapy achieved a 50% confirmed objective response rate, including one CR, in 20 efficacy-evaluable patients with heavily pretreated SS. Responding patients experienced a median tumor reduction of −63.4%, with a median duration of response of 12.7 months and a median overall survival of 18 months.16 A summary of clinical trials evaluating TCR-based therapies in sarcoma is provided in Table 2.

CAR-T cells

HER2–targeting CAR-T

In 2015, Ahmed et al. reported the safety and efficacy of HER2-CAR-T cells with CD28 zeta signaling domain in a phase I/II trial for patients with recurrent/refractory HER2-positive sarcomas. The cohort included 16 patients with osteosarcoma (n = 16/19, 85%) and one patient with Ewing sarcoma (5%), one patient with primitive neuroectodermal tumor (5%), and one patient with desmoplastic small round cell tumor (5%). The HER2-CAR-T cell therapy was well tolerated, and no dose-limiting toxicities were observed. Among the 17 patients analyzed, 4 (23.5%) experienced SD lasting between 12 weeks and 14 months. HER2-CAR-T cells persisted in the patients’ bloodstream for up to 6 weeks. Three of four patients with stable disease underwent tumor excision, with one demonstrating ≥ 90% tumor necrosis on pathology. The median OS of all patients was 10.3 months (range, 5.1 to 29.1 months).63

The authors further studied the safety and efficacy of the same HER2-CAR-T therapy in a second phase I trial that enrolled patients to be treated for a maximum duration of 6 months, with re-enrollment as an option. Among the 13 enrolled patients, one re-enrolled, contributing to 14 treatments across 13 patients with osteosarcoma (n = 8), rhabdomyosarcoma (n = 4), SS (n = 1), and metastatic primitive neuroectodermal tumor (n = 1). Overall, three of the 14 treatments achieved a CR (21.4%), including two CRs in the same patient with rhabdomyosarcoma who was re-enrolled at recurrence, and one CR in a patient with osteosarcoma. In addition, four achieved stability, resulting in an SD rate of 28.6% and a progressive disease rate of 50% of enrollments. Median PFS and OS were 2.4 months (95% CI, 1.3–8.7 months) and 8.2 months (95% CI, 4.4–17 months), respectively. Durable remission was observed among responders, with one rhabdomyosarcoma patient maintaining remission for more than 6 y following the second CR, and one osteosarcoma patient remaining disease-free for 42 months after pathological CR following lung nodule resection. Patient in cohort C received 1 × 10.8 CAR-T cells per m.2 and experienced dose-limiting toxicities, including grade 3–4 CRS, establishing the maximum tolerated dose as 1 × 10.8 total T cells per m.2 infused after fludarabine/cyclophosphamide lymphodepletion (cohort B). No cases of immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were reported, and cardiac function assessed by echocardiography at 6 weeks post-infusion remained unchanged from baseline. Quantitative PCR demonstrated a significant increase in HER2 CAR-Transgene levels in peripheral blood in cohort B, with a more modest increase observed in cohort A, compared with historical controls from the prior study.90

GD2-targeting CAR-T

In 2023, Locatelli et al. reported the feasibility and safety of GD2-CART01, a third-generation GD2-targeting CAR-T cell therapy, in a phase I-II clinical trial for relapsed/refractory high-risk neuroblastoma. These third-generation CAR-T cells incorporate both CD28 and 4–1BB as costimulatory domains. The CAR-T cells expanded in vivo and were detectable in 26 of 27 patients with a median of three months and up to 30 months after infusion. The objective response rate (ORR) was 63% (n = 17/27; CR, 33%; partial response, 30%). CRS occurred in 20 of 27 patients (74%), and of these, 95% were grade 1 or 2 CRS. The only occurrence of grade 3 CRS resolved rapidly after tocilizumab infusion. The iCasp9 suicide gene, which is a safety inducible switch leading to rapid death of cells expressing the construct, was activated by infusing rimiducid, a dimerizing agent, with rapid elimination of GD2-CART01 in 1 patient due to altered consciousness.61 The study found a 3-y OS of 60%, showing promising results for targeting GD2.

Apart from neuroblastoma, which expresses GD2 in 85% to 93% of cases,92 other sarcomas, including 84% of osteosarcoma, 50% of rhabdomyosarcoma, 40% of Ewing, and 10% of desmoplastic small round cell tumor, were also reported to have overexpression of GD2 by immunohistochemistry.93 A recent phase I trial by Sabina Kaczanowska et al. demonstrated the feasibility and safety of a third-generation GD2.CD28. OX40.z CAR-T in GD2-positive osteosarcoma (n = 12) and neuroblastoma (n = 3). Unlike other trials, this trial used only cyclophosphamide (1800 mg/m.2/day for 2 d) for lymphodepletion. Out of the 12 evaluable patients, 2 patients experienced progression (16.7%) and 10 experienced SD (83.3%), of whom only 2 continued to have SD at 60 d.91 Although the outcomes were poor, a subsequent analysis found that CXCR3-positive monocytes in pre-treatment apheresis were associated with good CAR-T expansion. Further studies and the development of GD2-targeting CAR-T cells for sarcoma are still needed before such therapy can become the standard of care, and clinical trials of this therapy are summarized in Table 3.

TIL therapies

In a 2021 study, Mullinax et al. reported the feasibility of generating TIL from 70 soft tissue sarcoma specimens, including dedifferentiated liposarcoma, UPS, well-differentiated liposarcoma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST), MRCL, gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), and other types that were obtained from patients who underwent curative-intent resection. TIL cultures using both fragment and digest methods achieved successful initial culture rates of 91% and 100%, respectively.94 As expected, the results for generating TIL cultures were much inferior to those reported for malignant melanoma; in fact, only 16 of the 70 tumor tissues (25%) generated a primary TIL culture above the threshold required for subsequent expansion to a clinically significant infusion product (≥2 × 10.7 live lymphocytes). Furthermore, only 40% to 56% of the resulting TIL product contained tumor-specific reactivity in soft tissue sarcoma, compared to a previous report in melanoma, which demonstrated successful TIL generation in approximately 72% of specimens under optimized culture conditions, with tumor-specific reactivity observed in about one-third of expanded TIL cultures.95 TIL expansion had large interpatient variability and was not unique to the histologic subtype.

A recently published pilot study, ATLAS-IT-04 (NCT03725605), has confirmed the safety and feasibility of combination treatment with intratumoral LTX-315 injection and intravenous infusion of autologous, in vitro-expanded TILs in 6 heavily pretreated patients with advanced/metastatic soft tissue sarcoma.96 The treatment protocol consisted of intratumoral LTX-315 injection, an oncolytic virus previously proved to increase intralesional CD8+T cells in solid tumors, followed by lymphodepleting chemotherapy (fludarabine 25 mg/m.2 × 5 d and cyclophosphamide 60 mg/kg × 2 d), TIL infusion, and subcutaneous IL-2 (2 MIU × 14 d).97,98 TIL expansion was successful in 4 of 6 patients (67%). While no objective responses were observed (ORR 0%), stable disease at 6 weeks was achieved in 3 of 4 treated patients, with progression-free survival ranging from 72 to 208 d (mean ~22 weeks). All treatments were well-tolerated with manageable toxic effects.96 Grade 3–4 adverse events were primarily related to lymphodepletion (neutropenia and thrombocytopenia in all 4 patients) and TIL/IL-2 infusion (fever, dyspnea, fatigue), whereas LTX-315 itself caused no grade 3–4 toxicities. Owing to the lack of a control arm in the study, further exploration of the clinical impact of TIL infusion is still needed.

Notably, Lifileucel (LN-145, Iovance Biotherapeutics’ autologous TIL product), a tumor-derived autologous TIL that was granted FDA approval in 2024 for previously treated unresectable or metastatic melanoma, is being evaluated in an ongoing single-center pilot trial (NCT05607095) in patients with metastatic or unresectable UPS and dedifferentiated liposarcoma. The protocol involves nonmyeloablative lymphodepletion followed by LN-145 infusion and high-dose IV IL-2 (600,000 IU/kg, up to 6 doses); preliminary data showed a confirmed ORR of 50% (n = 6). This led to a plan to initiate a single-arm registrational trial for second-line advanced UPS and dedifferentiated liposarcoma in the second quarter of 2026, seeking an accelerated approval pathway with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).99

Rationale and evidence for emerging antigen targets under active investigation in sarcoma

Several cell-surface antigens have emerged as promising therapeutic targets for cellular immunotherapy in sarcoma and are currently under active clinical investigation. GD2, a disialoganglioside (cell-surface glycolipid), is overexpressed in several sarcoma subtypes, particularly osteosarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma.100,101 GPC3, a cell-surface heparan sulfate proteoglycan and oncofetal antigen, is overexpressed in certain sarcoma subtypes, particularly osteosarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma.102 Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase, is frequently overexpressed across multiple adult soft tissue sarcoma subtypes, including synovial sarcoma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors, pleomorphic malignant fibrous histiocytoma and epithelioid sarcoma.103 Ephrin type-A receptor 2 (EphA2), another receptor tyrosine kinase, is overexpressed in bone sarcomas, such as osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma.104,105 In addition, the NKG2D receptor recognizes eight stress-induced ligands (MICA/B and ULBP1–6) that are commonly upregulated on sarcoma cells.106,107

The SSX family, with the SSX2 gene within this family, represents a class of cancer testis antigens that have highly restricted expression in normal tissues and are frequently overexpressed in SS, making SSX2 a promising target for TCR-based adoptive cell therapy.108,109 HER2 is overexpressed in a subset of osteosarcomas and other sarcomas, allowing exploration of HER2 CAR-T cell therapy.63,90

SART1 is another immunogenic antigen expressed in various cancers, including sarcomas, and is currently under preclinical evaluation for T cell-based therapy.110,111 B7-H3 (CD276), an extracellular antigen and member of the B7 family, is highly expressed in osteosarcoma and multiple soft tissue sarcomas, including leiomyosarcoma, liposarcoma, UPS, and SS. CAR-T cell therapies targeting B7-H3 are currently in early-phase clinical trials and are demonstrating their potential as a viable target for solid tumor immunotherapy.112

Apart from tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), which are non-mutated self-antigens that may potentially trigger central T cell tolerance and lead to poor T cell responses, a novel group of target antigens for immunotherapy being explored is neoantigens. Neoantigens are self-antigens generated by tumor cells due to genomic alterations. These alterations provide a tumor-specific advantage while being absent in normal tissue. An example of T cell therapy targeting neoantigens includes a Phase I study of T cell receptor (TCR) therapy targeting the TP53 R175H mutant in solid tumors (NCT05877599). This concept encourages further studies on fusion proteins found in fusion-associated sarcomas, such as SYT-SSX1 in synovial sarcoma and EWS-FLI in Ewing sarcoma, which are promising avenues for future development.113,114

Integrating neoantigen discovery with advanced TCR, CAR-T cell, and NK cell engineering is poised to transform the landscape of therapies for sarcoma. Continued research into antigen specificity, tumor expression profiles, and preclinical safety testing will be essential to translate these emerging targets into effective and durable clinical therapies.

Summary of current clinical evidence on cellular therapy for sarcoma and targeting antigens.

Ongoing clinical trials continue to explore the safety and efficacy of various cellular therapies across multiple sarcoma subtypes. These trials encompass TCR-T cell therapies targeting intracellular cancer testis antigens such as NY-ESO-1, MAGE-A4, and PRAME; CAR-T cell therapies directed against surface antigens including HER2, GD2, B7-H3, GPC3, CD22, and NKG2D; and TIL approaches. Most TCR trials require HLA-A*02 positivity and specific antigen expression by immunohistochemistry, with conditioning regimens commonly incorporating fludarabine and cyclophosphamide. CAR-T cell trials have expanded to include both conventional and armored constructs, with many trials focusing on osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, SS, and other soft-tissue sarcomas. Notably, early-phase trials evaluating cytokine-armored and suicide gene-modified CAR constructs are underway to enhance safety and persistence. TIL-based therapies, including combinations with oncolytic peptides such as LTX-315 or IL-12 expression, are also being investigated for metastatic or refractory sarcoma. Challenges in cellular therapies for sarcoma

In the clinical landscape of adoptive cell therapy for sarcoma, TCR-T has achieved notable advancement. This progress is underscored by the regulatory accelerated approval of afamitresgene autoleucel, primarily due to the identification of well-characterized intracellular cancer-testis antigens (e.g., NY-ESO-1, MAGE-A4), which are uniformly overexpressed at high levels and presented on restricted HLA alleles in specific sarcoma subtypes, such as SS and MRCL.15 Conversely, CAR-T, TIL, and NK cell therapies are still in the early stages of clinical development and exhibit highly variable clinical effectiveness.63,94 This delay in progress is mainly due to the scarcity of truly tumor-restricted surface antigens, significant antigen heterogeneity within the complex histology of sarcomas, and the immunosuppressive mesenchymal stroma that impairs the trafficking, survival, and sustained activation of immune effectors.115,116

An immunosuppressive TME in sarcoma

Besides specific subtypes such as ASPS, SS, and UPS, sarcomas are generally considered immunologically “cold” tumors and are typically characterized by an immunosuppressive TME, low tumor mutation burden, reduced T cell infiltration, and increased numbers of HIF-1α, macrophages, and neutrophils.6,115,117,118 Tumor-associated macrophages comprise the sarcoma TME at a higher density than TILs. These macrophages, which are predominantly of the M2 type, are of an immunosuppressive phenotype and are associated with a poorer prognosis.119,120

The sarcoma TME often features hypoxia, abnormal vasculature, and extensive stromal interactions, creating physical and biochemical barriers to immune cell infiltration,121–123 and the immunosuppressive TME further impedes infiltration by any T cell therapy.124 Furthermore, upregulation of inhibitory ligands, such as PD-L1, was observed in 24–58% of STS cases, depending on subtype, which were also shown to be associated with a worse prognosis.125,126 The expression of PDL-1 has been shown to promote T cell exhaustion and immune evasion, thereby limiting the efficacy of engineered T cell therapies. Distinct from epithelial malignancies, sarcomas originate from mesenchymal lineages, rendering their stroma uniquely hostile. A primary driver of this resistance is the dense accumulation of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs); however, because both the malignant cells and the CAFs share a similar mesenchymal origin, distinguishing between the tumor cells and the supporting stroma remains uniquely challenging, with some subtypes like rhabdomyosarcoma directly producing their own extracellular matrix (ECM) with minimal CAF involvement.127,128 When present, these CAFs actively deposit an immune-exclusion ECM architecture rich in fibrillar collagens, collagen VI, and tenascin-C rather than typical epithelial matrix components, which physically restricts and impedes T-cell migration and trafficking.116 Furthermore, sarcoma CAFs secrete high levels of TGF-β, stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1/CXCL12), and matrix metalloproteinases.129–131 This chemical cocktail suppresses effector T-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity by downregulating key cytolytic molecules such as perforin and granzymes,132,133 while actively recruiting monocytes and myeloid-derived suppressor cells to the tumor core via the SDF-1/CXCR4 and IL-6/STAT3 axis and CCL2/CCR2 signaling pathways.134,135 Disrupting these specialized collagen networks has been shown preclinically to rescue TIL infiltration, highlighting how these mechanisms conspire to create an impenetrable, immunosuppressive niche unique to mesenchymal tumors.136

Within the sarcoma TME, myeloid-derived suppressor cells suppress T cell proliferation and antitumor activity using mechanisms such as the production of arginase, TGF-β, inducible nitric oxide synthase, and IL-10.134,137,138 Tumor-associated macrophages, polarized to the immunosuppressive M2 subtype by sarcoma cells, release factors such as IL-10, TGF-β, and CCL22, facilitating tumor growth and metastasis.139,140 The immunostimulatory M1 tumor-associated macrophages, which can inhibit tumor progression, are less prevalent in the sarcoma TME.119 Understanding these mechanisms that hinder T cell infiltration and activation is key in developing future strategies to enhance the efficacy of adoptive cellular therapies.

To dismantle the immunosuppressive sarcoma TME, preclinical and early clinical strategies are shifting toward directly targeting the unique mesenchymal stroma. One prominent approach utilizes Fibroblast Activation Protein-targeting CAR-T cells, as well as bispecific and universal platforms that simultaneously target tumor antigens and FAP+ CAFs, successfully depleting the cellular stroma and enhancing T-cell infiltration.141,142 Collectively, these stroma-directed preclinical innovations represent a crucial paradigm shift necessary to achieve durable clinical responses in sarcomas.

On-target off-tumor (OTOT) toxic effects of CAR-T and TCR-T therapy

One significant challenge in using CAR-T and TCR-T therapies for sarcoma is the potential for OTOT toxicity. Most antigens targeted in sarcoma are tumor-associated antigens rather than tumor-specific antigens, meaning they are also expressed on healthy tissues, leading to unintended cytotoxic effects that can result in dose-limiting adverse events.63,90 In sarcoma-specific clinical trials, OTOT toxicity varies considerably depending on the target antigen and receptor engineering approach. HER2-targeting CAR-T cells carry inherent OTOT risk due to HER2 expression on normal lungs, cardiac, and gastrointestinal epithelium. However, in the phase I sarcoma trial by Ahmed et al., HER2 CAR-T cells infused at conservative doses without lymphodepletion did not produce OTOT toxicity in 19 patients with osteosarcoma (n = 16) and other sarcomas (n = 3).63 The subsequent trial by Hegde et al., which incorporated lymphodepletion and dose escalation, observed dose-limiting CRS at the highest dose level but no direct OTOT organ damage.90

For TCR-T therapies, the most instructive OTOT example involves affinity-enhanced MAGE-A3-targeting TCR-Ts. Although tested in melanoma and myeloma rather than sarcoma, MAGE-A3 is expressed in osteosarcoma and other sarcoma subtypes, making these findings directly relevant. Fatal cardiac toxicity occurred due to cross-reactivity with an unrelated target, a peptide derived from Titin, a striated muscle protein.143 In a separate trial, an affinity-enhanced MAGE-A3 TCR-T cross-reacted with MAGE-A12 in the brain, causing fatal neurotoxicity.144 In contrast, TCR-T therapies targeting cancer testis antigens with more restricted normal tissue expression, including MAGE-A4 (afamitresgene autoleucel) and PRAME (anzutresgene autoleucel/IMA203CD8), have demonstrated favorable OTOT safety profiles in SS and MRCL clinical trials, with no reported OTOT events.15,16,33 NY-ESO-1-directed TCR-T therapies have similarly shown manageable safety profiles in sarcoma, though rare cases of Guillain-Barré syndrome have been reported.145

Antigen expression, antigen escape, and reduced T-cell persistence in T-cell therapies

The primary challenge in CAR-T cell therapy for sarcomas is the presence of targetable antigens. CAR-T cells are engineered to target antigens found on the surface of tumors, which should ideally be specific to the tumor itself. However, sarcomas are known for their heterogeneity even within the same subtype, and the lack of tumor-specific surface antigens makes selecting appropriate antigens for treatment development more complicated.146,147 Several sarcoma-associated antigens amendable to CAR-T cell treatment have recently emerged and are being actively studied in early-phase clinical trials (Table 4).

In TCR-T therapy, apart from TCR mismatch, defective antigen presentation and HLA loss are the most clinically relevant challenges because TCR-T cells depend on peptide-HLA complexes for target recognition. Downregulation of MHC class I molecules was observed in 52–79% of bone and soft tissue sarcomas, which can reduce T cells’ ability to recognize tumor antigens, thereby limiting the efficacy of TCR-T therapy.148,149 Antigen escape is the main mechanism of tumor progression after initial response. NY-ESO-1 antigen loss was observed in multiple myeloma patients who had disease relapses after treatment with NY-ESO-1 TCR-T, while a selective loss of HLA-A*02:01 from the cell surface after treatment with NY-ESO-1 TCR-T has been observed in a xenograft study.150,151 Future approaches to prevent the emergence of antigen escape variants remain to be explored.

Furthermore, poor in vivo persistence of adoptively transferred T cells, driven by factors such as T cell exhaustion, metabolic stress, and inadequate costimulatory signaling, remains a major barrier to achieving sustained antitumor activity.124,152 These mechanisms, together with antigenic loss, contribute to relapses and treatment resistance following cellular therapy.

Operational and logistical challenges in cellular therapy

Operational challenges in cellular therapy are multifaceted and span the entire product lifecycle – from manufacturing to clinical implementation. The manufacturing process is complex and highly sensitive to variability in patient-derived materials, often resulting in inconsistent batch quality and yield. Supply chain logistics present additional hurdles, particularly in autologous therapies, where maintaining the chain of identity and custody during collection, transport, and processing is critical. Regulatory and administrative burdens further complicate the wide clinical adoption of cellular therapy, including detailed and at times redundant documentation and the need for ongoing cellular therapy accreditation and audits.153,154 Moreover, managing both acute and long-term toxic effects, addressing high treatment costs, and meeting infrastructure demands significantly limit accessibility. Specialized staff training and standardized protocols for infusion and post-infusion monitoring are also required for cellular therapy, adding to the operational complexity.

Conclusion

The journey of cellular therapies in sarcoma has transitioned from exploratory concepts to a standard approach for selected patients and is a rapidly evolving frontier of therapeutic innovation. Recent breakthroughs, including the FDA accelerated approval of afami-cel for SS targeting MAGEA4, represent a new era of antigen-directed immunotherapies targeting both intracellular and extracellular tumor antigens. Current evidence of therapeutic benefit remains strongest for HLA- and antigen-selected TCR-T therapies, whereas alternative platforms such as CAR-T, TIL, and NK-cell approaches still require further clinical validation and refinement. Despite these advances, meaningful challenges persist, including off-target toxic effects, low T-cell infiltration into the tumor, the complexity of the TME, and the need to optimize timing from manufacture to infusion. Addressing these barriers will require continued integration of bioengineering innovations, optimization of cellular constructs, and incorporation of multidimensional therapeutic strategies. The future of cellular therapies in sarcoma lies in fostering interdisciplinary collaboration and expanding the repertoire of targetable antigens, ultimately paving the way for personalized and effective treatment paradigms for this rare and challenging malignancy.
