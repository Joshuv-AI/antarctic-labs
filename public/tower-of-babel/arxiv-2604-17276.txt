Generalized Composed Alternating Relaxed Projection Algorithm for Two-Set Feasibility Problem
Xinxin Li, Yudong Wei, Hao Zhang

Abstract
We study the two-set feasibility problem of finding a point in the intersection X ∩ Y X\cap Y of closed convex sets in a Hilbert space. We propose a generalized composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm (gCARPA) that blends Douglas–Rachford-type and projection–reflection-type dynamics via an outer averaging step μ \mu and an internal relaxation ( γ , θ , η ) (\gamma,\theta,\eta) . The algorithm contains several classical projection methods as special cases. We also introduce its non-stationary variant, in which ( γ k , θ k , η k ) (\gamma_{k},\theta_{k},\eta_{k}) vary over iterations, and establish its convergence. For the subspace feasibility model, we derive an explicit spectral characterization via principal-angle block decompositions, yielding computable subdominant-eigenvalue factors and a minimax parameter-selection recipe in a symmetric regime that targets critical damping on principal-angle planes. Numerical experiments illustrate that the generalized relaxation and its non-stationary tuning can improve or match baseline methods in problem-dependent regimes.



Key words.
Feasibility problem, Projection, Relaxation, Nonstationary scheme, Friedrichs angle, Linear convergence

Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the following feasibility problem of two sets:

(1.1)

\mathrm{find}\;\bm{x}\in\mathcal{H}\ \ \textrm{such that}\ \ \bm{x}\in X\cap Y,

where
\mathcal{H}
is a real Hilbert space, and
X,Y\subset\mathcal{H}
are nonempty closed convex sets satisfying

X\cap Y\neq\emptyset.

Feasibility problems of the form (
1.1
) arise in a wide range of applications, including signal and image processing
[
15
,
13
]
, compressed sensing
[
38
,
39
]
, seismic data processing
[
22
,
37
]
, and many others. Projection-based methods are among the most popular approaches for such problems, due to their simplicity and their strong convergence theory in Hilbert spaces.

A fundamental method for (
1.1
) is the method of alternating projections (MAP)
[
23
,
3
]
. Over the years, numerous variants have been proposed to improve practical performance or to obtain sharper convergence properties, such as relaxed alternating projection (RAP) and partial relaxed alternating projection (PRAP)
[
5
]
, averaged alternating modified reflections (AAMR)
[
1
]
, relaxed averaged alternating reflections (RAAR)
[
30
]
, simultaneous projection (SP)
[
35
]
, and generalized relaxed alternating projection (GRAP)
[
21
,
17
]
. More recently, geometric acceleration ideas have also been explored, for instance via circumcentered-reflection type schemes
[
2
,
12
,
11
]
.

Projection algorithms can also be viewed through the lens of operator splitting, since the projection is a special case of the proximity operator
[
6
]
. Indeed, MAP can be interpreted as an instance of the backward–backward splitting method
[
8
]
. Moreover, several projection/reflection methods recover classical splittings such as Peaceman–Rachford
[
31
]
and Douglas–Rachford
[
19
]
under suitable parameter choices; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
1
]
and references therein.

The convergence theory of projection-based fixed-point iterations has also been extensively developed. Many algorithms can be written as Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann iterations of (firmly) nonexpansive or averaged operators, enabling global convergence guarantees and rate estimates; see, e.g.,
[
27
]
. Under additional regularity such as partial smoothness, one can obtain local linear convergence
[
24
,
25
,
28
]
. For nonconvex feasibility, convergence properties of Douglas–Rachford type schemes have been studied as well; see, e.g.,
[
26
]
. In the special case where
X
and
Y
are subspaces, sharp linear rates can be characterized in terms of the Friedrichs angle
[
4
,
9
,
1
]
; see also
[
18
,
7
,
10
]
for further developments.

1.1
Motivation and contribution

When
X
and
Y
are two subspaces, both MAP and Douglas–Rachford (DR) converge linearly, and the rate is governed by the Friedrichs angle between
X
and
Y

[
4
,
5
]
. However, despite its appealing theory, the DR fixed-point sequence may exhibit a pronounced spiraling trajectory
[
33
]
, which can lead to slow practical progress on subspace feasibility problems. This geometric phenomenon motivates algorithmic designs that retain the favorable nonexpansive structure of DR while mitigating its spiral behavior.

Recently, Shen et al.
[
36
]
proposed the composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm (CARPA), which blends DR-type composition with a projection-reflection correction, and demonstrated significant speedup over DR on several feasibility models. In this work, we further generalize CARPA by introducing relaxed reflections with parameters
\theta,\eta\in(0,1]
, leading to a generalized composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm (gCARPA), given in Algorithm
1
. Concretely, we use

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}:=(1-\theta)\mathcal{I}+\theta(2\mathcal{P}_{X}-\mathcal{I}),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}:=(1-\eta)\mathcal{I}+\eta(2\mathcal{P}_{Y}-\mathcal{I}),

where
\mathcal{P}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}_{Y}
denote the orthogonal projections onto
X
and
Y
, and
\mathcal{I}
is the identity operator. When
\theta=\eta=1
, the operators
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}
and
\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}
reduce to the standard reflections, in which case gCARPA coincides with CARPA in
[
36
]
. The additional parameters
\theta
and
\eta
provide a flexible interpolation between projection steps and reflection steps, and allow us to unify several classical schemes within a single template. For instance, the gCARPA update can be written as a relaxed fixed-point iteration

\bm{z}^{k+1}=(1-\mu)\bm{z}^{k}+\mu\,\mathcal{F}^{\theta,\eta}_{\gamma}(\bm{z}^{k}),

where the operator
\mathcal{F}^{\theta,\eta}_{\gamma}
is a convex combination of a DR-type averaging term and a projection term built from
\mathcal{P}_{Y}\circ\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}
. As special cases, one may recover relaxed DR (e.g.,
\gamma=0
), and relaxed alternating projections (e.g.,
\gamma=1
and
\mu=1
), highlighting gCARPA as a unifying algorithm.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

❖

We propose the generalized composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm for the convex feasibility problem (
1.1
). Compared with CARPA, the additional relaxation parameters
(\theta,\eta)
in gCARPA provide extra
degrees of freedom to control the balance between spiraling and damping in the
fixed-point dynamics. We establish its fixed-point characterization and global convergence under suitable parameter choices. We also introduce and analyze a non-stationary variant.

❖

For the feasibility problem of two subspaces, we derive explicit linear convergence rates of gCARPA and identify parameter regimes that yield provably optimal rates. In particular, when
\theta=\eta=1
, our results specialize to the CARPA setting of
[
36
]
.

❖

We provide numerical experiments on representative feasibility models, including subspace feasibility and additional structured examples, to illustrate how the extra relaxation parameters
\theta,\eta
can improve robustness and performance compared with existing projection/reflection methods.

Mathematical background

Throughout, let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space endowed with inner product
\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\rangle
and the induced norm
{|\kern-1.125pt|}\cdot{|\kern-1.125pt|}
.
We write
\mathcal{I}
for the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
When we specialize to the Euclidean setting,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional real vector space equipped with the standard inner product.

We use bold uppercase (lowercase) letters to denote matrices (column vectors) and calligraphic letters to represent operators. Given a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we denote by
\mathrm{ker}(\bm{A})
,
\mathrm{ran}(\bm{A})
, and
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{A})
the null space, range, and rank of
\bm{A}
, respectively. We use
\mathrm{span}(\cdot)
to denote the linear span of a set of vectors. We denote by
\mathrm{card}(\cdot)
the cardinality of a finite set, i.e., the number of elements in the set.

Operators.

We recall several notions on nonexpansive mappings and projection-type operators; see
[
6
, Chapter 4]
for further background.

Definition 1

(Nonexpansive and averaged operators)
.

An operator
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
nonexpansive
if

{|\kern-1.125pt|}\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})-\mathcal{F}(\bm{x}^{\prime}){|\kern-1.125pt|}\leq{|\kern-1.125pt|}\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}{|\kern-1.125pt|}\qquad\forall\,\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the operator
\mathcal{F}
is called
\alpha
-averaged
if there exists a nonexpansive mapping
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

\mathcal{F}=(1-\alpha)\mathcal{I}+\alpha\mathcal{N}.

When
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\mathcal{F}
is also called
firmly nonexpansive
; in this case
2\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{I}
is nonexpansive.

Definition 2

(Fixed point)
.

A point
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{H}
is a
fixed point
of
\mathcal{F}
if
\bm{x}=\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})
.
We denote the fixed-point set by

\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}):=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{H}:\ \bm{x}=\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})\},

which may be empty.

Definition 3

(Projection)
.

Let
X\subset\mathcal{H}
be nonempty, closed, and convex.
The
projection
onto
X
is

\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{y}\in X}\ {|\kern-1.125pt|}\bm{x}-\bm{y}{|\kern-1.125pt|}\qquad\forall\,\bm{x}\in\mathcal{H}.

It is single-valued and firmly nonexpansive
[
6
, Proposition 4.2]
.

Definition 4

(Reflection and relaxed reflection)
.

Let
X\subset\mathcal{H}
be nonempty, closed, and convex.
The
reflection
with respect to
X
is defined by

\mathcal{R}_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}2\mathcal{P}_{X}-\mathcal{I}.

For
\theta\in[0,1]
, the
relaxed reflection
is

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}(1-\theta)\mathcal{I}+\theta\,\mathcal{R}_{X}=(1-\theta)\mathcal{I}+\theta(2\mathcal{P}_{X}-\mathcal{I}).

Equivalently, letting
r:=2\theta-1\in[-1,1]
, one can write

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}=(1+r)\mathcal{P}_{X}-r\mathcal{I}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}
is nonexpansive, since it is a convex combination of
\mathcal{I}
and the nonexpansive mapping
\mathcal{R}_{X}
. When
\theta\in(0,1)
, it is in fact
\theta
-averaged.

For later use, we also set

\mathcal{R}_{Y}:=2\mathcal{P}_{Y}-\mathcal{I},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}:=(1-\eta)\mathcal{I}+\eta\,\mathcal{R}_{Y},\quad\eta\in[0,1].

Remark 1

(A useful identity for subspaces)
.

If
X
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, then its orthogonal complement is

X^{\perp}:=\{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{H}:\ \langle\bm{u},\,\bm{x}\rangle=0\ \ \forall\bm{x}\in X\}.

In this case,
\mathcal{H}=X\oplus X^{\perp}
and

\mathcal{P}_{X^{\perp}}=\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{X}.

Consequently,

2\mathcal{P}_{X}-\mathcal{I}\;=\;\mathcal{P}_{X}-\mathcal{P}_{X^{\perp}}.

Angles between subspaces.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be subspaces. We recall the principal angles and the Friedrichs angle; see
[
4
]
for more details.

Definition 5

(Principal angles)
.

Let
p:=\min\{\dim(X),\dim(Y)\}
.
The principal angles
\theta_{k}\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
,
k=1,\ldots,p
, between
X
and
Y
are recursively defined by

\cos(\theta_{k})=\max\left\{\langle\bm{u},\,\bm{v}\rangle\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\bm{u}\in X,\ \bm{v}\in Y,\ \|\bm{u}\|=\|\bm{v}\|=1,\\
\langle\bm{u},\,\bm{u}_{j}\rangle=0,\ \langle\bm{v},\,\bm{v}_{j}\rangle=0,\ j=1,\ldots,k-1\end{array}\right\},

where
\bm{u}_{j}\in X
and
\bm{v}_{j}\in Y
are maximizers chosen at previous steps.
The vectors
\bm{u}_{k},\bm{v}_{k}
need not be unique, but the angles are uniquely determined and satisfy

0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{p}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Definition 6

(Friedrichs angle)
.

The cosine of the Friedrichs angle between
X
and
Y
is defined by

\displaystyle c_{\scriptstyle\rm{F}}(X,Y)=\cos(\theta_{\scriptstyle\rm{F}})

\displaystyle:=\sup\Bigl\{|\langle\bm{u},\,\bm{v}\rangle|\ \Big|\ \bm{u}\in X\cap(X\cap Y)^{\perp},\ \bm{v}\in Y\cap(X\cap Y)^{\perp},\ \|\bm{u}\|,\|\bm{v}\|\leq 1\Bigr\}.

When there is no ambiguity, we write
c_{\scriptstyle\rm{F}}
for
c_{\scriptstyle\rm{F}}(X,Y)
.

Proposition 2.1

(Principal angles and the Friedrichs angle)
.

Let
s:=\dim(X\cap Y)
. Then
\theta_{k}=0
for
k=1,\ldots,s
, and
\theta_{s+1}=\theta_{\scriptstyle\rm{F}}>0
.

Block-diagonal matrices.

For square matrices
\bm{A}_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}_{m}
, we write

\mathrm{blkdiag}(\bm{A}_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}_{m}):=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}&&&\bm{0}\\
&\bm{A}_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
\bm{0}&&&\bm{A}_{m}\end{bmatrix},

i.e., the block-diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are
\bm{A}_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}_{m}
and whose off-diagonal blocks are zero.
In particular, if
\bm{A}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\bm{I}_{d_{i}}
, then
\mathrm{blkdiag}(\lambda_{1}\bm{I}_{d_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\bm{I}_{d_{m}})

is diagonal with
\lambda_{i}
repeated
d_{i}
times.

Convergence rate for matrix iterations.

For
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we denote by
\sigma(\bm{A})
and
\rho(\bm{A})
the spectrum and spectral radius, respectively.
We say that
\bm{A}
converges linearly to
\bm{A}^{\infty}
if there exist
r\in[0,1)
and constants
M>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|\bm{A}^{k}-\bm{A}^{\infty}\|\leq Mr^{k}\qquad\forall\,k\geq N.

An eigenvalue
\lambda\in\sigma(\bm{A})
is
semisimple
if

\mathrm{ker}(\bm{A}-\lambda\bm{I})=\mathrm{ker}\bigl((\bm{A}-\lambda\bm{I})^{2}\bigr).

We define the
subdominant eigenvalue modulus
by

\xi(\bm{A}):=\max\Bigl\{|\lambda|:\ \lambda\in\{0\}\cup\bigl(\sigma(\bm{A})\setminus\{1\}\bigr)\Bigr\}.

Fact 2.1

(
[
5
, Fact 2.4]
)
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{A}^{k}
exists if and only if
every eigenvalue
\lambda\in\sigma(\bm{A})
satisfies
|\lambda|<1
, except possibly
\lambda=1
, and if
1\in\sigma(\bm{A})
, then it is semisimple.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
5
, Theorem 2.12 and Theorem 2.15]
)
.

Suppose that
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is convergent to
\bm{A}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Then
\xi(\bm{A})=\rho(\bm{A}-\bm{A}^{\infty})<1
and

(\bm{A}-\bm{A}^{\infty})^{k}=\bm{A}^{k}-\bm{A}^{\infty}\qquad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover:

(i)

\bm{A}
is linearly convergent with any rate
r\in(\xi(\bm{A}),1)
;

(ii)

\xi(\bm{A})
is the optimal linear rate if and only if all subdominant eigenvalues are semisimple.

3
A generalized CARPA

This section is devoted to the formulation and analysis of our proposed algorithm.
Our starting point is the CARPA scheme of
[
36
]
, which blends a DR-type operator with an additional projection-type correction and achieves substantial acceleration on subspace feasibility problems. We further generalize CARPA by introducing relaxed reflections on both sets, leading to a more flexible family that (i) contains CARPA as a special case, (ii) unifies several classical projection/reflection methods, and (iii) provides extra degrees of freedom that can improve robustness in practice.
We first present the stationary algorithm and establish its fixed-point formulation, averagedness, and convergence.
A non-stationary variant is discussed afterwards.

3.1
The proposed algorithm and operator viewpoint

Motivated by the spiraling behavior of the DR fixed-point sequence on locally polyhedral problems
[
34
]
, we propose the generalized composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm (gCARPA), summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Initial:

\bm{z}^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
; choose
\gamma\in[0,1)
,

\mu\in(0,2/(1+\gamma))
, and
\theta,\eta\in(0,1]
;

repeat

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{z}^{k})
;

\bm{u}^{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}(\bm{z}^{k})=(1-\theta)\bm{z}^{k}+\theta(2\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k})
;

\bm{y}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\bm{u}^{k+1})
;

\bm{v}^{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}(\bm{u}^{k+1})=(1-\eta)\bm{u}^{k+1}+\eta(2\bm{y}^{k+1}-\bm{u}^{k+1})
;

\bm{z}^{k+1}=(1-\mu)\bm{z}^{k}+\mu\big((1-\gamma)\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{z}^{k}+\bm{v}^{k+1})+\gamma\bm{y}^{k+1}\big)
;

until

convergence
;

Algorithm 1

A generalized composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm (gCARPA)

Fixed-point operators.

From Algorithm
1
, we have

\bm{u}^{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}^{k},\qquad\bm{y}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}^{k},\qquad\bm{v}^{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}^{k}.

Hence,

\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{z}^{k}+\bm{v}^{k+1})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bigr)\bm{z}^{k},\qquad\bm{y}^{k+1}=(\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta})\bm{z}^{k}.

This motivates defining

(3.1)

\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{B}^{\theta}:=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta},

together with the CARPA-type convex combination

(3.2)

\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}:=(1-\gamma)\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}+\gamma\mathcal{B}^{\theta},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}:=(1-\mu)\mathcal{I}+\mu\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}.

With these definitions, Algorithm
1
is exactly the fixed-point iteration

\bm{z}^{k+1}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}(\bm{z}^{k}).

Remark 2

(Relation with CARPA and other methods)
.

We next clarify how gCARPA relates to the CARPA framework of
[
36
]
and how several classical schemes are recovered as special cases. When
\theta=\eta=1
, the relaxed reflections reduce to the standard reflections, namely,

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}=\mathcal{R}_{X},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}=\mathcal{R}_{Y}.

Consequently,

\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{R}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}),\qquad\mathcal{B}^{\theta}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X},

and Algorithm
1
reduces exactly to the CARPA scheme proposed in
[
36
]
.

Under the same choice
\theta=\eta=1
, the parameter
\gamma
continuously interpolates between two endpoint operators. On the one hand, when
\gamma=0
, the operator
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
becomes

\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{R}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}),

which is precisely the DR operator. On the other hand, when
\gamma=1
, one obtains

\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}.

Therefore, within the CARPA setting, the parameter
\gamma
controls the extent to which the iteration moves away from the pure DR-type mechanism toward a more projection-oriented correction.

Moreover, gCARPA also recovers the classical method of alternating projections. Indeed, if
\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}
, then
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}=\mathcal{P}_{X}
. Choosing in addition
\gamma=1
and
\mu=1
gives

\bm{z}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}^{k},

which is exactly the standard alternating projection iteration. In this case, the resulting scheme is independent of
\eta
.

3.2
Convergence of the gCARPA iteration

A key point in our design is that the desired fixed points coincide with the feasible set
X\cap Y
.
We start from a standard identity for alternating projections.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
. Then
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{P}_{X})=X\cap Y
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{z}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{P}_{X})
and set
\bm{x}=\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}
. Then
\bm{z}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\bm{x}\in Y
.
Take any
\bm{w}\in X\cap Y
.
From
\bm{x}=\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}
we have
\langle\bm{z}-\bm{x},\,\bm{w}-\bm{x}\rangle\leq 0
,
and from
\bm{z}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\bm{x}
we have
\langle\bm{x}-\bm{z},\,\bm{w}-\bm{z}\rangle\leq 0
, i.e.
\langle\bm{z}-\bm{x},\,\bm{w}-\bm{z}\rangle\geq 0
.
Since
\bm{w}-\bm{x}=(\bm{w}-\bm{z})+(\bm{z}-\bm{x})
,

\langle\bm{z}-\bm{x},\,\bm{w}-\bm{x}\rangle=\langle\bm{z}-\bm{x},\,\bm{w}-\bm{z}\rangle+\|\bm{z}-\bm{x}\|^{2}\leq 0.

Together with
\langle\bm{z}-\bm{x},\,\bm{w}-\bm{z}\rangle\geq 0
, this yields
\|\bm{z}-\bm{x}\|^{2}\leq 0
, hence
\bm{z}=\bm{x}\in X
.
Therefore
\bm{z}\in X\cap Y
. The reverse inclusion is immediate.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be nonexpansive and assume
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{1})\cap\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{2})\neq\emptyset
.
Then for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\mathrm{fix}\bigl((1-\gamma)\mathcal{T}_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{T}_{2}\bigr)=\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{1})\cap\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{2}).

Proof.

Let
\bm{p}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{1})\cap\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{2})
and set
\mathcal{T}=(1-\gamma)\mathcal{T}_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
If
\bm{z}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T})
, then by convexity of
\|\cdot\|^{2}
and nonexpansiveness,

\|\bm{z}-\bm{p}\|^{2}=\|\mathcal{T}\bm{z}-\bm{p}\|^{2}\leq(1-\gamma)\|\mathcal{T}_{1}\bm{z}-\bm{p}\|^{2}+\gamma\|\mathcal{T}_{2}\bm{z}-\bm{p}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{z}-\bm{p}\|^{2}.

Thus equality holds throughout, which forces
\mathcal{T}_{1}\bm{z}=\mathcal{T}_{2}\bm{z}
and then
\bm{z}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\bm{z}=\mathcal{T}_{2}\bm{z}
.
Hence
\bm{z}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{1})\cap\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T}_{2})
.
The reverse inclusion is obvious.
∎

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
and
\theta,\eta\in(0,1]
.
Then:

(i)

\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{B}^{\theta})=X\cap Y
;

(ii)

for any
\gamma\in(0,1),\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta})=X\cap Y.

Proof.

(i) If
\bm{z}\in X\cap Y
, then
\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}=\bm{z}
, so
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}=\bm{z}
, and hence
\mathcal{B}^{\theta}\bm{z}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\bm{z}=\bm{z}
.

Conversely, suppose
\bm{z}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{B}^{\theta})
, i.e.
\bm{z}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z})
.
Let
\bm{x}=\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}
.
Using
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}=(1-\theta)\bm{z}+\theta(2\bm{x}-\bm{z})=\bm{z}+2\theta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})
and the projector characterization

\bm{z}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\bm{w})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bm{w}-\bm{z}\in N_{Y}(\bm{z}),

we obtain
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\bm{z}=2\theta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\in N_{Y}(\bm{z})
. Here
N_{Y}(\bm{z}):=\{\bm{u}:\bm{u}^{\top}(\bm{y}-\bm{z})\leq 0\,\,\text{for all }\bm{y}\in Y\}
is the normal cone of
Y
at
\bm{z}
.

Since
N_{Y}(\bm{z})
is a cone and
\theta>0
, this implies
\bm{x}-\bm{z}\in N_{Y}(\bm{z})
, i.e.
\bm{z}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\bm{x}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}
.
By Lemma
3.1
, we have that
\bm{z}\in X\cap Y
.

For (ii), note that every
\bm{z}\in X\cap Y
satisfies
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}=\bm{z}
and
\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\bm{z}=\bm{z}
,
hence
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}\bm{z}=\bm{z}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\theta}\bm{z}=\bm{z}
. Therefore,

\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta})\cap\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{B}^{\theta})
is nonempty.
Since
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\theta}
are nonexpansive, Lemma
3.2
yields

\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta})=\mathrm{fix}\bigl((1-\gamma)\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}+\gamma\mathcal{B}^{\theta}\bigr)=\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta})\cap\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{B}^{\theta}).

Using
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{B}^{\theta})=X\cap Y
gives
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta})=X\cap Y
.
∎

Remark 3

(On the role of
\gamma
)
.

The case
\gamma=0
corresponds to the pure DR-type branch
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}
.
As in the classical DR method,
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta})
may contain points outside
X\cap Y
,
although the associated shadow sequences can still converge to a feasible point.
In this work we aim to obtain a fixed-point limit lying directly in
X\cap Y
.
For this reason we focus on
\gamma>0

guarantees

\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta})=X\cap Y
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathcal{H}
be nonempty closed convex sets and let
\theta,\eta\in(0,1]
.
For
\gamma\in[0,1)
define
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta},\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta},\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta},\mathcal{B}^{\theta}
as in (

) and (

(i)

(Nonexpansiveness and averagedness).

The relaxed reflections
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}
and
\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}
are nonexpansive.
Consequently,
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}
is firmly nonexpansive (
\tfrac{1}{2}
-averaged) and
\mathcal{B}^{\theta}
is nonexpansive. Moreover,
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is
\tfrac{1+\gamma}{2}
-averaged, and for any
\mu\in(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma})

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is
\beta
-averaged with
\beta=\frac{(1+\gamma)\mu}{2}\in(0,1)
.

(ii)

(Global convergence).

Assume
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
and let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and

\mu\in\bigl(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma}\bigr)
.
Then the sequence
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
, i.e.,
\bm{z}^{k+1}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}(\bm{z}^{k})
, converges weakly to some
\bm{z}^{\star}\in X\cap Y

\bm{z}^{k}\rightharpoonup\bm{z}^{\star}\in X\cap Y
.
If, in addition,
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional, then
\bm{z}^{k}\to\bm{z}^{\star}
and consequently

\bm{x}^{k}\to\bm{z}^{\star}
and
\bm{y}^{k}\to\bm{z}^{\star}
.

Proof.

(i) Since
\mathcal{P}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}_{Y}
are firmly nonexpansive, the reflections

\mathcal{R}_{X}=2\mathcal{P}_{X}-\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{R}_{Y}=2\mathcal{P}_{Y}-\mathcal{I}
are nonexpansive.
Because
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}=(1-\theta)\mathcal{I}+\theta\mathcal{R}_{X}
and

\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}=(1-\eta)\mathcal{I}+\eta\mathcal{R}_{Y}
are convex combinations of nonexpansive maps,
they are nonexpansive as well. Hence
\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}
is nonexpansive and

\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta})

is firmly nonexpansive. Also
\mathcal{P}_{Y}
is nonexpansive, so
\mathcal{B}^{\theta}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}

is nonexpansive.

Writing
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}
as
\tfrac{1}{2}
-averaged and
\mathcal{B}^{\theta}
as
1
-averaged,
the convex-mixture rule for averaged operators
[
14
, Proposition 2.2]
yields that
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is averaged with
parameter

(1-\gamma)\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma\cdot 1=\tfrac{1+\gamma}{2}.

Finally, if
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is
\alpha
-averaged with
\alpha=\tfrac{1+\gamma}{2}
, then

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}=(1-\mu)\mathcal{I}+\mu\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is
(\mu\alpha)
-averaged, i.e.,

\beta=\mu\alpha=\tfrac{(1+\gamma)\mu}{2}\in(0,1)
under
\mu\in(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma})
.

(ii) Assume
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
. By Proposition
3.1
,

\mathrm{fix}\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}\bigr)=\mathrm{fix}\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}\bigr)=X\cap Y.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is averaged by part (i), the Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann theorem for averaged
operators (e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 5.14]
) yields weak convergence

\bm{z}^{k}\rightharpoonup\bm{z}^{\star}\in X\cap Y
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional, weak convergence implies strong convergence.
By continuity of
\mathcal{P}_{X}
,
\mathcal{P}_{Y}
, and since
\bm{z}^{\star}\in X\cap Y
implies

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}(\bm{z}^{\star})=\bm{z}^{\star}
, we have

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{z}^{k})\to\bm{z}^{\star}
and

\bm{y}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}^{k})\to\bm{z}^{\star}
.
∎

Remark 4

(Asymptotic regularity and residuals)
.

Assume
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
and set
\mathcal{T}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
.
Since
\mathcal{T}
is averaged, it is strongly quasi-nonexpansive; in particular, for every

\bm{z}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{T})=X\cap Y
one has the descent inequality

\|\mathcal{T}\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}-\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\,\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\bm{x}\|^{2},

see, e.g.,
[
6
, Proposition 4.25]
, where
\beta\in(0,1)
denotes the averagedness constant.

Applying this with
\bm{x}=\bm{z}^{k}
yields that
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}
is Fejér monotone with respect to
X\cap Y
and that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\bm{z}^{k}\|^{2}<\infty,

hence
\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|\to 0
.

Moreover, since
\mathcal{T}
is the relaxed map
\mathcal{T}=(1-\mu)\mathcal{I}+\mu\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
with
\mu>0
, then

\|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}(\bm{z}^{k})-\bm{z}^{k}\|=\frac{1}{\mu}\,\|\mathcal{T}\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{k}\|=\frac{1}{\mu}\,\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|\ \longrightarrow\ 0.

These properties justify stopping criteria based on fixed-point residuals such as

\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|
or equivalently
\|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}(\bm{z}^{k})-\bm{z}^{k}\|
.

3.3
Optimal linear convergence rate of gCARPA

In this subsection, we derive explicit spectral formulas and optimal-rate consequences for gCARPA (Algorithm
1
) in the benchmark setting where
X
and
Y
are linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be subspaces with
\dim(X)=p\leq q=\dim(Y)
.
Denote by
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{p}\in[0,\pi/2]
, the principal angles between
X
and
Y
, and set

c_{i}:=\cos\phi_{i},\qquad s_{i}:=\sin\phi_{i},\qquad t_{i}:=s_{i}^{2}\in[0,1]\qquad(i=1,\ldots,p).

Let
s:=\dim(X\cap Y)
, so that
\phi_{1}=\cdots=\phi_{s}=0
and
\phi_{s+1}=\phi_{F}>0
is the Friedrichs angle.

Consequently, it suffices to analyze
p+q<n
.

By
[
5
]
, there exists an orthogonal matrix
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

(3.3)

\bm{P}_{X}=\bm{D}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{p}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}_{p}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}_{q-p}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}_{n-p-q}\end{bmatrix}\bm{D}^{\top},\quad\bm{P}_{Y}=\bm{D}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}^{2}&\bm{C}\bm{S}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{C}\bm{S}&\bm{S}^{2}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\bm{I}_{q-p}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}_{n-p-q}\end{bmatrix}\bm{D}^{\top},

where
\bm{C}=\mathrm{diag}(c_{i})_{i=1}^{p}
,
\bm{S}=\mathrm{diag}(s_{i})_{i=1}^{p}
, and
\bm{P}_{X},\bm{P}_{Y}
are the matrix representations of the projection operators
\mathcal{P}_{X},\mathcal{P}_{Y}
in the orthogonal basis induced by
\bm{D}
.

In the following analysis, we will use the abbreviations

a:=1-2\theta\in[-1,1),\qquad\kappa:=\gamma+(1-\gamma)\eta=\eta+\gamma(1-\eta)\in[\eta,1).

Theorem 3.2

(Spectral decomposition and linear rate of gCARPA)
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be subspaces with
p=\dim(X)\leq q=\dim(Y)
and
p+q<n
.
Fix
\gamma\in[0,1)
,
\theta,\eta\in(0,1]
, and
\mu\in\bigl(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma}\bigr)
.
Consider the fixed-point iteration

\bm{z}^{k+1}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}\bm{z}^{k}.

(i) Convergence.

\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is
\frac{1+\gamma}{2}
-averaged and

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is
\alpha
-averaged with

\alpha=\frac{(1+\gamma)\mu}{2}\in(0,1).

Hence, for any
\bm{z}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the sequence
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}
converges to a point in

\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta})
.

(ii) Block diagonalization and explicit eigenvalues.

In the principal-angle coordinates (
3.3
),

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}=\bm{D}\;\mathrm{blkdiag}\bigl(\bm{M}_{1},\ldots,\bm{M}_{p},\;\lambda_{q-p}\bm{I}_{q-p},\;\lambda_{n-p-q}\bm{I}_{n-p-q}\bigr)\;\bm{D}^{\top},

where for each
i=1,\ldots,p
,

(3.4)

\bm{M}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1-\mu\kappa t_{i}&\mu a\kappa c_{i}s_{i}\\[2.84526pt]
\mu\kappa c_{i}s_{i}&1-\mu\bigl((1+\gamma)\theta+a\kappa c_{i}^{2}\bigr)\end{bmatrix},

and the scalar blocks are

(3.5)

\lambda_{q-p}=1-\mu(1+\gamma)\theta,\qquad\lambda_{n-p-q}=(1-\mu)+\mu(1-\gamma)\bigl(1-\theta-\eta+2\theta\eta\bigr).

The two eigenvalues of
\bm{M}_{i}
are

(3.6)

\lambda_{i,\pm}=1-\frac{\mu}{2}\Bigl((1+\gamma)\theta+\kappa\bigl(1-2\theta c_{i}^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\pm\frac{\mu}{2}\sqrt{\Delta_{i}},

with discriminant

(3.7)

\Delta_{i}=\Bigl((1+\gamma)\theta+a\kappa-2(1-\theta)\kappa t_{i}\Bigr)^{2}+4a\kappa^{2}t_{i}(1-t_{i})=4\kappa^{2}\theta^{2}(t_{i}-\tau)^{2}+\Delta_{\min},

where

(3.8)

\tau:=\frac{(1+\gamma)(1-\theta)-a\kappa}{2\kappa\theta},\qquad\Delta_{\min}=(1-\gamma^{2})(2\theta-1)(1-2\eta).

(iii) Linear rate and optimal rate in the sense of
\xi(\cdot)
.

Let
\bm{A}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
and let
\bm{A}^{\infty}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{A}^{k}
.
Then
\bm{A}^{\infty}
exists and the convergence is linear.
Moreover, the subdominant-eigenvalue modulus (as defined in Section
2
) satisfies

\xi(\bm{A})=\max\Bigl\{\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\max\{|\lambda_{i,+}|,|\lambda_{i,-}|\},\;|\lambda_{q-p}|,\;|\lambda_{n-p-q}|\Bigr\}\in[0,1).

Consequently,
\bm{A}
is linearly convergent with any rate
r\in(\xi(\bm{A}),1)
.
If, in addition, all eigenvalues of modulus
\xi(\bm{A})
are semisimple, then
\xi(\bm{A})
is the optimal linear rate in the sense of Theorem 2.12–2.15 of
[
5
]
recalled in Section
2
.

(iv) Specialization to
X\cap Y
for
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

If
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta})=\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta})=X\cap Y

\bm{A}^{\infty}=\bm{P}_{X\cap Y},\,\text{hence}\,\,\bm{z}^{k}\to\bm{P}_{X\cap Y}\bm{z}^{0}\ \text{linearly.}

Here
\bm{P}_{X\cap Y}
is the matrix representation of the projection
\mathcal{P}_{X\cap Y}
.

Proof.

(i) The conclusion follows from Theorem
3.1
.

(ii)
Insert the matrix representation (
3.3
) into

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}=(1-2\theta)\mathcal{I}+2\theta\mathcal{P}_{X}
and
\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}=(1-2\eta)\mathcal{I}+2\eta\mathcal{P}_{Y}
,
then expand (
3.2
) in the orthogonal basis induced by
\bm{D}
.
On each principal-angle plane, the action is a
2\times 2
linear map, yielding (
3.4
).
On the residual subspaces of dimensions
(q-p)
and
(n-p-q)
, the action is scalar, giving (
3.5
).
This proves (ii).

(iii)
For
\bm{M}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\delta&\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}
,

\lambda_{i,\pm}=\frac{\alpha+\varepsilon}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\alpha-\varepsilon)^{2}+4\beta\delta}
.
Substituting (
3.4
) yields (
3.6
) with

\Delta_{i}=(\alpha-\varepsilon)^{2}+4\beta\delta=\Bigl((1+\gamma)\theta+a\kappa-2(1-\theta)\kappa t_{i}\Bigr)^{2}+4a\kappa^{2}t_{i}(1-t_{i}).

Completing the square gives the vertex form (
3.7
) and (
3.8
).

Since
\bm{A}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
is a linear averaged operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the limit
\bm{A}^{\infty}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{A}^{k}
exists
and the convergence rate is linear, as established in Fact 2.4 and Theorems 2.12–2.15 of
[
5
]
. By the block decomposition in (ii), the set of eigenvalues of
\bm{A}
is the union of

\{\lambda_{i,\pm}\}_{i=1}^{p}
and the scalar eigenvalues (
3.5
).
The quantity
\xi(\bm{A})
is therefore given by the stated maximum, excluding the eigenvalue
1
coming from the intersection component,
and the linear rate statement in (iii) follows from Theorem 2.12–2.15 of
[
5
]
.

(iv)
For
\gamma\in(0,1)
, Proposition
3.1
gives
\mathrm{fix}(\bm{A})=X\cap Y
.
In the principal-angle basis, the only eigenvalue equal to
1
occurs precisely on the intersection directions corresponding to
\phi_{i}=0
, while all other blocks have spectral radius strictly smaller than 1 because

.
Hence
\bm{A}^{k}
converges to the orthogonal projector onto the

so
\bm{A}^{\infty}=\bm{P}_{X\cap Y}

∎

Remark 5
.

The case
p+q>n
can be reduced to this setting by a standard lifting
[
32
]
.
Indeed, choose an integer
r\geq 1
such that
n^{\prime}:=n+r>p+q
, and define

X^{\prime}:=X\times\{0_{r}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}},\qquad Y^{\prime}:=Y\times\{0_{r}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}.

Then,
\dim(X^{\prime})=p
,
\dim(Y^{\prime})=q
, and
p+q<n^{\prime}
. Moreover, the principal angles between
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}

coincide with those between
X
and
Y
, and the lifted projectors satisfy

\bm{P}_{X^{\prime}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{P}_{X}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}_{r}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{P}_{Y^{\prime}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{P}_{Y}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}_{r}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence, any operator constructed from
\bm{P}_{X},\bm{P}_{Y}
and their reflections/relaxations inherit the same block-diagonal embedding,
so the principal-angle-dependent blocks, and therefore the linear rate are unchanged by the lifting.

The spectral characterization in Theorem
3.2
shows that, for subspace feasibility,
the linear convergence factor of gCARPA is determined by the spectral radii of the
2\times 2
principal-angle
blocks. In practice, however, directly minimizing the worst-case spectral radius over all principal angles

\{t_{i}=\sin^{2}(\phi_{i})\}
is cumbersome, since it leads to a nontrivial minimax problem in
\gamma
.

In the symmetric setting
\mu=1
,
p=q
, and
p+q=n
, the iteration has only these
2\times 2
blocks and the
principal angles fully govern the dynamics. Moreover, when
\theta,\eta\in[1/2,1]
, the worst-case block rate
as a function of
t
exhibits a decrease–then–increase pattern, implying that the maximum over

\{t_{i}\}
is attained at the two extreme angles, i.e., the Friedrichs angle and the largest principal angle.
This observation allows us to reduce the tuning of
\gamma
to comparing a small set of explicit candidates
obtained from a critical-damping condition
\Delta(t)=0
. The next theorem formalizes this endpoint-worst
property and provides an explicit minimax recipe for selecting
\gamma
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be two subspaces with
p=\dim(X),q=\dim(Y)
,
p=q
and
p+q=n
.
Assume
\mu=1
and
\theta,\eta\in[1/2,1]
.
Let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{p}\in(0,\pi/2]
be the nonzero principal angles between
X
and
Y
, and set

t_{i}:=\sin^{2}(\phi_{i})\in(0,1]
.
Denote by
\phi_{F}
the Friedrichs angle and by
\phi_{p}
the largest principal angle, and write

t_{F}:=\sin^{2}(\phi_{F})
and
t_{p}:=\sin^{2}(\phi_{p})
.

For each
t\in(0,1)
and
\gamma\in[0,1)
, let
\bm{M}(t,\gamma)
be the
2\times 2
principal-angle block of
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
obtained from (
3.4
) with
\mu=1
,
and let
\rho(\bm{M}(t,\gamma))
denote its spectral radius. Define

(3.9)

\displaystyle\psi(t)

\displaystyle:=t+\sqrt{2\theta-1}\sqrt{t(1-t)},

\displaystyle w_{\pm}(t)

\displaystyle:=(2\theta-1)+2(1-\theta)t\pm 2\sqrt{2\theta-1}\sqrt{t(1-t)}.

Moreover, set

\kappa(\gamma):=\eta+(1-\eta)\gamma,\qquad a:=1-2\theta\leq 0.

(i) Endpoint-worst property.

For every fixed
\gamma\in[0,1)
,

(3.10)

\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\rho\bigl(\bm{M}(t_{i},\gamma)\bigr)=\max\bigl\{\rho(\bm{M}(t_{F},\gamma)),\ \rho(\bm{M}(t_{p},\gamma))\bigr\}.

(ii) Critical damping candidates.

Let
\Delta(t,\gamma)
be the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of
\bm{M}(t,\gamma)
, i.e., the quantity in (
3.7
) with
t_{i}=t
.
If one enforces the critical damping condition
\Delta(t,\gamma)=0
for a given
t\in(0,1)
,
then necessarily

(3.11)

\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}(t):=\frac{\eta\,w_{\pm}(t)-\theta}{\theta-(1-\eta)\,w_{\pm}(t)},\qquad\text{whenever }\gamma_{\pm}(t)\in[0,1).

At such a parameter, the two eigenvalues of
\bm{M}(t,\gamma)
coincide and satisfy

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}(t)

\displaystyle=1-\kappa^{\star}(t)\,\psi(t),

\displaystyle\kappa^{\star}(t)

\displaystyle:=\kappa(\gamma_{\pm}(t))=\eta+(1-\eta)\gamma_{\pm}(t)=\frac{(1+\gamma_{\pm}(t))\theta}{w_{\pm}(t)}.

(iii) Minimax recipe.

Consider the minimax problem

\min_{\gamma\in[0,1)}\ \max_{1\leq i\leq p}\rho\bigl(\bm{M}(t_{i},\gamma)\bigr).

By (
3.10
), it reduces to minimizing

\max\{\rho(\bm{M}(t_{F},\gamma)),\rho(\bm{M}(t_{p},\gamma))\}
.
A practical minimax choice can therefore be obtained by evaluating
the endpoint critical-damping candidates
\gamma_{\pm}(t_{F})
and
\gamma_{\pm}(t_{p})
, whenever they lie in
[0,1)
, and selecting the one that gives the smallest value of

\max\{\rho(\bm{M}(t_{F},\gamma)),\rho(\bm{M}(t_{p},\gamma))\}
.

(iv) Reduction to CARPA.

When
\theta=\eta=1
, (
3.11
)–(
3.12
)
reduce to the CARPA formulas

\gamma^{\star}(t)=2\sqrt{t(1-t)}=2cs
and
\rho^{\star}(t)=|c^{2}-cs|
in
[
36
]
.

Proof.

(i) Fix
\gamma\in[0,1)
and a principal-angle parameter
t\in(0,1)
.
Write
c:=\sqrt{1-t}
and
s:=\sqrt{t}
so that
cs=\sqrt{t(1-t)}
.
Let
\lambda_{\pm}(t,\gamma)
be the eigenvalues of
M(t,\gamma)
and let
\Delta(t,\gamma)

be their discriminant.

If
\Delta(t,\gamma)<0
, then
\lambda_{-}(t,\gamma)=\overline{\lambda_{+}(t,\gamma)}
and hence

(3.13)

\rho(\bm{M}(t,\gamma))^{2}=|\lambda_{+}(t,\gamma)|^{2}=\det\bm{M}(t,\gamma).

Using the explicit block (
3.4
) with
\mu=1
and the abbreviations

\kappa=\kappa(\gamma)
and
a=1-2\theta
, a direct simplification yields

(3.14)

\det\bm{M}(t,\gamma)=(1-\gamma)\bigl(b-\theta\kappa\,t\bigr),\qquad b:=1-\theta-\eta+2\theta\eta.

Since
\theta>0
and
\kappa>0
, the map
t\mapsto\det M(t,\gamma)
is affine and strictly decreasing on
(0,1)
.
Therefore, in the complex-eigenvalue regime,
t\mapsto\rho(\bm{M}(t,\gamma))
is strictly decreasing.

If
\Delta(t,\gamma)\geq 0
, then
\lambda_{\pm}(t,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}
and

\rho(\bm{M}(t,\gamma))=\max\{|\lambda_{+}(t,\gamma)|,|\lambda_{-}(t,\gamma)|\}.

We now show that, on the boundary intervals where
\Delta(t,\gamma)\geq 0
holds,
the extremal eigenvalue branches are monotone.

Under
\theta,\eta\in[1/2,1]
, we have
a=1-2\theta\leq 0
and

\Delta_{\min}=(1-\gamma^{2})(2\theta-1)(1-2\eta)\leq 0
in (
3.7
).
Hence, the vertex form (
3.7
) implies that
\Delta(t,\gamma)\geq 0

holds only near the endpoints
t=0
and
t=1
, unless
\Delta_{\min}=0
, in which case
\Delta\equiv 0
.
More precisely, when
\Delta_{\min}<0
, there exists
t_{-}\leq t_{+}
such that

\Delta(t,\gamma)\geq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t\in[0,t_{-}]\cup[t_{+},1].

On
[0,t_{-}]
, the square-root term
\sqrt{\Delta(t,\gamma)}
decreases as
t
increases, since
\Delta
is a convex parabola with vertex at
\tau
, while the trace term

\mathrm{tr}\bm{M}(t,\gamma)
is affine and strictly decreasing in
t
due to the term
-\kappa\theta t
.
Consequently, the upper eigenvalue branch
\lambda_{+}(t,\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\bm{M}(t,\gamma)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Delta(t,\gamma)}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,t_{-}]
,
and hence
\rho(M(t,\gamma))=\lambda_{+}(t,\gamma)
decreases there.

On
[t_{+},1]
, the square-root term
\sqrt{\Delta(t,\gamma)}
increases in
t
whereas

\mathrm{tr}\bm{M}(t,\gamma)
keeps decreasing linearly. This implies that
\lambda_{-}(t,\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\bm{M}(t,\gamma)-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Delta(t,\gamma)}
decreases with
t
and becomes negative
for
t
sufficiently close to
1
; therefore
-\lambda_{-}(t,\gamma)
increases on
[t_{+},1]
.
As a result,
\rho(\bm{M}(t,\gamma))=\max\{\lambda_{+}(t,\gamma),-\lambda_{-}(t,\gamma)\}
is increasing on
[t_{+},1]
.

Combining this with Step 1, i.e., strict decrease in the complex regime, we conclude that for every fixed
\gamma
,
the function
t\mapsto\rho(\bm{M}(t,\gamma))
decreases for small
t
and increases for large
t
.
Hence, on any interval
[t_{F},t_{p}]
containing all
\{t_{i}\}
, the maximum is attained at an endpoint,
which proves (
3.10
).

(ii) Fix
t\in(0,1)
and enforce
\Delta(t,\gamma)=0
.
Using the explicit discriminant in (
3.7
) with
t_{i}=t
and
\mu=1
,
this condition is equivalent to

\Bigl((1+\gamma)\theta+a\kappa-2(1-\theta)\kappa t\Bigr)^{2}=4(2\theta-1)\kappa^{2}t(1-t).

Taking square roots and using
-a=2\theta-1
yields

\frac{(1+\gamma)\theta}{\kappa}=(2\theta-1)+2(1-\theta)t\pm 2\sqrt{2\theta-1}\sqrt{t(1-t)}=w_{\pm}(t).

Recalling
\kappa=\eta+(1-\eta)\gamma
, rearranging gives (
3.11
).

At
\Delta(t,\gamma)=0
, the eigenvalues coalesce to
\lambda^{\star}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\bm{M}(t,\gamma)
.
Substituting
\frac{(1+\gamma)\theta}{\kappa}=w_{\pm}(t)
into
\mathrm{tr}\bm{M}(t,\gamma)

yields (
3.12
), where
\psi(t)
is defined in (
3.9
).

(iii) By (
3.10
), the worst-case rate over
\{t_{i}\}
equals the endpoint maximum

\max\{\rho(\bm{M}(t_{F},\gamma)),\rho(\bm{M}(t_{p},\gamma))\}.

Therefore, the minimax problem reduces to
choosing
\gamma
to balance or reduce these two endpoint rates.
The critical-damping values
\gamma_{\pm}(t_{F})
and
\gamma_{\pm}(t_{p})
provide explicit endpoint candidates;
comparing their endpoint maxima yields the stated recipe.

(iv) If
\theta=\eta=1
, then
\sqrt{2\theta-1}=1
and
w_{\pm}(t)=1\pm 2\sqrt{t(1-t)}
.
Since
\eta=1
implies
\kappa\equiv 1
, (
3.11
) gives

\gamma^{\star}(t)=2\sqrt{t(1-t)}
, and (
3.12
) gives

\lambda^{\star}(t)=1-(t+\sqrt{t(1-t)})=c^{2}-cs
, hence
\rho^{\star}(t)=|c^{2}-cs|
.
∎

3.4
A fully non-stationary scheme (ns-gCARPA with varying
\gamma_{k},\theta_{k},\eta_{k}
)

The stationary gCARPA iteration
\bm{z}^{k+1}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}(\bm{z}^{k})
already enjoys global convergence
for closed convex feasibility problems, and in the subspace case we can further characterize its linear rate via
principal angles.
In practice, however, the best-performing parameters may depend on the local geometry and can vary along the iterates.
This motivates a fully non-stationary variant, Alogrithm
2
, in the spirit of non-stationary averaged-operator schemes; see, e.g.,
[
27
]
.

Initial:

k=0
,
\bm{z}^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
; choose

\gamma_{k}\in[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]\subset(0,1)
,
\theta_{k}\in[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]\subset(0,1]
,

\eta_{k}\in[\eta_{\min},\eta_{\max}]\subset(0,1]
;

\mu\in\bigl(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma_{\max}}\bigr)
;
constants
c_{\gamma},c_{\theta},c_{\eta},\delta>0
;

repeat

\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{z}^{k})
;

\bm{u}^{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta_{k}}(\bm{z}^{k})=(1-\theta_{k})\bm{z}^{k}+\theta_{k}(2\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k})
;

\bm{y}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\bm{u}^{k+1})
;

\bm{v}^{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta_{k}}(\bm{u}^{k+1})=(1-\eta_{k})\bm{u}^{k+1}+\eta_{k}(2\bm{y}^{k+1}-\bm{u}^{k+1})
;

\bm{z}^{k+1}=(1-\mu)\bm{z}^{k}+\mu\Big((1-\gamma_{k})\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{z}^{k}+\bm{v}^{k+1})+\gamma_{k}\bm{y}^{k+1}\Big)
;

Update
(\gamma_{k+1},\theta_{k+1},\eta_{k+1})
so that

|\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}|\leq\frac{c_{\gamma}}{(k+1)^{2+\delta}},\,\,|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}|\leq\frac{c_{\theta}}{(k+1)^{2+\delta}},\,\,|\eta_{k+1}-\eta_{k}|\leq\frac{c_{\eta}}{(k+1)^{2+\delta}},

and clip back to the above intervals if needed;

until

convergence
;

Algorithm 2

Fully non-stationary generalized scheme (ns-gCARPA)

Remark 6

(How to enforce the increment conditions)
.

Algorithm
2
only requires that successive parameter changes are summably small; the specific update rule
is otherwise flexible. A convenient way to guarantee the increment bounds is to proceed in two stages:
first generate trial parameters
(\tilde{\gamma}_{k+1},\tilde{\theta}_{k+1},\tilde{\eta}_{k+1})
by any practical
heuristic, then apply a diminishing damping step and clip.

For instance, let
\alpha_{k}:=\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2+\delta}}
and set

\displaystyle\gamma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]}\big((1-\alpha_{k})\gamma_{k}+\alpha_{k}\tilde{\gamma}_{k+1}\big),

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]}\big((1-\alpha_{k})\theta_{k}+\alpha_{k}\tilde{\theta}_{k+1}\big),

\displaystyle\eta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[\eta_{\min},\eta_{\max}]}\big((1-\alpha_{k})\eta_{k}+\alpha_{k}\tilde{\eta}_{k+1}\big),

where
\Pi_{[a,b]}
denotes the Euclidean projection onto the interval
[a,b]
.
Then

\displaystyle|\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{k}(\gamma_{\max}-\gamma_{\min}),

\displaystyle|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{k}(\theta_{\max}-\theta_{\min}),

\displaystyle|\eta_{k+1}-\eta_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{k}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min}),

so the increment conditions of Algorithm
2
hold.

As an example of a trial rule, one may use Armijo–Goldstein-like multiplicative updates driven by

\rho_{k}=\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|/\|\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{k-1}\|
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\gamma_{k}\cdot\varsigma_{\gamma},&\rho_{k}<c_{1},\\
\min\{\gamma_{k}/\varsigma_{\gamma},\gamma_{\max}\},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\theta_{k}\cdot\varsigma_{\theta},&\rho_{k}<c_{1},\\
\min\{\theta_{k}/\varsigma_{\theta},\theta_{\max}\},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\eta_{k}\cdot\varsigma_{\eta},&\rho_{k}<c_{1},\\
\min\{\eta_{k}/\varsigma_{\eta},\eta_{\max}\},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

with prescribed
\varsigma_{\gamma},\varsigma_{\theta},\varsigma_{\eta}\in(0,1)
and threshold
c_{1}>0
.
The damping step above then ensures the required summability regardless of how

(\tilde{\gamma}_{k+1},\tilde{\theta}_{k+1},\tilde{\eta}_{k+1})
are produced.

Deterministic schedules are also covered. For example, fix
(\bar{\gamma},\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta})
in the interior of the intervals and set

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]}\big(\bar{\gamma}+\frac{a_{\gamma}}{(k+1)^{\delta_{\gamma}}}\big),

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]}\big(\bar{\theta}+\frac{a_{\theta}}{(k+1)^{\delta_{\theta}}}\big),

\displaystyle\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[\eta_{\min},\eta_{\max}]}\big(\bar{\eta}+\frac{a_{\eta}}{(k+1)^{\delta_{\eta}}}\big),\qquad\delta_{\gamma},\delta_{\theta},\delta_{\eta}>1,

which yields convergent parameter sequences with summable deviations.

Define
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta},\mathcal{B}^{\theta},\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\theta,\eta},\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}
as in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), then the main part of Algorithm
2
can be written compactly as the non-stationary Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann iteration

(3.15)

\bm{z}^{k+1}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma_{k}}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}}(\bm{z}^{k}).

Lemma 3.3

(Uniform averagedness and common fixed points)
.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathcal{H}
be nonempty closed convex with
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
.
Assume
\gamma_{k}\in(0,1)
,
\theta_{k},\eta_{k}\in(0,1]
, and
\mu\in\bigl(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma_{k}}\bigr)
.
Then,
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma_{k}}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}}
is averaged, and

\mathrm{fix}\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma_{k}}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}}\bigr)=X\cap Y.

Moreover, if
\gamma_{k}\in[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]\subset(0,1)
and
\mu\in\bigl(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma_{\max}}\bigr)
,
then each
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma_{k}}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}}
is
\alpha_{k}
-averaged with

\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{(1+\gamma_{\max})\mu}{2}<1
, i.e., the averagedness constant can be chosen uniformly over the parameter intervals.

Proof.

This is a direct specialization of Proposition
3.1
and Item (i) of Theorem
3.1

, whose proofs do not rely on stationarity of the iterates.
∎

Following
[
27
]
, we view the simultaneous non-stationarity in
(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k},\eta_{k})

as a summable perturbation of a limiting averaged map. Under the increment conditions in Algorithm
2
, each of
\{\gamma_{k}\},\{\theta_{k}\},\{\eta_{k}\}
is Cauchy and hence convergent.
Let

\bar{\gamma}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k},\qquad\bar{\theta}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\theta_{k},\qquad\bar{\eta}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\eta_{k}.

Then (
3.15
) admits the perturbation decomposition

(3.16)

\bm{z}^{k+1}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\bar{\gamma}}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}}(\bm{z}^{k})+\underbrace{\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma_{k}}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}}-\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\bar{\gamma}}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}}\Big)(\bm{z}^{k})}_{=:~\bm{e}^{k}}.

The next lemma quantifies Lipschitz dependence on
(\theta,\eta)
and will be used to bound
\|\bm{e}^{k}\|
.

Lemma 3.4

(Parameter perturbation bounds)
.

Fix
\bm{z}^{\dagger}\in X\cap Y
. For any
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{H}
and any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in(0,1]
,
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in(0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta^{\prime}}\bm{z}\|

\displaystyle\leq 4|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\,\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\mathcal{B}^{\theta^{\prime}}\bm{z}\|

\displaystyle\leq 4|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\,\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}\bm{z}-\mathcal{A}^{\theta^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}}\bm{z}\|

\displaystyle\leq 4\big(|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|+|\eta-\eta^{\prime}|\big)\,\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|.

Proof.

Since
\bm{z}^{\dagger}\in X
, nonexpansiveness of
\mathcal{P}_{X}
yields

\|\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}-\bm{z}\|\leq\|\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|+\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|\leq 2\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|.

Moreover,

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta^{\prime}}\bm{z}=\bigl((1-\theta)-(1-\theta^{\prime})\bigr)\bm{z}+\bigl(\theta-\theta^{\prime}\bigr)(2\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}-\bm{z})=2(\theta-\theta^{\prime})(\mathcal{P}_{X}\bm{z}-\bm{z}),

hence
\|\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta^{\prime}}\bm{z}\|\leq 4|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\,\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|
.

The bound for
\mathcal{B}^{\theta}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}
follows from nonexpansiveness of
\mathcal{P}_{Y}
.

For
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta})
, write

\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta^{\prime}}\bm{z}=\big(\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}-\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta^{\prime}}\big)\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}+\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta^{\prime}}\big(\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta^{\prime}}\bm{z}\big).

Using nonexpansiveness of
\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta^{\prime}}
and repeating the first part with
Y
in place of
X
since
\bm{z}^{\dagger}\in Y
, we obtain

\big\|\big(\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta}-\mathcal{R}_{Y}^{\eta^{\prime}}\big)\bm{w}\big\|\leq 4|\eta-\eta^{\prime}|\,\|\bm{w}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|\qquad(\forall\bm{w}\in\mathcal{H}),

and since
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}
is nonexpansive,
\|\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta}\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|\leq\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|
.
Combining these estimates and multiplying by
\tfrac{1}{2}
yields the desired bound for
\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}
.
∎

Combining the perturbation decomposition (
3.16
), the summable error estimate implied by Lemma
3.4
, and the standard Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann scheme with summable errors, we obtain the convergence of the fully non-stationary scheme.

Proposition 3.2

(Convergence of the fully non-stationary scheme)
.

Assume
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
and
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\{\gamma_{k}\}\subset[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]\subset(0,1)
,

\{\theta_{k}\}\subset[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]\subset(0,1]
,

\{\eta_{k}\}\subset[\eta_{\min},\eta_{\max}]\subset(0,1]
,
and
\mu\in\Bigl(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma_{\max}}\Bigr)
.
Assume further that there exist
(\bar{\gamma},\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta})\in(0,1)\times(0,1]\times(0,1]
such that

(3.17)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(|\gamma_{k}-\bar{\gamma}|+|\theta_{k}-\bar{\theta}|+|\eta_{k}-\bar{\eta}|\Big)<\infty.

Then Algorithm
2
generates a sequence
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}
converging to a point
\bm{z}^{\star}\in X\cap Y
.
Moreover, the shadow sequences
\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{z}^{k})
and
\bm{y}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta_{k}}\bm{z}^{k})
satisfy

(\bm{z}^{k},\bm{x}^{k},\bm{y}^{k})\to(\bm{z}^{\star},\bm{z}^{\star},\bm{z}^{\star}).

Proof.

Fix any
\bm{z}^{\dagger}\in X\cap Y
and define

\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma_{k}}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}},\qquad\bar{\mathcal{F}}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\bar{\gamma}}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}}.

By Lemma
3.3
, each
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is averaged and hence nonexpansive, and

\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}_{k})=X\cap Y
. In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\bm{z}^{\dagger})=\bm{z}^{\dagger}
for all
k
.
Therefore,

\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|=\|\mathcal{F}_{k}(\bm{z}^{k})-\mathcal{F}_{k}(\bm{z}^{\dagger})\|\leq\|\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|\leq\|\bm{z}^{0}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|,

so
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}
is bounded, and indeed Fejér monotone with respect to
X\cap Y
.

From (
3.16
) we have

\bm{z}^{k+1}=\bar{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{z}^{k})+\bm{e}^{k},\qquad\bm{e}^{k}:=(\mathcal{F}_{k}-\bar{\mathcal{F}})(\bm{z}^{k}).

Using
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\gamma}^{\theta,\eta}=(1-\mu)\mathcal{I}+\mu\big((1-\gamma)\mathcal{A}^{\theta,\eta}+\gamma\mathcal{B}^{\theta}\big)
,
we expand

\mathcal{F}_{k}-\bar{\mathcal{F}}=\mu\Big[(1-\gamma_{k})\big(\mathcal{A}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}}-\mathcal{A}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}}\big)+\gamma_{k}\big(\mathcal{B}^{\theta_{k}}-\mathcal{B}^{\bar{\theta}}\big)+(\gamma_{k}-\bar{\gamma})\big(\mathcal{B}^{\bar{\theta}}-\mathcal{A}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}}\big)\Big].

Since
\bm{z}^{\dagger}
is a common fixed point of
\mathcal{A}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\bar{\theta}}
and both maps are
nonexpansive, we obtain

\|(\mathcal{B}^{\bar{\theta}}-\mathcal{A}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}})(\bm{z}^{k})\|=\|(\mathcal{B}^{\bar{\theta}}-\mathcal{A}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}})(\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{\dagger})\|\leq 2\|\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|.

By Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}^{\theta_{k}}\bm{z}^{k}-\mathcal{B}^{\bar{\theta}}\bm{z}^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq 4|\theta_{k}-\bar{\theta}|\,\|\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{\theta_{k},\eta_{k}}\bm{z}^{k}-\mathcal{A}^{\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta}}\bm{z}^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq 4\big(|\theta_{k}-\bar{\theta}|+|\eta_{k}-\bar{\eta}|\big)\,\|\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|.

Combining these bounds and using
\gamma_{k}\in(0,1)
gives

\displaystyle\|\bm{e}^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\,\mu\,\|\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|\Big(|\gamma_{k}-\bar{\gamma}|+|\theta_{k}-\bar{\theta}|+|\eta_{k}-\bar{\eta}|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\,\mu\,\|\bm{z}^{0}-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\|\Big(|\gamma_{k}-\bar{\gamma}|+|\theta_{k}-\bar{\theta}|+|\eta_{k}-\bar{\eta}|\Big),

for an absolute constant
C>0
(e.g.,
C=8
).
By (
3.17
),
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|\bm{e}^{k}\|<\infty
.

The iteration
\bm{z}^{k+1}=\bar{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{z}^{k})+\bm{e}^{k}
is a Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann scheme for the averaged map

\bar{\mathcal{F}}
with summable errors. Hence
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}
is quasi-Fejér monotone with respect to

\mathrm{fix}(\bar{\mathcal{F}})
and converges in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to some
\bm{z}^{\star}\in\mathrm{fix}(\bar{\mathcal{F}})

(see, e.g.,
[
6
, Proposition 5.34]
).
By Lemma
3.3
,
\mathrm{fix}(\bar{\mathcal{F}})=X\cap Y
, so
\bm{z}^{\star}\in X\cap Y
.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{X}
is continuous,
\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{z}^{k})\to\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{z}^{\star})=\bm{z}^{\star}
.
Moreover,
\theta_{k}\to\bar{\theta}
follows from (
3.17
), and for any fixed
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta_{k}}\bm{z}\to\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\bar{\theta}}\bm{z}
.
Thus,

\bm{y}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\theta_{k}}\bm{z}^{k})\to\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\bar{\theta}}\bm{z}^{\star}).

Since
\bm{z}^{\star}\in X
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{z}^{\star})=\bm{z}^{\star}
and hence
\mathcal{R}_{X}^{\bar{\theta}}\bm{z}^{\star}=\bm{z}^{\star}
.
Because
\bm{z}^{\star}\in Y
as well,
\mathcal{P}_{Y}(\bm{z}^{\star})=\bm{z}^{\star}
, so the right-hand side equals
\bm{z}^{\star}
.
Therefore,
\bm{y}^{k+1}\to\bm{z}^{\star}
.
∎

Remark 7

(A convenient sufficient condition)
.

A simple sufficient condition for (
3.17
) is the increment bound used in Algorithm
2
:
if for some
\delta>0
,

|\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}|\leq\frac{c_{\gamma}}{(k+1)^{2+\delta}},\,\,|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}|\leq\frac{c_{\theta}}{(k+1)^{2+\delta}},\,\,|\eta_{k+1}-\eta_{k}|\leq\frac{c_{\eta}}{(k+1)^{2+\delta}},

then each parameter sequence is Cauchy and admits a limit
(\bar{\gamma},\bar{\theta},\bar{\eta})
, and moreover

\sum_{k\geq 0}|\gamma_{k}-\bar{\gamma}|<\infty
,
\sum_{k\geq 0}|\theta_{k}-\bar{\theta}|<\infty
,

\sum_{k\geq 0}|\eta_{k}-\bar{\eta}|<\infty
, implying (
3.17
).

4
Numerical experiments

We evaluate the proposed generalized composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm (gCARPA) and its non-stationary variant (ns-gCARPA) on several representative feasibility problems of the form

\mathrm{find}\,\bm{x}\in X\cap Y
with nonempty intersection.
The goal of this section is threefold:
(i) to verify the theoretical rate characterization on subspace feasibility problems;
(ii) to illustrate, both quantitatively and geometrically, how the additional relaxation parameters

(\theta,\eta)
can further mitigate the spiraling behavior observed for Douglas–Rachford-type iterations;
and (iii) to assess the practical benefit and robustness of full non-stationarity in challenging
nonlinear feasibility settings.
Throughout, we include as baselines the classical Douglas–Rachford method (DR) and its non-stationary
variant (ns-DR)
[
29
]
, the method of alternating projections (MAP), and several modern
relaxed/averaged projection schemes (e.g., GRAP/RAAR/AAMR) whenever applicable.
We also include the stationary and non-stationary CARPA schemes from
[
36
]
to highlight
the additional gains enabled by the proposed generalization.

4.1
General setup

Environment.

All experiments are performed in
Matlab
R2023a on a desktop computer with an Intel Core Ultra 9 185H processor (2.30 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM.
The same random seed is used for all methods in each trial to ensure reproducibility.
Unless stated otherwise, all vectors are initialized by i.i.d. standard normal entries and then normalized.

Stopping criteria.

For a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, we stop when both a fixed-point residual and a feasibility residual are small:

(4.1)

\mathrm{FPR}^{k}:=\frac{\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|}{\max\{1,\|\bm{z}^{k}\|\}}\leq\varepsilon,

We also impose a maximum iteration cap
k_{\max}
and report “fail” if the stopping criterion is not
met within
k_{\max}
iterations.

Parameter selection.

•

Relaxation parameter
\mu
.

For methods using an outer relaxation, we ensure
\mu\in\bigl(0,\frac{2}{1+\gamma_{\max}}\bigr)
when
\gamma
varies, to satisfy the averagedness requirement. In the stationary runs we typically use

\mu=1
unless stated otherwise.

•

Stationary gCARPA parameters.

We consider
(\theta,\eta)\in(0,1]
and

•

Fully non-stationary ns-gCARPA.

Define the subspaces

(4.2)

\bm{P}_{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{p}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},\ \bm{P}_{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}^{2}&\bm{C}\bm{S}\\
\bm{C}\bm{S}&\bm{S}^{2}\end{bmatrix},\ \bm{C}=\mathrm{diag}(c_{i})_{i=1}^{p},\ \bm{S}=\mathrm{diag}(s_{i})_{i=1}^{p}.

(4.4)

\phi_{p}=\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad\phi_{1}=\phi_{F},\qquad\phi_{i}=\phi_{F}+\frac{i-1}{p-1}\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{2}-\phi_{F}\Bigr),\quad i=1,\ldots,p.

4.3

We next consider a simple yet notoriously difficult nonlinear feasibility instance in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

the intersection of the unit Euclidean ball and an affine line tangent to it.
This geometry is known to be challenging for projection-type schemes, as the two sets meet

In particular, the practical winner is often problem- and tolerance-dependent, and non-stationary
steps can be significantly more effective than any fixed parameter choice.

X
is tangent to
Y
and

with
\bm{x}^{\star}=\bm{a}
.

[

]

Sparse linear inverse problems

We consider the compressed-sensing feasibility model

(4.6)

X=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}\},\qquad Y=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \|\bm{x}\|_{1}\leq c\},

where
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has full row rank. Given a
\kappa
-sparse vector

\bm{x}_{\mathrm{sol}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\bm{b}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{\mathrm{sol}}
and
c=\|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{sol}}\|_{1}

The projection onto the affine set
X
is

\mathcal{P}_{X}(\bm{w})=\bm{w}-\bm{A}^{\top}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-1}(\bm{A}\bm{w}-\bm{b}),

and the projection onto the
\ell_{1}
-ball
Y
can be computed efficiently
[
16
,
20
]
.

In all methods tested below, the dominant cost per iteration is the application of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\top}

together with one
\ell_{1}
-ball projection. Since
\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is fixed and
positive semi-definite, we precompute a Cholesky factorization of
\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
once, and apply
(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-1}
via two
triangular solves when evaluating
\mathcal{P}_{X}(\cdot)
.

then
\bm{b}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{\mathrm{sol}}

\bm{x}_{\mathrm{sol}}\in X\cap Y

-projection output used by each method, i.e.,

\mathrm{card}(\mathrm{supp}(\bm{y}^{k}))

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed the generalized composed alternating relaxed projection algorithm (gCARPA) for the two-set convex feasibility problem,
together with its non-stationary variants ns-gCARPA.
The algorithm unifies and extends several classical fixed-point iterations, and in particular recovers CARPA as a special case.
On the theoretical side, we established their global convergence under standard assumptions.
For the subspace model, we derived a principal-angle spectral characterization that yields explicit predicted linear factors
and supports principled parameter selection, including a minimax/critical-damping recipe in a symmetric regime.
Numerically, experiments on subspaces, the ball–line instance, and sparse linear inverse problems illustrate that the
additional relaxation parameters
(\theta,\eta)
can be beneficial in certain regimes, and that non-stationary tuning can improve robustness across instances.
