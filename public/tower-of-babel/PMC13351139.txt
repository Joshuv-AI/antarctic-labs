TNAP-mediated hydrolysis of phosphorylated enamel matrix proteins drives local phosphate generation and enamel mineralization
Authors: Haya Kodama, Risako Chiba-Ohkuma, Ryuji Yamamoto, Shoji Hamanaka, Takatoshi Nagano, Yasuo Yamakoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental enamel is the most highly mineralized tissue in the body, and its formation requires a precisely regulated supply of Pi. Although phosphate transport in ameloblasts has been studied, how phosphate is generated within the enamel remains unclear. Tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) is a key enzyme in skeletal mineralization, but its functional contribution to enamel formation has not been fully established. Herein, we examined the presence, activity, and catalytic potential of TNAP in porcine enamel matrix. To determine the localization and functional contribution of TNAP within the enamel matrix during the secretory and maturation stages, we performed histochemical staining, gene expression analysis, and immunohistochemistry. TNAP activity, low-molecular-weight phosphate-related compounds, and levamisole-sensitive Pi production were quantified in enamel matrix extracts and ex vivo reactions. Purified enamel proteins were incubated with recombinant human TNAP to assess phosphate release. TNAP was detected and showed catalytic activity. PPi and ATP were present; Pi generation was significantly suppressed by levamisole, indicating TNAP dependence. Recombinant human TNAP directly hydrolyzed phosphorylated enamel proteins, including amelogenins and enamelin, as well as ATP, and TNAP-dependent hydrolysis of PPi was observed. Proteolytic processing of amelogenins enhanced TNAP-dependent phosphate release. Stage-dependent differences in enzyme distribution and substrate availability suggested regulation during enamel formation. These findings demonstrate that the enamel matrix is not merely a passive site of mineral deposition, but a functionally active compartment of phosphate metabolism, and identify TNAP as a central integrator that coordinates phosphate generation with protease-dependent processing of enamel proteins to support proper enamel mineralization.Keywords: ATP, biomineralization, enamel, extracellular matrix, inorganic phosphate, phosphatase, proteolysis, transporter, tooth

Dental enamel is the most highly mineralized hard tissue in the body and is composed predominantly of substituted hydroxyapatite crystals containing calcium ions (Ca2+) and Pi. Enamel formation proceeds through the highly oriented growth of hydroxyapatite crystals within the extracellular matrix secreted by ameloblasts, and its exceptional hardness and durability depend on a precisely regulated supply of Ca2+ and Pi (1, 2, 3). In addition to Ca2+ and Pi, pH buffering by bicarbonate ions (HCO3-) is indispensable for enamel mineralization, and the coordinated regulation of multiple ion transport pathways maintains a microenvironment suitable for mineral deposition (4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10). Disruption of these transport systems leads to hypomineralized enamel, resulting in reduced acid resistance and increased susceptibility to dental caries (11, 12).

Recent studies have demonstrated that Ca2+ and HCO3- are supplied through active transcellular transport by ameloblasts and that the coordinated functions of ion transporters and channels are important in enamel mineralization (8, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18). In contrast, the mechanisms underlying the transport and supply mechanisms of Pi remain limited. However, 32P labeling experiments have shown rapid incorporation of Pi into secretory stage (Sec) enamel (19, 20), suggesting that similar to Ca2+ and HCO3-, Pi is also subject to active regulatory control.

Unlike bone, enamel formation is a transient and irreversible mineralization process, and its regulatory mechanisms are thought to include enamel-specific features (1, 3). Although the expression of phosphate transporters and ATP metabolism-related molecules in ameloblasts has been reported (21, 22), how Pi is generated and supplied within the enamel itself, the actual site of mineralization, remains poorly understood.

Tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) is a central enzyme in bone and dentin mineralization, where it supplies Pi through the hydrolysis of PPi, and its deficiency causes severe mineralization disorders such as hypophosphatasia (23, 24, 25). Although TNAP activity has long been suggested to be present during enamel formation (26, 27, 28, 29, 30), recent studies have further demonstrated that TNAP expressed in the enamel epithelium plays an important role in enamel mineralization in murine models (31). In addition, TNAP has been shown to regulate the local Pi/PPi balance and is strongly localized in the stratum intermedium (SI) during amelogenesis (32). These findings suggest that TNAP may contribute to the local processing of phosphate-containing substrates, including enamel matrix proteins, within the enamel microenvironment.

During the Sec of enamel formation, phosphorylated proteins such as amelogenin and enamelin are abundant in the enamel matrix (33, 34, 35), raising the possibility that these proteins may serve as intrinsic phosphate-containing substrates. However, whether these substrates are hydrolyzed by TNAP to supply Pi has not been directly examined.

In this study, we investigated whether TNAP is present and catalytically active within the enamel matrix and whether it contributes to Pi generation during enamel formation.

Results

Verification of TNAP in enamel matrix

Alkaline phosphatase in situ activity

Figure 1 shows representative alkaline phosphatase (ALP) in situ activity images of porcine incisors from approximately 5-month-old animals. For specimen preparation, soft tissues, including the dental pulp and enamel organ epithelium (EOE), were removed mechanically as thoroughly as possible, followed by extensive washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). On the external surface (Fig. 1A), a broad area of purple-blue TNAP activity was observed predominantly on the root side, whereas staining on the crown side was relatively weak. On the internal surface after sectioning (Fig. 1B), continuous TNAP activity was detected along the exposed dentin and enamel. In contrast, the reddish soft tissue-like regions observed in the same field showed no apparent staining. Figure 1, C and D show the corresponding control specimens for Figure 1, A and B, respectively. In these control samples, no detectable TNAP activity following the tooth morphology was observed, indicating that the staining seen in Figure 1, A and B was not due to nonspecific deposition or intrinsic coloration of the specimens.

TNAP (Alpl) gene expression in secretory- and maturation-stage porcine EOE

Total RNA was extracted from porcine EOE corresponding to the Sec and maturation stage (Mat) from approximately 5-month-old animals (Fig. 2A), and TNAP (Alpl) expression was analyzed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) (Fig. 2B). To validate the stage identity of the samples, the expression of the stage-specific marker genes enamelysin (Mmp-20) and kallikrein-related peptidase 4 (Klk4) was first evaluated. The relative expression levels of these genes were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) as the endogenous control. The mRNA level of Mmp-20 was approximately 7.87-fold higher in Sec EOE than in Mat EOE. In contrast, Klk4 transcript levels were significantly higher in Mat EOE than in Sec EOE, showing an approximately 60-fold increase. These results confirmed that the prepared EOE samples corresponded to the Sec and Mat, respectively. Alpl mRNA was detected in both EOE stages; however, its expression was 13-fold higher in Sec EOE than that in Mat EOE.

Immunohistochemical localization of TNAP in porcine incisor tooth germs

The localization of TNAP was analyzed immunohistochemically using sections of porcine incisor tooth germs from approximately 5-month-old animals (Fig. 2C). During the Sec, TNAP immunoreactivity was clearly detected in the SI. In addition, a band-like immunoreactive signal was observed along the distal ends of ameloblasts facing the enamel, indicating localization in close proximity to the Sec enamel. In contrast, during the Mat, TNAP immunoreactivity was detected predominantly in the papillary layer (PL), showing a distribution pattern distinct from the SI-dominant localization observed during the Sec. In Mat ameloblasts, immunoreactivity was observed throughout the entire cell layer, in contrast to the distal end-dominant distribution seen at the Sec. No specific signals were detected in the negative control (NC) sections at either the Sec or Mat in which the primary antibody was omitted during the staining procedure.

Detection of TNAP activity in enamel matrix

Sec (soft enamel) and Mat (hard enamel) enamel samples were collected from the crowns of porcine incisors obtained from approximately 5-month-old animals (Fig. 3A and Table 1), and TNAP activity was analyzed in enamel matrix fractions extracted under neutral Tris-HCl conditions (T fraction) and alkaline carbonate–bicarbonate conditions (C fraction), respectively. Figure 3B shows the time-dependent changes in TNAP activity in enamel matrix fractions from soft and hard enamel, normalized to enamel sample weight. In both enamel samples, TNAP activity in the C fraction increased over time. Figure 3C shows TNAP activity after 20 min of incubation (n = 6). In soft enamel, TNAP activity was 2,626 ± 37 IU/L/mg enamel in the T fraction and 125,541 ± 15,824 IU/L/mg enamel in the C fraction. In hard enamel, the corresponding values were 869 ± 14 IU/L/mg enamel in the T fraction and 93,263 ± 13,742 IU/L/mg enamel in the C fraction. TNAP activity was barely detectable in the T fractions at both stages, whereas high activity was observed in the C fractions. Because TNAP activity was markedly higher in the C fraction, the amount of soluble protein in each fraction was also examined. In soft enamel, the amount of soluble protein was 7.64 ± 0.47 mg/g enamel in the T fraction and 42.0 ± 3.60 mg/g enamel in the C fraction. In hard enamel, the corresponding values were 2.02 ± 0.16 mg/g enamel in the T fraction and 6.92 ± 0.86 mg/g enamel in the C fraction. Specific activity was then calculated by normalizing TNAP activity to the amount of soluble protein in each fraction. In soft enamel matrix, the specific activity was 3.44 × 105 IU/L/mg protein in the T fraction and 2.99 × 106 IU/L/mg protein in the C fraction, representing an approximately 9-fold increase. In hard enamel matrix, the specific activity was 4.30 × 105 IU/L/mg protein in the T fraction and 1.35 × 107 IU/L/mg protein in the C fraction, corresponding to an approximately 31-fold increase. Thus, the higher TNAP activity observed in the C remained evident after normalization to protein content. Figure 3D shows TNAP protein levels quantified by ELISA and normalized to enamel weight. In soft enamel, TNAP protein levels were 0.088 ± 0.012 ng/mg enamel in the T fraction and 0.207 ± 0.058 ng/mg enamel in the C fraction. In hard enamel, the corresponding values were 0.080 ± 0.021 ng/mg enamel and 0.166 ± 0.059 ng/mg enamel. In both fractions, TNAP protein levels were significantly higher in soft enamel than in hard enamel (p < 0.05). Catalytic activity of recombinant human TNAP (rhTNAP) was also examined under neutral conditions. Although the reaction rate was markedly reduced compared with that observed at pH 10.8, catalytic activity was maintained at neutral pH (Fig. S1).

Analysis of candidate TNAP substrates and their supply mechanisms in enamel matrix

Presence of PPi and ATP in enamel matrix

Potential substrates for TNAP were examined in developing enamel matrix. PPi, a well-recognized physiological substrate of TNAP, was first measured in soft and hard enamel (Fig. 4A). The mean free PPi content (n = 6) was 2.91 ± 1.03 μg/g enamel in soft enamel and 5.19 ± 1.66 μg/g enamel in hard enamel. In contrast, the mean bound PPi content (n = 6) was 27.2 ± 9.46 μg/g enamel in soft enamel and 48.2 ± 13.0 μg/g enamel in hard enamel. In both enamel samples, the bound PPi content was significantly higher than the free PPi content (p < 0.05). In addition, the bound PPi content was significantly higher in hard enamel than in soft enamel (p < 0.05).

ATP was then measured in soft and hard enamel as a potential source of PPi generation through ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) activity (Fig. 4B). The mean free ATP content (n = 6) was 31.8 ± 5.63 μg/g enamel in soft enamel and 10.6 ± 3.10 μg/g enamel in hard enamel. The mean bound ATP content was 236 ± 23.1 μg/g enamel in soft enamel and 6.66 ± 2.20 μg/g enamel in hard enamel. In soft enamel, ATP content in the bound fraction was significantly higher than that in the free fraction (p < 0.05). Because the acidic demineralization conditions used to obtain the bound fraction (pH 2.7, 2 h) were potentially harsh for ATP and could have resulted in underestimation, ATP was also requantified using perchloric acid extraction, a method commonly used in energy metabolism analyses (Fig. S2). In this analysis, ATP was extracted and measured from whole enamel samples without separation into free and bound fractions. The mean ATP content (n = 6) was 259 ± 21.2 μg/g enamel in soft enamel and 14.3 ± 0.37 μg/g enamel in hard enamel, values comparable to those obtained using Tris-EDTA extraction. With both methods, ATP content in hard enamel was low overall and was significantly lower than that in soft enamel (p < 0.05).

TNAP-dependent Pi generation in an ex vivo reaction system using enamel matrix

Figure 4C shows changes in Pi levels in an ex vivo reaction system using soft and hard enamel. The value immediately after the start of the reaction (0 h), the values after 20 h of incubation, and those obtained in the presence of the TNAP inhibitor levamisole, exogenous PPi, recombinant human ENPP1 (rhENPP1), or ATP are shown. The Pi content at 0 h (Cont; n = 6) was 2.68 ± 0.54 mg/g enamel in soft enamel and 2.29 ± 0.13 mg/g enamel in hard enamel. After 20 h of incubation (Ori), the Pi content increased to 4.38 ± 0.11 mg/g enamel in soft enamel and 3.07 ± 0.49 mg/g enamel in hard enamel. In both soft and hard enamel, incubation for 20 h resulted in a significant increase in Pi compared with the initial values under conditions without exogenous additives. In the presence of exogenous PPi or rhENPP1, higher Pi levels were detected than under control conditions, and these increases in Pi production were significantly suppressed by levamisole. Similarly, in the presence of exogenous ATP, a significant increase in Pi content was observed compared with control conditions. Pi release from ATP was also evaluated in vitro using rhTNAP (Fig. S3). Quantitative data are summarized in Table 1, including the amounts of soluble proteins extracted with Tris-HCl and carbonate-bicarbonate buffers, as well as TNAP-specific activities normalized to protein content.

Expression and localization of molecules involved in PPi/Pi supply

The expression and localization of molecules potentially involved in the cellular supply of PPi and Pi to the enamel matrix were next examined. Gene expression analysis was performed for pannexin 3 (Panx3), an ATP-release transporter; Enpp1, which hydrolyzes ATP; progressive ankylosis protein (Ank), which is implicated in PPi export; and xenotropic and polytropic retrovirus receptor 1 (Xpr1), a phosphate transporter responsible for Pi export. Figure 5A shows the relative expression levels of Panx3, Enpp1, Ank, and Xpr1 normalized to Gapdh. The mRNA levels of Panx3 and Ank were higher in Sec EOE, showing 16.9-fold and 10.6-fold increases, respectively, compared with Mat EOE. In contrast, Enpp1 expression was higher in Mat EOE, showing a 6.89-fold increase compared with the Sec. Xpr1 expression also differed significantly between the two stages, although the magnitude of change was small (1.06-fold). The localization of PPi/Pi supply-related molecules (PANX3, ENPP1, ANK, and XPR1) was then examined by immunohistochemistry to identify the cell layers within the EOE associated with these expression patterns (Fig. 5B). PANX3 was mainly localized to the ameloblast layer (AM), with prominent staining along the distal ends of ameloblasts facing the enamel side (E side). The staining was more pronounced at the Mat than at the Sec. ENPP1 also showed predominant localization in the AM along the E side, with stronger staining at the Mat than at the Sec. ANK showed weak but detectable signals in the AM at both the Sec and Mat compared with the NC, with a tendency toward E side-biased localization. Although signals were also observed in the SI and PL, their localization was less distinct than that of PANX3 and ENPP1. In contrast, XPR1 was mainly distributed in the AM, showing E side-oriented localization at the Sec and enhanced staining in the AM at the Mat. To further examine the subcellular localization of TNAP in EOE, TNAP activity was measured in cytosolic and membrane fractions. As shown in Figure 5C, TNAP activity was predominantly detected in the membrane fraction, whereas only minimal activity was observed in the cytosolic fraction. These results are consistent with the known glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored nature of TNAP and support its localization at the cell membrane. These findings indicate that TNAP is predominantly associated with the membrane fraction in EOE.

Catalytic potential of TNAP toward enamel proteins

In addition to low-molecular-weight phosphorylated compounds such as PPi and ATP, as well as Pi itself and their cellular supply pathways, we examined whether phosphorylated enamel proteins could also serve as direct substrates for TNAP in the enamel matrix. To evaluate Pi release from enamel proteins, purified porcine 20-kDa amelogenin (P148 amelogenin), tyrosine-rich amelogenin peptide (TRAP), leucine-rich amelogenin peptide (LRAP), and 32-kDa enamelin were incubated with rhTNAP under identical conditions, and the amount of Pi generated was quantified as the difference between 20 h and the start of the reaction (t20 − t0). The released Pi was normalized to the molar amount of each substrate protein and expressed as mol ΔPi/mol protein (Fig. 6A). Pi release by rhTNAP was detected from all amelogenin-derived substrates. Among them, P148 amelogenin, the most abundant species in immature enamel, showed lower Pi release than TRAP and LRAP. Under the same conditions and incubation time, TRAP and LRAP released 1.76-fold and 1.75-fold more Pi than P148 amelogenin, respectively (n = 6). In contrast, Pi release from 32-kDa enamelin, when normalized on a molar basis, was higher than that from any of the amelogenin-derived substrates, including P148 amelogenin, TRAP, and LRAP (n = 6). SDS-PAGE analysis of P148 amelogenin TRAP, LRAP, and 32-kDa enamelin before and after rhTNAP treatment showed little change in band mobility for P148 amelogenin. In contrast, the protein bands of TRAP, LRAP, and 32-kDa enamelin shifted toward lower molecular weights after the reaction (red dashed lines, Fig. 6B).

We then examined the effect of amelogenin processing into lower-molecular-weight forms on TNAP activity. P148 amelogenin was incubated for 20 h in the presence or absence of rhTNAP and/or ex vivo matrix metalloproteinase-20 (MMP-20), and the released Pi was measured (Fig. 6C). Compared with the control incubated with P148 amelogenin alone (P148 only), the rhTNAP-treated group (P148 + rhTNAP) released 6.36-fold more Pi. The group treated with MMP-20 (P148 + MMP-20) in the absence of rhTNAP showed phosphate release at a level comparable to that of the control group. The combined treatment with rhTNAP and MMP-20 (P148 + MMP-20 + rhTNAP) resulted in a 27.1-fold increase in Pi release. Consistent with these findings, the corresponding SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 6D, left) showed a difference in the molecular weight of TRAP generated from P148 amelogenin between P148 + MMP-20 (lane 3) and co-incubation with MMP-20 and rhTNAP (lane 4), as indicated by the red arrow. In addition, faint low-molecular-weight bands (∼13 kDa) observed in lanes 1 to 4 were consistent with previously reported amelogenin processing products generated from P148 amelogenin. No clearly detectable protein bands were observed in the lane containing MMP-20 alone (lane 5) under the conditions used, likely because the amount present in the reaction mixture was near or below the practical detection limit of Simply Blue Safe Stain. Additional SDS-PAGE analysis using purified recombinant rhTNAP (Fig. 6D, right) demonstrated that rhTNAP became detectable when 50 ng was loaded, whereas the approximately 5 ng amount present in the reaction mixtures was barely detectable.

Effects of exogenous MMP-20 and KLK4 on Pi release from enamel matrix

The effects of partially purified porcine MMP-20 and KLK4 on TNAP-dependent Pi release from enamel matrix were examined in an ex vivo system (Fig. 6E). In the MMP-20 addition experiment using soft enamel (Fig. 6E, left), the Pi content increased significantly after 20 h of incubation under control conditions (Ori) compared with the initial value (Cont). Notably, the addition of MMP-20 further enhanced Pi release beyond this baseline increase, and this effect was significantly suppressed by levamisole. The Pi content at the start of the reaction (Cont) was 3.72 ± 0.29 mg/g enamel (n = 6), and it increased significantly to 4.22 ± 0.25 mg/g enamel after 20 h of incubation without MMP-20 (Ori). In the group incubated for 20 h with partially purified porcine MMP-20 (MMP-20), the Pi content further increased to 4.91 ± 0.39 mg/g enamel, which was significantly higher than that in both the Cont and Ori groups. When levamisole was added together with MMP-20 (MMP-20 + Lev), the Pi content was partially reduced to 4.54 ± 0.29 mg/g enamel. A similar trend was observed in the KLK4 addition experiment (Fig. 6E, right). As in the MMP-20 experiment, Pi content increased over time under control conditions and was further enhanced by KLK4, with this increase suppressed by levamisole. The Pi content at the start of the reaction (Cont) was 4.01 ± 0.12 mg/g enamel (n = 6), and it increased significantly to 4.79 ± 0.22 mg/g after 20 h of incubation without KLK4 (Ori). In the group incubated for 20 h with partially purified porcine KLK4 (KLK4), the Pi content further increased to 5.05 ± 0.11 mg/g enamel, which was significantly higher than that in both the Cont and Ori groups. In the presence of levamisole together with KLK4 (KLK4 + Lev), this increase was partially reduced to 4.47 ± 0.055 mg/g enamel.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that TNAP is present in the porcine enamel matrix and exhibits catalytic activity capable of generating Pi. This finding identifies the enamel matrix as a functionally active compartment of phosphate metabolism rather than a passive recipient of ions and provides a biochemical framework for understanding how local Pi is generated during amelogenesis. By integrating histochemical, molecular biological, and biochemical analyses, our findings support the concept that TNAP functions as a regulator of extracellular phosphate metabolism during enamel formation. Together, these results suggest that, similar to proteases such as MMP-20 and KLK4, TNAP serves as an important enzyme linking enamel mineralization with matrix biochemistry.

Previous studies in rodents have reported that TNAP is predominantly localized in the SI during the Sec (26, 27, 28). In the present study, TNAP was clearly localized at the distal end of ameloblasts and in the SI during the Sec and in the PL and AL during the Mat. These findings suggest that TNAP is not confined to supporting cell layers but may also function directly at the cell–matrix interface, consistent with a role in the regulation of phosphate metabolism within the enamel matrix. Consistent with this interpretation, our subcellular fractionation analysis demonstrated that TNAP activity was predominantly detected in the membrane fraction of EOE, with only minimal activity in the cytosolic fraction. This distribution is in agreement with the GPI-anchored nature of TNAP and supports its functional positioning at the cell surface, where it may directly participate in extracellular phosphate metabolism. Although the specificity of the TNAP antibody was not directly confirmed by preabsorption assays in the present study, multiple complementary approaches support the validity of our immunohistochemical findings. The observed localization patterns are consistent with previously reported distributions of TNAP, and the stage-dependent expression profiles are in agreement with our quantitative PCR results for Alpl. In addition, the immunohistochemical signals correspond well with histochemical ALP in situ activity, reflecting enzymatic activity. These findings are further supported by the independent detection of TNAP protein and activity in enamel matrix fractions by ELISA and enzymatic assays. Nevertheless, further validation using approaches such as preabsorption assays would strengthen the specificity assessment and represent an important direction for future studies.

Quantitative PCR analysis showed that Alpl is expressed in both Sec and Mat EOE, with significantly higher expression at the Sec. Together with the stage-dependent changes in TNAP localization observed by immunohistochemistry, these results indicate that both the abundance and the cellular and extracellular distribution of TNAP are reorganized according to the stage of enamel formation. This stage-dependent reconfiguration is consistent with the idea that the extracellular phosphate microenvironment is dynamically regulated during enamel formation.

TNAP is a GPI-anchored membrane protein (36, 37, 38, 39), and it has been reported to be cleaved and released from the cell surface by GPI-specific phospholipase D (40). In the present fractionation analysis, the majority of TNAP activity was recovered in the C fraction, whereas little activity was detected in the T fraction. In the present study, the T and C fractions should not be interpreted merely as operational extraction fractions but rather as reflecting distinct biochemical states of proteins and enzymes within the enamel matrix. The T fraction, obtained under neutral Tris-HCl conditions, is considered to represent predominantly water soluble and loosely associated components, including proteins and enzymes that are either freely diffusible or weakly bound to the matrix or crystal surfaces. In contrast, the C fraction, extracted under alkaline carbonate–bicarbonate conditions (pH 10.8), likely represents components that are tightly associated with the mineral phase, including proteins and enzymes bound to hydroxyapatite crystal surfaces or intercrystalline regions. In this context, the markedly elevated TNAP activity and specific activity observed in the C fraction cannot be explained solely by differences in extraction efficiency but instead suggest that catalytically active TNAP is preferentially associated with the mineral phase of the enamel matrix. This finding supports the notion that TNAP exists in a mineral-associated state and may function directly at the crystal interface to regulate local phosphate availability during enamel mineralization. At the same time, the detection of TNAP activity, albeit at lower levels, in the T fraction suggests the presence of a soluble or loosely bound pool of TNAP within the matrix. Such a fraction may exhibit greater mobility and accessibility to substrates, potentially contributing to more diffuse phosphate generation throughout the matrix. Taken together, these findings suggest that TNAP exists in at least two functional pools within the enamel matrix: a mineral-associated, high-activity form enriched in the C fraction and a more soluble or weakly bound form represented in the T fraction, which may play complementary roles in regulating phosphate dynamics during enamel formation.

This distribution suggests that TNAP is associated with components of the enamel matrix. An important conceptual distinction emerging from this study is that between total enzymatic contribution and enzyme enrichment. When normalized to enamel weight, TNAP activity appeared slightly higher at the Sec; however, when normalized to the amount of soluble protein, a marked increase in specific activity was observed in the C fraction at the Mat. This difference likely reflects not only variation in TNAP abundance but also differences in the total amount of extracted matrix proteins between stages. Thus, whereas the quantitative contribution of TNAP may predominate within the protein-rich matrix environment of the Sec, TNAP appears relatively enriched at the Mat as overall protein content declines, potentially reflecting a qualitatively enhanced enzymatic state. This reorganization suggests that extracellular phosphate metabolism is dynamically regulated in a stage-dependent manner during enamel formation. In other words, the pattern of TNAP localization and the surrounding matrix environment differ between the Sec and Mat, and the mechanism of Pi generation may be reconfigured accordingly. Furthermore, although TNAP protein levels were higher in soft enamel than in hard enamel, enzymatic activity in the soluble fractions did not differ significantly between stages. This discrepancy suggests that the effective catalytic activity of TNAP within enamel is determined not only by enzyme abundance but also by local factors such as substrate availability and the presence of potential inhibitors. Therefore, TNAP activity within the enamel matrix is unlikely to be dictated simply by expression level and is more likely regulated through stage-specific alterations in the matrix environment. While the present findings provide insight into the state and regulation of TNAP within the enamel matrix, the molecular mechanisms by which this GPI-anchored membrane enzyme are released into the enamel matrix, retained within it, and stabilized remain unresolved and represent important questions raised by this study.

Functionally, the significant suppression of Pi generation in enamel matrix by the alkaline phosphatase inhibitor levamisole strongly supports the conclusion that Pi production in the matrix is TNAP dependent. Levamisole is a well-established noncompetitive inhibitor of TNAP that interferes with its metal ion–dependent catalytic mechanism. Accordingly, the observed reduction in Pi generation in the presence of levamisole reflects the inhibition of TNAP-mediated hydrolysis of phosphate-containing substrates, including PPi, ATP, and phosphorylated enamel proteins. The partial inhibition (∼25%) observed in the presence of levamisole likely reflects the contribution of TNAP to total phosphate production within the enamel matrix, rather than the complete suppression of all phosphate-generating processes. The remaining activity may be attributable to incomplete inhibition under matrix conditions, contributions from other phosphatases, or minor nonenzymatic phosphate release. These findings indicate that TNAP actively contributes to local phosphate production within the enamel matrix by catalyzing the breakdown of multiple endogenous substrates. Moreover, the enhancement of Pi production by exogenous PPi and by rhENPP1 indicates that PPi present in the matrix can function as an intrinsic TNAP substrate and that ENPP1-mediated ATP hydrolysis can serve as a PPi supply pathway. However, because ENPP1 is a membrane-associated enzyme, it is unlikely that substantial endogenous ENPP1 activity is preserved in the enamel matrix fractions used here. Therefore, the Pi production observed in the enamel matrix cannot be fully explained solely by ENPP1-dependent ATP breakdown. The modest increase in Pi production following addition of exogenous ATP, together with our in vitro demonstration that recombinant TNAP can directly hydrolyze ATP to generate Pi, supports the possibility that matrix-associated TNAP can participate directly in ATP-derived phosphate metabolism. Nevertheless, because substantial Pi production was observed even in the absence of exogenous ATP, ATP is unlikely to be the major Pi source under our assay conditions. Instead, PPi and/or phosphorylated proteins present in the matrix are likely to represent the primary contributors. Although minor nonenzymatic contributions under ex vivo conditions cannot be entirely excluded, the consistent levamisole sensitivity and enhancement by PPi and rhENPP1 strongly support a dominant and physiologically relevant role for TNAP in enamel phosphate metabolism. Moreover, Sec enamel fluid has been reported to be near equilibrium with existing enamel crystals rather than highly supersaturated with respect to enamel mineral (41). Thus, under ex vivo conditions, disequilibrium could potentially lead to nonenzymatic release of loosely bound Pi from crystal surfaces or intercrystalline phases. However, considering levamisole sensitivity and the enhancement by PPi and rhENPP1, we conclude that TNAP represents a major contributor to Pi production in this system. While our in vitro dephosphorylation assays provide direct biochemical evidence that TNAP can hydrolyze phosphorylated enamel matrix proteins, we acknowledge that such assays do not fully recapitulate the complexity of the in vivo environment. In this context, our findings should be interpreted as mechanistic evidence that complements previous genetic studies demonstrating the essential role of TNAP in enamel formation. Taken together, these results support a primary and physiologically relevant role for TNAP in matrix Pi generation.

In the present study, PANX3, ENPP1, ANK, and XPR1 were localized to the distal membrane of ameloblasts during both the Sec and Mat. This spatial distribution suggests that phosphate-related molecular pathways from ameloblasts to the enamel are organized at the cell–matrix interface. Moreover, stage-dependent differences in ATP and PPi content, together with dynamic changes in ENPP1 and ANK expression, indicate that extracellular phosphate supply mechanisms are reorganized during enamel formation. Collectively, these findings support a model in which Pi production within the enamel matrix arises through coordinated ATP release via PANX3, extracellular conversion of ATP to PPi by ENPP1, PPi transport through ANK, direct Pi export via XPR1, and hydrolysis of ATP and PPi by matrix-localized TNAP.

Furthermore, under physiological conditions, phosphorylated enamel proteins are likely to serve as important substrates contributing to local Pi production. In porcine secretory enamel, full-length amelogenin (P173) is rapidly cleaved at the C terminus by MMP-20 after secretion, generating P148 amelogenin as the predominant species in immature enamel. P148 is further processed by MMP-20 to produce the P45 peptide TRAP, and the alternatively spliced product LRAP (P56) is also expressed (42). P148, TRAP, and LRAP share a single phosphorylated serine at position 16. In this study, purified porcine P148, TRAP, and LRAP were all dephosphorylated by rhTNAP, releasing Pi. TRAP and LRAP exhibited higher Pi release than P148 on a molar basis, indicating that even substrates with the same phosphorylation site can differ substantially in accessibility to TNAP depending on molecular size and structural state. We also found that 32-kDa enamelin, an acidic protein abundant in secretory enamel and phosphorylated at two serine residues (43), released more Pi than the amelogenin-derived substrates when normalized on a molar basis. Enamelin is known to persist into mature enamel (44). Even after dephosphorylation by TNAP, enamelin may remain associated with apatite crystals through interactions between its acidic Asp/Glu-rich clusters and Ca2+ on the crystal surface. In addition, incorporation into crystal surfaces or intercrystalline interfaces during crystal growth could physically trap enamelin fragments, allowing them to persist in mature enamel independently of phosphorylation state.

Amelogenin is known to self-assemble under near-neutral conditions into nanosphere-like supramolecular structures (45, 46). In such assemblies, the phosphorylated serine residue may be positioned within the interior, thereby restricting enzymatic access. Consistent with this idea, the three amelogenin substrates became turbid in neutral buffer in our assays, suggesting self-assembly, and P148 exhibited lower Pi release than TRAP and LRAP under these conditions. This difference may reflect partial shielding of the phosphorylation site by higher-order assembly. In contrast, TRAP generated by limited proteolysis by MMP-20 may adopt a structural state with reduced steric constraints, thereby increasing accessibility of the phosphorylation site to TNAP. This concept was supported by both in vitro experiments using P148 amelogenin and ex vivo experiments using Sec enamel sample. Under conditions in which MMP-20 was added, Pi content in the enamel matrix was significantly increased, and this increase was partially suppressed by levamisole, a TNAP inhibitor. These findings suggest that proteolytic processing of enamel proteins by MMP-20, resulting in the generation of lower-molecular-weight forms such as TRAP, enhances substrate availability for TNAP and thereby promotes Pi release.

The fundamental role of TNAP in promoting mineralization through the generation of Pi from phosphate-containing substrates is well established in bone. In this respect, the core enzymatic function of TNAP is likely to be conserved between bone and enamel. However, our findings suggest that the context in which TNAP operates in enamel is distinct from that in bone. In particular, our data indicate that in addition to PPi, multiple substrates—including ATP and phosphorylated enamel matrix proteins—can serve as sources of Pi within the enamel matrix. This broader substrate utilization contrasts with the classical view of TNAP function in bone, where PPi hydrolysis is considered the primary mechanism. Furthermore, we demonstrate that proteolytic processing of enamel matrix proteins by MMP-20 and KLK4 enhances TNAP-dependent Pi release, suggesting a functional coupling between proteolysis and phosphate generation. Such a mechanism differs from that observed in the context of bone mineralization. Taken together, these findings suggest that while the core enzymatic activity of TNAP is shared, the regulatory context and substrate environment in enamel are distinct, reflecting the unique and transient nature of the enamel matrix during amelogenesis.

A proposed working model of our findings is presented in Figure 7. Our data support a model in which phosphate metabolism and enamel protein proteolysis are tightly coordinated during both the Sec and Mat of enamel formation. During the Sec (Fig. 7A), ATP released via PANX3 is converted to PPi by ENPP1; PPi, together with PPi transported by ANK, serves as a TNAP substrate to drive local Pi generation. In parallel, Pi export via XPR1 may contribute to phosphate supply, indicating a multilayered system for establishing the Pi microenvironment in secretory enamel. Concurrently, TNAP can directly dephosphorylate enamel proteins, including amelogenins and enamelin, particularly after partial processing by MMP-20, which likely increases substrate accessibility. This coupling between proteolysis and dephosphorylation provides a mechanism for dynamic regulation of Pi supply within developing enamel. During the Mat (Fig. 7B), further protein degradation by KLK4 generates smaller enamel protein fragments that can be more efficiently dephosphorylated by TNAP, thereby enhancing Pi supply in maturing enamel. ATP release via PANX3 and ATP-to-PPi conversion by ENPP1 appear to be maintained at this stage, together with Pi export via XPR1, supporting continued phosphate supply even as total matrix protein decreases. In particular, increased ENPP1 expression and immunostaining patterns suggest that the contribution of the ATP-to-PPi conversion pathway becomes relatively greater during maturation than during secretion. Collectively, these pathways likely sustain TNAP-dependent Pi generation in Mat enamel and cooperate with Pi release from low-molecular-weight protein fragments generated by KLK4 to establish the phosphate environment characteristic of this stage.

This study has several limitations. The experiments were performed primarily in porcine and ex vivo systems, and some of the proposed phosphate supply pathways were inferred from localization and biochemical activity rather than directly tested in vivo. Future studies should therefore determine how TNAP is released and retained within the enamel matrix and define the specific functional contributions of PANX3, ENPP1, ANK, and XPR1 during enamel formation.

In summary, our results demonstrate that functional TNAP is present in the enamel matrix and plays a central role in Pi production during enamel formation. TNAP is expressed at both the Sec and Mat and undergoes stage-dependent reorganization in its localization, consistent with dynamic regulation of extracellular phosphate metabolism. The presence of PPi and ATP in the matrix, together with the localization of PANX3, ENPP1, ANK, and XPR1 at the cell–matrix interface, supports the existence of stage-specific ATP/PPi/Pi supply pathways. Moreover, phosphorylated enamel proteins, including amelogenins and 32-kDa enamelin, serve as direct TNAP substrates, and their proteolysis by MMP-20 and KLK4 enhances TNAP-dependent dephosphorylation and Pi release. Collectively, these findings support a model in which TNAP functions as a central integrator linking ATP/PPi metabolism with stage-specific proteolysis of enamel proteins to regulate dynamically the phosphate microenvironment required for proper enamel mineralization.

Experimental procedures

Because the samples used in this study were obtained from commercially available porcine mandibles collected at a slaughterhouse in Tokyo, Japan, institutional animal care and use committee approval was not required.

ALP (TNAP) in situ activity

Incisor tooth germs were collected from approximately 5-month-old pigs. The EOE was carefully removed using forceps, and the samples were gently blotted with Kimwipes (Kimberly-Clark Corporation). The incisors were then sectioned with a scalpel and washed with PBS and distilled water (dH2O). The samples were immersed in a reaction solution containing Naphthol AS-MX phosphate, N,N-dimethylformamide, 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), 2 mM MgCl2, and Fast Blue BB salt, and incubated at 22 °C for 30 min. After washing with dH2O, the samples were observed under a stereomicroscope (SZX7; Olympus). Images were acquired using a digital single-lens reflex camera (EOS Kiss X8i; Canon).

Collection of EOE and enamel samples

Permanent second molar or incisor tooth germs were collected from approximately 5-month-old pigs using dental forceps, and the EOE and dental pulp tissues were carefully separated. Sec EOE was peeled from the inner surface of the isolated EOE, whereas Mat EOE was collected from the labial surface of the incisor. Both Sec and Mat EOE samples were used for quantitative PCR analyses. In addition, cheese-like enamel (soft enamel) and chalk-like enamel (hard enamel) were separately collected from the crown region using a spatula.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from Sec and Mat EOE using Isogen (Nippon Gene). After reverse transcription of 2 μg of total RNA, qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche Diagnostics GmbH), forward and reverse primers (0.5 μM each), and 2 μl of cDNA template. qPCR was carried out on a LightCycler 96 system (Roche Diagnostics) using the SYBR Green method. Primer sequences were designed using Primer-BLAST (National Institutes of Health) (47). Details of the primer sequences and thermal cycling conditions are provided in Table S1. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) was used as the reference gene. The expression levels of Mmp-20, Klk4, tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (Alpl), pannexin 3 (Panx3), ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (Enpp1), progressive ankylosis protein (Ank), and xenotropic and polytropic retrovirus receptor 1 (Xpr1) were normalized to Gapdh and quantified by qPCR. All measurements were performed using six independent tooth germs (n = 6).

Immunohistochemistry

Incisor tooth germs were collected from approximately 5-month-old pigs, washed with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were then decalcified in 10% Na2-EDTA (pH 7.0) for 1 month, dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in Lemosol, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (3.5 μm thick) were cut using a microtome (LS-113; Yamato-Kohki) and used for hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunohistochemistry. For immunostaining, the sections were blocked with 1% BSA and 10% goat serum for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-TNAP polyclonal antibody (#bs-1535R; Bioss Antibodies Inc.; 1:400), anti-pannexin three polyclonal antibody (#ab237055; Abcam; 1:100), anti-ENPP1 monoclonal antibody (#ab223268; Abcam; 1:100), anti-ANKH polyclonal antibody (#ab211460; Abcam; 1:100), and anti-XPR1 polyclonal antibody (#HPA016557; Atlas Antibodies AB; 1:200). Histofine Simple Stain Mouse MAX-PO (#414341; Nichirei) was used as the secondary antibody and applied for 1 h at room temperature. NC sections were processed in parallel, with the primary antibody omitted. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (TaKaRa), and the sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. Images were acquired using an OLYMPUS DP73 camera mounted on an OLYMPUS BX53 microscope (Olympus).

Measurement of TNAP activity and protein concentration in enamel matrix and EOE fractions

Soft and hard enamel samples (approximately 20 mg each) were homogenized for 1 min in 0.2 ml of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete ULTRA Tablets, Mini, EDTA-free, EASY pack; Roche Diagnostics) and centrifuged at 9100×g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected as the T fraction, and this procedure was repeated three times. The insoluble residue was then subjected to the same procedure using 50 mM carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 10.8) containing the protease inhibitor cocktail, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the C fraction. Of the resulting T fractions (Soft-T and Hard-T) and C fractions (Soft-C and Hard-C), with a total volume of 0.6 ml for each sample, 50 μl was used to measure alkaline phosphatase activity using a commercial assay kit (DALP-250; BioAssay Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein concentration of each extract was determined using a 660 nm Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with bovine serum albumin used as the standard. All measurements were performed using six independent tooth germs (n = 6), and each assay was performed in triplicate. In addition, cytosolic and membrane fractions of EOE were prepared using the Mem-PER Plus Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. TNAP activity in each fraction was measured as described above.

ELISA

Detection and quantification of TNAP in Soft-T, Hard-T, Soft-C, and Hard-C were performed using a sandwich ELISA with a Human Alkaline Phosphatase ELISA Kit (#ab285249; Abcam). Each extract (0.6 ml) was concentrated to 150 μl using Amicon Ultra-0.5 ml 3K centrifugal filters (Millipore), and 100 μl of the concentrate was used for the assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All measurements were performed using six independent tooth germs (n = 6), and each assay was performed in triplicate.

Measurement of ATP, PPi, and Pi in enamel matrix

Low-molecular-weight metabolites in enamel were analyzed using an extraction method different from that used for TNAP activity measurements. EDTA was included in the extraction buffer to inhibit TNAP activity and prevent degradation of ATP and PPi during extraction. Soft and hard enamel samples (approximately 20 mg each) were homogenized for 1 min in 0.2 ml of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail and 5 mM EDTA using a PHYSCOTRON NS-310EII homogenizer (Microtec Nition). The homogenates were centrifuged at 9100×g for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected. This extraction was repeated three times, and the combined supernatants were pooled and defined as the unbound fraction (Free). The insoluble residue was then treated with 0.6 ml of 0.17 N HCl/0.98% formic acid (pH 2.7) for 1 h at 4 °C for demineralization, followed by centrifugation at 9100×g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was defined as the mineral-bound fraction (Bound). Each extract (Soft-free, Soft-bound, Hard-free, and Hard-bound) was diluted 50-fold. Aliquots of 100 μl were used for ATP and PPi measurements, and 50 μl was used for Pi measurement. ATP was measured using an ATP assay kit (TA100; Artience), PPi was measured using a pyrophosphate assay kit (MET-5159; Cell Biolabs, Inc.), and Pi was measured using the PiBlue Phosphate Assay Kit (POPB-500; BioAssay Systems), all according to the manufacturers’ instructions. All measurements were performed using six independent tooth germs (n = 6), and each assay was performed in triplicate.

Ex vivo evaluation of Pi generation in enamel matrix by recombinant ENPP1 and exogenous ATP

Soft and hard enamel samples (approximately 30 mg each) were suspended in 0.3 ml of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM MgCl2 and 0.05 mM ZnCl2 and incubated at 37 °C in the presence or absence of 5 mM levamisole hydrochloride (#123–04641; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), 2 mM sodium pyrophosphate (#529–91151; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), 400 ng rhENPP1(#6136-EN; R&D Systems), or 25 μg ATP (#A9187; Sigma-Aldrich). At 0 and 20 h after the start of the reaction, 40 μl aliquots were collected, and EDTA was added to a final concentration of 1 mM to stop the reaction. The samples were centrifuged at 9100×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected and diluted 200-fold with the same buffer. The amount of Pi in the supernatants was measured using the PiBlue Phosphate Assay Kit. All measurements were performed using six independent tooth germs (n = 6), and each assay was performed in triplicate.

Isolation and purification of enamel proteins and proteases

P148 amelogenin, TRAP, LRAP, 32-kDa enamelin, MMP-20, and KLK4 were isolated and purified according to our previously described methods (48, 49). Briefly, soft and hard enamel samples (10 g each) collected from porcine permanent second molar were extracted with 50 mM Sörensen buffer (pH 7.4), followed by stepwise extraction with carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 10.8). The extracts were subjected to ammonium sulfate fractionation and ion-exchange or heparin-affinity chromatography and were further purified, as needed, by gel filtration chromatography and reverse-phase HPLC.

In vitro evaluation of Pi generation from enamel proteins by recombinant TNAP

Purified P148 amelogenin, TRAP, LRAP, and 32-kDa enamelin (400 μg each) were suspended or dissolved in 0.5 ml of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM MgCl2 and 0.05 mM ZnCl2. rhTNAP (200 ng; #2909-AP; R&D Systems) was added, and 0.2 ml of the reaction mixture was immediately withdrawn and mixed with 20 μl of 0.17 N HCl/0.98% formic acid (pH 2.7) to stop the reaction (t0). The remaining 0.3 ml was incubated at 37 °C. After 20 h, 0.2 ml was withdrawn, and the reaction was stopped in the same manner (t20). Pi at each time point was measured using the PiBlue Phosphate Assay Kit, and the amount of released Pi was calculated as the difference between t20 and t0 (t20 − t0). For each protein (P148 amelogenin, TRAP, LRAP, and 32-kDa enamelin), measurements were performed using five independent preparations (n = 5), and each assay was performed in triplicate. Changes in the electrophoretic mobility of protein bands in each sample before the reaction (t0) and after 20 h incubation (t20) were evaluated by SDS-PAGE. SDS-PAGE analyses were performed in three independent experiments.

In vitro evaluation of Pi generation during amelogenin processing by ex vivo MMP-20

P148 amelogenin (400 μg) was suspended in 0.5 ml of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM MgCl2, 0.05 mM ZnCl2, and 5 mM CaCl2, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 h in the presence or absence of partially purified porcine MMP-20 (approximately 500 ng) and/or 200 ng rhTNAP. The amount of released Pi was determined as described above for the in vitro TNAP assay. For Pi quantification, five independent P148 samples were prepared for each condition (n = 5), and each sample was measured in triplicate. Amelogenin processing after 20 h was evaluated by SDS-PAGE. SDS-PAGE analyses were performed in three independent experiments.

SDS-PAGE

Protein samples were dissolved in 4 × sample buffer (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific) to a final concentration of 2 μg/μl without reduction or heating. Samples were separated on Novex 16% Tricine gels (Invitrogen) and stained with Simply Blue Safe Stain (Invitrogen). Apparent molecular weights were estimated by comparison with SeeBlue Plus2 Pre-Stained Standard (Invitrogen). As the primary objective of this analysis was to evaluate qualitative changes in electrophoretic mobility associated with TNAP-mediated dephosphorylation, rather than to perform quantitative comparisons of enzymatic activity, protein amounts were not strictly normalized across different substrates.

Ex vivo evaluation of Pi generation in enamel matrix by exogenous MMP-20 and KLK4

Soft and hard enamel samples (approximately 20 mg each) were suspended in 0.4 ml of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM MgCl2 and 0.05 mM ZnCl2. Partially purified porcine MMP-20 (20 μg of the crude fraction) was added to soft enamel samples, and KLK4 (10 μg of the crude fraction) was added to hard enamel samples, in the presence or absence of 5 mM levamisole hydrochloride. The samples were incubated at 37 °C. At 0 and 20 h after the start of the reaction, 100 μl aliquots were collected and immediately centrifuged at 9100×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. The amount of Pi in each supernatant was measured using the PiBlue Phosphate Assay Kit. All measurements were performed using six independent tooth germs (n = 6), and each assay was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was determined using a BellCurve for Excel software (Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the F test, Bartlett’s test, or Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, Welch’s t test, Student’s t test, or the Mann–Whitney U test was used as appropriate. For comparisons among three or more groups, Welch’s ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. When significant differences were detected, multiple comparisons between groups were performed using the Holm test, Tukey–Kramer test, or the Steel–Dwass test. Data are presented as box-and-whisker plots showing the median (center line) and interquartile range (25th–75th percentiles). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
