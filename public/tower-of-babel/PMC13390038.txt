DNA Structure-Dependent Enrichment of Oxidative Lesions
Authors: Maha Zewail-Foote, Alex W. Klattenhoff, Karen M. Vasquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxidative DNA lesions such as 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine (8-oxoG) and abasic (AP) sites contribute to mutagenesis and genetic instability. H-DNA, a naturally occurring intramolecular triplex structure, promotes genetic instability and is susceptible to oxidative damage. Here, we examined how oxidative lesions distribute within an H-DNA-forming sequence. Although oxidative stress increased oxidized purines overall, no template-dependent differences were detected at a distal B-DNA locus. In contrast, AP sites and FPG-sensitive lesions were enriched within the H-DNA region relative to the B-DNA control, even under basal conditions. These findings indicate that H-DNA topology enhances local susceptibility to oxidative damage and shapes the spatial distribution of oxidative DNA lesions that can contribute to mutagenesis.

DNA is continuously subjected to chemical damage arising from endogenous metabolic processes and exogenous environmental agents, generating DNA lesions that contribute to genomic instability. , Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during cellular metabolism, inflammation, and exposure to environmental toxicants can result in the formation of oxidative DNA lesions that can compromise genomic integrity. Among the most prevalent oxidative modifications are 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine (8-oxoG) and abasic (AP) sites, which contribute to mutagenesis, genome instability, and disease. ,

The formation and persistence of these chemical modifications are not only influenced by DNA sequence context but also by localized structural features. Guanine-rich sequences are particularly susceptible to oxidation due to the low redox potential of guanine, and adjacent guanines further enhance susceptibility to oxidative damage through charge delocalization mechanisms. − Genome-wide mapping studies have revealed that oxidative DNA lesions such as 8-oxoG are nonrandomly distributed and enriched at defined genomic regions that often overlap with repetitive elements and non-B DNA motifs. , These findings suggest that DNA conformation influences localized chemical vulnerability, underscoring the importance of structural context in shaping the genomic distribution and accumulation of DNA lesions.

Non-B DNA structures deviate from the canonical B-DNA duplex and are enriched at genomic hotspots. , Among these, H-DNA forms at polypurine-polypyrimidine mirror-repeat sequences and adopts a three-stranded intramolecular triplex structure (Figure ). In H-DNA, one strand participates in reverse Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding with the duplex, forming a three-stranded structure, while the other strand is displaced, creating an exposed single-stranded DNA region. This topology creates a solvent-accessible environment that may enhance chemical reactivity and lesion susceptibility. In addition, formation of non-B DNA structures can redistribute torsional stress within supercoiled DNA, altering DNA topology and potentially influencing ROS susceptibility in nearby duplex regions. Thus, H-DNA formation could affect the spatial distribution of oxidative damage beyond the H-DNA structure-forming sequence itself.

H-DNA is intrinsically mutagenic and has been shown to stimulate the formation of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) through aberrant processing via the nucleotide excision repair (NER) mechanism. Importantly, while non-B DNA structures are established drivers of genomic instability, far less is known about how these conformations influence the initial formation and distribution of oxidative lesions. In our previous study, we demonstrated that an H-DNA-forming sequence accumulates more 8-oxoG lesions under oxidative stress (OS) than a B-DNA control region, suggesting that DNA structural topology modulates oxidative lesion formation. However, it remains unclear whether oxidative lesions preferentially accumulate within the structure-forming sequence itself or whether structural transitions influence lesion formation in a canonical duplex DNA region outside the structure-forming sequence, a distinction that may provide insight into how non-B DNA structures contribute to the nonrandom distribution of mutagenic lesions within the genome. To address this, we quantified both FPG-sensitive lesions (e.g., 8-oxoG) and AP sites at the H-DNA-forming locus and at a distal canonical duplex region within the same supercoiled template under basal conditions and following OS treatment.

To determine whether DNA secondary structure influences oxidant-induced lesion formation, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on DNA substrates containing either an H-DNA-forming mirror-repeat sequence or a matched B-DNA control with a two base-pair substitution that disrupts mirror symmetry without altering the base content (Figure A). DNA templates were treated under ROS-generating conditions and subsequently linearized prior to qPCR analysis to eliminate potential effects of DNA topology on amplification efficiency. Lesion frequency was assessed within an amplicon encompassing the H-DNA-forming sequence (H-DNA locus) and at a distal duplex region located approximately 200 base pairs downstream of the structure-forming amplicon (distal locus) (Figures A). The distal duplex region has a slightly higher GC content (∼59%) than the H-DNA-forming locus (∼51%), including guanine-rich clusters, indicating that both regions have a comparable susceptibility for oxidation (Figures A, S1).

In the absence of FPG, reduced amplification efficiency was used as a measure of polymerase-blocking lesions, consistent with AP sites under these conditions. In the distal duplex region, polymerase-blocking levels were comparable between the H-DNA and control B-DNA templates under basal conditions and remained near baseline across OS conditions (Figure A), indicating that ROS treatment did not substantially increase detectable polymerase-blocking lesions at this locus. In contrast, the H-DNA-forming region exhibited a significantly elevated polymerase-blocking signal relative to the B-DNA control under basal conditions (Figure B). This enrichment persisted across OS conditions and did not display a dose-dependent increase with increasing ROS exposure. These results indicate that polymerase-blocking lesions are preferentially localized within the H-DNA-forming region and are present under basal conditions, rather than being uniformly induced by oxidative stress, consistent with an intrinsic sequence- and/or structure-dependent vulnerability within the H-DNA region.

We next evaluated oxidized base lesions using FPG-coupled qPCR, which enables detection of FPG-sensitive lesions, including oxidized purines and pre-existing AP sites. In the distal duplex region, OS produced a dose-dependent increase in FPG-sensitive lesions, reaching approximately 3–4-fold above baseline at the highest OS condition (Figure C). No significant differences were observed between the H-DNA and B-DNA templates at this distal locus, indicating comparable susceptibility of canonical duplex DNA to oxidant exposure in the presence or absence of the H-DNA structure. In contrast, within the H-DNA-containing amplicon, FPG-sensitive lesions were significantly enriched in the H-DNA template relative to the B-DNA control (Figure D). OS induced a dose-dependent increase in both templates; however, lesion levels were consistently higher in the H-DNA substrate across all conditions. These results indicate that oxidant-induced guanine damage is preferentially enriched at the H-DNA-forming sequence.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that H-DNA topology promotes the localized accumulation of oxidative lesions at the H-DNA-forming locus without producing detectable template-dependent differences at the distal duplex region. Whereas our previous work established that H-DNA accumulates oxidative damage under OS, the present findings demonstrate that this susceptibility is spatially confined to the structure-forming sequence itself and is detectable even under basal conditions, providing a chemical basis for how non-B DNA-forming sequences may contribute to the development of mutation hotspots. Notably, lesion enrichment within the H-DNA region was evident under basal conditions (i.e., without deliberate OS exposure) (Figure B, C), suggesting that H-DNA is intrinsically more susceptible to low levels of oxidative damage encountered during routine DNA propagation and handling. Even in the absence of exogenous oxidants, DNA is exposed to molecular oxygen and trace oxidants during DNA purification, processing, and storage, which can generate measurable DNA damage. The confinement of this damage to the H-DNA region suggests that the structure creates a constitutively lesion-prone microenvironment that becomes further enriched under OS. These observations suggest that H-DNA may function as constitutive chemical hotspots for oxidative damage. Because oxidative conditions generate a heterogeneous spectrum of lesions, the polymerase-blocking damage detected here likely reflects multiple lesion types, with AP sites as possible contributors.

The preferential accumulation of oxidative lesions within the H-DNA-forming region is consistent with structural features that alter local chemical susceptibility. The H-DNA-forming sequence contains a guanine-rich tract, which is particularly susceptible to oxidation, as well as a displaced single-stranded segment that increases solvent accessibility. These structural features likely promote the formation of oxidative lesions within the H-DNA region. Similar structure-dependent patterns of oxidative damage have been reported for other non-B DNA conformations, including G-quadruplexes, Z-DNA, and hairpin structures, all of which alter base exposure and local chemical reactivity. −

Oxidative lesions may also influence the structure and/or stability of H-DNA. In our previous work, we demonstrated that 8-oxoG and AP sites reduce the thermal stability of H-DNA, indicating that oxidative damage can destabilize the H-DNA structure. The accumulation of oxidative lesions within H-DNA-forming sequences therefore has the potential to not only promote lesion formation but also to alter the stability and persistence of the H-DNA structure. Such lesion-induced structural perturbations could further influence the accessibility of repair proteins and the processing of oxidative damage within H-DNA regions, consistent with previous work from our group and others demonstrating altered repair processing of non-B DNA containing oxidative lesions. ,−

These findings have several biological implications. H-DNA is enriched at chromosomal translocation hotspots and is known to stimulate aberrant repair processing and DSB formation. The present results suggest that, in addition to influencing downstream repair events, H-DNA topology modulates the initial formation of oxidative DNA lesions. By enhancing local susceptibility to oxidant-induced modification, non-B DNA structures may contribute to the nonrandom distribution of mutations within the genome. Local lesion enrichment may also increase the likelihood of clustered oxidative damage, which can promote error-generating repair outcomes.

Oxidative base lesions such as 8-oxoG and AP sites are typically repaired through the base excision repair (BER) pathway. However, our previous work demonstrated that H-DNA structures themselves are recognized and processed by the NER pathway. , In particular, recruitment of the NER protein XPA to H-DNA increases under OS, while the BER protein APE1 also associates with these sequences. These observations suggest that oxidatively damaged H-DNA may engage components of both BER and NER pathways. The coexistence of structure-directed NER recognition with lesion-directed BER processing may generate complex repair intermediates that contribute to mutagenesis and genomic instability. In this context, the formation of oxidative lesions within H-DNA may contribute to mutagenesis by increasing lesion frequency and by inducing error-generating repair.

These findings support a model in which H-DNA functions as a localized chemical hotspot for oxidative DNA damage, which can increase mutational vulnerability. Structural features of H-DNA promote lesion formation, which can in turn alter H-DNA stability and repair processing. This interplay between DNA topology, oxidative damage formation, and DNA repair provides a mechanistic framework for how non-B DNA structures give rise to mutation hotspots within the genome.
