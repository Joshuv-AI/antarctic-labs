Dissipative Losses In Black Hole-Induced Vacuum Decay
Michael Geller, Ofri Telem

Abstract
We address the long-standing puzzle of false vacuum decay catalyzed by black holes. Naively, small black holes with large Hawking temperatures can generate highly-boosted true vacuum bubbles in the early universe and trigger vacuum decay without any exponential suppression. Working in the thin-wall regime of the ϕ 4 \phi^{4} and sine-Gordon models, we show that radiative losses play a crucial role in decelerating these bubbles and preventing runaway vacuum decay. We find that while the production rate is enhanced compared with vacuum tunneling in some parts of the parameter space, it is always exponentially suppressed.

While the universe is initially in the higher (“false”) vacuum, bubbles of the lower (“true”) vacuum nucleate quantum mechanically. The evolution of these bubbles depends on a critical radius

E_{top}

Naively, the effect of a black hole Hawking temperature implies a standard thermal
O(3)
calculation of the Euclidean bounce. However, this is imprecise, as astrophysical black holes are not in thermal equilibrium with their surroundings

;
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2022
;
Briaud et al. 2022
address this question by solving for a generalized bounce satisfying Unruh boundary conditions. These calculations have so far only been performed for toy models, with promising results.

In this work, we present a complementary approach. In the thin-wall approximation of the
\phi^{4}
and sine-Gordon models, we show that the problem factorizes into distinct stages:
(i) Hawking production of boosted bubble walls in the near-horizon region;
(ii) a burst of radiative losses into scalar radiation, after which the bubble energy is capped at a scale
E_{\oplus}
that depends on the black hole mass.
(iii) if
E_{\oplus}>E_{top}
, unsuppressed classical expansion, while if
E_{\oplus}<E_{top}
, Coleman-like tunneling through the barrier; and
(iv) runaway bubble expansion beyond the critical radius.

Although our analysis does not prove the absence of other production modes, it paints a clear physical picture of bubble production. In particular, we identify the burst of radiative energy loss from highly boosted bubbles after their production as the main limitation on black-hole induced vacuum decay. This radiation caps the boost of bubbles that could otherwise classically traverse the surface-tension barrier. As a result, bubble production is always exponentially suppressed, albeit with a mild enhancement in parts of the parameter space.

II
Black hole induced False-Vacuum Decay

We consider a real scalar field in Lorentzian signature
(-,+,+,+)
, in the background of a Schwarzschild black hole,

\displaystyle S=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-V(\phi)\right]\,,

(1)

where the Schwarzschild metric is
ds^{2}=-f(r)\,dt^{2}+f^{-1}(r)\,dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}
, with
f(r)=1-r_{s}/r
and
r_{s}
the Schwarzschild radius. We assume that
V(\phi)
has at least two approximately degenerate minima at
\phi=\phi_{\pm}
. As in Coleman’s original setting, we assume that the universe is in a metastable (“false”) vacuum
\phi_{+}
, while the global (“true”) vacuum is
\phi_{-}
. In this case, the theory admits bubble solutions, interpolating between the true vacuum
\phi_{-}
at their center and the false vacuum
\phi_{+}
outside.

In this work, we compute the production rate of on-shell, spherically symmetric bubbles around the black hole. Working in the thin-wall limit, the evolution of these bubbles is controlled by the interplay between a bulk pressure term and a surface tension term. The latter provides a potential barrier for the radial growth of bubbles.

In this paper we study in detail the evolution of boosted bubbles as they traverse their surface-tension barrier, illustrated in Fig.
1
. We focus on small enough black holes
r_{s}\ll r_{c}
, where the generation process of on-shell bubbles factorizes into four distinct stages: (a) Hawking-production; (b) a burst of radiative losses; (c) classical/quantum motion over/through the barrier; and (d) the runaway evolution of on-shell bubbles. Our analysis focuses exclusively on spherically symmetric bubbles in the thin-wall limit. In addition, we assume that the bubble thickness is smaller than the Schwarzschild radius and that the bubble energy is much smaller than the black-hole mass, so that gravitational backreaction can be neglected.

III
Near-Horizon Kinks

Kinks are solutions of the equation

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}_{kink}(\xi)=V^{\prime}(\phi_{kink}(\xi))\,,

(2)

with
\lim_{\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty}\phi_{kink}(\xi)=\phi_{\mp}
. For
\xi=\gamma(r-vt-r_{0})
,
\phi_{kink}(\xi)
are also exact solutions for the Klein-Gordon equation in flat
1+1
dimensions. One can also define radial kink-like solutions to the KG equation in
3+1
dimensions; these, however, are not exact solutions and can radiate at sufficiently large Lorentz boosts.

We now consider kink solutions in the near-horizon region of a Schwarzschild black hole. As is well known, this region corresponds to Rindler space, namely, as
r\rightarrow r_{s}
the Schwarzschild metric approaches

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\frac{4r^{2}_{s}}{\exp(1)}\left[-dT^{2}+dX^{2}\right]+r^{2}_{s}d\Omega^{2}\,,

(3)

where
T=\tfrac{1}{2}(V+U),\,X=\tfrac{1}{2}(V-U)
and
U,V
are Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates,

\displaystyle U=-\exp\left[\frac{r_{*}-t}{2r_{s}}\right]~,~V=\exp\left[\frac{r_{*}+t}{2r_{s}}\right]\,.

(4)

Here
r_{*}\equiv r+r_{s}\log[(r/r_{s})-1]
is the usual Schwarzschild tortoise coordinate. The KG equation in the metric (
3
) has ordinary kink solutions
\phi=\phi_{kink}(\xi^{Rind})
where
\phi_{kink}
solves (
2
). The argument of this kink is

\displaystyle\xi^{Rind}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2r_{s}\left[\bar{\gamma}(X-\bar{\beta}T)+e^{\frac{\bar{r}_{*}}{2r_{s}}}\right]

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{s}\left[e^{-\frac{\bar{t}}{2r_{s}}}V-e^{\frac{\bar{t}}{2r_{s}}}U+2\gamma_{i}\right]~~

where
\bar{\gamma}=(1-\bar{\beta}^{2})^{-1/2}\equiv\cosh(\frac{\bar{t}}{2r_{s}})
is the boost in
T,X
coordinates, while
\gamma_{i}
is the initial value of the boost
\gamma_{w}
defined below in Section
V
. The conserved energy of this solution is

\displaystyle E=4\pi\sigma\gamma_{i}r^{2}_{s}\,,

(6)

with the tension
\sigma=\int d\phi\sqrt{2V(\phi)}
. Importantly, the flat metric (
3
) is only a truncation of the near-horizon limit of the Schwarzschild metric in
(T,X,\theta,\phi)
coordinates, neglecting gradients. As we show in the next section, highly boosted bubble walls described by
\phi=\phi_{kink}(\xi^{Rind})
in fact radiate and lose energy until they stabilize as thin walls in the Schwarzschild background.

For the purpose of connecting near-horizon kinks to their full Schwarzschild generalization, we express
\xi^{Rind}
in terms of
r_{*},t
,

\displaystyle\xi^{Rind}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2r_{s}\,\left[\gamma_{i}-e^{\frac{r_{*}}{2r_{s}}}\cosh\left(\frac{t-\bar{t}}{2r_{s}}\right)\right]\,.

(7)

In this case the near-horizon (tortoise) position
r_{*,w}(t)
of the kink is at

\displaystyle r_{*,w}(t)=-2r_{s}\log\left[\gamma^{-1}_{i}\cosh\left(\frac{t-\bar{t}}{2r_{s}}\right)\right]\,.

(8)

so that
\xi^{Rind}(r_{*,w}(t),t)=0
.

IV
Hawking production

We now focus on the special case in which
V(\phi)=V_{sG}(\phi)
where

\displaystyle V_{sG}(\phi)=\frac{m^{2}}{r_{s}^{2}\beta^{2}_{sG}}\left[1-\cos(r_{s}\beta_{sG}\phi)\right]\,,

(9)

is the sine-Gordon potential, adapted to the units of
3+1
dimensions. In this case, the near-horizon theory becomes effectively a
1+1
dimensional sine-Gordon theory. This theory has a famous dual description
Coleman 1975
;
Mandelstam 1975
, namely, the Thirring model of interacting
1+1d
fermions, with a coupling
\lambda=\pi\left(\frac{4\pi}{\beta^{2}_{sG}}-1\right)
. Under this duality, near-horizon kinks map to near-horizon massive interacting fermions. The duality was originally studied for flat
1+1d
space, but was also extended to
AdS_{2}

Callan 1982
, to the half-line
Liguori and Mintchev 1998
, and also used for the radial direction of magnetically charged black holes
Maldacena 2021
. Like any other particle-like state, the production rate of a Thirring fermion in the near-horizon region
2
2

2

More precisely, this is defined as the thermal flux at an intermediate scale
r_{s}\ll r_{m}\ll r_{c}
is Hawking-like:

\Gamma\propto e^{-\frac{E}{T_{H}}}

(10)

where
E
is given by (
6
), and
T_{H}=(4\pi r_{s})^{-1}
is the Hawking temperature. The duality argument can be pushed further to arbitrary periodic potentials
V(\phi)
, by Fourier transforming it and using the Abelian bosonization map

\displaystyle\cos\left(n\beta\phi\right)\leftrightarrow\left(\psi^{\dagger}_{L}\psi_{R}\right)^{n}+{\rm h.c.}\,

(11)

As a result, the KG action with a general periodic
V(\phi)
is equivalent to the massive Thirring model + higher left-right interactions
Sénéchal 1999
. While these interactions could lead to 1+1d confinement of the radial fermions into composites with energies
E_{n}
, Hawking radiation would still produce these composites with rate
\sim\exp(-E_{n}/T_{H})
.

To confirm our expectations of Hawking production, we also performed a complementary semiclassical analysis of bubble generation in the thin-wall limit (see Appendix
A
). This analysis is complementary in the sense that, on one hand, it does not rely on fermionization, while on the other hand it neglects energy loss to scalar radiation, and so is only valid for low initial boosts. The analysis in Appendix
A
mirrors the semiclassical approach to the Hawking radiation of particles
Parikh and Wilczek 2000
, namely, it considers the complex-time evolution of Nambu–Goto kinks, that ultimately ends at real time for
r_{w}\rightarrow\infty
. In this analysis, we take extra care to specify an initial condition for the Nambu–Goto analysis of the bubble generation rate, corresponding to an
Unruh
vacuum near the horizon
Unruh 1976
. Finally, we obtain the semiclassical bubble generation rate, which is just (
10
) evaluated at the energy of the dominant saddle of the path integral (“the bounce”). This energy is nothing but
E=E_{top}
, the energy at the top of the surface-tension barrier. We reiterate that the analysis of Appendix
A
is only reliable when the energy loss rate to scalar radiation is negligible – see the next section for the consideration of this crucial effect.

V
Bubble Walls in Schwarzschild

The bubble wall/kink solution
\phi=\phi_{kink}(\xi^{Rind})
with (
2
) is only valid in the near-horizon region, where the metric is approximately flat. Here we investigate the dynamics of a bubble wall beyond its near-horizon truncation. In this case we look for solutions for the (radial) Klein-Gordon equation in the full Schwarzschild metric,

\displaystyle\square_{Sch}\,\phi-V^{\prime}(\phi)=0\,,

(12)

where
\square_{Sch}=-f^{-1}\partial^{2}_{t}+r^{-2}\partial_{r}\left(r^{2}f\partial_{r}\right)
is the d’Alembertian in Schwarzschild coordinates. To find thin-wall solutions for (
12
), we set up
Gaussian normal coordinates
to the wall in the following manner. The worldsheet of this wall is parametrized by
x^{\mu}_{w}(\tau)=\left(t_{w}(\tau),r_{w}(\tau)\right)
, where we suppress the angular directions. The velocity of the wall is
u^{\mu}_{w}=\dot{x}^{\mu}_{w}
, where the dot is with respect to the wall’s proper time
\tau
. The latter is chosen so that
u^{2}_{w}=-1
. The normal to the wall is
n^{\mu}_{w}=(f^{-1}_{w}\dot{r}_{w},f_{w}\dot{t}_{w})
where
f_{w}=1-r_{s}/r_{w}
and
n^{2}_{w}=1,n_{w}\cdot u_{w}=0
. We also define the wall’s Schwarzschild “boost factor”
\gamma_{w}\equiv n^{r}=\sqrt{f_{w}+\dot{r}^{2}_{w}}
and its proper acceleration normal to itself,
a_{w}\equiv n^{\mu}_{w}u^{\nu}_{w}\partial_{\nu}u^{w}_{\mu}=(\ddot{r}_{w}+\tfrac{1}{2}f^{\prime}_{w})/\gamma_{w}
.

In the vicinity of the wall, we work in Gaussian normal coordinates (see, e.g.
Blanco-Pillado et al. 2025
)
(\tau,\xi)
, defined so that for any point
x^{\mu}=(t,r)
we have

\displaystyle x^{\mu}=x^{\mu}_{w}(\tau)+n^{\mu}_{w}(\tau)\,\xi\,.

(13)

The Schwarzschild metric in
(\tau,\xi)
coordinates is

\displaystyle g_{\tau\xi}={\rm diag}\left(-\mathcal{Z}^{2},1,r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right)+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2})\,.

(14)

where
\mathcal{Z}(\tau,\xi)=1+a_{w}(\tau)\,\xi
and
r(\tau,\xi)=r_{w}(\tau)+\gamma_{w}(\tau)\xi
. The trace of the extrinsic curvature of constant
\xi
slices is defined as
\mathcal{K}(\tau,\xi)\equiv\partial_{\xi}\log\sqrt{-g_{\tau\xi}}
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\tau,\xi)=\left(a_{w}+2\frac{\gamma_{w}}{r_{w}}\right)-\left(a^{2}_{w}+2\frac{\gamma^{2}_{w}}{r^{2}_{w}}\right)\xi+

\displaystyle\left[a^{3}_{w}+2\frac{\gamma^{3}_{w}}{r^{3}_{w}}+\frac{r_{s}}{r_{w}^{3}}\left(a_{w}-\frac{\gamma_{w}}{r_{w}}\right)\right]\xi^{2}+O\left(\xi^{3}\right)\,.

(15)

Note that
\mathcal{K}(\tau,0)
is the trace of the extrinsic curvature of the wall itself.

We now consider rigid wall solutions to the Klein-Gordon equation in
(\tau,\xi)
coordinates. The latter is given by

\displaystyle\square_{\tau\xi}\,\phi-V^{\prime}(\phi)=0\,.

(16)

where
\square_{\tau\xi}=D_{\mu}D^{\mu}
is the Schwarzschild d’Alembertian in
(\tau,\xi)
coordinates.
Specifically, we look for solutions of the form
\phi=\phi_{kink}(\xi)
solving (
2
).
To check the validity of these solutions, we substitute into (
16
) the ansatz
\phi=\phi_{kink}(\xi)+\delta\phi(\tau,\xi)
and expand to leading order in the fluctuation
\delta\phi
. We get

\displaystyle\left[\square_{\tau\xi}-V^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{kink})\right]\delta\phi=\mathcal{K}\phi_{kink}^{\prime}\,.

(17)

To get an ODE for the wall’s position
r_{w}(\tau)
, we project (
17
) on the translational zero mode
\phi^{\prime}_{kink}
:

\displaystyle 0=\int d\xi\,\,\phi^{\prime}_{kink}\left[\square_{\tau\xi}-V^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{kink})\right]\delta\phi=\int d\xi\,\,\mathcal{K}\left(\phi_{kink}^{\prime}\right)^{2}~\,

(18)

where the first equality stems from acting to the left on
\phi^{\prime}_{kink}
– c.f. the derivative of (
2
) with respect to
\xi
. From (
2
), we also know that
\phi_{kink}^{\prime}
is peaked around
\xi=0
with height
\Delta\phi
with width
\delta=m^{-1}
and
m^{2}=V^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{\pm})
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle a_{w}+\frac{2\gamma_{w}}{r_{w}}=\mathcal{K}(\tau,0)=m\,\mathcal{O}\left(\zeta^{3}_{w}\right)\,,

(19)

where
\zeta_{w}\equiv\frac{\delta\gamma_{w}}{r_{w}}
. The leading condition of vanishing
\mathcal{K}(\tau,0)
is exactly the Nambu–Goto equation of motion for the wall (with zero bias
\delta V
for now). In other words,
\mathcal{K}(\tau,0)=0
is equivalent to
\dot{E}_{NG}=0
, where

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{NG}=\frac{d}{d\tau}\left[4\pi\sigma\gamma_{w}r^{2}_{w}\right]=0\,.

(20)

We see that the backreaction from the fluctuation leads to corrections of order
\zeta^{3}_{w}
to the EOM. Thus
E_{NG}
is conserved up to perturbative corrections provided that
\zeta_{w}\ll 1
. For
r\rightarrow r_{s}
, the solution for (
20
) coincides with the Rindler solution (
8
). Consequently, for
\zeta_{w}\lesssim 1
, bubbles are generated in the near horizon as Hawking radiation, and subsequently evolve according to (
20
).

VI
Radiation from boosted vacuum bubbles

We now study the energy flux into scalar radiation generated by a near-horizon kink.
For
\zeta_{w}\lesssim 1
, we can estimate this flux analytically, while for
\zeta_{w}>1
perturbation theory breaks down and we rely on a full numerical solution of the Klein-Gordon PDE.

To estimate the rate for
\zeta_{w}\lesssim 1
, we consider the trace of the extrinsic curvature (
V
) evaluated on the Nambu–Goto solution, namely
a_{w}=-2\gamma_{w}/r_{w}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\tau,\xi)|_{NG}=-\frac{3}{2}a^{2}_{w}(\tau)\xi+O\left(\xi^{2}\right)\,.

(21)

We also greatly simplify our estimate by taking the near-horizon limit, in which
\square_{\tau\xi}=-\partial^{2}_{\tau}+\partial^{2}_{\xi}
. In this case the emitted power is given by

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\mathrm{loss}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi r^{2}_{s}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}a_{w}^{2}(\tau)a_{w}^{2}(\tau^{\prime})\times

(22)

\displaystyle\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,|I(k)|^{2}\cos\left[\omega_{k}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right]

where

\displaystyle I(k)=\int d\xi\,\xi\,\phi^{\prime}_{kink}(\xi)e^{-i\xi k}=i\partial_{k}\tilde{\phi}_{kink}(k)\,,

(23)

is the Fourier transform of the source,
\tilde{\phi}_{kink}(k)
is the Fourier transform of
\phi_{kink}(\xi)
, and
\omega_{k}=\sqrt{k^{2}+m^{2}}
. The
\tau^{\prime}
integral in (
22
) is dominated by its upper limit, and
a_{w}(\tau^{\prime})
on the Nambu–Goto solution is approximately
a_{w}(\tau^{\prime})=a_{w}(\tau)\exp\left[3m\zeta_{w}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)\right]
for
\tau^{\prime}\approx\tau
. Substituting this into (
22
) and performing the
\tau^{\prime}
integral, we get the normalized emission rate
R\equiv r_{s}\frac{\dot{E}_{\mathrm{loss}}}{E_{kink}}

\displaystyle R=36\,\zeta_{w}^{3}\frac{m^{3}}{\sigma}\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,|I(k)|^{2}\frac{6m\zeta_{w}}{k^{2}+(6m)^{2}\zeta_{w}^{2}+m^{2}}\,.~~~~~~

(24)

We normalized the rate by the inverse of the typical crossing time, which at
r_{w}\sim r_{s}
is simply
r^{-1}_{s}
. The rate

is shown in Figure

\displaystyle R(\zeta_{w})=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\zeta_{w}}{\zeta^{i}_{IR}}\right)^{4}&\zeta_{w}\lesssim 1\\

1.6

VII
Estimated Tunneling rate

In Section
IV
we showed that the production of a bubble in the near-horizon region is consistent with a Hawking radiation process. Naively, this would mean that a sufficiently small black hole will produce bubbles with enough energy to overcome the surface tension barrier and reach infinity, with no exponential suppression. In Section
VI
, however, we showed that
r^{\oplus}_{max}
for the bubbles saturates due to radiative energy losses, and so it is so far unclear whether smaller black holes with higher
T_{H}
are indeed efficient in generating runaway bubbles. In this section we show that smaller black holes are not particularly efficient at generating runaway bubbles. In fact, below a certain value of
r_{s}
, the production rate for runaway bubbles saturates.

We now estimate the overall rate for on-shell bubble generation. For each energy, the exponential suppression of the production amplitude is

\displaystyle f_{prod}(E_{i})\,\mathcal{T}_{barrier}(E_{\oplus}[E_{i}])\,.

(26)

The first factor

\displaystyle f_{prod}(E_{i})=e^{-E_{i}/2T_{H}}\,,

(27)

is simply the amplitude for the Hawking production of a bubble wall with energy
E_{i}
, generated predominantly at
r_{i}\sim r_{s}
. According to the previous section, bubbles generated with large enough
E_{i}
lose their energy quickly until their energy becomes

E^{\max}_{\oplus}\approx 4\pi\sigma\delta^{-1}r_{s}^{3}\,,

(28)

For this reason, their subsequent evolution is determined by the energy

E_{\oplus}
, defined as

E_{\oplus}\left[E_{i}\right]={\rm min}\left(E_{i},E^{\max}_{\oplus}\right)\,.

(29)

The transmission coefficient
\mathcal{T}_{barrier}
corresponds to the tunneling rate through the surface-tension barrier. To compute it, we make use of the Nambu–Goto action for the bubble, valid in our thin-wall limit,

\displaystyle S_{NG}=\int dt\,\left[-4\pi\sigma f_{w}\gamma^{-1}_{w}r_{w}^{2}+\overline{\delta V}r_{w}^{3}\right]\,,

(30)

where
\overline{\delta V}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\delta V
, and
\gamma_{w}=f^{1/2}_{w}(1-f^{-2}_{w}(dr_{w}/dt)^{2})^{-1/2}
is the same boost as before, only written in terms of a time derivative. From here and below, we solve for
r_{w}(t)
as a function of Schwarzschild time
t
rather than proper time, as is natural for a Nambu–Goto analysis. The tunneling rate through the surface tension barrier is computed from the momentum
p(E_{\oplus})
conjugate to
r_{w}(t)
. Computing this momentum from (
30
), we get

\displaystyle p(E_{\oplus})=f^{-1}_{w}\sqrt{\left[E_{\oplus}+\overline{\delta V}r^{3}_{w}\right]^{2}-f_{w}(4\pi\sigma r^{2}_{w})^{2}}\,.

(31)

Correspondingly, the tunneling factor is
4
4

4

\mathcal{T}_{barrier}
itself is defined as
\exp\left[i\,\int_{r_{s}}^{\infty}p(E_{\oplus})\,dr\right]
where the phase is not physically relevant

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}_{barrier}\right|=\begin{cases}\exp\left[-{\rm Im}\int_{r_{<}}^{r_{>}}p(E_{\oplus})\,dr\right]&E_{\oplus}<E_{top}\\
1&E_{\oplus}>E_{top}\end{cases}\,,

where
r_{<}(E_{\oplus})
and
r_{>}(E_{\oplus})
are the classical turning points of the barrier for energy
E_{\oplus}
, and

\displaystyle E_{top}\approx\frac{16\pi}{27}\sigma r^{2}_{c}\,,

(33)

is the energy at the top of the barrier, with
r_{c}=r_{>}(E_{\oplus}=0)
. To estimate the overall rate (to exponential accuracy), we can estimate:

\Gamma(t)\sim\left|\int_{0}^{E_{\oplus}^{\max}}dE_{i}\,e^{iE_{i}t}f_{prod}(E_{i})\,\mathcal{T}_{barrier}(E_{i})\right|^{2}

(34)

when
E_{\oplus}^{\max}>E_{top}
, we can estimate this integral with the saddle point approximation where the saddle point is
E_{i}^{\mathrm{saddle}}=E_{top}
(see Appendix
A
), while for
E_{\oplus}^{\max}<E_{top}
the endpoint of
E_{\oplus}^{\max}
dominates.

Putting everything together, the tunneling rate is

\displaystyle\log\Gamma=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-T^{-1}_{H}E_{\oplus}-2{\rm Im}\left[\int_{r_{<}}^{r_{>}}p(E_{\oplus})dr\right]&E_{\oplus}<E_{top}\\
-T^{-1}_{H}E_{top}&E_{\oplus}>E_{top}\end{cases}\,,

The results of this computation are presented in Fig.
5
. Three regimes appear in this plot: (a) for sufficiently small
r_{s}
, the energy of the kink after radiation is well below the surface-tension barrier, and the rate is effectively the standard Coleman rate; (b) for larger
r_{s}
(while still within
r_{s}\ll r_{c}
), the Hawking-produced bubbles remain energetic enough after radiation to move classically over the surface-tension barrier; and (c) in an intermediate regime, marginally boosted bubbles still require tunneling through the surface-tension barrier, but at an enhanced rate.

) normalized by the Coleman flat-space bounce action

\delta
.

The maximal possible rate is achieved when
E_{\oplus}\sim E_{top}
, or equivalently,

r_{s}\sim(\delta)^{\frac{1}{3}}r_{c}^{\frac{2}{3}}
.
In this case

\log\Gamma\sim-\frac{128}{81}\left(\frac{4\delta}{r_{c}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}S_{4}\,,

(36)

where

S_{4}=-2{\rm Im}\left[\int_{0}^{r_{c}}p(E_{\oplus}=0)dr\right]\,,

(37)

is the Coleman flat-space bounce action. The rate (
36
) is exponentially faster than the spontaneous quantum tunneling rate, however it is still always exponentially suppressed. This is most transparently seen for a tilted quartic double-well potential in the thin-wall regime, for which one finds parametrically

\log\Gamma\sim-\lambda^{-1}\left(\frac{r_{c}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{8}{3}}

(38)

For perturbative
\lambda\lesssim 4\pi
, we therefore find that as long as we are in the thin-wall regime
\delta\ll r_{c}
, the rate remains exponentially suppressed. Our thin-wall derivation of the
\lambda^{-1}
suppression of the exponential is consistent with the thick-wall derivation of
Strumia 2023
.

VIII
Conclusions

In this paper we studied the possibility of realistic (i.e. non-eternal) small black holes inducing false vacuum decay within the thin-wall regime of the
\phi^{4}
and sine-Gordon models. In this regime, the problem can be factorized into a Hawking production in the near horizon region, a subsequent propagation and tunneling through the surface tension barrier. For very small black holes, the Hawking temperature is high one might expect that bubbles with enough energy to overcome the surface tension barrier can be produced without any exponential suppression. However, we found that such highly boosted bubbles promptly lose energy via scalar radiation. As a result, while the vacuum-decay rate can be enhanced relative to the Coleman
O(4)
flat-space instanton, it is always exponentially suppressed. In our analysis we only considered spherical configurations and neglected gravitational backreaction and additional radiation channels. We see no immediate reason for these corrections to significantly impact our conclusions.

Appendix A
The Semiclassical Approach

In this paper we motivated the Hawking-production of boosted bubbles using fermionization in the near-horizon region. To further support our argument, we provide a self-contained semiclassical derivation of the bubble generation rate. The present derivation assumes a rigid kink and so is valid for bubbles generated with low boosts for which scalar radiation is negligible.

Our semiclassical analysis is valid in the thin-wall limit, where the dynamics of the rigid bubble is captured by the Nambu–Goto action (
30
). Importantly, we set up our semiclassical computation so that the initial false vacuum is an “Unruh” vacuum corresponding to an astrophysical black hole, rather than a “Hartle–Hawking” vacuum relevant for eternal black holes (see
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2021
;
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2022
for the importance of this fact). To specify an Unruh vacuum, we match our thin-wall solution to a full solution of the KG system (
1
) in the near horizon region, which is continuously connected to the false vacuum prior to black hole formation.

To compute the rate for bubble generation, we need to calculate the rate

\Gamma=|\left\langle\text{On-shell bubble state}|\mathcal{T}e^{-iH\Delta t}|\Omega_{\mathrm{false,Unruh}}\right\rangle|^{2}

(39)

with
\Delta t\to\infty
.
The exponential suppression of this amplitude is given by the saddle-point action as

\Gamma=\left|\int D\phi\,e^{-\mathrm{Im}[S(\phi)]}\right|^{2}

(40)

where
S
is the KG action (
1
) evaluated on a saddle point (“bounce”) trajectory. The boundary conditions on this path integral are false vacuum at
t\rightarrow-\infty
and an on shell bubble at
t\rightarrow\infty
. Since we are working in the thin-wall limit, we can instead use

\Gamma\approx\int Dr_{w}\,\,e^{-2\mathrm{Im}[S_{NG}(r_{w})]}\,,

(41)

where
S_{NG}
is the Nambu–Goto action (
30
), and the integration is over a trajectory
t(r_{w})
that solves the Euler-Lagrange equations derived from
S_{NG}
. The relevant saddle point trajectory starts at
t(r_{w}\rightarrow r_{s})\in\mathbb{C}
and ends at
t(r_{w}\rightarrow\infty)\in\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\infty
. Note, however, that to specify our initial false vacuum, we also need to specify a boundary condition on
(t,r_{w})
as
t\in\mathbb{R}\rightarrow-\infty
. To find the right boundary condition corresponding to an Unruh vacuum, we consider near-horizon dynamics.

A.1
Near-horizon Dynamics

In Section
III
we described the near-horizon dynamics of Schwarzschild bubble walls by working in the Rindler
truncation
of the metric near
r_{s}
. While this truncation was perfectly suitable for motivating the Hawking production of bubble walls using the duality to Thirring fermions, the semiclassical approach requires a slight refinement of the Rindler treatment. The reason is that in Rindler space, modes never reach
r\rightarrow\infty
. For this reason, there is no separation between left- and right-moving modes in Rindler space, and hence no Unruh vacuum
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2021
. In other words, our solution
\phi_{kink}(\xi^{Rind})
is generated in a
Hartle–Hawking
vacuum
Hartle and Hawking 1976
. Nevertheless, the solution (
5
) allows us to fix the near-horizon behavior of the bubble in the
full Schwarzschild metric
so that it starts from an Unruh vacuum as required.

Though the Rindler solution (
7
) is in a Hartle–Hawking vacuum and thus not directly applicable here, it does allow us to make an educated guess of the near-horizon boundary condition for the Nambu–Goto dynamics. To this end, we propose the near-horizon field configuration
\phi=\phi_{kink}(\xi^{NH})
where

\displaystyle\xi^{NH}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2r_{s}\,\left[\gamma_{i}-\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{r_{*}-\left(t-\bar{t}\right)}{2r_{s}}}\right]\,.

(42)

We immediately see that
\xi^{NH}\rightarrow\xi^{Rind}
as
t\rightarrow-\infty+i\,{\rm Im}(\bar{t})
. Similarly, the position of the kink (
42
) is given by

\displaystyle r^{NH}_{*,w}(t)=t-\bar{t}+2r_{s}\log 2\gamma_{i}\,,

(43)

and so
r^{NH}_{w}(t)\rightarrow r^{Rind}_{w}(t)
as
t\rightarrow-\infty+i\,{\rm Im}(\bar{t})
. In other words, the kink
\phi_{kink}(\xi^{NH})
represents the correct limit of the full Schwarzschild EOM at
t\rightarrow-\infty+i\,{\rm Im}(\bar{t})
and
r\rightarrow r_{s}
. Furthermore, we can choose
{\rm Im}(\bar{t})
so that
\phi_{kink}(\xi^{NH})
satisfies the correct Unruh boundary condition at
t\rightarrow-\infty
, and so it can be used to set the correct initial condition on the Nambu–Goto integral (
41
). Evaluating this solution at very early real times
t\to-\infty
, we can impose Unruh boundary conditions around the false vacuum (see
Shkerin and Sibiryakov 2021
). Close to the horizon, for
r_{*}\ll t-\bar{t}
, we find that
\mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{\xi^{NH}}{\delta}\right)\gg 1
(this is the thin wall assumption
r_{s}\gg\delta
that we use throughout the paper). Consequently, this region is at the tail of the kink, where
\phi
can be written as

\phi-\phi_{+}\propto e^{-m\xi_{NH}}=e^{-2mr_{s}\gamma_{i}}e^{r_{s}m\exp\left(\frac{r_{*}-t+\bar{t}}{2r_{s}}\right)}\,.

(44)

Here
m
is the mass term near the false vacuum. If we take a Fourier transform of this tail we find that

\tilde{f}(\omega)=e^{-2mr_{s}\gamma_{i}}\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\frac{m}{2\kappa}e^{\kappa(r_{*}-\bar{t})}\right)^{-i\omega/\kappa}\Gamma\!\left(i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}\right)

(45)

where
\kappa=\frac{1}{2r_{s}}
is the surface gravity. We immediately notice that these modes satisfy the thermal
conditions

\left|\frac{\tilde{f}(-\omega)}{\tilde{f}(\omega)}\right|=e^{-2\pi\omega/\kappa},

(46)

provided that we set
{\rm Im}(\bar{t})=2\pi r_{s}
. This solution includes only right moving modes, as expected from the Unruh vacuum.

All-in-all, we learn from our near-horizon analysis the correct initial condition for our Nambu–Goto analysis in the following manner. Looking at (
43
), we see that at
r_{*}\rightarrow-\infty
we need
{\rm Re}(t)\rightarrow-\infty
and
{\rm Im}(t)\rightarrow{\rm Im}(\bar{t})=2\pi r_{s}
. This is the correct initial condition reflecting generation from an Unruh vacuum.

A.2
Nambu–Goto Trajectory

We now consider solutions for the EOM of the Nambu–Goto action (
30
). To do this, we define the “potential”

\displaystyle U[r,\gamma]\equiv 4\pi\sigma\gamma r^{2}-\overline{\delta V}r^{3}\,,

(47)

where
f(r)=1-r_{s}/r
. The conserved energy of the bubble is given by
E=U[r_{w},\gamma_{w}]
where
\gamma_{w}=f^{1/2}_{w}(1-f^{-2}_{w}(dr_{w}/dt)^{2})^{-1/2}
. The EOM is then

\displaystyle 0=\frac{dE}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}U[r_{w},\gamma_{w}]\,.

(48)

For later reference, let us consider the point
r_{top}
, for which
\frac{d}{dr}U[r,\gamma=f_{w}^{1/2}]|_{r=r_{top}}=0
. For
E=E_{top}\equiv U[r_{top},\gamma=f_{w}^{1/2}]
, the wall reaches an unstable equilibrium at
r_{w}=r_{top}
where it rests on top of its potential barrier.
E_{top}
is very well approximated by (
33
). Following Coleman, from now on we focus on solutions with
E\approx E_{top}
. These will give the dominant contribution to the generation of on-shell bubbles.

To solve (
48
), we first convert it to an ODE for
t(r)
and integrate with respect to
t
. We get

\displaystyle t(r)=t_{0}+\int_{r_{0}}^{r}\,dr\,\frac{1}{f\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{4\pi\sigma f(r)r^{2}}{E+r^{3}\overline{\delta V}}\right)^{2}}}\,.

(49)

Here
(t_{0},r_{0})
are the initial time and position for energy
E_{top}
, which we get from the near horizon solution (
43
). Note that
{\rm Im}(t_{0})={\rm Im}(\bar{t})=2\pi r_{s}
in order to satisfy the Unruh boundary condition. Consider now analytically continuing by adding an infinitesimal complex part to
E
:

\displaystyle E=E_{top}(1-\epsilon e^{i\pi\delta_{E}})\,,

(50)

where
\epsilon\ll 1
but
\delta_{E}
isn’t necessarily small. We are looking for trajectories so that
t(r\rightarrow\infty)
is real, meaning that the bubble becomes asymptotically on-shell. In the thin wall limit, we can approximate the integrand of (
49
) exponentially well with a Breit-Wigner-like form

\displaystyle t(r)=t_{0}+\int_{r_{0}}^{r}\,dr\,\frac{N_{BW}}{\sqrt{(r-r_{top})^{2}+\Gamma_{BW}e^{i\pi\delta_{E}}}}\,,

(51)

where
N_{BW}=f^{-1}(r_{top})\sqrt{(E_{top}+r^{3}_{top}\overline{\delta V})/U^{\prime\prime}(r_{top})}
and
\Gamma_{BW}=2E_{top}\epsilon/U^{\prime\prime}(r_{top})
. Doing this integral analytically, we get

\displaystyle{\rm Im}[t(r\rightarrow\infty)]=\pi\left[2r_{s}+N_{BW}\left(1-\delta_{E}\right)\right]\,,

(52)

So that for
\delta_{E}=1-\frac{2r_{s}}{N_{BW}}
the bubble is generated on shell, independently of
\epsilon
, which we can take to be as small as we wish. To summarize, we found a trajectory
(t,r_{w})
that starts at complex time (due to Hawking generation) and ends at real time with an on-shell bubble at
r_{w}\rightarrow\infty
. See Fig.
6
for a representative trajectory, for the full integral (
49
), and the Breit-Wigner-like approximation (
51
).

The Nambu–Goto action evaluated on this trajectory gives the rate of on-shell bubble generation. This amplitude is given by:

\displaystyle\Gamma\sim\exp\left[-2{\rm Im}(S_{NG})\right]=\exp\left(-E_{top}/T_{H}\right)\,,

(53)

where

. Note that to reach

\epsilon\to 0
, which means that an on-shell bubble is generated with

.

10

We numerically evolve a spherically symmetric
