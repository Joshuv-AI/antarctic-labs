Assessment of Alfalfa varieties under salt stress through morpho-physiological and biochemical traits using a comprehensive membership function approach
Authors: Muhammad Daud, Wenrao Li, Haixia Qiao, Muhammad Adil, Muhammad Atiq Ashraf, Mohsin Khan, Hafsa Ijaz, Zahoor ul Haq, Nizakat Bibi, Shouming Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalt stress is widely recognized as a major abiotic constraint that limits plant growth and productivity. Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a globally important forage legume, exhibits considerable variability in salt toll,erance across its diverse cultivars. The present study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of eight alfalfa varieties by examining their morphological, physiological, and biochemical responses at four NaCl levels (0, 60, 120, and 180 mM NaCl).MethodologySalt tolerance is quantified using a combination of the membership function method, correlation analysis, principal component analysis, and different indices of salt tolerance.ResultsHigh salt stress significantly reduced plant height (PH), shoot dry weight (SDW), root dry weight (RDW), and number of leaves (NOL) of all alfalfa varieties by 47.21%, 61.36%, 64.19%, and 59.91%, respectively, compared with the control. Similarly, physiological traits including relative water content (RWC) and stomatal conductance (SC) declined by 43.93% and 60.87%, respectively, whereas electrolyte leakage (EL) and malondialdehyde (MDA) increased by 267.35% and 178.48%, respectively. Salt stress also markedly altered antioxidant responses among varieties, with superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) activities increasing by 111.27%, 170.87%, and 125.87%, while V5 (A'erjienong) and V2 (Weinasi) consistently showed tolerance, whereas V4 (Fudina) and V8 (Qianjing) exhibited susceptibility across varying stress levels. Principal component analysis (PCA) classified the traits into three main groups, which explained 93.15% of the total variation, and identified SOD, POD, CAT, SS, and Car as crucial factors affecting salt tolerance.ConclusionsComprehensive membership function analysis indicated that variety V5 (A'erjienong) was the most tolerant, while variety V4 (Fudina) remained the most sensitive. This study highlights the importance of morpho-physiological and biochemical traits, along with stress tolerance indices, as reliable indicators for evaluating salt tolerance in alfalfa. Future integration of these traits with advanced genetic and breeding approaches may facilitate the development of high-yielding, salt-tolerant alfalfa cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09157-7.

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is a perennial forage legume widely cultivated and highly valued for its significant agronomic value and strong adaptability across diverse environments [1, 2]. Because of its high protein content, rich nutritional value and high biomass yield, it is often called the "king of forages" [3, 4]. In addition to being used as a forage crop, alfalfa improves soil fertility and strengthens the physical structure of degraded and marginal soils, thus contributing to sustainable agricultural development [5, 6]. It efficiently fixes atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) through a symbiotic relationship with rhizobia, thereby increasing soil nitrogen content and reducing the need for synthetic fertilizers [7, 8]. In addition, it is often integrated into crop rotation systems to restore soil health, promote ecosystem sustainability, and increase the yield of subsequent crops [9]. Due to its multifaceted agronomic, ecological, and economic advantages, alfalfa is widely regarded as a key component of sustainable agriculture and the global livestock industry [10].

Despite its considerable agronomic importance and adaptability, alfalfa plants are continuously exposed to various biotic and abiotic stresses throughout their life cycle, which adversely affect their growth, development, productivity, and forage yield [11]. Among these stresses, soil salinization has emerged as a major global challenge affecting over 1.38 billion hectares, which accounts for almost 10.7% of global land area [12]. Soil salinization exhibits substantial spatial variability across the world, emphasizing its significance as a major global environmental constraint. Major alfalfa producing countries such as China, United states, Russia, Australia, Iran, Kazakhstan, and Mediterranean countries such as Morocco, are increasingly effected by soil salinity, posing a serious threat to alfalfa productivity [13]. According to the World Soil Information Service (WoSIS), approximately 18,148 soil profiles contain electrical conductivity (EC) data derived from saturated soil paste extracts, with salinity categorized as non-saline (< 2 dS m⁻1), slightly saline (2–4 dS m⁻1), moderately saline (4–8 dS m⁻1), highly saline (8–16 dS m⁻1), and extremely saline (> 16 dS m⁻1) [14]. Reported EC values vary widely among agricultural regions, ranging from 0.025–46.31 dS m⁻1 in California, 0.089–22.78 dS m⁻1 in China, 0.27–22.45 dS m⁻1 in Turkey, 0.15–26.61 dS m⁻1 in South Africa, 0.032–33.92 dS m⁻1 in Iran, and 0.22–20.25 dS m⁻1 in Brazil, indicating the widespread and heterogeneous nature of soil salinity and its potential to severely disrupt plant physiological and biochemical processes [15].

Salt stress severely limits plant growth, physiological functions, and yield potential [16–18]. The accumulation of toxic ions, particularly sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻), can disrupt normal metabolic and physiological processes in plants. Although alfalfa is relatively tolerant to salt stress and more resistant than many other forage crops, it remains susceptible to excessive salinity [19]. Furthermore, excessive soil salinization not only severely limits alfalfa production but also leads to progressive soil degradation [20]. To overcome this challenge, various chemical compounds, reagents, plant hormones and growth regulators have been developed and applied to mitigate the adverse effects of salt stress on plants [21].

The detrimental effects of NaCl stress on plants are highly complex and multifaceted, as salt stress interferes with a wide range of physiological, biochemical, and molecular processes [22]. Increased concentrations of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in the root zone disturb osmotic balance, limit water absorption, and cause cellular dehydration [23]. Therefore, excessive accumulation of ions can lead to ionic toxicity, which in turn causes nutritional imbalance and metabolic disruption, ultimately reducing photosynthetic efficiency, enzymatic activity, and cell membrane integrity [24]. The coexistence of osmotic pressure and ionic stress can lead to excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which ultimately causes oxidative damage to biomolecules such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids [25]. To mitigate these adverse effects, plants employ various defense strategies, including the synthesis of osmotic regulators, ion sequestration, and the activation of antioxidant defense systems, which, as a result, contribute to the maintenance of cellular homeostasis and the reduction of salt-induced oxidative damage [21]. However, the performance of these protective mechanisms varies considerably across plant species and genotypes, highlighting the need to identify and develop salt-tolerant varieties to ensure sustainable agricultural production under salt stress [26].

Given the complexity of plant responses to salt stress and the genotypic variation in adaptive mechanisms, developing a reliable and accurate method for assessing salt tolerance is essential for effectively identifying salt-tolerant plant varieties [27]. This tolerance is regulated by a complex network of molecular and physiological mechanisms; therefore, using a single index is insufficient to accurately reflect a plant's overall salt tolerance level [28]. Therefore, it is necessary to use multiple parameters and indices to evaluate plant salt tolerance comprehensively [29]. The comprehensive membership function is a widely used approach that integrates various quantitative traits related to salt tolerance into a unified overall assessment [30]. This method calculates membership function values based on the weights of each trait and evaluates the overall performance of plant varieties under salt stress. From these comprehensive values, it efficiently and intuitively distinguishes salt-tolerant varieties from salt-sensitive ones [26].

This study employed a comprehensive trait-based approach to identify highly tolerant and susceptible alfalfa cultivars under salt stress. By combining principal component analysis, correlation analysis, stress tolerance indices, and the comprehensive membership function approach, significant varietal differences in salinity response were identified. Overall, the findings provide both a theoretical basis and practical guidance for screening and selecting suitable alfalfa varieties under saline environments. Furthermore, integrating these key traits with advanced genetic and breeding strategies could contribute to the development of high-yielding and salt-tolerant alfalfa cultivars in the future.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

A greenhouse study was carried out at the School of Life Sciences, Henan University, Kaifeng, China, using a completely randomized design with a factorial arrangement of treatments. Each treatment was replicated four times, and the experiment was repeated twice. Eight varieties of alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), namely V1: 北极熊; Beijixiong, V2: 维纳斯; Weinasi, V3: 布里亚; Buliya, V4: 福蒂娜; Fudina, V5: 阿尔杰农; A'erjienong, V6: 陇东苜蓿; longdong muxu, V7: 燃力士; Ranlishi, V8: 前景; Qianjing, were purchased from the Beijing Best Grass Industry Co., Ltd., China, with a germination rate greater than 90%. Mature seeds were first briefly exposed to 70% ethanol for 30 s, followed by prolonged disinfection in a 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution for 20 min. Upon completion of the sterilization process, the seeds were thoroughly washed multiple times with sterile distilled water to remove any traces of residual disinfecting agents [31]. Sterilized seeds were sown in germination trays (200 × 160 mm) containing vermiculite as the growth medium. The trays were placed in a controlled growth chamber at 25 ± 1 °C, under a 16-h light and 8-h dark photoperiod. Seed germination and seedling emergence were monitored for five days. After the seedlings reached the three-leaf stage, four uniform and healthy seedlings were selected and transplanted in plastic pots (19 × 18 cm) with a perforated bottom containing 1:1 nutrient soil and quartz sand (purchased from Shouhe Seed Industry Co., Ltd.). After transplanting, all plants were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions and irrigated regularly to ensure optimal growth before the initiation of experimental treatments.

Experimental treatments

Three-week-old seedlings were exposed to NaCl treatments of 0, 60, 120, and 180 mM. To minimize osmotic shock, stress levels were gradually increased over a one-week acclimation period, starting with 60 mM NaCl on the first day for all treated pots. The NaCl concentration was then doubled incrementally every 2 days until the target stress for each treatment was reached. Importantly, the control treatment (0 mM NaCl) was maintained without NaCl application throughout the experiment, whereas salt-treated plants received gradual NaCl increments to reach the designated concentrations. Once the final NaCl levels were established, plants were irrigated daily with a half-strength nutrient solution supplemented with the corresponding NaCl concentration. The seedlings remained under these salt-stress conditions for 6 weeks, after which they were harvested for sampling.

At the end of the experiment, soil salinity was determined by measuring the electrical conductivity (EC) of the saturated soil paste extract (ECe) to verify the actual salinity levels developed in the pots following repeated NaCl applications. The measured soil ECe values increased progressively with increasing NaCl treatments and ranged from 1.4–1.9, 4.6–5.3, 9.5–10.8, and 16.4–18.5 dS m⁻1 under 0, 60, 120, and 180 mM NaCl treatments, respectively.

Morphological traits

At sampling, plants from each pot were carefully uprooted and washed under tap water. Roots were softly pressed with absorbent paper towels to remove excess water. Measurements were taken for growth parameters, including plant height (PH), number of leaves (NOL), root dry weight (RDW), and shoot dry weight (SDW). Plant height was recorded from the basal part of the stem to the tip of the main shoot with a ruler. The total number of fully expanded leaves per plant was determined by manual observation and counting. To determine dry biomass, samples were initially oven-treated at 105 °C for 30 min to eliminate residual surface moisture, and then dried at 65 °C until a stable, constant weight was attained [32].

Physiological assessments

Electrolyte leakage (EL) is often used as a measure of cell membrane integrity or injury. Newly collected leaf samples were thoroughly washed using deionized water and then placed into glass tubes filled with 10 mL of deionized water. Following vacuum infiltration, the tubes were shaken for 20 min, after which the initial electrical conductivity (C1) was measured. The samples were then autoclaved to completely release cellular electrolytes, and the final electrical conductivity (C2) was recorded according to Zheng et al. [33].

For the measurement of relative water content (RWC), leaves were rapidly collected from the plants, and fresh weight was noted immediately. After cutting, the samples were immersed in 10 mL of Millipore water and incubated at 4 °C to minimize metabolic activity and tissue deterioration during rehydration while allowing sufficient water uptake for the determination of turgid weight (TW), following previously published protocols. Similarly, oven drying at 80 °C for 24 h was performed until constant dry weight (DW) was achieved. To determine stomatal conductance (SC), a portable instrument, AP4 Porometer (Delta-T Devices, Cambridge, United Kingdom), was operated from 11:00 to 14:00 h under clear, sunny conditions to ensure uniform and maximal stomatal opening. Measurements were taken on the adaxial surface of three randomly chosen leaves per plant, and replicate means were determined.

Antioxidant enzymes and lipid peroxidation

The activities of major antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD), were calculated with the help of Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–Vis spectrophotometer, based on established protocols with minor modifications, as described by Aebi et al. [34], Beyer and Fridovich et al. [35], and Yadav et al. [36]. For SOD activity determination, the assay system was formulated by combining phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8), nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA), methionine, riboflavin, distilled water, and the enzyme extract in appropriate proportions. NBT reduction was used to evaluate the activity of SOD.

Peroxidase activity (POD) was assessed using a reaction mixture that included guaiacol as the hydrogen donor, phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and a defined volume of enzyme extract, with absorbance changes monitored spectrophotometrically. Catalase activity was measured by adding enzyme extract to a buffered hydrogen peroxide solution (pH 7.0), and the rate of H₂O₂ decomposition was subsequently recorded.

Lipid peroxidation was estimated by determining malondialdehyde (MDA) content according to the protocol described by Puckette et al. [37]. Fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 0.1% (v/v) trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged to obtain a clear supernatant. An aliquot of the extract was reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA), and the mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 30 min. After rapid cooling on ice, absorbance was noted at 450, 532, and 600 nm to determine MDA concentration as an indicator of membrane lipid peroxidation.

Osmotic substances and carotenoid contents

Soluble sugar (SS) accumulation in leaves was recorded using a Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–Vis spectrophotometer according to the protocol of Zheng et al. [33], with minor adjustments. Briefly, 0.3 g of freshly harvested leaves was finely homogenized in deionized water, after which the homogenate was subjected to thermal extraction by incubation in a boiling water bath for 20 min. Once cooled to ambient temperature, the extract was clarified by filtration, and its final volume was adjusted to 100 mL using deionized water. For colourimetric analysis, 1 mL of the prepared extract was mixed with 4 mL of anthrone reagent and incubated at 100 °C for 10 min to facilitate colour formation. After quickly cooling the reaction mixture, the absorbance at 620 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer, and the soluble sugar content was subsequently determined.

Proline accumulation (PC) in leaf tissues was estimated with a Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–Vis spectrophotometer using the classical assay of Bates et al. [38] with minor procedural adjustments. Fresh leaf samples (0.4 g) were heated in 3% sulfosalicylic acid for 10 min under constant agitation to ensure efficient solubilization. After cooling, the homogenate was clarified by filtration, and a 2 mL aliquot of the filtrate was reacted with an equal volume of glacial acetic acid and acidic ninhydrin. The reaction mixture was incubated at 100 °C for 30 min to promote chromophore development, then cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, 4 mL of methylbenzene was added to separate the coloured complex, and the absorbance of the organic phase was noted at 520 nm to calculate proline content.

Carotenoid content (Car) was determined spectrophotometrically using a Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–Vis spectrophotometer, according to the procedure described by Lichtenthaler et al. [39]. Fresh leaf samples were finely ground in 95% ethanol in the presence of silicon dioxide and calcium carbonate to facilitate efficient pigment extraction. The homogenate was then incubated at 4 °C under dark conditions until the tissue became completely decolorized. Following filtration and appropriate dilution of the extract, absorbance was noted at 470, 663, and 645 nm using a spectrophotometer, and carotenoid content was calculated accordingly.

Statistical analysis and formula description

The salt tolerance coefficient, comprehensive salt tolerance coefficient, salt tolerance index, and comprehensive tolerance membership function values were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2021. Correlation analysis, and principal component analysis were performed with R-studio software. The figures were created using OriginPro 2025b SR1. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of the data, and the residual was normally distributed. Homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. Thereafter, ANOVA was analysed through R-studio software to evaluate the salt levels and tolerance degree using the values of morphological (PH, SDW, RDW, NOL), physiological (EL, RWC, SC, and Carotenoids), antioxidant (SOD, POD, CAT, and MDA) and osmotic conditioning (SS, and PC) parameters. Following ANOVA, Tukey’s post hoc test was used to identify statistically significant differences at p < 0.05. Data presented in the figures are the mean values of four biological replicates (n = 4) ± standard error (SE).

Results

Effects of salt stress on growth attributes

Morphological assessment demonstrated that among the tested varieties, V5 consistently performed as the most salt-tolerant variety, whereas V4 remained highly sensitive under saline conditions (Fig. 2A-D). At 60 mM, V4 and V5 decreased by 19 ± 3.3% and 10 ± 2.7% in PH, 26 ± 3.1% and 9 ± 2.7% in SDW, 35 ± 2.7% and 13 ± 1.2% in RDW, whereas 24 ± 8.6% and 8 ± 6.9% in NOL, as compared to the control (Fig. 1). At 120 mM, the decline persisted (PH: 41 ± 4.6% and 13 ± 2.8%; SDW: 55 ± 5.0% and 39 ± 1.6%; RDW: 61 ± 2.6% and 35 ± 1.0%; NOL: 50 ± 7.6% and 34 ± 4.7%). Under 180 mM, reductions reached 52 ± 1.0% and 40 ± 1.6% in PH, 66 ± 2.8% and 48 ± 3.1% in SDW, 70 ± 2.3% and 54 ± 1.7% in RDW, and 61 ± 8.7% and 46 ± 5.2% in NOL, respectively, in comparison to the control. Physiological adaptations to salt stress

In response to stress, RWC decreased sharply, ranging from 56 ± 2.6% to 75 ± 1.6% at 60 mM and from 41 ± 1.9% to 67 ± 2.1% at 120 mM NaCl (Fig. 3A). Electrolyte leakage (EL) increased with increasing stress in all varieties. Under control conditions, EL remained reduced, ranging from 13 ± 2.5% to 24 ± 1.6%, with the highest EL observed in V8 and the lowest in V2. Under saline conditions, EL increased markedly, ranging from 31 ± 1.7% to 59 ± 2.1% at 60 mM and 51 ± 1.4% to 71 ± 0.8% at 120 mM NaCl (Fig. 2B). The most severe adverse effects were observed at 180 mM NaCl, with V5 (EL: 59 ± 1.7%) extremely resilient and V4 (EL: 78 ± 1.2%) strongly affected by NaCl stress.

Stomatal conductance (SC) declined steadily across all varieties as stress level increased. In control plants, SC maintained high levels (0.39 ± 0.02 to 0.56 ± 0.01 mol m−2 S−1), with V8 recording the lowest and V5 the highest (Fig. 3C). Moreover, exposure to salt stress led to a pronounced reduction in SC, which ranged from 0.28 ± 0.02 to 0.47 ± 0.02 mol m−2 S−1 at 60 mM and from 0.21 ± 0.02 to 0.40 ± 0.01 mol m−2 S−1 at 120 mM (Fig. 2C). At 180 mM NaCl, the impact of stress was most pronounced, with V5 (SC: 0.25 ± 0.02 mol m−2 S−1) showing exceptional tolerance and V4 (SC: 0.12 ± 0.01 mol m−2 S−1) displaying substantial sensitivity.

Antioxidant defense responses and lipid peroxidation patterns

With increasing NaCl concentration, the activities of antioxidant enzymes SOD, POD, and CAT showed a consistent upward trend across all varieties (Fig. 4A-C). Compared with the control treatment, varieties V2 and V5 showed the most significant increases in SOD, POD, and CAT activities under 60, 120, and 180 mM NaCl stress. Under 60 mM NaCl stress, V5 exhibited the strongest antioxidant response, followed by V2, with SOD and POD activities increasing by 37 ± 4.4% and 90 ± 3.2% in V5, compared with 30 ± 5.7% and 56 ± 3.6% in V2 relative to the control. A similar pattern was observed under 120 mM NaCl stress, where V5 again exhibited the strongest antioxidant response, followed by V2, with SOD and POD activities showing the same relative increases. Moreover, at 180 mM NaCl, V5 displayed the highest elevation in SOD and POD (165 ± 14.1% and 276 ± 5.5%), exhibiting high tolerance, whereas V4 showed a lowest increase (48 ± 2.6% and 76 ± 3.7%), indicating strong susceptibility to salt stress (Fig. 4A and B). Surprisingly, CAT activity under 60, 120, and 180 mM NaCl increased by 115 ± 8.8%, 186 ± 8.5%, and 213 ± 18.1% in V2, respectively, and remained higher than in V5, contrary to the pattern observed for SOD and POD (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, under 180 mM NaCl stress, V5 (813 ± 33.3%) showed the most significant increase, whereas V8 (242 ± 24.7%) showed the smallest increase relative to the control, contrary to the pattern observed in SS (Fig. 5B).

Carotenoid content (Car) declined steadily across all varieties with increasing salt stress. In control plants, carotenoids remained comparatively high, ranging from 24.46 to 42.14 mg g−1 FW. Moreover, exposure to stress led to a pronounced reduction in Car, ranging from 10.97 to 40.53 mg g-1 FW at 60 mM and from 9.56 to 38.45 mg g-1 FW at 120 mM NaCl (Fig. 5C). At 180 mM NaCl, the most substantial adverse effects on car contents were observed, with V5 showing a moderate reduction (31 ± 5.1%) and V4 showing a drastic reduction (82 ± 2.4%) compared to the control.

Correlation analysis

Both dendrogram clustering and correlation analysis jointly classified all indicators into three distinct groups (Fig. 6). The first group comprised Car, PH, SDW, NOL, RWC, and SC, which were positively correlated with each other, suggesting that improved water status, photosynthetic pigments, and growth-related traits were closely associated under salinity stress conditions. The second group included EL and MDA, both of which are commonly recognized as indicators of membrane damage and oxidative stress, and therefore their positive association may reflect increased cellular injury under higher salinity levels. The third group consisted of RDW, PC, SOD, POD, CAT, and SS, which also showed strong positive correlations within the group. The clustering of RDW with antioxidant enzymes and osmoprotective compounds suggests that enhanced antioxidant defense and osmotic adjustment may contribute to the maintenance of root biomass under salt stress conditions. Parameters in the first group exhibited strong negative correlations with those in the second group, indicating that improved growth performance and physiological stability were generally associated with lower oxidative damage. Moreover, most parameters in the first group, except Car, were negatively correlated with RDW and PC in the third group, possibly reflecting differential adaptive responses between shoot growth-related traits and stress-responsive defense mechanisms under increasing salinity stress. Both EL and MDA showed significant positive associations with RDW and PC, suggesting that increased oxidative stress may trigger enhanced osmoprotective and defense-related responses in alfalfa plants. Furthermore, PH exhibited weak negative relationships with both SOD and POD, whereas EL showed a weak association with POD (Fig. 6). Comprehensive membership function

An enhanced comprehensive membership function approach was employed to quantify and rank the salt tolerance of the eight varieties by integrating both trait interrelationships and their relative contributions to the salt tolerance index (Fig. 8A-B). In the first step, the salt tolerance coefficient (STC), comprehensive salt tolerance coefficient (CSTC), and salt tolerance index (STI) were measured for each variety (Table 1). After this, the weight of each trait was estimated by calculating the correlation coefficients (r) between the STC of individual traits and the corresponding STI values across varieties (Fig. 7A). These weighted parameters were then incorporated to estimate comprehensive tolerance membership function values, which provided an overall evaluation of salt tolerance. Based on this assessment, variety V5 exhibited the highest level of salt tolerance, whereas V4 was identified as the most salt-sensitive genotype (Fig. 7B). Given that correlation-based weighting combined with membership function analysis is widely applied in plant stress tolerance studies because it enables the integrated evaluation of multiple morpho-physiological and biochemical traits simultaneously. A similar approach was also recently employed by Fung et al. (2025) for screening plant varieties under salt stress conditions. In the present study, this method was adopted to comprehensively assess salt tolerance among alfalfa varieties by integrating diverse physiological and biochemical indicators. Discussion

When plants are exposed to elevated salt concentrations, the roots are generally the first organs to be adversely affected [43, 44]. Salt stress disrupts ionic homeostasis and osmotic balance, thereby restricting nutrient acquisition and reducing root hydraulic conductivity [45, 46]. Consequently, water transport to aerial tissues is impaired, leading to suppressed cellular expansion, metabolic dysfunction, and overall inhibition of plant growth and development [47]. In the present study, increasing NaCl concentrations led to a clear reduction in plant height, biomass, and the number of leaves per plant (Fig. 1). Among the evaluated alfalfa varieties, A’erjienong showed strong salt tolerance, with minimal damage across the stress treatments compared with the control. Because salt resistance is a complex quantitative trait influenced by multiple genetic and non-genetic factors [48], no single parameter can reliably reflect a variety's tolerance. Therefore, this study assessed salt tolerance using a comprehensive set of 14 parameters and 3 tolerance indices under 60, 120, and 180 mM NaCl stress.

The progressive decline in RWC and SC, together with the marked increase in EL under increasing NaCl concentrations, demonstrates the severe osmotic and ionic stress imposed on alfalfa seedlings in the present study (Fig. 3). Excessive accumulation of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions likely disrupted cellular ionic homeostasis, and it stimulated the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide, thereby intensifying oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [49, 50]. Such oxidative damage destabilizes membrane structures and increases cellular permeability, which explains the elevated EL observed under higher salinity levels and reflects substantial membrane injury and loss of cellular integrity [51–53]. Simultaneously, high external salt concentrations reduce soil water potential, restricting root water uptake and impairing cellular hydration, ultimately resulting in reduced RWC and disruption of hydration-dependent physiological processes [54–56]. The pronounced reduction in SC under 120 and 180 mM NaCl treatments further suggests that stomatal limitation is an adaptive response to minimize transpirational water loss under osmotic stress. However, prolonged stomatal closure also restricts CO₂ diffusion into leaf tissues, thereby limiting photosynthetic carbon assimilation and overall plant productivity under saline environments [57]. Our findings revealed a consistent decrease in RWC and a corresponding increase in EL with increasing NaCl concentration, demonstrating the combined osmotic and ionic stress imposed on alfalfa seedlings. These observations align closely with the results reported by Mansour et al. [50]. A similar pattern was observed for SC, which declined markedly under 120 and 180 mM treatments, indicating a substantial reduction in gas-exchange efficiency. These findings are consistent with the study by Liao et al. [58]. Notably, significant varietal differences in physiological tolerance to salinity stress were observed. V5 and V2 maintained relatively higher RWC, lower EL, and more stable SC under elevated salinity conditions, suggesting superior osmotic adjustment capacity and membrane stability. In contrast, V4 and V8 exhibited substantial reductions in RWC and SC along with elevated EL, indicating greater membrane damage and impaired water relations under salt stress (Fig. 2). These findings collectively suggest that physiological traits associated with water retention, membrane integrity, and stomatal regulation are highly sensitive indicators of salinity tolerance and provide deeper insights into stress adaptation mechanisms beyond morphological responses alone [21].

Plants exposed to salinity stress activate a highly coordinated antioxidant defense system comprising SOD, POD, and CAT, which collectively function to alleviate oxidative damage by scavenging excessive ROS [21, 59]. Accumulation of ROS, leads to membrane lipid peroxidation, cellular dysfunction, and oxidative injury if not efficiently detoxified. In the present study, significant varietal differences were observed in the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT under increasing NaCl concentrations, indicating substantial varietal variation in antioxidative defense capacity among the evaluated alfalfa varieties (Fig. 4). These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that different varieties exhibit distinct responses in antioxidant enzyme activities under salt stress [26]. Among the tested varieties, V2 and V5 exhibited comparatively greater tolerance to saline conditions, which may be attributed to their more efficient antioxidant defense system and improved ability to maintain cellular redox homeostasis under stress (Fig. 4A-C). Higher SOD activity in tolerant varieties likely promoted the rapid dismutation of superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, which was subsequently detoxified through the coordinated action of POD and CAT, thereby minimizing oxidative damage and preserving membrane stability [60]. In contrast, V4 and V8 exhibited lower SOD and POD activities, reflecting a weaker enzymatic defense system and, consequently, greater susceptibility to oxidative stress–induced cellular injury (Fig. 3A and 3B). Notably, V5 maintained relatively stable SOD and POD activities, whereas V2 sustained stable CAT activity even under severe salinity levels, suggesting superior ROS-scavenging efficiency and enhanced oxidative stress tolerance. Conversely, V4 remained highly susceptible to salinity stress, exhibiting reduced activities of all three antioxidant enzymes at 60, 120, and 180 mM NaCl treatments (Fig. 3A–C). These findings clearly demonstrate that antioxidant responses are strongly variety-dependent and constitute a critical physiological mechanism underlying salinity tolerance in alfalfa, which is in agreement with the observations reported by Yin et al. [61].

Moreover, the varietal differences observed in antioxidant enzyme activities were further supported by the accumulation pattern of malondialdehyde (MDA), a major end-product of membrane lipid peroxidation and a widely recognized indicator of oxidative membrane damage induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) under stress conditions [62, 63]. In saline environments, excessive NaCl disrupts reactive oxygen metabolism, accelerates ROS generation, and destabilizes cellular membranes, ultimately leading to enhanced lipid peroxidation and elevated MDA accumulation [64]. In agreement with previous findings reported by Zhang et al. [65], the present study also demonstrated a significant increase in MDA levels with increasing salinity stress (Fig. 4D), indicating intensified oxidative damage under higher NaCl concentrations. The pattern of MDA accumulation closely corresponded with the antioxidative responses of the evaluated varieties, further highlighting the critical role of antioxidant defense mechanisms in regulating oxidative stress tolerance [66–68]. Varieties exhibiting stronger antioxidant activities, particularly V5, maintained comparatively lower MDA accumulation across 60 to 180 mM NaCl treatments, suggesting a more efficient ROS-scavenging system and improved membrane stability under saline conditions (Fig. 4D). In contrast, V4 showed the greatest increase in MDA content, reflecting severe membrane lipid peroxidation and enhanced susceptibility to oxidative injury due to its comparatively weaker antioxidative defense capacity. These findings collectively suggest that the ability to regulate ROS detoxification and minimize membrane oxidative damage effectively is a fundamental physiological attribute contributing to salinity tolerance in alfalfa varieties [67].

Under saline conditions, plants activate osmotic adjustment mechanisms by accumulating compatible solutes, such as SS and PC, which play important roles in maintaining cellular osmotic balance, stabilizing membranes and proteins, preserving cellular hydration, and protecting metabolic activities from salt-induced osmotic stress [47, 69, 70]. Salinity stress reduces external water potential and disrupts cellular homeostasis, thereby restricting water uptake and altering carbohydrate metabolism [71]. In the present study, significant varietal differences in SS and proline accumulation were observed across increasing NaCl concentrations, indicating substantial genotypic variation in osmotic adjustment capacity among the evaluated alfalfa varieties. Consistent with previous findings in soybean reported by Noor et al. [72], salinity stress significantly affected SS accumulation, highlighting the important role of carbohydrate metabolism in plant adaptation to saline environments [60]. Under moderate salinity, increased SS accumulation likely contributed to osmotic regulation and cellular protection; however, severe salinity stress (180 mM NaCl) caused a marked decline in SS content in most varieties, possibly due to impaired photosynthetic efficiency, disrupted carbohydrate metabolism, and enhanced metabolic dysfunction under prolonged stress conditions [73]. In contrast, the continuous increase in proline accumulation under increasing salinity levels suggests the activation of a protective osmolyte-mediated defense mechanism that helps maintain osmotic equilibrium and alleviate oxidative damage under severe salt stress (Fig. 5B). Notably, V2 exhibited the greatest increase in SS accumulation under 180 mM NaCl stress, indicating superior osmotic adjustment capacity and enhanced metabolic resilience, whereas V4 showed the lowest SS accumulation, reflecting greater susceptibility to salt-induced osmotic imbalance and metabolic disruption (Fig. 4A). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that efficient osmotic regulation through compatible solute accumulation is an important physiological mechanism underlying salinity tolerance in alfalfa varieties.

The continuous increase in proline accumulation under increasing salinity levels suggests that alfalfa activates an effective osmolyte-mediated defense mechanism to maintain osmotic stability and enhance tolerance to severe salt stress (Fig. 5A). It acts as an important compatible solute that stabilizes proteins, nucleic acids, and cellular membranes, while also contributing to redox regulation, energy transfer, and post-stress recovery processes [73, 74]. Similar increases in proline accumulation under saline conditions have previously been reported in alfalfa by Chen et al. [75] and Min et al. [76], which is consistent with the findings of the present study (Fig. 5B). In the current investigation, proline accumulation progressively increased with increasing NaCl concentrations, reaching peak levels at 120 and 180 mM NaCl in all evaluated varieties. However, salt-tolerant varieties exhibited comparatively greater proline accumulation than susceptible ones, indicating superior osmotic adjustment capacity under saline conditions [21]. Among the tested varieties, V5 showed the greatest increase in proline content, whereas V8 exhibited the lowest accumulation (Fig. 5B). This enhanced endogenous proline accumulation in tolerant varieties likely contributed to reduced cellular osmotic potential, maintenance of cell turgor, and improved physiological resilience under salt stress, as also suggested by Ziche et al. [77].

Although the accumulation of osmoprotectants contributed to osmotic adjustment and cellular protection under saline conditions, these responses were insufficient to fully prevent oxidative damage to the photosynthetic machinery under severe salt stress [78]. Salinity-induced disruption of chloroplast structure and photosynthetic electron transport enhances reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, thereby accelerating pigment degradation and impairing photosynthetic efficiency [79]. Carotenoids (Car), which function as important photoprotective and antioxidative pigments, declined progressively with increasing NaCl concentration across all evaluated varieties, consistent with the findings of Feng et al. [26]. The decline was most pronounced at 180 mM NaCl, suggesting that severe salinity stress exceeded the plants’ pigment biosynthetic and antioxidative protection capacity, leading to accelerated carotenoid degradation (Fig. 5C). Notably, V5 exhibited only a minimal reduction in Car content, indicating greater photoprotective capacity and chloroplast stability under saline conditions, whereas V4 showed a much sharper decline relative to the control, reflecting greater susceptibility to chloroplast-level oxidative damage. These findings suggest that maintaining carotenoid stability is an important physiological trait associated with salinity tolerance in alfalfa varieties [67, 80].

Accurate assessment of salt tolerance is crucial for the practical identification of salt-resilient germplasm [29]. However, the complex nature of physiological responses to NaCl stress makes it insufficient to rely on a single trait to represent overall tolerance [26]. In the present study, correlation analysis demonstrated widespread positive and negative associations among the evaluated parameters (Fig. 6). Principal component analysis further grouped these traits into three primary components, which together accounted for 93.15% of the total observed variation, and identified antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT), along with SS and Car, as the key determinants for salt tolerance variation (Fig. 7). Membership function-based evaluation methods have been extensively adopted to assess plant performance under abiotic stress conditions [81]. In the same way, Gholizadeh et al. [82] successfully identified a highly salt-tolerant genotype by applying a comprehensive membership function approach to 110 varieties, while Feng et al. [26] employed a similar strategy to screen 30 alfalfa cultivars for superior salt stress tolerance. Consistent with these approaches, the current study examines the relationships between multiple morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits to explore their interrelationships under salt stress and to elucidate their collective contribution to salt tolerance indices in alfalfa. Lie et al. [83] calculated trait weights and applied a comprehensive membership function to assess stress tolerance, accounting for plant production performance. Accordingly, a comprehensive tolerance membership function was used to screen eight alfalfa varieties under NaCl stress, identifying V5 as highly tolerant and V4 as salt-sensitive.

Conclusion

This study evaluated changes in morphological characteristics, physiological parameters, antioxidant enzymes, and osmotic substances of eight alfalfa varieties under different salt stress conditions. We used a comprehensive membership-function approach integrated with several salt-stress indices to distinguish salt-tolerant and salt-intolerant varieties. The findings indicated that increased salt concentration inhibited the development of plant morphological traits and exacerbated cell membrane damage, as shown by higher levels of MDA and EL. In response, the plants activated internal defense mechanisms, characterized by increased antioxidant activity (SOD, POD, and CAT) and the accumulation of osmotic regulators (such as SS and PC), which helped neutralize reactive oxygen species, maintain osmotic balance, and stabilize cell structure. The extent of these responses varied considerably across varieties, indicating significant heterogeneity in their ability to withstand salt stress. In summary, variety V5 (A'erjienong) demonstrated significant salinity tolerance, while variety V4 (Fudina) proved highly sensitive to it. These findings provide a valuable foundation for future breeding and crop improvement programs aimed at enhancing salinity tolerance in alfalfa. Future research should focus on integrating key physiological and biochemical traits with molecular breeding, marker-assisted selection, genomic approaches, and stress-responsive gene characterization to accelerate the development of high-yielding and salt-tolerant cultivars. Additionally, multi-year field evaluations under naturally saline conditions are necessary to further validate the stability and practical applicability of these genotypes for sustainable forage production systems.
