Associations between sick leave during pregnancy and after childbirth and future labour force participation: a Dutch cohort study
Authors: Zayel Z Frijmersum, Eva Van Der Meij, Judith A Huirne, Petra C Bakker, Johannes Anema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWomen’s labour force participation often declines after childbirth, but the role of sick leave during pregnancy and after childbirth in sustained employment remains unclear. This study examined: (1) the association between sick leave during pregnancy and/or within 2 years after childbirth and sustained labour force participation, (2) whether sick leave during pregnancy predicts sick leave within 2 years after childbirth, (3) demographic and employment-related factors associated with sick leave length and labour force participation.MethodsProspective cohort study including 261 171 women in the Netherlands who applied for maternity benefits in 2020 or 2021. A subset also received sick leave benefits during pregnancy or after childbirth. Logistic regression assessed associations between sick leave timing and sustained labour participation. General linear models examined determinants of the total length of sick leave during pregnancy and within 2 years after childbirth.ResultsSick leave during pregnancy was associated with higher odds of sustained labour force participation (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.23), whereas sick leave within 2 years after childbirth was associated with lower odds (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.67). Sick leave during pregnancy strongly predicted sick leave within 2 years after childbirth (OR 3.63, 95% CI 3.53 to 3.74). Employment sector, contract type, residential area and age were associated with sick leave length and labour participation.ConclusionSick leave within 2 years after childbirth was associated with reduced sustained labour force participation, whereas sick leave during pregnancy was not negatively associated. Employment characteristics were strongly associated with both sick leave patterns and sustained labour force participation.Keywords: Occupational Health, Obstetrics, Sick Leave

Introduction

In the Netherlands, the employment rate among women aged 15–75 years was 69.2% in 2023 compared with 77.4% among men. From around age 25 years, corresponding to the typical childbearing period, women’s employment rate declines.1 This decline is partly explained by family-related factors, including childcare responsibilities and the availability and costs of childcare, which influence maternal labour force participation.2,5 Each year, approximately 165 000 women give birth in the Netherlands, and many reduce their working hours following maternity leave.6 7 Similar patterns have been reported internationally.8 Sick leave during pregnancy and after childbirth may also contribute to reduced labour force participation, as health complaints during these periods are common and can affect social participation and the ability to work.9 Supporting women’s workforce participation during and after the childbearing period is therefore crucial to address labour shortages and promote economic independence.

In the Netherlands, employed pregnant women are entitled to maternity leave benefits under the Work and Care Act (Wet Arbeid en Zorg (WAZO)), while women medically unable to work may receive sick leave benefits under the Sickness Benefits Act (Ziektewet (ZW)).10 These arrangements provide income continuity, although sick leave may also be medically advised as a preventive measure and caregiving responsibilities remain unevenly distributed.11 12 A detailed description of the Dutch maternity leave and sickness benefit system is provided in online supplemental addendum 1.

Across Europe, sick leave during pregnancy is common and has increased over recent decades, although patterns before, during and after childbirth vary substantially between countries.13 Self-reported prevalence ranges from one-third of pregnant women in Sweden to over two-thirds in Poland, reflecting differences in national policy contexts as well as health and workplace factors. Pregnancy-related complaints, including musculoskeletal pain, fatigue and sleep disturbances, are major contributors, alongside workplace factors such as physical workload, shift work and job strain.9 14 Dutch studies report that about half of employed women experience sick leave during pregnancy, often for extended periods.10 14 Despite this, little is known about how sick leave during pregnancy and after childbirth affects future labour force participation, as most studies focus on short-term outcomes.

This study therefore examined women who received WAZO maternity benefits in the Netherlands in 2020 or 2021, some of whom also received sick leave benefits during pregnancy or within 2 years after childbirth. Using nationwide administrative data, we described patterns of sick leave during pregnancy and after childbirth and examined their association with sustained labour force participation. Specifically, we assessed how sick leave during pregnancy and/or within 2 years after childbirth related to labour force participation, how sick leave during pregnancy predicted subsequent sick leave and which sociodemographic and employment characteristics are associated with the incidence and total length of sick leave.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study used administrative data from the Dutch Employee Insurance Agency (UWV). Exposure and outcome information were derived from existing administrative records, while labour force participation outcomes were observed during the subsequent 36-month observation period after cohort inclusion. The cohort included women residing in the Netherlands who gave birth in 2020 or 2021 and applied for maternity benefits under WAZO. Dutch citizenship was not required. Women were included regardless of contract type (permanent, flexible, temporary or self-employed).

A subset of women also received statutory sick leave benefits under the ZW during pregnancy and/or within 2 years after childbirth. All sick leave benefits were medically certified according to standardised UWV procedures. Exposure and outcome information were derived from existing administrative records, while labour force participation outcomes were observed during the subsequent observation period after cohort inclusion. WAZO and sickness benefits are available only to women entitled to employee-based social insurance. Some women did not have an active employment contract at the time of WAZO application (recently unemployed or self-employed with insurance entitlement), but women without entitlement to employee-based social insurance were not included. A detailed description of the Dutch maternity and sickness benefit system is provided in online supplemental addendum 1.

Data sources

Data were obtained from UWV administrative systems, including client records, medical assessments and employment declarations submitted by employers for social insurance purposes. Information on employment status and working hours was derived from these administrative employment records. The observation period spanned from January 2020 through June 2024, allowing up to 36 months of observation after childbirth. Information on death or emigration during follow-up was not available in the administrative data. The administrative dataset did not include the exact date of delivery. Childbirth was therefore identified through the WAZO maternity benefit episode recorded in the UWV system, which served as the reference point for defining the observation window and calculating labour force participation during the subsequent 36 months.

Independent variables

Sick leave was operationalised based on receipt of sick leave benefits, irrespective of diagnosis:

Sick leave during pregnancy (yes/no): any sick leave benefit receipt prior to the start of WAZO leave.

Sick leave during pregnancy (yes/no): any sick leave benefit receipt prior to the start of WAZO leave.

Sick leave within 2 years after childbirth (yes/no): any sick leave benefit receipt after the end of WAZO maternity leave and within the subsequent 2-year observation window. Because WAZO leave includes at least 10 weeks after childbirth, the observation window for postchildbirth sick leave begins after maternity leave rather than exactly at the date of delivery.

Sick leave within 2 years after childbirth (yes/no): any sick leave benefit receipt after the end of WAZO maternity leave and within the subsequent 2-year observation window. Total length of sick leave (days): cumulative number of sick leave days during pregnancy and within 2 years after childbirth (summed across episodes). This measure reflects accumulated sick leave days rather than the duration of individual spells. Sick leave could be granted on a full-time or part-time basis for up to 104 weeks. Biopsychosocial and employment characteristics included age, residential area (online supplemental figure 1), education level, employment sector, contract type and baseline working hours. Age was treated as continuous to retain statistical power and avoid information loss from categorisation. Diagnostic information was not available in a standardised format suitable for analysis and was therefore not included. Additional operational definitions are provided in online supplemental addendum 2.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was sustained labour force participation, defined as working ≥48 hours per month in paid employment for at least 24 of the 36 months after childbirth (dichotomous). This measure reflects cumulative labour force attachment rather than employment status at a single time point. Because both labour force participation and sick leave were measured as cumulative indicators within overlapping observation windows after childbirth, the analyses assess associations rather than temporal sequencing between sick leave and work participation.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised descriptively. Logistic regression models were used to estimate crude and adjusted ORs with 95% CIs for associations between sick leave and labour force participation outcomes. Adjusted models included age, residential area, employment sector, contract type and baseline working hours. Linear regression and general linear models (GLM) were applied for continuous outcomes, including total length of sick leave (days) and average working hours. Models examining the total length of sick leave were adjusted for the same covariates. Estimated marginal means were used to compare employment sectors where appropriate.

Analysis addressed four research questions (RQ):

Time-to-event models were not applied because the data did not include precise start and end dates of individual sick leave spells or return-to-work dates. Missing data were minimal due to the administrative nature of the dataset; complete-case analyses were conducted, with ‘unknown’ categories retained where applicable. Information on subsequent pregnancies or births during follow-up was not available. Sensitivity analyses tested interaction terms. Analyses were conducted in SPSS Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA), using a significance level of p<0.05.

At the time of WAZO application, 29.3% had no active employment contract, including women who were self-employed, recently unemployed or receiving other income-replacement benefits while remaining eligible for WAZO. Research question 1

What is the association between sick leave during pregnancy and/or within 2 years after childbirth and sustained labour force participation over a 3-year period among women who applied for a WAZO benefit, adjusting for age, residential area, employment sector, contract type and baseline working hours?

When a combined sick leave variable was included, representing women who experienced sick leave both during pregnancy and within 2 years after childbirth, the association with sustained participation was modest and not statistically significant after adjustment (adjusted OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.04). No statistically significant interaction was observed between sick leave during pregnancy and sick leave within 2 years after childbirth (interaction OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.04), and model fit was not improved.

Age and employment characteristics were also associated with sustained participation. Increasing maternal age was positively associated with participation (adjusted OR per year 1.01, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.01). After adjustment, residential area was not associated with participation. Compared with commercial services and retail, higher odds were observed for women in public and social services, whereas lower odds were observed for women in public and social services, while lower odds were observed for women in infrastructure and logistics and in agriculture, finance and other sectors.

Contract type and baseline working hours were strongly associated with participation. Women with a permanent contract had lower odds of sustained participation than those with a flexible contract (adjusted OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.67), while higher working hours were positively associated (adjusted OR per hour 1.004, 95% CI 1.004 to 1.004).

Women with sick leave during pregnancy had substantially higher odds of receiving sick leave benefits after childbirth than women without sick leave during pregnancy (adjusted OR 3.73, 95% CI 3.61 to 3.85, p<0.001).

Several employment characteristics were also associated with sick leave after childbirth. Increasing age was modestly associated with lower odds (adjusted OR per year 0.99, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.00), while women living in urbanised areas had higher odds than those in non-urbanised areas (adjusted OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.30). Compared with commercial services and retail, higher odds were observed for women working in the infrastructure and logistics sector, whereas lower odds were observed for women in public and social services. Women with a permanent contract had lower odds of sick leave after childbirth compared with those with a flexible contract (adjusted OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.90). Baseline working hours were not meaningfully associated with the outcome.

GLMs examined the total length of sick leave within 2 years after childbirth (online supplemental table S1). Employment sector and contract type were significantly associated with the accumulated number of sick leave days, although the explained variance was modest (adjusted R²=0.03). The longest adjusted mean lengths were observed in the infrastructure and logistics and commercial services and retail, while the shortest were observed in public and social services. Women with permanent contracts experienced longer sick leave lengths than women with flexible or no contracts. Sick leave during pregnancy was not significantly associated with total length of sick leave after childbirth.

Research question 3

What biopsychosocial factors are associated with the total length of sick leave during and within 2 years after childbirth among women who received sick leave benefits?

GLMs examined biopsychosocial and employment-related factors associated with the total length of sick leave during pregnancy and within 2 years after childbirth among women who received sick leave benefits.

For sick leave during pregnancy, employment sector, age, residential area and contract type were all significantly associated with accumulated sick leave length, although the explained variance was limited (adjusted R²=0.015). Women with permanent contracts and those residing in urbanised areas had longer sick leave length than those with flexible contracts or living in less urbanised areas. The association with age was statistically significant but small, indicating slightly longer sick leave with increasing maternal age.

Adjusted estimated marginal means showed the longest adjusted sick leave length during pregnancy in infrastructure and logistics (mean=71.7 days, 95% CI 62.2 to 81.2), followed by industry and manufacturing and commercial services and retail, while the shortest lengths were observed in agriculture, finance and other and public and social services (online supplemental figure 2). These estimates represent adjusted mean numbers of sick leave days among women who received sick leave benefits during pregnancy; corresponding crude estimates are provided in online supplemental figure 4.

For sick leave within 2 years after childbirth, employment sector, residential area, contract type and age were again significantly associated with accumulated sick leave length, with modest explained variance (adjusted R2=0.022). The longest adjusted sick leave lengths were observed in infrastructure and logistics (mean=387.4 days, 95% CI 346.1 to 428.7) and in commercial services and retail, while the shortest lengths were observed in public and social services (figure 1). Employment sector was associated with sustained participation. Compared with women in commercial services and retail, higher odds were observed for women working in public and social services (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.33) and infrastructure and logistics (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.69). Discussion

Principal findings

This study showed that sick leave during pregnancy and within 2 years after childbirth were differently associated with women’s future labour force participation. Sick leave after childbirth was associated with lower participation, whereas sick leave during pregnancy was not. Sick leave during pregnancy was also associated with a higher likelihood of sick leave after childbirth. Sick leave length varied by employment sector, residential area and contract type, with longer absence among women with permanent contracts and in urbanised regions. Participation was mainly associated with employment characteristics, particularly contract type, sector and working hours.

Interpretation of results

Sick leave during pregnancy and within 2 years after childbirth likely reflect different underlying processes rather than a single causal pathway.15 Sick leave during pregnancy may indicate employment security and supportive working conditions that allow temporary withdrawal without lasting consequences for labour market attachment.11 In contrast, sick leave after childbirth may reflect persistent health problems or insufficient workplace accommodations and was associated with reduced participation. Structural factors, including childcare availability and costs, may further influence women’s ability to maintain employment.4 5 16 17 The association of sick leave during pregnancy with both higher participation and a higher likelihood of sick leave after childbirth may reflect shared underlying vulnerabilities rather than a direct causal sequence.15 These findings highlight the importance of timing, as sick leave during pregnancy and after childbirth represent distinct signals with different implications for future labour market attachment.

Comparison with literature

Dutch research reports that sick leave during pregnancy is common, with 51% of women reporting >2 weeks of absence and 47% exceeding 12 weeks.14 Across Europe, prevalence rates vary widely from one-third of pregnant women in Sweden to over two-thirds in Poland, with pregnancy complications and musculoskeletal complaints frequently reported.13 A questionnaire-based study among employed pregnant women similarly found that 56% experienced sick leave during the first 32 weeks of gestation, with fewer than 10% attributing absence primarily to work-related conditions, suggesting that pregnancy-related health complaints rather than occupational exposures alone underlie much of the observed sickness absence.18

Systematic reviews further indicate that sick leave during pregnancy reflects a multifactorial phenomenon shaped by health-related complaints and work-related conditions.9 Comparative research from Sweden and Norway has likewise shown that sickness absence during pregnancy does not covary in a simple way with health status or working conditions, highlighting the importance of institutional context and social norms in shaping sick leave behaviour.19 In this context, the association observed in our study between sick leave during pregnancy and sustained labour force participation may reflect employment environments in which permanent contracts and workplace accommodations allow temporary withdrawal from work without jeopardising long-term labour market attachment. In contrast, sick leave within 2 years after childbirth may more often coincide with persistent health problems or less supportive workplaces and was associated with reduced participation.

Our finding that permanent contracts were associated with higher odds of sustained participation aligns with previous research showing that employment security and paid maternity leave facilitate continued labour market attachment after childbirth.20 Women with fewer working hours before childbirth were more vulnerable to labour market disengagement, consistent with research linking shift and night work to have adverse pregnancy outcomes and reduced employment after childbirth.21 Although earlier studies reported higher rates of early return to work among women of advanced age, maternal age was not a strong predictor of sustained participation in our study, likely reflecting differences in outcome definitions and follow-up duration.14 22

Finally, longer sick leave among women employed in physically demanding sectors is consistent with earlier studies, linking physically strenuous work to fatigue and musculoskeletal complaints during pregnancy.14 Register-based studies from Sweden similarly demonstrate clear occupational gradients in sickness absence around childbirth, with the largest differences observed in the period before childbirth.23 While most existing studies have focused on short-term outcomes, our findings extend this literature by linking occupational differences in sick leave to future labour market participation.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. The use of a large nationwide administrative dataset (over 260 000 women) provided substantial statistical power and enabled analyses across employment sectors, contract types and residential areas in the Netherlands. Objective administrative records reduced recall bias and ensured consistent measurement of benefit receipt, employment status and working hours over a prolonged observation period. Distinguishing sick leave during pregnancy from sick leave within 2 years after childbirth allowed examination within their distinct legal and occupational contexts.

Several limitations should be considered. First, sick leave was classified based on benefit receipt and timing rather than diagnosis, as diagnostic information was not available in a standardised, analysable format. Sick leave should therefore be interpreted as a composite indicator reflecting health needs, working conditions and sick leave behaviour rather than morbidity alone. Second, information on subsequent pregnancies was unavailable, and labour force participation may have been underestimated among women with short pregnancy intervals. Previous register-based research has shown that patterns of sick leave absence after childbirth are highly sensitive to selection mechanisms, particularly subsequent pregnancies, highlighting the difficulty of attributing post-childbirth sickness absence causally to motherhood alone.24 Third, the data lacked precise timing of individual sick leave spells, precluding time-to-event analyses and limiting assessment of sequencing between sick leave and return to work. Sick leave was operationalised as accumulated length, which may not reflect complete absence due to partial benefits. Fourth, the study population was restricted to women eligible for employee-based maternity and sick leave benefits, limiting generalisability to those outside the formal labour market. Finally, information on death or emigration during the observation was unavailable and could not be treated as competing risks. As an observational study, causal interpretations cannot be made, and unmeasured confounding (including previous work history, pre-existing health conditions, socio-economic circumstances and workplace characteristics) may partly explain the observed associations.

Implications for research and practice

Our study highlights the potential value of early identification and support for women at risk of prolonged sick leave or reduced labour force participation after childbirth. In clinical and occupational health practice, more standardised documentation of health complaints during pregnancy and after childbirth, particularly within sick-leave benefit assessments, could help identify women who may benefit from additional support. Strategies may include workplace adjustments, temporary task modification, timely referral to occupational doctors or physiotherapy and improved coordination between medical and occupational care providers. As sick leave may reflect both health-related needs and employment context, interventions should address medical recovery and psychosocial and workplace factors influencing labour market attachment. At policy and organisational level, employment security, contract stability and workplace flexibility appear to be associated with more sustained participation. Employers providing adaptable work arrangements and structured reintegration pathways may help reduce the risk of future disengagement. Future research should link administrative and clinical data to better understand health trajectories around childbirth and incorporate precise timing of sick leave spells and return to work.

Conclusion

Sick leave within 2 years after childbirth was associated with reduced sustained labour force participation, whereas sick leave during pregnancy was not negatively associated with sustained participation and may reflect more secure or supportive employment conditions. Employment characteristics, particularly contract type, sector and working hours, were consistently related to both sick leave patterns and sustained labour force participation. These findings suggest that sick leave during pregnancy and after childbirth represent distinct processes with different implications for women’s sustained labour market attachment, highlighting the importance of employment context and workplace conditions.
