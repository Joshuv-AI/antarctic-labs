Postmenopausal ovarian torsion with intratumoral gas and markedly elevated CA-125/CA 19-9: A case report
Authors: Ferehiwot Bekele Getaneh, Kenneth B Ruzindana, Emile Sebera, Joseph Musabyimana, Cherise Gahizi, Felix Manirakiza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdnexal masses in postmenopausal women represent a significant clinical challenge. This case report describes the clinical presentation, diagnostic evaluation, and surgical management of a large adnexal mass complicated by ovarian torsion in a postmenopausal woman. A 65-year-old postmenopausal woman with long-standing abdominal distention presented with acute pelvic pain, early satiety, and constipation. Computed tomography revealed a 20.6 × 16.5 cm torsed adnexal mass with a twisted vascular pedicle, intratumoral hemorrhage, intratumoral gas and peritumoral fluid. Mass shows no enhancement in post contrast images. Serum CA 125 and CA 19-9 were markedly elevated. Exploratory laparotomy revealed a twisted left ovarian mass with ischemic changes. Total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH+BSO) were performed. Following surgery, she had uneventful recovery and reported symptom resolution by day three. Gross pathology showed no features of malignancy. This case highlights that large adnexal masses in postmenopausal women require urgent evaluation. Although elevated tumor markers may mimic malignancy, torsion and benign pathology can present similarly. Final diagnosis was torsion of a benign ovarian mass with ischemic infarction. Early surgical intervention is essential to avoid such complications.Keywords: CA 125, CA 19-9, Intratumoral gas, Postmenopausal adnexal mass, Ovarian neoplasm, Ovarian torsion

Introduction

Adnexal masses in postmenopausal women represent a significant diagnostic and management challenge, with reported malignancy rates between 13% and 45% depending on imaging and population studies [1]. Ovarian torsion, although less common in postmenopausal women, accounts for 2%-15% of gynecologic emergencies [2]. Large ovarian masses exceeding 5 cm predispose to torsion due to altered adnexal mobility [[3], [4], [5]]. Serum biomarkers, particularly CA 125 and CA 19-9, are widely used but can be elevated in both malignant and benign conditions, including mucinous cystadenomas and torsion [6,7]. Imaging findings may overlap with malignant features, complicating diagnosis [8,9]. Prompt surgical intervention remains the gold standard to confirm diagnosis and prevent complications [10]. We present a rare case of a postmenopausal woman with a long-standing adnexal mass complicated by torsion, intratumoral gas, and elevated CA 125 and CA 19-9, mimicking malignancy.

Case report

A 65-year-old G5P5 postmenopausal woman with hypertension presented with several years of abdominal distension, worsening pelvic pain for 1-week, early satiety, and constipation. Abdominal examination revealed a large, firm, globular mass extending to the epigastrium, approximating a 32-week uterine fundal height. The mass was firm, nontender, and dull to percussion. Pelvic examination showed an atrophic cervix displaced anteriorly with an indistinct uterus. No ascites, nodularity, or systemic signs of malignancy were found. Serum CA 125 was 209.4 U/mL (normal <35) and CA 19-9 was 402.0 U/mL (normal <37). CEA and alpha-fetoprotein were normal.

Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis was performed, including noncontrast and portal venous phase imaging, with multiplanar reformations (axial, coronal, and sagittal). Images were reconstructed with thin slices to optimize evaluation of adnexal structures. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a 20.6 × 16.5 cm nonenhancing adnexal mass composed of intratumoral hemorrhage, gas, calcifications, and a twisted vascular pedicle consistent with ovarian torsion (Fig. 1). The twisted vascular pedicle (whirlpool sign) was best appreciated on coronal reformats, with supportive visualization on axial images. Peritumoral fluid and uterine displacement were present. No peritoneal implants, ascites, or lymphadenopathy were seen. There was no macroscopic fat to suggest dermoid cyst. No perilesional fat stranding, or adjacent bowel inflammatory process was demonstrated.

Adnexal torsion of a left ovarian mass. (A) Axial noncontrast CT shows intratumoral hemorrhage (asterisk). (B) Axial and (C) coronal contrast-enhanced CT images of the abdomen and pelvis show left ovarian mass migrated superiorly and to the right side with twisted, elongated, and congested pedicle (red arrows) and surrounding free fluid (arrowhead in B), suggesting adnexal torsion. Mass composes pockets of intratumoral gas, suggesting infarction (white circles).

Exploratory laparotomy revealed a large torsed left ovarian mass with 3-4 twists, smooth capsule, and hemorrhagic discoloration (Fig. 2). No ascites, peritoneal deposits, or papillary projections were noted. A TAH+BSO was performed.

Intraoperative image of the torsed ovarian mass. The serosal surface appeared dusky, violaceous to black, with patchy areas of deep red discoloration, consistent with hemorrhagic infarction and venous congestion. Focal surface ecchymoses and subserosal hemorrhagic suffusions are evident. The pedicle appeared thickened and congested, compatible with torsion-induced vascular compromise.

Gross pathology showed a tense encapsulated mass weighing 3260 g, measuring 27 × 21 × 11 cm and brown to chocolate in color. It had a firm to hard consistency, an intact capsule and a nodulated outer surface. On serial cut sections, the mass appeared predominantly solid and variegated color (from gray to brown) with scattered areas of calcification and necrosis without papillary excrescences, suggesting benign tumor. Histopathology confirmed a benign Brenner tumor complicated by torsion and hemorrhagic infarction (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Ovarian torsion in postmenopausal women is rare, usually associated with adnexal masses [[2], [3], [4]]. Tumor markers CA 125 and CA 19-9, while useful in malignancy evaluation, may be elevated in benign epithelial tumors and torsion [6,11,12]. In this patient, radiologic findings of a twisted pedicle and hemorrhage were strongly suggestive of torsion, while elevated tumor markers raised suspicion for malignancy. However, the absence of papillary projections, ascites, and peritoneal implants lowered the intraoperative likelihood of cancer. Elevated CA 19-9 (>400 U/mL) is unusual but has been reported in benign ovarian tumors [13]. Air pockets within the mass were atypical and may represent ischemic necrosis [14]. The patient had no clinical signs of infection, including absence of fever or systemic toxicity. Laboratory evaluation showed a normal white blood cell count; CRP was not performed. There was no clinical suspicion of bacteremia or sepsis. In addition, no adjacent bowel wall thickening, discontinuity, or fistulous tract was identified on CT or at surgical exploration. Given these findings, along with histopathologic confirmation of hemorrhagic infarction, ischemic necrosis was favored as the etiology of intratumoral gas over gas-forming infection or enteric communication) which in this case was confirmed on histopathological examination. This case underscores the limitations of relying solely on tumor markers and imaging to differentiate benign from malignant adnexal pathologies. Therefore, a definitive diagnosis using histopathology is of paramount importance.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic complexities of adnexal masses in postmenopausal women. Elevated tumor markers and radiologic findings mimicking malignancy ultimately corresponded to torsion of a benign ovarian tumor. Early recognition and prompt surgical intervention remain critical to avoid irreversible ischemic damage and ensure optimal outcomes.
