Fecal calprotectin as a biomarker for the diagnosis of gastrointestinal graft-versus-host disease following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: C Piñero-Pérez, A Velasco-Guardado, E Pérez-López, M Cortés-Rodríguez, M Cabrero-Calvo, AA Martín-López, F Sánchez-Guijo, L López-Corral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal graft-versus-host disease (GI-GVHD) is a major complication after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), often requiring invasive endoscopy for diagnosis. Fecal calprotectin (FC) offers a non-invasive marker of intestinal inflammation, but its utility in GI-GVHD needs clarification. In this prospective observational study of 165 adult allo-HSCT recipients, FC was measured on days +7, +14, and +21 post-transplant, at GI-GVHD onset, and 7 days post-treatment, alongside clinical, endoscopic, and histological data. GI-GVHD developed in 52.7% of patients, with histological confirmation in 90.3% of cases. FC lacked predictive value on day +7 (AUC = 0.50) but showed moderate-to-high accuracy on days +14 (AUC = 0.69) and +21 (AUC = 0.77), with an optimal day +21 cutoff of 52.5 µg/g (sensitivity 75%, specificity 87%). At diagnosis, median FC was 120 µg/g, correlating with endoscopic severity (r = 0.31; p = 0.02) but not clinical or histological grades. FC declined significantly post-treatment (120 to 51.5 µg/g; p = 0.04), though concurrent infections elevated levels without compromising discriminative ability. FC serves as a dynamic biomarker for predicting, diagnosing, and monitoring GI-GVHD, but requires integrated clinical interpretation due to limited specificity amid other inflammations. Subject terms: Haematological cancer, Stem-cell therapies

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is a curative procedure for a broad spectrum of diseases, primarily hematological malignancies [1]. Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is an immunological complication mediated by donor T lymphocytes attacking the recipient’s tissues. GVHD remains a major barrier to the success of HSCT [2].

Acute GVHD frequently involves the gastrointestinal tract and diagnosis relies on clinical manifestations and histological confirmation by endoscopic biopsy [3, 4]. However, endoscopy may be delayed or contraindicated due to thrombocytopenia, patient instability, or the need to exclude infectious causes. These limitations highlight the need for reliable non-invasive biomarkers for early detection and monitoring of gastrointestinal GVHD (GI-GVHD) after allo-HSCT [5].

Calprotectin is an antimicrobial protein of the S100 family and is released in large quantities by activated neutrophils. In conditions of intestinal inflammation, this protein migrates into the intestinal lumen and can be measured non-invasively in fecal samples [6]. In the clinical context, its utility has been validated in inflammatory bowel diseases, where elevated fecal calprotectin (FC) levels correlate with disease activity, risk of relapse, and treatment response [7]. Moreover, FC has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker for GI-GVHD, with several studies reporting significantly elevated levels in patients with GI involvement compared to those without GVHD, as well as a correlation with clinical, endoscopic, histologic severity and response to treatment [8–11]. Some studies suggest that FC may help distinguish GVHD from infectious colitis [12, 13].

Studies exploring the association between FC and GVHD face several methodological constraints. Small sample sizes, ranging from 19 to 54 patients in key studies and retrospective analysis limit statistical power and generalizability [8, 9, 11–14]. There is also a lack of standardized cut-off values, variability in timing of FC measurements and contradictory results [10, 15]. These limitations underscore the importance and relevance of our study, which aims to provide evidence on the clinical utility of FC in the diagnosis and monitoring of GI-GVHD in patients undergoing allo-HSCT.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective, descriptive study that included all consecutive adult patients undergoing allo-HSCT at the University Hospital of Salamanca from December 2019 to December 2022. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee, and all patients provided written informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. As a non-interventional protocol, no invasive procedures were performed beyond those required by the clinical team as part of routine care. As this was a prospective observational study including all consecutive allo-HSCT recipients during the study period, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. Given the limited and heterogeneous data available from previous studies, a consecutive inclusion design was used to maximize external validity.

Study endpoints and definitions

The primary endpoint was to evaluate FC as a predictive biomarker for the development of GI-GVHD. Secondary endpoints included assessing the relationship between FC levels and severity of GI-GVHD, endoscopic and histological findings, and response to treatment.

Clinical variables related to patient (age, sex), transplant (indication for transplant, donor type, HLA matching, conditioning regimen, transplant date, GVHD prophylaxis), clinical outcomes (mucositis, infections, mortality), and GVHD-related events (GI and non-GI acute GVHD, symptoms, endoscopic findings, histopathologic features, and GVHD relapse) were collected. Conditioning regimens were classified as myeloablative (MAC), reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC), or sequential regimens according to standard transplant definitions. Sequential regimens consisted of cytoreductive chemotherapy followed by reduced-intensity conditioning prior to allo-HSCT. In most patients, the graft source consisted of peripheral blood stem cells.

The Disease Risk Index (DRI) and Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation-Comorbidity Index (HCT-CI) were used to stratify patient risk before allo-HSCT [16, 17]. Mucositis was classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, acute GvHD was diagnosed and scored according to Mount Sinai Acute GvHD International Consortium (MAGIC) criteria [18], endoscopic findings were graded according to Cruz-Correa et al. [19], and histological findings were evaluated using the Washington et al. classification (modified Lerner classification) [4].

Serial FC measurements were obtained on days 7, 14, and 21 after allo-HSCT. An additional FC measurement was conducted on the day of GI-GVHD diagnosis and 7 days after initiation of treatment. GI-GVHD was suspected in any patient who, following allo-HSCT, presented with anorexia, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting (upper GI symptoms), or diarrhea (lower GI symptoms). Microbiological evaluation included stool cultures for bacteria and parasites, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests for adenovirus, rotavirus, and Clostridioides difficile toxins.

Gastrointestinal endoscopies for histological confirmation of GI-GVHD were performed at the discretion of the clinical team, provided there were no contraindications to the procedure and with the patient’s consent.

Fecal calprotectin analysis

FC samples were collected in sterile plastic containers and delivered to the Laboratory within 48 h, where they were stored at -20°C until analysis. FC concentration was determined using the DiaSorin LIAISON® XL analyzer, which employs a chemiluminescent immunoassay method with a paramagnetic microparticle solid phase (CLIA), employing a monoclonal antibody against human calprotectin. All samples were processed and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies (percentages), while quantitative variables were described using measures of central tendency and dispersion. Normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Comparisons between dichotomous variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons involving continuous and categorical variables. Correlations between quantitative variables were assessed with the Pearson´s correlation coefficient.

The cumulative incidence of GI-GVHD was estimated using competing risks analysis according to the Fine and Gray model, and odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI were derived from logistic regression models.

For analysis of FC, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were employed. Sensitivity and specificity values were calculated, and the optimal diagnostic cut-off points were determined. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method.

A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all confidence intervals were calculated at the 95% level. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 165 patients undergoing allo-HSCT at the University Hospital of Salamanca were included in the study. Median age was 55 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 45–63) and 52.1% (n = 86) were male. The median follow-up duration for surviving patients at the time of analysis was 320 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 176-525). Acute GI-GVHD of any grade occurred in 87 (52.7%) patients at a median time of 25 days (IQR, 19-51). The cumulative incidence of GI-GVHD at day +100 was 52% (95% CI, 0.45-0.60). Among the 87 patients who developed GI-GVHD, 55.8% (n = 48) presented with upper GI symptoms, 25.6% (n = 22) with lower gastrointestinal symptoms, and 18.6% (n = 17) experienced both upper and lower gastrointestinal symptoms. All patients diagnosed with GI-GVHD, except for three (3.4%), underwent endoscopic evaluation. Gastroscopy was performed in 71 patients (43%), colonoscopy in 10 patients (6.1%), and both procedures in 3 patients (1.8%). The mucosal findings on gastroscopy were described as normal in 42.2% (n = 35) of patients, grade I-II (erythema, edema) 41% (n = 34), grade III (erosions) in 2.4% (n = 2) and grade IV (ulcers) in 14.5% (n = 12). Among the 83 patients who underwent endoscopy, histological confirmation of GVHD was obtained in 75 cases (90.3%). The other 8 patients presented nonspecific histological findings. Regarding the histological grading of GVHD, 65.3% (n = 56) of patients were classified as grade I, 16% (n = 14) as grade II, 6.7% (n = 6) as grade III, and 12% (n = 11) as grade IV.

During follow-up, 39 patients (23.6%) died. The estimated mean overall survival was 609.5 days (95% CI: 563.3–655.8). The median overall survival was not achieved during the study period. Kaplan–Meier survival curves for the cohort are shown in Fig. 1.

Value of fecal calprotectin in estimating the risk of developing gastrointestinal GvHD

The median FC levels in patients post-allo-HSCT were 15 µg/g on day 7 (IQR: 7–26), 29 µg/g on day 14 (IQR: 12-48), and 50 µg/g on day 21 (IQR: 28-100).

Sixty-three patients (38.2%) developed mucositis after conditioning. Symptoms attributable to mucositis were not systematically recorded, and the presence of diarrhea in this subgroup could not be determined. Median FC levels were 20 µg/g (IQR: 10–43) on day +7, 33 µg/g (IQR: 14.7–50) on day +14, and 60 µg/g (IQR: 39–124) on day +21. Among these patients, FC levels were similar on day +7 in those who later developed GI-GVHD (20 vs. 20 µg/g; p = 0.78), but higher on day +14 (45 vs. 33 µg/g; p = 0.006) and day +21 (99 vs. 60 µg/g; p = 0.015).

Patients who developed GI-GVHD had a median FC value on day 7 of 11 µg/g (IQR: 5-27), compared to 10 µg/g (IQR: 5-20) in those who did not develop GVHD (p = 0.94). However, significant differences were observed on day 14 (41.5 µg/g [IQR: 13-59] vs. 25 µg/g [IQR: 10-38]) and on day 21 (79 µg/g [IQR: 35-175] vs. 31 µg/g [IQR: 18-42]), both with p-values < 0.001.

ROC curves were generated for days 7, 14, and 21 post-transplant, and optimal cut-off values were established based on the highest combined sensitivity (S) and specificity (Sp) (Fig. 2). The ROC curve for FC on day 7 yielded an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.5, indicating no discriminative value. On day 14, the AUC increased to 0.69, achieving statistical significance (p < 0.001; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.59–0.78). The optimal cut-off value on day 14 was 39 µg/g (S = 52%, Sp=84%). FC levels above 50 µg/g achieved a specificity of 90% but a lower sensitivity (37%). The highest discriminative AUC was observed on day 21, with an AUC of 0.77 (p < 0.001; 95% CI: 0.65–0.89). The optimal FC value on this day was 52.5 µg/g (S = 75%, Sp=87%).

When stratified by gastrointestinal presentation, patients who later developed only upper GI symptoms had median FC levels of 9.5 µg/g (IQR: 5–23.7), 29 µg/g (IQR: 14.7–51), and 106.2 µg/g (IQR: 33.5–111) on days +7, +14, and +21, respectively. Those with lower GI symptoms had median values of 17.5 µg/g (IQR: 8.2–35.5), 43 µg/g (IQR: 20–65), and 98 µg/g (IQR: 55–290), without statistically significant differences.

Diagnostic role of fecal calprotectin in acute gastrointestinal GVHD

FC and differential diagnosis of GI-GVHD

The median FC level at the time of diagnosis of GI-GVHD was 120 µg/g (IQR: 70-288). Most patients who developed gastrointestinal infection, TMA, or severe mucositis were also diagnosed with acute gastrointestinal GVHD. Only one patient without GVHD developed a gastrointestinal infection, one developed TMA, and two developed severe mucositis. None of them presented with pseudomembranous colitis. The development of GI-GVHD was significantly associated with a higher diagnosis of gastrointestinal infection (13.8% vs. 1.3%; p = 0.003) (OR = 12.3; 95% CI: 1.56–97.1). This association was not observed with the development of TMA (8% vs. 1.3%; p = 0.067), severe mucositis (4.6% vs. 2.6%; p = 0.6), or pseudomembranous colitis (4.6% vs. 0%; p = 0.12). There were no statistical differences between patients GI-GVHD patients and GVHD with concurrent gastrointestinal infections (120 µg/g vs. 150 µg/g; p = 0.86); TMA (120 µg/g vs. 142.5 µg/g; p = 0.84); severe mucositis (120 µg/g vs. 100 µg/g; p = 0.97) or pseudomembranous colitis (120 µg/g vs. 600 µg/g; p = 0.15).

Fecal calprotectin and response to treatment

Of the 87 patients who developed acute GVHD with gastrointestinal involvement, 71.1% (n = 59) received topical corticosteroids as first-line treatment, while 26.5% (n = 22) were treated with systemic corticosteroids. Two patients required a second line of treatment (2.4%). The overall response rate at day +28 was 97.5%. At the time of acute GI-GVHD diagnosis, the median FC level was 120 µg/g (IQR: 70–288). A significant decrease was observed 7 days after initiation of treatment, with median FC levels of 51.5 µg/g (IQR: 6.5–125) (p = 0.04).

Discussion

Fecal calprotectin as a biomarker in gastrointestinal Graft-versus-Host Disease

Given that GI-GVHD is characterized by mucosal inflammation, it has been hypothesized that FC could play a role in its diagnosis and monitoring. Metafuni et al. [11] concluded that fecal, but not serum, calprotectin was significantly elevated in patients with GI-GVHD. Similarly, Adam et al. [12] demonstrated that FC correlates not only with the presence of GI-GVHD but also with the severity of endoscopic and histological findings. However, the clinical utility of FC in GI-GVHD remains controversial. While several studies support its role as a non-invasive marker of intestinal inflammation, others have failed to confirm its diagnostic or prognostic value [15, 20]. These discrepancies may stem from small cohorts, heterogeneous definitions of GI involvement, and variability in FC assay methods. Moreover, FC lacks disease specificity, as elevated levels can also occur in infections or drug-induced mucosal injury.

Predictive capacity of fecal calprotectin

One of the most promising clinical applications of FC in the context of allo-HSCT is its ability to predict the development of GI-GVHD before the onset of overt clinical symptoms. Studies such as August et al. [21] have shown that low FC levels in the early post-transplant period (days +5 and +10) are significantly associated with an increased risk of developing GI-GVHD. In their pediatric cohort, patients with FC levels below 424 mg/kg on day +10 had a GI-GVHD incidence of 77.8%, compared to 0% in those with levels above this threshold (p = 0.002). Conversely, Metafuni et al. [11] reported that elevated FC levels ( > 500 µg/g) were associated with a higher risk and severity of GI-GVHD. In the study by Gurer et al. the expression of the S100A8 and S100A9 genes (which encode the FC) was analyzed in 579 intestinal biopsies from patients who had undergone allo-HSCT. A significant association was found between calprotectin expression and the presence of acute GI-GVHD. Neutrophil infiltration in gastrointestinal tissue also correlated with GVHD severity (p < 0.001), and differences were observed based on the location of the obtained intestinal tissue. The study demonstrates that tissue calprotectin is an independent marker of mortality risk [22].

Our results support this dynamic evolution. Although FC showed no discriminative capacity on day 7 post-transplant, it demonstrated moderate predictive ability on day 14, and reached optimal performance on day 21 (AUC = 0.77), with a sensitivity of 75% and specificity of 87% at a cutoff of 52.5 µg/g. These data suggest that the predictive utility of FC is highly dependent on the timing of measurement. Moreover, the findings indicate that FC may have potential as a screening tool between days 14 and 21 post-HSCT to identify patients at increased risk of developing GI-GVHD, potentially allowing for earlier preventive interventions.

Diagnostic utility and differential diagnosis

A major clinical challenge is the differential diagnosis between GI-GVHD and other causes of post-transplant diarrhea, such as gastrointestinal infections, pseudomembranous colitis, severe mucositis, or MAT. Chiusolo et al. [13] showed that FC levels were significantly lower in patients with infectious colitis compared to those with GI-GVHD. Adam et al. [12] reported similar findings in patients with cytomegalovirus colitis. However, Broglie et al. [15] found no statistically significant differences between groups, highlighting the heterogeneity in the literature and the potential influence of confounding factors.

In our study, the highest FC levels were observed in patients with pseudomembranous colitis (mean=1317.5 µg/g). Additionally, patients with concurrent viral or bacterial infections exhibited higher FC levels than those without infections (1085.55 vs. 289.6 µg/g), underscoring that inflammatory processes unrelated to GVHD can also contribute to FC elevation. A key limitation of our analysis is the very low number of patients who developed gastrointestinal complications in the absence of GI-GVHD. Consequently, we compared patients with isolated GI-GVHD to those with concurrent GI-GVHD and additional complications. In this setting, FC levels did not significantly discriminate between groups. These results illustrate the restricted specificity of FC in the post-transplant context, where overlapping inflammatory and infectious processes are common. FC should not be used in isolation. Interpretation of FC values requires an integrated diagnostic approach, incorporating clinical assessment, other complementary biomarkers (REG3α or ST2), microbiological studies, and, when necessary, endoscopic and histopathological confirmation.

Relationship with symptoms, clinical severity, endoscopic and histological features

The clinical presentation of GI-GVHD is heterogeneous, encompassing upper tract symptoms (nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain) and lower tract symptoms (diarrhea, lower abdominal pain, hematochezia). Several studies have explored whether FC levels vary according to the type and intensity of symptoms to assess the localization and severity of the inflammatory process [22].

Our results showed that patients with lower gastrointestinal symptoms showed higher FC levels than those with isolated upper symptoms, likely reflecting the predominant colonic origin of fecal calprotectin. In addition, when FC values measured during the early post-transplant period were stratified according to the subsequent type of gastrointestinal presentation, patients who later developed lower gastrointestinal symptoms tended to show higher FC levels than those with upper symptoms, although these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Regarding the clinical severity of GI-GVHD, assessed using the MAGIC criteria, a general trend of increasing FC levels with higher disease grades was observed (from 260.3 µg/g in grade 1 to 1097.33 µg/g in grade 4). However, this progression was not linear, with a notable decrease in grade 3 (313.3 µg/g), likely attributable to the small sample size or confounding factors such as concurrent infections. The statistical correlation was weak (r = 0.15, p = 0.28). Other studies have reported stronger associations. Metafuni et al. [11] found significantly higher FC levels in patients with grades 3–4 GI-GVHD, while Lorenz et al. [8] confirmed a progressive correlation between FC levels and the endoscopic and histological severity of the disease. In this context, we found a moderate and statistically significant correlation between FC levels and endoscopic severity (Pearson’s r = 0.31; p = 0.02).

Limitations

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design, the largest sample size to date, and the inclusion of endoscopic evaluation in most patients. Nonetheless, our study has several limitations. First, although the overall cohort size was large, subgroup analyses (particularly those stratified by GI-GVHD grade or by the etiology of non-GVHD diarrhea) remained underpowered to detect significant differences or correlations. Second, the presence of concurrent infections or other inflammatory GI conditions may have confounded FC levels, reducing their specificity. Additionally, the use of different immunosuppressive regimens and conditioning protocols was not controlled for, potentially influencing mucosal injury and FC expression. Several transplant-related factors such as conditioning regimen, stem cell source, GVHD prophylaxis, mucositis, and gastrointestinal infections may influence fecal calprotectin levels after allo-HSCT. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as non-adjusted relationships reflecting diagnostic discrimination rather than evidence of an independent effect of the biomarker. Because of the number of outcome events relative to the number of clinically relevant covariates, a stable multivariable model was not feasible without a substantial risk of overfitting. Consequently, the analyses were intentionally restricted to unadjusted and discriminative approaches, and the results should be considered exploratory rather than causal. Finally, while endoscopic confirmation was available in many cases, it was not universal, limiting the ability to correlate FC levels consistently with histological severity. In addition, the single-center nature of the study underscores the need for external validation in independent cohorts to confirm the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusions

FC may serve as a dynamic, non-invasive biomarker for predicting, diagnosing, and monitoring GI-GVHD after allo-HSCT. However, its specificity is limited in the presence of other inflammatory or infectious conditions, and results should be interpreted within the broader clinical context.
