Decisional conflict levels and associated factors among parents of pediatric patients undergoing emergency gastric lavage: a cross‑sectional survey
Authors: Ying Meng, Ran Li, Hongzhi Zhou, Dan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDecisional conflict—defined as a state of uncertainty and hesitation regarding the choice among alternative actions—can adversely affect decision quality, parental psychological well-being, and subsequent treatment adherence. While decisional conflict has been studied in elective and chronic care contexts, little is known about its magnitude and determinants in acute pediatric emergency procedures such as gastric lavage, where parents must make high-stakes decisions under extreme time pressure. This study aimed to assess the level of decisional conflict and identify its associated factors among parents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 266 parents of children who underwent emergency gastric lavage at a single tertiary children’s hospital between January 2025 and February 2026. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling. Data were collected with a self-developed, 20-item decisional conflict instrument designed for the emergency lavage context. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.902) and content validity [scale-level content validity index (CVI) =0.91]. Univariate analysis, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression were performed.ResultsThe mean age of the children was 4.2±2.8 years, and 69.2% of the parent respondents were mothers. The mean total decisional conflict score was 58.12±6.30 (standardized score 72.6±15.8), with 91.4% of parents exceeding the clinical threshold for significant conflict (standardized score ≥37.5). Multiple linear regression revealed that divorced/widowed/unmarried marital status (β=0.197, P<0.001), lower education level (β=−0.186, P=0.001), absence of a medical background (β=0.176, P=0.001), younger child age (β=−0.159, P=0.004), and lower family monthly income per capita (β=−0.155, P=0.009) were independently associated with higher decisional conflict. The model explained 30.9% of the variance. The “uncertainty and pressure” subscale had the highest mean item score (3.25±0.65).ConclusionsParents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage experience a high level of decisional conflict, particularly those who are socioeconomically disadvantaged, lack medical knowledge, or have limited spousal support. Clinicians should implement tiered decision support strategies targeting these high-risk groups, such as assigning dedicated social workers or volunteer companions for non-married parents, providing plain-language illustrated decision aids for parents with lower educational attainment, and proactively addressing financial concerns for low income families.Keywords: Decisional conflict, gastric lavage, emergency, parents, influencing factors

Highlight box

Key findings

• Parental decisional conflict during emergency gastric lavage was found to be at a moderate‑to‑high level. Higher conflict was independently associated with single or non‑intact marital status, lower educational attainment, lack of a medical or health‑related background, younger child age, and lower family income. Among all dimensions, the “Uncertainty and Pressure” component emerged as the most pronounced, indicating that time urgency and emotional distress are the primary drivers of decisional difficulty.

What is known and what is new?

• Decisional conflict is well recognised in acute care settings, but quantitative evidence specifically for paediatric gastric lavage has been lacking.

• This study provides the first focused characterisation of conflict in this context, identifying a distinct set of sociodemographic and clinical determinants, and importantly, demonstrates that affective stress—rather than purely cognitive information gaps—dominates the decision‑making burden.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Emergency departments should implement a stratified support framework that proactively identifies high‑risk parents—particularly single caregivers, those with limited socioeconomic resources or low health literacy, and those with younger children. Practical measures include the use of concise visual decision aids to offset time pressure, alongside structured provider training in empathic, trauma‑informed communication. The overarching goal is to reframe the consent process from a routine informed‑consent checklist into a therapeutic shared‑decision partnership that protects both clinical responsiveness and parental psychological well‑being.

Introduction

In pediatric emergency clinical practice, acute poisoning or accidental ingestion represents a common critical condition. Gastric lavage, as a key intervention for removing unabsorbed toxins from the stomach, often necessitates decision-making within an extremely short timeframe (1). For parents, however, this decision-making process is frequently accompanied by significant psychological stress and cognitive burden. On the one hand, gastric lavage is an invasive procedure, and parents commonly worry about the pain, risk of aspiration, and potential complications it may cause (2). On the other hand, delaying or forgoing lavage may result in further absorption of the toxin, leading to severe organ damage or even life-threatening consequences (3). In an urgent context characterized by incomplete information and time constraints, parents must weigh their options amid fear, uncertainty, and profound concern for their child’s well-being (4). This “dilemma” readily induces decisional conflict—a state of inner hesitation and unease arising from insufficient knowledge about the options, lack of clarity regarding personal values, or inadequate support when facing important medical choices.

A research (5) indicates that decisional conflict not only exacerbates negative emotions such as anxiety and depression in parents, but may also result in delayed decision‑making, misinterpretation of treatment options, distrust toward physicians, and even persistent regret and self-blame after treatment ends. In the specific context of emergency gastric lavage, the impact of decisional conflict is particularly direct and farreaching: if parents hesitate because of such conflict, the optimal window for lavage may be missed; conversely, if they make a hasty decision under high pressure, they may repeatedly question the correctness of their choice afterward, affecting overall satisfaction with the care process and subsequent treatment adherence (6). More importantly, this conflict may also influence the child’s emotional stability during the procedure through parentchild interaction, thereby interfering with the smooth conduct of the procedure (7,8).

Existing evidence on decisional conflict in emergency pediatric settings is scarce. Most studies focus on relatively stable clinical contexts such as chronic disease management, perioperative care, or oncology treatment (9-11). The few studies that have examined surrogate decisionmaking in acute care suggest that time pressure, emotional distress, and the invasive nature of procedures can sharply elevate decisional conflict (12,13). Emergency gastric lavage amplifies these challenges: it requires immediate consent for an invasive procedure with real risks of aspiration or mucosal injury, while the consequences of inaction may be life-threatening. This creates a unique decisional context in which parents must process complex risk-benefit information under conditions of cognitive overload and high emotional arousal—a constellation that is likely to produce substantial decisional conflict. However, empirical data on the magnitude and determinants of such conflict are lacking. We therefore hypothesized that parents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage would exhibit clinically significant levels of decisional conflict and that higher conflict would be associated with lower socioeconomic status, younger child age, absence of medical knowledge, and limited social support. We present this article in accordance with the SURGE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0322/rc).

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to systematically assess the level of decisional conflict among parents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage and to analyze its associated influencing factors. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 202606046-1). All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed of the study’s purpose, content, and confidentiality measures. Participants were free to withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason, and such withdrawal did not affect the medical rights or treatment of their child at the hospital.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using the n=Zα/22⋅σ2E2 for estimating a population mean with a specified precision (14). Based on pilot data from 30 parents, the standard deviation of the decisional conflict score was 6.30. With a desired margin of error of ±1.0 on the raw score scale [20–100] and α=0.05, the minimum required sample was 153. Anticipating approximately 10% invalid responses, we targeted at least 170 participants. Ultimately, 266 valid cases were included, meeting the requirements for statistical analysis.

Participants

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants from the Emergency Department of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between January 2025 and February 2026. Parents of children who underwent gastric lavage were selected as the study population. Inclusion criteria were: (I) the child required gastric lavage due to acute poisoning or accidental ingestion; (II) the parent was the primary decision-maker for the medical treatment and participated in the decision-making process; (III) age ≥18 years; (IV) possessed basic reading comprehension skills to complete the questionnaire independently; and (V) voluntarily agreed to participate and signed the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria were: (I) the parent had severe cognitive impairment or mental illness that prevented effective communication; (II) the child’s condition was critical and required immediate life-saving intervention, making it impossible for the parent to complete the questionnaire after the procedure; (III) the parent was unable to complete data collection due to the child’s death or transfer to another hospital.

Research instruments

Data were collected using a self-developed questionnaire titled “Questionnaire on Decisional Conflict Among Parents of Children Undergoing Emergency Gastric Lavage” (Appendix 1). The questionnaire consisted of two parts.

The first part was a baseline information form designed to collect factors that may influence decisional conflict, including parental sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, relationship to the child, ethnicity, marital status, education level, medical background), child characteristics (age, gender, underlying diseases, type of ingested substance, medical payment method), and family social support factors (family monthly income, presence of other family members accompanying the child, whether the decision was discussed with family members, prior experience with major medical decision-making). Variable selection was guided by the “Ottawa Decision Support Framework”, which posits that decisional conflict is influenced by decision-maker characteristics (e.g., knowledge, expectations), decision context (e.g., urgency, support), and decisional resources (15,16). Variables were chosen a priori to represent these domains.

The second part was the core Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS), which was developed based on a comprehensive review of domestic and international decisional conflict assessment tools and adapted to the clinical context of emergency gastric lavage. It aimed to assess parents’ cognitive and emotional experiences during decision-making. The scale consisted of 20 items across four dimensions: “informed decision-making” (items 1–5), “values clarity” (items 6–10), “decision support” (items 11–15), and “uncertainty and pressure” (items 16–20). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points). Total raw scores ranged from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating higher levels of decisional conflict. To facilitate clinical interpretation, raw scores could be converted to standardized scores on a 0–100 scale, with a standardized score ≥37.5 indicating clinically significant decisional conflict.

Internal consistency reliability was used to assess the reliability of the scale. In this study sample, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.902. For the subscales, Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.831 for the “informed decision-making” dimension, 0.814 for the “values clarity” dimension, 0.852 for the “decision support” dimension, and 0.876 for the “uncertainty and pressure” dimension, all exceeding 0.80, indicating good internal consistency. For content validity, five pediatric emergency medicine experts and three nursing experts were invited to evaluate the items using an expert review method. After two rounds of review, the scale‑level content validity index (CVI) was 0.91, and the item-level CVI (I-CVI) ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, indicating good content validity. For construct validity, exploratory factor analysis was conducted using principal component analysis on the 20 items. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.886, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2=1,724.63, P<0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. Using varimax rotation, four factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, cumulatively accounting for 67.83% of the total variance. All items had factor loadings greater than 0.50, and the factor structure was largely consistent with the prespecified four dimensions, suggesting satisfactory construct validity.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by uniformly trained emergency department nurses or researchers. The collection time point was after the child had undergone gastric lavage and achieved relative clinical stability, either before discharge or transfer to a hospital ward, to capture the parents’ complete experience during the decision-making process. Researchers first explained the study purpose, content, and confidentiality measures to eligible parents, and then administered the questionnaire after obtaining written informed consent. The questionnaire was primarily self-administered; for parents who were unable to complete it independently due to stress, fatigue, or reading difficulties, a researcher read the questions aloud in a neutral tone and recorded the responses, ensuring no guidance or interference in the answers. The questionnaire took approximately 5–8 minutes to complete. After collecting the questionnaire, researchers immediately checked for completeness and, in the case of missing items or logical inconsistencies, promptly verified and corrected the information with the parent.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous data, after testing for normality, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%). In univariate analyses, comparisons between two groups were performed using independent samples t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance. For correlation analyses, Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were used depending on the nature of the variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to identify independent factors associated with decisional conflict. Variables with P<0.10 in the univariate analysis and those considered clinically relevant were entered into the regression model using the enter method. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for all regression coefficients. To provide clinically interpretable information, we additionally report the prevalence of clinically significant decisional conflict (standardized score ≥37.5) by key demographic subgroups with unadjusted odds ratios in Table S1. The significance level was set at α=0.05 (two-tailed), and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 266 parents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage were enrolled in this study. The mean age of the children was 4.2±2.8 years, 57.1% were male, and 69.2% of the parent respondents were mothers. The mean decisional conflict score was 58.12±6.30, indicating a moderate-to-high level of decisional conflict overall. The mean standardized score was 72.6±15.8, and 91.4% (243/266) of parents had standardized scores ≥37.5, indicating clinically significant decisional conflict. As shown in Table 1, univariate analyses revealed that several factors were significantly associated with decisional conflict scores. Parents who were divorced, widowed, or unmarried reported significantly higher decisional conflict scores compared with married parents. Educational level also showed a significant inverse relationship, with parents having a bachelor’s degree or above reporting the lowest conflict scores, followed by those with an associate degree, while parents with a high school education or below reported the highest scores. Parents without a medical background had significantly higher decisional conflict scores than those with a medical background. Child’s age was also significantly associated, with parents of younger children reporting higher conflict scores. Family monthly income demonstrated a graded association, with lower income groups reporting progressively higher decisional conflict scores. In addition, parents who did not discuss the decision with other family members and those without prior experience in major medical decision-making reported significantly higher decisional conflict scores. No significant differences were observed for other variables, including parental age, gender, relationship to the child, ethnicity, child’s gender, underlying disease, type of ingested substance, medical payment method, or presence of other family members accompanying the child.

After adjusting for potential confounders, five variables emerged as independent predictors of decisional conflict. Parents who were divorced, widowed, or unmarried had significantly higher decisional conflict scores compared with married parents. Education level was negatively associated with decisional conflict, indicating that higher educational attainment was associated with lower conflict scores. Parents without a medical background reported significantly higher decisional conflict scores than those with a medical background. Child’s age was also a significant independent predictor, with younger child age associated with higher decisional conflict scores. While consulting with family members and prior experience with major medical decision-making were significant in univariate and correlation analyses, they did not retain statistical significance in the multivariate model after adjusting for other factors. Discussion

This study systematically assessed the level of decisional conflict among 266 parents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage and explored its associated influencing factors. The results showed that the total decisional conflict score was 58.12±6.30, indicating a moderate-to-high level of conflict. This finding suggests that in the unique context of emergency gastric lavage, parents commonly experience significant decisional distress. Drawing on the transactional model of stress and coping (17), this can be understood as a consequence of parents’ primary appraisal of the situation as threatening and their secondary appraisal of having limited coping resources. Under acute time pressure, cognitive overload impairs the systematic processing of risk-benefit information, directly contributing to decisional conflict (6,18). Further analysis identified marital status, educational level, medical background, child’s age, and family monthly income per capita as independent influencing factors of decisional conflict, though our model explained only 30.9% of the variance, indicating that important determinants—such as the quality of clinician-parent communication, waiting times, and emotional support provided by staff—remain unmeasured. Clinically, there is a clear need to develop targeted intervention strategies.

The present study found that parents who were divorced, widowed, or unmarried reported significantly higher decisional conflict scores compared with married parents (β=0.197, P<0.001). This difference underscores the critical role of social support networks in emergency medical decision-making. From a stress-coping perspective, the absence of a co-parent means that the entire cognitive and emotional burden of the decision rests on a single individual, elevating both threat appraisal and the perceived inadequacy of coping resources (19-21). When faced with decisional dilemmas, married parents can obtain emotional comfort and cognitive complementarity through immediate communication with their spouse, thereby reducing decisional uncertainty. In contrast, parents lacking stable partner support are more susceptible to decisional isolation, and their information processing and emotional regulation abilities may be compromised, which in turn amplifies their concerns and self-blame regarding the consequences of their decisions (22). This finding suggests that emergency healthcare providers should actively identify marital status as a potential risk factor when assessing parental decisional support needs (23,24). For parents who present alone, interventions such as involving medical social workers, psychological counselors, or contacting relatives and friends remotely by telephone may be considered to compensate for the lack of onsite social support resources.

Our results also revealed that parental educational level and family monthly income per capita were significantly negatively correlated with decisional conflict scores (β=−0.186, β=−0.155, both P<0.01), indicating that families with lower educational attainment and poorer economic status experience higher levels of decisional conflict. This finding can be understood from the perspectives of health literacy and resource accessibility. On the one hand, educational level is an important predictor of health literacy; parents with lower educational backgrounds face greater difficulties in understanding medical terminology, evaluating risk probabilities, and integrating decisional information, rendering them more prone to cognitive biases and decisional confusion (25,26). On the other hand, low-income families must not only weigh the safety and efficacy of treatment when faced with medical decisions but also consider the impact of economic burden on overall family stability. Financial strain represents an additional cognitive load that further compromises decisionmaking capacity under already demanding conditions (27,28). This multidimensional source of stress adds complexity to their decision-making process (29). Notably, these families often face the compounded challenge of limited access to information and a scarcity of external support resources (30). Therefore, when communicating with such families, healthcare providers should adopt a teach-back communication strategy to assess parental understanding by asking them to restate key information. Proactively inquiring about their financial concerns and, when necessary, coordinating hospital charity resources or providing guidance on medical insurance policies may help alleviate their decisional burden.

In this study, parents without a medical background had significantly higher decisional conflict scores than those with a medical background (β=0.176, P=0.001). Rather than simply reflecting superior medical knowledge, this difference likely involves multiple complementary mechanisms. Parents with a medical background may place greater trust in treating clinicians and be more willing to defer to expert recommendations, thereby experiencing less decisional conflict. Their familiarity with invasive procedures may also reduce the perceived threat associated with gastric lavage, leading to lower emotional arousal during the decision. However, it is important to emphasize that lower decisional conflict does not inherently equate to higher decision quality; it may also reflect an uncritical acceptance of medical authority, and further research is needed to examine whether conflict levels correlate with decisional outcomes such as informed choice or longterm psychological adjustment. For parents lacking medical knowledge, however, the combination of limited communication time in emergency situations and unfamiliar medical terminology constitutes a cognitive barrier, making them more likely to magnify procedural risks or misinterpret medical recommendations (31,32). This finding suggests that emergency communication should move beyond a unidirectional information‑giving model toward a cyclical “empathy-explanation-confirmation” communication strategy. For parents without a medical background, healthcare providers may use simple diagrams and analogical explanations (e.g., “gastric lavage is like a quick cleanup for the stomach”) to translate complex information into easy‑to‑understand lay language, helping them form reasonable expectations of the treatment within a short period.

The study found that younger child age was associated with higher decisional conflict scores among parents (β=−0.159, P=0.004). This result can be understood from both developmental psychology and clinical risk perception perspectives. Infancy and early childhood represent a period of heightened parental anxiety regarding caregiving. Young children are unable to express discomfort verbally, leaving parents to infer their level of distress through nonverbal cues; this inherent uncertainty itself constitutes a significant psychological stressor (22,33). Furthermore, as organ functions are not yet fully developed in young children, the pharmacokinetic characteristics of drug metabolism and toxin elimination are more complex, heightening parental concerns regarding procedural safety (34). In the present study, the “uncertainty and pressure” subscale had the highest mean item score (3.25±0.65), further confirming parents’ profound anxiety regarding decision correctness and potential risks under time pressure, with this anxiety being particularly pronounced among parents of young children. Therefore, when communicating with parents of young children, healthcare providers should emphasize differences in drug metabolism between infants and adults when explaining the necessity of the procedure, provide concrete data or successful case examples to enhance parental confidence, and offer adequate emotional validation, for instance, by stating, “I understand how much you care about your child’s comfort, and this is precisely why we take safety very seriously”.

Based on the above findings, this study proposes a multidimensional set of clinical intervention strategies. First, a stratified emergency decisionsupport intervention system should be established. Marital status, educational level, income level, medical background, and child’s age are recommended as key indicators for risk assessment. For high-risk families, a support strategy combining “green-channel access with dedicated communication” should be implemented (35). Emergency specialty nurses or medical social workers should conduct focused communication with parents prior to gastric lavage, using plain language to explain the necessity and risks of the procedure, while employing decision aids (such as simple risk-benefit comparison charts) to help parents quickly establish a cognitive framework (36,37). Second, familycentered emotional support mechanisms should be strengthened. In the emergency setting, healthcare providers can reduce parental psychological defensiveness through empathic opening statements (e.g., “I know this is a very difficult decision for you”) and encourage parents to consult with relatives or friends by telephone when circumstances permit, thereby sharing the decisional burden with family support (38-40). For parents without accompanying family members, volunteer companions or psychological counselors may be arranged to provide temporary emotional support. Third, context‑specific decision aids should be developed. For the unique scenario of emergency gastric lavage, illustrated brochures or short videos may be created, including schematic diagrams of the gastric lavage procedure, concise risk–benefit comparisons, answers to common questions, and key points for postprocedure observation (41-43). These tools can assist parents in obtaining critical information within a limited time frame, reducing information recall burden and decisional uncertainty. Fourth, targeted training in communication skills for emergency healthcare providers should be enhanced. Through scenario-based simulations and standardized patient training, healthcare providers can improve their skills in risk disclosure and empathic communication under high‑pressure, time-constrained conditions, with a focus on strategies for conveying information to populations with low health literacy, thereby promoting the effective implementation of shared decisionmaking in emergency settings (44,45).

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, convenience sampling was used, and all participants were recruited from a single tertiary children’s hospital. The exclusion of parents of critically ill children who required immediate life-saving intervention likely truncated the upper range of decisional conflict, leading to a conservative estimate. Furthermore, the cultural norms surrounding family-based decision-making and physician authority in China may limit the applicability of these findings to healthcare systems with different cultural or organizational contexts. The generalizability of the findings may be limited, and future multicenter studies with stratified sampling are needed. Second, the cross-sectional design of this study allows for the identification of correlations between variables but precludes causal inferences. For example, the association between decisional conflict and lack of family discussion may involve bidirectional influences or be confounded by other factors. Future studies employing prospective cohort designs or mixed-method approaches could help elucidate causal pathways and dynamic processes. Third, and most importantly, the DCS was administered after the child had undergone gastric lavage and before discharge. This retrospective measurement captures recalled rather than real-time decisional conflict, introducing the potential for recall bias, social desirability bias, and outcome bias. Parents whose children had an uneventful recovery may systematically underestimate the conflict they experienced during the actual decision, further attenuating our estimates. Future research should incorporate real-time assessments, such as brief pre-decision surveys or physiological stress indicators, to capture the peak conflict moment more accurately. Additionally, this study focused primarily on individual level and sociodemographic factors and did not systematically incorporate system level variables such as quality of clinician-parent communication, emergency department waiting times, or hospital organizational support. These omitted variables likely account for a substantial portion of the unexplained variance in decisional conflict. Subsequent research could integrate objective clinical data with qualitative interviews to construct a more comprehensive multilevel analytical framework, thereby more fully elucidating the mechanisms underlying decisional conflict among parents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage and identifying potential intervention targets. Finally, the use of a self-developed instrument, while addressing the specific features of emergency gastric lavage, limits direct comparability with studies using the validated DCS; future work should explore adapting and validating the DCS for emergency contexts.

Conclusions

This study, based on a survey of 266 parents of children undergoing emergency gastric lavage, found that the total decisional conflict score was at a moderate-to-high level, with over 90% of parents reporting clinically significant conflict, indicating that parents in this context commonly experience substantial decisional distress. Marital status (divorced, widowed, or unmarried), lower educational level, absence of a medical background, younger child age, and lower family monthly income per capita were identified as independent influencing factors, though the model explained only 30.9% of the variance, highlighting the likely importance of unmeasured system-level variables. Based on these findings, a tiered decision support system should be established in clinical practice. For high-risk families, strategies such as “green-channel access with dedicated communication” should be implemented, along with strengthened family-centered emotional support, the development of context-specific decision aids, and enhanced communication skills training for emergency healthcare providers, in order to reduce decisional conflict and improve decision quality among parents. Future research may adopt multicenter prospective designs, incorporate system-level variables such as clinician-parent communication quality and emergency workflow processes, and integrate qualitative interviews to further explore the mechanisms underlying decisional conflict, thereby providing a more robust evidence base for optimizing shared decision‑making in emergency settings.
