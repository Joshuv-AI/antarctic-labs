A mixed-methods study of patient grievances with phlebotomy services at a tertiary care hospital in eastern India
Authors: Akash Bansal, Jagriti, Md Abu Bashar, Prabhat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Patient satisfaction with phlebotomy services is an important indicator of healthcare quality and efficiency. It reflects not only clinical standards but also environmental and interpersonal dimensions of care. Understanding patient experiences can guide meaningful improvements in diagnostic service delivery, especially in high-volume public hospitals. The aim of this study was to assess patient satisfaction with phlebotomy services in a tertiary care hospital, identify critical factors influencing perceptions, and highlight specific areas requiring enhancement.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A sequential mixed-methods, cross-sectional survey was conducted among 100 patients visiting the phlebotomy section at All India Institute of Medical Sciences Gorakhpur, a tertiary care institute in eastern Uttar Pradesh, India. A 35-item structured questionnaire was administered in English or Hindi, according to participant preference. The tool included both closed-ended questions rated on a 3-point Likert scale and open-ended items capturing qualitative feedback. Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS, and thematic analysis was applied to open-ended responses. Gender-specific patterns in satisfaction were also explored.RESULTS:High levels of satisfaction were reported for hygiene practices (96%–99%), staff professionalism (94%–96%), and information sharing (77%). The availability of required laboratory tests was positively rated by 79% of participants. However, areas of concern included long waiting times (28% dissatisfaction) and toilet cleanliness (18% dissatisfaction). Thematic analysis revealed that staff attitude, cleanliness, communication, and waiting time were dominant influences on patient satisfaction, whereas pain and procedural delays emerged as common grievances. Gender-based analysis revealed that female patients expressed higher satisfaction with staff behavior and communication, whereas male patients were more satisfied with procedural and diagnostic efficiency.CONCLUSION:The study not only found overall high patient satisfaction with phlebotomy services but also identified actionable gaps in waiting time management, sanitation, and communication. Targeted improvements in these areas, especially with gender-sensitive approaches, can further enhance the patient experience and align phlebotomy services with patient-centered care models in tertiary care settings.

Introduction

Phlebotomy is the practice of drawing blood for diagnostic, therapeutic, or research purposes and is integral to clinical decision-making and patient management.[123] As the initial point of diagnostic contact for many patients, it significantly influences subsequent clinical decision-making.[4] As a frontline diagnostic service, it significantly influences the quality of laboratory results and the patient’s overall healthcare experience. Accurate blood collection, proper labeling, and timely sample handling are essential to ensure reliable test outcomes, which directly impact diagnosis, treatment decisions, and patient safety.[235]

Despite its clinical importance, patient satisfaction with phlebotomy services is often overlooked. In many healthcare systems, emphasis is often placed on physician–patient interactions and diagnostic interpretations, whereas the patient’s experience with foundational services like phlebotomy is frequently underestimated or ignored.[6] However, for patients, this step can set the tone for their entire hospital visit. Factors such as the phlebotomist’s technical skill, demeanor, communication, waiting time, privacy, hygiene standards all play a vital role in shaping patient perceptions. Even a technically successful blood draw can lead to a negative experience if communication is poor or environmental cleanliness is compromised.[78] Existing literature often generalizes laboratory experiences, conflating distinct components of the diagnostic workflow, and often fails to capture the nuances of the phlebotomy process itself. There is a distinct gap in understanding the specific pain points and grievances patients encounter during phlebotomy, especially in high-burden public healthcare settings in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC).[89] Moreover, the growing emphasis on patient-centered care and quality assurance frameworks such as NABH and ISO 15189 necessitates a closer evaluation of every touchpoint in the patient journey, including phlebotomy services.[10]

This study aims to explore the nature of patient grievances related to phlebotomy services at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS) Gorakhpur, a tertiary care teaching hospital in North India. It seeks to identify the underlying factors contributing to dissatisfaction, provide qualitative insights from the patient’s perspective, and highlight areas for targeted intervention. By addressing the real-world challenges faced by patients in a critical yet often neglected area of healthcare, this study aims to inform service improvements, promote equity in care delivery, and strengthen the culture of continuous quality enhancement in diagnostic services.

While previous studies have broadly assessed laboratory service satisfaction, very few have isolated the phlebotomy process as a discrete point of patient evaluation, particularly in LMIC public healthcare settings.[11] This study adds novel insights by focusing exclusively on the phlebotomy experience within a high-volume tertiary hospital [Figure 1]. Furthermore, it incorporates a gender-based analysis of satisfaction trends, an aspect that remains underexplored in current literature. The combination of quantitative satisfaction scoring and thematic grievance analysis offers a more nuanced understanding of service quality, uncovering operational and interpersonal factors specific to phlebotomy services that are often masked in broader laboratory assessments.[5]

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional mixed-method study was conducted from April 2024 to June 2024 at the Department of Biochemistry Laboratory, AIIMS, Gorakhpur, a tertiary care institute in the state of Uttar Pradesh, India. The study aimed to assess patient grievances and satisfaction with phlebotomy services using a structured questionnaire designed to capture both quantitative and qualitative data. The study hypothesized that while overall patient satisfaction with phlebotomy services would be high, operational factors such as waiting time and sanitation would emerge as key areas of dissatisfaction.

Study participants and sampling

A total of 100 adult patients (aged 18 years and above) who received phlebotomy services at the central sample collection unit during the study period were included through convenience sampling. The use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of findings. The sample size was relatively small and based on convenience sampling, which may limit generalizability. Additionally, convenience sampling may have introduced sampling bias, potentially affecting the representativeness of the findings. To ensure effective communication and reliable data, individuals who were critically ill, had diagnosed psychiatric conditions, or were pediatric patients were excluded. The age criterion was applied to facilitate informed consent and accurate self-reporting of experiences.

Data collection tools and techniques

Data were collected through a structured questionnaire consisting of 35 items. The tool was developed by a senior pathologist overseeing phlebotomy services, in consultation with resident doctors, technical staff, and prior literature. The questionnaire included both closed-ended items using a 3-point Likert scale (1 = very good, 2 = satisfactory, 3 = needs improvement) and open-ended questions to gather qualitative insights into patient experiences. Responses marked as “very good” or “satisfactory” were classified as “satisfied,” whereas “needs improvement” responses were categorized as “dissatisfied.” The questionnaire was pretested on 10 participants to ensure clarity and relevance. Internal consistency was confirmed with Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82, indicating good reliability.

Patients completed the questionnaire in either English or Hindi, according to their preference, either independently or with help from an attendant. The questionnaires were administered during their visit to collect laboratory reports. To ensure clarity and gather additional qualitative insights, brief follow-up interviews were conducted. All data collection was carried out by trained research staff adhering to standardized protocols.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Ethics Committee of AIIMS approved the study, Gorakhpur (Approval No: IHEC/AIIMS-GKP/BMR/342/2024). All participants provided written informed consent before their inclusion. Participation was entirely voluntary, and confidentiality was rigorously upheld throughout.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0.2.0 software, whereas qualitative data underwent thematic analysis using the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet to create the database. Qualitative data from open-ended responses were analyzed using thematic analysis. Two trained researchers independently read all responses multiple times to ensure familiarity with the data. Initial codes were generated inductively and then organized into broader themes through iterative discussions. A codebook was developed to ensure consistency in coding. Intercoder reliability was assessed through independent double-coding of 20% of responses, with an agreement rate of 90% achieved after discussion and consensus. Themes were finalized after achieving thematic saturation, ensuring that no new themes emerged from additional data. The percentage of satisfaction or dissatisfaction was calculated by dividing the number of satisfied or dissatisfied responses by the total number of patients. Only descriptive statistics were used in this analysis. No inferential statistical tests (such as Chi-square tests) were conducted.

However, also consistent with the hypothesis, certain operational domains showed lower satisfaction. Although 45% of participants rated the cleanliness of the phlebotomy environment as “Very Good,” the majority found it merely “Satisfactory,” indicating room for improvement. Specific concerns were observed in waiting time (with 2% of patients expressing dissatisfaction), toilet accessibility (3%), and toilet cleanliness (16%), reinforcing the predicted impact of environmental and process-related factors on patient perception.

As expected, gender-based differences were notable. Female respondents consistently rated staff behavior and courtesy more positively than male respondents, supporting the hypothesis that interpersonal interactions influence female patient satisfaction more strongly.

Prephlebotomy responses [Figure 2] showed that while 77% were satisfied with the information about the site of phlebotomy, parameters like queue management (60%) and procedure timing (62%) received comparatively lower “Very Good” ratings, indicating gaps in communication and scheduling, as anticipated.

Analysis of patient satisfaction data (before phlebotomy)

Postphlebotomy experiences [Figure 3] revealed high satisfaction with clinical execution, such as test availability and number of needle stick attempts, again confirming expectations. However, dissatisfaction with postprocedural instructions, physical comfort, and hygiene was evident, aligning with the hypothesis that nonclinical factors remain critical pain points.[1213]

Analysis of patient satisfaction data (after phlebotomy)

Five key themes emerged from the analysis: Staff Attitude and Professionalism, Cleanliness and Hygiene, Communication, Timeliness and Efficiency, and Pain/Discomfort.[1415] Finally, thematic analysis of qualitative responses [Figure 4] confirmed that staff attitude, hygiene, and communication were dominant influences on satisfaction. Areas of concern, including pain during sampling and procedural delays, further underscored the need to strengthen patient-centered service elements.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insight into patient satisfaction with phlebotomy services at a tertiary care institution in North India. The main findings indicate that while overall satisfaction is high, particularly regarding staff professionalism, hygiene practices, and the availability of laboratory tests, several operational aspects, such as waiting times, restroom hygiene, and privacy during procedures, require attention.[1617]

One of the most important findings was the high satisfaction rate related to staff courtesy and technical skill, echoing prior research emphasizing the critical role of staff–patient interactions in shaping healthcare experiences.[118] The use of disinfectants, availability of gauze and cotton, and single-needle attempts also received positive ratings, confirming adherence to safe and effective phlebotomy protocols. These aspects are fundamental to reducing procedural anxiety and ensuring clinical safety, thus validating current operational standards at the facility.[1019]

However, areas such as queue management and communication around procedure timing received comparatively lower ratings. Only 28% of patients rated waiting times as “Very Good,” and issues with toilet cleanliness were highlighted by 16% of respondents. These findings are consistent with prior studies linking patient satisfaction to environmental hygiene and service timeliness.[13] Similarly, the relatively lower satisfaction with privacy during sample collection suggests an overlooked but essential aspect of patient dignity and comfort.[19]

Gender-based differences in satisfaction, particularly the higher ratings by female patients for staff courtesy, highlight the need for gender-sensitive approaches in service delivery.[81819] For instance, female patients particularly appreciated detailed explanations and reassurance during the procedure, which suggests that incorporating more empathetic communication and allowing additional time for interaction with female patients could further enhance their satisfaction.[2021] This finding is consistent with previous literature that suggests personalized communication strategies may improve the overall care experience for different demographic groups.[1922]

The thematic analysis further reinforced these insights, revealing that cleanliness, staff professionalism, and communication are primary drivers of satisfaction, whereas timeliness and physical comfort remain key pain points.[23] For example, several participants expressed frustration with delays, with one noting, Waiting time is too long, especially for elderly patients. Others highlighted the importance of hygiene, stating that the cleanliness of the phlebotomy area needs improvement. Positive comments also emerged regarding staff professionalism, such as The staff were very polite and explained each step of the process clearly.[1924] These qualitative insights further validate the quantitative findings. These findings emphasize the multidimensional nature of patient experience, where both technical competence and nontechnical elements, such as empathy and environment, intersect to influence perceptions of care quality. The observed waiting time dissatisfaction (28%) aligns with trends reported in similar tertiary healthcare settings, where delays in service delivery remain a common challenge. In many LMIC hospitals, best practices such as implementing appointment systems, improving queue management, and optimizing staff allocation have been shown to reduce such operational inefficiencies.[11] Incorporating these strategies could help address the service gaps identified in our study. The qualitative themes largely mirrored quantitative findings, particularly highlighting communication, hygiene, and waiting times as persistent challenges.

Despite these strengths, the study has a few limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents assessment of changes over time, and the sample size, although adequate for initial insights, may not represent broader patient populations. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of response bias. The absence of in-depth qualitative interviews also limits a richer understanding of patient dissatisfaction.

Given these findings, several implications emerge for practice and future research. Immediate efforts should focus on reducing patient wait times, enhancing restroom maintenance, improving signage and communication about procedures, and strengthening privacy during blood draws. Regular training for staff on patient-centered care and empathy could help improve overall satisfaction, especially in interpersonal interactions.[8]

Future studies could adopt a longitudinal or mixed-methods approach to explore how service modifications affect satisfaction over time and to delve deeper into the causes of patient dissatisfaction.[24] Conducting semistructured interviews or focus groups may offer more nuanced perspectives that structured questionnaires alone cannot capture. Furthermore, including a larger and more diverse patient population would enhance the generalizability of results.[10]

In conclusion, this study highlights that while key clinical and interpersonal aspects of phlebotomy services at the studied center are well-managed, operational inefficiencies and environmental shortcomings merit focused improvement. A patient-centered approach, informed by ongoing feedback and evidence-based interventions, will be vital in closing these gaps and enhancing the quality of care in phlebotomy services.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several strengths. It employed a structured and validated questionnaire, with robust data collection protocols carried out by trained personnel. The inclusion of gender-based analysis adds an important dimension to understanding patient perspectives in phlebotomy services, an area underexplored in LMIC settings.[11]

However, the study also has limitations. The sample size was relatively small and based on convenience sampling, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, as a cross-sectional study, it lacks longitudinal follow-up to assess the evolution of patient perceptions or sustained service improvements over time.

Conclusion

This study explored how patients experience phlebotomy services and highlighted both what is working well and where improvements are needed. Most patients expressed satisfaction with hygiene, staff professionalism, and the availability of test areas that reflect good alignment with recognized quality standards like NABH and ISO 15189.

At the same time, concerns about long waiting times, unclear communication, and poor toilet hygiene point to practical issues that still need attention. These are not just operational gaps but also reflect deeper concerns around dignity and patient comfort values at the heart of the World Health Organization (WHO’s) patient-centered care framework.

Interestingly, the different satisfaction levels between male and female patients remind us that healthcare experiences are not one-size-fits-all. We need to design services that are respectful, inclusive, and responsive to individual needs.

For hospitals and administrators, these findings are a helpful reminder: maintaining clinical standards is crucial, but so is creating a welcoming, clean, and well-communicated environment. By taking patient feedback seriously and aligning services with national and global health benchmarks, we can create care experiences that are not only safe but truly satisfying.
