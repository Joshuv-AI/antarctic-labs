Analysis of Hessian Scaling for Local and Global Costs in Variational Quantum Algorithms Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Yihan Huang, Yangshuai Wang

Abstract
Barren plateaus in variational quantum algorithms are typically described by gradient concentration at random initialization. In contrast, rigorous results for the Hessian, even at the level of entry-wise variance, remain limited. In this work, we analyze the scaling of Hessian-entry variances at initialization. Using exact second-order parameter-shift identities, we write H j ​ k H_{jk} as a constant-size linear combination of shifted cost evaluations, which reduces Var ρ ⁡ ( H j ​ k ) \Var_{\rho}(H_{jk}) to a finite-dimensional covariance–quadratic form. For global objectives, under an exponential concentration condition on the cost at initialization, Var ρ ⁡ ( H j ​ k ) \Var_{\rho}(H_{jk}) decays exponentially with the number of qubits n n . For local averaged objectives in bounded-depth circuits, Var ρ ⁡ ( H j ​ k ) \Var_{\rho}(H_{jk}) admits polynomial bounds controlled by the growth of the backward lightcone on the interaction graph. As a consequence, the number of measurement shots required to estimate H j ​ k H_{jk} to fixed accuracy inherits the same exponential (global) or polynomial (local) scaling. Extensive numerical experiments over system size, circuit depth, and interaction graphs validate the predicted variance scaling. Overall, the paper quantifies when Hessian entries can be resolved at initialization under finite sampling, providing a mathematically grounded basis for second-order information in variational optimization.

The mechanisms driving this first-order information loss are now well understood. Theoretical analyses link vanishing gradients to factors such as excessive entanglement

[

[

Despite this progress, rigorous quantitative results for Hessian structure and scaling at initialization remain limited. Second-order methods, such as Newton-type updates
[
26
]
and the quantum natural gradient
[
33
]
, are standard tools in scientific computing, but in the quantum setting their usefulness is constrained by how accurately Hessian information can be estimated as the system size grows. While prior work indicates that higher-order derivatives can also vanish
[
10
]
and empirical studies have explored specific applications
[
31
]
, existing theoretical analyses largely focus on global operator norms or specific backpropagation schemes
[

. These approaches do not quantify the
entry-wise statistical resolvability
of the Hessian at random initialization, which is essential in practice where Hessian estimators are formed from finite-shot measurements. Consequently, a fundamental question arises: when first-order gradients vanish, does second-order information remain statistically accessible, and what precise scaling laws govern its variance?

In this work, we bridge this gap by quantifying the initialization scaling of Hessian-entry variance at random initialization.
Focusing on hardware-efficient architectures with bounded-depth locality, our analysis rests on two ingredients:
(i) exact parameter-shift rules

(ii) the role of cost-function locality in mitigating first-order barren plateaus

Using exact second-order parameter-shift identities, we express each Hessian entry as a constant-size linear combination of shifted objective evaluations.
This representation rewrites the entrywise variance
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
as a finite covariance–quadratic form and makes its scaling explicitly controllable through backward-lightcone geometry.

Our analysis yields two distinct scaling regimes at random initialization. Fig.

When the lightcone remains sub-extensive, these bounds yield a polynomial resolution cost for Hessian-entry estimation at initialization. We support the theory with numerical experiments that verify the predicted scaling laws, probe the Hessian eigenspectra at initialization, and quantify the corresponding sampling costs implied by our resolvability criterion.

2
Theoretical Background

We introduce the basic ingredients needed for the analysis of higher-order derivatives in VQAs. Specifically, the circuit model and objective functions, formalize locality notions that control operator spreading, and derive the parameter-shift identities for gradients and Hessian entries used throughout the paper. The initialization assumptions and the definition of higher-order barren plateaus are also stated here; these will be used to obtain the variance bounds in §
3
.

2.1
Parameterized quantum circuits

A parameterized quantum circuit is a family of unitaries
\{U(\bm{\theta})\}_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}
acting on the
n
-qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{n}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
, where
M
is the number of parameters. For each
\bm{\theta}
, the circuit prepares

|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle=U(\bm{\theta})\,|0\rangle^{\otimes n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}.

(1)

We focus on hardware-efficient architectures of depth
L
obtained by alternating fixed entangling layers and parameterized rotations. Specifically, each layer
\ell
consists of a parameter-independent entangling unitary
V_{\ell}
acting on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
, followed by
m_{\ell}
parametrized gates, so that

U(\bm{\theta})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(V_{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{m_{\ell}}\exp(-i\theta_{\ell,j}G_{\ell,j})\right),

(2)

with products ordered in increasing
\ell
and
M=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}m_{\ell}
. We assume that each parametrized gate in (
2
) carries its own independent parameter (i.e., no parameter sharing), so that the parameter vector can be written as
{\bm{\theta}}=\{\theta_{\ell,j}\}_{\ell=1,\dots,L;\,j=1,\dots,m_{\ell}}
.

To enable exact constant-cost parameter-shift rules for gradients and Hessians, we assume each parametrized gate has a two-point-spectrum generator (e.g., Pauli rotations). We note that more general spectra admit generalized shift rules with a larger constant overhead.

Assumption 1
(Two-point spectrum generators).

In (
2
), each generator
G_{\ell,j}
is Hermitian and supported on at most
r

qubits, where
r
is independent of
n
. Moreover,
G_{\ell,j}
has a two-point spectrum

\mathrm{spec}(G_{\ell,j})=\{\pm\gamma\}
for some
\gamma>0
. Without loss of generality we
take
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}
, i.e.,
G_{\ell,j}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}I
.
This includes Pauli rotations
G_{\ell,j}=P_{\ell,j}/2
with
P_{\ell,j}
a Pauli string
supported on at most
r
qubits.

2.2
Cost functions and locality

The objective is to minimize the expectation value of a Hermitian observable
O
under the variational state
|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle
:

C(\bm{\theta})=\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|\,O\,|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle=\langle 0|\,U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})\,O\,U(\bm{\theta})\,|0\rangle.

(3)

Locality of
O
controls the spatial scale on which the conjugated observable
U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})OU(\bm{\theta})
can spread, and thus the dependence structure underlying the variance bounds in §
3
.

Definition 2
(Local versus global objectives).

An observable
O
is
k
-local
if it acts non-trivially on at most
k
qubits, i.e.,

|\supp(O)|\leq k
for a constant
k
independent of
n
.
More generally, an objective
C(\bm{\theta})
is
k
-local term-wise
if it can be written as

C(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|\,O_{i}\,|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle
with each
O_{i}

being
k
-local.
We call an observable
O
(and the objective it induces via (
3
))
global
if it
has extensive support, i.e.,
|\supp(O)|
grows linearly
with
n
.

A canonical global example is

O_{\mathrm{glo}}=Z^{\otimes n},\qquad C_{\mathrm{glo}}(\bm{\theta})=\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|Z^{\otimes n}|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle,

(4)

which acts non-trivially on all qubits. In contrast, local objectives are typically constructed from bounded-support terms, e.g.,
Z_{i}
or
Z_{i}Z_{i+1}
.
For normalization one often considers averaged local costs, for instance

C_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|Z_{i}|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle,

(5)

which remains
1
-local term-wise while producing an
O(1)
-scaled objective.

2.3
Parameter-shift identities

Under Assumption
1
, derivatives of
C(\bm{\theta})
admit exact two-shift parameter-shift formulas
[
13
]
. In particular,

\frac{\partial C}{\partial\theta_{j}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big[C(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{j})-C(\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{j})\Big],

(6)

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
denotes the
j
-th canonical basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Applying (
6
) twice yields the Hessian entries. For
j\neq k
,

\displaystyle H_{jk}=\frac{1}{4}\Big[C(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{k})-C(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{k})

\displaystyle-C(\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{k})+C(\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{k})\Big],

(7)

and for
j=k
,

H_{jj}=\frac{1}{4}\Big[C(\bm{\theta}+\pi\mathbf{e}_{j})-2C(\bm{\theta})+C(\bm{\theta}-\pi\mathbf{e}_{j})\Big].

(8)

For more general generator spectra, generalized parameter-shift rules still yield finite linear combinations of shifted costs
[
36
]
. We note that our results only require an
O(1)
number of shifts per derivative.

2.4
Trainability metrics at random initialization

We study the initialization-time variance of gradient and Hessian entries. Unless otherwise stated, all expectations and variances are taken with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}\sim\rho
, where
\rho
is the product measure of i.i.d. uniform random variables on
[0,2\pi)
.

Assumption 3
(Depth regime).

The circuit depth
L
is either fixed or grows sublinearly with
n
; in particular,
rL=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
, where
r
is the gate locality from Assumption
1
(each generator acts on at most
r
qubits).

Under Assumptions
1
and
3
, and for a
k
-local observable
O
in Definition
2
, conjugation by
U(\bm{\theta})
expands the support of
O
only within a backward lightcone of radius
O(rL)
. We will quantify the corresponding neighborhood growth via the graph growth function
V_{G}(\cdot)
introduced in §
3.2
.

Assumption 4
(Observable normalization).

The observable
O
in (
3
) is Hermitian and satisfies
\|O\|\leq 1
. For term-wise local objectives
C(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|O_{i}|\\
\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle
, we also assume
\|O_{i}\|\leq 1
.

This normalization ensures that asymptotic variance rates reflect circuit geometry rather than trivial rescaling. We therefore quantify trainability at initialization through variance-based resolvability metrics as follows.

2.4.1
Variance of Hessian entries

For any indices
(j,k)
, we quantify the initialization-time variability of a Hessian entry by

\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{jk}^{2}]-\big(\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{jk}]\big)^{2}.

(9)

We interpret
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
as a proxy for the
statistical resolvability
of the entry
H_{jk}
under finite-shot estimation: smaller variance indicates that substantially more samples are required to distinguish the entry from sampling noise at a prescribed accuracy.

Definition 5
(Higher-order barren plateau).

Let
C(\bm{\theta})
be the cost (
3
) with Hessian
H(\bm{\theta})
. We say the circuit exhibits a
higher-order barren plateau at initialization (entry-wise)
if there exist constants
c,\alpha>0
independent of
n
such that

\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})\leq c\,e^{-\alpha n}\quad\text{for all }j,k\text{ and all sufficiently large }n.

(10)

Definition
5
is entry-wise and does not by itself imply spectral conditioning (e.g.,
bounds on extremal eigenvalues or condition numbers); small typical entries can still coexist with a
few directions of non-negligible curvature. Nevertheless, entry-wise second-moment control is the
natural notion for finite-sample shift-based estimation and yields, for instance,
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\|H\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\|H\|_{F}^{2}]=\sum_{j,k}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{jk}^{2}]
(cf. Lemma
14
).

The following lemma reduces Hessian-entry variance to a covariance–quadratic form over finitely many shifted objective evaluations, which is the starting point for our global/ local scaling analysis. The proof is given in Appendix
A.1.1
.

Lemma 6
(Parameter-shift covariance representation).

Under Assumption
1
, the diagonal Hessian entry admits the second-order parameter-shift representation (
8
).
Define
C_{+}:=C(\bm{\theta}+\pi\mathbf{e}_{j})
,
C_{0}:=C(\bm{\theta})
, and
C_{-}:=C(\bm{\theta}-\pi\mathbf{e}_{j})
, and let

\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
be the covariance matrix with entries
\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}:=\Cov_{\rho}(C_{\alpha},C_{\beta})
for
\alpha,\beta\in\{+,0,-\}
.
Then

\Var_{\rho}(H_{jj})=w^{\top}\Sigma w,\qquad w:=\tfrac{1}{4}(1,-2,1)^{\top},

(11)

and equivalently
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jj})
can be written as an explicit linear combination of variances and covariances among
\{C_{+},C_{0},C_{-}\}
.
An analogous covariance–quadratic representation holds for off-diagonal entries
H_{jk}
using (
2.3
).
More generally, whenever a derivative quantity admits a finite-shift representation

X(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}w_{\ell}\,C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s_{\ell})
with
m<\infty
, one has

\Var_{\rho}(X)=\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{m}w_{\ell}w_{\ell^{\prime}}\Cov_{\rho}\!\big(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s_{\ell}),C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s_{\ell^{\prime}})\big)
.

Lemma
6
extends to any differentiation scheme that represents
H_{jk}
as a finite linear combination of shifted costs, provided the number of shifted evaluations is
O(1)
, with constants determined by the shift coefficients.

3
Asymptotic Variance Analysis

This section derives asymptotic bounds for the entry-wise Hessian variance at random initialization.
We first express
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
as a finite covariance–quadratic form over a constant number of shifted cost evaluations.
We then bound these covariances using locality and backward-lightcone structure for local objectives, and using a cost-concentration condition for global objectives.

3.1
Variance representation

Let
C(\bm{\theta})
be the cost (
3
). The second-order parameter-shift rules (
2.3
)–(
8
) express each Hessian entry as a finite linear combination of shifted costs, and hence yield the covariance–quadratic form

\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{jk}}w_{s}w_{s^{\prime}}\Cov_{\rho}\big(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s),C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s^{\prime})\big),

(12)

where
\oplus
denotes component-wise addition modulo
2\pi
.
For the two-shift rules (
2.3
)–(
8
), one has
|\mathcal{S}_{jk}|\leq 4
and
w_{s}=O(1)
independent of
n
. Derived from Lemma
6
, the explicit diagonal and off-diagonal shift rules are listed in Appendix
A.1
; (
12
) is the starting point for the variance bounds in §
3.2
.

While (
12
) characterizes the initialization-induced variability of
H_{jk}
, practical estimation relies on finite-shot evaluations of the shifted costs. It is therefore natural to compare the shot-noise contribution to the estimator variance with the intrinsic scale
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
and to quantify the number of shots needed to separate the two. This motivates the following resolution criterion.

Definition 7
(Resolution criterion and shot requirement).

Let
\widehat{H}_{jk}
be a finite-shot estimator of
H_{jk}
constructed from shifted cost evaluations.
For
\eta\in(0,1]
, we say that
H_{jk}
is
resolved at initialization
if

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim\rho}\left[\Var_{\mathrm{sh}}(\widehat{H}_{jk}\mid\bm{\theta})\right]\leq\eta\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk}).

The corresponding
resolution shot requirement

N_{\mathrm{res}}
is the smallest number of shots per shifted cost evaluation for which the above condition holds.

3.2
Main results

We now derive explicit scaling bounds from (
12
) by bounding covariances between shifted costs. We treat global objectives first, where second-moment concentration transfers through the constant-size finite-shift rules, and then turn to term-wise
k
-local averaged objectives.

Assumption 8
(Second-moment concentration for global objectives).

Let
O
be Hermitian with
\|O\|\leq 1
and define the cost
C(\bm{\theta})
by (
3
).
Assume there exist constants
c_{\rm g}(r,L)>0
and
\alpha(r,L)>0
, independent of
n
, such that

\Var_{\rho}\big[C(\bm{\theta})\big]\leq c_{\rm g}(r,L)e^{-\alpha(r,L)n}.

(13)

Remark 9
.

Assumption
8
is a second-moment concentration condition on the objective value at random initialization. Its role is to serve as an input for the global regime; Lemma
10
shows that such concentration is uniform over the finite shift sets used in parameter-shift rules and is preserved under any constant-size finite-shift linear combination.

The assumption holds, for example, when the initialized state ensemble forms an (approximate) projective
2
-design, in which case
\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta})]
decays as
\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})
for
\|O\|\leq 1
(see Appendix
B.2
and Proposition
24
). In architectures where design guarantees are unavailable at the considered depth, (
13
) can be assessed directly by Monte Carlo estimation of
\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta})]
; by shift-invariance of
\rho
, the same estimate applies to all shifted evaluations appearing in the parameter-shift rules.

The next lemma formalizes this transference: second-moment concentration of
C(\bm{\theta})
is uniform over shifts and stable under finite-shift linear combinations.

Lemma 10
(Uniform shift concentration and finite-shift transference).

Let
\rho
be shift-invariant on the torus, i.e.,
\bm{\theta}\sim\rho\Rightarrow\bm{\theta}\oplus s\sim\rho
for all shifts
s\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Fix a finite shift set
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
and coefficients
\{w_{s}\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathbb{R}
, and define

X_{s}:=C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)
. If
\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta})]\leq v_{n}
, then the same bound holds uniformly over shifts,

\Var_{\rho}[X_{s}]=\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)]=\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta})]\leq v_{n}\qquad\text{for all }s\in\mathcal{S},

and moreover,

\Var_{\rho}\Big(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}w_{s}\,C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)\Big)\leq\Big(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|w_{s}|\Big)^{2}v_{n}.

Proof.

Shift-invariance implies
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\theta}\oplus s
have the same distribution under
\rho
, hence

\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)]=\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta})]
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. For the linear combination, by bilinearity,

\Var\Big(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}w_{s}X_{s}\Big)=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}w_{s}w_{s^{\prime}}\Cov(X_{s},X_{s^{\prime}}).

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

|\Cov(X_{s},X_{s^{\prime}})|\leq\sqrt{\Var(X_{s})\Var(X_{s^{\prime}})}\leq v_{n}
,
where the last inequality uses the uniform variance bound from the first part.
Therefore,

\Var\Big(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}w_{s}X_{s}\Big)\leq v_{n}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}|w_{s}||w_{s^{\prime}}|=\Big(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|w_{s}|\Big)^{2}v_{n}.

To make the locality bound explicit, we quantify how many local terms can have overlapping
backward lightcones, which controls the maximum degree of the associated dependency graph.
Let
G
be the interaction graph of the architecture and denote by
B_{G}(v,m)
the ball of radius
m

centered at
v
. Define the growth function

V_{G}(m):=\max_{v\in V(G)}\big|B_{G}(v,m)\big|.

(14)

For bounded-degree graphs,
V_{G}(m)
is finite for each fixed
m
; for
D
-dimensional lattices one has

V_{G}(m)=\mathcal{O}(m^{D})
. We are then ready to state our main theorem.

Theorem 11
(Asymptotic scaling of Hessian-entry variance).

Under Assumptions
1
,
3
, and
4
, the Hessian-entry variances satisfy, for all
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
:

(i)

Global objectives. If
|\supp(O)|
grows linearly with
n
and Assumption
8
holds, then there exist constants
\tilde{c}(r,L)>0
and
\tilde{\alpha}(r,L)>0
such that

\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})\leq\tilde{c}(r,L)\exp\big(-\tilde{\alpha}(r,L)n\big).

(15)

(ii)

Local objectives. Suppose the cost is of the form

C_{\rm loc}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|O_{v}|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle,\qquad\|O_{v}\|\leq 1,

where each
O_{v}
is supported on at most
k
qubits with
k
independent of
n
. Then there exists
c_{\rm loc}(k,r,L)>0
such that

\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})\leq c_{\rm loc}(k,r,L)\frac{V_{G}(k+2rL)}{n}.

(16)

In particular, if
V_{G}(m)=\mathcal{O}(m^{D})
for some fixed
D
, then

\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})\leq c_{\rm loc}(k,r,L)\frac{(k+2rL)^{D}}{n}.

(17)

Proof sketch.

By the parameter-shift representation, each Hessian entry
H_{jk}
is a fixed
O(1)
-term linear combination of shifted costs
C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)
, hence
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
reduces to a finite covariance–quadratic form over a constant-size shift set.

For global objectives, Lemma
10
transfers the assumed second-moment concentration of
C(\bm{\theta})
uniformly over the finite shifts; since the parameter-shift rule uses only
O(1)
evaluations with
O(1)
coefficients, the exponential rate in
n
is preserved up to a constant factor, yielding (
15
).

For local objectives, bounded lightcone growth implies that sufficiently separated local contributions depend on disjoint subsets of parameters; under the product initialization measure
\rho
, their covariances vanish. A dependency-graph argument then gives
\Var_{\rho}(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s))=\mathcal{O}(V_{G}(k+2rL)/n)
uniformly in
s
. Plugging this bound into the covariance representation yields (
16
). Details are deferred to Appendix
A.3
.

Remark 12
.

The local bound in Theorem
11
depends on depth only through the lightcone growth factor

V_{G}(k+2rL)
. Hence a sufficient condition for decay with system size is that the relevant backward lightcone remains

subextensive
,
V_{G}(k+2rL)=o(n)
. For bounded-degree
D
-dimensional lattices,
V_{G}(s)=O(s^{D})
up to the graph diameter and then reaches system size.
Therefore the bound remains vanishing provided
k+2rL=o(n^{1/D})
(equivalently,
L=o(n^{1/D})
up to constants),
whereas once
k+2rL
reaches the linear system size the lightcone becomes extensive and the locality-based suppression
may no longer decay with
n
. On small-diameter graphs (e.g., complete graphs),
V_{G}
can saturate rapidly, so this
locality protection can disappear at very shallow depth. For example, in 1D chains this permits
L=o(n)
, while in 2D grids it requires
L=o(\sqrt{n})
.

Remark 13
.

The estimate (
16
) is an upper bound obtained via a generic dependency-graph argument and is not expected to be exponent-tight in typical instances.
The main message of Theorem
11
is that the measurement cost of resolving Hessian entries is controlled by lightcone growth: exponential decay can occur for global objectives, while bounded-depth locality yields polynomial bounds.

3.3
From entry-wise variance to matrix norms and sample complexity

While the entry-wise bounds above quantify the typical fluctuation scale, the practical utility of curvature information depends on the number of shots required to separate these fluctuations from measurement noise, and on how they aggregate into matrix-level quantities.
Throughout this subsection,
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\cdot]
and
\Var_{\rho}(\cdot)
denote expectation and variance with respect to initialization
\bm{\theta}\sim\rho
. When finite-shot estimation is involved, we write
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{sh}}[\cdot\mid\bm{\theta}]
and
\Var_{\mathrm{sh}}(\cdot\mid\bm{\theta})
for expectation and variance over measurement noise (shots), conditional on a fixed parameter value
\bm{\theta}
.

We first record a standard implication of entry-wise second-moment control for matrix norms; a proof is included in Appendix
B.3
for completeness.

Lemma 14
(Entry-wise second moments imply Frobenius and spectral norm bounds).

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
be a random matrix with finite second moments. Then

\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[H_{jk}^{2}],\qquad\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2},\qquad\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2}}.

In particular, if
\Var(H_{jk})\leq v_{n}
and
\big|\mathbb{E}[H_{jk}]\big|\leq\mu_{n}
for all
j,k
, then

\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2}\leq M^{2}(v_{n}+\mu_{n}^{2}),\qquad\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{2}\leq M\sqrt{v_{n}+\mu_{n}^{2}}.

Lemma
14
converts entry-wise second-moment bounds into a global
norm scale
for the Hessian at initialization. These estimates quantify typical magnitude (e.g., for Hessian–vector products) but do not, by themselves, control conditioning or extremal eigenvalues without additional spectral information. Applying this tool to the scalings established in Theorem
11
yields the following two corollaries.

Corollary 15
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
11
. Suppose further that there exists a constant
c>0
,
independent of
n
, such that
|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{jk}]|^{2}\leq c\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
for all
j,k
. Let
v_{n}
denote the
entry-wise variance bound from Theorem
11
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\|H\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\|H\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim M^{2}v_{n},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\|H\|_{2}\lesssim M\sqrt{v_{n}},

with constants depending only on
c
.

While Corollary
15
bounds the intrinsic curvature scale, the key practical question is the resolution cost in Definition
7
under finite-shot sampling.

Corollary 16
(Absolute-accuracy shot cost at fixed parameters).

Let
\widehat{H}_{jk}(\bm{\theta})
be the estimator computed via Algorithm
1
using
N
independent shots per circuit evaluation.
Since the parameter-shift rule is a fixed linear combination, the shot-noise contribution to the estimator variance satisfies

\Var_{\mathrm{sh}}\!\left(\widehat{H}_{jk}(\bm{\theta})\mid\bm{\theta}\right)\leq\frac{c_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma^{2}}{N},

(18)

where
\sigma^{2}\leq 1
when
\|O\|\leq 1
and
c_{\mathcal{S}}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{jk}}w_{s}^{2}
depends only on the chosen shift rule.
Consequently, achieving
\mathrm{RMSE}\leq\varepsilon
for estimating
H_{jk}(\bm{\theta})
at fixed
\bm{\theta}
requires
N\gtrsim c_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}
.
This bound concerns
absolute
accuracy at fixed
\bm{\theta}
; resolvability relative to the initialization scale is addressed in Definition
7
and Corollary
17
.

Corollary 17
(Resolution shot requirement).

Under the assumptions of Corollary
16
, the resolution criterion in Definition
7
is ensured whenever

N\gtrsim\frac{c_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma^{2}}{\eta\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})}.

In particular, combining this with the variance bounds in Theorem
11
yields exponential scaling of the resolution cost for global objectives and polynomial scaling for term-wise local objectives (under sub-extensive backward-lightcones).

Combining Theorem
11
with Corollary
17
shows that the shot requirement for resolving Hessian entries is governed by the initialization variance
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
. For global objectives, exponential decay of
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
forces an exponentially growing resolution cost in
n
. For term-wise local objectives in bounded-depth circuits,
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
is bounded by a polynomial controlled by backward-lightcone growth, leading to polynomial resolution cost when the lightcone remains sub-extensive.

5
Conclusion

In this study, we quantified the scaling of entry-wise Hessian variances in variational quantum algorithms. Using exact second-order parameter-shift identities, we expressed each Hessian entry as a constant-size linear combination of shifted objective evaluations, thereby yielding a finite covariance–quadratic representation for
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
. This framework leads to two distinct scaling behaviors for the
resolvability
of Hessian entries under finite-shot sampling. For global objectives satisfying a standard second-moment concentration condition,
\Var_{\rho}(H_{jk})
decays exponentially in the number of qubits, and the corresponding resolution cost for Hessian-entry estimation grows exponentially with
n
. For term-wise
k
-local objectives in bounded-depth circuits, we obtain polynomial variance bounds governed by backward-lightcone growth on the interaction graph, which implies polynomial resolution cost whenever the lightcone remains sub-extensive. These conclusions are supported by numerical experiments across system sizes, circuit depths, and graph families.

Several avenues remain for future research. On the theory side, identifying conditions under which entry-wise resolvability correlates with informative spectral structure (e.g., conditioning or extremal eigenvalues) would clarify implications for Newton-type and preconditioned methods. On the practical side, extending the variance-based analysis to realistic noise models, including device noise and readout errors, is important for NISQ-relevant settings. More broadly, our results on derivative scaling inform higher-order quantum workflows in scientific computing, including quantum multiscale modeling
[
8
,
12
,
14
]
and quantum machine learning
[
15
,
23
]
. Understanding resolvability limits in these contexts will be important for designing robust algorithms that rely on higher-order information.

is supported within the
R_{L}
-neighborhood of
\Lambda_{B}
. Equivalently,
X(\bm{\theta})
depends only on the subset
of parameters associated with gates whose qubit supports intersect the backward lightcone of
\Lambda_{A}
, and likewise

Y(\bm{\theta})
depends only on parameters within the backward lightcone of
\Lambda_{B}
.

If
d>2R_{L}
, these two backward lightcones are disjoint, hence the corresponding parameter subsets are disjoint.
Under the initialization model in §
2.4
, parameters on disjoint subsets are independent, so

X(\bm{\theta})
and
Y(\bm{\theta})
are independent, proving (
22
).

For (
23
), unitary conjugation preserves operator norm, so
\|A(\bm{\theta})\|=\|A\|\leq 1
and

\|B(\bm{\theta})\|=\|B\|\leq 1
. Therefore
|X(\bm{\theta})|\leq 1
and
|Y(\bm{\theta})|\leq 1
, implying

\Var_{\rho}(X)\leq 1
and
\Var_{\rho}(Y)\leq 1
. Cauchy–Schwarz gives

|\Cov_{\rho}(X,Y)|\leq\sqrt{\Var_{\rho}(X)\Var_{\rho}(Y)}\leq 1
, proving (
23
).
Finally, (
24
) is the immediate combination of (i) and (ii).

Remark 21
.

Lemma
20
uses only bounded operator spreading and independence of initialization parameters.
It yields a hard-cutoff correlation structure: beyond distance
2R_{L}
the covariance vanishes, while for
d\leq 2R_{L}

we only use the trivial bound
|\Cov_{\rho}(X,Y)|\leq 1
. No nontrivial decay inside the lightcone is claimed.

A.3
A dependency-graph variance bound

Lemma 22
(Variance bound via a dependency graph).

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be real-valued random variables with finite second moments, and let

G_{\rm dep}=(\{1,\ldots,n\},E)
be a dependency graph in the following sense:
for any disjoint index sets
I,J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with no edges between
I
and
J
,
the families
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in J}
are independent.
Let
\Delta_{\rm dep}
be the maximum degree of
G_{\rm dep}
.
Then

\Var\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Big)\;\leq\;(\Delta_{\rm dep}+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(X_{i}).

(25)

Consequently, for
Z:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,

\Var(Z)\;\leq\;\frac{\Delta_{\rm dep}+1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(X_{i}).

(26)

Proof.

Expand the variance:

\Var\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(X_{i})+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Cov(X_{i},X_{j}).

If
\{i,j\}\notin E
, then there is no edge between
\{i\}
and
\{j\}
, hence
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are independent
by the dependency-graph property, so
\Cov(X_{i},X_{j})=0
.
Therefore,

\Var\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(X_{i})+2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E,\,i<j}\Cov(X_{i},X_{j}).

Taking absolute values and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\big|\Cov(X_{i},X_{j})\big|\leq\sqrt{\Var(X_{i})\Var(X_{j})}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\Var(X_{i})+\Var(X_{j})\big).

Hence,

\displaystyle\Var\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(X_{i})+2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E,\,i<j}\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\Var(X_{i})+\Var(X_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(X_{i})+\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\big(\Var(X_{i})+\Var(X_{j})\big).

Each
\Var(X_{i})
appears in the edge-sum exactly
\deg(i)
times, hence

\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\big(\Var(X_{i})+\Var(X_{j})\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\deg(i)\,\Var(X_{i})\leq\Delta_{\rm dep}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var(X_{i}).

Combining these yields (
25
), and (
26
) follows by dividing by
n^{2}
.

A.4
Proof of Theorem
11

Proof.

All expectations, variances, and covariances are with respect to the initialization law
\bm{\theta}\sim\rho
; write

\Var\equiv\Var_{\rho}
and
\Cov\equiv\Cov_{\rho}
.

Step 1: Global objectives

Assume
|\supp(O)|=\Theta(n)
and Assumption
8
holds. By shift-invariance of
\rho
on the torus
(see Lemma
23
), for every fixed shift
s
in a finite shift set,

\Var\!\big(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)\big)=\Var\!\big(C(\bm{\theta})\big)\leq c_{\rm g}(r,L)\,e^{-\alpha(r,L)n}.

Then for any
s,s^{\prime}
in the same finite set, Cauchy–Schwarz yields

\big|\Cov(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s),C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s^{\prime}))\big|\leq\sqrt{\Var(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s))\Var(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s^{\prime}))}\leq c_{\rm g}(r,L)\,e^{-\alpha(r,L)n}.

Substituting this uniform bound into (
2.3
) and using that
|\mathcal{S}_{ab}|=O(1)
gives

\Var(H_{jk})\leq\tilde{c}(r,L)\,e^{-\tilde{\alpha}(r,L)n},

for constants depending only on
(r,L)
and the chosen shift rule (via
\{a_{s}\}
and
|\mathcal{S}_{ab}|
). This proves
(
15
).

Step 2: Term-wise local averaged objectives (polynomial scaling)

Assume the objective is of the term-wise local averaged form

C(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\langle 0|\,O_{v}(\bm{\theta})\,|0\rangle,\qquad O_{v}(\bm{\theta}):=U(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}O_{v}U(\bm{\theta}),\qquad\|O_{v}\|\leq 1,

where each
O_{v}
is supported on at most
k
qubits (with
k
independent of
n
). Fix any shift
s
.
Define the local random variables

X_{v}^{(s)}(\bm{\theta}):=\langle 0|\,U(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)^{\dagger}O_{v}U(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)\,|0\rangle,\qquad C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}X_{v}^{(s)}(\bm{\theta}).

Shifting parameters does not change the circuit geometry, hence the backward-lightcone radius remains
O(rL)
for every
s
.
By bounded spreading (Assumptions
1
–
3
),
X_{v}^{(s)}
depends only on parameters in the
backward lightcone of
\supp(O_{v})
, whose qubit support is contained in a
(k+rL)
-neighborhood. Therefore, if

\dist_{G}(\supp(O_{v}),\supp(O_{w}))>k+2rL
, the parameter sets influencing

and
X_{w}^{(s^{\prime})}
are disjoint,
and the two variables are independent (Lemma
20
).

Construct a dependency graph on vertices
\{1,\dots,n\}
by connecting
v\sim w
whenever

\dist_{G}(\supp(O_{v}),\supp(O_{w}))\leq k+2rL
. Its maximum degree is bounded by

\Delta_{\rm dep}\leq V_{G}(k+2rL)-1

|X_{v}^{(s)}|\leq 1

\Var(X_{v}^{(s)})\leq 1

yields the uniform bound (in

\Var\!\big(C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)\big)=\Var\!\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}X_{v}^{(s)}\Big)\leq\frac{\Delta_{\rm dep}+1}{n^{2}}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\Var(X_{v}^{(s)})\leq\frac{V_{G}(k+2rL)}{n}.

(27)

The same construction applies to cross-covariances between two shifts
s

for some constant

B.1
Uniformity over finite shift sets

Lemma 23
(Uniform concentration over finite shift sets).

Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
be any finite set of shifts and interpret parameters on the torus

\mathbb{T}^{M}=(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{M}
.
Suppose the initialization distribution
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{M}
is shift-invariant with respect to
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,

\bm{\theta}\sim\rho\Longrightarrow\bm{\theta}\oplus s\sim\rho,\qquad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(29)

where
\oplus
denotes addition modulo
2\pi
componentwise.
Then Assumption
8
implies

\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Var_{\rho}\big[C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)\big]\leq c_{\rm g}(r,L)e^{-\alpha(r,L)n}.

(30)

Proof.

Fix
s\in\mathcal{S}
. By (
29
), the random variables

C(\bm{\theta})
and
C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)
have the same distribution under
\rho
, hence

\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta}\oplus s)]=\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta})]
. Taking the supremum over
s\in\mathcal{S}

yields (
30
).

If
\rho
is the product of i.i.d. uniform measures on
[0,2\pi)
,
then (
29
) holds for any finite shift set
\mathcal{S}
arising in parameter-shift rules.

B.2
A sufficient design-based route for global concentration

How to verify Assumption
8

Assumption
8
can be supported (i) theoretically via design/ mixing conditions, and/or
(ii) empirically by Monte Carlo sampling
\bm{\theta}^{(m)}\sim\rho
and estimating
\Var_{\rho}[C(\bm{\theta})]
from the sample variance.
By Lemma
23
, the same empirical test applies uniformly over the finite shift set used in
parameter-shift rules.

Proposition 24
(A sufficient condition for Assumption
8
).

Let
d=2^{n}
. Assume the induced state ensemble

\{|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle:=U(\bm{\theta})|0\rangle\}_{\bm{\theta}\sim\rho}

forms an exact complex projective
2
-design on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Then for any Hermitian observable
O
,

\Var_{\rho}\big[\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|O|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle\big]=\frac{\Tr(O^{2})-\Tr(O)^{2}/d}{d(d+1)}.

(31)

In particular, if
\|O\|\leq 1
then
\Tr(O^{2})\leq d
, hence

\Var_{\rho}\big[C(\bm{\theta})\big]\leq\frac{1}{d+1}=\mathcal{O}(2^{-n}),

(32)

so Assumption
8
holds with
\alpha=\log 2
up to constants.

Proof.

Let
|\psi\rangle
be distributed according to an exact projective
2
-design on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
By the defining second-moment property of a projective
2
-design, the second moment operator matches Haar:

\mathbb{E}\big[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes 2}\big]=\frac{I+F}{d(d+1)},

(33)

where
F
is the swap operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle\big]=\Tr\Big(O\mathbb{E}[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|]\Big)=\frac{\Tr(O)}{d},

using
\mathbb{E}[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|]=I/d
.
For the second moment, use the swap trick:

\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle^{2}=\Tr\big((O\otimes O)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes 2}\big),

hence by (
33
),

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle^{2}\big]=\Tr\Big((O\otimes O)\frac{I+F}{d(d+1)}\Big)=\frac{\Tr(O)^{2}+\Tr(O^{2})}{d(d+1)}.

Subtracting the squared mean
(\Tr(O)/d)^{2}
yields (
31
).
If
\|O\|\leq 1
, then
\Tr(O^{2})\leq d\|O\|^{2}\leq d
, giving (
32
).

We do not claim that modest-depth hardware-efficient ansätze form
2
-designs; the proposition is included only as a
verifiable sufficient route to global concentration when design/mixing guarantees are available.

B.3
From entry-wise second moments to matrix norm bounds

We provide a proof of Lemma
14
stated in the main text.

Proof of Lemma
14
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
be a random matrix with finite second moments. By definition,
\|H\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{M}H_{jk}^{2}
, and taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[H_{jk}^{2}]=\sum_{j,k=1}^{M}\Big(\Var(H_{jk})+(\mathbb{E}[H_{jk}])^{2}\Big).

Since
\|H\|_{2}\leq\|H\|_{F}
pointwise, we have
\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2}
.
Moreover, Jensen’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2}}.

If
\Var(H_{jk})\leq v_{n}
and
\big|\mathbb{E}[H_{jk}]\big|\leq\mu_{n}
for all
j,k
, then

\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{M}\Big(\Var(H_{jk})+\big(\mathbb{E}[H_{jk}]\big)^{2}\Big)\leq M^{2}(v_{n}+\mu_{n}^{2}),

and the spectral norm bounds above give

\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{2}^{2}\leq M^{2}(v_{n}+\mu_{n}^{2}),\qquad\mathbb{E}\|H\|_{2}\leq M\sqrt{v_{n}+\mu_{n}^{2}}.

This completes the proof.
