On the Reparameterization Between Cartesian Position–Velocity Vectors and Orbital Elements in the Kepler Problem ArviZ ( R. Kumar et al. 2019 ) , corner.py ( D. Foreman-Mackey 2016 ) , JAX ( J. Bradbury et al. 2018 ) , jnkepler ( K. Masuda 2025 ) , NumPyro ( D. Phan et al. 2019 )
Kento Masuda, Kansuke Nunota

Abstract
Reparameterization from the standard set of orbital elements to Cartesian position–velocity vectors can be computationally advantageous for orbit inference problems, particularly when orbital elements are weakly constrained. Here we present compact analytic expressions for the Jacobian determinants of this transformation and its variants, which enable consistent transformation of prior probability densities under reparameterization and are therefore useful for a Bayesian treatment of such problems. We then use these results to clarify the application of this reparameterization in microlensing and astrometric contexts. We first revisit the widely used formulation of lens orbital motion during binary microlensing events presented by J. Skowron et al. 2011 . We show that their parameterization inadvertently adopts an incorrect definition of the longitude of the ascending node with respect to the sky-projected binary axis at a reference epoch, which renders the intermediate Jacobian formally singular. Using our closed-form expression, we provide a corrected analytic derivation of the Jacobian for this transformation and show that the resulting formula remains effectively unchanged when the longitude of the ascending node is properly defined with respect to an axis independent of the binary orbit. We also perform an explicit quantitative comparison of astrometric orbit fitting using a gradient-based Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm under the two parameterizations, and find that reparameterizing to Cartesian state vectors improves sampling efficiency and robustness relative to orbital-element sampling.



I
Introduction

A bound orbit in the Kepler problem is an ellipse and is conveniently characterized by orbital elements,
which are functions of the constants of motion together with the orbital phase and provide a compact description of the size, shape, and orientation of the orbit
(
E. Poisson & C. M. Will 2014
, e.g.,)
.
On the other hand, observables of stellar binaries and planetary systems are often more directly related to the time-dependent position
\bm{x}(t)
and velocity
\bm{v}(t)
, and so the conversion from the elements
\bm{\theta}
to (
\bm{x}(t)
,
\bm{v}(t)
) plays a central role in modeling actual observations.
This conversion has long been established and is described in many standard textbooks
(
C. D. Murray & S. F. Dermott 1999
, e.g.,)
.

The connection between observables and
(\bm{x}(t),\bm{v}(t))
has motivated orbit-inference methods that use the Cartesian state vectors
at a chosen reference epoch

(\bm{x},\bm{v})
rather than orbital elements as sampling or optimization parameters, especially when the orbit is only weakly constrained
(
J. Skowron et al. 2011
;
R. Ferrer-Chávez et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.
1

1

This reparameterization is expected to be most useful when the orbit is weakly constrained and the posterior geometry in orbital elements is highly non-Gaussian or strongly correlated; if the posterior is already compact and well behaved in orbital elements, the improvement is expected to be modest.

When such a reparameterization is used in Bayesian inference, the one-to-one mapping between the orbital elements and state vectors alone is not sufficient.
Because Bayesian analyses involve prior probability density functions (PDFs) for the parameters in addition to the likelihood function, one also needs to transform PDFs:

\displaystyle p(\bm{x},\bm{v})d\bm{x}d\bm{v}=p(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}.

(1)

This also requires the determinant of the
6\times 6
Jacobian matrix:

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{Kep}}\equiv{\partial{(\bm{x},\bm{v})}\over\partial\bm{\theta}}.

(2)

For example, if we adopt a uniform prior for
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
,
p_{\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},\bm{v})}=\mathrm{const.}
, the corresponding prior PDF for
\bm{\theta}
is given by

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},\bm{v})}(\bm{\theta})=p_{\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},\bm{v})}(\bm{x},\bm{v})\left|\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}\right|\propto\left|\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}(\bm{\theta})\right|,

(3)

where the last expression indicates that
J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
is to be evaluated at
(\bm{x},\bm{v})=(\bm{x}(\bm{\theta}),\bm{v}(\bm{\theta}))
.
Alternatively, if we want a uniform prior for
\bm{\theta}
,
p_{\mathcal{U}(\bm{\theta})}=\mathrm{const.}
, the prior PDF for
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
should be

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{U}(\bm{\theta})}(\bm{x},\bm{v})={1\over|\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}|}\,p_{\mathcal{U}(\bm{\theta})}(\bm{\theta})\propto{1\over|\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}(\bm{x},\bm{v})|}.

(4)

This prior transformation is sometimes left implicit. For example,
R. Ferrer-Chávez et al. 2021
compared the efficiency of orbit fitting using standard sets of orbital elements and Cartesian state vectors, but did not explicitly account for the different priors induced by this transformation. As a result, the two parameterizations corresponded to different posterior distributions, making it difficult to isolate the effect of the reparameterization itself (see Section
IV
for more details).
When treated explicitly, the Jacobian determinant has been evaluated numerically in the exoplanet literature — e.g., by calculating the elements of
J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
using the well-known relation between
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
and elements
(
J. Skowron et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
, or by using automatic differentiation
(
W. Thompson et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
without deriving or implementing an analytic expression.

Although the determinant of this transformation has a simple analytic form that follows directly from the canonical phase-space volume element in Delaunay variables and has appeared in stellar-dynamical contexts
(
H. V. Peiris & S. Tremaine 2003
;
S. Tremaine 2020
, e.g.,)
, it does not appear to be routinely used in this form as a Jacobian determinant for prior transformations in exoplanet orbit-inference applications.
In Section
II
, we therefore review this derivation and present compact closed-form expressions for the Jacobian determinants associated with this transformation and its useful variants. These expressions make the prior conversion transparent and easy to implement in Bayesian orbit inference.
As an illustrative example of such an application, we revisit the formulation of the lens orbital motion by
J. Skowron et al. 2011
, which has been widely used in microlensing analyses (Section
III
). We point out a minor but conceptual error in their parameterization, present a modified derivation of their formula taking advantage of our analytic formulation, and explain why the result essentially remains
correct
, except that the definition of the longitude of the ascending node needs to be updated.
We also perform an explicit quantitative examination of the performance of such reparameterization for interpreting orbit data with incomplete phase coverage (Section
IV
).

II
Closed-Form Expressions for the Jacobian determinant

II.1
The Full Kepler Problem

Consider the Kepler problem with given total mass
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
be the Cartesian position and velocity vectors at a reference epoch
t_{\mathrm{ref}}
, respectively.
Let
a
,
e
,
i
,
\Omega
,
\omega
, and
M
be the semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of the ascending node, argument of periapsis referred to the ascending node, and mean anomaly at
t_{\mathrm{ref}}
, respectively.
Then the determinant of the Jacobian matrix for the transformation between
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
and
(a,e,i,\Omega,\omega,M)
is given by

\displaystyle\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}\equiv\det\left({\partial(\bm{x},\bm{v})\over\partial(a,e,i,\Omega,\omega,M)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mu^{3/2}\,a^{1/2}\,e\,\sin i,

(5)

where
\mu\equiv\mathcal{G}\mathcal{M}
with
\mathcal{G}
being Newton’s gravitational constant.
The
\sin i
dependence — along with the independence of
\Omega
and
\omega
— implies that the orbits are isotropically oriented when the joint distribution of
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
has a uniform density. The formula, in its present form, is valid only for bound orbits, for which
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
satisfy

\displaystyle{1\over 2}|\bm{v}|^{2}-{\mu\over|\bm{x}|}<0.

(6)

Derivation
— The Delaunay variables defined as

\displaystyle(l,g,h,L,G,H)=(M,\omega,\Omega,\sqrt{\mu a},L\sqrt{1-e^{2}},G\cos i)

(7)

form a set of canonical variables as are
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
for the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}={1\over 2}|\bm{v}|^{2}-{\mu\over|\bm{x}|}=-{\mu^{2}\over 2L^{2}}

(8)

defined as the energy per reduced mass
(
S. Tremaine 2023
, e.g.,)
.
The map between
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
and
(l,g,h,L,G,H)
is canonical, and we have

\displaystyle\det\left({\partial(\bm{x},\bm{v})\over\partial(l,g,h,L,G,H)}\right)=1.

(9)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\det J_{\rm Kep}=-\det\left({\partial(\bm{x},\bm{v})\over\partial(a,e,i,M,\omega,\Omega)}\right)=-\det\left(\frac{\partial(l,g,h,L,G,H)}{\partial(a,e,i,M,\omega,\Omega)}\right)=\det\left(\frac{\partial(l,g,h,L,G,H)}{\partial(M,\omega,\Omega,a,e,i)}\right)=\det\left(\frac{\partial(L,G,H)}{\partial(a,e,i)}\right),

(10)

where the last equality holds because
(l,g,h)=(M,\omega,\Omega)
.
2
2

2

The sign of the determinant flips depending on column ordering. Here we defined
\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
following the standard ordering of orbital elements, which is different from the natural ordering as implied by Delaunay elements. This results in sign flips in the determinant depending on whether the relevant permutation is even or odd, which also increases the risks of errors in the equations — though in practice, only
|\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}|
matters and the sign is irrelevant.

Furthermore, the last Jacobian is a lower triangular matrix:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(L,G,H)}{\partial(a,e,i)}=\begin{pmatrix}{L\over 2a}&0&0\\
{L\over 2a}\sqrt{1-e^{2}}&-{Le\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}&0\\
{L\over 2a}\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\cos i&-{Le\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\cos i&-G\sin i\end{pmatrix},

(11)

and its determinant is obtained by multiplying the diagonal elements.
Hence,

\displaystyle\det J_{\rm Kep}={L^{3}\over 2a}\,e\,\sin i=\frac{1}{2}\,\mu^{3/2}\,a^{1/2}\,e\,\sin i.

(12)

II.1.1
Expressions using Other Elements

One may prefer to use the orbital period
P
or mean motion
n=2\pi/P
instead of
a
; or the time of periastron passage
\tau
instead of
M=n(t_{\mathrm{ref}}-\tau)
.
The following relations can be used to convert the argument of
\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
:

\displaystyle{\partial M\over\partial\tau}

\displaystyle=-n=-\mu^{1/2}a^{-3/2},

(13)

\displaystyle{\partial a\over\partial n}

\displaystyle=-{2\over 3}{a\over n}=-{2\over 3}\mu^{1/3}n^{-5/3},

(14)

\displaystyle{\partial a\over\partial P}

\displaystyle={2\over 3}{a\over P}={2\over 3}(4\pi^{2})^{-1/3}\mu^{1/3}P^{-1/3}.

(15)

For example,

\displaystyle\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}^{(PM)}\equiv\det\left(\frac{\partial(\bm{x},\bm{v})}{\partial(P,e,i,\Omega,\omega,M)}\right)

\displaystyle={1\over 3}\mu^{3/2}a^{3/2}P^{-1}e\sin i={\mu^{2}\over 6\pi}\,e\sin i,

(16)

\displaystyle\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}^{(a\tau)}\equiv\det\left(\frac{\partial(\bm{x},\bm{v})}{\partial(a,e,i,\Omega,\omega,\tau)}\right)

\displaystyle=-{1\over 2}\,\mu a^{-1/2}e\sin i.

(17)

II.1.2
Reduced Jacobian Eliminating the Nodal Degree of Freedom

In some practical applications, one overall rotation of the coordinate system can be chosen so that a particular coordinate takes a prescribed value.
For example, in the microlensing application discussed in Section
III
, one may choose
\Omega
(or equivalently, the reference direction in the sky plane) such that the
y
-coordinate of the orbit vanishes at a given reference epoch. Imposing such a condition makes
\Omega
an implicit function of the remaining orbital elements, and the transformation from orbital elements to Cartesian phase-space coordinates effectively becomes five-dimensional.

Here we mainly focus on the case in which the reference direction is chosen so that the
y
-coordinate is fixed. In this representation, it is natural to work with the reduced Jacobian

\displaystyle J^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Kep}}\equiv{\partial(x,z,v_{x},v_{y},v_{z})\over\partial(a,e,i,\omega,M)},

(18)

which excludes both the redundant coordinate
y
and the corresponding independent variable
\Omega
. An expression for
\det J^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Kep}}
can be useful when one wishes to construct priors or coordinate parameterizations that reflect only the observable geometry, without carrying the extra degeneracy associated with
\Omega
.
While
\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
does not depend on
\Omega
,
\det J^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Kep}}
is clearly different from
\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
: compared to
J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
,
J^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Kep}}
removes the length
y
from the numerator, and the angle
\Omega
from the denominator, so its dimension differs from that of
J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
. We find that the full six-dimensional Jacobian factorizes as:

\displaystyle\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}=x\left.\det J^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Kep}}\right|_{y},

(19)

where the right-hand side of this equation is evaluated at a fixed
y
as follows: Given
y
, the orientation of the
xy
-axes is fixed, and so the longitude of the ascending node is determined implicitly as
\Omega^{*}=\Omega(a,e,i,\omega,M,y)
. The coordinates
(x,z,v_{x},v_{y},v_{z})
are then evaluated using this
\Omega^{*}
. This means both
x
and
\det J^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Kep}}
depend on the chosen value of
y
, but their product does not, as is the case for the left-hand side.

Derivation
— Because
\Omega
represents rotation within the reference plane,
\bm{x}
can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x(\bm{\theta})\\
y(\bm{\theta})\\
z(\bm{\theta})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Omega&-\sin\Omega&0\\
\sin\Omega&\cos\Omega&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\,\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\\
y_{0}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\\
z_{0}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\end{pmatrix},

(20)

where
\bm{x}_{0}
is the position vector that depends only on the elements other than
\Omega
, which we write as
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
. This yields

\displaystyle{\partial y\over\partial\Omega}=x_{0}\cos\Omega-y_{0}\sin\Omega=x.

(21)

Thus,

\displaystyle\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}=\det\left({\partial(x,y,z,v_{x},v_{y},v_{z})\over\partial(a,e,i,y,\omega,M)}\right)\cdot{\partial y\over\partial\Omega}=x\det\left({\partial(x,y,z,v_{x},v_{y},v_{z})\over\partial(a,e,i,y,\omega,M)}\right)=x\det\left({\partial(x,z,v_{x},v_{y},v_{z})\over\partial(a,e,i,\omega,M)}\right)=x\det J^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Kep}}.

(22)

From this derivation, it is straightforward to show that the pre-factor becomes
-y
when
x
is fixed instead. It is also possible to fix
v_{x}
or
v_{y}
, in which case analogous relations hold.

II.2
The 2D Kepler Problem

The orbits of transiting exoplanets have
i\approx\pi/2
by construction. If we further set
i=\pi/2
and
\Omega=0
or
\pi
, the orbit is restricted to the
y=0
plane, and the problem reduces to the 2D Kepler problem.
3
3

3

This reduction in dimensionality is qualitatively different from the one discussed in Section
II.1.2
, where one of the orbital elements is removed by expressing it as an implicit function of the remaining orbital elements. Here we are considering the case in which, for example,
i
is physically fixed to
\pi/2
, rather than being solved for from the other elements.

This is a common approximation when analyzing transit timing variations in multi-transiting systems
(
E. Agol & D. C. Fabrycky 2018
)
, where the deviation from
i=\pi/2
typically has a negligible impact on the observed transit times and the mutual orbital inclinations are often small. It is thus of interest to compute the Jacobian determinant in this case as well, although the merit of reparameterization using
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
has yet to be demonstrated for this class of problems. The result is:

\displaystyle\det J^{(4)}_{\mathrm{Kep}}\equiv\det\left({\partial(x,z,v_{x},v_{z})}\over\partial(a,e,\omega,M)\right)={\mu\over 2}{e\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}.

(23)

Derivation
— The derivation follows that of the 3D Kepler problem. The Delaunay variables in the 2D Kepler problem are:

\displaystyle\left(l,g,L,G\right)=\left(M,\omega,\sqrt{\mu a},L\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\right).

(24)

Thus,

\displaystyle\det J^{(4)}_{\mathrm{Kep}}

\displaystyle=\det\left(\partial(l,g,L,G)\over\partial(a,e,\omega,M)\right)=-\det\left(\partial(L,G)\over\partial(a,e)\right)

\displaystyle=-\det\begin{pmatrix}{1\over 2}{L\over a}&0\\
{1\over 2}{L\over a}\sqrt{1-e^{2}}&-{Le\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\end{pmatrix}={1\over 2}{L^{2}\over a}{e\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}={\mu\over 2}{e\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}.

(25)

III
Modeling of Lens Orbital Motion During Binary Microlensing Events

The simplest model of a microlensing event caused by a binary lens assumes that the lens binary is fixed during the event. But sometimes the orbital motion of a binary — namely changes in the sky-projected binary separation, rotation of the binary axis in the sky plane, and (to a subtler degree) motion along the line of sight — has a detectable impact on the microlensing light curve. This “lens orbital motion” effect can provide additional constraints on the orbital parameters of microlensing exoplanets that are generally difficult to obtain, and also constrain the physical dimension of the lens system via its orbital timescale.

Since the orbital period of a lens binary as typically detected by microlensing is longer than the timescale of the microlensing event itself, the orbital motion, even if detected, usually covers only a small fraction of the entire orbit
(
D. P. Bennett et al. 2010
, e.g.,)
. This motivates a standard practice to model lens orbital motion using the instantaneous position
\bm{x}
and velocity
\bm{v}
at a reference epoch chosen during the microlensing event, using a coordinate frame whose
x
-axis is aligned with the sky-projected binary axis at the epoch and
z
-axis points toward the observer; see figure 8 of
J. Skowron et al. 2011
. The magnification due to microlensing depends on
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
only through combinations normalized by the Einstein radius
r_{\mathrm{E}}
, and so the dimensionless parameters are introduced as
(
J. Skowron et al. 2011
)
:

\displaystyle\bm{x}=r_{\mathrm{E}}(s_{0},0,s_{z}),\quad\bm{v}=r_{\mathrm{E}}s_{0}\bm{\gamma}.

(26)

The dimensionless positions
(s_{0},s_{z})
and the velocity vector
\bm{\gamma}
,
which has the dimension of (time)
-1
, are used as the actual modeling parameters
(
V. Bozza et al. 2021
, e.g., VBBinaryLensing code by)
.
While this completes the description of the motion in the binary-oriented frame, the microlensing signal also depends on the motion of the source star relative to the lens orbit. This orientation at a reference time is specified by the angle
\alpha_{0}
, again defined with respect to the projected binary axis (
x
-axis) at the reference epoch. This angle
\alpha_{0}
, in turn, fixes the orientation of the binary orbit relative to the lens–source proper motion — so this degree of freedom corresponds to that of the longitude of the ascending node.

In summary, standard fitting parameters of the binary geometry used for optimization and posterior sampling are
(s_{0},s_{z},\bm{\gamma},\alpha_{0})
, while one often wants a prior PDF defined in the orbital element space. This is how the Jacobian determinant for the transformation between state vectors and orbital elements becomes relevant.

III.1
Jacobian in
J. Skowron et al. 2011

For the conversion of the prior PDF,
J. Skowron et al. 2011
proposed to compute the determinant of the matrix

\displaystyle j_{\rm kep}^{(\mathrm{S11})}\equiv{\partial(e,a,\tau,\Omega_{\rm node},i,\omega)\over\partial(s_{0},\alpha_{0},s_{z},\bm{\gamma})},

(27)

where
\Omega_{\mathrm{node}}
is the longitude of the ascending node defined with respect to the
sky-projected binary axis at the reference epoch
, or
x
-axis in the
binary-oriented frame
. They then argue that

\displaystyle\left|\det j_{\rm kep}^{(\mathrm{S11})}\right|^{-1}=r_{\mathrm{E}}^{-6}s_{0}^{-4}\left|\det\left({\partial(\bm{x},\bm{v})\over\partial(e,a,\tau,\Omega_{\mathrm{node}},i,\omega)}\right)\right|

(28)

holds, without providing detailed reasoning. Although they proposed to compute the last determinant using LUP decomposition of the matrix whose elements are computed via the standard relations between
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
and orbital elements, it can be computed analytically using Equation
17
derived in Section
II
. Indeed,
J. Skowron et al. 2011
correctly notes that this Jacobian determinant goes to zero in proportion to
e\sin i
— Equation
17
explicitly shows that this is the case for
any
values of
e
and
\sin i
.

III.2
Clarification of Their Derivation

Equation
28
is incorrect as written — the determinant of
j_{\rm kep}^{\mathrm{(S11)}}
defined by Equation
27
is
identically zero
, because none of the elements in the numerator depends on
\alpha_{0}
.
Here, it is essential to note that
\Omega_{\rm node}
in this equation is defined with respect to the
sky-projected binary axis
; thus, all of the orbital elements in the numerator are determined once the orbit and the line-of-sight direction (
z
-axis) are specified, regardless of the direction of the projected binary axis (
x
-axis) with respect to the lens–source proper motion, as specified by
\alpha_{0}
. This can be made more explicit by writing down the orbital elements using
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
in Equation
26
, as is also done in Appendix B of
J. Skowron et al. 2011
: The components of the Lenz vector
\bm{e}
and angular momentum vector
\bm{h}
in the binary-oriented frame can all be specified using the components of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
in the same frame, namely (
s_{0}
,
s_{z}
,
\bm{\gamma}
), without referring to
\alpha_{0}
. The same is true for the orbital elements referred to this frame, which are all scalars computed from the vectors
\bm{e}
and
\bm{h}
.

To correctly incorporate the information of
\alpha_{0}
into orbital elements, one needs to use
\Omega
defined with respect to the
reference direction that is not tied to the binary orbit
, such as the direction of the source trajectory at the reference epoch to which
\alpha_{0}
is referred, or the celestial north. If we choose the former — as
J. Skowron et al. 2011
separates the proper motion vector
\bm{\mu}
in the other block of the full Jacobian —
\Omega
is related to
\alpha_{0}
as:

\displaystyle\Omega=\Omega_{\rm node}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\pm\alpha_{0}+\mathrm{const.},

(29)

where the argument of
\Omega_{\rm node}
makes it explicit that this angle is determined by the other orbital elements
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=(e,a,\tau,i,\omega)
as in Section
II.1.2
,
and the “
\pm
” sign and constant depend on the convention on the positive direction and reference axis of the angle
\alpha_{0}
.
Regardless of these conventions, Equation
29
yields

\displaystyle\left|\det\left({\partial(e,a,\tau,\Omega,i,\omega)\over\partial(e,a,\tau,\alpha_{0},i,\omega)}\right)\right|=\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}\bm{1}&0\\
{\partial\Omega_{\rm node}\over\partial\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}&\pm 1\\
\end{pmatrix}\right|=1.

(30)

Thus, the corrected version of Equations
27
and
28
evaluates to:

\displaystyle\left|\det j_{\rm kep}^{\mathrm{(corrected)}}\right|^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left|\det\left({\partial(s_{0},\alpha_{0},s_{z},\bm{\gamma})\over\partial(e,a,\tau,\Omega,i,\omega)}\right)\right|\quad(\text{using\ correct}\ \Omega)

\displaystyle=\left|\det\left({\partial(s_{0},\alpha_{0},s_{z},\bm{\gamma})\over\partial(e,a,\tau,\alpha_{0},i,\omega)}\right)\right|=\left|\det\left({\partial(s_{0},s_{z},\bm{\gamma})\over\partial(e,a,\tau,i,\omega)}\right)\right|=r_{\mathrm{E}}^{-5}\,s_{0}^{-3}\left|\det\left({\partial(x,z,v_{x},v_{y},v_{z})\over\partial(e,a,\tau,i,\omega)}\right)\right|.

(31)

Using Equation
19
to evaluate the last Jacobian determinant and substituting
x=r_{\mathrm{E}}s_{0}
in the binary-oriented frame where
y=0
, we find

\displaystyle\left|\det j_{\rm kep}^{\mathrm{(corrected)}}\right|^{-1}

\displaystyle=r_{\mathrm{E}}^{-5}s_{0}^{-3}\left|\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}^{(a\tau)^{\prime}}\right|=r_{\mathrm{E}}^{-5}s_{0}^{-3}\left|{1\over x}\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}^{(a\tau)}\right|_{y=0}=r_{\mathrm{E}}^{-6}s_{0}^{-4}\left|\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}^{(a\tau)}\right|.

(32)

The final result numerically agrees with equation B6 of
J. Skowron et al. 2011
.

In summary, the formula given by
J. Skowron et al. 2011
is incorrect as written, in that their
\Omega
is defined with respect to the projected binary axis. When this error is corrected, their Jacobian determinant actually remains unchanged, because this determinant does not depend on the longitude of the ascending node in the first place, as shown in Section
II
.

III.3
Note on the “Uniform” Prior for the Orbital Elements

The above formulation by
J. Skowron et al. 2011
implicitly assumes that the prior PDF for
\bm{\theta}
be defined using
\tau
(rather than
M
), for which it is common and reasonable to adopt a uniform prior. This makes sense, but it may be worth noting that the normalization of the conditional prior
p(\tau|P)
affects the implied marginal prior on
P
. Because
\tau
is periodic, a sensible choice would be:

\displaystyle p(\tau|P)=\begin{cases}{1\over P}\quad&\text{for}\quad\tau_{\rm ref}<\tau<\tau_{\rm ref}+P\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(33)

where
\tau_{\rm ref}
is an arbitrary reference time.
If one then chooses a prior PDF for
P
as
\tilde{p}(P)
, the above choice ensures

\displaystyle p(P)=\int p(\tau,P)\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\int p(\tau|P)\,\tilde{p}(P)\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\tilde{p}(P),

(34)

as one would intend. In this case, the joint prior PDF for
(\tau,P)
does not have a uniform density in the (
\tau,P
)-plane even if
\tilde{p}(P)
is uniform:

\displaystyle p(\tau,P)=p(\tau|P)\,p(P)\propto{\tilde{p}(P)\over P}.

(35)

In some samplers like
emcee

(
D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
in which one defines a function that returns the logarithm of the prior probability density, the prior in Equation
33
needs to be implemented as
\log(\mathrm{prior})=\mathrm{const.}-\log P
, rather than
\log(\mathrm{prior})=\mathrm{const.}
.
If the “
-\log P
” term is omitted, the marginal PDF for
P
is:

\displaystyle p(P)=\int p(\tau,P)\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\int p(\tau|P)\,p(P)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\propto P\cdot\tilde{p}(P).

(36)

This prior gives more weight at longer
P
than Equation
34
because the corresponding range of
\tau
increases with
P
when
p(\tau|P)
is not properly normalized. This may not be the behavior one intends when specifying
\tilde{p}(P)
.

Equivalently, one can avoid this bookkeeping by using a phase variable normalized by the period, such as the mean anomaly at a reference epoch
M
, and assigning it a uniform prior
(
S. Blunt et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. When using
M
, for example, the resulting joint prior

p(M,P)=p(M|P)\,p(P)\propto\tilde{p}(P)
yields
p(\tau,P)=p(M,P)\,|{\partial M\over\partial\tau}|\propto\tilde{p}(P)/P
, which is equivalent to Equation
35
.

IV
Astrometry Fitting via Reparameterization using Cartesian State Vectors

Reparameterization using Cartesian state vectors can also be useful for orbit inference of directly imaged companions, particularly when the orbit is only partially observed and the constraints on the orbital elements are weak.
This idea has been explored in the literature
(
G. Chauvin et al. 2012
;
H. Beust et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
; in particular,
R. Ferrer-Chávez et al. 2021
performed a performance comparison and reported improved convergence when sampling in state vectors. For a fair Bayesian comparison between parameterizations, however, one must ensure that the same prior density is used in both spaces, i.e., account explicitly for how the prior transforms under reparameterization. Otherwise the implied priors (and hence the posteriors) may differ between parameterizations, making the performance comparison not directly comparable.
We therefore revisit this comparison using matched priors in the two parameter spaces, in order to isolate the impact of the reparameterization.
Here we employ a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm that makes explicit use of the gradient of the posterior probability density, as it has demonstrated a promising improvement of the sampling performance compared to random-walk based MCMC methods
(
W. Thompson et al. 2023
)
.

We simulated astrometric observations similar to those for HR 8799d analyzed in
Q. M. Konopacky et al. 2016
.
Specifically, we adopted the orbital elements in Table 2 of
K. K. O’Neil et al. 2019
, total mass of
1.5\,M_{\odot}
, and the parallax of
24.46\,\mathrm{mas}

(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023
)
as the true input parameters.
Then we drew a single set of simulated observed values of the RA offsets

), total mass

Q. M. Konopacky et al. 2016

This choice resulted in an orbital phase coverage of
6\%

For the errors of the total mass and parallax, we adopted
\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}=0.15\,M_{\odot}
and

In these regimes, reparameterization to Cartesian state vectors may offer a useful and broadly applicable alternative.

IV.1
Sampling from Cartesian State Vectors

Here we describe our method of sampling
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
from bound Keplerian orbits. In principle, for a random-walk Metropolis–Hastings sampler one could simply enforce the physical constraint by rejecting proposals with positive orbital energy. However, this approach can become inefficient in practice, as a substantial fraction of random-walk proposals fall into the unbound region and are rejected near the boundary, leading to poor mixing. We therefore construct a sampling scheme that generates proposals directly within the bound region. The same idea may also be useful for rejection-based samplers, where proposing only physically allowed orbits reduces wasted draws.

Given the total mass
\mu=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{M}
, we would like to sample
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
at a reference epoch from a uniform distribution whose support is the region of the
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
space corresponding to a bound orbit (i.e., negative total energy).
We implement this sampling in the following two steps: (i) first sample
\bm{x}
from a uniform distribution, and then (ii) sample
\bm{v}
uniformly so that the total energy remains negative, given the value of
\bm{x}
sampled in the first step.

Uniform Sampling of
\bm{x}

Given the total mass, the maximum orbital period translates into
a_{\mathrm{max}}
, the maximum possible semi-major axis. Given
a_{\mathrm{max}}
,
|\bm{x}|
must satisfy
|\bm{x}|<x_{\mathrm{max}}=2a_{\mathrm{max}}
. We thus sample isotropic
\bm{x}
that satisfies
x_{\mathrm{min}}<|\bm{x}|<x_{\mathrm{max}}
. The PDF for
|\bm{x}|
in this case is:

\displaystyle p(|\bm{x}|)={|\bm{x}|^{2}\over{x_{\mathrm{max}}^{3}/3-x_{\mathrm{min}}^{3}/3}},

(38)

whose CDF
F(|\bm{x}|)
for
x_{\mathrm{min}}<|\bm{x}|<x_{\mathrm{max}}
is

\displaystyle F(|\bm{x}|)={{|\bm{x}|^{3}-x_{\mathrm{min}}^{3}}\over{x_{\mathrm{max}}^{3}-x_{\mathrm{min}}^{3}}}.

(39)

So
|\bm{x}|
can be sampled by sampling
u_{x}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
and by computing
|\bm{x}|=\left[x_{\mathrm{min}}^{3}+u_{x}(x_{\mathrm{max}}^{3}-x_{\mathrm{min}}^{3})\right]^{1/3}
. To improve the numerical stability, here we also set a non-zero lower limit
x_{\mathrm{min}}
, which is chosen sufficiently small compared to the range allowed by the actual data. Note that
x_{\mathrm{min}}
cannot be simply related to either of minimum semi-major axis or maximum eccentricity.

This
|\bm{x}|
can then be multiplied by an isotropic 3D vector
\hat{\bm{x}}
whose norm is unity. This
\hat{\bm{x}}
is obtained by sampling each Cartesian component from independent normal distributions,
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
(
i=1,2,3
), and then by computing
\hat{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})/\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}}
. The full vector
\bm{x}
is then given as
\bm{x}=|\bm{x}|\hat{\bm{x}}
.

Uniform Sampling of
\bm{v}
Given
\bm{x}

Given
\bm{x}
, the norm of
\bm{v}
must satisfy

\displaystyle|\bm{v}|<\sqrt{\mu\left({2\over|\bm{x}|}-{1\over{a_{\mathrm{max}}}}\right)}\equiv v_{\mathrm{max}}(x)

(40)

so that the orbit is bound and has
a<a_{\mathrm{max}}
. The same algorithm as we used to sample
\bm{x}
allows us to sample from the PDF

\displaystyle p(\bm{v}|\bm{x})={1\over{4\pi\over 3}v_{\mathrm{max}}(x)^{3}},\quad 0<|\bm{v}|<v_{\mathrm{max}}(x).

(41)

However, this way we are not sampling from a uniform distribution for
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
, because the normalization of
p(\bm{v}|\bm{x})
in Equation
41
depends on
\bm{x}
: the joint PDF we are actually drawing from is

\displaystyle p(\bm{x},\bm{v})=p(\bm{v}|\bm{x})\,p(\bm{x})\propto{1\over{4\pi\over 3}v_{\mathrm{max}}(x)^{3}},

(42)

whose density is not uniform in the
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
-space.
6
6

6

This situation is analogous to what was discussed in Section
III.3
.
We therefore apply the log-probability correction
3\log v_{\mathrm{max}}(x)
after the above procedure, so that the resulting sampling is uniform over the bounded region in the
(\bm{x},\bm{v})
-space.

IV.1.1
Sampling via the Orbital Period

Sometimes additional data such as radial velocities may provide a better constraint on the orbital period
P
. Even with partial astrometry alone, the presence of other planets may provide information on the possible range of orbital semi-major axis (and hence orbital period). Including
P
as a sampling parameter may make it easier to incorporate such information.

This can be done by replacing one component of the Cartesian state vectors with
P
. One simple choice is to replace
|\bm{v}|
. Given
P
, the Kepler’s third law fixes the semi-major axis
a_{P}
, and hence the specific orbital energy
(1/2)|\bm{v}|^{2}-\mu/|\bm{x}|=-\mu/(2a_{P})
. Thus, given total mass,
P
, and
|\bm{x}|
, the norm of the velocity vector is uniquely determined as

\displaystyle|\bm{v}|_{P}=\sqrt{\mu\left({2\over|\bm{x}|}-{1\over a_{P}}\right)}.

(43)

Therefore, the sampling via
P
can be performed by (i) sampling
\bm{x}
as above, (ii) sampling
P
from a certain prior PDF, and then (iii) sampling
\hat{\bm{v}}
as above and by computing
\bm{v}=|\bm{v}|_{P}\,\hat{\bm{v}}
. Again, a caveat here is that this way we are sampling
(P,\hat{\bm{v}})
instead of
(|\bm{v}|,\hat{\bm{v}})
, and so the additional Jacobian correction for this transformation is needed. The Jacobian determinant corresponding to this transformation is:

\displaystyle\left|\det\left({\partial\bm{v}\over{\partial P\partial\hat{\bm{v}}}}\right)\right|=|\bm{v}|_{P}^{2}\left|{\partial|\bm{v}|_{P}\over\partial P}\right|=\mu{|\bm{v}|_{P}^{2}\over 2|\bm{v}|_{P}a^{2}}{2\over 3}{a\over P}={\mu|\bm{v}|_{P}\over 3a_{P}\,P}.

(44)

V
Summary

We have derived an analytic, closed-form expression for the Jacobian determinant of the transformation between Cartesian phase-space coordinates and the standard orbital elements in the Kepler problem. The resulting formula,

\displaystyle\det\left({\partial(\bm{x},\bm{v})\over\partial(a,e,i,\Omega,\omega,M)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mu^{3/2}\,a^{1/2}\,e\,\sin i,

is compact and straightforward to implement, and it enables transparent conversion of prior densities between the two parameterizations for Bayesian orbit inference without resorting to numerical Jacobians or automatic differentiation.

We illustrate the utility of this result in two settings. First, we clarify the widely used microlensing formulation of lens orbital motion by
J. Skowron et al. 2011
. We show that their definition of the nodal angle unintentionally ties the longitude of the ascending node to the sky-projected binary axis, rendering the intermediate Jacobian formally singular. When the nodal angle is instead defined with respect to an axis independent of the binary orbit, the corrected Jacobian is well-defined and practically remains unchanged, because
\det J_{\mathrm{Kep}}
carries no dependence on the longitude of ascending node.

Second, we quantify the impact of the same reparameterization on astrometric orbit fitting with a gradient-based MCMC sampler (NUTS). We find that reparameterizing to Cartesian state vectors improve the robustness and efficiency of the sampling over sampling in orbital elements. This provides a quantitative underpinning for the common but implicit expectation — also reflected in microlensing practice — that state-vector parameterizations can be advantageous in weakly constrained orbit inference.
