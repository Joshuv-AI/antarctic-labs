 Executive Summary
The purpose of this project was to develop innovative dryer technologies for the food and pulp &
paper industry sectors to reduce their corresponding drying energy by at least 25%. By working
on these novel drying technologies and the aid of smart sensors and a physics-based artificial
intelligence methodology, the foundation for the next generation energy efficient smart dryers
for the food and pulp & paper industry sectors have been developed. The innovative dryer
technologies developed are applicable to other energy intensive industries as well.

The novel technologies developed in this project are as follows,
 a- Slot jet reattachment nozzles for enhanced convective heating and drying
 b- Direct and airborne ultrasound technologies for dehydration of moist porous media
 c- Dielectrophoretic technology for extraction of vapor during drying of moist porous media

Furthermore, the following task has been accomplished as well.
 d- Integration of the above technologies for drying selected moist porous media.

The three novel drying technologies developed in this project have been scaled. Data show better
energy efficiency than conventional drying and increased production capacity through shorter
process times without negatively impacting the product quality.

In addition to the above technologies, novel sensors have been developed to measure in-situ
moisture content and strain within moist porous media during drying process. Fiber-optic based
moisture, humidity, and stress sensors that have been developed have also successfully been
tested in several large companies’ commercial units. Furthermore, physics-based artificial
intelligence optimization technique has been developed for selected drying cases.

Finally, two novel testbeds have been developed and fabricated in this project. The first testbed,
referred to as the Smart Dryer, is a fully electrical pilot-scale dryer that includes the above-
mentioned technologies. The second testbed is a unique contact ultrasound testbed. Since
commissioning, the Smart Dryer has been continuously used by large as well as by small
companies. It provides these companies with a cost-effective platform to develop the next
generation electric dryers. Because of its pilot-scale size, the data collected are applicable and/or
relevant for commercial scale systems.

Project Goals/Objectives
The purpose of this project was to develop innovative dryer technologies for the food and pulp &
paper industry sectors to reduce their corresponding drying energy by at least 25%. These
innovative dryer technologies realize improvement in product quality, increased production rates,
and reduced operating costs. Specifically, by working on these novel drying technologies and the
aid of smart sensors and a physics-based artificial intelligence methodology, the foundation for
the next generation energy efficient smart dryers for the food and pulp & paper industry sectors
are developed. The innovative dryer technologies developed are applicable to other energy
intensive industries as well.

Background
The background for this project is as follows,
 • Industrial drying consumes approximately 12% of total end-use energy in manufacturing
 or 1.2 quads annually. DOE estimates up to 40% or 0.5 quads can be saved with
 advanced drying technologies.
 • This project has developed novel drying technologies, advanced sensors and AI-enabled
 process optimization to deliver energy savings annually when adopted by food,
 paper/pulp, and other energy intensive industries.
 • This project makes U.S. industry more competitive by saving energy costs, reducing
 carbon footprint and process times, eliminating material wastes, and improving product
 quality when its technologies adopted by corresponding industries.
 • Three novel drying technologies have been developed and scaled. Data show better
 energy efficiency than conventional drying and increased production capacity through
 shorter process times.

I- Novel Smart Pilot-Scale Testbed
A novel testbed with a moving belt was designed in collaboration with the Reading Bakery
Systems (RBS). This pilot-scale testbed, located at WPI, was manufactured by RBS and is called
the Smart Dryer (see Figure I-1). The smart dryer’s CAD visuals are depicted in Figures I-2. The
smart dryer, fully electric, allows for various drying mechanisms to be included, making it
unique for development of energy efficient drying processes. The smart dryer is ~9m long with
an active drying length of 6.33m (20.3 ft). The conveyor belt is a mesh-type belt 61 cm (2 ft) in
width, and its speed ranges from 0.394-4.22 m/min. The baseline drying modules of the smart
dryer are convective air drying with perforated plates and radiative drying with infrared emitters.
It includes two main air plenums. A plug fan is connected to each plenum to provide the airflow,
as shown in Figure I-2. The bottom plenum is connected to a row of perforated plates which
cannot be interchangeable in contrast to the rest of the convective drying modules. The oven is
longer above the belt than below to accommodate the larger drying modules. Therefore, the
perforated plate modules are of various interchangeable buckets, as seen in Figure I-2. The Smart
Dryer’s active drying length is four-door-long, and one door space takes up three buckets or “one
module.” One door is blocked by the oven's control panel, as seen in Figure I-2. In addition,
there are three infrared emitters above the conveyor belt between every two doors.

II- Slot Jet Reattachment Nozzle (faculty member: Yagoobi at WPI)
The Slot Jet Reattachment (SJR) nozzle Technology Readiness Level (TRL) at the start of this
project was 4. The work conducted at WPI within the CARD (Center for Advanced Research in
Drying, an NSF Industry University Cooperative Research Center) had illustrated the superior
drying performance of the SJR nozzle versus commonly used perforated plate and slot jet
reattachment nozzle. Select results of this study are presented below. The work supported by
this DOE project was mainly focused on evaluating the performance of arrays of SJR nozzles
and more importantly, scaling them to retrofit into existing commercial ovens as well as
incorporating them into the next generation electric baking ovens. This scaling-up has been
successful and clearly has resulted in a significant reduction in energy consumption and
corresponding carbon footprint. In summary, the transfer of SJR nozzle technology has been
successful and its current TRL level is equal or higher than 7.

Under this DOE project, the SJR nozzle technology was an essential part of the Smart Dryer that
is frequently used by large and small companies (see Figure I-3). Furthermore, the integrated
performance of the SJR technology with others such as ultrasound (direct as well as airborne), IR
emitter, and DEP are presented in the later sections of this report.

The advantages of SJR nozzle against commonly used perforated plate and slot jet (SJ) nozzle
are as follows,
 • high heat transfer
 • control of incident pressure over product surface
 • feasible retrofitting of existing equipment
 • compact equipment

                                                   Page 12 of 68

The three comparison criteria are,
 • Identical mass flow rate
 • Identical fan power (most limiting scenario for SJR)
 • Identical peak pressure

Figure II-1 shows an SJR nozzle schematic with a 45o exit angle. In such configuration at the low
nozzle-to-surface spacing, turbulent mixing that happens at the boundaries of the free jet region
produces a secondary stream by mass entrainment and makes an oval-shaped reattachment on the
impingement surface. The secondary stream flow directs inward, under the bottom plate, and
outward, far from the nozzle center. Due to secondary stream phenomenon, a relative low-pressure
distribution is maintained. The intense turbulent flow is the dominant reason for the high heat and
mass transfer rates. Note that forming a wider turbulent reattachment area improves the transport
characteristics. A significant feature for the SJR nozzle is the nozzle exit angle that can be fixed
appropriately to generate net-zero or even negative force on the surface.

Figure II-1: Schematic of SJR nozzle with an exit angle of 45o.

As an example, Figure II-2 compares the drying characteristics of SJR nozzle with an exit angle
of 20o against SJ for sugar cookies under different comparison criteria. The enhancements
varied from 26% to 57% in drying time with SJR nozzle. Similar tests were conducted with
different products such as apple slices. Besides significant enhancements in drying time (or
rate), the SJR nozzle produced dried apple slices with less color deterioration and fewer
nutritional losses compared to those with the SJ nozzle. Therefore, SJR nozzle provides higher
drying rates while maintaining nutritional values at a desirable level.

III - Innovative technology for non-thermal drying of food and agricultural products:
Ultrasonic Contact Drying (faculty member: Feng initially at UIUC, currently at NCAT)
This project presents the development of a novel sub-pilot scale ultrasonic contact dryer for food
and agricultural dehydration with improved nutrient quality. This unique direct-contact
ultrasonic system provides uniform, repeatable, and homogeneous distribution of high-intensity
vibrations on the drying surface while avoiding the creation of stationary waves by
synchronously exciting multiple vibrating modes. More importantly, in a carefully designed
system, removal of moisture from food matrix can be achieved with minimal or no addition of
heat, thereby the heat-induced damage to the nutritional quality of food or food ingredient can be
minimized. The developed system has shown promising results in improving quality, reducing
energy consumption and drying time, and promoting sustainability. Additionally, a closed-loop
hot air-drying chamber with a rectangular cross-section was designed and fabricated to create a
(hot) air and ultrasound hybrid batch dryer. Non-destructive process analytical technologies were
developed to monitor and evaluate the moisture content and quality of the product during drying.
These results were used to develop predictive models for the dynamic optimization of the
process. Finally, SJR nozzle dryers, designed by WPI, were fabricated and retrofitted into the
                                                 Page 14 of 68

ultrasonic dryer and synergistic improvements were observed for both SJR and ultrasonic drying.
The results showed that ultrasonic contact drying can significantly reduce energy consumption
by more than 25% and improve product quality and nutrient retention.

 In this drying prototype, a novel multi-frequency, multimode, and modulated (MMM)

vibrating modes including harmonics and sub-harmonics of acoustic loads to create a uniform
vibration over the surface while avoiding the creation of stationary/standing waves by
synchronously excite several vibrating modes on a surface. Such a feature is ideal and crucial for
the drying system to provide a more uniform product.

Ultrasonic Contact Drying – Kinetics and Energy Reduction
A unique feature of direct-contact ultrasonic dehydration system is that the drying surface
undergoes vibration upon ultrasound exposure which has synergistic effect on moisture removal.
In essence, the relative magnitude of the surface tension at the liquid-air interface and ultrasonic
vibrational force controls droplet formation. This obviates the necessity of ultrasonic drying to
any warm-up and the free moisture on the product surface turns into mist instantly when
ultrasonic vibration is introduced, thereby most of the surface water could be removed rapidly.
As a result, drying kinetics of ultrasonic drying for a range of food and agricultural products
were evaluated and compared with conventional hot air drying (convection oven).
Drying of by-product in bioethanol production: Wet distillers’ grain (WDG) which is a by-
product in ethanol production from corn starch was dried using the ultrasonic drying prototype.
Dried distillers’ grain (DDG) was produced using ultrasonic drying and compared with hot air
drying and freeze drying. WDG, received from Flint Hills Resources, LLC in frozen form, was
stored in a cold room at -40°C. This WDG was a mechanically separated, high-protein distillers
grain produced from corn in a dry grind ethanol plant. Prior to each experiment, the samples
were thawed at room temperature for 24 hours. The initial moisture content of the WDG was
measured for each batch of samples and was approximately 76% (wet basis). Ultrasound was set
to the maximum power of 200 W, with the generator exciting the vibrating sheet at a central
frequency of 20 kHz and a sweeping frequency of 1 kHz. The sweep range was determined in
real-time using an adaptive control system to avoid the formation of stationary or standing
waves, which generate regions of high and low acoustic activity, typical of traditional ultrasonic
systems operating at a single frequency. To prevent overheating of the piezoelectric transducer
and reduce energy consumption, the ultrasound operated in pulsed mode (on/off mode).

Drying of crystal nanocellulose (CNC): Crystalline nanocellulose (CNC) has garnered increased
attention due to its renewable nature, abundance, and good mechanical properties. However, one
of the bottlenecks in its commercial production is the drying process, primarily because of the
low solid concentration of CNC suspension after isolation, requiring the removal of a large
amount of water to obtain dry products for the following utilization and saving shipping costs.
To address this problem, we utilized the ultrasonic drying technology developed at the
University of Illinois for CNC drying to improve product quality, reduce energy consumption,
and increase the production rate. In the drying process, the ultrasound is operated at 6-second-on
and 6-second-off mode. The effects of ultrasound power, circulation temperatures, and liquid
                                               Page 20 of 68

droplet volumes were studied. The aqueous CNCs concentrations were 1%, 2%, and 4%. The
droplet volumes were 5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µL. The ultrasonic drying was operated at 10%,
15%, and 20% of ultrasound power levels, and the surrounding temperature of 20, 40, and 60 ℃
was controlled by the air circulation heater. The air circulation heater constantly blew out an air
stream over the drying surface at 1.8 m/s. For a specific experiment, CNC solution was applied
to an aluminum foil attached to the drying surface in droplets after the temperature reached the
targeted temperatures. The weights of foil with samples were measured after a given time
interval. The CNCs dried with different drying methods were characterized by Fourier transform
infrared (FT-IR) spectra analysis, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, thermogravimetric analysis
(TGA), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and redispersibility to measure the quality and
property changes. Specific energy consumption for various drying methods was estimated, and
the potential for carbon dioxide reduction was evaluated.
Under the same temperature and airflow rate, ultrasonic drying enhanced drying rates, resulting
in at least a 50% reduction in drying time compared to hot air drying (Figure III-6 A-C). The
mean particle size of CNC from ultrasonic drying changed little with settle time, indicating good
redispersibility. In addition, the ultrasonic dried CNCs exhibited good stability, with the zeta
potentials ranging from –35 to –65 mV. Energy consumption of ultrasonic drying is significantly
reduced. With the same CNC concentration in the feeding solution, specific drying energies
follow the order of freezing drying > spray drying > hot-air drying > ultrasonic drying (Figure
III-6D-E). Depending on drying technologies and CNC feeding concentrations, ultrasonic drying
can significantly reduce CO2 equivalent emissions from 10 to 660 kg CO2 per kg CNC (Figure
III-6F). Moreover, compared to other methods, ultrasound is the only one that can reach net zero
emissions if powered by renewable electricity. The redispersibility tests proved hot-air drying
was inappropriate for CNC particle drying. In summary, ultrasound CNC drying provided an
energy-efficient, low-carbon footprint method to obtain CNC particles with nano properties.

The potential drying mechanism for ultrasonic drying of crystal nanocellulose (CNC) is shown in
Figure III-7. CNCs after hot air drying had rough surfaces, while CNCs after ultrasonic drying
had relatively flat surfaces. The enlarged surface area increases the evaporation rate, and the
reduced thickness facilitates water transportation to the surface. Thus, ultrasonic vibrating and
stretching enhance the drying process. Surface enlargement mainly works for low-power
percentage (or low ultrasound amplitude) ultrasound operations. During hot air drying, the
surface area decreases with water evaporation (Fig. III-7b), while the surface area is enlarged
during ultrasonic drying in low percentage mode. Fine droplets with a diameter magnitude of µm
are formed when high-power level ultrasound is applied. The drying process can be described as
the shrinking core model (Fig. III-7c). The water on the outer surface of the droplet is evaporated
in the hot air, and the CNCs move to the core with the evaporation process. Then, the water
inside the core diffuses to the surface to evaporate. The drying model for high-power level
ultrasound is like the spray drying model but can operate at a much lower temperature, such as
from room temperature to 60 ℃ for ultrasonic drying vs. above 175 ℃ for spray drying.

Drying of Paper Handsheet (batch operation)
A novel approach for paper drying using airborne ultrasound technology is presented. A unique
experimental setup is developed, and a systematic study is conducted using 23 factorial design of
experiments and Analysis of Variance. Three controlling factors are considered in the
experiments including the initial moisture content, basis weight, and refining condition. The
outcome of the experiments is compared with direct-contact ultrasonic drying of paper. The
results confirm that like direct contact, for airborne ultrasonic drying, the basis weight/thickness
of the sample is the most important factor in ultrasonic drying and it is followed by the effect of
initial moisture content. Using linear regression model, a correlation for predicting the total time
of ultrasonic drying is provided. Quality of the dried samples are evaluated, and the permeability
measurements confirmed the effect of pore characteristics on ultrasonic drying. The analysis for

                                           Page 24 of 68

energy consumption reveals that airborne ultrasonic drying is more efficient at higher moisture
contents.
Experimental Setup: A unique experimental setup was designed and assembled as shown in
Figure IV-1. The main components include the transducer part, electric power generator or EPG
(power amplifier and dynamic resonance controller) and sample holder. The sample holder is a
stainless-steel mesh with 5 mm size circular holes to allow for the moisture mist to escape from
the underneath of sample as well. The sound level around the transducer in free field is about 160
dB. For safety purposes, composite double layers soundproofing foams (quiet barrier specialty
composite, Soundproofing Cow company, Pennsylvania, USA) were used to reduce the sound
level around the transducer to less than 80 dB. This airborne transducer and EPG were purchased
from Pusonics S.L. According to the manufacturer, the transducer is composed of a piezoelectric
Langevin-type sandwich, a mechanical amplifier or horn, and an extensive radiator, that provides
the required impedance to match with the media. The transducer plate is made from titanium and
its dimensions are 43.3 23.4 3.14 cm. In this study, the ultrasound power is 225 W (the
maximum power recommended by the manufacturer) and working frequency is 21 kHz. The
weight of the samples was measured intermittently at 2 min intervals using a microbalance
(Sartorius BCE6200, with 0.001 g accuracy). The samples’ thickness was gauged using a digital
thickness gauge with 0.002 mm accuracy.

at atmospheric pressure (2256 kJ/kg) and PUS-rad is the radiated ultrasonic power to the sample.
This factor is determined as the ratio of the energy required for evaporation to the ultrasonic
power that is radiated to the sample. Knowing the surface area of the transducer plate (0.101 m2)
and the paper sample (0.0198 m2), out of the total applied power to the plate (225 W), the energy
radiated to the paper sample can be estimated as 44 W. This is determined by a simple view
factor calculation between two parallel plates of different sizes and with negligible loss through
the side openings. The view factor considers the percentage of energy leaving the transducer
plate and to be incident on to the sample surface.

Comparing Direct-Contact and Airborne Ultrasonic Drying: In direct-contact ultrasonic drying,
the moist sample must be directly in contact with the transducer, while in airborne ultrasonic
drying, the moist sample is placed underneath the transducer plate. The experiments for direct-
contact ultrasonic drying are carried out at 1.7MHz with 10 W power and airborne ultrasonic
drying experiments are conducted at 21 kHz and 225 W. The thermal heating for these two
setups is compared and it concluded that in direct-contact ultrasonic drying, the maximum
temperature of the transducer plate is 80 °C, while in airborne ultrasonic drying, the maximum
temperature is 40 °C. Therefore, the heating effect in direct-contact mechanism is substantially
higher compared to that of non-contact mechanism.
Since the diameter of the samples in direct-contact and non-contact ultrasonic drying is 1.98 cm
and 15.9 cm, respectively, the drying rates are reported in g/m2.s. Figure IV-4 compares the
drying rate and energy factor for S(softwood)-R(refined)-150%(DBMC)-160g/m2(basis weight)
dried using direct-contact ultrasonic drying and airborne ultrasonic drying. The energy efficiency
of the amplifiers for direct and airborne ultrasound units is assumed to be 100% in order to
exclude them in the energy factor calculations.

The general trend for both mechanisms is that the drying rate is higher at higher moisture
contents. However, by decreasing the moisture content, propagation of ultrasonic waves inside
                                                   Page 28 of 68

the sample is harder and as a result, the drying rate decreases. Therefore, the drying rate and
energy efficiency for ultrasonic drying of paper at moisture contents lower than 100% DBMC,
which is the typical moisture content at the entry of the dryer section, is estimated to be very low
and this is a limitation for ultrasound drying of paper. The low drying rate in the beginning for
direct-contact ultrasonic drying could be because higher frequencies have lower depth of
penetration inside the sample and therefore, drying has a “starting” period, initially. In addition,
the drying rate for direct-contact ultrasonic drying is an order of magnitude higher than non-
contact ultrasonic drying. This observation may also largely be influenced by the significant
heating effect generated in direct-contact ultrasonic drying, as discussed above. Although this
heating effect may not be always desirable, it has a major impact on the drying rate. Whilst the
papermaking machine is on a much larger scale, it should be notified that the evaporation rate in
papermaking machine is about 10 times higher than the drying rate for direct-contact ultrasonic
drying. In addition, the energy factors for direct-contact and non-contact ultrasonic drying are in
the same order of magnitude. One reason for low energy factor in ultrasonic drying might be due
to the bulging effect of the sample during drying, which results in imperfect contact with the
transducer in direct contact drying and increase in thickness at the edges in airborne ultrasonic
drying. Despite the higher drying rate for direct-contact ultrasonic drying, one of the biggest
challenges with this technology is that the sample must be in direct contact with the transducer
and therefore it is not adaptable to the current convective dryers. In this regard, non-contact or
airborne ultrasonic drying is the most ideal in terms of adapting to convective dryers. For paper
drying, one potential application of this technology is in the upstream papermaking machine to
control moisture profile in the cross-direction and reduce overheating. Another potential
application is the use of airborne ultrasound in the vicinity of the press section of a paper
machine. For batch processes, in the cases that low temperature drying is required to preserve
the properties of the products being dried, ultrasonic drying could be suitable, and it can be more
efficient in hybrid with other types of drying mechanisms. Nonetheless, the outcome of this
research is that at the current state, this technology is not ready for replacing the current
continuous drying technologies in industrial applications, such as paper drying, and more robust
and efficient designs are required. The biggest challenge in developing more efficient ultrasonic
transducers is to overcome the significant impedance mismatch between air and dense mediums,
which leads to substantial energy loss.
Conclusions: In this study, a straightforward comparison between high-power airborne ultrasonic
drying (21 kHz and 225 W) and direct-contact ultrasonic drying (1.7 MHz and 10 W) for hand-
sheet papers is provided. The results of 23 factorial design of experiments showed that for both
mechanisms, the basis weight/thickness of the sample has the maximum effect on total time of
ultrasonic drying and after that the initial moisture content. One of the most important take-
aways from this work is that ultrasonic drying is more effective at higher moisture content and as
the sample gets dried and the moisture content decreases, the drying rate and energy efficiency
decrease. Quality measurements for the dried samples by airborne ultrasonic drying agree with
the measurements for samples dried with direct-contact ultrasound. Compared to hot plate
drying, the brightness index and tensile strength of the samples are slightly improved by using
ultrasound mechanism. It was shown that permeability plays an important role in ultrasonic
drying behavior and the effect of ultrasound for samples with higher permeability is higher. In
addition, the thermal effect in direct-contact ultrasonic drying is very significant and it enhances
the drying rate but may also have undesirable impact on product properties. The energy
efficiency of airborne ultrasonic drying is in the same order of magnitude as direct-contact
ultrasonic drying. To be able to use this green technology in industrial applications, more
efficient designs for ultrasound transducers are required.

Drying Strawberries with Airborne Ultrasound and Integrated Dehydration Mechanisms
This study introduces a comprehensive investigation into the drying of strawberries using three
dehydration methods facilitated by a unique Smart Dryer developed in the Center for Advanced
Research in Drying (CARD) (see Figure I-1). This Smart Dryer integrates airborne ultrasound
(US) module, SJR nozzles, infrared (IR) emitters, and combinations of these technologies,
allowing for a systematic exploration of different drying mechanisms on the energy efficiency
and quality of drying of strawberries. Figure IV-5 illustrates the sequence of these technologies
assembled in the Smart Dryer. The results reveal significant differences in the impact of drying
methods on the quality and energy efficiency of the drying process. Key quality attributes of
dried strawberries, such as color and water activity, are evaluated to understand how each drying
method influences these parameters. Additionally, the study includes an analysis of power
consumption across the various drying techniques, providing insights into their energy efficiency
and sustainability.

Figure IV-5: Schematic of Smart Dryer with airborne ultrasound installed at its entrance.

Airborne Ultrasonic Drying in Batch Mode: Strawberry slices were subjected to dehydration
under a stationary airborne US transducer in batch mode under various conditions to evaluate the
efficacy of US drying technique. Initially, the study focused on comparing the wet basis moisture
content (WBMC) between strawberries dehydrated utilizing airborne US waves and those dried
through a conventional method, specifically by placing the samples in ambient air and allowing
them to dry over an extended period. Figure IV-6 shows the WBMC and drying rate variation for
drying with airborne US waves and with ambient air drying at temperature of 20◦C and relative
humidity of 57%. The WBMC curve for air drying is incomplete due to the lengthy drying
process, which took more than 24 hours for the strawberries to completely dry. This figure
shows that US dehydration with a power of 0.2 W/cm2 incident on the sample surface
significantly accelerates the reduction of moisture content in samples compared to air drying
alone. Thus, airborne US waves, which mostly remove water from the surface of strawberries
and enhance evaporation through thermal effects, can improve drying efficiency. While air
drying takes more than 24 hours to completely dry the samples, the US method achieves this in
only 85 minutes, demonstrating its potential as a faster and more effective dehydration technique.

The initial spike in drying rate can be attributed to the effective removal of surface moisture
using airborne US waves. This figure also shows the initial rise in temperature, which helps the
drying process, evaporation of water, and thus, increases the drying rate. This pattern highlights
the effectiveness of US drying in boosting drying efficiency, especially during the early stage. As
drying advances, the drying rate diminishes due to the depletion of surface moisture and the shift
                                                  Page 30 of 68

towards internal moisture removal, where moisture is more tightly bound and harder to extract.
Additionally, the rate of increasing temperature is reduced, and the temperature reaches quasi-
equilibrium conditions. This change causes drying to slow down, as moisture diffusion from the
interior to the surface becomes the limiting factor. The peak drying rate achieved was 0.062
g/min during the US drying of strawberries over 85 minutes. This process was conducted with an
incident power of 0.2 W/cm2, maintaining a 5mm distance between the sample and the
transducer plate, and with a sample thickness of 3mm, demonstrating the method’s effectiveness
under these specific conditions.

Figure IV-6: WBMC, drying rate, and sample temperature vs drying time for airborne ultrasonic dehydration at

As shown in Figure IV-7, two different sample holders were employed in this study. The first
sample holder was a solid plate placed underneath the sample, allowing drying only from the top
surface. The second sample holder was a mesh plate with small holes approximately 5 mm in
diameter. The results revealed a significant reduction in drying time when using a mesh plate,
with drying durations decreasing from 85 to 65 minutes—a notable 23% decrease compared to
using a solid plate. This enhanced performance of the mesh plate is attributed to the increase in
the exposure of the strawberry’s surface area to the ambient air. The effect of sample thickness
on US drying was studied as well by considering samples with 2 mm and 3 mm thicknesses. As
expected, the thinner samples experienced a more rapid reduction in drying time from 85 to 60
minutes (a reduction of 29% in drying time), achieving a higher peak in drying rate of 0.052
g/min. Figure 7 highlights that proximity to the ultrasonic transducer plays a vital role in the
effectiveness of the drying process. Samples located closer to the transducer (5mm away)
recorded a peak drying rate of 0.054 g/min, with a reduced drying time from 100 (12.5 mm
away) to 85 minutes, indicating a 15% reduction in drying time. This proximity facilitates a more
intense and direct transmission of ultrasonic energy, enhancing the energy transfer to the sample,
thus increasing the drying rate. The effect of varying power settings on the transducer - 0.175,
0.2, and 0.225 W/cm2 – on drying rate was analyzed too. The peak drying rate reached 0.065
g/min at 0.225 W/cm2 and the drying time was reduced from 100 to 70 minutes by increasing the
incident power to the surface of strawberries from 0.175 to 0.225 W/cm2, a 30% reduction in
drying time. As illustrated in Figure IV-7, the sample temperature was lower with a mesh plate
compared to a solid plate due to the increase in evaporation rate. Finally, Figure IV-7 also
presents the temperature profiles for samples with two different thicknesses, confirming that the
thinner samples experience faster drying time because of their reduced mass and a greater surface
area to volume ratio, leading to a rapid temperature rise.

Drying Strawberries with Integrated Drying Mechanisms: The combined effects of airborne US
module, IR emitters, and SJR nozzles on drying of strawberry slices were investigated using the
Smart Dryer (see Figure IV-5). The integration of these diverse technologies was explored to
develop a comprehensive approach to dehydration that leverages the unique advantages of each
method. This innovative drying strategy aims to improve the drying process while improving
product quality. Building on the findings from previous tasks, it has been observed that US
waves boost drying efficiency mainly at high moisture levels. Thus, the airborne US transducer
was installed at the entrance of the Smart Dryer. Initial experiments were conducted under two
conditions: one with a continuously moving conveyor belt at 6.25 mm/s, allowing samples to
pass under the transducer before entering the oven that was equipped with SJR nozzles and IR
emitters; and another with the belt being stationary, subjecting samples to US treatment in batch
mode before proceeding through the Smart Dryer operating in continuous mode. The analysis
revealed that US waves have a minimal impact on sample dehydration over short transit time of
75 seconds when the Smart Dryer’s belt is running, yielding in the WBMC reduction from 86%
to 85.9% under the transducer, indicating negligible impact. To enhance the impact of US waves
in continuous mode, either the residence time or the power applied to the transducer(s) must be
increased. Consequently, the airborne US transducer was utilized in a batch mode for 30 minutes
as a pretreatment to complement other technologies that followed within the Smart Testbed. The
data for WBMC, drying rate, and sample temperature were analyzed to evaluate the effectiveness
of this multi-mechanism drying system. As the base/reference case, strawberry slices were dried
inside the Smart Dryer with the stationary air at 50°C. It required over 8 hours (495 minutes) to
completely dry the samples. Figure IV-11 displays only the first 145 minutes of this reference
case due to its lengthy drying time. Figure IV-11 also illustrates the WBMC and drying rate as a
function of time for various combination of the targeted technologies. The data indicated
significant variations in drying performance among these various methods, with the combination
of all three technologies (US + SJR nozzles + IR) achieving the fastest drying time. Employing
the airborne US transducer solely in batch mode reduced drying time from 495 minutes (the
reference case) to 85 minutes, an 82% reduction. The SJR nozzles operating in continuous mode
decreased the drying time further to 64 minutes, an 87% reduction. The combination of SJR
nozzles (continuous mode) and the US transducer (batch mode for 30 minutes) reduced the
drying time to 54 minutes under the same conditions, demonstrating an 88% reduction in drying
time compared to the base case of drying at a stationary ambient temperature of 50oC. These
combinations also demonstrate that using the airborne US transducer for 30 minutes before
employing SJR nozzles can significantly reduce the time required for the SJR nozzles from 64
minutes (solely SJR nozzles) to 24 minutes (US + SJR nozzles). Utilizing SJR nozzles in
combination with three IR emitters also reduced the drying time from 495 minutes to 56 minutes,
an 88% reduction compared to the base case and a 12.5% reduction compared to using only SJR
nozzles. A brief exposure to IR emitters significantly enhances the drying time. The integration
of all three technologies was also investigated. Strawberries were exposed to the airborne US
transducer for 30 minutes in batch mode, followed by continuous operation of the SJR nozzles
and IR emitters. This approach achieved the shortest drying time of 46 minutes, an 89%
reduction compared to the reference scenario, highlighting the effectiveness of combining
different drying technologies. As shown in Figure IV-11, the convective drying with SJR nozzles
produces a high initial drying rate due to a high heat transfer taking place at the sample surface.
However, the peak drying rate for US waves occurs after a delay, attributed to the time required
to elevate the sample’s temperature, at which point the thermal effects of US waves become
more pronounced, leading to an increase in internal heat rather than merely affecting the
sample’s surface. This figure also shows the internal sample temperature in its center under
                                          Page 34 of 68

various conditions. The reference case exhibits the lowest and slowest rise in temperature among
all other methods. SJR nozzles, as expected, effectively increase the internal temperature.
Although US waves are typically considered a non-thermal drying method, they can also increase
the internal temperature of the sample because of the absorption of the US mechanical energy.
The effect of IR is evident, as it locally increases the internal sample temperature. As stated
previously, the combination of US module, SJR nozzles, and IR emitters resulted in the most
effective method to dehydrate strawberry slices. This method not only accelerates drying
significantly, it also results in reduced total energy consumption.

Product Quality Measurements: The color of samples dried under different conditions has been
measured and evaluated. Changes in color of the fresh and dried strawberry slices were
monitored with a reflectance colorimeter (LabScan XE, Hunter Associates Laboratories, Inc.)

plate. The L, a and b values were used to calculate the Browning Index (BI), a parameter to
measure the degree of brown color formation in the strawberry slices. It is also an important
parameter indicating if enzymatic and/or non-enzymatic browning reactions have taken place.

condition, impacts the color. The total color change value increases as the air temperature, IR

The microstructure of dried strawberry slices under different drying conditions was examined
using Environmental Scanning Electron Microscopy (ESEM). The images show that US drying
leads to a more collapsed cellular structure compared to the fresh sample. However, the structure
does not exhibit significant shrinkage or deformation, suggesting that US drying may be more
gentle than other methods. The convective drying using SJR nozzles results in a more fibrous
texture, indicating a more pronounced shrinkage and potential hardening of the cellular walls.
The surface seems less porous than the fresh sample, which might affect rehydration capacity.
This combination method appears to create a structure that is more similar to US drying alone but
with added fibrous textures similar to convective drying. It suggests that the combination may
mitigate some of the harsher effects of convective drying alone. The combination of convective
and IR drying, a noticeable presence of small cracks within the structure was observed, which
suggests that the IR may contribute to a more rapid heating and water evaporation on the surface,
leading to a more pronounced shrinkage and cracking. The texture changes implied by these
image analyses would also impact the rehydration ability and the textural properties of the dried
strawberries. The results indicate that the fresh strawberry has a relatively high mean roughness
(Sa), maximum height (Sz), and root mean square roughness (Sq). This suggests a fairly rough
and irregular surface, which is expected for the undried, natural texture of a strawberry. US
drying dramatically reduces the Sa and Sq values and significantly reduces the Sz value
compared to the fresh sample. This indicates that the US drying process creates a much smoother
surface, potentially because the ultrasonic waves evenly remove moisture without causing much
deformation or damage to the surface structure. The roughness parameters are lower than the
fresh sample but higher than the convective drying. This method yields a smoother surface than
the fresh sample but is not as smooth as the surface after convective drying, suggesting that while
convective drying removes moisture effectively, it might not preserve the surface structure as
well as US drying. Using IR results in the lowest values for Sa and Sq, and a significant
reduction in Sz compared to the fresh sample. This indicates an even smoother surface than both
the US and SJR nozzle convective drying alone. Overall, the roughness parameters correlate
with the textural changes observable in the microscope images. The fresh strawberry’s texture is
the most irregular, with the highest roughness parameters. US drying preserves some of this
texture but smoothens it out considerably. Convective drying with SJR nozzles alone creates a
texture that is less rough than the fresh sample and with US drying. Combining convective
drying with IR seems to result in the smoothest texture among the methods evaluated, which
could be beneficial or detrimental to the final product quality, depending on the desired
characteristics of the dried strawberry.

among the drying methods considered, suggesting that this method may preserve the structural
integrity of the strawberries better than the others. The rehydration ratio is the highest with the

absorbs less water compared with US drying. The combination of US and SJR nozzles presents
                                          Page 37 of 68

a tensile strength close to that of convective drying alone, but with a lower tensile strain. Its
rehydration ratio is higher than convective drying with the SJR nozzle but lower than ultrasonic
drying alone. This method might be a compromise between maintaining structural integrity and
achieving a specific texture. Drying in the oven in batch mode results in the lowest tensile
strength, which may imply that the strawberries are softer or more easily broken under tension
compared to the other methods. However, the tensile strain is high, which suggests a degree of

In summary, for the drying of strawberries, if the goal is to achieve a balance between structural
integrity and rehydration capacity, airborne US might be the preferred method. For the highest
elasticity and stretchability, SJR nozzles might be desirable. The combined methods (Ultrasonic
and Convective, and IR) seem to offer intermediate results, and the choice between them would
depend on the specific desired texture and rehydration properties.

V- Enhancement of Drying Rate of Moist Porous Media with Dielectrophoresis Mechanism
(faculty member: Yagoobi at WPI)
A novel technology, making use of the Dielectrophoresis (DEP) mechanism, is introduced to
enhance the drying process of a moist porous medium. DEP is a translational motion of neutral
matter caused by polarization effect in a non-uniform electric field. During drying, due to the
existence of an electric permittivity gradient between liquid and vapor interface, the DEP force
can act as an external force to effectively extract the vapor phase away from the product. This in
turn enhances the evaporation rate. This study investigates the influence of the DEP force on the
drying of moist paper handsheet samples. Specifically, the temperature profile and sample
weight are monitored under various applied electric potential. The non-uniform electric field is
generated with a unique electrode design. The experimental results show a significant impact of
the DEP force on the temperature profile and drying rate. In addition, the results also show
extremely low energy consumption and high energy efficiency associated with the application of
the DEP force for drying. With the application of the electric field, up to 10 OC drop in surface
temperature and a 33.6% reduction in drying time have been achieved for the tests conducted.
The handsheet samples were made from hardwood fibers, with a diameter of 159 mm and a
thickness of 0.4 mm. The initial DBMC of the samples was 680% ± 5%, corresponding to the
condition in the vicinity of a paper machine press section. The DEP electrode and lab-scale
experimental setup are shown in Figures V-1. A hot plate was used to supply a constant
temperature underneath the sample. The hot plate along with samples were placed on a
microbalance scale to simultaneously monitor sample weight as a function of time. To prevent
any perturbation from the ambient environment, a chamber made of plexiglass was used to
accommodate the entire setup. An infrared (IR) thermal camera (FLIR, T540sc) and a fiber optic
temperature sensor (Micronor, TS5), along with a signal conditioner (Micronor, FOTEMP4-
PLUS), were used for the surface and internal temperature measurements, respectively.

VI - Fiber optical humidity, moisture, and strain sensors (faculty member: Liu at WPI)
Fiber optical humidity and moisture sensor
Sensor Mechanism: We developed an optical fiber moisture content to enable the real-time
monitoring of the environmental relative humidity (RH) and moisture content (MC) inside the
product sample in drying, and thus by providing the real-time measurements, we can achieve the
feedback control of the smart dryer to maximum the energy efficiency and minimize the product
waste. Figure VI-1(a) shows the design of our optical sensor based on lossy-mode-resonance
(LMR). The fiber is side polished to nearly expose the core and then a hydrophilic dielectric film
was coated on the polished surface. In the LMR sensor, light that is guided in the fiber’s core
mode can be coupled into the lossy mode of the coated sensing layer on the polished surface. The
refractive index of the sensing film will change when it absorbs water molecules, which will
cause the LMR wavelength to shift and hence allow the RH/MC sensing. Since the optical fiber
sensor is small, highly sensitive, and can sustain harsh environments such as high temperature
and strong electromagnetic field, it is compatible with the smart dryer to enable the real-time and
in-situ monitoring of the drying process.

experimentally calibrate the sensor’s LMR spectra obtained at different RH from 7.6% to 85.7%
were shown in Figure VI-2(a). By linearly fitting the optical power at the 1336-nm wavelength

Humidity measurements: The humidity inside the dryer indicates the water evaporation rate from

Fiber optical strain sensor
Measurements of strain in food offer valuable insights into the food drying process, which can be
utilized to optimize energy consumption during drying without compromising food quality. For
foods, the soft structure and having large deformations (strains) during drying are characteristics
that make the measurements of the deformations a challenging task. To address this, an optical
fiber strain sensor has been developed following a literature review and considering food
properties and mechanical behaviors.
Sensing mechanism and sensor fabrication: As shown in Figures VI-5(a) and (b), the sensor
comprises an optical fiber sliding within a glass tube, with a mirror (metal disk) attached at one
end. Optical signals travel through the fiber core and reflect off the mirror. A portion of this
reflection returns to the fiber, which is measurable. The tube, mirror, and a segment of the fiber
are embedded in a soft silicone rubber buffer layer. The sensor readout system is depicted in
Figure VI-5(c). Light emitted by the light source travels through the fiber circulator and is
directed toward the sensor located at the fiber tip. Reflected light from the sensor travels back
through the circulator and is directed to a photodetector. The photodetector converts the optical
power of the reflected light into a proportional voltage output. When inserted into food samples,
the rubber layer contacts the food’s surface. As the food shrinks during drying, the rubber
contracts, moving the fiber closer to the mirror. This movement alters the reflected power
measured within the fiber. The sensor’s optical design enables it to operate effectively under
large deformations, while its soft structure ensures accurate measurement of food shrinkage.
COMSOL simulations indicate the sensor’s capability to measure strains of up to 20%. As
illustrated in Figures VI- 5(d) and (e), prototypes of the sensor have been successfully fabricated
based on the design through multiple steps. The fabrication process of the fiber optic food strain
sensor is aimed at miniaturizing its footprint, resulting in varying sensor sizes. Through this
process, the smallest viable sensor dimensions achieved were 3 mm in length and 1.2 mm in
diameter (Figure VI-5(f)), ensuring optimal sensor form. Different soft silicone rubber buffer
layers, varying in hardness, were evaluated to determine the most suitable material properties for
accurate measurements. A dedicated calibration setup was designed and implemented for the
                                          Page 46 of 68

fiber strain sensor. Calibration data were obtained for multiple sensors, focusing on three key
parameters: optical signal from the fiber strain sensor, the strain of food-mimicking rods, and
sensor strain captured through camera imaging. The calibration data enables each strain sensor to
accurately measure real-time strains in food during drying experiments.

 tube and disk. e) Gluing the fiber and buffer sleeve to the glass tube. f) Fabricated optical fiber
 strain sensor.

Conventional methods struggle to measure under-surface strains in banana slices because of their
soft texture, high moisture content, non-uniform and substantial deformations during drying, and
significant changes in mechanical stiffness. Cavendish bananas, with a Brix value between 20%
and 25%, were purchased from local food markets and peeled. These bananas were then sliced

from the banana slice sidewall along the radial direction, providing strain measurements in the
radial direction of the banana slice at the sensor location. To demonstrate local strain

VII- Development of Humidity and Temperature Sensors Based on Graphene Field-effect
Sensors (Faculty member: Nam initially at UIUC, currently at UC-Irvine)
In this task, we developed a robust, efficient, and highly cost-effective wireless graphene-based
humidity and temperature sensor. It features high accuracy and capability to operate under harsh
conditions, including high temperatures, strong air flow, strong electromagnetic waves,
vibrations and fast movements. The sensor’s conductive channel is made from a heterostructure
of graphene and TiO2 thin film. Changes in the conductivity of the graphene channel were
detected when ambient temperature or humidity change. In order to decouple the signals from
temperature and humidity, we developed both encapsulated and unencapsulated channels to
detect the temperature and humidity separately. The wireless communication function is
achieved by a custom-designed wireless circuit board, which connects to the graphene
conductive channels via wire bonding and soldering. The sensor was then packaged to protect it

VIII- Millimeter-wave/Terahertz Remote Volumetric Moisture Sensor (Faculty member:
Petkie at WPI)
This group focused on sensor development for volumetric moisture sensing of paper based on
electromagnetic (‘optical’) sensing using a standoff, non-destructive, and real-time method. The
sensor developed operators in the electromagnetic spectrum between the microwave and infrared
regions, called here the millimeter-wave (mmW) and terahertz (THz) regions, mmW/THz, where
interaction with targets varies dramatically from the lower region (frequency = 30 GHz,
wavelength = 1 cm) to the upper region (3000 GHz, 0.1 mm). Most dielectrics (e.g., dry paper
and low moisture food) have medium to high transparency, while liquid water is a strong
absorber. As you increase the frequency and/or the antenna/optical size, the spatial resolution of
the sensor can increase should evaluating the uniformity of the product be required. The team
studied signature science associated with the sensor in detail to select the operating frequency
and optimize the performance, evaluated the different mmW/THz sensing technologies that
could be applied, and implemented the selected sensor on the smart dryer testbed.
The technical objectives progressed from years one, two, and three. The first-year task focused
on the development, characterization, and optimization of the mmW/THz sensor module and
signature science based on the type of product, paper in this case, and coordinated with the other
members of the sensors team and the other thrust areas for the build-out of the smart dryer
platform. This included an extensive set of measurements from different systems on a variety of
paper samples. From years one to two, we transitioned to identifying a viable mmW/THz
technology platform and creating a sub-scale prototype while continuing to build out the
signature model to measure the moisture content in the production of paper to feedback into the
models to optimize the performance of the smart dryer. The last year of the program was spent
on the mmW/THz sensor system on the Smart Dryer which
was tested and implemented.
One of the fundamental keys to developing the mmW/THz
sensor is to identify the basic physics surrounding the
radiation's interaction with the target and how the moisture
levels affect the signal. The electromagnetic permittivity, or
complex dielectric constants, of liquid water and paper
(cellulose) products are the primary physical properties for
mmW/THz active standoff sensing. The modality of Figure VIII-1: Optical properties of water in
the sensor was planned to either be in reflection or the 50-3000 GHz range showing the drastic

vary depending on implementation), and the dielectric constant, the index of refraction, n, and
the absorption coefficient, α, in moist paper determine the fraction of reflection, absorption, and
transmission of the mmW/THz probe. In the case of paper, scattering did not need to be

combined our experimental drying measurements with these models to select the operating

situ measurement for the current study (and future developments) and guide any calibration

Process Optimization: Smart dryer model development and optimization
We developed an AI/ML based framework for the smart dryer at WPI to determine the best order
sequence of dryer modules to be used as well as the process parameters in each module. Based
on the physics-based drying model, we developed a C++ version of the model that offers two key
advantages: (1) an approximately 140-fold increase in computation speed compared to the
original Python model under the same test conditions, without compromising accuracy; and (2)
improved stability by reducing the simulation time-step size without incurring a significant
computational cost burden, thanks to the substantial speed improvements in (1). The model also
includes Python and MATLAB bindings to interface with various machine learning and
optimization software. Utilizing the fast C++ model, we performed rapid experimental validation
and aligned the simulation output with experimental results.

The drying model offers a great variety of optimizable parameters: (1) global parameters,
including air temperature, air flow rate, IR emitter surface temperature, and machine speed; and
(2) local parameters, namely the choice among multiple drying modules (perforated plate (PP)
nozzle; slot jet reattachment (SJR) nozzle; dielectrophoresis (DEP) mechanism; and solid plate
(SP)), which is also bound by multiple design constraints. We identified three types of
optimization methods including Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) [1], Non-Dominated
Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) [6], and Reinforcement Learning-guided Constrained
Beam Search (RLCBS) [3] to suit different optimization needs. A comparison of these

Conclusions
The following three novel technologies were developed in this project.

 a- Slot jet reattachment nozzles for enhanced convective heating and drying
 b- Direct and airborne ultrasound technologies for dehydration of moist porous media
 c- Dielectrophoretic technology for extraction of vapor during drying of moist porous media

Furthermore, integration of the above technologies was accomplished for drying selected moist
porous media. A physics-based artificial intelligence optimization technique was developed for
selected drying cases as well.

The three novel drying technologies developed in this project have been scaled. Data show better
energy efficiency than conventional drying and increased production capacity through shorter
process times without negatively impacting the product quality.

In addition to the above technologies, novel sensors have been developed to measure in-situ
moisture content and strain within moist porous media during drying process. Fiber-optic based
moisture, humidity, and stress sensors that have been developed have also successfully been
tested in several large companies’ commercial units.

Finally, two novel testbeds have been developed and fabricated in this project. The first testbed,
referred to as the Smart Dryer, is a fully electrical pilot-scale dryer that includes the above-
mentioned technologies. It can operate in batch as well as in continuous modes. The second
testbed is a unique contact ultrasound testbed operable in batch mode. Since commissioning, the
Smart Dryer has been continuously used by large as well as by small companies. It provides
these companies with a cost-effective platform to develop the next generation electric dryers.
Because of its pilot-scale size, the data collected are applicable and/or relevant for commercial
scale systems.
