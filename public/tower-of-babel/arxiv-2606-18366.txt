A closed-form sample size correction for always-valid inference with optional stopping
Mårten Schultzberg

Abstract
Sequential tests that allow continuous monitoring are common in A/B experimentation. Power calculations for these tests require simulations that are hard to scale across many metrics on an experimentation platform. Instead, a common sizing heuristic inflates the fixed-sample size until the marginal rejection probability at the planned endpoint reaches 1 − β 1-\beta . This last-point rule is conservative because always-valid (AV) power is the probability of a boundary crossing at any time during the run, not at the endpoint alone. We give a closed-form correction factor k ∗ ​ ( α , β , t 0 ) k^{\ast}(\alpha,\beta,t_{0}) expressed in elementary functions and the bivariate normal CDF, where t 0 = m / n z t_{0}=m/n_{z} is the burn-in fraction. The closed-form approximation depends on the boundary only through its value and slope at the planned endpoint and can be evaluated for any smooth concave boundary. We work out three cases: the confidence sequences of 13 and 7 , and the mixture sequential probability ratio test of 6 . Setting the total sample size to k ∗ ⋅ n z k^{\ast}\cdot n_{z} , where n z n_{z} is the fixed-sample size for allocation ratio r r , hits empirical power within approximately 3 3 percentage points of target in Gaussian simulations. The correction factor depends on the allocation ratio r r only through t 0 = m / n z ​ ( r ) t_{0}=m/n_{z}(r) . We study sensitivity to the burn-in parameter and show that the correction saves 8 8 – 20 % 20\% of the last-point sample budget across the operating range.



1
Introduction

Sequential tests that control type-I error under continuous monitoring without a
pre-determined max sample size are widely deployed on experimentation platforms

[
13
,
7
,
6
]
. They allow experiments to stop
early when evidence is clear, but sizing them correctly requires
simulation-based power calculations that are expensive at platform scale. A
common heuristic is the last-point rule
[
4
]
, which inflates
the fixed-sample size
n_{z}

until the marginal rejection probability at the planned endpoint reaches

1-\beta
. This heuristic is conservative because AV power includes
boundary crossings before the endpoint, not only at it. At
(\alpha,1-\beta)=(0.05,0.80)
the rule delivers empirical power between
0.86
and
0.88
,
oversizing by seven to nine percentage points.

We give a closed-form factor
k^{\ast}(\alpha,\beta,t_{0})
such that

n=k^{\ast}\cdot n_{z}
approximately hits target power without simulation,
where
t_{0}=m/n_{z}
is the burn-in fraction. The closed-form approximation
depends on the boundary only through its value and slope at the planned
endpoint and can be evaluated for any smooth concave boundary. We work out
three cases: the WSKR confidence sequence
[
13
]
, the Maharaj
confidence sequence
[
7
]
, and the
6

mSPRT. The saving over the last-point rule ranges from
8\%
to
20\%

across boundaries, burn-in values, and the
(\alpha,\beta)
grid.

2
Setup

Two-sample experiment.

Two arms with control mean
\mu_{C}
, treatment mean
\mu_{T}
, true effect

\delta=\mu_{T}-\mu_{C}
, and common variance
\sigma^{2}
. Observations
are i.i.d. within arm with finite
(2+\varepsilon)
-th moment.
Allocation ratio
r=n_{C}/n_{T}\geq 1
, total sample size
n=n_{T}(1+r)
,
standardised effect
\Delta=\delta/\sigma
. We write

\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}
for the planned minimum detectable effect.
The hypotheses are
H_{0}:\delta\leq 0
versus
H_{1}:\delta>0
at
one-sided level
\alpha
and target power
1-\beta
. The fixed-sample

z
-test sample size is
[
3
,
14
]

n_{z}=\frac{(1+r)^{2}}{r}\cdot\frac{(z_{\alpha}+z_{\beta})^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}^{2}},

(1)

where
z_{p}=\Phi^{-1}(1-p)
. Under equal allocation,

n_{z}\cdot\Delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}^{2}/4=(z_{\alpha}+z_{\beta})^{2}
,
where
\Delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}=\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}/\sigma
.

Sequential boundaries.

We consider three sequential testing boundaries. The burn-in
parameter
m
is the smallest
total
sample size at which the
confidence sequence is evaluated. In practice,
m
is a small
constant (e.g.
m=20
total, giving
10
per arm at
r=1
)
chosen so that the central limit approximation is reliable, typically

m\ll n_{z}
. The value of
m
affects both the boundary shape and the
monitoring window; Section
4.4
studies its impact
on
k^{\ast}
. For general
r
, define the standardised cumulative process

Z_{n}:=\frac{\hat{\delta}_{n}\sqrt{r\,n}}{\sigma\,(1+r)},\qquad\hat{\delta}_{n}=\hat{\mu}_{T,n}-\hat{\mu}_{C,n},

(2)

where
n
is the total sample size. Since

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\delta}_{n})=\sigma^{2}(1+r)^{2}/(rn)
, we have

\mathrm{Var}(Z_{n})=1
for every
r
. At
r=1
this reduces to

Z_{n}=\sqrt{n}\,\hat{\delta}_{n}/(2\sigma)
. The rejection event by
sample size
n
is
\{\tau\leq n\}
where

\tau:=\inf\{n\geq m:Z_{n}>f(n)\}
and
f
is the boundary
specific to the confidence sequence.

WSKR boundary.

13
establish a confidence sequence valid
uniformly over sample sizes
j\geq m_{\mathrm{arm}}
, where
j
is
the per-arm count. Applied to the
n/2
paired differences

D_{i}=X_{T,i}-X_{C,i}
at equal allocation, the CI becomes

\bar{C}_{n}^{(m)}(\alpha)=\hat{\delta}_{n}\pm\hat{\sigma}_{D,n}\,\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(n/m)}{n/2}},\qquad n\geq m,

(3)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{D,n}
is the sample standard deviation of the
paired differences and

\Lambda_{\alpha}:=\Psi^{-1}(1-\alpha)
with
\Psi
the CDF of the
Robbins-Siegmund limiting distribution
[
8
]

(
\Lambda_{\alpha}=9.50,7.67,6.35,4.93
at
\alpha=0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10
).
We follow the symmetric-CI calibration of
13
, in which

\Lambda_{\alpha}
controls the simultaneous two-sided miscoverage at
\alpha
, so
the one-sided test of
H_{0}:\delta\leq 0
has type-I error at most

\alpha
.
The closed-form analysis below replaces
\hat{\sigma}_{D,n}

with the known
\sigma_{D}=\sqrt{2}\,\sigma
, moving from the distribution-free
WSKR guarantee to a Gaussian approximation. The boundary in
Z_{n}

units is
f_{\mathrm{W}}(n)=\sqrt{\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(n/m)}
.

Maharaj boundary.

7
construct an asymptotic confidence sequence from
a Gaussian-mixture supermartingale calibrated at miscoverage
\alpha
,
with tuning constant

\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}(\alpha)=-W_{-1}(-\alpha^{2}/e)-1
, where

W_{-1}
is the lower real branch of the Lambert-
W
function

[
5
]
. The boundary in
Z_{n}
units is

f_{\mathrm{M}}(n)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}\cdot\frac{2(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}n/m+1)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}}\,\log\!\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}n/m+1}}{2\alpha}\right)}.

(4)

mSPRT boundary.

The mixture sequential probability ratio test

[
6
]
averages the likelihood ratio over a Gaussian
mixing distribution
N(0,\sigma_{\tau}^{2})
on the effect size,
producing a test statistic that coincides with the Bayes factor under
the same prior. Setting the mixing standard deviation to the MDE,

\sigma_{\tau}=\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}
, with

\kappa_{n}=\sigma_{\tau}^{2}/\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\delta}_{n})=\Delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}^{2}\,r\,n/(1+r)^{2}
,
the symmetric rejection rule
\mathrm{BF}_{n}>1/\alpha
, equivalent
to
|Z_{n}|>f_{\mathrm{J}}(n)
, gives the boundary

f_{\mathrm{J}}(n)=\sqrt{\frac{2(1+\kappa_{n})}{\kappa_{n}}\left(\log\frac{1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1+\kappa_{n})\right)}.

(5)

For the one-sided test
H_{0}:\delta\leq 0
, we use the positive
branch
Z_{n}>f_{\mathrm{J}}(n)
. Since

\{\exists n:Z_{n}>f_{\mathrm{J}}(n)\}\subseteq\{\exists n:|Z_{n}|>f_{\mathrm{J}}(n)\}
, the one-sided type-I
error is at most
\alpha
. For the composite null
\delta\leq 0
,
the crossing probability under any
\delta<0
is strictly smaller
than at
\delta=0
because
Z_{n}
under
\delta<0
is
stochastically dominated by
Z_{n}
under
\delta=0
.
Unlike the WSKR and Maharaj boundaries,
f_{\mathrm{J}}
depends on
the effect size through
\sigma_{\tau}
. When
\sigma_{\tau}=\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}

this dependence is absorbed into
n_{z}
. The closed-form
k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{J}}

depends on
t_{0}
through the monitoring window, but the dependence
is weak:
k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{J}}
varies by less than
0.5\%
across the range

t_{0}\in[0.001,1]
. Because the positive branch of

\mathrm{BF}_{n}>1/\alpha
is a stopping rule for declaring a
positive effect in Bayesian A/B testing
[
6
]
,

k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{J}}
applies directly to sample-size planning for that decision.

Boundary in rescaled time.

Rescaling normalises the fixed-sample endpoint to
t=1
and makes
the boundary crossing problem amenable to Brownian-motion analysis.
We work in the rescaled time variable
t=n/n_{z}
and the rescaled
cumulative process
Y_{t}=\sqrt{t}\,Z_{tn_{z}}
. Write

t_{0}:=m/n_{z}
for the burn-in in rescaled time. Under
H_{1}
with

\delta=\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}
and the moment conditions of

13
,
Y_{t}
is asymptotically Brownian motion with drift

\mu_{d}=z_{\alpha}+z_{\beta}
per unit
t
, started at

Y_{t_{0}}\sim N(t_{0}\mu_{d},t_{0})

(Appendix
A
). Because
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{n})=1

for any
r
by (
2
), the rescaled process
Y_{t}
has drift

\mu_{d}
and variance rate one regardless of
r
; the allocation ratio
is absorbed into
n_{z}
via (
1
). The correction factor

k^{\ast}(\alpha,\beta,t_{0})
therefore depends on
r
only through

t_{0}=m/n_{z}(r)
; at equal
t_{0}
,
k^{\ast}
is identical across
r
.
The WSKR boundary becomes

b_{\mathrm{W}}(t)=\sqrt{t\,(\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(t/t_{0}))},

(6)

and the Maharaj boundary becomes

b_{\mathrm{M}}(t)=\sqrt{t_{0}}\,g_{\mathrm{M}}(t/t_{0}),

(7)

where
g_{\mathrm{M}}(v)^{2}=(2(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}v+1)/\lambda_{\mathrm{M}})\,\log(1+\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}v+1}/(2\alpha))
.
With
\sigma_{\tau}=\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}
, the mSPRT boundary becomes

b_{\mathrm{J}}(t)^{2}=\frac{2(1+t\mu_{d}^{2})}{\mu_{d}^{2}}\,\left(\log\frac{1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1+t\mu_{d}^{2})\right),

(8)

where
\mu_{d}=z_{\alpha}+z_{\beta}
(since

\kappa_{tn_{z}}=\Delta^{2}r\,t\,n_{z}/(1+r)^{2}=t\mu_{d}^{2}
by (
1
)).
The mSPRT boundary does not depend on
m
.
All three boundaries are concave on the relevant range. For

b_{\mathrm{W}}
,
b_{\mathrm{W}}^{\prime\prime}(t)<0
on
[t_{0},\infty)

(Appendix
A
, A.1). For
b_{\mathrm{M}}
,

b_{\mathrm{M}}^{\prime\prime}(t)<0
on
[t_{0},\infty)
for all

\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}>0
and
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})

(Appendix
A
, A.1). For
b_{\mathrm{J}}
,
concavity is verified numerically on
[t_{0},k]
at every

(\alpha,\beta)
pair in the grid; users applying the formula at
untabulated parameters should verify that
b_{\mathrm{J}}^{\prime\prime}(t)<0

on a grid in
[t_{0},k]
.
The closed-form approximation requires concavity
(Section
3
) and depends on the boundary only through
b(k)

and
b^{\prime}(k)
at the planned endpoint.

Power.

We write
t_{0}=m/n_{z}
for the burn-in,
t=1
for the
fixed-sample baseline, and
k
for the planned endpoint, so

n=k\cdot n_{z}
. The AV power at horizon
k\cdot n_{z}
is

\pi(k;\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}):=P(\tau\leq k\cdot n_{z}\mid\delta=\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}),

(9)

the probability of a boundary crossing at any time from burn-in
m

to the planned endpoint
k\cdot n_{z}
. With
m\ll n_{z}
, the
monitoring window
[m,\,k\cdot n_{z}]
extends well before the
fixed-sample size.

Last-point sizing.

The last-point rule sets
n=k\cdot n_{z}
to the smallest value at
which the marginal rejection probability at the endpoint reaches

1-\beta
. Under
H_{1}
,
Y_{k}\sim N(k\mu_{d},k)
, so this
probability is
\Phi\!\big((k\mu_{d}-b(k))/\sqrt{k}\big)
.
Setting it equal to
1-\beta
gives

b(k)=\sqrt{k}\,(\sqrt{k}\,\mu_{d}-z_{\beta})
.
For the WSKR boundary this reduces to

\sqrt{\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(k/t_{0})}=\sqrt{k}\,\mu_{d}-z_{\beta}
,
and for each boundary the root
k_{\mathrm{naive}}
is found numerically.
Last-point sizing is conservative by construction, as the probability
of rejecting before the last planned time point is ignored.

3
The corrected factor

The corrected factor is the smallest
k>t_{0}
at which a tangent-line
approximation to the AV power (
9
) equals
1-\beta
.
Under the Brownian-motion approximation to the cumulative process
under
H_{1}
, this approximation depends on the boundary
b
only
through
b(k)
and
b^{\prime}(k)
; accuracy depends on how closely the
tangent tracks the boundary on
[t_{0},k]
and is checked by simulation
in Section
4
. Let

\mu_{d}:=z_{\alpha}+z_{\beta}
and define the tangent of
b
at the
planned endpoint
t=k
:

L_{t_{0}}(k):=b(k)-b^{\prime}(k)(k-t_{0}),\qquad s(k):=b^{\prime}(k).

(10)

Under concavity, the tangent lies above the boundary on
[t_{0},k]
.
Set
T:=k-t_{0}
,
c_{x}:=(L_{t_{0}}-t_{0}\mu_{d})/\sqrt{t_{0}}
,

\nu:=\mu_{d}-s
, and

\rho:=-\sqrt{t_{0}}/\sqrt{T+t_{0}}
. Then

\pi(k;\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}})\approx\Phi(-c_{x})+I_{1}(k)+I_{2}(k),

(11)

with

\displaystyle I_{1}(k)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{2}\!\left(c_{x},\ \tfrac{\nu T-\sqrt{t_{0}}\,c_{x}}{\sqrt{T+t_{0}}};\ \rho\right),

(12)

\displaystyle I_{2}(k)

\displaystyle=e^{2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu\,(c_{x}+\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu)}\,\Phi_{2}\!\left(c_{x}+2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu,\ \tfrac{-\nu T-\sqrt{t_{0}}\,c_{x}-2t_{0}\nu}{\sqrt{T+t_{0}}};\ \rho\right).

(13)

Here
\Phi_{2}(a,b;\rho)
is the bivariate standard normal CDF with
correlation
\rho
.
The derivation (Appendix
A
) uses tangent
linearisation, the Bachelier first-passage formula, and Gaussian
completion-of-the-square to integrate over the burn-in initial value

Y_{t_{0}}\sim N(t_{0}\mu_{d},t_{0})
. The three terms have plain
interpretations:

\Phi(-c_{x})
is the immediate-rejection probability at
t=t_{0}

for the tangent-line surrogate,

I_{1}
is the probability of crossing the linearised boundary during

(t_{0},k]
, and
I_{2}
is the Bachelier reflection correction. The
closed-form
k^{\ast}
is the smallest
k>t_{0}
solving

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(k)=1-\beta
, where
\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}

is the right-hand side of (
11
). A bracket search
returns
k^{\ast}
at two bivariate normal CDF calls per
evaluation.

Boundary-specific slopes.

For the WSKR boundary,

b_{\mathrm{W}}^{\prime}(k)=(\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(k/t_{0})+1)\,/\,(2\,b_{\mathrm{W}}(k))
.
For the Maharaj boundary,

b_{\mathrm{M}}^{\prime}(k)=g_{\mathrm{M}}^{\prime}(k/t_{0})\,/\,\sqrt{t_{0}}
,
where
g_{\mathrm{M}}^{\prime}(v)=(2q+\xi/(2\alpha+\xi))\,/\,(2\,g_{\mathrm{M}}(v))

with
\xi=\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}v+1}
and

q=\log(1+\xi/(2\alpha))
.
For the mSPRT boundary, with
\eta=k\mu_{d}^{2}
and

c=\log(1/\alpha)
,

b_{\mathrm{J}}^{\prime}(k)=(c+\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1+\eta)+\tfrac{1}{2})\,/\,b_{\mathrm{J}}(k)
.
The closed form satisfies

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(t_{0})<1-\beta
and

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(k)\to 1
as
k\to\infty
. A bracket search
finds a unique root
k^{\ast}
at every
(\alpha,\beta)
cell;
no second root was observed on the tested grid
(Appendix
A
).

4.2
Sample-size saving

The relative saving of
k^{\ast}
over
k_{\mathrm{naive}}
is

S(\alpha,\beta):=100\cdot\frac{k_{\mathrm{naive}}(\alpha,\beta)-k^{\ast}(\alpha,\beta)}{k_{\mathrm{naive}}(\alpha,\beta)}\quad\%.

(14)

Because
k^{\ast}
depends on
t_{0}
, the saving
S
is not a single number
but varies with the experiment’s effect size and burn-in.

The saving varies with the effect size because
t_{0}=m/n_{z}
depends
on
\Delta
: smaller effects give larger
n_{z}
and hence smaller

t_{0}
, wider boundaries, and lower savings.
Over the
(\alpha,\beta)
grid (Table
1
) and the
sensitivity analysis (Table
3
), the saving ranges
from
8\%
to
20\%
across all three boundaries and the full range of
burn-in values. Full simulation design is in
Appendix
B
.

5
Conclusion

The last-point rule for sequential experiments oversizes by seven to
nine percentage points at standard operating characteristics. The
closed-form factor
k^{\ast}
approximately corrects this via tangent
linearisation, verified across three boundary families and an
extended
(\alpha,\beta)
grid. The correction saves
8
–
20\%

of the last-point sample budget across the operating range.
At low base rates (
p_{C}\leq 0.01
), the CLT approximation degrades
and power falls below target (Table
7
).

With realistic burn-in (

), the total sample budget for a
confidence-sequence-based sequential test is
2
–
3
times the
fixed-sample size, reflecting the cost of the wider boundary that
validity over
[m,\infty)
requires.

The production validation on Spotify’s experimentation platform
shows a median sample-size saving of

across

Appendix A
Derivation of
k^{\ast}

The derivation strategy (tangent linearisation of a curved boundary,
Bachelier first-passage on the linear surrogate, integration over the
initial value) follows
11
, applied here to the
specific boundary families arising in modern confidence sequences.

The proof uses the WSKR boundary as the worked example; the Maharaj
boundary follows by substituting
b_{\mathrm{M}}(k)
and

b_{\mathrm{M}}^{\prime}(k)
for
b_{\mathrm{W}}(k)
and

b_{\mathrm{W}}^{\prime}(k)
in the final expressions. The derivation holds
for any allocation ratio
r\geq 1
: with

Z_{n}=\hat{\delta}_{n}\sqrt{r\,n}/(\sigma(1+r))
we have

\mathrm{Var}(Z_{n})=1
for every
r
, and under
H_{1}
the rescaled
process
Y_{t}=\sqrt{t}\,Z_{tn_{z}}
satisfies

E(Y_{t})=\mu_{d}\,t
and
\mathrm{Var}(Y_{t})=t
since

E(Z_{n})=\delta\sqrt{r\,n}/(\sigma(1{+}r))
and

n=t\,n_{z}
absorbs
(1{+}r)^{2}/r
through (
1
). The
boundary in rescaled time, the Bachelier passage formula, and the
integration over
Y_{t_{0}}
therefore depend on
r
only through

t_{0}=m/n_{z}(r)
. At equal
t_{0}
,
k^{\ast}
is identical across
r
.

A.1. Process and boundary on the rescaled time axis.

The proof works on the rescaled time variable
t=n/n_{z}
and the
rescaled cumulative process

Y_{t}:=\sqrt{t}\,Z_{tn_{z}},

(15)

where
Z_{n}=\hat{\delta}_{n}\sqrt{r\,n}/(\sigma(1{+}r))
has unit
variance for every
r
by (
2
). Therefore

\mathrm{Var}(Y_{t})=t\cdot\mathrm{Var}(Z_{tn_{z}})=t

on the rescaled axis;
Y
has variance rate one in
t
.
Increments of
Y
over disjoint
t
-intervals correspond to
non-overlapping blocks of observations, so they are
asymptotically independent under the i.i.d. assumption. Under
H_{1}

with
\delta=\delta_{\mathrm{MDE}}
and the moment conditions of

13
,
Y_{t}
converges weakly to Brownian motion with constant
drift
\mu_{d}=z_{\alpha}+z_{\beta}
per unit
t

[by the functional CLT;
2
, Theorem 14.1]
, with

Y_{t_{0}}\sim N(t_{0}\mu_{d},t_{0})
at the burn-in
t_{0}=m/n_{z}
. The WSKR boundary

f(j)=\sqrt{\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(j/m)}
acting on
\{Z_{n}\}
transforms onto

\{Y_{t}\}
via

Y_{t}>\sqrt{t}\,f(tn_{z})=\sqrt{t}\cdot\sqrt{\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(t/t_{0})}=\sqrt{t(\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(t/t_{0}))}=:b_{\mathrm{W}}(t),\qquad t\geq t_{0}.

(16)

Writing
u=\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(t/t_{0})
, so
b_{\mathrm{W}}(t)=\sqrt{t\,u}
and

du/dt=1/t
, the chain rule gives

b_{\mathrm{W}}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{u+1}{2\,b_{\mathrm{W}}(t)}=\frac{\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(t/t_{0})+1}{2\sqrt{t\,(\Lambda_{\alpha}+\log(t/t_{0}))}},\qquad b_{\mathrm{W}}^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\frac{u^{2}+1}{4\,b_{\mathrm{W}}(t)^{3}}<0\quad\text{on }[t_{0},\infty),

so
b_{\mathrm{W}}
is concave there.

For the Maharaj boundary, write
g(t)=b_{\mathrm{M}}(t)^{2}
and

\varrho=\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}t/t_{0}+1}\,/\,(2\alpha)
.
Since
b_{\mathrm{M}}=\sqrt{g}
, the sign of
b_{\mathrm{M}}^{\prime\prime}

equals the sign of
2g\,g^{\prime\prime}-(g^{\prime})^{2}
. After substitution, this
reduces to showing
\varphi(\varrho)<0
for
\varrho>0
, where

\varphi(\varrho)=\frac{2\varrho\log(1{+}\varrho)-\varrho^{2}}{(1{+}\varrho)^{2}}-4\log^{2}(1{+}\varrho).

The bound
\log(1{+}\varrho)<\varrho
gives

2\varrho\log(1{+}\varrho)-\varrho^{2}<\varrho^{2}
, so the first
term is below
\varrho^{2}/(1{+}\varrho)^{2}
. The bound

\varrho/(1{+}\varrho)<2\log(1{+}\varrho)
(verified by comparing
derivatives at
\varrho=0
) yields

\varrho^{2}/(1{+}\varrho)^{2}<4\log^{2}(1{+}\varrho)
, so

\varphi(\varrho)<0
and
b_{\mathrm{M}}
is strictly concave on

[t_{0},\infty)
.

A.2. Linearisation.

Replace
b
on
[t_{0},k]
by its tangent at
t=k
:

L(t)=b(k)+s(t-k),\qquad s=b^{\prime}(k),\qquad L_{t_{0}}=b(k)-s(k-t_{0}).

(17)

A.3. Bachelier first-passage on the linear boundary.

The classical Bachelier formula for Brownian motion
W_{t}
with drift

\mu
and variance rate
1
states that for a linear boundary

\ell(t)=a+\mu_{b}t
on
[0,T]
, with
W_{0}=0
and
a>0
,

P\!\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big(W_{t}-\ell(t)\big)\geq 0\right)=\Phi\!\left((\mu-\mu_{b})\sqrt{T}-\tfrac{a}{\sqrt{T}}\right)+e^{-2a(\mu_{b}-\mu)}\,\Phi\!\left(-(\mu-\mu_{b})\sqrt{T}-\tfrac{a}{\sqrt{T}}\right),

[
1
,
12
]
. Substituting
\mu=\mu_{d}
and

\mu_{b}=s
flips the sign in the reflection exponent since

-(\mu_{b}-\mu)=\mu_{d}-s
. Translating time so that
t=t_{0}

corresponds to time
0
in the standard Bachelier statement, with horizon

T=k-t_{0}
, drift
\mu=\mu_{d}
, and linear boundary slope

\mu_{b}=s
, the first-passage probability of
Y
over
L
on
[t_{0},k]

conditional on
Y_{t_{0}}=x<L_{t_{0}}
is

P(\tau\leq k\mid Y_{t_{0}}=x)=\Phi\!\left((\mu_{d}-s)\sqrt{T}-\tfrac{a}{\sqrt{T}}\right)+e^{2a(\mu_{d}-s)}\,\Phi\!\left(-(\mu_{d}-s)\sqrt{T}-\tfrac{a}{\sqrt{T}}\right),

(18)

with
a=L_{t_{0}}-x>0
. For
x\geq L_{t_{0}}
the trajectory
already lies above the linearised boundary at
t=t_{0}
and the
conditional probability is one.

A.4. Integration over the burn-in initial value.

Substitute
u=(Y_{t_{0}}-t_{0}\mu_{d})/\sqrt{t_{0}}\sim N(0,1)
with
standard normal density
\phi
. With

c_{x}=(L_{t_{0}}-t_{0}\mu_{d})/\sqrt{t_{0}}
and
\nu=\mu_{d}-s
, the
event
Y_{t_{0}}\geq L_{t_{0}}
becomes
\{u\geq c_{x}\}
and contributes

P(u\geq c_{x})=\Phi(-c_{x})
. The
remaining integral is

\int_{-\infty}^{c_{x}}P(\tau\leq k\mid Y_{t_{0}}=t_{0}\mu_{d}+\sqrt{t_{0}}\,u)\,\phi(u)\,du=I_{1}+I_{2},

(19)

where the two pieces correspond to the two terms of
(
18
) with
a=\sqrt{t_{0}}\,(c_{x}-u)
. The reduction
uses the following identity.

Lemma 1

(Gaussian convolution)
.

For real
A_{0}
,
B_{0}
, and
c
,

\int_{-\infty}^{c}\Phi(A_{0}+B_{0}u)\,\phi(u)\,du=\Phi_{2}\!\left(c,\ \tfrac{A_{0}}{\sqrt{1+B_{0}^{2}}};\ -\tfrac{B_{0}}{\sqrt{1+B_{0}^{2}}}\right).

(20)

Proof.

Let
V\sim N(0,1)
independent of
u
. Then

\Phi(A_{0}+B_{0}u)=P(V\leq A_{0}+B_{0}u\mid u)=P(V-B_{0}u\leq A_{0}\mid u)
. The pair
(u,V-B_{0}u)
is jointly Gaussian with

\mathrm{Var}(V-B_{0}u)=1+B_{0}^{2}
and

\mathrm{Cov}(u,V-B_{0}u)=-B_{0}
. Standardising the second
component gives correlation
-B_{0}/\sqrt{1+B_{0}^{2}}
, and the joint
event
\{u\leq c,\ V-B_{0}u\leq A_{0}\}
becomes
\{u\leq c,\ \widetilde{W}\leq A_{0}/\sqrt{1+B_{0}^{2}}\}
with
\widetilde{W}
unit
variance. The right-hand side of (
20
) follows.
∎

Applying Lemma
1
to the first term of
(
18
) with

A_{0}=\nu\sqrt{T}-\sqrt{t_{0}}\,c_{x}/\sqrt{T}
and

B_{0}=\sqrt{t_{0}}/\sqrt{T}
yields (
12
) after the substitutions

1+B_{0}^{2}=(T+t_{0})/T
,

A_{0}/\sqrt{1+B_{0}^{2}}=(\nu T-\sqrt{t_{0}}\,c_{x})/\sqrt{T+t_{0}}
,
and
-B_{0}/\sqrt{1+B_{0}^{2}}=-\sqrt{t_{0}}/\sqrt{T+t_{0}}=\rho
.

For the second term the integrand carries an extra factor

e^{2\sqrt{t_{0}}(c_{x}-u)\nu}
. Completing the square in the Gaussian
density gives the algebraic identity

e^{-2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu u}\,\phi(u)=e^{2t_{0}\nu^{2}}\,\phi(u+2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu),

(21)

so the prefactor combines as
e^{2\sqrt{t_{0}}(c_{x}-u)\nu}\,\phi(u)=e^{2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,c_{x}\nu+2t_{0}\nu^{2}}\,\phi(u+2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu)=e^{2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu(c_{x}+\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu)}\,\phi(u+2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu)
. The substitution
w=u+2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu
shifts
the upper limit from
c_{x}
to
c_{x}+2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu
.
Lemma
1
with

A_{0}=-\nu\sqrt{T}-\sqrt{t_{0}}\,c_{x}/\sqrt{T}-2t_{0}\nu/\sqrt{T}
and

B_{0}=\sqrt{t_{0}}/\sqrt{T}
then yields (
13
), with the leading
coefficient
e^{2\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu(c_{x}+\sqrt{t_{0}}\,\nu)}
. Adding the
three pieces gives (
11
).

A.5. Existence and uniqueness of
k^{\ast}
.

At
k=t_{0}
we have
T=0
and
\rho=-1
, so
\Phi_{2}(c_{x},-c_{x};-1)=0
and
I_{2}(t_{0})=0
, giving

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(t_{0})=\Phi((\mu_{d}t_{0}-b(t_{0}))/\sqrt{t_{0}})
.
Across
\alpha\in\{0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}
and
\beta\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20\}
,
\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(t_{0})
is strictly less than

1-\beta
at every cell for all three boundaries. As
k\to\infty
,

L_{t_{0}}(k)\to+\infty
, so
c_{x}\to+\infty
and

\Phi(-c_{x})\to 0
. The second argument of
I_{1}
grows without bound,
giving
I_{1}\to 1
. The
\Phi_{2}
factor in
I_{2}
decays
exponentially, dominating the prefactor, so
I_{2}\to 0
. Thus

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(k)\to 1
. Monotonicity of

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(k)
in
k
does not follow automatically
because the tangent surrogate changes with
k
(the crossing events
for different
k
are not nested). We evaluate

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}
on a fine grid of
k
values at every

(\alpha,\beta)
cell. At every cell, a bracket search
finds a unique root
k^{\ast}<k_{\mathrm{naive}}
; no second root was observed
on the tested grid. Uniqueness is a numerical observation, not a
theorem; we do not prove monotonicity of
\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}
in
general.

A.6. Numerical verification.

The closed form (
11
) agrees with the Monte Carlo
reference at the precision of the simulation. On the grid

\alpha\in\{0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}
and

\beta\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20\}
, the closed-form
k^{\ast}
matches
the Monte Carlo reference within
1.5\%
relative error (median

0.9\%
) for the WSKR boundary and within
0.93\%
(median
0.64\%
)
for the Maharaj boundary. Relative to the finite-sample discrete
simulations, the closed-form root may be slightly smaller because of
CLT and discrete-monitoring residuals that reduce empirical power.
Verification scripts are in the
simulations.py
file.

Appendix B
Simulation design

Data-generating process.

Each Monte Carlo replication generates
n_{T}=\lfloor n/(1{+}r)\rceil
treatment and
n_{C}=n-n_{T}
control observations for total
sample sizes
n
from
m
to

n_{\max}=\lceil k_{\mathrm{naive}}\cdot n_{z}\rceil
,
under one of three distribution families:

•

Gaussian.
X_{C,j}\sim N(\mu_{C},\sigma^{2})
,

X_{T,j}\sim N(\mu_{C}+\delta,\sigma^{2})
, with
\sigma=1

and
\delta
chosen so that the standardised effect

\Delta\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}
.

•

Bernoulli.
X_{C,j}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{C})
,

X_{T,j}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{C}+\Delta_{p})
, with

p_{C}\in\{0.05,0.20\}
and
\Delta_{p}
chosen so that

\Delta=\Delta_{p}/\sqrt{p_{C}(1-p_{C})}\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}
.
The treatment and control arms have unequal variances under

H_{1}
; these rows are robustness checks outside the
common-variance model.

•

Log-normal.
X_{C,j}=\exp(\xi_{C,j})
and

X_{T,j}=\exp(\xi_{T,j})+\delta
with independent

\xi_{C,j},\xi_{T,j}\sim N(0,1)
and
\delta
chosen to
give standardised effect
\Delta\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}

using the variance of
\exp(\xi)
.

Allocation ratios
r\in\{1,1.5,2\}
are simulated by drawing

n_{T}=n/(1+r)
treatment observations and
n_{C}=r\cdot n_{T}

control observations.

Confidence sequence and stopping rule.

For each replication, the test statistic
Z_{n}
from (
2
)
is evaluated at every total sample size
n
at which both arms have
gained at least one new observation (step size
\lceil 1{+}r\rceil
).
The boundary is compared in
Z_{n}
units:
Z_{n}>f(n)
, using the
WSKR, Maharaj, or mSPRT boundary as appropriate, with burn-in

m=20
(Table
1
uses
m=40
).
The first
n
at which the bound is exceeded is recorded as

\tau^{(\ell)}
. The empirical power at horizon
n
is

\hat{\pi}(n)=(1/B)\sum_{\ell}\mathbf{1}[\tau^{(\ell)}\leq n]
.

Parameter grid and replications.

The grid in Tables
2
fixes

\alpha=0.05
and
1-\beta=0.80
across three distributions,
allocation ratios
r\in\{1,1.5,2\}
, and effect sizes

\Delta\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}
. Each cell uses
B=50{,}000

replications. The Monte Carlo standard error on the empirical power
is below
0.0023
at every cell. The broader

(\alpha,\beta)
grid in Section
3
covers

\alpha\in\{0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}
and

\beta\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20\}
at
\Delta=0.2
and Gaussian
outcomes, with
k^{\ast}
obtained from the closed form

\pi_{\mathrm{closed}}(k)=1-\beta
. Monte Carlo is used only to
evaluate empirical power
\hat{\pi}(kn_{z})
at the resulting
closed-form sample size.

Burn-in.

All simulations use
m=20
unless stated otherwise.
Section
4.4
varies
m
across

\{20,40,100,200,1000,2000,n_{z}\}
.

Seed and code.

Simulation scripts and closed-form verification are in the

simulations.py
file. Random seed
2026
.
