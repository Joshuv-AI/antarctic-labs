Determinants of Family Members’ Willingness to Donate Organs in Intensive Care Units: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Lou’i Al-Husinat, Rana Haddad, Mohammad Ahmad Hailat, Ammar Badr, Karis Khattab, Sarah Al Sharie, Zaid Al Modanat, Mudhaffer Salah Touqan, Ala’ Riyad Balawi, Hesham Al Sharie, Nicolò Antonino Patroniti, Denise Battaglini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the lifesaving potential of organ donation, a gap persists between organ demand and availability. Family members of intensive care unit (ICU) patients play a key role in consent for donation, yet factors influencing their decisions are not fully understood. This study assessed the willingness of ICU patients’ family members to donate organs and explored associated sociodemographic, clinical, and attitudinal factors.MethodsIn this cross-sectional single-centered tertiary care center in northern Jordan, 155 adult family members of ICU patients completed a structured questionnaire on demographics, patient variables, knowledge of organ donation and brain death, religious and cultural beliefs, trust in healthcare, and exposure to public campaigns. Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics, and between-group comparisons were conducted using chi-square, Student’s t-test, or Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Firth’s penalized logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of willingness due to small sample size and sparse cell counts.ResultsOf 155 participants, 44 (28.4%; 95% CI: 21.9–35.9%) were willing to donate. High trust in the healthcare system was the strongest independent predictor of willingness (OR 7.73; 95% CI: 3.21–19.93; p<0.001), followed by a favorable religious stance toward donation (OR 4.75; 95% CI: 1.99–12.23; p<0.001) and high familiarity with organ donation (OR 2.78; 95% CI: 1.12–6.87; p = 0.028).ConclusionWillingness to donate organs among family members of ICU patients was primarily influenced by trust in the healthcare system, religious beliefs, and familiarity with organ donation. Context-specific interventions targeting these factors may help improve consent rates in similar settings.

Introduction

Organ donation represents one of the most remarkable achievements of modern medicine, offering patients with end-stage organ failure a chance to prolong survival and improve quality of life.1 Despite the growing success of transplant programs globally, a persistent gap exists between the demand for donor organs and their availability.2 Globally, large numbers of patients remain on transplant waiting lists each year, and many die before a suitable donor organ becomes available.2 This disparity highlights the urgent need to increase organ donation rates through multiple strategies, including living and deceased donation as well as public awareness initiatives.3

Families are central to the organ donation process.4 In the intensive care unit (ICU), where potential donors are often identified, relatives are frequently approached about donation, making their perspectives crucial for donation success.5,6 A clear understanding of attitudes and willingness, which reflect a complex mix of personal, cultural, social, and religious factors, is crucial for improving consent rates and increasing the number of lives saved.7 Trust in healthcare providers, knowledge of the donation process, perceptions of body integrity, and religious or spiritual beliefs strongly influence family decision-making.8 Grief and stress in the ICU further complicate choices, as families face the tension between the sudden loss of a loved one and the opportunity to save others.9,10 Therefore, the decision is not only medical or legal, but also deeply personal and ethical.11 The ICU setting adds further complexity: families face uncertainty, fear, and grief, while staff must navigate sensitive conversations and provide support.12,13 The quality, timing, and empathy of communication can strongly influence decisions,13,14 making research in this area critical for improving both family experiences and donation outcome.15

Regional differences in legislation and healthcare systems also influence how families are engaged in organ donation.16 While some countries use “opt-in” policies and others presumed consent, families are often still consulted, and their refusal can prevent donation.17 Thus, understanding family willingness remains globally relevant.18

Under an opt-in (explicit consent) framework, individuals must actively register or otherwise document their intent to donate, whereas under an opt-out (presumed consent) framework all individuals are considered potential donors unless they have formally registered a refusal; in practice, families are commonly consulted under either model, and their objection can override a deceased patient’s presumed or documented wishes.19 The present study was conducted in Jordan, a Middle Eastern country in which deceased organ donation is governed by an explicit (opt-in) consent model and remains heavily dependent on family authorization.20 National deceased-donation activity in Jordan is modest by international standards, and a substantial proportion of transplants performed locally rely on living related donors, leaving demand for deceased-donor organs largely unmet.21 Despite the existence of a national legal framework regulating organ donation and brain-death determination, public awareness of these provisions is limited, and there are few sustained, system-level initiatives to promote deceased donation. These contextual features, an explicit-consent system, a central family role, and limited public awareness, make the willingness of ICU patients’ family members a particularly important and locally relevant determinant of donation outcomes.

The primary aim of this observational, cross-sectional study is to assess the willingness of family members of ICU patients to consent to organ donation and to identify the demographic, cultural, religious, and psychosocial factors that influence their decision-making. We hypothesize that willingness to donate organs among family members of ICU patients is significantly associated with trust in healthcare providers, knowledge about the donation process, and religious or cultural beliefs, and that higher levels of trust and knowledge will be positively associated with donation willingness.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study employed a descriptive-analytical, cross-sectional survey design to evaluate the willingness of family members of ICU patients to donate organs and to identify factors influencing their decision-making. Data were collected from family members of patients admitted to the adult ICUs of Princess Basma Teaching Hospital, Irbid, Jordan which is an affiliated hospital with the Faculty of Medicine, Yarmouk University, Irbid, Jordan between January and June 2025. This study follows the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist for cross-sectional studies.22 In addition, we adhered to the CROSS (Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies).23

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the Faculty of Medicine, Yarmouk University, Irbid, Jordan, IRB reference number: IRB/2025/184. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Data were anonymized to ensure participant confidentiality.

Participants

Eligible participants were adult family members (≥18 years) of ICU patients who served as legal decision-makers or were directly involved in healthcare decisions. Families were invited to participate when their relative was admitted to the ICU with a condition that could prompt consideration of organ donation (eg., terminal illness, confirmed or potential brain death). Exclusion criteria included inability to provide informed consent, inability to communicate in Arabic (the language in which the interview was administered) such that informed participation could not be ensured, and lack of direct involvement in the patient’s care decisions. During the study period, 168 eligible family members were approached and invited to participate, of whom 155 completed the questionnaire, yielding a completion rate of 92.3%. The 13 non-completers either declined participation or did not finish the interview and were not included in the analysis. The sample size was not based on a formal a priori power calculation; rather, it reflected consecutive enrollment of all eligible and consenting family members presenting during the predefined study period. With 155 participants and 44 willingness events, the analysis was therefore powered pragmatically, and inferential findings should be interpreted in light of this constraint, as addressed in the Limitations.

Data Collection and Operational Definitions

Data were collected through structured, interviewer-administered questionnaires developed based on prior literature on family attitudes toward organ donation.6,8,22 The instrument was adapted to the Jordanian context and reviewed for content and face validity by a multidisciplinary panel comprising critical-care physicians and clinical researchers, and was pilot-tested on a small number of family members (whose responses were not included in the final analysis) to confirm clarity, cultural appropriateness, and feasibility of administration. The questionnaire was administered in Arabic by trained interviewers. The questionnaire comprised multiple domains, comprising items relating to the responding family member (sociodemographic characteristics; knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes; trust in healthcare; and exposure to public campaigns) and items relating to the ICU patient (age, primary diagnosis, clinical condition, previously expressed donation opinion, and advance directive).

Sociodemographic variables included age, gender, education level (categorized as primary, secondary, university, or postgraduate), employment status (employed, unemployed, retired), monthly household income, residence (urban vs. rural), marital status, and relationship to the patient. Monthly household income was self-reported and categorized into low, middle, and high income groups based on the distribution of reported household income within the study population. Monthly household income was categorized as low (<500 JOD), middle (500–1000 JOD), or high (>1000 JOD), based on typical income ranges reported in Jordan.24

Patient-related variables included age, primary diagnosis (categorized as stroke/brain injury, trauma, organ failure, observation, cardiac arrest, or other), clinical condition at the time of assessment, previously expressed opinion regarding organ donation (supportive, opposed, uncertain/mixed, or no opinion), and the presence of an advance directive (yes/no/unknown).

Knowledge, beliefs, and attitudinal variables were assessed using predefined items. Familiarity with organ donation was self-reported and categorized into three levels (low, medium, high).25 Familiarity reflected the participant’s self-rated knowledge of organ donation, including awareness of what organ donation involves, how the donation process works, and the existence of donation programs. “High” familiarity denoted participants who reported being well informed about organ donation across these aspects, “medium” denoted some awareness with notable gaps, and “low” denoted little or no prior knowledge. For regression analysis, familiarity was further dichotomized into high versus low/medium. Understanding of brain death was assessed through specific questions and categorized as good understanding versus poor/uncertain understanding. Awareness of legal procedures governing organ donation was assessed as a binary variable (yes/no). Religious stance toward organ donation was self-reported and categorized as favorable, neutral, or opposed/uncertain. Cultural beliefs were assessed based on participants’ responses to predefined statements and categorized as no influence, supportive influence, or negative influence.

Healthcare-related perceptions were also evaluated. Trust in the healthcare system and perceived influence of healthcare professionals were assessed using Likert-scale items and categorized into three levels (low, moderate, high). Fear of medical negligence was assessed as a binary variable (yes/no) and captured the participant’s concern that consenting to donation might be associated with substandard care, premature declaration of death, or that the patient might not receive full life-sustaining treatment once donation was considered. Exposure to media or public campaigns regarding organ donation was recorded as positive, neutral, or negative.

The primary outcome, willingness to donate organs, was assessed as a binary variable (yes/no). Participants were also asked about their preferred organ donation system (opt-in vs. opt-out), willingness to register for organ donation (yes/no/planning), and their desire for additional information (yes/no), as well as their preferred sources of information.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the self-reported willingness to donate organs (yes/no), defined specifically as the family member’s willingness to consent to donation of organs from their relative who was admitted to the ICU, should that relative become a potential donor, rather than willingness to donate their own organs in the abstract. Secondary outcomes included predictors of willingness, such as sociodemographic variables, patient characteristics, knowledge and beliefs, trust in healthcare, and exposure to media campaigns.

Statistical Analysis

Data were first checked for completeness and consistency before analysis. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using histograms and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots, in conjunction with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests for normality. Although normality was assessed, continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for consistency across analyses. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between family members who were willing and not willing to donate organs were performed using the Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were <5) was used to compare categorical variables. Multivariate analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of willingness to donate organs. Given the limited number of outcome events and small cell sizes in several predictor categories, standard logistic regression showed evidence of complete separation and was not stable. We therefore applied Firth’s penalized maximum likelihood logistic regression, which is specifically designed to address small-sample bias and separation, using the logistf package in R. The model was restricted to a small set of conceptually independent variables selected based on clinical relevance and consistency with the existing literature: trust in the healthcare system, religious stance toward organ donation, and familiarity with organ donation. Understanding of brain death was initially included but removed from the final model due to sparse cell counts that produced unstable estimates and did not contribute meaningfully to model fit. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and R (version 4.5.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Participants’ familiarity with organ donation, understanding of brain death, awareness of the legal framework, religious stance, cultural beliefs, and preferred donation system are presented in Table 3. Healthcare, Trust, and Awareness Factors Affecting Donation Decisions

The influence of healthcare professionals, trust in the healthcare system, fear of negligence, media exposure, and awareness of legal procedures among participants are summarized in Table 4. Influence of healthcare professionals was greater among willing participants (72.7%) than those with moderate (13.6%) or low influence (13.6%) (p = 0.050). Discussion

This study investigated the willingness of ICU patients’ family members to donate organs and the factors influencing their decisions. Three independent predictors emerged from the multivariate analysis: trust in the healthcare system, religious stance, and familiarity with organ donation. These findings highlight that willingness to donate is primarily shaped by relational, belief-based, and knowledge-driven factors rather than sociodemographic characteristics alone. Globally, organ donation consent systems are broadly categorised as explicit opt-in or presumed opt-out, with differences in legislation and practice influencing donor rates and family involvement.17,26 In the specific setting of Jordan, organ donation is based on an explicit consent model in which family authorization is central to the decision-making process, making consent particularly sensitive to cultural, religious, and trust-related factors.

Trust in the healthcare system emerged as the strongest independent predictor of willingness to donate in the multivariate analysis, underscoring that this factor is not merely a correlate but a fundamental driver of consent. Notably, none of the participants with low trust were willing to donate, suggesting that systemic distrust represents a critical barrier that may be difficult to overcome through isolated interventions. This aligns with prior evidence demonstrating that mistrust in the medical system significantly reduces readiness to consent to organ donation.27,28 An important and seemingly paradoxical finding warrants careful attention: while low trust was universally associated with refusal, fear of medical negligence was, counterintuitively, associated with greater willingness (93.2% of those who feared negligence were willing). These findings likely reflect different dimensions of perception. Systemic low trust represents a fundamental disengagement from the healthcare institution, whereas fear of negligence may reflect a situational concern that does not preclude altruistic action and may even motivate donation as a means of deriving meaning from a distressing circumstance. This distinction has practical implications: interventions should prioritize strengthening institutional trust through transparent, consistent, and relationship-centered communication, while concerns about negligence may be addressed through clear explanations of the organ procurement process and its separation from clinical care.

Religious stance was the second strongest independent predictor of willingness in the multivariate analysis (OR 4.75; 95% CI: 1.99–12.23; p<0.001). Participants with favorable religious views overwhelmingly supported donation, while those with uncertain or opposing views uniformly declined. This aligns with previous evidence showing that social, cultural, and demographic contexts influence willingness to donate organs.29,30 Importantly, the fact that religious stance remained an independent predictor after controlling for sociodemographic and attitudinal factors underscores that it operates through its own distinct pathway, independent of education, income, or cultural beliefs. These findings support the integration of religious leaders as trusted communicators in organ donation initiatives and highlight the importance of providing families with clear, accurate religious guidance during decision-making.

Familiarity with organ donation was the third independent predictor identified in the multivariate analysis (OR 2.78; 95% CI: 1.12–6.87; p = 0.028). This finding demonstrates that knowledge-related factors exert an effect on willingness beyond what can be explained by trust or religious stance alone. In our cohort, awareness of the legal framework was markedly low, with 94.2% of participants unaware of legal procedures, and only 31.6% reporting high familiarity. These findings suggest substantial scope for educational interventions. Improving understanding of brain death, the donation process, and the legal framework may directly enhance willingness, particularly when delivered through trusted channels such as healthcare professionals and religious leaders.

Several factors were associated with willingness at the univariate level, including education, income, admitting diagnosis, and media influence; however, none retained independent significance after adjustment, indicating that their effects are likely mediated through trust, religious stance, or familiarity. For example, the higher willingness observed among participants with secondary education and lower income may reflect differences in trust or exposure to skepticism rather than a direct effect of socioeconomic status.27–30 Similarly, the association with brain injury or stroke may reflect increased exposure to discussions of brain death, allowing greater time for cognitive and emotional preparation, while the influence of media likely operates indirectly through shaping awareness and perceptions. The direction of the education and income associations deserves particular comment, as it runs counter to the pattern reported in several other settings, where higher educational attainment and income are often linked to greater donation willingness. In our cohort, willingness was comparatively higher among participants with secondary (rather than university) education and among those in the lower-income category. Several non-causal explanations are plausible. First, more highly educated and higher-income respondents may have engaged more critically with the formal donation and registration system, and their greater awareness of procedural and institutional concerns may have translated into more guarded responses. Second, the finding may reflect uncontrolled confounding by religiosity, community ties, or differing exposure to public messaging, factors that were not fully captured and that may co-vary with both socioeconomic position and willingness. Third, given the modest sample and the small number of willingness events, these subgroup proportions are imprecise and may be unstable Importantly, because neither education nor income retained independent significance in the adjusted model, we interpret these patterns as descriptive associations whose effects are most likely mediated through trust, religious stance, and familiarity, rather than as evidence that lower socioeconomic position increases willingness.

The expressed opinion of the patient revealed an important disconnect. Although families were more likely to consent when patients had previously expressed support, a substantial proportion (99 of 137, 72.3%) were nonetheless unwilling to donate. This underscores that in the Jordanian context, where decision-making is family-centered, individual patient preferences may be subordinated to broader family concerns or unresolved uncertainties.

Finally, all 44 participants who expressed willingness to donate indicated they would not formally register as organ donors, a gap that likely reflects low awareness of the registration process, a cultural preference for informal family authorization, and residual mistrust of formal documentation systems even among those otherwise willing. This dissociation between expressed willingness and intended registration is practically important: it suggests that, in this setting, willingness is enacted relationally and at the bedside through family consent rather than through pre-committed individual registration. In a system where deceased donation depends on family authorization, a population may therefore harbor latent willingness that never becomes visible in formal donor registries, which in turn can lead policymakers to underestimate the true reservoir of potential consent. It also implies that interventions focused narrowly on driving registry sign-ups may have limited impact in this context, whereas approaches that legitimize and support family-mediated consent, normalize advance family discussion of donation wishes, and reduce procedural distrust may be more effective. Because the questionnaire did not capture the specific reasons participants declined to register, these interpretations remain provisional, and we identify the structured assessment of registration barriers as a clear priority for future work.

Taken together, these findings emphasize the interplay of sociodemographic, cultural, and emotional factors in shaping families’ decisions. While our results align with global evidence in many areas, particularly the importance of trust, awareness, and religious stance, they also diverge in important ways, such as the paradoxical role of education and income, the surprising relationship between fear of negligence and willingness, and the persistent gap between patient wishes and family action. These findings underscore the need for tailored, culturally sensitive interventions that prioritize trust-building, improve public and family-level understanding, and engage religious and community leaders to support informed decision-making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality between the identified factors and willingness to donate organs. The observed associations, particularly those related to trust, religious stance, and familiarity, should therefore be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center in northern Jordan, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings with different demographic, cultural, or institutional characteristics. In particular, attitudes toward organ donation may vary across urban and rural populations, as well as between public and private healthcare systems. Moreover, the sample size was relatively modest, with a limited number of participants expressing willingness to donate. This resulted in sparse data in several categories, necessitating the use of Firth’s penalized logistic regression. While this approach improves the stability of estimates in small samples, it may still limit the precision of effect estimates and the ability to explore a broader range of predictors in multivariable models. Additionally, several variables were based on self-reported measures, including familiarity with organ donation, trust in the healthcare system, and religious stance. These measures are subject to social desirability and recall bias, particularly given the sensitive context of ICU decision-making. Additionally, the categorization of these variables into discrete levels (eg., low, moderate, high) may have resulted in some loss of information. Further, the study was conducted in a highly emotional and time-sensitive ICU setting, where participants may have been experiencing significant psychological distress. This context may have influenced responses, potentially leading to variability in attitudes that might differ from those expressed in less acute settings. Finally, some findings, such as the discordance between willingness to donate and willingness to formally register, as well as the association between fear of medical negligence and increased willingness, should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect residual confounding, measurement limitations, or contextual factors not fully captured in the questionnaire.

Conclusion

This study highlights that the willingness of ICU patients’ family members to donate organs is shaped by a complex interplay of sociodemographic, attitudinal, and contextual factors. Trust in the healthcare system, favorable religious stance, and familiarity with organ donation emerged as the strongest independent predictors of willingness in the multivariate analysis. These findings underscore the importance of context-specific strategies to improve organ donation consent rates. Interventions that build public trust in healthcare institutions, engage religious and community leaders, and increase awareness and familiarity with organ donation, particularly in critical care settings, may enhance willingness to donate. Encouraging individuals to communicate their organ donation preferences in advance could further facilitate family decision-making at the bedside. Ultimately, improving consent for organ donation requires a multifaceted approach that respects families’ values and emotions while addressing knowledge gaps and systemic barriers. By adopting such strategies, healthcare systems can help bridge the gap between organ demand and availability and save more lives.
