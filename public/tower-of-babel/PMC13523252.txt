Normal tissue complication probability modelling and predicting for carbon-ion radiotherapy-induced symptomatic esophageal and tracheobronchial injuries in locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Xue Ming, Ningyi Ma, Yinxiangzi Sheng, Jian Chen, Kailiang Wu, Jingfang Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiation-induced injuries (RIs) to the esophagus (ESO) and proximal bronchial tree (PBT) represent one of the most frequently observed symptomatic adverse events in locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer (LA-NSCLC). The establishment of validated normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) models and subsequent dose constraint recommendations are especially essential in carbon-ion radiotherapy (CIRT), owing to its unique physical and biological properties.PurposeTo establish NTCP models and derive corresponding dose constraints for symptomatic or severe RIs in ESO and PBT in LA-NSCLC treated with local effect model (LEM)-I-based CIRT.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 184 LA-NSCLC patients treated with CIRT at a median prescription dose of 77 Gy (RBE) in 3.5 Gy (RBE) per fraction. Lyman-Kutcher-Burman (LKB) NTCP models were developed for acute esophagitis (ARI-ESO), late ESO reaction (LRI-ESO), and late PBT toxicity (LRI-PBT). Predictive performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) by comparing single dosimetric parameters versus multivariate nomograms.ResultsFor grade ≥ 2 (symptomatic) ARI-ESO and grade ≥ 2/≥3 (severe) LRI-PBT, low n values (0.053±0.097, 0.139 ± 0.085, 0.224 ± 0.135, respectively) indicated predominant dependence on high-dose regions. In contrast, higher n values (0.381/0.362) observed for grade ≥ 2/≥3 late ESO toxicities suggested the use of dual constraints incorporating both maximum dose (Dmax) and mean dose (Dmean). The best equivalent uniform dose (EUD) thresholds (ROC‑derived) defined a low‑risk cohort with an actual incidence of ≤1.9% for each adverse event. The proposed Dmax thresholds of 78 Gy (RBE) for ESO and 80 Gy (RBE) for PBT are expected to limit toxicity risk below 5%. Multivariate nomograms integrating high-dose metrics and Dmean achieved AUC-ROC values of 0.823–0.884.ConclusionsThe first NTCP models for symptomatic toxicities of ESO and PBT were established in LA-NSCLC patients receiving LEM-I-based CIRT at ~3.5 Gy (RBE) per fraction with sequential systemic therapy. The ROC‑derived optimal EUD thresholds for discriminating NTCP risk were obtained with substantial AUC-ROC values. We also proposed NTCP-derived Dmax constraints and developed multivariate nomograms to facilitate routine clinical use. Further prospective studies in larger cohorts with extended follow-up were warranted.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13014-026-02877-y.

Introduction

Carbon-ion radiotherapy (CIRT), with its superior dosimetric conformity, allows better sparing organs at risk (OARs) compared to conventional photon radiotherapy and showed reduced symptomatic CIRT-induced toxicities in locally advanced lung cancer [1, 2]. However, unlike the significant dose reduction achieved for the lungs and heart, sparing of the esophagus (ESO) and proximal bronchial tree (PBT) remains limited due to their immediate proximity to the gross tumor volumes (GTVs) in locally-advanced non-small cell lung cancer (LA-NSCLC) [1].

Consequently, radiation-induced injuries (RIs) in ESO and PBT represent the most frequently observed symptomatic adverse events in patients with LA-NSCLC [2]. Thus, ESO and PBT often become the most important dose-limiting structures in CIRT planning, and require validated normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) models to assess their toxicity risks in thoracic irradiation.

The Lyman-Kutcher-Burman (LKB) [3, 4]-based NTCP model is one of the most common approaches for characterizing the relationship between RIs and dose-volume histograms (DVHs). Although several LKB-based NTCP models for RIs in ESO have been established for both conventional photon radiotherapy (RT), including the 3-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT), intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), and passive-scattering proton therapy (PSPT) [5–7], these models may be incompatible with CIRT due to carbon-ion’s unique physical and biological properties.

Current clinical practice for CIRT still largely relies on dose constraints extrapolated from photon and proton data, which may lead to inaccurate estimation of RI risks to OARs such as ESO and PBT (RI-ESO and RI-PBT) [8, 9]. Furthermore, to our best knowledge, there are limited data available on validated LKB-based NTCP models and the corresponding evidence-based dose constraints derived from large-scale clinical outcomes, especially for LA-NSCLC treated with local effect model (LEM)-based CIRT.

A key motivation for developing local effect model (LEM)‑specific NTCP models lies in the inherent limitations of the LEM itself, including the relative biological effectiveness (RBE)‑weighted doses calculated by LEM not being biologically isoeffective across different tissues. This shortcoming is partly attributed to the use of a constant photon α/β ratio of 2 Gy in this RBE model, a value that is substantially lower than the actual α/β ratios reported for several OARs, such as ESO and PBT. Consequently, a generic NTCP model derived from LEM‑based doses cannot be directly transferred from other radiotherapy modalities. Therefore, establishing dedicated NTCP models are imperative to support clinical decision-making and optimize OAR sparing during treatment planning for LEM-based CIRT.

This study aimed to (1) develop LKB-based NTCP models of acute/late RIs in ESO (ARI-ESO/LRI-ESO) and late RIs in PBT (LRI-PBT) of LA-NSCLC patients treated with CIRT using pencil beam scanning (PBS) technique, LEM, in addition to sequential chemotherapy and immunotherapy, (2) propose dosimetric data-derived recommendations of maximum dose constraint for ESO and PBT based on the established NTCP models, and (3) establish clinically practical nomograms by integrating multiple dosimetric and volumetric parameters to predict individualized risks of RI-ESO and RI-PBT.

Material and methods

Patient cohort

Consecutive 184 patients with LA-NSCLC receiving CIRT who met the following inclusion criteria were enrolled in this retrospective study: (1) pathologically confirmed NSCLC, (2) staged IIIA–IIIC per American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition, (3) CIRT started during December 2016 to August 2023 at Shanghai Proton and Heavy Ion Center (SPHIC) (4) completed ≥ 95% of planned CIRT fractions, and (5) minimum follow-up of 6 months after CIRT initiation. Patients with prior thoracic radiotherapy or surgery for malignancies were excluded.

The study protocol was approved by SPHIC Institutional Review Board: SPHIC-TR-THLC-2026-02 (RS), with waived informed consent for anonymized retrospective data.

Treatment planning

The details of immobilization, CT simulation, and treatment planning were reported by the previous study [8]. CIRT plans were designed using Syngo© (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) treatment planning system (TPS), with RBE-weighted doses calculated based on LEM-I [10] and a constant photon α/β ratio of 2 Gy. Plans employed 2–4 (typically 3) rotating angles of the treatment couch with a fixed-oblique 45° beam to achieve optimal dose conformity. The third beam was typically selected from the superior-oblique direction, approximately 40º–50º away from the relatively closer field. All plans were optimized using PBS combined with multiple field optimization. Dose constraints to OARs were home-made, based on the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines for RT [9], published proton RT data [11–13], and the previous dose escalating studies with clinical observation in CIRT [2]. ESO and PBT were contoured on planning CT per RTOG consensus [14]. DVHs of the respective OARs were extracted for analysis.

The primary endpoints and radiation-induced injury assessment

Mitigating severe or clinically significant (symptomatic) RIs of ESO and PBT represents a key clinical objective in CIRT for LA-NSCLC. This study would establish NTCP models for all grade ≥ 2 (defined as clinically significant symptomatic events) or ≥ 3 (defined as clinically significant severe events) RIs corresponding to the three endpoints: ARI-ESO, LRI-ESO, and LRI-PBT. Furthermore, dose constraints for ESO and PBT will be proposed from the NTCP models specifically developed for grade ≥ 3 LRI-ESO and LRI-PBT, as well as for grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO (given the absence of observed grade ≥ 3 ARI-ESO events). Acute RI of PBT was not analyzed in this study due to its low incidence and mild severity (usually grade 1 toxicity).

Acute or late RI was defined as any event occurring within or after 3 months from the initiation of CIRT, respectively. All RIs were prospectively collected in accordance with the institutional protocol for particle therapy at SPHIC, and graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.0. Ambiguous cases underwent consensus reviews to ensure reproducibility. Follow-up assessments were performed every 3 to 4 months for the first 2 years after CIRT and every 6 months for the subsequent 3 years and annually thereafter, as previously reported [2].

LKB-based NTCP modeling and OAR dose constraint calculating

The LKB-based NTCP model quantifies the relationship between NTCP risks and dose-volume distribution through 3 key variables: (1) “n”, the volume-effect parameter, which reflects an organ’s characteristics to partial-volume irradiation; (2) “m”, radiation sensitivity parameter, which characterizes the steepness of the dose-response curve; (3) “TD50”, the tolerance dose at which a 50% NTCP occurs. where EUD denotes the equivalent uniform dose, and “vi” represents the percentage volume of the targets receiving dose “Di”. These parameters (n, m, TD50) in the LKB-based NTCP models were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). Proposing dose constraints for ESO and PBT derived from LKB-based NTCP models

In this study, approximate maximum dose (ADmax) was defined as the maximum doses to 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.5%, 1% and 2% volumes of the OARs (D0.1%, D0.2%, D0.5%, D1% and D2%), as well as the maximum doses to 0.1 cubic centimeters (cc), 0.2 cc, 0.5 cc, 1 cc and 2 cc (D0.1cc, D0.2cc, D0.5cc, D1cc and D2cc) of the OARs.

According to Eq. (1), the EUD value approaches the maximum dose (Dmax) to OAR when the volume-parameter n approaches 0, and approaches the mean dose (Dmean) while n approaches 1. We assumed that the computed EUD in the LKB-based NTCP models equals Dmax or ADmax when n is set to 0, and equals Dmean when n is set to 1.

Therefore, by setting n to 0 and 1, the variables m and TD50 for high-dose metrics of Dmax, ADmax, and Dmean were regressed to estimate the cut off values of the dosimetric parameters for clinically significant RIs, i.e., grade ≥ 2 or ≥ 3 acute or late RIs of the ESO or PBT in CIRT.

The suggested dose constraints were obtained in two different criteria: dose constraints (A) were under the evaluated NTCP risk of 5% and dose constraints (B) were under the evaluated NTCP risk of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve-driven best threshold in the respective LKB-based NTCP models by setting n to 0 or 1. Final constraints of Dmax values for ESO and PBT will be proposed by integrating both sets of the derived dose limits with practical clinical considerations.

Assessing the established NTCP model: accuracy and risk stratification power

The area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC) was used to assess the performance of the fitted NTCP models, with its 95% confidence interval (CI). The ROC curve was plotted by setting false positive rate on the x-axis and the true positive rate on the y-axis across various NTCP threshold values. The best threshold of NTCP risk was determined as the value corresponding to the maximum Youden index [15] on the ROC defined as ‘sensitivity + specificity −1’. The 95% CIs of AUC-ROC values were determined using 2000 stratified bootstrap replicates. Patients were then evaluated for their estimated NTCP risks and subsequently stratified into low- and high-risk cohorts based on the best thresholds derived from the ROC curves. The actual RI incidences in the two cohorts were calculated and statistical tests were performed to assess the significance of the difference between them.

Developing LASSO-based multi-dosimetric-variate NTCP nomogram

In the LKB-based NTCP models, the EUD equals to Dmax or Dmean when setting parameter n to 0 or 1 exactly. However, fitted values of n typically fall between 0 to 1, indicating a complex dosimetric translation from the OAR DVH to the corresponding EUD. To address this, multivariate analysis using LASSO-logistic regression was used to estimate the RI incidence, by cooperating multiple best thresholds-derived dosimetric constraints.

Specifically, the best thresholds-derived dosimetric constraints of the NTCP modelling were used to stratify the patients as 0 or 1 for each RI with specific grades if their values of the dosimetric parameters (Dmax, each ADmax, and Dmean) fell below or exceeded the respective best thresholds derived from the ROC curves. The LASSO [16] algorithm was applied to select the most relevant best thresholds-derived dose constraints, and reduce the collinearity and redundancy among the included variables. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined by 10-fold cross-validation to minimize prediction bias. in which xi indicated the classification by the best thresholds-derived dose constraints of the LASSO-selected dosimetric parameter/variable and ßi represented the corresponding coefficient derived from logistic regression. Each included variable is assigned a score on the top scale of the respective nomogram. A patient’s total score is the sum of these individual scores, which is then projected vertically downward to determine the corresponding NTCP risk of a certain RI.

Statistical analysis

The statistically significance of the difference between the observed AUC value and 0.5 (representing random classification) was tested using the Wilcoxon test. For comparisons of actual RI incidences between the low- and high-risk cohorts, the Chi-square test was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 3.3.1; https://www.r-project.org). A two-tailed p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Table 2 also presented the ROC-derived optimal EUD thresholds (the best threshold) for discriminating NTCP risk with substantial AUC-ROCs (0.749–0.814). The actual incidences of the respective adverse events in patients with EUD values below or above these thresholds were also listed. Patients whose EUD values of a certain endpoint fell below ROC curve-derived best thresholds were classified into the low-risk cohort. Within this cohort, the actual incidences of RIs ranged from 0.0% to 1.9%, which were significantly lower than that observed in the high-risk cohort (9.5%–19.0%, p < 0.001; Table 2). For instance, the actual incidence of grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO was 0.0% (0/98) in patients with an EUD ≤ 64.66 Gy (RBE) compared to 10.5% (9/86) in those with an EUD > 64.66 Gy (RBE).

Proposing dose constraints for ESO and PBT derived from LKB-based NTCP models

Per the definition of EUD in the LKB-based NTCP formula, the values of TD50 for Dmax, ADmax or Dmean for clinically significant (grade ≥ 3) RIs were derived by setting n to 0 or 1, respectively (details are provided in Supplement Tables S1–S4). Grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO was selected due to the absence of acute grade ≥ 3 toxicities in ESO.

For example, the estimated NTCP of grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO would be ≤ 5% when Dmax of the ESO (Dmax-ESO) was restricted to ≤78.24 Gy (RBE) (Supplement Table S1). When using the ROC curve-derived best threshold as the classification reference, the estimated NTCP risk would be ≤ 4.9% if Dmax-ESO was restricted to ≤78.14 Gy (RBE). Similarly, to restrict the estimated NTCP risk of clinically significant (grade ≥ 3) LRI-ESO or LRI-PBT to ≤5%, Dmax for these OARs should not exceed 79.49 or 80.41 Gy (RBE), respectively (Supplement Tables S2–S3).

Consequently, Dmax ≤ 78 Gy (RBE) for ESO and ≤80 Gy (RBE) for PBT were proposed as clinical dose constraints, which were derived from the conservative (lower) value between the NTCP ≤ 5% and NTCP ≤ the ROC curve-derived best threshold and rounded down to the nearest integer Gy (RBE). Among patients whose actual Dmax values fell below the proposed constraints, the observed incidences of these clinically significant toxicities were as follows: grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO 2.0% (2/100), grade ≥ 3 LRI-ESO 1.0% (1/100), and grade ≥ 3 LRI-PBT 2.6% (3/114). Of grade ≥ 2 LRI-ESO and grade ≥ 2 LRI-PBT, the observed incidences were 3.0% (3/100) and 5.3% (6/114).

Based on the prior retrospective study [2], the most frequently applied fractionation in clinical CIRT was 70–77 Gy (RBE) delivered in 20–22 fractions. Therefore, applying these two proposed Dmax constraints (≤78 Gy (RBE) for ESO and ≤80 Gy (RBE) for PBT) would be clinically feasible. These thresholds are compatible with our clinical planning objectives, ensuring the dose coverage requirement of “isodose line of 95% PD covering 99% target volume”, while simultaneously maintaining ESO and PBT Dmax below the proposed constraints in the actual clinical treatment planning.

The dose constraint suggestions of other DVH-based metrics of ADmax, detailed in Supplement Tables S1–S3, might serve as references for clinical practice.

Given that the n values of the established LKB-based NTCP models were all substantially less than 1 (all < 0.4), the EUD values calculated by fixing n = 1 are not appropriate for use as the dose constraints of Dmean in CIRT for LA-NSCLC.

Discussion

This study established the first LKB-based NTCP models for CIRT-induced ESO and PBT toxicities in a large and clinically homogeneous cohort of 184 LA-NSCLC patients. Treatments employed PBS delivery with RBE-weighted doses calculated via LEM model. Cohort characteristics in this study included: (1) consistent fraction size [60.9% patients used the median fraction size of 3.5 Gy (RBE) and 85.9% patients used a fraction dose of 3.4–3.6 Gy (RBE)]; (2) predominant sequential therapy (chemotherapy: 88.6%; immunotherapy: 90.8%) without concurrent anti-cancer medicine; (3) prospectively collected toxicity data per institutional protocol for particle therapy.

For grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO, the volume parameter n (0.053) derived from our CIRT cohort were approaching 0, indicated that high-dose exposure would predominantly drive this toxicity and necessitate strict high dose restriction (e.g. Dmax) to mitigate clinically significant reactions. Dmax ≤ 78 Gy (RBE) to ESO was recommended for clinical application after setting n to 0. However, the regression results of n were approaching yet failed to equal to 0, and D2cc-ESO was found to be the dosimetric discriminator with the highest AUC-ROC value (Supplement Tables S1). It suggested that hot-spot volume of ESO receiving high doses instead of the “pixel” maximum dose might be more meaningful when predicting its RIs and directing the personalized treatment planning in CIRT.

For grade ≥ 2 and ≥3 late PBT toxicities, n values (0.139 and 0.224) were slightly higher than that for grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO (0.053), while remained close to 0. This suggested that these adverse events were also significantly influenced by high-dose exposure, supporting the rationale for using Dmax as a dose constraint. However, Dmean still needs to be reduced to an acceptable level in the further clinical practice and researches for LA-NSCLC patients treated with CIRT (Supplement Tables S3).

For grade ≥ 2 and ≥3 late ESO toxicities, n values (0.381 and 0.362) were even higher than those for late PBT toxicities, suggesting that intermediate-dose volumes also contribute to late ESO toxicities in CIRT. This observed dose-volume dependency indicated that ESO exhibited both serial and parallel tissue response characteristics in the development of LRI-ESO, which thereby necessitated the implementation of dose constraints on Dmax, ADmax, and Dmean. Unfortunately, the present study could not directly derive a practical CIRT-specific Dmean constraint for late ESO toxicities due to n <<1, which warranted further investigation. Photon criterion (Dmean < 34 Gy) can provide interim guidance until CIRT-specific constraints are validated.

Based on the above findings, Dmax ≤ 78 Gy (RBE) for ESO and ≤80 Gy (RBE) for PBT, were proposed for LA-NSCLC using LEM-based CIRT with fraction size of ~3.5 Gy (RBE) and without concurrent anti-cancer medicine. In our cohort, patients received doses below these thresholds exhibited incidences of grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO, grade ≥ 3 LRI-ESO, and grade ≥ 3 LRI-PBT toxicities of only 2.0%, 1.0%, and 2.6%, respectively.

When obtaining dose constraints, two approaches were used: (A) setting NTCP at the fixed 5% level, and (B) identifying the ROC curve-derived best threshold (Supplement Tables S1–S4). For grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO and grade ≥ 3 LRI-ESO, the best thresholds for NTCP risks of all parameters were around 5% (2.5%–7.5%) and the actual incidence were all ≤ 1.6% in the low-risk group with AUC values of 0.723–0.794. However, for grade ≥ 3 LRI-PBT, only the best threshold of Dmean and Dmax for NTCP risks were close to 5% (2.7%–5.1%) and the actual incidence were ≤ 2.4% in the low-risk cohort with AUC values of 0.740–0.782. For the rest ADmax parameters, if using the best threshold of NTCP risks (all ~10%) as the stratifying value, the actual incidence in the high-risk cohorts (patients with ADmax larger than the best threshold) were > 20% (20.8%–26.3%) while those in the low-risk cohorts remained low (~3.1%) with AUC-ROC = 0.701–0.714. Therefore, the suggested dosimetric constraints derived from these two discrimination strategies (NTCP = 5% or the ROC curve-derived best threshold) could be referred as the “soft” and “hard” constraints to balance OAR sparing and target coverage.

Per LKB-based NTCP formula, when the volume parameter n approaches 0, EUD converges to Dmax. Consequently, for toxicities with fitted n ≈ 0 (e.g., grade ≥ 2 ARI-ESO and grade ≥ 2 LRI-PBT), TD50 derived under n = 0 assumption provided a reliable Dmax constraint. Conversely, for endpoints with higher n values (e.g., grade ≥ 3 LRI-ESO: n = 0.381; grade ≥ 3 LRI-PBT: n = 0.224), reliability of Dmax-only constraints diminishes, and the computed EUD under n = 0 become overly conservative. In other words, using this computed EUD as Dmax restriction might be conservative in terms of toxicity occurring. The derived dosimetric constraints of other ADmax in Supplement Tables S1–S3 could provide further references for clinical application to reduce symptomatic toxicities without significantly compromising the dose coverage to the targets. Validation of these suggested dosimetric constraints in larger cohorts with longer follow-up is warranted to confirm their clinical feasibility.

The fitting results (n, m, TD50) of the LKB-based NTCP models using CIRT in this study and the published studies using photon 3DCRT/IMRT and PSPT data presented unexpected larger difference of parameters n and m among the different studies (Supplementary Table S5, n = 0.03–0.44, m = 0.03–0.51). The disagreement in n and m values for acute or late ESO toxicities among different radiation modalities might arise from: (1) differences between RBE-weighted doses in CIRT or PSPT and physical doses in photon therapy; (2) the different fraction doses used among different NTCP studies for LA-NSCLC, which may alter dose-response dynamics, (3) different definition of the detailed endpoint; (4) potential (though limited) anatomic sparing of ESO and PBT by CIRT’s sharp dose falloff; and (5) different patient populations involved, such as administration with or without concurrent chemotherapy. The research by Zhu et al. [17] revealed that in 3DCRT, whether applying concurrent chemotherapy or not would vary the fitting values of parameters n, m, and TD50. Patients receiving concurrent chemotherapy exhibited a lower radiation tolerance: a lower n (0.09 vs 0.29, indicating the high-dose region becomes a stronger determinant of toxicity), a higher m (0.42 vs 0.15, indicating less steep dose-response), and a lower TD50 (36 vs 46, indicating 50% complication would occur by receiving a lower dose).

Notably, the recently published study [18] conducted on a different patient cohort that did not receive concurrent or sequential chemotherapy/immunotherapy reported model parmeters of n = 0.06, m = 0.16 and TD50 = 80 Gy (RBE) for evaluating the NTCP risk of grade 2 CIRT-induced acute esophagitis. This similar fitting results with independent validation cohort could partly certificate the reliability of model fitting of our study, which might serve as the generalized NTCP model in the CIRT for NSCLC with fraction dose of 3.0–4.0 Gy and using LEM-I.

Limited literature has been published on LKB-based NTCP models for either acute or late PBT toxicities. Accordingly, the finding of this study may serve as a reference for furture evaluations of RIs in CIRT and may also be informative for other radiation modalities.

Using the best threshold derived from the ROC curve of a single dosimetric parameter to stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups for RIs of ESO and PBT yielded moderate to good predictive accuracy. Critically, the volume-effect parameter n for all endpoints—though approaching 0—remained non-zero (range: 0.053–0.381), suggesting Dmean contributes partly to toxicity risk and thus was incorporated into multivariate LASSO regression along with high-dose metrics (Dmax and various ADmax). The resulting multivariate models did outperform single-variable predictors (ΔAUC: +0.034–0.090) with better discrimination (AUC: 0.823–0.884). The regressed values of coefficient β for Dmean were higher when n values increased (Table 3)—exhibiting its more significant contribution. Mean doses to these OARs should also be restricted to reduce clinically significant toxicity risk. A clinical decision-making tool was established based on the finalized nomograms (Fig. 3) for estimating personalized RI incidences of ESO and PBT during CIRT planning. These tools, both the proposed dose constraints to ESO/PBT and the clinical RI risk-predicting tool, provided a framework for safely exploring the optimal CIRT fractionation and potentially improving therapeutic ratios in LA-NSCLC.

The multivariable nomogram showed higher AUC values than the EUD‑based model. The difference might be originated from its greater flexibility and numbers of predictors, which carries a higher risk of overfitting. This is a trade-off between model complexity and generalizability. Therefore, we do not consider EUD technique suboptimal. The EUD model is a single‑parameter, theory‑driven summary of the entire DVH. Howevere, its clinical use is often limited by TPS implementation constraints (e.g., limited commercial availability and integer conversion of 1/n). Moreover, the fitted n values for symptomatic endpoints were not strictly 0 or 1 (e.g., n = 0.362 for grade ≥ 3 late esophageal toxicity), indicating mixed high‑dose and intermediate‑dose effects that a single dose metric may not fully separate. Combining high‑dose metrics with Dmean better captures this pattern and offers greater clinical convenience by offering direct DVH readability and TPS independence. We hereby propose a complementary two‑track strategy: EUD constraints are preferred when TPS supports them; otherwise, Dmax constraints (for plan optimization) or nomograms (for risk prediction) provide practical alternatives. Both approaches were validated in our cohort by risk discrimination for the actual incidences of RIs, and presented acceptable performance.

Nomograms remain a widely used tool in radiation oncology because they offer a transparent, visual, and immediately interpretable representation of the predictive models, particularly for readers without direct access to computational platforms.

The RBE-weighted dose in this study was calculated using LEM I [10], with a constant photon α/β ratio of 2 Gy. Notably, reported α/β ratios vary across tissues and endpoints: approximately 10 Gy for acute and 3 Gy for late esophageal toxicities [19], while linear-quadratic (LQ) modeling of clinical data of head-and-neck cancers yielded an α/β ratio of 4.71 Gy [20]. Similarly, an α/β of 3 Gy was applied for dose recalculation in NTCP analysis of airway toxicities following stereotactic radiotherapy [21, 22]. Using a single, fixed photon α/β ratio of 2 Gy in LEM I may lead to overestimation of RBE, a phenomenon supported by the long-term prognostic analysis in CIRT for prostate cancer [23]. Additionally, recalibrating RBE-weighted doses with an α/β ratio of 4 Gy would reduce the equivalent photon dose by approximately 20%.

Furthermore, associated systematics from LEM may manifest differently depending on patient anatomy, target geometry (position and shape), and treatment-planning parameters (e.g. beam number, their incidence angles as well as optimization techniques and goals). Consequently, the LKB-based NTCP parameters and derived dose constraints in this study are strictly applicable within the LEM I framework. Independent validation and careful translation are required to confirm their effectiveness in other clinical or dosimetric contexts.

The study on the long-term effectiveness of protons and carbon-ions in prostate cancer by Eichkorn et al. [23] revealed the significantly lower effectiveness of the calculated RBE-weighted doses in the CIRT with LEM I using a photon α/β ratio of 2 Gy. There might also exist the conversion factor of 0.4–2.0 for the prescribed carbon-ion doses from 1 to 60 Gy (RBE) between LEM I and Microdosimetric Kinetic Model (MKM) [24]. Even with the translation between the two RBE models of LEM I and the modified MKM (mMKM) in CIRT, the deviations were found between −8%–3.9% when matching the LEM constraints with the recommended mMKM constraints [25]. Therefore, accurate translation between the RBE-weighted by LEM I and mMKM, and between the RBE-weighted doses in CIRT and photon data are warranted to demonstrate the efficacy of the established NTCP models, which could be validated in an external dataset.

Other limitations of this study firstly lie in the nature of retrospective study. Secondly, the statistical power was constrained by the limited number of high-grade toxicity events (7–10 events/each endpoint). Thirdly, the difference between delivered and planned dose distributions existed due to the various uncertainties (e.g., range, setup, respiratory motion uncertainties) of CIRT using PBS technique for LA-NSCLC [26, 27], as well as size and location changes of the targets and OARs, etc. Therefore, the doses of the protection risk volumes (PRVs) of ESO and PBT combined with robustness analysis could be further explored to optimize the LKB-based NTCP modelling [28]. Fourthly, the limited number of patients enrolled hindered the current study to integrate patient-specific factors (e.g., medications used in the peri-irradiation period, baseline comorbidities, or genetic characteristics), which could further refine risk stratification. Finally, the use of ROC curves for evaluating NTCP models has been questioned due to inter‑patient variability [29]. Bahn et al. [29] observed that even with 500 patients, the 95% CI of AUC‑ROC spanned about 0.1. Uncertainty in fitted LKB parameters (e.g., n) may also affect ROC estimates. In our analysis, recalculating AUC‑ROC using n ± SD produced lower values than those obtained with the fitted n (Supplementary Table S6). To mitigate these limitations, we supplemented ROC analysis with chi‑square tests to compare toxicity incidences between low‑ and high‑risk cohorts (all p < 0.05, Table 2).

Therefore, prospective studies with more patients and longer follow-up are warranted to validate the discrimination power of the established NTCP models and the accuracy of the proposed dosimetric constraints recommendation in this study.

In summary, for patients with LA-NSCLC using sequential chemotherapy and immunotherapy, the first LKB-based NTCP models were established for acute or late, symptomatic ESO and PBT injuries caused by CIRT with the fraction dose of ~3.5 Gy (RBE) using LEM-I and PBS technique (AUC = 0.784–0.814). The optimal EUD thresholds (ROC‑derived) defined a low‑risk cohort with an actual incidence of ≤1.9% for each adverse event. Dose constraints of Dmax ≤ 78 Gy (RBE) for ESO and ≤80 Gy (RBE) for PBT were suggested. A handy clinical RI incidence-estimation tool was proposed using multivariate (Dmax, ADmax, and Dmean) nomograms with improved AUCs of 0.823–0.884. Further prospective studies with more patients and longer follow-up are warranted.
