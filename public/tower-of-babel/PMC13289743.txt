Cholesteryl ester storage disease—a rare case of elevated liver enzymes and cholesterol levels with similarities to familial hypercholesterolaemia: a case report

Introduction

Lysosomal acid lipase (LAL) is an essential enzyme responsible for hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters and triglycerides. LAL deficiency (LAL-D) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by homozygosity or compound heterozygosity of pathogenic genetic variants in the lipase A (LIPA) gene encoding LAL. LAL-D is characterized by progressive accumulation of cholesteryl esters and triglycerides in hepatocytes and macrophages, which may lead to organ damage. The severity of LAL-D depends on the specific pathogenic variants and their influence on the enzymatic function of LAL. In the absence of enzymatic activity in LAL, infants may develop Wolman disease with failure to thrive, adrenal calcification, hepatomegaly, and hepatic failure, resulting in a median lifespan of just 3.7 months if untreated.1 Late onset LAL-D presenting as cholesteryl ester storage disease (CESD) may be subclinical with hepatomegaly, increased hepatic enzymes, and high cholesterol levels.2,3 Treatment options for LAL-D include risk factor modification, long-term enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), dietary substrate reduction, haematopoietic stem cell transplant, and liver transplantation.4,5

LAL-D is a rare condition, and the exact prevalence is not known, although previous studies have reported a prevalence of LAL-D of approximately 4 to 6 cases per million inhabitants.6,7

However, individuals with LAL-D may present with severe hypercholesterolaemia and therefore may be misclassified as having familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH), which is far more common presenting in approximately 1:300 individuals.8

In this case report, we describe a girl, who was compound heterozygous for two pathogenic LIPA variants consistent with CESD and the challenges involved in diagnosing a rare subclinical disease with biochemical findings similar to FH.

Case description

A 13-year-girl was diagnosed with juvenile arthritis primarily affecting her right knee. During routine workup prior to initiating methotrexate, hepatic enzymes were measured, surprisingly showing an alanine transaminase (ALT) of 354 U/L (normal range: 10–45 U/L) and alkaline phosphate (ALP) of 479 U/L (normal range: 45–115 U/L). At this point, the patient was not experiencing any other symptoms or showing objective extraarticular findings of interest. Continuously increased ALT and ALP resulted in further blood tests showing total cholesterol of 9.3 mmol/L (normal range: <5.0 mmol/L), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) of 7.1 mmol/L (normal range: <3.0 mmol/L), and triglycerides of 2.7 mmol/L (normal range: <2.0 mmol/L). Additional blood tests including thyrotropin, blood glucose, bilirubin, ferritin, antitrypsin, ceruloplasmin, immunoglobulins, autoantibodies, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and C-reactive protein were within the normal range (Table 1). Serological test including cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus and Parvovirus showed no sign of viral infections. Hepatobiliary ultrasound showed hepatomegaly with no additional pathology, and liver elastography showed liver stiffness of 5.9 kPa (normal range: 2–7 kPa). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography confirmed hepatosplenomegaly and ruled out primary sclerosing cholangitis. A subsequent liver biopsy was performed, showing hepatocyte ballooning and clear cytoplasm, raising suspicion of glycogen storage disease. No signs of steatosis or autoimmune disease were found. She had no objective signs of cholesterol deposits such as xanthelasmata, arcus cornealis, tendon xanthomas, or eruptive xanthomas. Her father had an LDL-C of 2.4 mmol/L and her mother an LDL-C of 3.3 mmol/L. There was no family history of cardiovascular disease. The patient was initially genetically tested for the most common genetic variants causative of FH: the low-density lipoprotein receptor gene, the apolipoprotein B gene, and the proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 gene. No pathogenic genetic variants were identified, and the patient and her parents were referred for trio whole genome sequencing to identify rare lipid disorders and potential metabolic disorders.9,10 The patient was found to be compound heterozygous for NM_000235.4:c.894G>A p.(Gln298=) and NM_000235.4:c.419G>A p.(Trp140*) in the LIPA gene as NM_000235.4:c.894G>A p.(Gln298=) was inherited from the mother and NM_000235.4:c.419G>A p.(Trp140*) was inherited from the father. Both variants were classified as pathogenic, resulting in the patient being diagnosed with CESD (Figure 1). The patient has two siblings, a fraternal twin with LDL-C of 2.1 mmol/L, triglyceride of 1.1 mmol/L, and ALT of 12 U/L, and a younger brother with LDL-C of 2.1 mmol/L, triglyceride 0.5 mmol/L, and ALT of 14 U/L. The siblings have not yet been tested for the genetic variants found in the index patient.

After establishing the CESD diagnosis based on the genetic findings, the patient had her LAL activity measured showing unmeasurably low enzymatic activity. During the diagnostic process between June 2022 and January 2024, the patient showed variations in LDL-C between 4.9 and 7.1 mmol/L and in ALT between 92 and 262 U/L. The patient has not presented with any symptoms suggestive of organ damage, and due to no clinical suspicion of cardiovascular disease, no transthoracic echocardiography or cardiac CT scan has been performed.

The initial treatment efforts have been focused on dietary restrictions after a dietician consult and initiation of statin therapy, while treatment options with enzyme replacement are being discussed. In the 21 months of diagnostic testing and observation, there has been no progression in fibrosis (FibroScan 5.9 kPa in January 2023 vs. 5.3 kPa in October 2024). She will at minimum receive a clinical evaluation every 6 months, a yearly liver elastography, and, if deemed relevant, a liver biopsy.

Discussion

We present a rare case of CESD in a young girl compound heterozygous for c.894G>A p.(Gln298=) and c.419G>A p.(Trp140*) in the LIPA gene. The pathogenic variant c.894G>A p.(Gln298=) is the most common variant documented causing LAL-D11,12 and has previously been identified in a sibling pair of similar nationality diagnosed with CESD.13

Initial screening for FH showed no pathogenic mutations. This does not rule out FH, but diagnosing clinical FH in children represents a diagnostic challenge. The patient fulfilled the MEDPED criteria for FH, whereas the patient was classified as possible FH according to the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network criteria and the Simon Broome criteria. However, these criteria have not been formally validated in paediatric study populations. Recently, a paediatric diagnostic score for FH (FH-PeDS)14 has been developed, and according to this, the patient was classified as probable FH with a score of 12 points. Hence, it is very likely a patient as in this case report would be diagnosed with clinical FH and not receive targeted treatment for her underlying disorder.

Our patient had unmeasurable low LAL activity, but aside from elevated ALT, high lipid levels, and hepatosplenomegaly, she did not show signs of significant hepatic fibrosis and did not exhibit any other clinical symptoms at age 17. Bernstein et al.11 have previously reviewed 135 cases of LAL-D showing the unpredictable prognosis of CESD ranging from subclinical presentations to liver dysfunction and ultimately liver failure. In their review, 11/135 patients died between 7 and 56 years of age, primarily due to liver failure, and they noted a general lack of long-term follow-up data among patients with CESD.11

Treatment options in patients with CESD have previously been focused on dietary restrictions, lipid lowering drugs, and liver transplantation in case of liver failure. Since 2015, sebelipase alfa, an intravenous drug with recombinant human LAL, has been approved for use in LAL deficiency. A phase 3 trial (ARISE) of 66 CESD patients receiving sebelipase for 20 weeks has shown significant reduction in ALT (normalization in 31% vs. 7% in the placebo group) and improved lipid parameters and liver volume.15 These results were sustained over a 5-year open-label period, with mild adverse events and few developing anti-drug-antibodies. Despite being approved for use in patients with CESD, sebelipase is a very expensive drug, and no clear guidelines exist regarding when to initiate the treatment. Enzymatic replacement therapy requires infusion every other week, imposing a burden on the patients, in this case an active teenager with no symptoms. Patient preferences and treatment options will be evaluated regularly and discussed with her and her parents. However, lipid-lowering treatment has been initiated to lower her risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Our case report highlights the challenges in diagnosing CESD and the biochemical similarities to more common diseases such as FH. It also shows the difficulties in choosing between treatment options in CESD due to high variations in disease progression and limited but promising data on ERT.
