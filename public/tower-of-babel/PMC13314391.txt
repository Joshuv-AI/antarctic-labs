Drying Temperature Modulates Phytochemical Integrity and Antioxidant Potential of Sungkai (Peronema canescens Jack) Leaves
Authors: Tri Yulni, Leopold Oscar Nelwan, Sutrisno Suro Mardjan, Lamhot Parulian Manalu, Lusiana Kresnawati Hartono, Tantry Eko Putri Mariastuty, Himawan Adinegoro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sungkai (Peronema canescens Jack) leaves are traditionally used in Southeast Asian medicine and are enriched in bioactive compounds having antioxidant activity, which may help mitigate oxidative stress-related disorders. Preservation of such compounds during post-harvest processing is essential to maintain their functional potential for disease prevention. However, the influence of drying temperature on the quality of Sungkai leaf simplicia－dried plant material used for medicinal purposes－has not been systematically evaluated. This study investigated the effects of various drying temperatures: ambient, 40°C, 50°C, 60°C, and 70°C－on the physicochemical properties, phytochemical content, antioxidant capacity, color stability, microstructure, and metabolite profile. Drying at 40°C effectively preserved total flavonoid and phenolic contents, antioxidant activity, chlorophyll retention, and metabolite diversity while significantly reducing duration compared with ambient drying. In contrast, temperatures ≥50°C accelerated demoisturization and caused a substantial degradation of phytochemicals, enhanced browning, structural collapse, and reduced antioxidant capacity. Multivariate analysis confirmed drying temperature as the primary determinant of Sungkai leaf quality. These findings demonstrate that moderate temperatures optimize the processing efficiency-bioactive preservation balance, providing scientific guidance for standardizing the processing of Sungkai leaves as a functional herbal ingredient for preventive nutrition applications.

INTRODUCTION

Sungkai (Peronema canescens Jack) leaves are widely used in traditional medicine across Southeast Asia, particularly for managing symptoms of infectious diseases and fever. Traditionally prepared as a decoction, the leaves have been utilized to treat malaria, the common cold, fever, parasitic infections, and oral hygiene (Yani and Putranto, 2014; Muharni et al., 2021). Recent investigations have substantiated these traditional applications, reporting immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, anticholesterol, anti-infective, antibacterial, and antioxidant activities (Sitepu, 2020; Muharni et al., 2021; Pratiwi et al., 2021; Afnuhazi et al., 2022; Latief et al., 2023). These diverse bioeffects are primarily attributed to bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, phenolics, steroids, and terpenoids (Rahardhian et al., 2022).

Plant-derived phenolics and flavonoids are well recognized for their antioxidant properties and potential to mitigate oxidative stress, a crucial factor implicated in the development of chronic diseases (Young et al., 2011; Luna-Guevara et al., 2018; Ansari et al., 2025). Consequently, preserving these constituents during post-harvest processing is essential not only to maintain therapeutic efficacy but also to support their potential application as functional herbal ingredients within preventive nutrition frameworks.

Drying is the most common post-harvest method to produce herbal simplicia by reducing moisture content (MC) and extending shelf life (Babu et al., 2018). However, the related conditions can substantially influence physicochemical characteristics and phytochemical stability (Pin, 2023). Exposure to inappropriate temperatures may degrade heat-sensitive compounds through oxidation, hydrolysis, enzyme-catalyzed reactions, or Maillard-related browning, thereby reducing bioactivity and compromising product quality. Therefore, selecting appropriate parameters is critical to ensure the stability, safety, and functional integrity of medicinal plant materials drying (Ebadi et al., 2015; Belwal et al., 2022).

Despite increasing interest in Sungkai as a bioactive-rich medicinal plant, studies evaluating the impacts of leaf-drying parameters on its phytochemical quality remain scarce. A literature survey identified numerous pharmacological studies on dried Sungkai leaf extracts; however, none have systematically examined the mechanisms by which temperature influences the retention of bioactive compounds and physicochemical integrity. In most cases, drying was treated merely as a preparatory step rather than a determinant of functional quality.

Moreover, existing studies on the drying of medicinal plants predominantly focus on bulk phytochemical alterations, with limited integration of structural, chemical, and metabolite-level analyses. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of how drying temperature influences the structure-composition-function relationship remains lacking.

Addressing this gap is essential, as inadequate drying may lead to pronounced losses in bioactive compounds and variability in functional efficacy. Evidence from other medicinal plants indicates that excessive temperatures can cause pigment degradation, browning-related reactions, and reduced antioxidant activity, ultimately affecting pharmacological value and commercial acceptability (Ali et al., 2014; Pin, 2023; Manalu et al., 2025).

Therefore, this study investigates the effects of varied drying temperatures on the physicochemical characteristics, phytochemical profile, and its retention, color stability, and microstructural properties of the metabolite profile of Sungkai leaf simplicia. By integrating scanning electron microscopy (SEM), fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), and multivariate analysis, this study aims to elucidate the relationship between structural changes, chemical composition, and functional properties under different thermal conditions. The findings provide scientific guidance for optimizing these conditions to preserve bioactive integrity and support the development of Sungkai leaves as functional herbal ingredients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material and drying treatment

Sungkai leaves were freshly collected and cleaned. For each drying treatment, ∼60 g of leaves were used per replicate. The leaves were arranged as a single layer in a vertical (crosswise) orientation, such that the bed thickness effectively corresponded to the leaf width. Leaves were dried in a laboratory hot-air oven under natural convection, with no active control or measurement of air circulation.

The temperatures set were 40°C, 50°C, 60°C, and 70°C, while air drying at ambient temperature (AT), 25°C－30°C, was the reference. All samples were dried until the MC was <10% (wet basis), per the Indonesian Herbal Pharmacopoeia standards (Ministry of Health RI, 2017).

Water activity, moisture, and ash content analysis

The water activity (aw) of the dried Sungkai leaf samples was measured at 25°C using a benchtop temperature-controlled water activity meter (Aqualab). MC was determined by applying the gravimetric method according to AOAC (1990). Samples were dried in a hot-air oven (Memmert) at 105°C until a constant weight was reached. MC was calculated based on weight loss before and after drying. Ash content was analyzed following AOAC (1990). Samples were incinerated in a muffle furnace (Nabertherm) at 550°C until complete combustion of organic matter, leaving inorganic residues. The ash content, which indicates the mineral composition, was calculated by weighing the residue. All measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Color analysis

Color was measured using an Agera Spectrophotometer (HunterLab) operating in the CIE-Lab color system to obtain lightness (L*), red/green chromaticity (a*), and yellow/blue chromaticity (b*). The total color difference (ΔE) between fresh and dried leaves was calculated using Equation (1) (McGuire, 1992).

ΔE=([Li*−L*]2+[ai*−a*]2+[bi*−b*]2)1/2 (1)

Li*, ai*, and bi* represent the color parameters of fresh leaves, and L*, a*, and b* indicate those of dried leaves.

Chlorophyll analysis

Chlorophyll C was extracted by following the method of Ngcobo et al. (2024); 0.5 g of dried leaf powder was added to 50 mL of methanol. The mixture was homogenized with a magnetic stirrer for 5 min to ensure uniform dispersion and then incubated in a water bath at 45°C for 30 min to facilitate pigment extraction. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 450 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was carefully collected for spectrophotometric analysis.

The A663 and A645 of the supernatant were assessed using a Synergy HTX multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek). Methanol was used as a blank. Chlorophyll a (Chla) and b (Chlb) were quantified in triplicate based on Equations (3) and (4) (Lichtenthaler, 1987; Ngcobo et al., 2024). Total chlorophyll was expressed as Chla+Chlb.

Chlb=27.50 A645−11.210 A663 (4)

Antioxidant, total phenolic content (TPC), and total flavonoid content (TFC) analysis

Dried simplicia was extracted by mixing 0.5 g of Sungkai leaf powder with 20 mL of 50% aqueous methanol, and agitating on a shaker for 24 h at room temperature (Lemus-Mondaca et al., 2018). The mixture was then centrifuged at 2,800 g for 25 min, and the supernatant was collected for analyzing TFC, TPC, and antioxidant capacity (AC).

The TFC was determined following the method of Abdel-Aleem et al. (2019) and Rahim et al. (2022). A 0.3 mL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 3.4 mL of 30% methanol, 0.15 mL of 0.5 M NaNO2, and 0.15 mL of 0.3 M AlCl3. After 5 min of reaction, 1.0 mL of 1 M NaOH was added, and the mixture was vortexed until homogeneous. The A506 was measured with a Synergy HTX multi-mode microplate reader. Quercetin solutions at 10－100 µg/mL were prepared to plot a standard calibration curve, and all measurements were conducted in triplicate. TFC was expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalent per gram of dry matter (mg QE/g d.m).

The TPC was estimated using the Folin-Ciocalteu method as described by Yang et al. (2007). A 0.2 mL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 1.0 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After 3 min, 0.8 mL of 7.5% Na2CO3 was added. The mixture was incubated at 30°C for 90 min, and the A765 was measured in triplicate. A standard curve was generated using gallic acid solutions, and TPCs were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of dry matter (mg GAE/g d.m).

AC was evaluated using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay, following the method of Phonphoem et al. (2022). It is widely recognized for its simplicity, rapidity, and cost-effectiveness (Pyrzynska and Pękal, 2013; Gulcin and Alwasel, 2023). In this assay, 25 µL of the extract, blank, or standard was mixed with 200 µL of DPPH solution and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The A517 was recorded; methanol served as the blank, and ascorbic acid as the standard. All measurements were conducted in triplicate. A calibration curve was prepared using various concentrations of ascorbic acid. The AC was expressed as ascorbic acid equivalent antioxidant capacity per gram of dry matter (mg AEAC/g d.m).

Microstructure analysis

SEM in secondary electron mode (SU3500, Hitachi Ltd.) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV under vacuum was used to examine the microstructure of dried Sungkai leaves. The samples were cut into blocks with a cross-section of 5×2 mm, and microstructurally imaged at 500×.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy of dried Sungkai leaf samples was conducted with a Nicolet 50 FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a diamond single-reflection attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory featuring a zinc selenide crystal (Rashid et al., 2019). Spectral data were collected over a wavenumber range of 4,000－500 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1. Each spectrum was acquired by averaging 32 scans, with the background spectrum of the cleaned diamond crystal subtracted to ensure accuracy. The ATR crystal was cleaned with alcohol between measurements to prevent cross-contamination.

Metabolite profile (GC-MS)

For GC-MS analysis, the methanol extract was prepared by macerating 5 g of dried Sungkai leaf powder in 50 mL of absolute methanol for 24 h at room temperature. The mixture was then centrifuged at 2,800 g for 25 min, and the supernatant－at ∼1% (w/v)－was collected. The extract was passed through a 0.22 µm filter into GC-MS vials before injection.

GC-MS was conducted with a GC-MS-QP2010 Ultra system (Shimadzu) equipped with an AOC-20i auto-sampler and an SH-Rtx-WaxCap column (30 m×0.53 mm i.d., 0.25 µm film thickness). The injection volume was 1 µL in split mode (split ratio: 1.0) at an injector temperature of 260°C. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.21 mL/min. The oven temperature program started at 100°C, increased at 10°C/min to 200°C, then at 15°C/min to 300°C, and was held for 22 min. MS was operated in scan mode (m/z 40－600), with the Ion Source and Interface temperatures set at 250°C and 280°C, respectively. Compounds were identified by matching their mass spectra to the NIST05 spectral library (similarity indices >90%) and quantified based on relative peak area.

Data analysis

All data were stored in “.CSV” format and analyzed using Python. Prior to statistical analysis, potential outliers were screened using the interquartile range method. No observations were excluded from the dataset. The effects of temperature on the measured parameters were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance. When significant differences were observed (P<0.05), Fisher’s least significant difference test was employed to compare multiple treatment means. Pearson correlation analysis assessed the relationships among variables. Principal component analysis (PCA), an exploratory multivariate technique, was used to visualize patterns of association among measured variables and treatments. All results are reported as means±standard deviations.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Effect of drying temperature on physicochemical characteristics

The drying of Sungkai leaves was considered complete when the predicted MC decreased to <10%, for durations ranging from 90－1,200 min (Table 1). As expected, drying times were longer at lower temperatures, whereas higher temperatures markedly accelerated moisture removal. This phenomenon is primarily driven by a steeper temperature gradient between the leaf surface and interior tissues, which enhances the vapor pressure differential and facilitates moisture migration within the leaf matrix via diffusion (Bensebia and Allia, 2015; Babu et al., 2018; Chua et al., 2019).

Drying at AT required the longest processing time, underscoring the inefficiency of passive drying methods for time-sensitive post-harvest operations. In contrast, forced hot-air drying markedly reduced drying duration by improving convective heat and mass transfer. Continuous replacement of humid air with heated air increases evaporation efficiency and stabilizes the dehydrating environment (Amedorme et al., 2013).

Final MCs ranged from 7.63%－9.80% (w.b.), complying with the standards determined by the Indonesian Herbal Pharmacopoeia (maximum 10%, w.b.), confirming the suitability of these dried leaves for medicinal applications (Pin, 2023). In parallel, aw ranged from 0.561－0.617; it was inversely proportional to the drying temperature. Most values were below the critical threshold of 0.61, which limited microbial proliferation (Tapia et al., 2020). Under such low aw conditions, microbial survival was restricted due to osmotic stress during rehydration and an inability to resume metabolic activity during storage (Erkmen and Bozoglu, 2016).

A robust positive correlation between MC and aw further reflects the distinction between total water content and thermodynamically available water. While MC quantifies total water present, aw determines the fraction available for microbial growth, enzyme activity, and oxidative reactions (Malegori et al., 2022). Therefore, simultaneous control of both parameters is critical for ensuring physicochemical stability and longer shelf life of dried herbal materials.

Ash content ranged from 6.71% to 7.21%, well below the maximum permissible limit of 12.1% set by the Indonesian Herbal Pharmacopoeia. The absence of marked differences among treatments indicates that inorganic mineral components were thermally stable at the drying temperatures applied. This stability confirms that mineral integrity was preserved across treatments, reinforcing the suitability of Sungkai leaves for incorporation into herbal formulations and nutraceutical preparations (Pojić et al., 2015).

Effect of drying temperature on phytochemical content and AC

Phytochemicals serve as key indicators of medicinal plant quality due to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and protective roles against oxidative stress-related disorders (Embuscado, 2015; Pin, 2023). However, their stability is highly dependent on post-harvest processing conditions. Thermal drying may induce volatilization, oxidative degradation, hydrolysis, and structural transformation of bioactive compounds, thereby reducing their effectiveness (Pin, 2023).

In this study, drying temperature significantly reduced the TFC, TPC, and chlorophyll (Table 2). Under AT drying, TFC decreased from 26.365 to 8.428 mg QE/g d.m. at 70°C, while TPC declined from 18.279 to 4.870 mg GAE/g d.m. Such a reduction may be attributed to heat-induced cell wall disruption, followed by oxidation and degradation of released compounds (Rababah et al., 2015; Pin, 2023). These findings suggest that temperature intensity, rather than drying duration alone, is the dominant factor influencing phytochemical retention.

Although AT preserved the greatest TFC and TPC levels, AC did not vary markedly between AT and 40°C samples (∼10.5 mg AEAC/g d.m.). This pattern indicates that antioxidant activity is influenced not only by total concentration but also by structural stability and synergism among metabolites. A stronger Pearson correlation between AC and TFC (r=0.95) than between TPC and TFC (r=0.87) further suggests that flavonoids are the principal contributors to radical scavenging activity in Sungkai leaves.

At elevated temperatures (60°C－70°C), TFC, TPC, and AC were reduced significantly. The decline in antioxidant activity at 70°C can be attributed to accelerated oxidation, thermal decomposition, and the potential participation of phenolics in non-enzyme-catalyzed browning reactions (Chan et al., 2013; Babu et al., 2018). Given that phenolics are strongly associated with the traditional medicinal attributes of Sungkai leaves, excessive heat may compromise functional quality.

Interestingly, moderate drying (AT and 40°C) elevated TPC and TFC more than fresh leaves. This apparent increase likely reflects enhanced extractability during drying, resulting from partial structural disruption (Pin, 2023). Heat may disrupt cell wall structures, facilitating the release of bound compounds and enhancing their measurable levels (Hamrouni-Sellami et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2022).

Chlorophyll content decreased substantially from 7.400 mg/g d.m. in fresh leaves to 1.947 mg/g d.m. at 70°C, reflecting high thermal sensitivity and conversion to pheophytin under heat (Babu et al., 2018). Thermal processing may also promote enzymatic browning through exposure to degradation substrates (Thamkaew et al., 2021). Low to moderate drying temperatures (30°C－50°C), as recommended by Müller and Heindl (2006), may be most suitable for maintaining heat-sensitive bioactives and should be prioritized in post-harvest processing protocols for medicinal plants.

Effect of drying temperature on the color characteristics of Sungkai leaves

Color degradation during drying is primarily driven by chlorophyll breakdown and browning reactions (Krokida et al., 2001). As chlorophyll levels decline, visual attributes measured by L*, a*, b*, BI, and ΔE change measurably (Fig. 1).

Although no significant differences were observed among treatments, a decreasing trend in L* values with increasing temperature suggests progressive darkening of the leaf simplicia. High-temperature drying promotes enzymatic and non-enzymatic browning, including Maillard reactions and pigment oxidation (Islam and Mujumdar, 2008; Calín-Sánchez et al., 2020). The marked increase in a* values from −6.818 (fresh) to positive values in dried samples reflects a loss of greenness and conversion toward reddish-brown tones, consistent with chlorophyll degradation (Śledź and Witrowa-Rajchert, 2012). Meanwhile, decreased b* values at higher temperatures indicate a loss of yellow pigments and further pigment destabilization (Djendoubi Mrad et al., 2012).

Elevated BI and ΔE values at ≥50°C confirm a substantial deviation from the original color. Similar discoloration patterns under high-temperature drying have been observed in leafy vegetables and medicinal plants (Yilmaz and Alibas, 2021). Hot-air drying at elevated temperature accelerates non-enzymatic Maillard reactions and destabilizes natural pigments, enhancing susceptibility to thermally induced color loss (Chu et al., 2023; Soontharapirakkul and Kotpat, 2023). Thus, color changes serve as indirect indicators of phytochemical degradation, reinforcing the conclusion that temperatures >50°C compromised visual and biochemical quality.

Effect of drying temperature on microstructure and FTIR spectra

The microstructural integrity of Sungkai leaves was pronouncedly affected by temperature (Fig. 2). SEM analysis demonstrated progressive structural collapse at higher temperatures. Rapid moisture removal induces internal stress gradients that rupture cell walls and reduce porosity (Ngamwonglumlert and Devahastin, 2018). Morphological disintegration may enhance phytochemical exposure to O2, accelerating their oxidative degradation.

Conversely, moderate drying preserved cell integrity, potentially limiting O2 diffusion and protecting cellular compounds (Vega-Gálvez et al., 2012). Such structural preservation is consistent with elevated phytochemical retention at 40°C.

FTIR spectra (Fig. 3) confirmed the presence of key functional groups associated with phenolics, flavonoids, and carbohydrates (Mattinen et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2013; Masek et al., 2014; Preserova et al., 2015). A broad peak between 3,860 and 3,000 cm−1 was consistent across all samples, corresponding to O-H stretching vibrations indicative of −OH groups involved in hydrogen bonding (Liu et al., 2013). Peaks within the 2,800－3,000 cm−1 range were attributed to C-H stretching, typically associated with CH3 groups, carbohydrates, free amino acids, and phenolics (Mattinen et al., 2009). The region from 1,700 to 1,600 cm−1 indicated absorption associated with C=O and C=C stretching vibrations, characteristic of carbonyl groups in phenolic acids and flavonoids (Preserova et al., 2015). Additionally, bands between 1,545－1,200 cm−1 were linked to aromatic ring vibrations and C-O-C bonds of flavonoids and other polyphenols; however, peaks in the 1,200－1,027 cm−1 range were assigned to C-O and C-C stretching, indicating the presence of carbohydrates and phenolic ether groups (Masek et al., 2014).

Variations in peak intensity across treatments suggest temperature-dependent alterations in hydroxyl and aromatic groups. Enhanced preservation of phenolic-related bands at moderate temperatures aligns with quantitative TPC and TFC data (Ratseewo et al., 2025), further supporting temperature-mediated chemical stability.

The results demonstrate that FTIR is a valuable, non-destructive tool for rapidly assessing changes in phytochemical composition of dried medicinal plants. In the case of Sungkai leaves, FTIR analysis confirmed the presence of crucial bioactive functional groups and revealed the influence of thermal processing on their structural integrity and concentration. The differences in transmittance may be associated with variations in component concentration, structural modifications, and matrix interactions induced by drying temperatures (Kumar et al., 2024). Therefore, FTIR data can indicate relative changes in functional groups rather than definitive evidence of chemical transformation.

GC-MS analysis of the metabolite profile

GC-MS (Fig. 4) revealed that drying at 40°C yielded the broadest metabolite diversity and total ion chromatogram intensity. Moderate heat likely enhanced extractability by disrupting cellular structures without inducing extensive thermal degradation (Xing et al., 2017). In contrast, peak intensities declined markedly at 60°C－70°C, consistent with thermal oxidation and decomposition of temperature-sensitive metabolites (Chen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Lower intensities in AT samples may reflect limited structural disruption and slower mass transfer (Qin et al., 2022).

Detection of terpenoid, quinone, and steroidal compounds at 40°C (Table 3) suggests enhanced release and preservation of bioactive metabolites under moderate drying. Terpenoids exhibit antioxidant activity via free-radical scavenging, facilitated by conjugated double bonds that facilitate electron donation, thereby reducing oxidative stress markers (e.g., reactive oxygen species, malondialdehyde) and enhancing antioxidant enzymes (e.g., superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase) (Wojtunik et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025). Quinones act through redox cycling to neutralize free radicals, inhibit lipid peroxidation, and strengthen cell defense by inducing enzymes such as NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase (Dunlap et al., 2007; Lenaz and Genova, 2013; Rashid et al., 2021). Steroidal compounds modulate antioxidant enzyme expression and reduce ROS production, thereby maintaining cell redox homeostasis (Adler et al., 2012; Dembitsky and Terent’ev, 2026). Such metabolite diversity likely explains why AC at 40°C remained comparable to AT despite a lower TPC. Overall, the greater abundance of these compounds at 40°C suggests that moderate thermal conditions favor the retention of functional metabolites, whereas greater temperatures promote their degradation and reduce bioactive diversity.

Multivariate analysis: correlation and PCA

Correlation analysis (Fig. 5) revealed pronounced negative associations between color degradation indices (a*, ΔE) and phytochemical parameters (r=0.82－0.93), confirming that pigment loss parallels degradation of bioactive compounds. The stronger correlation between AC and TFC (r=0.95) than between AC and TPC (r=0.87) indicates that flavonoids are the primary contributors to antioxidant activity. Drying time was positively correlated with TPC and TFC, suggesting that lower-temperature, longer-duration drying favors phytochemical retention.

PCA explained 94.03% of the total variance, separating samples by drying temperature (Fig. 6). Treatment at 40°C clustered with AC, TFC, chlorophyll, and favorable quality attributes; however, those at ≥50°C were associated with browning indices and color deviation. These results confirm drying temperature as the dominant determinant of Sungkai leaf quality.

Overall, drying temperature significantly influences the physicochemical, phytochemical, structural, and metabolite characteristics of Sungkai leaf simplicia. Among oven treatments, 40°C optimally balanced drying efficiency with the preservation of AC, chlorophyll, and metabolite diversity. The optimal temperature identified in this study was specific to P. canescens; thus, the experimental conditions may vary for other plants. Nevertheless, drying at moderate temperatures represents a practical balance between quality preservation and processing efficiency. The integration of SEM, FTIR, and GC-MS data demonstrates that temperature-induced structural disruption governs the release, transformation, and degradation of bioactives, thereby determining antioxidant functionality. Such multi-level evidence highlights that the effects of drying temperature extend beyond simple changes in concentration, reflecting a complex structure-composition-function relationship. This study represents the first comprehensive evaluation of the impacts of drying temperature on Sungkai leaves using multi-dimensional analytical techniques, providing scientific guidance for the standardized processing of medicinal plant materials.

This study was limited to evaluating temperature as a single processing variable and the use of chemical antioxidant assays. Future studies should consider multi-factor optimization, including air velocity and humidity, as well as biovalidation using in vitro or in vivo models to better establish functional relevance.
