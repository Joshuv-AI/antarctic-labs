Executive Summary

 This project focused on investigating two acoustic-based processing methods: a
  nozzle-based printhead and a chamber that map to two different battery electrode
  architectures: (1) a line-pattern electrode and (2) a grid-pattern electrode, respectively.
  These two parallel manufacturing and electrode geometry explorations were proposed
  for the project to understand the process space of acoustic-based manufacturing methods
  to fabricate patterned battery electrodes. This two-path exploration also allowed us to
  de-risk the overall project and not rely on a single process for creating patterned
  electrodes. This project consisted of six high-level tasks aimed at transitioning the
  concept of acoustic focusing for battery electrodes from a technology readiness level
  (TRL) of 1 to 3 by project conclusion. Task 1.0 and Task 2.0 began at the start of the
  project and ran in parallel. Under Task 1.0, the University of Washington (UW), the
  prime institution team, defined the 3D Li-ion battery (3D LIB) structures, materials,
  particle morphology and overall acoustic process conditions that must occur for project
  success. Under Task 2.0, the University of California, Santa Barbara (UCSB), the
  partner institution team, worked with UW to set up and validate a prototype acoustic
  nozzle-based printhead for line-pattern battery electrode fabrication. Under Task 3.0,
  the prime institution took a prototype acoustic nozzle platform and adapted and
  optimized the platform to successfully pattern battery electrodes. Through this research,
  our team is the first (to our knowledge) to map out and define the process space for
  patterning materials in highly viscous material media which opens the door to other
  materials applications beyond the Li-ion batteries examined in this project.

 Under Task 4.0 (a and b), we investigated the initial design and physics behind
  acoustic chamber processing to fabricate grid-patterned electrodes. Exploring two
  fabrication approaches in Tasks 3.0 and 4.0 enabled the project team to define and
  investigate new manufacturing processes efficiently while delivering the best
  performing process and electrode geometries to AMMTO in an efficient manner. A
  prototype acoustic chamber was built and tested; The initial results were promising for
  rapid fabrication of grid-pattern materials on the time scale of seconds. Under Task 5.0,
  line-patterned electrodes were successfully fabricated, and initial electrochemical
  performance showed a discharge rate capability performance improvement up to ~115%
  over a conventional baseline battery. Under Task 6.0, we conducted an initial acoustic
  processing feasibility analysis for roll-to-roll processing for future large-area, high-
  volume manufacturing. Initial results show that our process has the potential to replace
  a slot die coater on a production line, but further analysis and testing is required to vet
  this technology at higher TRL levels. Overall, we believe we have developed a practical
  and high-impact processing method that is chemistry agnostic and suitable for large-area
  fabrication of both 3D LIBs and other functional material systems where structuring on
  the scale of tens of microns has the potential to break conventional bulk material trade-
  offs in performance. In the case of batteries for electric vehicles, structuring 3D LIBs
  with our acoustic process breaks traditional energy and power trade-offs observed with
  conventional flat battery packs. The introduction of electrode structuring with acoustic
  processing can significantly improve the performance of electric vehicle batteries
  without the need for new materials or chemistries.

1. Introduction

 The overall project objective was to develop a new, scalable manufacturing
 process that uses acoustic waves to rapidly print three-dimensional (3D) Lithium-ion (Li-
 ion) battery electrodes for future high-energy and high-power Li-ion battery packs.
 Today, commercialized Li-ion battery packs are comprised of flat, planar stacks of
 components. The key battery cell components that drive battery energy and power are
 the anode and cathode electrodes. A polymer separator sheet is placed in between each
 anode and cathode electrode to prevent electrical shorting and battery failure while
 current collectors function as both support for the electrodes and act as electrical
 conductors between electrode and external circuits of a battery pack. A 3D electrode,
 which may be more challenging to fabricate than today’s conventional unstructured Li-
 ion batteries, does not need to sacrifice energy for power density. Thus, using a process
 that permits efficient 3D electrode fabrication can lead to lower costs and lifecycle
 impacts for electric vehicles and other battery applications in the future. Our new
 Acoustophoretic Additive Manufacturing (AM) process uses acoustic waves, and these
 waves create an acoustic field (known as acoustophoretic) that can be used to assemble
 particles in a fluid on a micron-scale as shown in Figure 1. This process is compatible
 with existing Li-ion battery slurry materials used commercially and has the potential to
 enable scalable manufacturing of 3D battery electrodes.

 This project resulted in three key outputs: (1) successful application of
 acoustophoretic processing to Li-ion battery materials, (2) functional line-patterned
 cathodes that demonstrated ~16-115% improvement in capacity over a reference battery
 cell, and (3) a new acoustic process for fabricating grid patterned 3D electrode
 geometries. The goal of this project was to push this technology from a TRL 1 to 3 by
 project completion. We believe this novel and disruptive acoustophoretic process is an
 example of the manufacturing innovation needed for energy storage in the United States
 (US) because it will enable large-area, consistent, controllable, and robust manufacturing.
 This in turn puts US manufacturers on a path towards a significant reduction in overall
 battery $/kWh costs. Our process is promising for enabling lower cost targets for Li-
 ion batteries in the future and in turn reduce domestic bottlenecks in Li-ion batteries
 for all electric vehicles and electronic devices that require both high-energy and high-
 power batteries. As a project lead by two academic institutions, patents and licensing are
 the primary means by which commercialization can occur. As an initial step towards
 commercialization, the project team has filed a patent application on the technology, and
 the team continues to seek support from industry and the government to further develop
 this technology.

2. Background

 Today’s Li-ion electrodes are homogenized distributions of anode or cathode
 particles (with carbon black and polymer binder) and are considered one-dimensional
 (1D) in terms of ion transport which determines battery capacity. Standard electrodes
 fundamentally can only be optimized for either power or energy density (not both at the
 same time), and thus cannot meet DOE’s long-term goals [1]. To mitigate these issues,
 researchers have investigated the performance of different structured 3D electrodes
 through modeling and experiments over the last two decades [2–6]. However, finding
 efficient manufacturing processes to create these structures at scale has remained a
 challenge. Our 3D electrodes made with Acoustophoretic AM can increase both power
 and energy density simultaneously in a battery because ion transport (through 3D
 electrode architecture) is fundamentally faster and operates in three-dimensions leading
 to concurrent high-energy and high-power performance. Our new process is independent
 of material chemistry (ie, adaptable to multiple battery material systems) and can pattern
 battery particles on a time scale of seconds, enabling a radical change to existing
 manufacturing. We anticipate that our ≥ 15% improvement can be realized across
 multiple battery material systems, including new Lithium-ion chemistries in development
 in industry and other DOE programs.

3. Results and Discussion

 This project was divided into two budget periods and started during the global
 pandemic in 2020. This significantly hindered our ability to conduct in-person
 experimental research which was the focus of the project. Due to unforeseen
 experimental delays, the project team pivoted to analysis and modeling work in Tasks 1
 and 2 and developed a series of process models for acoustic focusing that resulted in
 three publications [7–9]. We formed collaborations with the Argonne National
 Laboratory (ANL) and received electrochemical testing guidance from Dr. Daniel
 Abraham. We also received experimental support from ANL’s Materials Engineering
 Research Facility (MERF) who provided the project team with LiNi0.6Mn0.2Co0.2O2

(NMC-622) powder when other vendors reduced or halted supplying lithium nickel
manganese cobalt oxides to academic institutions post-pandemic. The ANL MERF
material allowed us to test the impact of size-specific NMC-622 on our acoustic process.
The project team completed the experimental milestones for Tasks 3 and 4. As a result of
pandemic impacts and delays with acquiring NMC-622, the project was not about to fully
complete two milestones. Milestone 5.2.1 is 90% as of March 31, 2025, but all other Task
5 milestones are complete. UW made two line patterned electrode full cells (2032 coin

the baseline cell at 1C (milestone metric: > 10%) and demonstrate a 51% and 115%

on our acoustic manufacturing process has been conducted, but the milestone is

3.1. Task 1.0: 3D Battery Electrode and Materials Specifications

Task 1.1: Material Selection (Milestone 1.1.1)
 This subtask laid the foundation for the project and concentrated on collecting the
necessary active material, conductive additives, solvent, and electrolyte needed for the
entire project. Acoustic focusing is primarily driven by particle size and the density and
compressibility of the fluid and particles. To ensure that selected cathode active materials
would facilitate acoustic focusing, materials were screened for a particle d50 > 12 μm, a
density > 2 g/cm3, and a compressibility on the order of 10-11 - 10-12 Pa-1. We collected
information from 26 material vendors and evaluated material properties to ensure the
compatibility of cathode active materials with our new acoustic process. Since our project
focused on patterning the cathode electrodes, the following Li-ion cathode materials (and
derivative compositions) were evaluated: lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC),
lithium nickel cobalt aluminum oxide (NCA), lithium cobalt oxide (LCO), lithium iron
phosphate (LFP), and lithium titanate (LTO). Our goal was to use conventional Li-ion
slurries, thus N-Methyl-2-Pyrrolidone (NMP) and Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF), and
carbon black were selected for the slurry solvent, binder, and conductive additive
respectively. After reviewing vendor provided data, we determined that LCO and NMC
(all compositions) were the most suitable for acoustic processing; In consultation with
our program officers, we decided to proceed with NMC for the project due to its lower
cobalt content and relevance for current electric vehicles.
 To safely develop a new process for acoustic processing of battery electrodes, we also
chose test materials (silica and alumina) that mimicked the acoustic focusing behavior of
our planned Task 3 NMC slurry formulations. The rationale for choosing these test
materials as a preliminary working slurry was to ensure we could conduct safe
experiments in lab before moving the to the additional safety and hazardous material
handling requirements of NMC-based electrode slurries. For optimal acoustic focusing,
NMC slurries must exhibit be shear thinning behavior, have particle volume fractions
between 15 – 25%, and have particles with close to a spherical morphology. As a result,
test materials with a particle density of 4.5 - 4.8 g/cm3, a particle compressibility of 7.5 -
 5

 8.2 x 10-12 Pa-1, a fluid density around 1.03 g/cm3, and a fluid compressibility around 5.2
 x 10-10 Pa-1 were investigated in Task 2.

cathode electrodes. To define target geometries for acoustic processing, the
electrochemical performance of electrodes in literature with different thicknesses, pore
widths, and electrode widths (features noted in Figure 4) were extracted and compared.

3.2. Task 2.0: Acoustophoretic Process Specifications, Material Compatibility and
 Validation
 This task defined the acoustic system hardware and process parameters to achieve
control of particle packing and alignment and assembly of battery materials.
Specifications will be given on nozzle dimensions, ink compositions, and suitable
combinations of acoustic field strength, flow rate and pressures for acoustic printing.

Task 2.1: Process and Material Requirements (Milestone 2.1.1)
 As noted in Task 1.0, we selected a test material system to mimic the properties of
battery slurries for safe acoustic process development. When developing a new
manufacturing process, it is important to build an initial system around safe materials
before proceeding to more detailed implementation with actual battery materials which
require additional safety and handling considerations due to the hazards of NMP solvents.
This can sometimes constrain exploratory experiments which are critical for
understanding a new process space. For our test material system, alumina particles were
selected and suspended in a blend of epoxy, fumed silica, and acetone, to mimic the
rheological properties of battery materials. The test material rheology was validated to be
within the range of common battery slurries (between 1 – 200 Pa-s at shear rates between
0.1 – 100 1/s). Acoustic processing experiments were conducted, and we were able to
focus multiple lines of particles in viscous media (see Figure 5a). The degree of focusing
was found to decrease linearly with increasing flow rates (Figure 5b). This is likely due to
the fact that the particles were subjected to an acoustic field for a shorter period of time at
higher flow rates. Next, the range of particle loadings which could be focused was
investigated. Particle loadings from 10 weight percent (wt %) to 50 wt % were tested, and
focusing was visible across that full process range. We note that feature definition did
decrease with increased loading. This is because when more particles were present, a
larger number of unfocused particles were also present in the space between the focused
line features. The operational window of printing was identified for the nozzle and
printing hardware by varying flow rates, raster speeds, and nozzle height. The operational
window for printing was combined with the focusing operational window and
acoustically focused lines of alumina particles in epoxy were printed onto glass substrates
(Figure 5c). Line structures are visibly apparent in the printed features indicating success
for the initial phase of development with test materials.

Task 3.2: Acoustophoretic Processing with Li+ Materials (Milestone 3.2.1)
 We successfully developed acoustic processing with NMC-622 inks/slurries in
print nozzles and were able to achieve continuous line prints with the electrode inks
developed in Task 1.2. These prints exhibited 1.4% breaks in electrode material
continuity over a 25 mm by 50 mm sample area as shown in Figure 2. Ensuring minimal
breaks in material continuity is critical for ensuring good electrochemical performance
during battery cycling. Figure 11b sho s UW’s line break quantification of the sample
shown in Figure 11a. Microscope images were manually analyzed using ImageJ, which is
an open-source image processing program. Green lines indicate where lines are
continuous, while red lines indicate breaks in material continuity. Line breaks are defined
as those > 0.5 mm in length, or 1% of the sample length. The low contrast of these
samples and stitching artifacts (0.5 – 0.6 mm in width) limit the distinction of material
continuity breaks below this threshold. Based on this quantification, this sample has 1.4%
breaks in material continuity.

Task 4a.1: Modeling of Acoustic Chamber (M4a.1.1)
 To develop a model for an acoustic chamber-based process, analytical
descriptions for the pressure fields within focusing chambers were developed for three
different experimental configurations. The acoustic energy fields corresponding to these
pressure fields were also calculated. The acoustic energy fields were used to predict
where particles will be focused and the patterns which would result from filling different
volume fractions of particles into the chamber (see Figure 12). These patterns were then
compared to the feature targets from previous work in Task 1.0 to determine what
focusing frequency range and particle loading range would yield the necessary target
features for battery performance. Designs for chambers which could be used to generate
the desired pressure fields were compared and an initial design selected for use in the
next subtask. The model developed herein (Figure 12) and results of this analysis work
were published in a journal article by the project team [7].

4. Key Achievements

 To our knowledge, this project is the first successful demonstration of large-area,
 multi-line material patterning with acoustic focusing in high viscosity slurries, opening
 the door for new energy and other functional material applications [9]. The biggest
 advantage of this process is no specific material chemistry is required for successful
 patterning – our process is driven by particle size and density. In addition, we can pattern
 3D battery structures over cm-scale areas on the timescale of seconds or minutes,
 depending on the target structure and properties of the material system used for
 acoustophoretic patterning. This overcomes barriers with other electric-field assisted
 technologies and laser patterning, which have limitations on material chemistries and the
 range of electrode geometries that can be fabricated rapidly over large areas.

5. Lessons Learned

 We believe there are two key lessons learned during this project that can influence
 future research directions. The first lesson learned is selection of test or proxy materials
 for process development is integral to any new manufacturing process and can allow a
 researcher to rapidly iterate through multiple experiments before more hazardous
 materials are involved, which requires an additional layer of safety and precaution during
 development and testing. While the project team made a good effort to select alumina and
 silica proxy test materials, we found that some of the differences in particle distribution
 and rheology did not lend to a quick one-to-one swamp with battery slurries later. Thus,
 we recommend using proxy or test materials for an initial, safe process validation, and
 once that is complete, moving more rapidly to optimizing processes with the final battery
 materials where possible. Alternatively, pursuing test materials in parallel with the target
 functional battery materials can also lead to the development of robust process models.

 The second lesson learned is that acoustic models and simulations are critical for
 informing experiments and can help guide results at small scales to larger scales more
 quickly. Originally, this project did not plan to conduct extensive modeling and
 simulation research. However, the COVID-19 pandemic and our inability to work in lab
 at full capacity for almost a year gave us the opportunity to develop new models and
 simulate acoustic process conditions that could be explored experimentally in lab. This
 led to three publications over the course of the project [7–9], and has given the project
 team a strong modeling base and expertise in acoustic focusing that allows us to explore
 new material systems beyond batteries and their propensity to pattern without the need to
 conduct experiments. This is an important lesson in the importance of using modeling in
 conjunction with experiments to more efficiently and effectively develop a new
 manufacturing process.

6. Conclusions

 3D battery cathode electrodes with line features on the scale of tens to hundreds
 of microns can substantially improve the electrochemical performance of Li-ion batteries.
 We were able to show two acoustically patterned battery full cells that demonstrated a

 26

 16% and 20% improvement in mAh/g performance over a baseline cell at 1C discharge
 rate and demonstrate a 51% and 115% improvement in mAh/g performance over a
 baseline cell at a 2C discharge rate, as measured by electrochemical cycling tests (rate
 capability). These results illustrate that we can make better, high-performance batteries
 with our acoustophoretic additive manufacturing process, successfully taking the
 technology to a TRL of 3. We also developed a series of models for in-nozzle and
 chamber particle interactions during acoustophoretic patterning and in a high-viscosity
 material system (~10 – 100 Pa-s). We have also validated the ability of our models to
 predict line pattern formation and grid-pattern formation behavior with experiments,
 verifying we can pattern high viscosity, shear thinning materials – an achievement that
 has not been clearly shown in publicly available literature prior to this project. Acoustic
 frequency, particle radius, acoustic pressure, and particle loading affect patterning time
 scales and final line feature sizes for 3D battery electrodes but can be easily modified and
 adjusted to create new 3D structures once a new manufacturing platform is in place. This
 reduces the need for a battery manufacturer to continuously invest in new equipment for a
 new 3D electrode structure. As future commercialization pathways are considered, our
 results illustrate the need to further invest in a new platform for our acoustic processing
 technology to ensure the coin cell scale results from this project can translate to larger
 area batteries relevant for electric vehicles, grid storage, and portable devices in the
 future.

7. Future Work and Recommendations

 At project conclusion, the project team is still pursuing additional research and
 development (R&D) of this technology and is currently in the middle of a separate
 cooperative agreement award to begin scaling up the technology and further vet its
 reliability over larger areas. Due to the lower technology readiness level (TRL) of this
 project (TRL 1-3), the technology is not yet ready to be tied to a specific battery product
 or commercial technology. However, the project team is dedicated to supporting US
 manufacturing and commercialization. The project team has generated a patent
 application as noted in Section 8. For further commercialization advancement, UW and
 UCSB are likely to license the technology to others since licensing is the primary means
 by which technology commercialization can occur at universities unless a start-up
 company is formed. As with any new battery technology, moving from a small scale
 (TRL 1) to larger demonstration (TRL 4-5) requires additional monetary investments and
 partnerships.

 In Subtask 6.2, the project team conducted a preliminary scale-up assessment of
 acoustic printing at small slot-dire scales. Based on initial simulations and analysis we
 believe this technology shows promise for replacing a slot-die coater on a product line if
 line-patterned electrodes prove to be beneficial at scale. For further R&D development
 work, we recommend that new project partners be brought on to evaluate the supply
 chain required for the longer-term materials and equipment needed to manufacture the 3D
 batteries shown in this project at scales relevant for electric vehicles, grid storage, and
 portable electronics. The market for battery adoption is complex and requires the
 engagement of commercialization partners that range from material suppliers to OEMs
 27

 that cover the entire battery supply chain. This is important since our new acoustic
 processing may involve both the development of new equipment and material systems.
