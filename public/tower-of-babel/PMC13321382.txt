Humidity management modulates aroma deterioration in postharvest strawberry through volatile remodeling and membrane lipid metabolism
Authors: Jiali Wang, Jing Huang, Zhenbiao Li, Yanpei Chen, Qingqing Wang, Lin Zhu, Yuhong Wang, Hui Xu, Zisheng Luo, Yanqun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Strawberry aroma is a key determinant of fruit quality and consumer acceptance, but postharvest water loss during storage and distribution can disrupt aroma quality. In this study, strawberry fruit was stored at high relative humidity (RH), 90 % RH, or low relative humidity, 45 % RH, for 8 d to investigate how humidity management regulates volatile organic compounds (VOCs) profiles and membrane lipid metabolism. Compared with 90 % RH storage, 45 % RH storage promoted water loss and increased the total abundance of detected VOCs by approximately 17 %. However, this increase was accompanied by a marked imbalance in VOCs composition rather than improved aroma quality. Green aldehydes and terpenes, which are associated with fresh and floral aroma notes, declined under low humidity, whereas lipid-derived volatiles and furanones accumulated, indicating a shift in volatile metabolism during water-loss stress. Low humidity also increased malondialdehyde (MDA) content by 43 %, while phospholipase D (PLD) abundance and phosphatidic acid (PA) content decreased, suggesting enhanced membrane oxidative damage and attenuated PLD associated lipid signaling. Transcriptome analysis further indicated a stronger response of LOX related pathways than PLD related pathways under low humidity storage. Correlation analysis showed close associations between ester and aldehyde volatiles and membrane lipid metabolic pathways. These results suggest that high humidity storage helps maintain strawberry aroma quality by reducing water loss induced membrane lipid oxidation and volatile profile imbalance. This study provides mechanistic insight into humidity management as a strategy for preserving postharvest strawberry aroma.

Introduction

Globally, postharvest losses account for 25-50 % of the total weight loss in fresh fruit and vegetables (Bancal and Ray, 2022), causing approximately 1.3 billion tons of food waste annually, which is nearly one-third of the global production. As living tissues detached from plants, harvested fruits continue to lose water through transpiration and respiration (Ben-Yehoshua and Rodov, 2002; Wills and Golding, 2016). This water loss is a primary factor accelerating postharvest deterioration, including surface shriveling, texture softening, flavor loss, and decay, thereby reducing marketability and consumer acceptance. Nearly half of total postharvest losses can be attributed to water loss during postharvest handling and storage (Xue et al., 2021). Therefore, effective regulation of water loss is critical for maintaining postharvest quality, extending shelf life, and reducing food waste in fresh produce systems.

Strawberry is valued for its unique flavor and nutritional quality, which is a key determinant of consumer acceptance and market value (Fan et al., 2021). Strawberry aroma is primarily determined by a complex mixture of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Recent studies have shown that VOC-based profiling and odor activity value (OAV) analysis are effective for characterizing strawberry flavor differences among cultivars and fruit types (Xu et al., 2024). The composition and abundance of these VOCs are highly dynamic and sensitive to both pre- and postharvest factors, including genotype, maturity stage, and storage conditions (Xu et al., 2025a). To date, hundreds of volatile chemicals have been identified in fresh strawberries. Esters account for 25-90 % of all volatile substances in strawberries and are the main source of fruity and floral aromas (Yan et al., 2018). Aldehydes mostly occur in the early stage of maturation, contributing to the green aroma (Zhang et al., 2025). Despite their concentrations being far lower than those of esters, furanones, sulfur compounds, and terpenoids can disproportionately shape the overall aroma of strawberries owing to their low odor thresholds (Ulrich et al., 2007).

Based on the recognized role of VOCs in determining sensory quality, extensive studies have demonstrated that postharvest environmental factors can markedly reshape the composition and abundance of aroma-related volatiles in fruit. Postharvest treatments such as hormonal regulation (Makkumrai et al., 2014), temperature management (Obenland et al., 2013), and controlled atmosphere storage (Brian et al., 2025) have been shown to selectively modulate specific classes of VOCs, including esters, aldehydes, terpenes, and alcohols, thereby altering aroma profiles across diverse fruit species. More recently, humidity has emerged as an additional regulator of postharvest aroma evolution, with evidence suggesting that volatile composition (Zuo et al., 2025), particularly terpene accumulation, is highly sensitive to moisture-related conditions during storage. In Torreya grandis fruit, terpenes in the VOCs were particularly sensitive to humidity conditions. D-Limonene accounted for 63.0-90.8 % of total terpenes and increased under high-humidity storage (Hu et al., 2022). Temperature indirectly induces water loss, thereby altering alcohol dehydrogenase activity and terpene volatile contents in grape (Cirilli et al., 2012). In addition, airflow-induced water loss reshapes the aroma composition of white tea, improving its aroma quality (Feng et al., 2025). However, systematic research on whether and how postharvest humidity-driven water loss stress affects aroma composition remains relatively limited.

Water loss stress resulting from humidity imbalance during transportation and storage is often accompanied by oxidative stress, characterized by elevated reactive oxygen species and membrane lipid peroxidation (Meitha et al., 2020). Membrane-associated phospholipases are commonly activated under stress conditions, promoting phospholipid degradation (Ali et al., 2022). This process generates metabolites such as phosphatidic acid (PA) and lysophospholipids (LP), which are widely regarded as important indicators of compromised membrane integrity (Kong et al., 2017). Among them, phospholipase D (PLD)-mediated phospholipid hydrolysis plays a key role in membrane lipid turnover and signaling regulation (Zhao, 2015). Furthermore, membrane phospholipid degradation mediated by PLD leads to the release of polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid fatty acids (Wang et al., 2000). These fatty acids serve as primary oxidative substrates for lipoxygenase (LOX). Subsequently, LOX-catalyzed lipid peroxidation contributes to reduction in membrane lipid content and further impairing membrane integrity(Huang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2020). Therefore, membrane lipid degradation is generally attributed to the coordinated actions of membrane lipid-metabolizing enzymes, including PLD, lipases, and LOX (Rui et al., 2010). Accordingly, reducing water loss has been shown to delay membrane degradation by restraining lipid oxidation-related enzyme activities. Notably, lipid oxidation products generated during membrane lipid metabolism are closely associated with the formation of aroma-related volatiles (Tang et al., 2024). This suggests that compromised membrane integrity may serve as a critical interface linking water loss-induced oxidative stress to changes in volatile composition. However, how water loss-driven impairment of membrane lipid integrity and membrane lipid metabolism shape VOCs in strawberry remains largely unexplored.

Using strawberry as a model, we regulated water loss by adjusting storage relative humidity, measured VOCs and membrane integrity markers, and probed the causal links between membrane lipid metabolism and volatile profiles. The results show that relative humidity storage reshapes VOCs by changing fruit water status and membrane lipid condition, thereby driving the trajectories of aldehydes and esters. These findings deepen our understanding of the key regulatory nodes and transformation pathways underlying water loss induced postharvest aroma reprogramming.

Materials and methods

Fruit material and treatment

Strawberry fruit (Fragaria × ananassa Duch. ‘Benihoppe’) were harvested in March from Yushi Strawberry Farm, a commercial soil-cultivated greenhouse in Hangzhou, China, and transported to the laboratory within 2 h at approximately 20 °C. Fruit at the breaker ripening stage, with an average weight of approximately 25 g, were selected based on uniform appearance and size, and absence of visible defects or disease. To establish two contrasting postharvest water-loss conditions, strawberry fruit were stored under high relative humidity (HRH, 90 ± 2 % RH) or low relative humidity (LRH, 45 ± 2 % RH) in an artificial climate chamber (Safe PRX 450B, Ningbo, China) at 25 °C in darkness. The HRH level was selected because 90–95 % RH is recommended for strawberry storage and transport, whereas the LRH level was used to represent a practically relevant low-humidity exposure that may occur before effective cooling or under insufficient humidity control during postharvest handling (Ktenioudaki et al., 2019). Sampling was conducted at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 d of storage. At each time point, three independent replicate boxes were prepared, each containing 15 randomly selected fruit. After fresh measurements, fruit tissues were sliced, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Determination of weight loss and fruit quality traits

Fruit weight loss was determined by recording the initial weight of each fruit (W0) at the beginning of storage and the corresponding weight (Wt) at each subsequent sampling time. Fifteen fruit from each treatment group were individually weighed using an electronic balance with 0.01 g precision. Weight loss (%) was calculated using the following formula (1):(1)Weightloss(%)=[(W0−Wt)/W0]×100

The color of strawberry flesh was measured at each sampling point using a colorimeter (KONICA MINOLTA CR-400, Japan). At the three equatorial scanning sections, the CIELAB parameters were recorded: L∗ (luminance), a∗ (red-green axis), and b∗ (yellow-blue axis). The instrument was standardized with the manufacturer's white calibration plate before each use.

Fruit firmness was evaluated using a texture analyzer (TA-XT2i, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK). For each strawberry, hardness was measured at four equidistant points around the central region using a penetration probe. The probe advanced at a constant speed of 5 mm s−1 to a depth of 10 mm. The maximum force recorded during penetration was defined as the fruit firmness.

Soluble solid content (SSC) and titratable acidity (TA) were determined using a PAL-BX|ACID F5 (ATAGO, Japan) following the manufacturer's instructions and expressed as percentages (%). Briefly, approximately 10 g of strawberry fruit were homogenized, and the pulp was filtered through two layers of gauze to obtain clear juice. An aliquot of about 0.3 mL of juice was placed on the prism for SSC measurement. For TA determination, a separate 1 g juice was diluted with 49 g of distilled water according to the manufacturer's instructions, and 0.3 mL of the diluted juice was used for measurement.

Determination of malondialdehyde (MDA)

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using a plant MDA assay kit (A003-3-1, microplate method; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Briefly, strawberry tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized with 0.9 mL extraction solution on ice and centrifuged at 3500-4000 rpm for 10 min. For the assay, 0.05 mL of absolute ethanol, standard solution (10 nmol mL−1), or sample supernatant was added to the blank, standard, or sample tube, respectively. Then, 1 mL of freshly prepared working solution (reagent 1: reagent 2: reagent 3 = 0.1:3:1) was added to each tube. After vortexing, the mixtures were heated at > 95 °C for 20 min and cooled under running water. The empty plate was read first for correction, and 0.250 mL of each reaction mixture was then transferred to a 96-well plate. Absorbance was measured at 530 nm. MDA content was calculated using the following formula (2):(2)MDAcontent(μmolkg−1)=(Asample−AblankAstandard−Ablank)×Cstandard×VextractWwhere A_sample, A_blank, and A_standard are the absorbance values of the sample, blank, and standard, respectively; C_standard is 10 nmol mL−1; V_extract is the extraction volume (mL); and W is the sample weight (g).

Assays of phospholipase D (PLD) and phosphatidic acid (PA)

Phospholipase D (PLD) content was determined using a plant PLD ELISA kit (Cat. No. RX2D916316; Ruixin Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Briefly, strawberry tissue (0.1 g) was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized with 0.9 mL PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The homogenate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm at 4 °C for 30 min, and the supernatant was used for analysis. Standard solution, sample diluent, or sample supernatant was added to the standard, zero, or sample wells at 0.05 mL per well, respectively. HRP-labeled antibody (0.1 mL) was then added to each well except the blank well. After incubation at 37 °C for 60 min, the plate was washed five times. Substrate solution (0.1 mL) was added and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min, followed by 0.05 mL stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Spark® multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). PLD content was calculated from the standard curve and converted according to the extraction volume and sample weight, and expressed as μg kg−1.

Phosphatidic acid (PA) content was determined using a PA ELISA kit (Cat. No. HB018-SH; Hengyuan Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Briefly, strawberry tissue (0.1 g) was extracted with 0.9 mL butanol:methanol (5:25:70, v/v/v), and the extract was used for analysis. For the assay, standard solution was added to the standard wells, while 0.04 mL sample diluent and 0.01 mL sample extract were added to the sample wells. Enzyme reagent (0.05 mL) was then added to each well except the blank well. After incubation at 37 °C for 60 min, the plate was washed five times. Chromogenic solutions A and B were added at 0.05 mL each and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min. Stop solution (50 μL) was then added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm within 15 min. PA content was calculated from the standard curve and converted according to the dilution factor, extraction volume, and sample weight, and expressed as μmol kg−1.

Measurements of volatile organic compounds (VOCs)

Approximately 2 g of strawberry fruit tissue was homogenized with saturated NaCl solution (1:1, w/v), and 3 mL of the homogenate was transferred to a 20 mL headspace vial. 2,6-Dimethyl-2-heptanol was used as the internal standard, and 1 μL of the internal standard solution (300 ppm) was added to each vial. Volatiles were analyzed by HS-SPME-GC-MS using an Agilent GC-MS system coupled with a Gerstel MPS autosampler. Samples were equilibrated at 37 °C for 60 min and extracted at 60 °C for 45 min using a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS StableFlex SPME fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). The fiber was desorbed at 250 °C for 5 min in splitless mode. Separation was performed on an HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm), with helium as the carrier gas at 1.0 mL min−1. The oven temperature was held at 40 °C for 5 min, increased to 280 °C at 6 °C min−1, and held for 5 min. MS was operated in EI mode at 70 eV with a scan range of m/z 30–350. The quadrupole, ion source, and transfer line temperatures were 150 °C, 230 °C, and 280 °C, respectively. Volatiles were identified using the NIST20 library and Kovats retention indices. Semi-quantitative results were expressed as relative peak areas normalized to the internal standard. Odor activity value (OAV) evaluation

OAVs were calculated based on VOCs concentrations determined by GC-MS and odor thresholds reported in the literature. The detected aroma-active compounds were assigned to eight aroma categories according to previously reported strawberry sensory descriptors and the odor characteristics of individual VOCs (Leffingwell & Associates, 2026; Oliver et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2022). For compounds with reported threshold ranges, the geometric mean of the lower and upper limits was used as the representative threshold (Table S2). Compounds lacking reliable odor threshold information were excluded from the OAV-based aroma scoring. Compounds below the detection limit were assigned a value of zero in this analysis. For each biological replicate, the aroma score of each category was calculated as the sum of log-transformed OAVs of compounds assigned to the same category (3):(3)Categoryscore=∑log10(OAVi+1)

The category scores were used for statistical analysis. For graphical visualization, the mean category scores were further globally normalized to a 0-10 scale across all storage times, humidity treatments, and aroma categories. The normalized scores were used only for radar plot visualization.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, #RC401-01). RNA purity and concentration were measured with a Nanodrop(NanoQuant Plate, Tecan, USA), and RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme, #R333-01). qRT-PCR was conducted using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q712-02) on a QuantStudio® 3 system. UBQ11 was used as the reference gene, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences were listed in Table S1 (Merlaen et al., 2018).

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from strawberry fruit samples collected on 0 d, 2 d, and 6 d under different humidity conditions using the FastPure® Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Cat. No. RC401-01), following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and concentration were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Only samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.9 and 2.1 and clear 28S and 18S rRNA bands were used for subsequent library preparation. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. E7530) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) to generate 150 base pair (bp) paired-end reads. Three biological replicates were included for each time point. Raw reads were processed using fastp (version 0.20.1) to remove adapter sequences and low quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the Fragaria × ananassa reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.0.5). Transcript abundance was quantified with featureCounts (version 2.0.0), and gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (version 1.20.0), with the screening criteria set at |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value ≤0.05.

Correlation analysis

The relationships among VOCs, strawberry quality parameters and AQP genes expression were established by the Pearson correlation analysis. The statistical significance of the positive and negative correlations was considered at p < 0.05.

Mantel association analysis between gene expression and VOCs

To assess the correspondence between gene expression and VOCs profiles, gene-expression and metabolite-abundance matrices were constructed using the same set of samples (rows, variables; columns, samples). For each data block, Euclidean distance matrices were calculated and two-sided permutation Mantel tests were applied to all gene-metabolite combinations to obtain Mantel correlation coefficients (r) and P values. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), with significance set at FDR <0.05; only significant pairs are reported in the main text. Significant associations were visualized as networks, in which edge width represented |r| and edge colour indicated P-value classes (P < 0.001, 0.001 ≤ P < 0.01, 0.01 ≤ P < 0.05, P ≥ 0.05). A Spearman correlation heatmap of metabolites was included as a global structural reference. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.0) using the packages linkET, ggplot2, dplyr, and ggforce; parameter settings and code are archived to ensure reproducibility.

Data analysis

Each sampling point consisted of three biological replicates, with 15 fruit per replicate. All experiments were arranged in a completely randomized design. Statistical analyses were performed using Origin 2025 (OriginLab, USA) and R (v4.3.0). Differences between the two groups were evaluated using independent sample t-tests. Significance levels were indicated as p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), and p < 0.001 (∗∗∗). Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) based on three independent replicates.

Effects of relative humidity on quality index of postharvest strawberry

Strawberries exhibited significant water loss when stored at a low relative humidity (LRH, 45 ± 2 % RH). Compared with storage under high relative humidity (HRH, 90 ± 2 % RH), the weight decreased significantly and continuously (8 d: 30 % vs 9 %) (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this, firmness declined rapidly, with a 55.8 % loss by 2 d and significantly lower values thereafter (Fig. 1B), reflecting water loss-induced softening. Water loss also hastened early color development: peel lightness (L∗) decreased over time in both groups but remained lower at LRH (Fig. 1C). Chromatic parameters changed in opposite directions a∗ (redness) increased while b∗ (yellowness) decreased. Under LRH conditions, a∗ increased more rapidly during 0-4 d, while b∗ showed a steeper drop during 4-8 d (Fig. 1D and E). The flavour related SSC/TA ratio dropped initially and fluctuated thereafter, with the largest between-treatment difference at 6 d, when values were lower at water loss (Fig. 1F). Relative humidity-driven changes in volatile organic compound (VOCs) profiles of strawberry

A two-dimensional PCA showed that VOCs profiles changed during storage. Fresh samples were separated from stored samples mainly along PC1, which explained 66.1 % of the total variation. LRH and HRH samples also showed differences at several storage stages, suggesting that both storage time and humidity affected strawberry aroma profiles (Fig. 2A). Stacked component analysis showed that total VOCs increased during storage, with levels under LRH being about 16.8 % higher at 6-8 d (Fig. 2B). Aldehydes accounted for up to 80.8 % at early time points but declined to 3.1–4.0 % by 8 d, whereas esters increased progressively to 53.0 –63.7 %. Meanwhile, HRH fruit exhibited a higher late-stage contribution of terpenes (14.1 %). Individual volatile classes were further subjected to semi-quantitative analysis. Esters were generally higher under LRH than under HRH during storage, with a rapid increase from 2 to 6 d and high levels maintained on 8 d (Fig. 2C). Aldehydes declined sharply after 2 d of storage, with a faster decrease under LRH (Fig. 2D). Terpene levels were higher under LRH than under HRH from 2 to 6 d of storage. However, by 8 d, HRH showed a marked increase (Fig. 2E). Furans and acids exhibited a pronounced increase under LRH (Fig. 2F and G). Ketones remained at low levels, peaked at 4 d (0.02 μg kg−1), and then declined from 4 to 8 d, but remained higher under LRH than under HRH (Fig. 2H). Overall, water loss accelerated ester buildup and aldehyde depletion, while HRH maintained terpenes and slowed late-stage aldehyde loss. Odor activity values (OAVs)-based aroma attribute profiling under different storage humidity conditions

Overall, LRH showed higher OAV-derived aroma scores than HRH, but this enhancement was accompanied by an increase in deterioration-related odors. Effects of relative humidity on MDA, PLD and PA levels

To evaluate how water loss affected membrane integrity, we first examined FaAQP expression, given its role in regulating transmembrane water transport. Compared with HRH storage, the transcript abundance of FaAQP was significantly reduced under low-humidity conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among them, FaPIP1;2 and FaPIP2;2 exhibited a more pronounced overall decline, whereas FaPIP1;1 showed a more distinct responsiveness to humidity differences. These results indicated that sustained water loss under LRH was accompanied by altered membrane water transport capacity, which may have contributed to compromised membrane integrity. Under LRH conditions, MDA increased progressively and remained significantly higher than under HRH throughout storage. Its content reached a peak at the late stage of storage (approximately 120-140 μmol kg−1) (Fig. 4A), indicating aggravated membrane lipid peroxidation. During 2-8 d of storage, both PLD and PA levels in strawberry fruit remained above those at 0 d. For PLD, under continuous water loss at LRH, its level was significantly lower than that under HRH at 2 d, transiently higher at 4 d, and then lower again at 6-8 d. Overall, its average accumulation level under LRH was 13.0 % lower than that under HRH (Fig. 4B). PA showed a pattern broadly similar to that of PLD. By 8 d, PA content increased by about 22.9 % relative to 0 d under HRH, compared with an increase of about 16.4 % under LRH. Effects of relative humidity on membrane lipid metabolic reprogramming and the LOX pathway

Samples from 2 d and 6 d were selected to represent the early water-loss response and the later stage of VOCs and aroma divergence. We examined the expression of key genes involved in membrane lipid metabolism. The heatmap showed distinct temporal and pathway specific expression patterns under the two humidity conditions (Fig. 5A). Among them, the PLDδ (FxaYL_131g0779710 and FxaYL_121g0717770) are associated with water loss response and oxidative stress, were upregulated under HRH on 6d. The PLDα1 (FxaYL_641g0162410) related to phospholipid hydrolysis and water-status regulation, showed a similar late stage HRH response. In contrast, the PLDζ1 (FxaYL_621g0086860) was upregulated under HRH on 2 d but showed an LRH responsive pattern on 6 d. This gene is associated with membrane lipid remodeling. Together, these results indicate that PLD genes showed stage and humidity dependent expression patterns. In contrast to the above-mentioned suppression of transcriptional expression, LRH-induced water loss exerted an overall stronger induction on the LOX downstream pathway (Fig. 5B). By 6 d, the increased expression of β-oxidation related genes and LACS under LRH suggested enhanced formation of acyl-CoA intermediates. Meanwhile, AAT expression was also elevated. Correlation analysis among strawberry quality, membrane-related indicators, and aroma attributes

Pearson correlation analysis showed that aroma attributes exhibited two opposite correlation patterns (Fig. 6). Aldehydes and alcohols were positively correlated with hardness and aquaporin expression, but negatively correlated with weight loss, MDA, PA, and Esters. In contrast, esters, furans, terpenes, acids, and ketones were positively correlated with weight loss, MDA, and PA, but negatively correlated with hardness and aquaporin expression. Discussion

Humidity management is closely associated with aroma imbalance in strawberry fruit

Relative humidity is considered an important environmental factor influencing the evaporation rate of fruits and vegetables during postharvest storage (Zuo et al., 2025). High humidity storage (e.g., 85-95 % RH) plays a positive role in suppressing the evaporation of water from fruit and vegetable to the surrounding environment (Mukama et al., 2018). LRH accelerates the transpiration and water loss of fruit, causing water loss from the epidermis and stomata, which leads to a direct decrease in body weight (Xanthopoulos et al., 2017). Under these conditions, Water loss disrupts cellular water potential gradients, reduces turgor pressure, and promotes tissue softening (Wada et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown that postharvest water loss in strawberry is strongly governed by storage RH. Ktenioudaki et al. found that, after 7 d of storage, strawberry weight loss was limited to approximately 5.7-7.2 % at 90 % RH but increased to 15.5-19 % at 40 % RH (Ktenioudaki et al., 2019). Importantly, this weight loss is not only a physical reduction in mass, but also a direct driver of quality deterioration. For strawberry, 2.5-3 % weight loss may already reach an unacceptable threshold, while earlier studies proposed that around 6 % could be the upper limit for loss of marketability (Kessler et al., 2023). Our study demonstrated consistent results, with water loss occurring at LRH and causing a sustained, significant loss of strawberry weight and increased softening (Fig. 1A and B), accelerating ripening and premature decay. Water loss also alters transpiration and respiration rates, thereby affecting the sugar/acid pool and pigment stability. This was accompanied by a decrease in SSC/TA (Fig. 1F), and accelerated maturation and senescence with concomitant color deterioration (Fig. 1C–E). Although commercial strawberry storage is generally recommended at low temperature, the 25 °C condition used here served as an ambient postharvest model to capture accelerated RH-driven water loss and aroma remodeling. Under cold-chain storage, these responses may occur more slowly but remain relevant to humidity management during postharvest handling.

Postharvest water loss is usually accompanied by changes in VOCs and release intensity. Several studies have shown that VOCs in berries and berry-like fruits are highly sensitive to storage conditions (Zhao et al., 2024). Postharvest partial water loss of blueberries not only markedly increases aroma intensity but also regulated aroma biosynthetic pathways (Wang et al., 2023). A transient increase in VOCs has been observed in fresh-cut strawberries due to changes in moisture content (Baldwin et al., 2023). In our study, a similar trend was observed, particularly after 4 d of water loss, with the total amount of VOCs and each component increasing to varying degrees (Fig. 2B). This indicates that LRH storage not only leads to a decline in appearance quality, but also significantly alters the volatile metabolic characteristics of the fruit.

However, different VOCs classes responded differently to water loss. This class specific response is important because alcohols, esters, aldehydes, and other VOC groups have been reported to contribute to aroma discrimination among strawberry varieties (Xu et al., 2025b). Aldehydes are generally associated with green and fresh aroma notes and usually decrease gradually with fruit ripening (Li et al., 2021). In this study, LRH accelerated the decline in aldehydes (Fig. 2D). This suggests that LRH accelerates the shift toward ripening and senescence, leading to the loss of green notes. Consistently, LRH enhanced ripening related fruity and caramel notes, reduced fresh green and floral notes, and promoted deterioration related fatty and sour-like attributes (Fig. 3). This suggests that water loss may shift the aroma profile from a fresh and clean character toward an overripe and deteriorated one. The fatty attribute may reflect lipid oxidation or lipid derived odors, which may enhance aroma fullness at moderate levels but produce stale or less-fresh notes when excessive (Shahidi and Oh, 2020; Zhao et al., 2024). The sour-like attribute mainly represents rancid, fermented, or overripe odors rather than sour taste. Their enhancement under LRH suggests that water loss may promote aroma deterioration by intensifying membrane lipid oxidation and lipid-derived volatile formation. In contrast, HRH better preserved green and floral attributes, indicating that higher humidity may alleviate water loss induced VOCs compositional imbalance. The variations in terpenoids further support this point. Under HRH, terpene accumulation exceeded that under LRH, with the maximum level reaching about 1.5-fold the LRH maximum (Fig. 2E). This may be because milder water loss preserved cellular metabolic activity and terpene biosynthesis. Higher tissue hydration under HRH may also affect monoterpene diffusion and release (Bergman et al., 2025). Consistently, postharvest grape dehydration has been reported to reduce free terpene alcohols, such as linalool, nerol, and geraniol, suggesting that excessive water loss may be unfavorable for maintaining some terpene alcohols (Río Segade et al., 2018). Therefore, higher VOCs abundance under LRH likely reflects accelerated stress metabolism, lipid oxidation derived VOCs formation, and VOCs remodeling rather than improved desirable aroma quality.

Given the close association between water loss-induced aroma imbalance and lipid-derived volatile attributes, we next examined whether fatty acid-derived VOCs pathways were transcriptionally reprogrammed during storage. The heatmap focused on two major routes involved in the formation of fatty acid-derived volatiles in fruit: the β-oxidation pathway and the LOX pathway. β-Oxidation generates acyl-CoA intermediates with different chain lengths, thereby providing acyl donors for ester volatile biosynthesis (El Hadi et al., 2013). In parallel, the LOX pathway uses unsaturated fatty acids as substrates to produce C6/C9 aldehydes, which can be further converted into the corresponding alcohols and esters through the sequential actions of ADH and AAT (Matsui, 2006). In this study, water loss stress led to transcriptional differences in the β -oxidation and LOX pathways (Fig. 5B). Specifically, water loss upregulated the Lox and AAT gene during the later storage period, and the seven β-oxidase genes simultaneously showed dynamic changes. These changes promote the downward transformation of the LOX pathway and increase the volume of downstream esters (Fig. 2C). Consistent with previous reports, Zamboni et al. reported that genes related to fatty acid metabolism and β-oxidation in grapes were activated during dehydration, accompanied by metabolic changes related to aroma (Zamboni et al., 2008).

Low-humidity storage disrupts membrane lipid homeostasis in strawberry fruit

Low-humidity storage accelerates fruit water loss, disrupts cellular water balance, and weakens membrane stability. AQPs, particularly PIP-type aquaporins, are involved in plasma membrane water transport, and their expression can partly reflect the capacity for cellular water regulation. In strawberry, co-expression of FaPIP1;1 and FaPIP2;1 enhances membrane water permeability and is associated with fruit softening (Alleva et al., 2010). Thus, sustained AQP suppression under LRH suggests impaired water-regulation capacity and disturbed membrane function. Water loss may also intensify lipid peroxidation, as unsaturated membrane fatty acids are vulnerable to reactive oxygen species attack and generate MDA, a widely used indicator of oxidative membrane damage (Wen et al., 2017). Beyond passive membrane damage, membrane lipids are also actively remodeled through lipid-signaling pathways. PLD hydrolyzes phospholipids to produce PA, an important lipid signal involved in membrane turnover, remodeling, and stress responses (Wang, 2005). Although PLD/PA activation may help maintain membrane homeostasis under moderate stress, prolonged water loss may shift membranes from an adaptive state toward damage accumulation (Li et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019).

In this study, AQP expression was continuously suppressed under LRH, while MDA levels were significantly increased, indicating that low-humidity storage impaired transmembrane water regulation and intensified membrane lipid peroxidation and oxidative membrane damage (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 1). Moreover, both PLD abundance and PA content decreased under LRH (Fig. 4B and C), suggesting that PLD/PA-associated lipid signaling and membrane remodeling were not effectively maintained under water loss. Although PA may be further converted to DAG, RAP and lipase were not induced under LRH, suggesting that the lower PA level was not mainly explained by transcriptional activation of downstream PA conversion (Fig. 5). Together with the early decline of GPAT and PLA under LRH, this pattern further indicated that membrane lipid mobilization, lipid synthesis, and remodeling processes were broadly constrained, making PLD/PA lipid signaling difficult to maintain. Together, these results suggest that membrane lipid metabolism under LRH was not simply enhanced but became structurally imbalanced: membrane lipid peroxidation was intensified, whereas PLD/PA associated lipid signaling and repair-related remodeling were insufficient. This phenotype, characterized by enhanced membrane damage but limited PLD/PA regulation, is consistent with observations in other postharvest fruits. For example, during blueberry cold storage, decreases in PLDα1/α2 expression or activity were followed by membrane instability and surface pitting (Wang et al., 2019). Similarly, PLDα declined during prolonged cold storage in citrus, accompanied by aggravated chilling injury symptoms (Romero et al., 2025). These findings suggest that under sustained or severe postharvest stress, PLD is not necessarily continuously activated as a membrane lipid degrading factor; instead, its decline may reflect weakened lipid signaling and membrane remodeling capacity, contributing to membrane destabilization and quality deterioration.

In this study, the PLD/PA related pathway was suppressed under LRH, indicating that PLD/PA mediated lipid signaling, membrane lipid remodeling, and fatty acid release were not continuously maintained but weakened. By contrast, activation of the LOX pathway and β-oxidation branch suggested that lipid metabolism was redirected toward oxidative conversion and lipid derived VOCs formation. Thus, LRH induced water loss may shift membrane lipid metabolism from homeostasis maintenance toward oxidative deterioration, contributing to the transition of fruit aroma from fresh notes to overripe, fatty oxidized, or deterioration-related characteristics. To further examine the association between membrane lipid metabolism and volatile remodeling, Pearson correlation and Mantel analyses were performed. MDA and weight loss were associated with ester accumulation and deterioration-related volatiles, indicating that water loss and lipid peroxidation were closely linked to aroma changes. PA was statistically associated with ester accumulation, while PLD was negatively correlated with FaPIP1;1, suggesting a connection among lipid signaling, water transport, and volatile changes (Fig. 6). Mantel analysis linked membrane lipid metabolism with VOCs formation (Fig. 7). LOX and ADH were associated with aldehydes, especially hexanal and (E)-2-hexenal, supporting a LOX-ADH connection with fatty-acid-derived aldehydes. RAP and Lipase were associated with decanal, suggesting lipid turnover involvement in medium-chain aldehydes. FxaYL_121g0717770 (PLD) was selectively linked to (E)-2-hexen-1-yl acetate, implying a specific PLD/PA related ester association. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for future studies aimed at regulating membrane lipid metabolism to control aroma deterioration and maintain strawberry quality during postharvest storage.

Conclusion

In summary, this study focused on two RH conditions (45 % RH, 90 % RH) that can be encountered along the supply chain of strawberries. High humidity storage modified the strawberry aroma profile and maintained aroma quality, accompanied by reduced water-loss-induced membrane lipid oxidation. In contrast, low humidity storage accelerated membrane destabilization and lipid metabolic reprogramming, which may contribute to VOCs remodeling and aroma deterioration. Under excessive water loss, PLD and PA associated membrane lipid metabolism appears to have a weakened regulatory role. This was accompanied by stronger β-oxidation- and LOX-related responses. Consistently, aldehydes declined more rapidly during accelerated ripening, whereas esters and furanones tended to accumulate. Additionally, terpene production showed a high humidity dependent pattern. This study provides new insights into the relationship between membrane lipid metabolism and VOCs remodeling during strawberry quality deterioration, offering a basis for aroma oriented quality control and optimized humidity management strategies.
