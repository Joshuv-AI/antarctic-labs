Pectus Excavatum–Induced Preload Insufficiency Presenting as Long-Standing Sinus Tachycardia
Authors: Radwan Alkhatib, Fawzi Zghyer, Taha Hatab, Chadi Tabaja, Faysal Massad, Rochell Issa, Besir Besir, Shada Jadam, Daniel P. Raymond, Khaled M. Ziada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere pectus excavatum is an uncommon cause of hemodynamic compromise in adults and may present with unexplained tachycardia when preload filling is impaired by cardiac compression.Case SummaryA 65-year-old woman with long-standing palpitations presented with sinus tachycardia despite high-dose beta-blockade. Electrocardiogram and ambulatory monitoring showed sinus rhythm with marked heart rate variability. Right heart catheterization revealed low filling pressures, with a mean right atrial pressure of 1 mm Hg and preserved cardiac output. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance showed severe pectus excavatum (Haller index: 8.1) with anterior cardiac compression. Pectus-induced preload insufficiency was deemed responsible for her symptoms. She underwent a modified Ravitch repair with complete resolution of palpitations and normalization of heart rate.DiscussionChest wall deformity can cause clinically significant cardiac compression, preload insufficiency, and compensatory sinus tachycardia, even in late adulthood.Take-Home MessageSevere pectus excavatum should be recognized as a reversible cause of preload insufficiency and sinus tachycardia, and surgical correction may result in symptom resolution.

History of Presentation

A 65-year-old woman presented to our institution for evaluation of sinus tachycardia. She reported long-standing episodes of palpitations and occasional fatigue for the past few years, with heart rates occasionally reaching the 180s, reproducibly provoked by forward flexion, bending, and prone positioning. She denied chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, or other associated symptoms. She also reported significant blood pressure variability on her home log; nevertheless, she described an active lifestyle. She was taking metoprolol succinate 200 mg daily, which she had been prescribed for 12 years but which failed to control her symptoms. On physical examination, she was tachycardiac (heart rate: 138 beats/min) with a blood pressure of 134/94 mm Hg, afebrile, with an oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Severe pectus deformity was noted. The rest of her physical examination was unremarkable.Take-Home Messages•Severe pectus excavatum can produce clinically significant preload insufficiency through mechanical cardiac compression and impaired filling, leading to intermittent sinus tachycardia that is provoked with certain positions.•Recognition of this reversible hemodynamic disorder—particularly in older adults with long-standing, unexplained tachycardia—is essential, as timely surgical correction can restore cardiac filling and resolve symptoms.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of labile hypertension, hypothyroidism, fibromuscular dysplasia, and severe pectus excavatum. Her other medications included amlodipine, levothyroxine, and rosuvastatin.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of palpitations includes inappropriate sinus tachycardia, medication-induced tachycardia (levothyroxine, amlodipine), postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome/autonomic dysfunction, paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia or atrial tachycardia, and preload insufficiency.

Investigations

Laboratory tests showed a hemoglobin level of 12.4 g/dL (normal range: 11.5-15.5 g/dL) and thyroid-stimulating hormone of 1.8 mlU/L (normal range: 0.27-4.2 mlU/L). Initial electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus tachycardia with a heart rate of 113 beats/min (Figure 1A). A 14-day ambulatory Holter monitor showed a minimum heart rate of 61 beats/min, a maximum of 161 beats/min, and an average of 86 beats/min. The underlying rhythm was sinus without ectopy or arrhythmia. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal biventricular size and systolic function with ejection fraction of 61%. The patient was tachycardic during imaging (heart rate: ∼108 beats/min), with preserved cardiac output (4.7 L/min by left ventricular outflow tract) and a relatively small left ventricular cavity (indexed end-diastolic volume: 27.8 mL/m2). Inferior vena cava assessment demonstrated a small, collapsible inferior vena cava (1.7 cm with >50% inspiratory collapse), supportive of low right atrial pressure. Left heart catheterization demonstrated angiographically normal coronary arteries. Right heart catheterization showed a mean right atrial pressure of 1 mm Hg and a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 5 mm Hg with otherwise normal hemodynamics, including a cardiac output of 4.26 L/min measured by thermodilution.Figure 1Electrocardiogram Findings(A) Initial 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus tachycardia with a heart rate of 113 beats/min. Computed tomography angiography of the chest (Figure 2) excluded pulmonary embolism but revealed severe pectus with a Haller index of 5.3 and anterior cardiac compression causing dilated atria and moderate cardiomegaly. Stress cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging with velocity mapping and functional assessment (Figure 3) demonstrated preserved biventricular function and no evidence of inducible ischemia or perfusion defects, despite the presence of marked chest wall deformity (Haller index: 8.1). Resting heart rate during CMR was approximately 102 beats/min despite beta-blocker therapy, with preserved cardiac output (5.9 L/min), cardiac index (3.9 L/min/m2), and stroke volume (58 mL; indexed: 38 mL/m2). Given the absence of structural heart disease, coronary pathology, or pulmonary embolism, in addition to the presence of severe pectus excavatum with cardiac compression, pectus-induced preload insufficiency was suspected as the underlying cause of her appropriate sinus tachycardia.Figure 2Chest Computed Tomography Angiography(A) Axial and (B) sagittal image of the chest demonstrating severe pectus excavatum with a Haller index of 5.3 and marked anterior compression of the right heart chambers.Figure 3Stress Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance ImagingVelocity mapping and functional assessment showing preserved biventricular size and systolic function without inducible ischemia or perfusion defects. Management

After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient was referred to the thoracic surgery team for corrective repair. She underwent a modified Ravitch procedure without complication (Figure 4). At both 1- and 3-month follow-up, she reported complete resolution of palpitations, with resting heart rates persistently in the 60s (Figure 1B). Her metoprolol dose was reduced by 50% to 100 mg daily, with plans to taper further on upcoming visits. She also described significant improvement in exercise tolerance and functional capacity compared to her presurgical baseline. More importantly, she reported improvement in her quality of life, with resting heart rates persistently in the 60s to 70s and normalization of blood pressure readings. Discussion

Our patient was diagnosed with pectus excavatum–induced preload insufficiency leading to appropriate sinus tachycardia, a physiological sinus rhythm response to reduced preload state. Severe chest wall deformity causing hemodynamic compromise is a rare but clinically important phenomenon, as misdiagnosis can lead to delay in management. Prior reports have described variable presentations of pectus-related hemodynamic compromise, including asymptomatic positional right ventricular (RV) obstruction on echocardiography,1,2 syncope,3 and even malignant arrhythmias such as positional ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest.4,5

The underlying mechanism of hemodynamic disturbance in severe pectus excavatum primarily relates to right-sided cardiac compression, which impairs venous return or produces RV outflow obstruction. This causes a reduction in left-sided preload and stroke volume, thereby diminishing overall cardiac output.5,6 The resulting preload insufficiency can manifest clinically as fatigue and exercise intolerance, while compensatory sinus tachycardia develops to maintain cardiac output in the setting of reduced stroke volume.2,6,7 Although some patients adapt to the reduced preload state, symptoms may become overt with postural changes that exacerbate cardiac compression, resulting in worsening hemodynamics. Reported positions associated with symptom provocation include bending,5 sitting,2 prone,6 and expiration.1 We infer that this postural-related mechanism likely account for the broad variability in heart rate observed on Holter monitor as well as the labile blood pressure that fluctuated with postural changes, although we acknowledge the absence of invasive hemodynamic confirmation during symptom provocation.

Our case is unique given the delayed presentation and diagnosis at age 65, as most reports of severe pectus excavatum with hemodynamic compromise involve much younger patients. In unrepaired deformities, the depressed sternum may cause dynamic RV compression and impaired diastolic filling that can be initially well tolerated, while aging-related reductions in chest wall compliance and viscoelastic cardiovascular reserve may lead to a transition from compensated physiology to symptomatic preload limitation.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 In one of the largest surgical series, Jaroszewski and Fonkalsrud9 reported a mean age of repair of 27 years across 268 pectus excavatum cases.

Despite a long history of symptomatic tachycardia and palpitation requiring high-dose beta-blocker therapy, our patient remained relatively active, likely contributing to the delayed diagnosis. After surgical correction, however, she reported marked additional improvement in exercise tolerance and functional capacity, with resolution of palpitations. Nonetheless, earlier recognition and surgical correction of the underlying pathology would likely have mitigated years of symptoms and cardiovascular stress.

Conclusions

Severe pectus excavatum can cause significant preload insufficiency with intermittent sinus tachycardia and should therefore be considered in older adults with long-standing unexplained palpitations. Early recognition of this structural etiology is critical, as surgical correction can restore cardiac filling, improve functional capacity, and prevent long-term cardiovascular sequelae.
