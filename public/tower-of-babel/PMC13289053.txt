The impact of the regime change on polio outbreaks in Afghanistan
Authors: Mohammad Ismail Zubair, Swetha Ganesan, Kailey Love, William Riley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfghanistan is one of the two remaining polio-endemic countries in the world. The collapse of republic government and expected escalation of violence and its consequences associated with the regime change of August 2021 raised concern that polio eradication efforts would deteriorate, resulting in polio outbreaks throughout the country. The research question for this study is as follows: What is the relationship between the 2021 regime change and wild poliovirus type 1 (WPV1) in Afghanistan? This observational study explores the relationship of regime change on polio control in Afghanistan during two periods: (1) the 8-year period (2017–2024) surrounding the coalition departure from Afghanistan in 2021 and (2) the 8-year period 1997–2004 surrounding the coalition regime change in 2001. Two important conclusions can be drawn. First, a polio outbreak did not occur following the withdrawal of US forces from Afghanistan in August 2021. Second, similar patterns of WPV1 outbreak and control are observed in both the 2001 and 2021 regime changes in Afghanistan. The findings also indicate that polio vaccination programme efforts in Afghanistan did not decline following the 2021 regime change. The findings have important implications for understanding the relationship between regime change and polio eradication efforts and for future studies to explore the mechanisms underlying the relationship between regime change and polio control.

Introduction

Poliomyelitis (polio) is caused by the poliovirus, a highly infectious viral disease that can cause paralysis,1 largely affecting children under 5 years of age. The virus is transmitted through person-to-person contact, primarily through the faecal-oral route or droplets from a sneeze or cough from an infected person. Fewer than 1% of infections lead to irreversible paralysis, and among those paralysed, 5% to 10% die due to their respiratory muscles becoming immobilised.2 The main focus of current polio control efforts is the eradication of wild poliovirus type 1 (WPV1), the most contagious and highly transmissible strain of poliovirus.3 While there is currently no cure for polio, there is a safe and effective vaccine that provides robust immunity against the poliovirus and protects children from infection.

In 1988, the WHO declared polio a global emergency when 125 countries reported 350 000 annual cases of the disease. The global emergency led to the launch of the Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI) to eradicate polio.2 The GPEI consists of a six-organisation partnership: the WHO, Rotary International, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, UNICEF, the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation and Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance.4 5 Since its inception, the GPEI has made tremendous progress in decreasing the global incidence of polio by 99.9%,3 and protecting the global population from this disease. As of 2019, only 175 reported cases remain.6 A key strategy for polio elimination efforts has been to ensure high immunisation coverage among children under 5 years of age in low-middle-income countries.79 Until polio is globally eradicated, it continues to be a risk to children in all countries.

There are two countries that have not yet eradicated polio, Afghanistan and Pakistan.10 Failure to eradicate polio in these remaining countries could result in a resurgence of as many as 200 000 new cases globally.2 The collapse of the Republic of Afghanistan in 2021 caused significant disruption in its national polio eradication programme, creating concern that reduced vaccination coverage could lead to an increase in polio cases.11 While polio outbreaks are known to be related to fighting and war-torn conditions,12 there is no systematic examination regarding the effect of regime change on polio control in Afghanistan.

Polio eradication efforts in Afghanistan

Polio eradication efforts have been underway in Afghanistan for over 30 years. The first mass polio vaccination campaigns were conducted from 1994 to 1996, targeting almost 70% of Afghan children (aged <5 years) for oral polio vaccine and other routine immunisations.9 12 In 1994, a new strategy was implemented by the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) with the support of GPEI to replace earlier immunisation campaign efforts.13 This campaign strategy consisted of two components, the National Immunization Days (NIDs) and Sub-National Immunization Days (SNIDs). The NIDs are large-scale national efforts, while the SNIDs are comparatively small-scale polio vaccination campaigns in specific polio-endemic provinces and districts that targeted all children under 5 years of age for polio vaccination. There are typically three to six NIDs and three or five SNIDs annually aimed to achieve high vaccination coverage rates.14 The NIDs and SNIDs use house-to-house vaccination strategies with home visits to encourage vaccination. By 2000, epidemiological surveillance systems were implemented to more accurately count cases of polio.13 With the establishment of the Islamic Republic of Afghanistan in 2001, the MoPH assumed overall responsibility for coordinating polio eradication programmes. In 2015, the MoPH established the National Emergency Operation Center (NEOC) to lead the mission and leverage coordination among partners under a National Emergency Action Plan to better coordinate polio eradication.12 15 Subsequently, Regional Emergency Operation Centers were established in priority regions of the country (having higher acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) positive cases) to ensure quick evidence-based decision-making and timely coordination and communication.16

Regime change in Afghanistan

Four regime changes occurred since Afghanistan joined the GPEI in late 1980.17 The first change followed the departure of the Russian troops in 1988 leading to a subsequent collapse of the Moscow-backed government and Mujahedin takeover in 1992.9 The second regime change occurred when the Taliban assumed control in 1996.18 19 The third regime change occurred with the coalition-led intervention in October 2001 and the establishment of a US-backed republic government. During this period, the Islamic Republic of Afghanistan implemented positive changes to the national polio eradication efforts, including improved infrastructure, programme planning, funding raising and international cooperation.20 The fourth regime change transpired when the US-led coalition withdrew from Afghanistan in August 2021, resulting in the Taliban re-emergence.21 The MoPH lost much of its health stewardship role and its international recognition following the Taliban takeover. The majority of donors funding health services withdrew support, healthcare professionals left the country or found alternative employment20 22 resulting in a severe shortage of skilled public health providers,23 and a near collapse of the Afghanistan health service delivery system.20 The global donor community rechanneled all health funding through United Nations agencies (WHO and UNICEF), which are now financially leading health services for the country.24 25 These changes caused significant disruption to the Afghanistan national polio eradication programme, creating concern that efforts to eliminate global polio were jeopardised.26

This study has three objectives: (1) Assess the impact of the 2021 regime change on polio outbreak in Afghanistan; (2) evaluate the Polio Vaccination Coverage and Vaccination Refusal Rates surrounding the 2021 regime change; and (3) compare the pattern of WPV1 control between the 2001 and 2021 regime changes in Afghanistan.

Methods

Study design and setting

This interrupted time series (ITS)27 study examines the polio outbreaks over the 8-year period surrounding the regime changes of 2001 and 2021. The impact of the 2021 regime change on the Afghanistan polio vaccination programmes is also examined (these data are not available during the 2001 regime change). We used the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) guidelines.

Participants

This study was conducted using secondary data from reports obtained from the Afghanistan MoPH and the NEOC. The methodology for studying population selection (such as codes or algorithms used to identify subjects) is not provided. The population of focus in this study evaluates the total number of individuals reached, vaccination coverage and vaccination refusals in the NIDs and SNIDs during the Afghanistan childhood polio vaccination campaigns from 2019 to 2023. Quantitative variables

Dependent variables: Polio cases (WPV1), vaccination coverage rates and vaccination refusal rates. The exposure was WPV1 vaccination by workers during scheduled NIDs in the Afghanistan childhood polio vaccination campaigns. The main exposure was defined by vaccination records.

Independent variable: Regime change

Data sources and measurement

Routinely collected WPV 1 data were obtained from the WHO AFP surveillance report.28 The population figures were obtained from the NEOC and the MoPH. Data regarding vaccination refusal rates and vaccination coverage rates were obtained from MoPH reports (data for 2024 are unavailable). Bias

To address potential sources of bias, we established inclusion and exclusion criteria when collecting and analysing the data. Data were cross-referenced with other sources and literature to establish data validity and reliability. The study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board because this study does not involve human participants.

Statistical methods

Two types of control charts, run chart and C chart, were used to identify patterns in vaccination coverage rates, vaccine refusal rates and the incidence of WPV1. A control chart is a technique to analyse a longitudinal sequence to distinguish meaningful variation (signal) from random variation (noise) over time, and to identify process stability (the ability to predict the future process performance) and process capability (expected performance when a process is stable).3436 The C chart was an ITS analysis.27 These analytical techniques provide a comprehensive understanding of WPV1 patterns coincident with regime change as well as short-term vaccination rates and vaccination refusal rates.

Discussion

Key results

Major concern was expressed that a polio outbreak would occur following the coalition withdrawal from Afghanistan in August 2021 and the subsequent regime change.24 26 Contrary to these concerns, the study indicates there was not a polio outbreak. Rather, there was a significant 96% reduction in WPV1 cases, moving from a polio outbreak of 56 cases in 2020 to an average of four cases per year in 2021–2023. The incidence of WPV1 increased to 23 cases in 2024,37 however this was not a significant change.

The findings also suggest considerable progress was made in polio eradication efforts subsequent to the 2021 regime change. A significant 57.5% decrease in the polio vaccination refusal rate occurred during the 49-month period surrounding the 2021 regime change. Specifically, the vaccination refusal rate during the 22-month period prior to the regime change (579/100 000) dropped significantly compared with the 21-month period following the regime change (333/100 000). This decrease in vaccination refusal rates corresponds with a significant 36% increase in vaccination rates from 69% to 94% over the same 49-month time frame. While polio eradication is expected to get closer to zero as the years pass, the findings show that polio eradication came close to zero following both regime changes, only to re-emerge later.26 38

To better understand the impact of the 2021 regime change on WPV1 outbreak, we examined the pattern of WPV1 infections associated with the 2001 regime change for comparison. The analysis of WPV1 surrounding the regime changes of 2001 and 2021 shows similar trends. Specifically, a significant increase in WPV1 occurred prior to both regime changes, followed by a significant reduction in WPV1 immediately following the regime changes. A significant outbreak preceded both the 2001 regime change (N=329 cases, 1998–2000) as well as the 2021 regime change (N=56 cases, 2020).

Both the 2001 and 2021 WPV1 outbreaks were followed by a significant reduction in infections immediately after regime change. The 5-year period (2001–2005) after the 2001 regime change shows an annual average of 8.4 cases, while the 3-year period (2021–2023) after the 2021 regime change shows an annual average of 4 cases. These are only two time periods of WPV1 control over the entire 27-year timeframe (not shown).

Limitations

Several limitations to the data used in this analysis can affect reported vaccination rates and vaccination refusal rates. First, there may not be consistent accuracy of population and polio campaign data. Obtaining an accurate count of the target population (children under 5 years of age) and how many people live in remote areas for polio vaccination is challenging because birth records are absent, and population censuses are not consistent. Second, internal and external displacements of individuals create additional challenges in estimating population sizes. Third, the vaccination rates, vaccination refusals and children under 5 years of age population reported by the Afghanistan MoPH/NEOC, the WHO and other partner reports are not always consistent. The polio eradication programme in Afghanistan uses the estimated target population established in 2018,9 and this baseline has not been adjusted to reflect population growth. We made adjustments for population growth described in the methods section. Fourth, vaccine refusals can be defined as community-based refusals,39 or deliberate rejection of the polio vaccination by caregivers for a child.15 The Afghanistan MoPH used the latter definition and is adopted for this study. Fifth, polio vaccination campaign regions may fluctuate due to security threats to vaccination workers, active military conflicts lead to the exclusion of affected areas or provinces from campaign coverage, bans on polio vaccination imposed by certain military groups. Cold weather during winter in some parts of the country and inaccessibility of campaign workers to certain parts of provinces due to ban or security threats disrupt vaccination rates. It is also possible that the drop in reported cases during the period of regime change from 2021 and 2022 may reflect incomplete data rather than an actual reduction in polio. Sixth, this analysis examines WPV1 outbreaks for Afghanistan on a national level, a conventional method for reporting WPV1 incidence. Understanding subnational WPV1 variation is also critical for a full understanding of the complete picture of WPV1 infection. Seventh, the analyses of the vaccination rates and vaccination refusal rates do not use consistent time intervals due to different reporting periods. However, this does not violate the statistical assumptions made for the use of control charts in this study. Finally, the COVID-19 movement restrictions between 2020 and 2021 may have a potential confounding impact on these findings.4042

Conclusion

The three objectives established for this study were to: (1) assess the impact of the 2021 regime change on polio outbreak in Afghanistan; (2) evaluate the relationship between Polio Vaccination Coverage and Vaccination Refusal Rates; and (3) compare the pattern of WPV1 control between the 2001 and 2021 regime changes in Afghanistan. The findings indicate that following the 2021 regime change, the national polio vaccination rate improved significantly, coincident with a dramatic drop in WPV1 infections. For comparison, the patterns of WPV1 control surrounding the 2001 and 2021 regime changes in Afghanistan are notably similar. During both instances, there was a significant increase in WPV1 outbreak immediately preceding regime change and a significant decrease in WPV1 cases immediately following regime change. These findings have important implications for (1) understanding the impact of regime change on polio eradication efforts with implications for broader infectious disease control efforts and (2) future study to explore the mechanisms underlying the relationship between regime change and polio control.
