Loss of Nemp1 disrupts female meiosis and activates a conserved ATM-CHK2 checkpoint
Authors: Bilal Ahmad Hakim, Yonit Tsatskis, Ling Zhang, Esther Choi, Ying Zhang, Didier Hodzic, Que Wu, MuYun Zhang, Maryam Pashaei, Kyungwon Ha, Jannette Rusch, Julie A Brill, Miguel Angel Brieño-Enríquez, Andrea Jurisicova, Helen McNeill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemale germ cells must preserve the integrity of their genome and generate genetic diversity via meiotic recombination. This challenging process is error prone. Highly conserved checkpoint pathways detect errors in recombination and DNA damage, inducing the death of defective oocytes. Nuclear Envelope Membrane Protein (NEMP) homologs are highly conserved proteins critical for fertility in flies, worms, fish and mice. They localize to the inner nuclear envelope where they provide mechanical support. However, why NEMP homologs are specifically required for fertility is still unclear. Using both Drosophila and mouse models, we establish that loss of NEMP homologs leads to activation of ATM and CHK2 kinases and inhibition of CHK2 or ATM rescues oocyte loss. In the absence of Nemp1, meiotic progression is delayed and DNA damage is increased at zygonema and pachynema stages. Loss of Nemp1 also leads to defects in chromosome synapsis persisting through pachynema. We conclude that NEMP1 is needed to protect genome integrity and is crucial for accurate chromosome pairing and synapsis, supporting oocyte developmental competence and survival.Subject terms: Apoptosis, Chromosomes, Nuclear envelope, Meiosis, Checkpoint signalling

Introduction

Transmitting accurate genetic information to the next generation is critical for the maintenance of species. All the oocytes that a woman has for her entire reproductive lifetime are created during fetal life. Millions of germline cells are generated during early embryogenesis, and subsequently this pool is dramatically reduced, with more than 80% of oocytes dying during fetal life or shortly after birth1–3. Damage in oocytes can be devastating to the next generation, and canonical germline checkpoints, including the DNA integrity sensing pathways, are activated to ensure elimination of defective oocytes4,5. Numerous reasons for this drastic reduction of oocytes have been proposed, including inability to repair DNA damage incurred during meiosis, defective chromosome pairing and synapsis, or the selective death of germ cells within cysts to enrich the oocytes with cytoplasmic components6–10. Most of this death is thought to occur via apoptosis, although other modes have been proposed to cull defective oocytes11.

A new player in the field of germ cell biology is the transmembrane Nuclear Envelope Membrane Protein 1 (NEMP1)12. We have shown that loss of Nemp1 homologs in flies, worms, fish and mice impairs fertility. NEMP1 is localized to the inner nuclear envelope (NE) in oocytes, and loss of Nemp1 leads to dramatic reduction of the ovarian reserve and near-total sterility. Nemp1−/− females ovulate, but their oocytes lack developmental competence, and a subset of oocytes have defects of meiotic spindle organization12. In addition, NEMP1 supports the NE during erythroblast enucleation13. NEMP1 facilitates cell survival on mechanically challenging substrates in vivo and in vitro. Loss of the ovarian reserve and fertility in Nemp1−/− female mice can be partially rescued by softening the ovarian cortex in vivo14.

Biochemical experiments show that human NEMP1 forms a complex with Nesprins via their KASH domain, independent of SUN proteins, providing a link to the cytoskeleton14. BioID screens identified many potential NEMP1 partners, and validated binding to the LEM domain protein Emerin. Biophysical experiments in mammalian cultured cells showed that an Emerin-NEMP1 complex supported nuclear envelope mechanical stiffness in cultured cells and mouse oocytes12. Loss of emerin in Drosophila leads to loss of germ cells, and in mice leads to a reduced ovarian reserve, similar to loss of Nemp112,15. However, unlike loss of Nemp1, loss of Emerin does not affect the ability of YAP to respond to mechanical stress14. Vertebrate NEMP1 also binds RAN-GTP16, barrier-to-autointegration factor (BAF)17 and RanBP118.

Here we show that loss of nemp/Nemp1 leads to activation of the ATM-CHK2 pathway, in germ cells of flies and mice. Importantly, nemp/Nemp1 deficient oocytes can be rescued by inhibition of ATM or CHK2. Accelerated oocyte loss in Nemp1−/− begins during fetal stages, and leads to meiotic defects, including failure of pairing, synapsis and recombination. These data place NEMP1 as a critical component of mammalian meiosis and oocyte genome integrity.

Results

Loss of oocytes in Nemp1−/− mutants begins in fetal life and is due to apoptosis

Previously we showed that Nemp1-/- females have a severely reduced primordial follicle pool at post-natal day 28 (P28)12. The loss of primordial follicles could be due to excessive death or to an accelerated transition into the primary stage. We quantified primordial and primary follicle numbers in Nemp1-/- mutants at P2, P4, and P10. Histological analyses showed a significant reduction of primordial follicle number at all ages, and a reduced primary follicle pool at P10, suggesting accelerated transition into primary stages is not the cause of primordial follicle loss (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). TUNEL staining of P4 ovaries revealed increased TUNEL-positive primordial follicle oocytes in Nemp1-/- ovaries (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1b, c). In addition, TUNEL positivity persisted in primary oocytes and was also detected in rare somatic cells. Similarly, P2 Nemp1-/- ovaries, have increased number of oocytes with active caspase-3 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 1e), and TUNEL-positivity (P < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Collectively, these data indicate that loss of Nemp1 leads to increased apoptosis in postnatal oocytes.

To determine if NEMP1 could function earlier in development, we examined its expression in fetal and neonatal ovaries. Immunofluorescence revealed NEMP1 is strongly expressed and localized on the nuclear envelope of oocytes at embryonic day 17.5 (E17.5) (Fig. 1g) and maintained during the formation of follicles (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1f).

The increased apoptosis in Nemp1-/- neonatal ovaries and the prominent expression of NEMP1 in E17.5 oocytes led us to examine oocyte number and quality in fetal ovaries. We used wholemount analysis of intact fetal ovaries with the Clear, Unobstructed Brain/Body Imaging Cocktails and Computational analysis (CUBIC) method to clear ovarian tissues and reconstruct three-dimensional (3D) fluorescence images with the germ cell markers TRA98 and DDX4 (Fig. 1h). We analyzed E15.5, E18.5, and P0 ovaries from wildtype and Nemp1-/- animals. At E15.5, oocyte number in Nemp1-/- ovaries was comparable to WT, but by E18.5 there was significant loss of oocytes (reduction by ~39%; P < 0.001). Reduced oocyte number was also observed at P0 in Nemp1-/- ovaries (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1i). Taken together, these data indicate that NEMP1 is required for oocyte viability in fetal stages and to maintain the ovarian reserve postnatally.

LINE-1 expression has been linked to fetal oocyte attrition19. To investigate if increased LINE-1 could be the cause of early death we used RT-qPCR on E15.5 and E17.5 WT and Nemp1-/- fetal ovaries. However, there was no difference in the expression of LINE-1 between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 2a, d). LINE-1 ORF1p antibody staining at E15.5 and E18.5 also did not change (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c, e, f), implying fetal oocyte loss in Nemp1-/- is not associated with increased LINE-1 expression.

ATM and Chk2 activation triggers oocyte loss in nemp mutant flies

To understand the mechanism by which Nemp1 deficiency triggers oocyte loss, we turned to Drosophila, as flies are amenable to rapid genetic analyses. nemp-/- flies are viable but sterile, with significantly reduced ovary size and severe germ cell loss12. Expression of nemp RNAi in the Drosophila germ line by Nanos (nos)-Gal4 also leads to reduced size of ovaries and dramatic germ cell loss prior to initiation of meiosis. Rarely, however, a few escapers progress to the 16-cell cyst stage, though these are exceedingly uncommon (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). To investigate these escapers further, we stained ovaries with C(3)G (a synaptonemal complex component) and observed very few cells that contained C(3)G foci (Fig. 2a, b), consistent with acute depletion of germ cells before onset of meiosis. We examined oocyte specification using Orb. In WT after exit from the germarium, Orb marks the future single oocyte located at the posterior of the egg chamber (Supplementary Fig. 3c). In contrast, nemp knockdown resulted in ~7% of egg chambers containing two Orb positive cells instead of one (Supplementary Fig. 3c-d). In addition, the very rare eggs that were laid by nemp knockdown females showed open anterior eggshells (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). These defects could be due to many potential causes, including DNA damage or alterations in oocyte polarity, cell migration or meiotic defects20–22. However, nemp-/- females lay no eggs, and nemp knockdown females very rarely lay eggs, making mechanistic studies of the underlying cause of these defects challenging.

emerin (also known as otefin) mutants also lack oocytes23, and oocyte loss can be prevented by mutation or knockdown of the DNA damage response kinases, ATR and chk2, but not by inhibition of ATM or chk115. Because Nemp binds Drosophila Emerin12, we tested if oocyte loss caused by knockdown of nemp could be rescued by inhibition of components of the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway. We used RNAi knockdown of the DDR pathway components ATM (tefu), ATR (mei-41), chk1 (grapes), chk2 (loki, mnk), and p53 in combination with RNAi knockdown of nemp. As previously reported12, driving nemp RNAi in the germline with nos-Gal4 causes loss of oocytes, leading to reduced egg laying (Fig. 2c). Loss of egg laying and reduced ovary size in nemp RNAi flies was rescued by knockdown of the kinases ATM (P < 0.0001) or chk2 (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 5a). In contrast, loss of the DDR kinases ATR and chk1; did not rescue egg laying or ovary size in nemp knockdown flies. We assessed knockdown effectiveness of ATR and chk1 by RT-qPCR and found that their depletion was robust (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). These data imply that loss of nemp in germ cells triggers oocyte loss by activation of ATM-CHK2 pathway, pointing to double-strand DNA damage as a potential cause of oocyte loss in nemp-/- mutants.

Reduced oocyte number in nemp mutants is likely due to apoptosis as there is increased expression of Dcp-1, an effector caspase (Supplementary Fig. 5e). The increased Dcp-1 was eliminated in nemp/chk2 double knockdown, indicating that Chk2 activation drives apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 5e). We also performed double knockdowns of nemp with initiator caspases (dronc, dredd and strica) and executioner caspases (drice, dcp-1, decay and damm) (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Knockdown of drice (homolog of Caspase-3) in nemp RNAi flies partially rescued ovary size, but egg laying only slightly improved (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Knockdown of other caspases did not show any rescue, while RNAi of p53 resulted in a small rescue of ovary size of nemp RNAi (Supplementary Fig. 5a) but had no effect on egg laying.

ATM and CHK2 are activated in Nemp1-/- mouse oocytes

We next asked whether ATM and CHK2 are also responsible for oocyte loss in Nemp1-/- mice. To assess activation of ATM and CHK2, we immunostained E15.5 and E18.5 fetal and day 2 postnatal ovaries for ATM phosphorylated at S1981 and CHK2 phosphorylated at T6824,25. Notably, both pATM and pCHK2 are increased in germ cells and somatic cells in Nemp1-/- (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2d–k, Supplementary Figs. 7a–e, 8a–e and 9e, f), suggesting loss of Nemp1 activates a conserved DDR pathway.

Formation of primordial follicles, which starts shortly before birth and continues until ~day 4 postpartum, is also a period of selective germ cell death. Immunostaining of P2 and P4 Nemp1-/- ovaries revealed elevated expression of the DNA damage markers γH2AX (P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Figs. 9a, b and 10) and RAD51 (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). TAp63α (hereafter p63) a key transcription factor responsible for eliminating oocytes with unrepaired DSBs, was also elevated in Nemp1-/- neonatal ovaries (P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Figs. 9a, b and 11a).

Pharmacological inhibition of ATM and CHK2 rescues oocyte loss in Nemp1 mutants

To assess the role of ATM in Nemp1-/- in mice, we cultured WT and Nemp1-/- neonatal ovaries with an ATM inhibitor (KU55933; labeled ATMi). Pharmacological inhibition of ATM for 48 h prevented germ cell loss (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3a, b), as follicle number in ATMi-treated ovaries was comparable to that of WT ovaries. Importantly addition of ATMi reduced the pATM signal in treated ovaries (Supplementary Fig. 9g), validating the antibody. As anticipated, γH2AX was reduced in ATMi-treated Nemp1-/- ovaries (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3a, c. Expression of p63 was also reduced upon ATMi treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3a, d), while DMSO treatment had no effect on p63 expression (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c).

CHK2 inhibition (by CHK2i) also resulted in rescue of primordial follicle number in Nemp1-/- ovaries (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3g). γH2AX also decreased in CHK2i-treated Nemp1-/- ovaries (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3f, h). p63, a well-established CHK2 target in oocytes4, activates cell death, and is responsible for elimination of oocytes in primordial follicles with double-strand DNA breaks (DSBs). p63 expression was strongly reduced in the CHK2i-treated Nemp1-/- ovaries (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3f, i). These data indicate that CHK2 is activated in Nemp1-/- and that inhibition of CHK2 or its target p63 can rescue oocyte loss. In contrast, ATR or CHK1 inhibitors had no impact on Nemp1-/- primordial follicle numbers (Supplementary Fig. 12a). CHK2 can also activate p53 in oocytes, depending on the cause of DNA damage26,27. Irradiation of WT ovaries confirmed induction of p53 expression, as previously described4. However, we could not find any evidence of p53 protein increase in Nemp1-/- ovaries (Supplementary Fig. 12e). The p63 transcriptional targets Noxa (Pmaip1) and Puma (Bbc3) induce oocyte apoptosis28. Nemp1-/- P2 ovaries showed elevated expression of Noxa (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3e), but Puma transcript was undetectable.

Genetic inactivation of Chk2 and its target TAp63 prevents oocyte loss in Nemp1-/- mice

Our data indicate that pharmacological inhibition of ATM or CHK2 can suppress oocyte loss in Nemp1-/-. However, inhibitors can have off-target effects. Therefore, we generated Nemp1 Chk2 double knockout mice (Nemp1-/-;Chk2-/-). Importantly, genetic ablation of Chk2 in Nemp1-/- mutants partially rescued primordial follicle number (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3j). We note that loss of Chk2 alone leads to a small increase in the primordial follicle pool; however, follicle number was doubled in Nemp1-/-;Chk2-/-mutants. These data indicate that loss of Chk2 suppresses oocyte loss caused by Nemp1 deficiency.

We also examined the requirement of the CHK2 target p63 using oocyte-specific loss of p63 (Nemp1-/-;TAp63fl/fl ZP3-Cre). Inactivation of p63 in Nemp1-/- mice partially rescued the primordial follicle pool (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3j). Notably, the primordial follicle pool is not affected by inactivation of p63 alone (Supplementary Fig. 13a), consistent with previously published work29. Therefore, loss of p63 rescues oocyte loss caused by Nemp1 deficiency. Together, these results indicate that loss of Nemp1 activates a ATM-CHK2-p63 checkpoint to promote the elimination of defective oocytes.

Nemp1 deficient females ovulate oocytes with spindle defects12. To determine if loss of Chk2 can rescue the spindle defects of Nemp1 mutants, we performed acetylated tubulin staining on ovulated MII oocytes. CHK2 inactivation partially inhibited Nemp1 spindle defects and chromosome misalignment (Supplementary Fig. 13c) and reduced DNA damage as assessed by percent of DNA in the tail in comet assays (Supplementary Fig. 13d, e). In contrast, loss of p63 did not rescue spindle defects or chromosome misalignment, but partially reduced DNA comets (Supplementary Fig. 13d, e), suggesting that some DNA repair occurred. However, neither loss of Chk2 nor of p63 restored fertility in Nemp1-/- females (Supplementary Fig. 13f).

Nemp1-/- fetal oocytes show increased DNA damage

To understand if elimination of oocytes in Nemp1-/- mice is related to defects in pairing, synapsis or recombination checkpoint activation, we analyzed chromosome spreads in oocytes at E15.5 and E17.5. Meiotic prophase progression is defined by two features: the synapsis of homologous chromosomes and initiation of meiotic recombination by DNA double-strand break formation30,31. We defined meiotic prophase I substages using the cytological appearance of the synaptonemal complex proteins (SYCP1 and SYCP3)32,33. Leptonema is the first substage and follows pre-meiotic DNA replication that results in the sister chromatids being physically linked together via the cohesin complex34. Later, during zygonema, homologous chromosomes pair and begin to synapse as the central element proteins of the synaptonemal complex promote this process. Finally, once the homologous chromosomes are condensed and synapsed, cells in pachynema proceed to the last steps of repairing the programmed DSBs31.

We first analyzed DNA DSBs formation using γH2AX. As expected, in wildtype oocytes there is strong γH2AX during leptotene and zygotene stages, which decreases as DSBs are resolved in pachytene. However, in Nemp1-/-, ~54% of oocytes at late pachytene showed persistent γH2AX signal (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4a, b), compared to ~23% in wildtype. γH2AX staining was associated with both synapsed and asynapsed bivalents.

To understand if the repair of chromosomes in Nemp1-/- oocytes proceeds normally, we evaluated the spatial-temporal distribution of repair proteins. First, we evaluated the presence of single-stranded DNA from leptonema to pachynema, using replication protein A (RPA); we observed that the RPA foci number was not significantly different at leptonema. However, at zygonema Nemp1-/- oocytes showed a significant increase of RPA foci per nucleus, compared to controls (Fig. 4c, d). At pachynema, RPA foci declined in both genotypes, but Nemp1-/- retained more RPA foci than wildtype (Control 5.48 ± 0.64 vs. Nemp1-/- 28.09 ± 1.37) (Fig. 4c, d). Next, we analyzed if Nemp1 loss affects DNA damage and repair by staining for RAD51. As with RPA, no differences were observed at leptonema. However, at zygonema and pachynema, Nemp1 mutants showed a significant increase of RAD51 foci (zygonema: control 126.8 ± 5.33 vs. Nemp1-/- 145.7 ± 6.03; pachynema: control 5.43 ± 0.48 vs. Nemp1-/- 28.48 ± 1.18) (Fig. 4e, f). Taken together these data indicate that loss of Nemp1 leads to persistent DNA damage and potentially defects in recombination.

Nemp1 mutants are defective in synapsis and recombination

Analysis of chromosome spreads at P0 showed that a majority of Nemp1-/- oocytes (~60%) were in late-pachytene stage compared to only 35% in wildtype (P < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 14a) suggesting a delay in meiosis I. We next evaluated the progression of meiotic synapsis. We stained nuclear spreads using antibodies against synaptonemal complex proteins SYCP3 and SYCP1, which recognize synapsing chromosomes. We also stained with antibodies against HORMAD1, which is retained on unsynapsed regions of prophase chromosomes. Analysis of embryonic meiotic spreads revealed that ~50% of Nemp1-/- oocytes display asynapsis compared to ~20% in WT. We observed defects in synapsis characterized by both retention of HORMAD1 and lack of SYCP1 on chromosomes of pachytene-like cells (Fig. 5a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 14b, c).

We next evaluated formation of crossovers using MLH1 (MutL protein homolog 1). In Nemp1-/- oocytes, MLH1 average foci number decreased to 18.74 ± 7.018 foci (Fig. 5c, d), compared to WT (23.13 ± 0.22). Interestingly, the number of MLH1 foci varied in Nemp1-/- oocytes: with ~13% of oocytes completely lacking MLH1, while others had normal numbers (~18% of oocytes). We also performed metaphase-I chromosome spreads of in vitro matured oocytes to analyze chromosome abnormalities and chiasmata distribution. Nemp1-/- oocytes showed reduced chiasmata (21.79 ± 0.64 vs. 24.03 ± 0.36 in WT oocytes), accompanied by aberrant chiasmata, and chromosome fragmentation (Fig. 5e, f). Taken together these data indicate that NEMP1 has a crucial role in meiotic prophase, and absence of Nemp1 leads to defects in pairing, synapsis and repair, providing an explanation for the loss of oocytes.

Discussion

Our studies have identified the nuclear envelope membrane protein Nemp1 as a new player in mammalian female meiosis (Fig. 6). We show here that NEMP1 is required for chromosomal synapsis and recombination, and that loss of Nemp1 activates the ATM-CHK2 DNA Damage Response pathway. Importantly, inhibition of ATM or CHK2 can rescue oocyte loss in nemp flies and Nemp1-/- mice.

Graphical summary of Nemp1 deficiency triggered oocyte loss.

Loss of nemp/Nemp1 in oocytes leads to excessive DNA damage, which activates the ATM-mediated CHK2 checkpoint in both flies and mice. In mice, this activation further triggers p63, resulting in expression of Noxa and apoptosis. Inhibition of ATM or CHK2, as well as genetic inactivation of Chk2 or p63, prevents oocyte loss in Nemp1 mutant ovaries. This demonstrates that the loss of Nemp1 alone is sufficient to activate a conserved checkpoint pathway, emphasizing its crucial role in the nuclear envelope of germ cells for maintaining genome integrity. The canonical ATM-CHK2 checkpoint pathway is mainly activated by DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), whereas the ATR-CHK1 checkpoint responds to DNA replication defects, nuclear envelope mechanical stresses and other forms of DNA damage35. Our RNAi screens in Drosophila showed that loss of ATM and chk2 could rescue egg laying in nemp deficient females; however, loss of ATR and chk1 did not. Similarly, in Nemp1-/- mouse germ cells, activation of ATM and CHK2 at E15.5 was followed by dramatic loss of oocytes. Small molecule inhibition of ATM or CHK2 in Nemp1-/- cultured ovaries in vitro reduced DNA damage and prevented primordial follicle loss. In contrast, inhibition of CHK1 or ATR in ovarian culture did not prevent oocyte loss. These data point to a conserved response of the female germline to nemp/Nemp1 deficiency via activation of ATM-CHK2.

An important interacting partner of NEMP1, relevant to germ cell biology, is the LEM domain protein Emerin. In the female Drosophila germline, both nuclear lamina and DNA damage can trigger the loss of germline stem cells15,36. While nemp flies trigger oocyte loss by activation of the canonical ATM-CHK2 DDR pathway, emerin mutant germline stem cells can be rescued by loss of ATR or chk2. Loss of Emerin leads to nuclear lamina deformation, and centrosome maintenance issues, although emerin-/- germ cells have some DNA damage37.

Murine oocytes rapidly undergo meiotic events between E15 and E18, and perturbations during this period can lead to massive oocyte death via the DDR pathway4,5,7–9,38. We did not detect any evidence of premeiotic DNA damage by staining for γH2AX at E12.5 (Fig. S15a, b). Nemp1-/- perinatal oocytes possess elevated DNA damage as marked by γH2AX staining, increased RAD51, activated Caspase 3 and increased TUNEL staining. This increased level of DNA damage in Nemp1-/- oocytes could be caused by defects in DNA repair or alternatively by increased DNA damage caused by asynapsis or mechanical injury due to NE weakness.

CHK2, a downstream effector of ATM, is activated in the presence of unrepaired DSBs and plays a crucial role in oocyte death due to persistent DSBs or asynapsis of homologous chromosomes4,7. Interestingly, loss of Chk2 or p63 only partially rescues primordial oocytes lacking Nemp1. Recent data shows that NEMP1 has a critical function in protecting mouse oocytes from mechanical stresses, and a partial rescue of the ovarian reserve in Nemp1 mutants can be achieved by softening the ovarian cortex via reducing collagen cross-linking14. These data suggest that complete rescue of the ovarian reserve might be obtained by dual inhibition of the ATM-CHK2 pathway combined with softening of the ovarian environment.

Meiotic defects detected by CHK2 in oocytes trigger activation of the downstream p53/p63 pathway, followed by apoptosis4,7,28. Under normal conditions p63 is highly expressed in oocytes of primordial follicles39, where it exists predominantly in an inactive state40,41. DNA damage caused by irradiation or cyclophosphamide can induce activation of p63, and its eventual degradation42,43. In contrast, DNA damage by alkylating agents induces oocyte loss via stabilizing p63, increasing p63 protein levels44. We find that p63 protein levels increase in Nemp1-/- ovaries without substantial changes in transcript (Supplementary Fig. 12b–d). ATM inhibition dramatically decreased γH2aX, but only marginally decreased p63. In contrast, p63 declined more substantially when CHK2 was inhibited. We speculate that the reason for this difference is that CHK2 can be activated by other kinases than ATM upon loss of Nemp1. For example, DNA damage kinase (DNA-PKcs) can activate CHK245. While we have excluded activation of ATR upon loss of Nemp1, we have not examined the role of DNA-PKcs. Future experiments are needed to determine if inhibition of DNA-PKcs can account for this difference and to uncover the mechanism of stabilization of p63 in Nemp1-/- oocytes.

We observed a range of chromosomal abnormalities in Nemp1-/- oocytes during meiotic prophase I, including incomplete synapsis of homologous chromosomes, defective recombination, persistent γH2AX hyperphosphorylation and delay in meiotic progression. In mice, oocyte elimination is regulated by several quality control checkpoints. The synapsis checkpoint eliminates oocytes with widespread asynapsis, even in the absence of programmed DSBs, as observed in Spo11−/− females38,46–48. Meiotic silencing of genes on asynapsed chromosome can cause oocyte elimination when a small number of chromosomes fail to synapse and become silent49. These processes involve HORMAD1 and HORMAD2 proteins7,46,47,50,51, which persist in Nemp1-/- oocytes. Oocytes with faulty recombination repair, such as Dmc1−/− or Msh5−/− mutants, also show chromosome asynapsis and DNA damage, leading to elimination through the combined action of the synapsis and DNA-damage checkpoints28,52–55.

Because loss of Drosophila nemp leads to loss of germ cells prior to meiosis12, we were unable to assess a role for Drosophila nemp in synapsis or recombination. Thus, although nemp and Nemp1 share conserved functions in fertility and in regulation of an ATM-CHK2 checkpoint, whether they share a conserved function in synapsis and pairing is still unclear.

Why does loss of Nemp1 lead to synapsis and pairing defects, DNA damage and fetal oocyte death? NEMP1 is an inner nuclear membrane protein, and its absence leads to a softer nuclear envelope12. This softness may hamper telomere-led chromosome movements that occur at the nuclear envelope, impacting chromosome pairing56,57. NEMP1 forms a complex with the KASH domain of Nesprins14, which may provide a mechanical linkage needed for telomere movement and chromosome clustering during meiosis.

In addition, the pull exerted on the chromosome ends through the nuclear envelope is thought to reinforce establishment of the synaptonemal complex between homologous chromosomes and block nonhomologous synapsis58. Chromosome movements can also have a role in DNA repair by promoting the use of homologous recombination rather than the error-prone NHEJ pathway to repair DNA damage that forms during meiotic prophase. Future studies are needed to determine if NEMP1/nemp functions in DNA repair at the nuclear envelope as well as preventing DNA damage by facilitating chromosome synapsis or pairing.

Taken together, these data reveal NEMP1 as a critically important regulator of meiosis, and that Nemp1 loss leads to DNA damage, activation of the ATM-CHK2 pathway, asynapsis, and severely compromised genome integrity.

Methods

Drosophila melanogaster lines

WT flies are w1118 unless otherwise noted. RNA interference (RNAi) stocks for expression of double-stranded RNA under control of GAL4/UAS were obtained from Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (BDSC); nemp BDSC_57475, chk2 BDSC_35152, ATM BDSC_44417, chk1 BDSC_62155, p53 BDSC_41638, dcp1 BDSC_38315, decay BDSC_65879, dredd BDSC_34070, drice BDSC_32403, damm BDSC_63622, strica BDSC_54059, dronc BDSC_32963 or the Vienna Drosophila Resource Center (VDRC); ATR VDRC_110076. RNAi expression was driven using Nanos Gal4-VP16.

Mice

All mouse animal experiments were conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) office under protocol #24-0170 at Washington University in Saint Louis or in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC) guidelines for the Use of Animals in Research and Laboratory Animal Care, under protocol #AUP29-0016H, approved by the animal care committees of The Center for Phenogenomics, in the Lunenfeld Tanenbaum Research Institute, Toronto. All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free condition in a controlled environment with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Nemp1 mutant mice (Nemp1em#(TCP)McNeill) were generated as previously described12. Chk2 mutant mice (Chek2tm1b(EUCOMM)Hmgu) were obtained from Dr. Ewelina Bolcun-Filas and were genotyped as previously described26. Nemp1-/-;Chk2-/- mice were generated by crossing heterozygous Nemp1 females with homozygous Chk2 male mice. Conditional inactivation of TAp63 (Trp63tm1Elrf) on Nemp1 deficient background was achieved by introduction of an oocyte-specific Cre line (Zp3-cre,3Mrt). This Cre line is expressed in oocytes of primordial follicles with activity detected as early as day 17.5 dpc59. For embryonic experiments, females were set up in breeding and examined for copulation plugs daily. The plugging day was considered 0.5 days post coitum (dpc). Dams were sacrificed at 12.5, 15.5, 17.5, and 18.5 dpc.

Immunostaining - adult Drosophila ovaries

3-day old adult Drosophila ovaries were dissected in PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBT (0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS), blocked with PBT-BSA (PBT with 0.5% bovine serum albumin), incubated with Dcp1 Rabbit primary antibody (NEB Cat #9578S) in PBT-BSA at 4 C overnight, washed with PBT, incubated with FITC Rabbit secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Cat # 711-095-152) in PBT-BSA for 1 h along with Rhodamine-phalloidin (ThermoFisher Cat #R415), washed with PBT and mounted in fresh Vectashield with DAPI (VectorLabs Cat #H-1200-10). Imaging was done with a Nikon A1R confocal microscope. Images represent individual optical sections.

Follicle counting

Histomorphometric assessment of ovarian reserve at various timepoints was performed by systematic follicle counting of serial sections covering the entire ovary as previously described59. One ovary per animal was fixed in Dietrich’s fixative, paraffin embedded, sectioned at 5 μm and counterstained with Weigert’s Iron Hematoxylin/Picric Acid Methyl Green stain. Only follicles containing oocytes with clearly visible nuclei were included and follicle counts were expressed as the sum of follicles identified across all sections, multiplied by the number of sections examined, thereby representing the total ovarian follicle pool. Every third section was counted for day 2 and day 4 ovaries. In this case, the number of follicles identified was multiplied by 3. Every 5th section was counted for day 10 and every 10th section was counted for 4 weeks old animals. The total number of follicles at each stage was multiplied by 5 and 10, respectively.

Immunofluorescence of mouse ovaries

Whole ovaries harvested from embryos and neonates were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 h. For frozen sections, ovaries were allowed to equilibrate overnight in 30% sucrose/PBS at 4 °C. Ovary sections (20 μm) were collected on Superfrost Plus microscope slides (Fisher Scientific) from optical cutting temperature (OCT) embedded blocks using a cryostat (CM 1850, Leica). For paraffin sections ovaries were processed through routine embedding and sectioned at 5 μm. Before immunostaining, antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer was performed by heating sections. Afterward, sections were rinsed in PBS, permeabilized, and blocked with 10% donkey serum/0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h followed by an overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Primary antibody details are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Hoechst/DAPI and appropriate Alexa 488/594/647–conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to the blocking buffer for 2 h after rinsing in PBS. Finally, the sections were mounted with Dako fluorescence mounting medium (Agilent Technologies), and images were taken on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted confocal laser microscope (Nikon) with the NIS-Elements software using a 60× objective. For image quantitation of paraffin sections, groups were stained at the same time and imaged using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (Leica DMI6000B, Quorum Technologies) or BC43 Benchtop Confocal microscope (Oxford Instruments, Belfast, UK). All images comparing different experimental groups were acquired with the same exposure, laser power and camera settings to ensure quantitative comparison. The mean fluorescence intensity was measured using Volocity version 5.2.2 (Quorum Technologies) and displayed as arbitrary units (A.U.).

TUNEL immunofluorescence on cryosections

TUNEL labeling was performed on 20 µm cryosections to detect cell death with an in situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche, Cat #12156792910). For each animal, a single ovary was analyzed and the whole ovary was sectioned. Fixed ovary cryosections were rinsed in PBS and permeabilized with PBT (0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 20 min, again rinsed in PBS, incubated with 50 μl of TUNEL reaction mixture with enzyme solution in a humid chamber for 1 h at 37 °C. After rinsing the sections 3x with PBS, nuclei were stained with Hoechst for 20 min, rinsed again 3x in PBS, and mounted with Dako fluorescence mounting medium (Agilent Technologies). Cryosections digested with DNase I (Invitrogen) served as positive control whereas negative control sections were incubated without the TUNEL enzyme solution. Images were acquired as 5 μm Z-stacks with a step size of 1 μm on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted confocal laser microscope (Nikon). Non-overlapping oocytes were counted within each 5 μm Z-stack of every section and an average of 16–18 sections were counted per ovary. The percentage of TUNEL-positive oocytes was calculated as the number of oocytes with positive signals divided by the total number of oocytes.

Preparation and immunofluorescence of oocyte spreads

Female meiotic surface spreads of prophase I oocytes were prepared from two ovaries of each mouse as described previously48. Briefly, after dissection, ovaries were placed in 20 μl hypotonic extraction buffer containing 30 mM Tris-HCl, 17 mM Trisodium citrate dihydrate, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM sucrose, 0.5 mM PMSF, 0.5 mM DTT, 1x Protease Inhibitor Cocktail for 15 min at room temperature. Further ovaries were placed in 100 mM sucrose in 5 mM sodium borate buffer pH 8.5 and punctured by 29-gauge syringe needles to release oocytes. The cell suspension was incubated with 65 mM sucrose in 5 mM sodium borate buffer pH 8.5 for 3 min. The cell suspension was distributed into four 20 µL fixative drops (1% paraformaldehyde, 0.15% Triton X-100, 1 mM borate buffer, pH 9.2) on two glass slides enclosed by a hydrophobic barrier. Slides were incubated for 45 min in a humid chamber and then air dried. Further slides were washed in 0.4% Photo-Flo 200 solution, rinsed with distilled water, air dried again, and stored at −80 °C until staining. Chiasmata spreads of oocytes during meiotic maturation were prepared from fully grown oocytes collected in M2 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 1 µM milrinone (Sigma-Aldrich). Oocytes were matured in M16 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2 and oocytes that undergone GVBD after 90 min were selected and further cultured for 5 h. Zona pellucida-free oocytes were washed in M2 medium for 1 min twice, placed in a drop of fixative (1% paraformaldehyde, 0.15% Triton X-100, 3 mM dithiothreitol, pH 9.2) and then slowly dried. For immunostaining, slides were brought to RT and washed with 10% blocking buffer (3%BSA/10% goat serum/0.05% Tween-20) in PBS for 10 min. Slides were blocked for 1 h with blocking buffer followed by overnight primary antibodies incubation at 4 °C. Secondary antibody incubation was carried out for 1.5 h at RT. After washing, slides were mounted with Vectashield plus Antifade mounting medium with DAPI (VectorLabs Cat #H-2000-10).

Image acquisition was carried out with a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) for all the fetal spreads except the Chiasmata spreads of MI oocytes. that were acquired on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted confocal laser microscope (Nikon). Images were processed using Zen 2 software (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). The intensity and exposure time for each protein analyzed was set identically for wild-type and mutant oocytes. Focus counts, γH2AX, and other proteins were manually quantified by at least two individuals using a Photoshop counting tool, and the results were averaged prior to analysis. Variations between individuals were less than 5 percent.

Whole mount staining of ovaries and optical clearing

Ovaries were harvested and fixed immediately in 4% PFA for 24 h at RT and dehydrated in 30% sucrose for 12 h at RT. Ovaries with the same genotype were transferred into a bottle containing a blocking solution (10% donkey serum/1% Triton X-100) in PBS and incubated overnight. Next, 1ug primary antibodies were added to the bottle and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Ovaries were washed 5x in 2 ml PBS for 30 min each at 37 °C and transferred into a new bottle with secondary antibodies containing blocking solution for 24 h at 37 °C. Repeat washing as mentioned above was performed and ovaries were subjected to optical clearing into a new bottle containing 5 ml CUBIC-reagent-160 and incubated for 2 days. After washing 5x as above, ovaries were transferred into a new bottle containing 1 ml 50% of CUBIC reagent-2 and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Then ovaries were transferred into a new bottle containing 5 ml 100% of CUBIC reagent-2 and incubated for 1 hat 37 °C. Next, the ovaries were transferred to an imaging dish, and images were acquired on a Ti2 inverted confocal laser microscope (Nikon) with a 10× objective. Images were collected as Z-stacks with a step size of 3 μm. Oocytes were counted using markers Tra98 and DDX4. Imaris v 6.4 was used for image processing, and a spot-transformation algorithm was used to identify each oocyte for oocyte counting61.

Neonatal ovary cultures

Neonatal day 2 ovaries were collected and cultured on liquid/air interface using Waymouth’s MB752/1 medium (Gibco) supplemented with pyruvate, penicillin streptomycin and 10% FBS (Wisent) as previously described62 for 48 h with vehicle (DMSO, Sigma) or inhibitors (see Supplementary Table). After this period, tissue was fixed and processed either for immunohistochemical or histomorphometric analysis of paired ovaries.

Oocyte collection and spindle staging

Females were superovulated by giving 5 IU of pregnant mare’s serum gonadotropin (ProSpec) followed 48 h later by 5 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (Merck) intraperitoneally. Cumulus-oocyte complexes were recovered from oviducts of primed females 14 to 16 h after hCG injection. Oocytes were denuded from cumulus cells by brief incubation in hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for immunostaining with acetylated tubulin antibody.

Comet assay

Ovulated oocytes were used for analysis of DNA integrity by comet assay. To detect DNA breaks, the comet assay was performed under neutral conditions using Single cell Comet Assay Electrophoresis (R&D Systems) following manufacturer’s instructions. Comets were analyzed by two independent investigators using ImageJ, and data are shown as percent DNA in tail.

Fertility tests

Nemp1-/- and Nemp1-/-; Chk2-/- females of 6–8 weeks old were crossed to 8–12 week-old males with proven fertility. The reproductive activity of females was monitored for up to 6 months from the day of mating by recording the number of litters and pups delivered by each female.

Western blotting

Postnatal day 4 (P4) ovaries from WT, Nemp1-/-, and WT IRR mice were collected and pooled (six ovaries from three pups per biological replicate). For irradiation controls, P4 ovaries from C57BL/6 WT pups were exposed to a single 1 Gy (100 Rad) dose 4 h prior to collection. Tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (cOmplete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche, Cat# 11836170001, PhosSTOP, Roche, Cat# 4908645001). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Pierce). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST and incubated with primary antibodies in 1% milk overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were detected using UltraScence Western Substrate (FroggaBio).

Real-Time PCR

RNA was extracted from 3 pairs of gonads or neonatal ovaries of the same genotype using TRI Reagent (ThermoFisher). Obtained RNA pellet was dissolved in nuclease free water and any residual DNA was removed by brief digestion with RNAse free DNAse (Sigma) and reverse transcription was performed in a volume of 20 μl using Advanced cDNA Synthesis kit (Wisent). Subsequently 1 μl of RT was used in a real-time PCR reaction using Advanced SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Wisent) in CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Biorad). Samples were run in triplicates and analysis was done using delta/delta CT method. Beta actin was used to pre-screen the sample quality. However, DDX4 was used for normalization, as Nemp1-/- ovaries contain fewer germ cells and targets analysed are known to be expressed by oocytes. Bbc3: Forward: ATGCCTGCCTCACCTTCATCT/ Reverse: AGCACAGGATTCACAGTCTGGA; Pmaip1: Forward: ACTGTGGTTCTGGCGCAGAT/ Reverse: TTGAGCACACTCGTCCTTCAA; Ddx4: Forward: TGGCAGAGCGATTTCTTTTT/ Reverse: CGCTGTATTCAACGTGTGCT, Line1: Forward: ATGGCGAAAGGTAAACGGAG/ Reverse: AGTCCTTCTTGATGTCCTCT and p63: Forward: TGTATCCGCATGCAGGACT/ Reverse: CTGTGTTATAGGGACTGGTGGA.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical procedures were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0.621, and data were presented as means ± SEM. For comparisons of two unpaired samples, significance was determined by two tailed student t-test or Mann-Whitney nonparametric test. For two paired samples, significance was determined by two tailed paired t-test or Wilcoxon nonparametric test. To analyze the difference between more than two independent groups, statistical analysis was performed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the significance was determined by Tukey’s multiple comparison test or Kruskal-Wallis for nonparametric data with Dunn’s multiple-comparison test. The results were considered significant if the P value was less than 0.05.
