Abstract

1.
Introduction

In
[
1
]
, N. J. Laustsen and V. G. Troitsky
characterized the Archimedean vector lattices that admit a positively
homogeneous function calculus. More precisely, they showed that if
X

is an Archimedean vector lattice, and
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then the following are equivalent:

•

There is a vector lattice homomorphism
\Phi_{\bm{x}}\colon H_{n}\to X
with
\Phi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
H_{n}
denotes the vector lattice of positively
homogeneous continuous functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\pi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the
i
-th coordinate projection.

•

There exists
f\in X
such that
f\geq|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
and the norm

\|x\|_{f}=\inf\{\,\lambda\in[0,\infty):|x|\leq\lambda f\,\},

defined on the order ideal
I_{f}
generated by
f
, is complete
when restricted to the closed sublattice generated by

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in
I_{f}
.

The goal of this paper is to characterize, in a similar vein, the
Archimedean lattice-ordered algebras with identity that admit a
polynomial growth continuous function calculus. More precisely, if
X

is a lattice-ordered algebra with identity
1_{X}
, denote by

PG_{n}
the lattice-ordered algebra formed by the continuous
functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for which there exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
and

M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
|f|\leq M(\mathbb{1}+|\pi_{1}|+\cdots+|\pi_{n}|)^{N}
(where
\mathbb{1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the function that
is identically one). Then the following are equivalent:

•

There is a lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}\to X
with
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\mathbb{1})=1_{X}
and
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

•

There exist
f\geq 1_{X}\vee|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
and
an
f\!
-subalgebra
Y
of
X
such that
1_{X},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in Y

and, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{f^{m}}
is
complete on
Y\cap I_{f^{m}}
.

Compared to the Archimedean vector lattice setting, the condition at
hand is notably more intricate. The extra algebraic layer (and the
passage to algebra homomorphisms) is not really what drives this added
complexity; the real source is that
PG_{n}
has a richer internal
structure than
H_{n}
. Recall that
H_{n}
is an AM-space with unit, a
feature that keeps the corresponding conditions tidy. While
PG_{n}

itself fails to be an AM-space with unit, it does decompose as a
countable union of such spaces. Concretely, setting
d=\mathbb{1}\vee|\pi_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|\pi_{n}|
, one has

PG_{n}=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}I_{d^{m}},\qquad\text{with}\qquad I_{d^{m}}\subseteq I_{d^{m+1}}.

This particular structure will be central in proving that the two
conditions above are equivalent. The appearance of an
f\!
-subalgebra in the
condition is also expected, since
f\!
-algebras are the only lattice-ordered
algebras admitting a polynomial growth continuous function calculus
(
2.4
).

As noted in
[
1
]
,
positively homogeneous continuous function calculus plays a
fundamental role in the theory of Banach lattices. This form of
function calculus was first introduced by Yudin
[
2
]
and
Krivine
[
3
]
for
Banach lattices, and was subsequently generalized to uniformly
complete Archimedean vector lattices in a seminal paper by Buskes, de
Pagter and van Rooij
[
4
]
. In that work, the authors deal not only with
uniformly complete Archimedean vector lattices, but also with
uniformly complete Archimedean
f\!
-algebras. It is in the latter context
that the existence of a polynomial growth continuous function calculus
is established. Hence, just as Laustsen and Troitsky did for Archimedean
vector lattices, it seems relevant to provide a useful characterization
of the Archimedean lattice-ordered algebras that admit such a function
calculus.

Section

2
provides two further arguments to justify why
polynomial growth continuous function calculus is the natural function
calculus to consider in lattice-ordered algebras. The first shows that
this function calculus is the natural extension of positively
homogeneous continuous function calculus on vector lattices. The
second relies on viewing function calculus through the lens of free
objects. Although the latter is an approach certainly known and used
by experts, we have not found it in the literature. As part of this
approach, in
2.8
we characterize the free object
generated by finitely many elements in the category of uniformly
complete Archimedean
f\!
-algebras.

Section

3
contains the main result of this paper,

3.1
, which provides the equivalent conditions
described at the beginning of this introduction.
As a consequence, we obtain in
3.7
a
characterization of the Archimedean lattice-ordered algebras with
identity admitting a polynomial growth continuous function calculus.
We also use examples to illustrate the different ways in which a
lattice-ordered algebra may fail to admit such a function
calculus.

Finally, in
Section

4
, we prove that if a vector space admits a
sufficiently strong polynomial growth continuous function
calculus (without constants), then it is automatically a commutative
f\!
-algebra. This result is
an analogue of
[
1
, Theorem 1.4]
.

1.1.
Preliminaries and notation

For the unexplained terminology regarding vector and Banach lattices,
we refer the reader to the classical monographs

[
5
,
6
]
. An accessible
introduction to lattice-ordered algebras and
f\!
-algebras can be found in

[
7
]
. We will refer to the subspaces of a lattice-ordered algebra that
are at the same time a vector sublattice and a subalgebra as

sublattice-algebras
. When a sublattice-algebra is itself an

f\!
-algebra, we will call it an
f\!
-subalgebra
.

All the vector lattices considered in this paper are assumed to be
Archimedean. Let
X
be such a vector lattice. That a net

(x_{\alpha})\subseteq X
converges uniformly to
x
with regulator
e\in X_{+}
will be denoted by
x_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\rm u}x\;(e)
. We denote by

I_{e}
the order ideal generated by
e
, and by
\|{\cdot}\|_{e}
the
associated norm. An Archimedean vector lattice is
uniformly
complete
if
(I_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})
is norm complete for every
e\in X_{+}
; in keeping with the convention above, we assume throughout that
every uniformly complete vector lattice is Archimedean.

2.
Why polynomial growth continuous function
calculus?

The goal of this section is to introduce polynomial growth continuous
function calculus in lattice-ordered algebras with identity and to
justify, in two ways, why it is the natural function calculus to
consider in this category. We also show that every lattice-ordered
algebra having such a function calculus must be an
f\!
-algebra.

2.1.
Polynomial growth continuous function calculus as a
natural extension of positively homogeneous continuous function
calculus

A real-valued function
f
defined on a vector space
X
is said to be

positively homogeneous
if, for every
x\in X
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the identity
f(\lambda x)=\lambda f(x)
holds. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
H_{n}
the
vector lattice of positively homogeneous continuous functions defined
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is standard that
H_{n}
is lattice isomorphic to

C([-1,1]^{n})
, the isomorphism being the map that sends a function to its restriction
to
[-1,1]^{n}
. Pulling up the norm from
C([-1,1]^{n})
,
H_{n}

becomes a Banach lattice. In particular,
H_{n}
is uniformly complete.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the
i
-th coordinate projection

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\pi_{i}\colon&\mathbb{R}^{n}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})&\longmapsto&t_{i}\\
\end{array}

belongs to
H_{n}
. We shall denote the
i
-th coordinate
projection by
\pi_{i}
regardless of which
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is its domain. This
abuse in notation is justified if we think of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the first

n
coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and of
\pi_{i}
as the restriction to

\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the
i
-th coordinate projection in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Buskes, de Pagter and van Rooij showed in
[
4
, Theorem 3.7
]
that every uniformly complete
Archimedean vector lattice admits a positively homogeneous continuous
function calculus. More precisely, they showed that if
X
is a
uniformly complete Archimedean vector lattice, and
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then there exists a unique lattice
homomorphism
\Phi_{\bm{x}}\colon H_{n}\to X
satisfying
\Phi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for

i=1,\ldots,n
.

What is the natural extension of this function calculus to
lattice-ordered algebras? Suppose now that
X
is an
Archimedean uniformly complete lattice-ordered algebra.
Let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
and let
\Phi_{\bm{x}}\colon H_{n}\to X

be the unique lattice homomorphism introduced above. If we want to do
function calculus in lattice-ordered algebras, the least we should ask
is for the map to be a lattice-algebra homomorphism. But
H_{n}
is not an
algebra. Still, we can view
H_{n}
as a sublattice of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, the
space of all functions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with pointwise linear
and lattice structures. This space, when also equipped with the pointwise
product, is a lattice-ordered algebra, with identity the constant one
function
\mathbb{1}
. The weakest extension of the positively homogeneous continuous
function calculus to the algebraic setting would be a
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon\operatorname{VLA}(H_{n})\to X
, where

\operatorname{VLA}(H_{n})
is the smallest sublattice-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

containing
H_{n}
.
Since
X
is uniformly complete, we automatically obtain something stronger:

\Psi_{\bm{x}}
extends uniquely to a lattice-algebra homomorphism
on the smallest uniformly complete sublattice-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

that contains
H_{n}
(this is proved in
2.7
). Thus the
question becomes: can we identify the smallest uniformly complete
sublattice-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
that contains
H_{n}
?

A function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to have
polynomial growth
if there exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

|f|\leq M(\mathbb{1}+|\pi_{1}|+\cdots+|\pi_{n}|)^{N}.

The lattice-ordered algebra formed by the continuous polynomial growth functions in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
PG_{n}
. Consider the elements
f\in PG_{n}
for
which the limit

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(tx)}{t}

exists for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and is uniform on bounded subsets.
In this case, we denote
f_{h}(x)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}f(tx)t^{-1}
and say that
f_{h}
exists. Observe that
f_{h}\in H_{n}
,
hence the notation. Denote by
PG_{n}^{h}
the set of elements of
PG_{n}

for which
f_{h}
exists.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
4
, Propositions 3.6 and
4.9]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

The uniformly complete vector lattice generated by
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is
H_{n}
.

(ii)

The uniformly complete lattice-ordered algebra generated
by
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is
PG_{n}^{h}
.

(iii)

The uniformly complete lattice-ordered algebra generated
by
\mathbb{1},\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is
PG_{n}
.

We were looking for the smallest uniformly complete lattice-ordered
algebra in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
containing
H_{n}
. According to the previous
theorem, this is precisely
PG_{n}^{h}
.
And the smallest uniformly complete lattice-ordered algebra in

\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
containing
H_{n}
and
\mathbb{1}
is
PG_{n}
. In other words,
the weakest natural extension of the positively homogeneous
continuous function calculus to the algebraic setting is a
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}^{h}\colon PG_{n}^{h}\to X
(or a
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}\to X
satisfying

\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\mathbb{1})=1_{X}
, in the case that

X
has an identity
1_{X}
).

Inspired by
[
1
]
,
the main goal of this paper is to study which unital
lattice-ordered algebras admit a polynomial growth continuous
function calculus, meaning the following:

Definition 2.2
.

A lattice-ordered algebra
X
with identity
1_{X}
admits a
polynomial
growth continuous function calculus
if, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
each
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
, there is a
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}\to X
satisfying

(1)

\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\mathbb{1})=1_{X}\text{ and }\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n.

This is the analogue of
[
1
, Definition 1.1]
,
where the authors defined a vector lattice
that admits a positively homogeneous continuous function calculus
as a vector lattice
X
for which, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
, there is a vector lattice homomorphism

\Phi_{\bm{x}}\colon H_{n}\to X
satisfying
\Phi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Our focus will be on the case with identity, where we are truly dealing with
all functions of polynomial growth. However, the following is a
natural definition that will be useful at times.

Definition 2.3
.

A lattice-ordered algebra
X
admits a
polynomial
growth continuous function calculus without constants
if, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
each
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
, there is a
lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}^{h}\colon PG_{n}^{h}\to X
satisfying

(2)

\Psi_{\bm{x}}^{h}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n.

The following is a routine, but relevant, observation.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a lattice-ordered algebra that admits a polynomial
growth continuous function calculus without constants. Then
X
is a commutative

f\!
-algebra.

Proof.

Let
X
be a lattice-ordered algebra that admits a polynomial
growth continuous function calculus without constants. Let
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in X_{+}
,
and suppose
x_{1}\wedge x_{2}=0
. Let
\Psi\colon PG_{3}^{h}\to X
be a
lattice-algebra homomorphism such that
\Psi(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for

i=1,\ldots,3
. Since
\Psi
is a lattice-algebra homomorphism:

\displaystyle\Psi(|\pi_{1}|-|\pi_{1}|\wedge|\pi_{2}|)

\displaystyle=|x_{1}|-|x_{1}|\wedge|x_{2}|=x_{1},

\displaystyle\Psi(|\pi_{2}|-|\pi_{1}|\wedge|\pi_{2}|)

\displaystyle=|x_{2}|-|x_{1}|\wedge|x_{2}|=x_{2},

\displaystyle\Psi(|\pi_{3}|)

\displaystyle=|x_{3}|=x_{3}.

Since

(|\pi_{1}|-|\pi_{1}|\wedge|\pi_{2}|)\wedge(|\pi_{2}|-|\pi_{1}|\wedge|\pi_{2}|)=0

and
PG_{3}^{h}
is an
f\!
-algebra, it follows that

[|\pi_{3}|(|\pi_{1}|-|\pi_{1}|\wedge|\pi_{2}|)]\wedge(|\pi_{2}|-|\pi_{1}|\wedge|\pi_{2}|)=0.

Applying
\Psi
to the previous equality yields
(x_{3}x_{1})\wedge x_{2}=0
. Using the same idea, it is straightforward to check that
X

is commutative. Hence
X
is a commutative
f\!
-algebra.
∎

The previous proposition can be seen as yet another reason why

f\!
-algebras are particularly well-behaved and relevant among all
lattice-ordered algebras.

To close this circle of ideas, let us note that, just like every
Archimedean uniformly complete vector lattice admits a positively
homogeneous continuous function calculus, every Archimedean uniformly
complete
f\!
-algebra with identity admits a polynomial growth continuous
function calculus
[
4
, Theorem
4.12]
. We shall reprove this in the
next section using a completely different approach.

2.2.
Function calculus through free objects

In this section we show how to approach function calculus through free
objects. This way of understanding function calculus will be useful in
our later proofs, and provides a unified approach to positively
homogeneous and polynomial growth (without constants) continuous function
calculi. Moreover, it further justifies why polynomial growth
continuous function calculus is the natural function calculus to
consider on
f\!
-algebras.

Recall that a
free vector lattice
over a non-empty set
S
is a
pair
(\operatorname{FVL}(S),\delta)
, where
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
is a vector lattice and
\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FVL}(S)
is a map, such that, for every vector lattice
X

and every map
T\colon S\to X
, there exists a unique lattice
homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FVL}(S)\to X
satisfying
\hat{T}\delta=T
.
The existence of this object was first established implicitly in

[
8
]
; it was later constructed and studied in

[
9
,
10
]
.

The free vector lattice over a set
S
is essentially unique and can
be constructed explicitly in the following way: for every
s\in S
, define the point evaluation
\delta_{s}\colon\mathbb{R}^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\delta_{s}(x)=x(s)
; then the free vector lattice is the sublattice generated by

\{\delta_{s}:s\in S\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{S}}
together with the map

\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FVL}(S)
defined by
\delta(s)=\delta_{s}
.

Note that, with this particular realization, every element of
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
is
continuous in the product topology; hence
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
lives inside

C(\mathbb{R}^{S})
. Moreover, every element of
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
is positively
homogeneous; hence
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
lives inside
H(\mathbb{R}^{S})
, the vector
lattice of positively homogeneous continuous functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
.
Finally, restricting the functions from
\mathbb{R}^{S}
to
\Delta_{S}=[-1,1]^{S}
,
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
can be regarded as a vector sublattice of

H(\Delta_{S})
(see
[
11
, Lemma 5.1]
for the
details). Note that the latter space, being a closed sublattice
of
C(\Delta_{S})
, is a Banach lattice.

One can similarly define the free object in the category of uniformly
complete vector lattices. This notion was first considered in

[
12
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
S
be a non-empty set. A
free uniformly complete vector
lattice
over
S
is a pair
(\operatorname{FUCVL}(S),\delta)
, where
\operatorname{FUCVL}(S)

is a uniformly complete vector lattice and
\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FUCVL}(S)
is a map, such that, for every uniformly complete vector
lattice
X
and every map
T\colon S\to X
, there exists a unique
lattice homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FUCVL}(S)\to X
satisfying

T=\hat{T}\circ\delta
.

In
[
12
]
an abstract construction of this
object is provided through the uniform completion (called
r-completion by the authors): a
uniformly complete vector lattice
U
is a
uniform completion
of a
vector lattice
X
if there exists a vector lattice embedding
i\colon X\to U
such that, for each uniformly complete vector lattice
Y
and each
lattice homomorphism
T\colon X\to Y
, there exists a unique lattice
homomorphism
S\colon U\to Y
satisfying
T=S\circ i
. Uniform completions
are unique up to a unique lattice isomorphism. It is known (see

[
12
]
for the details) that, if
X
is a
vector sublattice of
Y
, and
Y
is uniformly complete, then the
uniform completion of
X
is the intersection of all the uniformly
complete vector sublattices of
Y
containing
X
.

By checking the universal properties, it is clear that
\operatorname{FUCVL}(S)

is the uniform completion of
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
. Since
H(\Delta_{S})
is a
Banach lattice, it is a uniformly complete vector lattice, and
therefore
\operatorname{FUCVL}(S)
is the intersection of all the uniformly complete
vector sublattices of
H(\Delta_{S})
that contain
\operatorname{FVL}(S)
. It follows
from
2.1
that, if
S
is finite, then
\operatorname{FUCVL}(S)=H(\Delta_{S})
, which is in turn isomorphic to
H_{|S|}
. Under this
isomorphism, and the identification of
S
with
\{1,\ldots,|S|\}
, the
\delta_{s}
become the projections
\pi_{i}
.
(Note that in this case the free uniformly complete vector lattice
coincides with the free Banach lattice over
S
, see

[
11
,
13
]
).

Now we can establish the relation of this theory with positively
homogeneous continuous function calculus. As usual, let
X
be a uniformly
complete Archimedean vector lattice, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
. The universal property of

\operatorname{FUCVL}(\{1,\ldots,n\})
asserts that there exists a unique lattice
homomorphism

\operatorname{FUCVL}(\{1,\ldots,n\})\to X

sending
\delta_{i}
to
x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. But, as noted above,

\operatorname{FUCVL}(\{1,\ldots,n\})
is essentially
H_{n}
and, with this
identification,
\delta_{i}
becomes
\pi_{i}
. Hence the above map is
the unique lattice homomorphism
\Phi_{\bm{x}}\colon H_{n}\to X
satisfying

\Phi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

So far we have only revisited well-known facts. However, looking back at this
approach, there is nothing preventing us from applying it to

f\!
-algebras. What kind of function calculus will appear in this case? Of
course, it will be the polynomial growth continuous function calculus. The
details follow.

A
free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
over a non-empty set
S
is a pair
(\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S),\delta)
, where
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
is an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra and
\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
is a map, such that, for every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra
A
and every map
T\colon S\to A
, there exists a unique lattice-algebra homomorphism

\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)\to A
satisfying
\hat{T}\delta=T
.
Similarly, a
free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity
over a non-empty set

S
is a pair
(\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S),\delta)
, where
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
is an Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity and
\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
is a map, such that, for every Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity
A
and every map
T\colon S\to A
, there exists a unique lattice-algebra homomorphism

\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)\to A
that sends the identity to the identity and
satisfies
\hat{T}\delta=T
. The
existence of these objects follows from the works

[
8
,
14
]
.

The free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra can be constructed explicitly as
the sublattice-algebra generated by
\{\,\delta_{s}:s\in S\,\}

in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}^{S}}
. Similarly, the free Archimedean
f\!
-algebra with identity can
be identified with the sublattice-algebra generated by
\{\,\delta_{s}:s\in S\,\}\cup\{\mathbb{1}\}
. Note that, with this particular
realization, every element of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
is continuous in the product
topology; hence
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
and
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
live inside

. However, the
parallelism with the free vector lattice ends here. The functions of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)

cannot be restricted to
\Delta_{S}
injectively since they are not,
in general, positively homogeneous: there exist non-zero elements of

\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
that vanish on
\Delta_{S}
.

One can still consider the free object in the category of uniformly
complete
f\!
-algebras.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
S
be a non-empty set. A
free uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra

(resp.,
free uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra with identity
)
over

is a pair
(\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(S),\delta)
(resp.,
(\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S),\delta)
), where
\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(S)
(resp.,
\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
) is a
uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra (resp.,
f\!
-algebra with identity) and

\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(S)
(resp.,
\delta\colon S\to\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
) is a
map, such that, for every uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra (resp.,
f\!
-algebra with
identity)
A
and every
map

, there exists a unique lattice-algebra
homomorphism
\hat{T}\colon\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(S)\to A
satisfying

(resp., and sending the identity to the

A priori, it is not clear at all whether such an object exists. The
following lemma allows for an abstract construction of the free
uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra as the uniform completion of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
Y
be a uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra and let
X
be a
sublattice-algebra of
Y
. Let
\bar{X}
be the intersection of
all the uniformly complete vector sublattices of
Y
containing

X
(i.e.,
\bar{X}
is the uniform completion of
X
). Then

(i)

\bar{X}
is a sublattice-algebra (and therefore an

f\!
-algebra),

(ii)

for every uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra
A
and every
lattice-algebra homomorphism
T\colon X\to A
there exists a
unique extension of
T
to a lattice-algebra homomorphism

\bar{T}\colon\bar{X}\to A
.

Proof.

Item
(i)
is
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
.
To show
(ii)
, let
A
be a uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra and
let
T\colon X\to A
be a lattice-algebra homomorphism. Since
\bar{X}
is
the uniform completion of
X
,
T
extends to a unique lattice
homomorphism
\bar{T}\colon\bar{X}\to A
. The goal is to show
that
\bar{T}
is an algebra homomorphism. Let
x\in Y_{+}
. Since

Y
is an
f\!
-algebra, the left multiplication operator
L_{x}\colon Y\to Y
,
L_{x}(y)=xy
, is a lattice homomorphism. If
x\in X_{+}
, then
we can consider its restriction
L_{x}|_{X}\colon X\to X
. Since

T
is an algebra homomorphism,
T\circ L_{x}|_{X}=L_{Tx}\circ T
. All
the operators in this identity are lattice homomorphisms. Note
that
L_{x}|_{\bar{X}}\colon\bar{X}\to\bar{X}
by item

(i)
, and that
\bar{T}\circ L_{x}|_{\bar{X}}
is a lattice
homomorphism that coincides with
T\circ L_{x}|_{X}
on
X
. By
uniqueness of the extensions,
\bar{T}\circ L_{x}|_{\bar{X}}
is the
extension of
T\circ L_{x}|_{X}
to
\bar{X}
. Similarly,

L_{Tx}\circ\bar{T}
is the extension of

L_{Tx}\circ T
to
\bar{X}
. From
T\circ L_{x}|_{X}=L_{Tx}\circ T
,
it follows that
\bar{T}\circ L_{x}|_{\bar{X}}=L_{Tx}\circ\bar{T}
. Hence, for
every
x\in X_{+}
and

,

\bar{T}(xy)=(\bar{T}\circ L_{x}|_{\bar{X}})(y)=(L_{Tx}\circ\bar{T})(y)=Tx\,\bar{T}y.

By linearity, this identity holds for all
x\in X
. Since the
product is commutative, we can rewrite the previous equality as

\bar{T}\circ L_{y}|_{X}=L_{\bar{T}y}\circ T
, where
L_{y}|_{X}\colon X\to\bar{X}
. If
y\in(\bar{X})_{+}
, then
L_{y}|_{X}
and

L_{\bar{T}y}
are lattice homomorphisms, and by uniqueness of the
extensions
\bar{T}\circ L_{y}|_{\bar{X}}=L_{\bar{T}y}\circ\bar{T}
. By linearity, this last equation holds for all
y\in\bar{X}
. Hence
\bar{T}
is an algebra homomorphism.
∎

The previous lemma implies that
\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(S)
(resp.
\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
) does
exist: it is the uniform completion of
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}(S)
(resp.
\operatorname{FA{\it f}\!A}^{1}(S)
).
The following result identifies these free objects when

is finite.
It follows immediately from the previous observation and

.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
S=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

(i)

The free uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra over

S
is
PG_{n}^{h}
together with the map
\delta\colon S\to PG_{n}^{h}
given by
\delta(i)=\pi_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(ii)

The free uniformly complete
f\!
-algebra with identity over

S
is
PG_{n}
together with the map
\delta\colon S\to PG_{n}
given by
\delta(i)=\pi_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

It is now time to revisit function calculus through the lens of
free objects. Let
X
be a uniformly complete Archimedean
f\!
-algebra, let

n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
. The
universal property of
\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(\{1,\ldots,n\})
asserts that there
exists a unique lattice-algebra homomorphism

\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(\{1,\ldots,n\})\to X

sending
\delta_{i}
to
x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. By previous
proposition,
\operatorname{FUC{\it f}\!A}(\{1,\ldots,n\})
can be identified with

PG_{n}^{h}
, and with this identification

becomes
\pi_{i}
.
Hence the above map is the unique lattice-algebra homomorphism

\Psi_{\bm{x}}^{h}\colon PG_{n}^{h}\to X
satisfying
\Psi_{\bm{x}}^{h}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for

i=1,\ldots,n
. In the case that

has identity
1_{X}
, this map can be
extended to a lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}\to X

satisfying also
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\mathbb{1})=1_{X}
.

Putting these observations together with
2.4
, we have the
following characterization of the Archimedean uniformly complete
lattice-ordered algebras that admit a polynomial growth
continuous function calculus (without constants).

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
X
be an Archimedean uniformly complete lattice-ordered
algebra.

(i)

X
admits a polynomial growth continuous function
calculus without constants if and only if
X
is an
f\!
-algebra.

(ii)

X
admits a polynomial growth continuous function
calculus if and only if
X
is an
f\!
-algebra with identity.

What about general Archimedean lattice-ordered algebras? Can we
characterize those that admit a polynomial growth continuous function
calculus? The
remainder of this paper is devoted to obtaining results analogous to
those in
[
1
]
for Archimedean lattice-ordered
algebras with identity.

3.
Necessary and sufficient conditions to admit a polynomial
growth continuous function calculus

The goal of this section is to prove the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be an Archimedean lattice-ordered algebra with identity
1_{X}
,
and let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

There is a lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}\to X
with
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\mathbb{1})=1_{X}
and
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(ii)

Let
e=1_{X}\vee|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
. There exists an

f\!
-subalgebra
Y
of
X
such that
1_{X},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in Y
and, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{e^{m}}

is complete on
Y\cap I_{e^{m}}
.

(iii)

There exist
f\geq 1_{X}\vee|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
and an

f\!
-subalgebra
Y
of
X
such that
1_{X},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in Y
and, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{f^{m}}

is complete on
Y\cap I_{f^{m}}
.

When one and hence all three of these conditions are satisfied,
the lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}
satisfying the conditions
in (i) is unique.

Compare this result with its analogue for positively homogeneous
continuous function calculus
[
1
, Proposition
2.2]
. We can recast the latter result to make
the analogy with
3.1
clearer.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X
be an Archimedean vector lattice, and let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The
following are equivalent:

(i)

There is a vector lattice homomorphism
\Phi_{\bm{x}}\colon H_{n}\to X
with
\Phi_{\bm{x}}(\pi_{i})=x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(ii)

Let
e=|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
. There exists a
sublattice
Y
of
X
such that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in Y

and the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{e}
is complete on
Y\cap I_{e}
.

(iii)

There exists
f\geq|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
and a sublattice
Y
of
X
such that

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in Y
and the norm

\|{\cdot}\|_{f}
is complete on
Y\cap I_{f}
.

When one and hence all three of these conditions are satisfied,
the lattice homomorphism
\Phi_{\bm{x}}
satisfying the conditions
in (i) is unique.

Of course, the conditions in
3.1
are more involved
than those in
[
1
, Proposition 2.2]
. This is
not so much due to the fact that we are dealing with the additional
algebraic structure (and algebraic homomorphisms), as it is to the
fact that the structure of
PG_{n}
is more complex than that of
H_{n}
.
It is well-known that
H_{n}
is an AM-space with unit. This allows for
much cleaner conditions. Also, the appearance of an
f\!
-subalgebra should
not surprise the reader in view of
2.4
.

Even though
PG_{n}
is not an AM-space with unit, it can be expressed
as a countable increasing union of AM-spaces with unit.
More precisely, if
d=\mathbb{1}\vee|\pi_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|\pi_{n}|
, then

PG_{n}=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}I_{d^{m}}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{d^{m}}\subseteq I_{d^{m+1}}.

This simple observation is at the core of the proof of

3.1
. Before proceeding to this proof, we need four
preliminary lemmas. The first is a slight generalization of

[
1
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Archimedean vector lattices, and let
T\colon X\to Y
be a lattice homomorphism. Let
e\in X_{+}
. Then

T(I_{e})\subseteq I_{Te}
, and the restriction
T|_{I_{e}}\colon(I_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})\to(I_{Te},\|{\cdot}\|_{Te})
is a
contractive operator. Moreover, if
(I_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})
is
complete, then the restriction of
\|{\cdot}\|_{Te}
to

T(X)\cap I_{Te}
is complete.

Proof.

If
x\in I_{e}
, then
|x|\leq\|x\|_{e}e
and

|Tx|=T|x|\leq\|x\|_{e}Te

because
T
is a lattice homomorphism. Hence
\|Tx\|_{Te}\leq\|x\|_{e}
. This proves that
T(I_{e})\subseteq I_{Te}
and that
the restriction
T|_{I_{e}}\colon(I_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})\to(I_{Te},\|{\cdot}\|_{Te})
is a contractive operator.

Suppose that
(I_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})
is complete. We will show that
every absolutely convergent series in
(T(X)\cap I_{Te},\|{\cdot}\|_{Te})
is convergent. Let
x_{n}\in T(X)\cap I_{Te}
be a
sequence for which the associated series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}
is
absolutely convergent in
\|{\cdot}\|_{Te}
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
y_{n}\in X
be such that
T(y_{n})=x_{n}
, and set
z_{n}=(y_{n}\wedge\|x_{n}\|_{Te}e)\vee(-\|x_{n}\|_{Te}e)
. Then
|z_{n}|\leq\|x_{n}\|_{Te}e
, so
z_{n}\in I_{e}
and
\|z_{n}\|_{e}\leq\|x_{n}\|_{Te}
. Moreover, since
|x_{n}|\leq\|x_{n}\|_{Te}Te
(i.e.,

-\|x_{n}\|_{Te}Te\leq x_{n}\leq\|x_{n}\|_{Te}Te
), we have
T(z_{n})=(x_{n}\wedge\|x_{n}\|_{Te}Te)\vee(-\|x_{n}\|_{Te}Te)=x_{n}
. By assumption,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}z_{n}
converges to a certain
z\in I_{e}
. Using
that
T|_{I_{e}}
is linear and continuous, it follows that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}=Tz
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be an Archimedean vector lattice. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
I_{n}\subseteq X

be a sequence of ideals such that
I_{n}\subseteq I_{n+1}
and

X=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}.

Then
X
is uniformly complete if and only if
I_{n}
is uniformly
complete for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

If
X
is uniformly complete, then every ideal in
X
is uniformly
complete. Conversely, suppose that
I_{n}
is uniformly complete for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
(x_{k})\subseteq X
be a Cauchy sequence in the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{y}
, for some

y\in X_{+}
. By keeping only a tail of the sequence and relabeling, we may assume
that
|x_{k}-x_{l}|\leq y
for all
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
. Since the sequence of
ideals
I_{n}
is increasing, there exists some
m\in\mathbb{N}
for which

y,x_{1}\in I_{m}
. Then

|x_{k}|-|x_{1}|\leq||x_{k}|-|x_{1}||\leq|x_{k}-x_{1}|\leq y

implies that
x_{k}\in I_{m}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
I_{m}
is
uniformly complete, the sequence
(x_{k})
is uniformly convergent
in
I_{m}
, and therefore also in
X
.
∎

For a positive element
e
in a lattice-ordered algebra
X
,
denote by
IA_{e}
the smallest subspace of
X
containing
e
that is,
at the same time, an order ideal and a subalgebra.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X
be an Archimedean lattice-ordered algebra and let
e\in X_{+}
be such
that
e^{m}\in I_{e^{m+1}}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

IA_{e}=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}I_{e^{m}}.

Proof.

Let
B
be the set on the right hand side. The condition

e^{m}\in I_{e^{m+1}}
implies that
I_{e^{m}}\subseteq I_{e^{m+1}}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that
B
, being a
nested union of ideals, is an ideal.
To show that it is an algebra, let
x
and
y
be elements of
B
, say
|x|\leq M_{1}e^{m_{1}}
and
|y|\leq M_{2}e^{m_{2}}
, for some
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

and
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

|xy|\leq|x||y|\leq M_{1}M_{2}e^{m_{1}+m_{2}}

and therefore
xy\in I_{e^{m_{1}+m_{2}}}\subseteq B
. Hence
B
is a
subalgebra and an order ideal that contains
e
. In particular,

IA_{e}\subseteq B
. Conversely, since
IA_{e}
is a subalgebra
containing
e
,
e^{m}\in IA_{e}
and, since it is an order ideal,

I_{e^{m}}\subseteq IA_{e}
for every

. It follows that

.
∎

Remark
.

If

has identity

The final preliminary lemma is only needed for uniqueness.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
and

be Archimedean vector lattices, with

uniformly complete,
and let

be a positive map. Let

and

be sublattices, with

Denote by

the uniform closure of

in

. If

T(X^{\prime})\subseteq Y^{\prime}
, then also

.

Proof.

To prove this result, we need a different description of the
uniform closure. Define a transfinite sequence
(Z_{\beta})_{\beta<\omega_{1}}
of sublattices of
X
as follows:
Z_{1}=X^{\prime}
,

Z_{\beta+1}=\{\,x\in X:x_{n}\xrightarrow{\rm u}x\;(y),\text{ for some
}x_{n},y\in Z_{\beta}\,\}

for all
\beta<\omega_{1}
and, if
\gamma<\omega_{1}
is a limit

Z_{\gamma}=\bigcup_{\beta<\gamma}Z_{\beta}
. It is
then true that

(3)

\overline{X^{\prime}}=\bigcup_{\beta<\omega_{1}}Z_{\beta}

(see
[
12

). We will prove by
transfinite induction on

.

To do so, first note that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\rm u}x\;(y)
in

implies

Tx_{n}\xrightarrow{\rm u}Tx\;(Ty)
in
Y
by the positivity of
T
. By

. Suppose that

and let

. Then there exist

x_{n},y\in Z_{\beta}
such that

. Applying

follows that

with

. Since

is uniformly complete, and uniform limits are unique, we must

. Hence

is a limit ordinal and

From (

.

.
Applying
3.6
it follows that
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(PG_{n})\subseteq Y^{\prime}
.
Recall from
2.8
that
PG_{n}
is the free object in the
category of uniformly complete
f\!
-algebras with identity. Hence
\Psi_{\bm{x}}

must be the composition of
the unique map
PG_{n}\to Y^{\prime}
sending
\mathbb{1}
to
1_{X}
and
\pi_{i}

to
x_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, with the natural inclusion

Y^{\prime}\subseteq X
.
∎

As a consequence of this theorem, we obtain a characterization of
the lattice-ordered algebras with identity that admit a polynomial growth
continuous function calculus.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
X
be an Archimedean lattice-ordered algebra with identity

1_{X}
. Then

X
admits a polynomial growth continuous function calculus if and
only if
X
is an
f\!
-algebra and, for every
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
,
there exists
f\geq 1_{X}\vee|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
such that, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the norm

\|{\cdot}\|_{f^{m}}
is complete on the closed sublattice
generated by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in
I_{f^{m}}
.

In this case, the polynomial growth continuous function calculus
is unique (in the sense that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{x}\in X^{n}
there is only one lattice-algebra homomorphism
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}\to X
which satisfies (
1
)), and

(4)

\Psi_{(\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f_{1}),\ldots,\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f_{m}))}(g)=\Psi_{\bm{x}}(g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m}))

for each
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{x}\in X^{n}
,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in PG_{n}
and
g\in PG_{m}
, where
f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the function defined by

(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m})(t)=(f_{1}(t),\ldots,f_{m}(t)),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

If
X
admits a polynomial growth continuous function calculus,
then
X
is an
f\!
-algebra by
2.4
. And if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
, then

e=1_{X}\vee|x_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|x_{n}|
has the stated properties
by
3.1
. The converse also follows directly from
3.1
.

Uniqueness was proved in
3.1
. To show
(
4
), let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in PG_{n}
and
g\in PG_{m}

be as stated. First we need to check that
g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m})\in PG_{n}
. Suppose
|g|\leq M(\mathbb{1}+|\pi_{1}|+\cdots+|\pi_{m}|)^{N}
for some
M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then it is immediate
to check that

|g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m})|\leq M(\mathbb{1}+|f_{1}|+\cdots+|f_{m}|)^{N}.

Since
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in PG_{n}
, the right hand side of this
equation belongs to
PG_{n}
. Since
PG_{n}
is an ideal in

C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m})
is
continuous, it follows that
g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m})\in PG_{n}
. Hence, it makes sense to evaluate
\Psi_{\bm{x}}
at
g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m})
. This is true of every
g\in PG_{m}
.
It is immediate to check that the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&PG_{m}&\longrightarrow&X\\
&g&\longmapsto&\Psi_{\bm{x}}(g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m}))\\
\end{array}

is a lattice-algebra homomorphism that maps
\pi_{i}
to
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,m
. By uniqueness, it must be the map
\Psi_{(\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f_{1}),\ldots,\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f_{m}))}
.
∎

To complete this section, we present three examples illustrating
3.1
.
The first one shows that, even if
X
is a uniformly complete
lattice-ordered algebra with identity, it may not contain sufficiently many

f\!
-subalgebras to admit a polynomial growth continuous function
calculus.

Example 3.8
.

Consider
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the usual vector lattice structure. Define
the product

(x,y)(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(xx^{\prime}+xy^{\prime}+yx^{\prime},yy^{\prime}).

It is immediate to check that
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with this product is a
lattice-ordered algebra with identity
(0,1)
. But it is not an
f\!
-algebra,
because
(1,0)(0,1)=(1,0)
. The element
(1,0)
is such that, if

Y
is a sublattice-algebra containing both
(1,0)
and the
identity
(0,1)
, then

Y=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is not an
f\!
-algebra. Hence there is no
f
-subalgebra
containing
(1,0)
. By
3.1
, we cannot perform
polynomial growth continuous function calculus on
(1,0)
.

The second example fails the other condition: it is an
f\!
-algebra, but
it does not contain sublattice-algebras
Y
for which the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{e^{m}}
is complete on
Y\cap I_{e^{m}}
(where we are using the notation of
3.1
).

Example 3.9
.

Consider the function
I\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
given by
I(x)=x

for all
x\in[0,1]
. Let
X
be the sublattice-algebra of

C[0,1]
generated by
I
and
\mathbb{1}_{[0,1]}
. Note that
X
is an

f\!
-algebra with identity. We have
\mathbb{1}_{[0,1]}\vee I=\mathbb{1}_{[0,1]}

and the ideal generated by
\mathbb{1}_{[0,1]}
in
X
is
X
.
Yet
X
is not complete for
\|{\cdot}\|_{\mathbb{1}_{[0,1]}}
. Indeed,
this norm is precisely the uniform norm, and
X
is not closed
in the uniform norm by the Stone–Weierstrass theorem.

The final example is a positive one: it shows how

3.7
can be used in practice to prove that a
non-uniformly complete lattice-ordered algebra with unit admits a
polynomial growth continuous function calculus. This example is a
continuation of
[
1
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Example 3.10
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space of infinite cardinality, and
let
s_{0}\in K
be an accumulation point of a countably infinite
subset of
K
. (For instance,
K=[0,1]
and
s_{0}=0
.) Let

X=\{\,f\in C(K):f\text{ is constant on some neighborhood of
}s_{0}\,\}.

It was shown in
[
1
, Proposition 3.1]
that
this set is a proper and dense sublattice of
C(K)
that is not
uniformly complete. It is easy to check that it is a
subalgebra and that
\mathbb{1}_{K}\in X
. Therefore,
X
is an Archimedean

f\!
-algebra with identity. We will show that it admits a polynomial
growth continuous function calculus by checking the condition of

3.7
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in X
and let
U
be a neighborhood of

s_{0}
in which
f_{1}|_{U},\ldots,f_{n}|_{U}
are all constant. Let

c=\max\{\|f_{1}\|_{\infty},\ldots,\|f_{n}\|_{\infty},1\}
, and let

Y=\{\,f\in C(K):f|_{U}\text{ is constant}\,\}.

Then
e=c\mathbb{1}_{K}
satisfies
e\geq\mathbb{1}_{K}\vee|f_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|f_{n}|
and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in Y
. Note that
Y
is a closed
sublattice-algebra of
C(K)
. Fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

I_{e^{m}}=X
and
\|{\cdot}\|_{e^{m}}
is the uniform norm;
it follows directly that

\|{\cdot}\|_{e^{m}}
is complete on
I_{e^{m}}\cap Y
. Hence,
even though
X
is not uniformly complete, it admits a polynomial
growth continuous function calculus.

4.
Vector spaces with polynomial growth continuous function
calculus

Our final result is an extension of
[
1
, Theorem
1.4]
. It states that if a
vector space admits a sufficiently strong polynomial growth
continuous function calculus without constants, then it is automatically a commutative
f\!
-algebra.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a vector space and suppose that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

and each
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
, there is a linear
map
\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}^{h}\to X
which satisfies (
2
) and
(
4
). Then
X

can be endowed with the structure of a commutative
f\!
-algebra that
admits a polynomial growth continuous function
calculus without constants.

Proof.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in PG_{n}^{h}
, denote
\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f)
by
f(\bm{x})
. In this
notation, the linearity of
\Psi_{\bm{x}}
reads

(f+\lambda g)(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})+\lambda g(\bm{x})

for all
f,g\in PG_{n}^{h}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Condition
(
2
)
becomes
\pi_{i}(\bm{x})=x_{i}
, while (
4
) has the form

g(f_{1}(\bm{x}),\ldots,f_{m}(\bm{x}))=(g\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{m}))(\bm{x}).

Consider the function of
PG_{2}
:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\sigma\colon&\mathbb{R}^{2}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&(t_{1},t_{2})&\longmapsto&t_{1}\vee t_{2}\\
\end{array}

and define a partial order
\leq
on
X
by setting
x_{1}\leq x_{2}

if and only if
\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{2}
. It is shown in

[
1
, Theorem 1.4]
that this order makes

X
into a vector lattice. Moreover, the supremum of
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
in this order is given by
\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})
.

Consider the function of
PG_{2}
:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\rho\colon&\mathbb{R}^{2}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&(t_{1},t_{2})&\longmapsto&t_{1}t_{2}\\
\end{array}.

Define a product on
X
by

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&X\times X&\longrightarrow&X\\
&(x_{1},x_{2})&\longmapsto&\rho(x_{1},x_{2})\\
\end{array}.

We claim that
X
, with this product, is a commutative real
algebra. Let us check the required axioms.

Commutativity.
Since
the product in
\mathbb{R}
is commutative,
\rho(t_{1},t_{2})=\rho(t_{2},t_{1})
holds for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. In other words, the identity
\rho\circ\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}=\rho\circ\pi_{2}\times\pi_{1}
holds in

PG_{2}
. Using (
2
) and (
4
), the following holds for all

x_{1},x_{2}\in X
:

(\rho\circ\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})(x_{1},x_{2})=\rho(\pi_{1}(x_{1},x_{2}),\pi_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}))=\rho(x_{1},x_{2}).

Similarly,
(\rho\circ\pi_{2}\times\pi_{1})(x_{1},x_{2})=\rho(x_{2},x_{1})
. Hence
\rho(x_{1},x_{2})=\rho(x_{2},x_{1})
.

Associativity.
The associativity of
\mathbb{R}

means that
\rho(\rho(t_{1},t_{2}),t_{3})=\rho(t_{1},\rho(t_{2},t_{3}))

for all
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
. This, in turn, implies that the
following identity holds in
PG_{3}
:

\rho\circ[(\rho\circ\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})\times\pi_{3}]=\rho\circ[\pi_{1}\times(\rho\circ\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})].

Applying this to
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in X^{3}
and simplifying
with (
2
) and (
4
) yields
\rho(\rho(x_{1},x_{2}),x_{3})=\rho(x_{1},\rho(x_{2},x_{3}))
.

Distributivity.
Distributivity in
\mathbb{R}
translates into
the identity

\rho\circ[\pi_{1}\times(\pi_{2}+\pi_{3})]=\rho\circ\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}+\rho\circ\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3}.

Applying this to
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in X^{3}
, using linearity and
applying (
2
) and (
4
) yields
\rho(x_{1},x_{2}+x_{3})=\rho(x_{1},x_{2})+\rho(x_{1},x_{3})
.
Distributivity on the right follows from commutativity and
distributivity on the left.

Associativity with scalars.
Fixed
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
associativity and commutativity in
\mathbb{R}
imply
\rho(\lambda t_{1},\mu t_{2})=\lambda\mu\rho(t_{1},t_{2})
for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. This means that

\rho\circ(\lambda\pi_{1})\times(\mu\pi_{2})=(\lambda\mu)(\rho\circ\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}).

Evaluating at
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
and using linearity and (
2
) and (
4
)
gives
\rho(\lambda x_{1},\mu x_{2})=\lambda\mu\rho(x_{1},x_{2})
.

Hence
X
is a real algebra when equipped with the product

x_{1}x_{2}=\rho(x_{1},x_{2})
. Next we show that it is also a
lattice-ordered algebra. For this we have to check that, if
x_{1},x_{2}\in X_{+}
, then
x_{1}x_{2}\in X_{+}
. Since in
\mathbb{R}
the product of positive
elements is positive, the identity

[(t_{1}\vee 0)(t_{2}\vee 0)]\vee 0=(t_{1}\vee 0)(t_{2}\vee 0)

holds for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. This implies that

\sigma\circ[\rho\circ(\sigma\circ\pi_{1}\times\mathbb{0})\times(\sigma\circ\pi_{2}\times\mathbb{0})]=\rho\circ[(\sigma\circ\pi_{1}\times\mathbb{0})\times(\sigma\circ\pi_{2}\times\mathbb{0})],

where
\mathbb{0}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the constant zero
function. Linearity of
\Psi_{\bm{x}}
implies that
\mathbb{0}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
for
all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
. Therefore the evaluation of the previous
identity at
(x_{1},x_{2})
yields

\sigma[\rho(\sigma(x_{1},0),\sigma(x_{2},0)),0]=\rho[\sigma(x_{1},0),\sigma(x_{2},0)].

This equation holds for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
. When
x_{1},x_{2}\geq 0

(that is, when
\sigma(x_{i},0)=x_{i}
for
i=1,2
) this equation
becomes

\sigma[\rho(x_{1},x_{2}),0]=\rho(x_{1},x_{2}).

This is, by definition, the same as
x_{1}x_{2}=\rho(x_{1},x_{2})\geq 0
.
Hence
X
is a lattice-ordered algebra.

To show that
X
is an
f\!
-algebra, we will use the following fact due
to G. Birkhoff
[
16
, Lemma 3]
: the lattice-ordered algebra
X
is an

f\!
-algebra if and only if the equalities

(x_{1}\vee 0)\wedge[((-x_{1})\vee 0)(x_{2}\vee 0)]=0=(x_{1}\vee 0)\wedge[(x_{2}\vee 0)((-x_{1})\vee 0)]

hold for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
. Since
X
is commutative, we
need only check the identity on the left. Since
\mathbb{R}
is an
f\!
-algebra,

(t_{1}\vee 0)\wedge[((-t_{1})\vee 0)(t_{2}\vee 0)]=0

holds for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
; let us rewrite it as

(-(t_{1}\vee 0))\vee[-((-t_{1})\vee 0)(t_{2}\vee 0)]=0

so that only suprema appear in the formula. This implies that

\sigma\circ[[-\sigma\circ(\pi_{1}\times\mathbb{0})]\times[-\rho\circ((\sigma\circ((-\pi_{1})\times\mathbb{0}))\times(\sigma\circ(\pi_{2}\times\mathbb{0})))]]=\mathbb{0}.

Applying this identity to
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
yields

\sigma(-\sigma(x_{1},0),-\rho(\sigma(-x_{1},0),\sigma(x_{2},0)))=0.

By
[
16
, Lemma 3]
,
X
is a commutative
f\!
-algebra.

Finally, to show that
X
admits a polynomial growth
continuous function calculus without constants, it suffices to check that

\Psi_{\bm{x}}\colon PG_{n}^{h}\to X
is a lattice-algebra homomorphism for
all
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
. To make the equations
clearer, denote
\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})
by
x_{1}\vee x_{2}
and
\rho(x_{1},x_{2})
by
x_{1}x_{2}
. Then for all
f,g\in PG_{n}^{h}
:

\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f\vee g)=\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\sigma\circ(f\times g))=(\sigma\circ(f\times g))(\bm{x})=\sigma(f(\bm{x}),g(\bm{x}))=\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f)\vee\Psi_{\bm{x}}(g),

and also

\Psi_{\bm{x}}(fg)=\Psi_{\bm{x}}(\rho\circ(f\times g))=(\rho\circ(f\times g))(\bm{x})=\rho(f(\bm{x}),g(\bm{x}))=\Psi_{\bm{x}}(f)\Psi_{\bm{x}}(g).\qed
