ABSTRACT

 This report summarizes the research activities, technical achievements, and outcomes of
the project Additive Manufacturing of Circumferentially Embedded Optical Sensor Modules for In
Situ Monitoring of Coal-Fueled Steam Turbines conducted under DOE Cooperative Agreement
DE-FE0031826. The project was a collaborative effort between Clemson University and General
Electric (GE) aimed at developing advanced optical sensing technologies and additive
manufacturing methods for condition-based monitoring of steam turbines operating in harsh coal-
fired power plant environments.

 The project successfully designed, fabricated, and experimentally validated three types of
optical sensor modules for in situ monitoring of turbine operating conditions, including
temperature, pressure, and blade tip timing. Fiber-optic temperature and pressure sensors based on
Fabry-Perot interferometric principles were developed using ultrafast laser micromachining
techniques and integrated into fused silica sensor packages. Extensive laboratory testing
demonstrated stable and repeatable sensor performance, including linear temperature and pressure
responses suitable for turbine monitoring applications.

 A major accomplishment of the project was the development of a novel optical blade tip-
timing sensor utilizing Clemson University's Integrated Additive and Subtractive Manufacturing
(IASM) technology. The IASM process combines glass additive manufacturing, picosecond laser
micromachining, CO₂ laser resurfacing, laser welding, and precision optical assembly to fabricate
complex fused silica sensor structures with embedded optical components. Significant advances
were achieved in the manufacturing of optically transparent fused silica components, precision
micro-features, laser-polished optical surfaces, and integrated optical assemblies capable of
operating in high-temperature and high-pressure environments.

 Comprehensive optical modeling and design optimization were performed using Zemax
OpticStudio to develop robust excitation and collection optical paths. Multiple sensing
architectures were evaluated, leading to the selection of a lens-prism-based optical design
enhanced with tapered optical waveguides to improve signal collection efficiency and alignment
tolerance. The resulting blade tip-timing sensor achieved sensing performance suitable for turbine
blade detection and vibration monitoring while meeting stringent dimensional and environmental
constraints.

 The fabricated sensor modules were integrated into GE’s Smart Ring concept and
subsequently adapted to radial probe configurations in response to evolving industrial
requirements. Laboratory and system-level testing demonstrated successful blade detection,
temperature and pressure sensing, and operation of fully assembled sensor modules. The project
also established manufacturing methodologies for embedded optical sensing systems, advanced
the maturity of the IASM technology platform, and provided a pathway for future industrial
deployment of smart turbine monitoring systems.

 Overall, this project demonstrated the feasibility of additively manufactured, fused-silica-
based optical sensor modules for harsh-environment turbine monitoring and established key
enabling technologies for future condition-based monitoring systems capable of improving the
efficiency, reliability, and operational safety of fossil energy power generation assets.

1.0 BACKGROUND

1.1 Technology needs and requirements

 Coal-fired power plants continue to play an important role in the United States power
generation portfolio. Steam turbine-generator systems are among the most critical assets within
these plants, converting the thermal energy of high-temperature, high-pressure steam into
mechanical shaft power and ultimately electricity. The reliable operation of steam turbines is
therefore essential for maintaining plant availability, operational efficiency, and grid stability.
However, steam turbine blade failures remain one of the leading causes of forced outages and
costly maintenance events in fossil energy power plants.

 Steam turbine blades operate under extremely demanding service conditions. During
operation, blades are subjected to elevated temperatures, high centrifugal stresses, cyclic
mechanical loading, steam-induced vibrations, and moisture-related corrosion mechanisms. These
harsh environmental and operational conditions make turbine blades particularly vulnerable to
degradation and failure over time. Historical industry surveys have shown that blade failures
account for a significant percentage of unplanned outages in coal-fired power plants, resulting in
substantial economic losses associated with lost generation capacity, repair costs, and extended
outage durations. Catastrophic failures can be especially damaging because detached blade
fragments may cause secondary damage to adjacent blades, rotors, casings, and other critical
turbine components. One well-documented example occurred at the Sherco power plant in 2011,
where turbine blade cracking led to a catastrophic generator failure that ultimately required repairs
exceeding $146 million.

 Steam turbines consist of high-pressure (HP), intermediate-pressure (IP), and low-pressure
(LP) sections, each serving distinct functions in the energy conversion process. Among these
components, the LP turbine blades are generally recognized as the most vulnerable to failure.
Although historical turbine efficiency improvements primarily focused on the HP and IP sections,
the LP section can contribute as much as half of the total turbine power output. Modern LP blades
are considerably longer than their HP and IP counterparts and operate under conditions that
increase their susceptibility to fatigue, stress corrosion cracking, corrosion fatigue, erosion, and
vibration-induced failures.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 21

 Industry studies indicate that nearly half of LP blade failures are associated with fatigue-
related mechanisms. Blade vibration caused by fluctuating steam loading and aerodynamic
excitation generates cyclic bending stresses that can initiate microscopic cracks. Once initiated,
crack propagation may proceed rapidly, eventually leading to catastrophic blade fracture. Failure
initiation sites have been observed throughout the blade structure, including shrouds, lacing holes,
airfoil regions, and blade attachment locations. The probability of failure generally increases with
blade length because longer blades experience greater dynamic stresses and more complex
vibration modes. Consequently, the monitoring and early detection of LP blade degradation have
become critical priorities for improving turbine reliability and reducing maintenance costs.

 The operating environment of coal-fired power plants has changed significantly in recent
years due to the increasing penetration of renewable energy sources. Traditionally, coal plants
were operated as baseload units with relatively stable operating conditions. Today, many plants
are required to perform load-following functions to support grid stability, resulting in more
frequent startups, shutdowns, rapid load changes, and cycling operations. These changing
operational requirements subject turbine components to increased thermal and mechanical stresses
and accelerate degradation mechanisms. In addition, many existing coal-fired generating units
have exceeded their original design life, further increasing the risk of unexpected component
failures. While LP turbine blades are typically designed for service lives approaching 30 years,
numerous cases of premature blade failure have been reported throughout the industry.

 To mitigate the risk of blade failures, power plant operators traditionally rely on preventive
maintenance programs and scheduled turbine inspections. Recommended maintenance practices
include routine daily, weekly, monthly, and annual inspections, as well as planned minor and major
overhaul outages occurring every few years. Major turbine overhauls often require outages lasting
several weeks during which turbine components are disassembled, inspected, repaired, or replaced.
Although these maintenance programs require substantial investments in labor, equipment, and
lost generation revenue, they cannot completely eliminate the occurrence of unexpected failures.
Furthermore, the increasing frequency of cycling operations has made traditional time-based
maintenance strategies less effective because component degradation rates may vary significantly
depending on actual operating conditions.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 22

 As a result, the industry has increasingly recognized the need for condition-based
monitoring (CBM) approaches. Unlike scheduled maintenance programs, CBM relies on
continuous or periodic assessment of component health using real-time operational data. The
objective is to detect abnormal conditions at an early stage, allowing maintenance actions to be
performed only when necessary and before catastrophic failures occur. For steam turbine blade
monitoring, relevant parameters include blade vibration, pressure fluctuations, temperature
distributions, acoustic emissions, strain responses, and debris generation. These measurements can
be analyzed using signal processing techniques, statistical methods, machine learning algorithms,
and artificial intelligence tools to identify developing faults and predict remaining useful life.

 Among the various CBM approaches, blade tip timing (BTT), blade tip clearance (BTC),
pressure monitoring, and temperature measurements have emerged as particularly promising
techniques for detecting early-stage blade degradation. Blade tip timing systems can detect
changes in blade vibration characteristics and resonance frequencies, which are often indicators of
fatigue damage. Pressure measurements provide insight into flow disturbances caused by blade
deformation, rubbing, rotor eccentricity, or other abnormalities. Temperature measurements can
reveal performance degradation, fouling, and thermal anomalies. Research has shown that
pressure-based monitoring may be more sensitive than conventional vibration monitoring for
identifying certain blade fault mechanisms. However, widespread implementation of these
monitoring approaches has remained limited.

 The primary barrier to deployment is not necessarily the availability of sensing
technologies, but rather the difficulty of packaging, protecting, and integrating sensors within the
harsh turbine environment. Numerous sensor technologies have been investigated for turbine
monitoring, including optical sensors, eddy-current probes, magnetoresistive sensors, microwave
sensors, inductive sensors, capacitive sensors, piezoelectric accelerometers, ultrasonic systems,
and infrared temperature measurement techniques. Many of these sensing technologies have
demonstrated promising laboratory performance. However, installation within operating steam
turbines remains extremely challenging because of the high temperatures, elevated pressures,
severe vibration environments, limited installation space, and stringent sealing requirements
associated with turbine casings.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 23

 Recent advances in harsh-environment sensing technologies have improved the capability
of sensors to survive turbine operating conditions. Nevertheless, practical deployment remains
hindered by the lack of robust packaging and installation methods that can reliably integrate
sensors into turbine structures without compromising turbine performance or sensor survivability.
This challenge has motivated the development of sensor-embedded smart components in which
sensing elements are directly integrated into structural parts rather than installed as external add-
on devices.

1.2 Smart Ring Concept

 While numerous sensing technologies have demonstrated the capability to measure
important turbine operating parameters, a major barrier to widespread implementation remains the
practical integration of sensors into operating steam turbines. Conventional sensor installation
methods typically require drilling individual access ports through the turbine casing and mounting
probes that directly face the rotating blades. Although effective for single-point measurements,
this approach becomes increasingly problematic when multiple sensors are needed to monitor
different turbine parameters simultaneously. Each additional sensor requires dedicated mounting
locations, wiring penetrations, sealing interfaces, and maintenance procedures, thereby increasing
installation complexity, cost, and the potential for steam leakage. Furthermore, retrofitting existing
turbines with multiple sensors often requires significant modifications to the turbine casing,
making large-scale deployment economically unattractive.

 To address these limitations, General Electric (GE) developed the Smart Ring concept, a
novel sensor integration architecture designed specifically for steam turbine condition-based
monitoring applications. The Smart Ring is a circumferential instrumentation platform that is
mounted flush with the inner wall of the turbine casing, shown in Figure 1. Unlike conventional
probe installations that rely on multiple individual casing penetrations, the Smart Ring provides a
dedicated instrumentation pathway capable of hosting numerous sensor modules within a single
integrated structure.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 24

 Figure 1: Circumferentially mounted blade tip-timing sensor illustration.

 The Smart Ring consists of a thin annular ring that is recessed into the turbine casing and
positioned such that its inner surface remains flush with the turbine flow path. This flush-mounted
configuration minimizes aerodynamic disturbances and avoids adverse impacts on turbine
performance. Embedded within the Smart Ring is an instrumentation track that serves as a
protected channel for sensor modules, optical fibers, electrical wiring, and supporting hardware.
Multiple sensing modules can be installed along the track to monitor different turbine operating
parameters including blade tip timing, blade tip clearance, temperature, pressure, vibration, and
other quantities of interest.

 One of the most significant advantages of the Smart Ring architecture is its modularity.
Individual sensor modules can be inserted, replaced, or repositioned within the instrumentation
track without requiring extensive modifications to the turbine structure. This modular design
provides flexibility for different turbine configurations and allows utilities and turbine
manufacturers to tailor sensing capabilities according to specific operational requirements. In
addition, all sensor connections can be routed through a common exit port, substantially reducing
the number of casing penetrations and simplifying installation, sealing, and maintenance
procedures.

 GE recognized that the Smart Ring could provide an enabling platform for next-generation
turbine monitoring systems and initiated internal research and development activities to advance
the concept toward field deployment. A patent application was subsequently filed to protect the
Smart Ring architecture and associated implementation methods. However, successful realization

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 25

of the Smart Ring concept requires the development of specialized sensor modules capable of
operating within the unique geometric and environmental constraints imposed by the
instrumentation track.

 Among the various sensing technologies considered for Smart Ring implementation,
optical fiber sensors emerged as particularly attractive candidates. Optical fiber sensors offer
several advantages for harsh-environment turbine applications, including compact size, immunity
to electromagnetic interference, high sensitivity, remote interrogation capability, and demonstrated
survivability under elevated temperature conditions. Furthermore, optical fibers can
simultaneously support multiple sensing modalities including temperature, pressure, strain,
vibration, and blade tip timing measurements. These characteristics make optical sensing an ideal
technology platform for integrated turbine health monitoring.

 Despite these advantages, significant challenges exist in adapting optical sensors for Smart
Ring deployment. Optical fibers are inherently fragile and susceptible to damage when subjected
to mechanical shock, vibration, thermal cycling, and installation stresses. Within a steam turbine
environment, optical components must survive elevated temperatures, high pressures, moisture
exposure, and continuous vibration while maintaining optical alignment and signal integrity.
Consequently, effective packaging, protection, and routing of optical fibers become critical design
requirements.

 The Smart Ring instrumentation track imposes several additional constraints on sensor
module design. First, the sensor modules must maintain an extremely low profile to fit within the
limited space available in the instrumentation track. GE specified that the sensor module thickness
should be less than approximately one-quarter inch, requiring highly compact packaging solutions.
Second, blade tip timing and tip clearance measurements require optical access to the rotating
blades. Because the optical fibers are routed circumferentially within the instrumentation track,
the sensor module must incorporate integrated optical components capable of redirecting the
optical beam radially toward the blade tips. This requires the incorporation of precision optical
elements such as prisms, mirrors, lenses, or waveguides directly within the sensor package. Third,
all structural and optical materials must withstand the harsh operating environment of steam
turbines while maintaining dimensional stability and long-term reliability.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 26

 To satisfy these demanding requirements, fused silica was selected as the primary material
for sensor module fabrication. Fused silica offers several important advantages for harsh-
environment optical sensing applications. Most importantly, its coefficient of thermal expansion
closely matches that of standard silica optical fibers, minimizing thermally induced stresses during
temperature fluctuations and improving sensor reliability. Fused silica also exhibits excellent
thermal stability, corrosion resistance, and mechanical durability under high-temperature and high-
pressure conditions commonly encountered in steam turbines.

 In addition to its structural advantages, fused silica possesses exceptional optical properties.
The material can be fabricated into transparent optical components such as lenses, prisms,
windows, and waveguides that are necessary for beam steering and optical signal collection. This
capability is particularly important for blade tip timing sensor modules, where optical beams must
be redirected from the circumferential fiber routing path toward the radial blade monitoring
direction. Integrating these optical functions directly into the sensor package reduces assembly
complexity and improves robustness compared to conventional multi-component optical
assemblies.

 However, fused silica is notoriously difficult to machine using traditional subtractive
manufacturing methods because of its hardness, brittleness, and susceptibility to cracking.
Producing miniature sensor modules with integrated optical features, embedded fiber routing
channels, and complex three-dimensional geometries presents substantial manufacturing
challenges. Conventional machining techniques are often incapable of producing such structures
efficiently or economically.

1.3 Clemson University IASM Technology

 The manufacturing challenges associated with the Smart Ring sensor modules motivated
the adoption of Clemson University's Integrated Additive and Subtractive Manufacturing (IASM)
technology. The IASM platform, developed by the Xiao Research Group, combines additive
manufacturing of fused silica glass with precision laser-based subtractive manufacturing
techniques to fabricate complex optical structures and sensor-embedded components that would
be difficult or impossible to produce using conventional manufacturing methods. The technology
was originally developed under previous DOE/NETL-supported research programs and has

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 27

demonstrated the capability to fabricate functional fused silica structures with integrated sensing
and optical functionalities.

 The fundamental concept of IASM is to integrate additive and subtractive manufacturing
operations into a unified fabrication workflow. In this approach, additive manufacturing is used to
build complex fused silica geometries layer-by-layer, while laser micromachining processes are
employed to create precision features, optical pathways, sensor cavities, mounting structures, and
embedded fiber-routing channels. The combination of these manufacturing capabilities enables the
production of highly integrated sensor modules that simultaneously provide structural support,
sensor protection, optical functionality, and mechanical mounting features.

 A key advantage of the IASM approach is its ability to fabricate monolithic fused silica
sensor packages with embedded optical components. Unlike conventional assembly methods that
require multiple discrete optical elements, adhesives, mechanical fixtures, and alignment
procedures, IASM allows optical functions such as beam steering, focusing, and signal routing to
be incorporated directly into the sensor module geometry. This capability is particularly important
for blade tip timing and blade tip clearance measurements, where the optical beam must be
redirected from the circumferential fiber routing direction within the Smart Ring to a radial
direction toward the rotating turbine blades.

 As shown in Figure 2, the Clemson IASM system incorporates multiple laser processing
technologies within a single manufacturing platform. Subtractive manufacturing operations are
performed using an ultrafast picosecond (ps) laser that enables precision micromachining of fused
silica glass with minimal heat-affected zones. The picosecond laser is used to cut, drill, and shape
fused silica structures with high dimensional accuracy. However, laser-cut glass surfaces typically
exhibit significant surface roughness and are not suitable for optical transmission applications. To
overcome this limitation, a carbon dioxide (CO₂) laser is employed as a secondary processing tool
for surface polishing and laser welding. The CO₂ laser locally reflows the glass surface,
significantly reducing roughness and increasing optical transparency. Through controlled laser
polishing, optical-quality surfaces can be produced directly within the manufactured component,
enabling the creation of integrated optical pathways and beam-steering elements.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 28

 Figure 2: Clemson University IASM System.

 The IASM manufacturing platform consists of a highly integrated laser processing system
that combines precision motion control, laser machining, optical metrology, and automated
process planning. The system includes a picosecond laser, a CO₂ laser, two high-speed galvo
scanners mounted on a vertical Z-axis stage, a precision vise mounted on an X-Y motion stage, a
centralized motion controller, and a computer-based manufacturing interface. The motion control
system synchronizes stage movements, galvo scanner trajectories, and laser firing sequences
through programmable manufacturing recipes. These recipes define the complete fabrication
process, including material deposition, laser cutting, polishing, welding, and feature generation
operations.

 To ensure dimensional accuracy and process repeatability, the IASM platform incorporates
advanced metrology and alignment capabilities. A Keyence confocal laser displacement sensor is
used for surface profiling, dimensional inspection, and Z-axis referencing during fabrication. This
enables precise control of feature depths and surface geometries throughout the manufacturing
process. In addition, a high-resolution USB camera mounted on the Z-axis stage provides visual
alignment and referencing capabilities for locating critical features in the X-Y plane. Together,
these sensing and control systems enable accurate registration between additive and subtractive

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 29

manufacturing operations and facilitate the fabrication of complex multi-feature fused silica
structures.

 The IASM technology provides several advantages that are directly relevant to Smart Ring
sensor module development. First, it enables fabrication of compact, low-profile sensor packages
capable of fitting within the stringent dimensional constraints of the Smart Ring instrumentation
track. Second, it allows optical fibers and sensing elements to be embedded and protected within
monolithic fused silica structures that provide excellent thermal and mechanical stability. Third,
the technology supports the fabrication of integrated optical elements, including lenses, prisms,
optical windows, and waveguides, which are essential for blade tip timing and clearance
measurements. Finally, the use of fused silica throughout the sensor module minimizes thermal
expansion mismatch between the optical fibers and structural package, thereby improving long-
term reliability in the high-temperature, high-pressure, and high-vibration environment of steam
turbines.

 By combining advanced optical sensing technologies, GE's Smart Ring architecture, and
Clemson University's IASM manufacturing capability, the project established a unique platform
for the development of sensor-embedded smart turbine components. This manufacturing approach
enables the creation of robust, highly integrated optical sensing modules specifically designed for
harsh-environment condition-based monitoring applications and provides a pathway toward future
deployment of intelligent turbine monitoring systems in utility-scale power generation facilities.

2.0 PROJECT OBJECTIVE

 The overarching goal of this project was to develop, manufacture, integrate, and validate a
new generation of optical sensing modules capable of performing continuous in situ monitoring of
critical operating parameters within coal-fueled steam turbines. The project addressed a long-
standing technology gap in turbine CBM by combining harsh-environment optical sensing
technologies, advanced additive manufacturing methods, and GE’s Smart Ring architecture into a
unified monitoring platform.

 Steam turbine blade failures remain one of the most significant causes of forced outages
and maintenance costs in coal-fired power plants. Existing monitoring approaches often rely on
periodic inspections and limited instrumentation that cannot provide continuous information
regarding the health and operating conditions of turbine components. The ability to directly

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 30

monitor blade vibration, blade tip timing, temperature, and pressure within the turbine environment
is critical for early fault detection, predictive maintenance, and improved operational reliability.
However, deployment of sensors within operating steam turbines is challenging due to the harsh
operating environment characterized by elevated temperatures, high pressures, severe vibration,
moisture exposure, and restricted installation space.

 To address these challenges, this project sought to develop a family of compact optical
sensor modules that could be embedded within GE’s Smart Ring platform and installed directly
into the turbine casing. The proposed sensor modules were designed to provide continuous
measurements of turbine operating conditions while maintaining survivability and performance
under harsh operating environments. By integrating multiple sensing modalities into a common
instrumentation platform, the project aimed to enable a comprehensive condition-based
monitoring capability for steam turbine assets.

 A key innovation of the project was the use of Clemson University’s IASM technology to
fabricate the sensor modules. The IASM platform combines additive manufacturing of fused silica
glass with precision laser micromachining, laser polishing, and laser welding processes, enabling
the fabrication of highly integrated sensor packages with embedded optical fibers, optical
components, and structural features. This manufacturing approach provides a pathway for
producing compact, low-profile, sensor-embedded smart components that can be readily integrated
into the Smart Ring architecture.

 The project focused on three complementary sensing functions that are critical for turbine
health monitoring:

 1) Temperature Monitoring – Measurement of local turbine temperatures to identify
 thermal anomalies, monitor operating conditions, and support turbine performance
 assessments.

 2) Pressure Monitoring – Measurement of local pressure variations within the turbine flow
 path to provide insight into aerodynamic performance, flow disturbances, and blade health
 conditions.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 31

 3) Blade Tip Timing and Clearance Monitoring – Non-contact optical measurement of
 blade arrival times, blade vibration characteristics, and blade tip clearance for early
 detection of blade fatigue, resonance, deformation, and other failure mechanisms.

 Successful development of these sensing technologies would establish the foundation for
a practical condition-based monitoring system capable of improving turbine reliability, reducing
maintenance costs, minimizing forced outages, and enhancing the efficiency and operational
flexibility of coal-fired power plants.

 To achieve these goals, the project pursued the following specific objectives:

Objective 1: Design and Develop Smart Ring-Compatible Optical Sensor Modules

 Design compact optical sensor modules capable of being circumferentially mounted within
GE’s Smart Ring instrumentation track for in situ monitoring of temperature, pressure, blade tip
timing, and blade tip clearance. The sensor modules were required to satisfy stringent dimensional,
thermal, optical, and mechanical requirements while maintaining compatibility with Smart Ring
installation and maintenance procedures. Particular emphasis was placed on developing robust
packaging strategies that protect optical fibers and sensing elements from the harsh turbine
environment.

Objective 2: Develop and Optimize the IASM Process

 Advance and optimize the IASM fabrication methodology for manufacturing fused silica
sensor modules with embedded optical sensing elements and integrated optical components. This
objective included the development of additive manufacturing processes for fused silica structures,
picosecond laser micromachining techniques for precision feature fabrication, CO₂ laser polishing
processes for optical surface finishing, and laser welding techniques for optical component
integration. The goal was to establish a repeatable manufacturing process capable of producing
high-performance sensor modules with the dimensional accuracy, optical quality, and
environmental robustness required for turbine applications.

Objective 3: Fabricate and Validate Prototypes Under Laboratory Conditions

 Fabricate prototype temperature, pressure, and blade tip timing sensor modules and
evaluate their performance under controlled laboratory conditions. Testing activities included
characterization of sensor sensitivity, repeatability, response time, survivability, environmental

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 32

stability, optical performance, and resistance to temperature and pressure variations. The
laboratory testing phase was intended to validate both the sensing technologies and the associated
manufacturing processes before integration into larger turbine monitoring systems.

Objective 4: Integrate Modules with the Smart Ring and Perform Industrial Validation

 Integrate the fabricated sensor modules into GE’s Smart Ring platform and evaluate their
performance in representative turbine environments. This objective included system-level
integration activities, installation within Smart Ring assemblies, and testing using industrial-scale
turbine testing facilities available at GE. The goal was to demonstrate the feasibility of the Smart
Ring concept and validate the operational performance of the sensor modules under conditions
representative of actual turbine operation.

Objective 5: Advance Technology Readiness and Establish Commercialization Pathways

 Develop technology maturation and technology transfer plans to facilitate future
deployment of the sensor technologies within commercial power generation systems. This
objective included assessment of technology readiness levels (TRLs), identification of remaining
technical challenges, evaluation of commercialization opportunities, and development of pathways
for transitioning the technology from laboratory-scale demonstrations to industrial implementation.
Particular emphasis was placed on supporting future adoption of sensor-embedded smart
components for condition-based monitoring applications in the power generation industry.

 Through the successful completion of these objectives, the project sought to demonstrate a
new paradigm for turbine monitoring based on additively manufactured, sensor-embedded smart
components capable of providing real-time operational intelligence within harsh-energy
environments. The resulting technologies are expected to contribute to improved turbine reliability,
enhanced plant availability, reduced maintenance costs, and increased operational flexibility for
existing and future fossil energy power generation systems.

TEMPERATURE SENSOR

 The temperature sensor module was designed based on a laser-assisted sensor embedding
methodology previously developed by the Clemson research team for the fabrication of harsh-
environment optical sensing systems. The objective was to create a compact, mechanically robust,
and thermally stable sensor package capable of protecting an embedded optical fiber temperature
sensor while maintaining reliable operation under the elevated temperatures, pressures, and
vibration levels encountered in steam turbine environments. To achieve this objective, a
manufacturing process was developed that combines additive manufacturing of fused silica glass
with precision laser micromachining and laser surface finishing technologies within the IASM
framework.

 The fabrication process, schematically illustrated in Figure 3, integrates four key
manufacturing technologies: fused silica paste extrusion for additive manufacturing, picosecond
(ps) laser micromachining for precision feature generation, CO₂ laser melting for glass
consolidation, and CO₂ laser resurfacing for optical-quality surface finishing. The combination of
these processes enables the fabrication of monolithic fused silica sensor modules with embedded
optical fiber sensors while maintaining high dimensional accuracy and excellent thermal
compatibility between the sensor and the structural package.

 The fabrication process begins with the additive manufacturing of a fused silica glass
substrate that serves as the foundation of the temperature sensor module. Using the glass 3D-
printing process developed at Clemson University, fused silica paste is deposited layer-by-layer
according to a predefined toolpath to create a glass base with the required dimensions and
geometry. Following deposition, a CO₂ laser is used to locally melt and consolidate the deposited
material, transforming the silica paste into a dense fused silica structure. This additive
manufacturing approach provides significant flexibility in component design and enables the
fabrication of customized sensor geometries that would be difficult to produce using conventional
machining techniques.

 After fabrication of the glass substrate, precision picosecond laser micromachining is
employed to create a microgroove for sensor embedment. The picosecond laser provides excellent
machining precision with minimal thermal damage to the surrounding material, making it
particularly suitable for processing brittle materials such as fused silica. The geometry of the

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 37

microgroove is carefully designed to closely match the diameter and profile of the optical fiber
sensor. This close geometric match allows the fiber to be securely positioned within the substrate
while minimizing residual stresses and movement during subsequent processing steps. The high
spatial resolution of the picosecond laser enables accurate control of groove depth, width, and
shape, ensuring consistent sensor placement and repeatable manufacturing results.

 Although picosecond laser machining provides excellent dimensional accuracy, the
resulting glass surfaces typically exhibit relatively high surface roughness due to the material
removal mechanism. Such rough surfaces can reduce optical quality, introduce localized stress
concentrations, and interfere with proper sensor embedment. To address this issue, a CO₂ laser
resurfacing process is applied following micromachining. During resurfacing, the laser locally
heats and reflows the glass surface, reducing surface roughness and improving surface quality.
This process produces a smooth microgroove with improved dimensional uniformity and enhanced
contact between the optical fiber and the surrounding fused silica structure. The resurfaced groove
provides an optimal environment for sensor embedment while minimizing the possibility of
mechanical damage to the fiber during assembly.

 Once the microgroove preparation is completed, the optical fiber temperature sensor is
positioned within the groove. The sensing element consists of an Intrinsic Fabry-Perot
Interferometer (IFPI) fabricated directly within a fused silica optical fiber. The IFPI sensor
operates by monitoring temperature-induced changes in the optical cavity length and refractive
index within the sensing region. Because the sensor is entirely fabricated from fused silica
materials, it exhibits excellent thermal stability, corrosion resistance, and compatibility with high-
temperature turbine environments. Furthermore, the all-glass construction eliminates many of the
thermal expansion mismatch issues commonly encountered with conventional sensor packaging
materials.

 After sensor placement, the additive manufacturing process resumes to encapsulate and
protect the embedded sensor. Additional fused silica material is deposited over the optical fiber
and consolidated using the CO₂ laser, thereby forming the final sensor module structure. This
overprinting process creates a monolithic fused silica package in which the optical fiber sensor
becomes an integral part of the structural component. The resulting sensor module provides

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 38

excellent mechanical protection, environmental isolation, and thermal compatibility while
maintaining direct optical access to the embedded sensing element.

 A unique aspect of the fabrication methodology is the continuous monitoring of sensor
performance throughout the manufacturing process. During each major fabrication step, including
laser machining, resurfacing, material deposition, and laser consolidation, the optical response of
the embedded IFPI sensor is monitored in real time. This in situ monitoring capability serves two
important purposes. First, it verifies the survivability of the sensor throughout the manufacturing
process and ensures that no damage has occurred during fabrication. Second, it provides immediate
feedback regarding the effects of processing parameters on sensor performance, enabling
optimization of laser power, scanning speed, deposition conditions, and thermal processing
parameters. By correlating sensor response with manufacturing conditions, the process can be
refined to maximize both fabrication quality and sensor reliability.

 The resulting temperature sensor module combines the advantages of fused silica optical
sensing with advanced additive manufacturing technologies. The monolithic fused silica structure
provides excellent thermal and mechanical stability, while the embedded IFPI sensor enables
accurate temperature measurements under harsh operating conditions. The manufacturing
approach also demonstrates the broader capability of the IASM platform to directly integrate
optical sensors into functional structural components, thereby establishing a foundation for the
development of sensor-embedded smart parts for condition-based monitoring applications in steam
turbines and other harsh-environment energy systems.

PRESSURE SENSOR

 The development of the pressure sensor module underwent several design iterations
throughout the project as the research team sought to balance sensing performance,
manufacturability, packaging constraints, and compatibility with GE's Smart Ring architecture.
Three different pressure sensor concepts were investigated and evaluated, each representing a
progressive refinement toward a practical solution for harsh-environment steam turbine
applications. The evolution of the pressure sensor design highlights the challenges associated with
integrating optical pressure sensing, compact packaging, and additive manufacturing technologies
within the dimensional constraints imposed by the Smart Ring instrumentation track.

Design #1. Additively Manufactured All-Glass Sensor

 The first pressure sensor design was based on a technology previously developed and
demonstrated by the Clemson research team. The sensor employed an Extrinsic Fabry-Perot
Interferometer (EFPI) configuration formed between the end face of an optical fiber and a thin
fused silica diaphragm. Pressure-induced deformation of the diaphragm changes the Fabry-Perot
cavity length, which can be measured through interferometric analysis of the reflected optical
signal.

 The sensor package was fabricated using Clemson University's fused silica additive
manufacturing technology. A transparent fused silica housing was directly printed onto a thin fused
silica substrate that simultaneously served as the pressure-sensitive diaphragm. As shown in Figure
4(a), the sensor consists of a fused silica diaphragm, an optical fiber, and a 3D-printed fused silica
housing that positions the fiber in close proximity to the diaphragm to form an extrinsic Fabry-
Perot cavity. The housing geometry consisted of a conical section transitioning into a hollow
cylindrical tube, as illustrated in Figure 4(b) and Figure 4(c). The conical geometry was selected
to provide mechanical support while gradually reducing the housing diameter to accommodate
optical fiber insertion.

Design #2. Lens-Prism-Substrate Based EFPI Sensor Module

 Although the design successfully demonstrated the feasibility of combining additive
manufacturing and optical interferometric pressure sensing within a monolithic fused silica
structure, the sensor package was not readily compatible with the packaging requirements and
dimensional constraints of the Smart Ring instrumentation track. To improve Smart Ring
compatibility, a second pressure sensor architecture was developed based on an EFPI formed
between an optical fiber and a thin fused silica pressure diaphragm. Similar to the first design,
pressure-induced diaphragm deflection changes the Fabry-Perot cavity length, resulting in
measurable wavelength shifts in the interference spectrum. However, this design introduced an
integrated optical beam-steering assembly to accommodate the Smart Ring geometry.

 Because optical fibers are routed circumferentially within the Smart Ring instrumentation
track while pressure measurements must be performed radially toward the turbine interior, a
mechanism was required to redirect the optical beam by 90°. To achieve this, a compact lens-prism

assembly was proposed. The assembly consisted of a plano-convex fused silica lens fused to one
face of a right-angle fused silica prism. The lens collimates the optical beam emerging from the
fiber, while the prism redirects the beam toward the pressure-sensitive diaphragm.

 The optical fiber was housed within a fused silica capillary tube to improve mechanical
robustness and facilitate precise optical alignment. For the proposed design, a 3 mm diameter fused
silica plano-convex lens with a focal length of approximately 6 mm and a 3 mm right-angle prism
were selected. The smaller optical components allowed the overall module dimensions to remain
within the Smart Ring design envelope while maintaining acceptable optical performance.

 A significant design consideration involved the manufacturability of the lens-prism
assembly. When the lens and prism possess identical dimensions, only a limited area remains
available for deposition of fused silica paste used during laser welding, as illustrated in Figure 5.
Conversely, when a smaller lens is used, a larger exposed prism surface becomes available for
welding material deposition, as shown in Figure 6, although alignment becomes more challenging.
Precision optical positioning techniques were therefore required to ensure accurate lens placement
prior to laser fusion.

Design #3. Laser Micromachined Fiber-Optic EFPI Sensor

 Because of the manufacturing and alignment complexities associated with the lens-prism
design, a simplified all-fiber sensor architecture was ultimately selected. The final pressure sensor
design, shown in Figure 13, consists of a laser-micromachined inline Extrinsic Fabry-Perot
Interferometer (EFPI) formed directly within a single-mode optical fiber. The sensor incorporates
an air cavity and a thin fused silica diaphragm created using precision laser micromachining,
eliminating the need for separate optical components while significantly reducing package size
and manufacturing complexity.

 The sensing principle remains based on Fabry-Perot interferometry. External pressure
causes the thin fused silica diaphragm at the fiber tip to deflect toward the internal air cavity,
changing the cavity length and thereby modifying the interference pattern generated by reflections
from the cavity interfaces. Pressure is subsequently determined by monitoring the wavelength shift
or phase change in the reflected optical signal.

 This design offered several important advantages over the previous sensor concepts. First,
the sensing element is entirely contained within the optical fiber, dramatically reducing sensor size
and simplifying packaging. Second, elimination of discrete optical components reduces alignment
requirements and manufacturing complexity. Third, the all-fiber architecture provides excellent
thermal compatibility, mechanical robustness, and resistance to harsh environmental conditions.
Finally, the compact form factor greatly facilitates integration into Smart Ring sensor modules and
other embedded sensing platforms.

 The optical path design includes input fiber with a light source, lenses for focusing, prisms

Optical Design Targets

 Based on the optical modeling, ray-tracing analyses, and performance simulations
conducted during this project, a set of design requirements and performance targets were
established for the blade tip-timing sensor module. These requirements served as the basis for
evaluating candidate optical architectures and guided the selection and optimization of optical
components for both the excitation and collection optical paths. Particular emphasis was placed on
achieving reliable blade detection performance while satisfying the stringent dimensional
constraints imposed by the Smart Ring platform and the harsh operating environment of steam
turbines.

1) General design targets for the blade tip timing modules:

 The primary objective of the blade tip-timing sensor is to accurately detect the arrival time
of individual turbine blades as they pass through the sensing region. Because blade vibration

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 53

measurements are derived from small variations in blade arrival times, the sensor must achieve
extremely high temporal resolution. Based on the anticipated turbine rotational speeds and blade
vibration amplitudes, the target edge-detection and timing accuracy was established in the
nanosecond regime. This level of precision is necessary to resolve blade vibration signatures and
identify subtle changes in blade dynamic behavior associated with fatigue, resonance, or structural
degradation.

 In addition to timing accuracy, sufficient optical signal strength must be maintained to
ensure reliable blade detection under varying operating conditions. A minimum detectable
received optical power corresponding to at least 1% of the transmitted excitation power was
established as the performance target. This requirement provides adequate signal-to-noise ratio for
robust signal processing while accounting for optical losses resulting from beam divergence,
surface scattering, alignment tolerances, and environmental contamination.

 The sensing geometry was also optimized through simulation and system-level analysis.
Results indicated that the nominal operating distance between the blade tip and the sensor module
should be approximately 2.5 mm, with an allowable variation of ±0.5 mm. This range
accommodates expected manufacturing tolerances, installation variations, and blade motion while
maintaining acceptable optical performance. Furthermore, the optical spot size at the blade tip was
targeted at 1.0 ± 0.2 mm. This spot size provides a balance between spatial resolution and optical
signal collection efficiency. A smaller spot improves edge-detection accuracy but is more sensitive
to alignment errors, while a larger spot reduces spatial resolution and may degrade timing precision.

2) Targets specific to laser-assisted fiber/lens/prism assembly design:

 To ensure reliable operation within the turbine environment, the optical components were
required to be integrated into a mechanically robust and compact assembly. The optical fibers,
lenses, prisms, and supporting structures were therefore designed to be packaged into a single
integrated optical assembly. This approach minimizes alignment drift, improves mechanical
stability, simplifies installation, and enhances reproducibility during manufacturing and
deployment.

 Separate optimization criteria were established for the excitation and collection optical
paths. The excitation path was designed to efficiently collimate and direct light from the optical
fiber toward the turbine blade tip while maintaining the required spot size and detection distance.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 54

The collection path was optimized to maximize the amount of reflected light captured from the
blade surface and coupled back into the receiving optical fiber. Because the reflected signal
strength directly influences timing accuracy and measurement reliability, maximizing collection
efficiency was a key design objective.

 Several candidate optical configurations were investigated to satisfy these requirements.
One approach utilized a lens-prism assembly consisting of a plano-convex lens and a right-angle
prism. The lens collimates the optical beam emerging from the fiber while the prism redirects the
beam from the circumferential direction of the Smart Ring to the radial direction required for blade
monitoring. An alternative approach employed an angled ball-lensed fiber in which the beam
steering and focusing functions are integrated directly into the fiber tip. This configuration offers
the potential for reduced size and simplified packaging.

 For the ball-lensed fiber design, simulations indicated that tilt angles between 3° and 9°
provided acceptable beam steering performance while maintaining efficient optical coupling. The
use of ball-lensed fibers offers opportunities for further miniaturization of the sensor module
because the separate lens and prism components can be eliminated. However, the reduced design
flexibility and tighter fabrication tolerances associated with ball-lensed fibers required careful
evaluation relative to the lens-prism approach.

Additional Optical Component Conclusions

 Based on the simulation results, the optical design was refined to satisfy three key
performance requirements: (1) a maximum collection efficiency greater than 10%, (2) a sensing
envelope exceeding 4 mm, and (3) collection efficiency that is relatively insensitive to component
alignment and assembly tolerances.

 The lens-prism assembly successfully met the first two requirements by providing high
optical collection efficiency and a sufficiently large sensing envelope. However, further analysis
revealed that the collection efficiency was highly sensitive to small alignment errors between
optical components, which could negatively impact manufacturing yield and long-term sensor
reliability.

 To improve alignment tolerance, several optical enhancement approaches were
investigated, including optical diffusers, gradient-index (GRIN) lenses, and tapered optical
waveguides. Optical diffusers were found to be impractical because the additional space required
exceeded the dimensional constraints of the sensor housing. GRIN lenses provided limited
improvement in alignment sensitivity and introduced concerns regarding performance degradation
under elevated temperatures. In contrast, tapered optical waveguides demonstrated the ability to

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 70

significantly increase light collection while maintaining compact dimensions and reducing
sensitivity to component misalignment.

 Based on this evaluation, tapered optical waveguides were selected as the preferred
solution for the receiving optical path. Their incorporation into the sensor design improved
collection robustness, enhanced manufacturability, and enabled the optical assembly to meet all
established performance requirements while remaining compatible with the size constraints of the
Smart Ring sensor module.

Tapered optical waveguide geometry optimization

 The original tapered optical waveguide’s geometry was 3 mm long with a 1 mm D entrance
face and a 0.1 mm D exit face, as shown in Figure 36(a). We found that this geometry was difficult
to fabricate. We proposed a longer tapered optical waveguide to solve the problem. The new
geometry was 5 mm long with a 1 mm D entrance face and a 0.125 mm D exit face, as shown in
Figure 36(b). The end face was enlarged to meet the diameter of the optical fiber. The performance
of the new design was assessed through simulations. The maximum collection efficiency was 27%.
The width of the sensing envelope exceeded 4.5 mm. And the minimum alignment tolerance of
the taper’s horizontal positioning was larger than 100 μm.

Optical Transparency

 The laser melting process, which is applied to melt the glass paste during 3D printing, was
optimized to improve the optical transparency of 3D-printed fused silica glasses. To study the
effect of laser processing parameters on glass transparency, the glass paste was processed by
different laser power at a fixed scanning speed of 1 mm/s and a fixed spot size of 2 mm. As shown
in Figure 49(a), the transparency of the laser-melted fused silica glass was improved by increasing
the laser power from 12.5 W to 15 W. As shown in the cross-section SEM images of the printed

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 79

glasses (Figure 49(b)), the increasing of the laser power reduced the porosity of the laser-melted
glasses, resulting in the improvement of the optical transparency. This indicates that higher laser
power induces higher temperature on the glass paste so that denser fused silica glass can be
obtained. In addition, the flexibility of the IASM system in material properties control during the
fabrication process is demonstrated for fused silica glass fabrication.

Figure 49: Pictures of the 3D printed fused silica glass and the cross-section SEM images of the printed
glass melted by different laser power (a) 12.5 W, (b) 15 W (Scanning speed: 1 mm/s, Spot size: ~2 mm).

 The transmission spectrum of the 3D-printed fused silica glass was measured to estimate
the optical transparency. The surface of the 3D-printed fused silica glass was polished to optical
finishing before the measurement. The transmission spectrum was recorded by a UV-Vis
spectrometer in the wavelength range between 200 nm and 2000 nm. A commercial fused silica
glass product, the Corning 7980 fused silica substrate, was also measured in the same optical range
for comparison.

 Figure 50 shows the transmission spectrum of the measured glass substrates. The light
transmission percentage of the 3D-printed fused silica glass is ~90% when the wavelength is larger
than ~650 nm. However, as the wavelength decreased from 650 nm to 200 nm, the transmission
quickly decreases and reaches 0% at ~280 nm. For the Corning 7980 substrate, the light
transmission percentage keeps at ~97% throughout the measured wavelength range. The optical

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 80

loss in the UV to visible region of the 3D-printed fused silica glass is probably caused by the
micro-pores inside the 3D-printed glass, which scatter the light at this wavelength range. In the red
to infrared region, the 3D-printed glass shows comparable optical transmission to the commercial
fused silica glass product, indicating that the 3D-printed fused silica glass can fulfill optical
functions such prism and lens needed in the proposed sensor modules.

Laser kerf issue

 When cutting a rectangular slot, the width of the bottom of a cut slot is smaller than the
width at the top, as shown in Figure 62. This is due to the laser beam’s triangular shape at the focal
point which, at the edges of a cut, is partially blocked or scattered by the sidewalls and less power
is focused on the layer of material to be cut, therefore less material is removed. For multi-layer
cuts, this results in a smaller 3D shape being cut. To counter this effect, a taper angle on the
sidewalls and rounded corners have been included in the CAD model. This angle was measured
from the cut in Figure 62 and placed on all four sidewalls in the design, as shown in Figure 63.
This ensured all the features of the design were cut. The addition of tapered walls in the model
accounted for the kerf effect to ensure that optical components would not be mechanically affected,
shown in Figure 64.

Positional accuracy

 Two other fabrication techniques have also been used to improve the laser machining
process. The first was using edge finding for the design of the sensor within our motion control
software. The motion control software is used to control the translation stages and lasers for the
manufacturing process. A recipe with tool paths is designed in the software and executed. The top
left corner of a glass substrate is used as a reference point from which laser cutting tool paths are
placed, as seen in Figure 65. A camera is first used to find the coordinates of the reference point
within our defined workspace in the motion control software. It is important to use a reference
point with our system because we have determined the geometrical coordinate offsets between the

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 90

camera and lasers. Additionally, it allows us to easily place tool paths on the substrate with
geometrical reference to any point. For example, if we determine that the top left corner lies at the
x- and y-coordinates of (100, 25), we can input them as constants with names such as XRef and
YRef. Then, when we are determining what coordinates to place the laser tool path at, we can just
add a numerical offset from the reference constants to place the tool path where we want it. The
coordinates for the laser cutting would then look something like (XRef + 10, YRef – 10) to place
the path 10 units to the right of the reference point in the x-direction, and 10 units below the
reference point in the y-direction. This is extremely important for picosecond laser cutting where
we want to ensure cutting accuracy meets the requirement (~50µm).

Laser micro-welding technology

 Laser micro-welding technology has been developed using the IASM system. We have
applied this technology to fuse the fused silica optical components, such as lenses, prisms and
optical fibers, to the bulk glass components for optical assembly prototypes fabrication. The optical
performance of the fabricated prototypes such as transmission loss and divergence angle have been
measured.

 The laser micro-welding technology is developed by applying the glass 3D printing process.
Three kinds of welding strategies, shown in Figure 66, have been designed to meet the fabrication
requirements of the optical sensor modules designs. To fix optical components or assemblies on

the lens-prism assemblies, the similar welding process is performed at the connection between the

the optical fiber is first inserted into the tube, and a CO2 laser is applied to soften the glass tube to

3.3.3 Subtask 3.2 Fabricate and package the temperature sensor modules

 The temperature sensor module was fabricated using the Integrated Additive and
Subtractive Manufacturing (IASM) process. This process combines fused silica glass 3D printing,

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 94

picosecond laser micromachining, CO₂ laser melting, and CO₂ laser bonding to embed an optical
fiber Intrinsic Fabry-Perot Interferometer (IFPI) temperature sensor inside a fused silica glass
structure. First, several layers of fused silica glass were printed onto a fused silica substrate. During
each printing step, glass paste was extruded along a programmed toolpath using a precision paste
dispenser. A CO₂ laser was then used to melt and consolidate the deposited paste within each
printed layer and between adjacent layers. This laser-assisted glass 3D printing process produced
a fused silica base structure for sensor integration.

 After the initial glass layers were printed, a picosecond laser micromachining system was
used to fabricate a microchannel for optical fiber placement. Compared with conventional glass
3D printing, which typically provides dimensional accuracy on the order of tens to hundreds of
micrometers, the integrated picosecond laser process enables micron-scale feature fabrication.
This high dimensional accuracy is essential for embedding optical fiber sensors because the sensor
features and fiber diameter are on the micron scale.

 After the microchannel was fabricated, a femtosecond-laser-machined IFPI sensor was
placed and fixed inside the channel. The IFPI region was located using a red laser pointer coupled
into the fiber. As shown in Figure 70 (a), the two red spots indicate the positions of the two IFPI
reflection mirrors, confirming that the sensing region was properly positioned inside the laser-
machined channel. Once the IFPI sensor was positioned, additional fused silica glass paste layers
were deposited over the channel and embedded fiber. To prevent sensor damage during CO₂ laser
melting, multiple paste layers were first deposited above the fiber before laser irradiation. The CO₂
laser was then scanned along the channel direction to melt the top paste layers, bond the new
material to the underlying printed glass, and secure the optical fiber within the surrounding fused
silica matrix.

 In this demonstration, five glass layers with a total thickness of approximately 500 µm and
a length of approximately 2 cm were first printed and melted onto the fused silica substrate. A
sensor placement channel with dimensions of approximately 150 µm × 300 µm was then fabricated
using picosecond laser micromachining. After the IFPI sensor was fixed in the channel, two
additional glass paste layers were deposited and consolidated by CO₂ laser irradiation to complete
the embedding process.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 95

 Cross-sectional SEM images of the fabricated module are shown in Figure 70 (c)–(e). The
optical fiber was surrounded by the 3D-printed glass structure, confirming successful sensor
embedding. The images also show close contact between the optical fiber and the printed glass,
with no visible gap at the fiber-glass interface. This result indicates that the optical fiber maintained
its structural integrity during the embedding process and that effective bonding was achieved
between the fiber and surrounding fused silica. A void was observed beneath the fiber due to
incomplete filling of the micromachined channel; this feature can be reduced in future fabrication
by optimizing the picosecond laser ablation parameters and paste deposition process.

 During CO₂ laser melting, the embedded IFPI sensor was connected to an optical sensing
interrogator for real-time monitoring. The interrogator recorded the interference spectra and
calculated the IFPI cavity length every 1.2 seconds. As shown in Figure 70 (f), the cavity length
varied rapidly during laser melting, reflecting interactions among the glass paste, laser irradiation,
polymer burnout, paste shrinkage, and fiber-glass bonding. After the module cooled to room
temperature, the cavity length returned to nearly its original value, indicating that the IFPI sensor
survived the CO₂ laser melting process without permanent damage.

 A second CO₂ laser melting pass was performed to improve consolidation and bonding
between the optical fiber and the surrounding fused silica. The second pass used a slightly lower
laser power while maintaining the same scanning speed and spot size. The cavity length again
changed during laser irradiation but returned close to its original value after cooling. The smaller
cavity length deviation observed during the second pass was attributed to reduced paste shrinkage,
increased glass densification after the first pass, and improved bonding between the optical fiber
and the surrounding glass.

 Figure 70(g) compares the IFPI spectra before and after the complete fabrication process.
The sensor retained a clean interference pattern after embedding, with acceptable optical loss and
no significant distortion of the optical signal. The observed loss was likely caused by minor fiber
micro-bending introduced during laser processing and by surface roughness or porosity in the
surrounding printed glass. These losses can be further reduced by optimizing CO₂ laser scanning
parameters, including the use of a two-step heating strategy in which the paste is first preheated at
lower laser power and then consolidated at higher power. Nevertheless, the measured fringe
visibility of approximately 16 dB was sufficient for temperature sensing applications.

3.3.7 Subtask 3.4 Develop superhydrophobic coatings to minimize condensation effects

 The aim is to minimize the condensation effects of the liquid inside the turbine during
measurement. Rare-earth oxides (REOs) is a good candidate for this objective because of their
hydrophobic nature and high temperature tolerance. Owing to the unique electronic structure of
rare-earth ions, the hydrogen bonding between the coating surface and the interfacial water
molecules is minimized, resulting in hydrophobicity of REOs materials.

Materials and methods

 The coating materials selected for the optical sensor modules is cerium oxide (CeO2),
which is prepared using sol-gel processing. Ce(AC)3-ACAC sol was used as the precursor
materials for the CeO2 coating, and 2 wt% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and 1.5M Acetic acid (HAc)
solution were applied to the sol composition to adjust the viscosity for the coating process.

 The prepared sol composition was dip-coated on fused silica glass substrates. After drying
in air at 80ºC for 24 hours, the coated substrates were first heat-treated at 300ºC and 400ºC for 2
hours in an electric furnace to improve the crystallinity, and then further treated at high vacuum
(10-6 torr) environment for different duration. Contact angle measurement using water was
performed on the obtained samples to estimate the hydrophobicity.

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 105

Characterization of the CeO2 coating

 Since the contact angle is proportional to the thickness of the coating, different dipping
speeds (100, 200, 250, 500 mm/min) were applied in the dip-coating process to obtain the highest
crack-free thickness of the CeO2 coating. As shown in Figure 83, the highest crack-free thickness
have been obtained when the dip-coating speed is set at 250 mm/min. No cracks occurred on the
surface of this sample after heat treatment at 400ºC.

3.4.5 Subtask 4.3 Test and evaluate the blade tip timing/clearance sensor modules

Testing during fabrication process development

 Based on the simulation results in previous reports, the fabrication and alignment error
should be confined within 100 μm to ensure the fabricated sensor meets the prescribed
requirements. However, fabrication errors were inevitable and could accumulate in every step.
Thus, we proposed a fabrication roadmap to divide the whole fabrication process into two major
steps: fabrication of the excitation path and the receiving path. The excitation path was made first,
and the receiving path would be fabricated and aligned accordingly to compensate for the
fabrication error accumulated in the excitation path.

 The fabrication roadmap is described below:

 1. Fabricate the excitation path.

 2. Place a mirror under the excitation path to locate the reflected light spot.

 3. Fabricate and align the receiving path according to the reflected light spot.

 4. Assemble the excitation path and receiving path together to form a prototype. Test the
prototype under laboratory conditions.

 After the completion of work in Q11, the design of the sensor was re-evaluated and updated

glass block with two identical optical paths and optical fibers. Optical fibers deliver light to the
input ports of the block. It is then collimated and redirected to the output port of the block. Light
exits the block and travels towards a blade, reflects off, and returns to collection optical fibers. The
sensor design and optical beam path are shown in Figure 154 below.

 An input port of the block consists of a slanted plane with convex lens on the surface. The
purpose is to both collimate and refract light to create a V-shaped beam path between the output
port and collection optical fiber. Raytracing simulations were performed to determine an
appropriate angle for the slant to meet sensing envelope requirements and to ensure that light will
have an unobstructed path to the fibers as well as meet angular acceptance of the fiber. The size
requirements are that the entire radial probe fit must within a 5mm diameter tube. Optical fibers
placed on either side of the glass block will collect light reflected from the turbine blade. From
simulations, light should strike the blade at an angle of around 17-18° and strike optical fiber at an
angle of around 17-18° which is within the acceptance range of the fiber which is around 30°.

3.6 Task 6.0 Develop plans for technology transfer and maturation

3.6.1 Technology Transfer Plan

1. Technology Overview

 This project successfully developed and demonstrated three optical sensing technologies
for harsh-environment monitoring of steam turbines:

 1) Fiber-Optic Temperature Sensor – An intrinsic Fabry-Perot interferometric (IFPI)
 temperature sensor fabricated using femtosecond laser micromachining and integrated into
 additively manufactured fused silica sensor modules.

 2) Fiber-Optic Pressure Sensor – An optical pressure sensor based on Fabry-Perot
 interferometry and laser micromachined optical fiber structures capable of operating in
 elevated temperature and pressure environments.

 3) Optical Blade Tip-Timing Sensor – A non-contact optical sensing system fabricated
 using Clemson University's Integrated Additive and Subtractive Manufacturing (IASM)
 technology for blade tip timing, blade vibration, and blade clearance measurements.

 Together, these technologies establish a multifunctional sensing platform capable of
providing real-time, in situ measurements of critical turbine operating parameters, including
temperature, pressure, blade vibration, blade tip timing, and blade tip clearance. The technologies
directly support condition-based monitoring (CBM), predictive maintenance, and digitalization
efforts within the power generation industry.

4.0 CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES

 The primary objective of this project was to develop, manufacture, and validate optical

sensor modules for in situ monitoring of temperature, pressure, and blade tip timing in steam

turbines. Through a collaborative effort between Clemson University and General Electric (GE),

the project successfully advanced several key sensing and manufacturing technologies that address

long-standing challenges associated with condition-based monitoring of steam turbines operating

in harsh environments. The work combined advanced optical sensing technologies, innovative

fused silica manufacturing methods, and novel sensor packaging concepts to establish a foundation

for next-generation turbine health monitoring systems.

 The project successfully completed the design, development, and laboratory validation of

three complementary sensing technologies: a fiber-optic temperature sensor, a fiber-optic pressure

sensor, and an optical blade tip-timing sensor. Significant progress was achieved in Task 3 through

the design and optimization of the sensing architectures, optical assemblies, sensor packaging

approaches, and Smart Ring integration concepts. Extensive modeling, simulation, and optical

design studies were performed to establish sensor performance requirements, optimize optical

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 191

component configurations, and ensure compatibility with the geometric and environmental

constraints of steam turbine applications.

 Task 4 was successfully completed through the fabrication of multiple sensor prototypes

using Clemson University's Integrated Additive and Subtractive Manufacturing (IASM)

technology. The project demonstrated the unique capability of combining glass additive

manufacturing, picosecond laser micromachining, CO₂ laser polishing, laser welding, and optical

assembly techniques within a unified manufacturing platform. These integrated processes enabled

the fabrication of complex fused silica sensor modules with embedded optical fibers, precision

optical features, and integrated beam-steering components that would be difficult or impossible to

produce using conventional manufacturing approaches.

 A major technical accomplishment of the project was the successful demonstration of

sensor embedding within additively manufactured fused silica structures. Optical fiber sensors

were integrated into monolithic glass components while maintaining sensor functionality and

structural integrity. The work demonstrated that laser-assisted additive manufacturing can be used

not only to fabricate structural components but also to create smart components with embedded

sensing capabilities. This represents an important advancement in the development of sensor-

integrated structures for harsh-environment applications.

 The temperature sensing technology demonstrated the successful embedding of

femtosecond-laser-fabricated intrinsic Fabry-Perot interferometer (IFPI) sensors within fused

silica substrates using the IASM process. The embedded sensors maintained their optical integrity

throughout the manufacturing process and exhibited stable, repeatable responses during high-

temperature testing. Laboratory validation demonstrated reliable operation up to 1000 °C,

 DE-FE0031826 – Closeout Report Page - 192

confirming the suitability of the sensing approach for harsh-environment turbine monitoring

applications.

 The pressure sensing effort investigated multiple sensor architectures and manufacturing

approaches, including additively manufactured extrinsic Fabry-Perot interferometer (EFPI)

structures, integrated lens-prism optical assemblies, and laser-micromachined all-fiber pressure

sensors. Through iterative design and evaluation, the project identified sensing configurations that

balance measurement performance, packaging constraints, and manufacturability. The resulting

pressure sensor concepts demonstrated the feasibility of optical pressure measurements in compact

sensor packages suitable for future turbine integration.

 The blade tip-timing sensor development resulted in the design and fabrication of a

compact optical sensing module capable of measuring blade arrival times, blade vibration, and

blade tip clearance. Comprehensive optical modeling and Zemax ray-tracing analyses were

performed to optimize both excitation and collection optical paths. Multiple optical architectures

were evaluated, including lens-prism assemblies, ball-lensed fibers, optical diffusers, GRIN lenses,

and tapered optical waveguides. The final design successfully met the performance requirements

for signal collection efficiency, sensing envelope, and packaging constraints while maintaining

compatibility with GE's Smart Ring architecture. In addition, the project developed a radial probe

version of the blade tip-timing sensor to accommodate evolving industrial requirements and

support GE's existing turbine testing methodologies.

 Laboratory testing completed under Task 5 demonstrated that all sensor technologies

achieved their intended performance objectives and figures of merit. The temperature, pressure,

and blade tip-timing sensors were successfully evaluated under controlled laboratory conditions,

validating their fundamental sensing principles, optical performance, and packaging approaches.

The testing results confirmed the viability of the proposed sensing technologies for future

condition-based monitoring applications in steam turbines.

 The project also produced several important advances in manufacturing science and

engineering. New approaches were developed for laser processing of fused silica, including

precision picosecond laser cutting, controlled CO₂ laser resurfacing, optical-quality surface

generation, laser-assisted optical assembly, and monolithic optical component fabrication. The

integration of additive and subtractive manufacturing processes enabled improved dimensional

accuracy, greater geometric flexibility, and enhanced optical quality compared to conventional

manufacturing methods. In particular, the ability to fabricate monolithic fused silica optical

structures with embedded sensing functionality represents a significant advancement for the

broader field of smart structures and embedded sensing systems.

 Although the project successfully achieved laboratory-scale validation of the sensing

technologies, the originally planned industrial-scale turbine testing activities could not be

completed within the project period. As a result, full validation of sensor performance under

representative turbine operating conditions remains an important next step toward technology

maturation. Future efforts should focus on integrating the sensor modules into Smart Ring and

radial probe configurations, conducting long-duration testing under elevated temperature, pressure,

and vibration conditions, and performing demonstrations in industrial turbine test facilities and

operating power plants.

 The technologies developed under this project have significant potential beyond the

immediate application of coal-fired steam turbines. The combination of harsh-environment optical

sensing and additive manufacturing of fused silica structures can be extended to gas turbines,

nuclear power systems, concentrated solar power plants, aerospace propulsion systems, chemical

processing facilities, and other industrial environments where real-time monitoring of temperature,

pressure, vibration, and structural health is required. Furthermore, the IASM platform developed

during this project provides a versatile manufacturing framework for the fabrication of sensor-

embedded smart components across a wide range of engineering applications.

 Looking forward, the integration of optical sensing technologies with digital condition

monitoring platforms, predictive maintenance algorithms, and digital twin technologies offers a

promising pathway toward intelligent energy systems. The ability to directly measure critical

turbine operating parameters in real time can enable earlier fault detection, improved maintenance

planning, enhanced reliability, and reduced operating costs. Continued maturation of the sensing

technologies, manufacturing processes, and integration methods developed in this project will

contribute to the realization of smart turbine systems capable of supporting the future needs of the

power generation industry.

 Overall, this project successfully demonstrated the feasibility of additively manufactured

optical sensor modules for harsh-environment turbine monitoring and established key enabling

technologies for future deployment of embedded sensing systems in energy infrastructure. The

accomplishments achieved provide a strong foundation for continued technology development,

industrial validation, and eventual commercialization of advanced optical sensing solutions for

condition-based monitoring and asset management applications.
