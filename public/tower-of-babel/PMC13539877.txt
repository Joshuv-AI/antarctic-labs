The role of STORCH infections in shaping neurodevelopment: a longitudinal case-control study in preterm children
Authors: Luana Marques, Alessandra Bombarda Muller, Carolina Panceri, Renato S. Procianoy, Rita C. Silveira, Nadia Cristina Valentini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital infections, often asymptomatic for both mother and fetus, can affect neurodevelopment. This study aimed to analyze neurodevelopmental outcomes and associated risk factors in preterm children with and without congenital STORCH infections. A case-control study was conducted, with children assessed longitudinally. A retrospective case-control study was conducted including 248 preterm children. The control group showed higher cognitive and language scores than the STORCH case-group. The STORCH case-group had a significant decline in cognitive scores from 6 to 18 months (p<0.05), although their motor scores improved from 6 to 24 months (p<0.05). Risk factors for neurodevelopment in the STORCH case-group included APGAR, NICU seizures and stay, familyincome, sepsis, and twin birth (beta 0.27–0.49). For the control-group, risk factors included NICU seizures and stay, APGAR, family income, sepsis, mechanical ventilation, maternal education, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (beta 0.12–0.31). Children with STORCH infections showed poorer cognitive and language development. Early prevention of STORCH infections is crucial to prevent negative developmental outcomes.

Introduction

Congenital infections are those acquired by the fetus during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding [1]. The infections caused by syphilis, toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex are collectively known as STORCH. These infections are a meaningful public health concern worldwide, with approximately two million pregnant women infected each year. However, less than 10% of the cases are diagnosed and treated [2]. The burden of STORCH infections is particularly high in developing countries, where approximately 90% of cases occur [3]. Brazil, due to its large population and high prevalence of syphilis during pregnancy, is one of fifteen countries identified as a priority for controlling congenital syphilis [4]. Between 2011 and 2017, in Brazil,17.6% increases in congenital syphilis cases, followed by a period of stability, and further 16.7% increases in 2021, accompanied by a rise in congenital syphilis deaths among children [5].

Many congenital infections are asymptomatic for both the mother and fetus, but when left untreated, they can have severe consequences. If detected and treated early in pregnancy, the risk of vertical transmission and associated complications can be significantly reduced [6–8]. However, untreated infections may lead to a range of clinical manifestations, including hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, central nervous system damage, visual and auditory impairments, chorioretinitis, and intrauterine growth restriction [7, 9]. Besides, the increase in rate of prematurity and neonatal morbidity and mortality has been associated with these infections [1, 3]. Children born preterm are already at higher risk of brain injury and long-term neurodevelopmental delays[10], and the presence of a congenital infection may further exacerbate these risks.

Despite the known associations between congenital infections and negative health outcomes, including higher rates of prematurity and neonatal morbidity, the long-term effects on children’s neurodevelopment remains poorly understood. The limited available research, particularly in the context of preterm children, often focused on short-term outcomes [3], with little attention given to how these infections impact cognitive, language and motor development beyond the neonatal period. This gap in understanding underscores the need for further investigation. This study aims to fill this gap by assessing neurodevelopment outcomes of preterm children with and without congenital STORCH infections during their first two years of life. Additionally, it will analyze longitudinally the risk factors associated with neurodevelopment in preterm children with and without STORCH infections. It is hypothesized that preterm children with congenital STORCH infections will exhibit lower cognitive, language, and motor developmental scores, during the first two years of life, compared to those without these infections.

Methods

Study design, context, and participants

A retrospective case-control study was conducted to investigate how exposure to STORCH infections might affect the longitudinal neurodevelopment of preterm children. This study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines; a clinical trial registration number was not applicable for the present study. The data supporting the findings of this study are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Hospital de Clínicas de Porto Alegre (IRB No. 20190321), in compliance with Resolution No. 466/12 of the Brazilian National Health Council, which regulates research involving human participants. As all participants were infants under 24 months of age, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians prior to participation. This study was financially supported by CAPES (Coordination for the Improvement of Higher Education Personnel) and CNPq (National Council for Scientific and Technological Development).

The number of cases was defined by all available children with confirmed congenital STORCH infections within the cohort. Because these infections were relatively uncommon, all eligible cases were included. A 1:7 case–control ratio was adopted to improve statistical efficiency and precision of effect estimates while maintaining analytical feasibility, based on the prevalence of STORCH infections from a previous study [11]. Although gains in statistical efficiency tend to decrease beyond case–control ratios of approximately 1:4, the inclusion of additional controls may still improve the precision of effect estimates when the number of cases is limited [12–14].

Sample size estimation was performed using G-Power, considering ANOVA with repeated measures (for within-and between-subject interactions), an effect size of 0.25, an alpha error probability of 0.05, and a power of 0.90. Based on this, a minimum sample size of 231 children was required. This calculation assumed balanced group sizes and should therefore be interpreted as a conservative minimum reference rather than reflecting the final case–control allocation. The final sample exceeded this requirement.

Additionally, sample adequacy for regression analyses was considered based on commonly used rules of thumb regarding the number of observations per predictor [15, 16]. Given that the final regression models included a limited number of predictors (approximately 5–7 variables), the available sample size was considered acceptable for parsimonious modeling, although caution is warranted due to the relatively small number of STORCH cases [17].

The eligible participants were preterm children (gestational age - GA < 32 weeks; n = 417) born between 2015 and 2022. These children were followed by a multidisciplinary team at an outpatient clinic that provides clinical services to children born at less than 32 weeks of gestation and/or with a birth weight under 1,500 g in a large public hospital in southern Brazil. The clinical follow-up on children includes consultation with a multidisciplinary team comprising neonatologists, speech pathologists, neurologists, physiotherapists, nutritionists, physical education professionals, and social workers. At the clinic, children were enrolled in monthly follow-up appointments until they reached 6 months of corrected age, bi-monthly visits from 7 to 12 months of corrected age, quarterly visits from 13 to 24 months of corrected age, and annual visits up to age 5, following the hospital’s routine practice.

Children (n = 101) diagnosed by neonatologists with chronic diseases, congenital malformations, genetic syndromes, or cerebral palsy were excluded from the study, as were children who either received interventions or missed longitudinal assessments. Out of the 316 children, 31 (9.8%) had congenital STORCH infections and composed the case-group, 19 with syphilis (61.3%), 5 with toxoplasmosis (16.1%), 5 with herpes simplex (16.1%), and 2 with cytomegalovirus (6.5%); no cases of rubella or Zika virus were diagnosed. The remaining 285 children were eligible for the control group. To maintain a 1:7 case-to-control ratio, 217 controls without STORCH infections were required according to prevalence of STORCH in preterm children[11]. These 285 children without STORCH infections were matched with the case group based on age, sex, and assessment data. Thus, the final study sample consisted of 248 preterm children (31 in the case group and 217 in the control group), who were assessed for motor, cognitive, and language development at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months of corrected age.

Instruments

Sample characterization and medical records

Parents completed a questionnaire about economic and social information, while the children’s birth and clinical history were obtained from the hospital medical records. This included information such as sex, date of birth, gestational age, birth weight, length, and head circumference, APGAR score, length of stay in the NICU, and diagnoses of conditions like bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD - defined as oxygen dependence at 36 weeks of corrected gestational age [18]), early and late sepsis, antibiotic use, seizures, peri-intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL) - the last two diagnosed through serial cerebral ultrasounds and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) - and the heel prick test for STORCH infections. The diagnosis of neurosyphilis is established based on clinical findings and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities, including a reactive CSF-Venereal Disease Research Laboratory test, along with supportive findings such as elevated CSF protein levels, following hospital standardized protocols. Maternal factors, including the number of prenatal consultations, history of preeclampsia, and clinical exam results, were also recorded.

Bayley scale of infant and toddler development 3rd edition - BSITD-III

The BSITD-III [19] was used to assess children’s motor, cognitive, and language development. The BSITD-III is a widely recognized tool and a gold standard for evaluating development during the early years of life. The cognitive scale consists of 91 items, grouped by age, and evaluates sensory-perceptual skills, exploration, manipulation, memory, problem-solving, and concept formation. The language scale assesses language comprehension and production, organized into two subscales: receptive communication (49 items) and expressive communication (48 items). The motor scale is organized into two subscales: gross motor skills (72 items) and fine motor skills (66 items). This scale evaluates axial and appendicular movements, static and dynamic balance, motor planning, grip strength, perceptual-motor integration, and object manipulation. The BSITD-III provides raw scores, composite scores, and established cutoff points to categorize a child’s development. These categories are as follows: extremely low (69 or less), borderline (70 to 79), below average (80 to 89), average (90 to 109), above average (110 to 119), and high (120 to 129).

The affordances in home environment for motor development – infant scale (AHEMD-IS)

The AHEMD-IS [20] was used to evaluate the home environment’s opportunities for supporting the child’s development. The AHEMD-IS consists of several sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics of the child and family (15 items), (2) physical space of the home, both internal and external (10 items), (3) daily activities of the child (11 items), and (4) availability of fine and gross motor toys (20 items). The items are simple dichotomous (yes/no) and Likert-scale (four levels) responses. Total score and categorization are provided (less than adequate: 0–18; moderate adequate: 19–23; adequate: 24–27; excellent: 28–49).

Procedures

Children’s biomedical data during hospital stays and follow-up were collected prospectively, including gestational age, birth weight and length, head circumference, APGAR score, and details on mechanical ventilation. Additionally, parents completed the AHEMD-IS, with the help of the researchers, and a survey regarding socioeconomic factors. The BSITD-III assessments were conducted at pre-scheduled visits at 4, 8, 12, and 24 months of corrected age. Parents or legal guardians were present during the assessment sessions; if the child showed signs of discomfort, intolerance or was sick, sleepy, or fatigated, the assessment was rescheduled the assessment. All assessments were carried out by two trained professionals with over 5 years of experience using the BSITD-III. They conducted the assessments together, scoring and analyzing the children’s performance independently. Results from 50% of the sample were compared to assess inter-rater reliability, which was found to be high (ICC > 87).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS 21.0). Descriptive statistics (frequency, means, and standard deviation), and variability were provided. ANOVA one way was used to compare sample characteristics. The MANOVA with repeated measures was used for the longitudinal data to compare the cognitive, language, and motor scores within and between groups.

To investigate factors associated with developmental outcomes, regression analyses were conducted separately for the STORCH and control groups, given differences in group size and the limited number of cases in the STORCH group. Initially, 17 biological and contextual risk variables were considered. To reduce the number of predictors and minimize the risk of overfitting, only variables showing significant associations with each outcome in preliminary analyses were entered into the regression models. Final models were estimated using a backward elimination procedure, resulting in parsimonious models including approximately 5–7 predictors. This approach was adopted to improve model stability and reduce the risk of overfitting, particularly given the relatively small number of STORCH cases [16] Effect sizes for the multivariate regression were reported using R2, adjusted R2, Cohen’s f2, with recognized cut-off (small: <0.15; moderate:0.15 to.34: large: >0.35). The significance level adopted was p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Children and families’ characteristics

In the present study, the STORCH case group was composed by 31 children diagnosed with syphilis (n = 19; 61.3%), toxoplasmosis (n = 5; 16.1%), herpes simplex (n = 5; 16.1%), and cytomegalovirus (n = 2; 6.5%). The STORCH case-group had younger mothers, lower income, and less formal education, as well as a higher number of previous children and infants born small for gestational age. No other significant differences were observed. Risk factors associated with neurodevelopment for by groups

Different factors varied in their association with children’s neurodevelopment in the first two years of life for the STORCH case-group. The regression analysis revealed that APGAR score, NICU seizures, and NICU stay were significant factors associated with the cognitive model. For the language model, the number of previous pregnancies, NICU seizures, family income, and twin births were significant factors. Discussion

Perinatal and neonatal characteristics and groups’ sociocultural factors

Among the sociocultural factors, our results align with previous studies, indicating that mothers in the STORCH case group were younger – congenital syphilis is more commonly observed in women aged 20 to 34 years [21]. These mothers also lived in social vulnerability [22], had lower levels of formal education [23], and exhibited a higher prevalence of previous pregnancies. Young women with low income are particularly vulnerable to infections, especially syphilis, and often face limited access to healthcare services [24, 25] which results in less frequent prenatal monitoring. Moreover, the quality of prenatal care is usually compromised for women with multiple children and low-income [25–28]. Additionally, lower maternal education levels are directly associated with reduced understanding of disease progression, the severity of health issues, and continuity of medical guidance [23, 29, 30].

These factors collectively impact the health of the unborn child. Adverse clinical outcomes, during the pregnancy and neonatal period [26, 28], are exacerbated by socioeconomic vulnerability and limited access to healthcare – factors that were also observed in the present study. Families in economic vulnerability, especially those with a high number of children, often face low levels of formal education, challenges in generating income due to a lack of professional careers, and difficulties in family planning – issues that are particularly common among young mothers in Brazil [23]. The combination of these risk factors was confirmed in our study, especially within the households of the STORCH case-group.

In addition, the STORCH case-group exhibited a higher prevalence of infants born small for gestational age (SGA). Congenital infections during the first trimester can cause symmetrical growth restriction in the infant [29–31]. Growth restriction may result from maternal health factors such as hypertension, diabetes, preexisting conditions, twin pregnancies, pre-eclampsia, premature rupture of membranes, the use of certain medications, and substance abuse, as well as fetal factors like congenital defects, infections, and genetic anomalies [31]. Consistent with previous studies, growth restriction has been more prevalent among children of mothers with low income and lower levels of maternal education [31, 32], which aligns with the findings of the present study.

Children neurodevelopment: the longitudinal effect of STORCH

The children in the STORCH case-group showed lower cognitive and language performance at 18 months compared to the control group, although no differences were observed in motor development. Additionally, the STORCH case-group exhibited declines in cognitive scores between 6 and 18 months and 12 to 18 months. In contrast, no declines were found in language or motor scores. For the control group, language scores declined from 12 to 18 months, while motor scores increased from 6 to 18 months and from 12 to 18 months and 24 months.

Previous studies have reported developmental delays in children with congenital STORCH infections [3, 5, 33], with these delays often persisting throughout childhood, particularly affecting neurodevelopment [34, 35]. One plausible explanation for these delays is the high prevalence of congenital syphilis in our study. Of the 17 cases of congenital syphilis, the most common congenital infection among preterm infants (60.73%), seven (41.2%) were diagnosed with neurosyphilis – significantly higher than previously reported rates. Neurosyphilis often remains asymptomatic, and cerebrospinal fluid tests may appear normal, leading to underdiagnosis and, consequently, a lack of treatment. This untreated condition can negatively affect neurodevelopment due to the degenerative processes in brain tissue [36, 37], the unfavorable effects may persist across childhood [35]. Furthermore, one of the clinical manifestations of congenital syphilis is chronic meningovascular disease, which typically appears in the second year of life and can lead to neurodevelopmental declines [36, 38]. This clinical course likely explains the cognitive declines observed in the present study between the first and second years of life.

Congenital toxoplasmosis and herpes simplex virus (HSV), each with a 16.1% prevalence in the present study, also have been reported as risk for severe neurological impairments such as hydrocephalus, microcephaly, intellectual impairment, and ocular and hearing loss [39–41]. These poor clinical outcomes are further exacerbated by socioeconomic and nutritional limitations, which contribute to the severity of the disease [42]. Similarly, impairments in cognitive, language, and motor skills have been reported in children with HSV infections [43]. These findings are consistent with the outcomes observed in the STORCH case-group in the present study.

It is also important to highlight that the combination of congenital infections and the adverse effect of prematurity meaningly elevate the rates of deficits, raising serious concerns about the future development of these children. This underscores the urgent need for public health policies that focus on prevention and, when prevention fails, on effective intervention.

Preterm children with and without STORCH: Neurodevelopment risk factors

The second objective of this study was examining longitudinally the risk factors associated with neurodevelopment in preterm children with and without STORCH infections. For the STORCH case-and control-groups several clinical outcomes (NICU seizures and stay, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, APGAR score, mechanical ventilation, early and late sepsis, twin births) and contextual factors (family income, maternal formal education, number of previous children) varied in their strength and role regarding children’s neurodevelopment along the first two years of life. Regarding contextual factors, two factors – family income and mother formal education – were longitudinally associated with the neurodevelopment of children with and without STORCH. The family income, the stronger contextual factor, was significantly associated with cognitive (control-group at 12 and 24 months), language (case-group at 18 months; control-group at 6 months), and motor (control-group at 12 months), similar to previous studies with preterm children [27, 44–48]. Families with higher incomes, generally have better access to information, housing, toys, and child care practices, which enhance opportunities for exploration and development in children [28, 49]. The mother’s formal education was associated with language (control-group at 12 months) and motor (case-group at 24 months) development; the higher educational level, the higher the motor and language scores, result also aligns with previous studies [27, 28, 44–47]. Mothers with higher formal education, several times combined with family higher income tend to expose children to well-organized environment, implemented appropriate parenting practices, and varied daily stimulation – factors that positively influence development [45, 47], explain the present findings.

Another environmental risk factor identified in this study was the number of children in the household. In the case-group, a negative association with language scores was observed, consistent with prior research [27, 44]. A higher number of children under the same mother’s care divides her attention and time, often prioritizing basic needs such as hygiene, feeding, and sleep, which leaves less time for play and development activities [27]. Involving fathers, siblings, and other family members who can engage directly with the child may help mitigate delays in younger children from larger families.

Regarding clinical factors, a prolonged NICU stay was strongly negatively associated with cognitive scores (case-group at 24 months; control-group at 6, 12 and 18 months), language scores (control-group at 12, 18 and 24 months), and motor scores (control-group at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months). The length of NICU stay reflects the severity of neonatal morbidities and the number of clinical interventions required, which is particularly high in preterm infants [25–27]. Although the NICU is essential for critical care, its limits infant contact with family members and creates a stressful environment with factors like noise, bright lights, movement, and frequent invasive procedures [26, 27, 50].

During their NICU stay, many children experience neonatal seizures, the most prevalent risk factor associated with neurodevelopmental scores in this study. Seizures, an acute manifestation of central nervous system dysfunction, are common in high-risk children [51], and their prognosis for neurodevelopmental impairments is poor [27]. Additionally, many of the children in the NICU required ventilation assistance. For those children, a longer duration of mechanical ventilation was associated to worse neurodevelopment outcomes [52], particularly in language development at 6 months in the control-group. Prolonged mechanical ventilation and oxygen therapy in the NICU often lead to bronchopulmonary dysplasia, a chronic lung disease that disrupts normal lung development. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia is a common complication in preterm infants and has been associated with neurodevelopment delays [24], particularly in motor skills at 12 months in the control-group,

Three other clinical factors were associated with neurodevelopment scores, although feeble. APGAR scores were a significantly associated with cognitive (case-group at 6 and 12 months; control-group at 6 and 12 months) and motor (case-group at 24 months; control-group at 12 and 8 months) development. In previous studies [27, 28], higher APGAR score at 5 min post birth were related to better cognitive and motor outcomes. The APGAR score helps assess the newborn’s response and adaptation to life outside the womb, identifying children in need special care and enabling timely intervention.

The occurrence of early and late sepsis was also associated with neurodevelopment delays. Early sepsis was significantly associated with low motor scores in the case-group, while late sepsis was linked to low cognitive scores for the control-group, both at 24 months, like previous study [53]. Sepsis, caused by bacterial infection in the bloodstream, is a leading cause of neonatal mortality and developmental impairments in preterm infants [53, 54]. Finally, twin births were associated with lower language and motor scores for the case-group. As reported in the literature, twin births are a recognized risk factor for neurodevelopmental impairments, especially when combined with other risks like congenital infections [55].

In summary, these clinical and environmental factors contribute to poor neurodevelopmental outcomes, with the risks compounded in children with congenital infections due to the added fragility of the disease.

Limitation and strengths

There was limitation in the present study. First, the study focuses on a specific population of preterm infants with and without STORCH infections, which may not be representative of the broader population of preterm children. The neurodevelopment delays may result from compounding effects of social vulnerability and perinatal risk factors. Second, STORCH group included different infections with biological heterogeneity. Due to the limited number of individual non-syphilis cases, conducting independent subgroup analyses was impossible in the present study. Third, while there was control for factors like maternal education and income, there may be other unmeasured sociocultural or environmental variables such as family dynamics, caregiver involvement that could also influence development; these unaccounted factors might affect the strength of the findings.

The longitudinal design enables to observe trends and changes in cognitive, language, and motor development over critical stages of early childhood and can reveal causal relationships and highlight the long-term effects of early-life factors. Second, the examination of both sociocultural and clinical factors allows for an understanding of how a combination of environmental, social, and medical factors affects child development providing a more complete picture of the complex factors influencing neurodevelopment in preterm children which is more reflective of real-world conditions. Conclusion

This study highlights the complex interplay between perinatal, neonatal, and sociocultural factors in shaping the neurodevelopment of preterm children, particularly those affected by STORCH infections. The findings reinforcing the significant impact of socio-economic vulnerability on both maternal and child health. Young mothers with lower levels of formal education, limited income, and previous pregnancies were found to be particularly susceptible to infections like congenital syphilis, which, combined with poor prenatal care, creates a detrimental environment for fetal and neonatal development. These factors were especially evident in the STORCH case-group, where adverse outcomes such as growth restriction and developmental delays were more pronounced.

The presence of congenital infections, particularly syphilis, toxoplasmosis, and herpes simplex virus, was associated with significant cognitive and language delays in the children studied. These findings emphasize the long-term risks that untreated infections pose to neurodevelopment, as some infections, like neurosyphilis, may go undiagnosed and untreated, leading to progressive damage in brain tissue. Congenital infections, compounded by the vulnerabilities of prematurity, further increase the likelihood of developmental deficits, underlining the urgent need for more effective prevention and intervention strategies particularly for families living in socioeconomical vulnerability.

Several clinical factors, including prolonged NICU stays, neonatal seizures, mechanical ventilation, and the presence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia, were identified as significant risk factors for poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes. These factors were particularly impactful in children from the STORCH case-group. The NICU environment, while necessary for intensive care, often deprives preterm infants of the early sensory stimulation needed for healthy brain development, which is compounded by the physical stress of medical interventions. Sociocultural factors such as family income and maternal education also emerged as crucial protective factors. Higher income and education levels were associated with better neurodevelopmental outcomes, especially in the control-group, demonstrating how socio-economic resources can enhance access to healthcare and developmental stimulation, which ultimately support better long-term outcomes for children.

This study underscores the critical need for targeted public health interventions aimed at improving maternal and neonatal healthcare, particularly in economically vulnerable populations. Strengthening prenatal care, improving access to education, and providing early interventions for at-risk infants can help mitigate the adverse effects of congenital infections and prematurity. These efforts are essential to reduce the risk of neurodevelopmental delays and to support the future development of these children.
