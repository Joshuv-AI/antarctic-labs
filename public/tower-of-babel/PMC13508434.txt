Feasibility and psychometric evaluation of large language model–generated radiology learning materials for board examination preparation: a prospective pilot study
Authors: Yoojin Nam, Taein An, Sung Il Hwang, Chawoong Jeon, Ji Won Jeong, Juhyun Jeong, Dong Yeong Kim, Pyeong Hwa Kim, So Yeon Kim, Sungkwan Kim, Yangwon Kim, Kwang Hwi Lee, Taehee Lee, Hee Sang Oh, Joon Hyung Park, Seunghyeon Shin, Yongsik Sim, Jeong Min Song, Seunghyun Song, Pa Hong, Namkug Kim

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) can generate structured educational content at scale, yet their role in postgraduate radiology training remains untested.MethodsThis pilot study evaluated the feasibility of deploying LLM-generated radiology board preparation materials, the psychometric properties of adapted survey instruments, and exploratory effect-size estimates for sample-size planning of a future multicentre trial. We conducted a prospective observational pilot among 41 radiology residents preparing for the 2026 Korean Radiology Board Examination. A governed seven-stage LLM pipeline generated 6,000 Anki flashcards, 833 infographic summaries, and presentation slides. Materials were distributed freely, and 24 completers self-selected into users (n = 15) and non-users (n = 9). Outcomes included enrollment yield, retention rate, voluntary adoption rate, internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of adapted cognitive load and satisfaction scales, and change scores in extraneous cognitive load (ECL) and self-efficacy (SE), compared using Mann–Whitney U tests with Cohen’s d and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsOf 114 registered candidates, 44 were recruited (38.6%); 41 were enrolled (yield 93.2%); retention was 58.5% (24 of 41), below the prespecified 80% threshold; and voluntary adoption was 62.5%. The ECL scale showed acceptable reliability (alpha = 0.87 baseline, 0.80 post-examination). After reverse-coding one reverse-worded item (E3), the corrected three-item trust-in-AI alpha was 0.583 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.151 to 0.746), below the conventional 0.70 threshold. Between-group differences were non-significant for ECL (d = − 0.28; 95% CI − 1.50 to 0.59) and SE (d = 0.26; − 0.62 to 1.24). Among users, educational quality was rated 3.33 of 5.00, and 86.7% reported encountering no factual inaccuracies. A medium-effect scenario (d = 0.50) would require 160 participants.ConclusionsRetention (58.5%), the trust-scale alpha of 0.583, and the − 40 Net Promoter Score together indicate that the data-collection protocol, the AI-trust instrumentation, and the user-experience design all require modification before an efficacy trial is justified. Operational deployment was feasible at the levels of enrollment yield and voluntary adoption, but not retention. Adapted cognitive load and satisfaction scales reached acceptable reliability; the trust-in-AI and single-item self-efficacy outcomes did not meet conventional thresholds. Exploratory between-group point estimates were small and unstable across sensitivity strata; they are reported as inputs for sample-size planning and should not be interpreted as evidence for or against educational benefit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09704-8.Keywords: Large language model, Radiology education, Board examination, Pilot study, Feasibility, Psychometric evaluation, Cognitive load, Spaced repetition, Anki, Artificial intelligence

Background

Radiology board examinations demand synthesis of imaging findings, differential diagnoses, and clinical correlations across more than 1,700 learning objectives spanning 11 subspecialties. Traditional preparation resources—textbooks, lecture slides, and question banks—are produced manually, are updated infrequently, and are rarely tailored to learner gaps [1–3]. Spaced repetition systems such as Anki have demonstrated efficacy in medical education; consistent evidence shows that learners using Anki achieve improved knowledge retention and examination performance [1–3]. Producing high-quality flashcard decks for an entire subspecialty curriculum, however, requires hundreds of hours of expert effort, which limits their availability and currency.

Large language models (LLMs) offer the potential to generate structured learning materials at scale [4–6]. A systematic review of LLM-generated multiple-choice questions found that most studies focused on undergraduate medical education, with limited evidence from postgraduate specialty training where domain accuracy requirements are substantially higher [4, 5]. A recent scoping review of LLM applications in graduate medical education identified radiology as the subspecialty with the highest publication count (21 of 102 studies), yet nearly all examined LLM performance on existing questions rather than the generation of new educational content for learner use [7]. ChatGPT has demonstrated variable performance on radiology board-style examinations [8], and a professional society task force has outlined potential applications of LLMs in radiology education [9].

A central concern in deploying LLM-generated educational materials is the risk of factual inaccuracy (“hallucination”) and propagation of bias, which has been documented across general medical content and may be particularly consequential in board examination preparation, where learners may lack the expertise to detect subtle errors [10]. Whether LLM-generated materials can be deployed in a real examination preparation setting, whether learners will voluntarily adopt them, and how their perceived quality and trustworthiness compare across learner-facing artifact types have not been evaluated in postgraduate radiology training.

MeducAI (Medical Education AI) is a governed seven-stage pipeline that uses Anthropic’s Claude family of large language models to generate radiology learning materials under a formal quality assurance framework. The pipeline produces Anki flashcards, infographic summary tables, and presentation slides covering the full scope of the Korean radiology board examination curriculum. In a companion validation manuscript that is currently unpublished and under peer review, expert radiologists rated 95.7% of pipeline-generated content as acceptable, and the system achieved a first-pass acceptance rate comparable to single-expert authoring while reducing production time from an estimated 600 h of manual effort to under 72 h of compute time (Nam Y, An T, Hwang SI, et al., unpublished).

Before investing in a fully powered efficacy trial, it is necessary to establish whether the deployment infrastructure, survey instruments, and outcome measures function as intended in a real training environment. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of deploying MeducAI materials during board examination preparation, assess the psychometric properties of adapted survey instruments, and estimate effect sizes for planning a future multicentre trial.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective observational pilot study under single-institution ethical approval (Samsung Changwon Medical Center, South Korea) among radiology residents from multiple training hospitals across the country. The study followed a naturalistic comparison design: participants received free access to MeducAI materials and used them at their own discretion during examination preparation, with no randomisation, masking, control arm, or causal comparison. Accordingly, the user/non-user contrast is reported descriptively and is not interpreted as evidence of efficacy. This design was chosen to evaluate real-world adoption behaviour while avoiding ethical concerns associated with restricting access to potentially beneficial study materials during high-stakes examination preparation. To mitigate bias associated with the non-randomised design, all participants received identical surveys regardless of usage status, and the analyst was not involved in material distribution. Because the study involved only the distribution of educational materials and collection of online survey responses, with no access to clinical data or hospital-specific procedures, ethical oversight by a single institutional review board was considered sufficient for participants across multiple training sites. This single-site oversight is consistent with Korean IRB guidance issued by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, which permits a designated lead institutional review board to provide ethical oversight for minimal-risk educational research distributed across multiple training sites. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Changwon Medical Center (SCMC 2025–12–020), the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was not registered in a clinical trial registry, consistent with its observational design.

Participants

A total of 114 radiology residents from training hospitals nationwide were registered for the 2026 Korean Radiology Board Examination. Participants were recruited through a nationwide online messaging group for board examination candidates. Forty-four residents were assessed for eligibility; of these, three were excluded for having served as expert evaluators in the MeducAI content validation study, and all 41 remaining residents provided informed consent and were administratively enrolled between January 7 and February 13, 2026 (the latter being the final-results announcement date, by which administrative enrollment was confirmed); all 24 analysed participants completed the baseline survey before the written board examination on February 3, 2026. In the Korean radiology residency system, board examination eligibility is ordinarily conferred upon completion of the fourth year of training. Accordingly, most participants were fourth-year residents (the typical examination cohort). A small number of physicians held examination eligibility through alternative pathways: re-examination after a prior unsuccessful attempt, or resumption of eligibility following interruption of training for mandatory military service. These non-standard candidates had completed equivalent training requirements but were not classified as current fourth-year residents at the time of the study.

Intervention

MeducAI materials were distributed as a complete Anki deck containing 6,000 flashcards (basic recall and multiple-choice question pairs), a set of 833 infographic summary tables in PDF format, and presentation slides summarizing key topics by subspecialty. The materials covered the full radiology board examination curriculum across 11 subspecialties. Materials were made available on January 7, 2026, approximately four weeks before the board examination, which consisted of a written component (February 3, 2026) and an oral component (February 9, 2026). The post-examination survey was administered between February 19 and February 28, 2026, after the final results were announced (February 13, 2026). Participants used the materials alongside their existing study strategies at their own discretion. Usage was self-reported (item A5, hours) and was not corroborated by automated access logs; dose–response inferences are therefore subject to recall imprecision. A high-level overview of the recruitment, intervention, and outcome assessment phases is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Outcomes

Feasibility outcomes (primary)

The primary objective of this pilot study was to assess feasibility of the study procedures and materials. Pre-specified feasibility outcomes were: (a) enrollment yield, defined as the proportion enrolled among those assessed for eligibility; (b) retention rate, defined as the proportion of enrolled participants with complete analytic data, that is, who completed both the baseline and post-examination surveys with non-missing educational change outcomes; and (c) voluntary adoption rate, defined as the proportion of analysed completers who reported using MeducAI materials. Post-examination survey completion is reported separately from complete-case analytic retention because two participants completed the post-examination survey but lacked complete educational change outcome data. This pilot was designed to inform progression to a definitive trial rather than test formal go/no-go thresholds. Pragmatically, we considered high enrollment yield, majority voluntary adoption, and acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.70) as supportive of progression, whereas retention below 80% would signal the need for protocol modification before proceeding. These four thresholds (enrollment, retention, voluntary adoption, scale internal consistency) correspond to the four operational uncertainties the pilot was designed to resolve before an efficacy trial is justified: adherence, acceptability, operational delivery, and instrument reliability.

Psychometric outcomes

We assessed the psychometric properties of adapted survey scales. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha with 95% confidence intervals for all multi-item scales: extraneous cognitive load (three items adapted from Leppink et al. [11]) at both baseline and post-examination, educational quality (four items, B1–B4), and satisfaction/technology acceptance (five items, G1–G5, adapted from the Technology Acceptance Model [12]).

Educational outcomes (secondary)

Two exploratory educational change outcomes (ΔECL, ΔSE) were tabulated to generate descriptive standardised effect-size prior information for future trial sample-size planning: change in extraneous cognitive load (ΔECL = ECL_final − ECL_baseline; three-item mean, 1–7 Likert scale, higher scores indicating greater extraneous load) and change in self-efficacy (ΔSE = SE_final − SE_baseline; single matched item, 1–7 Likert scale, higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy). As a single-item measure, self-efficacy reliability cannot be formally assessed, and this outcome should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Structured measures of total study time, prior academic performance, competing AI-tool exposure, and institutional setting are not available in the de-identified analysis dataset; between-group contrasts are therefore confounded in directions the present data cannot estimate. Baseline learning-tool variables and non-user context variables specified in the protocol are likewise not available in the de-identified analysis dataset; this gap is addressed in the future trial protocol.

Secondary user-experience outcomes, assessed among users only, included educational quality (B1–B4, 1–5 scale), satisfaction and technology acceptance (G1–G5, 1–5 scale), trust in AI-generated content (E1–E3; E3 was reverse-coded prior to analysis [E3_r = 6 − E3] because its item wording [“critically reviewed the content with potential errors in mind”] is directionally opposite to E1 and E2), the Net Promoter Score [13] (H1, 0–10 scale), perceived factual inaccuracy (C3, binary), and usage patterns (components used, total hours).

Group allocation

Participants were classified as users or non-users based on self-report at the post-examination survey (item A1). This naturalistic allocation reflects real-world adoption behaviour; its implications for internal validity are addressed in the Limitations.

Data collection

All data were collected through online surveys delivered via Google Forms; no in-person data collection was performed. The baseline survey was administered at enrollment and captured demographics, training status, prior LLM experience, baseline cognitive load, self-efficacy, and behavioural covariates (stress, sleep quality, mood, exercise frequency). All 24 participants included in the analysis completed the baseline survey before the board examination, so that baseline cognitive load and self-efficacy measures preceded the examination for every analysed participant. The post-examination survey was administered after the board examination and captured post-examination cognitive load, self-efficacy, and—for users—detailed evaluations of the MeducAI materials by artifact type. Strategies, total study hours, and resources used by non-users were not collected; consequently, the comparator condition was not characterised in a structured manner. This limitation is addressed in the Discussion and motivates a structured comparator log in the future trial.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were pre-specified in the study protocol prior to data collection and frozen before unblinding. Because the study was designed for estimation rather than hypothesis testing, 95% confidence intervals are reported as the primary inferential output, and p-values are descriptive. Because all educational change analyses were exploratory and underpowered, no confirmatory multiplicity-adjusted inference was used; Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values for the supplementary sensitivity family (dose–response, threshold, baseline straight-lining, and fourth-year subset) are provided in Supplementary Table S5 for transparency. Likert-scale variables were treated as approximately continuous for descriptive purposes (mean, standard deviation) and analysed with non-parametric tests that do not assume interval-level measurement. Statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.x using pandas, scipy, numpy, and pingouin, with a random seed of 42 for all bootstrap procedures.

Primary analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between users and non-users using Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Internal consistency was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha and bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples, percentile method). For the exploratory educational change outcomes (ΔECL and ΔSE), change scores were compared between groups using Mann–Whitney U tests with rank-biserial correlation as the non-parametric effect size; bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were computed for mean differences, and Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals was calculated to provide standardised effect sizes for future sample size planning. Within-group changes were assessed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The trust-in-AI scale (E1–E3) was analysed after reverse-coding E3 (E3_r = 6 − E3). Internal consistency of the corrected three-item scale was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha and bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples, seed 42). The composite trust score (mean of E1, E2, E3_r) is reported as median (interquartile range) and as mean with a small-sample 95% confidence interval (t-interval), with a one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test against the neutral midpoint of 3; individual items are also tabulated (Table S3) using the same approach.

Missing data

The analysis population consisted of participants who completed both surveys with non-missing exploratory educational change outcome data (complete-case analysis). Item-level missingness was absent among the 24 completers, so imputation was not required. Among participants enrolled but not analysed (n = 17), specific reasons for non-response were not collected; the implications for non-response bias are discussed in the Limitations.

Sensitivity analyses

Four pre-specified sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary estimates. First, usage-threshold sensitivity restricted users to those reporting at least 1, 3, or 5 cumulative hours of MeducAI use; a ≥ 10-h cutoff was not evaluated because fewer than three users met that threshold. Second, training-year sensitivity restricted the analysis to fourth-year residents (the typical examination cohort) to address the imbalance in training status between groups. Third, response-quality sensitivity excluded baseline straight-liners (participants who gave identical responses to all three ECL items at baseline) to assess robustness to potentially careless responding. Fourth, dose–response relationships among users were summarized using Spearman rank correlations and quartile descriptive bins between total usage hours and change scores; quantile regression was not performed because the user sample (n = 15) was too small to support regression estimation. An additional pre-specified analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for each exploratory educational change outcome, with the corresponding baseline value and training status as covariates, was specified in the protocol but not conducted because the analysis sample (n = 24) yielded fewer than 10 observations per predictor, making regression estimates unstable. User-experience outcomes were summarized descriptively by artifact type (Anki flashcards, infographic tables, presentation slides).

Sample size estimation for a future study

A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed for the present pilot; instead, all eligible residents were enrolled to maximize the precision of effect size estimates, consistent with published guidance on pilot study sizing [14, 15]. Sample size requirements for a future definitive trial were estimated using the observed standardised effect sizes, targeting 80% and 90% power with a two-sided alpha of 0.05. A 20% attrition buffer was applied as a pragmatic target for the redesigned future trial, which will implement retention-enhancing procedures, rather than as a direct extrapolation of the 58.5% retention observed in the present pilot.

Reporting standards

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist [16] is the single primary reporting framework for this observational pilot; the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary Material 1. The CONSORT 2010 extension to randomised pilot and feasibility trials [14] (Supplementary Material 4) and the TRIPOD-LLM guideline (Gallifant et al. [17]; Supplementary Material 5) are reported only as supplementary cross-walks for future-trial planning and LLM-pipeline disclosure; neither implies a randomised-trial or clinical-prediction-model claim for the present pilot. TRIPOD-LLM subitems relating to clinical prediction model performance are marked not applicable for the present educational deployment context.

AI-assisted writing disclosure

An artificial intelligence language model (Claude, Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA) was used to assist with manuscript drafting, including structuring sections, refining prose, and checking internal consistency between the manuscript text and the prespecified analysis tables. All factual statements, citations, and statistical values were reviewed by the authors against the original source data and analysis outputs, and all content was approved by all authors, who take full responsibility for the integrity of the work. The AI tool was not used for study design, data collection, statistical analysis, or interpretation of results, and was not used to generate, modify, or alter any data, figures, or tables.

Results

Feasibility outcomes

Of 114 nationally registered candidates, 44 were recruited through the online messaging group (national recruitment rate, 38.6%). Of these 44 residents assessed for eligibility, three were excluded for prior involvement in MeducAI content validation, and all 41 remaining residents consented to participate (enrollment yield, 41 of 44 assessed = 93.2%). Twenty-six participants completed the post-examination survey (post-examination survey completion, 26 of 41 = 63.4%), of whom 24 had complete exploratory educational change outcome data (complete-case analytic retention, 24 of 41 = 58.5%). Among these 24 completers, 15 (62.5%) reported using MeducAI materials (users) and 9 (37.5%) did not (non-users). The participant flow is shown in Fig. 1. Non-users continued their existing preparation strategies, but the comparator condition was not characterised in a structured manner in the present pilot.

A comparison of completers (n = 24) and non-completers (n = 17) revealed no significant differences in age (median 32.0 vs. 33.0 years, p = 0.621), sex (50.0% vs. 35.3% male, p = 0.524), fourth-year training status (91.7% vs. 94.1%, p = 1.000), prior LLM experience (91.7% vs. 82.4%, p = 0.633), baseline stress (both median 5.0, p = 0.688), baseline ECL (median 4.8 vs. 4.3, p = 0.292), or baseline self-efficacy (median 5.0 vs. 4.0, p = 0.408), suggesting that attrition was not systematically associated with measured baseline characteristics (Table S1). The 17 non-completers did not respond to the post-examination survey; specific reasons for non-response were not collected, but the timing of the survey—administered during a period of reduced engagement following the examination—likely contributed to the dropout.

Scale reliability

The adapted extraneous cognitive load scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency at both timepoints: Cronbach’s alpha was 0.87 (95% CI 0.73–0.94) at baseline and 0.80 (95% CI 0.61–0.91) post-examination. Among user-only scales, educational quality (B1–B4) yielded alpha = 0.79 (95% CI 0.54–0.92), and satisfaction/technology acceptance (G1–G5) yielded alpha = 0.88 (95% CI 0.75–0.95). After reverse-coding item E3 (E3_r = 6 − E3), internal consistency of the three-item trust scale improved from the original alpha = − 0.570 to alpha = 0.583 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.151 to 0.746). All three corrected inter-item Pearson correlations were positive (E1–E2: r = 0.370; E1–E3_r: r = 0.166; E2–E3_r: r = 0.446). The corrected alpha of 0.583 is a modest positive value that remains below the conventional 0.70 threshold, which is acknowledged given the small user sample (n = 15). Item-level statistics are provided in Table S3.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of users and non-users are presented in Table 1. The two groups were comparable in age, sex, baseline stress, prior LLM experience, baseline cognitive load, and baseline self-efficacy (all p > 0.10). All 15 users were fourth-year residents, compared with 7 of 9 non-users (77.8%); the two non-users who were not fourth-year residents held examination eligibility through alternative pathways (re-examination after a prior attempt or resumption of eligibility following military service interruption). This difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.130) but is a potential confounding factor, as training status may be associated with study habits and examination motivation.

Response consistency

Straight-lining (identical responses across all three ECL items) was observed in 7 of 24 respondents (29.2%) at baseline and 2 of 24 (8.3%) at the post-examination timepoint, with 1 respondent straight-lining at both timepoints. Because the ECL scale uses three items on a 1–7 range, some degree of uniform responding cannot be unambiguously distinguished from inattentive responding; interpretation of any uniform-response pattern at n = 24 with three items is intrinsically ambiguous, and this pattern is treated as a measurement caveat in the sensitivity analyses below. Future trial instruments will include attention-check items. All 24 completers had non-missing data for both exploratory educational change outcomes; no item-level missingness was observed.

Between-group comparison of change scores

Neither exploratory educational change outcome showed a statistically significant between-group difference (Table 2, Fig. 2). For extraneous cognitive load, mean ΔECL was − 0.29 ± 1.51 in users and 0.11 ± 1.20 in non-users; the between-group 95% confidence interval (− 1.50 to 0.59) was wide and crossed zero, so this is uninformative for inference. The mean difference was − 0.40, corresponding to Cohen’s d of − 0.28 (95% CI − 1.12 to 0.55; U = 67.5, p = 1.000). For self-efficacy, mean ΔSE was 0.20 ± 1.21 in users and − 0.11 ± 1.17 in non-users; the mean difference was 0.31 (95% CI − 0.62 to 1.24), corresponding to Cohen’s d of 0.26 (95% CI − 0.57 to 1.09; U = 80.0, p = 0.454). Within-group changes were not statistically significant for either outcome in either group (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: users ΔECL, W = 50.0, p = 0.875; non-users ΔECL, W = 13.5, p = 0.969; users ΔSE, W = 18.0, p = 0.584; non-users ΔSE, W = 6.5, p = 1.000). The wide confidence intervals reflect the small sample size; for a two-sample comparison (15 users vs 9 non-users) at 80% power and a two-sided α of 0.05, the minimum effect detectable in this sample was d = 1.24, confirming that the study was substantially underpowered to detect small-to-medium educational effects. After Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment of the 16 exploratory p-values across the dose–response and pre-specified sensitivity tests (Supplementary Table S5), no test reached q < 0.05; this is consistent with the estimation-only framing and was expected given the pilot’s design.

User experience outcomes

Among the 15 users, Anki flashcards were the most commonly used component (14 of 15, 93.3%), followed by infographic summary tables (6 of 15, 40.0%) and presentation slides (4 of 15, 26.7%) (Table 3; Supplementary Table S4). Nine users (60.0%) reported using one component, three (20.0%) used two, and three (20.0%) used all three. Median total usage was 3.0 h (range 0.5–15.0) overall, 3.0 h among users of one component, 3.0 h among users of two components, and 8.0 h among users of all three components, indicating that broader artifact uptake was associated with longer cumulative engagement. Component-level satisfaction ratings were not collected separately by artifact type, which is acknowledged as a limitation and motivates artifact-specific outcome measures in the future trial.

Educational quality across the four-item scale was rated 3.33 ± 0.75 (of 5.00; Table 4, Fig. 3). Item-level analysis indicated that core concept coverage received the highest rating (B2, 3.67 ± 0.72), while inclusion of irrelevant content received the lowest (B3, 2.80 ± 1.15). Satisfaction and technology acceptance averaged 3.45 ± 0.89, with behavioural intention to continue using similar tools rated highest (G5, 3.73 ± 1.10). Thirteen of 15 users (86.7%) reported encountering no factual inaccuracies (C3), while 2 (13.3%) reported noticing errors. Dose–response analysis

Among users, total usage hours showed imprecise inverse point estimates with both exploratory educational change outcomes (Spearman rho = − 0.29 with ΔECL, 95% CI − 0.81 to + 0.31, p = 0.287; rho = − 0.29 with ΔSE, 95% CI − 0.79 to + 0.35, p = 0.303). Among the user-experience outcomes, total usage hours showed a positive but imprecise point estimate with the composite trust score (Spearman rho = 0.256, bootstrap 95% CI − 0.32 to + 0.74, p = 0.358); the user sample of 15 is too small to characterise any such relationship, and the point estimate is reported only for design-planning purposes. Associations with the Net Promoter Score and per-item correlations are summarised in Table S3. These dose–response patterns should be regarded as descriptive given the user sample of 15.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the primary effect-size estimates, four prespecified sensitivity analyses (usage-threshold restriction at ≥ 1, ≥ 3, and ≥ 5 cumulative hours; restriction to fourth-year residents; exclusion of baseline straight-liners; and dose–response by usage quartile) were performed and are reported in full in Supplementary Table S2. Given the small sample, they are summarised here only as instability indicators. Across all four analyses the 95% confidence interval for ΔECL crossed zero in every stratum, and both the ΔECL and ΔSE point estimates were unstable across these defensible analytic choices at n = 24. Quantile regression was not performed because the user sample was too small to support regression estimation. These patterns are reported as indicators of estimate instability rather than as evidence for or against an educational effect.

Sample size estimation for a future study

Based on the larger of the two observed standardised effect sizes (d = 0.28 for ΔECL), a two-sample t-test at alpha = 0.05 with 80% power would require 202 participants per arm (404 total) (Table 5). Using a conventional medium effect (d = 0.50)—which represents a clinically meaningful improvement—128 participants would be needed, or 160 after applying a pragmatic 20% attrition buffer for the redesigned future trial. For 90% power at d = 0.50, the requirement increases to 172, or 215 with the attrition buffer.

Discussion

Principal findings

This prospective pilot study evaluated the feasibility of deploying LLM-generated radiology board examination preparation materials, the psychometric properties of adapted survey instruments, and preliminary effect-size estimates in a real training environment. Three findings emerged. First, operational deployment was achievable, but post-examination retention fell to 58.5%, substantially below the prespecified 80% threshold; the binding feasibility constraint was the data-collection protocol, not the materials themselves. Second, the adapted extraneous cognitive load and satisfaction scales reached acceptable internal consistency. The trust-in-AI scale, after correction for item directionality, achieved only modest positive reliability and remained below the conventional 0.70 threshold; a validated multi-item instrument is warranted in future work. Third, between-group differences in cognitive load reduction and self-efficacy gain were small and non-significant. This pilot was not powered to detect educational benefit, and the estimates are reported as inputs for sample-size planning.

We distinguish three feasibility constructs that have been conflated under the single term “feasibility” in earlier framing and are reported separately here: (i) operational viability (whether the governed pipeline can deliver materials into a real preparation cycle), answered affirmatively by the 93.2% enrollment yield and on-time distribution; (ii) voluntary adoption (whether residents choose to engage when materials are made available), partially answered, with 62.5% of completers reporting any use; and (iii) educational acceptance (whether residents endorse the materials to peers), not supported, with a Net Promoter Score of − 40. The present study answers (i), partially answers (ii), and does not support (iii). Educational efficacy is a separate question that this pilot was not powered to address.

Feasibility and retention

The national recruitment rate of 38.6% (44 of 114 registered candidates) indicates that the online messaging group reached approximately one-third of the target population. Among those reached, the 93.2% enrollment yield and 62.5% adoption rate indicate that residents who engaged with the study were willing to incorporate LLM-generated materials into their preparation. This finding addresses a prerequisite for any future trial: if the target population does not adopt the materials, questions of educational benefit become moot. The 58.5% retention rate, however, fell below the commonly recommended 80% threshold for pilot studies [14] and is the primary feasibility concern. Attrition likely reflects the timing of the post-examination survey, administered during a period of reduced engagement following a high-stakes examination. The absence of structured exit reasons from the 17 enrolled but unanalysed participants further constrains the interpretation of attrition, and we cannot rule out the possibility that participants with negative experiences were differentially less likely to complete the post-examination survey. A future study should implement timed reminders, shortened instruments, exit-reason capture for non-completers, and incentive structures to improve retention and characterise non-response bias. Therefore, the retention result should be interpreted not as evidence that the educational materials were infeasible, but as evidence that the post-examination data-collection protocol requires modification before a definitive trial.

Psychometric properties

The three-item AI-trust scale did not achieve acceptable internal consistency. Even after correcting a reverse-worded item (E3, “critically reviewed the content with potential errors in mind”, whose direction is opposite to E1 general accuracy trust and E2 use without separate verification, and which was therefore recoded as E3_r = 6 − E3), Cronbach’s alpha was 0.583 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.151 to 0.746), below the 0.70 acceptability threshold. Trust outcomes are therefore reported at the item level (Table 4, Supplementary Table S3); the composite is shown for completeness but should not be interpreted as a validated scale score. All three corrected inter-item Pearson correlations were positive (E1–E2: 0.370; E1–E3_r: 0.166; E2–E3_r: 0.446). The single-item self-efficacy outcome similarly lacks formal reliability evidence. Both instruments are scheduled for replacement in the definitive trial protocol, by a multi-item self-efficacy scale such as the academic self-efficacy subscale of the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ) [18] and by a validated multi-item AI trust instrument. By contrast, the extraneous cognitive load scale, adapted from Leppink et al. [11], achieved Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.87 at baseline and 0.80 post-examination, and the satisfaction/technology acceptance scale yielded alpha = 0.88, both exceeding the 0.70 threshold. The present trust composite and single-item self-efficacy outcome should not be carried forward as inferential endpoints in the definitive trial; they were retained here only to estimate the order of magnitude of effect for power planning.

User experience

Among the 15 users, Anki flashcards dominated artifact uptake (93.3%), followed by infographic summary tables (40.0%) and presentation slides (26.7%); broader artifact uptake was associated with longer cumulative engagement (median 8.0 h among three-component users versus 3.0 h otherwise). This differential adoption suggests that the three artifact types serve distinct roles in examination preparation and should be evaluated independently in future studies. The companion validation manuscript (Nam Y et al., unpublished) established content quality through expert review. The present study adds the learner perspective, with moderate educational quality ratings (3.33 of 5.00) and low perceived factual inaccuracy (86.7% of users reported encountering no errors), although this self-reported figure should be interpreted alongside our companion expert validation rather than as an independent accuracy estimate. The Net Promoter Score was − 40, with 7 of 15 users classified as detractors. Without instrumented usability data, we cannot attribute the negative NPS to any single mechanism; usability, workflow integration, and onboarding are a-priori targets for measurement in the definitive trial rather than confirmed problems in the present materials. The composite trust dose–response point estimate (Spearman rho = 0.256, bootstrap 95% CI − 0.32 to + 0.74) is too imprecise at n = 15 to characterise any relationship between cumulative engagement and trust.

Comparison with literature

Prior work on LLM-generated educational content has focused predominantly on benchmarking LLM performance against existing examination questions or expert-curated content. Artsi et al. [4] systematically reviewed LLM-generated examination questions and found high factual accuracy but limited evidence of educational impact on learners. Lucas et al. [5] surveyed LLM applications across medical education and identified a gap in studies evaluating learner outcomes, as opposed to content quality alone. More recent radiology-specific evaluations by Çamur et al. [19] and Cesur et al. [20] benchmarked LLM accuracy on radiology board-style and case-based questions, showing that contemporary LLMs approach but do not consistently match expert performance, and Bhayana et al. [8] reported variable ChatGPT performance on radiology board-style examinations. A learner-perspective comparison of LLM-generated and expert-authored content in radiology [21] reported that ChatGPT-generated multiple-choice questions were rated comparable in perceived quality and yielded similar correct-answer rates to attending-written questions, although residents were less likely to attribute the LLM-generated items to an attending radiologist. The present study extends this prior content-focused literature in study design rather than in efficacy claims: it evaluates a governed pipeline deployed in postgraduate examination preparation, rather than benchmarking model accuracy on a fixed question set. In the broader context of Anki-based learning, Gilbert et al. [1] demonstrated that Anki use was associated with improved examination performance in medical school, and Deng et al. [3] identified retrieval practice as a predictor of licensing examination scores; whether these benefits transfer to LLM-generated flashcards remains to be established in an adequately powered trial. Studies of LLM performance on standardised examinations measure model capability, not learner outcomes; the present pilot addresses educational implementation, a methodologically distinct objective with which model-performance benchmarks should not be conflated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should inform the design of a future trial; they are grouped here by category.

Design and internal validity

Self-selection bias is the dominant internal-validity threat; its direction cannot be determined from the present data, and the planned randomised allocation in the future trial is the only adequate remedy. Recruitment via a nationwide messaging group also introduces convenience sampling, with engaged residents likely to be more motivated and more technologically familiar than the underlying resident population. As shown in Table 1 and detailed in the Baseline characteristics subsection of the Results, all 15 users were fourth-year residents while 2 of 9 non-users held examination eligibility through non-standard pathways; the fourth-year-only sensitivity analysis yielded estimates close to those from the full sample, but training environment and examination familiarity remain plausible confounders. No information was collected on the study methods, total study hours, or competing AI-tool exposure of non-users, so observed differences may reflect total study effort rather than material quality; a structured comparator log will be a design requirement for the future trial. Although participants were drawn from multiple training hospitals, individual institutional affiliations were not collected, precluding analysis of centre-level effects.

Measurement

The self-efficacy outcome relied on a single Likert item, which precludes formal reliability estimation. The three-item trust-in-AI scale, after reverse-coding the reverse-worded item E3, yielded Cronbach’s alpha of 0.583, which did not meet the 0.70 threshold; a validated multi-item AI-trust instrument is specified for the future trial. The common final cognitive load items referred to the overall study experience rather than MeducAI-specific cognitive load, diluting the ability to detect material-specific effects; MeducAI-specific items are specified for the future trial. Component-level satisfaction ratings were not collected separately by artifact type, so claims about which artifact (flashcards versus summary tables versus slides) contributed most to any signal cannot be supported by the present data. All measures were self-reported, which raises recall and social desirability concerns. Baseline learning-tool variables and non-user context variables specified in the protocol are not available in the de-identified analysis dataset, as noted in the Outcomes subsection of the Methods.

Power and timing

The small sample of 24 completers limits statistical power. Because this pilot was not powered for medium effects, the lack of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of no educational benefit; the observed effect-size point estimates and 95% confidence intervals should be used for sample size planning rather than as estimates of educational efficacy. The planned ANCOVA sensitivity analysis adjusting for training status could not be conducted owing to the small sample. The four-week window between material distribution and the board examination may have been too short for cumulative learning to manifest, and a future trial should evaluate longer exposure windows.

Transparency and generalizability

The study was not registered in a public clinical trial registry. Because this observational pilot did not prospectively assign participants to an intervention, it does not meet the ICMJE and WHO definitions of a clinical trial requiring prospective registration; nonetheless, the future definitive trial will be prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, CRIS/KCT, or another WHO ICTRP Primary Registry, prior to enrollment of the first participant. This study was conducted in a single national context using a single LLM pipeline; generalisability to other specialties, countries, or LLM systems should not be assumed without further evaluation.

Implications for a definitive trial

These limitations restrict causal interpretation but do not negate the pilot-study contribution. Rather, they identify the specific operational, measurement, and comparator-design elements that must be modified before a definitive efficacy trial: retention procedures, randomised allocation, structured comparator logging, material-specific cognitive-load assessment, validated multi-item self-efficacy and AI-trust instruments, and artifact-level user-experience measurement. The estimates and design lessons reported here provide a concrete foundation for a fully powered, multicentre randomised controlled trial. Sample-size projections indicate that 160 participants would provide 80% power to detect a medium effect (d = 0.50). Given the 38.6% national recruitment rate observed in this pilot, achieving this target from the 114 annual board examination candidates alone would require multi-year recruitment; a more practical strategy is to extend recruitment to the annual in-training examination, which encompasses radiology residents across all four training years at a comparable difficulty level and provides a substantially larger eligible pool. The future trial should incorporate (1) randomised rather than self-selected group allocation, with stratification by training year and institution; (2) standardised comparator-arm assessment via a structured comparator log capturing preparation strategies, total study hours, and resources used; (3) artifact-specific outcome measures separating Anki flashcards, infographic summary tables, and presentation slides; (4) a multi-item self-efficacy scale (for example, the MSLQ academic self-efficacy subscale [18]) and a validated multi-item AI trust instrument; (5) MeducAI-specific cognitive load items rather than common-experience items, to detect material-specific cognitive load effects; and (6) operational improvements including timed reminders, shortened instruments, exit-reason capture, and incentives for the post-examination survey. Implementation considerations for a multicentre rollout will also need to be addressed in the trial protocol, including local information-technology support, integration with existing institutional learning management systems, sustainability of pipeline updates with each examination cycle, and clarification of authorship and accountability for AI-generated educational content. Although this study was conducted in a single national context using a single LLM pipeline, the feasibility metrics, retention challenges, and effect-size estimates reported here may guide researchers planning similar LLM-based educational interventions in other medical specialties and other national training systems.

Conclusions

Taken together, the 58.5% retention rate, the trust-scale alpha of 0.583, and the − 40 Net Promoter Score indicate that the operational delivery protocol, the AI-trust instrumentation, and the user-experience design all require modification before an efficacy trial is justified. Deployment of a governed LLM pipeline was operationally feasible at the levels of enrollment yield (93.2%) and voluntary adoption (62.5%), but not at the level of post-examination retention. The adapted extraneous cognitive load and satisfaction scales showed acceptable psychometric properties for use in a future trial; the trust-in-AI scale and single-item self-efficacy outcome did not meet conventional reliability thresholds and should be replaced by validated multi-item instruments. As expected for a pilot study, between-group differences in cognitive load and self-efficacy were small and non-significant; the observed effect-size point estimates are reported as inputs to sample-size planning, not as evidence for or against educational benefit. The study was conducted in a single national context using a single LLM pipeline, and the operational and measurement lessons reported here may inform researchers planning similar LLM-based educational interventions in other medical specialties and other national training systems.
