Periprocedural allergy evaluation is safe and effective to guide subsequent anesthesia administration
Authors: Jane Liao, Ami Belmont, Chang Su, Lucia You, Jessica Oh, Moeun Son, Jason H Kwah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are limited data on allergy evaluation following suspected periprocedural hypersensitivity reactions, and skin testing for these reactions is not yet validated.ObjectiveWe sought to describe the epidemiology of periprocedural reactions and safety and effectiveness of allergy testing in a contemporary US patient cohort.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of adult patients evaluated at a tertiary-care academic center for presumed periprocedural hypersensitivity reaction from March 2013 to June 2023. We examined patient demographics, index reactions, tryptase levels, skin testing results, and tolerance of subsequent anesthesia.ResultsOver 10 years, 74 patients underwent comprehensive allergy evaluation. Testing was positive in 30% (22/74). The most common culprits included antibiotics (10, 45%), local anesthetics (3, 14%), and neuromuscular blocking agents (3, 14%). Of 29 patients who received subsequent anesthesia, 28 (97%) didn’t have reactions. Patients with perioperative anaphylaxis were more likely to have positive testing compared to patients with low-grade reactions (63% vs 9%, P < .001). Fifty-one percent (19/37) of patients tested within ≤6 months of index reaction had positive testing, versus 11% (4/38) tested at >6 months (P < .001).ConclusionPeriprocedural allergy testing was safe and effective in preventing future reactions. Testing was more likely to be positive if performed within 6 months of hypersensitivity reaction and in patients with high-grade index reactions. Antibiotics were the most common culprits; emerging agents like chlorhexidine and sugammadex were also identified. The landscape of POA is evolving, and formal US guidelines are needed to guide clinicians in evaluation.Key words: Intraoperative anaphylaxis, perioperative anaphylaxis, skin testing, drug allergy, anesthesia, tryptase, cefazolin, midazolam, neuromuscular blocking agents

Perioperative hypersensitivity (POH) reactions including anaphylaxis are rare but potentially life-threatening events. There has been considerable variability in reported incidence as a result of many factors, including regional differences in selection of drugs, lack of consensus in diagnostic workup, and heterogeneity of studies. The reported incidence of perioperative anaphylaxis (POA) ranges from approximately 1 in 320 procedures (reported in the United States)1,2 to 1 in 10,000 (reported in the United Kingdom). Identification of a culprit agent in POH can be beneficial in stewarding the safe administration of medications during subsequent procedures and surgeries. However, this can be diagnostically challenging because skin testing for most agents, aside from penicillin antibiotics,3,4 has not been validated. Additionally, evaluation in private clinic offices and the community setting can be limited, as some anesthetic medications are controlled and distributed through inpatient pharmacies. Finally, it is important to interpret testing results in the context of local and current drug allergy epidemiology, which is not always well established.

There are currently limited data on perioperative evaluations and outcomes, with a small number of large published studies from Europe and several smaller retrospective studies from the United States.1,5, 6, 7, 8 These data have shown that antibiotics were the most common culprit agents identified in the United States, accounting for 40% to 55% of anaphylactic reactions.1,6,9 In contrast, neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBA) were more prevalent causes in Europe, Australia, Hong Kong, and New Zealand, where they accounted for 50% to 70% of POA.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Most US studies were conducted between 1992 and 2017, and since then, case series have reported emerging causes of anaphylaxis, such as sugammadex.14, 15, 16 To date, we are aware of one multisite US study (n = 100), which included data up to 2021 on POH; this study did not identify any cases of confirmed sugammadex allergy. Additionally, it did not include a site in the Northeast United States.17 There has been a call for additional research on causative agents in POH, with increasing recognition that drug allergy epidemiology is changing, and that there can also be significant regional differences in causative agents.16,18

We conducted a retrospective cohort study with the following aims: (1) to assess contemporary epidemiology of POH at a large tertiary-care academic center in the Northeast United States, (2) to evaluate the safety of perioperative allergy testing and its effectiveness in informing future anesthesia, and (3) to explore factors that may influence the identification of a culprit agent.

Methods

We performed a retrospective cohort study of all patients who underwent comprehensive allergy evaluations for perioperative/periprocedural reactions at the Yale–New Haven Hospital allergy and immunology clinic from March 2013 to June 2023. Patients were identified via the clinic pharmacy log of all skin testing procedures, then cross-referenced with corresponding electronic medical records (EMRs). Additionally, the Joint Data Analytics Team at Yale University performed a query of the EMRs of all patients who had undergone drug allergy testing using Current Procedural Terminology code 95018. This list was filtered by an allergy and immunology fellow and attending physician to identify those who underwent POH drug allergy testing by manual chart review. We included adult patients (≥18 years old) who underwent allergy testing after a suspected immediate hypersensitivity reaction (HSR)3 during the perioperative period of a surgery (during induction, maintenance, or immediate recovery) or a procedure with anesthesia. Reactions were considered immediate on the basis of the evaluation from allergy and immunology providers. This study was approved by the Yale–New Haven Health System institutional review board. No patient consent was required. We adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for data reporting.

All patients in this cohort received comprehensive allergy evaluations during their initial clinic visits with allergy and immunology, which included a detailed review of the EMR, including anesthesia, surgical, operative, and nursing records. There were no significant changes to the EMR or documentation during the 10-year study period. When clinically appropriate, our clinic performed skin testing to potential culprit drugs administered during the perioperative and periprocedural period using previously published nonirritating concentrations (Table I),5,6,19 with positive (histamine) and negative (saline) controls. All testing was performed using the same technique by various members of our nursing team. We considered a skin test result to be positive if the wheal diameter was ≥3 mm larger than saline control, with flare greater than wheal. When possible, drug challenges were administered after negative skin test results. Each comprehensive allergy evaluation included recommendations for subsequent introduction of anesthesia.

We collected data on the following: sociodemographic background, atopic history, drug and vaccine allergy history, index reactions, skin testing results, tryptase levels, and subsequent tolerance of anesthesia. We described index reactions on the basis of organ system involvement including cutaneous, cardiovascular, pulmonary, neurologic, oropharyngeal, and gastrointestinal manifestations. Reactions were further classified on the basis of the Ring and Messmer grading scale (I-IV), as follows: I (skin symptoms and/or mild fever reaction), II (measurable but not life-threatening cardiovascular reaction [tachycardia, hypotension], gastrointestinal disturbance [nausea], respiratory disturbance), III (shock, life-threatening spasm of smooth muscles [bronchi, uterus, etc], and IV (cardiac and/or respiratory arrest).20 Grade III and IV reactions were considered life-threatening reactions requiring immediate treatment. POA was defined as reactions with grade III/IV severity or grade II reactions with hypotension.1,20,21 We collected data on subsequent operative/procedural encounters by detailed EMR review of all available internal and external anesthesia, surgical, operative, and nursing notes with notation of readministration of agents tested during allergy evaluation and any recurrence of HSRs.

We generated descriptive statistics for variables of interest, including frequency distributions for categorical variables, and mean (or median) and standard deviations (or interquartile ranges) for continuous variables. Fisher exact tests were used to compare proportions between groups. Statistical analysis was performed by and figures created in Microsoft Excel v16.100.3 (Microsoft, Redmond, Wash) and Stata v18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Tex). P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics

Of 74 patients who completed testing (Fig 1), 61 (82%) were female (Table II), with a mean ± standard deviation age at time of evaluation of 49.3 ± 18.1 years. The median (interquartile range) time from index reaction to initial evaluation was 115.5 (40.0-1095.0) days. Most patients reported history of other drug allergy (62%, 46/74) and/or at least one atopic disorder (62%, 46/74). A history of mast cell disorder was reported in 2 patients (3%).

Index reaction to anesthesia

Forty-six patients (62%) had grade I or II reactions, and 28 (38%) had grade III or IV reactions (Table II, Fig 2). Two patients with grade II reactions developed hypotension as part of their reaction. Thirty cases (40%) met the definition for POA. Fifty patients (68%) had cutaneous symptoms, 26 (35%) had pulmonary symptoms, 25 (34%) had cardiovascular symptoms, 17 (23%) had oropharyngeal symptoms, 11 (15%) had neurologic symptoms, and 2 (3%) had gastrointestinal symptoms. Forty-five percent (21/47) of atopic patients had POA, while 60% (9/15) of nonatopic patients had POA.

Serum tryptase was measured within 4 hours of index reaction in 10 patients (Table III). Five patients had mean (interquartile range) acute tryptase levels of >11.4 (24.4-215) ng/L, and 5 had levels <11.4 (2.4-9.1) ng/L. Two patients had acute tryptase levels <11.4 ng/L but met the criteria for mast cell activation on the basis of a clinically significant increase from baseline tryptase.22 In total, 7 patients were considered to have elevated tryptase levels at time of reaction, all of whom had high-grade reactions. A baseline tryptase was measured in 22 patients (29%): 3 between 8.5 and 9.1 ng/L, 1 at >11.4 ng/L, and the remainder <8 ng/L. Three patients with acute tryptase levels measured at the time of reaction did not have baseline levels for comparison.

Allergy evaluation and recommendations

Among the 74 patients who underwent testing, at least one causative agent was identified by skin testing or drug challenge in 22 patients (30%) (Table III, Fig 3). Ten patients (45%) tested positive to antibiotics (Fig 3), with cefazolin the most common (80%, 8/10). The second most common drug classes were NMBAs (14%, 3/22) and local anesthetics (14%, 3/22). One patient with a grade III index reaction had a positive lidocaine challenge despite negative skin testing, and 2 patients had grade III and IV index reactions with positive skin testing to local anesthetics (lidocaine, bupivacaine). Two patients had positive testing to benzodiazepines, 2 to chlorhexidine, 2 to corticosteroids, 2 to propofol, and 1 to sugammadex. No patients developed systemic reactions to skin testing. Fifty-one percent (19/37) of patients tested within 6 months of index reaction (37/74) were positive, versus 11% (4/38) of patients tested >6 months after index reaction (Fisher exact test; unadjusted odds ratio = 8.97; 95% confidence interval, 2.65-30.35; P < .001). Among cases that met the criteria for POA, 63% (19/30) patients had positive testing, versus 9% (4/47) of patients with low-grade reactions (P < .001). All 4 patients with baseline tryptase levels >8 had positive testing. Three patients had grade III reactions, and 1 patient had a grade II reaction.

After comprehensive allergy evaluation, 97% (28/29) of all patients who received subsequent procedures with anesthesia did not have a HSR (7 patients with positive evaluations and 21 with negative evaluations). One patient tested positive to chlorhexidine, then was inadvertently reexposed to hidden chlorhexidine in a triple lumen catheter during a subsequent procedure and experienced reaction again. Among patients who tolerated subsequent anesthesia, 82% (23/28) were reintroduced to at least one agent to which they had tested negative. Among patients with POA who underwent another procedure, 92% (11/12) tolerated subsequent anesthesia.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this was the first study evaluating the contemporary epidemiology of POH in the Northeast United States since 2017. Using a comprehensive algorithm for allergy evaluation, a potential culprit was found in approximately one third of patients, which was consistent with previous studies.1,5,6 Additionally, 97% of patients who received subsequent procedures tolerated them, including 92% of patients who met the criteria for POA. This suggests that perioperative allergy evaluation helped facilitate the safe passage of subsequent procedures, even in the most severe cases. Positive testing, suggesting an IgE-mediated mechanism, was more common in patients with POA (compared to lower-grade reactions), as well as among patients with testing performed within 6 months of the index reaction. Emerging agents relevant to the current landscape of POH in the United States, such as chlorhexidine and sugammadex, were also identified in this contemporary cohort.

In our study, a causative agent was identified in 30% of patients. This is consistent with other US studies, which reported positives tests in 18% to 50% of patients.1,5,6,17 A recent US-based multicenter study reported a 50% positivity rate; however, only patients with suspected POA were included in the study.17 In comparison, European studies12,23,24 have reported positive testing in 50% to 78% patients, and a large Hong Kong study13 found a culprit in 75% of cases. While skin testing is reported as the most sensitive testing modality overall, basophil activation tests and specific IgE tests, which are not commercially available in the United States, were found to be more sensitive and specific than skin testing for chlorhexidine in Hong Kong.13 This finding suggests the possibility of lower sensitivity in US cohorts due in part to the utilization of skin testing alone. Differing study methodology (eg, inclusion of patients with more mild reactions) may also explain differences in positive testing between studies.

Our study identified antibiotics, especially cefazolin, as the most common culprit of periprocedural HSR, aligning with previously published US data.1,5,6,9 Similar to previously published US studies, NMBAs were not the most common culprits compared to European cohorts; this may be due to regional differences in sensitization (eg, due to differing patterns of anesthesia utilization and/or exposure to cross-reactive products), as well as differences in methods of testing.12,24 NMBAs and antibiotics such as fluroquinolones can cause nonspecific mast cell degranulation through the Mas-related G protein-coupled receptor member X2 (MRGPRX2), triggering non–IgE-mediated immediate reactions. As such, there is a possibility that positive skin testing to NMBAs and fluroquinolones in this cohort could have been due to direct activation through MRGPRX2. In vitro testing, which was unfortunately unavailable, could have been helpful to further evaluate this. Local anesthetics were identified as culprits in 14% of patients in our cohort. This was surprising because local anesthetics have not been previously identified as a leading cause of POH or as a common culprit for IgE-mediated allergy, which accounts for <1% of adverse reactions to local anesthetics.1,5,6,17,25 Two patients were identified by positive skin testing and 1 by positive challenge. Although nonirritating concentrations published in the literature were used for local anesthetic testing, there remains the possibility of false-positive results, considering the low prevalence of IgE-mediated allergy reported in the literature. Alternatively, lidocaine allergy prevalence could be an increasing cause of POH.

Sugammadex, a reversal agent for neuromuscular blockade, was identified as a culprit agent in one patient in this cohort. Sugammadex is increasingly identified as an emerging culprit in POA, with 0.3% incidence of anaphylaxis reported by the manufacturer’s package insert (n = 299). However, larger real-world studies showed much lower incidence. For example, in several large multicenter studies in Japan and Australia, the reported incidence ranges from 0.004% to 0.02%.26,27 The most common presenting symptoms of sugammadex hypersensitivity are hypotension and flushing/erythema. However, identification may be delayed, as onset of reaction may range between 5 and 30 minutes after administration.15 The patient with sugammadex allergy in our study had a grade III reaction; allergy was identified on the basis of a positive skin prick test. Sugammadex was approved for use in the United States in 2015. Although we only identified a single positive case in our cohort, which limits the ability to draw strong epidemiologic conclusions, our results highlight a need to understand the epidemiology of sugammadex allergy in the United States, especially with increasing incidence of exposure over time.

Similarly, chlorhexidine is also an increasing concern in contemporary POA, with a recent study reporting it as among the top 4 most common culprit of POA in pooled data from 8 of 13 clinics from the United Kingdom, Australia, and Denmark.28 Hypersensitivity to this agent can be life-threatening, with severe presentations marked by hypotension and cardiovascular compromise.29 Chlorhexidine is commonly included in skin preps, skin wipes, and impregnated central venous catheters; however, it can also be present in many hidden forms, such as gels and devices, which are often not recorded in the medical record.28 Chlorhexidine was found as a culprit in 2 patients in our cohort. Given its common use in the surgical setting and its emergence as a potential culprit, routine chlorhexidine testing as part of POA evaluation should be considered. We also note that the one patient in our cohort with positive testing to chlorhexidine experienced a grade IV reaction when he was inadvertently reexposed to chlorhexidine during a subsequent surgery through an impregnated catheter. This highlights a need for increased awareness of chlorhexidine allergy and that its presence can be hidden in surgical materials, such as catheters. Notably, chlorhexidine is also present in small amounts in many over-the-counter products (eg, mouth rinses, lozenges, creams). As such, another research need is to determine how much patients with chlorhexidine allergy are at risk for reactions when exposed to minute amounts in household products.

In our cohort, 97% patients receiving subsequent surgeries did not have HSRs. These results are similar to those published recently by Gonzalez-Estrada et al,17 who reported that 100% of patients evaluated for POA tolerated subsequent surgeries without POA. Previously Banerji et al1 reported a 91% tolerance rate (78/85) for subsequence anesthesia from their Northeast US cohort spanning 2003 to 2017. Tolerance rates above 90% in this study and others continue to indicate the importance of POH evaluations, to allow for the safe administration of future anesthesia.

Our data are consistent with previous data that support that a causative agent is more likely to be found if the patient undergoes evaluation within 6 months of the index reaction. In a large retrospective study of 677 patients, a causative agent was found in 74% of patients tested within 6 weeks, 62.6% of patients tested between 6 weeks and 6 months, and 50% of patients tested >6 months after index reaction.30 Given the wide variation in timing of evaluation in our cohort, the positivity rate could have been affected by falsely negative results with delayed testing. Non–immune-mediated reactions are additional reasons to consider for negative skin testing. Overall, this suggests that allergy testing should occur promptly after suspected POH.

In our study, patients who met criteria for POA were more likely to have positive testing compared to patients with low-grade reactions, suggesting severe perioperative reactions are more likely to be IgE mediated. Akten et al31 found positive β-lactam skin tests were more common in patients with grade III/IV reactions than those with grade I/II reactions. Similarly, a study of 162 patients in France found higher skin positivity rates among patients with high-grade reactions.32 These findings suggest that severity of perioperative reactions may be a predictor of positive allergy testing (and perhaps IgE-mediated allergy).

Furthermore, an acute tryptase level was sent in only 10 patients in our cohort and was more likely to be sent in patients with high-grade reactions. This could be because of a variety of reasons, including lack of immediate laboratory access (ie, in dental offices), variable understanding of anaphylaxis workup, and strict timing requirements. Acute tryptase is ideally sent within 2 hours of symptom onset, and accurate interpretation requires baseline levels after resolution of symptoms,21 which is difficult in most emergency department workflows. The sensitivity of acute tryptase for anaphylaxis is 21% in the emergency department33 and 64% in the perioperative setting,21 which may also contribute to lack of uniform consensus on its utility. Additionally, acute tryptase results are not immediately available in most cases, so management is typically based on clinical presentation and not on testing alone.33 It is also interesting to note that all 4 patients with baseline tryptase >8 had positive testing and 3 patients had high-grade reactions, which is consistent with a previous study that showed association between high baseline tryptase and reaction severity.34 Elevated baseline tryptase (≥8 ng/mL) is found in hereditary α-tryptasemia, which is associated with an increased risk of severe drug allergy and anaphylaxis.35 Although these observations are important, acute and baseline tryptase were sent for a small number of patients in our cohort, which limits the strength of conclusion on the role of tryptase. Future research to better understand the underlying mechanism and drug allergy prevalence is needed in these patients.

Limitations of this study include its relatively small sample size, retrospective single-center study design, and lack of utilization of in vitro IgE testing, which may limit generalizability of findings. Although our cohort tested emerging agents such as chlorhexidine and sugammadex, other agents, including latex and patent blue dye, were not universally evaluated in our cohort. In addition, while our center used nonirritating skin testing concentrations reported in major publications,3,19,21,36 variability of skin testing concentrations exists across the literature, which may affect the findings. Furthermore, there is variation on what is considered a positive intradermal skin test in the literature as well, which can affect the reproducibility of the results. All of these reasons highlight the importance of developing US guidelines to allow for greater standardization of testing moving forward. Referral bias could have also limited the inclusion of some milder cases, although these likely would not have been as clinically relevant. Variability in time between index reaction and allergy testing could have increased false-negative testing results. However, a major strength of this study is its large contemporary cohort from a robust urban academic health system that specializes in complex drug allergy evaluations.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that a comprehensive allergy evaluation is safe and effective in preventing future perioperative/periprocedural reactions. A culprit was identified in about a third of patients, and antibiotics were the most common culprit. We also identified emerging culprit agents, including chlorhexidine and sugammadex, in this contemporary Northeast US cohort. Patients whose disease met criteria for POA and those with testing within 6 months of index reactions were more likely to test positive; however, nearly all patients with high-grade reactions tolerated future anesthesia after appropriate allergy evaluations. Our findings provide a valuable addition to the current literature on the evolving topic of POH reactions and further encourage the development of formal US guidelines to support clinicians in the evaluation of perioperative reactions and guidance for future administration of anesthesia.
