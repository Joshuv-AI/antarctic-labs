3. Executive Summary:

The incorporation of distributed energy resources (DERs) such as solar generation and
energy storage, as well as digital processes for automation, remote control, and data
acquisition technologies, has the potential to enhance the capability of the power grid to
withstand high-impact events (e.g., faults caused by natural disasters) that can
adversely affect the continuous supply of electricity to customers, incurring substantial
economic losses. The growing use of communication networks and digital devices,
however, has made power distribution systems vulnerable to cyberattacks.
Conventional network security tools, such as firewalls, two-factor authentication, and
signature-based intrusion detection systems may be insufficient to protect against
sophisticated and motivated adversaries. As a consequence, attacks tailored to imitate
the physical signatures of a fault (e.g., forced relay trip attack) would be rather difficult to
differentiate, inducing inadequate restoration actions (e.g., dispatch a crew to find a
nonexistent line fault). Thus, system operators who have traditionally dealt with and
responded to faults must also face cyberattacks, which are, by nature, deceptive and
stealthy and require different response actions.

This project focused on designing, developing, and validating the Automated Resilience
Management System (ARMS) solution, which integrates different systems to collect,
store and process relevant information from the power distribution system to
automatically manage the operation of multiple energy assets to enhance the resilience
of the power grid against high-impact physical incidents (e.g., hurricanes) and cyber
threats. The developed solution comprises a suite of novel AI-based algorithms
designed to first detect, locate, and classify anomalies by collecting and analyzing data
from multiple monitoring and control devices. After an incident is located and classified,
the ARMS solution uses a novel hierarchical architecture that combines the benefits of
AI and mathematical optimization to compute the set of actions to control distributed
energy resources such as solar energy systems and energy storage systems as well as
the distribution system topology in order to restore the electricity supply and reduce the
impact caused to the system. The ARMS solution has been tested and validated using
IEEE benchmark systems as well as one of PacifiCorp’s circuits in Utah using a
hardware-in-the-loop real-time testbed. The results have shown the feasibility of the
developed technology to quickly respond after faults or cyber attacks, decreasing
restoration times and providing operators with enough information to improve their
decision-making process. The proposed technology is expected to have widespread
applications for power grid operators, and it will directly impact the reliability of electricity
for critical loads such as hospitals and government facilities. In particular, the ARMS
solution has the potential to enhance the capabilities of electricity utilities to execute
contingency plans necessary to restore the normal operation of the power grid and
increase the situational awareness of the power grid enabling operators to identify the
type of anomaly in order to take adequate restoration actions.

5. Background:
High penetration of small-scale distributed energy resources (DERs) including battery
energy storage systems (BESSs) has offered an unprecedented opportunity to reinforce
the grid at the local level and reduce vulnerability to upstream disruptions [1], [2].
BESSs integrated with solar generation in particular offer operational flexibility and
efficiency that can be leveraged to support the operation of the power grid. As of 2020,
more than 20 GWh of energy storage systems (ESSs) have been installed in power
grids around the world in a variety of forms, including BESSs and pumped-storage
hydropower (PSH). This figure is anticipated to grow to 160 GWh by 2030 [3]. Despite
the promising growth in penetration of ESSs in power grids, the mass operation of these
distributed units is quite challenging. ESS operation requires both strategic response in
normal operation to provide economic operational flexibility, and prompt response in
emergency to restore critical loads after outages. The optimal scheduling of ESSs in
power distribution grids is extensively studied in the literature. Optimization models have
been developed in [4], [5], [6], [7] for scheduling ESSs in distribution systems to provide
services in electricity markets and to counter the uncertainty of renewable energy
sources. Multi-level and hierarchical mixed-integer programming has also been used for
the operation of ESS units in distribution grids [8]. However, by increasing the number
of ESS units and uncertainty sources (such as renewable generation), the
dimensionality of the stochastic optimization problems increases, which makes their
solution too slow and intractable for real-time operation applications. Various techniques
have been suggested to mitigate this curse of dimensionality through dynamic
programming [9]. For instance, stochastic dual dynamic programming has been
proposed in [10] to capture the temporal dependencies of uncertain variables and to
reach a higher computational efficiency. Heuristic methods are also investigated to
minimize the operation cost of ESSs under uncertainty (see e.g., [11]).
Capabilities of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) in finding fast and near-optimal
solutions in stochastic high-dimensional environments has led to its extensive use in
operational research, including DER scheduling in power distribution grids [12], [13]. A
variety of DRL algorithms with continuous or discrete action domain have been adopted
for optimal operation of distributed resources. Using simple actor-critic models [14] or
more advanced Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C) models [15] are common
approaches to generate continuous control signals for DERs. The scalability issue of
using DRL for scheduling numerous DERs is addressed in [16] by using Deep
Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) and breaking down the action space into a
sequence of smaller actions. Optimizing ESS charging schedules has been a
particularly popular application of DRL, as it requires decision-making with long-term
considerations. Discrete reinforcement learning (RL) [17] and deep Q-learning [18] are
two effective methods investigated for selecting the dispatch level of ESSs and optimal
energy management of batteries, with the objective of maximum self-sufficiency. Less
common RL methods, such as a model-free state-action-reward-state-action approach,
have also been studied in [19] to manage the uncertainty of wind generation using
ESSs. Even though DRL can very well handle uncertainty and high dimensionality, it
falls short when feasibility and safety assurances are required. Although unsafe actions
are penalized during DRL training, there is no guarantee that the output domain of the
 Page 5 of 48

DRL-trained agent does not overlap the unsafe or infeasible space. This is particularly
concerning when scheduling ESS units coincides with critical operations such as fault
isolation, load shedding, and voltage control, where taking wrong actions would be quite
costly. A viable solution is to divide operational tasks between intelligent and
optimization-based algorithms, such that high-dimensional decisions are assigned to
intelligent and data-driven methods, leaving the optimization method with a smaller
problem of taking critical actions which can be solved quickly and efficiently. As an
example, Deep Q-Network (DQN) is utilized in [20] to control voltage regulation devices
in long time scales, while integer optimization is simultaneously used to control fast
devices. In [21], multiaccess edge computing in microgrids is decomposed into a high-
level task assignment problem that is solved by combinatorial optimization and an
energy supply planning that is solved by a model-based DRL approach. Data-driven
methods have also been used to simulate constraints [22] or to enhance searching
efficiency [23] of integer programming in the optimal operation of power systems. Prior
efforts for combining DRL and gradient-based optimization for grid operation are either
focused on specific operational tasks, e.g., [21], or on improving the optimization
performance, as in [22], [23]. A comprehensive DRL-aided operational framework that
can schedule numerous DER units under uncertainty, while making safe decisions for
critical tasks such as fault management, is yet to be developed. To the best of our
knowledge, no prior effort has been made to combine data-driven and optimization-
based methods for fast and reliable operation of ESSs in distribution grids.

6. Project Objectives:.

This project demonstrated the integrated, scalable and cost-effective automated
resilience management system (ARMS) solution for enhancing the resilience of U.S.
critical infrastructure by improving the situational awareness and flexibility of solar PV
systems. The ARMS include five key technologies: 1) real-time communication, control
and outage data collection system, 2) cyber-physical outage data management (CP-
ODM) system, 3) State-Awareness for Resilience (StAR) System, 4) Cyber-Secure
Resilient Control (CSRS) System, and 5) visualization engines for situational awareness
and resilience. The project tested and validated the ARMS solution using cyber-physical
distribution testbeds and implemented it on select PacifiCorp circuits serving critical
infrastructure in the state of Utah. The ARMS solution enables situational awareness
and quick response in the presence of different type of events including extreme
weather conditions and cyber attacks. These in turn facilitates the adequate
coordination of large amounts of distributed energy resources and other energy assets
to minimize operation costs, address the intrinsic uncertainty introduced by renewable
resources, and ensure the reliability and resilience of the power distribution system.
The ARMS solution was developed and demonstrated over 12 tasks for three budget
periods. The project tasks included five major efforts as follows:
Effort 1: Research and development of Visualization Engine and Outage Database
(BP1: Tasks 1 and 3, BP2: Task 7)
This effort researched and developed the Visualization Engine (VizEng) and cyber-
physical outage data management (CP-ODM) system. The VizEng is the conduit,

 Page 6 of 48

collator, and distiller of the information from its sources to the ultimate decision makers
through the several applications. The CP-ODM is a database and analytics package for
consolidating the cyber-physical outage data coming from different sensors,
communication medium and protocols. The CP-ODM distributes the outage data
available to StAR and CSRC systems for situational awareness and control purposes.
Effort 2: Research and development of infrastructure and cyber state awareness
engines (BP1: Task 2, BP2: Task 6)
This effort researched and developed the infrastructure and cyber state awareness for
resilience (InStAR and CyStAR) engines. The InStAR engine provides the capability for
optimally integrating, monitoring, and controlling the distributed solar systems, DERs
and automated switches to prioritize the emergency response to critical infrastructure
with a heightened state awareness in spite of possible uncertainty in operating
conditions. The CyStAR engine provides the capability for detecting and evaluating
cyber threats to allow threat accommodation and reconfiguration of the system against
attacks.
Effort 3: Research and development of cyber secure resilience control system
(BP1: Task 4, BP2: Task 8)
This effort researched and developed the cyber secure resilience control (CSRC)
system. The CSRC system utilizes the distributed solar PV systems, flexible loads,
energy storage, and automated switches to enhance the resilience of critical
infrastructure. Furthermore, the controller integrates a real-time cyber physical security
module that is able to determine the location of anomalies and classify them as faults,
cyber attacks, and other type of events.
Effort 4: Integration, testing and validation of ARMS solution (BP2: Task 9, 10,
BP3: Task 12)
During this effort, the development and integration of the different components of the
ARMS solution were finalized. The ARMS solution was tested and validated using a
cyber-physical distribution testbed and implemented in a high-fidelity emulation of select
PacifiCorp circuits.
Effort 5: Project management, outreach and commercialization (BP1: Task 5, BP2:
Task 11, BP3: Task 13)
This effort coordinated the project tasks and communication activities with project
members to maintain an up-to-date work plan. Periodic financial and technical reports
were prepared and submitted as part of this effort.

7. Project Results and Discussion:

Each of the tasks and milestones defined in the SOPO were successfully completed
and delivered according to the defined success values. The following summary includes
results towards the development and validation of the ARMS solution. Tasks and
milestones have been organized according to the SOPO to clearly assess the progress
throughout the project duration. Some tasks laid the foundation for technical
developments with more tangible results in later tasks.

Task 1: Initial research and development of Visualization Engine (VizEng)

The Visualization Engine (VizEng) takes the available information from field devices and
computed state awareness metrics and displays them in a way it is informative and
useful for decision makers (e.g., system operators, IT/OT team). The milestones were
completed to progressively develop the VizEng in close collaboration with utility partners
and integrate it with the different available sources of data. In particular, the VizEng
encompass two main parts: 1) Situational Awareness Visualization used to provide data
to operators from different roles and facilitate the decision-making process; 2) Control
and Automated Response Visualization, which provides real-time information about the
automated actions taken by the cyber-secure resilience Control System (CSRC) of the
ARMS solution.

by exploiting various points of entry. A cyber sensor is being designed and implemented

the network traffic. The sensor is connected to a switched port analyzer (SPAN) port in

Through the application of anomaly detection, which ingests and evaluates both cyber
and physical datasets, a correlation of undesired behaviors and the distinction between
malicious and benign was achieved. Anomalous behavior corresponds to any change in
the system, either from natural causes or man-made, that causes the power distribution
to operate different from its normal behavior. An anomaly can be either physical (e.g.,
drastic current increase caused by a short-circuit fault) or cyber (e.g., sudden
disconnection of a protection relay by a malicious actor), The initial anomaly detection
prototype consisted of two anomaly detection systems (ADSs): 1) a physical ADS which
detects anomalous behavior from the physical data 2) a cyber-ADS which detects
anomalous behavior from the packet features extracted with the cyber sensor. The ADS
systems developed in this effort are data-driven systems that are trained to identify
events that deviate from expected normal operation which is automatically inferred from
by trusted datasets of normal operation. After obtaining anomaly reports from both
cyber and physical ADSs, the metrics algorithm provides a weighted evaluation of the
cyber and physical contribution to provide evidence to the root cause.

The cyber-physical outage data management system is used to collect, process, and
distribute the data from multiple sources to the respective modules. The CP-ODM
system is composed by the data management module, which incorporates the APIs and
scripts to establish communication (e.g., Modbus TCP Client/Slave) with different
devices and then publish them to the data base.

Subtask 4.1: Distributed fault detection in distribution networks

The project team proposed and developed the Fault and Attack Location and
ClassificatiON (FALCON) system to classify and locate cyber and physical anomalies,
including false data injection attacks on protection devices, replay attacks in
communication networks, and physical faults on distribution lines. More specifically, the
proposed FALCON system receives data and selects relevant features from protection
relays and Fault Indicators (FIs or also referred as CFCI) to determine whether an
anomaly in a power distribution system is caused by a physical fault or a cyber-attack
and identify the location of the anomaly. The FALCON system is capable of identifying
and locating short-circuit faults as well as false data injection and replay attacks
(targeting up to two devices simultaneously) in distribution systems. At its core, the
FALCON system is a multi-class classifier based on a trained deep neural network
(DNN) that classifies multiple types of faults and cyber-attacks using the outage data
received from protections relays and FIs as feature inputs to the DNN. The numerical

studies on IEEE 13-bus test feeder demonstrate that FALCON maintains a high
accuracy and outperforms alternative approaches in classifying and locating various

FALCON: The initial development of FALCON used a DNN to determine the type of
anomaly and its location. However, the proposed approach was not scalable due to a
class was based on the duple type and location, such that a large number of locations will
exponentially increase the number of classes. To address this issue, FALCON was
enhanced using multi-task learning (MTL), such that anomaly class and location are found
separately, significantly reducing the number of classes. In addition, to better handle the
inherent transient in power distribution systems, long-short term memory (LSTM)
recurrent neural network was used.

Falcon Safeguards: In addition, since the ARMS workflow relies on FALCON’s accuracy,
errors could lead to the re-energization of faulted or compromised parts of the network.
To ensure this does not happen and enhance system reliability, several safeguards verify
FALCON’s outputs. A red flag is set, and automation is halted if any of the following
conditions are met: i) A fault current indicator is triggered, but FALCON classifies the
anomaly as an attack; ii) FALCON identifies a fault upstream of a protection device that
tripped due to a fault; iii) FALCON locates a fault downstream of a protection device that
did not trip; iv) FALCON detects a fault downstream of a fault current indicator that did
not activate. When a red flag is set, automation is paused to prevent the re-energization
of compromised devices or faulted infrastructure, ensuring safe and reliable grid operation
and an alarm is triggered such that system operators can verify the output of FALCON
and analyze the collected data.

Hierarchical Resilience Controller: The IRC developed in task 4 was able to quickly
respond to critical events such as hurricanes and reschedule DER setpoints. However,
given that ML-based solutions are not fully able to guarantee feasibility of their responses,
the project team has developed a hierarchical resilience controller architecture that
devises fast real-time operation decisions to strategically dispatch distributed generation
(DG) and energy storage (ES) units for restoring power to customers after sudden
outages by combining mathematical optimization with deep-reinforcement learning. The
proposed hierarchical operation model consists of a central controller (CC), multiple local
controllers (LC) in various distribution zones, and a designated flow of communication

                                                Page 26 of 48

and commands between them, as shown in Fig. 8. Within each zone, the active power
dispatch of ESS, as well as upper and lower bounds on reactive power generation is
determined by the LC. In the grid level, the CC solves a power flow problem for the whole
system, where each zone is seen as a node with a net power demand, and determines
the reactive power required at each node (i.e., zone) to maintain the voltage within limits.
Once the CC determines the reactive power setpoint for each zone, it sends them back
to LCs that use it to schedule the reactive power of all inverter-based DERs (ESS and
DG units) within their zones. The CC, through the FISR, also operates tie-switches to
isolate zones that contain fault and restore power to healthy interrupted zones. In zones
that are isolated by the CC due to existence of a fault, all DERs within the zone will be
shut down. Figure 17 illustrates the division of operational tasks between CC and LCs,
and the hierarchical sequence of decision making in each time step.

Central Controller
Fault Isolation and Service Restoration (FISR): Particularly, after an event has been
located and classified, the central controller, through the FISR algorithm, reconfigures the
network to isolate the fault and impede its propagation. To address these complexities,
the proposed FISR model formulation consists of a mixed integer programming problem
that integrates binary variables to characterize the status of lines (i.e., energized, de-
energized) in the network, and the power flow constraints. Two power flow
approximations are proposed, namely quadratic constrained (QC) and linear constrained
(LC).

Local Controller
The local setpoints for active power generation computed by each LC utilizes deep
reinforcement learning. In this work, TD3 algorithm, which is a robust variation of DDPG,
is used to implement DRL and identify optimal policies in a continuous action space. The
training process goes on for as many episodes as needed to converge the Q-values.
Once the network is trained and the loss function converges to below a certain value, the
trained model is detached from the training setup and used directly to schedule ESS units
within each zone.

Subtask 12.3: Finalize testing and validation of ARMS solution components
In this task, the project team has performed a large variety of testing for different scenarios
to validate the performance of the ARMS solution. Scenarios include different number of
zones, different number of grid-forming inverters as well as the computation of resilience
curves for faults and cyber attacks. In addition, the central controller optimization has
been enhanced to include a reconfiguration against remote-tripping attacks in protection
devices.
Response to Remote-Tripping Attacks: One of the most impactful cyberattacks are the
ones that are able to send remote tripping commands to a recloser causing an outage to
the downstream zone. The main challenge however for the operator is that even if the
attack is localized, an attacker may still have control over the device while adequate
measures are taken. Therefore, conventional reconfiguration is no longer viable. If an
attacker starts switching on and off the recloser, it may cause significant damage to the
equipment by powering on and off the area. In addition, if conventional reconfiguration
actions were taken and the affected zone is powered by a tie-line, then if the attack closes
the recloser it would cause loops that could instabilize the system. For this reason, it is
necessary to reconfigure the entire system in a way that the adjacent zones to a
compromised recloser are isolated. In that way, intermittent switching is not going to have
an impact on any zone adjacent to the affected device. To this end, we formulated an
optimization problem that aims to minimize the load not served while ensuring that any
zone adjacent to an affected device is isolated. In the example (see Fig. 12), we consider
an attack in recloser 5 (R5). Tripping R5 will cause an outage on Zone 5. The optimal
reconfiguration is then to isolate Z3 such that if R5 is closed again, Z5 remains still
isolated, avoiding damages caused by intermittent power. Given that to isolate Z3 it is
necessary to trip R7, R4, and R3, the optimal solution is to close T2 to provide power to
Z4, and activate the grid-forming capabilities in Z8. In that way, the attack will have
minimum impact on the system, giving time to the system operators to coordinate
adequate actions to remove the attack.

Circuit Selection: The digital real time simulator used in the CPSR testbed uses
ephasor sim, which is a powerful tool to model detailed dynamics of the system.
However, the maximum number of buses that can be simulated goes up to 120 buses.
In addition, given that the implementation of the proposed ARMS solution in the CPSR
testbed requires the digital real time simulator to handle large amounts of data to
interface the simulation with the real world, the number of buses that can be simulated
is limited to about 80 about nodes. The 10 circuits provided by PacifiCorp have
significantly larger number of buses for which it is necessary to perform a node
reduction procedure that preserves the main connections of the circuit and still enable
their real-time simulation. The selected circuit, corresponds is fed by a 12.45kV
substation and includes a significant number of houses and small businesses with solar
generation. The maximum consumption of the circuit is 6.8 MW, according to the
obtained load profiles. The original circuit has 487 buses and it was reduced to 48
buses, combining three-phase and single-phase ones. The total solar generation
capacity is about 830 kW. Two CFCIs have been installed in specific locations to quickly
determine faulty branches and it is already operational and collecting and transmitting
information to the control center.

Test scenarios: Using Opal RT python scrip functionality, hundreds of simulations were
automated for multiple initial conditions. These simulations included starting at different
times such that solar capabilities are different, multiple SOC to validate the cases where
charging or discharging is not possible, and different load profiles. For each scenario,
short-circuit faults were considered at each line. Similarly, remote tripping cyber attacks

were considered for each protection device. Finally, cases considering 4 zones and 8
zones, as well as cases with 1 to 8 grid-forming inverters were considered. The results of
all the scenarios were used to compute different metrics that hep to assess the
capabilities and benefits of the ARMS solution.

Changing the number of zones: Figure 13 illustrates the comparison of different metrics
when 4 and 8 zones are considered. Notice that having 8 zones clearly improves the
capabilities of the ARMS solution to mitigate the impact after a fault. In particular, the
CCIC (critical customer interruption cost, in $/h, which is a measure of the cost of critical
customer load that is lost due to the anomaly) and the CIC (critical interruption cost, which
is a measure of the cost of non-critical customer load lost due to the anomaly) are
decreased, showing the economic benefit of considering more zones.
 Computation time is not affected due to the parallel computation of DRL agents.
Therefore, having 8 zones allows the ARMS solution to better isolate faulty areas leading
to a higher load recovery and significantly lower interruption costs. Having more zones in
general increases the granularity of control and isolation of faults. However, it comes with
a cost in terms of communication requirements, protection devices, more local controllers.
Therefore, it is important to understand the economic limitations to implement the ARMS
solution for a specific number of zones in contrast with the expected economic benefit by
being able to better recover customers after an event.

Changing the number of grid-forming inverters
To evaluate the impact of grid forming inverters in multiple zones, the not supplied load
after a fault is compared for different number of installed grid-forming inverters. For
PacifiCorp’s circuit with 8 zones, increasing the number of GFI does not necessary
reduce the impact of faults. For instance, Fig. 17 shows that the optimal number of GFI
is two. This depends on the system topology, the location of protection devices and tie-
switches and it illustrates the importance of performing cost-analysis before installing

GFIs. Increasing the number of GFIs will require significant investments in terms of
equipment and interconnections with the system.

Requirements for Real Implementations

Hardware/Software Implementation: The overall development of the ARMS solution, its
implementation in the testbed, and the multiple conversation with stakeholders such as
PacifiCorp have provided valuable insights on the requirements to deploy it in real
applications. The first requirements, is access to SCADA data from devices in
substations and the newly installed field devices such as reclosers and CFCIs.
PacifiCorp installation of these devices as part of this project have shown that the data
is generally available in their operation center either through dedicated channels and/or
the use of cellular communications. The data is then collected in third-party platforms
such as AMPLE and in internal databases such as OSISoft. Therefore, to be able to
integrate the data from different sources with the ARMS modules such as FALCON it
would be necessary to use APIs to interface with existing databases, which is typically
available for most systems. Once the data is available, the second requirement is to
choose to run the different modules either using internet-based cloud-services or a
local cloud. Using internet-based cloud services such as AWS provide multiple benefits
by removing the need of using servers or specialized computer to run the different
algorithms, specially during the training process, were powerful computation may be
needed. However, local clouds are also an option to avoid sharing sensitive data
through the internet despite there are many ways to secure the sending of data using
encryption and making sure to have strong access control policies. Finally, in terms of
hardware implementations, local controllers are devices that need to be placed in the
field and that are able to collect data from multiple devices in a zone. A local controller
needs to be able to operate independent of weather conditions, and should have at
least to network interfaces, one to interact with the central controller in the utility control
center, and the other to interact with the different DERs in the network. The local

                                              Page 38 of 48

controller then needs to be able to send setpoints to DERs and IBRs in real-time. This
can be challenging due to the lack of standards for communications in DERs and IBRs.
Therefore, the local controller should be capable of interact with multiple DERs and
IBRs and multiple communication protocols. In practice, this would require to setup a
communication network in each zone that is secure and resilient to node failure. Some
options could be using IoT networks such as LoRaWAN.

Training Data: In terms of data, the different algorithms require training machine
learning models. To this end, it is necessary to use data from multiple scenarios, which
is not necessarily available. For instance, for FALCON, it is necessary to collect data of
fault and attack events, which are most likely not available because these are rare
events that may not have occurred yet. To address this challenge, it would be possible
to use testbeds to generate synthetic data of the transient behavior of faults in different
locations. Models could be validated with historical data of faulty events. The synthetic
data should include a large variety of scenarios including different load profiles
(depending on the time of the year), different parameters of each fault, and changes on
the network topology. On the other hand, to train the deep reinforcement learning
agents, it is possible to use power flow-based formulations that can be done using open
source software. Therefore, it is possible to train for hundreds of thousands of scenarios
in a generally short amount of time.

 • The milestones and GNG goals have been successfully and timely completed
 according to SOPO.
 • The cyber-physical power system resilience testbed has been expanded to

 Furthermore, one of PacifiCorp’s circuits has been modeled using the testbed.
 • The real-time anomaly localization and classification is able to distinguish, in real-

 and fault indicators with an average accuracy of 98%.
 • The novel hierarchical architecture is able to reduce the complexity and
 computational burden to manage the optimal operation of DERs in normal
 operation and in the presence of anomalies such as faults and cyber attacks.
 Particularly, it takes about 10 seconds to localize the anomaly and compute the
 optimal action, as opposed to conventional approaches that depend on phone
 calls and need to solve complex stochastic optimization problems.

 increases in average the amount of recovered load by about 40% when
 compared to the case with 4 zones.

 proposed approach as well as the significant role of integrating grid-forming
 capabilities into the system to enhance the resilience of the power distribution

Lessons Learned
 • Working in close collaboration with industry partners helped to better understand
 the specific limitations in terms of data availability, software use, visualization
 requirements.
 • Testing and validation in a testbed allows to address some of these constraints,
 and present to stakeholders realistic demonstrations
 • AI/ML algorithms are not bullet proof and they have their own limitations.
 Combining them with mathematical optimization helps to provide feasibility
 guarantees
 • There is still a lack of trust of AI/ML-based solutions by utility operators.
 However, combining them with mathematical optimization facilitates their
 potential implementation by utilities. This in turn would require field
 demonstrations to first validate the ARMS technology in the field under real and
 controlled scenarios.
 • Data communications are often ignored in theoretical-based approaches.
 Understanding the available data, sampling rates, communication protocols is of
 paramount importance to create solutions that are transferrable to industry
 applications.

9. Path Forward:
Technology Improvement
Future research and development efforts for the FALCON AI agent focus on expanding
its capabilities to detect and classify a wider variety of faults and cyber threats, as well
as advancing its application in resilience management and wildfire mitigation. To
enhance fault classification, FALCON will be trained to distinguish between various
types of electrical faults, including single line-to-ground, line-to-line, and high-
impedance faults. Training for low-impedance faults, such as single line-to-ground and
line-to-line faults, will primarily involve adding more classification categories to the
neural network. In this case, existing inputs like relay trip signals and fault current

                                        Page 42 of 48

indicators are appropriate and sufficient. However, detecting high-impedance faults will
require significant modifications to the model. Instead of relying solely on device tripping
events, FALCON will continuously monitor real-time "snapshots" of current and voltage
readings from protection devices, outputting probabilities of high-impedance faults. This
approach could be tested in wildfire prevention schemes, with the goal of providing
operators with actionable insights to decide whether to inspect or de-energize lines,
improving distribution system safety and resilience.

Another critical development area is enhancing FALCON's ability to detect and respond
to cyberattacks. Future iterations might include detecting advanced threats such as
man-in-the-middle attacks or other sophisticated cyber intrusions. These capabilities
may require additional inputs, such as data from a digital twin of the network or
replicated data paths for cross-verification. Further, the system’s controllers could
incorporate mitigation schemes like algorithms that anticipate and counter attacker
strategies or topologies designed to isolate compromised devices without causing load
loss. These enhancements would not only improve the system’s ability to maintain
operational integrity under attack but also offer robust solutions for critical device and
infrastructure protection.

Lastly, a wildfire mitigation-specific controller represents a pivotal next step in improving
distribution system climate resilience, particularly in wildfire-prone regions of the
western United States. Efforts are underway to develop a mitigation and adaptation
control scheme for wildfire prevention and operation, and it will be verified using the
PHIL testbed. This controller would be tailored to address the increasing frequency and
intensity of wildfires, leveraging FALCON's capabilities to proactively identify
vulnerabilities and guide preventive actions, and the local and central controllers to
manage power flow and device operation to minimize risk. By integrating these
technological advancements into operational testbeds, this research can significantly
enhance grid reliability and safety by testing and validating the smart grid system before
it is deployed in a real distribution circuit, paving the way for resilient energy systems.

Pilot Demonstration
The ARMS solution has been validated using testbed implementations that integrate
communications, protection devices, and real controls. However, it is necessary to
perform pilot demonstrations to fully demonstrate the feasibility of the ARMS solution
and better understand the necessary requirements to be able to implemented and
integrated it into an utility systems. To continue the development of the ARMS solution
and take advantage of the momentum and capabilities enabled by the SolarSTARTS
project, proposal has been submitted to the STRIVES solicitation to develop a DSO
model that integrates commercial software, allows utilities to leverage DERs and IBRs
to provide different services to the grid, while providing resilience and security, and will
be demonstrated in the circuits of two partner utilities.
Commercialization
Along with the pilot demonstration, future directions consist on development a
comprehensive market analysis and commercialization plan to clearly identify the value

                                        Page 43 of 48

proposition and competitive advantage of the ARMS solution. This would require to
perform customer discovery to better understand the needs of utilities from different
sizes and capabilities, identify specific requirements (e.g., regulations, standards) that
need to be satisfied for hardware and software components to take the ARMS
technology to the market, perform stress testing, and define a cost model.
