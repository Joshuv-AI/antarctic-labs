In situ reprogramming of CAR-alveolar macrophages via liposomal nanomedicine for lung cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Hui Li, Chaoyi Lyu, Xiaoyao Cai, Yufeng Zhang, Qian Guo, Yanbin Chen, Cuihong Yang, Zujian Feng, Yumin Zhang, Jianfeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immunotherapy has revolutionized lung cancer treatment; however, response rates remain suboptimal. Alveolar macrophages (AMs) within the tumor microenvironment contribute to immunotherapy resistance by inhibiting T cell function through metabolic exhaustion, as well by promoting tumor progression via a pro-tumor M2-like phenotype. Here, we describe a precision-engineered, cascade-targeted liposomal nanomedicine (pCAR-P3/LNP) that enables in situ reprogramming of AMs via a multi-step targeting strategy—including lung accumulation via intrapulmonary nebulization, active cellular targeting, and promoter-driven activation—to generate functionally optimized chimeric antigen receptor-expressing AMs (CAR-AMs). The CAR-AMs mediate synergistic antitumor efficacy through three integrated mechanisms: targeted phagocytosis of lung cancer cells, enhanced antigen presentation, and M1-like repolarization. Furthermore, CAR-AM-induced immune activation and memory potentiate the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors and suppress metastatic progression in lung cancer models in female mice. This nanomedicine-based cell reprogramming strategy provides an approach to overcome immunosuppressive barriers in lung cancer immunotherapy and exemplifies the convergence of nanotechnology with immunology for enhanced therapeutic outcomes.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most aggressive and prevalent malignancies globally1,2. Although immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape for lung cancer, therapeutic efficacy nevertheless remains suboptimal3–5. For example, 30–50% of patients receiving first-line immune checkpoint inhibitors show only transient or no response6,7. This limited efficacy stems from inadequate immune cell activity and a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), featuring abundant regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and metabolic competition within tumor niches8–10. As the most abundant immune cells in the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) regulate immunometabolic balance through phenotypic plasticity and dynamic reprogramming11–14. In addition to originating from circulating monocytes, TAMs can also arise from tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs)-long-lived innate immune cells that self-renew and persist in normal tissues such as the brain, liver, and lungs15. A key example is alveolar macrophages (AMs), a self-renewing TRM subset located in the alveolar space. They serve as the first line of defense against inhaled pathogens and particles, and play essential roles in maintaining pulmonary homeostasis, metabolic balance, and tissue repair via efferocytosis and cytokine signaling16,17.

During early-stage of lung cancer, AMs colocalize with tumor cells and promote carcinogenesis by facilitating epithelial-mesenchymal transition and enhancing invasiveness. Simultaneously, AMs induce robust regulatory T cell (Treg) responses via immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, creating an immune-evasive niche that shields tumors from adaptive immunity18. Additionally, AMs undergo senescence during pulmonary pathogenesis and accumulate in tumor niches, where they foster progression by suppressing CD8+ T cell infiltration and adopting a pro-tumor M2-like phenotype19,20. Conversely, in certain contexts, AMs exhibit antitumor functions. For instance, influenza virus (IAV)-induced trained immunity enhances their phagocytic and cytotoxic capacity of AMs, providing long-term lung protection against tumors21. Moreover, despite their tissue-resident nature, AMs can initiate systemic antitumor immunity through cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-18), promoting CD8+ T cell recruitment and memory T cell generation17. Thus, beyond local immune regulation, AMs contribute to systemic antitumor responses via metabolic reprogramming and cytokine networks, highlighting their potential as targets for cancer immunotherapy.

Here, capitalizing on the inherent self-renewal and immunomodulatory capacities of AMs, we develop an in vivo engineering strategy for generating chimeric antigen receptor-modified AMs (CAR-AMs) using a cascaded targeted liposomal nanomedicine (pCAR-P3/LNP, Fig. 1A). The pCAR-P3/LNP enables precise in situ reprogramming of AMs through lung accumulation via intrapulmonary nebulization, cell-specific recognition, and promoter-driven CAR expression (Fig. 1B). We employ a rationally designed anti-hEGFR CAR architecture that redirects AMs to specifically recognize and attack EGFR-positive tumor cells via an anti-EGFR scFv. The resulting CAR-AMs exhibit specific recognition of lung tumor cells and orchestrate three synergistic antitumor mechanisms (Fig. 1C): enhanced phagocytic clearance of EGFR-expressing tumor cells, T cell priming facilitated by MHC-I upregulation, and induction of a pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype. Collectively, this in vivo gene editing approach represents an in situ immunotherapy that potentiates response to immune checkpoint inhibition and suppresses metastasis and recurrence in lung cancer.Fig. 1In situ reprogramming of CAR-AMs via cascade-targeted nanomedicine for synergistic lung cancer immunotherapy.A The self-assembly process of the pCAR-P3/LNP. Results

Preparation of the cascaded targeted liposomal nanomedicines for in situ engineering the CAR-AMs

To enable in vivo generation of CAR-AMs, we developed a cascaded targeted liposomal nanomedicine for precise CAR plasmid delivery to AMs via robust lung accumulations through intrapulmonary nebulization, cell-specific recognition, and promoter-driven expression. First, we synthesized a nuclear localization signal (NLS) peptide-modified cationic polymer (PEG-PAMA-PDPA) according to the route illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1A, as previously described in ref. 22. The structure of the resulting polymer was unequivocally confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). This polymer was complexed with CAR plasmid via nanoprecipitation to form spherical nanoparticles (pCAR-P3) averaging 140 nm in diameter (Fig. 2A, B). Gel retardation assays indicated complete plasmid enc apsulation at polymer: plasmid weight ratios ≥ 4:1 (Fig. 2C), supported by a reduction in zeta potential from +36.15 to +4.82 mV (Fig. 2D). Next, liposomal nanoparticles (LNPs) were prepared by thin-film hydration using anti-Siglec-F-conjugated DSPE-PEG2000 (Supplementary Fig. 1D), cholesterol, and DOPC. Following the assembly process illustrated in Fig. 1A, the pCAR-P3 nanoparticles were encapsulated within the LNPs to form the final nanocomplex, designated as pCAR-P3/LNP. Successful incorporation was verified by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis using FITC-labeled LNPs and Rhodamine B-labeled P3 nanoparticles (Fig. 2E), where energy transfer occurs between the FITC donor and rhodamine B acceptor fluorophores23. The ratios of the LNP components (Supplementary Table 1) were optimized based on EGFP expression efficiency, with the highest incremental cost-effectiveness ratio observed at a Cholesterol: DSPE-PEG2000-anti-siglec-F: DOPC ratio of 1:1:5 (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Following uniform size reduction of LNPs using a lipid extruder, the resulting pCAR-P3/LNP exhibited a uniform spherical morphology with a diameter of approximately 400 nm (Fig. 2F, G)—optimal for AMs phagocytosis24. Additionally, the pCAR-P3/LNP displayed a weakly negative surface charge (Fig. 2D). Over 3 days at 4 °C, freshly prepared pCAR-P3/LNP maintained a consistent hydrodynamic diameter of 400 ± 10 nm and a low polydispersity index (PDI < 0.4) (Supplementary Fig. 1F, G); Using a dye exclusion assay (e.g., Gelred), we detected no significant increase in free, unencapsulated plasmid or nucleic acid leakage over the same period, verifying effective payload protection (Supplementary Fig. 1H). Biocompatibility assessments of pCAR-P3/LNP revealed low cytotoxicity (Fig. 2H), negligible hemolysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A), and no significant histopathological changes in major organs (Supplementary Fig. 2B), and minimal systemic toxicity, as indicated by stable hematological parameters (Supplementary Fig. 2C–H). Considering that the pCAR-P3/LNP expresses a CAR that recognizes the hEGFR protein, we then performed additional biosafety evaluations using hEGFR-humanized mice. Complete blood count (CBC) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3A–F) and blood biochemical parameters related to liver and kidney function showed no significant differences between PBS and pCAR-P3/LNP groups (Supplementary Fig. 3G–O). Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining of lung tissue and other major organs indicated no apparent histopathological damage (Supplementary Fig. 3P, Q). Subsequent research focused on constructing a cascade targeting strategy based on the pCAR-P3/LNP for in vivo generation of CAR-AMs. Firstly, pCAR-P3/LNP exhibited pronounced lung accumulation (Fig. 2I, J) following pulmonary delivery25. Furthermore, compared to non-antibody-conjugated pCAR-P3/LNP, it showed significantly reduced accumulation in the liver. This reduction is likely attributable to enhanced uptake by AMs in the lungs, thereby limiting systemic distribution to other tissues. Secondly, the FITC-labeled CAR plasmid encapsulated in pCAR-P3/LNP demonstrated enhanced co-localization with AMs within the lung parenchyma compared to non-antibody-conjugated pCAR-P3/LNP (Fig. 2K), confirming successful cell-specific targeting. Flow cytometry results further demonstrated that pCAR-P3/LNP conjugated with a Siglec-F antibody exhibited a distinct targeted delivery capability toward AMs compared to neutrophils and eosinophils (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). The observed cell-specific targeting of pCAR-P3/LNP could be mechanistically attributed to enhanced AM internalization via antigen-antibody interaction (Supplementary Fig. 4C), rapid lysosomal escape (Supplementary Fig. 4D), and NLS-mediated nuclear delivery of the pCAR (Supplementary Fig. 4E). Thirdly, incorporation of macrophage-specific promoters (SP-107) into the pCAR construct (Fig. 2L and Supplementary Fig. 5A) enabled specific mCherry expression in AMs, with minimal detection in the alveolar epithelial cell line MLE-12 (Fig. 2M and Supplementary Fig. 5B), confirming successful promoter-driven targeting. Finally, CAR expression in AMs was analyzed at 24 h after pulmonary delivery of pCAR-P3/LNP using an anti-G4S linker antibody (Alexa Fluor® 647 conjugate) via flow cytometry and confocal microscopy26. This method leverages the consistent presence of the G4S linker within the single-chain variable fragment of the CAR structure (Fig. 2L), serving as a universal tag for detecting CAR expression. Flow cytometry confirmed that pCAR-P3/LNP-mediated in vivo editing achieved high G4S linker expression in approximately 30% of AMs (Fig. 2N, O), successfully generating CAR-AMs. Intracellular CAR expression (Fig. 2P) was further verified via confocal microscopy, which demonstrated clear co-localization between Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated G4S linker (red) and the AM-specific marker Siglec-F (green). Flow cytometry was employed to track dynamic changes in CAR expression levels in AMs over a 5-day period, revealing peak expression on day 1 followed by a gradual decline, with levels decreasing to <10% by day 5 (Fig. 2Q). We assessed potential off-target CAR expression in mice via flow cytometry and immunofluorescence. Flow cytometric analysis of neutrophils, eosinophils, T cells, macrophages in lymph nodes and peripheral blood mononuclear Cells (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D), together with immunofluorescence staining for the G4S linker across major organs (Supplementary Fig. 5E), confirmed the absence of significant off-target expression. Meanwhile, the pCAR-P3/LNP group with SP-107 promoter showed stronger Siglec-F/G4S linker co-localization than the EF-1α promoter group (EF-1α pCAR-P3/LNPsiglec-f-) in lung sections, further supporting the superior specificity of the SP-107 promoter. (Supplementary Fig. 5E).

pCAR-P3/LNP mediate the reprogramming of CAR-AMs

Based on the CAR design (Fig. 2L and Supplementary Fig. 5A), the anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (anti-EGFR) domain enables targeted recognition of tumor cells with high hEGFR expression. The intracellular domain incorporates 4-1BB, CD3ζ, and TIR signaling modules, which synergistically enhance CAR-AM activation, proliferation, and M1-like polarization. We generated a series of single-domain control CAR constructs (Fig. 3A) to evaluate the functional contribution of each domain to anti-tumor activity. Among these, the dEGFR group—which lacks the anti-hEGFR scFv extracellular domain—served as a negative control devoid of specific extracellular stimulation. In the EdU assay used to assess cellular proliferation (Fig. 3B), the 4-1BB domain exhibited the strongest pro-proliferative capacity. Although the CD3ζ, TIR, and dEGFR also exhibited modest pro-proliferative effects, this may stem from nonspecific stimulation. The co-stimulation by multiple modules also enhanced the phagocytic function of AMs (Fig. 3C). In the assay using hEGFR-conjugated FITC-silica beads, full-length CAR-AMs displayed robust phagocytic activity, with 52.57% of cells internalizing SiO2 beads. The TIR domain played a dominant role in M1-like polarization (Fig. 3D), whereas 4-1BB and CD3ζ alone showed almost no polarizing effect. Notably, even in the absence of exogenous stimulation, we observed a modest enhancement of certain anti-tumor functions in AMs, which we speculate may arise from low-level, nonspecific dimerization of cell surface proteins. To further evaluate the anti-tumor functionality of fully structured CAR-AMs, we conducted a series of in-depth assessments. The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that CAR programming enhanced cellular activity in both primary AMs and the MH-S cell line (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Importantly, 72 h after intrapulmonary administration of pCAR-P3/LNP, lung tissue sections revealed a significant increase in Siglec-F⁺ AMs (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Analysis of immunofluorescence co-localization of Ki67 and Siglec-F in lung cryosections following varying numbers of administrations (Supplementary Fig. 6C) showed that as the number of doses increased, Siglec-F-positive cells progressively increased and co-localized with Ki67. This indicates that the observed expansion is attributable to the proliferation of AMs. Since the AMs were equipped with CARs targeting hEGFR-high-expressing tumor cells, we next assessed their infiltration capacity into 3D tumor spheroids composed of hEGFR-high-expressing LLC cells. As shown in Fig. 3E, F, the pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs exhibited significantly deeper infiltration into the spheroids compared to control groups (p = 0.0069 vs. PBS; p = 0.0043 vs. Vector). The dEGFR group exhibited a significant reduction in this infiltration phenomenon (Supplementary Fig. 6D). A similar enhancing effect was also observed in CAR-modified MH-S cells (Supplementary Fig. 6E). In lung cryosections from a mouse orthotopic lung cancer model, we observed that AMs in the pCAR-P3/LNP group exhibited enhanced infiltration into tumor regions (Supplementary Fig. 6F). Following CAR delivery to AMs via pCAR-P3/LNP, Fig. 3G demonstrates that CAR-AMs exhibited a more than threefold increase in phagocytic efficiency against tumor cells. When pre-incubated with an excess of hEGFR ligand, the phagocytic activity of CAR-AMs against hEGFR-expressing LLC cells was significantly reduced, confirming that this targeted phagocytosis is mediated by specific recognition of the EGFR receptor. Reprogramming of the CAR-AMs into immunostimulatory M1-like phenotypes is critical for reversing ITM and activating anti-tumor immune response20,28,29. Here, we incorporated the TIR domain into the CAR construct to drive M1-like repolarization30. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6G, pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered AMs displayed distinct pseudopod-like structures with increased CD86⁺ expression. Flow cytometry revealed a significant increase in M1-like-polarized (CD86⁺) macrophages following pCAR-P3/LNP engineering compared to PBS and Vector controls (Supplementary Fig. 6H, I). To determine whether the M1-like polarization is specifically triggered by hEGFR engagement, we co-cultured differentl formulations treated AMs with either LLC or hEGFR-LLC cells. While pCAR-P3/LNP engineering alone induced M1-like (CD86⁺CD206⁻) polarization in ~15% of AMs when co-cultured with LLC cells, the proportion increased markedly to 32.03% (p < 0.0001) upon co-culture with hEGFR-LLC cells (Supplementary Fig. 6J, K). Together, these data demonstrate that while CAR engineering itself can induce an M1-like phenotype, the full polarization is specifically enhanced by, and dependent on, antigen recognition through EGFR-CAR binding. Notably, this strategy effectively reprogrammed IL-4-induced M2-like AMs (F4/80⁺CD206⁺) into tumor-suppressive M1-like phenotypes (F4/80⁺CD86⁺), with marked reduction in CD206⁺ expression (Fig. 3H, I). RT-qPCR analysis indicated that CAR programming significantly downregulated the transcriptional levels of M2-like markers (Arg1 and Cd206) in IL-4-induced AMs, while markedly upregulating the transcriptional levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine gene Il12 (Supplementary Fig. 6L). Meanwhile, pCAR-P3/LNP-driven reprogramming of AMs significantly altered their cytokine secretion profile (Supplementary Fig. 6M–R), marked by robust upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and concurrent downregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and IL-4). This phenotypic shift toward an immunostimulatory state may enhance the antigen-presenting capacity of AMs31. As shown in Fig. 3J, major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC I) expression was significantly upregulated on the surface of the CAR-AMs compared to both PBS and Vector controls (p < 0.0001 for each). This enhancement is attributed to the integrated 4-1BB co-stimulatory signaling within the CAR design.

Following the co-incubation protocol outlined in Fig. 3K, pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered AMs robustly enhanced T cell activation. As shown in Fig. 3L, M, this immunostimulatory effect was marked by a significant increase in CD8⁺ T cell activation compared to both PBS- and Vector-treated controls (p = 0.0013 vs. PBS; p = 0.0007 vs. Vector), underscoring the enhanced immunostimulatory potency of the engineered AMs. We further evaluated the antitumor efficacy of pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered AMs using the assay illustrated in Fig. 3K. The results demonstrated that CAR-AMs significantly augmented the tumor-killing capacity of CD8⁺ T cells compared to unedited AMs (p < 0.0001 vs. PBS; Fig. 3N). Furthermore, CD8⁺ T cells activated by CAR-AMs demonstrated potent tumor-lytic capacity, effectively destroying 3D tumor spheroids in a co-culture model (Fig. 3O). Overall, these findings demonstrate that CAR-AMs drive potent antitumor immunity through direct tumor targeting phagocytosis, immunomodulatory reprogramming, and enhanced T cell priming, offering a promising multifaceted strategy for lung cancer therapy.

Biological mechanism of the pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs via RNA sequencing

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CAR-AMs’ immunomodulatory plasticity, we performed transcriptomic profiling of pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered AMs. From 15 sample sets profiling 16,643 genes, 9 homogeneous replicates were selected for analytical mapping (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). Compared to PBS controls, pCAR-P3/LNP engineering induced profound transcriptomic alterations in AMs, with 1239 genes upregulated and 929 downregulated (Fig. 4A). Specifically, this engineering upregulated numerous genes linked to macrophage anti-tumor activity, including M1-like macrophage signature genes (Cd86, Cd40, Ccl5, Cxcl10), while downregulating M2-like macrophage signature genes (Cd163, Il4, Slc43a2), This may be related to the TIR domain, which serves as a central innate immune adaptor that directly drives M1-like polarization in macrophages30. To further characterize the functional underpinnings of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered AMs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping. GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 4B) revealed that the DEGs were predominantly enriched in immune-related biological processes, including immune response, immune system process, and innate immune response. Notably, functional crosstalk analysis identified critical regulatory networks bridging innate immune response and adaptive immune response, highlighting their extensive reciprocal influence (Fig. 4C). These findings demonstrate that pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs achieve comprehensive immune reprogramming through the coordinated activation of multiple interconnected signaling pathways. To confirm the therapeutic mechanism of pCAR-P3/LNP therapy, we generated heat maps to visualize DEGs related to cell proliferation, tumor infiltration, macrophage polarization, cytokine production, and antigen processing and presentation. pCAR-P3/LNP treatment induced upregulation of proliferation-related genes (e.g., Creb5, Cflar, Birc3; Fig. 4I), tumor infiltration and phagocytosis-related genes such as Icam1, Socs3, and Mmp9 (Fig. 4J), M1-like polarization and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion-related genes (Ifnar2, Il21r, and Csf3; Fig. 4K); and potentiation of antigen presentation machinery-related genes (Psme2, Hspa1, Tap; Fig. 4L). Additionally, the genes associated with immune cell recruitment (Cxcl10, Cxcl3, Ccl5) was significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 7G), suggesting their potential key role in remodeling the ITM. Collectively, these significant DEG alterations drove robust functional enhancement in the pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs, including proliferation, tumor infiltration, phagocytic capacity, phenotypic reprogramming, and antigen presentation.

CAR-AMs mediated T cells immune response and ITM rebuilding

In lung cancer, Macrophage typically display immunosuppressive functions that reinforce the ITM, thereby contributing to immunotherapy resistance28. Our therapeutic approach leverages in vivo reprogramming to engineer AMs into CAR-AMs, triggering enhanced phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and M1-like polarization—synergistically rebuilding the ITM and potentiating T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. To assess the immunomodulatory effects of pCAR-P3/LNP within the lung cancer microenvironment and associated T-cell responses, we established a syngeneic orthotopic murine model using murine Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells engineered to co-express human epidermal growth factor receptor (hEGFR) and a luciferase reporter gene. This enabled non-invasive bioluminescence imaging to monitor tumor progression (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). After successful establishment of the model, intrapulmonary inhalation administration was performed every two days. Immune parameters in mice were assessed 48 h following the completion of the third administration. As demonstrated in Fig. 5A, B of PBS and Vector groups, histopathological assessment of the lung tissues revealed significant enrichment of Macrophage displaying an M2-like-polarized phenotype, marked by dual positivity for the prototypic surface markers CD206+ and CD163+, suggestive of immunosuppressive pro-tumor functions. Notably, treatment with pCAR-P3/LNP induced profound reprogramming of the AMs (Fig. 5B–D), driving a phenotypic switch from pro-tumorigenic M2-like states (CD206+ downregulation) to anti-tumor M1-like polarization (CD86+ upregulation). Furthermore, this pCAR-P3/LNP-mediated reprogramming of AMs promoted deep intratumoral infiltration of CD86+ macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 9). Meanwhile, this reprogramming of AMs mediated by pCAR-P3/LNP is pivotal for remodeling the ITM. This reprogramming drives a fundamental shift in the cytokine profile (Fig. 5E), characterized by marked upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and downregulation of immunosuppressive mediators (IL-10, TGF-β). Following the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, antigen presenting cells (APCs) within the TME were activated, as evidenced by increased MHC I expression on AMs (Fig. 5F, G) and a significant elevation in the number of dendritic cells (DCs) (Fig. 5H, I). The activated APCs further enhanced antitumor T-cell immunity, leading to significant clonal expansion of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells (p = 0.006 vs. PBS; p = 0.0039 vs. Vector; Fig. 5J, K). Concurrent with the coordinated downregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10: 44% decrease; TGF-β: 45% decrease, Fig. 5E), we observed a marked reduction in immunosuppressive Treg (CD25+FoxP3+ cells reduced from 25.9% to 4.3%) and MDSC (Ly6G−Ly6C+ populations decreased from 33.87% to 15.43%) infiltration within the TME (Fig. 5L–O). Lung cancer immunotherapy of the in situ edited CAR-AMs

The therapeutic response was systematically assessed according to the experimental timeline detailed in Fig. 6A. Tumor-bearing mice were randomized into three experimental groups: PBS (control), Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), and pCAR-P3/LNP. Therapeutic interventions were initiated after tumor engraftment was confirmed by in vivo imaging, followed by three intrapulmonary administrations and subsequent longitudinal monitoring of tumor growth. As illustrated in Fig. 6B, mice treated with PBS or Vector control exhibited aggressive and invasive tumor proliferation. In contrast, the pCAR-P3/LNP group showed profound suppression of tumor growth. Quantitative analysis of tumor growth kinetics (Fig. 6C–F) further corroborated the potent antitumor effect of pCAR-P3/LNP, with a tumor inhibition rate approximately 90% (Fig. 6G). This significant suppression of tumor progression consequently led to a markedly improved survival rate in the pCAR-P3/LNP-treated cohort (Fig. 6H). Histopathological evaluation of tumor sections revealed a pronounced upregulation of the cytotoxic mediators granzyme B and perforin in the pCAR-P3/LNP group compared to both PBS and Vector controls (Fig. 6I). Consequently, TUNEL assays identified the highest level of apoptosis in pCAR-P3/LNP-treated tumors (Fig. 6I), providing compelling pathological evidence that the treatment achieves synergistic antitumor activity through enhanced immunogenic cell death and effective proliferation arrest. Importantly, mice administered PBS or Vector exhibited significant reductions in body weight on days 10 and 14 (Fig. 6J), temporally correlating with rampant tumor progression (Fig. 6B). In contrast, the pCAR-P3/LNP group maintained stable body weight throughout the 14-day treatment period, demonstrating not only robust antitumor efficacy but also an excellent safety profile. CAR-AMs mediated immune memory suppresses cancer metastasis

Building upon the pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs–mediated remodeling of ITM and T cell activation, we found that this intervention induced a significant increase in IL-18 secretion (Fig. 7A, p = 0.0001 vs. PBS), a key cytokine driving the differentiation of CD8⁺ tissue-resident memory T cells32. Mechanistically, this engineered AM strategy establishes an immunometabolic niche conducive to memory precursor formation. Flow cytometry confirmed a marked elevation in effector memory T cells (CD44⁺CD62Llow) within splenic tissues (p = 0.0266 vs. PBS; Fig. 7B, C). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that this approach induces durable adaptive immune memory capable of suppressing metastatic outgrowth and preventing relapse in lung cancer models. To test this, we performed tumor re-challenge in therapeutically cured mice via subcutaneous injection of hEGFR⁺ LLC cells, using naive mice bearing hEGFR⁺ LLC xenografts as controls (Fig. 7D). The pCAR-P3/LNP-cured mice rejected nearly all subcutaneous tumor growth (Fig. 7E–G). However, upon depletion of CD3⁺ T cells using an αCD3 monoclonal antibody, subcutaneous tumors in cured mice resumed progressive growth, with tumor expansion rates comparable to those in naive mice (Fig. 7H). Notably, by study day 27—when PBS control mice reached the ethical endpoint (tumor diameter ≥ 2 cm) and required euthanasia, pCAR-P3/LNP-treated mice in complete remission remained free of recurrence (Fig. 7I–K). These cured mice also showed survival without body weight loss (Fig. 7L, M), in contrast to T cell-depleted and naive mice. CAR-AMs augments immune checkpoint therapy against lung cancer

In this study, we demonstrate that localized engineering of CAR-AMs via pCAR-P3/LNP promotes CD8⁺ T cell activation and markedly reduces the proportions of Treg cells and MDSCs through cascading immunomodulatory mechanisms (Fig. 5). Based on these results, we propose that this in situ CAR-AM engineering strategy may sensitize orthotopic lung cancer to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Therefore, we studied the therapeutic efficacy of pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs in combination with αPD-L1 in orthotopic lung cancer models. The mice bearing orthotopic lung cancer were randomized into four treatment cohorts: (1) PBS control, (2) pCAR-P3/LNP monotherapy, (3) αPD-L1 monotherapy, and (4) combination therapy with pCAR-P3/LNP and αPD-L1, with the complete therapeutic regimen outlined in Fig. 8A. In contrast, pCAR-P3/LNP monotherapy yielded superior and durable response rates (Fig. 8B and E). Discussion

AMs, a population of TRMs in the lungs, play a critical role in the initiation and progression of lung cancer18,33. Owing to their distinct immunological properties, AMs have also emerged in recent years as a novel therapeutic target for preventing lung cancer recurrence and metastasis21. In this study, we developed an in vivo gene editing strategy targeting AMs to generate CAR-AMs, which enhance AM function through augmented phagocytic capacity, improved antigen presentation, and promoted M1-like polarization. This approach remodels the ITM and potentiates T cell-mediated antitumor immunity, enabling in situ immunotherapy for primary lung cancer. Notably, since TAMs in liver and lung cancers primarily originate from TRMs34, reprogramming TRMs directly—rather than editing TAMs within tumor tissue—bypasses the challenge of vector penetration into dense tumor masses35, resulting in substantially higher editing efficiency. This TRM-centric engineering approach offers broad translational potential for immunotherapy in glioblastoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and other solid tumors.

Compared to ex vivo immune cell engineering, in vivo genome editing remains considerably challenging due to multi-faceted biological barriers—including both tissue- and cellular-level obstacles36. To overcome these limitations, we designed a liposomal nanomedicine, pCAR-P3/LNP, with cascade-targeting functionality. This system achieves sequential tissue-specific accumulation, cell-specific internalization, and promoter-driven transgene expression, enabling in situ generation of CAR-AMs. Despite suboptimal editing efficiency (Fig. 2), pCAR-P3/LNP-generated CAR-AMs still elicited significant therapeutic responses in vivo (Fig. 6). To further improve editing efficacy, subsequent efforts will prioritize the structure-guided development of advanced delivery platforms. Specifically, we will focus on: (1) designing lung-tropic lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) through surface modification to improve pulmonary accumulation37,38, and (2) engineering membrane-fusogenic LNPs that facilitate efficient CAR-mRNA delivery via enhanced fusion with macrophage membranes39,40.

In designing the CAR for AMs engineering, modular function and synergy were guided by classical immunology and innate immune reprogramming insights, validated by our data. The 4-1BB domain was incorporated to reprogram macrophages, leveraging its intracellular domain to enhance alveolar macrophage survival and proliferation41. Consistent with this role, 4-1BB-CD3ζ constructs boosted AM proliferation compared to CD3ζ alone, effectively acting as a metabolic and proliferative amplifier (Fig. 3A–D). The TIR domain, derived from the innate adapter TLR4, was included to address the need for sustained M1 polarization in myeloid cells42. Experimental data confirmed that TIR-containing constructs upregulated M1-associated genes and cytokines, functioning as a “polarization switch”. The synergy of these domains from their complementary actions: CD3ζ initiates activation and phagocytosis via ITAM/Syk signaling; 4-1BB amplifies cellular expansion and metabolism; and TIR skews AMs toward a persistent M1-like state. Together, this tripartite effect generates a large, active, and polarized pool of tumor-targeting macrophages, effectively transforming AMs into self-sustaining anti-tumor effectors.

Furthermore, we demonstrate that pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs counteract the immunosuppressive legacy of native AMs in the lung cancer microenvironment by orchestrating a self-reinforcing immunomodulatory cascade through four interconnected mechanisms (Fig. 3): enhanced phagocytic clearance of tumor cells, M2-like-to-M1-like phenotypic reprogramming, elevated antigen presentation via MHC-I upregulation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine-mediated recruitment of CD8⁺ T cells. Leveraging the self-reinforcing immunomodulatory cascade initiated by CAR-AMs, this approach induced robust antitumor immunity and reprogramming of the ITM (Fig. 5), thus achieving nearly 90% tumor inhibition rate in an orthotopic lung cancer mice model (Fig. 6). Importantly, the in situ engineered CAR-AM approach directly tackles two fundamental challenges in cancer immunotherapy: primary resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and metastatic recurrence43. CAR-AMs significantly sensitized orthotopic lung tumors to ICB therapy (Fig. 8) and induced durable immune memory that effectively prevented tumor rechallenge (Fig. 7). Thus, it establishes a treatment paradigm that enhances frontline immunotherapy by reprogramming the TME and sustains long-term antitumor surveillance through T cell memory.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although pCAR-P3/LNP demonstrated a favorable safety profile—including low cytotoxicity, negligible hemolysis, and minimal systemic toxicity, its long-term safety remains to be thoroughly evaluated before advancing to clinical trials. Second, while RNA sequencing indicated that multiple signaling pathways contribute to CAR-AM reprogramming, the precise molecular mechanisms and crosstalk among these pathways require further mechanistic validation. Third, despite the potent antitumor activity observed with the combination of pCAR-P3/LNP-engineered CAR-AMs and αPD-L1 in orthotopic lung models, subsequent studies are needed to explore potential synergies with other immunotherapeutic agents (such as αCD47 antibodies) or conventional treatments (including chemotherapy and radiotherapy)44,45. Fourth, due to the limitations of mouse models, future clinical translation still faces multiple challenges: (1) human alveolar macrophages (hAMs) differ from murine AMs in ontogeny, surface marker expression, and signaling responses, requiring validation of CAR functionality (e.g., 4-1BB/TIR domain efficacy) in hAMs; (2) the anti-EGFR scFv, co-stimulatory domains, and promoter elements must be tested for optimal expression and activity in human macrophages. Fifth, the function of CAR-AMs in suppressing tumor recurrence and metastasis warrants further investigation, particularly through the establishment of more clinically relevant orthotopic lung recurrence models.

In summary, this work pioneers an in vivo immune cell engineering strategy that repurposes TRMs into biologically programmable platforms for cancer immunotherapy. By employing a cascade-targeted nanomedicine, we equip endogenous AMs with chimeric antigen receptors, bypassing the need for ex vivo manipulation, avoiding tumor penetration barriers, and harnessing the innate self-renewal and tissue-adaptive properties of TRMs. This approach transforms immunosuppressive AMs into potent and sustained antitumor effectors locally, offering a versatile and scalable platform applicable not only to lung cancer but also to other TRM-enriched solid tumors. Our strategy integrates immunology, nanotechnology, and gene editing to advance next-generation in situ cellular reprogramming therapies.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations. All animal experiments were performed according to protocols approved by the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, following the American Physiological Society Guidelines for the Care and Use of Animals (approval protocol numbers: IRM-DWLL-2021058 and IRM/2-IACUC-2409-003). Female C57BL/6 (Charles River Laboratory), aged 8 weeks, were used in the study. Humanized hEGFR-C57BL/6 mice (C57BL/6Smoc-Egfrtm1(hEGFR)Smoc) (Shanghai model organisms), aged 6 weeks, were used in biosafety assessment. The maximal tumor size or burden permitted by the ethics committee was 20 mm in the longest diameter. In addition, the following criteria were applied for early euthanasia in the orthotopic lung cancer model: severe respiratory distress (labored breathing, wheezing, or cyanosis), body weight loss exceeding 20% of the initial body weight, or inability to reach food or water. Animals meeting any of these endpoints were humanely euthanized. Animals were monitored daily, and tumor dimensions were measured every 2–3 days using a digital caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the standard formula: volume (mm3) = (shortest diameter)2 × (longest diameter) × 0.5. No animal exceeded the permitted maximal tumor size during the study. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under controlled environmental conditions: a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 AM to 7:00 PM), ambient temperature maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50±%. Animals were provided with standard laboratory chow and autoclaved water ad libitum.

Cell lines

LLC cell line (OriCell, catalogue no. M0-0101) was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), containing 10% FBS. Construct stable transfection hEGFR-LLC cell line overexpressing human EGFR genes using lentivirus (OBiO Technology, catalogue no. H20284). Construct a stable transfection hEGFR-luc-LLC cell line with dual expression of human EGFR and Luciferase using lentivirus (OBiO Technology, catalogue no. H20284 and H16547), which is used for in vivo imaging experiments in mice. MH-S cell line (OriCell, catalogue no. M0-0401) was cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI-1640) medium containing 10% FBS. MLE-12 cell line (ATCC, catalogue no. CRL-2110) was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) medium containing 10% FBS, Medium was refreshed every other day.

Isolation and culture of mouse alveolar macrophages

Female C57BL/6 mice, aged 5–6 weeks, were utilized for the isolation and culture of mouse AMs. The isolation and culture procedures followed the methodology outlined in Subramanian’s article27, with the macrophages being utilized for experimentation after one week of in vitro culturing (with 20 ng/mL GM-CSF added to the medium).

CAR design and plasmid loading

The hEGFR-specific scFv-based CAR transgenes were described previously. In brief, CAR transgene design consisted of a target-binding domain hEGFR-specific scFv connected to a CD8 hinge, a CD8 transmembrane domain followed by the 4-1BB intracellular signaling domain, a ζ signaling domain of the TCR (CD3 ζ), and TIR (utilizing the TIR region of TLR4 derived from mice). As detailed in the Results section. To assess the functional contribution of each domain, we constructed a series of CAR-expressing plasmids: versions encoding individual intracellular costimulatory domains (4-1BB, CD3ζ, or TIR), a CAR variant lacking the TIR domain (4-1BB-CD3ζ), and a truncated anti-hEGFR single-chain variable fragment (scFv) coupled with the full intracellular signaling cassette (dEGFR CAR). In some experiments, the CARs have fused to emerald mCherry for visualization (Details are provided in Supplementary Table 2). All the pCAR plasmids were constructed by the Shandong Gene&Bio Co. Ltd (Shandong, China) (Vector: ZBFV-066) and purified using an endotoxin-free plasmid extraction kit (TIANGEN, DP120-01). Use the protein nucleic acid micro-quantifier and nucleic acid electrophoresis to determine concentration and quality.

The synthesis of NLS-PEG-PAMA-PDPA

The nuclear localization signal (NLS) peptide derivative (PKKKRKVS) was synthesized using the conventional solid phase peptide synthesis method. According to the proposed synthesis route, the cationic polymer MAL-PEG-PAMA-PDPA was synthesized through a reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer polymerization. Subsequently, the NLS peptide was attached to the hydrophilic end of the polymer using a click reaction, yielding NLS-PEG-PAMA-PDPA.

Nanomedicine assembly and plasmid loading

5 mg of NLS-PEG-PAMA-PDPA was dissolved in 1 mL of trifluoroethanol, stirred, and dropped into 10 mL of ddH2O at a rate of 100 µL/min, then evaporated for 24 h to remove the organic solvent. For LNP preparation, 0.8 mg of cholesterol, 0.8 mg of DSPE-PEG2000-anti-Siglec-F (prepared by adding 100 µg of anti-Siglec-F and 8 mg of DSPE-PEG2000-NHS in 2 mL PBS, vortexed at 400 rpm overnight, and freeze-dried), and 4 mg of DOPC were weighed, dissolved in tribromomethane, transferred to a flask, and rotated to evaporate the solvent, forming a thin film. The plasmid (pCAR or pVector) was mixed with NLS-PEG-PAMA-PDPA in proportion, and ddH2O (pH 3) was added to a final volume of 5 mL. The mixture was transferred to the pear-shaped flask containing the LNP thin film (dried by rotary evaporation) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to ultrasonication at 100 W for 3 min. The resulting formulation was repeatedly extruded through a 400 nm pore size membrane using a lipid extrusion device. The final concentration of pCAR-P3/LNP plasmid used in cell and animal experiments was 50 µg/mL. The concentration of Vector (pVector-P3/LNP) was the same as that of pCAR.

Characterization of pCAR-P3/LNP

The structure of the compounds was confirmed by a high-resolution mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher, LTO-OrbitrapXL) and a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (Bruker, AVANCE IIIHD 300 MHz). Transmission electron microscopy images were collected using an electron microscope (FEI, Talos F200C). Zeta potentials were measured on a zeta potential analyzer (Brookhaven, ZetaPALS). Perform fluorescence excitation detection and FRET detection (Thermo, Thermo ScientificTM Varioskan Flash).

Tumor inoculation and inhalable drug delivery

C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethane to establish a lung tumor model by in situ injection of 1 × 106 hEGFR-LLC cells mixed with Matrigel containing a luciferase reporter gene. Tumor bioluminescence signals were quantified using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system following intraperitoneal injection of the D-fluorescein substrate (PerkinElmer). After confirming the tumor engraftment, pCAR-P3/LNP, Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), or PBS control was administered using a Micro Sprayer Aerosolizer (Shanghai Yuyan Instruments Co. Ltd, product number: YAN-30012) at a dose of 2.5 μg of plasmid per animal (50 μL). For the biodistribution study, CY5.5 dye was conjugated to the LNP. Animals were euthanized 72 h after the last treatment, and blood and organs were collected for flow cytometry and histological examination. For the rechallenge experiment, C57BL/6 mice that had been cleared of primary tumors were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 106 hEGFR-LLC cells.

Cell expression

After incubating AM cells with PBS, Vector, and pCAR-P3/LNP for 24 h, CAR expression was detected using flow cytometry with an anti-G4S linker antibody. Changes in CAR expression levels were monitored on days 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.

Promoter-specific expression

MLE-12 and AM cells were incubated with PBS, Vector, EF-1α pCAR-P3/LNP, and SP107 pCAR-P3/LNP (all pCAR plasmids carrying the mCherry marker) for 24 h. Expression of different promoters in various cell types was then assessed via flow cytometry and immunofluorescence imaging.

Target-specific CAR expression in mice

To assess the specificity of CAR expression in vivo, mice were administered the formulations via pulmonary inhalation three times at two-day intervals. Animals were divided into four experimental groups receiving either PBS (vehicle control), Vector (pVector-P3/LNP, empty vector control), EF-1α pCAR-P3/LNPsiglec-F- or SP-107 pCAR-P3/LNP. 24 h after the final administration, mice were euthanized, and tissues were collected for analysis. The heart, liver, spleen, and kidney were fixed and processed for immunofluorescence staining using an antibody against the G4S linker to evaluate CAR expression in major organs. Lung tissues were subjected to double immunofluorescence staining for Siglec-F (a marker for AMs) and the G4S linker, followed by DAPI counterstaining to visualize nuclei. Additionally, lymph nodes and peripheral blood were collected, and mononuclear cells were isolated. Flow cytometry was performed to determine the percentage of G4S linker-positive cells within specific immune cell populations, including neutrophils, eosinophils, T cells, and AMs.

Stability assessment of the formulated nanoparticles

The stability of the prepared pCAR-P3/LNP formulation was evaluated. Samples were stored at 4 °C following preparation, and their DLS and PDI profiles were measured on day 0 and day 3. Additionally, samples were collected on days 0, 1, 2, and 3 post-preparation for nucleic acid electrophoresis analysis to assess the integrity of the encapsulated pCAR plasmid.

Assessment of transfection efficiency

Plasmids encoding enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) were complexed with P3 before being encapsulated into liposomal nanoparticles (LNPs) prepared at different compositional ratios (e.g., varying lipid-to-component proportions). Following incubation of primary AMs with the resulting pEGFP-P3/LNP formulations, transfection efficiency was evaluated by flow cytometry based on the percentage of EGFP-positive cells within the viable macrophage population. Untreated cells and cells treated with empty LNPs (lacking plasmid) were included as controls to account for autofluorescence and non-specific signal.

In vitro proliferation assay of alveolar macrophages

The proliferative effects of a series of CAR constructs were assessed using an EdU (5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine) incorporation assay followed by flow cytometric analysis. Primary AMs were isolated from mice and co-cultured with hEGFR protein factor in the presence of pCAR-P3/LNP formulations corresponding to different CAR designs. After 24 h of incubation, EdU was added to the culture medium for a final labeling period. Cells were then harvested, fixed, and processed for Click-iT EdU staining according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The percentage of EdU-positive proliferating cells within the macrophage population was quantified by flow cytometry.

Phagocytosis assay of alveolar macrophages

The phagocytic capacity of a series of CAR constructs was evaluated using a standardized in vitro assay. Primary AMs were incubated with pCAR-P3/LNP formulations corresponding to different CAR designs. After 24 h, FITC-conjugated silica beads (200 nm) coated with recombinant human EGFR protein (hEGFR-SiO₂-FITC) were added to the culture medium at a defined multiplicity and co-incubated for 2 h. Following incubation, cells were washed extensively with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-phagocytosed beads, detached, and subjected to flow cytometry. The phagocytosis efficiency was quantified as the percentage of FITC-positive cells within the live AM population, with appropriate compensation and isotype controls included in each experiment.

In vitro polarization assay of alveolar macrophages

The polarization effects of a series of CAR constructs were evaluated using an in vitro macrophage polarization assay. Primary AMs were incubated with pCAR-P3/LNP formulations corresponding to different CAR designs in the presence of soluble recombinant human EGFR protein. After 24 h, cells were harvested, washed, and stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against CD86 (an M1-like macrophage marker) and CD206 (an M2-like macrophage marker). Flow cytometry was performed to quantify the surface expression levels of these markers. Data were analyzed to determine the polarization state of macrophages induced by each CAR construct, with results expressed as the percentage of positive cells or median fluorescence intensity for each marker.

AMs were plated into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well. Following treatment with PBS, Vector, or pCAR-P3/LNP, the cells were co-cultured with LLC or hEGFR-LLC cells for 24 h. Subsequently, the expression levels of the surface markers CD86 and CD206 on AMs were assessed using flow cytometry.

AM cells were seeded in confocal microscope at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. The following day, pCAR-P3/LNP, Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), and PBS were added and incubated for 24 h with 20 ng/mL recombinant human EGFR protein. For cytoskeleton visualization, cells were stained with fluorescent phalloidin (e.g., Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated phalloidin) to label F-actin. For surface marker detection, cells were incubated with a fluorochrome-conjugated anti-CD86 antibody (or corresponding isotype control). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The stained samples were imaged using a confocal microscope. Image analysis was performed to assess morphological changes in cytoskeletal organization and to quantify CD86 expression intensity or distribution.

Re-polarization assessment of alveolar macrophages

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in induction medium containing 20 ng/mL IL-4 for 48 h. A non-induced control group (control) was maintained in parallel. Following induction, cells were treated with PBS, Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), or pCAR-P3/LNP and incubated for an additional 24 h.

Following the treatment regimen, cells were harvested, washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD86 and CD206 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After incubation and washing, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol-chloroform method and reverse transcribed into cDNA using a commercial transcription kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mRNA expression levels of Arg-1, Cd206, and Il12 were quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) with gene-specific primers. The primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Relative expression was normalized to an appropriate housekeeping gene and analyzed using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method.

In vivo proliferation assay of alveolar macrophages in mice

Mice were allocated into four experimental groups: a control group that received intrapulmonary inhalation of PBS and three treatment groups administered the therapeutic agent once, twice, or three times, respectively. Each administration was separated by a two-day interval. 24 h after the final administration, the lungs were harvested, embedded in OCT compound, and rapidly frozen for cryosectioning. Immunofluorescence staining was performed on the tissue sections using antibodies against Ki67 (a proliferation marker) and Siglec-F (a marker for AMs). The stained sections were then imaged using confocal microscopy for qualitative and quantitative analysis.

Biosafety assessment

Cell viability of MLE-12 cells, AMs, and MH-S cells was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates, treated with Vector (pVector-P3/LNP) or pCAR-P3/LNP at the therapeutic dose (50 μg/mL), and incubated for 24 h. CCK-8 reagent was then added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Viability was normalized to PBS controls.

Heparinized rat whole blood was washed with PBS and resuspended to a 2% RBC solution. 250 µL of RBC suspension was mixed with an equal volume of serially diluted pCAR‑P3/LNP, 1% Triton X‑100 (positive control), or PBS (negative control). After 4 h incubation at room temperature, samples were centrifuged and photographed. Supernatant absorbance was measured at 540 nm to quantify hemolysis.

Humanized hEGFR-C57BL/6 mice (C57BL/6Smoc-Egfrtm1(hEGFR)Smoc) were divided into groups receiving either PBS (control) or pCAR-P3/LNP (treatment) according to the established therapeutic regimen. 24 h after the final administration, blood was collected via retro-orbital bleeding. Subsequently, mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested, fixed, and processed for paraffin embedding and sectioning. H&E staining was performed for histopathological analysis.

Serum samples were analyzed using a fully automated veterinary biochemistry analyzer (BS-240VET, Mindray, Shenzhen, China). The following parameters were measured: alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin, total protein, albumin, uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine.

CBC analysis was conducted using a fully automated veterinary hematology analyzer (BC-2800VET, Mindray, Shenzhen, China).

C57BL/6 mice were administered drugs according to the prescribed regimen, and 72 h later, mice were euthanized, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested, fixed, and processed for paraffin embedding and sectioning. H&E staining was performed for histopathological analysis. Blood samples were collected from the mice via ocular extraction, treated with anticoagulant, and analyzed using the Veterinary Hematology System (EXIGO, exigo Vet).

Targeted in vivo delivery in mice

To evaluate targeted cellular delivery in vivo, plasmids labeled with FITC were loaded into two types of liposomal nanoparticles (LNPs): one conjugated with an anti-siglec-F antibody (P3/LNP) and one without the antibody (P3/LNPsiglec-F-). The formulations were delivered via intrapulmonary inhalation to mice (n = 3 per group). 24 h post-administration, Lung tissue was excised, with portions embedded in OCT for cryosectioning. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to colocalize siglec-F protein with FITC-labeled plasmids, followed by imaging. The remaining tissue was processed into a single-cell suspension. Mononuclear cells were isolated from lung homogenates using a mouse lymphocyte separation kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow cytometry was performed to quantify the percentage of FITC‑positive cells within each defined immune cell population (neutrophils, AMs, and eosinophils).

Transcriptome analysis

AM cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. The following day, pCAR-P3/LNP, Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), and PBS were added and incubated for 48 h. Cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS, followed by the addition of 1 mL TRIzol (Invitrogen, Item No. 15596026CN) per well, incubated on ice for 20 min, and collected into 1.5 mL EP tubes and stored at −80 °C. Sequencing was performed by Nanjin Personal Geneology Co., Ltd using an Illumina sequencer in PE150 mode. Transcriptome bioinformatics analysis was conducted using Genescloud (https://www.genescloud.cn), and mapping was plotted by https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn46, an online platform for data analysis and visualization.

Tumor inhibition experiment in mice

Mice bearing hEGFR-luc-LLC lung carcinoma in situ were subjected to in vivo imaging on day 3 post-inoculation to validate tumor cell colonization in the lungs. Subsequently, PBS, Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), and pCAR-P3/LNP were administered on days 4, 7, and 10. Six mice per group underwent imaging on days 6, 10, 14, and 17 for luciferase intensity assessment, tumor growth curve, and tumor inhibition curve analysis. Additionally, five mice per group were weighed, and the survival of all mice (n = 10) was monitored.

Tumor inhibition study of pCAR-P3/LNP combined with PD-L1 in mice

Following inoculation of mice with hEGFR-luc-LLC lung carcinoma in situ, pre-dose small animal imaging was conducted on day 6 to verify tumor cell colonization in the lungs. Subsequently, on days 7, 10, and 13, the mice were administered PBS, Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), and pCAR-P3/LNP, respectively, as detailed earlier. Additionally, αPD-L1 was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 100 μg/mouse on day 7. Photos of six mice in each group were taken on days 9, 13, 17, 21, and 27. Subsequent luciferase intensity analysis was conducted, and tumor growth curves along with tumor inhibition curves were plotted. The survival of mice was monitored throughout the study, with a sample size of n = 10.

Antibodies, diluted in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), were incubated overnight, followed by washing and incubation with secondary antibodies, and DAPI staining. Confocal microscopy (Nikon) was used to capture images, which were analyzed with NIH ImageJ software.

Analysis of macrophage-infiltrating tumor spheres

Tumor cell killing assay

AM cells were treated with pCAR-P3/LNP, Vector (pVector-P3/LNP), and PBS for 24 h, followed by the addition of wild‑type mouse spleen-derived naive (non‑activated) T cells (T cell were isolated from the spleens of naive, wild-type C57BL/6 mice (unimmunized, no tumor antigen exposure) using Mouse Lymphocyte Separation Medium (iodixanol-based density gradient) to obtain total lymphocytes) and a 24 h co-incubation. T cell activation was assessed via flow cytometry. Subsequently, a mixture of macrophages and T cells was combined with hEGFR-LLC tumor cells at a ratio of 100,000 immune cells to 10,000 tumor cells per well for 24 h. Suspended cells were washed away, and cell killing was evaluated using CCK-8 assay. Killing efficiency was calculated as: (1-OD of treatment group/OD of PBS control group) × 100%. In cases where the calculated killing efficiency was negative, the value was set to 0%, indicating no detectable cytotoxic activity. In parallel, hEGFR-LLC tumor spheres were cultured, with 1 × 106 immune cells added per well, and the morphology of the tumor spheres was documented at 72 h.

Tumor rechallenges study

In vivo depletion of specific cell subpopulations was achieved by administering InVivoMAb anti-mouse CD3 (clone no. 17A2, catalog no. BE0002, BioXCell) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in physiological saline. Mice harboring lung cancer received an intraperitoneal injection of 100 μg, followed by subcutaneous tumor implantation after 24 h. Subsequently, the mice’s weight and tumor progression were monitored and documented.

Statistical analysis

For in vitro experiments, biologically independent samples refer to independently cultured cell samples or separate cell passages. The experimental data were analyzed using standard statistical methods. Quantitative measurements are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Dunnett’s, Tukey’s HSD, or Sidak’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons, as indicated in the figure legends. For tumor growth curves, treatment effects were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA (for longitudinal data) or linear mixed models (REML) with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test, as indicated in the figure legends. For survival analysis, the Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate survival curves. Overall survival was compared using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. The log-rank test for trend was used to assess dose-dependent or ordered group effects. Pairwise comparisons were performed using post-hoc log-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

For the T cell-mediated tumor cell killing assay, killing efficiency was calculated as: (1-OD of treatment group/OD of PBS control group) × 100%. In cases where the calculated killing efficiency was negative, the value was set to 0%, indicating no detectable cytotoxic activity.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Exact p-values are provided in the figures, figure legends, or Source Data file. Confidence intervals are reported in the Source Data file where applicable. No exclusion criteria were applied in the experimental design.

Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53t; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).
