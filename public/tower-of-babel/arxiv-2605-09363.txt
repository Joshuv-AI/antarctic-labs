Near-Optimal Last-Iterate Convergence for Zero-Sum Games with Bandit Feedback and Opponent Actions
Soumita Hait, Ping Li, Haipeng Luo, Mengxiao Zhang

Abstract
Last-iterate convergence of learning dynamics in games has attracted significant recent attention. In two-player zero-sum games with bandit feedback, where only the loss of the selected action pair is observed, Fiegel et al. 2025 show a separation between average-iterate and last-iterate convergence in duality gap: while the optimal t − 1 2 t^{-\frac{1}{2}} rate after t t rounds is achievable for the former via standard no-regret algorithms, the latter cannot converge faster than t − 1 3 t^{-\frac{1}{3}} in expectation or t − 1 4 t^{-\frac{1}{4}} with high probability. However, in many practical settings (such as preference learning), the players observe not only their loss but also the opponent’s action. This raises a natural question: can such additional information enable faster last-iterate convergence? We answer this question affirmatively, showing that t − 1 2 t^{-\frac{1}{2}} last-iterate convergence is achievable with high probability in this setting, via an efficient algorithm that updates its strategy infrequently by solving an estimated log-barrier-regularized game. We identify fundamental obstacles preventing standard analysis for multi-armed bandits (the single-player case) from generalizing to games, and develop a novel analysis to overcome them. Experiments confirm that our algorithm indeed converges faster than naive baselines and prior methods that do not exploit opponent-action feedback. Finally, we note that our results also improve those for dueling bandits, a special case with skew-symmetric game matrices.

Introduction

Understanding learning dynamics in games is an important area in game theory and machine learning. In particular, last-iterate convergence to Nash equilibria of two-player zero-sum games has received increasing attention since it does not require averaging iterates (which could be cumbersome for neural nets) and provides strong day-to-day behavior guarantees. There has been a long line of research on this topic, much of which focuses on an idealized setting where each player obtains gradient feedback of their strategies. In this case, it has been shown that
t^{-1}
last-iterate convergence in duality gap is achievable after playing the games for
t
rounds; see e.g.
Diakonikolas 2020
;
Yoon and Ryu 2021
;
Cai and Zheng 2023a
;
Cai and Zheng 2023b
;
Cai et al. 2024
;
Cai et al. 2025
.

However, gradient feedback might be too much to ask for in many applications. Often, players can see a (noisy) loss/reward of their selected action, but not the gradient of their mixed strategy. This is referred to as the bandit feedback setting and can be viewed as a direct generalization of stochastic multi-armed bandits (that is, single-player games) to multi-player games. Under this more challenging model, even for the weaker notion of average-iterate convergence, the best rate one can hope for is
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
due to standard statistical barriers, and any bandit algorithms with standard
\sqrt{t}
-regret achieve such average-iterate convergence
(
Freund and Schapire 1999
)
.

Achieving last-iterate convergence with bandit feedback, however, is even more challenging due to the severer tension between exploration and exploitation. Earlier results establish slow rates such as
t^{-\frac{1}{8}}

(
Cai et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
,
t^{-\frac{1}{6}}

(
Cai et al. 2023
)
, and
t^{-\frac{1}{5}}
(
Cai et al. 2025
)
. More recently,
Fiegel et al. 2025
;
Fiegel et al. 2026
show that with bandit feedback, there is indeed a strict separation between average-iterate convergence, for which the optimal rate is
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, and last-iterate convergence, for which the optimal in-expectation rate is
t^{-\frac{1}{3}}
and the optimal high-probability rate is
t^{-\frac{1}{4}}
.

While bandit feedback is more realistic than gradient feedback, in many situations, in addition to the losses, the players also observe their opponents’ actions. For example, in pricing, companies can naturally observe their competitors’ price; in online advertising auctions, bidders can observe the winning bid; and in security games, a defender may observe which targets an attacker chose to probe. There are also games where a central controller decides the actions for all players, in which case the action information is also directly available — a salient example is dueling bandits
(
Yue et al. 2012
)
or more generally learning from human preference feedback, often modeled as learning over a skew-symmetric preference game matrix where the algorithm decides which pair of actions to recommend to the user; see e.g.,
Munos et al. 2024
;
Swamy et al. 2024
. Such additional feedback intuitively facilitates estimation and mitigates the exploration burden, leading to a central question that our work focuses on:
does observing the opponent’s actions enable faster last-iterate convergence in two-player zero-sum games?

Contribution

We answer this question affirmatively and develop an algorithm with
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
high-probability last-iterate convergence, which, up to dimension dependence, is optimal and closes the gap between average-iterate and last-iterate convergence.
Our algorithm is computationally efficient and only updates its strategy
\log t
times after
t
rounds, where each update solves an estimated log-barrier-regularized game.

Our algorithm shares similarity with the FALCON algorithm of
Simchi-Levi and Xu 2022
for contextual bandits and can be viewed as a generalization of their algorithm to two-player zero-sum games (but specialized to the non-contextual setting).
However, we highlight that our analysis significantly deviates from theirs and is the main technical contribution of our work.
Indeed, we provide extensive discussion in
Section
3
on why even with the extra information of opponent actions, achieving
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence is still highly challenging and existing analysis in the bandit literature does not generalize to games.
This is true even when the game has a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium.
At a high level, the key obstacle is that estimating the duality gap of a strategy requires playing other (potentially bad) strategies.
Nevertheless, we overcome this obstacle via a novel analysis that relies on proving multiplicative stability between two consecutive strategies and might be of independent interest even for the single-player case.
We also highlight that since dueling bandits are a special case of our setting, our results also strengthen this line of work by providing last-iterate convergence guarantees rather than only regret bounds.

Finally, we conduct experiments on the security game benchmark of
Krever et al. 2025
, showing that our algorithm consistently outperforms both existing methods that ignore opponent-action feedback and naive baselines that exploit it.

Related work

There is a vast literature on learning in games. We refer the readers to the related work section of
Cai et al. 2025
for prior work under the gradient feedback model and only focus on related work under the bandit feedback model here.

Regret and average/random-iterate convergence

The seminal result of
Freund and Schapire 1999
shows that average-iterate convergence rate in two-player zero-sum games is controlled by the social regret of the players.
In particular, standard multi-armed bandit algorithms
(
Auer et al. 2002a
;
Zimmert and Seldin 2021
)
with optimal
\sqrt{t}
-regret ensure
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
average-iterate convergence.
To go beyond the
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
barrier, recent work either considers weaker regret notions or develop instance-dependent regret bounds
(
Maiti et al. 2025
;
Ito et al. 2025
;
Ito et al. 2026
)
.

Sessa et al. 2019
;
O’Donoghue et al. 2021
;
Ito et al. 2026
also study the effect of opponent-action information on regret, and
we refer to
Ito et al. 2026
for more discussion related to the line of research on Markov games where the opponent-action information also plays an important role.

On the other hand, social dynamic regret, which is equivalent to the cumulative duality gap, controls
the random-iterate convergence of the learning dynamic.
The algorithms of
Xie et al. 2020
;
Bai and Jin 2020
based on the well-known optimism principle enjoy
\sqrt{t}
social dynamic regret (with opponent-action information) and thus
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
random-iterate convergence.
Note that our
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence trivially implies the same for random-iterate convergence, but not the other way around.
In fact, it is known that such optimistic algorithms provably fail to achieve last-iterate convergence even for multi-armed bandits
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

Last-iterate convergence

As mentioned, a line of recent work studies last-iterate convergence without opponent-action feedback, improving the results from
t^{-\frac{1}{8}}
down to the optimal in-expectation rate
t^{-\frac{1}{3}}
and the optimal high-probability rate
t^{-\frac{1}{4}}

(
Cai et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Cai et al. 2025
;
Fiegel et al. 2025
;
Fiegel et al. 2026
)
.

Maiti et al. 2026
achieve similar
t^{-\frac{1}{4}}
rate but require seeing the loss of the mixed strategies instead of the loss of the selected actions.
Another recent work by
Ito et al. 2025
establishes
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence for games with a unique pure strategy Nash equilibrium, but the bound involves game-dependent constants that can be arbitrarily large.
To our knowledge, no prior work has studied the benefits of opponent-action information for last-iterate convergence.

Connection to bandits

When one player has only one feasible action, our problem becomes a single-player game and recovers the classic stochastic multi-armed bandit problem
(
Lai and Robbins 1985
)
.
Similarly, most work in this area focuses on developing regret guarantees.
To our knowledge,
Liu et al. 2024
are the first to explicitly study last-iterate convergence, although an earlier result by
Simchi-Levi and Xu 2022
also achieves last-iterate convergence implicitly for the more general contextual bandit problem.
We will discuss in depth why these approaches do not generalize to game in
Section
3
.
Similar to
Ito et al. 2025
,
Zhan et al. 2025
develop last-iterate convergence rate (for multi-armed bandits) that involves potentially large instance-dependent constants.

Preliminaries

General notations

For a positive integer
k
, let
[k]
denote the set
\{1,2,\dots,k\}
.
Define
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
to be the positive orthant of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space, and
\Delta_{d}\triangleq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1\}
to be the
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex.
For a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
v_{i}
its
i
-th entry.
Similarly, for a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we denote by
M_{ij}
its
(i,j)
-th entry.
Let
e_{i}
be the one-hot vector in an appropriate dimension with the
i
-th entry being
1
and all other entries being
0
. Let
\mathbf{1}
denote the all-one vector in an appropriate dimension.

Two-player zero-sum games

We study two-player zero-sum games specified by a game matrix
A\in[-1,1]^{d\times d}
, where
A_{ij}
denotes the expected loss incurred by the row player and, equivalently, the expected reward obtained by the column player, when the row player selects action
i
and the column player selects action
j
.
1
1

1

For the ease of presentation, we assume that both players have the same number of actions, but our results directly generalize to the case where they have different number of actions.

For a mixed strategy pair
(x,y)\in\Delta_{d}\times\Delta_{d}
, the expected loss of the row player is given by
x^{\top}Ay
(which is also the expected reward of the column player), and the duality gap of this strategy pair is defined as

\text{\rm DGap}(x,y)\triangleq\max_{y^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d}}x^{\top}Ay^{\prime}-\min_{x^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d}}x^{\prime\top}Ay=\max_{i,j\in[d]}x^{\top}Ae_{j}-e_{i}^{\top}Ay,

which is always non-negative by definition.
It is well-known that a strategy pair
(x,y)
has
0
duality gap if and only if it is a Nash equilibrium of the game (that is, both players are best-responding to each other:
x^{\top}Ay^{\prime}\leq x^{\top}Ay\leq{x^{\prime}}^{\top}Ay
for all
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d}
).
Therefore, duality gap is a natural measure for convergence rate to Nash equilibria.

While Nash equilibria always exist,
a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (PSNE) of the form
(e_{i},e_{j})
for some
i,j\in[d]
might not.
In
Section
3
, we will discuss why our problem is challenging even when the game has a unique PSNE, but we emphasize that our results hold generally, without any assumption on the games (other than boundedness).

Learning via repeated play with bandit feedback and opponent actions

We consider a standard repeated play setting in which both players have no prior knowledge of the game matrix
A
. In each round
t=1,2,\ldots
, the row player selects a mixed strategy
x_{t}\in\Delta_{d}
and samples an action
i_{t}\sim x_{t}
;
similarly, the column player selects a mixed strategy
y_{t}\in\Delta_{d}
and samples an action
j_{t}\sim y_{t}
. Following that, both players observe bandit feedback, that is, a noisy loss/reward signal
r_{t}\in[-1,1]
satisfying
\mathbb{E}\left[r_{t}\right]=A_{i_{t}j_{t}}
.
In addition, row player observes the opponent’s action
j_{t}
and similarly the column player observes
i_{t}
.

An algorithm satisfies last-iterate convergence if it ensures
\text{\rm DGap}(x_{t},y_{t})\rightarrow 0
as
t
increases, which is stronger than other heavily-studied measures such as average-iterate convergence:
\text{\rm DGap}\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}x_{\tau},\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}y_{\tau}\right)\rightarrow 0
,
random-iterate convergence:
\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\text{\rm DGap}(x_{\tau},y_{\tau})\rightarrow 0
, and best-iterate convergence:
\min_{\tau\leq t}\text{\rm DGap}(x_{\tau},y_{\tau})\rightarrow 0
.
As discussed, the focus of our work is to show how the additional opponent-action information helps speed up last-iterate convergence beyond the
t^{-\frac{1}{3}}
(in-expectation) or
t^{-\frac{1}{4}}
(high-probability) rate that are optimal in the absence of opponent-action feedback, a question that has not been explored in the literature to our knowledge.

We also recall the real-life examples in
Section
1
that illustrate the ubiquitousness of opponent-action information in practice.
In particular, we point out again that dueling bandits are a special case of our setting where the game matrix
A
(known as the preference matrix in this case) is skew-symmetric (
A=-A^{\top}
), and the algorithm needs to select a pair of actions
(i_{t},j_{t})
to solicit preference feedback from the user.
In this case, the information
(i_{t},j_{t})
is obviously known to the algorithm itself.

Finally, since we will use multi-armed bandits as an illustrative special case in many subsequent discussions, we remind the reader that when the game matrix
A
is
d
by
1
, our setting recovers the standard stochastic
d
-armed bandit problem.
Since the game matrix is just a vector in this case, we will denote it by
\ell\in[-1,1]^{d}
instead.
It is clear that the duality gap of a strategy
x\in\Delta_{d}
in this case reduces to the (instantaneous) regret of this strategy compared to the best action, which we denote as
{\mathrm{Reg}}(x)=\left\langle{x,\ell}\right\rangle-\min_{i\in[d]}\ell_{i}
.

3
The Challenges: Why Existing Approaches Fail

Before introducing our solution, this section provides an in-depth discussion on the difficulty of the problem and why existing approaches in the bandit literature do not generalize to games, thereby highlighting both the need for a fundamentally different approach and the novelty of our work.

First and foremost, we recall the difficulty of achieving last-iterate convergence compared to other goals such as achieving low regret, even in the special case of multi-armed bandits.
Indeed, while it is well-known that a key challenge for any learning problems with partial-information feedback is the exploitation-exploration trade-off,
ensuring last-iterate convergence creates even stronger tension between them.
This is because last-iterate convergence requires that in
every
round, a reasonably good strategy is played, while achieving low regret only requires that in
most
rounds, a good strategy is played.
For example, while optimism-based approaches, such as the classic Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm
(
Auer et al. 2002b
)
, ensures optimal regret in many bandit problems,
the fact that it occasionally selects bad actions for exploration renders them incompatible with last-iterate convergence, as formally proven in
Liu et al. 2024
.

Naively, to play a good strategy in every round while ensuring sufficient exploration, one can mix the empirically best strategy with a uniform exploration strategy.
However, it is well-known that such a naive exploitation-exploration trade-off fails to achieve
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
convergence even for bandits.
Indeed, in
Appendix
C.1
, we prove that for our problem, this naive strategy only achieves
\text{\rm DGap}(x_{t},y_{t})=\mathcal{O}(d^{\frac{1}{2}}t^{-\frac{1}{4}})
with high probability.
We will use this strategy as one of the baselines in our experiments in
Section
5
.

So how do we achieve
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
convergence even in bandits?
To our knowledge, there are essentially two types of approaches in the literature: hard-elimination and soft-elimination.
We review both approaches and discuss why they do not generalize to games.

Hard-elimination

Liu et al. 2024
study different variants of the hard-elimination approach from the literature, but they all share the same idea:
eliminate actions that, based on current observations, cannot be the best action (with high probability), and (almost) uniformly explore the remaining actions.
Such uniform exploration among plausibly good actions makes sure that in each round
t
, every action
i
that is not eliminated yet has regret
{\mathrm{Reg}}(e_{i})
less than
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{d/t})
, meaning that it is always “safe” to play any of them.

When trying to generalize hard-elimination to games, the first challenge one immediately meets is that we are no longer searching for the best strategy over a finite space; indeed, while in bandits the best strategy can always be in the form of
e_{i}
for some
i
, it is entirely possible that a game has no PSNE of the from
(e_{i},e_{j})
for some
i
and
j
.
Therefore, the search space in game becomes
\Delta_{d}\times\Delta_{d}
instead of
[d]\times[d]
.

However, we emphasize that
even when a PSNE exists, hard-elimination over the space
[d]\times[d]
is still a bad idea.

This is due to the following key obstacle:
estimating
\text{\rm DGap}(e_{i},e_{j})=\max_{h,k\in[d]}A_{ih}-A_{kj}
for some pure strategy
(e_{i},e_{j})
cannot be done by simply playing
(e_{i},e_{j})
itself since this only yields samples of
A_{ij}
; instead, by definition, it requires playing
(e_{i},e_{h})
and
(e_{k},e_{j})
where
e_{h}
and
e_{k}
are the best response to
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
respectively that realize the maximum in the duality gap definition.
Importantly,
these strategies
(e_{i},e_{h})
and
(e_{k},e_{j})
themselves can be bad strategies with a large duality gap!

To see this, simply consider a
3
by
3
example
A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&-\varepsilon\\
0&\varepsilon&0\end{bmatrix}

for some small and unknown
\varepsilon>0
.
The strategies
(e_{2},e_{3})
and
(e_{3},e_{2})
have a large duality gap of
1+\varepsilon
, but if we eliminate them early, then there is no way to get samples from
A_{23}
or
A_{32}
to determine the value of
\varepsilon
and thus no way to estimate the duality gap of
(e_{3},e_{3})
(which is
2\varepsilon)
.
This is in sharp contrast to the bandit case where playing an action naturally yields samples that helps estimate the quality of this action itself.

Soft-elimination

We use the term soft-elimination to refer to approaches that never completely eliminate any actions, but instead adjust the probability of selecting an action based on its estimated quality.
While there is no explicit study of this approach for achieving
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence in multi-armed bandits,
the idea is in fact implicitly described in several works on contextual bandits, starting from
Agarwal et al. 2014
.
To facilitate discussions, we describe an algorithm based on this idea in
Algorithm
1
, which is most closely related to the FALCON algorithm of
Simchi-Levi and Xu 2022
for contextual bandits and can be seen as a variant of it specified to the non-contextual setting.

Specifically,
Algorithm
1
proceeds in epochs with doubling length.
Within each epoch
s
, a sampling strategy
x_{s}\in\Delta_{d}
for this epoch is computed via
\argmin_{x\in\Delta_{d}}\langle x,\widehat{\ell}_{s}\rangle+\gamma_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\frac{1}{x_{i}}
,
where
\widehat{\ell}_{s}
is a loss estimator computed by simply averaging data from the last epoch,
\gamma_{s}>0
is a learning rate,
and the part
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\frac{1}{x_{i}}
is often known as the log-barrier regularizer, included to encourage exploration (since it explodes when
x
concentrates on a subset of actions).
In other words, we solve an estimated log-barrier-regularized bandit problem in each epoch.
With this strategy
x_{s}
, we simply sample
i_{t}
from
x_{s}
for all rounds
t=2^{s-1},\ldots,2^{s}-1
within this epoch,
and finally use the observed data to compute the next loss estimate
\widehat{\ell}_{s+1}
.

Input:

Learning rates
\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s\geq 1}
.

Initialize loss estimator
\widehat{\ell}_{1}
to be the all-zero vector.

for

epoch
s=1,2,\dots

do

Compute sampling strategy:
x_{s}=\arg\min_{x\in\Delta_{d}}\langle x,\widehat{\ell}_{s}\rangle+\gamma_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\frac{1}{x_{i}}
.

Initialize counters:
n_{s,i}=0
for all
i\in[d]
.

for

round
t=2^{s-1},\ldots,2^{s}-1

do

Sample
i_{t}\sim x_{s}
and observe realized loss
r_{t}
such that
\mathbb{E}[r_{t}]=\ell_{i_{t}}
.

Increment counter
n_{s,i_{t}}\leftarrow n_{s,i_{t}}+1
.

Compute loss estimate
\widehat{\ell}_{s+1,i}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\left\{n_{s,i}\neq 0\right\}}{n_{s,i}}\sum_{\tau=2^{s-1}}^{2^{s}-1}\mathbbm{1}\left\{i_{\tau}=i\right\}\cdot r_{\tau}
for all
i\in[d]
.

FALCON for Stochastic Multi-Armed Bandits

Other than specifying the FALCON algorithm to the non-contextual setting and instantiating its regression oracle with the simple empirical mean estimators,
the only slight difference between
Algorithm
1
and FALCON is how
x_{s}
is defined, but it is known that our definition shares the same properties as theirs; see e.g.
Foster and Rakhlin 2020
;
Foster et al. 2020
;
Zhang and Luo 2024
.
Our definition is also closely related to the well-known Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) framework, and using log-barrier regularizer in this framework for bandits has been extensively studied since the early work such as
Foster et al. 2016
;
Wei and Luo 2018
.
However, an important distinction is that these FTRL algorithms all use importance-weighted estimators instead of the empirical mean estimators that
Algorithm
1
uses.

The key idea in the analysis for such soft-elimination methods is to show that, with an appropriate choice of
\gamma_{s}
(that is of order
1/\sqrt{d2^{s}}
), for each action
i
, its regret
{\mathrm{Reg}}(e_{i})
is close to the estimated version
\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(e_{i})
, where

\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(x)=\langle x,\widehat{\ell}_{s}\rangle-\min_{i\in[d]}\widehat{\ell}_{s,i}
, in a multiplicative sense:
{\mathrm{Reg}}(e_{i})\leq 2\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(e_{i})+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\gamma_{s})
and similarly
\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(e_{i})\leq 2{\mathrm{Reg}}(e_{i})+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\gamma_{s})
.
This is enabled by the following key low-regret-low-variance property of the algorithm that underscores the essence of a line of previous work on contextual bandits; see e.g.,
Agarwal et al. 2014
,
Simchi-Levi and Xu 2022
, and
Zhang and Luo 2024
.

Lemma 3.1

(Low-Regret-Low-Variance)
.

For every epoch
s\geq 1
,
Algorithm
1
satisfies the following:

\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(x_{s})\leq d\gamma_{s},

(1)

\frac{1}{x_{s,i}}\leq d+\frac{\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(e_{i})}{\gamma_{s}},~\forall~i\in[d].

(2)

Note that
x_{s,i}
is the probability of getting a sample for action
i
in each round of epoch
s
and thus
1/x_{s,i}
controls the variance of the loss estimate for this action.

Eq. (
2
)
therefore states that the variance for action
i
is controlled by its estimated regret: the better the action appears to be, the lower the estimation variance is,
which is exactly the key to show the multiplicative closeness between
{\mathrm{Reg}}(e_{i})
and
\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(e_{i})
.
Such a low-variance property holds thanks to the exploration induced by the special log-barrier regularization.
On the other hand, due to such closeness, the low-estimated-regret property of
Eq. (
1
)
thus immediately implies that the true per-round regret of the algorithm is also small, hence achieving
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\gamma_{s})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{d/t})
last-iterate convergence
(
Simchi-Levi and Xu 2022
, Lemma 9)
.

To understand the difficulty of generalizing such soft-elimination methods to games, let us consider again the simpler case with a PSNE.
The most obvious generalization would be to try to ensure multiplicative closeness between
\text{\rm DGap}(e_{i},e_{j})
and its estimated version for any action pair
(e_{i},e_{j})
.
This is impossible due to the exactly same obstacle mentioned before: estimating
\text{\rm DGap}(e_{i},e_{j})
requires playing other potentially bad strategies.
Indeed,
Saha and Krishnamurthy 2022
already use the same aforementioned
3
by
3
example to show that if such multiplicative closeness holds, then the algorithm cannot have
\sqrt{t}
regret, let alone
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence.
In
Appendix
C.2
, we provide more discussion on why a low-duality-gap-low-variance property similar to
Lemma
3.1
, which our final algorithm
does
ensure, fails to show
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence.

We point out that
Saha and Schapire 2025
make an attempt to get around this obstacle in the contextual dueling bandit setting by imposing a strong "Idempotent Best-Response" assumption.
When specified to our setting, this would mean that their results only hold for a special subset of games with a PSNE.
Also, their algorithm requires performing least squares regression over this class of games, which is unlikely to be computationally efficient.

4
Our Algorithm and Analysis

Given all the obstacles discussed above, it might seem that all hope is lost.
Nevertheless, we propose a solution that algorithmically follows the soft-elimination approach but analytically requires a different and novel analysis.
Specifically, our algorithm, called Phased Minimax Optimization with Log-Barrier Regularization (PMO-LB), is shown in
Algorithm
2
.
It follows
Algorithm
1
with the same epoch schedule and empirical mean estimator
\widehat{A}_{s,ij}
for each
(i,j)
entry of the unknown matrix
A
,
and generalizes it in how the sampling strategy is computed:
(x_{s},y_{s})
is the Nash equilibrium of the estimated log-barrier-regularized game specified by
\Phi_{s}
in
Eq. (

. Equivalently,
x_{s}=\argmin_{x\in\Delta_{d}}\max_{y\in\Delta_{d}}\Phi_{s}(x,y)

.
Such a (unique) Nash equilibrium must exist since
\Phi_{s}

When the game matrix is
d

1
, it is clear that our algorithm reduces to
Algorithm
1
.
Before analyzing our algorithm, we make the following important remarks.

Uncoupled/coupled dynamics

While we present both players’ algorithm together in
Algorithm
2
,
it is clear that these two players can compute their own strategy
independently without any extra coordination/coupling/shared randomness
.
Indeed, thanks to the opponent-action information, each player can construct the estimator
\widehat{A}_{s}
and compute their strategy on their own.
Whether this is considered as an uncoupled or coupled learning dynamic depends on how one define them; for example,
Fiegel et al. 2025
define uncoupled learning dynamics as those that do not have the opponent-action information.

Connection to existing algorithms

Other than being a generalization of
Algorithm
1
(which itself is a variant of FALCON
(
Simchi-Levi and Xu 2022
)
),
our algorithm is also connected to several others in the literature.
For example,
Saha and Schapire 2025
also generalize the FALCON algorithm to contextual dueling bandits.
Their algorithm shares similarities with ours but importantly computes a
joint
distribution over action pairs and thus requires coupling when sampling actions.
Moreover, as mentioned in
Section
3
, their results hold only under a strong assumption, which is inevitable since their analysis still follows the same low-regret-low-variance approach discussed in
Section
3
, proven to fail generally according to
Saha and Krishnamurthy 2022
.
On the other hand, our algorithm also shares similarity with
Cardoso et al. 2019
, with the following differences: first, they use importance-weighted estimators instead of empirical mean estimators; second, they use entropy regularizer instead of the log-barrier regularizer (the latter is critical for our analysis as it will become clear);
finally, their algorithm is designed for a quite different problem with time-varying game matrices, and the metric they study is a notion called NE regret instead of last-iterate convergence.
Finally, we note that the algorithm of
Fiegel et al. 2026
for the setting without opponent-action information also uses log-barrier regularization but in the FTRL framework and does not involve minimax optimization.
Their algorithm is closer to that of
Cai et al. 2023
with the difference being the form of the regularizer.

Input:

\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s\geq 1}

Phased Minimax Optimization with Log-Barrier Regularization (PMO-LB)

4.1
Main Results and Analysis

Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
,

Algorithm
2
with
\gamma_{s}=128d\cdot 2^{-s/2}\sqrt{\log(8d^{2}s^{2}/\delta)}
guarantees that with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\text{\rm DGap}(x_{t},y_{t})=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(d\log t/\delta)}{t}}\right)
for all
t\geq 1
, where
(x_{t},y_{t})
denotes the strategy pair played in round
t
.

As mentioned, the best existing results prior to our work are those that do not utilize the opponent-action information:
t^{-\frac{1}{3}}
(in-expectation) from
Fiegel et al. 2025
and
t^{-\frac{1}{4}}
(high-probability) from
Fiegel et al. 2026
.
Our work is the first to show that with opponent-action feedback,
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence is achievable, eliminating the gap between last-iterate and average-iterate convergence, at least in terms of the dependence on
t
.
As for the dependence on
d
, the best lower bound is the standard
\Omega(\sqrt{d/t})
for average-iterate convergence, coming from the bandit literature
(
Auer et al. 2002a
)
.
We are unaware of tighter lower bounds for last-iterate convergence with opponent-action information, but we suspect that our
d^{2}
dependence is suboptimal.

Despite our algorithm being a direct generalization of
Algorithm
1
, as mentioned, our analysis is (and has to be) different to overcome the obstacles discussed in
Section
3
.
To illustrate our core ideas, we discuss the single-player case again as a warm-up, which can be seen as an alternative analysis of
Algorithm
1
. (All omitted proof can be found in the appendix).

Warm-up for the single-player case

Our starting point is the following property that is strictly stronger than the low-regret-low-variance property of
Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every epoch
s\geq 1
,
Algorithm
1
satisfies

\langle x_{s},\widehat{\ell}_{s}\rangle-\widehat{\ell}_{s,i}\leq d\gamma_{s}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{x_{s,i}},~\forall~i\in[d].

(4)

This is obtained by simply applying the first-order optimality condition to the optimization objective that defines
x_{s}
.
It is stronger than
Lemma
3.1
since it trivially implies
Eq. (
1
)
by dropping the negative term
-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{x_{s,i}}
,
and it also implies
Eq. (
2
)
by rearranging terms and realizing
\widehat{\ell}_{s,i}-\langle x_{s},\widehat{\ell}_{s}\rangle\leq\widehat{\ell}_{s,i}-\min_{j\in[d]}\widehat{\ell}_{s,j}=\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(e_{i})
.
This new lemma is useful since it asserts that
the algorithm’s estimated regret compared against a particular action is much smaller when this action is selected with a low probability, which in turn compensates the large estimation variance for this action.
Indeed, consider bounding the true regret of
x_{s}
as follows (with
i^{\star}\in\argmin_{i\in[d]}\ell_{i}
being an optimal action):

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Reg}}(x_{s})

\displaystyle=\langle x_{s},\widehat{\ell}_{s}\rangle-\widehat{\ell}_{s,i^{\star}}+\langle x_{s}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell-\widehat{\ell}_{s}\rangle\leq d\gamma_{s}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{x_{s,i^{\star}}}+\frac{\beta_{s}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,i^{\star}}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{s}x_{s,i}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,i}}},

where the inequality uses
Lemma
4.2
and standard concentration to bound
\lvert\widehat{\ell}_{s,i}-\ell_{i}\rvert
for each
i
by
\frac{\beta_{s}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,i}}}
with
\beta_{s}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{2^{s}})
, since
\widehat{\ell}_{s,i}
is constructed using samples collected from strategy
x_{s-1}
.
At this point, it is clear that if
x_{s}
and
x_{s-1}
are close enough in a multiplicative sense, that is, if
x_{s,i}\leq\alpha x_{s-1,i}
holds for all
i
and some
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(d))
,
then we can continue with

{\mathrm{Reg}}(x_{s})\leq d\gamma_{s}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{\alpha x_{s-1,i^{\star}}}+\frac{\beta_{s}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,i^{\star}}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{s}\sqrt{\alpha}x_{s,i}}{\sqrt{x_{s,i}}}\leq d\gamma_{s}+\frac{\alpha\beta_{s}^{2}}{4\gamma_{s}}+\beta_{s}\sqrt{\alpha d},

where we use AM-GM inequality:
-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{\alpha x_{s-1,i^{\star}}}+\frac{\beta_{s}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,i^{\star}}}}\leq\frac{\alpha\beta_{s}^{2}}{4\gamma_{s}}
, illustrating again the importance of the negative term from
Eq. (
4
)
in compensating the potentially large estimation error
\frac{\beta_{s}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,i^{\star}}}}
.
This concludes the
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
last-iterate convergence after setting
\gamma_{s}
appropriately.
It thus remains to prove the multiplicative stability, which, thanks to the log-barrier regularization, indeed holds with
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(d)
; see
Lemma
A.5
in the appendix.
Although it is known that log-barrier regularization ensures multiplicative stability in the FTRL framework, as first shown by
Wei and Luo 2018
and later utilized in many other works, our proof is different and needs to resolve several new challenges, including handling the time-varying learning rate
\gamma_{s}
and understanding how
\widehat{\ell}_{s}
deviates from
\widehat{\ell}_{s-1}
, the latter of which itself requires using multiplicative stability from previous epochs.

Formally, we show a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/\sqrt{t})
last-iterate convergence rate for
Algorithm
1
using this new analysis (see
Proposition
A.1
), slightly worse than the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{d/t})
rate obtained from the analysis discussed in
Section
3
.
However, the benefit is that this new analysis immediately generalizes to games (unlike the one in
Section
3
that provably cannot).

Generalization to games

The generalization starts with the following analogue of
Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every epoch
s\geq 1
,
Algorithm
2
satisfies:

\displaystyle x_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}e_{j}-e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}y_{s}\leq 2d\gamma_{s}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{x_{s,i}}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{y_{s,j}},\;\forall i,j\in[d].

(5)

This lemma shows that the lower the sampling probability of an action, the smaller the increase in payoff one can obtain by deviating unilaterally to this action.
We note that Eq. (2) of
Saha and Schapire 2025
is close to our
Eq. (
5
)
, but theirs involves a joint distribution over all action pairs and is thus fundamentally different from ours.
Next, we generalize the multiplicative stability as follows.

Lemma 4.4

(Multiplicative stability)
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
,
Algorithm
2
with
\gamma_{s}
set to the value stated in
Theorem
4.1
ensures
for all
s\geq 2
and
i,j\in[d]
,

x_{s,i}\leq 16dx_{s-1,i}
,

y_{s,j}\leq 16dy_{s-1,j}
.

The proof of this lemma is the most technical part of our analysis.
It not only needs to address all the issues mentioned earlier in the single-player case, but also needs to take into account the fact that
x_{s}
is the solution of an optimization defined in terms of
y_{s}
(and vice versa) and both of them are changing over time.
With these two key lemmas, the proof of our main result
Theorem
4.1
can be proven following similar ideas outlined earlier for the single-player case; see
Appendix
B
for details.

Lemma A.4

Proof of
Lemma
A.3

Fix

For any arm

Now suppose

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}\left\{n_{s-1,i}<\frac{2^{s-2}x_{s-1,i}}{2}\right\}\leq e^{-\frac{2^{s-2}x_{s-1,i}}{8}}\leq e^{-2\log(4d/\xi_{s})}.

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}\left\{\lvert\widehat{\ell}_{s,i}-\ell_{i}\rvert>\sqrt{\frac{16\log(4d/\xi_{s})}{2^{s-2}x_{s-1,i}}}\right\}\leq e^{-2\log(4d/\xi_{s})}+2e^{-16\log(4d/\xi_{s})}\leq\frac{\xi_{s}}{d}.

\displaystyle\lvert\langle x_{s+1}-x_{s},\widehat{\ell}_{s}-\widehat{\ell}_{s+1}\rangle\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{|x_{s+1,i}-x_{s,i}|}{\sqrt{x_{s,i}}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{|x_{s+1,i}-x_{s,i}|}{\sqrt{x_{s+1,i}x_{s,i}}}\sqrt{x_{s+1,i}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{s}\sqrt{Q_{s}},

where the last step follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Combining the bounds,

Q_{s}\leq(\rho_{s}-1)d+\frac{\rho_{s}\varepsilon_{s}\sqrt{Q_{s}}}{\gamma_{s}}.

Using
a\sqrt{Q_{s}}\leq(Q_{s}+a^{2})/2
with
a=\frac{\rho_{s}\varepsilon_{s}}{\gamma_{s}}
,

Q_{s}\leq 2(\rho_{s}-1)d+\frac{\rho_{s}^{2}\varepsilon_{s}^{2}}{\gamma_{s}^{2}}.

By the parameter choice and
\varepsilon_{s}=(1+\sqrt{\alpha})\max\left\{\beta_{s},\beta_{s+1}\right\}
,

Q_{s}\leq\left(\sqrt{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{2}.

Thus, for each
i\in[d]
,

\frac{x_{s+1,i}}{x_{s,i}}+\frac{x_{s,i}}{x_{s+1,i}}-2=\frac{(x_{s+1,i}-x_{s,i})^{2}}{x_{s+1,i}x_{s,i}}\leq Q_{s}\leq\alpha+\frac{1}{\alpha}-2.

Since
h(u)\triangleq u+u^{-1}-2
is increasing for
u\geq 1
, taking

implies that either
u\leq 1
or
u\leq\alpha
whenever
u>0
. In either case, we have

x_{s+1,i}\leq\alpha x_{s,i},

as
\alpha\geq d\geq 1
.
This completes the induction.
∎

We show that the choices of

and
r_{t}
with
\mathbb{E}[r_{t}]=A_{i_{t}j_{t}}
.

Increment counter
n_{s,i_{t}j_{t}}\leftarrow n_{s,i_{t}j_{t}}+1
.

Compute game estimator
\widehat{A}_{s+1,ij}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\left\{n_{s,ij}\neq 0\right\}}{n_{s,ij}}\sum_{\tau=2^{s-1}}^{2^{s}-1}r_{\tau}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{i_{\tau}=i,j_{\tau}=j\}
for all
i,j\in[d]
.

Algorithm 3

Empirical NE with uniform exploration

Proposition C.1
.

For any fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
,

Algorithm
3
guarantees that with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\text{\rm DGap}(x_{t},y_{t})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d\log(d\log t/\delta)}t^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)
for all
t\geq 1
, where
(x_{t},y_{t})
denotes the strategy pair played in round
t
.

Proof.

We prove the claim on the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
, which holds with probability at least
1-\delta
by
Lemma
B.2
. Since epoch
s
has length
2^{s-1}
, it suffices to show that
\text{\rm DGap}(x_{s},y_{s})=\mathcal{O}(2^{-s/4}\sqrt{d\log(d^{2}s^{2}/\delta)})
. Let
(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{y}_{s})
denote a Nash Equilibrium of
\widehat{A}_{s}
.
Fix any epoch
s\geq 2
. We begin by controlling the duality gap with respect to the empirical estimate
\widehat{A}_{s}
. Note that
\widehat{A}_{s,ij}\in[-1,1]
holds for all
i,j\in[d]
. From the sampling strategy of
Algorithm
3
, we have
\|x_{s}-\widehat{x}_{s}\|_{1}\leq 2\alpha_{s}
and
\|y_{s}-\widehat{y}_{s}\|_{1}\leq 2\alpha_{s}
.
Therefore, for all pairs
i,j\in[d]
,

\displaystyle x_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}e_{j}\leq\widehat{x}_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}e_{j}+2\alpha_{s}\leq\max_{y\in\Delta_{d}}\widehat{x}_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}y+2\alpha_{s}=\widehat{x}_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}\widehat{y}_{s}+2\alpha_{s},

\displaystyle e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}y_{s}\geq e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}\widehat{y}_{s}-2\alpha_{s}\geq\min_{x\in\Delta_{d}}x^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}\widehat{y}_{s}-2\alpha_{s}=\widehat{x}_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}\widehat{y}_{s}-2\alpha_{s}.

Thus, for all
i,j\in[d]
, we have
x_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}e_{j}-e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}y_{s}\leq 4\alpha_{s}
.

It remains to bound the last two estimation error terms in
Eq. (
19
)
. For the first term,
Lemma
B.2
implies

\displaystyle x_{s}^{\top}(A-\widehat{A}_{s})e_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}x_{s,k}\left|A_{kj}-\widehat{A}_{s,kj}\right|\leq\beta_{s}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{x_{s,k}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,k}y_{s-1,j}}}\leq\frac{\beta_{s}d}{\alpha_{s-1}}=\frac{\beta_{s}d}{2\alpha_{s}},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
x_{s-1,k}\geq\frac{\alpha_{s-1}}{d}
and
y_{s-1,j}\geq\frac{\alpha_{s-1}}{d}
for all
k,j\in[d]
, due to mixing with the uniform distribution.

Similarly, for the other term

\displaystyle e_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{A}_{s}-A)y_{s}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}y_{s,\ell}\left|\widehat{A}_{s,i\ell}-A_{i\ell}\right|\leq\beta_{s}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{y_{s,\ell}}{\sqrt{x_{s-1,i}y_{s-1,\ell}}}\leq\frac{\beta_{s}d}{\alpha_{s-1}}=\frac{\beta_{s}d}{2\alpha_{s}}.

Combining the three bounds, for every
i,j\in[d]
, we get

\displaystyle x_{s}^{\top}Ae_{j}-e_{i}^{\top}Ay_{s}\leq 4\alpha_{s}+\frac{\beta_{s}d}{\alpha_{s}}.

We have
\beta_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{16\log(8d^{2}s^{2}/\delta)}{2^{s-2}}}
. Setting
\alpha_{s}
optimally as
\sqrt{\beta_{s}d}
yields
\text{\rm DGap}(x_{s},y_{s})=\max_{i,j\in[d]}\left\{x_{s}^{\top}Ae_{j}-e_{i}^{\top}Ay_{s}\right\}=\mathcal{O}(2^{-s/4}\sqrt{d\log(d^{2}s^{2}/\delta)})
.
∎

C.2
Discussion on Low-Regret-Low-Variance Property in Games

In this subsection, we explain why a low-duality-gap-low-variance property, analogous to
Lemma
3.1
, is insufficient to establish last-iterate convergence in the game setting. We begin by presenting the following lemma, the game counterpart to
Lemma
3.1
, which follows directly from
Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma C.2
.

Define
\widehat{\text{\rm DGap}}_{s}(x,y)=\max_{j\in[d]}x^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}e_{j}-\min_{i\in[d]}e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}y
for all
s\geq 1
. For every epoch
s\geq 1
,
Algorithm
2
satisfies:

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{\rm DGap}}_{s}(x_{s},y_{s})

\displaystyle\leq 2d\gamma_{s},

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{s,i}}+\frac{1}{y_{s,j}}

\displaystyle\leq 2d+\frac{\widehat{\text{\rm DGap}}_{s}(e_{i},e_{j})}{\gamma_{s}},~\forall i,j\in[d].

(21)

Proof.

By
Lemma
4.3
, for all
i,j\in[d]
, we have:

\displaystyle x_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}e_{j}-e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}y_{s}\leq 2d\gamma_{s}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{x_{s,i}}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{y_{s,j}}\leq 2d\gamma_{s}.

Furthermore, rearranging the terms in
Eq. (
5
)
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{s,i}}+\frac{1}{y_{s,j}}\leq 2d-\frac{1}{\gamma_{s}}\left(x_{s}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}e_{j}-e_{i}^{\top}\widehat{A}_{s}y_{s}\right)\leq 2d+\frac{\widehat{\text{\rm DGap}}_{s}(e_{i},e_{j})}{\gamma_{s}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Unlike the single-agent case, however, we cannot directly bound the true duality gap
\text{\rm DGap}(x_{s},y_{s})
even if the following multiplicative closeness analogous to the single-player case holds due to
Eq. (
21
)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{\rm DGap}}_{s}(e_{i},e_{j})

\displaystyle\leq 2\text{\rm DGap}(e_{i},e_{j})+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\gamma_{s}),

\displaystyle{\text{\rm DGap}}(e_{i},e_{j})

\displaystyle\leq 2\widehat{\text{\rm DGap}}_{s}(e_{i},e_{j})+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\gamma_{s}).

This is because of the following crucial distinction: In the single-agent case, expectations translate linearly (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{i\sim x_{s}}[\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(e_{i})]=\widehat{{\mathrm{Reg}}}_{s}(x_{s})
and
\mathbb{E}_{i\sim x_{s}}[{{\mathrm{Reg}}}(e_{i})]={{\mathrm{Reg}}}(x_{s})
), which allows multiplicative closeness for pure actions to neatly imply closeness for mixed strategies. In the game setting, this linear translation breaks down. Instead, we are restricted to Jensen’s inequality (
\mathbb{E}_{i\sim x_{s},j\sim y_{s}}[\text{\rm DGap}(e_{i},e_{j})]\geq\text{\rm DGap}(x_{s},y_{s})
), which fundamentally prevents the application of a similar argument.
