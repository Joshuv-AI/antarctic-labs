Socioeconomic deprivation, disease complications and access to preventive care in sickle cell disease in mainland France
Authors: Ondine Walter, Pierre Cougoul, Maryse Lapeyre-Mestre, Michelle Kelly-Irving, Margaux Lafaurie, Guillaume Moulis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral studies have assessed the impact of socioeconomic factors on the health of sickle cell patients, yielding heterogeneous results. We conducted a population-based study using a health-insurance database in France to investigate this association, using longitudinal data over a 9-year follow-up time. Socioeconomic factors were assessed using the validated French deprivation index, an area-level index of social disadvantage. The study population comprised 17,315 patients with a median age of 23 years and a median follow-up of 8.1 years. Patients living in the most disadvantaged areas were at greater risk of vaso-occlusive events (VOEs): 70.4% of patients with a median cumulative time of hospitalization of 33.5 days over the study period in the disadvantaged socioeconomic group, compared to 67.9% of patients and 29.9 days in the comparison group (p = 0.035 and p < 0.001). There was no difference regarding chronic complications, but a trend towards higher mortality in the disadvantaged group (HR: 1.13, 95% CI: 0.94—1.36). There was no difference between groups in access to preventive and curative care recommended in mainland France (vaccination, folic acid supplementation, antibiotic prophylaxis, hydroxycarbamide use).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00277-026-07038-5.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, Sickle cell anemia, Epidemiology, Longitudinal study, Socio-economic factors, France

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most prevalent inherited blood disorder in France and worldwide. The presence of sickle-cells in blood induce in certain environment-driven factors (hypoxemia, stress, dehydratation, fever…) acute, painful ischemic crises like vaso-occlusive events (VOEs) and chronic ischemic complications, with increased morbidity and mortality as compared with the general population [1–3]. Advances in the management of SCD have led to improved survival, especially in high-income countries [4–7]. As patients with SCD live longer, the challenges in the management and prevention of the disease are twofold, involving acute events (VOEs, infections, priapism, stroke…) on one hand, and chronic complications (renal and cardiac insufficiency, retinopathy…) on the other [8–12]. Identifying populations at risk of acute and chronic complications of SCD is crucial for improving patient survival and quality of life, through personalized prophylactic measures.

Patients with SCD are often from disadvantaged socioeconomic groups [13–19]. In 2010, an international study involving 1,000 children living with SCD from France, Canada and the USA found that 53.7% of the patients were living below the poverty line [14]. Recently, according to the results from newborn screening in the USA, approximately two-thirds (67%) of mothers of newborns with SCD resided in counties with high or very high levels of social vulnerability [18].

Several studies have assessed the impact of these socio-economic factors on the health of sickle cell patients, yielding heterogeneous results [20]. Some studies have found an increase in emergency room attendance among patients from disadvantaged socioeconomic groups [13–15, 21, 22]. Conversely, other studies found a positive effect of race, socioeconomic disadvantage, and rural geographic location on health outcomes, [19, 21, 23] highlighting the significance of family and community as positive support for patients with SCD. These monocentric and retrospective studies are plagued by various biases, [20] complicating the interpretation of their conflicting results and highlighting the necessity for longitudinal data that include socioeconomic factors [24]. These studies have mainly examined emergency department attendance (number of admissions, readmission rate, number of hospitalizations) and consequently acute events. Few studies have evaluated the impact of socioeconomic disadvantage on chronic complications, [16, 25–27] and mortality [16]. Moreover, most studies come from the US, and this research question deserves to be conducted in a country that virtually offers equal access to health care resources whatever the income.

Consequently, the objectives of this study were to assess, in a national prospective cohort of patients with SCD in France, the association between socioeconomic disadvantage and (1) the occurrence of acute events, (2) the prevalence of chronic complications, (3) mortality and (4) the access to preventive care.

Methods

Data source

The study was conducted using data from the French national health insurance system database, the SNDS (Système National des Données de Santé – National Health Data System). This database prospectively records individualized, anonymous, and linkable data for the entire French population (68.4 million individuals). Data collected include demographics (age, sex, city of residence, vital status and date of death), out-of-hospital data (including all dispensing of reimbursed drugs and out-of-hospital procedures performed as well as “long-term diseases” (LTDs) allowing full reimbursement of health expenditure and coded by general practitioners) and an hospital database, named “Programme de médicalisation des systemes d’information” (PMSI), that contains data of all hospital stays of both private and public hospitals. It includes admission and discharge dates, discharge diagnoses coded with the international classification of disease, 10th version (ICD-10), and procedures (like red blood cell transfusions) [28–30].

Study population

The study population was the French national cohort of all patients with SCD (both adults and children) who received care between January 2010 and December 2018. Patients were identified either through hospitalization for VOE or by the recognition of SCD as a LTD. We previously demonstrated the accurate identification of SCD patients using this algorithm [31–33]. All genotypes (homozygous SS-SCD and double-heterozygous SCD) are included without possible distinction.

Socioeconomic deprivation

The variable used to assess the socioeconomic status of patients was the French Deprivation Index (FDEP), an area-level index of social disadvantage that characterizes the socioeconomic environment of individuals in mainland France [34]. It is calculated at the “commune” geographical level (equivalent of small townships; the smallest administrative unit in France, 30,500 units in total) using socioeconomic data from population censuses and household tax income records. The score (principal component of a factor analysis) considers household tax income, proportion of blue-collar workers in the “commune”, average educational level and unemployment rate. Based on the FDEP score of their commune of residence, the French population is inherently divided into five quintiles. For the purposes of the study, the population was categorized in two groups according to the FDEP: (1) disadvantaged socioeconomic environment including patients in the fourth and fifth FDEP quintiles; and (2) advantaged socio-economic environment, for patients between the first and the third FDEP quintiles. This threshold was chosen because we expected more disadvantaged patients in the SCD population in comparison with the general population, resulting in balanced numbers of patients in each group.

Outcomes

We assessed the incidence of acute events searched in the hospital database (PMSI), which included: (1) VOE: number of hospital admissions and number of days of hospitalization for VOE; (2) hospitalization for stroke (ischemic, hemorrhagic and thrombophlebitis); (3) hospitalization for priapism (including at emergency departments); (4) admission to intensive care unit (ICU): number of hospitals stays and number of days of hospitalization in ICU. Unfortunately, acute chest syndrome cannot be identified accurately in the SNDS. We assume that patients developing ACS are part of those admitted in ICU for severe VOE.

The following chronic complications were measured: (1) chronic heart failure (CHF), identified using hospital and LTD codes; (2) chronic kidney disease (CKD) with or without dialysis identified using hospital and LTD codes as well as dialysis procedures; (3) retinopathy treated with retinal laser photocoagulation, identified by photocoagulation procedures (Supplementary Material, Table A.1). These complications were chosen because of their frequency and severity, but also because they can be identified by a validated algorithm in the SNDS [35, 36].

To evaluate access to preventive care, we created several indicators in accordance to the French national guidelines at the time of the study: (1) prescription of hydroxycarbamide for patients experiencing three or more VOEs within a year (as recommended at the time of the study in France); (2) administration of folic acid before the age of one year; (3) 23-polysaccharide pneumococcal vaccine (23PPV) administration before the age of three years; (4) administration of phenoxymethylpenicillin before six months of age; (5) transcranial doppler (TCD) screening conducted before the age of two years [8, 9]. The outcomes (2) to (5) were assessed in children born during the study period.

The dates of death (all-cause mortality) were extracted from demographic records and hospital databases.

All codes, algorithms and classifications used to identify these outcomes in our database are detailed in Supplementary Table A.1. We used, when available, previously validated codes or algorithms [31, 35, 36].

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were presented for the prevalent, incident (children born during the study period), and the overall study population (both prevalent and incident populations). To compare the incidence of acute events and the prevalence of chronic complications, we used the prevalent population to ensure that the duration of follow-up was consistent between the two groups (disadvantaged and advantaged groups). We conducted sensitivity analyses stratified by age group (< 10 years, 10–19 years, 20–29 years and ≥ 30 years), and on the overall population. Comparisons of demographic data and acute events between the two groups were performed using Chi-square tests and Student's t-tests. For mortality analysis, we conducted a multivariate Cox regression analysis adjusted for age and sex. Follow-up ended at death or end of study period. Due to the nature of the database, the data are transmitted directly by insurance schemes, community pharmacies and hospitals and we did not observe missing data regarding the measured variables. The absence of FDEP calculation in oversea areas and the possibility of miscoding cannot be overcome a priori, and their possible impact on the results are discussed in the Limitation section below. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Guide™ (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Ethics

The present study, declared to the Toulouse University Hospital, has been authorized as part of the University Hospital's access to the SNDS portal since 2021(original access to the cohort: authorization by the French institute of health data (Institut des Données de Santé, numbered 2,990,547) and by the French personal data protection authority (Commission nationale de l’informatique et des libertés), numbered 921,170).

Results

Study population

Our study population is described in Fig. 1. Using our algorithm, we identified 23,543 patients with SCD in France over the period 2010–2018. Among these patients, 14,611 (62.1%) were hospitalized for VOE. We excluded 4,334 patients (18.4%) living overseas (where FDEP is not available) and 1,894 patients (8.0%) for missing data on place of residence preventing from FDEP calculation. Thus, our final study population comprised 17,315 patients with SCD. The median age was 23.0 years, 53.2% (9,214) were females, 86.1% (14.916) had an LTD designation for SCD, and 2.8% (491) died during the study period. The median follow-up duration was 8.1 years (SD: 2.1) (Table 1, Supplementary Material, Tables A.2. and A.3.). Among this population, 13,730 constituted the prevalent population (born before January 1, 2010) and 3,585 constituted the incident population.

Social deprivation

The distribution of patients by FDEP quintiles is shown in Fig. 1: 26.7% of patients were in the 5th quintile (the most disadvantaged), compared to the expected 20% in the general population. Overall, 7,490 patients (43.3%) were classified into the disadvantaged group and 9,825 (56.7%) into the advantaged group (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Acute events

Among the overall study population, 11,689 patients (67.5%) presented at least one VOE during the study period, 305 patients (2.2%) at least one stroke and 120 patients at least one episode of priapism (0.8%). A higher number of cumulative days of hospitalization for VOE were observed in the disadvantaged group (mean: 33.5 days) than in the advantaged group (mean: 29.9 days; p < 0.001) (Table 2). A greater proportion of patients in the disadvantaged group was hospitalized at least once for VOE (70.4% vs. 67.9%; p = 0.035). The sensitivity analysis conducted in the overall population showed similar results (data not shown). Concerning the other acute events, the cumulative number of days spent in ICU (15.0 days in disadvantaged group vs 13.8 days in advantaged group) and the number of patients hospitalized at least once for priapism (0.98% in disadvantaged group vs 0.72% in advantaged group) were also slightly higher in the disadvantaged group. There was no difference in the occurrence of stroke.

Chronic complications

Within the prevalent population, there was no difference according to socioeconomic deprivation for CKD, retinopathy and heart failure (Table 3). Mortality

Over the study period, we observed 491 deaths in the total population, at a median age of 41.6 years (Q1-Q3: 26.9—56.5). We observed 25 deaths in the incident population. The Cox model performed on the prevalent population, adjusted for age and sex, showed a trend towards an excess mortality in the disadvantaged group, with an adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of 1.13, 95% CI: 0.94—1.36 (p = 0.19).

Access to preventive care

In the overall population, 68.9% of patients hospitalized ≥ 3 times for VOE within one year were subsequently dispensed hydroxycarbamide. In the incident population; 78.6% of patients had received folic acid supplementation before the age of 1 year; 72% had received phenoxymethylpenicillin before the age of 6 months; only 48% had been vaccinated with the 23PPV before the age of 2 years, and 32.5% had received TCD before the age of 3 years. There was no difference between disadvantaged and advantaged groups regarding these preventive measures (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study supports an increase in VOEs and ICU stays in SCD patients who are in the disadvantaged group, even though the crude difference between the groups was small. These results are consistent with the pathophysiology of SCD. Indeed, known VOE-promoting factors such as cold, stress, dehydration, and sleep deprivation are associated with socioeconomic disadvantage.

As regards chronic complications, a crude comparison of the two groups did not reveal any significant difference in the prevalence of retinopathy, heart failure and CKD. An age-stratified analysis showed a slight increase in the prevalence of CKD in the disadvantaged group in the youngest age groups (0–10 and 10–20 years). Prior research has shown that CKD in SCD may be consequence of the repetition of acute VOEs, and through repeated renal "aggression" during hospitalizations for VOE (volume variations, administration of toxic drugs, ischemia…) [37]. Therefore, the socio-economic environment, by favoring VOEs, may play an indirect role in the development of renal failure. On the other hand, we found no association between the socioeconomic disadvantage and the occurrence of stroke, CHF and retinopathy. This may be explained by the fact that the pathophysiology of these complications is not linked to VOEs [38, 39].

We also observed a trend toward an increase in mortality among the most disadvantaged patients. This is consistent with the initial FDEP validation study for the entire French metropolitan population between 1997 and 2001, [34] and with an observational study conducted in the UK in 2013 involving over 6,000 patients with SCD [16].

We were able to demonstrate that access to recommended preventive therapeutic measures did not depend on socioeconomic disadvantage. Furthermore, our results suggest that the most disadvantaged patients are equally as adherent to care as their advantaged counterparts in France.

The strength of our study lies in the use of a health insurance database (SNDS), allowing exhaustive information from the general population of health care users, over a long period, without information or memory bias [28, 30]. Patient identification algorithms have been validated beforehand, [31–33] and the patients' epidemiological data correspond to previously published French data from medical records [40, 41]. Most previous studies investigating the association between socioeconomic disadvantage and health outcomes in patients with SCD have been carried out in the USA, where access to care is not equivalent according to resource level [27, 42–44]. It was therefore interesting to investigate this association in France, which has a healthcare system that theoretically provides equity of access to care for all patients, regardless of resources.

Our study has limitations. Despite prior validation of codes and algorithms, we cannot exclude residual classification errors. The higher proportion of women observed in our population was not expected and could reflect a residual misclassification of women with sickle cell trait, particularly in maternity-related hospitalizations. These identification and coding concerns could have affected our results if differentially distributed between the two study groups. Another limitation of our study is the absence of any information on patient genotype in the SNDS [32, 45]. Genotype depends on patients' ethnic origin, data which is not recorded in the SNDS due to French regulation about ethnicity recording. Sß0 and Sß+ genotypes are predominant in the Mediterranean basin and India, while SS and SC genotypes are more common in sub-Saharan Africa [1, 46]. Since the place of residence in France (and therefore FDEP) may depend on ethnic origin, and the severity of pathology depends on genotype, this may have introduced residual confounding. The absence of genotype discrimination in our study may explain some of our results, notably the low percentage of TCD performed in our population (recommended only for patients with SS or Sß0 genotypes). Migration status is not recorded in the SNDS. Similarly, the SNDS does not provide access to the results of biological tests, which are known to be factors associated with disease severity (notably hemoglobin or leucocytosis). However, they are intermediate variables for the question asked, and do not introduce any bias in the causal relationship between socioeconomic disadvantage and the outcomes. The FDEP is not available for oversea areas, where SCD patients are more likely to be socioeconomically disadvantaged. We could not include these patients in our study. Finally, the FDEP is an average indicator of socioeconomic deprivation measured at a geographical area and not at the individual level. As such, it is prone to ecological bias, the fact of assigning an average exposure level to an individual that does not correspond to their true exposure, a bias that increases with the size of the area. However, even if the scale is small, there is always a bias in using ecological indices of deprivation to approximate individual socioeconomic position, which leads to an underestimation of the extent of social inequality that would be observed with individual indicators [47, 48]. We lack other indicators of individual socio-economic deprivation. The universal insurance coverage, given to patients in France upon request in case of low financial resources, was nearly similar between our two groups (data not shown), and captures only the level of income in a binary way, reflecting less the global socioeconomic deprivation than the FDEP.

In conclusion, in this population-based study of 17,315 patients with SCD, a disadvantaged environment was associated with an increased incidence of acute complications, and a trend to excess mortality. The access to preventive measures did not depend on socioeconomic disadvantage level, though they were insufficiently followed overall. These results support an independent role of the socioeconomic environment on the health of patients with SCD.
