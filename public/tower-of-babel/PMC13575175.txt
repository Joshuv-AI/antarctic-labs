META-DIFF: a k-mer-based pipeline that detects differentially abundant sequences in metagenomics whole genome sequencing
Authors: Louis-Maël Guéguen, Alban Mathieu, Simon Pelletier, Anthony Woo, Namita Misra, Magali Moreau, Olivier Perin, Arnaud Droit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional case-control metagenomic studies are constrained by their dependence on taxonomic and functional databases. Because annotation occurs before differential analysis, they are limited to known elements and keep function and taxonomy separate. Although binning strategies have emerged to reconstruct genomes and mitigate this issue, they still require an assembly step, preventing the use of all available sequencing data. Here, we introduce META-DIFF, a pipeline based on differentially abundant k-mers independently of any prior annotation. From those k-mers, it reconstructs longer sequences and provides biological context, as well as the best set of unitigs to discriminate between conditions. Across both taxonomy-centric and functionally-centric benchmarks, it showed robust performance and displayed great reproducibility. It also behaved more conservatively than did other univariate methodologies, i.e. it maintained a high precision at the expense of recall, particularly in conditions of low fold-change and limited sequencing depth. The efficacy of META-DIFF was further validated through its application to a real-world colorectal cancer dataset, which produced both confirmatory and novel results compared with those of previous publications. The pipeline is able to exploit all reads and identify differentially abundant elements, including unknown DNA, prior to annotation. With the guidelines provided, META-DIFF provides users with great exploratory power to unravel microbiome changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59138-x.Keywords: Metagenomics, Shotgun, Differential abundance, k-mer, UnitigsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Microbiology

Introduction

Metagenomics is a community-based approach that is essential for understanding the composition and functions of microorganisms in relation to their environment1. Whole genome sequencing (WGS or Shotgun) directly sequences the entire DNA extracted from a sample, bypassing cultivation issues2. With sufficient sequencing depth, shotgun sequencing enables the characterisation of bacteria, archaea, viruses, and eukaryotes at the species level. Additionally, it allows for the characterisation of genes carried by the identified genomes. Furthermore, shotgun sequencing data can be assembled and then binned into metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs)3,4,. These MAGs can be used to determine the constitution of the microbial community and its specificity compared to a reference5.

Metagenomics studies aim to find a set of microbiome members or functions that are differentially abundant in case datasets compared to controls. In recent years, links between microbiomes and complex phenotypes have been discovered6–9.

However, several challenges remain. WGS with adequate sequencing depth generates a vast number of reads, leading pipelines to often discard the rarest to alleviate computational cost. Another issue is identification of the reads and thus quantification, from which differential abundances are derived. Reads are assigned to species through alignment methods (e.g., BLAST10 or k-mer counts and presence/absence11,12. This step is crucial, but it reintroduces the reliance on reference genomes, which are often cultivated microbes, and restricts discoveries to closely related known microorganisms. Despite offering strain-level resolution, MAGs are not an ideal solution. Different binning methods have different outcomes and caveats13 and they often use only the portions of the sequencing data that can be assembled (i.e., the most abundant genomes), leaving other biologically relevant information unused. To address these issues, scalable and reference-free methods that leverage all reads are needed.

k-mer-based methods fulfill these criteria. k-mers are substrings of length k extracted from a text (here reads) by sliding a window of length k over consecutive positions in the text. k-mers are the basis for a wide suite of tools for various experiments, including the identification of sequences associated with a phenotype. Their advantage in this particular analysis is finding much finer differences than at the species-level. DBGWAS14, a k-mer-based tool, associates variants of different sizes (from SNPs to genes and beyond) with a phenotype. However, it cannot handle the size of metagenomic data sets.

In this work, we introduce an innovative k-mer-based pipeline that moves the differential abundance before annotation. It is based on the Kmdiff application15, a tool for differential k-mer analysis. The pipeline leverages all reads to identify enriched or depleted k-mers, independent of their rarity. The k-mers are then assembled into unitigs (non-ambiguous path in a De Bruijn Graph) which are only then annotated to obtain insight into their taxonomy and function. Using machine learning, the pipeline selects the best model and features (unitigs) for classification of the samples, and computes the contribution of each feature. This step yields candidates for biomarker discovery and provides additional exploratory potential.

The pipeline efficiency is supported by a benchmark on simulated datasets against different known univariate methodologies. The results also provide guidelines for assessing the sensitivity of detecting various fold changes of genomes between conditions associated with different sequencing depths. The pipeline is finally applied to a colorectal cancer dataset to test it in a real-case scenario and compare it with the results of the original publication.

Method

META-DIFF can be broken down into several stages: (i) computation of differentially abundant elements (k-mers), (ii) reconstruction of sequences (unitigs), (iii) taxonomic and functional annotation, and (iv) feature selection of unitigs to define the shortest signature discriminating between the conditions. The first two steps were benchmarked to demonstrate the accuracy of the method at the both k-mer and unitig levels. Taxonomic annotation was used to compare META-DIFF against univariate methodologies known to be used in metagenomic studies. The entire pipeline was applied to a real colorectal cancer dataset available in the DDJB as DRA006684 and DRA008156. The pipeline is described in Fig. 1.

Schematic of the pipeline. Each coloured panel describes a step.

Pipeline implementation

Step 1: differentially abundant k-mers.

Differentially abundant 31-mers are extracted using Kmdiff (v1.1.0, multiple test correction method = Benjamini-Hochberg, significance threshold = 0.05 and cutoff = 105)15.

Step 2: unitigs reconstruction.

The differentially abundant k-mers are used as input for Bcalm (v2.2.3)16 to build unitigs (minimum abundance = 1).

Step3a: taxonomic annotation of unitigs.

The unitigs are taxonomically assigned with Kraken2 (v2.1.12)11 using a custom database comprising genomes from the RefSeq genomic database (version 2022), including bacteria fungi, viruses and archaea, totaling 123k genomes (genome assembly levels selected: chromosome, complete genome and scaffold).

Step 3b: functional annotation of unitigs.

Prodigal (V2.6.3)17 predicts gene sequences in unitigs with lengths equal to or greater than 1000 nucleotides. MicrobeAnnotator (v2.0.5, default settings)18 annotates the resulting protein sequences. A table is generated to report which gene and its sequence is detected in which unitig, with its annotation (KEGG19 orthology term, function, taxonomy). Taxonomic assignments are summarised by summing the number of nucleotides assigned to each level of taxonomy reported by Kraken211. This yields a list of taxa and their abundance for each condition.

Step 4: Search of biomarker candidates.

A script aggregates the p-values of k-mers to obtain those of unitigs using the Cauchy combination test (1) as described by Liu and Xie20, using the implementation in SciPy21(v1.12.0). Unitigs are then sorted by ascending p-value, then descending length for equal p-values. Following this order, the 5k top unitigs from both the controls and cases (total 10k) are queried in the index of the samples using kmindex22. In case a dataset yields fewer than 5k unitigs in a condition, all the unitigs from that condition are used. This query yields a matrix of abundances of the unitigs in the samples. The resulting matrix serves as input for training machine-learning models, to learn which set of unitigs (features) is best to classify the samples. Bayesian optimisation is used to explore and fine-tune hyper-parameters of several models (Random Forest, K-nearest neighbours, Support Vector Machine, Linear Discriminant Analysis, XGBoost). Shapley values are calculated to understand the importance of each feature.

All figures were generated in R23 with ggplot2 v3.4.324, cowplot v1.1.125.

The detailed materials and methods are provided in the Supplementary information.

Pipeline evaluation

Sequencing dataset generation for benchmark analysis

Sequencing data for the different conditions were prepared following the method described in Fig. 2. A real public dataset from Barnard et al.26 was used to quantify species (Kraken211, Bracken12 and select the most abundant species, thus providing reference data. Replicas of abundance profiles were simulated by adding Gaussian noise to the reference abundances (2). Four selected species were over-represented by increasing their abundances in a condition by a given fold-change: 1.5, 2 or 3. CAMISIM27 was then used to obtain sequencing files with different sequencing depths (10 M, 20 M, 40 M reads, 300 bp, paired-end). The detailed method is described in the Supplementary Method.

Method for building the benchmark data. The first step consisted of identifying the 150 most abundant species. For each of these the reference genome was retrieved from the RefSeq database. Replicates of the reference distribution were generated by adding Gaussian noise, and the signal was added by applying the fold-change to the targeted species abundances. Then, CAMISIM was used with the generated abundances and reference genomes to simulate reads.

For a subset of conditions, functions absent from selected reference genomes were added to the targeted species to assess the ability of META-DIFF to detect functional enrichment. 7 genes were included in the genome of C. acnes (operon of efflux protein for bacilysin excretion), 5 in S. epidermidis (operon of ptxB pertussis toxin binding) and 5 in S. pyogenes (operon of nodulation proteins).

Benchmark metrics

META-DIFF was evaluated at the level of k-mers and unitigs. It was also compared against other univariate methods. The metrics used were precision (3) and recall (4).They were calculated from a presence/absence matrix of the enriched k-mers in the genomes used for read simulation and sets of unique k-mers from the four over-represented species. Unique k-mers from each over-represented genome were obtained using UniqueKMER (v0.1.0)28. The matrix was built using Bifrost (v.1.2.0)29.

Precision-recall of enriched k-mers

A global precision was computed to assess Kmdiff’s15 ability to detect as enriched k-mers from the over-represented species. Enriched k-mers present in at least one of the four over-represented species were counted as true positives and others as false positives. The precision and recall were then narrowed down to unique k-mers of the over-represented species. An enriched k-mer was considered a true positive for a species if it was unique to its genome, or a false positive if it was present in the genome of the species but not unique to it.

Precision-recall of unitigs

A unitig was identified as present in a reference genome if all its constituent k-mers were present in the genome. A unitig present in at least one of the over-represented species was counted as a true positive, otherwise as a false positive.

Precision-recall of functions

Prodigal (V2.6.3)17 predicted gene sequences in the unitigs obtained from the differentially abundant k-mers. The translations of the reference genes were searched in the results. Complete or partial recovery was counted as a true positive.

Results

Benchmark: precision and recall of enriched k-mers

With the notable exception of FC1.2 and FC1.5 at 10 M reads, for which no enriched k-mers were detected, most of our precision metrics were above 99% (see Supp. Figure 5 A).

Recall of enriched unique k-mers

The recall of unique k-mers was calculated for each enriched species (Fig. 3B). Notably, the recall scores were generally low in most of the tested scenarios, primarily because the number of differentially abundant k-mers was low relative to the total number of unique k-mers within each genome. These recall results also highlighted how the sequencing depth and the relative abundances of the species influenced the detection of unique k-mer. For instance, the recall of C. acnes (the most abundant species in our datasets) first reached 1 (all unique k-mers detected) at FC2 with 20 M reads, and was high at FC1.5 and 40 M reads. In contrast, for M. restricta, the maximum recall score of 1 was achieved only at FC3 with 40 M reads. Similarly, S. epidermidis recall scores also reached 1 at FC3 with 40 M reads, but the other values were lower than those of M. restricta under the same parameters. Finally, the recall score for S. pyogenes reached a maximum of 0.62 at FC3 with 40 M reads.

Precision of enriched unique k-mers

The precision of enriched unique k-mers per species is presented in Fig. 3A. The precision scores reached a plateau for all four species. For the most abundant species C. acnes and M. restricta, this maximum was reached at lower FC values and sequencing depths than for S. epidermidis and S. pyogenes. The precision plateau actually corresponded to the proportion of unique k-mers in the genomes for this dataset composition. The recall of unique k-mers (Fig. 3B) explained the plateau observed in the precision metric boxplots (Fig. 3A). For the three organisms whose recall reached 1, the precision plateau was the proportion of unique k-mers in the genomes in the context of this dataset.

Overall, the benchmark metrics for unique k-mers showed that the precision converged quickly toward the proportion of unique k-mers in the genome and that low fold-change were challenging for a strong recall.

Benchmark: precision and recall of unitigs

Taxonomic precision and recall

The capability of META-DIFF to rebuild correct sequences is presented in Fig. 3C. All precision metrics were greater than 0.74 and were very consistent across replicates. This demonstrated that the number of chimera (sequences composed of sub-sequences from different species) produced by META-DIFF was low.

Functional precision and recall

The recall of genes in the functional enrichment simulation is illustrated in Fig. 3D. The 7 genes of C. acnes could be detected even with the lowest combination of parameters (FC1.5, 10 M reads). Between 3 and 4 of the genes of S. epidermidis were detected, and no genes were recovered for the extremely low abundant S. pyogenes. As the amino acid sequences recovered from unitig could be short (the shortest was 20aa), an alignment with BLASTP30 of the proteins against the NCBI database was performed to verify that the results produced were usable. Only one sequence of 246 did not have any hit. Others were easy to identify from the blast results.

META-DIFF has a more conservative behaviour than alternative methods

The results of META-DIFF were compared with those of 5 univariate analysis methods.

Precision results, illustrated in Fig. 3E, revealed distinct patterns. ANCOMBC231, ALDEx232 and limma33 consistently exhibited decreasing precision as the FC increased. Furthermore, both ALDEx232 and Wilcoxon’s test demonstrated poor overall precision. In contrast, the META-DIFF pipeline consistently achieved a precision close to 1 across most conditions, with variability observed only for results at FC1.2.

The recall results are presented in Fig. 3F. ALDEx232, ANCOMBC231, DESeq234 and limma33 achieve scores of 1 in a majority of experiments. The Wilcoxon test performed poorly overall, with only three non-zero recall measures. META-DIFF presented a more gradual recall progression. Its lowest recall was 0 for FC1.2. Any higher FC values yield a minimum recall of 0.25. As the abundance of the species decreased, both the FC and sequencing depth needed to be increased to obtain a positive recall.

Compared to common univariate methods, META-DIFF showed higher precision at the cost of low recall for lower fold-changes.

Application to real case scenarios: CRC Stage I/II versus Healthy

The META-DIFF pipeline was then applied to actual metagenomic datasets to validate its accuracy. Specifically, an investigation into the link between the microbiome and stages I/II colorectal cancer (CRC) was conducted. This investigation aimed to compare the results with previous observations and identify potential new targets.

P. copri and F. prausnitzii showed a metabolic shift between stages I/II and Healthy

A total of 532M k-mers were detected as differentially abundant in the stages I/II group, and 482 M in the Healthy group. After DBG compaction, the corresponding numbers of unitigs were 16.5 M and 16.7 M.

Following taxonomic annotation we observed that both conditions had different unitigs assigned to the same taxa, i.e., P. copri, F. prausnitzii, P. plebeius and the genus Klebsiella. However the genes identified in the enriched unitigs assigned to these shared taxa differed (both in sequence and functions) between stages I/II and Healthy conditions. Bacteroides fragilis and Bacteroides uniformis were among species with the highest abundance in both conditions, with hundreds of genes identified within their assigned unitigs.

Genus Bacteroides dominates the composition of the top biomarker candidates

The best signature discriminating early stages colorectal cancer (stages I/II) and healthy conditions was obtained with an SVM model that selected 9581 features (unitigs). The confusion matrix and the Shapley values histogram are shown in Supp. Figure 11. The top 10 features of the model are shown in Table 1. The highest Shapley values were substantially higher than expected given the large number of features. The bacterium B. cellulocysticus seems to be particularly relevant for the model’s performance.

Hardware and run time

The pipeline was executed on a server equipped with 1 Tb of RAM, 2 AMD EPYC 7601 32-Core Processors, running Debian 12.2.0–14 (Linux kernel 6.1.0–12-amd64). It took 2 days to complete the process of the 1.7 TB of the CRC dataset with 120 threads and a peak RAM usage of approximately 700GB.

Discussion

Overview of the simulation

The simulation mimicked a simple but real microbiome composition and allowed measurement of the impact of the main parameters of a case-control metagenomic study. The four species selected for enrichment were Cutibacterium acnes, Malassezia restricta, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Staphylococcus pyogenes. The mock community was dominated by C. acnes, M. restricta. S. epidermidis had an intermediate abundance and S. pyogenes was a rare taxon. M. restricta was chosen to observe the result of the pipeline on a eukaryotic genome, which was expected to have more unique features than bacterial genomes due to the composition of the dataset. k-mer size was set to 31, as metagenomics is already a computationally intensive task, and benchmarking the k-mer size would be time consuming, so choosing this default size was considered the most reasonable choice. As enrichment in a case compared to a control is respectively a depletion in the controls compared to the case, we verified that Kmdiff15 was yielding the inverted results when swapping cases and controls, with just changed labels (Supp. Figure 9). We also verified that Kmdiff15 could detect an enrichment and a depletion at the same time in Supp. Figure 10. The META-DIFF pipeline showed promising performance in the simulated datasets. Kmdiff15 was able to detect a significant number of k-mers truly enriched from noise with a very high precision. The benchmark at the unitig level also reached high precision scores, and the analysis of precision-recall values against other univariate methods together demonstrated that the META-DIFF pipeline differential analysis had a conservative behaviour. This particularity was observable when the simulated tests were close to the detection threshold, i.e., when there was a low FC, or a low sequencing depth. By inserting genes to simulate strain level differences, we proved that the pipeline was able to detect enriched genes with a high accuracy even at low FC (1.5) and/or low sequencing depth (10 M). This ability was not possible to benchmark simultaneously with the other univariate methods since they require an annotation table to perform differential analysis, emphasising the interest of META-DIFF across state of the art methodologies.

Our experimental design aimed to determine the resolution of our methodology and characterise the conditions necessary for detecting changes in the microbiome composition, including minimal fold change, sequencing depth, and the influence of the specific microbiome composition. In the results, above the values of FC2 and 20 M reads, all enriched species could be detected. One parameter that was not fully investigated was the complexity of the microbiome. We tried to set different scenarios using different species with different characteristics, but a non-realistic number of models would be necessary to represent the different microbiome structures possible. The number of species and phylogenetic isolation may play an important role. In our analysis, it impacted the uniqueness of the k-mers (whether they were shared by several species or not), which in turn affected the De Bruijn graph and the resulting unitigs. The simulation further demonstrated an asymmetrical number of enriched k-mers, a finding that may run counter to intuition and warrants further discussion. In a compositional dataset with very few elements, changing (absolute or relative) the abundance of one element has a strong effect on the abundances of other elements. But in metagenomics, the number of elements (both species and k-mers) is sky-high. Thus, a strong positive change in the abundances of a high number of elements might not result in a comparable negative change, as the abundance adjustment can be delayed on millions of counts.

The combined interpretation of precision and recall metrics indicated that the META-DIFF model has conservative behaviour, which comes at the cost of lower recall at lower FC. This conservative approach is uncommon in univariate analysis in metagenomic analysis. However, for fold changes FC ⩾ 2 and sequencing depths ⩾ 20 millions, META-DIFF’s recall matches that of other methods. The observed trend of decreasing precision with increasing fold change for ANCOMBC2, DESeq2 and limma could be explained by the greater number of reads attributed to the enriched species and thus also attributed to closely related species. Counts for these species are therefore higher, and the species were detected as differentially abundant. The poor capacity of the Wilcoxon’s test to detect differential changes was unexpected. It is possible that different normalisation methods would have helped increase its performance, but the scope of this paper was not to comprehensively benchmark differential abundance methods, and the CLR is considered as one of the best transformation methods for metagenomic data35.

Application in colorectal cancer (CRC) dataset

In the colorectal cancer (CRC) dataset, we detected a high number of k-mers as differentially abundant, reflecting both the microbiota’s complexity and the impact of cancer on the microbiome. The larger sample size likely increased our statistical power. Our analysis retrieved well-documented results, such as the importance of the genus Bacteroides7,36,37 in CRC, the potential role of A. muciniphila38, and the depletion of the anti-inflammatory Roseburia species39 in CRC patients. However, META-DIFF also yielded results that may contribute to ongoing investigations: P. copri was described as depleted in cancer by Obuya et al.40, but the species was also associated with inflammatory lesions by Li et al.41. The ambiguous behaviour at the species level may be consistent with our META-DIFF results: unitigs assigned to P. copri were detected in both conditions. A metabolic shift of members of the same species may be a correct interpretation, as suspected by Abdelsalam et al.42. From a functional perspective, some polyketides have been associated with E. coli strains in CRC43. A detailed comparison with the literature is given in Table 2 for taxonomy and with the original publication in Table 3 for functions. The three species (Chitinophaga vietnamensis, Kluyvera intermedia, Bacteroides cellulocysticus) were shown only to illustrate that species not yet described as associated with CRC were detected by META-DIFF. As the strength of the pipeline is to detect differentially abundant sequences regardless of their annotation, it is important to state that both tables only contain results with an annotation. More than 20k genes and 16 M unitigs need further characterisation and represent a pool of elements for interpretation. Thus any interpretation should be careful as unknown or missing elements, as well as potential false positives, may skew the conclusion drawn from the results.

Search for biomarkers candidates in annotated and unnanotated unitigs

Biomarkers reduce diagnostic costs by focusing on a few quantifiable targets and, in CRC, may replace invasive procedures. We evaluated model performance using MCC (critical in cancer research) and accuracy. The number of features given as inputs for the models was chosen after different values were compared. Higher values dramatically increased run time with no significant gain in performance. While the overall accuracy was favorable, the MCC value remained modest (0.35). This could be caused by several factors, including inherent cancer heterogeneity, and/or the merge of stages I and II. From a Machine-learning perspective, the limiting factor is the low number of samples, which is not compensated for by the high number of input features. Comparisons between healthy versus stages I/II achieved perfect training metrics, suggesting potential over-fitting despite our precautions during model training. Additionally, it is important to underline that a single dataset was used for training and evaluation and that the signature was not tested on an external dataset, as it was developed as a proof of concept rather than a biological study. It might then be specific to his cohort. The top features included gene-coding unitigs, some of which matched high-rank taxa. One peculiar finding involved a unitig annotated as D. orientis that, with BLAST, aligned only to Oscillospiraceae, a gut taxon linked to inflammatory bowel disease49. One of the key advantages of the pipeline is its ability to detect unknown organisms or functions. In the CRC analysis, unclassified sequences accounted for the highest cumulative length, and the highest percentage of unitigs (50%). Moreover, the detected genes were not annotated in majority (77.7%). These figures underscore the importance of detecting enriched DNA sequences prior annotation. This approach allows the inclusion of uncharacterised microbes, thereby highlighting novel targets for future research.

Despite these encouraging results, the pipeline also has limitations. The interpretation of the results might not be straightforward due to the proportion of unknown sequences. In the context of functional annotation, the limited length of reconstructed unitigs can impede gene sequence prediction, thereby hindering the interpretation of results. Moreover, the results of the pipeline are numerous and some may be false positives despite the conservative behaviour. Finally, as always with differential abundance analysis, the absence of an element from the result does not necessarily mean it is irrelevant to the study, but – in the case of META-DIFF - the detection power was insufficient.

Conclusion

Our pipeline is innovative in its methodology by moving the detection of differential abundance before the annotation step. It allows to analyse complex metagenomic datasets at a fine resolution, leveraging all the data, with the integration of biological information and definition of potential biomarkers. It compares well with other differential abundance methods while providing richer information. The identification of enriched elements requires no prior knowledge, they directly link functions to organisms, and can span any length. This study also provides guidelines for understanding the interplay between biological and methodological factors, and their influence on the detection capacity for microbiome changes. The META-DIFF pipeline was successfully validated on colorectal cancer metagenomic data by yielding previously known results but also opened up new avenues of research. Some results were correlated with knowledge of the literature, others were completely new or debated. The results illustrated the exploratory potential of the pipeline. META-DIFF was implemented as a Snakemake workflow and is freely available on GitHub at https://github.com/Louis-MG/META-DIFF/.
