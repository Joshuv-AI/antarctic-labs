Efficacy and safety of orforglipron in obesity with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a GRADE-assessed meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Ahmed Emara, Mohamed Emara, Ahmed Mansour, Mostafa H Elkholy, Omar Fayez Abbas, Hamza A Abdul-Hafez, Mohammad Al Diab Al Azzawi, Abdalhakim Shubietah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) frequently coexist and markedly increase cardiometabolic risk. Orforglipron is a novel oral glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist developed to improve adherence compared with injectable therapies. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate its efficacy and safety.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Web of Science were searched through January 2026 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing orforglipron with placebo in adults with obesity and T2DM. Random-effects models were used to pool mean differences (MDs) and risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThree RCTs, including 2,505 participants, were analyzed. Dose-subgroup analyses demonstrated a consistent dose–response pattern across all efficacy outcomes. For body weight, reductions versus placebo ranged from MD − 2.43% at 3 mg to MD − 7.80% at 24 mg. BMI reductions ranged from MD − 0.88 kg/m² at 3 mg to MD − 2.70 kg/m² at 45 mg. Waist circumference reductions were significant at doses ≥ 6 mg, reaching MD − 5.90 cm at 24 mg. HbA1c reductions ranged from MD − 0.80% at 3 mg to MD − 1.67% at 45 mg, and fasting serum glucose reductions ranged from MD − 20.62 mg/dL at 3 mg to MD − 44.80 mg/dL at 45 mg. Treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was higher with orforglipron across doses, while serious adverse events were not significantly different from placebo at any dose.ConclusionsOrforglipron may improve weight and glycemic outcomes in obese patients with T2DM; however, these findings are based on only three randomized controlled trials, and several key efficacy outcomes were rated as low certainty, so the results should be interpreted cautiously pending larger confirmatory studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02218-9.Keywords: Orforglipron, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Obesity, Meta-analysis, Randomized controlled trials.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity are closely related global public health problems with rapidly rising prevalence. In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated 2.5 billion adults (43%) were overweight, and 890 million (16%) were obese worldwide [1, 2]. Additionally, the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimates 589 million adults have diabetes, with over 90% of these cases having T2DM [3].

Obesity is a major, independent risk factor for the development and progression of T2DM, driven by mechanisms such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and dysregulated energy homeostasis [4, 5]. The coexistence of T2DM and obesity markedly increases cardiometabolic risk, and patients with both conditions exhibit higher cardiovascular risk and all-cause mortality [6–8]. Obesity is also associated with comorbidities such as obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), hypertension, dyslipidemia, osteoarthritis, and coronary artery disease [9]. Likewise, T2DM commonly coexists with hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease [10]. Among patients with T2DM, those with concomitant obesity are more likely to develop comorbid neuropathy, nephropathy, cardiovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, and other complications [11, 12]. These dual epidemics lead to disability and drive healthcare costs, highlighting the need for integrated, effective management strategies.

Current treatment strategies for patients with concomitant obesity and T2DM include lifestyle modification and glucose-lowering or anti-obesity pharmacotherapies, but durable simultaneous improvement in body weight and glycemic control remains challenging in routine practice. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have substantially improved the management of both conditions by producing clinically meaningful weight loss and improving glycemic control [13–15]. Injectable GLP-1RAs such as liraglutide, semaglutide, and the dual GLP-1/GIP agonist tirzepatide achieve substantial weight reduction, yet important limitations remain, including the burden of subcutaneous administration, gastrointestinal tolerability issues, cost and access barriers, and reduced real-world persistence [13–15]. Even oral semaglutide requires a strict fasting regimen, which may further complicate adherence. In parallel, some proposed interventions for obesity and diabetes continue to be supported mainly by preclinical rodent evidence rather than robust human randomized trials, underscoring the need for effective and convenient therapies with stronger clinical translational evidence [16].

Orforglipron is a novel, orally available, small-molecule GLP-1RA with high potency and a long half-life that supports once-daily dosing without strict fasting or fluid restrictions [17]. These properties suggest orforglipron may overcome adherence barriers associated with peptide GLP-1RAs and simplify therapy for patients.

Recent randomized trials have shown that orforglipron improves glycemic control and reduces body weight in adults with obesity and T2DM, with larger effects generally observed at higher doses [18–20]. Early studies demonstrated reductions in fasting serum glucose, HbA1c, and body weight compared with placebo, while subsequent larger trials further supported beneficial effects across additional cardiometabolic outcomes. However, the literature remains difficult to interpret clinically because the magnitude of benefit varies with dose, follow-up duration, and reported endpoint, and the safety profile, particularly gastrointestinal adverse events, hypoglycemia, and treatment discontinuation, requires a balanced evaluation. Although a previous meta-analysis suggested favorable dose-dependent metabolic effects, the evidence base has expanded with newer randomized data and still requires an updated synthesis with formal certainty assessment focused on patients with coexisting obesity and T2DM [18–20]. Therefore, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials of orforglipron in obese patients with T2DM, using the GRADE approach to assess the certainty of the evidence.

Methods

Protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [21] (Supplementary Table 1) and the methodological framework proposed by the Cochrane Handbook [22]. The protocol has been registered prospectively in PROSPERO under the identifier CRD420261298078.

Data sources & search strategy

Four biomedical databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL)) were systematically searched through 20 January 2026. The electronic database searches were conducted independently by two reviewers (A.M. and A.E.) using the same prespecified search strategy, and any discrepancies were resolved by discussion. We created the search terms to extract studies that evaluated the efficacy and safety of orforglipron in obesity with type 2 Diabetes Mellitus using a mixture of free-text keywords and MeSH terms such as “Orforglipron”, “Obesity”, “type 2 Diabetes Mellitus”, and “Randomized Controlled Trial”. The entire search syntax of each database is listed in Supplementary Table 2. Manual backward and forward citation analyses were done for all the references of the included studies.

Eligibility criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English in peer-reviewed journals. This language restriction was applied for feasibility and to ensure consistent full-text screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment. Eligible studies then followed the following PICO criteria: population (P): Adults patients ≥ 18 years with obesity (defined as BMI ≥ 23.0 kg/m²) and type 2 DM (defined as HbA1c ≥ 7.0%); intervention (I): Orforglipron, administered once daily (across various doses including 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 45 mg doses), as an adjunct to the patient’s background oral antihyperglycemic medications such as metformin; control (C) Matching placebo, as an adjunct to the oral antihyperglycemic medications; and outcomes (O): Efficacy outcomes included absolute change from baseline in waist circumference (cm), percentage change from baseline in body weight, absolute change from baseline in BMI (kg/m²), HbA1c, and fasting serum glucose (mg/dL), percentage change from baseline in total serum cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides, and absolute change from baseline in SBP, DBP, and HR. Safety outcomes included any Adverse Events (AEs), serious AEs, AEs leading to discontinuation of medication, death, acute pancreatitis, incidence of hypoglycemia events, diabetic retinopathy, Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE), and rescue therapy for severe persistent hyperglycemia. All outcomes were calculated at the last follow-up period.

Exclusion criteria were non-randomized or quasi-randomized trials, narrative reviews, books, theses, observational studies (cohort or case-control), abstracts, protocols, case reports, animal studies, studies enrolled participants with obesity without concomitant T2DM, participants with T2DM without obesity, healthy volunteers, or were early-phase exploratory/pharmacokinetic trials not designed to answer the present focused clinical question, and trials with insufficient data on outcomes to extract.

Because all eligible studies were randomized, placebo-controlled trials addressing the same prespecified population, intervention, comparator, and outcomes, quantitative pooling was retained despite the limited number of studies; however, all pooled estimates were interpreted cautiously.

Study selection

The screening process was carried out in two stages. First, four author reviewers (O.F.A., H.AA., M.E., and M.A.D) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts to assess their relevance. Next, the full texts of the selected articles were examined in detail to determine final eligibility for inclusion in the quantitative analysis. Discrepancies were resolved by adjudication with a third reviewer (A.E.). The screening was conducted using the Rayyan platform [23]. To assess screening reliability, we calculated the percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient between the primary reviewers before adjudication at the title/abstract screening stage. The initial agreement was 87%, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.72, indicating substantial agreement. A total of 54 records were initially discordant and were resolved through discussion and adjudication by a third reviewer (A.E.)

Data extraction

We conducted a pilot extraction after retrieving the complete texts of relevant papers in order to prepare the data extraction sheet appropriately. The data extraction sheet, which is structured in Excel (Microsoft, USA), is divided into three sections. The first part included the summary characteristics of the included studies (study ID, trial name, design, phase, blinding, register number, location, number of centers, study period, sample size, study arms, inclusion criteria, primary outcomes, and follow-up duration). The second part included the baseline information of the participants (total group participants, age, sex (male), race, BMI, weight, HbA1C, %, fasting serum glucose, duration of DM in years, eGFR, triglycerides level, HDL-C, LDL-C, non-HDL-C, SBP, DBP, HR, and metformin uses). Finally, the third part included outcomes data as previously described. Independently, four reviewers (O.F.A., H.AA., M.E., and M.A.D), at least two for each trial and item, were responsible for data extraction. Any disputes between extractors are resolved by A.E consultation.

Quality assessment

Quality for each of the included trials was assessed with the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB) 2.0 tool [24] that accounts for five risk domains: randomization process, deviations from planned interventions, completeness of outcomes data, outcome measurement, and selection of the result that is reported. Each of the five domains received the rating of “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk”. All ratings were conducted independently by two reviewers (O.F.A., and M.A.D), with conflicts resolved by consultation.

In addition, M.A.A. used the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) framework to assess the overall certainty of evidence for each outcome [25]. This approach considers ROB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Certainty ratings were categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low, and were summarized in a GRADE evidence profile.

Pairwise meta-analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using RevMan software version 5.4. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for binary outcomes, and mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were used for continuous outcomes. For continuous efficacy outcomes, the metric pool depended on the prespecified endpoint. Percentage change from baseline was used for body weight and lipid outcomes (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides), whereas absolute change from baseline was used for waist circumference, BMI, HbA1c, fasting serum glucose, SBP, DBP, and HR, as reported in the original trials.

For lipid outcomes, percentage change from baseline was extracted directly when reported. When percentage change was not reported, it was derived from the available baseline and follow-up means using the formula: ((follow − up mean−baseline mean)/baseline mean) ×100. If the corresponding SD of the change was not directly reported, it was estimated from the available standard error, 95% confidence interval, or p-value using standard methods recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. When neither the mean change nor a dispersion measure could be derived from the published report, the outcome was not included in the quantitative synthesis [22]. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of < 0.05. For trials with zero-event cells, a continuity correction of 0.5 was used to enable effect size calculation. The random effect model was used in all measured outcomes as it accommodates a more significant standard error in the pooled estimate, making it suitable for inconsistent or controversial estimates. All pooled analyses were performed using a random-effects model in Review Manager (RevMan), with between-study variance (τ²) estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird method.

Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed because only three studies were eligible for inclusion. Under these circumstances, funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test are not considered reliable or informative, as such methods have very limited power and may produce misleading results when fewer than ten studies are available [26].

Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic and the chi-square (χ²) test. The I² values were interpreted according to the Cochrane Handbook [1] as follows: 0–30% (not important), 30–50% (moderate), 50–75% (substantial), and 75–100% (considerable). A p-value < 0.1 from the χ² test was considered indicative of statistically significant heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis and subgrouping

We conducted sensitivity analyses using a leave-one-out technique to assess the impact of individual studies on the overall estimates. We performed subgroup analyses by Orforglipron doses: (3, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 45 mg).

Clinical relevance assessment

To determine whether statistically significant findings were clinically meaningful, we compared pooled dose-specific estimates against published minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for each outcome. MCID thresholds were derived from established clinical guidelines and consensus statements, including the American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care 2024, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) lipid and hypertension guidelines, the European Society of Cardiology/European Society of Hypertension (ESC/ESH) 2023 guidelines, and FDA/EMA obesity treatment benchmarks. Findings were classified as clinically meaningful if the pooled effect met or exceeded the corresponding MCID threshold.

Results

Search results

Our search identified 612 records through four databases. After removing 200 duplicate records, 412 unique records were screened; of these, 10 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility following title and abstract screening. After full-text review, seven studies were excluded as detailed in Supplementary Table 3, three RCTs were included in the meta-analysis [18–20]. Characteristics of the included studies

Our study encompassed three RCTs offering a comprehensive evaluation of the safety and efficacy of orforglipron in patients with obesity and T2DM. The studies were multi-center and conducted across diverse geographic regions, including North America, South America, Europe, and Asia. All trials were phase 2 and phase 3. All trials were double blinded. The orforglipron dose ranged from 3 to 45 mg once daily. The follow-up duration ranged from 10 months up to 17.5 months. Risk of bias

The risk of bias in the included RCTs was assessed using the ROB-2 tool. Across the three included trials, two trials were judged to have a low risk of bias across all five domains. This was primarily due to the robust methodological quality of the trials: both were double-blinded and employed objective outcome measures, thereby minimizing the potential for performance and detection bias. While only one trial (Frias et al. 2023) was judged to have a high risk of bias due to missing outcome data (Fig. 2).

Efficacy outcomes

Change in waist circumference (cm)

It was reported in all three included trials. Orforglipron demonstrated a clear dose–response pattern for waist circumference reduction versus placebo, with no significant effect at 3 mg (MD − 1.53 cm; 95% CI − 4.32 to 1.26, I² = 80%; P = 0.28) and progressively larger, statistically significant reductions at doses ≥ 6 mg, including 6 mg (MD − 2.60 cm; 95% CI − 3.65 to − 1.55; P < 0.00001), 12 mg (MD − 3.01 cm; 95% CI − 3.13 to − 2.88, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), 24 mg (MD − 5.90 cm; 95% CI − 8.56 to − 3.24; P < 0.0001), 36 mg (MD − 5.69 cm; 95% CI − 5.82 to − 5.57, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), and 45 mg (MD − 4.40 cm; 95% CI − 6.80 to − 2.00; P = 0.0003) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of change in waist circumference (cm). MD: Mean Difference, CI: Confidence Interval.

Percentage change in body weight

It was reported in all three included trials. Orforglipron demonstrated a clear dose–response pattern for percentage body weight reduction versus placebo, with significant weight loss observed at all doses, including 3 mg (MD − 2.43%; 95% CI − 3.58 to − 1.27, I² = 8%; P < 0.0001), 6 mg (MD − 2.60%; 95% CI − 3.65 to − 1.55; P < 0.00001), 12 mg (MD − 4.37%; 95% CI − 5.11 to − 3.63, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), 24 mg (MD − 7.80%; 95% CI − 9.96 to − 5.64; P < 0.00001), 36 mg (MD − 6.80%; 95% CI − 7.61 to − 6.00, I² = 3%; P < 0.00001), and 45 mg (MD − 7.40%; 95% CI − 9.38 to − 5.42; P < 0.00001) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of the percentage Change in the body weight. MD: Mean Difference, CI: Confidence Interval.

Change in BMI (Kg/m2)

It was reported in all three included trials. Orforglipron demonstrated a clear dose–response pattern for BMI reduction versus placebo, with significant reductions at all doses, including 3 mg (MD − 0.88 kg/m²; 95% CI − 1.45 to − 0.31, I² = 47%; P = 0.002), 6 mg (MD − 0.90 kg/m²; 95% CI − 1.26 to − 0.54; P < 0.00001), 12 mg (MD − 1.56 kg/m²; 95% CI − 1.81 to − 1.31, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), 24 mg (MD − 2.60 kg/m²; 95% CI − 3.38 to − 1.82; P < 0.00001), 36 mg (MD − 2.35 kg/m²; 95% CI − 2.64 to − 2.06, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), and 45 mg (MD − 2.70 kg/m²; 95% CI − 3.41 to − 1.99; P < 0.00001) (Fig. 5).

Change in fasting serum glucose in mg/dl

It was reported in all included trials. Overall, orforglipron significantly reduced fasting serum glucose versus placebo in a clear dose–response manner, with significant reductions at all doses, including 3 mg (MD − 20.62 mg/dL; 95% CI − 28.07 to − 13.16, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), 6 mg (MD − 21.20 mg/dL; 95% CI − 27.19 to − 15.21; P < 0.00001), 12 mg (MD − 31.66 mg/dL; 95% CI − 39.81 to − 23.50, I² = 68%; P < 0.00001), 24 mg (MD − 41.10 mg/dL; 95% CI − 52.76 to − 29.44; P < 0.00001), 36 mg (MD − 33.05 mg/dL; 95% CI − 42.00 to − 24.10, I² = 70%; P < 0.00001), and 45 mg (MD − 44.80 mg/dL; 95% CI − 55.31 to − 34.29; P < 0.00001) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Change in HbA1c

It was reported in all included trials. Orforglipron significantly reduced HbA1c compared with placebo in a dose-dependent manner across all included trials. The reduction was observed at all doses: 3 mg: MD − 0.80% (95% CI − 1.03 to − 0.58, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), 6 mg: MD − 0.75% (95% CI − 0.93 to − 0.57; P < 0.00001), 12 mg: MD − 1.15% (95% CI − 1.39 to − 0.91, I² = 60%; P < 0.00001), 24 mg: MD − 1.36% (95% CI − 1.74 to − 0.98; P < 0.00001), 36 mg: MD − 1.27% (95% CI − 1.53 to − 1.01, I² = 61%; P < 0.00001), 45 mg: MD − 1.67% (95% CI − 2.02 to − 1.32; P < 0.00001) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Percentage change in LDL cholesterol

It was reported in all included trials. Dose-specific analyses showed no significant LDL-C reduction at 3 mg (MD − 10.43%; 95% CI − 24.39 to 3.52, I² = 71%; P = 0.14), 6 mg (MD 3.40%; 95% CI − 1.72 to 8.52; P = 0.19), or 12 mg (MD − 1.43%; 95% CI − 5.07 to 2.20, I² = 0%; P = 0.44). Higher doses demonstrated greater LDL-C lowering effects, with a non-significant reduction at 24 mg (MD − 13.30%; 95% CI − 27.17 to 0.57; P = 0.06) and a 36 mg dose (MD − 5.61%; 95% CI − 12.77 to 1.55, I² = 64%; P = 0.12). The 45 mg showed a statistically significant reduction (MD − 13.80%; 95% CI − 26.75 to − 0.85; P = 0.04) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Percentage change in HDL cholesterol

It was reported in all included trials. Dose-specific analysis showed that the 6 mg dose showed a statistically significant increase (MD 3.90; 95% CI 1.49 to 6.31, I² = 76%; P = 0.002). The 12 mg dose demonstrated a borderline significant increase (MD 2.74; 95% CI 0.02 to 5.46; P = 0.05), while the 36 mg dose also showed a statistically significant increase (MD 4.48; 95% CI 0.10 to 8.86, I² = 72%; P = 0.04). In contrast, the 3 mg (MD − 0.63; 95% CI − 7.67 to 6.42, I² = 76%; P = 0.86), 24 mg (MD 1.20; 95% CI − 4.46 to 6.86; P = 0.68), and 45 mg (MD − 0.60; 95% CI − 5.83 to 4.63; P = 0.82) doses showed no significant effect on HDL-C compared with placebo (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Percentage change in total serum cholesterol

It was reported across three included trials. At lower doses, the effect was not significant with 3 mg (MD − 6.51%; 95% CI − 18.21 to 5.19, I² = 86%; P = 0.28) and 6 mg (MD − 0.70%; 95% CI − 3.03 to 1.63; P = 0.56), while the 12 mg dose showed a non-significant trend toward reduction (MD − 2.13%; 95% CI − 4.45 to 0.19, I² = 0%; P = 0.07). In contrast, higher doses demonstrated significant cholesterol lowering, including 24 mg (MD − 9.80%; 95% CI − 17.62 to − 1.98; P = 0.01), 36 mg (MD − 5.62%; 95% CI − 10.48 to − 0.75, I² = 72%; P = 0.02), and 45 mg, which produced the greatest effect (MD − 12.60%; 95% CI − 19.82 to − 5.38; P = 0.0006) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Percentage change in triglycerides

It was reported across three included trials. At the 3 mg dose, no significant reduction was observed (MD − 4.18%; 95% CI − 11.72 to 3.37, I² = 0%; P = 0.28). In contrast, significant triglyceride lowering was statistically significant at 6 mg (MD − 11.10%; 95% CI − 16.24 to − 5.96; P < 0.0001) and 12 mg (MD − 11.62%; 95% CI − 15.80 to − 7.43, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), with consistent effects and no heterogeneity. The 24 mg dose did not reach statistical significance (MD − 12.10%; 95% CI − 26.39 to 2.19; P = 0.10). Higher doses demonstrated robust and significant reductions, including 36 mg (MD − 15.44%; 95% CI − 20.06 to − 10.82, I² = 13%; P < 0.00001) and 45 mg, which showed the greatest effect (MD − 20.70%; 95% CI − 33.56 to − 7.84; P = 0.002) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Change in SBP

It was reported across three included trials. At the 3 mg dose, the reduction was borderline and did not reach statistical significance (MD − 2.24 mmHg; 95% CI − 4.50 to 0.03, I² = 0%; P = 0.05). Significant reductions in SBP were observed with 6 mg (MD − 2.70 mmHg; 95% CI − 4.47 to − 0.93; P = 0.003) and 12 mg (MD − 3.25 mmHg; 95% CI − 4.63 to − 1.86, I² = 0%; P < 0.00001). The 24 mg dose showed no significant effect (MD − 1.20 mmHg; 95% CI − 6.01 to 3.61; P = 0.63). In contrast, the 36 mg dose demonstrated a significant reduction (MD − 4.29 mmHg; 95% CI − 6.00 to − 2.58, I² = 10%; P < 0.00001), whereas the 45 mg dose did not achieve statistical significance (MD − 3.20 mmHg; 95% CI − 7.62 to 1.22; P = 0.16) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Change in DBP

It was reported across three included trials. Subgroup analysis showed no significant effect of orforglipron on this outcome across all dose levels. The 3 mg dose yielded an MD of − 0.12 (95% CI − 1.30 to 1.06; I² = 0%; P = 0.84). The 6 mg dose showed an MD of 0.00 (95% CI − 1.13 to 1.13; P = 1.00). For the 12 mg dose, the MD was − 0.10 (95% CI − 1.03 to 0.82; I² = 0%; P = 0.82). The 24 mg dose demonstrated an MD of − 0.50 (95% CI − 3.37 to 2.37; P = 0.73). The 36 mg dose showed an MD of − 0.25 (95% CI − 1.08 to 0.58; I² = 0%; P = 0.56), while the 45 mg dose yielded an MD of 0.20 (95% CI − 2.42 to 2.82; P = 0.88) (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Change in heart rate

Across individual doses, HR increases were consistently statistically significant, including 3 mg (MD 3.37 bpm, 95% CI 1.77 to 4.98; I² = 0%; P < 0.0001), 6 mg (MD 3.00 bpm, 95% CI 1.86 to 4.14; P < 0.00001), 12 mg (MD 4.78 bpm, 95% CI 3.23 to 6.34; I² = 51%; P < 0.00001), 24 mg (MD 5.40 bpm, 95% CI 2.00 to 8.80; P = 0.002), 36 mg (MD 5.35 bpm, 95% CI 3.66 to 7.04; I² = 54%; P < 0.00001), and 45 mg (MD 7.20 bpm, 95% CI 4.05 to 10.35; P < 0.00001) (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Safety outcomes

Any adverse events

Dose-specific analyses demonstrated no statistically significant differences versus placebo at 3 mg (RR 1.01; 95% CI 0.87 to 1.16; I² = 13%; P = 0.92), 6 mg (RR 0.95; 95% CI 0.89 to 1.01; P = 0.08), 12 mg (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.20; I² = 57%; P = 0.21), 24 mg (RR 1.20; 95% CI 0.92 to 1.57; P = 0.17), and 36 mg (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.20; I² = 55%; P = 0.14), whereas the 45 mg dose was associated with a statistically significant increase in adverse events (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.64; P = 0.04) (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Adverse events leading to drug discontinuation

Compared with placebo, orforglipron was associated with increased risks of adverse events leading to drug discontinuation across doses, reaching statistical significance at 3 mg (RR 2.77, 95% CI 1.01 to 7.57; ; I² = 0%; P = 0.05), 12 mg (RR 2.37, 95% CI 1.55 to 3.64; ; I² = 0%; P < 0.0001), and 36 mg (RR 2.51, 95% CI 1.64 to 3.84; I² = 0%; P < 0.0001), while non-significant but elevated risks were observed at 6 mg (RR 1.32, 95% CI 0.76 to 2.30; P = 0.33), 24 mg (RR 3.51, 95% CI 1.01 to 12.22; ; P = 0.05), and 45 mg (RR 2.33, 95% CI 0.65 to 8.35; P = 0.19) (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Serious adverse event

No significant differences in serious adverse events were observed across dose levels, including 3 mg (RR 1.37; 95% CI 0.56 to 3.35; I² = 0%; P = 0.49), 6 mg (RR 0.84; 95% CI 0.53 to 1.32; P = 0.44), 12 mg (RR 1.15; 95% CI 0.79 to 1.68; I² = 0%; P = 0.45), 24 mg (RR 1.95; 95% CI 0.49 to 7.73; P = 0.34), 36 mg (RR 1.16; 95% CI 0.80 to 1.68; I² = 0%; P = 0.44), and 45 mg (RR 0.29; 95% CI 0.03 to 2.72; P = 0.28) (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Incidence of diabetic retinopathy

No significant differences in diabetic retinopathy incidence were observed across dose levels, including 3 mg (RR 0.80; 95% CI 0.25 to 2.57; P = 0.71), 6 mg (RR 1.02; 95% CI 0.63 to 1.67; P = 0.92), 12 mg (RR 0.76; 95% CI 0.29 to 1.98; I² = 42%; P = 0.57), and 36 mg (RR 1.31; 95% CI 0.56 to 2.64; I² = 48%; P = 0.46) (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Rescue therapy for severe persistent hyperglycemia

Dose-specific analyses showed statistically significant reductions at most doses, including 3 mg (RR 0.28; 95% CI 0.15 to 0.52; I² = 0%; P < 0.0001), 6 mg (RR 0.27; 95% CI 0.20 to 0.38; P < 0.00001), 12 mg (RR 0.18; 95% CI 0.13 to 0.26; I² = 0%; P < 0.00001), and 36 mg (RR 0.19; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.37; I² = 36%; P < 0.0001). In contrast, 24 mg did not show a statistically significant difference versus placebo (RR 0.50; 95% CI 0.14 to 1.83; P = 0.30). The 45 mg dose demonstrated a borderline but statistically significant reduction (RR 0.12; 95% CI 0.02 to 0.98; P = 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Incidence of hypoglycemia

Dose-specific analyses showed that most dose groups did not individually reach statistical significance, including 3 mg (RR 1.93; 95% CI 0.18 to 21.04; I² = 0%; P = 0.59), 6 mg (RR 4.79; 95% CI 0.93 to 24.54; P = 0.06), 12 mg (RR 2.09; 95% CI 0.12 to 36.47; I² = 63%; P = 0.61), 24 mg (RR 3.50; 95% CI 0.15 to 83.94; P = 0.44), and 45 mg (RR 2.63; 95% CI 0.11 to 63.15; P = 0.55). In contrast, the 36 mg dose was associated with a statistically significant increase in hypoglycemia risk (RR 4.38; 95% CI 1.27 to 15.14; I² = 0%; P = 0.02) (Supplementary Fig. 15).

Incidence of acute pancreatitis

No significant differences in acute pancreatitis incidence were observed across dose levels, including 3 mg (RR 1.02; 95% CI 0.06 to 16.16; I² = 0%; P = 0.98), 6 mg (RR 0.38; 95% CI 0.02 to 7.95; P > 0.05), 12 mg (RR 0.97; 95% CI 0.16 to 5.92; I² = 0%; P = 0.97), 24 mg (RR 3.51; 95% CI 0.15 to 84.04; P > 0.05), 36 mg (RR 0.63; 95% CI 0.08 to 4.87; I² = 0%; P = 0.65), and 45 mg (RR 0.87; 95% CI 0.02 to 43.32; P > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 16).

MACE

No significant differences in the incidence of MACE were observed across dose levels, including 3 mg (RR 0.97; 95% CI 0.02 to 48.30; P > 0.05), 6 mg (RR 0.21; 95% CI 0.03 to 1.67; P > 0.05), 12 mg (RR 1.64; 95% CI 0.66 to 4.10; I² = 0%; P = 0.29), and 36 mg (RR 1.48; 95% CI 0.57 to 3.83; I² = 0%; P = 0.41) (Supplementary Fig. 17).

Death

No significant differences in death incidence were observed across dose levels, including 3 mg (RR 1.05; 95% CI 0.16 to 7.10; I² = 0%; P = 0.95), 6 mg (RR 0.21; 95% CI 0.01 to 3.94; P > 0.05), 12 mg (RR 1.35; 95% CI 0.43 to 4.27; I² = 0%; P = 0.60), 24 mg (RR 0.39; 95% CI 0.02 to 9.34; P > 0.05), 36 mg (RR 0.65; 95% CI 0.17 to 2.51; I² = 0%; P = 0.53), and 45 mg (RR 0.29; 95% CI 0.01 to 7.01; P > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Clinical relevance: minimal clinically important differences

Statistical significance does not inherently imply clinical meaningfulness. To contextualize our findings, we compared each pooled dose-specific estimate against published MCID thresholds for each outcome. For body weight, the MCID is conventionally defined as a reduction of ≥ 5% from baseline, a threshold associated with clinically meaningful improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors. This threshold was met or exceeded at doses of 24 mg (MD − 7.80%), 36 mg (MD − 6.80%), and 45 mg (MD − 7.40%), while lower doses (3–12 mg) did not cross this threshold. For HbA1c, the MCID of ≥ 0.5% reduction was surpassed at all doses, ranging from MD − 0.75% at 6 mg to MD − 1.67% at 45 mg, indicating consistent glycemic benefit across the entire dose range. Waist circumference reductions exceeded the MCID of ≥ 4 cm at doses of 24 mg (MD − 5.90 cm) and 36 mg (MD − 5.69 cm). For SBP, reductions at most doses fell below the MCID of ≥ 5 mmHg, suggesting that while statistically significant, the observed blood pressure effects may not be independently clinically meaningful in isolation. Regarding HR, the observed increases across all doses (range: +3.00 to + 7.20 bpm) remained below the threshold of ≥ 10 bpm generally considered clinically significant, and no dose approached arrhythmia-relevant levels. BMI reductions met or exceeded the MCID of ≥ 1.0 kg/m² at doses of 12 mg and above. These findings indicate that orforglipron achieves clinically meaningful thresholds for glycemic control and weight reduction at therapeutic doses (≥ 12 mg), while blood pressure and HR effects, though statistically significant, remain below established clinically important thresholds. GRADE assessment

The overall findings of the GRADE assessment are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. The certainty of evidence for efficacy outcomes (BMI, percentage body weight, waist circumference, and HbA1c) was rated as low, primarily due to high risk of bias in one contributing trial and substantial inconsistency across studies (I² ≥ 50%). For safety outcomes, the certainty of evidence for serious AEs was rated as low, downgraded for risk of bias and imprecision, as the CI included both potential benefit and harm. The certainty of evidence for hypoglycemia was rated as moderate. Evidence for AEs leading to treatment discontinuation was also rated as moderate, reflecting greater confidence in this estimate compared with other safety outcomes.

Discussion

This meta-analysis of three RCTs provides a focused evaluation of the efficacy and safety of orforglipron, an oral non-peptide GLP-1 receptor agonist, specifically in obese patients with T2DM. Our pooled results demonstrate that orforglipron induces significant, dose-dependent improvements across a broad spectrum of cardiometabolic parameters, including glycemic control, body weight, adiposity measures, lipid profiles, and systolic blood pressure, underscoring its potential role in obesity and metabolic regulation. These benefits, however, are accompanied by an increased risk of treatment discontinuation due to adverse events and a dose-dependent increase in heart rate.

Orforglipron produced clinically meaningful reductions in body weight, BMI, and waist circumference compared to placebo [27–29]. The clear dose-response relationship observed for these anthropometric measures underscores the agent’s potency, with higher doses 24 mg, 36 mg, 45 mg yielding the most substantial effects [29]. The reduction in waist circumference is particularly noteworthy, as central adiposity is a key driver of insulin resistance and cardiovascular risk [17]. These findings indicate that orforglipron may provide benefits beyond weight loss, possibly through preferential targeting of visceral adipose tissue deposition. The magnitude of weight loss, while significant, appears slightly more modest in this diabetic population compared to effects sometimes reported in mixed or non-diabetic obese cohorts, a phenomenon consistent with the known pathophysiology of T2DM, which can impair weight loss responses [30]. Despite being more modest than the weight loss observed with injectable GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide and tirzepatide (semaglutide 15–17%; tirzepatide 15–22%), these reductions remain clinically relevant and align with outcomes seen with other oral anti-obesity treatments [31, 32].

The glycemic efficacy of orforglipron was robust. The overall reduction in HbA1c and in fasting plasma glucose is clinically significant, aligning with thresholds known to reduce microvascular complications [27, 29]. The effect on HbA1c exhibited a pronounced dose-response, with the 45 mg dose achieving a reduction of -1.67% [29]. This potent glucose-lowering effect was further corroborated by a markedly reduced need for rescue therapy for severe hyperglycemia [27]. These findings position orforglipron as a highly effective oral agent for glycemic management in T2DM, with efficacy comparable to or exceeding that of some injectable GLP-1 receptor agonists oral semaglutide (HbA1c reduction of 0.9%–1.4%) in similar populations [33, 34]. These glucose-lowering effects arise from several complementary actions, including improved glucose-dependent insulin release, reduced inappropriate glucagon secretion, slower gastric emptying, and enhanced insulin sensitivity [35].

Beyond glycemia and weight, orforglipron demonstrated favorable effects on cardiovascular risk markers. It significantly improved the lipid profile, reducing total cholesterol, triglycerides, and increasing HDL-C, with higher doses generally producing more pronounced effects [29]. The reduction in SBP, is also a meaningful benefit, contributing to overall cardiovascular risk mitigation [36]. These pleiotropic effects are consistent with the class effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists and suggest potential for cardiovascular benefit, although our analysis found no significant difference in MACE incidence, likely due to the relatively short duration and limited power of the included trials to assess this hard endpoint [37, 38].

The safety profile reveals important considerations. While the overall risk of any AEs was not significantly increased except at the 45 mg dose, the risk of AEs leading to drug discontinuation was more than twofold higher with orforglipron [28, 29]. This highlights tolerability as a key challenge, likely driven by gastrointestinal effects common to the drug class [36]. A notable finding was the consistent, dose-dependent increase in HR, a known effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists whose long-term clinical significance requires further study [39]. The increased incidence of hypoglycemia warrants careful patient selection and monitoring, particularly when used with insulin or insulin secretagogues [40]. Reassuringly, risks of serious AEs, mortality, acute pancreatitis, and diabetic retinopathy were not significantly different from placebo [36]. This interpretation is supported by a recent frequentist network meta-analysis focused specifically on gastrointestinal, hepatic, and pancreatic safety, which demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in gastrointestinal adverse events and treatment discontinuation with orforglipron, without a corresponding increase in pancreatitis or evidence of clinical hepatic or pancreatic injury [41].

Indeed, the majority of prior GLP-1 receptor agonist systematic reviews and meta-analyses have focused on injectable agents or combined oral and injectable formulations across very heterogeneous patient populations [34, 37, 42]. In contrast, this study specifically examines orforglipron and uniquely narrows inclusion to patients with both obesity and T2DM, a subgroup truly at high cardiometabolic risk.

Existing evidence has shown that orforglipron improves body weight and glycemic outcomes, and previous reviews have generally supported a favorable efficacy signal [13, 18–20]. However, much of the literature has addressed broader populations or more heterogeneous treatment settings [13]. In contrast, the present review was specifically designed to evaluate adults with concomitant obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus, a subgroup with particularly high cardiometabolic risk.

Within this focused population, our findings were broadly consistent with the existing literature. Orforglipron significantly improved body weight, BMI, waist circumference, HbA1c, fasting serum glucose, and several cardiometabolic markers, while also reducing the need for rescue therapy for severe persistent hyperglycemia [27–29]. At the same time, treatment discontinuation due to AEs, hypoglycemia, and heart rate increase remained important safety considerations [28, 29, 36].

The clinically meaningful contribution of this review is therefore not simply that orforglipron appears effective, but that this efficacy remains evident in the specific subgroup of adults with both obesity and T2DM, where simultaneous improvement in weight and glycemic control is especially important [12, 30–34]. This focused interpretation complements the broader existing literature while also highlighting that the current evidence base remains preliminary and should be interpreted cautiously.

Moreover, compared to previous SRMAs on the early-phase effect-data pattern for the orally active GLP-1 receptor agonists, the current analysis has the advantage of including additional information regarding the effect on body weight, the cardiometabolic profile, and a wide array of safety variables, including hypoglycemia, change in HR, and treatment discontinuation. Moreover, the consistent dose-response relationship between the different variables for effectiveness and security only adds further biological validity to our analysis. Finally, Tantoush et al. [43] reported a frequentist network meta-analysis that included six randomized trials and used indirect comparisons to evaluate dose-specific effects across a broader population spectrum. In contrast, the present study was restricted to adults with concomitant obesity and T2DM, and used direct pairwise placebo-controlled meta-analysis to answer a narrower clinical question. Accordingly, the two reviews should be viewed as complementary; the network meta-analysis is useful for dose ranking across broader populations, whereas our review provides a focused estimate of efficacy and safety in the specific subgroup of obese adults with T2DM and additionally evaluates certainty of evidence using GRADE.

Some limitations should be highlighted. Firstly, there were only three randomized controlled studies available to be included. Accordingly, the pooled estimates should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution, particularly for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity and for key efficacy outcomes rated as low certainty by GRADE. Secondly, there was significant heterogeneity identified for some of the efficacy measures. This may be attributed to the varying designs of the studies, dose escalation, antidiabetic agent backgrounds, and varying periods of the studies. Thirdly, the absence of head-to-head studies with injectable GLP-1 RA agents also makes it difficult to draw definitive efficacy and tolerability profiles. Also, the period of the studies was too short to define the long-term effects on the cardiovascular and kidney systems. In addition, grey literature was not included; therefore, although our pooled estimates were based on peer-reviewed full-text trials, the possibility of publication bias due to unpublished negative or inconclusive studies cannot be completely excluded. Moreover, because only three trials were included, publication bias could not be assessed formally using funnel plots or regression-based methods, and therefore, the possibility of small-study effects or unpublished negative studies cannot be completely excluded. Finally, we restricted inclusion to English-language publications, which may have introduced language bias and may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages, thereby potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the review. Subgroup analyses based on clinically relevant participant characteristics (including age, sex, duration of disease, baseline BMI, and disease severity) were not feasible due to inconsistent reporting of stratified outcome data across the included trials and the limited number of studies, which precluded reliable meta-regression or more granular subgroup analyses. Consequently, dose was the only variable suitable for subgroup evaluation. Additionally, MCID thresholds for some outcomes (e.g., waist circumference, fasting serum glucose) are not universally standardized, and our threshold selections, while guideline-based, involve an element of clinical judgment.

Despite these limitations, however, this study has some notable advantages. First, this study is considered to be the first meta-analysis study focused specifically and entirely on coexisting patients with obesity and T2DM who have utilized orforglipron, including all available doses, with the first time to incorporate the 6 mg dose. Second, only high-quality and double-blind randomized controlled studies were considered. The findings also followed a dose-response pattern, most notably for all study outcomes. Third, the trials were conducted worldwide, including in North America (the United States and Canada), Europe, and several additional countries. All included trials were phase 2 and phase 3, increasing the external validity and generalizability of the findings. The trials showed superior methodological quality, including double-blinding and objective outcome measures, thereby minimizing the risk of performance and detection bias. In addition, the GRADE approach was used to rate the certainty of the evidence, strengthening the robustness and interpretability of the results. Finally, our study included the largest new trial, ATTAIN-2, with the largest sample size.

Poor adherence to injectable therapies remains a major barrier in the long-term management of obesity and T2DM [44]. The availability of an effective oral GLP-1 receptor agonist such as orforglipron may substantially improve treatment acceptance, adherence, and persistence, while maintaining efficacy comparable to injectable agents [33, 34]. This represents a potentially important advance in the therapeutic armamentarium for cardiometabolic disease.

Current clinical guidelines already emphasize the central role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in the management of both obesity and T2DM, particularly in patients with high cardiometabolic risk [17, 30]. The emergence of an oral agent with robust efficacy may further expand the reach and impact of this drug class in real-world clinical practice.

Future large-scale, long-term randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate the durability of weight loss, long-term safety, and cardiovascular outcomes associated with orforglipron. Head-to-head comparisons with established injectable GLP-1 receptor agonists will also be important to clarify its relative efficacy, tolerability, and cost-effectiveness in different patient populations.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that orforglipron may improve body weight, glycemic control, and selected cardiometabolic risk factors in adults with concomitant obesity and T2DM, while its tolerability profile warrants careful consideration. However, because only three randomized trials were eligible for quantitative synthesis, and the certainty of evidence for key efficacy outcomes was low, these findings should be regarded as preliminary rather than definitive. Larger and longer-term randomized trials are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn regarding the overall benefit-risk profile and clinical positioning of orforglipron.
