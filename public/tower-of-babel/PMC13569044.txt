Water Droplet Hammer: Piezoelectric Sensing of Ultrafast Mechanical Transients During Droplet Impact
Authors: Ian Kim, Chen Lin, Zuer Wu, Siyuan Rao, Qianbin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTErosion and fracture caused by repeated water‐droplet impacts are often attributed to splash‐mediated material removal, high‐pressure impulsive loading, or cavitation. Yet, the mechanical response during the earliest instant of impact remains difficult to capture. Using a high‐temporal‐resolution polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) piezoelectric sensor combined with high‐speed imaging, we directly measure substrate‐coupled mechanical transients during a single water‐droplet impact. A strong impulsive strain response appears within the first 2 ms after contact, followed by coherent 100–500 Hz oscillations at the water–solid interface during the first 20 ms, before any rebound occurs. The oscillation frequency is modulated by an effective interfacial stiffness, whereas the impulsive peak‐to‐peak response scales with droplet size and impact velocity. The experimentally captured signals provide direct experimental evidence of ultrafast substrate‐coupled mechanical transients during droplet impact and establish a minimal inertial‐capillary framework for understanding mechanical signatures that may contribute to low‐ to moderate‐speed droplet‐induced erosion and fatigue.Keywords: droplet impact, interfacial oscillation, PVDF piezoelectric sensing, substrate‐coupled strain, superhydrophobic surface

Introduction

Droplet impacts have long been recognized as a source of material erosion and fracture [1, 2, 3]. The idea that persistent water droplets can wear down even the hardest surfaces appears in cultural observations dating back thousands of years, including the ancient Chinese proverb “drops of water wear holes in stone” and Ovid's remark that “dripping water hollows out stone” (Figure 1A). Modern studies typically attribute droplet‐induced erosion to splash erosion, high‐pressure impulsive loading, or cavitation, yet the mechanical events occurring at the moment of impact remain incompletely resolved [4, 5, 6].

When a liquid droplet impacts a solid surface, high‐speed imaging reveals rapid deformation, spreading, and either rebound or deposition on timescales of several to tens of milliseconds [5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Imaging from a high‐speed camera provides rich morphological information from multiple viewing angles [12, 13], but it does not directly measure substrate loading, and it lacks an intrinsic temporal reference for interfacial stress [14, 15]. Manual synchronization based on frame rate limits the ability to resolve microsecond‐to‐millisecond mechanical transients. Direct force measurements have revealed a secondary force spike during water droplet impact on superhydrophobic surfaces before rebound [16, 17, 18], indicating complex early‐stage dynamics, but the detailed temporal structure of this loading has not been directly resolved.

Results

Piezoelectric Sensing Reveals a Two‐Stage Substrate‐Coupled Response to Droplet Impact

We developed a high temporal resolution strain‐sensing platform based on a PVDF piezoelectric film coupled to a custom amplifier to directly measure substrate‐coupled mechanical signals during droplet impact (Figure 1B). Because PVDF functions as a dynamic piezoelectric sensor, the measured voltage reflects the time‐dependent deformation of the coupled sensor–substrate system and is inherently sensitive to the rate of mechanical loading. Under fixed sensor geometry, substrate configuration, and amplifier gain, the peak‐to‐peak PVDF sensor signal is therefore interpreted as a relative measure of transient, impact‐induced loading, rather than an absolute or statically calibrated force. The sensor is bonded to a glass substrate and read out by a charge amplifier and is integrated with a high‐speed camera that captures the droplet morphology. Typical droplet impacts exert forces on the order of millinewtons and even micronewtons during rebound [19, 20]. To resolve the associated small signals, we implemented a 100× amplifier, achieving an effective gain of 96.9 as verified by comparing raw and amplified signals (Figure 1C and Figure S1).

For a representative water droplet of radius ∼1 mm (γ = 0.072 N/m) impacting a superhydrophobic surface with a contact angle of 154.0°, the complete impact and rebound sequence is captured in both the high‐speed camera images and the PVDF sensor's signal (Figure 1D,E, and Figure S2). The image sequence identifies the major morphological events, including initial contact, spreading, recoiling, and rebound, whereas the PVDF sensor's signal resolves the corresponding substrate‐coupled mechanical response with higher temporal resolution. During each millisecond‐scale contact event, including rebounds at approximately 21.8, 97.7, and 164.7 ms, the PVDF sensor's signal consistently exhibits a similar 100–500 Hz oscillatory structure (Figure 1E and Figure S3). The initial impact, occurring between 20 and 40 ms in the PVDF sensor readout, exhibits a clear two‐stage signature: a strong, short‐duration impulsive PVDF sensor response within the first 1–2 ms after contact, followed by a sequence of damped oscillatory waves.

To probe the role of surface wettability, we prepared substrates with different equilibrium contact angles: glass coated with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, contact angle 34.4° ± 4.4°), bare glass (67.4° ± 12.7°), and glass coated with hydrophobically modified nanoparticles (NPs) to produce a superhydrophobic surface (154.0° ± 3.2°). For all three cases, the PVDF sensor readout shows the same qualitative temporal sequence: an intense impulsive strain pulse followed by coherent oscillations in the first 15 ms before rebound (Figure 1F). Both the half‐period of the impulsive response and the oscillation period decrease monotonically as the surface changes from superhydrophobic to more hydrophilic.

The observed waveform suggests that the droplet‐substrate system can be interpreted as a coupled inertial interfacial oscillator. Building on classic inertio‐capillary theory and the Rayleigh frequencies of droplet oscillations [21, 22, 23, 24], we describe the interaction using a minimal two‐degree‐of‐freedom mass‐spring‐damper system (Figure 1G). This reduced‐order model is not intended to replace the full hydrodynamic description of droplet spreading or contact‐line motion, but rather to capture the dominant substrate‐coupled temporal features measured by the PVDF sensor during the earliest stage of impact. A lower spring‐mass element represents the interfacial liquid layer that deforms together with the substrate and is characterized by an effective contact stiffness k c. An upper mass m 1 represents the remaining portion of the droplet that retains downward inertia after impact and is connected to the interfacial layer by a capillary spring of stiffness k γ that captures the restoring action of surface tension. Within this framework, a center of mass (COM) mode corresponds to the motion of m 1 against the combined stiffness k c+ k γ. This mode generates a short, intense inertial hammer at first touchdown and sets the amplitude of the initial impulsive strain peak [18, 25, 26]. A second, capillary‐mediated mode arises from relative motion between the interfacial region and the deforming free surface, with a characteristic timescale related to inertio‐capillary droplet recovery and classical Rayleigh‐type oscillations [21, 22, 23]. In the PVDF measurements, this coupled two‐mode oscillator produces the coherent oscillations that follow the impulsive event and, on low‐adhesion hydrophobic substrates, a secondary strain peak that reflects impact‐driven deformation and recovery of the local droplet–substrate interface.

Wettability Modulates Rebound Behavior and Early‐Stage Oscillation Dynamics

To minimize chemical heterogeneity while tuning wettability, glass substrates were coated with polystyrene (PS) containing different volume fractions of 1H,1H,2H, and 2H perfluorooctyltrichlorosilane (PFOTS)‐modified silicon dioxide (SiO2) nanoparticles, which introduce controlled micro‐and nanoscale roughness [27, 28, 29]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirms that surface roughness increases with nanoparticle loading (Figure 2A and Figure S4). Pure PS (0% NPs) yields a relatively smooth surface with a static contact angle of 100.4° ± 5.2°, while 10% (v/v) NPs raises the contact angle to 123.2° ± 9.3°. Increasing the NP fraction from 25% to 75% produces superhydrophobic surfaces with contact angles exceeding 150°, with no significant differences among these highly rough surfaces (Figure 2B).

High‐speed imaging shows that droplets on high adhesion surfaces (0% and 10% NPs) pin and undergo damped oscillations without rebounding, whereas droplets on superhydrophobic, low adhesion surfaces (contact angle >150°) rebound robustly (Figure 2C), consistent with the simultaneous PVDF measurements (Figure S5A) and previous reports on bouncing and contact time on hydrophobic and superhydrophobic substrates [20, 30, 31]. The first rebound time increases systematically with NP concentration (Figure S5B), indicating that reduced droplet surface adhesion and drag promote faster recoiling and rebound. Although the adhesive forces are too small to measure reliably with standard microbalances for the 25%–75% NPs groups, the PVDF sensor resolves subtle differences in rebound dynamics through precise timing.

We next focus on the first 20 ms following initial impact, before any rebound occurs. PVDF traces for substrates with different wettabilities reveal a robust pattern (Figure 2D). We define the first positive PVDF peak as T 0 = 0 ms, the subsequent negative peak as T 1, and the next positive peak as T 2. Here, T 1 captures the initial post‐impact reversal, while T 2 defines the characteristic timescale of the first oscillation cycle. The peak‐to‐peak strain amplitude (P–P amplitude) varies only weakly across wettability (Figure 2E). In contrast, both T 1 and T 2 increase systematically with hydrophobicity, from 1.43 ± 0.10 to 2.10 ± 0.08 ms for T 1 and from 2.95 ± 0.17 to 3.98 ± 0.10 ms for T 2, as the contact angle increases from 100.4° (0% NPs) to 154.0° (75% NPs) (Figure 2F).

This behavior can be rationalized by treating the PVDF sensor output as a relative measure of substrate‐coupled dynamic strain under fixed sensor geometry and amplifier conditions. During the earliest stage of impact [5, 6, 32], the characteristic inertial loading exerted by the droplet scales as Finertial∼ρV02D02, where ρ is the liquid density, V 0 the impact velocity, and D 0 the droplet diameter [23, 25, 26]. If the effective contact stiffness of the sensor‐substrate system is k c and the characteristic strained length is L eff, the impact‐induced displacement scales as x∼Finertial/kc, and the corresponding substrate strain scales as ε ∼ x/L eff. Therefore, the peak‐to‐peak dynamic strain is associated with the first impact response εpp∼ρV02D02/(kcLeff). Since k c is dominated by substrate elasticity and depends only weakly on surface wettability over these sub‐millisecond inertial timescales, the impulsive strain amplitude, and hence the first PVDF sensor's signal voltage peak, is essentially independent of contact angle. Under the same droplet size and release height, the initial momentum transfer to the substrate is similar across surfaces (Figure 2E).

In contrast, the timing parameters T 1 and T 2 reflect the post‐impulse dynamics of the local liquid–solid interface [6, 21, 22, 23, 32]. In a simplified per‐area description, the interfacial region can be treated as an effective mass coupled to an effective stiffness, where represents contact‐mediated stiffness and represents capillary restoring stiffness. As the contact angle decreases, increased liquid–solid contact area, stronger contact‐line constraint, and enhanced capillary coupling are expected to increase the effective interfacial stiffness. The characteristic oscillation period, therefore, scales as . Accordingly, increasing shortens the oscillation period T 2, and the first‐lobe time T 1 follows the same decreasing trend. Thus, lowering the contact angle reduces both T 1 and T 2 (Figure 2F), consistent with enhanced effective interfacial stiffness and stronger liquid–solid coupling.

Weber Number and Inertial Impact Time Govern the Amplitude and Timing of Early PVDF Responses

We next examined the dependence of the PVDF sensor‐detected signal waveform on droplet diameter D 0 and impact height h on a fixed superhydrophobic substrate. The impact velocity, V 0, was calculated from the release height as V0=2gh, and the corresponding Weber number was defined as We=ρV02D0/γ. Consistent with this inertial scaling, normalizing the P–P PVDF amplitude by droplet diameter, V PP/D 0, increases approximately linearly with We (Figure 3A). These trends can be understood from the inertial loading scale during the earliest stage of impact [23, 26]. The characteristic normal loading scales as Finertial∼ρV02D02. Using We=ρV02D0/γ this expression can be rewritten as F inertial ∼ γD 0 We. Because the PVDF voltage is used here as a relative measure of substrate‐coupled dynamic strain under a fixed sensor geometry and amplifier configuration, the first P–P voltage amplitude (V PP) is expected to follow the same inertial loading trend [33, 34, 35]. Therefore, V PP scales with D 0 We, and the normalized response V PP/D 0 scales approximately with We. Notably, impacts in the higher regime (We > 120, Figure S6), where splashing or secondary droplet breakup occurs, still follow the same overall trend, indicating that the first PVDF amplitude is primarily governed by the initial inertial loading rather than by later‐stage droplet morphology.

(B) First‐lobe time T 1 plotted against the inertial impact time τρ = D 0 /V 0. (C) Oscillation period T 2 plotted against the inertio‐capillary time τγ=(ρD03/γ)1/2. Black dots denote low‐We non‐splashing impacts (15 < We < 120); green dots denote high‐We splashing impacts (120 < We < 400). Each data point represents an individual repeat experiment.

The characteristic times T 1 and T 2 reflect early substrate‐coupled waveform dynamics following the first impact‐induced PVDF peak [6, 22, 36]. The first‐lobe time T 1 correlates positively with the inertial impact time τρ = D 0 /V 0 for both non‐splashing and splashing impacts, indicating that this initial post‐peak reversal is governed primarily by local impact‐driven deformation and momentum transfer (Figure 3B). In contrast, T 2 reflects the first oscillatory recovery after the impulsive event and depends on the impact regime. For non‐splashing impacts, T 2 correlates positively with the inertio‐capillary time τγ=(ρD03/γ)1/2, suggesting capillary‐mediated recovery of a coherent droplet–substrate interfacial region (Figure 3C). For splashing impacts at higher Weber numbers, T 2 becomes nearly insensitive to droplet diameter, suggesting that breakup interrupts the global droplet‐scale recovery and confines the measured response to a localized substrate‐coupled oscillation.

Discussion

A high temporal resolution PVDF strain sensor reveals a two‐stage substrate‐coupled mechanical response when water droplets impact solid surfaces at the initial contact stage. Across a broad range of wettability, droplet sizes, and impact velocities, each impact consists of an intense impulsive PVDF sensor's response followed by coherent interfacial oscillations whose timing is governed by an effective inertial capillary stiffness. The impulsive component is controlled by droplet inertia, as shown by the increase in diameter‐normalized P–P amplitude with Weber number, while surface wettability and interfacial structure tune the oscillation period and damping. On a fixed superhydrophobic substrate, T 1 remains closely associated with the inertial impact time τρ, consistent with an initial post‐peak reversal governed by impact‐driven deformation and momentum transfer. In contrast, T 2 reflects regime‐dependent interfacial recovery: coherent non‐splashing impacts retain inertio‐capillary behavior, whereas splashing disrupts droplet‐scale recovery and localizes the measured response [37]. A minimal reduced‐order model provides a physical mapping between the PVDF sensor‐detected signal waveform and the underlying early impact dynamics.

These mechanical signatures may also have broader implications for damage initiation and fatigue under repeated droplet impacts. The initial impulsive pulse acts as a localized, shock‐like load that stresses the near‐surface region on millisecond timescales, potentially approaching the dynamic strength limits of brittle materials. The subsequent oscillations occur at frequencies comparable to the natural resonance band of many rocks, concretes, and engineered ceramics, typically in the range of 100–500 Hz. Repeated impacts could therefore activate a coupled mechanism in which the impulsive loading seeds microcracks, while the oscillatory interfacial forcing drives their growth through fatigue and resonant coupling. These findings suggest that the combined impulsive and oscillatory responses may contribute to progressive material weakening during rainfall‐induced weathering and other droplet‐driven erosion processes [1, 2]. In addition, the modulation of early‐stage oscillation dynamics by surface wettability indicates that superhydrophobic modification can alter these oscillations and may help protect surfaces from impact‐induced erosion and fracture.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the PVDF sensor's output reports a relative dynamic loading response rather than a static force. Because piezoelectric signals are sensitive to time‐dependent deformation and can be affected by charge leakage and circuit‐dependent decay, the measured waveform should be interpreted under the fixed sensor geometry and amplifier configuration used here. Absolute force quantification would require independent dynamic calibration of the complete sensor–circuit system. Second, this study focuses on water droplets in low‐ to moderate‐speed impact regimes. For more viscous liquids, viscous dissipation may damp or shift the early interfacial oscillations. At higher Weber numbers, splashing, compressibility, water‐hammer pressure, and cavitation may become increasingly important and may alter the measured waveform. Future work will extend this measurement approach across broader Weber‐number, Reynolds‐number, and Ohnesorge‐number regimes to capture transitions among inertial, capillary, viscous, and compressible impact dynamics.

Conclusion

In summary, a high‐temporal‐resolution PVDF strain sensor reveals a two‐stage substrate‐coupled mechanical response during the initial contact stage of water‐droplet impact on solid surfaces. Across a broad range of wettability, droplet sizes, and impact velocities, each impact produces an intense impulsive PVDF response followed by coherent interfacial oscillations. The impulsive component is governed by droplet inertia, as shown by the increase in diameter‐normalized P–P amplitude with Weber number, while the early waveform features arise from impact‐driven deformation and momentum transfer. The subsequent oscillations reflect substrate‐coupled deformation–recovery dynamics at the water–solid interface, with surface wettability, interfacial structure, and impact regime modulating the oscillation period and damping. These measurements and the reduced‐order model provide a physical framework for mapping PVDF waveforms to early droplet‐impact dynamics.
