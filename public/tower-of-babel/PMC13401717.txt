Oral Nicotine Pouches and the Emerging Addiction Crisis Among Youth in Pakistan: A Perspective
Authors: Wasay Mumtaz Awan, Muhammad Shafay Aamir, Mohid Zulfiqar, Bilal Islam, Aabia Talha, Hermann Yokolo, Syed Ahmed Ali Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsOral nicotine pouches (ONPs) are smokeless, flavored products marketed as safer alternatives to cigarettes, but their rapid uptake among adolescents has raised serious public health concerns. This Perspective examines the emerging use of ONPs among youth in Pakistan, the marketing and regulatory environment driving their spread, and the policy responses needed to prevent a new wave of nicotine addiction.MethodsWe synthesized recent peer‐reviewed evidence, including national survey and point‐of‐sale data from Pakistan, alongside international literature on ONP use, marketing, and adolescent nicotine addiction, to characterize the scale of the problem and identify regulatory gaps.ResultsONPs are widely available across Pakistan, which has become the third‐largest market globally for the leading brand, Velo. Products are sold cheaply, in youth‐oriented flavors, and are often displayed near schools and alongside confectionery; some retailers sell to minors. National data show that more children report using e‐cigarettes and nicotine pouches than conventional cigarettes, yet public awareness of ONP content and risks remains low. Adolescents are neurobiologically vulnerable to nicotine addiction, and ONPs may act as a gateway to long‐term dependence rather than a cessation aid. Critically, ONPs are not legally classified as tobacco products in Pakistan and therefore fall outside existing tobacco‐control legislation.ConclusionUrgent, dedicated regulatory action is needed—including age verification, advertising bans, retail‐zoning restrictions, mandatory health warnings, surveillance, and public‐awareness campaigns—to prevent ONPs from addicting a new generation of Pakistani youth.

Introduction

Originally promoted as a safer, tobacco‐free substitute for cigarettes, oral nicotine pouches (ONPs) are now a serious cause of concern due to their increasing popularity among adolescents. These discreet, flavored pouches, which are placed between the lip and gum, deliver potent doses of nicotine without the smoke, smell, or mess associated with cigarettes or chewing tobacco. Their slim design and wide variety of flavors (e.g., mint, fruit, dessert) enhance their appeal to young people. ONPs are smokeless and odorless, making them easy to conceal and attractive for use in environments where smoking and vaping are restricted, such as schools and colleges. These features, combined with their resemblance to everyday consumer goods like gum or mints, have driven growing experimentation among adolescents and young adults, many of whom are already using other nicotine products [1].

Despite growing international concern, the specific drivers and implications of ONP use among Pakistani youth have received limited focused attention. In this Perspective, we draw on recent national and point‐of‐sale evidence from Pakistan, together with international literature, to argue that ONPs represent an emerging and under‐recognized addiction threat to Pakistani adolescents. We examine the marketing and accessibility environment enabling their spread, the neurobiological vulnerability of adolescents to nicotine, and the regulatory gap that currently leaves these products largely unregulated, and we set out the targeted policy measures needed to respond.

Discussion

Regarding these increasing concerns, ONPs often lack clear and prominent health warnings, unlike traditional cigarettes, which display explicit messaging such as “Smoking Kills.” The ambiguous packaging of ONPs increases the risk of unintentional ingestion, especially among youth and other vulnerable populations [2]. This issue is particularly pressing in Pakistan, where, according to UNICEF, approximately half of the population is under the age of 18.

The ONP industry in Pakistan operates with minimal regulatory oversight, enabling companies to market their products freely, often in ways that appeal directly to youth. Evidence from a Karachi‐based study conducted between 2022 and 2023 indicates that social media serves as the primary channel through which many young users are first exposed to ONPs [3]. The same study also revealed that concerning marketing practices at retail points of sale, a significant portion of which were located near schools. More than half of these outlets displayed promotional material for ONPs, and many strategically placed the products alongside candies and snacks, not only intensifying their visual appeal to children but also increasing the chance of unintentional use of nicotine. Shopkeepers reported being incentivized by companies through sales commissions and bonuses, further encouraging distribution. Disturbingly, some sellers acknowledged selling ONPs to underage students, highlighting the widespread accessibility of these products to minors and the urgent necessity for regulatory action [3].

In their promotional efforts, companies frequently collaborate with celebrities and youth influencers, portraying ONPs as a cleaner, more modern alternative to smoking. Qualitative research with Pakistani adolescents aged 10–16 has shown that Velo is widely recognized and perceived as a stylish, modern, and relatively harmless alternative to traditional smokeless tobacco, with its appeal shaped by packaging, discrete use, point‐of‐scale display, and social media, though some participants did acknowledge harms such as addiction [4]. This strategy has proven effective; industry reports show rapid market growth. Within 5 years of their introduction, Pakistan has become the third‐largest market globally for Velo, the leading ONP brand, produced by the Pakistan Tobacco Company (PTC) [5]. A nationwide point‐of‐sale survey of 711 retail outlets across all four provinces found that Velo sold in six flavors and three nicotine strengths, captured roughly 85% of the local market, was available at an average price of only 133 Pakistani Rupees (about US$0.44) per tin, and was advertised on in‐store display stands at 12.3% of outlets [5]. The combination of low prices, youth‐oriented flavor range, and visible retail promotion makes these products particularly accessible and appealing to young people.

The growing popularity of ONPs among youth appears to be a direct consequence of aggressive marketing tactics and a lack of regulatory oversight. While global cigarette smoking rates have declined over recent decades, which is driven partly due to increased awareness and public health campaigns, this decline has been accompanied by a surge in the use of alternative nicotine products such as e‐cigarettes and ONPs [6, 7]. Although these products are often marketed as harm‐reduction tools, their rapid uptake, especially among adolescents, has raised serious concerns.

Recent research from the United States highlights a worrying trend: a noticeable proportion of youth have experimented with ONPs, and a subset continues to use them regularly [8, 9]. Adult use is also rising, with more individuals reporting having tried ONPs in recent years. These patterns suggest that ONPs are not merely replacing cigarettes but may be introducing new users to nicotine, including those who might never have smoked in the first place.

Pakistan appears to be following a similar trajectory. A recent cross‐sectional survey of 14,232 children aged between 10 and 16 years old, conducted between December 2023 and May 2024, found that more children reported using e‐cigarettes and nicotine pouches than conventional cigarettes, underscoring how rapidly these newer products are reaching the youngest age groups [10]. Despite this, public understanding of the product remains limited. Fewer than half of the participants demonstrated adequate awareness of ONP contents and associated risks, and many expressed unfavorable views after learning more about them [11]. This knowledge gap, combined with appealing marketing and easy accessibility, increases the risk that ONPs may act as a gateway to long‐term nicotine dependence, particularly among non‐smokers and youth [12].

Adolescents are especially susceptible to nicotine addiction due to the heightened sensitivity of their developing brain's reward system. Studies have shown that younger individuals are more likely to form strong preferences for nicotine, and these cravings can persist well beyond initial use, even after periods of abstinence [13]. This biological vulnerability is exacerbated by ONPs, which deliver concentrated nicotine doses in flavors that are intentionally designed to appeal to youth.

Emerging evidence from the U.S. indicates that a large proportion of adolescents and young adults who use ONPs are also cigarette smokers [14]. This overlap suggests that instead of helping users quit, ONPs may reinforce nicotine dependence. By normalizing daily nicotine use through discreet and socially acceptable formats, ONPs risk creating a pathway to traditional tobacco products. Without timely intervention, such patterns could entrench nicotine addiction in younger generations and potentially reverse progress made in tobacco control efforts.

The rising use of oral nicotine pouches (ONPs) among adolescents poses a serious public health threat in Pakistan. These products, often containing higher nicotine concentrations than traditional cigarettes or e‐cigarettes, significantly increase the risk of early and long‐term addiction. Adolescents, whose brains are still developing, are especially vulnerable to the neurobiological effects of nicotine. High‐dose exposure not only accelerates the onset of dependence but also entrenches compulsive use, making cessation increasingly difficult over time. Far from being a harm‐reduction tool, ONPs may act as a gateway to lifelong nicotine addiction, undermining decades of progress in tobacco control. A central obstacle to addressing this problem is a fundamental regulatory gap. Unlike combustible cigarettes and oral tobacco, nicotine pouches are not legally classified as tobacco products in Pakistan; because they are tobacco‐free, they fall outside the scope of the country's existing tobacco‐control legislation, including the smoking‐ban ordinance, and are instead sold as ordinary consumer goods with no specific age restrictions, mandatory health warnings, advertising limits, or excise taxation [4, 5]. Calls to regulate ONPs “like tobacco” therefore cannot rely on current law; meaningful control will require either explicit legislative reclassification of ONPs or new, dedicated regulation.

To address this emerging crisis, Pakistan must act swiftly and decisively. We therefore propose the following targeted measures. First, strict age‐verification protocols should be enforced at all retail and online points of sale to prevent access by minors. Second, comprehensive advertising bans are needed, particularly on platforms popular among youth, including social media. Third, retail‐zoning regulations should prohibit ONP sales within a minimum 500‐m radius of schools, colleges, and playgrounds. Fourth, mandatory graphic health warnings should be required on ONP packaging, comparable to those mandated for combustible tobacco. Fifth, a national awareness campaign led by the Ministry of Health should educate parents, teachers, and adolescents about the risks of ONPs. Finally, surveillance and reporting mechanisms should be established to monitor ONP use and ensure regulatory compliance. Because nicotine pouches currently fall outside Pakistan's tobacco‐control legislation, implementing these measures will require either legislative reclassification of ONPs or new, dedicated regulation.

Conclusion

The unchecked rise of ONPs threatens to addict a new generation to nicotine under the guise of “safer alternatives.” Protecting Pakistan's youth demands immediate regulatory action, robust enforcement, and sustained public health advocacy.
