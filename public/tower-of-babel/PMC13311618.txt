Development of a LASSO-based machine learning model for relapse prediction in lupus nephritis
Authors: Hao Liu, Yan Zheng, Jin Ding, Ying Li, Jinghua Wang, Wenjuan Zhang, Zhaohui Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify the predictors of renal relapse in patients with lupus nephritis (LN) and develop a predictive model.MethodsThe patients with LN who received renal biopsies from 2005 to 2024 in Xijing Hospital were enrolled. This study was designed as a cohort study. Clinical and serological characteristics at the time of renal biopsy, nephritis histology, the duration to remission and treatment regimen were evaluated. Variables were screened using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox analyses, followed by the application of multivariate Cox regression to construct a relapse risk prediction model. The performance of the model was evaluated using bootstrap resampling. A landmark analysis was performed at 12 months, excluding patients who relapsed within this period. The model is intended for application at the 12-month postbiopsy time point.ResultsA total of 831 patients were enrolled in the study, 225/831 patients (27.08%) experienced relapse. Compared with the patients without relapse, patients with relapse demonstrated younger age of onset (24.0 vs 27.0 years, p=0.001), higher SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) (12.0 vs 7.0, p=0.001) and proteinuria (1.2 vs 0.6 g/day, p=0.001). However, the nephritis histology showed no significant difference between the two groups. The proportion of patients receiving intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy or belimumab in the non-relapse group was significantly higher. Using LASSO-Cox regression, four variables associated with LN relapse were identified, including level of 24-hour proteinuria, SLEDAI 2000 (SLEDAI-2K), low complement C3 level and remission within 12 months. The predictive model exhibited good predictive performance, with a bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.75 (95% CI 0.72 to 0.79).ConclusionsHigher level of 24-hour proteinuria, higher SLEDAI-2K score and low complement C3 level were risk factors for LN relapse, while remission within 12 months was a protective factor. The LASSO-Cox-based predictive model exhibited good performance and may serve as a useful tool for the clinical management of LN.

Introduction

SLE is a complex disease of increasing prevalence that presents substantial challenges in both diagnosis and management.1 It can affect multiple organs, including the kidneys, skin and nervous system. Lupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most severe complications of SLE, developing in approximately 20%–60% of patients.14 Previous studies reported that the estimated relapse-free renal survival rates were approximately 69% at 5 years and 57% at 10 years.57 Furthermore, between 5% and 30.1% of patients with SLE who have LN progress to end-stage kidney disease within 15 years after diagnosis, while the incidence may be higher in Asian populations.8 9 LN relapse is a major contributor to the persistent deterioration of renal function and accelerated progression to end-stage renal disease.10 11 Despite significant advances in treatment strategies, relapse remains a major challenge in the management of LN. Early identification of risk factors for relapse, along with precise guidance for individualised monitoring and intensive treatment during remission, is essential for improving patient outcomes.

Studies across diverse populations identified several risk factors for LN relapse.1215 Patients with proliferative and/or membranous LN were reported to have an HR of 5.94 for subsequent relapse.7 Low levels of complement and persistently positive or rising anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies are associated with an increased risk of LN relapse.4 16 Withdrawal of hydroxychloroquine or chloroquine in remission is associated with a threefold increased risk of relapse.17 In contrast, low level of 24-hour proteinuria (Upro24h) (<700–800 mg/24 hours by 12 months), using mycophenolate over azathioprine, adding belimumab to standard therapy, maintaining immunosuppressive or biological treatment for at least 3–5 years and using hydroxychloroquine may reduce the risk of renal relapses.4 However, LN relapse is the outcome of multifactor interaction, and constructing a risk stratification model with integrated risk factors can enhance the accuracy of single-factor predictions and is critical for personal management. Existing prediction models vary widely in inclusion criteria and selected variables. For instance, some studies omitted key clinical features such as initial symptoms or treatment regimens,18 while Chen et al incorporated demographics, nephritis histology, clinical parameters and induction therapy at baseline to develop an XGBoost-based model.19 However, treatment remission status during follow-up is often absent in previous prediction models. The predictive validity of the model might be constrained by ethnic differences, confounding variables, inconsistent definitions of relapse and population heterogeneity.

In this study, we aimed to characterise remission and relapse in the LN population in Northwest China, including the clinical and serological characteristics, renal histology and treatment regimens. We also developed a machine learning-based multivariable model to predict the relapse risk of LN and to provide evidence for clinical management.

Methods

Study population

Patients were enrolled from the Xijing Hospital cohort and followed up from 2005 to 2024. The participants met the American College of Rheumatology 1997 classification criteria or the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics 2012 criteria.20 21 Renal histology was assessed according to the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) 2003 and 2018 criteria.22 23 The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of other renal diseases (eg, diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy or primary glomerular diseases) that could result in renal impairment or proteinuria and (2) coexistence of other immune diseases (eg, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, polymyositis or dermatomyositis) to avoid confounding immunopathological mechanisms that could affect relapse assessment (online supplemental figure 1). This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Assessment of patients

Data collected during renal biopsies at our centre were used as the baseline. Information extracted from medical records included demographic variables (age, sex), disease duration, renal histological classification, clinical manifestations, disease activity (SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) score), laboratory assessments, induction therapies and comorbidities at baseline. Clinical remission was evaluated at 3, 6 and 12 months following the initial renal biopsy. Long-term follow-up focused on relapse events, including adverse events such as osteoporosis and infection. After 12 months, laboratory data were recorded at 12-month intervals (±2 months) to assess treatment response and relapse.

Definition of clinical remission and relapse

Complete remission (CR) was defined as (1) Upro24h <0.5 g/day and (2) stable or improved renal function (fluctuation within ±10%–15% of baseline). Partial remission (PR) was defined as: (1) Upro24h reduction ≥50% from baseline and <3 g/day and (2) stable or improved renal function (fluctuation within ±10%–15% of baseline). No kidney response (NR) was defined as failure to achieve CR or PR.24

Relapse was defined as an increase in Upro24h >0.5 g/day, new glomerular haematuria or an increase in serum creatinine >0.3 mg/dL; fulfilment of any one criterion sufficed.24 25 The time to renal relapse was measured from the date of the first CR or PR until relapse. Participants who missed more than one consecutive visit were censored on the week of the last recorded visit.

Statistical analysis

We used a landmark analysis approach. The landmark time point was defined as 12 months after kidney biopsy. Variables included general characteristics and laboratory parameters at the time of renal biopsy, as well as landmark-time variables (12-month remission status and belimumab use within 12 months). Patients who experienced relapse within 12 months were excluded. Data from participants with missing follow-up at 3, 6 or 12 months were handled using complete-case analysis (CCA). All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics V.26 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and R V.4.5.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables with non-normal distributions were expressed as median (IQR) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Stratified analysis was performed for confounding factors (treatment era). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted using the corrplot package in R V.4.6.0. After excluding highly correlated variables, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO-Cox) regression for variable selection (lambda.1se) was performed using the glmnet package in R. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to construct survival curves for variables associated with relapse. The log-rank test was used to compare between-group differences. Multivariable Cox regression was adjusted for disease activity (SLEDAI-2K). Selected variables were used to compute the area under the curve (AUC) values, followed by multivariate Cox regression modelling (control for confounding) and nomogram construction. The model predicts the risk of relapse at 3 and 5 years from the biopsy date (corresponding to 2 and 4 years from the landmark time point at 12 months, respectively). We plotted calibration curves and decision curves to validate the model. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value of <0.05.

Results

Baseline clinical and laboratory features of patients with LN

A total of 831 patients were enrolled, of whom 225 (27.1%) experienced relapse. The median follow-up for the entire cohort was 4.9 years (IQR 1.6–7.9), while the relapse group had a median follow-up of 7.8 years (IQR 4.8–10.6). The median time from first remission to relapse was 3.5 years (IQR 2.1–5.5). Compared with the non-relapse group, patients who relapsed were significantly younger at the onset of SLE (median 24.0 vs 27.0, p<0.05) and at the onset of LN (median 27.0 vs 31.1, p<0.05) (table 1).

The distribution of the ISN/RPS classes was similar in both groups (p=0.224), with a higher prevalence of class III (39.1% and 42.4%) and class IV (22.7% and 17.0%) compared with class II (15.6% and 19.1%) and class V (18.7% and 19.3%). The proportion of class I and class VI in the relapse group was 2.7% and 1.3%, respectively.

No significant differences were found between groups regarding sex or autoantibody profiles. However, the relapse group exhibited significantly higher disease activity (median SLEDAI-2K score, 12.0 (8.0–15.5), p<0.05), greater baseline Upro24h (median 1.2 g/day (0.6–2.8), p<0.05) and higher prevalence of hypertension (47.1% vs 21.2%, p<0.05) and hyperlipidaemia (24.0% vs 1.7%, p<0.05). Significant differences were also observed in erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C reactive protein, C3 and C4 levels.

All patients received oral corticosteroids at baseline. However, only 15 patients (6.67%) in the relapse group received intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (IVMP), a proportion significantly lower than that in patients without relapse (40.9%) (p<0.05). Stratified analysis by treatment era (2005–2019 vs 2020–2024) showed that the proportion of IVMP was higher after 2019 (40.6% vs 27.7%, online supplemental table 1).

For induction therapy, most of the patients in both groups used HCQ (98.2% and 95.8%, respectively). The proportion of mycophenolate mofetil usage in both groups was 56.9% and 64.2%, and the proportion of cyclophosphamide was 35.6% and 29.2%, respectively. Notably, only 34 patients (15.1%) in the relapse group received belimumab during follow-up, which was significantly lower than the proportion in the non-relapse group (p<0.05).

Short-term treatment response and complications

The relapse-free survival rates of the entire cohort were 96.5%, 91.8%, 85.0%, 78.2%, 71.1% and 45.3% at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 10 years, respectively (online supplemental figure 2). A significant difference in 1-year treatment response was observed between the relapse group and non-relapse group (table 2). The relapse group achieved significantly lower CR rates at 3, 6 and 12 months (7.7%, 44.2% and 48.0%, respectively) than the non-relapse group (all, p<0.05). The rates of non-response at 3, 6 and 12 months (17.0%, 21.9% and 22.5%, respectively) were significantly higher in the relapse group. Regarding long-term complications, osteoporosis was documented in 194 patients (23.3%) in the entire cohort. Infections occurred in 51 patients (22.7%) within the relapse group, with a significantly higher proportion than in the non-relapse group (p<0.05). We observed a statistically significant difference in drug withdrawal between the two groups (11.1% vs 3.1%, p<0.05).

Predictive factors for relapse

Spearman’s correlation analysis of 43 candidate variables revealed strong pairwise correlations: age at LN and SLE diagnosis (rs=0.9, p<0.001), serum creatinine (SCr) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (rs=−0.68, p<0.001), IgG and globulin (GLB) (rs=0.68, p<0.001), complement C3 and C4 (rs=0.56, p<0.001), anti-Ro52 antibody and anti-SSA antibody (rs=0.59, p<0.001), remission at 3 and 6 months (rs=0.69, p<0.001), remission at 12 and 6 months (rs=0.51, p<0.001) and remission at 12 and 3 months (rs=0.48, p<0.001) (online supplemental figure 3). To avoid multicollinearity, variables with high correlation (rs>0.5) and lesser clinical relevance were excluded, resulting in the removal of seven variables (age at SLE diagnosis, anti-SSA antibody, SCr, GLB, complement C4 and remission at 3 and 6 months).

Subsequently, LASSO-Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation (λ=0.11785) was applied, identifying three candidate variables (online supplemental figure 4). The remaining four variables were entered into a multivariate Cox regression model, with disease activity (SLEDAI-2K) included for adjustment. Multivariate analysis identified those independent factors for relapse: elevated Upro24h (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.19), higher SLEDAI-2K score (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.11), low complement C3 level (HR 3.69, 95% CI 2.03 to 6.70), treatment non-response at 12 months (HR 3.71, 95% CI 2.58 to 5.32) (table 3). To further illustrate the prognostic impact of each independent factor, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed after dichotomising continuous variables using cut-off values determined by time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis (1.3 g/day for Upro24h and 9.5 for SLEDAI-2K; online supplemental figure 5). All curves showed significant separation between subgroups (log-rank p<0.01), supporting the predictive value of these factors for relapse risk.

A nomogram based on four variables was developed to visualise the risk of relapse (figure 2). In the landmark analysis, 217 patients were excluded due to missing treatment response data at 12 months (among these, 25 had relapsed and 192 had not). This nomogram was developed based on multivariable Cox regression (with variables preselected via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression) to estimate the individualised relapse risk in patients with remission-achieved LN. The nomogram includes four variables: Upro24h, 24-hour urinary protein excretion at renal biopsy; SLEDAI, SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 score at renal biopsy; C3, complement C3 level (binary: 0=normal, 1=low); Remission_at_12 months, remission status at 12 months postinduction (categorical: 1=complete remission, 2=partial remission, 3=no kidney response). For each patient, locate the value of each predictor on its axis, draw a vertical line upwards to the Points axis to obtain the corresponding points, then sum these to get the Total Points. The total score is then projected onto the bottom axis to obtain the predicted 3-year and 5-year relapse rates.

Sensitivity analysis for treatment era

To control for confounding by treatment era, separate models were constructed for each era (2005–2019 vs 2020–2024, online supplemental table 2). The cutoff of 2019 was chosen based on: (1) the updated European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/European Renal Association (ERA)-EDTA guidelines recommending treat-to-target principles in LN in 2019 and (2) belimumab was approved for LN in China in 2019. Most variables showed significant associations in both models (p<0.05), except for complement C3 level. In the 2020–2024 era, the lack of statistical significance for low complement C3 level was likely due to the small number of relapse events (n=28), which limited statistical power, as reflected by the wide CI. Given that low complement C3 level is a well-recognised risk factor for relapse, we retained it in the final model based on clinical grounds.

Internal validation

After bootstrap internal validation (1000 replicates), the optimism-corrected C-index was 0.75 (95% CI 0.72 to 0.79). To further evaluate calibration and clinical utility, we constructed calibration curves (using bootstrap internal validation with 1000 replicates) and performed decision curve analysis (DCA) (online supplemental figure 6). The calibration curves demonstrated good agreement, confirming the nomogram’s suitability for individualised risk stratification at both short-term (3-year) and long-term (5-year) time points. DCA indicated that the model had superior clinical utility for long-term (5-year) risk stratification compared with short-term (3-year) prediction. Specifically, the 5-year DCA plot showed a positive net benefit across decision thresholds ranging from 0% to 85%, whereas the 3-year DCA plot showed an applicable range of 0%–75%.

Discussion

In this study, low C3 level, higher Upro24h (≥1.3 g/day) and higher SLEDAI-2K score (≥9.5) were identified as risk factors for LN relapse, whereas 12-month remission was associated with a favourable outcome.

We employed a LASSO-based machine learning approach for variable selection and constructed a four-variable predictive nomogram to predict the risk of relapse in patients with LN. This model demonstrates excellent performance and could provide an important basis for disease management.

LN is a major cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD), particularly end-stage renal disease (ESRD), and has been extensively researched. Previous studies have indicated multiple factors that may influence the risk of LN relapse.4 26 Our study identified higher SLEDAI-2K scores (≥9.5), higher UPro24h (≥1.3 g/day) and low complement C3 level as independent risk factors for relapse, consistent with previous studies.2730 Previous studies have suggested that proliferative and/or membranous LN is associated with unfavourable prognosis.26 Due to missing Activity Index (AI)/Chronicity Index (CI) data in 438 cases, we did not identify a significant association between histological features and relapse risk. In future studies, we will select cohorts with complete pathological data to validate the predictive value of pathological features for relapse. In our cohort, a total of 499 patients were diagnosed with proliferative LN (including class III and class IV). Among these, 139 experienced relapse, while 360 did not. In the relapse group, only 6.7% received IVMP at baseline, compared with 40.9% in the non-relapse group.

After 2019, a more standardised treatment approach for LN was implemented at our centre following the updated EULAR/ERA-EDTA recommendations.31 Among these patients, the overall use of IVMP was relatively low (263/831, 31.7%); however, detailed stratification analysis revealed a significant increase after 2019 compared with the period before 2019 (from 27.7% to 40.6%). In addition, only 6.7% of the relapse group received IVMP, compared with 40.9% of the non-relapse group, suggesting a protective association of IVMP with relapse. However, this observation may be affected by differences in disease severity and treatment choices. Before 2019, AI/CI scores were not routinely evaluated (available in only 229/575, 39.8%), resulting in a high rate of missing data. The proportion of patients with AI/CI (164/256, 64.1%) increased after 2019, reflecting improved pathological standardisation. The updated 2019 EULAR/ERA-EDTA guidelines highlighted the importance of standardised LN treatment.31 These results further confirm the importance of implementing standardised clinical management (treat-to-target strategies).

In addition, some studies focused on the influence of HCQ reduction or withdrawal on LN relapse,17 but our study did not find this impact due to regular HCQ therapy (96.5%, 802 cases). Belimumab, an anti-B-cell activating factor monoclonal antibody approved for SLE,32 has been shown to reduce the relapse risk.3335 Our cohort showed that the proportion of patients treated with belimumab during the disease course was significantly higher in the non-relapse group (47.9% vs 15.1%, p=0.004). Belimumab was approved for LN in China in 2019. Among patients receiving belimumab within 12 months, the non-relapse proportion was higher (72/84 vs 12/84, table 2). But this study did not address the duration and dosage of belimumab agent, so further research is needed to evaluate the efficacy during the maintenance phase. Belimumab use is treatment-related and not randomly assigned, which introduces risk of confounding by indication.

The progression of LN is influenced by multiple factors including clinical characteristics, pathological classification and treatment strategies. With the increase in data information abundance and the advancement of statistical methods, many machine learning approaches are attempting to provide guidance and evidence for the early treatment of LN by constructing multifactor integrated prediction models.36 For instance, Huang et al constructed an interpretable multivariable long short-term memory model based on serological and renal biopsy for LN relapse risk prediction.37 In our study, we employed LASSO-Cox regression (based on a regularisation equation) to screen variables from clinical, pathological and treatment-related characteristics while addressing multicollinearity. The final prediction model exhibited good accuracy with a bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.75 (95% CI 0.72 to 0.79) after the 12-month landmark, providing a reliable tool for relapse prediction. The resulting nomogram stratified patients into three risk groups based on their relapse risk: low (10%–30%), intermediate (30%–70%) and high (70%–90%), thereby facilitating the individualised treatment strategies for patients in different risk strata.

We used a landmark analysis approach. The landmark time was defined as 12 months after kidney biopsy. The model is applied at the 12-month time point after biopsy, using the 12-month remission status as a key predictor. It integrates 12-month remission status and laboratory parameters at the time of renal biopsy to predict relapse at 3 and 5 years from the biopsy date in patients with LN. The optimal timing for discontinuation of immunosuppressive agents remains unclear.38 In patients with sustained complete renal response, gradual withdrawal of immunosuppressive and/or biologic therapy should be considered after 3 years of therapy following response, taking into consideration the risk for flare.26 Therefore, this model can assist clinicians in evaluating the probability of relapse at 3 and 5 years from the biopsy date, thereby facilitating personalised medical care.

This study has several limitations. First, bias may arise from the prolonged follow-up duration and evolution of treatment concepts. Differences in the use of therapy (eg, belimumab and IVMP) across different treatment eras may introduce treatment-related confounding. Second, follow-up data at 3, 6 and 12 months were incomplete (CCA). This attrition might lead to missing data that are not completely random, potentially introducing bias in the longitudinal analyses. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the observed trends in treatment response. Third, the definition of relapse used in this study was based on a previous study because no unified definition for LN relapse exists. Definitions vary across studies, which may limit comparability. Fourth, our study is a single-centre study and lacks external validation. Future research with larger samples and multicentre designs is needed to validate the conclusions.

In summary, we developed a predictive model based on four variables that exhibited satisfactory performance, and systematically compared the clinical, pathological and treatment characteristics of patients with and without relapse. The visual nomogram we developed may serve as a useful tool for predicting the risk of relapse among patients with LN.
