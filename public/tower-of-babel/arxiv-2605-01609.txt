Concepts Whisper While Syntax Shouts: Spectral Anti-Concentration and the Dual Geometry of Transformer Representations
Pratyush Acharya, Nuraj Rimal, Habish Dhakal

Abstract
We test whether the causal inner product of 32 —defined by the unembedding covariance Σ \Sigma —enables cross-lingual concept transport. Across 17 models and 4 language pairs, a matched-spectrum randomization test finds that Whitened Causal Alignment is indistinguishable from spectral regularization alone ( p = 0.95 p=0.95 ). However, this failure reveals a broader phenomenon: anti-concentration is observed in residual-stream difference-of-means vectors across five architecture families ( p < 10 − 33 p<10^{-33} ) and supported by SAE features (e.g., p = 4.5 × 10 − 19 p=4.5\times 10^{-19} ) and linear probes on Gemma and Llama. We discover a dual geometry : activation-space concept directions anti-concentrate in the spectral tail, while static unembedding-row contrasts concentrate in high-variance directions ( p < 10 − 4 p<10^{-4} ). Split-injection causal interventions support the functional basis on Gemma and Llama (Cohen’s d d up to 1.80 1.80 ), and POS-tag probing across 8 models shows syntax preferentially encodes in the high-variance subspace in 6 of 8 architectures ( p < 0.013 p<0.013 ), with the Qwen 2.5 family showing a significant reversal consistent with architecture-specific spectral structure. These results suggest transformers may rotate semantic content into spectrally quiet regions during contextualized processing, encoding concepts where they can be manipulated with reduced grammatical disruption.

Introduction

The mechanistic interpretability of large language models (LLMs) rests on a foundational empirical observation: high-level concepts appear to be encoded as linear directions in the model’s activation space
[
25
,
26
,
21
,
40
,
27
]
. This
Linear Representation Hypothesis
(LRH) has been formalized by
32
, who demonstrate that the appropriate geometric structure for these representations is not the standard Euclidean inner product, but rather a
causal inner product
defined by the inverse covariance of the unembedding matrix. Under this metric, causally separable concepts are provably orthogonal
[
32
,
31
]
.

Simultaneously, the
Platonic Representation Hypothesis

[
15
]
and related work on universal feature spaces
[
17
]
suggest that sufficiently capable models converge toward shared internal representations, raising the possibility of
cross-lingual concept transport
: extracting a concept direction from one language’s representation and injecting it into another’s via Procrustes alignment
[
4
,
5
]
.

The intersection of these two hypotheses—that concepts live in a specific causal geometry, and that this geometry is shared across languages—motivates what we term
Whitened Causal Alignment
(
wca
): aligning representation spaces after whitening with the inverse square root of their respective unembedding covariance matrices. If the causal geometry hypothesis is correct,
wca
should outperform naive Procrustes alignment by stripping away language-specific anisotropy
[
20
,
43
]
before alignment.

We originally set out to validate this hypothesis. Our experiments across 17 models spanning 5 architecture families and 4 cross-lingual pairs tell a different story. The causal geometry—specifically, the eigenvector directions of
\Sigma
—does not measurably improve cross-lingual transport in our matched-spectrum test (
Section

4
). But the failure of the geometric hypothesis revealed something more interesting: activation-space concept representations systematically
anti-concentrate
in the eigenspectrum of
\Sigma
, preferentially encoding in low-variance directions (
Section

5
). This phenomenon is not an artifact of the extraction method—it persists across sparse autoencoder features, linear probes, and difference-of-means vectors (
Section

6
). Moreover, the
static vocabulary
shows the opposite pattern: concept-specific unembedding-row differences
concentrate
in high-variance directions, revealing a
dual geometry
between the vocabulary space and the reasoning space (
Section

7
).

We provide functional evidence for why this dual geometry exists through two complementary experiments: steering experiments showing that injecting concept vectors along high-variance eigendirections causes significantly more perplexity degradation than injection along low-variance directions on Gemma and Llama (
Section

8.1
), and a POS-tag probing experiment showing that syntactic information is preferentially encoded in the high-variance subspace for most tested architectures (
Section

8.2
).

Our contributions are:

1.

A negative result for causal eigendirections in cross-lingual transport, established via matched-spectrum randomization across 17 models (
Section

4
).

2.

The discovery and validation of spectral anti-concentration of activation-space concept representations, supported by three independent extraction methods across five architecture families (
Sections

5
and
6
).

3.

The identification of a dual geometry between vocabulary-space concentration and activation-space anti-concentration, with a random-pair null model showing concept-specific structure (
Section

7
).

4.

Functional evidence for why concepts anti-concentrate, via split-injection steering experiments across five models and syntactic probing of spectral subspaces across eight models (
Sections

8.1
and
8.2
).

2
Background

2.1
The Linear Representation Hypothesis

The idea that concepts are linearly encoded in neural network representations traces back to the word embedding literature
[
25
,
26
]
. Recent work has extended this to contextualized LLM representations, demonstrating linear encoding of truth
[
21
,
3
]
, sentiment
[
40
]
, world state
[
27
]
, and refusal behavior
[
1
]
.
32
provide the first rigorous formalization, defining linear representations via counterfactual pairs and proving connections between subspace representation, measurement (probing), and intervention (steering).
8
show that some features are inherently multi-dimensional, suggesting the one-dimensional LRH is an approximation.

2.2
The Causal Inner Product

A key contribution of
32
is the identification of a non-Euclidean inner product under which causally separable concepts are orthogonal. Let
W_{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}
be the unembedding matrix mapping from the
d
-dimensional residual stream to
V
-dimensional logits. The theoretical causal inner product is defined via the second moment of the unembedding vectors
[
32
]
.

In practice, we compute an
empirical
variant restricted to language-specific token subsets. Given a set of tokens
\mathcal{L}
belonging to a particular language (identified via Unicode script filtering), we define:

\Sigma_{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{L}|}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}}\gamma(w)\gamma(w)^{\top}+\lambda I

(1)

where
\gamma(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unembedding vector (row of
W_{U}
) for token
w
and
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter.
2
2

2

When
\mathcal{L}
is the full vocabulary and all tokens are weighted equally, this reduces to
(1/V)W_{U}^{\top}W_{U}+\lambda I
, the form given by
32
. We compute this over language-specific subsets to isolate the geometry relevant to each language independently.
The causal inner product is then
\langle u,v\rangle_{C}=u^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}v
, under which causally separable concepts are orthogonal
[
32
]
. The extension to categorical and hierarchical concepts is developed in
31
.

2.3
Cross-Lingual Alignment and Whitened Causal Alignment

Cross-lingual transfer methods typically align representation spaces via orthogonal Procrustes analysis
[
4
]
, finding a rotation
Q
minimizing
\|X_{\text{src}}-X_{\text{tgt}}Q\|_{F}
subject to
Q^{\top}Q=I
. However,
20
show that language-specific latent processes hinder cross-lingual transfer, and
43
demonstrate that the “shared” semantic space in multilingual LLMs is predominantly English-centric.

Combining the causal inner product with Procrustes alignment yields
Whitened Causal Alignment
(
wca
). Given source and target covariances
\Sigma_{\text{src}}
and
\Sigma_{\text{tgt}}
, the
wca
transport of a concept vector
v_{\text{src}}
is:

v_{\text{raid}}=\Sigma_{\text{tgt}}^{1/2}\;Q^{\top}\;\Sigma_{\text{src}}^{-1/2}\;v_{\text{src}}

(2)

where
Q
is the Procrustes rotation solved in the whitened space. We evaluate transport quality via the cosine similarity between
v_{\text{raid}}
and the ground-truth target concept vector
v_{\text{tgt}}
.

2.4
Anisotropy in Transformer Representations

Transformer representations are known to be highly anisotropic—activations cluster in a narrow cone of the full space
[
9
,
10
]
. This anisotropy is driven by a few “outlier” dimensions with disproportionately large variance, often linked to token frequency
[
41
,
33
,
16
]
.
12
show that some of these high-variance dimensions encode spatial and temporal features, suggesting that the dominant eigenspace carries structured information rather than pure noise. The relationship between this anisotropy and the spectral structure of the unembedding covariance is central to our findings.

2.5
Sparse Autoencoders

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) decompose polysemantic neural activations into sparse, interpretable features
[
2
,
6
,
39
]
. The decoder column vectors of a trained SAE represent directions in activation space corresponding to individual learned features. Crucially, SAE features are extracted from
contextualized
residual stream activations via reconstruction loss—not contrastive subtraction—making them methodologically independent of difference-of-means concept extraction. We use open SAE suites including Gemma Scope
[
19
]
and Llama Scope
[
13
]
.

3.3
Concept Extraction: Three Independent Methods

A central methodological concern is whether any single extraction method introduces spectral biases. We therefore employ three independent approaches, each operating on different inputs and using different algorithms:

Method 1: Difference-of-means on residual activations (subtractive).

For each concept, we compute the mean difference of pooled residual-stream activations from the final layer:

v_{\text{dom}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(h(w_{i}^{+})-h(w_{i}^{-})\right)

(3)

where
h(w)
is the model’s pooled residual-stream representation for word
w
when presented in isolation. This operates in activation space, not in the static unembedding matrix. As a subtractive method, it can mechanically cancel shared high-variance components between paired words, creating a potential artifact bias toward low-variance directions (see
Section

6
).

Method 2: Sparse autoencoder features (contextualized, unsupervised).

We use pre-trained SAEs from Gemma Scope
[
19
]
and Llama Scope
[
13
]
to extract concept directions from
contextualized
residual stream activations. For each concept, we pass word pairs through the model, record SAE feature activations, and select the top-
k
most differentially activating features. The corresponding SAE decoder column vectors serve as concept directions. No subtraction is involved; features are learned via reconstruction loss on the full activation distribution.

Method 3: Linear probes (contextualized, supervised).

We train L2-regularized logistic regression classifiers on
raw, uncentered
contextualized activations at the final layer, predicting concept membership. The classifier weight vector defines the concept direction. L2 regularization biases the solution toward high-variance directions (opposing anti-concentration), making this a conservative test.

The convergence of all three methods—one activation-based/subtractive, one contextualized/unsupervised, one contextualized/supervised—is the core evidence for our claims.

3.4
Spectral Analysis Framework

All spectral analyses project concept vectors onto the eigenbasis of the language-specific unembedding covariance
\Sigma_{\mathcal{L}}
(
Equation

1
).

Definition 1

(Spectral Energy Distribution)
.

Given a concept vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the eigendecomposition
\Sigma=U\Lambda U^{\top}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}
, the fractional spectral energy at eigenvector
u_{i}
is
E_{i}=(v^{\top}u_{i})^{2}/\|v\|^{2}
. The cumulative energy is
C(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}E_{i}
.

Definition 2

(Cumulative Variance)
.

V(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}/\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)
. We plot
C(k)
against
V(k)
—not the raw index
k/d
—to enable comparison across models with different hidden dimensions.

Definition 3

(Gini Deviation)
.

The signed area between the concept’s cumulative energy CDF (plotted against
V(k)
) and the uniform baseline (
y=x
). Negative values indicate anti-concentration (energy in the spectral tail); positive values indicate concentration (energy in top eigenvalues).

Definition 4

(Spectral Center of Mass)
.

The
V(k)
value at which
C(k)
reaches 50%. An
scm
near 1.0 means concepts encode primarily in low-eigenvalue directions.

For POS probing and split-injection experiments, we partition the eigenbasis at
k=\lfloor 0.1d\rfloor
(top/bottom 10% of eigenvectors). We test robustness to this cutoff by repeating POS probing at
k\in\{5\%,10\%,20\%\}
: the qualitative pattern (6/8 English models with positive gaps, 2 Qwen 2.5 models reversing) is invariant across all three cutoffs (
Appendix

H
).

The Causal Geometry Does Not Aid Cross-Lingual Transport

To determine whether
wca
’s benefit stems from the specific geometric directions of
\Sigma
or merely from its spectral shape, we construct
matched-spectrum fake
covariance matrices:
\Sigma_{\text{fake}}=V\Lambda V^{\top}
, where
\Lambda
contains the true eigenvalues and
V
is a random orthogonal matrix (Haar-distributed via QR decomposition). We run the full
wca
pipeline with
\Sigma_{\text{fake}}
for both source and target, repeating with 5 random seeds across all 17 models
\times
4 language pairs.

\Sigma
for all 17 models, which naturally led us to examine how concept vectors distribute across these eigenbases. The result was a striking empirical regularity: activation-space concept vectors consistently place disproportionate energy in the
low-variance
tail of the eigenspectrum.

Across all 17 models, difference-of-means concept vectors show highly significant anti-concentration (mean Gini
=-0.282
, all within-model
p<10^{-6}
). The SCM gap between concept and random baselines is
+0.169
on average, and crucially, this gap is independent of the background spectral shape (correlation between random
scm
and concept
scm
:
r=0.019

p=0.94
). Anti-concentration is not a ceiling effect driven by eigenvalue concentration at scale.

p=0.73

7.1
Unembedding Vectors Concentrate

We construct a separate set of concept directions directly from static unembedding rows:
v_{\text{unembed}}=\text{mean}_{i}(\gamma(w_{i}^{+})-\gamma(w_{i}^{-}))
, restricted to word pairs where both tokens are single-token in the model’s vocabulary. Strikingly, these directions
concentrate
in the high-variance eigenspace (positive Gini), showing the opposite spectral tendency from activation-space concept representations.

7.3
Interpretation

The dual geometry can be summarized as:

•

Vocabulary space:
The unembedding matrix places concept contrasts along high-variance directions, where they maximally influence logit differences during token prediction.

•

Reasoning space:
Activation-space representations (difference-of-means vectors, SAE features, probe directions) encode concept information in the spectral tail, away from the dominant structural variance.

The transformer appears to perform a spectral rotation between these two regimes during forward processing, though the layer-wise timing of this rotation remains to be measured directly.

8
Why Concepts Anti-Concentrate

The dual geometry motivates a functional explanation. We provide two complementary experiments: a split-injection steering test measuring perplexity degradation as a proxy for grammatical interference, and a POS-tag probing experiment characterizing what the spectral subspaces encode.

Discussion

Scaling behavior.

The Qwen 2.5 family (0.5B–14B) and Qwen 3 family (0.6B–8B) allow us to check whether anti-concentration changes with model capacity. The SCM gap remains positive at all tested scales (
+0.205
to
+0.286
for Qwen 2.5;
+0.215
to
+0.259
for Qwen 3), but does not follow a clear monotonic trend. We interpret this as evidence that anti-concentration is a robust property present at all tested scales, rather than a phenomenon that systematically deepens with capacity. Full scaling data are in
Appendix

D
.

Architecture effects.

Gemma and Mistral models exhibit the highest condition numbers (
1.6\times 10^{5}
to
2.8\times 10^{5}
) and most aggressive eigenvalue concentration, driven by their vocabulary characteristics. Despite this, both Gemma and Llama families show significant anti-concentration, and MoE models (OLMoE, JetMoE) exhibit similar patterns. This suggests that anti-concentration is not specific to dense FFNs versus sparse expert mixtures. See
Appendix

E
for details.

Connection to superposition.

7
demonstrate that neural networks represent more features than dimensions via superposition, exploiting feature sparsity. Our findings suggest a spectral dimension to this phenomenon. If the high-variance eigenspace is “claimed” by frequent, high-information syntactic patterns (articles, punctuation, positional structure), then the effective dimensionality available for semantic concept encoding is
d_{\text{eff}}\ll d
. This forces the network into superposition for semantic features even when
d
is large, because the usable subspace is restricted to the low-variance tail. Anti-concentration may thus be the spectral signature of how transformers allocate representational capacity: syntax gets the “loud” subspace where it can robustly survive the noise of the residual stream, while semantic concepts must share the “quiet” subspace via superposition
[
7
,
34
]
.

Implications for interpretability.

Methods that focus only on top principal components—such as PCA-based visualization or low-rank approximations—risk missing concept-level information. Our results suggest that interpretability tools should attend to the tail of the eigenspectrum. Conversely,
35
show that removing high-order SVD components (LASER) can
improve
reasoning, consistent with our finding that semantic content often appears in lower-variance directions.

Implications for steering.

The interference asymmetry (
Section

8.1
) suggests a practical refinement to activation engineering
[
42
,
29
]
: projecting steering vectors into the low-variance subspace before injection could achieve concept manipulation with lower fluency disruption. This may explain why naive steering often causes “aphasia”—the vector’s projection onto high-variance directions may disrupt syntactic routing
[
1
]
.

The Qwen 2.5 anomaly.

Both Qwen 2.5 models (3B, 7B) show reversed POS probing results and no interference asymmetry, while the closely related Qwen 3 (4B) shows the expected English POS gap. Chinese UD probing shows a bottom-
k
advantage for both Qwen 2.5-3B and Qwen 3-4B, so the anomaly cannot be reduced to a single vocabulary-level explanation. A natural hypothesis is that Qwen 2.5’s relatively flat eigenspectrum (the lowest top-10% variance capture in our sample at 27%) makes the 10% cutoff insufficient to separate syntactic from semantic directions. However, our
k
-sensitivity analysis (
Appendix

H
) argues against this explanation: the Qwen 2.5 reversal persists at
k=5\%
,
10\%
, and
20\%
, while all six English confirming models maintain positive gaps across the same range. The anomaly is therefore likely architectural or training-dependent rather than an artifact of the spectral cutoff. This warrants further investigation into what specific aspects of Qwen 2.5’s training produce a different allocation of syntactic information across the eigenspectrum.

Limitations.

Our concepts are derived from word-level counterfactual pairs
[
32
,
11
]
, which capture morphological and simple semantic contrasts. Whether anti-concentration holds for more abstract concepts (e.g., honesty, sarcasm, planning) remains open. Additionally, our spectral analysis uses an uncentered, uniformly weighted unembedding second moment from a single matrix; centered, frequency-weighted, layer-wise, or activation-covariance spectra may reveal different structure. A layer-wise analysis of how anti-concentration develops through the transformer stack could reveal when the vocabulary-to-reasoning rotation occurs. Our split-injection experiments steer at 4 layers simultaneously (50%, 62%, 75%, 87% depth); per-layer interference profiles were not logged separately, so we cannot report whether the asymmetry peaks at a particular depth—this would be informative future work. The functional evidence (
Section

8
) is strongest on Gemma and Llama; the null results on Qwen 2.5 and Mistral suggest the spectral separation of syntax and semantics may be architecture-dependent. POS probing significance is based on 5 cross-validation folds plus token-level bootstrap intervals; future work should use sentence- or document-clustered resampling. All tested models are decoder-only; encoder or encoder-decoder architectures may behave differently.

Related Work

Spectral analysis of neural networks.

23
and
22
use Random Matrix Theory to characterize weight matrix spectra, identifying heavy-tailed self-regularization.
24
show that spectral metrics predict model quality without training data.
36
demonstrate that small singular values carry significant information in transformers, consistent with our finding that concepts reside in this regime.

Representation geometry.

30
identify Neural Collapse in classification networks.
44
explore analogous phenomena in language models. Our anti-concentration finding is conceptually distinct: rather than class means collapsing toward specific vertices, concepts are pushed
away from
the dominant spectral subspace.

Whitening in NLP.

37
and
14
show that whitening improves sentence embedding quality by counteracting anisotropy. Our results suggest a possible mechanistic explanation: whitening amplifies low-variance directions where many of our measured concept directions reside.

Steering and representation engineering.

42
,
29
,
45
, and
18
develop methods for steering LLM behavior via activation addition.
38
extract latent steering vectors. Our split-injection results suggest that effective steering implicitly targets the spectral tail.

11
Conclusion

We set out to test whether the causal inner product provides a geometrically meaningful basis for cross-lingual concept transport. In our experiments, matched-spectrum randomization finds no advantage for the real causal eigendirections over randomized eigendirections with the same spectrum, suggesting that Whitened Causal Alignment acts primarily as spectral regularization. But the failure revealed a broader pattern in how transformers organize information.

Word-level concept directions—as measured by sparse autoencoder features, linear probes, and difference-of-means vectors across 17 models and 5 architecture families—systematically avoid the high-variance eigendirections of the unembedding covariance. They place disproportionate energy in the spectral tail, where our split-injection experiments on Gemma and Llama show reduced perplexity disruption relative to high-variance injection. POS-tag probing across 8 models shows that the high-variance subspace preferentially encodes syntactic information in 6 of 8 architectures. The static vocabulary shows the opposite pattern, placing concept contrasts along principal axes for maximal influence on token prediction.

This dual geometry—
concepts whisper while syntax shouts
—has immediate practical implications for how we probe, interpret, and steer large language models.
