The neuropsychological impact of early childhood trauma on cognitive development: A narrative review of mechanisms, outcomes, and interventions
Authors: Kalliopi Megari, Kremena Dinkova Genova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch indicates that early traumatic experiences can profoundly affect a child's ability to manage emotions, develop social skills, and adapt effectively. Still, the impact of trauma on children goes beyond just emotional and behavioral dimensions. The enduring consequences of early trauma on the development of children are projected to be significant, particularly regarding its long‐lasting influence on their cognitive functions. Exploring the effects of early adverse experiences on cognitive development is of paramount importance in practice. The main objective of this narrative review is to clarify how trauma interferes with cognitive development by impacting brain structures and functions, as well as to offer theoretical backing for approaches in education, psychology, and healthcare, aiding in the formulation of specific strategies that can foster healthy development for children who have gone through trauma.Keywords: cognitive development: mechanisms, early childhood trauma, interventions, neuropsychological impact, quality of life

INTRODUCTION

Trauma often denotes occurrences involving real or imminent death, severe injury, or sexual violence. These occurrences may be personally experienced, seen in person, or learnt about when they affect others in close proximity. 1 Stressors are broader and encompass life events or persistent situations that challenge an individual's capacity to cope. These stressors might be acute, like job loss, or chronic, such as enduring poverty or coping with a long‐term illness. 2 Trauma is often abrupt and severe, while stressors may be insidious and persistent; both may profoundly affect psychological health. Distinguishing between trauma and stressors is crucial, since each affects mental health in distinct ways. Acute trauma is often associated with powerful, instantaneous responses, such as PTSD, but chronic stress correlates with gradual, cumulative consequences that may result in depression, anxiety, and burnout. 3 However, researchers are increasingly seeing trauma and stress as components of a continuum rather than as distinct categories. Lots of people encounter both forms of adversity (i.e., persistent, challenging conditions or environmental challenges (e.g., poverty, systemic discrimination, chronic sickness); it constitutes a condition rather than a single occurrence), and the cumulative stress is often linked to worse outcomes and increased functional impairment. 4

In developmental terms, trauma may interfere with the development of regulatory abilities and executive functions that usually evolve during childhood and adolescence, potentially heightening susceptibility to future psychopathology. 5 Although many young individuals exhibit resilience, a portion manifests enduring posttraumatic stress symptoms with more extensive internalizing or externalizing challenges. PTSD involves symptoms, such as re‐experiencing, avoidance, negative cognitive and mood changes, and hyperarousal 5 , 6 and often appears alongside depressive and anxiety disorders. Furthermore, compelling evidence indicates that experiencing trauma and PTSD are actually linked to observable alterations in cognitive functioning, including attention, working memory, processing speed, and executive control, which are crucial for both academic success and social growth. 5 , 7

Neurobiological models emphasize changes in the fronto‐limbic circuitry. Increased sensitivity of the amygdala to threat cues, along with reduced regulatory involvement of the prefrontal cortex, may lead to ongoing hypervigilance, irritability, and challenges in managing emotions. 5 Individuals who have experienced trauma frequently find it challenging to interpret social cues and emotional expressions, which can hinder their ability to navigate relationships and engage socially. 8 In addition, extended exposure to stress hormones can also affect negatively the development and functioning of the hippocampus, resulting in challenges with information processing and recall, as well as the ability to differentiate between safe and threatening contexts. Chronic stress can hinder the neural connections, leading to diminished cognitive processing and less mental adaptability. 1 However, neuroplasticity does provide a credible mechanism by which early, developmentally appropriate treatments might facilitate recovery and restore functional trajectories.

Exploring the effects of early adverse experiences on cognitive development is of paramount importance in practice. The enduring consequences of early trauma on the development of children are significant, particularly regarding its long‐lasting influence on their cognitive functions. The influence extends beyond children's academic achievements in their formative years, significantly shaping their overall quality of life as adults. 9 The trauma‐related cognitive challenges frequently appear in daily activities and at school, preventing children from achieving their maximum capabilities. Consequently, investigating the neuropsychological foundations of trauma to reveal its mechanisms influencing cognitive development is extremely important. This narrative review seeks to clarify how trauma interferes with cognitive development by impacting brain structures and functions and also to offer theoretical backing for approaches in education, psychology, and healthcare, aiding in the formulation of specific strategies that can foster healthy development for children who have gone through trauma.

METHODS

In order to investigate the neuropsychological effects of early childhood trauma on cognitive development, this study used a thorough narrative review design. Major electronic databases, such as PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Google Scholar, were used to do a thorough literature search. Combinations of “early childhood trauma,” “cognitive development,” “neuropsychology,” “hippocampus,” “amygdala,” “prefrontal cortex,” “executive function,” “memory,” “attention,” “PTSD in children,” and “intervention” were among the keywords and search terms used. Studies published between 2000 and 2025 were the main focus of the search, while earlier fundamental studies were included when appropriate.

English‐language articles and reviews, meta‐analyses, or empirical research on early childhood trauma (ages 0–6 or broadly defined childhood) and on the outcomes related to cognitive development (e.g., memory, attention, executive function) were evaluated. Research investigating the neuropsychological or neurobiological factors, as well as different intervention studies that concentrate on the effects of trauma on kids or teenagers, was also included. Nevertheless, research that only considers adult trauma without taking into account childhood experiences, or articles unrelated to neuropsychological or cognitive results, and non‐peer‐reviewed sources (unless they offer crucial theoretical background) were excluded.

RESULTS

Neuroplasticity and the consequences of stress and cortisol on cognitive development

The early years represent a distinctive phase for cognitive development, marked by significant adaptability. Neuroplasticity refers to the process by which neural pathways are created and modified in response to experiences, leading to functional alterations in the brain. 10 In the formative years, the brain is particularly susceptible to structural alterations, which may result in alterations within the neural circuits. 11 Children's brains undergo synaptogenesis, during which an extensive network of connections is formed, surpassing the quantity that will be necessary in adulthood. 12 Synapses develop early and diminish due to synaptic pruning, as the child goes into adulthood, pruning back the connections for enhanced efficiency. 13 The process enhances the brain's responsiveness to its surroundings, influencing it in both beneficial and detrimental manner. 14 During safe circumstances, plasticity can foster cognitive growth, positive social connections, and emotional management. Conversely, during unsafe circumstances, the pathways that are reinforced are mostly those associated with fear, resulting in children experiencing heightened stress in the absence of support, which impedes their decision‐making and emotional development. Traumatized children, for instance, are unable to differentiate between safe and unsafe surroundings, leading to pervasive anxiety. 15

The stress reaction is a defensive mechanism initiated by the brain to address possible dangers. The hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis regulates the body's stress reaction, and after trauma, it may become overactive in a child. This HPA axis is part of the neuroendocrine system that secretes cortisol in response to stress, establishing a hormonal feedback loop for stress regulation. 16 The repeated activation of this axis leads to elevated levels of cortisol and other hormones, which can impact growth and synaptic connectivity. 17 Individuals who have undergone childhood abuse exhibit imbalanced cortisol responses. Acute cortisol secretion helps in managing crises, but prolonged exposure to elevated cortisol levels may adversely affect brain development, particularly in children undergoing rapid development.

Cortisol has a detrimental effect on neurons associated with learning and memory, particularly in the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex. The hippocampal neurons exhibit a heightened sensitivity to cortisol. High levels of cortisol can lead to the death or dysfunction of the hippocampal neurons, which has a direct impact on the processes of memory formation and retention. 18 Extended exposure to cortisol may impede the development of the PFC, adversely affecting executive functions. The prefrontal cortex is particularly susceptible to elevated cortisol levels, which may result in notable cognitive impairments in impulse control, behavioral planning, and sustained attention. Stress also influences brain development by interfering with synapse formation and increasing synaptic pruning. 18 Synapses are crucial for neuronal transmission, underpinning learning and memory. Increased cortisol levels not only reduce synapse quantity but also impede the establishment of new connections, hindering children's brains from recuperating from cognitive deficiencies induced by trauma.

Significant brain areas impacted by childhood trauma

Experiences of trauma in early development can also lead to enduring alterations in brain function, particularly affecting three critical areas: the amygdala, the hippocampus, and the prefrontal cortex (PFC) 19 (Table 1). They play a crucial role in identifying threats, memory processing, and emotion regulation, all of which can become unbalanced in trauma‐affected children. 20 Threat include experiences of harm, such as abuse and violence, that impact emotional processing. Deprivation, on the other hand, refers to the lack of anticipated cognitive and social stimuli, such as neglect and poverty, which impacts cognitive development. They elicit distinct neurological and psychological effects. When individuals are repeatedly exposed to trauma, their brain systems are rewired, potentially resulting in chronic fear responses in children that can be challenging to manage. Research has demonstrated the presence of physical indicators in brain scans that reflect neural activity. Brain scans from individuals with PTSD show increased activity in emotional brain areas, while scans from healthy individuals exhibit blood flow in the lower areas of the brain. 21

Consequences of the early trauma and its neuropsychological implications.

The hippocampus is intricately involved in the generation and retention of memories and significantly contributes to regulating the HPA axis. 16 In contrast, the amygdala, the fear center of the brain, is associated with emotional reactions and the perception of threats. 10 Experiencing trauma can result in neuronal reduction in these particular regions, as well as diminished neural connectivity and even a decrease in the overall size. Research indicates that the hippocampus in children who have experienced abuse is notably smaller compared to that of their peers, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 which could contribute to the difficulties in memory‐related tasks. 10 This illustrates the impact of PTSD on individuals, as they experience the feeling of reliving traumatic events and find it challenging to separate their present reality from past trauma. Research by Acheson et al. 26 involving adolescents with PTSD revealed that the individuals with early trauma exhibited significantly reduced hippocampal size. This decrease in size was associated with a range of more intense symptoms, including heightened flashbacks, increased emotional distress, and confusion, among others. These hippocampal alterations result in hindered learning, increased confusion under stress, and persistent fear even in secure settings.

In children who have faced trauma, the amygdala also tends to become more active, leading to heightened reactivity to stimuli that are not actually threatening 10 (Table 1). Research indicates that children who undergo early trauma show heightened amygdala activity in response to negative emotional facial expressions or distressing scenes. 27 Young individuals experiencing PTSD exhibit heightened sensitivity to emotional cues, even in the absence of immediate threats. This may result in symptoms like heightened alertness and emotional outbursts. In another study, Davis and Hamner 28 revealed that structural alterations in the amygdala result in increased volume in the brains of traumatized children. This is likely a result of increased activity stemming from intense fear. When there are interruptions in the amygdala and prefrontal cortex connectivity, the trauma can also lower the brain's ability to regulate proper fear responses. 29 This presents a multifaceted challenge during the transition into adolescence, a critical period for the development of emotional regulation. 30 This may result in the amygdala misinterpreting neutral expressions as menacing, hence exacerbating challenges in children afflicted by trauma. 31 Therefore, additional research is necessary regarding innovative treatment strategies based on our understanding of brain circuitry and the amygdala's role in PTSD, along with the potential benefits of integrating pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy to enhance the management of PTSD symptoms. 28

Last but not least, the prefrontal cortex plays a vital role in enabling critical thinking, managing impulses, and regulating emotions (Table 1). This area of the brain develops at a slower pace compared to the other two regions and exhibits heightened sensitivity to stress. Early trauma can disrupt development and hinder the brain's ability to regulate the amygdala and manage emotional responses. The disparity between an overactive amygdala and an underdeveloped prefrontal cortex results in emotional instability and impaired decision‐making. 10 Disruptions in the dorsal prefrontal networks may manifest clinically as diminished processing speed, decreased working memory, and impaired flexible control of attention, characteristics that might exacerbate avoidance and sustain symptomatology. 32 While the literature on neurocircuitry related to PTSD is vast, findings in pediatric populations are varied, underscoring the necessity for developmentally sensitive designs and for meticulous management of comorbidities and effects of medication. The results must be taken with caution due to the prevalence of small sample sizes, cross‐sectional designs, and inadequate control for confounding factors, such as comorbidities and socio‐economic challenges. 5

Another study indicated that children who have experienced trauma exhibit reduced activation in the prefrontal cortex while attempting to regulate their emotional responses. This reduced activity can create challenges in calming someone during difficult times, potentially leading to impulsive choices and a tendency to embrace the risks. 33 Other studies indicate that early trauma diminishes gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex, leading to decreased attention and behavioral regulation. The continuous activation results in elevated cortisol levels, which disrupt the development of neurons. The reduction in gray matter and the weakening of the neural connections in the prefrontal cortex can impair its capacity to regulate impulse control and maintain attention. 34 This leads to children displaying behaviors akin to ADHD, including tendencies to be easily distracted, hyperactive, and impulsive. The diminished regulation from the area may clarify why individuals who have experienced trauma are more prone to resorting to substance use or self‐harm as a means of coping. 35 This occurs because they find it challenging to handle emotions in healthy ways and require a means to deal with the intense feelings.

Collectively, these essential areas of the brain establish an intricate system that plays a crucial role in identifying threats, assessing danger, managing emotional reactions, and establishing memories. Experiencing trauma during early development disrupts the system on multiple levels. It can lead to heightened threat detection in the amygdala, reduced memory processing in the hippocampus, and delays in regulatory and control functions in the prefrontal cortex. 36 These areas are interdependent, and when functioning in isolation, trauma‐induced communication results in the brain being hyperreactive, emotionally susceptible, and overburdened. 37 The significant alterations observed can clarify the reasons behind the misdiagnosis of children who have experienced trauma, as well as the emergence of symptoms related to ADHD, anxiety disorders, or PTSD. Chronic stress leads to changes in the brain's wiring, creating ingrained fear responses and emotional instability, which significantly alter the developing mind. 38

The influence of early childhood trauma on cognitive development

Additionally, studies show that children who have experienced trauma encounter considerable difficulties in learning new information and accessing memories stored in the long‐term memory; this is, in part, a result of structural alterations and functional deficits in the hippocampus. 39 Children who have experienced trauma often exhibit symptoms of inattention, are prone to distraction, and face challenges completing longer tasks. This challenge might arise from the delayed development of the prefrontal cortex, which plays a crucial role in higher‐level cognitive functions, such as maintaining focus on tasks, impulse control, and selective attention. Experiencing trauma during childhood can lead to notable difficulties in working memory, cognitive flexibility, inhibitory control, and the capacity to develop abstract concepts. 32 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 The challenges faced by traumatized children can significantly impact their academic performance, social interactions, and everyday functioning. This is partly due to the potential underdevelopment of their prefrontal cortex, which restricts the efficiency of higher‐order cognitive processes.

Impact of childhood trauma on executive function

Deficits in executive function related to trauma seem to increase progressively throughout development, potentially becoming more significant when trauma occurs at an early age. In cross‐sectional research examining childhood trauma, DePrince et al. 45 observed that with each 1.0 SD increase in exposure to familial interpersonal traumatic events, there is a corresponding .30 SD decrease in overall executive function in children. Additionally, Cowell et al. 46 identified impairments in inhibitory control and working memory among children who experienced maltreatment, especially when such maltreatment started during infancy and persisted through various developmental stages. Furthermore, these deficits are expected to continue into the teenage years and into adulthood. Retrospective studies examining adults who report experiences of childhood maltreatment indicate a negative correlation between such maltreatment and various cognitive functions, including working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility of adults. 47 , 48 Additionally, findings from a prospective study suggest that maltreatment, especially neglect during childhood, is a predictor of cognitive flexibility at the age of 41. 49

The functioning of the prefrontal cortex significantly impacts working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. 50 Consequently, the relative deficits seen in these areas among individuals who have experienced childhood trauma highlight the prefrontal cortex as a crucial area for understanding neurobiological responses to early stress. In relation to this, variations in the structure and function of the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and amygdala, along with differences in the structural and functional connectivity of the prefrontal cortex to both the hippocampus and amygdala, have been noted between individuals who have experienced childhood trauma and those who have not. 51 , 52 , 53 Teicher et al. 54 conducted a review that highlighted the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal regions—specifically the orbitofrontal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices—as key areas of the brain significantly affected by childhood maltreatment. Notably, the prefrontal regions and the anterior cingulate cortex emerged as the most consistently impacted areas in cases of abuse. These maltreatment‐related changes in the structure, function, or connectivity of prefrontal areas are linked to deficits in conflict monitoring, decision‐making, and emotion regulation in individuals with maltreatment‐related PTSD, in contrast to non‐maltreated controls. 52 , 55 , 56 , 57

The effects of childhood trauma on memory processing

Studies consistently show that children who undergo trauma during important developmental stages face an increased likelihood of memory deficits 10 with both short‐term and long‐term memory. Short‐term memory pertains to the brief retention and processing of information, typically aligned with working memory. Children who have experienced trauma tend to exhibit considerably poorer performance compared to their peers in tasks that assess working memory. 10 They find it challenging to handle multiple tasks at once or to quickly retain and process information. 39 The deficiency in working memory will have a direct impact on their ability to learn effectively, particularly in areas that necessitate the synthesis of intricate information, like mathematics and language. It is important to consider the impact on long‐term memory, as well. The development of long‐term memory in the hippocampus, and as mentioned previously, traumatic experiences can cause the hippocampus to shrink, thereby affecting the encoding and storage of information. The development of long‐term memory relies on the hippocampus, and as previously stated, traumatic events may lead to hippocampal atrophy, thereby impairing the encoding and retention of information. 25 , 58 , 59 , 60 Although they may acquire new information, they struggle to recall and apply it properly when required. 39

The effects of childhood trauma on attention and concentration

Traumatic experiences often lead to significant difficulties for children regarding their ability to control attention, especially during longer tasks where sustaining focus can prove challenging. 32 , 56 , 57 Neuroimaging research of children who have faced trauma reveals that there are often indications of underdevelopment in their prefrontal cortex. 61 This challenge leads to obstacles in maintaining focus and finishing tasks. Children who have experienced trauma tend to be more vulnerable to distractions from their surroundings when compared to their peers who have not faced such challenges. This inclination to become easily distracted is particularly evident during learning tasks that require sustained attention, which further impairs academic achievement and overall cognitive involvement. 10 For instance, lots of children who have experienced trauma struggle to focus when the teacher is giving instructions during class, often becoming easily distracted and inattentive. 62 This deficiency in attention management adversely affects their academic achievement and diminishes their social abilities.

Children who have experienced trauma sometimes find it challenging to concentrate during conversations, resulting in misconceptions and communication issues. Their lack of focus may lead to overlooking social cues or inadequately participating in discussions, complicating the formation and maintenance of connections with peers. As a result, these attentional issues may exacerbate social isolation, sustaining an ongoing pattern of academic and social struggles. Prolonged traumatic events result in persistent activation of the stress response system and elevated cortisol levels. This prolonged state of stress impairs the neuronal network associated with attention regulation, particularly affecting the prefrontal cortex, resulting in attention impairments. Multiple studies indicate that traumatized children exhibit symptoms related to ADHD, indicating that stressful events may intensify the manifestations of attention disorders. 63

The effect of childhood trauma on behavioral and emotional regulation and cognitive control

Challenges in managing emotions, stemming from early traumatic experiences, can result in rapid shifts in mood, heightened anxiety, and impulsive behaviors. The presence of these symptoms is associated with PTSD and significantly interferes with daily activities both at school and at home. A review of research and diagnostic challenges elucidates how emotional dysregulation stemming from childhood trauma and PTSD leads to enduring development and mental health issues. 64 A separate study emphasizes the detrimental effects of trauma on emotional, behavioral, and cognitive development, potentially impacting attention and self‐regulation. 65 The link between the actual effects of trauma and neuropsychological mechanisms resides in how trauma modifies the brain's structure, stress response systems, and ensuing cognitive development. When a child or adolescent undergoes trauma, his or her brain transitions from a state of learning and development to survival mode. Over time, these survival adaptations result in behavioral, emotional, social, and functional difficulties.

Experiencing trauma can actually elicit regressive behaviors in children, causing them to return to the earlier developmental stages, including bedwetting, clinginess, tantrums, thumb sucking, and infantile speech. 66 These behaviors serve as mechanisms for managing stress when stress surpasses the child's capacity to control emotions or adequately assimilate events. Research indicates that prolonged exposure to trauma alters the brain's response to stress, leading to additional impairment in the management of fear. 67 , 68 Neurobiologically, trauma disrupts the oxytocin and dopamine pathways that regulate social bonding and reward mechanisms. Moreover, if a child's initial caregivers were the origin of trauma, their attachment system is impaired. The brain perceives proximity to others not as a source of safety, but as a potential source of unpredictable danger. This regression highlights the significant impact that trauma can have on development, particularly when an individual's sense of safety is compromised. The difficulties in managing emotions are further exacerbated by the compromised functioning of the prefrontal cortex, which hinders their capacity to maintain calm or engage in rational thought amidst emotional shifts. Owing to PFC impairment, the brain exhibits cognitive tunneling, a structural incapacity to perceive alternative solutions or future possibilities. In conjunction with a diminished HPA axis, the individual endures a distressing condition of emotional numbness or intense psychological anguish, perceiving suicide as the sole viable escape from an incessantly “unsafe” environment. Experiencing trauma has been linked to changes in the structure of the cerebellum, which affects its connections with regions that manage fear, impulse control, and executive functioning. 69 Research utilizing functional imaging suggests that dysfunction in the cerebellum due to trauma can result in difficulties with emotional processing, proper stress responses, and adapting to the environment. 32 The anterior cingulate cortex, particularly its dorsal subregion, plays a role in identifying significant stimuli and managing emotions, exhibiting changed activation patterns in individuals with PTSD. 60 , 70

Children exposed to trauma may have difficulties in academic environments or interpersonal connections, since trauma may hinder behavior in the classroom and learning, leading to diminished motivation for education, poor performance in school, disciplinary issues, or absences. Behaviors, such as social disengagement, impulsivity, aggression, and disobedience, may disrupt interactions with peers and adults, adversely affecting social development. 65 Chronic trauma inundates the brain with cortisol and adrenaline, which toxicologically compromises the hippocampus. 71 This impedes neurogenesis and interferes with executive functioning. Working memory is compromised by intrusive thoughts or hypervigilance. Cognitive flexibility diminishes, making it exceedingly challenging to transition between tasks or acquire new, abstract concepts. Regression may affect academic performance, since children with trauma may have difficulties with previously acquired abilities, like reading, mathematics, or the basic classroom routines. As children regress to earlier stages of development, it highlights how trauma can profoundly impact attention and memory. 66 They may also exhibit social regression by becoming excessively attached to teachers and specific peers, leading to a heightened sense of dependency in their relationships. 72 Children may also exhibit dissociative responses, when they freeze and attempt to detach themselves from the situation. It begins to function as an involuntary protective response. Young individuals might inadvertently display this behavior during their learning experiences, whether in educational settings or social interactions, which could hinder their cognitive engagement and potentially affect their social or academic growth. These academic hurdles may elevate the chance of school dropout, diminished future aspirations, suicidal ideation, and prolonged social difficulties. 73

The inability to manage emotions significantly undermines cognitive functioning, creating obstacles for children who have experienced trauma in focusing on tasks or making effective decisions. This lack of cognitive control is evident not just in educational settings, but also in everyday situations. Children who have experienced trauma often exhibit a sense of powerlessness when it comes to addressing challenges, making plans for the future, or managing intricate circumstances. This reduced cognitive control heightens the chances of experiencing feelings of being overwhelmed or even giving up when confronted with difficult tasks. Also, these emotional and cognitive difficulties may result in tensions during social interactions. Children who have experienced trauma frequently find it challenging to manage their emotions and behaviors, leading to difficulties in their interactions with both peers and adults. The challenges in social interaction can intensify feelings of loneliness and lead to a cycle of emotional turmoil and hindered social abilities.

Early intervention and rehabilitation strategies

Early intervention is essential, as it can help avert the lasting effects stemming from childhood trauma. It can diminish the intensity of PTSD symptoms and may also lead to alterations in the brain structures associated with stress management. 74 Creating nurturing environments and fostering positive connections can enhance recovery and offer children the stability essential for their healthy development. In the absence of early intervention, traumatic stress can interfere with typical neurodevelopment and may heighten susceptibility to anxiety, depression, or cognitive impairments. 75 Recognizing symptoms early is crucial, as children might not openly express their distress. Instead, their struggles may manifest through various behavioral changes or academic challenges. Recognizing and addressing these symptoms promptly can aid in avoiding lasting harm and ineffective coping strategies, which can be more challenging to address later in life. 76 Also, early intervention aids in stabilizing the regulation of stress hormones and diminishes the likelihood of developing chronic symptoms associated with PTSD. Many of the early interventions and rehabilitation strategies can be used to mitigate the neuropsychological effects of early childhood trauma.

Cognitive behavioral therapy

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) offers a structured framework for children to assist them in grasping the links between their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors. So, children can recognize, question, and reassess their negative thoughts effectively. CBT primarily utilizes a “top‐down” approach. It focuses on the prefrontal cortex to manage and regulate the heightened emotional responses. CBT effectively enhances the prefrontal cortex, with a specific focus on the dorsolateral region. Guiding individuals to recognize and confront cognitive distortions, it improves their working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. In anxiety and depression, there is an increased activity in the amygdala, whereas the prefrontal cortex shows reduced activity. CBT enhances the inhibitory pathways from the prefrontal cortex to the amygdala. This top‐down regulation enables individuals to assess threats with greater precision and cool down emotional surges. Effective CBT has also demonstrated the ability to change gray matter density in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, as well as restore functional connectivity within the default mode network, which is frequently disrupted by persistent negative thoughts and rumination. CBT includes various behavioral strategies aimed at promoting behavioral change, with key components, such as reinforcing supportive behaviors, managing contingencies, evaluating unhelpful beliefs, and gradually confronting traumatic memories. 77 , 78 , 79 , 80

The anticipated outcomes of this therapeutic approach pertain to each of the subsequent aspects of the framework: Cognitive modification, leading to a decrease in anxiety or negative thinking; Enhanced emotional regulation, resulting in decreased anxiety and depressive symptoms; Alterations in behavior that foster enhanced self‐belief; consequently, the child acquires the ability to confront and navigate through traumatic events and difficulties. 81 , 82 , 83 It plays a crucial role in changing patterns of overgeneralizing negative experiences and fostering healthier, more balanced perspectives on traumatic events. 84 CBT demonstrates effectiveness comparable to, or exceeding that of, other psychological treatments and medications, including those delivered through digital platforms. 85 Progressive muscle relaxation and controlled breathing are valuable techniques that help individuals manage stress responses, foster a sense of control, and enhance resilience. 86 Evidence indicates that the advantages of this therapeutic approach stem from both neurological and psychological transformations. 87 In addition, CBT has demonstrated effectiveness in addressing trauma among preschool‐aged children and adolescents, resulting in favorable outcomes. 88 , 89 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 93 , 94 , 95

Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing therapy

Moreover, Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy, a bottom‐up, somatic method, is employed for children and adolescents experiencing PTSD, using rhythmic eye movements to help process and reduce the impact of trauma. Traumatic memories tend to be held in separate, fragmented neural networks within the right hemisphere and subcortical areas, devoid of temporal and contextual clarity. EMDR aids in the process of memory reconsolidation. EMDR integrates aspects of exposure therapy and cognitive restructuring alongside bilateral stimulation techniques, like eye movements or tapping, to assist individuals in processing traumatic memories and alleviating distressing symptoms. The bilateral stimulation engages working memory, which diminishes the vividness and emotional weight of the memory during recall, facilitating its integration into long‐term cortical storage. The alternating bilateral stimulation encourages communication between the left and right hemispheres of the brain. It activates a reflex that redirects focus from internal discomfort, enabling the mind to process the trauma without eliciting a complete fight‐or‐flight reaction. By reducing the excessive activation of the amygdala, EMDR enables the hippocampus to function effectively. Research indicates that there is an increase in volume in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex after successful EMDR treatment, which helps to transform a memory from an active emotional threat into a historical fact. EMDR has also shown effectiveness, particularly in environments that foster a strong therapeutic alliance and incorporate appropriate relaxation strategies, and in reducing symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety when compared to other therapeutic approaches and control treatments. The evidence from controlled and uncontrolled studies clearly demonstrates the appropriateness and efficacy of this intervention for children and adolescents.

Karadag et al., 96 for instance, investigated the efficacy of EMDR as a therapeutic approach and its impact on anxiety levels in a sample of 30 children and adolescents (aged 6–18 years) diagnosed with PTSD. The participants filled out self‐administered questionnaires, such as the Child Post‐Traumatic Stress Reaction Index Scale (PTSD‐I) and the State‐Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) prior to therapy and 6 weeks post‐therapy. The findings indicated that EMDR is a beneficial approach for children and adolescents experiencing PTSD, effectively addressing both post‐traumatic and anxiety symptoms. Van Pelt et al. 97 evaluated the efficacy of an intensive trauma‐focused treatment plan that integrates EMDR, exposure therapy, and physical exercises for adolescents with severe PTSD. A total of 27 individuals, ranging in age from 12 to 18 years, had experienced one or more interpersonal traumatic events. Twenty‐two of these individuals also met the diagnostic criteria for a coexisting psychiatric disorder. The majority of individuals were referred due to challenges encountered in prior treatment or the anticipation of potential complications. Severity of PTSD symptoms and the existence of a PTSD diagnosis were evaluated using the CAPS‐CA IV at baseline, after treatment, and again at a 3‐month follow‐up. The findings indicate that an intensive trauma‐focused plan that integrates prolonged exposure, EMDR therapy, and physical activity may serve as an effective and safe approach for adolescents experiencing severe PTSD alongside various comorbid psychiatric conditions.

De Roos et al. 98 conducted a comparison of the effects of EMDR and CBT on a cohort of 52 children aged 4–18 years. These children had encountered a disaster in their community, specifically the explosion of a fireworks factory, 1 to 3 years prior, and exhibited symptoms related to the disaster while not undergoing any other treatment. Both conditions produced noteworthy outcomes on self‐report and parent‐report assessments. Outcome measures included: symptoms of PTSD, anxiety, depression, and behavioral issues. There weren't any significant differences between the two groups. The improvements observed after treatment were sustained at the 3‐month follow‐up. For efficiency, a statistically significant difference was observed in the EMDR condition: the results were attained in fewer sessions (average of 3.2 sessions for EMDR compared to 4 sessions for CBT).

Furthermore, Diehle et al. 99 investigated the effectiveness and efficiency of EMDR and TF‐CBT. Forty‐eight children aged 8–18, experiencing symptoms of posttraumatic stress, such as flashbacks, nightmares, severe anxiety and uncontrollable thoughts about the event they experienced were randomly assigned to participate in eight sessions of either TF‐CBT or EMDR. was the symptoms of posttraumatic stress, assessed using CAPS‐CA. Additional outcomes encompassed parental reports regarding the child's PTSD diagnosis and assessments related to accompanying issues. The Children's Revised Impact of Event Scale was utilized throughout the treatment process. Consequently, caregivers of children undergoing TF‐CBT observed a notable decrease in accompanying depressive and hyperactive symptoms. TF‐CBT and EMDR demonstrate significant effectiveness and efficiency in alleviating posttraumatic stress symptoms in children.

Kemp et al. 100 explored the effectiveness of four sessions of EMDR against a 6‐week wait‐list control condition in treating 27 children (ages 6 to 12) experiencing ongoing PTSD symptoms following a motor vehicle accident. An effect for EMDR was observed on primary outcome and process measures, which included the Child Post‐Traumatic Stress Reaction Index, clinician‐rated diagnostic criteria for PTSD, Subjective Units of Disturbance, and Validity of Cognition scales. Every participant initially fulfilled two or more criteria for PTSD. Following the EMDR intervention, the percentage dropped to 25% in the group receiving the treatment, while it stayed at 100% for those on the wait‐list. Evaluations from parents regarding their child's PTSD symptoms indicated no progress, and similarly, there was no change in various non‐trauma child self‐reports or reports from the parents. The progress achieved in treatment was sustained during the follow‐up assessments at 3 and 12 months. The results indicate that EMDR may be beneficial for addressing PTSD symptoms in children.

Play therapy

Play therapy also serves as a highly effective intervention approach, and is an experiential and developmentally suitable approach predominantly utilized with children. It stimulates the brain via creative, sensory, and relational mechanisms. Play enhances the synthesis of BDNF, a protein essential for neuronal survival and the formation of new synapses. A stimulating, engaging environment fosters synaptogenesis in developing brains. Children frequently exhibit insufficient frontal lobe development to articulate trauma or significant emotions. Play therapy engages the limbic system and brainstem via metaphors, narratives, and toys. It enables children to externalize and process stressful experiences within a secure “as‐if” environment, thereby diminishing instinctive survival responses. Play inherently requires executive functions. Engaging in activities, such as negotiating game rules, orchestrating scenarios with action figures, or self‐regulating to maintain character, enhances the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and the prefrontal cortex, thereby improving self‐regulation and attention span.

According to Ray et al., 101 during play, the professional may mirror the child's emotions, reflect on the content, monitor behavior, assist in decision‐making, promote creativity, support the child, nurture relationships, and establish boundaries for the child. Play therapy offers a secure space for children to navigate intricate emotional and cognitive challenges via symbolic expression. 102 Various studies on play therapy have focused on its efficacy for children who have undergone traumatic experiences. For instance, a case series from India highlighted the positive outcomes of play therapy for six children experiencing emotional challenges, including separation anxiety, GAD, ADHD, and depressive conduct disorder, ranging from 5‐year‐olds to 11‐year‐olds. The average number of sessions for play therapy was 11. All children exhibited symptomatic improvement, with an average enhancement of approximately 80%. 103 Schottelkorb et al. 104 also showed that child‐centered play therapy was as effective as TF‐CBT in addressing trauma symptoms. Reyes and Asbrand 105 conducted a longitudinal study to investigate the effects of play therapy on children who have experienced sexual abuse, finding a significant reduction in trauma symptoms among the participants. Triasari et al. 106 explored the impact of play therapy on alleviating traumatic stress in children impacted by earthquakes, demonstrating that this therapeutic approach effectively lessened traumatic stress in young individuals. Individuals often convey their emotions through the sharing of personal narratives and experiences with their counterparts. In addition, Dugan et al. 107 also presented case studies highlighting positive outcomes for children influenced by Hurricane Katrina.

Engaging in family therapy can play a crucial role in assisting children to navigate the negative impacts of trauma by enhancing communication and fostering understanding among family members. In this supportive environment, caregivers discover ways to offer emotional assistance and create a feeling of security, which is essential for aiding children in the gradual restoration of their usual cognitive and emotional well‐being. For instance, Warren et al. 108 observed that parent–child interaction therapy resulted in notable decreases in the recurrence of maltreatment, as well as reductions in parenting stress, child behavior issues, symptoms of trauma in children, parental mental health challenges, and negative parenting techniques. Parent–child interaction therapy (PCIT) consists of joint sessions where parents receive guidance on fostering child‐directed interactions to enhance the bond with their child, as well as on implementing parent‐directed strategies to manage oppositional behavior, thereby reducing the risk of developing conduct issues. 109 Guidance is provided from behind a one‐way mirror, with professionals communicating with parents via a “discreet bug” in the ear. Generally, the therapeutic process involves around 14 to 16 sessions. For caregivers who feel uncertain about treatment, extra initial sessions can be added to the usual approach to boost their willingness to participate in the therapeutic process.

Additionally, the involvement of educational institutions is crucial in the recovery journey for children who have experienced trauma. The compassion and insight demonstrated by educators and classmates can foster a welcoming and secure educational atmosphere, greatly reducing both academic and social stressors. Educational institutions have the capacity to aid traumatized children in adapting to school life and fostering their cognitive development through the introduction of targeted psychological counseling initiatives or individualized learning plans designed to meet their specific needs. Educators and school personnel can identify signs of trauma and implement trauma‐informed methods to support the students. 110 Support from the community and social services is vital, providing necessary external resources for children who have experienced trauma. Community‐based play therapy offers young children a valuable opportunity to navigate trauma in situations where formal therapeutic options are limited. 111 Psychoeducation and coping skills training help children understand their emotional reactions, while also equipping caregivers to create a more stable environment. 112

Research indicates that early psychological and behavioral approaches can mitigate the neuropsychological impacts of trauma. 113 Enhancing secure caregiving connections can protect children from the detrimental impacts of trauma, leading to better long‐term social and emotional results. 114 Neuroplasticity elucidates the enduring effects that interventions can produce. The evolving brain is adaptable, allowing young individuals to possess a heightened ability to reorganize the neural circuits related to managing emotions. 115 Supportive relationships and therapeutic interventions promote plasticity by strengthening neural pathways through the establishment of safety and trust. Research indicates that approaches aimed at enhancing neuroplasticity can help avert the development of long‐term psychological issues stemming from trauma, highlighting the significance of early assistance in fostering healing 32 , 116 , 117 (Table 2). Through collaborative efforts, intervention can achieve lasting effectiveness, fostering positive growth and development. 118 , 119 , 120

CONCLUSION

In summary, this research has underscored the significant influence of early childhood trauma on cognitive development, particularly its repercussions on memory, attention, and executive functioning. From a neuropsychological perspective, it is evident that trauma interferes with the typical course of brain development, especially in vital regions like the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex. This disruption can result in considerable cognitive challenges that impact children's ability to learn effectively and manage their emotions, ultimately affecting their social interactions and academic success. This research emphasizes that early traumatic experiences can negatively impact emotional and behavioral well‐being, as well as cognitive abilities, potentially leading to lasting effects into adulthood (Figure 1). However, this study highlights the critical need for early intervention to alleviate the long‐term neuropsychological effects, stressing the necessity of timely and targeted therapeutic approaches, such as CBT, EMDR, and play therapy. The integration of this knowledge is essential for clinicians, enabling informed therapeutic methods that comprehensively address the physiological and psychological aspects of trauma.

Nonetheless, this study does have its constraints. The emphasis on neuropsychological mechanisms indicates that certain features, like the influence of environmental factors and cultural contexts in shaping the impact of trauma, were not thoroughly examined. Future research could gain from a more comprehensive perspective, exploring the interplay between socio‐economic factors, educational settings, and cultural differences in relation to trauma and cognitive development (Table 3). Longitudinal studies may offer a more valuable understanding of the lasting impacts of early trauma and the efficacy of different interventions as time progresses (Figure 2).
