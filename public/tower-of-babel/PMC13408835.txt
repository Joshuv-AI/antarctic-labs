Single-cell map of the healthy human immune system across the lifespan reveals unique infant immune signatures
Authors: Djamel Nehar-Belaid, Asa Thibodeau, Alper Eroglu, Radu Marches, Giray Eryilmaz, Titas Grabauskas, Luke Trinity, Derya Unutmaz, Chris P Verschoor, Jinghua Gu, Uthra Balaji, Asunción Mejías, Virginia Pascual, George A Kuchel, Octavio Ramilo, Jacques F Banchereau, Duygu Ucar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human immune system undergoes dynamic remodeling from infancy through old age. We profiled PBMCs from 167 healthy individuals (ages 2 months to 105 years): infants (n = 36), children (n = 26), adolescents (n = 20), young adults (n = 24), middle‑aged (n = 16), older adults (n = 33) and oldest old (n = 12) using scRNA‑seq and snATAC‑seq (n = 23). MAIT and γδ T cells showed a “rise and fall” pattern, rising in childhood, peaking in young adulthood, and declining with age. Conventional CD8⁺ T cells were most profoundly altered with age, with decreasing naïve and increasing GZMK⁺ and TEMRA cells. The oldest old had increased TEMRA, adaptive NK, and KLRF1⁺ γδ T cells. Infants showed increased CD16⁺ monocytes and pDCs, constitutive interferon‑stimulated gene expression, and expanded SOX4⁺ naïve T cells. Inflammatory and stress‑response pathways increased with age, while interferon pathways declined. This map provides insights into human immune system dynamics across the human lifespan, emphasizing unique features of the infant immune system.Subject terms: Immunology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Gene regulation in immune cells, Ageing, Translational immunology

Introduction

The human immune system is highly dynamic, continuously shaped by environmental exposures, infections, and vaccinations across the lifespan. Immune development begins in utero, and changes are especially pronounced during early life and older age. In infancy, the immune system undergoes rapid transitions as maternal antibodies wane and adaptive immunity develops1–3. Aging is associated with the accumulation of memory T cells and a decline in naïve cell populations, while T cells also undergo alterations in signaling pathways and acquire innate-like functional properties4. Aging is also associated with chronic, persistent low-grade inflammation (termed as “inflammaging”) driven by dysregulated myeloid cells, as well as the expansion of pro-inflammatory T cell subsets, including GZMK⁺ CD8 T cells4–6. Understanding how immunity develops, evolves, and declines across the lifespan is essential for designing age-appropriate therapies and vaccines for both infectious5,7 and non-infectious age-associated diseases.

Recent advances in high-throughput genomics, particularly single-cell technologies, have revolutionized our ability to dissect the cellular and molecular architecture of the human immune system. Studies using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from young and older adults have identified age-associated immune features, such as expansions of HLA-DR⁺ CD4⁺ memory T cells and GZMK⁺ CD8⁺ T cells linked to chronic inflammation8,9. A comprehensive lifespan atlas revealed that T cells undergo the most pronounced age‑related remodeling, including sharp declines in naïve subsets and expansion of differentiated and cytotoxic phenotypes10. Another recent study mapped CD8⁺ T cells heterogeneity across nine decades, identifying eleven subpopulations with distinct aging trajectories11. An integrative meta‑analysis spanning more than one million PBMCs showed consistent declines in CD8⁺ naïve and MAIT cells and expansion of non‑classical monocytes with age12. Studies of centenarians have revealed signatures of exceptional longevity, such as increased CD4+ TEMRA frequencies13, elevated ribosomal activity and reduced inflammation14, and the upregulation of DNA damage response pathways15. While there are many studies focusing on immune aging trajectories among adults, very few studies have focused on early life, especially the first months of infancy. This is due to logistical and practical challenges of recruiting and collecting samples in this population.

Infancy represents a uniquely critical window for immune development as initial exposures to certain antigens and priming occur and have direct implications for pediatric and overall immune health. Due to their distinct immune system, infants are more vulnerable to severe infections from respiratory pathogens like influenza and respiratory syncytial virus, while paradoxically showing resilience against other viral respiratory infections such as SARS-CoV-2 that disproportionately affect older adults16–20. These contrasts suggest that infant immunity is qualitatively distinct and may also offer insights into protective mechanisms against certain pathogens that decline with age.

In this study, we leveraged single-cell RNA (n = 167) and single-nucleus ATAC sequencing (snATAC-seq) (n = 23) to profile PBMCs from healthy individuals encompassing the human lifespan, from 2-month-old infants to 105-year-old adults. This dataset allowed us to map immune changes across the lifespan with an unprecedented resolution. Our analyses identified infancy as the most dynamic period of immune remodeling with unique features, particularly a predominance of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), an expanded pool of SOX4⁺ naïve T cells, and constitutive interferon signaling in CD4+ T cells. These findings highlight early-life immune features, some of which might serve as possible targets to better support infant immunity and may also inform strategies to enhance immune resilience in older adults.

Results

Infant PBMCs stand apart from those of children and adults

We profiled PBMCs from a cohort of 167 healthy individuals (86 females, 81 males; Supplementary Fig. 1a) spanning ages 2 months to 105 years (Supplementary Data 1) using scRNA-seq. This study integrates scRNA‑seq data generated in-house (n = 95) with publicly available data (n = 67; Supplementary Fig. 1b, c)10,21–23. 1,191,216 cells were profiled across seven age groups: infants (n = 36, 2–24 months, 274,547 cells), children (n = 26, 2–12 years, 206,324 cells), adolescents (n = 20, 12–18 years, 130,442 cells), young adults (n = 24, 18–40 years, 190,624 cells), middle-aged (n = 16, 45–65 years, 120,142 cells), older adults (n = 33, 65–85 years, 191,470 cells) and oldest old (n = 12, 85–105 years, 77,667 cells) (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Data 2). We identified nine major immune cell subsets based on canonical marker gene expression: CD4+ T, CD8+ T, γδ T cells, B cells, NK cells, monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), plasma cells (PCs), and hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Data 3a). Both adaptive and innate immune cells exhibited age-related remodeling (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Innate immune cell frequencies increased with age, including monocytes (R = 0.46; p < 0.001), DCs (R = 0.53; p < 0.001), and NK cells (R = 0.47; p < 0.001). Conversely, most of the adaptive immune cell frequencies declined with age, including CD4+ T cells (R = −0.44; p < 0.001), B cells (R = −0.54; p < 0.001), and plasma cells (R = −0.26; p = 0.0011). Total HSPC frequencies remained stable throughout lifespan (R = 0.007; p = 0.94) (Supplementary Fig. 2a).

We further clustered PBMCs into 18 immune subsets, identifying CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes, CD16+ and XCL1+ NK cells, naïve and memory T and B cells, interferon-stimulated gene-expressing ISGhi CD4+ T cells, and Tregs (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Data 3b). Principal component analysis (PCA) on the frequency of these 18 immune subsets separated infants from other age groups, with PC1 explaining ~70% of the variance (Fig. 1d). This separation was largely driven by age-associated frequencies of naïve CD4+ T cells (~47% of infant PBMCs vs. ~20% in older adults, and ~7.5% in oldest old) and low frequency of CD14+ monocytes (~5% of infant PBMCs vs. ~16% in older adults, and ~18% in oldest old) (Fig. 1e). We also detected ISGhi CD4+ T cells that constitutively express interferon-stimulated genes (e.g., ISG15). ISGhi CD4+ T cells were more abundant in infants, as ~1% of their PBMCs were ISGhi, compared to ~0.4% in older age groups (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). Infant PBMCs were predominantly composed of naïve T and B lymphocytes. Naïve CD4+ T cells decreased sharply after infancy, naïve B cells showed more variation between adolescents and adults, while naïve CD8+ T cells declined in older adults, suggesting that lymphocyte populations follow distinct kinetic patterns across different age groups (Fig. 1g).

Within their respective lineages, naïve CD4+ T and B cells declined gradually, with relatively modest changes between younger and older adults. In contrast, naïve CD8+ T cells showed a sharp and continuous decline across the lifespan (Fig. 1h).

These data revealed that infants’ and the oldest old’s immune systems stand apart from those of other age groups. The following sections further dissect immune remodeling across the human lifespan in each lineage.

Infants display low cDCs and high pDCs

To investigate myeloid cell dynamics, we analyzed DC subsets (n = 11,038 cells): cDC1 (CLEC9A, XCR1), cDC2 (CD1C, CLEC10A), monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs; CD14), AXL+ DCs (AXL), and pDC (IRF7) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 4a). moDCs (R = 0.2, p = 0.038) and cDC2 (R = 0.24; p = 0.0021) frequencies increased with age (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). The largest expansion in cDCs occurred from infancy to childhood, with cDC1, cDC2, AXL+ DC, and pDC frequencies increasing significantly during this time (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b). DC subset frequencies remained mostly stable between young and older adults, with the exception of cDC2, indicating that most DC remodeling happens earlier in life.

Although infants had fewer DCs overall, their DC composition was distinct from that of other age groups. Infants exhibited a predominance of pDCs (~53% of total DCs), while older adults had higher frequencies of cDC2 (~49% of DCs) and moDCs (~11% of DCs) (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 3c).

Monocyte analysis identified three subsets: CD14+ monocytes (CD14; 130,703), CD16+ monocytes (FCGR3A; 32,448), and CD14+ monocytes expressing interferon-stimulated genes (ISGhi; ISG15; 16,475) (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 4b). Both CD14+ and CD16+ monocyte frequencies increased with age, with CD14+ monocytes exhibiting a more pronounced expansion (p < 0.001) between younger and older adults (Fig. 2f, g).

Infants and children had overall fewer monocytes than older age groups, and their composition was distinct. Specifically, infants and children had a higher frequency of CD16+ monocytes (~23% of total monocytes versus ~15% in other age groups) and fewer CD14+ monocytes (~64% of total monocytes versus ~75% in other age groups) (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). During the first 24 months of life, monocyte subset frequencies increased with age, whereas cDC subset frequencies remained stable (Supplementary Fig. 3h–k).

Infants exhibit higher frequencies of immature and proliferating NK cells

NK cells (n = 139,202 cells) clustered into four subsets: CD56dim NK (FCGR3A), KLRC2+ CD16+ NK (KLRC2/NKG2C), CD56bright NK (NCAM1) and proliferating NK cells (MKI67) (Fig. 3a). Mature CD16+ NK cell frequencies increased with age (R = 0.52, p < 0.001), constituting ~5% of PBMCs in infants and increasing to ~10% in older adults and 15% in oldest old (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 4c). The frequencies of CD56bright NK cells remained largely unchanged across age groups. In contrast, frequencies of KLRC2⁺ CD16⁺ NK cells (adaptive NK cells) increased steadily with age, potentially driven by CMV, rising from ~1–2% of PBMCs in infants to ~2–3% from childhood through older adulthood and reaching ~6% in the oldest‑old (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Infants exhibited a distinct NK cell profile, characterized by lower frequencies of mature CD56dim and adaptive NK cells. Conversely, the oldest adults exhibited higher frequencies of mature CD56dim, adaptive, and proliferating NK cells. Proliferating NK cell frequency declined during the first months of life (R = −0.37, p = 0.032) (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 4c).

Infant B cells are enriched in naïve and transitional subsets

B cells (n = 111,405) clustered into six subsets: naïve (IGHD, CCR7), transitional (CD9, MME), memory (CD27), age-associated B cells (ABCs; ITGAX, TBX21, FCRL5, lack of CD27 and IGHD), ISGhi (ISG15, IFI44, MX1), and plasma cells (PCs; JCHAIN, TNFRSF17/BCMA) (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data 4d). Naïve, ISGhi and transitional B cell frequencies were higher in infants and children than in adults and negatively correlated with age (R = −0.53 and p < 0.001 for naïve; R = −0.26 and p = 0.002 for ISGhi; R = −0.52 and p < 0.001 for transitional) (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 4e). Children had higher frequency of plasma cells (PC) compared to adolescents (p = 0.028) (Fig. 4b). Within B cells, memory B cell and ABC frequencies increased (R = 0.32, p < 0.001 for memory B cells; R = 0.26, p < 0.001 for ABCs), while transitional B cell frequencies declined (R = −0.46, p < 0.001) with age (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 4f). As shown in Fig. 4d; memory, ISGhi and ABC frequencies increased rapidly during infancy, while transitional and naïve B cell frequencies declined.

CD4+ T helper cells, CD4+ TEMRAs, and memory Tregs expand with age

CD4+ T cells (468,879 cells) clustered into five major subsets: naïve (CCR7), naïve ISGhi (ISG15), regulatory (Tregs; FOXP3), Proliferating (MKI67), and memory (S100A4) CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 4e). Naïve CD4+ T cell frequencies declined with age (R = −0.56, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 5a), representing ~47% of infant PBMCs, 22% in children, ~20% in young and older adults, and ~7% in the oldest old (Fig. 5b). ~1% of infant’s PBMCs were naïve CD4+ T cells-expressing ISGs in the absence of any reported acute infection or recent vaccination (Supplementary Data 1), compared with 0.4% in other age groups (Fig. 5b). Conversely, memory CD4+ T cells (R = 0.59, p < 0.001) and Treg (R = 0.16, p = 0.037) frequencies increased with age (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Among infants, naïve CD4+ T cell frequencies declined alongside an increase in memory frequencies, whereas the other subsets remained largely unchanged during development (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d).

We further analyzed each of these compartments in detail (Supplementary Data 4f). Tregs were further clustered into naïve (CCR7) and memory (S100A4) subsets (Fig. 5c). Within PBMCs, naïve Tregs were more frequent in infants compared to children (p < 0.001) and young adults (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5d), whereas memory Treg frequencies increased with age (R = 0.49, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Within Tregs, the ratio of naïve to memory shifted from ~75:25 in infants to 25:75 in older adults, marking a clear transition with aging (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 5f). Among infants, naïve Treg frequencies increased with age, while memory Tregs showed a decrease (Supplementary Fig. 5g, h).

Memory CD4⁺ T cells accounted for ~5% of infant PBMCs, increased to about 9% in children, 12% in adolescent and reached ~14% in adults (Fig. 5b). Further clustering of memory CD4+ T cells identified three main compartments: CXCR5+ TFH-like (CXCR5), TEMRA (GZMB, PRF1), and other T helpers (Fig. 5f). T helper compartment (excluding TFH-like) was further resolved into six subsets (Fig. 5g): Th22 (CCR10), Th17 (CSTH, RORC), Th1-like (TBX21, IFNG-AS1), Th2 (PTGDR2, NKG7), Th10 (IL10), T peripheral helper (Tph) cells (IL21) (Fig. 5g). Most memory CD4⁺ T cell subsets expanded from infancy through older adulthood, followed by a decline in the oldest old. In contrast, CD4⁺ TEMRA cells increased in the oldest old, reaching ~10% of their PBMCs (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). The frequency of CXCR5+ TFH-like cells increased progressively with age until midlife and subsequently declined in older adults when compared to middle-aged adults (p = 0.0001) (Fig. 5h).

Although infants had fewer memory CD4+ T cells than other age groups, their memory compartment was enriched in CXCR5+ TFH-like cells, which represented ~60% of memory T cells (Fig. 5i). Dynamics of memory CD4+ T cells during infancy revealed rapid increases in different T helper subsets (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d).

Memory CD4+ T cells were dynamically remodeled across the lifespan, shifting from naïve to memory subsets, with infants exhibiting unique features: (i) high abundance of naïve CD4+ and naïve Tregs and (ii) low abundance of memory T cell populations, with few helper populations enriched in CXCR5+ Tfh-like cells.

Rise and fall of MAIT and γδ T cells across the lifespan

Due to their transcriptional similarity, we analyzed CD8+ T, γδ, and MAIT cells together (n = 273,482), which clustered into seven subsets: naïve CD8+ T (CCR7), central memory CD8+ T (CM; S100A4, CCR7), GZMK+ CD8+ (GZMK), CD8+ TEMRA (GZMB, PRF1), CD8aa (ZNF683, RTKN2), MAIT (ZBTB16, KLRB1), and γδ T cells (TRDC, TRGC1) (Fig. 6a, b, Supplementary Fig. 7a and Supplementary Data 4g). γδ T cells clustered with CD8+ T cells, despite lacking the expression of CD8A and CD8B, consistent with prior studies24. All CD8+ T, MAIT, and γδ T cell subsets showed significant age-associated changes (Fig. 6c). Naïve CD8+ T cell frequencies declined sharply with age (R = −0.79, p < 0.001), constituting ~64% of infant CD8⁺ T cells but only ~11% in older adults, and ~4% in the oldest old (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Frequencies of GZMK+ (R = 0.71, p < 0.001) and TEMRA cells (R = 0.62, p < 0.001) increased with age (Fig. 6c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7a), that together comprise ~78% of oldest olds’ CD8⁺ T cells (~23% GZMK+ and ~55%, TEMRA), compared to ~69% (~20% and 49% respectively) of older adults’, ~39% (~14% and ~25%) of young adults’ and ~18% (~5% and 13%) of infants’ CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 6c). The expansion of these memory CD8+ subsets began during infancy (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d).

MAIT and γδ T cells exhibited a “rise and fall” pattern, as frequencies peaked in childhood and young adulthood before declining in older adulthood (Fig. 6c, d). MAIT cell frequencies were highest in adolescent, young, and middle-aged adults (up to 2.5% of PBMCs) but were rare in infants (~0.3%) (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 7c, d) and lower in older adults.

Consistent with a recent study24, γδ T cells clustered into five subsets (Fig. 6e): γδ1 naïve (TRDV1, CCR7), γδ1 SOX4⁺ (TRDV1, SOX4), γδ1 KLRF1⁺ (TRDV1, KLRF1), γδ2 GZMB⁺ (TRDV2, GZMB) and γδ2 GZMK⁺ (TRDV2, GZMK) (Supplementary Data 4h). The majority of circulating γδ T cells belonged to the Vδ2 lineage (~73% of all γδ T cells). Frequencies of γδ1 naïve and γδ1 SOX4⁺ cells decreased with age, while frequencies of γδ1 KLRF1⁺, γδ2 GZMB⁺, and γδ2 GZMK⁺ cells increased (Fig. 6f, g). γδ1 KLRF1⁺ cells represented >25% of γδ T cells of the oldest old group. A substantial proportion of infant γδ T cells were SOX4⁺ (~25%), compared to 12% in children, ~3% in adults, and <1% in the oldest old (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 7e). Reanalysis of sorted γδ T cell scRNA-seq data from newborns and adults25 confirmed high expression of SOX4 in newborn γδ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 7f, g).

CD8+ T cells significantly remodel across the lifespan. Infant CD8+ T cells were mostly composed of naïve CD8+ T cells. MAIT and γδ T cells exhibited a “rise and fall” pattern. Infants’ γδ T cells were enriched in SOX4⁺ γδ T cells. As expected, there was a progressive expansion of memory subsets with age.

Age‑associated gene expression and chromatin accessibility changes

We identified age‑associated genes across DCs, monocytes, NK cells, B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and γδ T cell subsets and mapped these genes to pathways (Hallmark gene sets) using gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA)26 (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Data 6 and 7). Across both innate and adaptive compartments, aging was consistently associated with the upregulation of inflammatory, metabolic, apoptotic, and stress‑response pathways, together with downregulation of interferon signaling pathway genes, including ISG15 and MX1 (Supplementary Data 6). This pattern was observed in DCs, monocytes, NK cells, B cell subsets, memory CD4⁺ T cells, naïve/memory CD8⁺, and γδ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a–g). Naïve CD8+ and CD4+ T cells exhibited the greatest age-related transcriptional remodeling, with 1098 and 330 genes being age-associated in these subsets, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Age‑associated analyses within naïve CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells identified SOX4 as the most significantly down‑regulated gene (p = 2 e⁻¹²² in naïve CD4⁺ T cells; p = 2.4 e⁻⁴⁸ in naïve CD8⁺ T cells; Supplementary Data 10). Other downregulated genes in naïve T cells included SMC4, a gene involved in the chromatin organization27, and IKZF2, a marker of recent thymic emigrant (RTE) phenotype28 with age (Supplementary Fig. 9b).

We next identified age-associated chromatin accessibility changes using snATAC-seq data generated from a subset of donors (n = 23). Integration of accessibility and transcriptional data revealed that regions gaining accessibility with age were enriched for inflammatory pathway genes in monocytes, including loci near NFKB1 and AHR. In contrast, regions losing accessibility in naïve CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells were enriched for genes involved in TGF-β signaling, including loci near TGFBR1 and SMAD1/6/7 (Supplementary Data 8 and Supplementary Data 9).

Together, these findings indicate a coordinated age-associated shift toward heightened inflammatory and metabolic activation across immune lineages, coupled with diminished interferon and TGF-β signaling at both transcriptional and epigenetic levels.

SOX4+ naïve T cells are enriched in infants and exhibit stemness features

Given the significant age-associated decline in SOX4 expression in naïve T cells (Fig. 7a), we investigated the abundance and characteristics of SOX4-expressing naïve T cells in greater detail. SOX4 has been linked to RTE T cells28, and acts as a downstream mediator of TGF‑β signaling29,30. CD38 expression similarly declined with age (Fig. 7a) and was previously associated with RTEs28. Consistent with these roles, pathway enrichment analysis of age‑associated genes in both naïve CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell subsets revealed downregulation of thymocyte‑associated genes and TGF‑β signaling with age (Fig. 7b). At single‑cell resolution, SOX4 was expressed in ~17% of naïve CD4⁺ T cells and ~10% of naïve CD8⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). SOX4+ T cells were predominantly found in infants, representing ~18% of their PBMCs, compared with ~4% in children, <0.2% in older adults, and 0.03% in oldest old (Fig. 7c, d and Supplementary Fig. 9e). The frequency of SOX4+ naïve T cells declined rapidly during infancy (Fig. 7e, f and Supplementary Fig. 9f). In contrast, SOX4− naïve T cells gradually declined from childhood to late life (Supplementary Fig. 9g, h).

To further characterize the transcription factor (TF) landscape of SOX4+ naïve T cells, we performed ChromVAR analysis, which examines genome-wide chromatin accessibility at inferred TF binding sites36. These analyses revealed that SOX4+ naïve CD4+ T cells exhibited preferential accessibility for the binding sites of TFs related to: (i) stemness (TCF7L, LEF1), (ii) TGF-β signaling (SMAD3, TGIF1), and (iii) Th2 commitment (GATA3, CCR4) (Fig. 8d, e, Supplementary Fig. 10e and Supplementary Data 11). SOX4+ naïve CD8+ T cells displayed a similar TF enrichment profile (TCF7L, LEF1, SMADs) but lacked CCR4 accessibility (Supplementary Fig. 10e). Further supporting the Th2 lineage bias, SOX4+ naïve CD4+ T cells lacked accessibility for the Th1-associated TF TBX21 (T-Bet) (Supplementary Fig. 10g). Binding site accessibility for TCF7L, SMADs, SOX4, LEF1, TGIF1, declined with age in naïve T cells (Fig. 8f and Supplementary Fig. 10h), consistent with the observed age-related decline in SOX4+ T cell frequencies. In addition, binding site accessibility for CCR4 and GATA3 declined with age in naïve CD4+ T cells (Fig. 8g).

These data establish SOX4+ naïve T cells as a key feature of infant immunity, characterized by stemness and thymic emigrant signatures, and epigenetic priming toward Th2 differentiation.

Effects of biological sex and CMV on immune aging trajectories

We examined the effect of biological sex across all immune subsets and age groups. Significant sex differences included increased frequencies of CD14⁺ monocytes in older men, alongside increased frequencies of memory B cells in older women, consistent with our prior work37. Additionally, we observed distinct γδ1 T cell profiles between men and women, with young and older women exhibiting higher proportions of naïve and SOX4⁺ γδ1 T cells (Supplementary Fig. 11). This sex‑biased distribution aligns with earlier work demonstrating intrinsic sex‑related differences in γδ T cell composition and function38. Together, these findings reinforce the concept that biological sex contributes to immune aging trajectories, influencing both innate and adaptive subsets and might explain observed sex differences in immune diseases and vaccination7,39,40.

Latent CMV infection is another major contributor to immune remodeling, promoting expansion of TEMRA cells and reduced adaptive immune diversity41,42. We have quantified latent CMV serostatus in a subset of older adults using IgG ELISA (n = 15). To systematically study CMV’s effects, we used CMVerify to infer CMV status from PBMC scRNA‑seq data43 (Supplementary Fig. 12a). We first used CMVerify on donors with known CMV serostatus, which demonstrated high prediction accuracy (97%), correctly identifying 14 of the 15 donors with available serostatus. Next, we used CMVerify to infer CMV status across the entire cohort (n = 167), which revealed ~55% CMV positivity. Predicted CMV positivity was associated with increases in CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ TEMRA cells, GZMK⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, adaptive NK cells, and Vδ1 KLRF1⁺ γδ T cells, along with a decrease in Vδ1 naïve γδ T cells, consistent across nearly all age groups (Supplementary Fig. 12b). These shifts, including the ones within γδ T cells, confirm known CMV‑associated cell-compositional changes, including from our independent study43. Despite the significant remodeling of γδ T cell subsets with latent CMV, the “rise and fall” pattern observed in these subsets was not explained by the CMV status, as the same trend was also observed in CMV‑negative individuals.

Together, these data showed that both biological sex and latent CMV significantly contribute to variation in circulating immune cell frequencies.

Discussion

We present a comprehensive single-cell map of PBMCs from 167 healthy individuals across the human lifespan. This dataset enabled us to delineate cross-sectional immune remodeling trajectories across age groups and revealed infancy as the most distinct period, characterized by unique immune features and significant remodeling in both innate and adaptive cell compartments. Major findings from this study included: (i) increased frequencies of monocytes and DCs with aging, (ii) more pronounced naïve-to-memory transitions in CD8⁺ than CD4⁺ T and B cells and (iii) an abundance of pDCs in infant DCs, (iv) the presence of constitutive ISGhi T cells in early life, and (v) high frequencies of SOX4+ naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in infants.

All myeloid subsets, including monocytes and DCs, increased with age, with the most pronounced expansion occurring during the transition to childhood. However, cDC1, which constitute ~10% of DC subsets and are responsible for presenting antigenic peptides to CD8+ T cells44,45, displayed a distinct pattern by expanding later, in adulthood, unlike other DC subsets (e.g., cDC2 and moDC). This delayed expansion may be linked to age-associated decline in naïve CD8+ T cells and the concurrent rise in memory CD8+ T cells.

Lymphocytes showed, as expected, a transition from naïve to memory phenotypes with age. This shift was more pronounced in the CD8⁺ T cell compartment than in CD4⁺ T cells, which might stem from three possible mechanisms: (i) cumulative effects of persistent viral infections (e.g., CMV, EBV, VZV)24,43,46, (ii) age-related differences in thymic output favoring CD4⁺ T cells41,47, and (iii) differences in activation and maintenance programs between CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells48. Among CD8⁺ T cell subsets, TEMRA and GZMK⁺ populations showed the greatest expansion, constituting ~14% of PBMCs in older adults, which is in line with previous reports8, which both expand with CMV43. In contrast, CD4⁺ memory T cell expansion was more modest, and we did not observe significant age-associated shifts within HLA-DR⁺ or Th2 cells in this cohort, in contrast to previous reports9,24.

MAIT and γδ T cell frequencies increased from childhood to middle-aged adulthood but declined in older adults, following a characteristic “rise and fall” pattern that has been documented in other cohorts49–52. This pattern was particularly observed in Vδ2 γδ T cell subsets and was not linked to CMV positivity. Age-dependent microbial exposures might be involved in this pattern, as MAIT cells respond to microbial-derived metabolites, while γδ T cells are activated by phosphoantigens produced by microbes53. Their expansion during childhood may reflect increased microbial priming during this window54, while the decline with age could be due to reduced microbial diversity and/or compromised barrier integrity53. Hormonal changes during adolescence and menopause/andropause, which affect commensal communities55, may also contribute to these trends. Age‑related remodeling of Vγ9/Vδ2 γδ T cells has been observed in other cohorts38, where frequencies rise until puberty and decline after the third decade of life, with men showing a more pronounced loss of effector γδ T cell subsets compared to women. We observed significant sex differences within γδ1 subsets, where young and older women showed more naïve and SOX4+ γδ1 T cells. However, we did not detect significant sex differences in γδ2 T cells.

A previous study identified blood signatures of centenarians, including the expansion of CD4⁺ TEMRA cells13. In our cohort, the oldest‑old (>85 years) displayed distinct immune features, including increased frequencies of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ TEMRA populations, comprising approximately 10% and 20% of their PBMCs, respectively. These changes were accompanied by expansions of GZMK⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, adaptive NK, and KLRF1⁺ γδ T cells. These cytotoxic and innate‑like lymphocyte subsets have been associated with enhanced tissue surveillance and effector functions8,56 and may represent compensatory mechanisms that help maintain immune competence in advanced age. However, because all the oldest old individuals10,23 analyzed here were CMV positive, it was not possible to disentangle the relative contributions of chronological age and CMV to the expansion of these cytotoxic populations.

Across major immune lineages, aging was associated with upregulation of genes linked to inflammatory, metabolic, apoptotic, and cellular stress pathways, most prominently in monocytes, DCs, memory CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, γδ T cells, and NK cell subsets. In contrast, younger donors consistently exhibited stronger basal expression of interferon signaling genes, including ISG15 and MX1, particularly in B cells, monocytes, and CD4⁺ T cell subsets. This heightened baseline interferon tone may contribute to the more robust immune responses to vaccination and infection observed in younger adults57,58 and may explain observed heterogeneity among older adults5,7. Future studies are needed to determine how these cell-compositional and cell-intrinsic signatures relate to immune responses in the clinic.

Strikingly, several features of infant immunity were associated with antiviral responses. In infants, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) comprised ~50% of the DC pool, compared to ~28% in adults. Given their potent type I interferon production, the abundance of pDCs may highlight prompt innate responses to compensate for the still-developing adaptive immunity1,59. Additionally, a greater proportion of infant T cells constitutively expressed interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) even in the absence of known acute infection or recent vaccination. This ISG-high state may reflect a pre-activated antiviral program19,60–62, potentially compensating for the immaturity and limited repertoire of the infant’s adaptive immune system.

Infant PBMCs had a high frequency of SOX4⁺ naïve T cells in both the CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ compartments—accounting for ~20% of infant naïve T cells, a population that sharply declined with development and aging. SOX4 expression has been linked to RTEs28, which may possess distinct effector functions, including a reduced capacity for Th1 polarization63 and a bias towards regulatory T cell generation64. Our epigenetic profiling of SOX4+ T cells revealed increased chromatin accessibility at the GATA3 and CCR4 loci, suggesting an epigenetic Th2 bias, which may contribute to the predisposition of infants toward Th2 responses and increased susceptibility to allergy upon early-life allergen exposure64. Indeed, recent studies showed a Th2 skew in infant CD4+ T cell responses in both humans65,66 and mice67. Other studies reported that SOX4⁺ naïve T cells are functionally hyporesponsive to activation in vitro28,68; however, their in vivo roles and responses warrant further investigation. A previous report showed that SOX4 promotes the formation of CXCL13-producing CD4+ helper T cells at sites of local inflammation30. This suggests that a subset of infant SOX4+ CD4+ T cells may contribute to nascent humoral immunity by promoting CXCL13-producing helper T cell development.

SOX4 expression was not restricted to conventional naïve T cells, as a SOX4⁺ γδ T cell population with naïve-like features was abundant in infants and declined with age. A SOX4+ memory CD4+ T cell subset was also identified, resembling previously described tissue-resident cells-expressing LEF1 and IKZF2 (Helios) that are detected in multiple tissues in children (0–10 years)69. These cells may represent a circulating reservoir of stem-like memory T cells involved in establishing and maintaining tissue niches69.

Together, our findings underscore the dynamics and unique features of infant immunity, including enhanced antiviral preparedness and the abundance of SOX4+ naïve T cells, while also providing a comprehensive map of immune remodeling across the human lifespan. This resource lays a foundation for better understanding age-specific immune vulnerabilities and could inform strategies to enhance immune function in both early and late life.

Limitations of the study

While our study provides a comprehensive single-cell map of immune changes across the human lifespan, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our analysis is limited to PBMCs, which do not capture tissue-resident immune cells. Future studies integrating tissue-specific immune profiles could provide a more holistic understanding of immune development and aging. Our cross-sectional design limits the ability to directly infer longitudinal immune dynamics, and prospective longitudinal studies within age groups are needed to confirm age-related trends and transitions. On the other hand, our strategy, including individuals across well-characterized age groups, allowed us to identify major differences in cell population frequencies. Indeed, the age-associated shifts in various immune subsets reported in this study call for further mechanistic investigations, particularly exploring their connections with changes in metabolites and microbiome compositions. Although we provide absolute cell counts in a subset of individuals (n = 5), this information was not systematically collected across the entire cohort. Similarly, CMV serostatus was not systematically quantified across the entire cohort and was inferred using CMVerify43. Despite these limitations, our study included a unique cohort of healthy individuals spanning from 2 months to 105 years of age and offers valuable insights into the cell-compositional and cell-intrinsic changes that shape the human immune system across the lifespan, highlighting unique features of the infant immune system.

Methods

Study design

This study included data from 95 in‑house-generated samples and 67 samples obtained from the publicly available datasets described below. Detailed participant demographic information is provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Pediatric cohorts

Healthy infants were enrolled at primary care offices during well-child visits or in the operating room while undergoing minor scheduled surgical procedures not involving the respiratory tract, as previously described19,62,66. Written informed consent was obtained from all children’s guardians before study participation. The study was approved by the Nationwide Children’s Hospital (NCH) IRB (18-00591) and Baylor Research Institute (#092010-167).

Adult cohorts

Healthy young adults were recruited during 2017–2018 from communities surrounding Hartford, Connecticut, USA. Healthy older adults were recruited during 2017–2018 from communities surrounding Hartford, Connecticut, USA, and Sudbury, Ontario, Canada. Both young and older adults were free from confounding autoimmune diseases, were not undergoing treatments that might influence immune function and composition (e.g., immunotherapy), and were not recently vaccinated prior to blood collection, as previously described7. The study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Connecticut Health Center (UConn Health IRB #14-194J-3, #16-071J-1, #20-130J-1, #18-126J-1) and the Health Sciences North Research Ethics Board (#985). Blood preparation for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

PBMCs were thawed quickly at 37 °C and into DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were quickly spun down at 400 × g for 10 min. Cells were washed once with 1x PBS supplemented with 0.04% BSA and finally re-suspended in 1x PBS with 0.04% BSA. Viability was determined using trypan blue staining and measured on a Countess FLII. Briefly, 12,000 cells were loaded for capture onto the Chromium System using the v2 single-cell reagent kit (10X Genomics). Following capture and lysis, cDNA was synthesized and amplified (12 cycles) as per the manufacturer’s protocol (10X Genomics). The amplified cDNA was used to construct an Illumina sequencing library and sequenced on a single lane of a HiSeq 4000.

Blood preparation for snATAC-seq

For snATAC-seq experiments, viable single-cell suspensions from each sample were used to generate snATAC-seq data using the 10x Chromium platform according to the manufacturer’s protocols (10x Genomics, protocols CG000169; CG000168). Briefly, >100,000 cells from each sample were centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed without disrupting the cell pellet. Lysis Buffer was added for 5 min on ice to generate isolated and permeabilized nuclei, and the lysis reaction was quenched by dilution with Wash Buffer. After centrifugation to collect the washed nuclei, diluted Nuclei Buffer was used to resuspend nuclei at the desired nuclei concentration as determined using a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter and combined with ATAC Buffer and ATAC Enzyme to form a Transposition Mix. Transposed nuclei were immediately combined with Barcoding Reagent, Reducing Agent B, and Barcoding Enzyme and loaded onto a 10x Chromium Chip H for droplet generation, followed by library construction. The barcoded sequencing libraries were subjected to bead clean-up and checked for quality on an Agilent 4200 TapeStation, quantified by KAPA qPCR, and pooled for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 50 bp libraries).

Single-cell raw data processing and sample aggregation

For in-house profiled samples (n = 95), Illumina basecall files (*.bcl) were converted to fastq files using cellranger v3.0.2, which uses bcl2fastq v2.17.1.14. FASTQ files were then aligned to the hg19 genome and transcriptome using the cellranger v3.0.2 pipeline, which generates a gene - cell expression matrix. The samples were combined using cellranger aggr from cellranger, which aggregates outputs from multiple runs, normalizing them to the same sequencing depth (normalize=mapped) and then re-computing the gene-barcode matrices and analysis on the combined data. The sequencing information for each sample included in the study (e.g., number of reads per cell or UMI counts per cell) is shown in Supplementary Data 2.

Data integration with publicly available datasets

Datasets were concatenated using the concatenate() function with batch_key = “Batch,” treating each dataset as an independent batch. Samples exhibiting low‑quality profiles were excluded from downstream analyses.

Scrublet for multiplet prediction and removal

Generally, we expected about 2–8% of the cells to be hybrid transcriptomes or multiplets, occurring when two or more cells are captured within the same microfluidic droplet and are tagged with the same barcode. Such artificial multiplets can confound downstream analyses. We applied Scrublet70 Python package to remove the putative multiplets. Scrublet assigns each measured transcriptome a “multiplet score,” which indicates the probability of being a hybrid transcriptome. Multiplet scores were determined for each sample.

Single-cell data preprocessing, clustering, and annotation

The cleaned (after multiplet removal using scrublet)70 aggregated matrices were fed into the Python-based Scanpy71 workflow (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/), which includes preprocessing, clustering, visualization, and differential expression testing.

Quality control and cell filtering

We applied the following filtering parameters: (i) all genes that were not detected in ≥3 cells were discarded, using pp.filter_genes function, (ii) cells with less than 400 total unique transcripts were removed prior to downstream analysis using pp.filter_cells function, (iii) cells in which >25% of the transcripts mapped to the mitochondrial genes were filtered out, as this can be a marker of poor-quality cells and (iv) cells with >3500 genes were considered outliers and discarded.

Data normalization, log transformation, and scaling

We normalized the data using the pp.normalize_per_cell function. Thus, library-size normalization was performed based on the gene expression for each barcode by scaling the total number of reads per cell to 10,000. We log-transformed the data using the pp.log1p function and scaled to unit variance using the pp.scale function (with the following parameters: max_value = 10). The 2000 highly variable genes (HVG) were identified using the filter_genes_dispersion function (with the following parameters: min_mean = 0.0125, max_mean = 3, min_disp = 0.5).

Linear dimensional reduction using PCA

To reduce the dimensionality of the data, we ran PCA using the tl.pca function, which reveals the main axes of variation and denoises the data. The contribution of each PC to the total variance was assessed using the pl.pca_variance_ratio function.

Neighborhood graph computing, embedding, and clustering

The neighborhood graph of cells was computed based on the PCA representation of the data matrix, using the pp.neighbors function (with the following parameters: n_neighbors=10, n_pcs=40). The neighborhood graph was then embedded using UMAP72 (tl.umap function) and visualized using pl.umap function. We finally used the Leiden graph-based clustering using the tl.leiden function with resolution = 1.2.

Batch effect correction

To account for the technical source of variation due to chemistry (V2 vs. V3), datasets and 10X runs (eight samples per run), we applied Harmony73 for batch correction, as detailed in the following commands:

sc.tl.umap(har_corrected_object, min_dist = 0.3, n_components = 3)

sc.tl.leiden(har_corrected_object, resolution = 1.2, key_added = ‘Res1_2_Harm’)

Finding marker genes/evaluation of cluster identity

To annotate the clusters generated from the Harmony corrected object, we used both differential expression analysis between clusters and classification based on putative marker gene expression. We applied the tl.rank_genes_groups function to compute a ranking for the differential genes in each subcluster, comparing each cluster to the rest of the cell using the Wilcoxon test. We only consider subclusters that showed distinct transcriptomic programs. The top 100 marker genes for clusters and subclusters are included in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4.

Subclustering parameters and data cleaning

We only considered subclusters defined by distinct gene sets by merging similar ones. Based on the number of cells and to avoid over-clustering, we used different clustering resolutions. Subcluser cell frequencies across donors are available in Supplementary Data 5. We performed hierarchical clustering at four resolutions. Level 1 (LS_L1) consisted of 9 broad immune subsets; Level 2 (LS_L2) resolved 19 clusters; Level 3 (LS_L3) further refined these into 32 subsets; and Level 4 (LS_L4) provided the highest resolution, identifying 46 subsets.

Automatic cell annotation using CellTypist74

To improve cell annotation, CellTypist was used with two models: (i) the Allen Institute for Immunology Level 3 (High Resolution, i.e., AIFI_L3) to annotate all PBMCs and (ii) a model trained on sorted CD4+ T cells from Balasubramanian et al.75 was used to refine annotations within the memory CD4⁺ T cell compartment.

Differential gene expression analysis for age-associated changes

For each subset, we constructed sample‑level pseudobulk counts (genes × samples) by aggregating cell counts per sample (sample_id), requiring ≥2 unique samples and minimal expression (≥10 total counts and detection in ≥3 samples). Age effects on gene expression were modeled as a continuous variable scaled per decade (10 years) using DESeq276 (pyDESeq2), adjusting for platform and batch (Platform, Studies). Differential expression significance was defined as FDR‑adjusted p < 0.05 with |log2 fold‑change| > 0.3. Lists of genes that exhibited significant age‑associated up‑ or down‑regulation are provided in Supplementary Data 6.

Differential gene expression analysis for SOX4+ and SOX4− comparisons

Differential expression between SOX4⁺ and SOX4⁻ naïve T cell populations was assessed within CD4+ and CD8+ lineages. Samples with fewer than 25 cells in the respective population were excluded. Genes in fewer than 33% of the samples were excluded. Sample and gene filtered count matrices were then filtered and normalized in EdgeR77 (version 4.1.26) using filterByExpr and calcNormFactors functions. Differential gene expression analyses were performed using the estimateDisp and glmQLFTest functions, blocking on sample identifier to perform pairwise comparisons between SOX4+ and SOX4− subsets within the same sample. P-values were adjusted using p.adjust (method=fdr), and differentially expressed genes were identified using abs(logFC) > 0.3 and adjusted p value < 0.05. Corrected log2CPM count matrices for visualization purposes were obtained by applying ComBat78 using the sample identifier as the batch variable. Lists of genes that exhibited significant up‑ or down‑regulation are provided in Supplementary Data 10.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA26) on age-associated genes

We performed preranked GSEA (pygsea) across lineage‑level differential expression tables. For each DESeq2 result, we constructed a preranked gene list using the Wald statistic when available, or a signed –log₁₀(p) score derived from the reported p‑values and the sign of the log₂ fold‑change. Preranked GSEA was run with GSEApy (permutation = 1000; seed = 6) against the MSigDB Hallmark collection, retaining gene sets with sizes between 10 and 500. Enrichment outputs were aggregated across subsets (see Supplementary Data 7). fGSEA79 package was used to visualize the data.

snATAC-seq data processing

snATAC-seq reads were aligned (10x Genomics GRCh38 reference 2020-A) and processed using Cell Ranger ATAC v2.1.0. High-quality single-nucleus data were selected based on the following criteria: percent of reads within exclusion list regions <5%, nucleosome signal <4, percent of fragments in peaks >15%, number of peak region fragments >3000, percent fragments at transcription start sites (TSS) >10%, and percent mitochondrial read fragments <10%. Multiplets were identified by applying AMULET80 on each sample independently using FDR < 5%. Clusters with a high frequency of multiplets were excluded, followed by the removal of all remaining multiplets identified by AMULET.

To define a unified set of peaks (genomic regions) for initial clustering, individual sample peaks from Cell Ranger ATAC were combined by merging peaks if they overlapped by 1 base pair. Peaks with lengths <20 bp; >10,000 bp, falling on chromosome Y, or represented by fewer than 3 samples were excluded. Read count matrices were generated from the remaining peaks for each sample and were concatenated and normalized by applying a Python reimplementation of the term frequency inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) normalization method in Signac81. Scanpy objects were generated using the combined TF-IDF normalized matrix, and singular value decomposition (SVD) was performed using the pca function in Scanpy with zero_center=False to perform truncated SVD. To measure read counts at TSS and gene bodies, gene activity scores were computed using a Python reimplementation of the gene activity score method described in Signac81. Gene activity scores were quantified using reads within the gene body and 2000 base pairs upstream from the TSS using TSS and transcription termination sites from UCSC hg38 Refflat database as a ref.82.

snATAC-seq label transfer from scRNA-seq

Nuclei/cells from snATAC-seq and scRNA-seq were divided into B, CD4+ T, and CD8+ T/γδ T, NK, and Myeloid subsets based on gene activity scores and gene expression values, respectively. Within each subset, cluster annotations from scRNA-seq were transferred onto snATAC-seq cells using FindTransferAnchors with reduction=“CCA” and TransferData functions in Signac (version 1.7.0)81 and Seurat (version 4.1.1)83. Clusters identified in snATAC-seq data were assigned scRNA-seq annotations based on transfer label probability scores and gene activity scores of known marker genes.

Differential accessibility analysis for age-associated changes

For each subset where at least 10 samples (out of 23) had >50 nuclei, we constructed sample‑level pseudobulk counts (peaks × samples) by aggregating cell counts per sample (sample_id). Peaks were recalled for each subset using MACS284. Peaks within exclusion list regions (e.g., high repeat regions) and chrY were excluded. Peaks were further filtered, requiring ≥10 total counts and detection in ≥3 samples. Age effects on gene expression were modeled as a continuous variable scaled per year using DESeq276 (pyDESeq2). Differential accessibility significance was defined as FDR‑adjusted p < 0.05 with |log2 fold‑change| > 0.03.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on age-associated peaks

We performed preranked GSEA (pygsea) across lineage‑level differential accessibility tables. For each DESeq2 result, genes were ranked by the product of log(1 + number of associated peaks) and the mean log2 fold change in accessibility at those peaks. Preranked GSEA was run with GSEApy (permutation = 1000; seed = 6) against the MSigDB Hallmark collection, retaining gene sets with sizes between 10 and 500. Enrichment outputs were aggregated across subsets (see Supplementary Data 9). The fGSEA package was used to visualize the data.

Differential accessibility analysis for SOX4+ and SOX4− comparisons

Aggregated raw read count matrices were obtained for each annotated subset using peaks called for the respective subset to define the genomic regions used in these matrices. Samples with fewer than 50 cells in the respective population were excluded. Peaks are accessible in fewer than 33% of the samples at greater than 10% of cells for the sample. Sample and peak filtered count matrices were then filtered and normalized in EdgeR77 (version 4.1.26) using filterByExpr and calcNormFactors functions. Differential accessibility analyses were performed using the estimateDisp and glmQLFTest functions, blocking on sample identifier to perform pairwise comparisons between SOX4+ and SOX4− within the same sample. P values were adjusted using p.adjust (method=fdr), and differentially accessible peaks were identified using abs(logFC) > 0.5 and adjusted p value < 0.05. Corrected log2CPM count matrices for visualization purposes were obtained by applying ComBat using sample ID as the batch variable. Lists of peaks that exhibited significant up‑ or down‑regulation are provided in Supplementary Data 10.

GSEA for SOX4+ and SOX4− comparisons

Genes were ranked using sign(log2 Fold change)*-log(p-value) for differential expression analysis or as the product of log(1 + number of associated peaks) and the mean log2 fold change in accessibility at those peaks for differential peak analysis. Enrichments were calculated using the fGSEA package in R.

Transcription factor motif binding site accessibility for SOX4+ and SOX4− comparisons

Transcription factor motif binding sites accessibility deviations were calculated using chromVAR version 1.16.036. For each PBMC subset, genomic regions were defined by extending MACS2 summit positions by ± 250 base pairs (bp) to generate read count matrices. TF motif deviations were then calculated using computeDeviations in chromVAR, using motifs from getJasparMotifs(), (CORE set JASPAR 202485, homo sapiens). Motifs used for downstream analyses were selected based on TFs with expression >0 within the respective PBMC subset. Paired tests were applied on median motif deviation scores within the same donor (SOX4+ and SOX4− cells) using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons within each PBMC subset using Benjamini/Hochberg procedure.

Cytomegalovirus serostatus in older adults

CMV serostatus was determined by ELISA using the CMV IgG ELISA kits (Aviva Systems Biology and Genesis Diagnostics Ltd; see Supplementary Data 1). Serum samples were thawed at room temperature, diluted, and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Calculation of the results was done based on the appropriate controls included in the kit.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R/4.0.2. Tests were used to determine data distribution, and depending on the normality of the data, comparisons were performed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed rank test (for two groups) or the Student t-test (for two groups, parametric) with two-tailed P values unless otherwise stated.
