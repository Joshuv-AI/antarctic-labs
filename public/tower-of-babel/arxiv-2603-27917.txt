Contextuality of quantum non-demolition measurement via state discrimination Preprint: APS/123-QED
Min Namkung, Ilhwan Kim, Hyang-Tag Lim

Abstract
Quantum non-demolition measurements facilitate various quantum technologies, including quantum communication. Notably, their operational structure can be replicated by a classical model–referred to as a noncontextual model–making it crucial to identify which features prevents such models from reproducing the corresponding quantum measurements. In this work, we theoretically demonstrate contextual features inherent in the structure of quantum non-demolition measurements. These features not only reveal the nonclassicality of unambiguous state discrimination, but also extend to sequential unambiguous discrimination and probabilistic quantum cloning, both of which involve post-measurement states. Moreover, our analysis extends to noisy scenarios, highlighting its potential relevance for practical implementations. We believe that our results broaden the scope of observing nonclassicality in quantum systems and ultimately contribute to the advancement of various quantum technologies.

The aforementioned contextuality-related tasks are primarily formulated within a prepare-and-measure scenario between a sender and a receiver. Notably, quantum theory also allows for quantum non-demolition measurements

, which do not completely extract all the information from the prepared state

. This enables various schemes such as sequential unambiguous state discrimination

In probabilistic quantum cloning, it is possible to produce perfect copies with a certain probability. Moreover, quantum non-demolition measurements have been experimentally demonstrated in various platforms, such as optical systems

and spin systems

These observations suggest that contextual aspects of quantum non-demolition measurements may provide additional advantages for more sophisticated quantum information tasks that are experimentally accessible. However, one should be cautious: as in randomness certification, a noncontextual measurement formalism can deceptively mimic the quantum description

;
footnote
, despite fundamental differences. This raises the following question: to what extent can quantum non-demolition measurements be reproduced by a noncontextual model, and where does this reproduction break down?

In this work, we theoretically demonstrate contextual features of quantum non-demolition measurements and systematically address the above question. We first formulate quantum non-demolition measurements within the noncontextual framework introduced by Spekkens
r.w.spekkens
. We then show that, although quantum non-demolition measurements can be equivalently described within both quantum and noncontextual models at the operational level, there exist regimes in which the noncontextual framework fails to reproduce the quantum predictions. We use this observation to establish contextual advantages in various protocols, including optimal unambiguous discrimination, sequential unambiguous discrimination, and probabilistic quantum cloning. Moreover, our formulation extends to noisy scenarios, highlighting its practical relevance, and clarifies the origin of contextuality enhancement in maximal-confidence discrimination

;
k.flatt2

and communication

II
Preliminaries

We first briefly introduce the formulation of states, transformations, and measurements based on the noncontextual ontological model proposed in Refs.
r.w.spekkens
;
d.schmid
. In this framework, a state is described by a probability distribution over a hidden variable
\lambda\in\Omega
, denoted by
\mu(\lambda)
. Here,
\lambda
and
\mu(\lambda)
are referred to as the ontic state and the epistemic state, respectively. By definition,
\mu(\lambda)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\ \ \forall\lambda\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}d\lambda\,\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=1.

(1)

A transformation from one state to another is described by a non-negative function
\mathcal{L}(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda)
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,\mathcal{L}(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda)=1,\ \ \forall\lambda\in\Omega,

(2)

which induces the transformed epistemic state

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega}d\lambda\,\mathcal{L}(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda)\,\mu(\lambda).

(3)

A measurement
\mathcal{M}
with discrete outcomes
k
is described by response functions
\xi_{k}(\lambda)
satisfying

\displaystyle\xi_{k}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\ \ \forall k,\ \forall\lambda\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\xi_{k}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=1,\ \ \forall\lambda\in\Omega.

(4)

Using this formalism, the probability of obtaining outcome
k
when measuring the transformed epistemic state is given by

\displaystyle p(k|\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L},\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,\xi_{k}(\lambda^{\prime})\mu^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}\times\Omega}d\lambda^{\prime}d\lambda\,\xi_{k}(\lambda^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda)\mu(\lambda),

(5)

where “
\times
” denotes the Cartesian product. This expression shows that the framework provides an operational description of physical processes without invoking additional postulates.

III.1
Replicating non-demolition measurement

Remarkably, quantum non-demolition measurements take the following structure, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a): preparation of an auxiliary system; interaction between the system to be measured and the auxiliary system; and a direct measurement on the auxiliary system to extract information about the initial state
m.a.neumark
. It is also worth noting that the auxiliary-state preparation and the interaction can be unified into a single quantum operation, represented by an isometry from a Hilbert space to an extended Hilbert space
p.-x.chen
;
y.sun
;
s.franke-arnold
;
m.agnew
;
s.goel
;
k.-m.hu
.

Under this structure, we reproduce the non-demolition measurement within the noncontextual model, as shown in Fig.
1
(b). Let us consider an initial epistemic state
\mu(\lambda_{\rm S})
defined on a system
\rm S
. The state to be measured is transformed by a map
\mathcal{L}
from the ontic space
\Omega_{\rm S}
to the extended space
\Omega_{\rm S}^{\prime}\times\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime}
, resulting in

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega_{\rm S}}d\lambda_{\rm S}\,\mathcal{L}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S})\mu(\lambda_{\rm S}).

(6)

The resulting epistemic state is locally measured by a direct measurement, described by a set of response functions
\Pi=\{\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\}
on
\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime}
satisfying

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\geq 0,

\displaystyle\ \ \ \forall k,\ \forall\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})=1,

\displaystyle\ \ \ \forall\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\pi_{j}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})=\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\delta_{jk},

\displaystyle\ \ \ \forall j,\ \forall k,\ \forall\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime},

(7)

with the Kronecker delta
\delta_{jk}
. This implies that the support spaces between
\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})
and
\pi_{k^{\prime}}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})
with
k\not=k^{\prime}
are orthogonal with each other.

Substituting Eqs. (
6
) and (
III.1
) into Eq. (
II
), the probability of obtaining outcome
k
is given by

\displaystyle p(k|\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd},\mu)=\int_{\Omega_{\rm S}^{\prime}\times\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime}}d\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime}d\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\mu^{\prime}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Omega_{\rm S}^{\prime}\times\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime}\\
\times\Omega_{\rm S}\end{subarray}}d\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime}d\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}d\lambda_{\rm S}\pi_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S})\mu(\lambda_{\rm S}),

(8)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}=(\Pi,\mathcal{L})
denotes the non-demolition measurement. This description is compatible with the general measurement formalism in Eq. (
II
), as discussed in Appendix A.1.

We further note that, since the isometry in quantum non-demolition measurements preserves the fidelity between quantum states on a closed system, the transformation
\mathcal{L}
must preserve

\displaystyle\Big(\mu(\lambda_{\rm S}),\nu(\lambda_{\rm S})\Big)_{\Omega_{\rm S}}=\Big(\mu^{\prime}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}),\nu^{\prime}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\Big)_{\Omega_{\rm S}^{\prime}\times\Omega_{\rm A}^{\prime}},

(9)

where
\big(\mu(\lambda),\nu(\lambda)\big)_{\Omega}
denotes the confusability between
\mu(\lambda)
and
\nu(\lambda)
on
\Omega
, defined as

\displaystyle\big(\mu(\lambda),\nu(\lambda)\big)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{supp}[\mu(\lambda)]}d\lambda\,\nu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{supp}[\nu(\lambda)]}d\lambda\,\mu(\lambda),

(10)

with
\mathrm{supp}[\mu(\lambda)]
denoting the support of
\mu(\lambda)
. The confusability quantifies the overlap between two epistemic states and corresponds to the overlap
|\langle\psi_{\mu}|\psi_{\nu}\rangle|^{2}
between the associated quantum states

\mathcal{L}
acts on a closed system, and thus any increase or decrease in confusability arising from information exchange with an external system is not allowed. Therefore, it is rational to consider that the confusability is preserved as described by Eq. (
9

III.2
Optimal unambiguous state discrimination

We quantitatively analyze the capability of non-demolition measurements in several discrimination tasks. We first consider unambiguous state discrimination, in which two epistemic states
\mu_{1}(\lambda_{\rm S})
and
\mu_{2}(\lambda_{\rm S})
are discriminated without error. To this end, a non-demolition measurement can be constructed using the following transformation, as illustrated in Fig.
2
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\rm S}}d\lambda_{\rm S}\,\mathcal{L}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S})\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mu}_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime})\left\{\alpha_{j}{\sigma}_{j}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})+(1-\alpha_{j}){\sigma}_{0}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\right\}.

(11)

Here,
\alpha_{j}\in[0,1]
denotes the probability of obtaining outcome
j
. The state
\widetilde{\mu}_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime})
represents the post-measurement epistemic state, and
{\sigma}_{k}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})
with
k\in\{j,0\}
are epistemic states that are orthogonal to each other, perfectly distinguished by the direct measurement as defined in Eq. (
III.1
). When the outcome is
j\neq 0
, the prepared state
\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S})
can be identified without error; otherwise, the outcome corresponds to failure.

Given prior probabilities
q_{j}
for
\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S})
, the average success probability is

\displaystyle P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}

\displaystyle=q_{1}p(1|\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd},\mu_{1})+q_{2}p(2|\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd},\mu_{2})

\displaystyle=q_{1}\alpha_{1}+q_{2}\alpha_{2},

(12)

which coincides with the corresponding expression in the quantum model.

The conditions on
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
under which
\mathcal{L}
in Eq. (
III.2
) is physically valid follow from the confusability-preserving condition in Eq. (
III.1
) (see Appendix B.1 for details):

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Big(\mu_{1}(\lambda_{\rm S}),\mu_{2}(\lambda_{\rm S})\Big)_{\Omega_{\rm S}}}_{=c}=\underbrace{\Big(\widetilde{\mu}_{1}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime}),\widetilde{\mu}_{2}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime})\Big)_{\Omega_{\rm S}^{\prime}}}_{=c^{\prime}}(1-\alpha_{k}),

(13)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=1-\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}.

(14)

Since
\alpha_{k}\in[0,1]
, it follows that
c^{\prime}\geq c
. This suggests that it is physically valid to reduce the region of
\alpha_{k}
to
[0,1-c]
, making a right rectangular region
[0,1-c]\times[0,1-c]
. Furthermore, all response functions derived from Eq. (
III.2
) correspond to points
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
lying below a straight line with intercepts in
[0,1-c]
, as shown in Appendix B.2. Consequently, the physically allowed region is given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\geq 0,\ \forall k\in\{1,2\},

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\leq 1-c.

(15)

Maximizing Eq. (
III.2
) over all
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
satisfying Eq. (
III.2
) yields

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}=\max_{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}=\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}(1-c),

(16)

recovering the result of previous work
j.shin2
.

By contrast, when
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
correspond to a quantum non-demolition measurement, they must satisfy
m.namkung_anal

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\geq 0,\ \forall k\in\{1,2\},

\displaystyle(1-\alpha_{1})(1-\alpha_{2})\geq c.

(17)

This leads to a maximum success probability that exceeds
\max_{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}
when the prior probabilities are sufficiently close (
|q_{1}-q_{2}|
small), and does not otherwise
g.jaeger
.

Both Eqs. (
III.2
) and (
III.2
) indicate that the noncontextual framework imposes stronger constraints on the structure of non-demolition measurements than the quantum model. Consequently, although the noncontextual model can reproduce the structure of non-demolition measurements, as in Eq. (
III.2
), it fails to fully replicate discrimination tasks in which states are identified without error.

III.3
Sequential unambiguous state discrimination

We remark that non-demolition measurements do not completely destroy the information contained in the initial state. This implies that the remaining information can be accessed by a subsequent receiver who also performs unambiguous state discrimination, a scenario referred to as sequential unambiguous state discrimination
j.a.bergou_seq
. For simplicity, we consider two consecutive receivers discriminating between two epistemic states
\mu_{1}(\lambda_{\rm S})
and
\mu_{2}(\lambda_{\rm S})
, as shown in Fig.
3
. This scenario can be generalized to
N
receivers.

The non-demolition measurement performed by the
r
-th receiver is described by

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\rm S}}d\lambda_{\rm S}\,\mathcal{L}^{(r)}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S})\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S})

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}^{(r)}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime})\left\{\alpha_{j}^{(r)}{\sigma}_{j}^{(r)}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})+(1-\alpha_{j}^{(r)}){\sigma}_{0}^{(r)}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\right\}.

(18)

Here,
\alpha_{j}^{(r)}
denotes the probability of obtaining outcome
j
, and
\{{\sigma}_{k}^{(r)}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})\}_{k=0}^{2}
describes the orthogonal epistemic states.

The probability that both receivers successfully identify the state is

\displaystyle P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\{1,2\}}q_{k}\,p(k|\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}^{(1)},\mu_{k})\,p(k|\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}^{(2)},\mu_{k}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=q_{1}\alpha_{1}^{(1)}\alpha_{1}^{(2)}+q_{2}\alpha_{2}^{(1)}\alpha_{2}^{(2)}.

(19)

This quantity is maximized over
\alpha_{j}^{(r)}\geq 0
subject to

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{(1)}+\alpha_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\leq 1-c,

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{(2)}+\alpha_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\leq 1-c^{(1)},

(20)

where
c^{(1)}
denotes the confusability between
\mu_{1}^{(1)}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime})
and
\mu_{2}^{(1)}(\lambda_{\rm S}^{\prime})
.

The maximum success probability is obtained as

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}^{(1)},\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}^{(2)}}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}=\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}(1-\sqrt{c})^{2},

(21)

as derived in Appendix C. This coincides with the case in which only a single quantum state is discriminated using a quantum non-demolition measurement.

For comparison, the maximum success probability in the quantum model under equal priors is
j.a.bergou_seq
;
c.-q.pang

\displaystyle\max P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(Q)}=\begin{cases}(1-c^{1/4})^{2},&c<\sqrt{3-2\sqrt{2}},\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{c})^{2},&c\geq\sqrt{3-2\sqrt{2}}.\end{cases}

(22)

This shows that as
c
increases, the contextual advantage in sequential unambiguous state discrimination diminishes. In particular, the noncontextual model fails to reproduce the quantum result only in the regime of small confusability.

When the number of receivers is generalized to
N
, the maximum success probability becomes

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{\rm nd}^{(N)}}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}=\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}(1-c^{1/N})^{N},

(23)

which again coincides with the case of discriminating a single quantum state in the quantum model.

We compare with the quantum model providing the maximum success probability
m.namkung_gen

\displaystyle\max P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(Q)}=\begin{cases}(1-c^{1/2N})^{N},&c<(2^{1/N}-1)^{N/2},\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-c^{1/N})^{N},&c\geq(2^{1/N}-1)^{N/2},\end{cases}

(24)

under equal priors. It can be verified that the region in which
\frac{1}{2}(1-{c}^{1/N})^{N}
becomes the maximum increases with
N
. This suggests that, when the number of the receivers becomes larger, the maximum success probability tends to be reproduced by the noncontextual model.

III.4
Probabilistic quantum cloning

We further analyze the contextual advantage of probabilistic quantum cloning, which produces perfect clones with a certain probability. We first consider type-I probabilistic cloning, in which the information about the prepared state is also revealed to the receiver, followed by type-II probabilistic cloning

III.4.1
Type-I probabilistic cloning

Type-I probabilistic cloning, illustrated in Fig.
4
(a), is implemented via a transformation
\mathcal{L}_{\rm I}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\rm S}}d\lambda_{\rm S}\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm I}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S2}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S})\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}\,\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime})\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S2}^{\prime}){\sigma}_{j}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\alpha_{j})\,\chi_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S2}^{\prime}){\sigma}_{0}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}),

(25)

followed by a direct measurement on the auxiliary system, described by orthogonal epistemic states
{\sigma}_{0}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})
,
{\sigma}_{1}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})
, and
{\sigma}_{2}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})

Here,
\chi_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S2}^{\prime})
denotes the post-measurement epistemic state on

corresponding to the failure outcome
j=0
. When the measurement outcome is nonzero, the epistemic state
\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S})
is perfectly cloned into

and

) to Eq. (

(26)

where
c
denotes the confusability between
\mu_{1}(\lambda_{\rm S})
and
\mu_{2}(\lambda_{\rm S})
. This relation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{c}{\Big(\chi_{1}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{S2}^{\prime}),\chi_{2}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{S2}^{\prime})\Big)_{\Omega_{\rm S1}\times\Omega_{\rm S2}}}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1-c.

in Eq. (

in Eq. (

III.4.2
Type-II probabilistic cloning

Type-II probabilistic cloning, illustrated in Fig.
4
(b) and originally proposed by Duan and Guo
l.m.duan_cl
, is described by a transformation
\mathcal{L}_{\rm II}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\rm S}}d\lambda_{\rm S}\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm II}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S2}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S})\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}\,\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime})\mu_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S2}^{\prime}){\sigma}_{\rm s}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\alpha_{j})\,\chi_{j}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{\rm S2}^{\prime}){\sigma}_{\rm f}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime}),

(29)

followed by orthogonal epistemic states
{\sigma}_{\rm s}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})
and
{\sigma}_{\rm f}(\lambda_{\rm A}^{\prime})
, corresponding to success and failure outcomes, respectively.

The transformation
\mathcal{L}_{\rm II}
satisfies the confusability-preserving condition if the following relation holds (see Appendix D.1 for details):

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}c^{2}+(1-\alpha_{k})\Big(\chi_{1}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{S2}^{\prime}),\chi_{2}(\lambda_{\rm S1}^{\prime},\lambda_{S2}^{\prime})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{k}c^{2}+1-\alpha_{k},

(30)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{1+c}.

(31)

Since
\alpha_{k}
denotes the success probability of cloning for a given input
\mu_{k}(\lambda_{\rm S})
, this yields the maximum success probability

\displaystyle\max_{\mathrm{type}\text{-}\mathrm{II}}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}=\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}\frac{1}{1+c},

(32)

for given prior probabilities
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
.

This analysis can be extended to the case of generating
m>n
copies from
n
initial copies, leading to

\displaystyle\max_{\mathrm{type}\text{-}\mathrm{II}}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(NC)}=\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}\frac{1-c^{n}}{1-c^{m}},

(33)

as shown in Appendix D.2.

For comparison, the corresponding quantum bound is given by
w.-h.zhang

\displaystyle\max_{\mathrm{type}\text{-}\mathrm{II}}P_{\rm succ}^{\rm(Q)}=\frac{1-(\sqrt{c})^{n}}{1-(\sqrt{c})^{m}}.

(34)

This indicates that, for equal prior probabilities, the success probability achievable within the noncontextual model exceeds that of the quantum model for all

. This gap implies contextuality in terms of cloning success probability.

On the other hand, when the prior probabilities become highly imbalanced, the quantum model approaches a noncontextual description. For example, when
q_{1}\gg q_{2}
(or vice versa), the maximum success probability in the quantum model becomes

IV
Conclusion

We rigorously verified the contextual aspects of quantum non-demolition measurements used in various discrimination tasks. We first formulated a framework for non-demolition measurements within a noncontextual model, in terms of state preparation, transformation, and measurement, as established in previous works
r.w.spekkens
;
d.schmid
. Based on this framework, all discrimination tasks considered in this work are
analytically
characterized, as summarized in Table
1
.

For unambiguous state discrimination, contextuality enhancement is always (conditionally) observed under equal (unequal) prior probabilities, consistent with the predictions of our methodology. In sequential unambiguous state discrimination, contextuality tends to decrease as the number of receivers and the degree of confusability increase, even when the two states are equiprobable. We further classify probabilistic quantum cloning into two types. In type-I probabilistic cloning, contextuality exhibits the same behavior as in unambiguous state discrimination. Type-II cloning is also enhanced by contextuality under equal priors, while tending to admit a noncontextual description under unequal priors.

We believe that our methodology provides a unified approach to identifying quantum resources relevant for advancing various quantum technologies, as well as for deepening the understanding of the foundations of quantum measurements. Quantum non-demolition measurements are widely used in quantum communication tasks
r.han
, where security against eavesdroppers is ensured
m.namkung_qkd
. In particular, probabilistic quantum cloning can be generalized to probabilistic quantum broadcasting
l.li
, which distributes copied information to multiple parties via entanglement. Moreover, since quantum non-demolition measurements have been experimentally implemented in various setups
p.grangier
;
t.nakajima
, our method may pave the way for observing nonclassical features in experimental realizations.

E.1. Dual problem for evaluating maximal confidence

We begin by formulating the primal optimization problem for maximal confidence as

maximize

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}d\lambda\,\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda)Q_{k}(\lambda)

subject to

\displaystyle Q_{k}(\lambda)\geq 0,\ \forall\lambda\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}d\lambda\,Q_{k}(\lambda)=1.

To derive the dual problem, we consider the Lagrangian

\displaystyle g(l_{k},Z_{k}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\max_{Q_{k}(\lambda)}\left\{\int_{\Omega}d\lambda\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda)Q_{k}(\lambda)+l_{k}\left(1-\int_{\Omega}d\lambda Q_{k}(\lambda)\right)+\int_{\Omega}d\lambda Z_{k}(\lambda)Q_{k}(\lambda)\right\}

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda)-l_{k}+Z_{k}(\lambda)=0,

\displaystyle l_{k}-\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda)\geq 0.

(68)

Substituting
\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda)=q_{k}\mu_{k}(\lambda)/\mu(\lambda)
, we obtain the dual problem

minimize

\displaystyle l_{k},

subject to

\displaystyle l_{k}\mu(\lambda)-q_{k}\mu_{k}(\lambda)\geq 0,\ \forall\lambda\in\Omega.

(69)

;

;

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}d\lambda\,Z_{k}(\lambda)Q_{k}(\lambda)=0.

(70)

1. Concise setup discussion

We further discuss contextuality enhancement in unambiguous and maximal-confidence discrimination using a linear optical setup, as illustrated in Fig.

|\psi_{j}\rangle=a_{j}|H\rangle+b_{j}|V\rangle
(

q_{j}
, under ideal conditions. The receiver employs a displaced Sagnac interferometer to discriminate the prepared states
m.a.solis-prosser

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle\otimes|p_{\rm init}\rangle\rightarrow\sqrt{1-\alpha_{j}}\,|\phi_{j}\rangle\otimes|p_{\rm fail}\rangle+\sqrt{\alpha_{j}}\,|\chi_{j}\rangle\otimes|p_{\rm succ}\rangle.

(72)

Here,
|p_{\rm fail}\rangle
and
|p_{\rm succ}\rangle
denote path states corresponding to failure and success outcomes, respectively. We note that
|\chi_{1}\rangle
and
|\chi_{2}\rangle
are orthogonal, implying that these polarization states can be perfectly discriminated into path modes labeled by
|p_{1}\rangle
and
|p_{2}\rangle
using a half-wave plate (HWP) followed by a polarizing beam splitter (PBS). Subsequently, additional HWPs are placed in modes
|p_{1}\rangle
and
|p_{2}\rangle
to complete the transformation:

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle\otimes|p_{\rm init}\rangle\rightarrow|\phi_{j}\rangle\otimes\left(\sqrt{1-\alpha_{j}}\,|p_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{\alpha_{j}}\,|p_{j}\rangle\right),\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

(73)

We further introduce an additional HWP with polarization angle
\varphi

2. Unambiguous state discrimination

We discuss how to implement unambiguous discrimination between two unequiprobable qubit states. We begin by parameterizing the pure states as

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1+s}{2}}|H\rangle+(-1)^{j+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-s}{2}}|V\rangle,

(74)

with
s\in[0,1]
denoting the overlap between
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
.

The interferometer implements the following transformation:

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle\otimes|p_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left\{h_{j}(s,\varphi)\cos 2\mu|H\rangle-v_{j}(s,\varphi)\cos 2\nu|V\rangle\right\}\otimes|p_{\rm succ}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left\{v_{j}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\nu|H\rangle+h_{j}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\mu|V\rangle\right\}\otimes|p_{\rm fail}\rangle,

(75)

where

\displaystyle h_{j}(s,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+s}{2}}\cos 2\varphi+(-1)^{j+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-s}{2}}\sin 2\varphi,

\displaystyle v_{j}(s,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+s}{2}}\sin 2\varphi-(-1)^{j+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-s}{2}}\cos 2\varphi.

(76)

The success components must be mutually orthogonal, which leads to

\displaystyle h_{1}(s,\varphi)h_{2}(s,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\mu+v_{1}(s,\varphi)v_{2}(s,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\nu=0.

(77)

Under this condition, the transformation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle\otimes|p_{0}\rangle\rightarrow\begin{cases}\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}\,|H\rangle\otimes|p_{1}\rangle+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{1}}\,|\phi_{1}\rangle\otimes|p_{0}\rangle,&j=1,\\
\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}\,|V\rangle\otimes|p_{2}\rangle+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{2}}\,|\phi_{2}\rangle\otimes|p_{0}\rangle,&j=2,\end{cases}

(78)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=h_{j}^{2}(s,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\mu+v_{j}^{2}(s,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\nu,

(79)

and the post-measurement states are given by

\displaystyle|\phi_{j}\rangle=\frac{v_{j}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\nu|H\rangle+h_{j}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\mu|V\rangle}{\sqrt{v_{j}^{2}(s,\varphi)\sin^{2}2\nu+h_{j}^{2}(s,\varphi)\sin^{2}2\mu}}.

(80)

Finally, additional HWPs with angles

\displaystyle\xi_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{h_{1}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\mu}{v_{1}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\nu},\quad\xi_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{h_{2}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\nu}{v_{2}(s,\varphi)\sin 2\mu}

(81)

complete the transformation in Eq. (
73
).

From Eqs. (
76
)–(
80
), the optimization problem for the success probability becomes

maximize

\displaystyle P_{\rm succ}(\varphi,\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=q_{1}\alpha_{1}+q_{2}\alpha_{2},

subject to

\displaystyle h_{1}h_{2}\cos^{2}2\mu+v_{1}v_{2}\cos^{2}2\nu=0,

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{1}|\phi_{2}\rangle=1.

(82)

Since
\alpha_{j}
matches the form in Eq. (
73
), the optimal solution attains the IDP bound

;
d.dieks
;
a.peres
;
g.jaeger
.

3. Maximal-confidence discrimination

When prepared states are subject to noise, they cannot be distinguished with zero error probability. For instance, consider noisy polarized single-photon states of the form

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|H\rangle-(-1)^{j+1}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|V\rangle,

(83)

which can be generated using a depolarizing channel
y.-c.jeong
. The corresponding mixed states are described by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{j}

\displaystyle=p|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|+(1-p)\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2}=\frac{1+p}{2}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|+\frac{1-p}{2}|\psi_{j}^{\bot}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}^{\bot}|,

(84)

where
|\psi_{j}^{\bot}\rangle
is orthogonal to
|\psi_{j}\rangle
.

The key idea is that maximal-confidence discrimination between the mixed states in Eq. (
84
) is equivalent to unambiguous discrimination between the complementary states
h.lee_mc2

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}^{\rm(c)}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1+p\cos\theta}{2}}|H\rangle-(-1)^{j+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-p\cos\theta}{2}}|V\rangle.

(85)

This suggests that maximal-confidence discrimination can be implemented by configuring the setup in Sec. E.2.1 to unambiguously discriminate these complementary states.

Within the interferometer, both
|\psi_{j}\rangle
and
|\psi_{j}^{\bot}\rangle
evolve as

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle\rightarrow|\Psi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\{h_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\cos 2\mu|H\rangle-v_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\cos 2\nu|V\rangle\right\}\otimes|p_{\rm succ}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left\{v_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\sin 2\nu|H\rangle+h_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\sin 2\mu|V\rangle\right\}\otimes|p_{\rm fail}\rangle,

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}^{\bot}\rangle\rightarrow|\Psi_{j}^{\bot}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\{\bar{h}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\cos 2\mu|H\rangle-\bar{v}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\cos 2\nu|V\rangle\right\}\otimes|p_{\rm succ}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left\{\bar{v}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\sin 2\nu|H\rangle+\bar{h}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)\sin 2\mu|V\rangle\right\}\otimes|p_{\rm fail}\rangle,

(86)

where

\displaystyle h_{j}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\cos 2\varphi-(-1)^{j+1}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin 2\varphi,

\displaystyle v_{j}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin 2\varphi+(-1)^{j+1}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos 2\varphi,

\displaystyle\bar{h}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos 2\varphi+(-1)^{j+1}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin 2\varphi,

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin 2\varphi-(-1)^{j+1}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\cos 2\varphi.

(87)

Conditioned on detecting the path state
|p_{\rm succ}\rangle
, the polarization is measured in the orthonormal basis

\displaystyle|\pi_{k}^{\rm(c)}\rangle=\frac{h_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)\cos 2\mu|H\rangle+v_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)\cos 2\nu|V\rangle}{\sqrt{h_{k}^{\rm(c)2}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\mu+v_{k}^{\rm(c)2}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\nu}},

(88)

with

\displaystyle h_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+p\cos\theta}{2}}\cos 2\varphi-(-1)^{k+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-p\cos\theta}{2}}\sin 2\varphi,

\displaystyle v_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+p\cos\theta}{2}}\sin 2\varphi+(-1)^{k+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-p\cos\theta}{2}}\cos 2\varphi.

(89)

Using Eqs. (
86
) and (
88
), the probability of obtaining outcome
k
from
\hat{\rho}_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[k|\hat{\rho}_{j}]=\frac{(1+p)f_{jk}(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\nu)+(1-p)\bar{f}_{jk}(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\nu)}{2\left\{h_{k}^{\rm(c)2}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\mu+v_{k}^{\rm(c)2}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\nu\right\}},

(90)

where

\displaystyle f_{jk}(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\nu)=\left\{h_{j}(\theta,\varphi)h_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\mu+v_{j}(\theta,\varphi)v_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\nu\right\}^{2},

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{jk}(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\nu)=\left\{\bar{h}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)h_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\mu+\bar{v}_{j}(\theta,\varphi)v_{k}^{\rm(c)}(\theta,\varphi)\cos^{2}2\nu\right\}^{2}.

(91)

Consequently, the maximal-confidence optimization problem becomes

maximize

\displaystyle C_{k}(\varphi,\mu,\nu)=\frac{q_{k}\,\mathrm{Pr}[k|\hat{\rho}_{k}]}{q_{1}\,\mathrm{Pr}[k|\hat{\rho}_{1}]+q_{2}\,\mathrm{Pr}[k|\hat{\rho}_{2}]},

subject to

\displaystyle h_{1}^{\rm(c)}h_{2}^{\rm(c)}\cos^{2}2\mu+v_{1}^{\rm(c)}v_{2}^{\rm(c)}\cos^{2}2\nu=0,

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{1}|\phi_{2}\rangle=1.

(92)

For example, for

and
