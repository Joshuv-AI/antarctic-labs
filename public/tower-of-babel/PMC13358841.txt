Exercise-induced gut microbiota metabolites and the gut-lung axis: implications for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Zhiwei Fan, Jun Chen, Junyi Fang, Weiyi Yan, Weibing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is increasingly recognised as a systemic disorder associated with gut dysbiosis and impaired gutlung communication. COPD-associated gut dysbiosis suggests potential bidirectional interactions between the gut and lung, which may be mediated by circulating immune cells, gut microbiota-derived metabolites and systemic inflammatory mediators. Emerging evidence suggests that exercise may improve COPD-related symptoms and quality of life not only through direct cardiopulmonary adaptations but also by reshaping gut microbiota composition and metabolic function. Microbiota-derived metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), secondary bile acids (SBAs) and indole derivatives, may act as key mediators linking exercise-induced microbial changes to pulmonary immune regulation, inflammatory signalling, oxidative stress and epithelial barrier integrity. However, current evidence remains fragmented, and the mechanisms by which exercise-responsive microbial metabolites influence COPD-related pulmonary inflammation, barrier dysfunction and immune homeostasis have not been fully clarified. This review synthesises evidence from human studies, animal models and mechanistic investigations to clarify the relationship among exercise, gut microbiota and COPD, with a focus on how exercise-responsive microbial metabolites may contribute to improved pulmonary health. By integrating current evidence within an exercise-gut-lung axis framework, this review provides a mechanistic basis for developing microbiota-targeted exercise strategies for COPD prevention and management.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic inflammatory airway disorder characterised by persistent and incompletely reversible airflow limitation. Its pathological hallmarks include small airway remodelling and emphysema, and it is clinically manifested by progressive dyspnoea, chronic cough, sputum production and excessive airway mucus secretion (1,2). Current therapeutic strategies for COPD remain largely symptom-oriented, primarily relying on bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids to alleviate airflow obstruction and airway inflammation (3). Although these treatments are effective in reducing symptom burden, lowering the risk of acute exacerbations and improving health-related quality of life, their ability to fundamentally alter long-term lung function decline or disease progression remains limited (4). Coupled with the continuously rising global prevalence of COPD and its substantial socioeconomic burden, the disease has emerged as a major public health challenge. Consequently, a more comprehensive understanding of COPD pathogenesis is essential for improving early diagnosis, preventing disease onset, delaying progression and ultimately reducing disease-related mortality.

In recent years, rapid advances in gut microbiota research have reshaped current perspectives on the pathophysiology and management of chronic diseases (5). Growing evidence indicates that patients with COPD frequently exhibit gut microbial dysbiosis, which may disrupt intestinal barrier integrity and mucosal immune homeostasis, thereby exerting detrimental effects on both intestinal and pulmonary systems (6). However, due to pronounced inter-individual heterogeneity in clinical populations, delineating the precise contribution of gut microbial compositional changes to COPD pathogenesis remains challenging. Gut microbiota metabolites have therefore been proposed as an alternative and potentially more informative link between microbial alterations and host disease processes. Accumulating evidence suggests that microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), indole derivatives and secondary bile acids (SBAs), are associated with COPD-related outcomes. These metabolites may influence pulmonary health by modulating immune cell function, regulating inflammation-related signalling pathways and preserving epithelial barrier integrity.

Exercise training is widely recognised as a cornerstone of pulmonary rehabilitation and represents one of the most effective non-pharmacological interventions for improving exercise capacity, symptom burden and quality of life in individuals with COPD (7). Regular physical activity has been shown to markedly influence gut microbiota composition and diversity, as well as to modulate the production of multiple microbiota-derived metabolites. On this basis, we propose the exercise-gut-lung axis as an integrative framework linking exercise training, gut microbial metabolism and pulmonary pathophysiology in COPD. Within this framework, exercise-induced alterations in gut microbiota composition and metabolite production may act as upstream modulators of immune, inflammatory and epithelial barrier-related pathways that shape COPD-related outcomes. However, direct evidence demonstrating that exercise improves COPD outcomes via modulation of the gut-lung axis remains scarce, and the underlying mechanisms have yet to be fully elucidated. Therefore, this review focuses on the regulatory role of gut microbiota metabolites within the gut-lung axis and systematically synthesises current evidence on the effects of exercise interventions on microbial metabolite profiles. By integrating findings from human and experimental studies, this review aims to provide a conceptual framework to guide future basic and clinical research, and to offer novel perspectives for the development of comprehensive COPD prevention and management strategies.

Gut-lung axis and COPD

Although the human gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts differ markedly in anatomical structure and physiological function, both originate from the endoderm and share several fundamental features, including mucosal barriers, epithelial cell layers, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue and complex resident microbial communities (8). On the basis of these shared developmental and immunological features, McDermott and Bienenstock proposed the concept of the “common mucosal immune system” (9), whereby peripheral mucosal organs, including the gut and lung, are immunologically interconnected through immune cell trafficking and coordinated mucosal signalling. One illustrative example of such cross-mucosal immunity is the production of secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) in both the intestinal and respiratory mucosa. sIgA plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of multiple pulmonary diseases, including COPD, asthma and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (10). Beyond its role in host defence, sIgA contributes to the containment of pathogenic microorganisms, limits their systemic dissemination and actively shapes gut microbiota composition and function (11). Collectively, the immunological actions of sIgA highlight a shared mucosal immune network linking the gut and lung, thereby providing a molecular and cellular basis for bidirectional communication along the gut-lung axis.

In patients with COPD, accumulating evidence indicates that disruption of the common mucosal immune system may exert reciprocal effects on both intestinal and pulmonary compartments. Gut microbial dysbiosis is frequently observed in COPD and is characterised by enrichment of inflammation-associated taxa, particularly members of the phylum Proteobacteria, including Streptococcus, Enterococcus, Romboutsia and Corynebacterium, alongside a concomitant reduction in Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes and SCFA-producing commensals (2,12,13). Such alterations in microbial composition may compromise intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, thereby increasing systemic exposure to endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and facilitating the propagation of inflammatory signals to distal organs. Under physiological conditions, mucins secreted by intestinal epithelial cells form a protective barrier that restricts microbial translocation across the epithelium. However, gut dysbiosis-induced mucosal injury may weaken this barrier (5), substantially increasing the risk of translocation of gut-derived bacteria or bacterial components to extraintestinal sites (14). Supporting this concept, members of the family Enterobacteriaceae have been detected in the lungs during episodes of acute lung injury, suggesting a potential gut-to-lung microbial migration under pathological conditions (15). Moreover, elevated circulating levels of zonulin have been reported in patients with COPD, further indicating impaired intestinal barrier function (16). Together, these findings provide converging evidence for bidirectional communication between the gut and lung in COPD.

In summary, the gut and lung share substantial similarities in structural organisation, developmental origin and immunoregulatory mechanisms, collectively forming the biological foundation of the gut-lung axis. Furthermore, immune cell interactions, microbial components (e.g. LPS) and gut microbiota metabolites collectively mediate bidirectional communication between the intestine and lung (Figure 1).

Interactions among exercise, gut microbiota, and COPD

Effects of exercise on COPD

Skeletal muscle dysfunction and impaired cardiopulmonary function together constitute a major component of disease burden in patients with COPD (17). Exercise training is an effective therapeutic intervention. Accordingly, all major international clinical guidelines, including the 2024 American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) pulmonary rehabilitation guideline, national respiratory society recommendations and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence quality standards, consistently endorse pulmonary rehabilitation incorporating exercise training as a standard component of COPD management in stable disease and following acute exacerbations (18). Such interventions have been shown to substantially enhance exercise tolerance, alleviate dyspnoea, improve health-related quality of life and reduce hospitalisation rates and the risk of acute exacerbations (19). A meta-analysis further demonstrated that aerobic exercise training leads to consistent improvements in functional exercise capacity, as reflected by increases in the 6-min walk distance, alongside reductions in perceived dyspnoea and moderate gains in maximal work rate (20). Ercin et al. (21) reported that supervised continuous aerobic training can improve exercise capacity, health-related quality of life and psychological outcomes in overweight and obese patients with COPD. Importantly, this training modality also reduced exercise-related dyspnoea and leg fatigue. In addition, a large community-based cluster randomized controlled trial involving 472 individuals with moderate COPD demonstrated that a 6-month Baduanjin exercise intervention significantly reduced symptom burden, as reflected by lower COPD Assessment Test and modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale scores, while concomitantly improving overall health status and functional exercise capacity (22).

Despite the well-established rehabilitative benefits of continuous aerobic training, patients with moderate-to-severe COPD often experience ventilatory limitation and dynamic lung hyperinflation, which may preclude the maintenance of sufficient exercise intensity or duration under constant-load conditions (23). In this context, interval training, characterized by alternating bouts of high-intensity exercise and recovery periods, has been regarded as an important alternative to traditional continuous aerobic training (24). Compared with continuous aerobic training, interval training can provide a greater peripheral muscular stimulus within a shorter exercise duration, while the recovery intervals may reduce the symptom burden associated with sustained exercise; accordingly, it has received increasing attention in pulmonary rehabilitation for COPD (25). Evidence from short- and medium-term intervention studies suggests that high-intensity interval training (HIIT) is feasible and well tolerated in patients with COPD and may improve exercise capacity and quality-of-life-related outcomes. However, current evidence has not demonstrated that HIIT directly improves alveolar-capillary reserve, lung tissue mass, or pulmonary perfusion distribution, indicating that its benefits may be primarily attributable to extrapulmonary adaptations (25-27). Beyond endurance-based training, resistance training (28), eccentric cycling (29), and downhill walking (30) have also increasingly been recognized as important intervention modalities in pulmonary rehabilitation for COPD. A shared feature of these approaches is their emphasis on localized mechanical stimulation of peripheral skeletal muscles, rather than reliance solely on sustained whole-body aerobic loading. Accordingly, they may enhance training stimuli in specific muscle groups while imposing relatively lower ventilatory and cardiometabolic demands. Current evidence suggests that these modalities may improve exercise capacity, muscle function, and symptom burden; however, the optimal dose, long-term safety, and target populations for different training modalities remain to be further clarified (Table 1).

In summary, exercise training is an effective intervention for improving functional capacity, symptom burden and quality of life in patients with COPD. Given the heterogeneity in physiological demands and metabolic stimuli across different exercise modalities, individualised exercise prescriptions tailored to disease severity and symptom burden are essential in clinical practice.

Changes in gut microbiota composition in COPD

Compared with healthy individuals, patients with COPD commonly exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis (31). In a cross-sectional study involving patients across different GOLD stages, advanced COPD was associated with significantly reduced α-diversity and pronounced alterations in overall gut microbial community structure relative to healthy controls (12). At the taxonomic level, patients with COPD stages III–IV showed broad compositional shifts, including depletion of potentially beneficial taxa and enrichment of potentially pro-inflammatory or opportunistic taxa. Notably, members of the family Prevotellaceae were relatively enriched in patients with early-stage COPD (stages I–II). Across the broader COPD population, gut microbial profiles tended to shift towards a Prevotella-dominant enterotype, suggesting that gut dysbiosis may evolve in a stage-dependent manner during disease progression (12). Consistent with this notion, more recent metagenomic evidence further supports stage-dependent remodeling of the gut microbiota in COPD. In a cohort including healthy controls and patients with GOLD I–II or GOLD III–IV COPD, early- and advanced-stage disease exhibited distinct species-level microbial signatures, with advanced COPD characterized by enrichment of potentially opportunistic or inflammation-associated taxa and depletion of selected Bacteroides species (Table 2) (32).

Beyond these compositional associations, COPD-associated gut dysbiosis may contribute to disease progression under environmental insults. To explore the potential contributory role of gut microbiota alterations in COPD pathogenesis, faecal microbiota from patients with COPD and healthy controls were transplanted into mice, which were concurrently exposed to biomass fuel smoke to establish a COPD-like phenotype. Mice receiving microbiota from patients with advanced COPD (stages III–IV) exhibited more severe systemic and pulmonary inflammatory responses, accelerated lung function decline and aggravated emphysematous changes compared with controls. In contrast, administration of the commensal bacterium Parabacteroides goldsteinii, isolated from the gut microbiota of patients with COPD, ameliorated COPD-like phenotypes in experimental models (12,33). Collectively, these findings suggest that COPD-associated gut dysbiosis is not merely a secondary phenomenon, but may actively participate in and exacerbate disease progression in the presence of environmental insults (12).

Effects of exercise on the gut microbiota

Accumulating evidence indicates that exercise represents one of the important extrinsic modulators of gut microbiota composition and function. A study involving 8,416 middle-aged and older adults from the Swedish SCAPIS cohort showed that individuals with higher levels of physical activity generally exhibited greater gut microbial a-diversity and species richness, accompanied by increased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Roseburia. In contrast, sedentary behavior was associated with enrichment of potentially unfavorable taxa, including Escherichia coli, Ruminococcus gnavus, and Eggerthella lenta. Preliminary interventional evidence supporting this association has also been reported in patients with COPD (34). Tashiro et al. (35) investigated 37 male patients with stable COPD and found that, compared with sedentary individuals, those with regular exercise habits exhibited a higher relative abundance of Faecalibacterium and Roseburia, accompanied by more favourable pulmonary function parameters. These findings suggest that exercise capacity, physical activity behavior, and the gut microbiota may be interrelated in patients with COPD, although the underlying causal relationships remain to be further clarified.

From a prescription-dependent perspective, exercise-induced gut microbial adaptations appear to be influenced by exercise modality, intensity, frequency, and intervention duration. Moderate- to moderately high-intensity aerobic training tend to produce more consistent microbial regulatory effects, particularly when the intervention lasts at least 8-week, is performed ≥3 times per week, consists of 30–90 min per session, and reaches a total exercise volume of approximately 150–270 min per week. Notably, Faecalibacterium correlated with lower fat mass and higher fat-free mass, suggesting a role for exercise-induced gut microbiota in improving body composition (36,37). In contrast, short-term, low-dose, or insufficiently prolonged interventions usually induce only limited microbial alterations (38). Although short-term HIIT may improve cardiorespiratory fitness, body composition, or metabolic phenotypes, its effects on the overall gut microbial structure remain inconsistent (39). More pronounced changes in specific functional taxa and metabolic pathways are more likely to occur when HIIT is extended to 8–12 weeks and combined with resistance training or dietary control (40,41). Resistance training alone generally exerts weaker effects on gut microbial composition than aerobic or combined training; however, it may indirectly modulate the intestinal ecological niche by improving skeletal muscle metabolism, enhancing mucus production-related functions, and reducing markers of intestinal barrier permeability (Table 3) (42).

With respect to microbial metabolic function, regular exercise is more commonly associated with enrichment of functional taxa with the potential to promote SCFA production, maintain the mucus layer, participate in bile acid transformation, and regulate immune homeostasis. For example, exercise interventions may increase SCFA-associated genera, including Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Coprococcus, Ruminococcus, and Anaerostipes, as well as beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacterium and the mucus-associated bacterium Akkermansia muciniphila (43). Among these, F. prausnitzii is a representative butyrate-producing bacterium, and its increased abundance is closely associated with the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity, promotion of mucosal repair, and inhibition of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses (44-46). Therefore, exercise-induced enrichment of F. prausnitzii may indicate a potential anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective mechanism, providing an indirect mechanistic basis for understanding how exercise may alleviate the systemic low-grade inflammation commonly observed in patients with COPD. Collectively, exercise-induced gut microbial remodeling should not be interpreted merely as a shift in taxonomic composition; rather, it may reflect broader improvements in microbial metabolic function, intestinal ecological balance, and host immune homeostasis. Given that COPD is commonly accompanied by systemic low-grade inflammation, increased oxidative stress, impaired intestinal barrier function, and metabolic abnormalities, these exercise-induced changes in the gut microbiota and its metabolic functions may have important implications for the gut-lung axis.

In summary, exercise promotes restoration of intestinal metabolic and immune homeostasis by increasing gut microbial diversity, optimising microbial community structure and enhancing intestinal barrier function. These coordinated adaptations may, in turn, indirectly modulate pulmonary inflammation and tissue remodelling via the gut-lung axis, thereby contributing to the attenuation of COPD progression. Exercise interventions on gut microbiota in COPD: current evidence and limitations

Direct evidence regarding the effects of exercise on the gut microbiota and its metabolic functions in patients with COPD remains limited. Existing evidence suggests that COPD patients with higher exercise capacity exhibit gut microbial features distinct from those with lower exercise capacity. Although no significant difference in α-diversity was observed between the two groups, the high-exercise-capacity group showed higher relative abundances of Prevotella and Faecalibacterium and a lower relative abundance of Bacteroides. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis further revealed enrichment of Faecalibacterium, Lachnospira, Roseburia, and the Eubacterium hallii group in the high-exercise-capacity group. In parallel, these patients exhibited more favorable pulmonary function-related indices, including higher forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and inspiratory capacity, as well as greater lower-limb muscle mass and higher levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. These findings suggest that gut microbial profiles in patients with COPD may be associated with exercise capacity and pulmonary ventilatory reserve; however, the causal nature of these associations remains to be further verified (35). Comini et al. (47) reported that, among patients with COPD characterized by static lung hyperinflation, those who participated in a 4-week endurance training program showed a reduced fecal abundance of Saccharomyces spp. and lower fecal zonulin levels compared with untrained COPD patients. These findings suggest that exercise training may be associated with better-preserved intestinal barrier function in this specific COPD phenotype and may attenuate fungal-related mucosal immune stimulation. In addition to these gut-centered studies, the clinical response to pulmonary rehabilitation in patients with COPD has been linked to changes in the salivary microbiota and inflammatory biomarkers. Although the oral or salivary microbiota cannot directly represent the gut microbiota, and such changes should not be simply extrapolated as evidence of gut microbial remodeling, these findings provide an additional line of support for the broader concept that structured pulmonary rehabilitation may influence host-microbiome interactions in COPD (48).

Building on these preliminary yet fragmented findings, we propose an “exercise-gut-lung axis” as a conceptual framework to integrate the available evidence and guide future mechanistic research. Within this framework, exercise is not viewed merely as a systemic rehabilitation strategy, but rather as an upstream modulator of gut microbial ecology and metabolic output. Exercise-induced alterations in microbial composition and function may modify the production and bioavailability of microbiota-derived metabolites, thereby influencing pulmonary inflammation, immune cell function, and epithelial barrier homeostasis along the gut-lung axis. Importantly, this framework should currently be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating model rather than a fully established causal pathway. Nevertheless, positioning microbiota-derived metabolites as key intermediaries linking exercise intervention, gut microbial remodeling, and pulmonary physiological adaptation may help establish a more mechanistically coherent explanatory pathway across existing indirect evidence, thereby providing a testable theoretical direction for future longitudinal, multi-omics, and interventional studies.

Potential roles of exercise-induced gut microbiota metabolites in COPD

Microbiota-derived metabolites may represent important mechanistic intermediaries through which exercise-induced gut microbial remodeling influences pulmonary outcomes in COPD. In particular, SCFAs, SBAs, and indole derivatives may affect lung-related physiological and pathological processes by regulating inflammatory signalling, epithelial barrier integrity, immune cell function, and host immunometabolism along the gut-lung axis. SCFAs

SCFAs may influence COPD pathophysiology through multiple complementary mechanisms, primarily involving immune modulation, barrier protection and antioxidant defence. First, SCFAs can directly or indirectly regulate pulmonary immune balance, thereby mitigating inflammatory processes relevant to COPD progression. In a murine model of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus induced bacterial pneumonia, infection not only disrupted the pulmonary microbiome but also altered gut microbial composition, with a marked reduction in butyrate-producing Lachnospiraceae accompanied by parallel decreases in intestinal and circulating butyrate levels (49). By contrast, regular exercise can significantly increase circulating butyrate concentrations. For example, Ma et al. (50) found that 8-week of regular exercise significantly increased the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria in mice, thereby elevating serum butyrate levels. Increased butyrate availability suppressed histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) activity, reduced its interaction with signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), enhanced STAT1 acetylation and inhibited its phosphorylation and dimerisation, thereby blocking STAT1-mediated pro-inflammatory signalling (49). This epigenetic modulation promoted a phenotypic shift of alveolar macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 state towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, accompanied by marked reductions in pulmonary tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-6 expression. These changes contribute to improved pulmonary tissue architecture and attenuation of inflammatory pathological alterations (49).

Second, SCFAs may attenuate COPD-related inflammatory amplification by preserving intestinal barrier integrity and limiting gut-derived endotoxin translocation. In the pathological context of COPD, gut microbial dysbiosis and impaired intestinal barrier function may promote the translocation of gut-derived microbial molecules, such as LPS, allowing them to enter the systemic circulation and act on lung tissues, thereby amplifying distal pulmonary inflammation. LPS can activate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) expressed on alveolar macrophages, airway epithelial cells, and peripheral immune cells, initiating myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)-dependent nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) inflammatory signaling (51,52). This process induces the release of inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8)/IL-8, thereby promoting neutrophil recruitment, oxidative stress, mucus hypersecretion, and lung parenchymal injury (53). Regular exercise may reshape gut microbial composition and metabolic function, enrich SCFA-producing bacteria, and enhance the potential for SCFAs production. As key microbial metabolites, SCFAs may help strengthen intestinal epithelial tight junctions, maintain mucosal barrier integrity, and reduce intestinal permeability, thereby limiting LPS entry into the systemic circulation and reducing the likelihood of sustained TLR4 activation in peripheral immune cells and lung tissues at its source (54).

In addition, exercise-associated SCFAs metabolic adaptations may cooperate with exercise-induced nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)-mediated antioxidant responses to regulate oxidative lung injury in COPD. Animal studies have shown that exercise promotes Nrf2 nuclear translocation and upregulates the expression of antioxidant proteins, including haem oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), thereby attenuating cigarette smoke (CS)-induced emphysema and oxidative stress-related injury (55). These exercise-induced antioxidant adaptations may be reinforced by SCFAs, which have been reported to activate the Nrf2 signalling pathway, promoting the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1, NQO1 and SOD. Through these effects, SCFAs attenuate oxidative stress-induced lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby mitigating smoking- or pollutant-induced oxidative lung injury (56).

In summary, SCFAs may influence the development and progression of COPD through multiple interconnected mechanisms. However, the available evidence remains largely indirect, primarily based on associative findings, for example, links between dietary fibre intake, enhanced SCFAs production and improved COPD-related outcomes (57). To date, direct interventional evidence supporting exercise-induced SCFA-mediated benefits in COPD remains scarce. Whether exercise-induced SCFAs exert similar protective effects in patients with COPD therefore remains to be elucidated and represents an important avenue for future investigation.

SBAs

Gut microbiota-derived SBAs and their derivatives may represent another important class of metabolic mediators involved in the gut-lung axis in COPD. After intestinal absorption, these metabolites can enter the systemic circulation and subsequently act on distal airway epithelial cells or pulmonary immune cells. Given that the generation of SBAs is highly dependent on microbial metabolic activity, exercise-induced remodeling of the gut microbiota may influence their signaling role within the gut-lung axis by altering bile acid biotransformation (58).

Current evidence indicates that exercise interventions do not substantially alter total bile acid levels, but instead primarily reshape gut microbiota composition, thereby influencing specific functional microbial taxa involved in bile acid biotransformation and modulating the microbial conversion of primary to SBAs. Long-term exercise significantly increases the abundance of bacterial groups such as Turicibacter, which possess 7a-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase activity, thereby enhancing the conversion of chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) into anti-inflammatory SBAs, including ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) and its conjugated form tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) (59). After entering cells, SBAs can bind to the ligand-binding domain of farnesoid X receptor (FXR), inducing conformational changes in FXR, promoting the dissociation of corepressors, and facilitating the recruitment of coactivators. Subsequently, activated FXR forms a heterodimer with retinoid X receptor (RXR) and binds to FXR response elements in the promoter regions of target genes, thereby regulating the transcriptional expression of genes involved in bile acid transport, lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress (60,61). On this basis, activation of FXR signaling may suppress the excessive recruitment and activation of pro-inflammatory pulmonary macrophages, thereby limiting NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling and reducing the expression of macrophage pro-inflammatory activation markers such as cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 11 (ARL11). This anti-inflammatory effect may help decrease the release of inflammatory mediators and alleviate oxidative stress responses, as reflected by the attenuation of abnormal increases in stress-response markers such as HO-1. In addition, FXR may help maintain macrophage lipid homeostasis, reduce oxidized lipid accumulation and foam-like macrophage formation, and limit the sustained activation of cluster of differentiation 163 (CD163)- and STAT6-positive M2-like macrophages with pro-fibrotic features during the later phase of injury. Through the coordinated regulation of inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, macrophage polarization, and lipid handling, FXR activation may ultimately attenuate alveolar epithelial injury, collagen deposition, and progressive pulmonary fibrosis (62). On the other hand, both aerobic and resistance exercise can induce changes in lithocholic acid (LCA) and UDCA levels, thereby influencing bile acid receptor signaling by altering the availability of active ligands for Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) (63). Upon activation, TGR5 can increase intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels and initiate protein kinase A (PKA) signaling, thereby inhibiting NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and reducing caspase-1-mediated maturation and release of IL-1β, ultimately alleviating pulmonary inflammation (64). Moreover, activation of TGR5 in intestinal epithelial cells can enhance the expression of tight junction proteins, such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin, inhibit epithelial cell apoptosis, and attenuate mucosal inflammation, thereby maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. Preservation of the intestinal barrier may help restrict the translocation of gut-derived microbial products, such as LPS, into the systemic circulation, preventing their action through the gut-lung axis on alveolar macrophages, airway epithelial cells, and peripheral immune cells. This, in turn, may reduce the activation of inflammatory pathways such as TLR4/NF-κB and NLRP3, thereby limiting the amplification of pulmonary inflammatory responses (65).

Indole derivatives

Tryptophan can be metabolised by the gut microbiota into a range of indole derivatives, including indole, indole-3-aldehyde (I3A), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) (66). Accumulating evidence indicates that microbiota-derived indole metabolites of tryptophan serve as important endogenous ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and participate in host immune regulation through activation of AhR signalling pathways (67). Approximately 20% of the differentially expressed metabolites identified in patients with COPD have been reported to be closely associated with indole derivatives (2). Exercise may reshape indole derivative profiles and modulate functional tryptophan metabolic pathways through regulation of gut microbiota-associated tryptophan metabolism. The resulting remodeling of indole derivatives may increase the bioavailability of AhR ligands and induce transcriptional programs involved in mucosal homeostasis and immune tolerance, including stabilization of the Treg phenotype, enhancement of the IL-22-mediated barrier-protective axis, and suppression of excessive Th17-driven inflammation, thereby functionally shifting AhR signaling toward an immunoprotective profile in this context (68,69).

Recent evidence has shown that short-term high-intensity exercise combined with tryptophan supplementation can significantly increase the concentrations of multiple indole derivatives, including IAA, I3A, and indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), while simultaneously activating AhR signaling and exerting anti-inflammatory effects (70). Among these metabolites, I3A has been shown to suppress CS extract (CSE)-induced phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and reduce the expression of inflammasome-related proteins, including NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, and IL-1β. The same study further demonstrated that I3A can inhibit the NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammatory cascade through AhR-mediated suppression of HDAC5/6 activity, thereby alleviating COPD-related pulmonary inflammation (71). In addition, the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2) and MMP12 was markedly elevated in the lung tissues of CS-induced COPD mice, whereas I3A intervention partially reversed these abnormal increases (71). Given that matrix metalloproteinases are key proteases involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and degradation, their persistent upregulation may contribute to alveolar destruction, airway remodeling, and emphysema progression (72). Therefore, the inhibitory effects of I3A on MMP2/MMP12 suggest that its protective role may extend beyond suppression of inflammatory mediators to include regulation of protease-mediated structural lung injury (71).

On the other hand, six months of moderate- to high-intensity aerobic exercise has been shown to upregulate indole-related metabolic pathways, particularly ILA, while increasing the abundance of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, which are associated with intestinal homeostasis and indole biosynthesis (73). Mechanistically, ILA activates AhR signaling and significantly upregulates the expression of tight junction proteins, including ZO-1 and occludin, in Caco-2 cells, thereby enhancing intestinal barrier integrity and reducing intestinal permeability. Both in vivo and in vitro studies demonstrated that the AhR antagonist CH223191 completely abolished these protective effects, confirming AhR as a key mediator of intestinal barrier repair (74). Improved intestinal barrier function may prevent the translocation of bacterial products and inflammatory mediators into the systemic circulation, thereby attenuating gut-lung axis-associated inflammatory cascades and oxidative stress-mediated pulmonary injury. Collectively, these findings suggest that exercise-induced modulation of gut microbiota-derived indole metabolites may represent an important potential mechanism for alleviating COPD-related inflammation and oxidative stress.

Challenges and uncertainties

Although the exercise-gut-lung axis provides a biologically plausible framework for understanding the potential mechanisms by which exercise may improve COPD, several challenges and uncertainties remain. At present, it has not been sufficiently demonstrated whether exercise-induced alterations in gut microbial metabolism can be directly translated into clinically meaningful benefits in patients with COPD. Therefore, this framework should be regarded as an emerging mechanistic hypothesis rather than an established therapeutic mechanism.

In patients with COPD, ventilatory limitation, skeletal muscle dysfunction and exercise intolerance may restrict the achievable exercise dose, meaning that exercise-microbiota responses observed in healthy individuals or animal models may not be directly reproducible in clinical populations. Moreover, the complex clinical background of patients with COPD may independently influence gut microbiota composition, metabolite production and host immune responses. Without adequate control of, or stratification by, these variables, microbiota-derived metabolic changes observed after exercise intervention cannot be confidently attributed to exercise per se. In addition, although animal models provide important mechanistic insights, they cannot fully recapitulate the complex pathological environment shaped by human-specific microbial configurations, long-term smoking exposure, comorbidities and pharmacological treatments. Thus, metabolite-mediated pulmonary protective effects observed in animal studies should be interpreted as biologically plausible evidence supporting a hypothesis, rather than as direct evidence of clinical efficacy.

More importantly, it remains difficult to determine whether changes in gut microbiota-derived metabolites represent key mediators of exercise-induced improvements in COPD or merely accompanying biomarkers of broader physiological adaptations. Future studies should integrate standardised exercise prescriptions, dietary and medication control, longitudinal microbiome sequencing, targeted metabolomics, inflammatory phenotyping and COPD-related clinical outcomes to further clarify whether the exercise-gut-lung axis truly represents a clinically translatable pathway in COPD management.

Conclusions

In recent years, the gut-lung axis has emerged as an important conceptual framework for understanding how extra-pulmonary factors influence COPD pathophysiology. Gut microbial dysbiosis and alterations in its metabolic function may contribute to the development and progression of COPD by regulating airway inflammation, oxidative stress, epithelial barrier integrity and immune cell function. As an important non-pharmacological intervention, exercise not only improves exercise tolerance, symptom burden and quality of life in patients with COPD, but may also further influence host metabolism, immune homeostasis and chronic low-grade inflammation by reshaping the gut microbial ecosystem and its metabolite profile. Based on current evidence, exercise-induced alterations in SCFAs, SBAs and Indole derivatives may represent key mediating links connecting exercise intervention, gut microbiota regulation and improvement of pulmonary pathology. Although direct causal evidence remains to be further established, the exercise-gut-lung axis provides a new integrative perspective for understanding the potential mechanisms through which exercise improves COPD. Future studies that further clarify the relationships between specific exercise modalities and distinct microbial metabolic pathways will help promote the transition of exercise intervention from an experience-based rehabilitation approach toward a personalised, mechanism-driven, non-pharmacological strategy.
