Asymmetric deformation law and differentiated control technique for the roadway of an isolated working face in an extra-thick coal seam
Authors: Han Wu, Peilin Gong, Tong Zhao, Libin Bai, Junwei Yan, Azuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rib corners and roof of the 23,109 material roadway of the island working face in Xiegou Coal Mine suffer severe deformation and lacks effective support during its entire life cycle. In response to this problem, the surrounding rock deformation characteristics and stress transfer law during both the excavation and mining phases of the roadway were elucidated through an integrated research approach incorporating on-site measurement, theoretical calculation, and simulation analysis. The following research findings were yielded: After the mining of the adjacent 23,111 and 23,107 working faces, the edge of the 23,109 working face becomes a fractured zone where the stress is highly concentrated in a circular shape, while the central area remains within the original rock stress zone with a relatively low degree of stress concentration. When the roadway is excavated in such an environment where the stress is high at both ends and low in the middle, the surrounding rock experiences tensile failure in the shallow part and shear failure in the deep part. Besides, the initially approximately symmetrical deformation of the roof, floor, and ribs transforms into asymmetric deformation characterized by faster subsidence of the ribs and roof. During the mining of the 23,109 working face, the advanced abutment stress is adjusted. At a distance of 7 m ahead of the 23,109 working face, the maximum principal stress concentration zone shifts towards the side of the 23,109 working face. Meanwhile, the original plastic zone in the roof, particularly near the rib corners, expands rapidly and connects with the newly formed plastic zone in the overburden. Based on the asymmetric plastic failure characteristics of the roadway, a zonal differentiated synergistic control support scheme was proposed and applied on site. Field test results demonstrate that the roadway deformation satisfies the safe production requirements of the mine.Keywords: Extra-thick coal seam, Isolated island, Asymmetrical, Maximum principal stress, Stress deflectionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

To ensure normal mining face succession and reduce interference from roadway excavation during mining, the skip-mining method is often adopted for working faces within a single or double wing in the same mining area during mining succession. Along the strike direction of the working face, there is at least one isolated island working face with both sides being mined-out areas1,2. Currently, under the mining conditions of isolated island working faces in various coal mines, the ground pressure manifestation is intense, and its ground pressure manifestation pattern is significantly different from that of normal mining faces. Especially in the context of extra-thick coal seams, the overlying strata of isolated island working faces and mining roads are significantly affected by the mining of high-mining-height working faces, resulting in obvious stress manifestation, rapid and drastic deformation and failure of the surrounding rocks in the roads, which seriously affects the safe production of coal mines3–6. Therefore, mastering the stress evolution law and deformation and failure characteristics of the surrounding rocks in the roads during mining of isolated island working faces is a key issue that needs to be urgently addressed to effectively control the deformation and failure of the surrounding rocks in the roads of isolated island working faces and achieve normal mining face succession.

Extensive research has been conducted on the ground pressure characteristics, deformation and failure characteristics of surrounding rocks in roadways, changes in stratum structure, and the reasonable width of coal pillars left in isolated island working faces affected by multiple mining voids in extra-thick coal seams, and corresponding achievements have been made. Yun et al.7,8 explored the deformation and damage characteristics of surrounding rocks in recovery roadways of isolated island working faces at different stages, revealing the damage mechanism and evolution law of surrounding rocks in these roadways. Liu et al.9,10 analyzed the basic laws of ground pressure manifestation in isolated island working faces in "three-soft" coal seams, as well as the deformation and instability mechanisms of surrounding rocks in recovery roadways, providing theoretical support for safe mining in isolated island working faces. Jia et al.11 derived an expression for the support force of coal seams in isolated island working faces by analyzing the “umbrella arch” of overlying strata, verifying the structural mechanism in mining of isolated island working faces, and forming an asymmetric stress relief control mechanism for surrounding rocks in roadways during mining. Wang et al.12,13 studied the heterogeneity of coal and rock materials from the perspective of numerical simulation, revealing the dynamic characteristics of energy release during periodic roof pressure on isolated island working faces, and analyzing the mechanism of energy release surge ahead of the working face. Wei et al.14–16 used numerical simulation and theoretical analysis to analyze the stress distribution patterns and surrounding rock failure characteristics during roadway excavation and recovery periods in isolated island working faces, and obtained the reasonable size of protective coal pillars. Jiang et al.17,18 analyzed the dynamic changes in roof structure of irregular mined-out areas on both sides during recovery in isolated island working faces, using high-precision micro-seismic monitoring technology to obtain the movement angle and contact angle of the overlying strata in the mine’s mined-out areas, and proposed an instability analysis method for calculating the media support pressure formula in isolated island working faces. Huang et al.19,20 proposed a technical solution for releasing blasting pressure on the roof of isolated island working faces based on different impact hazard zones and mining areas, and verified the anti-impact effect through hole detection and infrared radiation data; Wang et al.21–23 proposed a directional hydraulic fracturing control technology for the roof stress distribution characteristics of isolated island working faces in extra-thick coal seams through on-site measurements and numerical simulation experiments. Wang et al.24 studied the bedding separation grouting subsidence reduction technology for the soft and hard rock inter-layer overburden of isolated island working faces under villages through theoretical analysis, engineering experiments, and on-site monitoring. Xue et al.25–27 revealed the failure mechanism of surrounding rock in isolated island working face tunnels through theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and experimental methods, and proposed a composite support scheme of "bolt + anchor cable + lining".

In summary, numerous scholars have conducted extensive research on the deformation and failure patterns of surrounding rocks in gateway extraction tunnels of isolated coal faces. However, the study on the deformation and failure forms of surrounding rocks, as well as the stress evolution patterns and control techniques, remains insufficiently deep. Specifically, after the adjacent panels were mined out, the goafs on both sides, the residual coal mass, and the overlying strata jointly formed a ring-shaped stress redistribution structure around the isolated working face. This structure breaks the original symmetry of the stress field and produces uneven confinement and asymmetric load transfer around the roadway. Therefore, the roadway instability can be understood as a typical failure mode caused by asymmetric boundary constraints. From a broader mechanics perspective, the roadway of an isolated working face can be regarded as an underground opening embedded in a non-uniform boundary-constrained medium. After the adjacent panels are mined out, the surrounding goafs, residual coal mass, and overlying strata jointly create an annular or ring-shaped stress redistribution zone around the isolated panel. This stress field is essentially characterized by strong spatial gradients, uneven confinement, and asymmetric load transfer. Therefore, the instability of the gateway is not only a specific mining-induced phenomenon, but also a representative problem concerning the stability of openings under non-uniform boundary conditions. Similar mechanical features can be found in eccentric loading around deep excavations, failure localization in gradient-structured materials, and differential degradation in geological media, where asymmetric boundary constraints drive the directional accumulation of deviatoric stress and eventually lead to localized failure. Therefore, taking the 23,109 isolated working face in Xiegou Coal Mine as an engineering case, this study investigates the asymmetric deformation of a gateway subjected to non-uniform boundary constraints and a strong gradient stress field. Field monitoring, theoretical calculation, and FLAC3D numerical simulation are combined to reveal the evolution of principal stresses, deviatoric stress concentration, and plastic-zone migration during roadway excavation and working face mining. The study aims to clarify how the ring-shaped stress field induced by the isolated-panel structure is transferred to the roadway scale and how it drives the transition from approximately symmetric deformation to asymmetric failure. On this basis, a differentiated support scheme is proposed. The results provide field validation and mechanical evidence for understanding the stability of underground openings under non-uniform boundary conditions.

Engineering geological conditions

The 23,109 working face is located in the central area of the north wing of the 21 mining area, bordering the 23,107 working face on the west and the 23,111 working face on the east. The working face, which is buried at a depth of approximately 400 m, is overlain by the 8# coal seam and affected by multiple goafs. Its strike length and dip width are 2,924.3 m and 253.2 m, respectively. The thickness of the coal seam ranges from 13.03 to 15.80 m and averages 14.86 m, and the Protodyakonov hardness of the coal seam lies in the range of 2–3. The 23,109 material roadway, with a width of 5.5 m and a height of 3.8 m, is arranged within the coal seam, and a 20-m-wide coal pillar is retained. The immediate roof of the roadway is composed of mudstone; the main roof consists of fine-grained sandstone; and the immediate floor comprises mudstone. The deformation of the roadway was controllable during roadway excavation, whereas severe asymmetric deformation occurred during working face mining, primarily characterized by significant roof subsidence on the side of the working face. Asymmetric deformation mechanism of the roadway caused by deviatoric stress

Under the long-term action of in-situ stress, the rock strata maintain an overall equilibrium state. During working face mining and roadway excavation, the original stress equilibrium state is disrupted. Accordingly, the stress environment is continuously adjusted, transitioning from linear elastic behaviors to plastic behaviors. Following the dynamic adjustment of advanced support pressure, the rock mass undergoes local plastic instability first. During the subsequent yield-induced plastic flow, minor displacements caused by elastic deformation, i.e., volume changes arising from the spherical stress, are ignored. Instead, the focus is placed on the dilatancy and tensile failure resulting from joint surface slippage and twisting under the action of deviatoric stress. In summary, the Mohr–Coulomb yield criterion, the Tresca yield criterion, and the von Mises yield criterion all employ the differences in principal stresses in various directions as key variables, comprehensively consider the interactions between the maximum, intermediate, and minimum principal stresses, For a rock mass surrounding an underground opening, the hydrostatic stress component mainly governs volumetric compression, whereas the deviatoric stress component controls distortional deformation, shear slip, tensile splitting, and crack coalescence. Under a nearly uniform boundary condition, micro-cracks tend to initiate and propagate in a relatively balanced manner around the opening, and the plastic zone generally presents an approximately symmetric morphology. However, in the isolated working face investigated in this study, the roadway is located in a stress environment with high stresses at both sides and relatively low stress in the middle. Such a non-uniform boundary condition causes the principal stress direction and the principal stress difference to become spatially unbalanced around the roadway. At the meso-mechanical scale, the side with a larger principal stress difference is more prone to directional tensile splitting in the shallow surrounding rock and shear slip in the deeper confined rock mass. With continued excavation or mining-induced stress adjustment, these locally initiated tensile and shear cracks propagate preferentially along the direction of the stress gradient. The originally scattered damage zones then coalesce, forming an asymmetric plastic zone at the macroscopic scale. Therefore, the asymmetric deformation of the roadway is not simply a consequence of strength reduction after yielding, but a progressive cross-scale failure process controlled by the migration of the deviatoric stress field: non-uniform boundary constraint → principal stress deflection → directional crack initiation and shear slip → plastic-zone coalescence → macroscopic asymmetric deformation. and effectively reveal the process of plastic failure in rock masses arising from stress concentration induced by mining disturbances. Therefore, in numerical simulation analysis, it is of paramount importance to research on both the principal stresses and their differences in principal stress space. Specifically, the maximum and minimum principal stresses, as well as the deviatoric stress programmed with user-defined additional variables in FLAC3D, can be utilized to analyze the asymmetric deformation characteristics of the roadway resulting from non-uniform stress distribution under the action of advanced abutment pressure.

Numerical inversion of the influence range of advanced abutment pressure

In extra-thick coal seams, the roadway cross-sections expand with the increase of mining height. Under the action of advanced abutment pressure, the exposed goaf-side surface without surrounding rock confinement is particularly prone to rib spalling. Such local instability may trigger global safety hazards. Section "Asymmetric deformation mechanism of the roadway caused by deviatoric stress" is mainly dedicated to the theoretical calculation of the influence range of advanced abutment pressure. In the numerical simulation, monitoring lines containing a total of 20 monitoring points (spaced at 2.5 m intervals) are placed respectively at the edge and in the central area of the working face. After the working face has advanced for 65 m, the monitoring data were extracted to validate the influence range of advanced abutment pressure (Fig. 4).

Deformation during roadway excavation

The excavation of the 23,109 material roadway starts after the simulation on the stress environment of the 23,109 isolated island working face. The peak influence range of advanced abutment pressure has been determined to be 15 m in Sect. "Asymmetric deformation mechanism of the roadway caused by deviatoric stress" and "Numerical inversion of the influence range of advanced abutment pressure". Considering this finding, slices of the roadway cross-section are taken at distances of 2 m, 7 m, and 15 m from the excavation face to explore how the plastic zone expands during excavation (Fig. 7).

Cloudy diagram of roadway at different excavation distances.

At a distance of 2 m from the excavation face, the roadway cross-section transitions rapidly from a triaxial stress state to a biaxial stress state as a result of excavation. Such a rapid and intense stress adjustment causes the plastic zone in the roof and floor to extend to a depth of 7 m and about 4 m, respectively. Meanwhile, the plastic zones in both ribs are nearly symmetrical. The overall deformation of roadway surrounding rock is approximately 180 mm.

At a distance of 7 m from the excavation face, the plastic zone experiences a transition from symmetrical deformation in the roof, floor, and ribs to asymmetric deformation. To be specific, the plastic zone in the roof expands abruptly. Tensile failure zones emerge in the shallow surrounding rock, particularly on the side adjacent to the goaf. The rib adjacent to the working face moves toward the excavation space. The roof and rib corners deform severely, as evidenced by a displacement of up to 40 cm, whereas the floor deforms mildly.

At a distance of 15 m from the excavation face, the plastic zone shape has completely transformed from a symmetrical “ellipse” to an asymmetric pattern. A triangular plastic zone appears in the main roof above the roadway. Roof deformation reaches approximately 600 mm, but the growth of displacement decelerate. As the excavation face moves further away, the stress adjustment gradually stabilizes. This evolution indicates that roadway excavation first induces relatively balanced unloading damage around the opening. As the stress adjustment continues, the spatial difference in principal stresses becomes more pronounced, and the deviatoric stress begins to concentrate preferentially at the roof and rib corners. The shallow tensile plastic zones can be regarded as the macroscopic representation of tensile crack initiation and opening, whereas the deeper shear plastic zones indicate shear slip and crack propagation under higher confinement. The transition from an approximately elliptical plastic zone to an asymmetric plastic zone therefore reflects the directional development and partial coalescence of local damage zones controlled by deviatoric stress.

Deformation during working face mining

In the numerical simulation, slices of the roadway cross-section are taken at distances of 2 m, 7 m, and 15 m from the 23,109 working face to investigate the deformation patterns of roadway surrounding rock during the mining of the 23,109 working face.

As shown in Fig. 8, the mining of the 23,109 working face causes secondary disturbances to the stress field of roadway surrounding rock. The stress is redistributed to a new equilibrium state, which further deteriorates the roadway surrounding rock and drives the elastic–plastic limit equilibrium zone to move deeper. At a distance of 15 m from the 23,109 working face, the mining-induced stress adjustment of roadway surrounding rock brings about intensified expansion of the plastic zone. The plastic zone in the roof reaches a depth of approximately 20 m, and the displacement increases to 800 mm. The areas with notable rib displacement also expand gradually compared to the excavation phase.

Cloudy diagram of roadway at different distances of mining at the working face.

The peak advanced abutment pressure appears near the position 7 m away from the 23,109 working face. At this position, the plastic zones in the roof and floor begin to connect with those induced by the mining stress. As a consequence, the expansion of plastic zones accelerates noticeably. The connection between the pre-existing excavation-induced plastic zone and the newly formed mining-induced plastic zone suggests a damage coalescence process from the roadway scale to the overburden scale. Under the action of advanced abutment pressure, the deviatoric stress concentration accelerates the directional propagation of tensile and shear damage. Once these damage zones become connected, the roadway surrounding rock loses its local bearing capacity more rapidly, and the asymmetric deformation changes from a local failure phenomenon to a macroscopic instability pattern. In the meantime, the area with a larger roof displacement starts to shift towards the retained coal pillar.

At a distance of 2 m from the 23,109 working face, the influence of advance abutment pressure adjustments on roadway deformation becomes negligible. The displacements of roadway surrounding rock and the range of plastic zones grow slightly compared to the case at a distance of 7 m.

Evolutions of principle stresses

As presented in Fig. 9, at a distance of 15 m ahead of the 23,109 working face, the maximum principal stress around the roadway is 39.2 MPa, and the stress is concentrated on the side adjacent to the 23,111 working face. The minimum principal stress is concentrated at approximately the same position as the maximum principal stress. The stress reduction zone takes on an “elliptical” shape. The maximum principal stress difference amounts to 27.8 MPa, and the stress is intensively distributed in the 23,109 working face and its overburden. The large deviatoric stress leads to a strong expansion of the plastic zone around the roadway.

Diagram of principal stress at different distances of mining.

The stress adjustment is the most intense at a distance of 7 m ahead of the 23,109 working face. The area where the maximum principal stress is concentrated shifts from the side of the 23,111 working face to the side adjacent to the 23,109 working face, the maximum principal stress being 31.6 MPa. The plastic zone around the roadway expands, causing a gradual expansion of the stress reduction zone.

At a distance of 2 m ahead of the 23,109 working face, since the mining-induced stress redistribution has basically stabilized, the deformation of roadway surrounding rock shows slower growth than in the initial stage of advanced abutment stress loading, and the surrounding rock tends to stabilize. The area where the maximum principal stress is concentrated, i.e., the area on the side of the 23,109 working face, expands slowly. As the principal stress deflects, the plastic zone in the roof and the rib corners become connected with the plastic zone caused by the mining of the 23,109 working face. The minimum principal stress and the maximum principal stress share consistent variation laws. The uneven stress distribution, i.e., the principal stress difference, causes remarkable deformation of the plastic zone in the roof. Therefore, the principal stress difference provides a mechanical indicator for identifying the directional development of damage around the roadway. The deflection of the maximum principal stress changes the local loading path of the surrounding rock, while the increase in deviatoric stress promotes tensile cracking in the shallow unloading zone and shear failure in the deeper confined zone. The asymmetric plastic zone observed in the simulation can thus be interpreted as the macroscopic outcome of stress-gradient-controlled crack initiation, propagation, and coalescence.

Evolution of deviatoric stress and deformation energy density along critical paths

The initiation of local damage is associated with the concentration of deviatoric stress, the migration of damage is controlled by the spatial gradient of deviatoric stress, and the final connection of the plastic zone is accompanied by the continuous accumulation and transfer of deformation energy density.

Differentiated support scheme for roadway surrounding rock in the working face

Support scheme

The uneven deformation and failure of the 23,109 material roadway is introduced as follows: According to on-site observations, the original support scheme managed to effectively control the deformation during roadway excavation. However, during the mining of the 23,109 working face, asymmetric deformation characteristics become apparent after entering the influence range of advanced abutment pressure (within 73 m ahead of the working face). In this case, a differentiated reinforcement support scheme is needed, especially for the severe roof subsidence on the side of the working face. To address the above asymmetric deformation, without changing the row and column spacings, large-diameter high-preload anchor cables with a structure of 1 × 7, a diameter of 22 mm, and a length of 14,000 mm are additionally installed at a position 150 mm away from the roof of the roadway on the side of the working face and at an angle of 15° to the roof, and a ZL1700 drilling rig is employed to drill Ф28 mm holes to match the diameter of anchor cables correspondingly. The pre-load is raised from 220 to 350 kN for the purpose of anchoring deeper rock strata and suspending the immediate roof. Meanwhile, high-strength anchor bolts with a diameter of 22 mm and a length of 2200 mm are installed at a position 900 mm away from the roof in the hope of enhancing the overall strength of the shallow surrounding rock. Furthermore, W-shaped steel straps are used in conjunction with anchor bolts and cables to expand the preload range. The original and improved support forms are exhibited in Fig. 11, and the contour maps of support stress before and after the optimization are compared in Fig. 12.

Support scheme.

Comparison of stress fields of different support schemes.

Under the original support scheme, the stress is highly concentrated on the side of the working face, the maximum stress value being approximately 4.25 MPa. Consequently, the roof and rib corners deform seriously. In the optimized support scheme, the addition of anchor cables and bolts increases the overall support density, provides a relatively high surrounding pressure for the surrounding rock that has undergone plastic deformation before support, and transforms the supported area in a high-preload triaxial stress state. Moreover, the combination of the W-shaped steel straps with anchor bolts and cables leads to the formation of compressive stress zones that are interconnected and continuous. Such a combination manages to effectively resist the combined failure mode of tensile failure in the shallow part and shear failure in the deep part and enhance the strength and anchoring mechanical properties in the reinforced support area, which is reflected in parameters such as cohesion and internal friction angle. The maximum stress in the high-stress field is 8.75 MPa. The optimized scheme, in which anchor cables provide anchorage in the deep part and anchor bolts provide reinforcement in the shallow part, achieves a synergistic support effect and ensures the effectiveness of the overall support.

After support, the roadway convergence was monitored on site for 80 days, with results given in Fig. 13. The roadway deformed rapidly during the first 20 days after excavation, and the deformation basically stabilized after 25 days. The maximum roof-to-floor convergence and rib-to-rib displacement rates reached 200 mm/day and 150 mm/day, respectively, and the maximum roof subsidence and rib displacement amounted to 290 mm and 170 mm, respectively. However, in the final stage of stress adjustment, i.e., beyond the influence range of peak advanced abutment pressure, the surrounding rock displacement gradually stabilized, and the support effect was enhanced, which ensures safe production in the coal mine. As shown in Fig. 14, the cracks and local peeling of the roadway roof are not uniformly distributed along the circumference of the borehole, but mainly concentrated in a limited area of the borehole wall. The main characteristic of damage morphology is the development of axial to sub axial cracks, accompanied by local oblique coalescence and blocky peeling. Compared with the other side of the borehole, the concentrated area shows stronger crack development and stronger local degradation, indicating that damage accumulates preferentially on one side rather than symmetrical evolution. This uneven fracture distribution is consistent with the numerical simulation results, showing the movement of the main stress concentration zone and the asymmetric evolution of the plastic zone. From a mechanical perspective, the concentration of cracks on one side indicates that the surrounding rock is subjected to a spatially unbalanced stress path, with deviatoric stress preferentially concentrated on one side of the roadway, leading to directional tensile shear damage propagation. Therefore, the qualitative confirmation of drilling images confirms the explanation for the asymmetric deformation of the roadway, which is controlled by the migration of the main stress concentration zone and the non-uniform expansion of the plastic zone, and can be well matched with the numerical results.

Discussion: generalized mechanical implication of asymmetric roadway failure under non-uniform boundary constraints

From a cross-scale perspective, the asymmetric failure process can be interpreted as follows. At the stress-field scale, the isolated-panel structure generates a ring-shaped and non-uniform stress redistribution field. At the roadway scale, this non-uniform field induces principal stress deflection and uneven principal stress differences around the opening. At the meso-damage scale, tensile cracks are more likely to initiate in the shallow unloading zone, while shear cracks and slip surfaces develop in the deeper confined zone. As stress adjustment continues, these tensile and shear damage zones propagate and coalesce, eventually forming a macroscopic asymmetric plastic zone. Therefore, the observed roof subsidence and rib-corner failure are the macroscopic manifestation of a progressive cross-scale process driven by deviatoric stress migration.

The asymmetric failure index proposed in this study provides a semi-quantitative way to connect the deviatoric stress tensor with the morphology of the plastic zone. The equivalent deviatoric stress q describes the intensity of distortional deformation, whereas the asymmetric coefficient A_s describes the spatial imbalance of the deviatoric stress path on both sides of the roadway. Therefore, the term A_s q can be regarded as an additional damage-driving component induced by non-uniform boundary constraints. When this component increases, tensile and shear damage tend to accumulate preferentially on the side with a higher deviatoric stress level, which explains the directional propagation and coalescence of the plastic zone. It should be emphasized that the modified failure index proposed here is a mechanistic interpretation rather than a fully calibrated constitutive model. The coefficient λ still requires further calibration using laboratory tests, acoustic emission monitoring, micro-CT reconstruction, or more detailed field measurements. Nevertheless, this index helps bridge the gap between the conventional elastoplastic failure criterion and the observed asymmetric deformation of the roadway under a strong gradient stress field.

Although the numerical simulation reveals the shift of the principal stress concentration zone and the asymmetric connection of the plastic zone, direct field evidence for the rotation of principal stress direction and the dominant fracture propagation direction is still limited. In the present study, the field monitoring mainly included roadway convergence measurements, and no in-situ borehole stress meters, borehole imaging, acoustic emission localization, or microseismic monitoring data were available to directly capture the internal stress-path evolution and fracture-field asymmetry. Therefore, the proposed deviatoric-stress-driven asymmetric failure mechanism should be regarded as a plausible mechanical explanation and a working hypothesis supported by theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and macroscopic deformation monitoring, rather than as direct field proof of the full cross-scale failure process. Future studies should combine in-situ stress monitoring, borehole imaging, acoustic emission localization, and microseismic monitoring to directly verify the principal stress rotation, fracture propagation direction, and asymmetric plastic-zone evolution under non-uniform boundary constraints.

The findings of this study also provide a generalizable design paradigm for predicting and controlling asymmetric failure in underground engineering. First, the stress-gradient environment around the opening should be diagnosed by identifying the spatial distribution of principal stress difference, equivalent deviatoric stress, or deformation energy density. Second, the potential asymmetric damage zone should be determined according to the direction of deviatoric stress migration and the asymmetry coefficient of the stress path. Third, support measures should be arranged in a differentiated and zonal manner, with higher support density, greater preload, and deeper anchorage applied to the side where deviatoric stress and damage energy are more strongly concentrated. Therefore, the control of asymmetric roadway deformation should not rely only on increasing the overall support strength. Instead, it should follow a stress-gradient-oriented support strategy, namely: stress-gradient diagnosis → asymmetric damage identification → zonal differentiated reinforcement. This design paradigm may be applicable to other underground openings subjected to asymmetric boundary constraints, such as tunnels crossing faults, deep roadways adjacent to goafs, asymmetric slope excavations, and hydraulic fracturing zones in reservoir engineering.

Conclusions and outlook

This study reveals that, for an underground opening located in a strong gradient stress field, asymmetric failure is governed not only by the absolute magnitude of stress, but also by the spatial divergence and directional migration of the deviatoric stress field. The failure zone tends to initiate and propagate toward the direction where the principal stress difference and deformation energy density are more strongly concentrated.

After the adjacent 23,111 and 23,107 working faces were mined, a ring-shaped stress redistribution structure was formed around the 23,109 isolated working face. The goaf-side edges were characterized by stress relief, fracture development, and local stress concentration, while the central area retained a relatively higher bearing capacity. This non-uniform boundary condition created a strong stress-gradient environment for subsequent roadway excavation and working face mining.
