Explainable AI-based optimization framework for sustainable fly ash concrete design
Authors: Morteza Khorshidi, Pourya Nejatipour, Amirehsan Teimortashlu, Giuseppe Oliveto, Ehsan Afaridegan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFly ash is widely recognized as an effective cement replacement material for reducing the environmental impact of concrete production while maintaining acceptable mechanical performance. This study develops an explainable artificial intelligence-based multi-objective framework for the sustainable design of fly ash concrete by combining predictive modeling, feature interpretation, uncertainty assessment, and optimization within a single workflow. A compiled dataset of 1,062 concrete mix samples was used, from which outlier detection was applied only to the training subset, resulting in 58 anomalous samples being removed from the training data while the independent test set remained unchanged. Four artificial intelligence models, namely TabNet, SVR, NGBoost, and SAINT, were trained and compared for predicting 28-day compressive strength. Among them, NGBoost achieved the best testing performance, with RMSE = 4.897, MAE = 3.492, MAPE = 7.844, and R2 = 0.92. Feature interpretation showed that cement content, water content, and MCS-28 were the most influential variables governing strength prediction. To further evaluate model reliability, a bootstrap-based uncertainty analysis was conducted, showing that TabNet and NGBoost exhibited comparatively narrower uncertainty bounds. The best-performing predictive model was then coupled with four nature-inspired optimization algorithms to generate concrete mix designs that maximize compressive strength while minimizing CO2 footprint. The optimization results demonstrated that the proposed framework identifies theoretical Pareto-optimal candidate mixtures within the dataset domain, highlighting its potential as a data-driven decision-support tool for sustainable concrete mix design. The optimized mixtures are computational Pareto-optimal solutions and not experimentally validated production mixes. A limitation of this study is the lack of source-level labels in the compiled dataset, which prevented grouped validation across original studies. Introduction

Background and motivation

Concrete is one of the most widely used construction materials due to its strength and durability1. By the year 2050, the estimated total concrete output is expected to reach 18 billion tons, indicating the rising use of concrete in modern construction practices2–4. Cement is one of the dominantly used constituents of concrete5. However, the manufacture of cement is highly polluting. Around 6.1 billion tons of cement are annually produced worldwide, contributing around 6% of the carbon dioxide (CO2) footprint2. It needs about 3650 megajoules of fossil fuel energy and 140 kilowatt-hours of electricity for the manufacture of only one ton of cement6,7. Because of the high energy consumption and CO2 emissions during cement manufacturing, it is essential to reduce the use of cement in concrete and explore greener substitutes8. Fly ash is a fine material obtained from coal-based power plants, which can be used as a substitute for cement in concrete manufacturing9. By reacting with calcium hydroxide, fly ash produces calcium iron silicate, the main cementitious compound, which enhances the workability, durability, and strength of the concrete10–12. Typically, fly ash replacement levels of 15–25% are widely used to maintain a balance between strength and durability under standard conditions13,14. Higher replacement levels (up to 50%) have been reported in specific applications, particularly when optimized mix designs and controlled curing conditions are employed15. However, such high replacement ratios may lead to limitations, including reduced early-age strength and increased sensitivity to curing conditions.

AI-based modeling in concrete

Previous studies16–18 have examined the effects of using fly ash in concrete on CO2 footprint, demonstrating its potential to significantly lower the environmental footprint of cement-based materials. In addition, some studies19–21 have focused on the effects of fly ash on the durability and mechanical properties of concrete. On the other hand, Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques are rapidly advancing and have proven to be effective tools for solving complex problems. Some studies have also used these techniques for the prediction of the compressive strength of fly ash concrete. For example, Jiang et al.22 used a database of 270 fly ash concrete samples, and they used the Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Regression (SVR) machine learning approaches, which are among the traditional machine learning approaches, for the prediction of the compressive strength. Their results indicated that the SVR approach performed better than the RF approach, suggesting that the compressive strength can perhaps be predicted without doing laboratory tests. Additionally, their results indicated that the fly ash parameter had a lower importance, which can be explored thoroughly. In their study, Khursheed et al.23 used various machine learning approaches, including Genetic Programming and Minimax Probability Machine Regression (MPMR), for the prediction of the concrete strength (CSC). They used a database of 112 samples, where the input variables included cement, fly ash, coarse aggregate, fine aggregate, and water. Finally, the approaches were compared, and the results indicated that the MPMR technique performed better for the prediction of the compressive strength compared to the other approaches. However, the results were not analyzed thoroughly, and the study ended with comparing the approaches. Wadhawan et al.24 used the nine conventional machine learning approaches, including Linear Regression (LR), Gradient Boosting (GB), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machine (SVM), M5P, Genetic Programming (GP), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), for the prediction of the concrete strength. They used a database of 455 samples, where the 12 variables were used as the input variables. The approaches were compared on the basis of their accuracy, and the results indicated that GB performed better compared to the other approaches. However, the results were not analyzed for the determination of the effects of the input variables on the predictions, which reduced the applicability of the approaches for practical purposes. Also, Wang et al.25 explored how incorporating fly ash (FA) into concrete impacts both compressive strength and carbon emissions throughout the material’s lifecycle. They employed several ML models, including artificial neural networks and traditional algorithms like support vector machines and random forest, to predict concrete strength and CO2 footprint. While the ANN model delivered the most accurate results, the study primarily relied on conventional modeling approaches. In a recent work, authors Kumar et al.26 used sophisticated models of ML: XGBoost, Random Forest, and SVM-RBF for the prediction of the CSC, with good results using XGBoost. However, the paper, despite showing the efficacy of the used model for addressing the nonlinear problem, used a comparatively small data sample of 192 data points, which might affect the model’s universality. Additionally, the study did not contain a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the model or the importance of the input parameters. In addition, Naseri et al.3 developed predictive models for the CSC mixtures—without the inclusion of fly ash—using six metaheuristic optimization algorithms. Their study introduced multi-objective functions to balance compressive strength, cost, embodied CO2 footprint, and material consumption, ultimately proposing 16 sustainable mix designs. The results indicated that the water cycle algorithm outperformed the other methods. It is important to note that conventional ML methods generally offer significantly higher prediction accuracy compared to models that solely provide explicit mathematical formulations through optimization techniques.

Recent studies have further emphasized the growing importance of advanced, interpretable, and sustainability-oriented data-driven approaches in concrete research27,28. For example, Khan et al.29. demonstrated that explainable AutoML models combined with Optuna, SHAP, and ensemble learning can provide accurate and interpretable prediction of high-performance concrete strength, highlighting the value of combining predictive capability with model transparency. In a related direction, another recent study employed automated machine learning and hyperparameter optimization for structural concrete-related prediction tasks, further confirming the increasing role of AutoML-based frameworks in civil engineering applications30. At the materials level, recent research has also shown that the incorporation of sustainable constituents such as sugarcane bagasse ash and stone dust can improve the environmental and mechanical performance of concrete, reinforcing the importance of optimization-based sustainable mixture design31. Similarly, Akhlaq et al.32 reported that partial replacement of cement with GGBS can improve the rheological, workability, and mechanical behavior of pumpable concrete, demonstrating the broader relevance of supplementary cementitious materials in performance-oriented and sustainable concrete design. Moreover, recent physics-guided machine learning developments for asphalt concrete fracture prediction further indicate a broader trend toward integrating intelligent predictive models with engineering knowledge to improve the reliability and applicability of material-performance prediction frameworks33.

Recent studies have further highlighted the growing role of hybrid and interpretable machine learning models in cement-based material design and strength prediction34–36. For example, advanced hybrid models integrating support vector regression with metaheuristic optimization algorithms have been successfully applied to estimate the compressive and tensile strength of basalt fibre reinforced concrete, where SHAP analysis was used to identify the most influential variables and improve model interpretability37,38. Similarly, machine learning models combined with hyperparameter tuning and optimization have shown strong capability in predicting the compressive strength of cement-based mortar materials, while also providing deeper interpretation of variable effects through SHAP and dependence-based analyses39. In another related study, innovative hybrid machine learning models were developed for estimating the compressive strength of copper mine tailings concrete, demonstrating the potential of combining sustainable material design with optimized predictive modeling and explainable analysis40. Taken together, these studies reinforce the growing importance of combining predictive modeling, interpretability, and optimization in sustainable concrete research, which directly supports the motivation of the present study.

Explainability and optimization approaches

The development of sustainable cementitious materials has attracted increasing attention due to the urgent need to reduce the environmental impact of construction industries while maintaining the mechanical reliability and long-term serviceability of infrastructure systems41–44. In this context, the incorporation of supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash has been widely recognized as an effective strategy for improving sustainability, refining microstructure, and controlling the strength development of cement-based materials42,44. Previous studies have shown that water condition, hydration evolution, pore structure, and internal material composition play critical roles in determining the mechanical response of mineral-based materials, while strength and failure behavior are also governed by stress state and structural characteristics42–44. Meanwhile, recent developments in advanced cementitious composites have demonstrated that microstructural tailoring and material modification can substantially enhance strength-related and impact-resistant performance41,44. In parallel, the increasing complexity of engineering materials and infrastructure systems has accelerated the use of data-driven and artificial intelligence-based methods for capturing nonlinear input–output relationships more effectively than conventional empirical approaches45–49. More importantly, hybrid frameworks that integrate predictive models with optimization strategies have shown strong potential for solving multi-objective engineering problems involving performance, reliability, efficiency, and sustainability trade-offs50–56. Recent studies have also emphasized the value of explainability, feature analysis, and intelligent model refinement for improving the practical applicability of AI systems in complex engineering domains57–59, while integrated optimization and scheduling formulations in other industrial systems further confirm the growing importance of systematic decision-support frameworks under coupled constraints60,61. Taken together, these developments provide a meaningful foundation for integrating explainable artificial intelligence and multi-objective optimization in the sustainable design of fly ash concrete52,53,58.

Research gap

Previous studies have extensively applied machine learning models to predict concrete properties, often achieving high accuracy62–64. However, many of these studies are primarily focused on prediction tasks and lack integration with optimization frameworks. In addition, limited attention has been given to simultaneously addressing both mechanical performance and environmental impacts, such as CO2 emissions. Furthermore, issues such as dataset dependency, generalization capability, and the absence of practical design constraints remain key limitations. Therefore, there is a need for a more integrated approach that combines accurate prediction with multi-objective optimization to support sustainable and practical concrete mix design.

Despite the growing number of studies employing machine learning (ML) models to predict the compressive strength of concrete containing fly ash, several critical gaps remain. While advanced machine learning and deep learning approaches have demonstrated high predictive performance in concrete strength prediction, their application is often limited to standalone prediction tasks without integration with interpretability and optimization frameworks. Many existing studies rely on relatively small and less diverse datasets, which can restrict the generalizability of the developed models. Furthermore, comprehensive analyses—such as sensitivity analysis, interpretability assessment, and monotonicity evaluation—are not consistently performed to better understand model behavior. Importantly, the integration of ML models with optimization frameworks for generating actionable insights and supporting sustainable and performance-based concrete mix design remains relatively underexplored.

Anomaly detection using local outlier factor (LOF)

The Local Outlier Factor (LOF) algorithm was employed to identify and remove anomalous data points from the dataset. LOF is an unsupervised anomaly detection method that evaluates the local density deviation of each data point with respect to its neighboring samples. Unlike global approaches, LOF is capable of detecting local anomalies—observations that significantly deviate from their surrounding data distribution in the feature space65. To avoid data leakage and ensure an unbiased evaluation, the dataset was first randomly divided into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. The LOF model was then fitted exclusively on the training dataset. Outliers were identified based on the LOF scores and removed only from the training data. The test dataset was kept completely unseen during this process and was not involved in outlier detection. In this study, the LOF algorithm was implemented using the following hyperparameters: number of neighbors (nneighbors = 20), contamination rate = 0.05, and Euclidean distance as the distance metric. These values were selected based on commonly adopted practices in the literature for datasets of similar size and characteristics. Data points with anomaly scores exceeding the threshold determined by the contamination parameter were classified as outliers. Across the training dataset, a total of 58 data points (5.46% of the original dataset) were identified as outliers and removed. The remaining data were used for model training. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of detected anomalies, where normal data points are shown in blue and outliers in red.

The removed outliers are likely associated with experimental inconsistencies, reporting errors, or heterogeneous testing conditions, rather than representing physically meaningful extreme mix designs.

In this study, no missing data were present, and therefore, no specific methods were required for handling missing values. All data used were complete and available. Additionally, data consistency was ensured through thorough review and preprocessing to eliminate any discrepancies across sources.

Z-score normalization of features

Z-score normalization was applied to standardize the input features and eliminate the effect of differing scales. In this approach, each feature is transformed by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, resulting in a distribution with zero mean and unit variance. To prevent data leakage, normalization parameters (mean and standard deviation) were computed exclusively from the training dataset. The same parameters were then applied to transform the test dataset. This ensures that no information from the test data influences the training process. Figure 3 illustrates the data distribution before and after normalization, demonstrating the effectiveness of the transformation in standardizing feature scales.

Advanced data transformation techniques such as log transformation and Box–Cox transformation were also considered. However, these methods were not applied in this study, as the input variables did not exhibit severe skewness requiring such transformations, and the adopted machine learning models are capable of handling nonlinear relationships without strict assumptions regarding data distribution.

Statistical analysis of factors affecting CSC

To estimate the CSC, a set of input parameters, including Fly Ash Addition, Water, Sand, Coarse Aggregate, Cement, Admixtures, and MCS-28, were considered. These independent variables are vital for determining the concrete’s performance and properties.

To assess the statistical significance of these factors, an F-test for regression (f_regression) was employed. This method evaluates the linear relationship between each continuous input variable and the target variable (CSC) by computing the corresponding F-statistic and p-value, without requiring categorical grouping of the data. The results provide valuable insights into the relative importance of each predictor in influencing compressive strength. Independent variables with a p-value less than the predefined significance level (typically 0.05) are considered to have a statistically significant effect on CSC66.

Sensitivity analysis using SHAP

To better understand the influence of each input feature on model predictions, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was employed for sensitivity analysis. Since NGBoost showed the best predictive performance among the evaluated models, the SHAP analysis was performed on the NGBoost model 67. SHAP is a widely used explainability approach based on cooperative game theory that quantifies the contribution of each feature to an individual prediction. In this framework, the SHAP value for feature i is defined as:

where ϕi is the SHAP value of feature i, F is the set of all input features, S is a subset of features excluding feature i, and fS(xS) denotes the model prediction based on the subset S. This formulation represents the average marginal contribution of each feature over all possible feature subsets, thereby providing a theoretically grounded interpretation of feature importance and local prediction behavior68–70.

In the present study, SHAP values were computed using the KernelExplainer, which is a model-agnostic method suitable for explaining complex predictive models such as NGBoost. The SHAP analysis was conducted using the test dataset only, in order to provide an unbiased interpretation of the generalization behavior of the selected model and to avoid potential data leakage. This analysis enabled both global interpretation, by identifying the overall importance of input features across the dataset, and local interpretation, by showing how each feature contributed to individual predictions71,72. Therefore, the SHAP analysis helped reveal the relative importance of the input variables and provided additional insight into the relationship between the model inputs and CSC predictions73,74.

Artificial intelligence models

In this study, four AI models—TabNet, SVR, NGBoost, and SAINT—were selected to develop the predictive framework for estimating CSC using fly ash addition, water, sand, coarse aggregate, cement, admixtures, and MCS-28 as input variables. The selection of these models was intentionally made to ensure a comprehensive representation of different learning paradigms for modeling complex, nonlinear relationships in tabular data. Specifically, SVR was included as a well-established kernel-based machine learning method that serves as a strong classical benchmark for nonlinear regression problems. NGBoost was chosen due to its probabilistic nature and its ability to provide both accurate predictions and uncertainty quantification, which is particularly important in material performance prediction. TabNet was incorporated as a state-of-the-art attention-based deep learning model specifically designed for structured tabular data, enabling automatic feature selection and improved interpretability. Finally, SAINT was selected as a transformer-based architecture capable of capturing complex inter-feature dependencies through self-attention mechanisms. By combining these four complementary modeling approaches, the study ensures a fair and robust comparison across classical machine learning, probabilistic boosting, and advanced deep learning architectures, thereby strengthening the reliability and generalizability of the predictive framework.

The dataset was randomly divided into 80% for training and 20% for testing. To enhance model robustness and reduce sensitivity to data partitioning, a fivefold cross-validation procedure was applied to the training dataset during model development and hyperparameter tuning. This approach helps mitigate overfitting and improves the stability of the learned models75,76. Model performance was then evaluated and compared, and the best-performing model was subsequently used as the surrogate predictor in the multi-objective optimization framework.

The selected machine learning models were chosen to represent diverse methodological families suitable for tabular data. SVR was employed as a classical kernel-based method, while TabNet and SAINT represent recent deep learning approaches tailored for structured data. NGBoost was selected for its ability to provide probabilistic predictions and uncertainty quantification. This combination allows for a comprehensive comparison of model performance and interpretability across different algorithmic paradigms.

Hyperparameter tuning

Hyperparameter tuning was carried out using the Optuna framework. For each model, the objective function was defined as minimizing the RMSE of the predictions over 150 trials using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler. Early stopping of unpromising trials was applied to improve computational efficiency, and a fixed random seed of 42 was used to ensure reproducibility. The optimal hyperparameter values obtained from this process were used to train the final models86,87.

Optimization algorithm

After model evaluation, the best-performing predictive model was integrated into a multi-objective optimization framework to maximize CSC and minimize CO2 footprint. In this framework, the AI model served as a surrogate evaluator, allowing rapid assessment of candidate mix designs without repeated experimental testing. Four metaheuristic algorithms—MFO, IWO, PO, and OO—were used to search for optimal solutions67,88.

Moth flame optimization (MFO) algorithm

MFO was used as a population-based search algorithm in which each candidate solution moves toward promising solutions through a spiral updating mechanism. In this study, it was applied to explore the trade-off between CSC and CO2 footprint89.

Improved whale optimization (IWO) algorithm

IWO was employed as an enhanced whale-based search algorithm with improved exploration and exploitation capability. It was used to search the concrete mix design space under the defined objective functions and constraints90.

Pelican optimization (PO) algorithm

PO was applied as a two-phase metaheuristic algorithm involving exploration and exploitation steps. In the present work, it was used to identify candidate mixtures with favorable mechanical and environmental performance91.

Osprey optimization (OO) algorithm

OO was used as a bio-inspired optimization algorithm with separate search and refinement phases. In this study, it was implemented to improve the search for optimal concrete mixtures within the multi-objective framework92.

Model ranking approach

To effectively evaluate and rank the performance of various AI models in predicting CSC, the Taylor diagram method was employed. This approach provides a comprehensive graphical summary of how well each model reproduces the observed data by simultaneously considering three key statistical parameters: the PCC, SD, and cRMSD103–105.

The Taylor diagram allows for the visualization of these metrics in a polar coordinate system, where the radial distance represents the standard deviation, the angular coordinate corresponds to the correlation coefficient, and concentric contours indicate the cRMSD values106–108. By integrating these parameters into a single plot, the diagram facilitates a clear comparison of model accuracy, precision, and overall agreement with experimental observations109–111. This method enhances the reliability of model ranking by considering multiple statistical indicators collectively rather than in isolation, leading to a more robust assessment of predictive capabilities during both training and testing stages.

This section presents a structured analysis of the key steps undertaken to evaluate and optimize CSC prediction models and concrete mix designs. First, a sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of input features on CSC prediction, providing insights into the relative importance of each variable. Further, hyperparameter optimization was performed to improve the results of various AI models, such as NGBoost, SAINT, SVR, and TabNet. Extensive testing of these models was performed on the training and testing sets using varied statistical parameters to assess the adequacy of the proposed approaches. Finally, a multi-objective optimization approach was utilized in the selection of optimal concrete composite designs that addressed both mechanical strength and environmental factors. Within this framework, four nature-inspired metaheuristic algorithms—MFO, IWO, PO, and OO—were utilized for efficient exploration of the solution space.

According to the feature importance plot, cement content was identified as the most influential variable in modeling CSC, followed by water content, MCS-28, and sand content. While this general ranking is consistent with established concrete behavior, the SHAP results provide additional insight into how these variables influence strength development in fly ash concrete. In particular, the dominant role of cement should not be interpreted only as an increase in binder quantity; rather, higher cement content also contributes to improved matrix continuity, greater formation of hydration products, and enhanced strength development of the cementitious system.

The SHAP summary plot further clarifies the direction of these effects. For cement, higher values (shown in pink) are generally associated with positive SHAP values, indicating that increasing cement content tends to increase the predicted CSC. In contrast, higher water contents are mostly associated with negative SHAP values, confirming their adverse effect on compressive strength. From an engineering perspective, this negative influence can be attributed to the increase in the effective water-to-binder ratio, which leads to higher capillary porosity after hydration, a weaker interfacial transition zone, and reduced compactness of the hardened matrix. As a result, the load-bearing capacity of the concrete decreases, leading to lower compressive strength.

The influence of MCS-28 suggests that the intrinsic mechanical quality of the cement mortar also plays a meaningful role in the final concrete strength, while the relatively lower importance of fly ash addition, coarse aggregate, and sand indicates that their effects are more dependent on interaction with the binder system than on direct individual contribution alone. Moreover, the influence of water content is strongly coupled with binder composition, since together they govern the effective water-to-binder ratio and, consequently, the compressive strength of concrete. Overall, the SHAP analysis provided an interpretable explanation of the model behavior and helped reveal not only the ranking of variables, but also the underlying engineering trends governing CSC prediction.

It should be noted that correlation analysis evaluates linear relationships between individual variables and the target, whereas SHAP values reflect the model-based contribution of each feature, accounting for nonlinear effects and feature interactions. Therefore, discrepancies between correlation trends and SHAP importance may arise. In addition, potential collinearity between input variables, particularly between cement content and fly ash replacement, may influence SHAP attribution. Since fly ash partially replaces cement, their effects may overlap, leading to a redistribution of feature importance in the SHAP analysis.

One of the main objectives of this study is to design a fly ash concrete mixture that not only achieves desirable CSC but also reduces the environmental impacts associated with concrete production. To this end, two primary goals are defined for mixture optimization: maximizing CSC as a technical and practical target for producing high-quality, durable concrete, and minimizing the total CO2 footprint as an environmental and sustainable goal to reduce the negative effects of concrete production on climate change and pollution. To achieve these conflicting objectives, a multi-objective optimization framework is employed, where the NGBoost model is used as a surrogate model to predict the CSC.

Within this framework, four nature-inspired optimization algorithms—MFO, IWO, PO, and OO—are utilized to search for concrete mixture compositions that maximize compressive strength while minimizing the total CO2 footprint. The CO2 emission factors of concrete constituents were adopted from Naseri et al. 3, where emissions are reported based on a cradle-to-gate system boundary. In this study, only the emissions associated with material production are considered, while other life cycle stages (2) such as transportation, construction, and end-of-life are excluded. This assumption allows for a consistent and focused evaluation of the environmental impact of different concrete mix designs at the material level. The optimization problem was formulated as a Pareto-based multi-objective task aimed at simultaneously maximizing compressive strength and minimizing total CO2 footprint. In this framework, the NGBoost model, identified as the best-performing predictive model, was used as a surrogate model to estimate the compressive strength of candidate mixtures during the optimization process. Rather than combining the two objectives into a single weighted function, compressive strength and CO2 footprint were treated as two independent and conflicting objectives. Therefore, the final solutions were selected from the Pareto-optimal set, in which no further improvement in one objective can be achieved without deterioration in the other objective.

Following the formulation of the multi-objective optimization problem and the definition of all constraints, the proposed metaheuristic algorithms were employed to explore the feasible design space and identify optimal concrete mixtures. In this study, the MFO, IWO, PO, and OO algorithms were used to generate candidate solutions under the defined bounds and volumetric constraint.

These parameter settings were adopted to achieve a balanced trade-off between search diversity, convergence quality, and computational efficiency. Specifically, a population size of 300 was considered sufficient to ensure broad coverage of the feasible design space and preserve solution diversity, while 700 iterations provided adequate optimization time for the algorithms to approach stable and representative Pareto-optimal solutions. Therefore, this configuration helps reduce the likelihood of premature convergence and enhances the robustness and reliability of the final optimization outcomes. During the optimization, the NGBoost model was used to predict the compressive strength of each candidate mixture, while the total CO2 footprint was calculated using the predefined emission factors.

As suggested by previous studies114,116,117, uncertainty analysis is crucial for assessing the robustness of predictive models. The methods applied in this study follow established practices in the literature, where 95% confidence intervals and uncertainty indices like R-Factor have been used to assess model stability.

By combining CI, R-Factor, and confidence-band visualization, the robustness and predictive reliability of the developed models can be assessed more comprehensively.

From a practical perspective, the proposed framework can assist engineers in designing concrete mixtures more efficiently by identifying key influential variables and predicting compressive strength without extensive experimental trials. In addition, the integration of predictive modeling with multi-objective optimization enables the selection of mix designs that balance performance and sustainability, leading to potential reductions in material costs and CO2 emissions in real-world construction projects.

Compared with many previous studies118–120 that primarily relied on conventional machine learning techniques and focused only on strength prediction, the present study employed several advanced AI models, including TabNet, SAINT, and NGBoost, to better capture the complex nonlinear behavior of fly ash concrete mixtures. The results demonstrated that the probabilistic NGBoost model achieved superior predictive performance, highlighting the effectiveness of uncertainty-aware learning for concrete property prediction. In addition, unlike many earlier studies121–123 that were limited to predictive modeling alone, the proposed framework further integrated multi-objective optimization to determine theoretically optimal benchmarks concrete mix designs by simultaneously maximizing compressive strength and minimizing environmental impact.

Conclusion

This study presents an integrated framework for predicting and optimizing the compressive strength of fly ash concrete while minimizing its carbon footprint. A dataset of 1,062 concrete mix samples was used to develop and evaluate four artificial intelligence models, namely TabNet, SVR, NGBoost, and SAINT. Among these, NGBoost demonstrated the best predictive performance, while SHAP analysis revealed that cement content, water content, and MCS-28 are the most influential parameters governing compressive strength.

The predictive model was subsequently coupled with four metaheuristic optimization algorithms to generate concrete mix designs that balance mechanical performance and environmental impact. The Pareto analysis demonstrated a clear trade-off between compressive strength and CO2 footprint, highlighting the presence of multiple efficient design alternatives rather than a single optimal solution. The relatively small differences among the optimization algorithms further indicate that the proposed framework is robust and capable of consistently identifying near-optimal solutions. The optimized mixtures represent computational Pareto-optimal solutions derived from the proposed framework and should be interpreted as theoretical candidates within the dataset domain rather than experimentally validated production mixes.

Practical implications

The proposed framework can serve as a practical decision-support tool for engineers and practitioners in designing sustainable concrete mixtures. By integrating predictive modeling with multi-objective optimization, the approach enables systematic evaluation of trade-offs between strength and environmental impact, allowing more informed and performance-based mix design decisions tailored to specific project requirements.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is the lack of source-level labels in the compiled dataset, which prevented the use of grouped validation strategies such as leave-one-study-out cross-validation. In addition, the optimization process was limited to the variables available in the dataset and did not explicitly incorporate certain practical design constraints that may influence real-world applications.

Future research

Future studies may consider incorporating additional physical and practical constraints, such as water-to-binder ratio limits, minimum paste volume requirements, admixture dosage restrictions, and workability-related criteria, to further improve the realism and constructability of optimized concrete mix designs. In particular, integrating constraint-handling techniques within multi-objective optimization frameworks could provide a more systematic way to balance feasibility and performance. In addition, extending the framework to include factors such as cost, durability, and long-term performance, as well as validating the proposed approach using experimental or field data, would further enhance its practical applicability and robustness. Future studies may also explore hybrid or physics-informed optimization approaches that combine data-driven models with domain knowledge to improve reliability and interpretability. Furthermore, expanding the dataset to include more diverse and standardized experimental data, as well as investigating transferability across different material systems, could improve generalization capability. Future work may also incorporate additional strong baseline models, such as Random Forest and XGBoost/LightGBM, to broaden the comparative evaluation of predictive performance.
