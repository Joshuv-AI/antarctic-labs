Hydroxyl radical as an indoor oxidant
Authors: Charles J. Weschler, William W Nazaroff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hydroxyl radical (OH) is the dominant oxidant in outdoor air. Indoors, its role is secondary to ozone (O3). However, growing evidence indicates that OH contributes more significantly to indoor oxidation chemistry than previously recognized. This review critically examines the state of knowledge regarding OH as an indoor oxidant, synthesizing findings from modeling studies, laboratory experiments, and field measurements. Indoor OH concentrations range from 1 to 100 ppq under common conditions, with a central tendency of ∼5 ppq—one to two orders of magnitude below urban daytime outdoor levels and comparable to urban nighttime values. The primary sources of OH in occupied environments are gas-phase reactions between ozone and monoterpenes or alkenes, particularly limonene, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, and geranyl acetone. These compounds are also important sinks for OH, and these dual roles tend to buffer indoor OH concentrations. Photolysis of nitrous acid can be an important indoor OH source under some conditions. The dominant sink for indoor OH is homogeneous gas-phase reactions with volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Total OH reactivity is commonly on the order of 50 s−1 for occupied residences and can be much higher during events that release VOCs, such as cooking and cleaning. Radical cycling via the NO + HO2 reaction can significantly amplify indoor OH concentrations, depending on indoor NO levels. OH-initiated autoxidation chemistry contributes to the long-term oxidation of semivolatile organic compounds on indoor surfaces. OH chemistry, spatially variable and strongly influenced by occupant behavior, warrants continued investigation given its relevance to indoor air quality and human health.

Environmental significance

Oxidation reactions transform organic compounds emitted indoors into chemicals that are of greater health concern than their precursors. This review finds that the hydroxyl radical is responsible for more indoor oxidation, both in air and on surfaces, than has been previously recognized. Because OH reacts with a wide range of compounds, it affects both the composition of indoor air and the chemical character of indoor surfaces in ways that differ from ozone, the primary indoor oxidant. The central relevance of OH to indoor oxidation processes justifies measuring its concentration in a wider variety of indoor environments and a broader range of laboratory experiments. Doing so would support investigations that probe the influence of OH on chemical exposures occurring indoors.

Introduction

Oxidation is a ubiquitous chemical transformation process that occurs throughout the atmosphere, both outdoors and indoors. Molecular oxygen (O2) was identified as a major constituent of air by Joseph Priestly and, independently, by Carl Wilhelm Scheele in the 1770s. Two centuries then passed before Hiram Levy II showed that the hydroxyl radical (OH) is formed in ambient air. Shortwave photons (290–340 nm) in sunlight react with ozone, producing excited oxygen atoms that then react with water vapor to generate OH.1 Levy's discovery fundamentally changed our understanding of oxidation chemistry in the troposphere.

The hydroxyl radical is the dominant oxidant in outdoor air, with ozone playing a supporting role. In contrast, indoors, with attenuated photolysis, the roles are reversed: ozone is the dominant oxidant, and the hydroxyl radical plays a supporting role. Until the 1990s, the hydroxyl radical was considered a minor indoor oxidant that could be ignored unless there was sufficient UV radiation penetrating through openings in the building envelope to markedly increase photolysis rates. Over the past three decades, many studies have shown that the hydroxyl radical can be responsible for more indoor oxidation than was previously understood. Just how important OH is as an indoor oxidizing agent remains unresolved.

Why should anyone care about oxidation reactions that occur indoors? More than 90% of the organic compounds found in indoor air are emitted from indoor sources.2 Organic compounds emitted indoors tend to have low oxygen-to-carbon ratios (O/C). Such chemicals are susceptible to transformation reactions with various oxidants, including ozone and the hydroxyl radical. Oxidation of an organic compound increases its O/C ratio, increases its water solubility, decreases its volatility, and increases the chemical's tendency to partition into respiratory tract fluids if inhaled.3,4 Furthermore, relatively benign compounds can be oxidized to compounds that are potentially harmful to health. An example illustrates the broader point. The common breath constituent, isoprene, itself benign, reacts with the hydroxyl radical to produce methacrolein and methyl vinyl ketone (MVK), which are known mucous membrane irritants.5,6 Although ozone can react with isoprene to generate these products, at typical indoor levels, ozone's reaction rate with isoprene is too slow to compete with air-change rates. Indeed, in indoor air, OH is capable of quickly oxidizing many organic compounds that would react with ozone too slowly (or not at all) to constitute meaningful indoor transformations.

In addition to ozone and OH, other oxidants influence indoor chemistry. Among these are the nitrate radical;7–10 chlorine-containing reactive species;11,12 and hydrogen peroxide.13–15 Current evidence suggests that ozone and hydroxyl radicals are the dominant oxidants most of the time in typical indoor environments.12

In 2015, Gligorovski et al.16 reviewed the occurrence and reactivity of OH radicals in natural environments and in related laboratory studies. The bulk of their article discussed OH in outdoor atmospheres and in aquatic environments. A subsection on OH radicals in indoor atmospheres began as follows: “Whereas the OH radical concentrations in the troposphere have been a topic of intense research, the OH radical within indoor environments has received little attention”. In recent years, many new studies have characterized indoor OH. We deem that it is time for a critical review to address the state of knowledge regarding OH as an indoor oxidant. This report provides such a review.

This review proceeds as follows. Section 2 compiles modeled and measured indoor OH concentrations, establishing that under typical residential conditions OH is present at a concentration on the order of 5 ppq—about an order of magnitude below outdoor daytime urban levels and about six orders of magnitude below co-occurring indoor ozone concentrations. Section 3 identifies the primary sources of indoor OH, principally the gas-phase reactions of ozone with monoterpenes and alkenes and, under some conditions, the photolysis of nitrous acid and other species. Section 4 examines the sinks for indoor OH, showing that homogeneous reactions with VOCs and a few inorganic gases dominate its removal. The total OH reactivity (i.e., its reaction loss rate) is on the order of 50 s−1 indoors and rises sharply during cooking, cleaning, and the use of personal care products. Section 5 explores the role of radical cycling, in which the HO2 + NO reaction regenerates OH, amplifying the effects of primary production. Section 5 also broadly organizes the array of factors that influence indoor OH, identifying three regimes that commonly occur during a diurnal interval. Section 6 addresses two further considerations: (a) the dual character of monoterpenes and alkenes as both sources and sinks of OH, which tends to buffer indoor OH concentrations; and (b) the flux of OH to indoor surfaces. Section 7 reviews the kinetics and mechanisms of OH reactions including autoxidation processes. Section 7 also compares OH with O3 as indoor oxidants. Section 8 concludes the review, summarizing the state of knowledge and outlining priorities for future research.

OH concentrations

Units of measure

We begin with a note about units of measure. The literature that addresses OH in outdoor environments commonly uses molecules per cm3 as the concentration unit for OH. In this article, focusing on indoor environments, we will use the unit parts per quadrillion (ppq), corresponding to a relative (molar) abundance of 10−15. For standard indoor conditions, the conversion between these units is 1 ppq = 2.46 × 104 molecules per cm3 (note: unless otherwise stated, all conversions in this paper are relative to air at a pressure of 1 atm and a temperature of 298 K). There are two advantages of using ppq as a unit of measure for OH indoors: (a) abundance levels are in a range that is easy to express and compare (often between 1 and 100 ppq); and (b) the ppq unit enables a direct, million-to-one comparison with ozone concentrations as commonly expressed in ppb units. Specifically, 5 ppb = 106 × 5 ppq, so ozone at a common indoor abundance of 5 ppb would be present at a million times higher molar concentration than OH at a common indoor abundance of 5 ppq.

Outdoor OH concentrations

During the day, outdoor OH concentrations at ground level can vary over two orders of magnitude depending on sunlight and on the other chemicals present in the air. Photolysis (of ozone, nitrous acid, and aldehydes) is the major process that directly and indirectly generates OH outdoors during daytime. In relatively clean continental air masses, typical daytime OH concentrations are 20–200 ppq (∼0.5–5 × 106 molecules per cm3), whereas in urban and other polluted air masses the concentrations tend to be somewhat higher, 40–400 ppq (∼1–10 × 106 molecules per cm3). At night, absent photochemistry, the major source of OH is ozone/alkene reactions. Nighttime OH concentrations are substantially lower than the daytime values: <4 ppq (<0.1 × 106 molecules per cm3) in clean continental air masses and 4–20 ppq (∼0.1–0.5 × 106 molecules per cm3) in polluted air.17–23

Modeling indoor OH concentrations

The concentration of OH derives from a balance between its rate of generation (source term) and its rate of consumptive reaction (sink term). Indoors, primary generation is dominated by rapid O3 reactions with monoterpenes and alkenes, with contributions under some conditions from photolysis of HONO. Consumption is almost exclusively a result of fast homogeneous chemical reactions; other loss processes contribute negligibly to the overall balance. Another important factor influencing the source-sink balance for OH is the radical cycling that occurs with the hydroperoxyl radical, HO2. The role of that phenomenon is discussed in Section 5.

Two basic approaches have been used to model indoor OH concentrations. The first approach analyzes detailed kinetic mechanisms that examine the time dependence of chemical concentrations in a reacting system. The second approach uses a relatively simple steady-state calculation that estimates OH concentration from the ratio of the net OH production rate to the net OH removal rate. The short lifetime of the hydroxyl radical (∼20 milliseconds for common conditions) makes a steady-state approximation appropriate for estimating both time-dependent and time-averaged indoor concentrations.

Nazaroff and Cass24 published the first study that used a detailed kinetic model to investigate gas-phase chemistry that occurs indoors. That work combined measurements of chemicals within a museum gallery with a mathematical model analyzing fifty-seven chemical reactions. The OH radical was among twenty species whose concentrations were calculated. In the base case, VOCs were transported from outdoors, and there were assumed to be no indoor VOC sources. (At the time of that study, the dominance of indoor VOC sources over VOCs transported from outdoors was yet to be demonstrated). For base-case conditions (which included a time-averaged concentration of 14 ppb O3 indoors), the authors calculated an indoor OH concentration of 2.2 ppq. For a hypothetical simulation in which the gallery was assumed to have glass walls, photochemistry contributed to enhanced indoor OH formation and the resulting calculated concentration increased to 24 ppq.

A decade later, Weschler and Shields25 used a steady-state model to estimate the indoor concentration of hydroxyl radicals and calculated OH production and removal rates using concentrations of key chemicals that had been measured previously in surveys of numerous residences. The model implicitly assumed that indoor NO concentrations were low enough that OH production from the NO + HO2 reaction was relatively slow compared to the rate of OH production from ozonolysis. Their analysis indicated that reactions of ozone with monoterpenes, especially d-limonene, were important sources of indoor OH. For base case conditions (assuming 20 ppb O3 and 2.9 ppb limonene), they calculated an indoor OH concentration of 6.9 ppq. At a lower O3 concentration of 5 ppb, the calculated OH level was 3 ppq.

Sarwar et al.26 expanded the scope of kinetic modeling by adapting an existing kinetic model of atmospheric chemistry27 to indoor settings. The reaction set included O3 reactions with d-limonene, α-pinene, and 3-carene. This model calculated an indoor OH concentration of 4.9 ppq for baseline conditions. (The indoor O3 concentration was 20 ppb in that study).

In 2007, Carslaw28 introduced the Indoor Detailed Chemical Model (INDCM). It was based on the Master Chemical Mechanism (MCM) v3.1, which included 15 400 reactions and 4700 species. At an indoor O3 level of 12 ppb, the model calculated an indoor OH concentration of 13 ppq for a typical UK urban residence. INDCM has continued to evolve since its initial publication. For example, Kruza et al.29 added the influence of O3 surface interactions to the model, and Carter et al.30 expanded the treatment of surface chemistry to include interactions with additional indoor surfaces. In 2023, Shaw et al.31 introduced Indoor CHEMical model in Python (INCHEM-Py v1.2), which includes a description of how this open-source code model evolved from the original closed-source code INDCM. Since then, further applications of INCHEM-Py have been reported.32,33 The base-case version of INCHEM-Py does not include 6-MHO and GA (commonly generated by O3 reactions with skin oil) nor does it include indoor sources of limonene or other common terpenes. This is relevant when applying INCHEM-Py to determine OH levels in occupied indoor spaces.

Mendez et al.34 constructed a model that modified the semi-explicit chemical mechanism in SAPRC-07 to indoor conditions. This model predicted OH concentrations that were roughly 35% higher than those of the Carslaw INDCM model. Results generated by the two models for similar scenarios were well correlated.

Waring and Wells35 created a time-averaged indoor chemistry model that considered a relatively small set of reactions. The less computationally intensive approach worked well for their interest in screening many cases. They applied the model in a parametric simulation to assess indoor settings with four different emission scenarios. At 3 ppb O3, the median OH concentration in their simulations was 2 ppq.

Measuring indoor OH concentrations

It is challenging to measure OH, especially at the low concentrations prevalent indoors. Both indirect and direct methods have been used, as summarized in the following paragraphs.

Indirect methods

Chemical tracers

Chemicals tracers have been employed in several studies to measure OH indoors. When choosing a chemical tracer, it is important that the compound does not react rapidly with other indoor oxidants. Examples of compounds that have been used as tracers include 1,3,5-trimethyl benzene36–38 and isoprene.39 Only one of these studies made measurements under typical indoor conditions, in two unoccupied seminar rooms.39 Researchers measured an OH concentration of 15 ppq in a 48 m3 room with ∼2 ppb O3 and 2.6 ppq in a 316 m3 room with ∼5 ppb O3.

Total OH reactivity

The OH reactivity of a specific chemical refers to the rate (units: s−1) at which that chemical reacts with OH in the gas-phase at its given concentration. For the chemical in question, it is calculated as the product of its gas-phase concentration and its second-order rate constant for reaction with OH. Total OH reactivity is the sum of the OH reactivities for all OH reactive compounds present in the air of a given environment (Subsection 4.1). Instruments have been designed to measure total OH reactivity.40,41 Zannoni et al.42 used such an instrument to measure total OH reactivity in a series of experiments in which four people were exposed to 35 ppb ozone in a 23 m3 stainless-steel chamber ventilated at an air-change rate of 3 h−1. In those experiments, VOC concentrations were simultaneously measured by PTR-MS. The OH concentration was then calculated as the net production of OH via reaction of ozone with VOCs divided by the net measured total OH reactivity.42 The average OH concentration in the four experiments was 29 ± 8 ppq. This value is higher than the expected OH level in the absence of human occupants. Ozone reactions with 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one (6-MHO) and geranyl acetone (GA), both products of ozone reactions with the major skin oil constituent squalene, were key to the production of OH. Indeed, more than 80% of OH production is estimated to have originated from O3/squalene chemistry. (It is noteworthy that the estimated concentration of NO in the chamber was too low for the chain-propagation reaction, NO + HO2, to materially contribute to the indoor OH level). Surprisingly, the study by Zannoni et al. (2022)42 was the first to investigate the influence of human occupancy on indoor OH concentrations, although earlier studies pointed toward that potential.43,44 Results from an analogous set of experiments in which the occupants had applied either a lotion or a perfume indicated that personal care products can further influence indoor OH concentrations.45 In particular, the use of personal care products increased total OH reactivity more than it increased the production of OH, resulting in diminished OH concentrations (see Subsection 4.2).

Direct methods

Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) with a fluorescent assay by gas expansion (LIF-FAGE)

Atmospheric measurement of OH and HO2 by LIF-FAGE has been discussed in several papers.46–48 In 2013 Gómez Alvarez et al.49 published the first paper reporting direct measurements of indoor OH and HO2. They used an LIF-FAGE instrument that they placed in an unoccupied classroom. When sunlight was shining directly into the classroom, they recorded indoor OH as high as 73 ppq; the co-occurring indoor O3 concentration was 5 ppb. During this sunlit period, they attributed OH formation primarily to photolysis of nitrous acid (HONO) since “OH concentrations were found to follow a linear dependence with the product JHONO × [HONO]”, where JHONO is the photolysis reaction rate constant. On an occasion with no direct sunlight but elevated indoor O3 (25 ppb), they measured OH concentrations as high as 32 ppq, presumably resulting from O3-initiated reactions. Other studies also have measured indoor OH using LIF-FAGE.50–52 Reidy et al.52 reported measurements during a large field campaign conducted in a test house. Hydroxyl radicals were measured near a window during cooking events that occurred on two separate days. When cooking occurred, occupants were present and the concentrations of HONO and certain terpenes were elevated. While cooking with a propane burner at a time when direct sunlight entered the measurement area, the OH concentration averaged around 120 ppq and peaked at 440 ppq shortly after the HONO concentration peaked. Using the oven and multiple burners of a gas-stove, the OH levels peaked at about 240 ppq. As was the case in a prior study,49 for periods when sunlight entered the area being measured, photolysis of HONO was a major source of OH. It should be noted that LIF-FAGE may suffer from interferences under certain conditions including during periods with high O3 and monoterpene concentrations.53–57 LIF-FAGE measurements may represent upper limit values if interferences are not taken into account.

CIMS—chemical conversion of isotopically labelled sulfur dioxide to sulfuric acid followed by chemical ionization mass spectrometry

In this approach, hydroxyl radicals in the sampled air react with isotopically labeled SO2 to produce H2SO4. A chemical ionization mass spectrometer (CIMS), using nitrate (NO3−) as the ionizing agent, quantifies the resulting HSO4−.58–60 Using this approach, Fiorentino et al.61 measured OH concentrations in an unfurnished 33 m3 room during multiple experiments. Throughout, O3 levels were low (≤3 ppb). Focusing on the experiments absent TiO2-painted surfaces, the weighted average OH concentrations were 6.5 ppq when the room had anti-UV windows, and 23 ppq when the room had UV transmitting windows. When the windows allowed UV transmission, OH production was much smaller in the shaded volume than in the sunlit volume (see Subsection 3.2). Reactions of ozone with alkenes produced OH under all conditions. More than 97% of OH loss was attributable to reaction with VOCs.

In addition to providing calculated or measured OH levels for various indoor environments, the studies whose results are summarized in Fig. 1 have resulted in improved understanding of indoor OH dynamics. These studies support the following findings:

(a) Because of the large indoor concentrations of organic compounds, the lifetime of indoor OH is very short, typically a few tens of milliseconds.

(b) Given its short lifetime, the OH molecule can only travel centimeter-scale distances indoors before reacting. Hence, spatially heterogeneous production of OH indoors will yield spatially variable concentrations.64

(c) The hydroperoxyl radical, HO2, has a longer lifetime than OH, long enough for some transport and mixing to occur in many circumstances. Subsequent reaction of HO2 with NO to regenerate OH (see Subsection 5.1) would result in OH concentrations that are more homogeneous than would otherwise be the case.61

(d) The short lifetime of OH means that outdoor-to-indoor transport contributes negligibly to indoor OH levels. Instead, indoor OH levels are governed by OH production indoors, which is strongly influenced by outdoor-to-indoor transport of O3.

(e) Sunlight entering indoors directly (e.g. through unglazed window openings) can combine with elevated indoor HONO levels to be a strong source of photolytic OH generation.49 However, photolytic reactions driven by indoor lighting are typically a small source of indoor OH (see Subsection 3.2).

(f) O3 reactions with human skin oil constituents generate unsaturated products such as 6-MHO and geranyl acetone65 that further react with O3 to produce OH radicals at relatively high rates. In the presence of ozone levels that are common indoors, humans materially alter the OH concentration in their immediate surroundings.42

Sources and production rates of indoor OH

Ozone reactions with monoterpenes and alkenes

Ozone reactions with monoterpenes and alkenes are major chain-initiating sources of indoor hydroxyl radicals under common conditions. The reactions of ozone with constituents of scented cleaning agents and “air fresheners” are known sources of indoor OH.37,38 So too are the reactions of ozone with 6-MHO and geranyl acetone, which are themselves products of ozone reactions with human skin oil.42 The constituents of personal care products such as lotions and perfumes can influence the production (and removal) rates of indoor OH.45 Limonene, α-pinene, 6-MHO, and GA are among the most important OH precursors in occupied indoor environments.

Photolysis as a source of indoor OH

Outdoors, photolysis of ozone during daylight hours is an important source of hydroxyl radicals. Indoors, given the small actinic flux in the wavelength vicinity of 300 nm, photolysis of ozone is most often negligible.16 However, some chemicals commonly present in indoor air can photolyze to directly or indirectly produce OH in sunlit interiors or in the presence of indoor lighting with meaningful actinic flux below 400 nm. Chief among these species is nitrous acid (HONO).49,80,81 Others include hypochlorous acid (HOCl), and molecular chlorine (Cl2).11,82 Under some conditions, photolysis of these species can be a larger source of the OH radical than occurs by ozonolysis of alkenes.49,52,80 The next few paragraphs discuss indoor species that may generate OH upon photolysis.

Nitrous acid (HONO) is transported indoors from polluted outdoor air and is emitted by indoor combustion including gas stoves and ovens, unvented combustion heaters, and candles.80,83 Nitrous acid is also generated by the disproportionation of NO2 on indoor surfaces (Collins et al.84 and references therein). In addition, light can induce nitrous acid production from heterogeneous reactions of NO2 on indoor surfaces.85 HONO partitions between air and surface reservoirs, modulating its gas-phase concentration.84 Acid–base chemistry influences this partitioning.86

HONO has significant optical absorption bands at wavelengths up to 390 nm and photolyzes under conditions often encountered indoors:49,80,81,87R1HONO + hν (λ < 390 nm) → OH + NO

Assuming a photolysis rate constant for nitrous acid of J(HONO) = 7.2 × 10−4 s−1, which is appropriate for an indoor setting during a period of direct solar irradiation through an open window with no attenuation by glass, and assuming an indoor HONO concentration of 4 ppb, the indoor OH production rate by photolysis would be 10 ppb h−1,16 corresponding to 2800 ppq s−1, about 20× higher than the typical rate of chemical production using the entries in Table 1. However, this estimate assumes an unusually high photolysis rate for indoor environments. If sunlight passes through a single pane of window glass instead of through an open window, the rate constant J(HONO) in directly illuminated interior spaces substantially decreases, to about 1–1.5 × 10−4 s−1.88 For indoor spaces that are not illuminated by direct sunlight, gas-phase ozone/alkene chemistry is anticipated to be the more important source of OH.

Hypochlorous acid (HOCl) and molecular chlorine (Cl2) are emitted into indoor air when chlorine bleach or bleach-containing products are used to clean or disinfect indoor surfaces.11,82 Photolysis of these species can be a meaningful source of hydroxyl radicals. Photolysis of HOCl generates OH directly:R2HOCl + hν → OH + Cl˙

Photolysis of Cl2 generates OH indirectly:R3Cl2 + hν → 2Cl˙R4Cl˙ + VOCs → RO2 + …R5RO2 + NO → RO + NO2R6RO + O2 → HO2 + carbonylR7HO2 + NO → OH + NO2

Cl2 photolysis effectively boosts the total radical pool. Wang et al.82 have estimated that the indirect production rate of OH from Cl2 photolysis during bleach cleaning is about an order of magnitude larger than direct production from HOCl photolysis. Carslaw and coworkers have modeled OH concentrations during bleach cleaning experiments in the HOMEChem test house33 and compared their results to measurements made during this bleach cleaning period. The modeled OH level peaked at about 570 ppq (1.4 × 107 molecules per cm3), similar to the measured peak level of about 490 ppq (1.2 × 107 molecules per cm3) (see Fig. 2 of the cited paper). The OH level returned to its background value about an hour after the end of bleach cleaning.

Quinones and hydroperoxy compounds (ROOH) photolyze to generate OH in outdoor air during daylight hours.89,90 Quinones are present indoors both in the gas phase (e.g., oxidized PAHs) and associated with indoor particles (e.g., motor vehicle exhaust transported indoors or tobacco smoke emissions occurring indoors). Hydroperoxy compounds are present indoors from ozone-terpene reactions and from ozone-skin oil chemistry. However, photolysis of quinones and hydroperoxy compounds is driven by near-UV light at wavelengths (250–350 nm) that are largely absent from indoor environments. Hence, under commonly occurring indoor conditions, photolysis of quinones and hydroperoxy compounds is not expected to be a major source of indoor OH.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is used as a cleaning agent and indoor disinfectant and is also a product of ozone-initiated chemistry.14,91 However, even when its indoor concentration is elevated, indoor photolysis of H2O2 is a negligible source of OH. It has a very low absorption cross section for wavelengths larger than 300 nm.16

Kahan and colleagues have measured wavelength-resolved UV fluxes for artificial lighting and attenuated sunlight, as well as analyzing scenarios addressing lighting and sunlight as photolytic sources of indoor OH.92–94 Under typical conditions, most types of indoor lighting, including incandescent, compact fluorescent, and LED lightbulbs, are not a major source of indoor OH, whereas bare fluorescent light tubes can promote a modest increase in indoor OH under certain conditions.31,95 These features are illustrated in Fig. 4 of Shaw et al.31 Quoting from that study: “Without sunlight there is very little OH produced by the indoor lighting …”.

Another potential driver of indoor photolysis is germicidal UV radiation (GUV), used to inactivate pathogens in indoor air or on indoor surfaces. Both upper room UV (at 254 nm) and far UV (at 222 nm) are being promoted towards this end.96 In each instance, there is the potential to generate OH via photochemistry.32,96–98 Using the INCHEM-Py model, Carter et al.32 estimated indoor OH concentrations in a simulated occupied classroom with an air change rate of 0.5 h−1. The results are complicated with GUV 254 having a larger impact on indoor OH concentrations than GUV 222. (See Fig. 4 of the referenced paper). When either UV lamp was operating, increasing the relative humidity increased OH. It would be valuable to measure OH concentrations in settings where germicidal UV lamps are deployed.

Moreso than for long-lived species, indoor OH concentrations vary spatially within a room. The half-life of OH (∼20 ms) is orders of magnitude shorter than the time scale for air to move across a room (3 m ÷ 10 cm s−1 ∼30 s), and the gap is even larger when the OH chemical lifetime is compared with the characteristic mixing time for room air, which is on the order of several minutes or longer.99,100 Consequently, spatial gradients in OH arise whenever precursor concentrations are not uniform. This issue is potentially most important for OH generated by photolysis, because photon fluxes indoors may have large spatial variability—as occurs, for example, when direct sunlight enters through a window and directly illuminates only a fraction of a room's interior volume. In some regions, at certain times of year, open windows during sunlit periods are common; depending on the orientation of the windows and the angle of the sun, photolysis of species such as formaldehyde and ozone can then generate a substantial increase in indoor OH, with concentrations in the illuminated region of the room substantially larger than in shaded regions. Relative to this point, Blocquet et al.101 examined the spatial distribution of sunlight within a room as a function of solar angle and window type. Fiorentino et al.61 reported spatial variability of photon fluxes in sunlit and shaded volumes of a room; Fig. 2–4 of this latter paper illustrate how lighting variability affects OH, HO2 and RO2 budgets in the shaded versus sunlit volumes. The spatial heterogeneity of OH is somewhat mitigated by transport of peroxy radicals (HO2 and RO2) throughout the room.61 However, unless the concentration of NO is much less than a ppb, the lifetimes of HO2 and RO2 are too short28 to permit more than a soft smearing of the OH concentration gradients resulting from uneven illumination.

OH reactivity

Indoors, the dominant sinks for hydroxyl radicals are homogeneous chemical reactions. Other removal processes (ventilation, surface reaction) contribute negligibly to the overall balance between OH production and OH removal.

Total OH reactivity

The concept of “total OH reactivity” (LOH,total), introduced in Subsection 2.4.1, quantifies the rate at which OH radicals are removed from air by reactions with co-occurring gas-phase constituents. Its reciprocal is the OH lifetime. In addition to reacting with most organic compounds, certain inorganic gases react with OH. Table S2 lists rate constants for OH and common inorganic gases; these rate constants can be combined with typical concentrations to estimate the rate at which an inorganic gas reacts with OH. In urban areas in the U.S. and Europe, carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and nitric oxide (NO) are responsible for roughly half of the net chemical removal of OH from outdoor air.102 Indoors, CO and NO2 are also meaningful sinks for OH. Nitrous acid (HONO) tends to be a more important indoor sink than NO. At commonly occurring indoor concentrations, CO, NO2, and HONO have OH reactivities of 3 s−1, 3 s−1, and 0.7 s−1, respectively (Table 3). At typical levels of 1 ppb for indoor NO and 40 ppb for indoor NH3, these species each contribute small OH reactivities, about 0.2 s−1. Sulfur dioxide levels indoors are typically not large enough for SO2 to be a meaningful sink for OH. The reaction of O3 with OH is too slow (with a second-order rate constant of 7.3 × 10−14 cm3 per molecules per s or 0.0018 ppb−1 s−1)103 to influence indoor removal of OH.

Influence of occupant emissions and their activities on total OH reactivity

Table S3 summarizes results from studies that probed the influence of the occupants themselves or activities such as cooking or cleaning on indoor OH reactivities. In Subsection 2.4.1 we discussed experiments that measured total OH reactivities in an occupied stainless-steel chamber using a dedicated instrument based on the comparative reactivity method (CRM).41 These were a subset of experiments that probed more broadly the effect of human emissions on OH reactivity as measured by the CRM method.42,45,121,122 All these experiments were conducted with four occupants in a 23 m3 chamber operated at an air-change rate of 3 h−1. Under baseline conditions, when the occupants did not use personal care products and had recently showered, Wang et al.121 measured an average total OH reactivity of 16 ± 2 s−1 at low O3 concentrations (<2 ppb) and 33 ± 1 s−1 at an elevated O3 concentration (∼35 ppb). The total OH reactivities, calculated by summing the reactivities of VOCs measured by PTR-MS, agreed reasonably well with those measured by the CRM instrument.

Zannoni et al.122 examined the effects of ozone, temperature, relative humidity, and clothing on OH reactivity under baseline conditions (no personal care products and subjects having recently showered). Total OH reactivities were substantially larger in the presence of ∼35 ppb of O3 compared to conditions with low O3. They further found that total OH reactivity “increased with exposed skin surface, higher temperature, and higher humidity”. Zannoni et al.45 examined the influence of personal care products on total OH reactivity. They found that VOCs volatilized from a fragrance or a lotion led to total OH reactivities larger than measured when the occupants did not apply such products. Ethanol emitted by the fragrance volatilized quickly and dominated OH reactivities over relatively short intervals. Phenoxyethanol emitted by the lotion dominated OH reactivity for a longer period, reflecting that fact that it volatized more slowly than ethanol.

Price et al.118 reported on the influence of human occupants on the total OH reactivities calculated using VOC concentrations measured in a university art museum and in a classroom. Events with high human occupancy increased the OH reactivity by ∼75% in the museum and by ∼35% in the classroom. (See Fig. 3A of Price et al.118). In the museum, the VOCs that made the largest contribution to total OH reactivity were isoprene, monoterpenes, methanol, acetaldehyde, and acetone.

The effect of cooking and cleaning on total OH reactivities was explored in a test house during the HOMEChem campaign.33 On days in which cooking and/or cleaning occurred, the background total OH reactivity was 20–40 s−1, whereas, shortly after cooking and cleaning, the modeled total OH reactivity increased to a higher range, 140–160 s−1.

OH cycling (chain propagation)

The concentration of OH indoors reflects not only the initiation processes described in Section 3 but also chain propagation (“radical cycling”) reactions in which OH, once consumed, is regenerated via the coupled chemistry of peroxy radicals—chiefly through the reaction of HO2 with NO. This section examines that cycling and the conditions under which it meaningfully amplifies indoor OH levels. Subsection 5.1 presents the mechanistic details, illustrating how OH-initiated oxidation of CO and volatile organic compounds produces HO2 and RO2 radicals whose subsequent reactions with NO regenerate OH. Chain-terminating reactions that limit the cycle are also identified. Because the HO2 + NO reaction is central to this process, Subsection 5.2 surveys measured indoor NO concentrations, which span roughly four orders of magnitude and depend on outdoor levels, ventilation, and the presence of unvented combustion sources. Subsection 5.3 then integrates initiation and cycling processes, identifying three regimes that govern the diurnal pattern of indoor OH concentrations and assessing when radical cycling contributes appreciably to OH generation.

Mechanistic details

Chain propagation reactions promote oxidation without net consumption of radicals. Consider a simple illustration, relevant to indoor environments, that couples the hydroperoxyl radical (HO2) with the hydroxyl radical. It begins with the reaction of OH with carbon monoxide:R8OH + CO → CO2 + H

The resulting H atom rapidly combines with O2 to form the hydroperoxyl radical:R9H + O2 → HO2

Although there are multiple species that can react with HO2, if the NO concentration is high enough (Subsection 5.2), reaction (R7) (introduced earlier) dominates:R7NO + HO2 → OH + NO2

Reaction (R7) is fast, with a second order rate constant of 0.20 ppb−1 s−1. To illustrate its speed, consider this: if the NO concentration is 1 ppb, the pseudo first-order rate constant for HO2 loss by this reaction is 0.2 s−1, three orders of magnitude faster than HO2 removal by ventilation at a typical residential air-change rate (0.5 h−1 = 0.00014 s−1). Indoors, the net result of these three reactions is summarized in reaction (R10):R10OH + CO + NO + O2 → OH + CO2 + NO2

Overall, CO is oxidized to CO2, NO is oxidized to NO2, and OH is regenerated, without net loss. Outdoors or indoors, the NO + HO2 reaction converts HO2 to the more reactive OH and completes a cycle in which the OH and HO2 radicals catalyze species oxidation without being consumed. Indoors, the reaction of OH with CO is a meaningful sink for OH (Table 3), although it is of negligible importance as a sink for CO (air exchange removes CO orders of magnitude faster).

Many analogous cycles operate in which an organic molecule is attacked by an OH radical, leading to its oxidation, with the OH radical subsequently being regenerated via HO2. One case from the broader class of processes is described next.

When OH abstracts a hydrogen atom from a volatile organic compound, an alkyl radical (R˙) is formed. Consider the OH reaction with ethane:R11

The resulting alkyl radical quickly reacts with oxygen (O2) to form an alkylperoxy radical (generically ; ethyl peroxy in this example):R12

The alkylperoxy radical most often reacts with either NO or another peroxy radical. In the latter case, consider the reaction of ethyl peroxy with the methylperoxy radical , the most abundant alkylperoxy radical indoors, to produce an alkoxy radical (generically RO˙; specifically, CH3CH2O˙ here):R13

CH3CH2O˙ reacts further with oxygen to form acetaldehyde and the hydroperoxyl radical (HO2):R14CH3CH2O˙+ O2 → CH3CHO + HO2

Analogously, CH3O˙ can react to form formaldehyde and the hydroperoxyl radical:R15CH3O˙+ O2 → HCHO + HO2

If sufficiently abundant, NO can then react with HO2, producing NO2 and regenerating OH. Hence, OH has oxidized a hydrocarbon, generating an RO2 radical. The RO2 radical has reacted with an RO2 radical already present, producing two alkoxy radicals (RO˙). The alkoxy radicals react with O2 to form two aldehydes and two HO2 radicals. NO then reacts with HO2, generating two OH radicals. In brief, each OH-initiated oxidation of ethane, coupled with an existing RO2 radical, yields two carbonyl products (acetaldehyde and formaldehyde) and two HO2 radicals, both of which can regenerate OH. Viewed through a lens that focuses on the pool of radicals, which remains constant, this process is commonly referred to as “OH cycling” or “radical cycling.”

OH-initiated reactions are not the only source of indoor HO2 radicals. They are also produced by O3 reactions with monoterpenes as well as photolysis of formaldehyde and other carbonyls under certain conditions. That said, these alternate pathways to HO2 formation tend to be minor compared to OH-initiated reactions.

In their modeling study, Sarwar et al.26 reported that, for base case conditions, the NO + HO2 reaction generated OH at a rate that was about ¾ that of O3 + alkene reactions. For base-case conditions in the Carslaw28 modeling study, OH propagation via the NO + HO2 reaction had a larger influence on the overall OH concentration than did the sum of ozonolysis chemistry and HONO photolysis. In the modeling study of Mendez et al.,34 a relatively high air-change rate (2 h−1) increased the rate of NO transport from outdoors, resulting in an increased influence (compared to the base case) of the NO + HO2 reaction on the indoor OH concentration. In contrast, a low air-change rate (0.2 h−1) reduced the influence of the NO + HO2 reaction.

For indoor OH levels measured by Fiorentino et al.61 in a new office, the largest source of OH was cycling via NO + HO2 (see Table A.5 of the cited paper). For the five manipulated conditions, the HO2/OH ratios were in the approximate range 100–200. The co-occurring O3 concentrations were ≤3 ppb and NO concentrations were similar, or slightly larger than those of O3. In the study measuring indoor OH by Carslaw et al.,51 when a lemon-scented cleaner was applied to desk surfaces in a computer room, the rate of OH production from propagation reactions was estimated to be about ¾ that resulting from reactions that produced Criegee radicals. The investigators measured HO2/OH ratios of ∼20 for background conditions and ∼100 during desk cleaning. The co-occurring O3 concentration was 20–25 ppb, and the NO level was estimated to be <1 ppb. It should be noted that both these measurement efforts were conducted in unoccupied rooms.

Outdoors, the terms “low NOx” and “high NOx” are relevant to the nitrogen species that drive the formation of O3 in the troposphere. In high NOx environments, peroxy radicals oxidize NO to NO2, whose subsequent photolysis produces NO plus O˙; the latter rapidly reacts with O2 to form O3. In low NOx environments, peroxy radicals react primarily with themselves, producing hydroperoxides and peroxides, but not forming O3.123 However, as noted by Wennberg,124 “high NOx” and “low NOx” do not have “precise or accepted definitions” and “cause more confusion than they do insight”. A similar concern arises indoors. Here photolysis of NO2 is negligible, and consequently there is no O3 formation via the reaction of O˙ with O2. Also, surface-to-volume ratios are several orders of magnitude higher indoors than outdoors, suggesting that surface removal could become an important sink for HO2. Indoors, it is more appropriate to speak of “low NO” and “high NO” regimes—that is, to focus specifically on NO rather than the NO + NO2 pair that comprises NOx. Low NO environments indoors favor HO2 termination reactions whereas high NO environments favor OH cycling via the NO + HO2 reaction. Ozone reacts rapidly with NO such that, when O3 levels are elevated indoors (and in the absence of significant indoor NO sources), the indoor NO level necessarily becomes low,125 substantially diminishing the importance of the NO + HO2 reaction. On the other hand, the NO + HO2 reaction can be influential when indoor NO levels are elevated, for example owing to emissions from unvented combustion.126,127

To summarize, the NO + HO2 reaction can be viewed as part of a process of cycling OH, in contrast to initializing OH production as occurs when O3 reacts with CC bonds or light photolyzes HONO. The reaction of NO with HO2 increases the prevailing OH concentration. By modulating radical cycling between OH and HO2, the abundance of NO plays a noteworthy role affecting OH levels indoors. With such a cycle, the OH/HO2 and OH/RO2 radical couples act effectively as catalysts, promoting oxidation of airborne organic compounds (and other species) without their own net consumption in the process.

The cycle of radical oxidation involving OH and HO2 is limited by “chain terminating reactions” that remove radicals from the air with no regeneration. Such reactions include OH combining with NO2, organic nitrates, peroxyacyl nitrates (PAN), and multifunctional carbon species. In the Carslaw modeling study,28 reaction with NO2 removed 53% of OH from the system, reaction with organic nitrates removed 16%, reaction with multifunctional carbon species removed 14%, and reaction with PAN removed 12%. Reactions with NO2 are also terminating for HO2 and RO2 radicals.51 Unvented combustion is a source of NO2 as well as NO, meaning that an activity such as using an unvented gas appliance increases the rate of termination reactions with NO2 as well as OH propagation reactions via NO + HO2. The existing body of published research does not effectively address which of these effects dominates, to what degree, and how the balance is affected by specific conditions.

Indoor NO concentrations

Given that the HO2 + NO reaction is central to radical cycling, the magnitude of the indoor NO concentration is of special interest. The key question is whether NO is present at a concentration sufficient to compete with RO2 and HO2 (self-reaction) as a reaction partner for HO2. If the NO concentration is too low, the regeneration of OH via the reaction of HO2 with NO will not occur at a meaningful rate.

NO and O3 cannot coexist indoors at elevated levels.125 The reaction between O3 and NO is fast, with a second-order rate constant (kNO+O3) of 1.6 ppb−1 h−1. At a typical indoor O3 concentration of 5 ppb, NO is removed by reaction with ozone at a pseudo-first order reaction rate of 8 h−1, far faster than its removal from a building by ventilation at typical air change rates. In the absence of indoor NO sources, eqn (2) expresses the relationship between indoor NO ([NO]in) and outdoor NO ([NO]out) for steady-state or time-averaged conditions:2Here AER is the air change rate. The average residential air change rate is 0.5 h−1 (ref. 128) and the average indoor O3 concentration is 5 ppb.129 Under such conditions, the indoor NO concentration would be only 6% of the co-occurring outdoor value.

In regions in which photochemical air pollution is a concern, outdoor O3 concentrations tend to be elevated from mid-morning until mid-to late afternoon. Throughout this interval the outdoor NO concentration is low, on the order of 0.3–3 ppb.130–133 Absent indoor emission sources, the indoor NO concentration would be even lower, as eqn (2) indicates. Radical cycling indoors is therefore expected to be weak at these times. However, indoor NO concentrations can sometimes be elevated well above this illustrative midday case.

Field measurements demonstrate that indoor NO concentrations span roughly four orders of magnitude, from the sub-ppb levels anticipated by eqn (2) to hundreds of ppb. In buildings without indoor NO sources, indoor concentrations track outdoor levels—an anticipated coupling that has been demonstrated in studies of a museum,24 an office,125 and school rooms134 in Southern California. In the school rooms, indoor NO ranged from below the limit of detection to about 60 ppb during morning rush hours. Conditions may differ in residences, where unvented combustion can occur, such as from natural-gas cooking. Low air change rates and near-complete titration of indoor O3 during cooking periods combine to yield indoor NO levels that routinely exceed outdoor levels. In an occupied Syracuse home with gas appliances, the background NO averaged 4 ppb; cooking events drove concentrations to above 100 ppb, reaching values as high as 340 ppb.135 Averaged over a two-season monitoring campaign, NO in that residence (mean 17 ppb; indoor/outdoor ratio 12) far exceeded concentrations in a laboratory (1.0 ppb) and in a nearby office (0.5 ppb; indoor/outdoor ratio 0.1).136 Mean NO values reported for North American residences range from 30 to 77 ppb (Zhou et al.135 and references therein). Scripted operation of natural gas cooking burners in nine California homes likewise produced kitchen NO concentrations of hundreds of ppb.137 In summary, although indoor NO is often too low to sustain meaningful radical cycling during midday hours, concentrations can reach tens to hundreds of ppb during either of these two common conditions: (a) periods dominated by traffic emissions with weak outdoor photochemistry; and (b) during and after unvented indoor combustion activities.

Unvented indoor combustion emits nitrogen oxides both as NO and as NO2. In the scripted operation of natural gas burners, NO2 contributed only about one quarter of the fuel-normalized emission rate; the balance was emitted as NO.137 Cooking with a gas stovetop or oven produces sharp excursions in indoor NO, with peak levels of combustion byproducts scaling with burner operating time.135 Controlled cooking experiments have yielded peak NO concentrations of 100–2000 ppb.135,138 Venting range hoods mitigate but do not eliminate these emissions.135,137 Continuously burning pilot lights, present in older gas-fired cooking appliances, constitute a steady, low-rate NO source capable of sustaining modestly elevated indoor NO between cooking events.139 Portable combustion-based and unvented space heaters (kerosene or natural gas fueled) emit NO and NO2 at substantial rates.126,140,141 Measurements in 30 homes using unvented gas fireplaces documented substantially elevated concentrations of nitrogen oxides.142 A common feature of these sources is that the emitted NO simultaneously supplies the reactant needed for OH regeneration via HO2 + NO and titrates indoor O3, suppressing the ozonolysis reactions that initiate radical production—a tension examined in the next subsection.

OH cycling and generation regimes

The indoor presence of OH radicals at meaningful levels begins with initiation reactions that generate OH. Typically, only two categories of initiation reactions are relevant indoors: ozone oxidation of monoterpenes and alkenes (Subsection 3.1) and photolysis of nitrous acid and other species (Subsection 3.2). Radical cycling (Subsection 5.1) requires initiating steps that create a pool of participating radicals. Radical cycling amplifies the OH in this pool, but it cannot act without initiation reactions.

When is radical cycling important? More broadly, how do indoor OH concentrations vary with time of day? Absent indoor emissions sources of NO, three regimes can be identified as relevant for controlling the diurnal pattern of indoor OH concentrations; these are summarized in Table 5.

Regimes governing indoor OH concentrations absent indoor NO emissions

In Regime I, outdoor O3 concentrations are at moderate to high levels, outdoor NO concentrations are relatively low (∼0.3–3 ppb), and indoor NO concentrations are even lower (∼0.02–0.1 ppb). At such low indoor NO levels, the reaction of HO2 with NO is generally slow relative to the primary OH generation reactions. Consequently, in this regime radical cycling tends to be weak compared to OH production by ozonolysis and/or photolysis. Representative conditions are detailed in Table S4, which lists values for the rate of OH regeneration via the reaction, HO2 + NO, calculated using typical midday concentrations for key species in rural, suburban, and urban locations together with known rate constants. These values can be compared to the rate of OH generation by ozonolysis. The table contains results for three scenarios, which vary in the concentration ratios of HO2/OH—specifically ratios of 20, 100, and 1000. Ratios of these scales have been reported in both modeling and measurement studies.31,33,52,61,143,144

Consider the magnitude of the rate of OH generation by radical cycling compared to the sum of the primary production rate and radical cycling. For the illustrative calculations reported in Table S4, the results are summarized as follows. (i) At an HO2/OH ratio of 20 : 1, the rate of OH generation by radical cycling is very weak, contributing a small percentage (∼1% or less) to the total OH generation rate. (ii) At an HO2/OH ratio of 100 : 1, the rate of OH generation by radical cycling varies from 1% (rural) to 5% (urban) of the total rate. Cycling is a weak amplifier of indoor OH. (iii) At an HO2/OH ratio of 1000 : 1, the radical cycling reaction is a moderate amplifier to indoor OH generation, accounting for 8% (rural), 32% (suburban), or 54% (urban) of the sum of radical cycling plus primary generation rates. Hence, in these illustrative calculations, for HO2/OH ratios of 100 : 1 or less, a large majority of the generation of indoor OH is attributable to primary production in Regime I. At an HO2/OH ratio of 1000 : 1, a transition from Regime I to Regime II can occur, especially under more heavily polluted outdoor conditions.

We emphasize that the analysis in Table S4 simplifies a complex system. Detailed models are required to adequately capture the full chemical complexity of chain initiating, chain propagating, and chain terminating reactions for various conditions. Table S4 should be viewed as exploratory, aiming to illustrate some key points. Simple calculations such as these, utilizing available empirical evidence, can help guide more detailed calculations.

Regime I can also occur at night downwind of urban centers. Such locations experience ozone plumes that are regionally transported during nighttime hours.145,146 Plumes of high outdoor ozone can elevate indoor ozone concentrations, resulting in generation of OH via ozonolysis. During such episodes, indoor NO is expected to be below the level necessary for meaningful rates of radical cycling, reflecting the low levels of outdoor NO.

Regime II is expected to occur during periods on the order of an hour during midmorning and again in the mid-to-late afternoon. During these “crossover intervals,” outdoor NO and O3 concentrations change in their respective prominence. In the morning crossover, the shift is from high to low NO levels accompanied by a shift from low to high O3 levels. In the afternoon crossover, the pattern is reversed. Because of the fast rate of reaction between NO and O3, concentrations of the two species cannot simultaneously remain elevated. Instead, the outdoor concentrations of these co-occurring species are typically in the range 1–10 ppb during the crossover intervals. Indoor O3 is at levels sufficient for meaningful ozonolysis, but not so high as to almost completely titrate indoor NO.24,125 Consequently, indoor NO can be at levels sufficient for radical cycling to contribute meaningfully to the total rate of OH generation indoors.

Other periods when Regime II applies are (i) when indoor O3 is at levels sufficient for ozonolysis and unvented indoor combustion occurs from small sources (e.g., pilot lights);129 and (ii) when photochemistry generates indoor OH, and NO (either from outdoors or unvented combustion) is sufficient to support radical cycling.

The moderate to strong radical cycling that occurs in Regime II does not necessarily result in high indoor OH concentrations. For example, with ∼1 ppb of both NO and O3 indoors, radical cycling can be a moderate amplifier of a small OH pool, resulting in concentrations of OH that are on the order of 1–2 ppq. In contrast, with 10 ppb indoor O3 and negligible NO (Regime I), the indoor OH concentration could be on the order of 10 ppq with no contribution from radical cycling.

Regime III is a condition in which indoor oxidative capacity is weak. Very low indoor OH levels (<1 ppq) are expected to occur at night during periods when outdoor ozone concentrations are very low. Under such conditions, there is negligible ozonolysis. Given the absence of sunlight and expectedly weak contributions to photolysis from artificial lighting, the rate of OH generation by indoor photochemistry is very small in Regime III.

Regime III can also occur when indoor NO concentrations are very high due to supply by ventilation of outdoor air with high NO levels or due to emissions from unvented combustion sources. To be in Regime III, OH from photolysis must be negligible and NO levels must be high enough to titrate indoor O3 to below levels that would generate OH at meaningful rates.

To summarize, we judge that ozonolysis is a more common generator (initiator) of indoor OH than photolysis of HONO and other species. Average indoor O3 concentrations are about 5 ppb,129 sufficient for meaningful OH production by ozonolysis. Indoor concentrations of monoterpenes and alkenes that are susceptible to ozonolysis tend to be elevated owing to indoor emission sources (Tables 1 and 2). As discussed in Subsection 3.2, OH production from indoor lighting tends to be small. Photolysis of HONO by sunlight can be an important source of OH, but only in highly illuminated indoor spaces.

Other considerations relevant to indoor OH chemistry

Net effect of monoterpenes and alkenes on the indoor OH concentration

Monoterpenes and alkenes serve as both sources and sinks of the OH radical. The source strength is governed by the ozone and alkene concentrations, the ozone plus alkene reaction rate constant (kO3,i), and the OH yield (Y). For the sink term, the key parameter is the second-order rate constant for OH combining with the alkene (kOH,i).

Consider the respective source and sink strengths for OH of isoprene and 6-MHO. Isoprene's rate constant for reaction with OH is 7.7 × 106 times larger than its rate constant for reaction with O3. That ratio is much larger than the ratio for 6-MHO, 0.41 × 106. The OH yields for O3 reacting with isoprene and 6-MHO are 0.27 and ∼1.0, respectively. Under typical indoor conditions, with an O3 concentration 106 times larger than that of OH, isoprene is more of a sink for OH than it is a source, such that an increase in the concentration of isoprene would result in a net removal of indoor OH. In contrast, 6-MHO is more of a source for OH than it is a sink, such that under the same conditions an increase in the concentration of 6-MHO would result in a net increase in indoor OH.

As this example illustrates, an alkene can be either a net source or a net sink of OH radicals in indoor air. Furthermore, whether it is a net source or net sink is influenced by the relative abundance of ozone and OH.147 These features can be described analytically. We begin with eqn (3), describing the net source minus sink rate of OH generation attributable to a particular organic compound “i”:3[d([OH])/dt]i = YkO3,i[O3][Orgi] − kOH,i[OH][Orgi]

The ratio of OH production to consumption rates for organic compound “i” is given by the expression YkO3,i[O3]/(kOH,i[OH]). Note that this expression scales in proportion to the species concentration ratio [O3]/[OH]. A key question emerges: for a given alkene or monoterpene, what is the “breakeven” or “balance point” value of [O3]/[OH]? At the breakeven level ([O3]/[OH] = Rcrit = kOH,i/YkO3,i), the source and sink effects for OH would be balanced for that particular compound. If [O3]/[OH] > Rcrit, then reactions involving that organic would constitute a net source of OH. Conversely, if [O3]/[OH] < Rcrit, then an increase in the level of that organic compound would constitute a net sink of OH.

To summarize a key point, increases in the concentration of an alkene do not necessarily increase the production rate (or decrease the removal rate) of indoor OH. Attention must be given to both the rate at which OH is produced (YkO3,i) and the rate at which it is consumed (kOH,i). The relative balance is influenced by the [O3]/[OH] ratio. All else being equal, an elevated OH level in a room would tend to dampen the net effect of ozone/alkene chemistry as a source of indoor OH by decreasing the [O3]/[OH] ratio. Conversely, a diminished OH level would tend to amplify the net effect of ozone/alkene chemistry as a source of indoor OH by increasing the [O3]/[OH] ratio. More specifically, consider a room at steady state with respect to the sources and sinks of OH. If HONO photolysis were to suddenly become a strong source of indoor OH and the resulting [O3]/[OH] became smaller than Rcrit for co-occurring monoterpenes and alkenes, then these compounds would become net sinks for indoor OH. Conversely, if the concentration of ethanol (an OH sink) suddenly increased during an indoor activity (e.g., cooking or application of a fragrance) and the resulting [O3]/[OH] ratio became larger than Rcrit for co-occurring monoterpenes and alkenes, then these compounds would become net sources for indoor OH. The net effect on the OH concentration would also depend on the relative importance of ozonolysis compared to radical propagation. Under conditions where ozonolysis of monoterpenes/alkenes is the dominant factor influencing indoor OH concentrations (i.e., when indoor NO levels are very low), the OH concentration is tugged toward a central tendency and buffered from what might otherwise cause large swings in concentration.

Flux of OH radicals to indoor surfaces

Oxidation reactions that occur on indoor surfaces can influence indoor environmental quality. Among other considerations, sorptive partitioning of organics to surfaces can extend the time available for chemical transformations. Surface properties may also alter the kinetics and mechanisms of oxidative chemistry as compared to gas-phase reactions. An important feature of oxidative surface chemistry is the mass transport of an oxidant to an indoor surface. The flux of an oxidant to a surface (Fox, units of molecules per cm2 per s) can be expressed as the product of its deposition velocity (vd, cm s−1) and its concentration in nearby air ([ox, molecules per cm3]):4Fox = vd × [ox]

The deposition velocity can be estimated as the ratio of a species molecular diffusivity (D, cm2 s−1) to the concentration boundary layer thickness (L, cm):5vd ∼ D/L

For a moderately stable species of intermediate molecular weight, such as ozone, the chemical boundary layer thickness is comparable to the heat transfer and fluid-mechanical (frictional) boundary layer thicknesses. Indoors, this thickness is typically in the range 0.5–2 cm, depending on surface roughness, near surface airflow, and temperature difference between bulk air and surface.148,149 Given an ozone diffusivity of 0.16 cm2 s−1 (ref. 150) and assuming a boundary layer thickness of 1 cm, the mass-transport limited deposition velocity for ozone to an indoor surface would be approximately 0.16 cm s−1 (5.8 m h−1). The actual loss of ozone to indoor surfaces is typically less than the mass-transport limited value, attenuated by its limited chemical reactivity on surfaces.148 The average deposition velocity of ozone to room surfaces has been estimated to be about 0.028 cm s−1 (1 m h−1).129

The hydroxyl radical reacts readily with organics on indoor surfaces, and so its uptake on surfaces should occur at the mass-transport limited rate. However, being quite short-lived, OH cannot diffuse through a cm-thick boundary layer before it decays by a gas-phase reaction. Instead, the flux of OH to an indoor surface originates from OH production within the boundary layer, e.g. by gas-phase ozone reactions with monoterpenes and alkenes in the thin layer immediately adjacent to indoor surfaces.149,151 The effect of this phenomenon is that the chemical boundary layer thickness for the OH radical is much smaller than for ozone, yielding a larger deposition velocity.

The boundary layer thickness for OH (BLOH) can be estimated by equating the time to diffuse through the boundary layer (τDiff) with the chemical lifetime of OH (τChem) in indoor air.151 Setting these intervals equal leads to the estimate:6BLOH ∼ (2 × τChem × DOH)1/2

To illustrate, assume the total OH reactivity = 55 s−1 (Subsection 4.1) so that τChem = 0.018 s. The diffusion coefficient for OH in air (DOH) is 0.26 cm2 s−1,152 resulting in an estimated chemical boundary layer thickness of BLOH ∼0.1 cm. The estimated OH deposition velocity would then be (0.26 cm2 s−1)/(0.1 cm) ∼2.6 cm s−1 = 95 m h−1, which is two orders of magnitude larger than the typical deposition velocity for O3 to indoor surfaces.

The significance of OH as an indoor surface oxidant can now be contrasted to that of ozone. At an indoor concentration of 5 ppq, the flux of OH to a surface would be (1.2 × 105 molecules per cm3) × (2.6 cm s−1) ∼3 × 105 molecules per cm2 per s. In contrast, at an indoor ozone concentration of 5 ppb, the flux of O3 to a surface would be (1.2 × 1011 molecules per cm3) × (0.028 cm s−1) ∼3 × 109 molecules per cm2 per s. In summary, the very much higher concentration of ozone (∼106×) outweighs the somewhat higher deposition velocity of OH (∼100×) to produce this result: the molecular flux of ozone to an indoor surface is typically 10 000× the molecular flux of OH to that surface. (Note: this simplified comparison has used the OH concentration in bulk air. A more nuanced analysis would use the OH concentration occurring at its characteristic BL thickness of 1 mm rather than that in bulk air. The OH generation rate occurring at 1 mm distance would be smaller than that in bulk air because the O3 concentration would be smaller at that near-surface position).

Another factor to consider when estimating the flux of OH to indoor surfaces is the potential for OH-initiated autooxidation reactions to occur in organic surface films (see Subsection 7.3.2). Such autoxidation processes can have turnover rates approaching a hundred molecules of oxidized organics per molecule of OH consumed, resulting in higher levels of oxidation attributed to OH fluxes than would otherwise occur. However, even accounting for oxidation efficiencies of the autoxidation process, for unsaturated organics the conclusion remains that the effective rate of oxidation on surfaces induced by ozone is much larger than that induced by the OH radical. Worth mentioning is the fact that when O3 reacts with an unsaturated molecule that is surface bound, some OH forms at the surface leading to further oxidation by this OH molecule.

In the case of surface-associated saturated organics, it is important to remember that these species commonly react with OH via H-atom abstraction and that they do not react with O3. In air, the abstraction of an H-atom by OH is often too slow to compete with removal from indoor air by air change. However, on surfaces there can be ample time for this chemistry to occur with compounds that partition favorably to surface materials. In sum, the resulting oxidation state of the chemicals that constitute an aged surface film are a consequence of not only ozone reactions but also hydroxyl radical reactions.149

Multiphase and condensed-phase phenomena

In addition to reacting with most other molecules present in air, hydroxyl radicals can react with organic molecules on indoor surfaces. For a given organic compound that occurs indoors, the likelihood of it being oxidized on surfaces versus in air depends in part on its partitioning between air and surfaces. Partitioning to surfaces is the dominant fate for indoor organic molecules with low volatilities.151,157–159 Fahy et al.160 have developed a fugacity-based model to assess the partitioning and fate of indoor chemicals, including those generated by indoor chemistry. This model indicates that multiphase chemistry is most important for organic compounds with octanol/air partition coefficients (Koa) larger than about 108.

Palmitic acid and di(n-octyl)phthalate (DnOP) are commonly occurring indoor organic compounds with large Koa values (log Koa ∼12–13). Palmitic acid is the most abundant fatty acid in human skin oil and a major constituent of organic films on indoor surfaces.161 DnOP is a plasticizer found in indoor dust and surface films. Neither compound contains a carbon–carbon double bond that can react with ozone. In a pioneering study, Abbatt's group explored the kinetics of OH reaction with thin films of these semivolatile organic compounds in a flow reactor.151 They subsequently conducted similar experiments with actual indoor films that had formed on glass capillaries exposed to office air for several days. Comparable behaviors were observed with respect to OH oxidation for both the films of pure chemicals and the mixed films that had formed on the glass capillaries. Oxidation of the constituents in the indoor surface film proceeded at a moderate rate. Extrapolating from the conditions used in these experiments, the investigators estimated that the lifetimes of palmitic acid and DnOP, with respect to oxidation by OH in indoor environments, would be in the ranges 5–50 days and 3–30 days, respectively.

Related experiments revealed that di(2-ethylhexyl)phthalate (DEHP) undergoes OH oxidation on indoor surfaces to produce numerous oxidation products.162 These products were subsequently identified in pure DEHP films exposed to actual indoor air as well as in genuine indoor dust samples. Assuming an OH concentration of about 8 ppq (2 × 105 molecules per cm3), the investigators estimated the half-life of DEHP in thin films to be about 2 weeks. This evidence suggests that OH oxidation is relevant to the fate of phthalates sorbed to indoor surfaces. In another study, the reactions of OH with bisphenol A (BPA) and six of its potential substitutes were examined in a flow reactor.163 Measurements indicated that these bisphenols are susceptible to oxidation by OH at “environmentally relevant exposures.” Fourteen products were identified, each predicted to persist and accumulate on indoor surfaces.

Autoxidation

In the atmospheric chemistry community, the term “autoxidation” has been used to refer to regenerative peroxy radical formation following intramolecular hydrogen shifts in the gas phase.164,165 In indoor air, autoxidation reactions involving OH can generate highly oxidized molecules (HOMs) with large O/C ratios,166 contributing to secondary organic aerosol formation (SOA). On indoor surfaces, OH can initiate autoxidation reactions in lipid films leading to the oxidation of film constituents that would not be oxidized by O3. The repeated oxidation cycles that occur during autoxidation reactions without consuming OH can amplify the overall importance of OH in indoor environments.

Autoxidation in the gas phase

Historically, scientists thought that sulfuric acid was necessary for the formation of atmospheric aerosols. In a series of groundbreaking studies published about a decade ago, investigators demonstrated the formation of atmospheric aerosols in the absence of sulfuric acid. Instead, autoxidation processes initiated by O3 reacting with terpenes can generate HOMs at a sufficiently fast rate to produce new aerosol particles in outdoor air.167–169 More recently, Pagonis et al.166 reported measurements of HOMs in an art museum, suggestive of autoxidation of limonene indoors. When OH abstracts an H-atom from an organic molecule in air, an alkylperoxy radical (RO2) is quickly formed. While indoor OH has a lifetime of milliseconds, RO2 radicals have lifetimes of minutes indoors. Importantly, RO2 radicals are key players in gas-phase autoxidation reactions. The process begins with a rearrangement, called an “H-shift,” in the RO2 radical. When indoor HO2 and NO levels are low, indoor RO2 has longer lifetimes and more time to rearrange. Following rearrangement, O2 addition and fast unimolecular reactions occur, ultimately producing highly oxidized molecules (HOMs) with large O/C ratios.164,170

Møller et al.171 have calculated rate coefficients for H-shifts in RO2 radicals formed by OH and O3 additions to various monoterpenes. Their results suggest that “ring-opened α- and β-pinene isomers as well as isomers of limonene and terpinolene have unimolecular reactions” that are fast enough to be important in the atmosphere. In related studies, Chen et al.172 have calculated differences in reactivity between the peroxy radicals formed by either O3 or OH reactions with limonene. They concluded that, compared to O3, OH addition to double bonds in limonene produces fewer types of peroxy radicals that undergo unimolecular reactions fast enough to be atmospherically important. Piletic and Kleindienst173 calculated prominent HOM formation from OH-addition reactions for α-pinene, β-pinene, and limonene. Such processes can contribute to the formation of SOA in indoor as well as outdoor air.172,174

Autoxidation in aerosols and surface films

Given the relatively low concentration of OH in indoor air, OH-induced oxidation of organic compounds in aerosols and surface films was considered unimportant. This thinking changed with the advent of measurements indicating the occurrence of autoxidation processes. In a series of experiments, researchers from Wilson's lab exposed different aerosols consisting of common indoor lipids (e.g., squalene, oleic acid, linoleic acid) to hydroxyl radicals.175,176 Measurements provided evidence for autoxidation reactions initiated by OH addition to CC bonds, and then driven by Criegee intermediates during propagation. At OH levels of ∼30 ppq (∼7 × 105 molecules per cm3), for every OH collision with squalene in an airborne particle, ∼70 molecules were consumed in chain reactions. As the OH concentration decreased, the number of free radical chain reaction cycles per collision increased. However, it appears that reactions of OH radicals with particles in indoor air occur too slowly to compete with removal by air exchange. This conclusion is based, in part, on studies in which OH reactions with outdoor organic aerosols have been shown to be an important pathway to their oxidative aging.152,177 For example, George et al.152 estimated an oxidation lifetime of 2.6 days for an aerosol consisting of saturated organic compounds. While this lifetime is relevant over the time scale of regional and continental atmospheric transport, in indoor settings it is substantially longer than hour-scale persistence times governed by air change rates, e.g. in residences.128 In contrast to airborne particles, much longer time scales are available for autoxidation reactions to occur in deposited particles including settled dust.

Autoxidation in aerosols and surface films is an active area of research.162,175,176,178,179 Current evidence suggests that OH often initiates autoxidation processes on indoor surfaces. Many of the autoxidation mechanisms investigated involve propagation by H-shift chemistry of peroxy radicals. Criegee intermediates and ROOH appear to be the key species in the repeated oxidation cycles (propagation) that define autoxidation.175,176,179 Investigators from Abbatt's lab probed the autoxidation of unsaturated lipid films (methyl linoleate and canola oil) under varying indoor-relevant conditions.179 In a flow reactor with air containing relatively high levels of OH, fast autoxidation of thin films of methyl linoleate were observed to produce primarily organic hydroperoxides (ROOH). In a dark desk drawer, the kinetics of ROOH formation were the same as in the flow reactor even though the concentration of OH was orders of magnitude less. The findings suggest that reactive oxygen species (e.g., ROOH, OH, RO2) accelerate fast autoxidation in a dark indoor setting. Autoxidation may also have played a role in the oxidation of DEHP, BPA, and BPA analogs in surface films that was reported above.162,163 Indeed, many plasticizers, flame retardants, UV stabilizers, and other organic additives that are often present in indoor surface films180 may subsequently oxidize via such processes.

Summary and outlook

Summary

Indoor OH concentrations span roughly two orders of magnitude, with a central tendency on the order of 5 ppq under typical residential conditions. The primary source of indoor OH is the gas-phase reaction of ozone with monoterpenes and alkenes, especially limonene, 6-MHO, and geranyl acetone (Subsection 3.1). Photolysis of nitrous acid (HONO) constitutes another important source, particularly when combustion processes—such as cooking with natural gas—generate elevated indoor HONO levels and direct sunlight enters through open windows (Subsection 3.2).

The dominant sink for indoor OH is homogeneous gas-phase reaction with VOCs, with a total OH reactivity in occupied residences typically on the order of 50 s−1 (Subsection 4.1). This homogeneous reaction loss rate is orders of magnitude faster than the loss rates for surface removal and ventilation. Monoterpenes, formaldehyde, ethanol, acetaldehyde, CO, NO2, and isoprene are among the most important contributors to total OH reactivity indoors. During episodic activities—cooking, cleaning, or application of fragranced personal care products—total OH reactivity can rise to 150 s−1 or higher, further reducing OH lifetime and limiting peak OH concentrations even as precursor emissions increase. A notable feature of the indoor OH budget is its buffering: many of the compounds that generate OH by reacting with O3 also serve as sinks for OH, tending to modulate excursions in the indoor OH concentration (Subsection 6.1). At the breakeven level of the [O3]/[OH] ratio for a given monoterpene or alkene ([O3]/[OH] = Rcrit = kOH,i/YkO3,i), the source and sink effects for OH are balanced. The Rcrit values vary substantially among compounds that are both sources and sinks for OH. (Specifically, Rcrit spans at least two orders of magnitude between values of 0.18 × 106 for geranyl acetone and 29 × 106 for isoprene).

Radical cycling can amplify indoor OH concentrations via the HO2 + NO reaction. The importance of this cycle depends on the level of NO (Subsection 5.1). The influence of cycling increases as the indoor NO level increases; however, increased NO levels also attenuate indoor O3 levels, which diminishes primary OH production. Indoor NO concentrations can span four orders of magnitude (Subsection 5.2). Absent indoor sources, indoor NO tracks outdoor levels and is typically well below 1 ppb during midday hours, when radical cycling is consequently weak. Unvented combustion—such as from natural-gas cooking—can drive indoor NO concentrations to tens or hundreds of ppb. Combustion sources introduce a tension for indoor OH levels, because the emitted NO simultaneously supplies the reactant for OH regeneration via HO2 + NO and titrates indoor O3, thereby suppressing the ozonolysis that initiates radical production (Subsection 5.2). The NO concentration that defines a “low NO environment” for indoor radical chemistry has not been well characterized. (It is expected to differ from levels that pertain to outdoor urban air where photochemistry is more important and surface chemistry is less important). Reactions of NO2 with OH, HO2, and RO2 are chain terminating; their influence on cycling increases as the indoor NO2 level increases. The coupling that occurs indoors between NOx chemistry and the OH budget deserves increased attention, particularly in residences with unvented combustion appliances such as gas stoves.

Together, these source and cycling influences produce a diurnal pattern of indoor OH that, absent indoor NO sources, can be organized into three regimes (Subsection 5.3). In Regime I (typically midmorning to late afternoon), moderate-to-high indoor O3 and very low indoor NO favor OH production by ozonolysis with weak-to-moderate cycling, yielding low-to-moderate OH levels. In Regime II—chiefly the morning and afternoon “crossover” intervals, each on the order of an hour, during which outdoor NO and O3 are roughly 1 to 10 ppb—radical cycling is moderate to strong, but the resulting OH concentrations remain low to moderate because of low indoor O3 levels. In Regime III (overnight, or whenever high indoor NO titrates O3 while photolysis is negligible), indoor oxidative capacity is weak and the OH concentration can be below ∼1 ppq.

The role of OH as a gas-phase oxidant can be compared to that of O3 (Table 8). The hydroxyl radical can oxidize saturated organic compounds, including aldehydes, which are not oxidized by O3. With respect to its role as a surface oxidant, the flux of OH to indoor surfaces—while orders of magnitude lower than the ozone flux—is consequential for the long-term (weeks or months) oxidation of low-volatility organic compounds in surface films (Subsections 6.2 and 7.2), especially saturated organics that are not oxidized by ozone. Autoxidation, initiated by OH and subsequently propagated by Criegee intermediates and organic hydroperoxides, can yield effective oxidation turnover rates approaching ∼100 molecules per initial OH encounter. The effectiveness of autoxidation suggests that the influence of OH on surface chemistry is greater than molecular flux estimates would imply (Subsection 7.3.2). Whether ozone can itself initiate autoxidation in surface films remains an open question.

In summary, ozone and the hydroxyl radical are dominant indoor oxidants. While indoor OH is most often derived from O3 chemistry, the two oxidants differ in their sources, sinks, reaction mechanisms, and products. Specifically, OH drives chemical transformations beyond those attributable to O3 alone.

Outlook

Recognizing important gaps in our current understanding of OH as an indoor oxidant can suggest priorities for future research. The following paragraphs highlight key opportunities.

Measurement methods

Direct real-time measurement of OH indoors remains technically demanding. The LIF-FAGE technique has enabled high-quality measurements in a few settings,49–52 and CIMS methods have been applied productively in targeted campaigns.61 However, instruments are large, expensive, and not suited for widespread deployment. Development of more portable, lower-cost OH measurement approaches—or validated proxies based on easily measurable quantities—would substantially accelerate progress in understanding the full scope of indoor OH chemistry.

Field campaigns in test houses

Detailed indoor chemistry models have grown substantially in scope and sophistication.28,32,33 Nevertheless, model-measurement comparisons for indoor OH remain limited by the scarcity of high-quality field data. The HOMEChem and CASA campaigns,33,52,181,182 conducted in test houses, have provided valuable data for several simulated scenarios under controlled conditions. Data from these studies have already served to inform and constrain models. Further campaigns targeting specific questions in indoor OH chemistry and including simultaneous measurement of OH, HO2, NOx, VOCs, HONO, and photolysis rates would provide valuable tests and reduce uncertainties in current mechanistic models.

Field campaigns in normally occupied environments

A central theme of this review is that indoor OH chemistry is highly variable in space and time, reflecting heterogeneous precursor concentrations and photolysis fields, as well as the short OH lifetime (∼20 ms). The limited chemical lifetime precludes meaningful transport and mixing of OH across room-scale distances. Concentrations of OH are also strongly modulated by occupant behaviors such as cooking, cleaning, and the use of personal care products (Subsection 4.2). Ventilation choices further influence indoor OH chemistry. While field campaigns in test houses are useful, such houses are commonly unoccupied. Measurements relevant to indoor OH chemistry have been conducted for extended periods in several occupied homes and classrooms.74,183–188 However, the diversity of occupant behaviors, such as in cooking practices and cleaning product use, underscores the need for a richer set of field observations spanning more diverse occupied indoor environments (residences, offices, schools) and climates.

Near-source OH chemistry

Because the OH lifetime is so much shorter than room mixing times, near-source regions—adjacent to human bodies, cooking appliances, or sunlit windows—can generate OH concentrations that are an order of magnitude or more above the room-average value. The human oxidation field42,45 and related CFD investigations120 illustrate how profoundly occupants themselves shape their perihuman environment. Systematic characterizations of near source OH chemistry would be valuable, including the sampling of biomarkers indicative of oxidant exposures. Such studies could be conducted in both chambers and test houses.

Photolysis associated with germicidal UV

As germicidal UV (GUV) technologies are being deployed more widely to reduce pathogen transmission, their unintended effects on indoor photochemistry deserve careful evaluation. Modeling32 and limited measurements97 suggest meaningful perturbations to OH budgets when GUV lamps are in operation. It would be prudent to better understand the nature and magnitude of such effects before proceeding with large-scale deployment of germicidal UV. Attention to relevant photochemistry and its influence on indoor OH levels is warranted for both upper-room 254 nm systems and far-UVC 222 nm sources.

Multiphase chemistry and health-relevant products

The transformation of semivolatile organic compounds in indoor surface films by OH-initiated processes, including autoxidation, generates a complex mixture of hydroperoxides, alcohols, epoxides, and highly oxygenated molecules (HOMs) (Subsections 7.2 and 7.3). The toxicity of these products—many of which are more water-soluble and potentially more irritating than their precursors—is largely unknown. Closing this gap is essential for connecting indoor OH chemistry to human health outcomes.

Other needs

Other needs include carefully examining the effects of occupancy, indoor ozone concentration and ventilation rate on indoor OH levels; better defining the boundaries of the various regimes for indoor OH dynamics (Table 5); and generating improved empirical evidence regarding low indoor NO levels when indoor O3 concentrations are in the vicinity of 5 ppb or higher. Regarding this last point, the studies that report measured indoor NO concentrations have used standard commercial instruments with limits of detection in the vicinity of 0.5 ppb. Instruments with better NO sensitivity have been deployed in outdoor field campaigns;21,189 we are unaware of any studies that have used such sensitive instruments indoors. Indoor NO concentrations below 1 ppb are expected to occur commonly and are worthwhile to characterize in relation to the importance of radical cycling as an OH amplification mechanism.

Conclusion

How important is OH as an indoor oxidant? The evidence reviewed here indicates that OH is a significant, if secondary, indoor oxidant whose influence extends well beyond what ozone alone can accomplish. Whereas ozone is both the principal oxidant indoors and a dominant source of indoor OH, ozone reacts almost exclusively with compounds containing CC bonds. OH, by contrast, attacks the full breadth of indoor organic compounds—saturated hydrocarbons, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and aromatic compounds among them—transforming species that ozone cannot meaningfully oxidize on indoor timescales. Indoor OH concentrations under common conditions are on the order of 5 ppq, a million times lower than co-occurring indoor O3 levels. Lower concentrations of OH are counterbalanced by its much greater reactivity—sufficient to meaningfully oxidize monoterpenes and alkenes in typical indoor environments. The central tendency for total OH reactivity in the air of occupied residences is typically ∼50 s−1, implying an OH lifetime of only ∼20 ms. That is, almost every OH molecule produced in indoor air reacts promptly with VOCs, CO, and NO2, its major chemical sinks indoors. When the concentration of NO is high enough to compete with other processes for HO2 radicals, the importance of OH can be amplified by radical cycling (Section 5). Indoor OH concentrations are also modulated by occupant behavior. Cooking, cleaning, and the use of personal care products can elevate OH an order of magnitude or more above background levels, and the human oxidation field itself creates a chemically distinct perihuman environment. Significantly, indoor OH concentrations are buffered by the dual character of monoterpenes and alkenes as sources and sinks. On indoor surfaces, OH-initiated autoxidation of semivolatile organic compounds generates carbonyls, alcohols, hydroxycarbonyls, organic acids, organic nitrates, peroxy nitrates, hydroperoxides, and highly oxygenated molecules (HOMs) that tend to persist indoors longer than their precursors. Because OH reacts with such a wide range of compounds, it influences both the composition of indoor air and the chemical character of indoor surfaces in ways that ozone alone cannot. The human health implications of this chemistry—particularly the toxicity of oxidation products inhaled or absorbed through the skin—remain poorly understood and represent a compelling motivation for continued research.

While the general framework for understanding indoor OH chemistry is in place, numerous qualitative and quantitative features remain to be resolved. Regardless, it has been established that the hydroxyl radical is a ubiquitous indoor oxidant of diverse significance. Its relevance to indoor air quality and human health justifies continued investment in measurement, mechanistic modeling, and the development of practical approaches for characterizing and managing its role in indoor oxidative chemistry.
