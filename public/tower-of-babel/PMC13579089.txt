Intrinsically Stretchable Piezoelectric Nanocomposites for Artificial Ultrasonic Sensory Synapse
Authors: Trung Dieu Do, Tran Quang Trung, Priyanuj Bhuyan, Minh Chien Nguyen, Que Thi Nguyen, Namjun Jo, Yeonsu Kim, Thanh Duy Cam Ha, Myung‐Gil Kim, Sungjune Park, Woo Jong Yu, Nae‐Eung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntrinsically stretchable piezoelectric materials are very promising for next‐generation stretchable and conformal electronics. However, enhancing their performance in terms of ferroelectric phase fraction and piezoelectric properties remains a major challenge. Here, we present a cross‐linked intrinsically stretchable piezoelectric nanocomposite (IS‐PNC) of poly(vinylidene fluoride‐trifluoroethylene)‐poly(ethylene glycol) diamine (P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG) incorporating (3‐aminopropyl)triethoxysilane‐functionalized BaTiO3 nanoparticles (BTO@APTES NPs). The strong hydrogen‐bonded interfaces formed between the BTO@APTES NP fillers and the stretchable P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG matrix promote the formation of ferroelectric phase without compromising stretchability. Furthermore, comprehensive ferroelectric hysteresis investigations reveal the polarization switching mechanisms of the cross‐linked IS‐PNCs, strongly influencing overall ferroelectric responses. Benefiting from the increased ferroelectric phase, improved ferroelectricity, and aligned dipoles, the optimized IS‐PNC at 1 wt% BTO@APTES exhibits intrinsic stretchability and stable piezoelectricity under strain up to 50%, maintaining robust electromechanical responses. Leveraging these properties, an artificial ultrasonic sensory synapse combining the IS‐PNC sensor and a flexible synaptic transistor was demonstrated, mimicking the ultrasonic detection functions found in dolphin biological echolocation. The IS‐PNCs have great potential for stretchable piezoelectronics.

Introduction

Soft electronic devices with mechanical stretchability need to be sufficiently elastic and mechanically robust to conform to biological tissues and accommodate dynamic bodily movements [1, 2, 3, 4]. These mechanical requirements have become essential characteristics of the materials used in skin‐like stretchable electronics [5, 6]. Conventionally, stretchable electronic materials are realized either through structural engineering of rigid or flexible components [7, 8, 9] or by incorporating functional materials into non‐piezoelectric elastomeric matrices [10, 11, 12] to achieve mechanical stretchability. Due to the complexity of fabrication processes and/or limited performance in those approaches, stretchable electronic sensors and circuits based on intrinsically stretchable conductors, dielectric materials, and semiconductors have been widely investigated to enable stable and intimate interfaces with biological systems, including human and animal tissues [12, 13, 14]. Compared to intrinsically stretchable conductor and semiconductor materials [15, 16, 17], searches for intrinsically stretchable stimuli‐responsive functional materials, including ferro‐piezoelectric materials, have lagged. Therefore, such materials with intrinsically stretchable behaviors and high performance would significantly broaden the application scope of conformal and wearable electronics [18, 19, 20].

Ferro‐piezoelectricity is characterized by reversible spontaneous polarization and distinctive electromechanical properties, enabling applications ranging from electronic devices to bioelectronic systems. Ferro‐piezoelectric polymers based on poly(vinylidene difluoride) (PVDF) and its copolymers with trifluoroethylene (P(VDF‐TrFE)) are highly flexible and demonstrate as a potential candidate [21, 22]; however, they typically undergo plastic deformation and fail to fully recover after stretching [23, 24]. In contrast, conventional inorganic ferro‐piezoelectric materials exhibit strong ferroelectric and piezoelectric responses yet are intrinsically brittle and prone to fracture under mechanical loading [25]. Incorporating inorganic ferroelectric nanofillers into P(VDF‐TrFE) can enhance the overall polarization and promote or stabilize the β‐phase of P(VDF‑TrFE) through interfacial interactions, thereby improving dipole alignment [26, 27, 28, 29]. However, achieving uniform and effective filler dispersion within the polymer matrix requires cautious control over multiple factors, including filler size, surface chemistry, dispersion homogeneity, loading fraction, ferroelectric domain orientation, and poling protocol [28, 30, 31]. Consequently, developing intrinsically stretchable ferro‐piezoelectric nanocomposites that compatibly integrate brittle inorganic ferro‐piezoelectric constituents with flexible polymer counterparts remains crucial for conformal stretchable electronics, yet continues to pose a substantial challenge.

Recently, poly‐ethylene glycol (PEG)‐induced slight chemical cross‐linking of PVDF and its copolymers have emerged as an effective approach to impart intrinsic stretchability to ferroelectric polymers while preserving their ferroelectric functionality [32, 33, 34]. Despite these advances, PEG‐cross‐linked elastic ferroelectric polymers still encounter several limitations. First, the introduction of cross‐linking reduced crystallinity, thereby constraining achievable polarization. Second, the deformation of ferroelectric hysteresis behavior in these elastic polymers remains insufficiently clarified, restricting fundamental insight into polarization switching mechanisms. Besides, mechanical‐ and ultrasound‐to‐electrical transduction capacities in intrinsically elastic ferroelectric polymers have not been thoroughly explored, despite being crucial for stretchable piezoelectric sensors. The incorporation of BaTiO3 nanoparticles (BTO NPs) offers a promising strategy to overcome these challenges by introducing stronger ferroelectric polarization, enhancing interfacial polarization, and improving electromechanical coupling without compromising the elastic polymer network. Furthermore, a detailed study of ferroelectric hysteresis deformation in these nanocomposites is crucial to accelerating the development of intrinsically stretchable ferro‐piezoelectric materials for emerging electronic sensors.

In this study, an intrinsically stretchable piezoelectric nanocomposite (IS‐PNC) of poly(vinylidene fluoride‐trifluoroethylene)‐poly(ethylene glycol) diamine (P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG) incorporating (3‐aminopropyl)triethoxysilane‐functionalized BTO NPs (BTO@APTES NPs) was developed by introducing selective cross‑linking within the amorphous regions of the P(VDF‐TrFE) matrix. This process generates a new ─C═N─CH2 group via a reaction between the ─C─F group of P(VDF‐TrFE) and the ─NH2 group of PEG‐diamine. Simultaneously, BTO NPs were surface‐functionalized with hydrogen peroxide and APTES to produce amine‐terminated BTO@APTES nanofillers capable of forming strong hydrogen bonds with the cross‐linked polymer chains, yielding a soft yet structurally reinforced network. The resulting IS‐PNC exhibits markedly enhanced interfacial interactions and β‐phase crystallinity compared with the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG matrix, while maintaining stretchability up to 90% strain. With the incorporation of BTO@APTES NPs, the cross‐linked IS‐PNC shows significantly stronger ferroelectric behavior compared with cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG, even under tensile strains up to 60%. Moreover, the changes in the ferroelectric hysteresis behaviors between pure P(VDF‐TrFE), cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG, and IS‐PNCs are further elucidated by the deformations of ferroelectric hysteresis corresponding to changes in the instantaneous current (IC). Benefiting from the increased β‐crystalline phase, preserved stretchability as well as stronger ferroelectric features, the IS‐PNC containing 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs exhibited higher piezoelectric performances compared with cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG, even with an increase in stretching strain up to 50%. Owing to these unique piezoelectric outputs, the IS‐PNC sensor on the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate as a low‐loss ultrasonic absorption layer was further utilized as an underwater ultrasonic sensing component to activate a flexible synaptic transistor on the polyimide (PI) substrate, featuring a reduced‑graphene oxide (rGO) channel and an ionogel dielectric layer, for an artificial ultrasonic sensory synapse (AUSS) to emulate the dolphin‑like echolocation. Under ultrasonic stimulations, the IS‐PNC sensor generated piezoelectric potentials to trigger the changes of post‐synaptic current (PSC) and synaptic plasticity in the synaptic transistor, which mimics dolphin biological auditory receptors, highlighting its potential for underwater stretchable sensing applications. The IS‐PNC with slight cross‐linking represents a promising stretchable material platform for advancing stretchable piezoelectronics.

Results and Discussion

Design and Fabirication Stratergies of an AUSS Utilizing an IS‐PNC Ultrasonic Sensor

Dolphin biological echolocation system is a connection of signal transmission and auditory reception through ribbon‐synapse‐like structures between neuron terminals, where synaptic functions are characterized by connection strengths between neurons (synaptic weight, SW) (Figure 1a). Within the ranges of 5 to 100 kHz or higher frequencies, dolphins rely on ultrasonic waves transmitted from the water directly onto the bony ear complex and cochlea via low‐loss acoustic fat bodies [35, 36]. Vibrations transmitted to the cochlea drive motion of the basilar membrane within its fluid‑filled chambers. This motion deflects the stereocilia bundles of inner hair cells, opening mechanoelectrical transduction channels and allowing cation influx (mainly K+ and Ca2+), depolarizing the hair cells and triggering action potential [36, 37]. At the pre‐synaptic terminal of the inner hair cell, a ribbon synapse organizes synaptic vesicles containing glutamate [36, 38]. When hair cells are depolarized, voltage‐gated Ca2+ channels open, Ca2+ influx promotes vesicle fusion, and glutamate is released into the synaptic channel. Glutamate activates α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid)‐type receptors (AMPARs) on the auditory nerve fibres, generating post‐synaptic potentials that relay encoded information about echo timing and intensity of ultrasonic waves to the brain [36, 38]. Based on the unique dolphin echolocation, an AUSS is proposed to mimic the functions of signal transmission and ultrasonic recognition in dolphin echolocation systems.

To realize the AUSS, we initially developed the stretchable ultrasonic sensor based on the IS‐PNC. BTO@APTES NPs primarily obtained by surface functionalization of BTO NPs (Figures S1–S3) were incorporated with the concentrations of 1 and 2 wt% into a P(VDF‐TrFE) matrix, considering a decrease of crystallinity of P(VDF‐TrFE) at higher content of BTO NPs [28]. Then, PEG‐diamine was introduced as a crosslinker into the above solution to synthesize an elastic network within the nanocomposite [32]. The strategy of forming a slightly cross‐linked polymer network relies on a thermal cross‐linking reaction between the ─NH2 groups of PEG‐diamine and ─CF2 groups of P(VDF‐TrFE), resulting in new imine (─C═N) bonds within the stretchable and elastic polymer matrix (Figures 1b and S4) [32]. Additionally, strong hydrogen bonds (described as ─N─H…F─C─) are formed between the ─NH2 groups on BTO@APTES and the ─CF2 groups of P(VDF‐TrFE), as well as the ether (─O─) groups in PEG, thereby enhancing interfacial interactions within the nanocomposite and promote the formation of β‐phase content and dipole alignments of P(VDF‐TrFE) in the matrix (Figure 1b). Building upon the applicability of the cross‐linked IS‐PNC, an IS‐PNC sensor was fabricated by a sandwich structure of the IS‐PNC layer between liquid metal (E‐GaIn) electrodes patterned on soft PDMS substrates to investigate its piezoelectric responses and underwater ultrasonic detection capabilities, exploiting the stretchability imparted by the IS‐PNC (Figure 1c). The IS‐PNC sensor on the PDMS substrate as a low‐loss ultrasonic absorption layer served as an underwater ultrasonic sensing component and was subsequently integrated with a flexible synaptic transistor (Figure S5) to realize AUSS (Figure 1c). Under ultrasonic excitations, the IS‐PNC sensor generates a piezoelectric potential that serves as dynamic gate biases for the synaptic transistor, enabling the device to perceive and distinguish ultrasonic information through changes in the PSC, analogous to signal recognition in biological auditory receptors.

Structural Characterizations of Cross‐Linked IS‐PNCs

To further investigate the formation of cross‐links within the IS‐PNCs, attenuated total reflectance (ATR)—Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed. As shown in Figure S6, a distinct absorption peak at 1664 cm− 1 corresponding to the imine (─C═N─) group was observed in the IS‐PNCs containing 1 and 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs, confirming the occurrence of thermal cross‐linking between PEG‐diamine and P(VDF‐TrFE) (Figure S6b–d) compared with pure P(VDF‐TrFE) (Figure S6a). After cross‐linking, the characteristic β‐phase peaks at 844 and 1284 cm− 1 remained in the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG and IS‐PNCs, indicating preservation of ferroelectric crystalline phases (Figure S6b–d). Additionally, x‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis further verifies the coexistence of the β‐phase and 3/1‐helical phase in cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG, with characteristic peaks at 19.16° and 18.92°, respectively, compared with the only β‐phase peak of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) (Figure S7) [32]. Similarly, these reflections at 19.16° and 18.92° also appear in cross‐linked IS‐PNCs, demonstrating that incorporation of BTO@APTES NPs sustained the formation and retention of the cross‐linked structure in the polymer network (Figure S7). On the basis of the Beer–Lambert law, the β‐phase contents of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and cross‐linked IS‐PNCs films were quantified by the equation: Fβ=Aβ1.26Aα+Aβ through the FTIR spectrum in Figure S6 using the absorbance intensities of A α (766 cm−1) and A β (844 cm−1), together with the factor of 1.26 derived from absorption coefficients at two wave numbers (K α = 6.1 × 104 and K β = 7.7 × 104 cm2 mol−1) [39]. The results revealed a decrease of β‐phase content in cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG compared with pure P(VDF‐TrFE) (Figure S8), due to the increased amorphous regions formed during cross‐linking. In the cross‐linked IS‐PNCs, the β‐phase content increases to 78.33% ± 0.37% at 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs but decreases at 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs (Figure S8), which is attributed to the uniformity and agglomeration of BTO@APTES NPs within the cross‐linked matrix, respectively (Figure S9). These trends can be clarified by the interfacial interactions between BTO@APTES NPs and the cross‐linked network during the crystalline formation.

To assess the role of interfacial interactions on crystallization, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was employed to characterize the thermal behaviors of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and cross‐linked IS‐PNCs (Figure S10). Compared to pure P(VDF‐TrFE), the introduction of PEG‐diamine as a soft cross‐linking segment led to pronounced decreases in the glass transition, melting, and crystallization temperatures to 61.44°C, 154.25 °C, and 134.51 °C, respectively, reflecting decreased crystallinity in the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG due to the formation of additional amorphous regions (Figure S10). Upon incorporation of 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs into the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG matrix, the IS‐PNC raised the glass transition, melting and crystallization temperatures to 61.74°C, 154.70°C, and 135.44°C (Figure S10), consistent with strong interfacial bonding enabled by the uniform dispersion of BTO@APTES NPs within the polymer network (Figure S9c). Hydrogen bonding interactions between the ─NH2 groups on the BTO@APTES surface and the ─CF2 groups of P(VDF‐TrFE) (denoted as ─N─H…F─C─) likely serve as nucleating agents, reinforcing the ferroelectric phase development during crystallization. By contrast, the aggregation of 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs (Figure S9d) led to a decrease in the β‐phase content. This was observed in the decrease of glass transition temperature to 61.15°C (Figure S10a) and only minor upshifts in melting and crystallization temperatures to 154.83°C and 135.93°C, respectively (Figure S10b,c). Such aggregation thus generates the intrinsic effect, restricted metameric motion of polymer chains in the matrix, thereby diminishing the crystallization efficiency of P(VDF‐TrFE) segments in the IS‐PNC [28, 40].

Mechanical Behaviors of Cross‐Linked IS‐PNCs

Based on the cross‐linked structure, the IS‐PNCs motivate an examination of their mechanical behaviors, as shown in Figures 2 and S11. As a reference, the pure P(VDF‐TrFE) films displayed the typical response of a non‐stretchable polymer (Figure 2a) with a high modulus of 257.1 ± 3.7 MPa (Figure 2e), an elongation at break of 167.2% ± 12.0% (Figure 2f) and fracture at a strain of only 43.6% ± 0.3% under ultimate stress (Figure 2g,h). Following cross‐linking, the mechanical responses of P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG altered significantly (Figure 2b), reducing the modulus to 96.1 ± 2.4 MPa (Figure 2e) and increasing the elongation at break to 316.9% ± 13.7% at the higher ultimate stress (Figure 2f–h). This transition from non‐stretchable to stretchable deformation behavior is accompanied by an increase of the elastic regime around 8% strain in P(VDF‐TrFE) to over 20% in the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG (Figure 2i). In the IS‐PNCs, BTO@APTES NPs served as inorganic fillers that form strong interfacial bonding with the polymer matrix at 1 wt% loading, causing a slight increase in modulus (Figure 2e) and a moderate reduction in elongation at break (Figure 2c,f) while preserving stretchable deformation (Figures 2c and S11) and even slight increasing the elastic regime up to 24% (Figure 2i). This balance illustrates the effectiveness of the cross‐linked architecture and BTO@APTES incorporation in enhancing the ferroelectric phase and maintaining mechanical strength. At 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs, the stress–strain curves reveal pronounced variations of elongation at break (Figure 2d,f), with increased strain at break at higher ultimate stress (Figure 2f–h) but a slight decrease in modulus (Figure 2e) and elastic regime (Figure 2i), which is attributed to nonuniform distributions of BTO@APTES NPs in the matrix.

To investigate the mechanical properties in more detail, the cyclic stress‐strain behaviors of the IS‐PNC with 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs were compared with those of P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG. The cross‐linked IS‐PNC film was subjected to cyclic tensile loading at strains from 10% to 90% for 15 cycles (Figure 2j,k). During the first cycles at each strain level (Figure 2j), a noticeable hysteresis was observed, arising from the rearrangement of polymer chains in the matrix in response to applied stress. In the subsequent cycles, the stress–strain curves largely overlapped and were consistent with the elastic recovery observed in Figure S12, indicating an intrinsically elastic response up to 40% at 90% strain of the IS‐PNC. When compared with the P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG at 70% strain, the IS‐PNC film exhibited similar hysteresis behavior, with only a slight increase in applied stress (Figure S13), further demonstrating that the incorporation of BTO@APTES NPs contributes to preservation of the intrinsically stretchable and elastic characteristics of the IS‐PNC.

Ferroelectric Responses of Cross‐Linked IS‐PNCs

To demonstrate the ferro‐piezoelectricity of IS‐PNCs, piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) was employed to visualize spontaneous polarization within local domain structures (Figure S14). PFM images under the domain structures of pure P(VDF‐TrFE), cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG, and IS‐PNCs, associated with the topographic images, are shown in Figure S14a,e,i,m, respectively. For pure P(VDF‐TrFE), the PFM box‐in‐box amplitude and phase patterns (Figure S14b,c) were reversibly switched by applying opposite tip voltages of ±10 V to the central regions. The associated butterfly‐shaped and phase‐hysteresis curves also demonstrate the ferroelectric switching at coercive voltages of ±2.7 V within a ±10 V bias window (Figures S14d and S15), with two polarization states separated by 180°, indicating the pronounced ferroelectric characteristic of P(VDF‐TrFE). After the cross‐linking, the participation of soft and non‐ferroelectric PEG segments within the P(VDF‐TrFE) matrix leads to a reduction in switching polarization of stretchable P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG under applied electric fields, as shown in Figure S14f,g. Even so, the PFM images and local hysteresis curves demonstrate the reversible switching of ferroelectric domain polarizations within the crystalline P(VDF‐TrFE) regions, with butterfly and phase‐hysteresis curves still representing the ferroelectric behavior while the coercive voltages decreased to ±2.2 V (Figures S14h and S15). The appearance of BTO@APTES NPs in the cross‐linked IS‐PNCs serves as a ferroelectric nanofiller that reinforces overall ferroelectric domain polarization by promoting stronger dipole alignment at their distribution sites within the matrix. Consistently, the PFM amplitude and PFM phase images reveal more pronounced contrast between two oppositely applied electric fields, as shown in Figure S14j–k,n–o, respectively. Additionally, broader hysteresis curves and increased coercive voltages at ±2.4 and ±3.1 V in the IS‐PNCs containing 1 and 2 wt% of BTO@APTES NPs (Figures S14l,p, and S15), respectively, indicate the enhanced ferroelectric polarizations related to stretchable P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG. Therefore, the scanned mapping images of piezoelectric coefficient (d33) with the three different areas were also evaluated from PFM amplitudes at +10 V for comparison of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and IS‐PNCs, as shown in Figure S16. The results also reveal that average d33 values decreased after cross‐linking due to soft and non‐ferroelectric PEG segments within the P(VDF‐TrFE) matrix, and then gradually increased by increasing the BTO@APTES content (Figure S17 and Table S1), confirming the intrinsic piezoelectric influence of BTO@APTES within the IS‐PNCs. The observed increase of the piezoelectric coefficients with the BTO@APTES content increased also revealed a strong intrinsic piezoelectric contribution of BTO@APTES in the matrix.

Furthermore, the ferroelectric behaviors of IS‐PNCs were examined using polarization–electric field (P–E) measurements to clarify the changes in ferroelectric hysteresis corresponding to variations in external applied voltages and frequencies (Figures S18 and S19, respectively). With the domains of ferroelectric crystalline β‐phase present in the matrix, the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG exhibits evident P–E hysteresis, showing progressively broadened P–E curves as the applied voltage increases from ±20 to ±140 V, together with an increase in maximum polarization (P max) to 4.79 µC cm− 2 and remanent polarization (P r) to 3.61 µC cm− 2 at ±140 V and 100 Hz (Figure S18a). In the IS‐PNCs, BTO@APTES NPs, as the intrinsic ferroelectric inorganic fillers, contribute to the increased ferroelectric responses of cross‐linked IS‐PNC films. The IS‐PNC containing 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs achieved the P max of ±140 V and P r values of 5.79 and 4.63 µC cm− 2, respectively. And P r values of the IS‐PNC containing 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs reached 5.95 and 6.36 µC cm− 2 (Figure S18b,c), respectively. Additionally, the influence of frequency (10–200 Hz) on ferroelectric switching was further assessed at a constant voltage of ±120 V (Figure S19). As the frequency decreases, the ferroelectric hysteresis curves widen markedly, representing more prolonged ferroelectric domain switching in the cross‐linked films, which implies slower domain response and improved polarization at lower frequencies. However, the ferroelectric response of the IS‐PNC containing 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs at 200 Hz could not be measured due to the high leakage current (Figure S19c). Notably, the cross‐linked IS‐PNCs evidently exhibited stronger ferroelectric responses compared with P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG, confirming the beneficial role of BTO@APTES NPs in promoting ferroelectric characteristics.

The broadened ferroelectric hysteresis curve is shown in Figure S20, where the IC value of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) returned to zero at 10 Hz under ± 120 V (Figures S20b and S21), confirming its characteristic ferroelectric switching behavior. However, compared with pure P(VDF‐TrFE), the IC values of cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG (Figure 3a,d) and IS‐PNCs containing 1 and 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs (Figure 3b,c,e,f, respectively) failed to return to zero at ±120 V even both at 100 and 10 Hz, and instead increased with higher BTO@APTES NPs loading (Figure S21). This deviation indicates the emergence of leakage current (often described as the dynamic leakage current compensation) originating from several factors [41]. First, the incorporation of flexible and non‐ferroelectric segments of PEG introduces dielectric heterogeneity, promoting conductive pathways for leakage currents. Second, the enlarged amorphous polymer fraction by cross‐linking enhances charge transport, further magnifying leakage current. Additionally, free charges are generated and trapped at BTO@APTES‐polymer interfaces in the nanocomposites due to the interaction between the intrinsic dipoles of the fillers and the surrounding polymer chains under an external electric field [28, 30].

Consequently, the measured ferroelectric hysteresis of the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG IS‐PNCs arises from both the intrinsic ferroelectricity of the β‐phase and the contribution of leakage current within the matrix. Nevertheless, the switching peaks remain dominant over the leakage component, confirming the retention of distinct ferroelectricity in the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG (Figure 3a,d). Similar features are observed in the cross‐linked IS‐PNC containing well‐dispersed 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs, where the increased β‐phase content and incorporation of the intrinsic ferroelectric nanofiller contribute to the pronounced ferroelectric switching at 100 and 10 Hz (Figure 3b,e, respectively). By contrast, agglomeration of BTO@APTES NPs in the cross‐linked IS‐PNC with at a higher fraction of 2 wt% NPs introduces structural defects that reduce formation of the β‐phase and promotes more charge trapping at the heterogeneous interfaces [28, 30]. As a result, a new IC peak associated with the intrinsic dipole of BTO@APTES NPs emerges at 100 and 10 Hz (Figure 3c,f, respectively) and the IC values approach the leakage current level at 10 Hz (Figure 3f), accompanied by disappearance of remanent polarization (Figure 3f), highlighting that the apparent ferroelectric enhancement with the loading of 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs is dominated by interfacial polarization and leakage rather than genuine ferroelectric switching.

To further elucidate these phenomena, the dielectric constant and dielectric loss were also analyzed to investigate the function of BTO@APTES on the polarization behavior of the IS‐PNCs. As shown in Figure S22a,b, the dielectric constant of cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG is significantly higher than that of pristine P(VDF‐TrFE), which is attributed to enhanced interfacial polarization within the matrix. Moreover, the dielectric constant significantly decreases, whereas the dielectric loss increases, with increasing frequency, reflecting dielectric heterogeneity between the ferroelectric P(VDF‐TrFE) phase, and the non‐ferroelectric PEG segments. A similar trend is observed upon incorporation of BTO@APTES, with an even more pronounced effect. In particular, the dielectric loss increases by approximately 2 times at 2 wt% BTO@APTES in the IS‐PNCs. As illustrated in Figure S22c, charge accumulation at the interfaces between P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG and agglomerated BTO@APTES generates more interfacial polarization under an applied electric field. This intensifies more interfacial heterogeneity and charge trapping, thereby increasing dielectric loss and suppressing the apparent switching contribution at high filler loading.

Guided by the increase of β‐phase contents, the observed deformation of ferroelectric hysteresis, and changes of dielectric constant of cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG and IS‐PNCs, the optimized stretchable cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG films and corresponding IS‐PNCs containing 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs were fabricated to assess their ferroelectric response under applied strain prior to piezoelectric evaluation. The schematic structure and optical image of the samples are described in Figure S23a,b, and the samples were mounted on a tensile clamp and stretched incrementally up to 60% strain (Figure S23c). The P–E curves of the fully stretchable samples were measured over an applied voltage range from ±20 to ±120 V as the strain was increased from 0% to 60%. Polarizations progressively broaden with increasing voltage, similar to the behavior observed in rigid devices, and exhibit a gradual enhancement with increasing strain (Figures S24 and S25). This phenomenon can be attributed to strain‐induced dipole rearrangement driven by alignment of polymer chains along the stretch direction, which reduces local field inhomogeneities around cross‑links and interfaces, increases the ferroelectric phase fraction and promotes more favorable dipole orientations along the applied electric field, thereby yielding higher polarization than in the unstretched, less ordered state. Consistently, the P max and P r values at 6.59 and 3.62 µC cm− 2 extracted from the P–E curves for the cross‐linked IS‐PNC with 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs exceed those of the cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG even under strain up to 60% (Figure 3g–k). These investigations indicate that their ferroelectric and associated piezoelectric responses remain stable under applied tensile strains.

Piezoelectric Performance of Cross‐Linked IS‐PNCs

Owing to their intrinsic stretchable ferroelectric properties, IS‐PNC sensors are continuously fabricated based on a sandwich structure of cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG and its nanocomposite (1 wt% of BTO@APTES NPs) layers for comparison, respectively, between patterned stretchable E‐GaIn electrodes on soft PDMS substrates (Figure 4a). Then, their piezoelectric performances were evaluated by measuring the open‐circuit output voltage (V OC) in response to external pressure under different strains. To elucidate the effect of BTO@APTES NP content in P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG, we compared the V OC before and after the poling process. Prior to poling, the V OC value of cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG is higher than that of the cross‐linked IS‐PNC (Figure S26), attributed to effective response to an applied external pressure by the initial dipole orientation of the P(VDF‐TrFE) phase after cross‐linking [29, 42]. Upon poling, dipoles in the matrix reoriented along the direction of the applied electric field, resulting in a notable increase in V OC values and an enhanced piezoelectric response in the cross‐linked IS‐PNC up to 0.91 V compared with 0.77 V of cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG (Figure S26). Consequently, the increased β‐phase content and improved ferroelectricity of the IS‐PNC facilitated more efficient dipole alignment (Figure S27), which plays a crucial role in increasing the piezoelectric responses even under varying applied pressures (Figure S28).

To further assess mechanical deformability and stability toward high‐performance stretchable piezoelectronics, the poled IS‐PNC sensors were stretched incrementally up to 50%, as shown in Figure 4b. Analogous to the stretch‐dependent ferroelectric behavior, both P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG and IS‐PNC with 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs exhibited enhanced piezoelectric responses to a constant pressure of 468.75 kPa under increasing strain conditions from 0% to 50% (Figure 4c,d). This trend suggests that more strain‐induced dipole rearrangement within the matrix strengthens the piezoelectric coupling to external pressure. Under 50% strain, the IS‐PNC generated a V OC of approximately 2 V at the applied pressure, which is higher than that of the pure P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG (Figure 4e). Increased piezoelectric responses were also observed under increased applied external pressures during different strains (Figures 4f and S29). In addition, the cross‐linked IS‐PNC maintained a stable piezoelectric response over 1000 pressure cycles at 40% strain. Notably, the piezoelectric response increased during the initial loading cycles, which can be attributed to the redistribution of interfacial charges, stabilization of contact and deformation, and progressive alignment of internal dipoles. After this initial enhancement, the output remained stable throughout subsequent cycles (Figure 4g). To further assess its durability, real‐time piezoelectric responses were initially monitored under varying strain conditions, revealing a gradual increase in output with increasing strain during cyclic loading (Figure S30). As a result, the cross‐linked IS‐PNC achieved a signal intensity of up to 1.5 V and maintained consistent performance under constant pressure even after 1000 and 2000 stretching cycles at 40% strain compared with before stretching (Figure S31), demonstrating its exceptional mechanical and piezoelectric durability. Comparative analysis with other reported stretchable piezoelectric sensors in Table S2 further highlights the superior performance of the cross‐linked IS‐PNC. These results indicate that strong interfacial interactions between BTO@APTES NPs and the cross‐linked polymer matrix enhance β‐phase formation, preserve mechanical robustness, and improve ferroelectric polarization alignment, collectively enabling the remarkable stretchable piezoelectric response of the IS‐PNC with 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs.

Synaptic Characteristics of AUSSs

Leveraging the piezoelectric responses of IS‐PNCs, an underwater ultrasonic IS‐PNC sensor was integrated with a synaptic transistor to construct an AUSS that mimics dolphin echolocation (Figure 5a). To demonstrate the efficiency of underwater ultrasound‐to‐electrical transduction of the IS‐PNC sensor, the sensors were fabricated on both flexible PI and stretchable PDMS substrates for comparison. The cross‐linked IS‐PNC (1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs) sensors were immersed at the center of a water tank with ultrasonic waves at 40 kHz generated from the bottom. Upon ultrasonic excitation, the IS‐PNC sensor on the PDMS substrate produced the V OC roughly three times larger than that on the PI substrate (Figures 5b–d and S32), indicating more efficient stimulus transmission through the soft and highly deformable PDMS substrate [14].

To probe the synaptic gating behavior, the IS‐PNC sensor on PDMS substrate, serving as the ultrasonic sensing component that converts ultrasonic pulses into piezopotentials, was connected to a synaptic transistor on the PI substrate, as shown in the optical image (Figure S33). These potentials modulate the PSC of the synaptic transistor, as shown in Figure 5e,f. Within the synaptic transistor, ions (EMIM+ and TFSI−) in the ionogel function analogously to neurotransmitters, while the ionogel itself acts as both an ionic buffer and a rectifier, mitigating distortions from opposite‐piezoelectric pulses [43, 44, 45]. The resulting piezopotential is effectively transferred to the synaptic transistor as an equivalent gate bias, driving redistribution of cations (EMIM+) and anion (TFSI−) species at the gate‐ionogel and ionogel‐rGO interfaces from the initial equilibrium state. The ionic polarization generates the hole accumulation and increases the concentration of hole carriers in the p‐type rGO channel, thereby enhancing the PSC as the depolarization of membrane potential in biological receptors [46]. Following the removal of ultrasonic excitation, the ions gradually redistribute to the equilibrium state, decreasing the PSC of the synaptic transistor to the initial current. The dynamic modulation of PSC by ultrasonic pulses exhibits biological synaptic behaviors such as different numbers of pulses, intensities, and duration times, respectively. The corresponding SW, quantifying the strength of the ultrasonic sensory connection, was evaluated through the equation, SW = [I PSC, after—I PSC, initial)/I PSC, initial] × 100, where I PSC, initial and I PSC, after denoting the PSC values before and 15 s after stimulation, respectively (Figure 5g). Therefore, the ∆PSC and SW values of the AUSSs showed through a varied number of pulses (Figure 5h,i), duration times (Figures 5k and S34), and intensities (Figures 5l and S35) under the applied ultrasonic stimulations, revealing increased ∆PSC and SW values of the AUSS with a greater number of pulses, longer durations and higher intensities. When subjected to 30% tensile strain, the piezoelectric sensor exhibited a pronounced enhancement in V OC, reaching approximately 7 V under ultrasonic excitation relative to the unstretched state (Figure S36). This increase reflects strain‐induced polarization amplification arising from mechanical deformation of the cross‐linked IS‐PNC network. Correspondingly, the ∆PSC after removing stimulation and SW values in the ultrasonic sensory synapse were markedly elevated from 0.13 to 0.18 µA and 2.06% to 2.52%, respectively, under 30% stretching of the piezoelectric sensor compared with the unstrained condition (Figure 5m,n), confirming effective transduction of the piezopotential to the synaptic transistor channel. To expand functional applicability, the V OC of the IS‐PNC sensor during 30% stretching scaled with the intensity of ultrasonic stimulation (Figure S37), demonstrating stable piezoelectric functionality during strain. The synaptic transistor also exhibited enhanced ∆PSC and SW values with increasing pulse duration (Figures 5o and S38) and intensity (Figures 5p and S39), underscoring the robust and stretchable nature of the IS‐PNC sensor for dynamic underwater sensing applications.

Conclusion

We introduced IS‐PNC materials to function as an ultrasonic‐sensing component for realizing AUSSs. The IS‐PNC relies on the thermal cross‐linking of P(VDF‐TrFE) with PEG‐diamine, incorporating BTO@APTES NPs that form strong hydrogen‐bonded interfaces within the polymer matrix. These robust interfacial interactions not only promote β‐phase formation in P(VDF‐TrFE) but also preserve the mechanical integrity of the cross‐linked IS‐PNC compared with cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG under deformation. Thorough ferroelectric investigations provided fundamental insight into polarization switching mechanisms of the cross‐linked IS‐PNCs, revealing that leakage currents and interfacial charge trapping under applied electric field strongly affected the overall ferroelectric response. With minimal content of 1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs, the cross‐linked IS‐PNC matrix enhanced the ferroelectric phase of P(VDF‐TrFE), improved the ferroelectric polarization, collectively enabling a markedly improved stretchable piezoelectric response under strain up to 50% compared with cross‐linked P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG. From unique piezoelectricity, the cross‐linked IS‐PNC (1 wt% BTO@APTES NPs) on the soft PDMS substrate was employed as an underwater ultrasonic piezoelectric sensor, demonstrating the ability to emulate the synaptic plasticity of a synaptic transistor in AUSSs even under mechanical strain, recognizing ultrasonic information found in dolphin‐like echolocation. Consequently, intrinsically stretchable piezoelectric materials have great potential for advanced stretchable piezoelectronics into wearable, body‐conformal or implantable sensing applications.

Experiments and Characterizations

Materials

BTO NPs (∼50 nm in diameter), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, ≥ 30 wt % in H2O), ethyl alcohol (C2H5OH, ≥ 99.5%), acetic acid (CH3COOH, ≥ 99%), (3‐aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (APTES, 99%), dextran powders ((C6H10O5)n, from Leuconostoc spp, M r ∼70 000), PEG‐diamine (M n ∼2000, M w/M n ≤ 1.4), cyclohexanone (C6H10( ═O), 99.8%), poly(4‐vinyl phenol) (PVP) powders, propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA; ≥ 99.5%), poly(melamine‐co‐formaldehyde) methylated (PMF; 84 wt% in 1‐butanol), poly(diallydimethylammonium chloride) solution (PDDA; 35 wt% in H2O), graphene oxide solution (GO; 0.4 wt% in H2O), ascorbic acid (AA, ≥ 99%), polyurethane solution (PU; 88 wt% in H2O), and 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([EMIM][TFSI]; ≥ 99%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). P(VDF‐TrFE) copolymer powders (55/45 mol % VDF/TrFE ratio) were supplied by Piezotech (France). Liquid metal (E‐GaIn, 78.6 Ga‐21.4 In) was purchased from Indium Incorporation (USA). Silicone elastomer (PDMS Sylgard‐184) was purchased from 4science (Korea).

Surface Functionalization of BTO NPs

To enrich hydroxyl groups on the surface of BTO NPs, the mixture of BTO NPs and 30 mL of an aqueous solution of H2O2 (30 wt%) was sonicated for 30 min and then stirred at 90°C for 6 h. A white powder was recovered after centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 15 min and washed 2 times with deionized water, dried at 80°C for 12 h in a vacuum oven. It was finally masticated to obtain the hydroxylated BTO NPs.

Continuously, a solution of 30 mL C2H5OH and 3 mL APTES was stirred for 15 min, and pH was adjusted to 8–8.5 using CH3COOH. This mixed solution and hydroxylated BTO NPs were sonicated for 20 min for the improvement of dispersion and then continuously stirred at 80°C for 12 h. After the reaction, the hydroxylated BTO NPs functionalized with APTES (BTO@APTES NPs) were centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 10 min and washed 2 times using C2H5OH. Finally, the BTO@APTES NP powders were dried at 80°C for 12 h in a vacuum oven [28].

Synthesis of IS‐PNCs

Using BTO@APTES as a nanofiller, the IS‐PNCs were synthesized by dispersing in a concentration range of 0, 1, and 2 wt% BTO@APTES NPs of the total nanocomposite in a 3 mL cyclohexanone solvent by sonicating for 20 min. Then, 0.5‐gram P(VDF‐TrFE) powders were dissolved in the BTO@APTES‐dispersed solution by stirring at 60°C for 3 h. Next, 2 mL solution of 0.075‐gram PEG‐diamine powders in cyclohexanone was added drop‐wise slowly to the 3 mL above P(VDF‐TrFE)/BTO@APTES solution and stirred continuously for 2 h. The final solution was drop‐casted onto the Teflon substrate and left naturally for 12 h in a fume hood. After evaporating most of the solvent, the cast film was cured at 50°C for 4 h and cross‐linked at 240°C for 1 h in the vacuum oven. The thickness of the film was approximately 100 ± 3 µm.

Ferroelectric Characteristics of IS‐PNCs

The final solution was prepared similarly to the above process. The solution was dropped onto the Cr–Au/Si wafer (15 × 15 mm2) and spin‐coated at 500 rpm for 5 s and 3000 rpm for 35 s to obtain a uniform film. The film was dried naturally for 12 h in the fume hood, then cured at 50°C for 4 h and cross‐linked at 240°C for 1 h in the vacuum oven. Finally, a film with a thickness of approximately 800 nm was achieved (Figure S40). Using the stencil mask, the liquid metal (E‐GaIn) layer (4 × 4 mm2) as a top electrode was sprayed in 2 s. Therefore, the ferroelectric sample with the active area of 4 × 4 mm2 was formed with the E‐GaIn/IS‐PNC/Cr‐Au/Si wafer structure.

Preparation of the Stretchable Ferroelectric Device for Ferroelectric Responses Under Different Strains

A glass substrate was cleaned with chemical drying etching by O2 gas for 2 min, and a dextran solution (10 wt% in water) was stirred at 90°C for 6 h in a vial with a cap and spin‐coated on the cleaned glass substrate at 1000 rpm for 5 s and 1500 rpm for 25 s. The substrate was then dried on a hot plate at 80°C for 1 min and 180°C for 40 min to fully eliminate the trapped water. Continuously, the P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG nanocomposites solutions were spin‐coated on the top of the dextran layer with the pattern (2 × 2 cm2) at 500 rpm for 5 s and 2000 rpm for 40 s and then dried naturally for 12 h in the fume hood. Next, the sample was cured at 50°C for 4 h and cross‐linked at 240°C for 1 h in the vacuum oven. The thickness of the resultant cross‐linked films was approximately 1.3 µm (Figure S41). After cooling to room temperature, liquid metal (E‐GaIn) (as a bottom electrode) was sprayed on the surface of the cross‐linked nanocomposite film for 2 s to form ribbon electrode patterns (1 × 4 mm2), which was captured by Silicone elastomer (PDMS—silicone elastomer base and curing agent at a weight ratio of 10:1) by spin‐coating at 200 rpm for 5 s and 500 rpm for 40 s. After curing at 80°C for 6 h, the product was immersed in deionized water for 20 h to dissolve the dextran layer and peel off from the glass substrate. Finally, a liquid metal (E‐GaIn) layer (as a top electrode—1 × 4 mm2) was sprayed in the perpendicular direction to the previous E‐GaIn bottom electrode to form an E‐GaIn/cross‐linked nanocomposite film/E‐GaIn/PDMS sandwich structure with each stretchable ferroelectric device being 1 × 1 mm2.

Preparation of IS‐PNC Sensors

As similar with stretchable ferroelectric devices, the P(VDF‐TrFE)‐PEG‐nanocomposites solutions were spin‐coated on the top of the dextran layer on the glass substrate with the pattern (2 × 2 cm2) at 500 rpm for 5 s and 2000 rpm for 40 s and then dried naturally for 12 h in the fume hood. Next, the sample was cured at 50°C for 4 h and cross‐linked at 240°C for 1 h in the vacuum oven. Then, the E‐GaIn (as a bottom electrode) was sprayed on the surface of the cross‐linked nanocomposite film for 2 s to form a serpentine electrode pattern with the sensor area of 8 × 8 mm2, which was also captured by PDMS layer. After curing at 80°C for 6 h, the product was immersed in deionized water for 20 h to dissolve the dextran layer and peel off from the glass substrate. Besides, the E‐GaIn as a top electrode with serpentine electrode pattern was continuously sprayed on another PDMS layer for 2 s with the area of 8 × 8 mm2 and then was attached onto the IS‐PNC film. The sandwich structure of IS‐PNC sensor was poled under 30 V of DC power for 24 h to induce piezoelectricity.

Fabrication of AUSS

First, the synaptic transistor was fabricated on a PI substrate. The PVP solution of 3.04‐gram of PVP powders, 15 mL PGMEA, and 1.2 mL PMF was spin‐coated onto the cleaned PI substrate at 1500 rpm for 30 s and dried at 200°C for 1 h to achieve a planar surface. The dimensions of rGO channel (length of 4 mm and width of 1 mm) in synaptic transistor were applied particularly by a stencil mask onto the PVP layer. PDDA (which performs as a reducing agent and a stabilizer to obtain rGO nanosheets) was dropped on the channel area for 20 min and cleaned by DI water flow for 1 min. Then, the absorbed‐GO layer onto PDDA/PVP layer was reduced to 1 wt% ascorbic acid solution at 85°C for 3 h to obtain the rGO channel. The Cr (5 nm)/Au (60 nm) source, drain, and extended gate electrodes were deposited on the substrate via metal mask by e‐beam and thermal evaporations, respectively. The source and drain electrodes were encapsulated by the PDMS layer, serving to inhibit any leakage current. A layer of ionogel (1 g of [EMIM][TFSI] ionic liquid into 5 mL of ethanol, and 5 mL of PU) was spin‐coated onto both the rGO channel and gate electrode at 1000 rpm for 30 s. To measure the responses to ultrasonic stimulations, the top and bottom electrodes of the optimized IS‐PNC sensor after poling with the opposite direction were connected with gate and source electrodes of the synaptic transistor using a silver paste.

Characterizations

FTIR measurements of NPs, pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and cross‐linked nanocomposite films were performed with a Nicolet iS10 FTIR spectrometer. XRD analysis of NPs, pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and cross‐linked nanocomposite films were performed with AERIS x‐ray diffractometer from Malvern Panalytical Ltd with Cu Kα (1.540598 Å) source. Morphology images of NPs were confirmed by HR‐TEM (JEM ARM 200F). Thermal characteristics of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and cross‐linked nanocomposite films were determined by DSC (DSC25, TA Instruments) in a temperature range of 25°C to 200°C at a heating rate of 10°C min−1 in a nitrogen atmosphere. The FE‐SEM images of the pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and nanocomposites were obtained using a Carl Zeiss SIGMA apparatus at the Chung‐Ang University Center for Research Facilities. The mechanical properties of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and cross‐linked nanocomposite files were designed initial length and width of 40 × 6 mm2 and obtained by using a universal testing machine (QUASAR2.5, Galdabini 1890, Italy) with stretching rate of 4 mm min−1. PFM results of pure P(VDF‐TrFE) and cross‐linked nanocomposite layers were collected by atomic force microscopy (NX10, Park Systems Corporation) using a NSC36‐B cantilever with a frequency of 17 kHz, a scan rate of 0.8 Hz, and a tip‐sample contact force of 1.75 N m−1 under an AC bias of 2 V in lock‐in mode, and PFM amplitude and PFM phase images were characterized by applying consecutive DC biases of ±10 V in the designated areas. PFM hysteresis curves were further measured at the scanned areas corresponding to the PFM amplitude and PFM phase images within a DC bias range of ±10 V. Polarization‐electric field and IC‐electric field curves were measured with a ferroelectric tester (Radiant Precision Premier II) with applied voltages from ±20 to ±140 V in frequency ranges from 10 to 200 Hz. The dielectric constant was measured with an impedance analyzer (Precision LC II) at frequencies from 102 to 105 Hz. The V OC values of the cross‐linked piezoelectric sensors under applied external pressures of a custom‐built pusher system and stimulations of ultrasonic generator (NXPC‐1505, 40 kHz) were measured using a DS1104B oscilloscope (Rigol). The electrical characteristics of the synaptic transistor and AUSS were evaluated using a semiconductor analyzer (Keithley 4200A‐SCS).
