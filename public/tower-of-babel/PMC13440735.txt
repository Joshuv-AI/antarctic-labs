Adolescent Psychosocial Profiles and Early Cyberbullying Histories: Longitudinal Associations and Patterns of Change Across Development
Authors: Taliah Prince, Amanda Boyes, Maddison Crethar, Christina Driver, Lia Mills, Daniel F. Hermens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCyberbullying is highly prevalent during adolescence and is consistently associated with poor mental health outcomes. However, it remains unclear whether these difficulties develop into distinct psychosocial profiles, limiting understanding of how early cyberbullying relates to longitudinal patterns of psychological distress and wellbeing in adolescence. This study aimed to identify profiles of functioning, examine associations with earlier cyberbullying involvement, and characterize developmental patterns across adolescence.MethodsData were drawn from the Australian Longitudinal Adolescent Brain Study (N = 86; 52.3% female, 48% male). Ward's hierarchical cluster analysis identified psychosocial profiles (i.e., clusters) based on psychological distress, body dissatisfaction, social connectedness, psychological wellbeing, and quality of life in mid‐adolescence (time‐points 9–15). Earlier cyberbullying involvement was examined using data from time‐points 1–8 (Mage = 13.8 years), utilizing generalized additive mixed models to characterize psychosocial trajectories according to derived cluster membership.ResultsThree profiles emerged: (i) flourishing, (ii) broad impairment, and (iii) appearance‐specific. Early cyberbullying involvement was associated with membership in the broad impairment cluster but not the appearance‐specific cluster. Longitudinal modeling indicated that the flourishing group showed stable functioning, the broad impairment group showed significant change across domains, and the appearance‐specific group showed change in social connectedness. Eating disorder symptoms were most prevalent in the appearance‐specific profile, present in the broad impairment profile, and absent in the flourishing profile.ConclusionsCyberbullying was associated with widespread psychosocial impairment, whereas body dissatisfaction may reflect a more domain‐specific vulnerability pathway. Differences in developmental trajectories across profiles support person‐centered, longitudinal approaches for targeted prevention.

Introduction

Cyberbullying is highly prevalent during adolescence and has been consistently associated with poorer mental health outcomes, including elevated psychological distress and reduced wellbeing (Bansal et al. 2023; Kowalski et al. 2014; Nixon 2014). However, these associations are not uniform. While some young people experience broad psychosocial impairment, others demonstrate more selective difficulties or maintain relatively adaptive functioning despite exposure (Kumar and Goldstein 2020). From a developmental psychopathology perspective, such heterogeneity reflects processes of multifinality and equifinality, whereby similar exposures may give rise to different outcomes, or similar outcomes may arise from distinct pathways (Gee 2021; Nelson et al. 2020). In adolescence, these processes are typically shaped by the interplay of interpersonal stressors, such as cyberbullying, and broader sociocultural influences, including appearance‐related pressures (Gill and Farnham 2020). Accordingly, person‐centered approaches may provide important insight into the heterogeneity of adolescent functioning by identifying distinct psychosocial profiles that reflect the co‐occurrence of these experiences within individuals over time (Handley et al. 2024). Yet, most existing research has examined these factors in isolation, providing limited insight into how different dimensions of psychosocial functioning co‐occur within individuals across adolescence.

Adolescent psychosocial functioning spans multiple domains of risk and adaptation. Psychological distress, reflecting internalizing symptoms such as depression and anxiety, is associated with impairments in social, academic, and health outcomes (Kim 2021; Wang et al. 2024). Body dissatisfaction represents another key domain, typically intensifying across adolescence and consistently linked to emotional difficulties and broader psychosocial impairment (Merino et al. 2024; Rodgers et al. 2023; Voelker et al. 2015). Importantly, body dissatisfaction is a well‐established precursor to eating psychopathology, with longitudinal evidence linking dissatisfaction to the onset and persistence of eating disorder symptoms (Stice and Shaw 2002). In contrast, wellbeing, quality of life, and social connectedness represent adaptive resources that support positive developmental outcomes and buffer against stress exposure (Adelantado‐Renau et al. 2018; Blum et al. 2022; Driver et al. 2024; Marquez et al. 2023; Xia et al. 2025), including cyberbullying (McLoughlin et al. 2022).

These domains do not operate in isolation but interact to shape broader patterns of adolescent functioning. For example, adolescents may experience elevated distress alongside relatively preserved wellbeing, or low distress without flourishing, consistent with dual‐continua models of mental health (Crisp et al. 2025; Iasiello and Van Agteren 2020; Keyes 2005). Body dissatisfaction frequently co‐occurs with psychological distress, reflecting bidirectional relationships between negative self‐perceptions and emotional difficulties (Al‐Musharaf et al. 2022; Barnes et al. 2020; Merino et al. 2024; Sharpe et al. 2018). However, it may also emerge in the relative absence of broader impairment, indicating a more domain‐specific pattern of vulnerability often described as “normative discontent” (Merino et al. 2024; Voelker et al. 2015). Together, these findings indicate that adolescent vulnerability may manifest as broader psychosocial impairment or more specific difficulties within particular domains.

Variation in psychosocial functioning may also be shaped by contextual and demographic factors, including sex and socioeconomic status. Females typically report higher levels of psychological distress (Campbell et al. 2021; Levenstein et al. 2023; Yoon et al. 2023) and body dissatisfaction (Lacroix et al. 2023; Quittkat et al. 2019), while males consistently exhibit lower social connectedness (Shin and Park 2023; Umberson et al. 2022). Socioeconomic disadvantage has similarly been associated with increased risk for mental health difficulties and reduced wellbeing (Gautam et al. 2023; Salem and Robenson 2025), although its relationship with body dissatisfaction is less consistent. These factors may therefore shape adolescents' vulnerability to interpersonal stressors and contribute to differences in psychosocial functioning across adolescence.

Within this multidimensional context, cyberbullying represents a key interpersonal stressor through which these patterns may emerge. Cyberbullying is defined as intentional and repeated aggression enacted through digital means, differing from traditional forms of bullying in its persistence, anonymity, and lack of temporal or spatial boundaries (Kowalski et al. 2014; Patchin and Hinduja 2011; Tokunaga 2010). Adolescents may be involved as victims, perpetrators, or bully‐victims, with each role linked to poorer adjustment. Cybervictimisation has been consistently associated with elevated psychological distress and internalizing symptoms (Fisher et al. 2016), alongside diminished wellbeing and reduced quality of life (Nixon 2014; Yin et al. 2024), suggesting that cyberbullying may act as a cross‐domain stressor influencing both negative and adaptive aspects of functioning. When appearance is targeted, cyberbullying has been further associated with body dissatisfaction, appearance‐modifying behaviors and disordered eating (Prince et al. 2024). Despite these established associations at the level of individual outcomes, it remains unclear whether cyberbullying is linked to broader patterns of psychosocial functioning across domains, or whether its impacts are more domain specific.

Most existing research has examined psychosocial domains and their predictors at the level of individual variables, limiting understanding of how these factors combine within individuals to form distinct patterns of functioning. In addition, much of the literature is cross‐sectional, providing limited insight into how these patterns develop and change over time (Zhu et al. 2021). Person‐centered approaches, such as cluster analysis, offer a means of identifying subgroups characterized by distinct configurations of functioning (Handley et al. 2024), while longitudinal approaches can provide insight into how these patterns unfold across development (Sullivan et al. 2015). Integrating these approaches may therefore offer a more comprehensive understanding of both the composition and developmental course of adolescent psychosocial functioning. Despite this, few studies have integrated both positive and negative domains of functioning, including body‐related constructs, while linking these patterns to earlier cyberbullying exposure within a longitudinal framework. As a result, it remains unclear how these factors jointly shape developmental patterns of adolescent adjustment.

The present study addresses this gap by integrating these domains within a longitudinal, person‐centered framework and linking them to earlier cyberbullying exposure. Psychosocial profiles were first derived using cluster analysis of self‐reported psychological distress, wellbeing, quality of life, social connectedness, and body dissatisfaction. Associations between cluster membership and earlier cyberbullying were then examined, alongside eating disorder symptoms, sex, and SES, with eating disorder symptoms conceptualized as a related outcome rather than a clustering variable. Finally, longitudinal modeling was used to characterize developmental trajectories across early adolescence, to determine whether profiles differed in their patterns of change over time.

It was anticipated that adolescents would group into distinct profiles reflecting variation across these domains, with some profiles characterized by elevated psychological distress and body dissatisfaction alongside lower wellbeing, quality of life, and social connectedness (i.e., more severe profiles), and others reflecting more adaptive functioning across these domains (Keyes 2005; Iasiello and Van Agteren 2020). Adolescents with earlier cyberbullying involvement, particularly bully‐victims, were expected to be disproportionately represented in more severe profiles, in line with evidence linking cyberbullying to elevated psychological distress and reduced wellbeing (Nixon 2014; Fisher et al. 2016). Eating disorder symptoms were expected to be most prevalent in profiles characterized by elevated body dissatisfaction, consistent with longitudinal findings identifying body dissatisfaction as a precursor to eating pathology (Merino et al. 2024; Rodgers et al. 2023). Females and adolescents from lower socioeconomic status backgrounds were also expected to be overrepresented in more severe profiles, consistent with established disparities in adolescent mental health (Campbell et al. 2021; Salem and Robenson 2025). Finally, profiles were expected to differ in their developmental trajectories across early adolescence, with more severe profiles showing greater change over time, indicative of the cumulative and reinforcing nature of psychosocial difficulties across adolescence.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Data were drawn from the Australian Longitudinal Adolescent Brain Study (LABS), an ongoing prospective cohort study of adolescent neurodevelopment and mental health (Beaudequin et al. 2020). Data collection occurred between 2018 and 2025, with participants assessed approximately every 4 months across up to 15 time‐points, commencing at around age 12.

Ethics approval was obtained from the University of the Sunshine Coast Human Research Ethics Committee (A181064). Written informed consent was obtained by both parents/caregivers and participants at study entry and re‐confirmed annually.

Participants

Participants were drawn from an eligible cohort of N = 179 and were included in the current analysis if they had valid self‐report data from at least two assessments during early adolescence (time‐points 1–8) and at least two assessments during mid‐adolescence (time‐points 9–15). Exclusion criteria included missing psychosocial data during time‐points 9–15, or the absence of cyberbullying data during time‐points 1–8.

The final analytic sample comprised N = 86 adolescents (52.3% female). The mean age was 13.8 years (SD = 0.82) during time‐points 1–8 and 16.10 years (SD = 0.56) during time‐points 9–15. Across time‐points 1–8, age increased from 12.64 to 14.96 years (see Table 1). The sample was predominantly from higher socioeconomic status backgrounds, with 80.2% in quintile 4 and 11.6% in quintile 5; smaller proportions were in quintile 1 (3.5%) and quintile 2 (4.7%), with no participants in quintile 3.

Procedure

LABS participants were assessed in person approximately every 4 months over a 5‐year period (up to 15 time‐points). At each wave, participants completed a battery of self‐report measures assessing psychological, social, and behavioral functioning. All measures were administered electronically during in‐person assessment sessions under researcher supervision. Full details of the LABS study design, recruitment, and inclusion and exclusion criteria are described elsewhere (Beaudequin et al. 2020). The present study utilized existing data from the LABS cohort, with analyses conducted using available data within the specified time‐point ranges.

Measures

Psychosocial functioning was assessed across multiple domains, including cyberbullying involvement, body dissatisfaction, psychological distress, wellbeing, quality of life, social connectedness, and eating disorder symptoms as measured by the following scales.

Cyberbullying: Victimization and Perpetration

The Berlin Cyberbullying‐Cybervictimisation Questionnaire (BCyQ; Schultze‐Krumbholz and Scheithauer 2009) assessed cyberbullying experience over the past 6 months. The scale includes 18 perpetration items (range 18–90) and 17 victimization items (range 17–85), rated on a 5‐point scale (1 = never, 5 = several times a week). Participants with scores > 17 on victimization and ≤ 18 on perpetration were classified as victim‐only; those with > 17 and > 18 (exceeding thresholds on both subscales) were classified as bully‐victims; and those at or below thresholds on both subscales were classified as no involvement. Two adolescents met criteria for bully‐only (perpetration > 18, victimization ≤ 17); these were analyzed with bully‐victims due to low frequency. Internal consistency was high for both victimization (α = 0.90) and perpetration (α = 0.80) subscales.

Body Dissatisfaction

The Body Shape Questionnaire 8‐item subscale (BSQ‐8C; Welch et al. 2012) was used to assess body dissatisfaction. The BSQ‐8 comprises 8 items that assess factors such as preoccupation with weight, dissatisfaction with specific body parts, and the desire to change one's body shape over the past month on a 6‐point Likert scale (1 = never, 6 = always). Total scores for the BSQ‐8 ranged from 8 to 48, with higher scores indicating higher levels of body dissatisfaction. This scale was shown to have high internal consistency in this study (α = 0.95) and in past research with adolescent samples (Prince et al. 2024).

Wellbeing

The COMPAS‐W is a 26‐item mental wellbeing scale measuring both eudemonic or psychological wellbeing (own‐worth, mastery and achievement subscales), and hedonic or subjective wellbeing (composure, positivity and satisfaction subscales; Gatt et al. 2014). Participants rate each item on a 5‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), yielding a total wellbeing score ranging from 26 to 130, with higher scores indicating greater wellbeing. The COMPAS‐W has been shown to have good internal consistency in adolescent samples (Boyes et al. 2025; Driver et al. 2024) and in the present study (α = 0.90).

Psychological Distress

Psychological distress was assessed using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10; Kessler et al. 2003), a widely used 10‐item self‐report questionnaire designed to measure non‐specific psychological distress over the past 30 days. Participants rated each item on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = none of the time to 5 = all of the time), with total scores ranging from 10 to 50. Higher scores indicate greater psychological distress. The K10 has been widely used in population‐based mental health research (Iorfino et al. 2017) and is considered a reliable and valid measure of psychological distress in adolescents aged 12–19 (Chan et al. 2014). In the present study, the K10 demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.91).

Quality of Life

Quality of life was measured using the World Health Organization Quality of Life questionnaire (WHOQOL‐BREF; World Health Organization Quality of Life Group 1993). The WHOQOL‐BREF is a 26‐item instrument that measures overall perceptions of quality of life and health, including: physical health (e.g., energy levels, fatigue, pain, sleep), psychological health (e.g., self‐esteem, bodily acceptance, emotions), social relationships (e.g., personal relationships, social support), and environment (e.g., financial resources, safety, access to healthcare). Participants rated each item on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 5 = extremely). This measure has been validated across diverse adolescent populations (Shawver et al. 2016) and showed good internal consistency (α = 0.94) in this study.

Social Connectedness

The 15‐item Social Connectedness Scale (SCS) measures the extent to which young people feel connected to their peers, family, and broader community. Originally developed with college students (Lee et al. 2008; Lee et al. 2001), the SCS has been successfully applied to adolescents aged 12–17 in various studies, including those examining cyberbullying and well‐being in online contexts (Grieve et al. 2013; McLoughlin et al. 2022). Total scores range from 15 to 90, with higher scores indicating stronger social connectedness. Internal consistency in the current sample was excellent (α = 0.94).

Eating Disorder Symptoms

Presence of eating disorder symptoms was assessed using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents (MINI‐KID; Sheehan et al. 1998). The interview covers DSM‐IV/5 ED categories (e.g., anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge‐eating disorder, and other specified feeding or eating disorders). Participants were coded dichotomously as 1 (presence of lifetime eating disorder symptoms) or 0 (no eating disorder symptoms) based on endorsement at any assessment wave (time‐points 1–15). The MINI‐KID has demonstrated good reliability and validity for diagnosing psychiatric disorders in adolescents (Sheehan et al. 2010).

Socioeconomic Status

Socioeconomic status was indexed using the Postal Area Index of Relative Socio‐economic Advantage and Disadvantage (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2021), which ranks postal areas from most disadvantaged (quintile 1) to most advantaged (quintile 5).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Analyses proceeded in three stages: (1) derivation of psychosocial profiles via cluster analysis; (2) examination of demographic, clinical, and cyberbullying characteristics according to cluster membership; and (3) modeling of developmental trajectories across early adolescence.

Psychosocial variables (psychological distress, body dissatisfaction, social connectedness, psychological wellbeing and quality of life) were aggregated across time‐points 9–15 to derive mean scores for clustering. Mean scores across time‐points 9–15 were used to represent mid‐adolescent functioning, accommodating variability in the number of completed assessments. All outcomes were standardized (z‐scores) prior to analysis, with variables reflecting risk (body dissatisfaction and psychological distress) inverted so that higher scores indicated more adaptive functioning. Outliers exceeding ± 3 SD were winsorised (≤ 3.8% of values per variable). All clustering variables were composite scale scores and were treated as continuous, consistent with established practice in psychological research (Norman 2010). Hierarchical cluster analysis using Ward's method (squared Euclidean distance) was conducted to identify psychosocial profiles. Determination of cluster number was informed by inspection of agglomeration coefficients, dendrogram structure, scree plot examination, and interpretability of the resulting profiles. Cluster stability was assessed by replicating the solution using k‐means clustering and examining consistency of cluster membership across methods. Between cluster differences were examined using Analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey post hoc tests and Cohen's d calculated to quantify pairwise effect sizes. A Discriminant Function Analysis (DFA) assessed classification accuracy and variable contributions, with cross‐validation performed using the leave‐one‐out method to further support the robustness of the cluster solution. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene's tests, with Welch's ANOVA conducted where assumptions were violated. Equality of covariance matrices for DFA was assessed using Box's M test.

Associations between cluster membership and demographic (age, sex, socioeconomic status) and clinical variables (e.g., eating disorder symptoms) were examined descriptively using cross‐tabulations (χ2 or Fisher's exact tests) and were not included as covariates in risk ratio analyses. Associations with earlier cyberbullying involvement were restricted to time‐points 1–8 to maintain temporal separation from clustering variables. Cyberbullying exposure was examined both dichotomously (any vs. none) and by role (no involvement, victim‐only, bully‐victim), with bully‐only cases (n = 2) collapsed into the bully‐victim category due to insufficient frequency for independent analysis, as very small cell sizes can produce unstable estimates and limit interpretability. Risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated from cross‐tabulations where appropriate.

Developmental trajectories across early adolescence (time‐points 1–8) were examined using generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) implemented in R (mgcv package). Separate models were fitted for each outcome (cybervictimisation, cyberbullying perpetration, psychological distress, social connectedness, quality of life, and psychological wellbeing). Body dissatisfaction was excluded from trajectory analyses as the measure was introduced later in the LABS protocol, resulting in substantial missingness across early assessment waves (time‐points 1–8; 46/483 observations available, 9.5%). Models included cluster as a fixed effect and time‐point modeled using cluster‐specific smooth terms (s(tp, by = cluster, k = 4, bs = “cr”)), estimated via restricted maximum likelihood (REML). A random intercept for participant (s(id, bs = “re”)) accounted for repeated observations. Predicted marginal values and 95% confidence intervals (excluding random effects) were extracted and plotted alongside observed cluster means (± 1 SE).

Results

Across the sample (N = 86), participant age increased from 12.64 years at time‐points 1 to 14.96 years at time‐points 8, reflecting progression across early adolescence (see Table 1 for breakdown of age at each time‐point). Psychosocial profiles were derived using aggregated psychosocial functioning data collected during mid‐adolescence (time‐points 9–15), during which participants had a mean age of 16.10 years (SD = 0.56; range = 14.80–17.80 years).

Cluster Characteristics

Hierarchical cluster analysis of psychosocial functioning during time‐points 9–15 supported a three‐cluster solution based on inspection of the agglomeration schedule, scree plot, dendrogram, and interpretability of the cluster structure. Visual inspection indicated a clear separation at the three‐cluster solution (see Supporting Figures S1 and 2). Replication using k‐means clustering produced a highly similar pattern of cluster membership and profile characteristics across methods, supporting solution stability. The final solution comprised three subgroups: flourishing (n = 49), broad impairment (n = 18), and appearance‐specific (n = 19). These three clusters differed significantly across all psychosocial variables (all p < 0.001), as presented in Table 2. Levene's tests indicated unequal variances for psychological distress and body dissatisfaction; however, Welch's ANOVAs produced the same pattern of significant results (all p < 0.001).

The flourishing cluster was characterized by high psychological wellbeing, social connectedness, and quality of life, together with lower psychological distress and body dissatisfaction. In contrast, the broad impairment cluster showed the poorest functioning across domains, with lower wellbeing, social connectedness and quality of life, alongside elevated psychological distress and body dissatisfaction. The appearance‐specific cluster was characterized by elevated body dissatisfaction alongside relatively average functioning across other psychosocial domains.

Pairwise comparisons indicated large differences between the flourishing and broad impairment clusters across all variables (d ≥ 0.80). Comparisons involving the appearance‐specific cluster were more selective. Relative to the flourishing cluster, the appearance‐specific cluster showed a large difference in psychological distress (d = 1.48) and body dissatisfaction (d = 4.13), moderate differences in quality of life (d = 0.57) and psychological wellbeing (d = 0.55), and little difference in social connectedness (d = 0.12). In contrast, the broad impairment and appearance‐specific clusters differed substantially across all domains (ds ≥ 0.90).

Discriminant Function Analysis

Discriminant function analysis (DFA) supported the three‐cluster solution. The first function accounted for 70.6% of the variance (Wilks' λ = 0.118, χ2(10) = 173.14, p < 0.001) and, according to structure matrix loadings ≥ 0.40, reflected a general psychosocial dimension defined by psychological distress, quality of life, social connectedness, and psychological wellbeing. The second function accounted for additional variance (29.4%; Wilks' λ = 0.456, χ2(4) = 63.61, p < 0.001) and was defined primarily by body dissatisfaction. Classification accuracy was high (97.7% of original cases; 95.3% cross‐validated), supporting stability of the cluster solution. Although Box's M test was significant, indicating unequal covariance matrices, the high cross‐validated classification accuracy supported the robustness of the DFA solution.

Associations with Demographics and Eating Disorder Symptoms

Chi‐square analyses indicated no significant associations between cluster membership and baseline sex or socioeconomic status (both p > 0.05), although socioeconomic status results were interpreted with caution due to small, expected counts in several categories. In contrast, lifetime eating disorder symptoms were significantly associated with cluster membership (p = 0.006), with high prevalence observed in the appearance‐specific and broad impairment clusters, and absent in the flourishing cluster (Table 3).

Cyberbullying and Cluster Membership

Early cyberbullying involvement (i.e., during early adolescence; time‐points 1–8) was significantly associated with cluster membership (in mid‐adolescence). All participants in the broad impairment group reported some prior exposure to cyberbullying, precluding logistic regression analyses; Fisher's exact tests confirmed significant associations for exposure (p = 0.005) and role (exact p = 0.001). No significant associations were observed for the appearance‐specific cluster (p > 0.80), indicating that this association was driven primarily by the broad impairment cluster (Table 4).

Longitudinal Trajectories of Psychosocial Functioning

GAMM‐estimated trajectories indicated distinct patterns of change across early adolescence (time‐points 1–8) for the three clusters (Figure 1). The broad impairment group showed consistently poorer outcomes over time, the flourishing group remained stable and adaptive, and the appearance‐specific group showed more selective differences across domains. The flourishing group demonstrated stable trajectories across all outcomes (all p > 0.27), indicating consistently high functioning across early adolescence. In contrast, the broad impairment group showed significant change over time for cyberbullying perpetration (p = 0.014), psychological distress (p = 0.049), social connectedness (p < 0.001), quality of life (p < 0.001), and psychological wellbeing (p < 0.001), with a marginal effect for cybervictimisation (p = 0.050). Modeled trajectories indicated overall declines in functioning across these domains, with this group remaining below the other two clusters throughout time‐points 1–8.

Discussion

Adolescence is a developmental period when peer evaluation and appearance pressures intensify, increasing vulnerability to both online and offline social stressors (Sisk and Gee 2022). While cyberbullying has been repeatedly linked to poorer mental health outcomes (Kowalski et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2021), less is known about how these difficulties cluster across domains of functioning and relate to earlier cyberbullying histories. Using a person‐centered, data‐driven approach, the present study identified three psychosocial profiles (i.e., clusters) – flourishing, broad impairment, and appearance‐specific – and demonstrated that these profiles differed in both their composition and developmental trajectories across early adolescence. Consistent with the study aims and hypotheses, profile membership was also associated with differing histories of cyberbullying involvement. These findings are discussed below in relation to profile composition, cyberbullying involvement, and developmental trajectories.

Psychosocial Profiles of Mid‐Adolescent Levels of Functioning

The flourishing profile was characterized by high wellbeing, social connectedness, and quality of life alongside low psychological distress and body dissatisfaction in mid‐adolescence, reflecting a pattern of positive mental health across domains. In this subgroup, eating disorder symptoms were rare, consistent with evidence highlighting the protective roles of wellbeing and social connectedness (Blum et al. 2022; Marquez et al. 2023). These findings align with prior person‐centered research identifying resilient subgroups in adolescence (Hammond et al. 2022; Schmidt et al. 2022). Emerging longitudinal evidence also suggests that wellbeing and distress may be partially independent dimensions (Boyes and Hermens 2026). The flourishing profile may therefore represent a pathway in which multiple protective factors co‐occur and operate synergistically to buffer against stress exposure and support positive developmental trajectories.

In contrast, the broad impairment profile was characterized by lower wellbeing, social connectedness, and quality of life alongside higher psychological distress and body dissatisfaction in mid‐adolescence, reflecting a pattern of widespread psychosocial difficulty. Although eating disorder symptoms were present, they did not dominate this profile, suggesting generalized, rather than domain‐specific, risk. This pattern is consistent with transdiagnostic models of psychopathology (Lahey et al. 2017; Nolen‐Hoeksema and Watkins 2011; Rogers and Zvolensky 2021), and developmental cascade frameworks, in which difficulties in one domain contribute to impairments across others over time (Butter et al. 2024; Mahon et al. 2022; Masten and Cicchetti 2010).

The appearance‐specific profile captured a distinct pattern characterized by elevated body dissatisfaction alongside relatively typical functioning across most psychosocial domains, in mid‐adolescence. Eating disorder symptoms were most prevalent in this group, consistent with extensive evidence linking body dissatisfaction to eating pathology (Bazo Perez and Frazier 2024; Stice and Shaw 2002). Although body dissatisfaction is often described as a near‐normative experience during adolescence (Merino et al. 2024; Voelker et al. 2015), these findings point to a more domain‐specific vulnerability pattern, without the widespread psychosocial difficulties observed in the broad impairment profile. While sex did not significantly predict cluster membership, females were descriptively overrepresented in this group, aligning with literature on gendered exposure to appearance pressures and self‐objectification (Tiggemann and Slater 2015). These pressures are reinforced by both digital and offline environments, including social media comparison and peer or family commentary (Fardouly and Vartanian 2016; Rodgers et al. 2015). Beyond the differences in psychosocial profile composition in mid‐adolescence, findings also revealed distinct associations with cyberbullying exposure and developmental trajectories among these three subgroups during early adolescence, which are discussed below.

Cyberbullying Trajectories According to Identified Profiles

Consistent with our hypotheses, early cyberbullying involvement was most pronounced in the ‘broad impairment' profile subgroup, with bully‐victims being overrepresented. This aligns with evidence indicating that bully‐victims experience particularly severe adjustment difficulties relative to victims or perpetrators alone (Balan et al. 2023; Fabris et al. 2022; Wolke and Lereya 2015). These difficulties may be amplified during early adolescence, when sensitivity to peer evaluation and social stressors is heightened (Sisk and Gee 2022; Somerville 2013). Theoretically, this profile is also consistent with models emphasizing rejection sensitivity and difficulties in emotion regulation, processes that are amplified within digitally mediated social environments (Ahmed et al. 2015; Blakemore and Mills 2014; Prince et al. 2025). Longitudinal findings further indicated that this profile was characterized by worsening trajectories across multiple psychosocial domains over early adolescence, consistent with developmental cascade models in which early stress exposure contributes to escalating difficulties over time (Masten and Cicchetti 2010). Although causal inference is not possible, these findings align with systematic reviews and meta‐analyses linking cyberbullying to broad psychosocial difficulties (Bottino et al. 2015; Li et al. 2024), while also supporting emerging conceptualizations of cyberbullying as a form of adverse developmental stress (Hinduja and Patchin 2025).

In contrast, the appearance‐specific profile was not differentiated by cyberbullying histories, suggesting that body dissatisfaction may arise primarily from broader sociocultural pressures rather than direct exposure to online victimization. This interpretation aligns with models emphasizing the cumulative influence of media, peer, and cultural factors on body image (Rodgers et al. 2023). Accordingly, sociocultural appearance pressures may represent one potential mechanism underlying the appearance‐specific profile. Given the modest sample size and incomplete early‐wave body image data, further research is needed to clarify whether cyberbullying, particularly appearance‐related cyberbullying, may contribute to or exacerbate appearance‐related concerns under specific conditions. This may be especially plausible given that appearance‐related cyberbullying directly targets physical appearance and social self‐presentation (Prince et al. 2024), potentially reinforcing body dissatisfaction even in adolescents who otherwise demonstrate relatively intact psychosocial functioning. Finally, the flourishing profile showed minimal cyberbullying involvement and was characterized by consistently higher wellbeing and social connectedness across both early and mid‐adolescence, suggesting that these factors may have protective roles in buffering against exposure to digital harms (Blum et al. 2022; Marquez et al. 2023).

Implications

The current findings highlight the importance of moving beyond single‐outcome conceptualizations of adolescent adjustment toward multidimensional models that capture co‐occurring patterns of wellbeing, distress, and body‐related vulnerability. The identification of distinct psychosocial profiles suggests that cyberbullying may operate within broader configurations of developmental vulnerability and protection. These findings support stratified prevention frameworks, whereby adolescents experiencing broad psychosocial impairment may benefit from integrated, multi‐domain supports, while those showing more circumscribed appearance‐related difficulties may require targeted body image interventions (Das et al. 2016) – for example, the Body Project, Media Smart, or Butterfly Body Bright – alongside universal approaches that strengthen protective factors.

Methodologically, the findings demonstrate the value of integrating person‐centered and longitudinal approaches to capture heterogeneity in adolescent development (Butter et al. 2024; Laursen and Hoff 2006).

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

Strengths of this study include the use of a longitudinal cohort with repeated assessments across adolescence, enabling the characterization of psychosocial functioning over a sustained developmental period and supporting the identification of robust, person‐centered profiles. The temporal depth of the data reduces the likelihood that identified profiles reflect transient fluctuations. In addition, the use of a cross‐validated clustering approach enhances the stability and reproducibility of the profiles and is consistent with best practice in person‐centered research (Laursen and Hoff 2006). The integration of generalized additive mixed models represents a further strength, as trajectories were estimated using participant‐level data while accounting for within‐person dependence, providing a rigorous assessment of differences in patterns of change across profiles (Wirtz and Pfenninger 2025).

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. The modest sample size reduced power for subgroup comparisons, and small cell counts in cyberbullying x cluster analyses limited precision. These factors may also affect the stability and replicability of the identified cluster solutions, particularly the appearance‐specific profile, which should therefore be interpreted cautiously pending replication in larger samples. In addition, collapsing bully‐only cases with bully‐victims may have reduced specificity in distinguishing perpetration‐only involvement from combined perpetration‐victimization experiences. Additionally, reliance on self‐report measures introduces possible shared‐method, social desirability, and recall biases (Rosenman et al. 2011), which may have influenced reported cyberbullying experiences and their associations with psychosocial profile membership. Profiles were derived from averaged scores across mid‐adolescence (time‐points 9–15) and therefore reflect relatively stable patterns of functioning, rather than within‐person change at the individual level, potentially obscuring intra‐individual variability over this period (Boyes et al. 2025). Although longitudinal modeling was incorporated, evidence of time‐varying change was not observed consistently across all clusters or outcomes, and the study design does not permit inference regarding the directionality or causal nature of associations between cyberbullying and psychosocial functioning. Finally, incomplete body dissatisfaction data at earlier waves precluded trajectory modeling, which limited the ability to examine developmental changes in body dissatisfaction and may have reduced insight into the emergence of the appearance‐specific profile over time.

Future research should seek to replicate the identified profiles in larger and more diverse cohorts to enhance generalizability and statistical power. Extending longitudinal approaches will also be important for characterizing developmental processes, particularly through methods capable of modeling individual‐level trajectories and transitions between profiles over time, such as growth mixture modeling or latent transition analysis. Notably, profiles were distinguished by multiple psychosocial domains, suggesting that their combined influence may be more informative than any single factor alone. Evidence of pluripotentiality in adolescent psychosocial functioning supports this possibility (Kennedy et al. 2025), highlighting the need to better understand the mechanisms underpinning distinct developmental pathways. Further investigation of the appearance‐specific profile is warranted to determine whether it reflects a relatively stable, domain‐specific pattern of functioning or a precursor to broader psychosocial impairment. Future studies should additionally distinguish perpetration‐only and bully‐victim experiences and examine these profiles across diverse cultural contexts, as variation in cyberbullying exposure, appearance‐related pressures, and social connectedness may influence their emergence and developmental course (Demaria et al. 2024; Mahatmya et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2021).

Conclusion

This study identified three distinct psychosocial profiles of adolescent functioning – flourishing, broad impairment, and appearance‐specific – characterized by differences in wellbeing, psychological distress, social connectedness, quality of life, and body dissatisfaction, and further demonstrated that these profiles were differentiated by distinct developmental patterns across early adolescence. Cyberbullying involvement was not uniformly associated with these profiles, being most strongly linked to the broad impairment group, and was more strongly aligned with wider, transdiagnostic difficulties in some adolescents. In contrast, the appearance‐specific profile was characterized by elevated body dissatisfaction and the highest prevalence of eating disorder symptoms, consistent with a more domain‐specific vulnerability. Quality of life and social connectedness emerged as key differentiating features across profiles, pointing to potential leverage points for prevention and intervention. Overall, these findings support the use of person‐centered, developmentally informed approaches to better capture the heterogeneity of adolescent adjustment and to inform more targeted, stratified responses to digital stressors.
