Power Within: a meta-analysis of the roles of self-efficacy in type 2 diabetes self-care and glycaemic control
Authors: Kenni Wojujutari Ajele, Erhabor Sunday Idemudia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-efficacy is a critical psychological factor in managing type 2 diabetes (T2D), yet findings across populations and measurement tools remain inconsistent. This meta-analysis investigates the associations between self-efficacy, self-care behaviours and glycaemic control (HbA1c), accounting for diabetes duration, geographic region and the type of self-efficacy scale used. A systematic search of six databases from 2007 to 2024 identified studies examining self-efficacy in relation to self-care and HbA1c in adults with T2D. Random effects models were used to pool data from 62 studies (n=19 231). Subgroup analyses explored differences by diabetes duration, global region, and measurement tool. Heterogeneity was assessed using I² and τ² statistics. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, Egger’s test and the trim-and-fill method. Self-efficacy was significantly associated with better self-care behaviour (r=0.44; 95% CI 0.36 to 0.51, p<0.001) and lower HbA1c (r=–0.27; 95% CI –0.37 to –0.17, p<0.001). Diabetes-specific self-efficacy measures yielded the strongest correlation with self-care (r=0.50; 95% CI 0.40 to 0.59). Stronger associations were observed in participants with shorter diabetes duration (r=0.51) and in low-income countries in East Asia (HbA1c: r=–0.34; 95% CI –0.49 to –0.17). Egger’s test showed no significant publication bias (p=0.21). Sensitivity and subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of findings despite high heterogeneity. Self-efficacy is consistently linked to improved self-care and HbA1c in T2D. Targeted interventions that enhance diabetes-specific self-efficacy, particularly early in the disease and in low-resource settings, may significantly improve diabetes outcomes globally.PROSPERO registration number CRD42024595997.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic condition affecting over 537 million people globally,1 with projections estimating this number will rise to 783 million by 2045.1 2 According to the WHO, over 530 million people were living with diabetes in 2022.3 More recent estimates from the International Diabetes Federation place this number at 589 million adults globally in 2024, with projections reaching 853 million by 2050.4 The disease is characterised by persistent hyperglycaemia caused by defects in insulin secretion or action and is a leading contributor to morbidity and mortality worldwide.57 Poor glycaemic control (HbA1c) is associated with complications such as cardiovascular diseases, retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy, which significantly burden individuals and healthcare systems.810 Effective diabetes management relies on adherence to self-care practices, including medication use, dietary regulation, physical activity and regular blood glucose monitoring.1114 However, psychological and behavioural barriers often hinder these efforts, resulting in suboptimal outcomes.

Self-efficacy, first conceptualised as refers to an individual’s confidence in performing goal-directed behaviours under challenging conditions,15 has emerged as a critical psychological resource in diabetes management. In the context of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), self-efficacy underpins effective self-care behaviours that influence HbA1c and reduce complications.16 Higher self-efficacy is strongly associated with better adherence to self-care behaviours, improved blood glucose monitoring and lower HbA1c levels.17 18 This study explores these dynamics through the ‘Power Within’ framework, which integrates personal empowerment with contextual enablers such as resilience and autonomy to enhance diabetes management outcomes.

Despite its importance, the role of self-efficacy in diabetes self-care and HbA1c is not consistent across populations and contexts.1923 Factors such as diabetes duration, geographic region, socioeconomic disparities and the specificity of self-efficacy measurement tools contribute to this variability.111324 For instance, general self-efficacy scales often fail to capture diabetes-specific challenges, potentially underestimating its influence on key outcomes.12 14 14 19 25

Several cross-sectional and interventional studies have shown that higher diabetes self-efficacy is associated with improved adherence to diet, physical activity and medication, which in turn supports better HbA1c.19 26 Meta-analytic evidence further suggests that behavioural and educational interventions targeting self-efficacy can lead to meaningful improvements in diabetes outcomes.2729 However, inconsistencies in outcomes are common due to differences in study design, measurement tools and population characteristics.

This meta-analysis builds on existing research by systematically examining the relationships between self-efficacy, self-care behaviours and HbA1c in individuals with T2D.19 26 30 Unlike prior studies,27 31 this work focuses on the influence of diabetes duration, geographic disparities and measurement tools to provide a comprehensive understanding of these factors. The findings aim to inform tailored interventions and standardise measurement strategies, ultimately contributing to improved diabetes management outcomes globally.

Methods

Design

We examined the relationships between self-efficacy, self-care behaviours and HbA1c in individuals with T2D. The protocol was preregistered on PROSPERO (CRD42024595997) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.32 This meta-analysis focused on variations in diabetes duration, geographic region and self-efficacy measurement tools.

Search strategy

A systematic search of Google Scholar, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, EBSCOhost and PubMed was conducted for studies published between 2007 and September 2024. Search terms combined keywords and phrases related to self-efficacy, diabetes management, self-care behaviours and glycaemic control. Boolean operators were used to construct the search string: (“Self-Efficacy” OR “Diabetes Self-Efficacy”) AND (“Self-Care” OR “Diabetes Management”) OR (“Glycaemic Control” OR “HbA1c”) AND (“Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus” OR “T2D”) (see Online supplemental appendix 1 full search strategy). The reference lists of the included studies were screened for additional relevant articles. Two independent reviewers screened the studies for eligibility and resolved the conflicts through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) involved adults diagnosed with T2D; (2) investigated relationships between self-efficacy, self-care behaviours and/or HbA1c; (3) used validated measurement tools for self-efficacy and reported quantitative data suitable for meta-analysis, such as correlation coefficients or effect sizes and (4) were published in peer-reviewed journals in English. Studies were excluded if they: (1) focused on non-T2D populations, such as paediatric patients or individuals with gestational diabetes; (2) employed qualitative methods or lacked quantitative statistical analyses and (3) did not provide adequate data for calculating effect sizes.

Data extraction

The Covidence software was independently used by two reviewers (KWA and ESI) to extract data for systematic management. Microsoft Excel was then used for additional data processing and organisation. Key study characteristics included author names, publication year, sample size, geographic location and participant demographics (eg, age, gender, diabetes duration). Data specific to the study objectives, such as self-efficacy measurement tools, self-care behaviours and HbA1c, were recorded. Correlation coefficients, effect sizes and CIs were extracted for the meta-analysis. Subgroup-specific data (eg, by diabetes duration or region) were documented to support further analyses. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus, ensuring accuracy and reliability.

Risk of bias assessment

The ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies–of Interventions) tool was employed to assess potential bias in the included studies.33 Seven domains were evaluated: confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement and reported results. The domains were rated as low, moderate, severe or critical risk of bias (online supplemental figure S1). Studies with high or critical risk of bias were excluded from the sensitivity analyses. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion, and results were summarised using publication-quality visualisations.

Statistical analysis

Effect sizes were calculated using a random-effects model with the restricted maximum likelihood estimator to account for heterogeneity across diverse study settings. Correlation coefficients (r) were transformed using Fisher’s z transformation to stabilise variance prior to aggregation. Summary effect sizes and their 95% CIs were computed using inverse-variance weighting. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q statistic, I² and between-study variance (τ²). The Q statistic tested for significant heterogeneity, I² quantified the proportion of variation due to heterogeneity, and τ² estimated the absolute between-study variance. Values of I² exceeding 50% were considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on diabetes duration (5 years or less vs more than 5 years), geographic region (Global North vs Global South) and type of self-efficacy measurement tool (general, cultural-specific or diabetes-specific). Subgroup differences were statistically tested using the Q-test for subgroup differences.

Countries were grouped into Global North and Global South using a hybrid classification based on World Bank income categories and geopolitical conventions.34 The Global North included high-income countries with developed economies such as the USA, Canada, Australia, Western Europe, Japan, South Korea and Israel. The Global South encompassed low- and middle-income economies primarily located in Asia, Africa, Latin America and parts of Eastern Europe, including China, India, Indonesia, Iran, Thailand and Turkey. These classifications allowed for the exploration of how economic and structural differences may influence the association between self-efficacy and diabetes outcomes. This regional framework is consistent with prior global health research that contrasts access to care, health system resources and chronic disease management across economic settings.

These categories were selected based on their relevance to self-efficacy development, including variations in healthcare infrastructure, disease adaptation and measurement tool specificity. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of findings by excluding studies at high risk of bias and outliers with extreme effect sizes. Publication bias was evaluated through funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test. The trim-and-fill method was applied when evidence of asymmetry was detected. All analyses were performed in R V.4.4.2 using the ‘meta’ and ‘metafor’ packages.35 Additional visualisations were created with ggplot2 to aid interpretation.

Results

PRISMA flow diagram

The study selection process adhered to the PRISMA guidelines (figure 1).32 A total of 4447 records was identified through systematic database searches. After removing 2430 duplicate records (2425 using automated tools and 5 manually), 2017 unique records remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. Following this screening, 1776 studies were excluded due to irrelevant outcomes, ineligible populations or non-correlational study designs. The full texts of the remaining 262 studies were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. From these, 213 studies were excluded for the following reasons: non-English language (n=6), wrong outcomes (n=26), wrong indications (n=21), inappropriate study designs (n=37), paediatric populations (n=36), wrong patient populations (n=31) or lack of correlation coefficients (n=43). In total, 62 studies were included in the final analysis: 22 focused on self-efficacy and self-care, 19 focused on self-efficacy and HbA1c and 21 addressed all three outcomes.

Characteristics of the study

This review included 62 studies that examined the relationship between self-efficacy, self-care behaviours and HbA1c. Among these studies, 22 focused on self-efficacy and self-care,14 22 36 19 investigated self-efficacy and HbA1c3739 and 15 addressed combined relationships across these variables.4042 The study design was primarily cross-sectional, with a single prospective cohort study.22 Sample sizes ranged from 60 participants39 to 1758 participants.43 The age distribution varied widely from younger ages 18 years, Kurnia et al to older adults 80+ years.22 36 Gender proportions also differed, with some studies having predominantly female (66.55%)22 and others mostly male (7.3%).44 Geographically, the studies represented diverse regions, including Asia,14 45 North America,38 46 Europe,23 47 the Middle East40 44 and Australia.48

Most participants had T2D, with diabetes durations ranging from less than 1 year49 to over 40 years.21 Self-efficacy measurement tools included the Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES),25 44 Lorig’s Diabetes Self-Efficacy Scale22 and the Perceived Diabetes Self-Management Scale.50 51 These studies demonstrated considerable variability in self-efficacy scores and methods, reflecting differences across populations and measurement scales. Overall, the findings highlight the diversity and complexity of the relationship between self-efficacy, self-care and HbA1c in individuals with diabetes.

Self-efficacy on self-care and HbA1c

This meta-analysis included 62 studies (n=19 231) examining the association between self-efficacy and both self-care behaviours and HbA1c. The overall pooled effect size was small and not statistically significant (r=0.11, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.22, p=0.06), with high heterogeneity (I²=98.3%, τ²=0.22) (table 1, figure 2). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant positive association between self-efficacy and self-care (r=0.44, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.51, p<0.01; I²=95.0%, τ²=0.0644) and a significant inverse association with HbA1c (r=–0.27, 95% CI –0.37 to –0.17, p<0.01; I²=95.9%, τ²=0.094). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (Q=109.30, df=1, p<0.01), indicating a stronger effect on self-care behaviours than on HbA1c (figure 2). These findings underscore the critical role of self-efficacy in diabetes management, while also highlighting substantial between-study variability.

Publication bias

Funnel plots were visually examined for asymmetry (figure 3). Egger’s regression test was conducted to quantify potential small-study effects. For the overall analysis of self-efficacy and diabetes outcomes, Egger’s test was not statistically significant (p=0.21), indicating no substantial publication bias. For self-efficacy and self-care behaviours, Egger’s test yielded p=0.126, and for self-efficacy and HbA1c, p=0.154, suggesting minimal funnel plot asymmetry. The trim-and-fill method did not materially alter the pooled effect size (adjusted r =–0.036, 95% CI –0.16 to 0.09, p=0.57), supporting the robustness of the results despite high heterogeneity.

Funnel plot for publication bias in self-efficacy studies.

Prediction intervals

To improve interpretability and generalisability, 95% prediction intervals were calculated. For self-efficacy and self-care behaviours, the pooled effect size was COR=0.44 (95% CI 0.36 to 0.51) with a prediction interval of 0.15 to 0.66, indicating a positive association across most settings. For self-efficacy and HbA1c, the pooled effect size was COR = –0.27 (95% CI –0.37 to –0.17) with a prediction interval of –0.49 to –0.04, suggesting the effect remains negative but varies in magnitude across contexts.

Self-efficacy in self-care domains

This meta-analysis assessed the influence of self-efficacy on self-care behaviours in individuals with T2D. Subgroup analyses covered specific domains: diet and nutrition (k=10, n=3376), physical activity (k=10, n=3376), medication adherence (k=6, n=1512), blood glucose monitoring (k=9, n=3198) and foot care (k=5, n=1217) (table 1). The overall analysis showed a significant positive correlation (r=0.25, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.32, p<0.01). Significant associations were found for diet (r=0.30, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.47, p<0.01), glucose monitoring (r=0.29, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.39, p<0.01) and foot care (r=0.30, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.42, p<0.01) (online supplemental figure S2). Physical activity (r=0.17, 95% CI –0.01 to 0.34, p=0.07) and medication adherence (r=0.18, 95% CI –0.07 to 0.40, p=0.16) were not significant. Subgroup differences were also non-significant (Q=2.26, df=4, p=0.69), with high heterogeneity (I² = 96.7%) but minimal publication bias. These findings highlight self-efficacy’s strong role in supporting dietary control, glucose monitoring and foot care, reinforcing its value in diabetes self-management.

Self-efficacy by global regions

We examined the influence of self-efficacy on self-care behaviours in individuals with T2D. Subgroup analyses compared effect sizes between the Global North (k=8, n=1502) and low-income countries in East Asia (k=26, n=8131). The overall analysis showed a significant positive correlation (r=0.44, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.51, p<0.01; table 1, online supplemental figure S3). Associations were stronger in the Global North (r=0.53, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.64, p<0.01) than in the Global South (r=0.41, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.49, p<0.01), though the subgroup difference was not significant (Q=2.11, df=1, p=0.15). Heterogeneity was high across both regions (Global North: I²=86.1%; Global South: I²=95.8%). Online supplemental figure S4 shows consistent positive associations from 2007 to 2024, with slightly stronger effects in the Global North, highlighting the global relevance of self-efficacy in diabetes self-care.

Self-care by self-efficacy measurements

We examined the role of self-efficacy on self-care behaviours by diabetes duration, comparing individuals with long (>5 years; k=23, n=5204) and short (≤5 years; k=5, n=1386) durations. The pooled analysis revealed a significant positive correlation (r=0.44, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.51, p<0.01; online supplemental figure S5). Subgroup analyses by measurement tool showed the strongest association for diabetes-specific measures (r=0.50, 95% CI 0.40 to 0.59, p<0.01), followed by culture-specific (r=0.41, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.50, p<0.01) and general self-efficacy measures (r=0.30, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.49, p=0.01). However, subgroup differences were not statistically significant (Q=3.67, df=2, p=0.16). Heterogeneity was high across all tools (I²=87.2%–96.4%), reflecting variability in effect sizes. As shown in table 1, diabetes-specific tools yielded the most consistent results, supporting their use in targeted diabetes management.

Self-care by duration with diabetes

The contribution of self-efficacy to self-care behaviours was examined by diabetes duration, comparing individuals with long (>5 years; k=23, n=5204) and short (≤5 years; k=5, n=1386) durations. The pooled analysis showed a significant positive correlation (r=0.46, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.54, p<0.01; table 1). Subgroup analysis showed similar correlations for long duration (r=0.45, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.52, p<0.01) and short duration (r=0.51, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.73, p=0.01). The subgroup difference was not significant (Q=0.14, df=1, p=0.71; online supplemental figure S6). Heterogeneity was high in both groups (long: I²=91.3%; short: I² = 98.6%), though self-efficacy consistently predicted better self-care, with slightly stronger effects among those with shorter diabetes duration.

HbA1c levels by regions

We examined the relationship between self-efficacy and HbA1c by geographic region. Subgroup analysis compared the Global North (k=14, n=3917) and low-income East Asian countries (k=18, n=5681). Overall, higher self-efficacy was linked to lower HbA1c (r=–0.27, 95% CI –0.37 to –0.17, p<0.01; table 1). The correlation was stronger in the Global South (r=–0.34, 95% CI –0.49 to –0.17) than the Global North (r=–0.18, 95% CI –0.24 to –0.13), though subgroup differences were not statistically significant (Q=2.86, df=1, p=0.0907; online supplemental figure S7). Heterogeneity was high (Global South: I² = 97.5%; Global North: I² = 57.2%). Online supplemental figure S8 shows consistently negative correlations over time (2009–2024), with stronger effects in low-income East Asian countries.

HbA1c by diabetes duration

We analysed the relationship between self-efficacy and HbA1c by diabetes duration, comparing long (>5 years; k=20, n=6530) and short (≤5 years; k=4, n=1124) durations. Higher self-efficacy was linked to better HbA1c (r=–0.25, 95% CI –0.33 to –0.15, p<0.01; table 1). The correlation was stronger in the short-duration group (r=–0.40, 95% CI –0.65 to –0.07, p=0.02) than in the long-duration group (r=–0.21, 95% CI –0.30 to –0.12, p<0.01), though the subgroup difference was not significant (Q=1.19, df=1, p=0.27; online supplemental figure S9). Heterogeneity was high in both groups (long: I²=89.3%; short: I²=95.9%). These findings suggest consistent associations between self-efficacy and HbA1c across durations, with slightly stronger effects in shorter-duration diabetes.

Discussion

Our findings highlight self-efficacy as a critical factor influencing self-care behaviours and HbA1c in individuals with T2D. The meta-analysis revealed significant associations between higher self-efficacy and greater adherence to self-care practices, including dietary regulation, blood glucose monitoring and physical activity. Moreover, individuals with higher self-efficacy consistently exhibited lower HbA1c levels, underscoring the importance of psychological factors in achieving optimal HbA1c. While funnel plots suggested slight asymmetry for HbA1c, Egger’s test results were not statistically significant, and trim-and-fill adjustments did not alter the direction or strength of the pooled effects. These findings suggest that the observed associations are unlikely to be meaningfully biased by selective publication. The 95% prediction intervals indicated that although average effect sizes were statistically significant, the magnitude of associations may vary across populations, settings and measurement tools. This underscores the importance of tailoring self-efficacy interventions to specific contexts. These results are consistent with prior research highlighting the central role of self-efficacy in diabetes self-management and outcomes.111417 18

The findings align with the social cognitive theory position, which identifies self-efficacy as a determinant of behaviour change.15 Also in accordance with studies highlighting that self-efficacy’s role in facilitating diabetes self-management.41 52 53 These studies consistently reported that individuals with higher self-efficacy are more likely to engage in self-care behaviours that improve health outcomes, such as regular physical activity and dietary adherence. Studies found that peer-led support groups enhance self-efficacy and self-care behaviours by promoting a sense of empowerment and shared experiences.17 18 25

The negative association between self-efficacy and HbA1c is consistent with the findings that identified that individuals with greater confidence in managing diabetes tend to achieve better HbA1c.13 19 20 22 43 The findings align with a study highlighting regional disparities in healthcare socioeconomic inequalities among Global South and regions.54 These findings reinforce the importance of addressing systemic inequalities to support diabetes management globally.

Stronger associations observed in the Global South suggest that self-efficacy compensates for systemic gaps in healthcare provision, where limited access to healthcare infrastructure and resources places greater reliance on psychological resources.22 51 55 Socioeconomic factors, including poverty and health literacy, further shape self-efficacy’s role in diabetes management in these regions.54 56 Community-based programmes and culturally tailored interventions, such as peer support groups and mobile health technologies, have been shown to enhance self-efficacy and improve HbA1c in such contexts effectively.25 57 Conversely, in high-income settings with well-established healthcare systems, external supports, such as structured diabetes education programmes, often moderate the role of self-efficacy, reducing the reliance on individual psychological resources alone.46 These findings highlight the importance of adapting the ‘Power Within’ framework to address varying systemic and socioeconomic contexts globally.

The findings indicate that self-efficacy is more prominent in individuals with shorter diabetes durations. This finding aligns with studies that revealed self-efficacy is particularly influential during the early stages of diabetes management when patients are forming new habits.56 Studies highlighted the importance of structured diabetes self-management education programmes in equipping newly diagnosed patients with the skills and confidence needed to establish sustainable self-care behaviours.36 41 However, the relatively weaker associations observed in patients with longer diabetes durations suggest the need for ongoing reinforcement of self-efficacy to prevent declines in adherence over time.58

Diabetes-specific self-efficacy scales, such as the DMSES, are designed to assess confidence in performing specific diabetes-related behaviours, including glucose monitoring and medication adherence. In contrast, general self-efficacy scales (eg, the General Self-Efficacy Scale) measure broader confidence in managing life challenges, and cultural-specific tools may reflect beliefs and practices relevant to sociocultural contexts. In this meta-analysis, diabetes-specific tools were associated with stronger and more consistent findings. This supports the recommendation that using targeted, condition-specific instruments may yield more actionable insights for diabetes self-management interventions.59 60 Standardising these tools is essential for ensuring consistency across studies and clinical settings. The framework further underscores the importance of tailoring measurement tools to reflect diverse populations’ cultural and contextual needs.17

The findings validate the applicability of the ‘Power Within’ framework, which integrates personal empowerment with contextual enablers such as resilience and social support. For example, implementing peer-led support groups or community-based education programmes can enhance self-efficacy by fostering a sense of empowerment and shared experience.57 Additionally, mobile health technologies could operationalise the framework by providing consistent support, tailored education, and regular self-care reminders to individuals in resource-limited settings.24 25 45 This framework expands Bandura’s theory by addressing structural and systemic factors that influence self-efficacy, particularly in low-resource settings.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis identified significant associations between self-efficacy, self-care behaviours and HbA1c. However, high heterogeneity (I²>90%) across most analyses reduces the precision of pooled estimates. Likely sources of variability include differences in study design, as most studies were cross-sectional and used varied sampling methods. Participant characteristics, including age, gender, cultural context and comorbidities, also differed across studies and may have influenced the results. In addition, measurement tools varied. General, culturally specific and diabetes-specific scales assess different dimensions of self-efficacy, which limits comparability.

Regional variation in healthcare access, education and health literacy may have shaped how self-efficacy impacts outcomes. Stronger associations observed in the Global South suggest a greater reliance on self-management in low-resource settings. Sensitivity analyses that excluded high-risk studies and outliers yielded consistent effect sizes, which supports the robustness of the findings. Future research should apply meta-regression to explore whether study-level factors, such as sample size, publication year, regional income or risk of bias, account for some of the observed heterogeneity.

Future directions

Future research should prioritise developing and standardising diabetes-specific self-efficacy measurement tools to enhance consistency across studies. Additionally, interventions tailored to cultural and socioeconomic contexts should be designed and evaluated, particularly in low-resource settings. Longitudinal studies are essential to establish causal pathways and assess the sustainability of self-efficacy-enhancing interventions over time. These directions will refine the ‘Power Within’ framework and guide the creation of targeted strategies to improve diabetes outcomes globally.

Conclusions

Our findings reaffirm self-efficacy’s central role in diabetes management and its strong association with self-care and HbA1c. By situating the findings within the broader literature, this meta-analysis highlights the importance of tailored, context-specific interventions to empower individuals and improve diabetes outcomes globally.
