Experimental evolution of Rc-o319 sarbecovirus spike protein reveals limited ACE2 adaptability
Authors: Jorge Moreno-García, Jérémy Dufloo, Rafael Sanjuán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBat sarbecoviruses include several potentially human-infective viruses, and evaluating their evolvability is therefore important for assessing emergence risks. In particular, the interaction between the viral spike protein and the host ACE2 receptor is critical for cross-species transmissibility. Here, we focused on the spike protein from Rc-o319, a bat sarbecovirus related to SARS-CoV-2 but unable to infect humans. Using a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), we found that Rc-o319 spike protein ACE2 usage was restricted to its natural host, Rhinolophus cornutus, and to mouse ACE2 at low levels, but not to R. sinicus, ferret, cow, or human ACE2 homologues. Experimental evolution passages of the recombinant VSV expressing the Rc-o319 spike enabled us to study spike adaptation without requiring the authentic virus or posing a risk to humans. We found rapid spike optimization for viral entry through mouse ACE2, driven by a single substitution in the receptor-binding motif. In contrast, repeated passaging under strong selective pressure failed to yield variants capable of using human ACE2, indicating substantial evolutionary constraints. Together, our results demonstrate that the Rc-o319 spike can readily optimize interactions with already permissive ACE2 receptors but is limited in its capacity to acquire usage of a new ACE2 homologue, particularly human ACE2, suggesting a low zoonotic risk. However, some evolved spikes appeared to acquire a residual capacity for ACE2-independent viral entry, although this observation remains to be conclusively demonstrated and requires further investigation.Keywords: sarbecovirus, Rc-o319, spike protein, ACE2 usage, experimental evolution, host adaptation, zoonotic risk

Introduction

The Coronaviridae family infects a variety of hosts, including mammals and birds. Several coronaviruses can infect humans, causing a range of symptoms, primarily gastrointestinal and respiratory tract infections of varying severity (Li 2016). Within this family, of particular note is the Sarbecovirus group of betacoronaviruses, which has gained increasing importance in recent decades due to its zoonotic potential, as evidenced by the emergence of SARS-CoV and the SARS-CoV-2 pandemics (Ksiazek et al. 2003, Zhou et al. 2020). The main natural reservoirs of sarbecoviruses are bats, particularly species belonging to the Rhinolophus genus in East and Southeast Asia, although some representatives have also been found in Rhinolophus bats in Africa and Europe (Li et al. 2005, Drexler et al. 2010, Hu et al. 2017, Tao and Tong 2019, Crook et al. 2021, Wacharapluesadee et al. 2021, Alkhovsky et al. 2022). The zoonotic potential of these viruses and the risk of new viral outbreaks have motivated studies focused on enhancing the discovery of new sarbecoviruses through sequencing techniques, primarily in bat samples but not exclusively. This has led to a considerable increase in the number of available sarbecovirus sequences and to the discovery, and in some cases isolation, of new viruses genetically related to SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 (Lau et al. 2005, Drexler et al. 2010, Menachery et al. 2015, Hu et al. 2017, Tao and Tong 2019, Crook et al. 2021, Guo et al. 2021, Wacharapluesadee et al. 2021, Zhou et al. 2021, Alkhovsky et al. 2022, Temmam et al. 2022).

All sarbecoviruses for which the primary cellular receptor is known use the ACE2 (angiotensin-converting enzyme 2) protein to enter host cells (Ge et al. 2013, Guo et al. 2021, Wells et al. 2021, Zhou et al. 2021, Roelle et al. 2022, Starr et al. 2022). Indeed, it has been argued that the use of ACE2 is an ancestral trait of sarbecoviruses that some members of the group have subsequently lost (Starr et al. 2022). The viral spike glycoprotein mediates viral entry by binding to ACE2 on the host cell surface through its receptor-binding domain (RBD) (Li 2016). The RBD shows wide sequence diversity throughout the sarbecovirus group and this diversity is particularly concentrated in a region of the RBD known as the receptor-binding motif (RBM), the subregion that makes direct contact with the ACE2 residues (Hu et al. 2017, Lan et al. 2020, Letko et al. 2020, Starr et al. 2020, Zhou et al. 2021, Hauser et al. 2024, Si et al. 2024).

Based on the RBD sequence, phylogenetic analyses can classify sarbecoviruses into different clades, each with different sequence and ACE2-binding characteristics (Hu et al. 2017, Letko et al. 2020, Roelle et al. 2022, Starr et al. 2022, Guo et al. 2023, Si et al. 2024). Clade 1 comprises sarbecoviruses that use ACE2, and most of its members possess the longest RBMs. In turn, Clade 1 can be divided into three subclades: 1a (SARS-CoV-1-related sarbecoviruses), 1b (SARS-CoV-2-related sarbecoviruses), and 1c (referred to as Clade 4 in other works). Clade 2 includes sarbecoviruses that do not use ACE2 and have smaller RBMs due to two deletions in Regions 1 and 2 of the RBM. Finally, Clade 3 consists of sarbecoviruses identified in Europe and Africa that have a small deletion in RBM Region 1. They also use ACE2 as a receptor and are thought to be close to the ancestor of sarbecoviruses.

ACE2 gene orthologs are also diverse across different mammalian species (Guo et al. 2020, Yan et al. 2021, Li et al. 2023). In fact, the ACE2 gene has been found to be under strong selective pressure in bats, the group that constitutes the main reservoir for sarbecoviruses (Demogines et al. 2012, Frank et al. 2022). This indicates that ACE2 gene evolution has been shaped not only by its conserved physiological role but also by pressures from episodic viral infections. Comparative analyses demonstrate that amino acid substitutions at key positions in the ACE2 interface can markedly alter binding affinity to sarbecovirus spike proteins (Damas et al. 2020, Guo et al. 2020, Melin et al. 2020, Wells et al. 2021, Yan et al. 2021, Roelle et al. 2022, Starr et al. 2022, Li et al. 2023). This results in interspecies gene variation and pronounced differences in susceptibility to sarbecovirus infection. The diversification of both viral RBDs and ACE2 proteins makes the host ACE2 tropism of sarbecoviruses highly specific and complex, and draws an evolutionary scenario shaped by a variety of selective pressures on both sides of virus–host interactions (Demogines et al. 2012, Guo et al. 2020, MacLean et al. 2021, Yan et al. 2021, Frank et al. 2022).

In this study, we have focused on the bat-derived virus Rc-o319, a Clade 1b sarbecovirus phylogenetically related to SARS-CoV-2 that was discovered and fully sequenced in Japan from samples obtained from the Rhinolophus cornutus bat (Murakami et al. 2020, 2022). Rc-o319 is an early-diverging relative of SARS-CoV-2 and shares 81% genome identity with it (Murakami et al. 2020). Rc-o319 has a 9-amino-acid deletion in Region 2 of the RBM (Si et al. 2024), making Rc-o319 quite unique compared to the other known sarbecoviruses (Si et al. 2024). Rc-o319 can bind to the ACE2 of its host, R. cornutus, but not to the human homologue, making it incapable of infecting humans (Murakami et al. 2020, 2022).

In this study, we aim to test the potential ability of the Rc-o319 spike to adapt to ACE2 receptors from species other than its natural host by experimentally evolving its spike protein. Several previous studies have demonstrated how experimental evolution can clarify possible viral adaptations to different conditions and hosts (Letko et al. 2018, Gloria-Soria et al. 2020, Al-Zaher et al. 2021, Yan et al. 2022, Carrascosa-Sàez et al. 2024, Dufloo and Sanjuán 2024, Soriano-Tordera et al. 2025). Rather than using the authentic Rc-o319 virus, we opted to use a replication-competent recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) expressing the Rc-o319 spike protein instead of the VSV-G envelope glycoprotein (Tani et al. 2012, Dieterle et al. 2020, Carrascosa-Sàez et al. 2024, Dufloo and Sanjuán 2024, Soriano-Tordera et al. 2025). This surrogate system allows us to focus our experimental evolution on the Rc-o319 viral spike and avoid gain-of-function risks. We demonstrate the ability of the Rc-o319 spike to optimize mouse ACE2 usage, a host that is only weakly susceptible to the wild-type virus. In contrast, the Rc-o319 spike was unable to acquire human ACE2 usage under our experimental evolution conditions, suggesting a low zoonotic risk.

Methods

Cells

Baby Hamster Kidney 21 (BHK-21) cells were obtained from the ATCC (ATCC, 283 CCL-10). BHK-G43 cells, which are BHK-21 cells that can induce the VSV-G protein expression after mifepristone treatment (Hanika et al. 2005) were kindly provided by Dr Gert Zimmer. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1% non-essential amino acids, 10 units/ml penicillin, 10 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco), and 250 ng/ml amphotericin B (Gibco) at 37°C and 5% CO2. For passaging, cells were resuspended with trypsin (Gibco), appropriately diluted, and transferred to new flasks. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays regularly confirmed the absence of mycoplasma contamination.

Transfection of ACE2 expression plasmids

BHK-21 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. The following day, cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1 expression plasmids encoding ACE2 orthologs from different mammalian species (R. cornutus, R. sinicus, Mus musculus, Bos taurus, Mustela putorius, and Homo sapiens), or with an empty vector used as a negative control. A GFP expression plasmid was included as a control for transfection efficiency. Transfections were carried out using 100 ng/well of DNA and Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Generation of BHK-21 cells stably expressing different ACE2 orthologs

ACE2 gene orthologs from human, mouse, or R. cornutus were cloned into a lentiviral expression vector containing a puromycin resistance gene and a fluorescent reporter gene (mCherry for the vector with the human ACE2 gene and miRFP670nano for the vectors encoding mouse or R. cornutus ACE2 genes). The ACE2 gene was expressed under a cytomegalovirus promoter, while the puromycin resistance and fluorescent reporter genes were separated by a P2A peptide sequence and expressed under the same EF-1α promoter. Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the lentiviral expression vector, helper packaging plasmids encoding core viral components (Gag/Pol, Rev, and Tat), and an expression plasmid encoding the VSV-G glycoprotein, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). At 48 h post-transfection, supernatants were harvested, cleared by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min, aliquoted and stored at −80°C. To generate cells stably expressing ACE2, BHK-21 cells were transduced with lentiviral particles by spinoculation and 48 h post-transduction placed under 1 μg/ml puromycin selection to eliminate untransduced cells. As a control, untransduced BHK-21 cells were also placed under puromycin selection, and antibiotic-induced cell death was verified. Expression of the fluorescent reporters (mCherry or iRFP) in transduced cells was confirmed by an IncuCyte SX5 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius).

Generation of recombinant VSVs

The production of replication-competent recombinant VSV bearing the Rc-o319 spike (wild-type or mutants) or the SARS-CoV-2 spike from either the Wuhan or Omicron BA.4.5 variants was performed using a plasmid encoding the VSV antigenome, lacking the VSV-G glycoprotein gene and carrying the eGFP reporter gene (pVSV-eGFP-ΔG). The Rc-o319 spike gene or the SARS-CoV-2 spike gene from the Wuhan-Hu-1 or Omicron BA.4.5 strains was cloned in this pVSV-eGFP-ΔG plasmid (pVSV-eGFP-∆G-Rco319-S-∆Ct; pVSV-eGFP-∆G-Wu-S-∆Ct; and pVSV-eGFP-∆G-BA.4.5-S-∆Ct). The last 20 C-terminal amino acids of the Rc-o319 spike and the last 21 C-terminal amino acids of the SARS-CoV-2 spike were deleted. The resulting viral genome plasmids were used to recover infectious particles as previously described (Marqués et al. 2024). BHK-G43 cells, which express the VSV-G glycoprotein upon mifepristone induction, were seeded at a density of 105 cells/ml in DMEM 10% FBS without antibiotics in 12-well plates (1 ml/well). The following day, cells were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with the viral genome plasmids together with helper plasmids encoding the VSV-P, -N, and -L proteins, as well as the T7 RNA polymerase. After 3 h at 37°C, the medium was replaced with DMEM 10% FBS supplemented with 10 nM mifepristone to induce VSV-G expression. Cells were then incubated at 33°C for 36 h, followed by 48 h at 37°C. Supernatants harvested from GFP-positive cell cultures were purified by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min, and used to infect non-VSV-G-expressing BHK-21 cells to amplify the produced viruses and clear residual VSV-G. The viral inoculum was removed after 1 h at 37°C and cells were washed five times with PBS. Then, infected cells were incubated in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and 25% anti-VSV-G neutralizing monoclonal antibody obtained in-house from a mouse hybridoma cell line. Supernatants were harvested at 24–48 hpi, clarified by centrifugation, aliquoted and stored at −80°C.

rVSV-Rc-o319 experimental evolution

Four different evolution regimes were performed: two using monocultures of either BHK-21-mACE2 or BHK-21-RcACE2 cells and two using co-cultures of BHK-21-hACE2 with either BHK-21-mACE2 or BHK-21-RcACE2 cells. When seeding the cell mixtures for co-cultures, 90% of the cells were BHK-21-hACE2 and the remaining 10% were either BHK-21-mACE2 or BHK-21-RcACE2 cells. For all four evolution conditions, the rVSV expressing the Rc-o319 spike was serially passaged in the corresponding culture system in six-well plates until completing 10 passages. Three independent evolution replicates were performed for each evolution regime. These replicates were initiated using the same viral rescue. For each passage, confluent six-well plates were infected with 200 μl of viral inoculum and incubated for 1 h at 37°C, shaking every 15 min. Then, cells were supplemented with complete DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and incubated at 37°C for 24 h (monocultures) or 48 h (co-cultures) to allow viral growth. For the first viral passage in monocultures, infected cells were incubated for 60 h in BHK-21-mACE2 cultures and 40 h in BHK-21-RcACE2 cultures. After each passage, supernatants were collected, clarified by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. The harvested supernatants were then used to infect fresh, confluent monolayers in new six-well plates, thereby initiating the subsequent passage. In each passage, infection levels were monitored by GFP signal in virus-infected cells using an IncuCyte SX5 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius), and the viral inoculum was appropriately diluted between passages to avoid infection saturation.

Viral growth curves

Confluent BHK-21 cells seeded the previous day were inoculated with recombinant VSV and incubated at 37°C with gentle shaking every 15 min. After 1 h, cells were supplemented with DMEM containing 10% FBS and placed in an Incucyte SX5 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius) at 37°C for imaging over the course of infection. The progression of the infection was monitored by the virus-encoded GFP signal emitted by infected cells. The percentage of GFP-positive area and cell confluence were quantified using the IncuCyte analysis software, and the percentage of infected cells was calculated as the ratio between GFP confluence and cell confluence. Endpoint supernatants were harvested, clarified by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min, and stored at −80°C. Viral titres were determined as described below. The multiplicity of infection (MOI) used in each experiment is indicated in the figure legends. Log10-transformed infection data was used when calculating the area under the curve (AUC) values.

Virus titration

BHK-21 cells were seeded in 24-well plates. Next day, virus supernatants were serially diluted and 100 μl of each dilution were used to infect the confluent monolayers. After incubating the cells with the viral inoculum for 1 h at 37°C, 500 μl of DMEM containing 2% FBS and 0.6% agar was added to each well. Plates were incubated at 37°C and 18–24 hpi were imaged in the Incucyte SX5 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius) to manually count GFP-positive foci. Viral titres were calculated as PFU/ml. Endpoint titres harvested from growth curves were normalized relative to the titre of the viral inoculum used to initiate the infection.

Sanger sequencing

Viral RNA from the founder viral stock and the passage-10 evolved viruses was extracted using the Quick-RNA Viral Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted viral RNA was reverse-transcribed using SuperScript IV (Invitrogen) and a primer targeting a sequence in the VSV genome located upstream of the Rc-o319 spike gene (Supplementary Data S1). The resulting cDNAs were used for PCR amplification using Phusion Plus DNA Polymerase (Thermo Scientific) with specific primer pairs targeting the 5′ and 3′ ends of the spike gene (Supplementary Data S1). PCR products were purified using the DNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then sent to Sanger sequencing. The complete spike gene was sequenced using the previously mentioned spike start and end primers, as well as six additional primers every 500–600 bp (Supplementary Data S1).

Site-directed mutagenesis of Rc-o319 spike

Rc-o319 spike mutations were individually introduced into the VSV genome plasmids encoding the Rc-o319 spike gene (pVSV-eGFP-∆G-Rco319-S-∆Ct) by site-directed mutagenesis with the QuikChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent) using a pair of overlapping primers containing the desired mutation (Supplementary Data S1) and following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each reaction was performed with 100 ng of plasmid template to be mutated, dNTPs (25 nM each), 250 ng of each primer pair, 2.5 U/μl of PfuUltra High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, using the recommended PCR cycling parameters. PCR products were digested at 37°C for 1 h with FastDigest DpnI (Thermo Scientific) and transformed into NZY5α competent cells (NZYTech). The presence of the desired mutation in individual bacterial clones was confirmed by whole-plasmid sequencing (Eurofins Scientific) using Oxford Nanopore technology.

Biosafety

All experiments with recombinant VSVs and cells were conducted under appropriate biosafety level 2 (BSL2) laboratories. The experiments were approved by the Biosecurity Committee of the University of Valencia and the Spanish National Biosafety Commission. VSV-based recombinant viruses represent a surrogate system that enables the study of coronavirus spikes at BSL2 facilities, the appropriate containment level for VSV, thereby avoiding the use of the authentic coronavirus.

Results

The Rc-o319 sarbecovirus spike exhibits narrow ACE2 usage across species

We generated a replication-competent rVSV expressing the green fluorescent protein (GFP) and the Rc-o319 spike instead of the cognate VSV-G envelope glycoprotein (rVSV-Rc-o319). To evaluate the ACE2 usage range of the Rc-o319 spike, we cloned and expressed ACE2 from six species: R. cornutus (Japanese horseshoe bat, the known host of Rc-o319), R. sinicus (Chinese horseshoe bat), M. musculus (mouse), M. putorius (ferret), B. taurus (cow), and H. sapiens (Fig. 1). The ACE2 homologues were selected from these species to cover a broad range of different mammalian taxonomic orders. In addition, a species belonging to the same genus as the natural host of Rc-o319 was included to evaluate the ability of the Rc-o319 spike to utilize ACE2 from different species within that genus. Expression plasmids of these ACE2 homologues were used to transfect BHK-21 cells, which do not express a receptor compatible with the Rc-o319 spike, but are highly susceptible to VSV. We found that rVSV-Rc-o319 showed robust infection exclusively in cells expressing R. cornutus ACE2, although low levels of infection were also detected in cells expressing mouse ACE2 (Fig. 1). No infection signal was detected for any of the other ACE2 homologues. This indicates that the virus has a very limited species tropism at the entry stage, which is consistent with previous studies showing reduced ACE2 adaptability for Rc-o319 (Si et al. 2024, Kosugi et al. 2025).

Rapid adaptation of the Rc-o319 spike to permissive ACE2 homologues

We set out to use experimental evolution to evaluate whether the Rc-o319 spike could improve mouse ACE2 usage or acquire new tropisms. First, lentiviral transduction was used to generate BHK-21 cells stably expressing human (h), mouse (m), or R. cornutus (Rc) ACE2. The lentiviral vector included a puromycin selection gene and a fluorescent reporter (mCherry for hACE2, and NIR for mACE2 and RcACE2), which was detected as expected. To more directly verify ACE2 expression in the established cell lines, we used rVSV-Rc-o319 and rVSV-SC2-Wuhan. Consistent with the results obtained using transient expression, rVSV-Rc-o319 infected cells stably expressing RcACE2 and, to a lesser extent, mACE2, but not hACE2. We also confirmed that rVSV-SC2-Wuhan only infected cells stably expressing hACE2 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To assay Rc-o319 spike adaptation to mACE2, rVSV-Rc-o319 was serially passaged on mACE2-expressing cells (Fig. 2A). As a control, we also conducted serial passages of the virus in cells expressing ACE2 from R. cornutus, which was already competent for viral entry (Fig. 2A). Each evolution regime was performed in triplicate using the same initial viral stock and maintained for 10 serial passages. GFP signal increased strongly throughout the experiment in all cases, suggesting viral adaptation (Fig. 2B). For viruses evolved in mACE2-expressing cells, this was clearly confirmed using growth curves of the founder versus passage-10 viruses, whereas the gain in fitness was observable but less obvious for lines evolved in RcACE2-expressing cells (Fig. 2C). This smaller improvement was expected, because the founder rVSV-Rc-o319 virus already infected BHK-21-RcACE2 cells efficiently.

We then examined whether the evolved viruses showed an expanded ACE2 usage range compared with the founder rVSV-Rc-o319 virus. To this end, BHK-21 cells were transfected with plasmids expressing ACE2 orthologs from the mammalian species described above and were then infected with the founder or evolved rVSV-Rc-o319 viruses. No changes were observed in ACE2 usage for any of the evolved viruses (Supplementary Fig. S2).

The Rc-o319 spike mutation H470Y specifically improves mACE2 usage

Sanger sequencing of the spike gene from the six rVSV-Rc-o319 viruses evolved in BHK-21-mACE2 or BHK-21-RcACE2 cells revealed several fixed mutations that could be responsible for the observed adaptation (Fig. 3A). In the case of viruses passaged in BHK-21-mACE2 cells, a substitution at spike residue 589 emerged in all three evolution replicates (I589T and I589R). In addition, the spike carrying the I589R change acquired two additional substitutions (H470Y and D533G). Moreover, the H470Y change was also present in one of the two evolved spikes that had the I589T mutation. Regarding viruses evolved in BHK-21-RcACE2 cells, only the I589T mutation was observed, and it appeared in all three evolution replicates. Whereas residue 589 was unlikely to be involved in ACE2 species specificity because it was mutated regardless of whether viruses were passaged in the presence of mACE2 or RcACE2, the H470Y and D533 changes appeared only in lines evolved with mACE2. Interestingly, residue 470 is located in the RBM, suggesting direct involvement in ACE2 binding specificity, whereas the other changes map to regions of the spike without known functional domains (Fig. 3B). Sanger sequencing of the founder viral stock confirmed that no mutations were present initially at high frequency. However, the convergence observed for some mutations may still be driven by variants already present in the founder population at low frequencies. The mutated positions 470, 533, and 589 in the Rc-o319 spike correspond to SARS-CoV-2 spike positions 505, 568, and 624, respectively.

To determine the effect of each fixed spike substitution, we used site-directed mutagenesis. rVSV-Rc-o319 viruses encoding wild-type or mutant spikes were used to infect BHK-21 cells stably expressing mACE2 or RcACE2. We carried out fluorescence-based growth curves (Fig. 3C) and quantified endpoint titres by plaque assay (Fig. 3D). This showed that the I589R and I589T substitutions were strongly selectively advantageous in BHK-21-mACE2 cells, as shown by AUC changes (t-tests comparing with wild-type: I589R P = .015, I589T P = .018) and an > 1000-fold increase in titre (t-test: I589R P = .0066, I589T P = .0021). Consistent with the above observations, these mutations were also advantageous in BHK-21-RcACE2 cells but to a lower extent (AUC change over wild-type: I589R P = .022, I589T P = .020; 28-fold and 54-fold titre increase for I589R and I589T, respectively, I589R P = .015, I589T P = .022). In contrast, the H470Y change was strongly advantageous in mACE2-expressing cells (AUC change over wild-type: P = .022; 3198-fold titre increase, P = .0016), but had a weak effect in RcACE2-expressing cells (AUC change over wild-type: P > .05; 4.61-fold titre increase, P > .05), consistent with the observation that this mutation emerged only in BHK-21-mACE2 cells. Finally, the D533G mutation was found to be neutral.

The Rc-o319 spike fails to adapt to hACE2 but might acquire ACE2-independent entry

To explore whether the Rc-o319 spike could acquire the ability to use hACE2, we used serial passaging of rVSV-Rc-o319 in co-cultures of BHK-21-hACE2 and BHK-21-mACE2, or of BHK-21-hACE2 and BHK-21-RcACE2 (Fig. 4A). In both co-culture regimes, the BHK-21-hACE2 cell line represented 90% of the cells, such that potential Rc-o319 spike mutants with the ability to use hACE2 would have a strong selective advantage.

Following 10 serial passages in co-cultures of BHK-21-hACE2 and BHK-21-RcACE2 cells, none of the three rVSV-Rc-o319 viruses acquired the ability to infect BHK-21-hACE2 cells. In contrast, for viruses passaged in co-cultures of BHK-21-hACE2 and BHK-21-mACE2 cells, all three lineages acquired the ability to infect BHK-21-hACE2 cells, although with low efficiency (Fig. 4B). However, this did not indicate specific adaptation of the spike to hACE2, since the evolved viruses showed similar infectivity in standard BHK-21 cells not expressing hACE2. BHK-21 cells are derived from Golden Syrian hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus), and SARS-CoV-2 is known to use hamster ACE2 as a receptor (Sia et al. 2020, Mohl et al. 2024). However, standard BHK-21 cells were not infected by rVSV-SC2-Wuhan, thus indicating a lack of hamster ACE2 expression, as also shown previously (Yan et al. 2022). This was further verified by RT-qPCR (data not shown). This observation raises the possibility that rVSV-Rc-o319 viruses evolved in co-cultures of BHK-21-hACE2 and BHK-21-mACE2 cells might have developed a limited capacity to infect cells independently of ACE2. However, we were unable to serially propagate or further amplify these viruses in standard BHK-21 cells due to poor infectivity, which also prevented us from characterizing the spike mutations potentially responsible for this phenotype.

Discussion

Sarbecoviruses have demonstrated their zoonotic potential and ability to undergo host jumps (Ksiazek et al. 2003, Zhou et al. 2020). Therefore, this group of viruses has been the subject of several studies that seek to understand the basis of their receptor-dependent tropism and their multi-species adaptability (Letko et al. 2020, Wells et al. 2021, Roelle et al. 2022, Starr et al. 2022, Si et al. 2024). Previous studies have already shown the limited host range of the Rc-o319 virus (Si et al. 2024, Kosugi et al. 2025). Here, we further investigated the adaptability of the Rc-o319 spike using VSV as a vector, which prevents the gain-of-function risks associated with using authentic viruses. The notably restricted host ACE2 usage range of the Rc-o319 spike suggests that its current zoonotic potential is relatively low compared to that of other sarbecoviruses, but future zoonotic risk assessments also depend on viral adaptability.

We have shown that the Rc-o319 spike can be evolutionarily optimized in the presence of an already permissive ACE2, such as RcACE2 and, most notably, mACE2. We have also identified H470Y as an mACE2-specific adaptation in the Rc-o319 spike. Interestingly, the homologous position in the Wuhan SARS-CoV-2 spike contains a tyrosine (Y505). Therefore, the mutation that appeared in Rc-o319 as a result of adaptation to mACE2 leads to the acquisition of the same residue as SARS-CoV-2 at that position. However, Wuhan SARS-CoV-2 is unable to use mACE2 (Gu et al. 2020, Rawle et al. 2021, Yan et al. 2022), whereas the Omicron variants show a Y505H substitution and can use mACE2. Hence, adaptation to mACE2 might obey opposite genetic changes in Rc-o319 and SARS-CoV-2. In line with this, specific individual mutations can have markedly different effects across viruses. For instance, the nearby N501Y mutation enhances binding to human ACE2 in several SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern, yet the homologous mutation in SARS-CoV-1 reduces binding affinity (Starr et al. 2022). This N501Y appeared in SARS-CoV-2 Alpha and subsequent variants, and allows the virus to use mACE2 for entry (Gu et al. 2020, Yan et al. 2022, Fujita et al. 2023). Thus, this region of the sarbecovirus spike may be crucial for binding to mACE2. Additional studies have found that the Q493R, Q493K, Q498H, and Q498R mutations in SARS-CoV-2 spike appear to be related to mACE2 binding (Leist et al. 2020, Huang et al. 2021, Zhang et al. 2021, Yan et al. 2022).

Residue 589 of the Rc-o319 spike was mutated in all evolution replicates performed in BHK-21-mACE2 or BHK-21-RcACE2 cells, and both I589R and I589T mutants showed increased performance. The general beneficial effect of these mutations and the fact that they appeared in all evolutionary lineages do not allow us to conclude that their effects were directly related to ACE2 usage. However, several studies have shown that residues distal to the RBD-receptor interface in some sarbecoviruses, as is the case for position 589, can also affect RBD conformation and accessibility, receptor recognition, and viral entry (Tse et al. 2024, 2025). Indeed, Rc-o319 spike trimers have been reported to adopt two highly stable locked prefusion conformations with limited RBD accessibility (Wang et al. 2026). It is possible that this position plays a role in the packing interactions that stabilize this locked conformation, and that the I589R/I589T mutations promote a more open state with increased RBD accessibility. In addition, mutations at position 589 may also be associated with changes in spike proteolytic processing and cell–cell fusion dynamics. Alternative possible mechanisms include adaptation of the virus to BHK-21 cells or to general cell culture conditions. It is noteworthy that these changes were not found to be naturally present in the spikes of other sarbecoviruses.

It is also important to consider the possibility that the identified mutations simply enhance spike incorporation into VSV particles. However, because the mutated residues are located in the S1 region rather than in the S2 C-terminal/transmembrane regions typically involved in spike incorporation, they are unlikely to directly affect this process. Moreover, the effects of the mutations on viral replication differ between mACE2- and RcACE2-expressing cells, with H470Y showing a much stronger effect in mACE2 cells and I589R/T enhancing replication to different extents depending on the receptor background. If increased spike incorporation were the main driver of adaptation, similar effects would be expected across both cell types.

Interestingly, recombinant viruses carrying individual mutations showed that both H470Y and I589R/T had similar effects on viral replication in BHK-21-mACE2 cells, and the three lineages evolved in these cells displayed comparable replication kinetics. However, lineages R1 and R2 carry two mutations (H470Y and I589R/T), whereas lineage R3 carries only one (I589T). This suggests that simultaneous mutations at both positions may be redundant, or that their effects are not additive.

We also found that the Rc-o319 spike was unable to acquire hACE2 usage despite repeated passaging under conditions that would strongly favour this change. The VSV replication machinery naturally introduces many more point mutations than insertions or deletions (Chare et al. 2003, Novella et al. 2010, Simon-Loriere and Holmes 2011), which might be required for the evolution of new ACE2 usage specificities. Rc-o319 is characterized by the presence of a deletion in Region 2 of the RBM, and it has been argued that the virus may need to modify this RBM deletion to alter its host tropism (Si et al. 2024). Indeed, it has been shown that when the deletion is filled by replacing the entire RBM Region 2 of Rc-o319 with that of SARS-CoV-2, the resulting chimeric virus can use more ACE2 orthologs than the wild-type, including human ACE2 (Si et al. 2024). Other viral sequences highly related to Rc-o319 have been identified since (Murakami et al. 2022, Okuya et al. 2025). They all share the same deletion in the RBM Region 2, which leads us to suggest that they may exhibit the same limited zoonotic adaptability as Rc-o319. Another important aspect for future investigation would be to examine whether prior adaptation to ACE2 receptors from intermediate hosts could facilitate subsequent adaptation to the human ortholog.

Following serial passages in BHK-21-mACE2 and BHK-21-RcACE2 co-cultures, the observations are consistent with a limited ACE2-independent infection of BHK-21 cells by the evolved virus, albeit at low levels. However, further experiments are required to fully confirm this possibility and, if validated, to elucidate the underlying entry pathways and genetic changes responsible for this pattern. Although BHK-21 cells are not expected to express hamster ACE2, even minimal basal expression could influence the observed results. It is possible that adaptation to mACE2 during evolution in co-cultures also confers the ability to utilize low endogenous levels of hamster ACE2, although we consider this possibility unlikely. In a previous work where SARS-CoV-2 was adapted to use mACE2, a variant capable of infecting BHK-21 cells independently of ACE2 was also reported (Yan et al. 2022). This study suggested that the E484D substitution in the SARS-CoV-2 spike may be involved in the ACE2-independent viral entry.

In conclusion, the Rc-o319 spike has a very narrow ACE2 usage host range and limited adaptability to hACE2 through point mutations, which places it as a virus with relatively low zoonotic potential compared to other sarbecoviruses.
