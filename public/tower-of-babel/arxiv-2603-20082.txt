Inference in high-dimensional logistic regression under tensor network dependence
Josh Miles, Sohom Bhattacharya

Abstract
We investigate the problem of statistical inference for logistic regression with high-dimensional covariates in settings where dependence among individuals is induced by an underlying Markov random field. Going beyond the pairwise interaction models such as the Ising model, we consider a framework to accommodate more general tensor structures that capture higher-order dependencies. We develop a two-step procedure for low-dimensional linear and quadratic functionals. The first step constructs a regularized maximum pseudolikelihood estimator, for which we establish consistency under high-dimensional features. However, as in other classical high-dimensional regression problems, this estimator is biased and cannot be directly used for valid statistical inference. The second step introduces a bias-correction that yields an asymptotically normal estimator from which one can construct confidence intervals and test hypotheses. Our results move beyond the existing literature, where only estimation guarantees were available or only for pairwise interaction models. We complement our theoretical analysis with simulation studies confirming the effectiveness of the proposed method.



1
Introduction

Regression with dependent observations has gained considerable attention in recent network analysis research
[
46
,
21
]
. Given a network, the primary objective in this framework is to efficiently estimate the regression coefficients while accounting for dependencies among observations.
The problem is particularly relevant in scenarios where the standard independence assumption on the response variables is violated. Such dependencies commonly arise in settings where data is collected in temporal or spatial domains or within social networks, where interactions between individuals introduce correlation structures through peer effects
[
6
,
23
,
53
,
57
]
. While recent work has advanced understanding in such models, most results are restricted to low-dimensional covariates or focus only on consistent estimation. As a result, inference for high-dimensional regression under dependence remains poorly understood. This paper addresses this important gap by studying statistical inference for low-dimensional functionals in high-dimensional logistic regression with dependent binary outcomes, where dependence is induced by an underlying hypergraph.

To achieve this, we consider a discrete exponential family of binary outcomes, where individuals are connected via a Markov Random Field.
A widely studied model considers quadratic interactions, which correspond to classical Ising model
[
33
]
. Originally introduced in statistical physics to study ferromagnetism, the Ising model has since found broad applications in spatial statistics, social networks, computer vision, and neuroscience
[
2
,
24
,
27
,
43
]
. Further, the Ising model with external fields has been a popular framework for modeling logistic regression with peer effects
[
20
,
46
]
. Related problems of consistent estimation and hypothesis testing from a single realization of an Ising model have been studied extensively in the past couple of decades
[
16
,
9
,
44
,
45
]
. However, in many real-world datasets, pairwise interactions are insufficient to capture complex dependency structures; higher-order peer-group effects must also be considered. Motivated by this, we study hypothesis testing under general hypergraph-induced dependence.

Inference in high-dimensional linear and generalized linear models (GLMs) has been well- studied in the last decade. It is known
[
13
,
59
]
that while regularized estimators such as the Lasso achieve consistent estimation, they cannot be directly used for hypothesis testing. A substantial line of work has therefore focused on debiasing such estimators to obtain asymptotically normal statistics for inference in both linear regression
[
63
,
35
,
36
]
and GLMs
[
15
,
52
,
40
]
. Importantly, all of these results assume independent samples. In contrast, our work develops valid confidence intervals for low-dimensional functionals in high-dimensional logistic regression with tensor network dependence, thus broadening the applicability for a wide class of dependent data models.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows. We study the problem of statistical inference and hypothesis testing in high-dimensional logistic regression models with dependent observations induced by an underlying tensor network structure. We propose a bias-corrected estimator (Section
2.1
) based on the
\ell_{1}
-regularized maximum pseudo-likelihood estimator (
2.3
), which is computationally efficient even under higher-order dependence. The proposed estimator is asymptotically normal and enables a valid construction of confidence intervals for both linear and quadratic functionals of the regression parameter. We establish non-asymptotic error bounds for the pseudo-likelihood estimator and provide rigorous justification of the inference procedure through Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.1
. Our proof-techniques can be of independent interest for high-dimensional nonlinear statistical inference problems for network data. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to demonstrate the validity of projection-based inference (
2.15
) under general network and tensor dependence.

2
Results

Consider the canonical Markov Random Field (MRF) model induced by a hypergraph
\mathbf{G}_{n}=([n],E)
. The conditional p.m.f. of the response
\mathbf{y}
given the covariates is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{Z(\bm{\theta})}\exp\left(\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in E}g_{\mathbf{e}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{e}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{\theta}\right),\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n},

(2.1)

for some
g_{\mathbf{e}}\geq 0
,
\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{e}}=\prod_{i\in\mathbf{e}}y_{i}
, and
Z(\bm{\theta})
denote the normalizing constant

\displaystyle Z(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\exp\left(\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in E}g_{\mathbf{e}}\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{e}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{\theta}\right)

(2.2)

Here the observed covariates are denoted by the matrix

\bm{X}^{\top}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
. We will assume throughout that the covariates
\mathbf{X}_{i}
are i.i.d. with common distribution
\mathbb{P}_{X}
.
Our parameter of interest is
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will consider the traditional high-dimensional scenario where
d\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
but the parameter is sparse. Given a single realization
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
of the model, the main objective of the paper is to perform inference on the unknown regression parameter
\bm{\theta}
.

2.1
Inference for linear functionals

In this section, we describe our method of performing inference on a linear functional
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}
, for some
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will first obtain a consistent estimator of
\bm{\theta}
, and then perform a bias-correction to obtain an estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
which is asymptotically normal. We describe the details of our two-step method below.

Estimation of parameters in MRFs is challenging due to the fact that the normalizing constant
Z(\bm{\theta})
, defined by (
2.2
) is computationally intractable. As a viable alternative, pseudo-likelihood based estimators
[
8
,
16
]
have been quite popular for Ising models. However, the majority of prior works that deal with consistent estimation are restricted to pair-wise interaction. Moreover, inferential properties of pseudo-likelihood-based estimators are poorly understood.
To construct a consistent estimator
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
of
\bm{\theta}
, we proceed as follows.
Given a hypergraph
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, we call a subset of vertices
I\subset[n]
a strong independent set
[
18
]
if, for every hyperedge
\mathbf{e}\in E
, we have
|I\cap\mathbf{e}|\leq 1
.
Note that for a graph
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, this definition coincides with that of a standard independent set.
We begin by choosing a strong-independent set
S:=S(\mathbf{G}_{n})
of the hypergraph
\mathbf{G}_{n}
. We will split vertices in
S
into two parts
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
of equal size (for simplicity, we will assume throughout that
|S|
is even, otherwise one of the parts will have one extra vertex). We will use the vertices
S_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
during the
i
-th step of our method. Note that, conditional on
\mathbf{y}_{-S},\mathbf{X}
, the random variables
y_{i}
,
i\in S
are independent by property of MRF. Our initial estimator maximizes the conditional probability mass function

\prod_{i\in S_{1}}p(y_{i}|\mathbf{y}_{-S},\mathbf{X})
with an
\ell_{1}
regularization:

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}=\operatorname{argmin}_{\mathbf{b}}\left\{L_{S_{1}}(\mathbf{b})+\lambda_{n}\|\mathbf{b}\|_{1}\right\},

(2.3)

for some regularization parameter
\lambda_{n}>0
. Here, the log-pseudo-likelihood
L_{S_{1}}(\mathbf{b})
is defined by

\displaystyle L_{S_{1}}(\mathbf{b})=-\frac{1}{|S_{1}|}\sum_{i\in S_{1}}\left\{y_{i}(m_{i}(\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{i}\mathbf{b})-\log\cosh(m_{i}(\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{i}\mathbf{b})\right\},

(2.4)

where

m_{i}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{e}:i\in\mathbf{e}}g_{\mathbf{e}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{e}\setminus i}.

(2.5)

We will show the consistency of this estimator
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
in Proposition
2.3
. As commonly seen in high-dimensional statistics, one needs to de-bias the
\ell_{1}
regularized estimator to perform precise statistical inference
[
36
,
35
,
63
]
. To describe our debiasing method based on
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
, we first transform the response variables
\overline{y}_{i}:=(y_{i}+1)/2\in\{0,1\}
. Define the function

\displaystyle f(x)=e^{x}/(e^{x}+e^{-x}).

(2.6)

Further define the quantities

v_{i}=m_{i}(\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{\theta},\quad\tilde{v}_{i}=m_{i}(\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{i}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}.

(2.7)

The rationale behind introducing the notation is
\overline{y}_{i}
,
i\in S_{2}
are independent Bernoulli random variables given
\mathbf{y}_{-S},\mathbf{X}
with

\mathbb{P}(\overline{y}_{i}=1|\mathbf{y}_{-S},\mathbf{X})=f(v_{i}).

(2.8)

We consider the following bias-corrected estimator of the linear functional
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\theta
, for some
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}\Big[w_{i}(\overline{y}_{i}-f(\tilde{v}_{i}))\Big]\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i},

(2.9)

where
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
and
\tilde{v}_{i}
are defined by (
2.3
) and (
2.7
) respectively. Here,
w_{i}
’s are suitable data-dependent weights and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a projection vector we will construct below.

Note that, using (
2.8
), we have for
i\in S_{2}
,
\text{Var}[\overline{y}_{i}|y_{-S},\mathbf{X}]=f(v_{i})\big(1-f(v_{i})\big)
. Define

\varepsilon_{i}=\overline{y}_{i}-f(v_{i}).

(2.10)

Applying a Taylor series expansion of
f(\cdot)
around
\tilde{v}_{i}
to the summand in (
2.9
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}\Big[w_{i}\big(\overline{y}_{i}-f(\tilde{v}_{i})\big)\Big]\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\Big[\big(\overline{y}_{i}-f(v_{i})\big)+\big(f(v_{i})-f(\tilde{v}_{i})\big)\Big]\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\epsilon_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}+\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\Big[f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{\theta}-\tilde{\bm{\theta}})+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i}+t\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{\theta}-\tilde{\bm{\theta}}))\big(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{\theta}-\tilde{\bm{\theta}})\big)^{2}}_{=:\mathcal{R}_{i}}\Big]\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\epsilon_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}+\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\Big[f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{\theta-\hat{\theta}})\Big]\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}+\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\mathcal{R}_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i},

(2.11)

for some
t\in(0,1)
. Substituting (
2.11
) into (
2.9
) yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}=\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\epsilon_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle+\Bigg\{\bigg[\Big(\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\Big)-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bigg](\bm{\theta}-\tilde{\bm{\theta}})\Bigg\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\mathcal{R}_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}.

(2.12)

We will choose the weights
w_{i}
and the projection vector
\mathbf{u}
in a way such that the first term is asymptotically normal and whose asymptotic variance is minimized; and the second and third summand will be negligible compared to the first quantity.
By the definition of
\varepsilon_{i}
given by (
2.10
), we have

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}\Bigg(\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w_{i}\epsilon_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}\Bigg|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}_{-S}\Bigg)=\frac{1}{|S_{2}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}w^{2}_{i}f(v_{i})(1-f(v_{i}))(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i})^{2}

Balancing the weights of each term of the above display with the second summand of the RHS, we will choose weights
w_{i}
such that
w_{i}f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})=w^{2}_{i}f(\tilde{v}_{i})(1-f(\tilde{v}_{i}))
, which implies that

w_{i}=\frac{f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})}{f(\tilde{v}_{i})(1-f(\tilde{v}_{i}))}.

(2.13)

However, plugging the definition of
f
defined by (
2.6
), simple algebra yields that
w_{i}=2
. Given these constant weight, we finally choose the projection vector. Define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\Big\|\frac{2}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})\bm{X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}u}-\mathbf{c}\Big\|_{\infty}\leq C_{1}\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}},

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{2}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i})(\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{i}\mathbf{c})-\|\mathbf{c}\|^{2}_{2}\Big|\leq C_{2}\|\mathbf{c}\|^{2}_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}},\quad\max\limits_{i\in S_{2}}|\bm{X_{i}^{\top}u}|\leq C_{3}\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}\sqrt{\log n}\Bigg\},

(2.14)

for some
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
. Our choice for the projection
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}:=\operatorname{argmin}_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{2}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i})^{2},

(2.15)

With this choice of
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
given by (
2.15
) and weights
w_{i}
given by (
2.13
), we have our bias-corrected estimator

\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{2}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}\Big[\overline{y}_{i}-f(\tilde{v}_{i})\Big]\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i},

(2.16)

The use of projection vectors for bias-corrected estimation has been extensively studied in the context of generalized linear models with independent observations
[
52
,
15
,
29
]
. Our main methodological contribution is to construct projection vectors
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
to settings with tensor network dependence among observations, and to establish asymptotic normality of linear functionals based on pseudo-likelihood estimators. Note that, unlike many prior works, we do not assume any
\ell_{1}
or
\ell_{2}
-bounds on
\mathbf{c}
, instead, our formulation in (
2.1
) accommodates general
\mathbf{c}
. If
\|\mathbf{c}\|_{1}=O(1)
, the second constraint in (
2.1
) is automatically satisfied by the first. As a consequence, to construct bias-corrected estimator for
\theta_{j}
for any
j\in[d]
, we do not need the second constraint. This makes the computation of the projection vector faster.

2.2
Hypothesis testing

We will show in Section
2.4
how the bias-corrected estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
is asymptotically normal under mild assumptions of covariates and standard sparsity assumptions. The main application of Theorem
2.1
is to derive confidence intervals and do statistical hypothesis
tests for our high-dimensional network model.

Confidence Interval:

To construct a confidence interval of
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}
, we need to estimate the asymptotic variance of
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}
. Note that the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
, conditioned on
y_{-S},y_{S_{1}}\mathbf{X}
is given by

\displaystyle V=\frac{1}{|S_{2}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}4f(v_{i})\big(1-f(v_{i})\big)(\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i})^{2}.

(2.17)

Given the definition of weights
w_{i}
by (
2.13
), we can estimate
V
by plugging in the initial estimator
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
defined by (
2.3
):

\widehat{V}=\frac{1}{|S_{2}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}4f(\tilde{v}_{i})\big(1-f(\tilde{v}_{i})\big)(\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i})^{2},

(2.18)

where
\tilde{v}_{i}
is defined by (
2.7
). The natural
(1-\alpha)
-level confidence interval for
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}
is now given by

\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}(\alpha)=\left[\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}},\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}}\right],

(2.19)

where
z_{\alpha}
denotes the
\alpha
-th quantile of a standard normal distribution, and
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
is defined by (
2.16
). Theoretical justification of this confidence interval is given by Proposition
2.1
under the standard sparsity assumption
s\ll\sqrt{n}
(up to logarithmic factors). Note that, to construct an adaptive confidence interval without the knowledge of sparsity parameter
s
, one requires the condition
s\ll\sqrt{n}
for independent data (
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{G}_{n})\equiv 0
) in both linear regression
[
36
,
59
]
and logistic regression
[
15
]
problems.

An immediate consequence of the construction of the confidence interval
\mathcal{C}
is that it can be used to conduct a test for the statistical hypothesis
H_{0}:\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}=c^{\star}
. We choose the test statistic

\displaystyle T=\frac{(\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}-c^{\star})}{\sqrt{\widehat{V}}}.

(2.20)

An
\alpha
-level test is the one which rejects
H_{0}
if and only if
|T|\geq z_{\alpha/2}
. By selecting
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{e}_{j}
, we obtain inferential guarantees for each coordinate of the regression parameter
\theta_{j}
,
j\in[d]
. For such example, to denote the dependence on
j
, in the sequel we will denote the test statistic for testing
\theta_{j}
by
T_{j}
and corresponding estimated variance by
\widehat{V}_{j}
.

Simultaneous Inference

Our method can be further used to perform multiple hypothesis testing for a number of regression coefficients simultaneously with desired control on Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) or False Discovery Rate (FDR). In the standard multiple hypothesis problem, we are interested in
simultaneously testing the null hypotheses
H_{0j}:\theta_{j}=0,\,j\in\mathcal{J}
for any subset
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[d]
. Let
\{\hat{\theta}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
and

\{\widehat{V}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
be the proposed bias-corrected estimators (
2.16
) and their corresponding estimated variances (
2.18
) respectively for
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{e}_{j}
,
j\in\mathcal{J}
.

To control FWER at a level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the traditional method is to do a Bonferroni correction. This implies for any
j\in\mathcal{J}
, we reject the null hypothesis
H_{0j}
whenever
|T_{j}|\geq z_{\alpha/(2|\mathcal{J}|)}
, where
T_{j}=\hat{\theta}_{j}/\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{j}}
.
By the standard union bound, FWER can be controlled by

\mathrm{FWER}\leq|\mathcal{J}|\mathbb{P}_{H_{0j}}\!\left(|T_{j}|\geq z_{\alpha/(2|\mathcal{J}|)}\right)\leq\alpha.

However, if
|\mathcal{J}|
is too large, such a procedure might yield low power. Therefore one might use the bootstrap-based alternative cutoffs
[
17
,
22
]
popular in high-dimensional statistics.Since Bonferroni procedure is more conservative, it is often preferable to consider FDR control instead. A standard method in this regard is the modified Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) algorithm
[
4
]
which, for a carefully chosen cutoff
\kappa
, reject all the null hypothesis
H_{0j}
such that
|T_{j}|\geq\kappa
. If
\mathcal{J}_{0}
denote the subset of all
true
null hypothesis in the set
\mathcal{J}
, we have

\mathrm{FDR}(\kappa)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{0}}1\{|T_{j}|\geq\kappa\}}{\max\{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}1\{|T_{j}|\geq\kappa\},1\}}\right].

Here,
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}1\{|T_{j}|\geq\kappa\}
denote the total number of rejected hypothesis. By assuming number of true alternatives are sparse, the proportion of nulls falsely
rejected among all the true nulls at the threshold level
\kappa
,
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}_{0}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{0}}1\{|T_{j}|\geq\kappa\}
, can be approximated by standard normal tail

2-2\Phi(\kappa)
. This approximation implies that for
0<\alpha<1
, the choice of cutoff
\hat{\kappa}
is given by

\hat{\kappa}:=\inf\left\{0\leq\kappa\leq\sqrt{2\log|\mathcal{J}|-2\log\log|\mathcal{J}|}:\frac{|J|\{2-2\Phi(\kappa)\}}{\max\{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}1(|T_{j}|\geq\kappa),1\}}\leq\alpha\right\}

The range of
\kappa
and the proof that the above method controls FDR at level-
\alpha
follows the same steps as
[
38
,
52
]
as
n,|\mathcal{J}|\rightarrow\infty
.

2.3
Inference for quadratic functionals

Our method developed in Section
2.1
can be further extended for statistical inference of quadratic functional
Q=\bm{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\bm{\theta}
, for some symmetric positive definite
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The common choice of
\mathbf{M}
are
\mathbf{I}
or
\bm{\Sigma}=\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
. The quadratic form has been used often in applications related to heritability estimation in Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS)
[
41
,
62
]
. Often in literature, the estimation procedure of heritability relies on the
classical asymptotics that does not take into account the high-dimensionality of the SNPs compared to the sample sizes
[
14
]
or consider continuous traits
[
30
,
64
]
or does not consider dependence among phenotypes
[
40
]
. We address these gaps by providing inference under quadratic functionals under dependent observations.

Note that our first-step estimator
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
has the following bias:

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}-Q=2\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta})-(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{M}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}).

(2.21)

The second term in the RHS of the above display is of smaller order compared to the first term, which causes the choice of estimator
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
biased. However, the first summand can be expressed as the linear form
\mathbf{c}^{\top}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta})
for
\mathbf{c}^{\top}=2\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}
. Hence, by our construction of bias-corrected estimator of linear functional, we choose the projection vector
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{M}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{M}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}})
given by (
2.15
). Note that, here we choose the constrained set
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{M})
will be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\Big\|\frac{2}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})\bm{X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}u}-\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\Big\|_{\infty}\leq C_{1}\|\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}},

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{2}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}f^{\prime}(\tilde{v}_{i})(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}_{i})(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i})-\|\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\|^{2}_{2}\Big|\leq C_{2}\|\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\|^{2}_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}},

\displaystyle\max\limits_{i\in S_{2}}|\bm{X_{i}^{\top}u}|\leq C_{3}\|\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\log n}\Bigg\},

(2.22)

for some constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
. Given such a projection vector
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{M}
, we define the estimator of
Q
as
\widehat{Q}=\max(\tilde{Q},0)
where
\tilde{Q}
is given by:

\tilde{Q}=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{4}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}\Big[\bar{y_{i}}-f(\tilde{v}_{i})\Big]\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i},

where recall the definition of the function
f
from (
2.6
). Since
\mathbf{M}
is positive definite matrix, truncating at
0
yields a better estimator than using
\tilde{Q}
. Next, we estimate the variance of the estimator (conditional on
\mathbf{y}_{-S},\mathbf{y}_{S_{1}},\mathbf{X}
) by

\widehat{V}_{\mathbf{M}}=\frac{16}{|S_{2}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in S_{2}}f(\tilde{v}_{i})\big(1-f(\tilde{v}_{i})\big)(\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{n}.

(2.23)

Here we have added the quantity
1/n
as the upper bound of the second summand in (
2.21
); similar modification is common in classical i.i.d. setup
[
40
]
. As a consequence, the natural
(1-\alpha)
-level confidence interval can be given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{M}}=\left[\max\Big(\widehat{Q}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{\mathbf{M}}},0\Big),\widehat{Q}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{\mathbf{M}}}\right]

(2.24)

We want to highlight that throughout the section we have assumed that the matrix
\mathbf{M}
is known to the statistician. However, there might be applications where
\mathbf{M}
is unknown. For example, in population genetic, one needs to estimate heritability given by
Q=\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\theta}
, where the population covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
is unknown to a geneticist. In that case, we can plug-in an estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}
in our procedure to perform statistical inference; similar idea has been explored for i.i.d. datasets recently
[
30
,
40
]
. In the semi-supervised setting
[
56
]
, where additional unlabeled covariates are available, one can use them to construct an estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}
.

2.4
Theoretical guarantees

In this section, we provide theoretical justification behind our proposed estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
defined by (
2.16
). We begin by stating our assumptions on the interaction tensors
\mathbf{A}_{n}
, the regression parameter
\bm{\theta}
and the covariate distribution
\mathbb{P}_{X}
. For any vertex
i\in[n]
of the hypergraph
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, we define its neighbors by

\mathcal{N}_{i}:=\left\{j\in[n]:\exists\mathbf{e}\in E\quad\text{such that}\quad i,j\in\mathbf{e}\right\}

(2.25)

Assumption 2.1
.

The hypergraph
\mathbf{G}_{n}
satisfies bounded degree condition
\sup_{n}\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{\mathbf{e}:i\in\mathbf{e}}g_{\mathbf{e}}\leq 1
and
\sup_{n}\max_{i\in[n]}|\mathcal{N}_{i}|=\Delta<\infty
.

Assumption 2.2
.

The regression coefficients
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
\|\bm{\theta}\|_{0}=s
,
\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}\leq C
for some
C>0
.

Assumption 2.3
.

The covariates
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are i.i.d. sub-Gaussian vectors with bounded sub-Gaussian norm
C_{1}>0
. Define by
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
the covariance matrix of the covariates. There exists
\kappa>0
independent of
n
and
d
such that

\frac{1}{\kappa}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq\kappa.

Note that, the assumptions on the regression parameters and covariates are standard in high-dimensional statistics literature
[
13
]
. In Assumption
2.1
, we assume the underlying tensor to be sparse. This is required to obtain the strong-independent set
S(\mathbf{G}_{n})
large. Indeed if the maximum size of the neighborhoods
\max_{i}|\mathcal{N}_{i}|\leq\Delta
, then the graph contains a strong-independent set of size at least
\lfloor n/(\Delta+1)\rfloor
. Further, one can efficiently find such a strong-independent set of size
\Omega(n)
by a greedy algorithm. Therefore, we will assume throughout that the set used to construct our estimator satisfies

|S(\mathbf{G}_{n})|=\Omega(n).

(2.26)

We are now ready to state our first theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose the Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
hold and
V
is defined by (
2.17
). If
s=o\Big(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{\log n}\log d}\Big)
and
\lambda_{n}=C\sqrt{\log d/n}
for some
C>0
, then we have

\frac{(\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta})}{\sqrt{\widehat{V}}}\xrightarrow{D}Z,

(2.27)

where
Z\sim N(0,1)
and
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
is defined by (
2.16
).

The result characterizes the precise asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
. The asymptotic variance involves the quantity
V
which is generally unknown to the statistician. As we will show in Proposition
2.1
below, replacing
V
with its natural estimator
\widehat{V}
defined by (
2.18
) yields a valid confidence interval. Theorem
2.1
contributes to the long line of literature of inference in generalized linear models
[
59
,
63
,
15
,
29
,
34
]
. However, to our knowledge, ours is the first theorem to provide inferential guarantees under network dependence models in high dimension. Further we note that many of the existing methods of statistical inference requires boundedness on the individual probability, i.e., assumes there exists
\mu\in(0,0.5)
such that
\mathbb{P}(y_{i}=1|\mathbf{X}_{i})\in(\mu,1-\mu)

[
50
,
54
]
. However, the current work does not posit such conditions. Given our tensor dependence among the subjects, the individual probabilities can get arbitrarily close to
0
or
1
.

Our next result provides the asymptotic coverage guarantee of the confidence interval
\mathcal{C}
defined by
2.19
, along with an upper bound on the length of the confidence interval.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then for any
0<\alpha<1
, we have

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C})\geq 1-\alpha,

where
\mathcal{C}
is defined by (
2.19
). Further, defining
L(\mathcal{C})
to be the length of the confidence interval, we have for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\Big(L(\mathcal{C})\geq(1+\delta)2z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{V}\Big)=0,

where
V
is defined by (
2.17
).

Similar to traditional i.i.d. settings
[
3
,
59
]
, the confidence interval proposed here is agnostic of true sparsity level and remains valid for sparsity level
o(\sqrt{n})
, ignoring the logarithmic factors. Further, note that if
\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}=O(1)
, then with high probability the length of the confidence interval is
O(n^{-1/2})
, matching with the optimal interval length in the classical i.i.d. response setting. We note that for quadratic functionals, the theoretical justification of the confidence interval
\mathcal{C}_{M}
defined by (
2.24
) holds by the same argument.

A natural consequence of our result is the validity of hypothesis testing using the test-statistic constructed in Section
2.2
. Consider the hypothesis testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}:\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}\leq 0,\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}=\upsilon n^{-1/2},

(2.28)

for some
\upsilon>0
. We consider the test which rejects
H_{0}
if
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}\geq z_{\alpha}\sqrt{\widehat{V}}
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}
and
\sqrt{\widehat{V}}
are defined by (
2.16
) and (
2.18
) respectively. We have the following theoretical guarantee for this testing procedure as an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.1
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Consider the hypothesis testing problem given by (
2.28
) for some
0<\alpha<1
, we have
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}(\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}\geq z_{\alpha}\sqrt{\widehat{V}})\leq\alpha
for any
\bm{\theta}\in H_{0}
. Further, for any
\bm{\theta}\in H_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\theta}}\geq z_{\alpha}\sqrt{\widehat{V}}\right)-\left\{1-\Phi\left(z_{\alpha}-\frac{\upsilon}{\sqrt{nV}}\right)\right\}\right|=0.

Proposition
2.2
implies that our testing procedure has the desired type-I error. For any
\upsilon>0
, the result also provides the precise asymptotic power of the testing procedure. Note that by the definition of
V
in (
2.17
), we have
\sqrt{nV}/\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}
both upper and lower bounded. Therefore if we have
\upsilon/\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}\rightarrow\infty
, the asymptotic power will be exactly
1
.

The performance of the bias-corrected estimator hinges on the estimation of the regularized pseudo-likelihood based estimator
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
which is summarized in the following result.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that assumptions of Theorem
2.1
hold, and
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
is defined by (
2.3
). If we select the regularization parameter
\lambda_{n}=C\sqrt{\log d/n}
in (
2.3
) for some
C>0
,

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2}=O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{s\log d}{n}}\bigg)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}\|_{1}=O\bigg(s\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}\bigg),

with probability
1-o(1)
as
n\to\infty
and
d\to\infty
such that
s\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}=o(1)
.

Our result achieves the same estimation error rate as the Lasso estimator in classical high-dimensional settings
[
13
]
. The key distinctions between Proposition
2.3
and
[
46

lie in the model and estimation framework: our analysis accommodates general hypergraph-induced dependence and sub-Gaussian covariates, thereby broadening the applicability of the result. Moreover, to enable bias correction, we employ a maximum pseudolikelihood estimator constructed on a strong independent set. These features make our proof different from that of
[
46
, Theorem 2.9]
, and we provide the proof of Proposition
2.3
in the Appendix.

4
Discussion and future directions

Our work develops a statistical inference framework for logistic regression in the presence of network dependence, where the dependence structure is induced by a hypergraph. Moving beyond consistent estimation problems in Markov random fields with pairwise (quadratic) interactions, we establish inferential guarantees for both linear and quadratic functionals in tensor MRFs. Our results contribute to the broader literature on inference in dependent data settings and suggest several promising directions for future research. We highlight three such directions below.

First, our current framework focuses on sparse networks, which facilitates the identification of strong independent sets. An important open question is how to extend these ideas to dense graphs. Although estimation and testing problems in dense Ising models have been studied extensively in the past decade
[
11
,
44
,
45
]
, these works typically do not incorporate high-dimensional covariates. Developing inference procedures that simultaneously account for dense dependence structures and high-dimensional covariates remains an interesting challenge. Second, the estimation of causal effects under network interference has attracted considerable recent attention
[
55
,
12
]
. These works, however, focus on settings without high-dimensional covariates. Our proposed framework naturally suggests a way to incorporate high-dimensional predictors and may provide inferential guarantees for both direct and spillover effects in such causal inference problems. Extending our bias-correction and projection techniques to this setting would be an important contribution. Finally, we have restricted our analysis to binary data. It would be of substantial interest to extend our framework to general network-assisted generalized linear models. In particular, it remains to investigate how the construction of the projection vector (cf. (
2.15
)) can be generalized to accommodate more general responses and link functions beyond the logistic. Such extensions would further broaden the applicability of our approach to a wide range of network-dependent data structures.
