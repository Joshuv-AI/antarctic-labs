Access to care for adults living with spinal muscular atrophy in the UK

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an autosomal recessive neuromuscular disorder resulting from progressive degeneration and loss of motor neurones in the spinal cord. Current standards of care guidelines focus on a multidisciplinary approach and include recommendations for nine different aspects of care. Although intended for use in all patients with SMA, the guidelines are focused on paediatric best practices and evidence regarding care provision in adults with SMA remains limited. This cross-sectional analysis of a longitudinal registry cohort of adults with SMA study aimed to evaluate the clinical features and corresponding care provision to assess alignment with current care guidelines. Data from 426 patients with genetically confirmed SMA were analysed, including information on respiratory function, bulbar involvement, musculoskeletal complications and daily living support. Results demonstrated a high prevalence of respiratory impairment, bulbar dysfunction, contractures and significant limitations in activities of daily living. However, the care provision observed in this adult cohort did not consistently reflect the recommended standards outlined in the established SMA standards of care recommendations. In particular, gaps were noted in access to respiratory support, physiotherapy and nutritional management. These findings suggest that the application of current standards of care to the adult population is inconsistent. There is a need for improved translation of care provision into adult services to ensure comprehensive and equitable management of SMA across the lifespan.

Introduction

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an autosomal recessive neuromuscular disorder (NMD) resulting from progressive degeneration and loss of motor neurones in the spinal cord.1 Commonly, this occurs due to disruption of the survival motor neuron (SMN1) gene by genetic variations.1 Severity and phenotype are inversely correlated to SMN2 copy number; higher copy number corresponds to milder disease.24 However, there is a large degree of heterogeneity in the clinical presentation depending on factors including age of onset, highest motor milestone achieved, SMN2 copy number and prognosis.

In 2017, standards of care (SOC) for diagnosis and management of SMA were published.5 6 These guidelines highlighted a multidisciplinary approach with recommendations for nine aspects of care.5 6 These guidelines are referenced internationally for care optimisation and clinical trial standardisation. Although applicable to all SMA patients, these SOC primarily focus on paediatric best practices which may not correspond to needs of adult patients.7 There is also lack of clarity about the degree of implementation and adherence to these standards globally. An international survey of healthcare providers caring for patients with SMA highlighted a large degree of variability in access to genetic testing, newborn screening and types of disease-modifying therapy (DMTs) available to patients.8 The economic burden of diagnostic testing and treatments, as well as difficulties with insurance coverage and medical infrastructure were the main reasons contributing to these global discrepancies.8 It is essential to identify and act on barriers to care to ensure patients with SMA are receiving an appropriate level of care globally as well as within the UK.

A recent study assessed access to SOC for patients with SMA in the UK.9 Discrepancies were found between recommendations and access to resources, notably between paediatrics and adults. Children have increased access to specialised services, including neurology, physiotherapy, occupational therapy (OT), dietetics and speech and language therapy (SALT).9 Results from this paper and the notion that SOC are based on paediatric best practices prompts questions about clinical phenotypes seen in adults with SMA, as well as access to and provision of adequate and appropriate care.

Adult SMAREACH is a multicentre longitudinal observational study which started in 2022. It collects data from patients with SMA during routine clinical visits from eighteen sites in the UK. The study includes adult patients aged sixteen and older with genetically confirmed 5q SMA. Data are collected at 6-monthly intervals for treated patients (ie, taking Risdiplam or Nusinersen) and 12-monthly intervals for non-treated patients according to Managed Access Agreement requirements. Given the high uptake of DMTs in the UK, the majority of patients in SMAREACH are considered ‘treated’.9 10 Currently, SMAREACH has recruited over 400 participants and continues to support the standardised assessment and management of adults with SMA. In this paper, we assess care provision for this patient cohort, taking into consideration the phenotypic variability in this population.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional analysis using data from Adult SMAREACH, a longitudinal registry of adult patients with SMA. Demographic data, clinical data and information about access to/use of management/intervention were included. Patient characteristics were collected via questionnaires, physiotherapy and medical assessments. For analytical purposes, patients were categorised by functional status: non-sitter, sitter and walker. Non-sitters were unable to sit without support. Sitters could sit independently for at least ten seconds but were unable to walk. Walkers were able to walk at least five steps independently.

Patients were assessed and asked about access to corresponding management based on clinical presentation. Clinical data included respiratory function, bulbar function, contractures, scoliosis and independence in activities of daily living (ADLs). Further data included exercise, living arrangements and vocational status.

Respiratory and bulbar function

Respiratory function was assessed via forced vital capacity (FVC) and peak cough flow (PCF) obtained by neuromuscular physiotherapists or nurse specialists using standardised protocols. FVC and PCF measurements were obtained from sitting; per cent predicted (FVCpp) values were calculated based on age, sex and height (standing height or arm span for non-ambulant patients). FVCpp and PCF were categorised into groups based on severity.6 11 Information about use of ventilatory support and/or cough augmentation was obtained.

Bulbar function and corresponding management were assessed via clinical examination and patient-reported data.

Scoliosis and contractures

Presence of scoliosis and contractures, with corresponding management, was reported. Contractures were categorised by physiotherapists according to impairment for common tasks: mild for no influence on function, moderate for some impact on function or severe for contractures making tasks impossible or extremely difficult. Patient-reported information provided information about stretching regime and orthotic prescription.

ADLs, living arrangements, vocational status and exercise

Patient-reported information was collected about the remaining variables of interest, including ADLs, living arrangements and vocational status.

The Rapid Assessment of Physical Activity (RAPA) assessed level of physical activity. RAPA 1 provided information about current aerobic exercise, and RAPA2 included data about strengthening and stretching.12 Results from the RAPA were combined with self-reported explanations of patients’ current exercise plans to give a picture of type, intensity and frequency of exercise.

Data completeness

Data completeness was calculated for each variable to ensure adequate interpretation of data and statistical analyses. If data were missing or unknown, this was documented as ‘unknown’ to calculate data completeness. This provided a true representation of the available data used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variable distributions were summarised; the number (n) and percentage (%) were reported by stratification of subgroups based on motor function. Categorical variables were compared according to functional status using Pearson’s χ2 test to assess whether distributions differed between groups. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to determine which groups contributed to significant differences. To address potential type 1 and 2 statistical errors and confirm robustness of χ2 results, Fisher’s exact test was applied when cell counts were <5. Given the exploratory nature and limited number of comparisons, unadjusted p values are reported.

Results

426 patients were included; 47.1% were female. Average age was 37 years. At last visit, most (51.9%) patients had SMA type III, followed closely by SMA type II (44.1%). Only one patient was documented as having SMA type IV. When considering functional status, most (41.6%) were non-sitters, followed by sitters (40.1%). Table 1 illustrates that most non-sitters (73.4%) had SMA type II, whereas most sitters (63.7%) and all walkers had type III or IV. Most patients (91.5%) were using a DMT. Similar trends were seen with regard to PCF. Most non-sitters had low PCF measurements of <270 L/min and used cough augmentation devices as management, whereas most sitters and walkers had a good cough when measured via PCF.

Bulbar function

Relatively few patients reported problems with bulbar function, including chewing and swallowing. However, those who did report difficulties were most often non-sitters, reflecting their limited functional ability. Management for bulbar dysfunction largely focused on assessment with SALT and implementation of assistive devices such as Therabite. Due to lack of data completeness, assessment of the remaining patients with documented swallowing difficulties was unclear (table 2).

Given their lack of functional and ambulatory abilities, it was unsurprising to see that contractures were common and relatively severe in non-sitters and sitters. Some walkers (20.6%) also reported contractures; however, these tended to be milder. Upper and lower limbs were affected across functional groups (figure 2). Knees were the most affected joint in non-ambulant patients, whereas ankles were most commonly affected in walkers. Shoulders and elbows were most commonly affected in the upper limbs.

Stretching and orthotic use are common approaches for contracture management. However, only a small proportion of the cohort reported stretching for more than an hour a week, and very few had an orthotic prescription.

OT and formal care

Access to OT varied by functional status as expected, with most non-sitters (57.1%) and sitters (71.1%) reporting regular access to OT compared with walkers (41.9%), suggesting reduced service utilisation among more functionally able individuals online supplemental table 1.

Interpretation of access to formal care was limited by low response rates (20.4%). Among those who responded, most had access to one or two carers, indicating modest levels of formal support.

Social situation

Living arrangements reflected functional status. Non-sitters (68.1%) and sitters (49.4%) were more likely to live with formal or informal support, whereas walkers predominantly lived with informal support (51.0%) or independently, with very few relying on formal care (online supplemental table 2).

Vocational status also differed by functional group. Walkers were more likely to be in full-time employment (52.1%). Sitters and non-sitters were more evenly distributed in terms of their vocational status, with higher rates of unemployment and educational engagement reported. Ultimately, greater functional ability was associated with increased workforce participation and independence.

Exercise

Physical activity levels were low across functional groups, with the majority of patients (48.2%) scoring a one on the RAPA 1, indicating minimal activity. Only a small proportion reported being engaged in regular physical activity (online supplemental table 3).

Most non-sitters and sitters reported no weekly strength or stretching exercise, although such inactivity remained substantial even among walkers. Of those who engaged in exercise, strength training was more commonly reported than stretching or combined stretching and strength.

Discussion

With the implementation of DMTs, more patients with SMA are reaching adulthood. However, information about access to SOC within this population is limited.7 9 13 This study aimed to describe the clinical phenotype of a group of adults with SMA, understand care provision, and identify management gaps.

Unsurprisingly, given the autosomal recessive inheritance of SMA, there were no significant differences in gender. Most patients within our cohort had SMA type III, followed by type II. Across the cohort, disease duration correlated with progression of weakness, illustrated through the majority of the cohort being non-sitters followed closely by sitters. Most patients were treated with a DMT as expected.

Respiratory function differed significantly among functional groups and corresponded to disease severity, indicating that patients with more severe disease had a higher incidence of respiratory impairment.14 15 Current SOC suggest that symptomatic respiratory impairment and/or FVCpp <50% are criteria to initiate non-invasive ventilation (NIV) in patients with NMDs,11 16 and patients with FVCpp <40% should be initiated on NIV regardless of symptoms.6 11 Moreover, current guidelines suggest that reduced PCF (<270 L/min) should be managed with a combination of chest physiotherapy and mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MI/E). These should be available to all non-sitters and introduced proactively to prevent respiratory impairment and infection.6 In our cohort, there were clear gaps which did not match the recommended prescription of NIV, chest physiotherapy and MI/E. Of those with reduced respiratory function, a significant number reported limited or complete lack of access to ventilatory support and cough augmentation. Given the clinical significance of respiratory morbidity in SMA and the fact that this is an aspect of SMA that has been identified by the patient community as a priority to be improved,17 it is concerning that management does not appear to match clinical need in the adult SMA population. Adequate respiratory care prevents the evolution of chest wall deformities and thoraco-abdominal asynchrony, prevents serious complications of respiratory tract infections, and enhances long-term respiratory health and function.14 Therefore, it is essential that patients are being managed accordingly. It is unclear why individuals with reduced respiratory function are not receiving care as outlined in the SOC, but possible reasons for this include regional disparities in access to specialised equipment and/or inadequate transitions between paediatric and adult care.9

Conversely, some individuals with relatively preserved (>60%) or well-preserved (>80%) respiratory function appeared to have access to and use of NIV. An evidence-based and equitable approach to ventilatory support is urgently needed for adults living with SMA in the UK.

Bulbar dysfunction contributes to communication and feeding difficulties in patients with SMA. This may result in malnutrition, aspiration, pneumonia and life-threatening sequelae.18 19 A relatively small proportion of non-sitters in our cohort reported bulbar dysfunction, and cases reported in sitters were anecdotal. These results are significantly lower than other studies,20 possibly because information on bulbar dysfunction in our cohort was self-reported rather than formally assessed, meaning symptoms and subsequent care provision may be underreported. These results highlight the need for better and more consistent assessment of bulbar function in the adult SMA population. Bulbar dysfunction in SMA is usually managed in a conservative manner, with SALT assessment, regular monitoring, diet modification, and jaw rehabilitation (eg, Therabite). If these approaches are unsuccessful, gastrostomy or nasogastric feeding may be considered.5 Among those with bulbar dysfunction in our cohort, most were managed according to SOC, regardless of functional group.

Presence of scoliosis followed the expected pattern, with more severe types having more frequent development and rapid progression.21 22 Surgical intervention was the most common form of management. This reflects current SOC which suggest that spinal instrumentation is indicated to support trunk stability, restore thoracic alignment for respiratory function, and improve quality of life.5 This finding also reflects the expected limited presence of conservative management of scoliosis in the adult population.

In SMA, muscle weakness causes limited range of movement, static positioning, and fibrotic changes, ultimately resulting in contractures.23 Considering the natural progression of the disease, we expected that contractures would be more prevalent and severe in adults compared with paediatrics. Moreover, we expected that the presence of contractures would be highest among non-sitters and lowest among walkers.2426 Despite loss of ambulation among both non-sitters and sitters, significantly more non-sitters had contractures, which is a recognised phenomenon within SMA.25 Current SOC suggest frequent stretching and daily orthotic use to promote function and range of movement in patients with contractures.5 They also recommend stretching for segments at risk of contracture development (eg, hip, knee and ankle, wrist and hand).5 Despite a high frequency of contractures in our cohort, stretching and orthotic use was low. This is distinct from the paediatric population where a more prevalent use of orthoses and a reduced presence of contractures have been described.2427 Low rates of contracture management may be due to limited access to physiotherapy input compared with the paediatric population9 or because patients perceive that their contractures do not have a significant impact on their function or quality of life.28 Further research is required to better understand the factors contributing to the lack of contracture management in adults with SMA, particularly barriers related to patient preferences and access to physiotherapy services and equipment.

Findings relating to ADLs highlight the significant burden of disease associated with SMA across all phenotypes, with increasing dependence observed in those with more severe disease. The higher reliance on full-time assistance among non-sitters, and to a lesser extent sitters, underscores the progressive nature of the disease and the impact of motor impairment on daily function. The differences in living arrangements and formal care provision further highlight that care needs are not only greater in more severe phenotypes, but may also require formalised support systems. Conversely, the greater independence among walkers likely reflects preserved functional ability and reduced need for external support. These patterns reflect the literature and reinforce the relationship between disease severity and functional dependence.29 Such disability impacts quality of life and hinders engagement in professional and social activities.30 Furthermore, greater care needs in more severe phenotypes likely contribute to a substantial caregiver burden, with personal, professional and economic impacts.31

Regular OT input is recommended to enhance patients’ abilities to complete ADLs.5 6 32 Just over half our cohort reported access to OT on a regular basis or when required, reflecting recent research.9 However, receipt of OT in adults is significantly less than paediatrics, signifying an area of need.9 Proper referral and implementation of OT may help ease patient and caregiver burden and improve quality of life.

Current guidelines recommend regular exercise via hydrotherapy, aerobics and general conditioning, depending on SMA type and functional ability.5 This advice is supported by literature suggesting that exercise is safe and may contribute to improved muscle strength, function and overall fitness.33 34 The consistently low activity levels across our cohort suggest a substantial gap between SOC and real-world practice. Differences in aerobic activity among non-sitters and sitters, compared with walkers, were expected given functional limitations. However, the low levels of engagement across all groups indicate additional barriers to exercise beyond functional ability. Notably, the lack of difference in stretching and strength exercise across functional groups supports the notion that inactivity is not solely driven by disease severity. This raises questions about whether current guidance is effectively being communicated to patients or whether further barriers, such as limited access to exercise programmes, lack of specialist guidance or fear of overexertion, are limiting participation. Evidence suggests exercise is safe and beneficial for patients with SMA; however, our findings suggest that reassurance in the literature is not sufficient to create behaviour change in patients. Our results align with previous research which highlights limited exercise exposure for people living with NMD.9 35 Addressing this gap will likely require improved access to personalised interventions, clearer guidance for both clinicians and patients, and a better understanding of patient-perceived barriers.

Significant disease burden, physical disability and reliance on caregivers make employment difficult for adults with SMA.29 Employment rates in our cohort reinforce the socioeconomic impact of the disease, with just less than half our cohort being employed, reflecting other studies.29 36 37 The differences in employment among non-sitters and sitters, compared with walkers, likely reflect physical limitations as well as structural barriers in the workplace. Unemployment was prevalent across all functional groups, suggesting that challenges extend beyond disease severity. Although not assessed in this study, previous research indicates that among patients who are employed, work productivity lost due to having SMA is significant.29 Taken together, these findings highlight a gap between functional capacity and workplace inclusion, suggesting there is a need for reasonable adjustments to support sustained professional success.38

The UK National Health Service (NHS) is unique in that it is a centralised system that provides comprehensive healthcare that is free to all residents at the point of delivery. Despite being highly regarded for equity worldwide, there are instances in which the NHS struggles to provide optimal care, especially in the realm of rare diseases. The recent ‘England Rare Diseases Action Plan 2026’ highlights that diagnostic delays remain long, care is fragmented, and access to specialist care and treatment remains a challenge.39 Adult patients with SMA undoubtedly face such difficulties with accessing diagnostics and care, and results from our cohort highlight these challenges. Increased education for frontline healthcare professionals about rare diseases, improved access to specialist diagnostics and management, and coordinated transitions from paediatric to adult services will help enhance care for adult patients with SMA moving forward.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inference and makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about care provision in the UK SMA adult population. Moreover, we acknowledge that we did not adjust for confounding variables and maintained unadjusted p-values without correction, increasing the risk of type I error. Moreover, we did not perform multivariable analyses to identify predictors of care gaps; we recognise this as something to consider in future analyses on this dataset. Another major limitation of this study was variable data completeness across data points, especially when comparing symptom presence and severity with associated care provision. For example, respiratory function data were approximately 50% complete, whereas access to ventilatory support was near 100%, largely due to logistical difficulties with spirometry follow-up. In contrast, for variables such as bulbar function, symptom prevalence was well documented, while data on management were less complete. This is likely because symptom presence was clinician-reported instead of being formally assessed. This variability may introduce bias; however, analyses were only considered conclusive when group representation was proportional to the overall study population.

Conclusions

This was a large cohort study providing information about clinical phenotype, care provision and unmet needs for adults with SMA in the UK. A more nuanced understanding of the phenotypic heterogeneity of adults with SMA is valuable for clinicians managing the disease and post-marketing surveillance for approved DMTs. A well-established SOC exists for SMA. However, our findings and previous research suggest that many adult patients do not receive recommended multidisciplinary management, especially compared with their paediatric counterparts.5 6 9 Insights from our study can be used to coordinate a systematic approach to address gaps in care provision among adults with SMA. Future work is needed to explore barriers to care from clinician, patient and caregiver perspectives.
