Executive Summary
 This final report summarizes the developments in field assisted sintering technology (FAST)
for rapid fabrication of ceramic matrix composite (CMC) materials with integrated environmental
barrier coatings (EBCs), as well as their performance in a representative gas turbine combustor
environment including stagnant flame zones, and high velocity product zones. CMCs are a
possible high temperature tolerant material system for gas turbine engines, which are currently
the dominant source of electric power production and the primary source of aircraft propulsion.
However, CMCs are costly and time-consuming (on the order of weeks to months) to fabricate,
and CMCs based on silicon carbide (SiC) ceramics are susceptible to water vapor degradation
requiring the use of specialized EBCs. The manufacturing technique known as FAST can create
consolidated parts in as little as a few hours using high pressure and temperature, and may be
useful to integrate EBCs into CMCs directly during consolidation.
 The project consisted of five major tasks, consisting of baseline and optimized CMC and EBC
development using FAST; design and fabrication of a combustion test rig; operability of the test
rig with various types of fuels including high hydrogen content; exposure of CMCs to combustion
conditions; and evaluation of degradation mechanisms of the CMCs.
 On the materials development front, the team developed layup processes that would result in
high densification of the CMC and identified time/temperature requirements for the FAST process
to optimize density of the final parts without causing degradation of the matrix and fibers. The
team also identified the process requirements to get optimal conventional (mullite-based) and
next-generation oxide EBC materials (hafnia/ytterbium disilicate based), and steps required to
integrate these coatings directly with the CMC material. From the materials development, several
1” diameter and 1/8” thick test coupons for combustion testing were created: monolithic silicon
carbide (SiC), uncoated SiC/SiC CMCs, and EBC-coated SiC/SiC CMCs.
 The other major development in this project was a high temperature exposure facility that
mimicked conditions that would be found in gas turbine engine combustors and would be highly
fuel flexible, including up to 100% hydrogen fuel. To ensure flame stability, the facility was
designed as a trapped vortex combustor, where a flame sits in a cavity in the flowpath. CMC test
articles were attached to the bottom of the cavity where they were exposed to low velocity active
combustion, as well as downstream of the cavity where they were subjected to high velocity mixed
combustion products. These regions simulate the range of flow conditions in a typical combustor.
Novel instrumentation was designed for the material test articles, including an in-situ heat flux
measurement and through-flame infrared thermography for top-side surface temperatures of the
articles.
 After subjecting the material test coupons to combustion temperatures for over 100 hours,
they were analyzed using a variety of techniques, including micro X-ray computed tomography
(CT), optical profilometry for recession/surface roughness, X-ray diffractometry (XRD), and cross-
sectional microscope imaging. From this, it was determined that the uncoated CMCs had
somewhat minor recession, likely requiring a longer test time or higher water vapor content. The
thin EBC-coated CMCs also had limited recession and robustly protected the underlying SiC/SiC
substrate from exposure. However, for the thicker modified coating architecture, extensive
spallation was observed due to higher thermomechanical stresses that built up during repeated
on/off cycles from the combustion testing and led to increased defect evolution (e.g., cracks).
 Overall, this project successfully created EBC-coated CMCs using the FAST process and
showed that they would survive representative combustor conditions for the time subjected.
Future work should increase the residence time of the materials to map longer-term degradation
mechanisms and identify pathways to reduce themomechanical stress between thick coatings
and the base CMC. Generating more complex shapes for the CMC using FAST, as well as
methods of providing cooling to CMCs to enable even higher temperature operation, would benefit
the maturation of this technology to improve gas turbine engine efficiency.

Project Objectives
 The overall objective of this project was to significantly improve the temperature performance
by at least 150°C of ceramic matrix composite (CMC) materials in high-hydrogen environments
using field assisted sintering technology (FAST). FAST is a relatively new material synthesis
technology that allows for novel materials to be processed at significantly shorter fabrication times
by using low voltage/high electrical currents to drive rapid, high-intensity heating.
 The above objective was achieved by the following methodology. FAST-derived CMCs in
conjunction with in-situ advanced ceramic coatings was developed to adapt to higher combustor
temperatures with higher humidity contents as a result of the high-hydrogen fuel. In conjunction,
a combustion test facility was designed to obtain detailed characterization of water vapor content
and temperature of the gases near the samples as well as local heat fluxes that are important for
material degradation modeling boundary conditions. The fabricated samples were tested at a
range of combustor operating conditions with up to 100% hydrogen fuel to understand the impact
of different CMC formulations and combustion gas environments on the material performance.
Analytical characterization and testing of the microstructure and mechanical properties was done
to observe the impact that up to 100% hydrogen environments can have on the lifetime and
performance of the fabricated CMCs.
 The outcome of this project was a lab scale demonstration that the FAST-based
manufacturing technology can enable in-situ coated high density silicon carbide (SiC) based CMC
parts with superior water vapor recession resistance, such that their upper use temperature can
significantly exceed that of state-of-the-art nickel superalloys and conventional SiC/SiC CMCs.
These results enable an increase in efficiency and reduction in fuel required per unit of power
output for combined cycle combustion turbines which use hydrogen as a zero-carbon fuel source.

Task 1.2 – Technology Maturation Plan
Technology Readiness Assessment System
 The system for which this research was conducted is a power generation gas turbine engine.
These engines supply a large portion of U.S. electricity currently and are projected to continue to
be critical to the electricity infrastructure of the U.S. Power generation gas turbine engines consist
of four major components: a compressor that brings in air and increases its pressure; a combustor
that burns fuel with the compressed air to generate hot high pressure gas; a turbine that extracts
energy from the gas to drive the compressor and any other loads; and an electric generator or
some other mechanical load that is the desired output from the engine. The components that
make up the combustor and turbine, referred to as the hot section, are subjected to extreme

 4

temperatures that exceed the melting point of the current state of the art superalloy materials, so
internal cooling and special coatings are necessary to prevent premature degradation. The intent
of this study is to mature high temperature tolerant materials such as ceramic matrix composites
(CMCs), which can have a maximum use temperature at least 200K higher than superalloys.
These materials unfortunately are very costly and time-consuming to fabricate and have severe
recession in high water vapor environments such as found in combustion products of gas turbines.
 The current project is investigating the hot section subsystem of a gas turbine engine;
specifically components that might be found in the combustor section such as the combustor liner
that contains the hot gases.
Target Commercial Application
 The target commercial application for the environmental barrier coated CMCs are gas turbine
engine hot section components. The thermal efficiency of gas turbines can be improved by
increasing the combustor exit temperatures, which increases the thermal loads that must be
sustained by the hot-section materials. Furthermore, gas turbines are able to operate on a variety
of fuels including new fuels being investigated in the U.S. and abroad; some of these fuels
(particularly hydrogen) can increase the water vapor content in the combustion products
noticeably which increases degradation rates for silicon carbide materials. In this work, we
address both goals of efficiency and fuel flexibility through the use of CMCs, which are very high-
temperature materials. These CMCs must be coated with environmental barrier coatings in order
to survive the high-H2O environments present in combustion, particularly for hydrogen fuels.
Initial Technology Readiness Level
 At the start of this work, the method of constructing ceramic matrix composites (CMC) and
integrated coatings was at a Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of 2. The field-assisted sintering
technology (FAST) has been used previously for CMC layups, meaning that the “basic principles
have been observed,” but now the application to realistic configurations “must be invented,” to
quote the TRL definitions provided by the DOE in Appendix F of the Funding Opportunity
Announcement (FOA). In particular, the combination of the CMC and environmental barrier
coatings that are manufactured together using FAST will be an extension of the basic technology
into an application for gas turbine engines.
Proposed Research
 The proposed work seeks to achieve two goals. First, we seek to show that we can lay up and
rapidly manufacture materials with environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) directly applied to CMC
substrates through the use of the FAST manufacturing process. In this way, we are able to make
a higher-temperature, more robust material system in a fraction of the time. Second, we seek to
test these materials in combusting environments with a range of fuels, from 100% natural gas to
100% hydrogen, to test their behavior in these adverse environments. The goal is to achieve
improvement in temperature performance by 150-200 °C in an environment that is hotter than
1500 °C and 15% environmental water content, per the requirements of the original FOA.
 The performance of these new materials will be tested in a combustion environment. The
combustor is a fuel-flexible design that will allow for materials to be placed in two different types
of flow fields – one with high levels of recirculation and one with high levels of velocity – to mimic
the realistic flow, temperature, and chemical conditions that these materials will be subject to in
power-generation gas turbine engines. Detailed measurements of the boundary conditions
around the materials will be made during testing and then the material performance will be
assessed using a variety of techniques, including surface imaging with high-resolution
microscopy, nano-CT imaging, and mechanical failure testing.
 Showing improvement in material properties through these tests will move the technology
from a TRL of 2 to a TRL of 3 during the project. At TRL of 3, we would achieve a proof of concept
using “laboratory-scale studies to physically validate the analytical predictions of separate

 5

elements of the technology,” per the FOA. In these cases, we would be testing the robustness of
the material to environmental stresses in reacting environments, which have not been tested
before for these materials or others like it. Close coordination with our industry partners,
particularly the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI), will help to ensure that the environments
tested mimic the environments that are expected in next-generation gas turbine engines.
Potential Post-Project Maturation and Commercialization
 The post-project work that would be necessary to attain a TRL of 4 would be manufacturing
of these materials into a combustor panel configuration, rather than the test coupons that will be
made for this particular study. The combustor panel would be tested in a more realistic combustor
configuration, with real fuel-injection hardware and potentially cooling features that would normally
be found in a gas turbine combustor liner. This configuration is not being tested in the current
program for two reasons. First, we have not proven the technology enough to know whether these
materials will work well in combusting environments, and so smaller scale demonstration is
necessary. Second, this material is extremely expensive and so it is both scientifically and
financially prudent to test the materials at a small scale before trying to manufacture a larger
component. Proving the material capability at the bench-scale in the current work will provide an
important foundation for the development of more realistic geometries.
 Note that the authors of this report submitted a grant application to the Department of Energy
(DE-FOA-003016) in December 2023 to develop capability to create film cooling in CMC materials
using the FAST process, which would mature the technology closer to TRL 4. As of this writing,
the grant was under contracting, which has not yet been completed.

Task 2: CMC development with FAST
Task 2.1 – Baseline Fabrication
 In this subtask, raw materials were procured and characterized, and necessary studies of
densification based on FAST process parameters was conducted for monolithic SiC. Several
initial trials of construction were conducted using carbon fiber analog (to avoid using up the
expensive SiC fibers), which identified promising pathways for composite assembly consistent
with the FAST process. Optimal FAST fabrication settings were identified to create SiC/SiC
composites with distinguishable fibers, that were eventually used to create test coupons for the
combustion facility.

woven cloth (Ceramic Grade Nicalon™ 8HS & Hi-Nicalon™ Type S OX 8HS (NLS107 8HS)) was

orientation of the fibers. Furthermore, based on the apparent pore/fiber gap size, it was
determined that SiC matrix particles less than 2 μm are necessary to infiltrate the interstices of
the SiC fibers.

 SiC powder was also procured and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to
observe the powder particle size, distribution, and morphology (Figure 2). Through SEM analysis
the SiC particles ranged in size from approximately 2.5-5µm. Likewise, based on laser diffraction

measurements, the as-received SiC powder had a Dv50 of ~2 μm, suggesting that minimal
processing was needed to reduce particles into the desired size for interstitial packing.
 To further reduce SiC particle size for infiltration, preliminary ball milling efforts were
performed with cumulative milling studies from 24-72 hrs using dry and wet alcohol-based slurries.
Although up to a systematic ~0.5um average decrease in SiC particle size was found,
considerations with respect to the nature of the particle segregation and agglomeration were more
influential in the resulting processed particles. For example, dry milling was limited after ~24-48

milling resulted in agglomerates after drying, which directly inhibit their ability to infiltrate pores.

surfactants was performed (e.g. 2 wt% Stearic acid, SDS, PVP, CTAB, PEG, & CTAB/PEG). It
was found that PEG showed slight improvement relative to the other surfactants.

CMC Assembly/Infiltration Trials
 Initial studies of CMC fabrication were undertaken with preliminary green body development.
Due to the expensive cost and delicate cutting necessary for SiC cloth, an analog carbon fiber
(Cf) cloth and chopped fibers were procured for initial prototyping without risking the costly SiC
fibers. To cut Cf cloth into 1” discs for assembling CMC layers, it was found that double-sided tape
lamination enabled precise cuts without damaging the fibers. After cutting, the use of a solvent
(e.g. IPA) was used to efficiently delaminate/peel the tape from the disks without disrupting fibers.
Using wet milled slurry SiC and 5 wt% stearic acid, various infiltrations of the carbon fiber discs
were tested for green body development to determine practical cohesion/adhesion during cold-
pressing. An example of a carbon fiber disk and pressed SiC-based green body are provided in
Figure 7.

 Ultimately, the use of polyethylene glycol (PEG 8000) for green bodies in coating/infiltrating
both SiC particles and fiber cloths was found to be critical in obtaining desirable results compared
to other organic additives (e.g. stearic acid, CTAB, etc.). Analog carbon fiber cloths with SiC-
based slurries were analyzed with SEM in Figure 8 to discern the extent of particle coverage
around the fibers. It was found that the SiC particle size was sufficiently small to decorate the
fibers to maintain matrix bonding for further CMC processing.

Figure 8: a) Digital image of 1” OD analog carbon fiber cloth disc for CMC testing, b-d) SEM of

 Progress in cold-pressing thinner SiC matrix layers was successful by the use of PEG-binding,
pulverization of binded powder (i.e. mortar & pestle), and sieving of fines (e.g. -60 mesh) to obtain
desirable results. Thin green SiC disks were integrated into multilayer subunits with the PEG-
infiltrated carbon fiber analog cloth (e.g. SiC/Cf/SiC) through the use of cold-pressing to adhere
them and silicone-mold annealing to fill/heal defects caused by the pressing step. Issues
pertaining to the fiber/matrix interface after pressing were identified, as cracks/delamination can
lead to defects or flaws preserved in the microstructure after firing. With a silicone-mold annealing
step, these defects are healed with the molten viscous PEG/SiC slurry filling in voids and enabling
more fused contact between these distinct layers.
 Leveraging the binder/surfactant insights for stabilizing SiC particles, a new optimized
SiC/PEG aqueous slurry formulation was developed to maximize the infiltration and robustness

 13

of green CMC sheets for further processing (i.e. cutting, stacking, fusing). Using this slurry and
heat treating at ~100°C/24hrs to remove the water and melt infiltrate the PEG/SiC mixture into the
CMC fabric sheet pores, preliminary green CMC architectures were fabricated accordingly using
custom silicone molds (1” OD).
 With green CMCs developed from the slurry infiltration/silicone mold process, solvent-based
debinding approaches were studied to extract the organic PEG binder out of the green CMCs to
prepare them for FAST without volatilization/thermal decomposition of residual organics that
would pose processing issues. It was found that the most efficient, scalable, and simplest
approach was to submerge the green CMCs in a vat of DI water for ~24hrs with a wax-paper lined
basket cage for the PEG to permeably dissolve in solution while leaving the green structure in-
tact. After replacing the PEG-DI solution with fresh DI water for another day of aqueous debinding,
the green bodies were dried and visibly shrank due to the removal of PEG binder volume and
capillary forces. Through this approach, debinded green CMC bodies were developed for
subsequent FAST processing.
 To load these debinded green CMCs into the graphite FAST dies, it was found that the edges
of the sheet-based CMCs required some trimming with a razor to ensure a snug-fit and that no
fibers/sheets would bend or distort during loading to ensure a straight lay-up under uniaxial
pressure. Once complete, the FAST die assembly was put-together and ran with a preliminary
baseline Cf/SiC CMC at 2200°C/30min/30-55 MPa. This baseline FAST CMC processing lifecycle
is graphically summarized below in Figure 9.

 Figure 9: Graphical schematic overview of latest baseline FAST CMC processing lifecycle

 The resulting FAST-pressed CMC was a hard/rigid ceramic disc that had almost no
resemblance to the original soft/flexible & fragile stacked fabric sheets in the green state. Surface
texturing of the exposed CMC was observed after grit-blasting the outermost carbide layer,
showing the original woven structure. From the 10x stacked green fabric sheets used, it is
estimated that the approximate thickness shrinkage from FAST processing was ~80%, resulting
in average sheet layer thickness of ~250μm from a total thickness of ~2.5mm and an approximate
1” OD diameter (oversized 1.0166”, ~200μm edge growth).
 With micrographic analysis of the first baseline Cf/SiC CMC, it was found that although the
10x layered architecture of interwoven parallel/perpendicular tows were preserved, due to the
intrinsically low-sinterability and high compressibility of carbon fibers relative to SiC particles,
observable defects such as continuous pores, interfilament cracks and compressed/segregated

 14

regions were present. Despite this heterogeneous microstructure, it was confirmed that the SiC
particles were able to fill between the fiber interstices to form a SiC matrix; however, further
densification optimization would be needed to close the remaining void space in the matrix phase.
Thus, from an analog CMC development standpoint, although many critical insights were obtained
with Cf/SiC from the underlying processing lifecycle, challenges and defects provided justification
to move forward with developing SiCf/SiC.
 As a possible alternative CMC infiltration step, vat photopolymerization (Mars 4 DPL, Elegoo
system) to additively print/laminate fiber sheets with resin was investigated as seen in Figure 10.
Compared to cold-pressing, the UV-cured photopolymer acts as a robust binder and can be an
additional reactive pre-ceramic SiC source.
 Using the vat photopolymerization insights, SMP-10 SiC-preceramic resin mixed with a
photoinitiator (BAPO) were UV-printed into 1” OD green discs on sapphire substrates for vacuum-
pyrolysis under 1400°C/12hrs. Although the SiC discs themselves cracked into pieces due to the

Construction of Baseline SiC/SiC Composites
 Using previous monolithic SiC matrix FAST parameter optimization developments, soak
conditions of 2400°C/90min/30-55 MPa were selected as the starting point of the first baseline
FAST SiCf/SiC CMC. Given that the highest relative densities (~94%) of SiC were previously
obtained with these parameters, it was anticipated that the CMC version would at least densify
similarly to the pure powder matrix, but likely retain agglomerate-like pore defects where grain
growth would be unable to densify/close all open pore interstices between the filaments. Using
an identical CMC processing lifecycle as the previous Cf/SiC, but replacing the carbon fiber with
Hi-Nicalon Type S β-SiC fibers, this CMC was FAST-processed. Astonishingly, the sinterability of
the SiCf/SiC CMC was so compelling that an even higher relative density of ~98.5% was obtained
and no resemblance of the pre-FAST fabric texture was observed. Although a finer average layer
thickness of ~130μm per sintered sheet was obtained, the effective consolidation led to
challenging difficulties in recognizing the original fiber filament architecture. Due to this featureless
microstructure devoid of defects, artifacts, and fiber interfaces previously seen in the Cf/SiC CMC,
it is presumed that crystalline processes such as grain coarsening, β/α transitions, and
recrystallization of the SiC fibers are highly dominant at these conditions. Hence, further
optimization of CMC-specific SiCf/SiC FAST parameters to yield sufficient activated diffusion to
densify with minimal composite changes in order to balance between max density and fiber/matrix
interfaces is critical.
 Based on these established insights and the literature, the baseline FAST SiCf/SiC CMC was
repeated with a lower soak temperature of 1700°C to reduce the extent of these crystalline
processes. As seen in Figure 13 and Figure 14, the resulting microstructure falls intermediately
between the previous CMC structures in terms of shrinkage (~194μm per layer), retained fiber
architecture, and void/defect density. The matrix phase appears to fully infiltrate the
preserved/closely-packed fiber bundles with no previous interfilament cracking or macroscopic

 16

defects. Instead, the remaining pores tend to decorate microscopically in the bulk SiC matrix and
mostly along the fiber interfaces (~26% open pores, ~5% closed pores). Some of these fiber-to-
fiber interfaces appear to demonstrate necking between each other in proximity, but the majority
are encapsulated in the matrix phase. Regardless, this latest baseline FAST-based SiCf/SiC CMC
promisingly approaches a greater balance between max density and preserved fiber/matrix
interfaces.

 On top of this EBC cloth-based layer processing viability evaluation, further developments
comparing dry and wet layering of oxides were conducted. As seen in Figure 25, dry layering of

 Figure 25: Graphical comparisons evaluating processing viability of dry layering powders

 Coarse, free-flowing mullite powders showed ~100μm layers were attainable with large multi-
sieve shaking, while fine powders (e.g. SiC) showed poor mobility and non-uniform layering in the
as-received state. To improve fine powder layering, IPA was used to agglomerate the SiC from a
wet slurry into dry granule-like agglomerates. These coarse granules were sifted into finer
agglomerates and provided a slightly thicker/coarser dry layer of powder (“coffee-cake” texture)
compared to traditional sieving with the previous mullite (“powder-sugar” texture). As
demonstrated, dry layering powders is a simple and easy means for building coarse EBC layers
(~100-250μm range) in the green state for subsequent FAST processing.
 To develop thinner EBC layers in microscale regime (e.g. ~10-50μm), a wet-based layering
approach is needed to achieve this resolution. The simplest and most effective method to achieve
this is by using a slurry-based spray to coat the substrate. As seen in Figure 26, an example of
this with a 50/50 SiC-Mullite composition with 80wt% IPA dilution that deposits a thin, uniform
slurry-sprayed coating onto a substrate (~32μm). This demonstrates as a viable means to deposit
thin EBCs in the green state onto a non-porous substrate for subsequent FAST.

 Figure 26: Example of wet-sprayed slurry coating for thin layering in green state

 To spray-coat a slurry onto a porous substrate (e.g. SiC/oxide cloths) however, the effects of
the solvent phase permeating into the pores cannot be neglected. Hence, a test was conducted
to evaluate these effects in the case of woven and felt-based porous cloth substrates that were
post-infiltrated with matrix powder. As seen in Figure 27, with increasing number of sprays
between drying cycles, the felt steadily accumulates mass with a deposited coating while the
woven cloth does not. This is due to the large continuous pores of the woven cloth acting as a
liquid channel that permeates completely through the cloth and drains out excess slurry that
displaces material out of it. Meanwhile, the felt cloth more effectively resists this permeation with
finer pores to slow infiltration and allow more drying of deposited coating material. It should be
noted that as further sprays are increased, the felt cloth eventually will also become saturated
and limit further coating deposition, but has a substantially larger window for processing compared
to the woven cloth. Therefore, wet spraying slurries onto non-porous and felt-based substrates
provides a viable means for thin layering of EBC coatings for subsequent FAST-based integration
of CMCs.

 Suitable oxide-based SiC sintering aids and viable FAST parameters established from initial

 Figure 30: Scalable layup design of first pilot integrated CMC-EBC via FAST

Final Development of Integrated CMC-EBC Coupons
 Systematic developments in both baseline and improved CMC-EBCs were yielded from
careful experimental optimization. For instance, delicate extraction procedures were established
to preserve the integrity of pressed CMC-EBCs for subsequent testing. As seen in Figure 44, the
residual surface layers are illustrated for an example baseline CMC-EBC (FGM HfO2/doped SiC).

  Figure 44: Example of baseline FGM CMC-EBC (Run# 5267) extraction considerations - a)
 outer residual carbon foil, b) inner carbide layer, c-d) effects of post-grit blast extraction.

 In the as-extracted state of Figure 44a, the CMC-EBC is enveloped in a soft carbon foil layer
from the linings in the graphite die. This layer can be readily removed with simple mechanical
exfoliation (i.e., razor blade scraping) to clean the surface and reveal the inner carbide layer. This
carbide layer in Figure 44b is harder than the previous residual carbon foil and cannot be directly

 36

removed through mechanical exfoliation. Attempts with conventional grit blasting were
undertaken (Figure 44c-d) but resulted in surface crack initiation and EBC damage. This
propagated cracks during handling, causing notable damage. Although further attempts at more
gentle conditions (e.g., shallower incident angles, lower pressure, longer standoff), cumulative
damage was still observed. Considering the high adhesion/brittleness of the carbide layer and the
relative softness of the underlying EBC oxide top coat (HfO2) to the impinging erodent (Al2O3),
uniform/damage-free layer removal via grit blasting appears to be infeasible.
 As such, the remaining fabricated CMC-EBCs have been standardized in extraction to avoid
potential damage or defects that may artificially accelerate the combustion-induced degradation
of subsequent testing. All samples have been mechanically exfoliated with razor blades to provide

 By applying thin layers (~125 μm) of dry powder for the graded 50/50 vol% SiC-oxide bond
coat and 100% oxide topcoat to the CMCs (12x SiC cloth layers each), highly reproducible & in-

standardized baseline CMC-EBCs fabricated in this manner were processed Since both top and
bottoms of the CMCs were coated with EBCs symmetrically, the exposed sides can be compared
to non-exposed sides to assess relative differences in combustion-induced degradation.
 From the completion of these baseline CMC-EBC samples, progress into developing
improved formulations was achieved. A desirable formulation was to utilize rare earth silicates as
the EBC, which would provide greater water vapor degradation resistance. Nonetheless, the
majority of viable rare earth silicates (e.g., YDS, YbMS, YbDS) possess low melting points that

 37

are <1850°C and therefore are not viable for solid state sintering at the optimized CMC FAST
processing temperatures (>1900°C). Due to this major technical challenge, an alternate
processing approach was explored to avoid both melting and sinterability issues. This approach
was a multi-step joining process between CMC/EBCs discretely. The CMCs are first pressed
uncoated with optimized FAST parameters (1900°C/30min) and then post-machined along
circumference for refitting precisely back into the graphite FAST die. The CMC is then re-pressed
with dry powder layers of EBC for solid state joining at tailored oxide sintering conditions
(1700°C/90min) established with prior monolith trials.
 For the interest of improving the EBC properties while avoiding thermal expansion issues
between the discrete EBC/CMC interface, a functionally graded trilayer design was opted for the
rare earth silicate EBC. A 50/50 at% YbDS/YDS bond coat that CTE-matches SiC is deposited
first, then with a 75/25 at% intermediate layer connecting the 100 at% YbDS topcoat for maximum
water vapor protection (~125μm each layer). This design approach was attempted and the results
are highlighted in Figure 46.

  Figure 46: Results of multi-step CMC/EBC FAST joining attempts for improved formulation
 (trilayer YbDS/YDS EBC on SiC/SiC) – a-b) first attempt, c) second attempt.

 Although dense cohesive CMC-EBCs were obtained, it is clear from Figure 46a-b that
substantial interfacial cracking and poor adhesion between CMC/EBCs are evident. After a
second identical attempt (Figure 46c), extensive cracking appeared to be unavoidable with this
approach. Although thermal expansion mismatch was minimized from the EBC selection,
adhesive failure was likely due to coherency, surface energy, and spring-back stiffness
differences between the SiC/oxide interface. Although such residual stresses are unavoidable
between these disparate materials, further advanced developments of suitable bond coats (e.g.,
metal silicides, modified SiAlONs) that can also be sintered and adhere well to both materials
would be needed to make this multi-step approach feasible.
 From these insights, a new improvement formulation route was then developed and applied
to extract enhanced benefits from the previous baseline single-step CMC-EBCs with new
augmented protection via the inclusion of rare earth silicate oxides in the EBC composition. The
first improvement was to utilize increased gradings between the CMC/EBC layers. Instead of the
previous baseline FGM CMC-EBC 3x step sequence from doped SiC to HfO2 (0, 50, 100 vol%),
a more graded 5x step sequence was employed: 0, 25, 50, 75, 100 vol% (125 μm each). This
enabled both increased adhesion, cohesion, and a thicker overall protective barrier for the CMC.
The second improvement was to replace the pure HfO2 from the baseline with a composite doped

 38

mixture of 90/10 vol% HfO2/YbDS. Although the CMC sintering conditions would inevitably melt
the rare earth YbDS phase, the relative proportion is low enough to avoid molten flashing/leakage
from the FAST die due to green body capillarity, but high enough to act as an effective liquid
phase sintering aid to bind the HfO2 grains with a more water vapor resistant oxide. This improved
design results in a highly protective, robust, and dense ceramic composite with enhanced water
vapor resistance that can be efficiently processed in a single step via FAST.

Task 3: Experimental Facility Development and Build
Task 3.1 – Experimental Design and Fabrication
 The test section was designed in cooperation with partners at EPRI, who cost-shared the
project and provided funds for fabrication of the facility. The requirements for the experiment
included the ability to test components in regions with both flame and flame products. To achieve
this goal, a trapped-vortex combustor (also referred to as a cavity combustor) was designed with
two zones for the samples. In the flame zone, the CMC sample is located in a low-velocity zone,
below a recirculation zone that is established in the cavity. Additionally, the first CMC sample may
have the flame directly impact on the surface of the sample, as the flame stabilizes in the shear
layer that sheds off the leading edge of the cavity. The second CMC sample is downstream of the
cavity in a high-velocity region of the flow. Having these samples in two different velocity regions
allowed us to test the impact of flow residence time on recession rate of the material.
 Detailed design followed the initial design meeting with EPRI. The final design included an
experiment that is 9 feet long and can be broken into three main portions, as seen in Figure 49:
the preconditioning section, the test section, and the exhaust. The entire test facility is made of
316 stainless steel and, wherever possible, the design uses 2” x 6” rectangular tubing for the main
structure. The preconditioning portion is shown in Figure 50. This portion adjusts the flow so that
it is steady and uniform when it enters the test section. This design implements adjustable
positioning for the turbulence plates and ceramic honeycomb to avoid acoustic oscillations.
 The test section is shown in Figure 51 and Figure 52. There are a total of six windows for
viewing and instrumentation. Two CMC samples are placed in the cavity and two are placed
further downstream. Each sample is mounted on a heat flux block, which is the mode by which
heat flux through the CMC samples is measured. The heat flux is calculated from a series of
temperature measurements made by thermocouples along the length of the block. The heat flux
can be controlled by varying the flow of cooling water at the base of each block. Water channels
were machined into cavity walls to supply cooling so that the facility can be run for long periods
of time. Additionally, a manifold was designed to be machined in both the upstream and
downstream cavity walls to provide uniform air/fuel injection in the cavity. The air diverter shown
in Figure 53 has two purposes. The first is to divert the mainstream air in order to aid in
entrainment into the cavity. The diverter is bolted rather than welded in place so that different
configurations can be tested if desired. The second purpose is to cool the large windows on the
top of the test section. Cooling air is routed through the diverter and be released just below the
windows to provide film cooling across them, protecting them from high temperatures.
 The exhaust portion is shown in Figure 54. A short plain section was designed to provide
options for future research. The rest is there to cool the exhaust gases through water injection to
a reasonable temperature before entering a muffler and subsequently the building’s exhaust
system.

 To confirm the accuracy of the heat flux block measurements when installed in the combustion
rig, we tested the blocks under non-reacting conditions to determine the cavity convection
coefficient, allowing us to baseline our measurements with similar studies. These experiments
were done by preheating mainstream air at 200 °C at various flow rates and comparing the heat
flux measurements with data from several studies measuring heat transfer in similar cavity
geometries. For ease of comparison, the heat transfer coefficient is described non-dimensionally
as the Nusselt number (NuL).
 Figure 62 compares the measured NuL from the present work at a range of Reynolds numbers
to data and correlations from various other studies. The data shown from the present work are
the average of the measurements from the two heat flux blocks positioned in the cavity. The
difference between the two heat flux block measurements was 3% on average across all
Reynolds numbers. The curve fit for these data is shown by black dashes. It should be noted that
our data is the local NuL at the central plane of the cavity bottom wall, whereas Richard et al.’s [1]
and Yamamoto et al.’s [2] correlations in Figure 62 are for the average NuL along the entire bottom
wall of the cavity and Fox’s [3] and Haugen et al.’s [4] correlations are for the average NuL along
all walls of the cavity. The results show that all of the studies have similar trends with the Reynolds
number, but different magnitudes due to variance in the experimental setup.
 Haugen et al.’s experimental setup most closely reflects the present work. Their findings are
marked in red in Figure 62. Like us, they used a turbulence screen upstream of the flow channel,
which resulted in a thick boundary layer and a relatively high upstream turbulence intensity. The
only major difference between their work and our own is that the range of Reynolds numbers that
they investigated is higher, 3.3 x 104 to 1.4 x 105 versus our 7.6 x 103 to 3.1 x 104. Their average
Nusselt number along the bottom wall was found to be proportional to the Reynolds number to
the 0.75 power, which is near our own finding which showed a proportionality of the Reynolds
number to the 0.67 power. More importantly, however, is the similarity of the local heat transfer
coefficient at the center of the bottom wall of the cavity. With a cavity aspect ratio of one and a

 49

Reynolds number of 1.3 x 105, their local Nusselt number at the center of the bottom wall of the
cavity was between 1365 and 1510 depending on the thickness of the boundary layer used and
is marked with open squares in Figure 62. This Reynolds number is beyond the capability of our
experiment, but when our curve fit is applied at that Reynolds number, our calculated Nusselt
number is within 9% or less depending on the boundary layer thickness in Haugen et al.’s
experiment. This agreement of the local heat transfer coefficient from an experiment very similar
to our own supports the accuracy of the measurements obtained from the heat flux block.
 The data from the other three studies (Yamamoto, Richards, and Fox) show similar trends to
our data in Figure 62 but are significantly lower in magnitude. This difference is presumably due
to the present work’s higher upstream turbulence intensity from the use of a perforated plate
upstream of the cavity.

Task 4.2 – Added H2O Testing
 The final set of testing was conducted by exposing the samples to consistent flame conditions
for 3-4 hour increments to represent the durations of a gas turbine used for peaking power. One
of the complexities of testing with the TVC burner rig rather than a furnace is that the burner
requires active supervision of the flame at all times. This requirement makes testing materials for
thousands of hours unfeasible, so the number of hours necessary was determined using
monolithic SiC samples. These samples were tested under combustion conditions at increasing
exposure durations until sufficient oxidation could be observed in post-characterization. Durations
began at only 8 hours with no observable effects of oxidation until nearly 50 hours. From this initial
testing, it was decided that 75-100 hours of exposure to the flame would provide sufficient time
for oxidation to occur.
 SiC/SiC CMC’s vulnerability to water vapor is of primary concern for hydrogen-fired gas
turbines due to the increased volume of water vapor. For that reason, this work aimed to test the
samples in combustion atmospheres with elevated concentrations of water in the products.
However, since the availability and price of burning 100% hydrogen for ~100 hours per set of
materials was unfeasible, the materials were instead exposed to 100% natural gas combustion
with additional water injected. Deionized water was vaporized upstream of the test section by the
pre-heated mainstream air. The amount of water injected upstream was such that the total mass
flow of water in the products matched 100% hydrogen at the same conditions. This strategy
offered a more efficient, cost-effective method of subjecting the samples to environments that
mimic the elevated water-vapor concentration from hydrogen combustion.
 The heat transfer from the combustion environment to the materials is affected by the
Reynolds number of the flow, the equivalence ratio of the flame, the thermal power of the flame,
and the fuel composition used. In order to produce comparable data, each of these parameters
were kept almost entirely constant throughout materials testing. The Reynolds number based on
the mainstream air velocity and cavity length, L, was held at 15,300 and the fuel was composed
of 100% natural gas with additional water injected for all tests. The cavity equivalence ratio, which

 56

was determined from the mass flow rate of fuel and air injected into the cavity without taking
mainstream air into account, was held constant at 0.55. The thermal power was kept at 30 kW for
nearly all testing.
 Uncoated CMC samples were tested in the combustor for 100 hours. Digital images of the
samples throughout the duration of testing can be seen in Figure 68, with the marked locations
corresponding to the locations where the radiometric surface temperature was the highest as
shown in Figure 69. These samples remained intact with no obvious macroscopic failure.
Interestingly, despite being tested at identical conditions, the heat flux data in Figure 70 show that
the heat flux through the sample in the cavity increased linearly by 10% over the duration of
testing. In contrast, the measurements through the sample in the channel show no statistically
significant change in the heat flux during testing. These trends were seen in earlier testing of
FAST fabricated ytterbium disilicate (YbDS) monoliths as well. The observations of the differing
responses of heat flux through the samples over time are corroborated by the IR images in Figure
69. The surface temperature of the cavity sample increased by about 15-20 °C over the course
of testing, while the measurements of the surface temperature of the channel sample show no
meaningful trend with time. The heat transfer and surface temperature of samples could be
affected by changes in surface properties including surface roughness or by the formation of
oxides. The non-uniformity of the trend over time depending on the location of exposure shows
that the flow regime that components are placed in can have diverse effects on the materials.

Longer Term Exposure Results
 After all remaining samples underwent >100 hour combustion exposure testing in Task 4,
subsequent characterization was performed to assess the CMC-EBC performance. For example,
as seen in Figure 79, baseline CMC-EBCs at the cavity and channel locations within the burner
rig exhibit notable differences in apparent recession. The more direct, hotter flame exposure in
the cavity shows a ~30% greater areal recession compared with the indirect channel position.
This areal recession shows the carbide layer (dark) was burned off and reveals lighter oxide EBC
layer (white) underneath.
 Further phase analysis of these top surfaces via XRD in Figure 80 highlight notable
differences in residual phases at different depths between these two exposure conditions.
Although both EBCs were mostly composed of monoclinic hafnia (HfO2), small traces of carbon-
rich phases (e.g., C, HfC) were found in the channel condition, but not in the cavity condition.

 63

Since these materials were found at the limit of detection threshold (~1 wt%), it is likely that cavity
condition has an undetectably low concentration of these phases. Regardless, this shows that the
channel condition maintained these shallower carbon-rich phases as a natural protective case
layer (analogous to a seal coat) that can still remain even under highly oxidizing conditions. This
can be attributed to the graphite-based dies used in the FAST processing, which conformally coat
the sample exteriors with diffused carbon to react into these residual phases. Meanwhile in the
cavity case, only deeper phases such as the oxide sintering aids (e.g., Y2O3) were detectable due
to the nearly complete oxidation/recession of the carbide layer under the most aggressive tested
flame conditions. The extent of recession between the samples is further visually highlighted in
Figure 81 with additional topographic and UV fluorescence contrast associated with the exposed
EBC oxide.

Task 5.2 –Mechanical Property Testing
 In addition to the direct post-combustion material characterization of the CMC-EBCs, further
insights into their mechanical properties were obtained through final supplemental testing and

 77

analysis. Given the time and resource intensive nature of preparing characterization specimens
from the original set of post-100hr exposed samples, unfortunately very limited space was
available and viable for machining conventional mechanical testing bars. Hence, small slices from
the original exposed CMC-EBC discs were cut into select specimens (~1/8 x 1/8 x 7/8” bars) for
relative mechanical comparisons. Specifically, these specimens were subjected to cyclical
compression with a circular probe (~3 mm OD) to determine any notable differences in long-term
fatigue survivability associated with the extreme vibratory environment of turbomachinery. About
10 N load or ~1.4 MPa of peak stress was applied for over 100+ cycles across both the exposed
cavity baseline and improved samples. As summarized in Figure 102, it was found that the full
baseline CMC-EBC passed the cyclical compression test (no observed damage), while the
improved EBC failed. After a single compression cycle, notable probe displacement and
audible/visible cracking of the coating was observed. Nonetheless, the damaged coating
remained attached after the 100+ cycles and the underlying CMC itself appeared undamaged
itself. This suggests the thick “improved” EBC architecture likely contains excessive residual
stress and defects that dramatically sensitizes its failure response to mechanical load compared
to the stable thin baseline EBC.

 To isolate the origin of this mechanical stress sensitivity and nature of the post-100hr exposure

future work that applying thinner, smoother, and more diffuse EBC layers be utilized for achieving
optimal robustness and that further realistic combustion testing and post-exposure analysis (esp.

Conclusions
 The overall goals of this project were to create silicon carbide-based ceramic matrix composite
(CMC) materials with integrated environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) using field assisted
sintering technology (FAST) and test them in a high-water vapor combustion environment
representative of gas turbine combustors. Over the course of the project, FAST manufacturing
process parameters for both the CMC and EBC materials were determined, as well as assembly
techniques to combine the CMC and EBC together. Several test coupons were ultimately
generated, including monolithic silicon carbide (SiC), monolithic EBCs, uncoated CMCs, and
EBC-coated CMCs. A combustion test facility capable of fuel blends from 0% (i.e. 100% methane)
to 100% hydrogen was designed, built, and tested, including validation of in-situ measurement
capabilities for sample heat flux and surface temperature using infrared cameras. Uncoated
CMCs and CMCs with EBC coatings were tested in the facility at durations of over 100 hours and

 79

evaluated for degradation mechanisms. Although the exposure time was somewhat limited,
evaluation of test coupons indicated that the FAST-constructed CMC materials had good
durability and thin EBC coatings had good adherence to the CMC substrate due to the well-
integrated construction. Thicker coatings developed greater thermomechanical stresses leading
to spalling/delamination, suggesting a need to optimize thick film architectures further in future
efforts. To conclude, this work successfully establishes the initial groundwork for improved, rapid,
and scalable manufacturing of robust CMC-EBCs via FAST for applied hot turbine engine
components exposed to real-world combustion environments.
