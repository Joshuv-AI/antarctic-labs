High-resolution UAV imagery-based instance segmentation for automatic counting of rubber trees across phenological seasons
Authors: Jiangquan Zeng, Mingjie Lv, Guoxiong Zhou, Xiangjun Wang, Exian Liu, Huaiqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate counting of rubber trees is important for yield estimation, refined field management, and the sustainable development of the rubber industry. In deep-learning-based counting, reliable canopy delineation is a prerequisite for accurate segmentation and subsequent statistical analysis. However, maintaining stable canopy segmentation under long-term and complex field conditions remains difficult, mainly because of overlapping crowns, interference from background vegetation, and pronounced seasonal changes. To address these issues, this study proposes CSAF, a rubber tree canopy segmentation model designed for high-precision canopy segmentation and counting from UAV imagery. CSAF consists of three main components: the Boundary Continuity Modelling Module (BCMM), the Physical Morphology Constraint Module (PMCM), and the Cross-Temporal Learning Adaptively Module (CTLAM). BCMM improves the representation of complex canopy boundaries by using the Fourier transform to suppress feature noise and state-space modelling to refine boundary responses. PMCM introduces a Poisson diffusion prior to constrain the segmentation results according to canopy morphology, thereby reducing the influence of irregular background vegetation. CTLAM adjusts the replay ratio and scale through a state-feedback mechanism, which helps mitigate inter-seasonal variation and improve cross-temporal generalization. In addition, this study presents RT-Set, a UAV-based rubber tree canopy dataset containing 5281 high-resolution images that cover the full growth cycle of rubber trees, from the budding stage to the defoliation stage. RT-Set provides a dedicated benchmark for precise rubber tree canopy segmentation and counting. On RT-Set, CSAF achieves an AP50 of 76.63%, outperforming the compared methods. Experiments on three additional public datasets further show that CSAF remains competitive with mainstream approaches, including DetecTree2 and Cascade Mask R-CNN. In the counting experiments, CSAF also shows high accuracy and stable performance, suggesting its practical value for rubber tree counting under complex field conditions. The RT-Set dataset is available at https://github.com/zengjiangquan1/RT-Set.Keywords: Rubber tree segmentation, UAV imagery, Deep learning, Canopy counting

Introduction

As the primary global source of natural rubber, rubber tree plantations are vital to the raw material supply chains of the tire, automotive, and healthcare industries [1]. Market demands and climate risks are now more volatile. This intensifies the need for precise tree counting to guide yield estimation and resource allocation [2]. The prerequisite for such accurate counting is the precise segmentation of individual canopy boundaries within UAV-acquired imagery. Consequently, developing a method capable of achieving high-precision, stable, and efficient canopy segmentation on drone imagery is essential.

Traditional tree counting relies on manual tallying by forestry personnel, a process that is highly subjective, time-consuming, labor-intensive, and associated with high management costs [3,4]. With advances in computer vision technology, tree counting methods have progressively shifted from manual counting to image-based segmentation, demonstrating substantial application potential. Conventional individual tree segmentation methods can be broadly categorized into six groups: template and shape matching approaches [5], active contour models [6], graph-based energy minimization [7], clustering and density estimation [8], object-based watershed family methods [9], and region competition methods [10]. Template and shape matching methods involve the cross-correlation of elliptical or circular templates with images in an attempt to identify tree crown regions. These methods are reliable in a structured plantation setup but are highly prone to deformation errors in the case of species with large crown variability, thereby causing false positives. Active contour models are a family of methods that involve the evolution of curves towards the boundary of tree crowns based on certain energy functions; in these methods, the weights of internal and external forces are adjusted manually and are highly dependent on the initial contour position, which often gets stuck in a local minimum in a closed canopy setup. Graph-based energy minimization methods involve the segmentation of trees as a pixel-level foreground and background labeling problem using color and boundary energy functions, which are further utilized in graph construction and computation of the maximum flow-minimum cut value; in cases where spectral differences are small among different tree species or in cases of blurry crown boundaries, the energy functions are unable to segment the different crown regions of trees in a distinct manner. Clustering and density estimation methods involve the adaptive clustering of points based on spectral and height density maxima; in cases of overlapping canopy regions, the presence of an overlapping density peak causes unstable segmentation outcomes. Object-based watershed segmentation methods involve the segmentation of images based on the topographic concept of image gradients or super-pixel images as a topographic surface, which are further flooded based on local minimum values; in these methods, the presence of image noise and understory shadows causes difficulties in segmentation, which are overcome through post-processing methods involving the removal of false positives of crown segmentation. Region competition methods involve the evolution of boundaries based on multi-region energy competition, which allows for the estimation of crown number and contours in a single pass; in these methods, a large number of parameters are involved, which makes the method non-adaptable in a structurally complex forest setup.

However, with the emergence of deep learning techniques, specifically the widespread adoption of convolutional neural networks, there has been an increase in the adoption of canopy segmentation techniques using CNNs in an attempt to overcome the shortcomings of classical techniques in canopy segmentation. Yue et al. introduced TreeUNet, which involves the incorporation of tree-structured convolutional blocks and tree-cutting techniques in an attempt to automatically build binary decision trees in sub-meter ultra-high definition images, which were found to be highly successful in ultra-high definition canopy segmentation tasks [11]. Long et al. proposed the Scale Pyramid Graph Neural Network (SPGN), which constructs a scale pyramid module to extract multi-scale features at pixel, superpixel, and subgraph levels. By employing subgraph position encoding to represent the natural spatial relationships of these features, the model achieves substantial improvements in canopy segmentation and classification accuracy [12]. Secondly, deep learning models can focus on segmenting specific targets. Möhring et al. employed a Transformer architecture for image feature extraction and developed a four-dimensional sampling scheme to ensure robust feature representations during training, substantially improving deadwood segmentation performance [13]. Beyond this, an increasing number of studies have expanded their focus beyond mere canopy segmentation to encompass extensive phenotyping research. For instance, Li et al. employed a deep learning framework to predict and quantify tree abundance and height across Denmark [14]. Ding et al. employed an enhanced Mask R-CNN model for individual tree segmentation and stand age extraction in eucalyptus plantations, providing data support for evaluating stand uniformity and productivity in tree-based plantations [15]. These studies demonstrate that deep learning methods hold significant potential for application in canopy segmentation and phenotypic quantification. However, their implementation in rubber plantations remains subject to certain limitations.

Based on our observations, three characteristics of UAV imagery make rubber tree instance segmentation particularly difficult, as illustrated in Fig. 1. First, rubber trees in plantations are usually planted at high density, leading to frequent canopy overlap. Such overlap obscures crown boundaries and may also hide useful visual cues, including leaf texture and branch structure. This makes individual crowns difficult to separate and can result in both under-segmentation and over-segmentation, thereby reducing counting accuracy. Second, understory vegetation introduces additional interference. Rubber plantations often contain weeds, shrubs, and other background plants beneath or around the tree crowns, many of which have spectral responses similar to rubber tree foliage. These similarities can cause non-target vegetation to be mistakenly segmented as rubber tree canopies. Third, rubber trees show marked seasonal changes in appearance. Their canopy color changes from bright green at the budding stage to darker green during growth, then gradually to yellow and withered brown during maturation and defoliation. Their crown structure also changes: canopies are generally smoother and more continuous during the first three stages, whereas defoliation produces fragmented crown patterns with exposed branches and visible ground regions. These temporal changes alter both the color and structural cues used by segmentation models, making stable recognition across seasons difficult. This instability remains a key obstacle to applying rubber tree counting models in real field scenarios.

Three challenges in rubber tree canopy segmentation.

To address the issue of blurred boundaries caused by overlapping tree crowns, Tong et al. drew inspiration from the radial symmetry exhibited by tree crowns in their phenotypical growth pattern from the centre outward. They employed a star-shaped convex polygon to model the tree crown, enabling the generation of smooth, closed outlines that align with tree growth characteristics even in areas of blurred contours where crowns overlap [16]. Additionally, Duan et al. enhanced the accuracy of boundary definition in areas of canopy overlap using attention-gated units [17]. Indeed, the issue of unclear object segmentation contours frequently arises in tasks such as building extraction [18], farmland segmentation [19], and change detection [20]. Boundary features can be improved by using boundary branches, edge-guided modules, or morphological edges and global contexts. But despite showing promising results, these approaches have shown several drawbacks in rubber tree plantations. This is because these approaches neglect the fact that there is a great similarity in color and texture of trees within a rubber tree plantation, which makes it difficult for attention mechanisms to identify boundaries. At the same time, it is often noticed that the shapes of rubber trees do not follow a star-convex geometry; therefore, segmentation can often go wrong. The remaining methods for enhancing boundary features are primarily designed for other tasks and do not fully utilise the phenotypic characteristics of rubber trees, which often makes it difficult to effectively enhance the contour information of rubber trees.

With respect to background-plant interference, point cloud data acquired by UAV LiDAR can help distinguish canopy objects from understory vegetation by exploiting height differences [21]. However, LiDAR-based data collection is costly, and the corresponding training process is often time-consuming and difficult to implement. At present, such data therefore cannot fully replace the convenience and accessibility of two-dimensional UAV imagery. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have specifically examined the misclassification of rubber tree canopies caused by background vegetation. By contrast, background interference has been widely studied in SAR-based ship detection. For example, the 3SD-Net model proposed by Ma et al. uses bidirectional coordinate attention to reduce over-segmentation between targets and interfering regions [22]. Qin et al.’s RDB-DINO improves target separability through fully contrastive denoising training and maximum-norm backpropagation, thereby mitigating interference from non-target objects [23]. However, these approaches are not directly suited to rubber plantation scenes. In such settings, rubber tree crowns and understory vegetation are densely intermixed, which weakens the geometric separation and object independence assumptions that are often available in ship-detection scenarios. Moreover, understory vegetation and rubber tree foliage are highly similar in color and texture, resulting in weak boundary contrast between target and background regions. Compared with many other detection or segmentation tasks, rubber tree canopy segmentation therefore requires stronger constraints to suppress background vegetation interference and to preserve reliable crown boundaries.

In terms of the problem of catastrophic forgetting in the models caused by seasonal variation, the work of Liang et al. showed that the integration of data of both phases of the year, spring and autumn, can improve the accuracy of the models [24]; meanwhile, Zhao et al. employed a Beck logistic function to fit EVI2 time series, controlling detection errors during the critical spring growth phase within 3–5 days [25]. Similarly, in the field of fault diagnosis, Zhan et al. constructed memory modules for feature replay through product-quantisation encoding, combined with a semi-freezing, semi-updating incremental strategy, effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting under unlabelled cross-domain conditions [26]. Although previous approaches have been proposed, significant challenges were also involved. Liang and Zhao's method only took into account the performance gain in specific seasons, not meeting the counting task in the long term. Feature replay is very unstable in training when used in the updating of the weights in real time. Also, the changes in the canopy in the specific seasons were more complicated, and the mere replaying of the features was not adequate in representing the changes.

Based on the aforementioned analysis, we present a new canopy segmentation method, CSAF, which is capable of overcoming the three above-mentioned challenges and thus seeks a solution for the problem of stable counting in a real-world forest scenario over a longer term. Our model consists of three primary modules: BCMM, PMCM, and CTLAM. The BCMM first transforms the extracted features into a form with a higher signal-to-noise ratio using a Fourier transform, which highlights boundary features, and these are then utilized by the state-space model to represent continuous boundaries, thus improving the network's ability to extract boundary features. In an effort to overcome the influence of background vegetation, the features are then sent to the PMCM module. This module combines the Poisson prior model directly into the spatial attention mechanism, which yields a probabilistic map using a multilayer perceptron and satisfies the Poisson diffusion equation, which is then mapped into an attention embedding in the feature space, thus overcoming the influence of background vegetation. At the same time, the CTLAM is utilized in optimizing the learning procedure. This procedure allows the forgetting factor to be adjusted in real time, thus controlling the number of samples in the replay procedure and maintaining a balanced approach between the current and replayed objectives.

In addition, since there are few publicly available dataset options for estimating the accuracy of long-cycle counts, this research proposes the creation of a comprehensive dataset with diversity, RT-Set. This dataset has 5281 images from the budding stage through leaf fall, representing one entire year, focusing on three difficult scenarios for tree crown segmentation. This comprehensive dataset holds promise as a valuable resource for evaluating and advancing tree crown segmentation and counting techniques. Its purpose is to support the creation of more robust and generalisable models for subsequent extension studies.

RT-set

Study area

RT-Set was collected in the northwestern part of Danzhou in Hainan Province (19°31′50.59″N, 109°28′52.62″E), in a long-term research plantation under the management of the Rubber Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences. This area belongs to the southern subtropical monsoon climate zone with small temperature variations throughout the year, with mean values of 22.5°C to 25.6°C. The lowest temperature is in January, and the highest is in July, thus presenting a warm and stable climate. The total number of sunshine hours in a year is between 1780 and 2,600, and the total rainfall is between 900 and 2400 mm.

Hainan Island is located along the northern boundary of the South China Sea and is typically affected by typhoons, particularly from June to October. Danzhou, situated in the northwestern part of the island, is less frequently hit by direct typhoon landfalls but remains influenced by the outer cloud bands of tropical cyclones. In this region, disturbances caused by intense winds and rainfall often lead to tree lodging, trunk breakage, and canopy fragmentation, which result in satellite imagery capturing various levels of environmental disruption. Consequently, this area provides an ideal case study to assess the stability and generalization capability of models within complex and disturbed environments.

Dataset construction

RT-Set was intended to record the entire phenological cycle of rubber tree plantations; hence, aerial images were taken several times per year. The data was collected through the DJI Phantom 4 RTK drone equipped with a 1-inch CMOS sensor with a resolution of 20.48 megapixels. During data collection, the flight height was maintained at 80 m, the airspeed was set to about 6 m/s, and the overlapping ratio in both the forward and side directions was maintained at 85% to meet the mosaicking requirements. Later, the raw images were mosaicked and orthorectified in the Pix4DMapper software to create georeferenced orthomosaics. The orthomosaics were then split into image tiles of 640 × 640 pixels to make it easier to annotate and conduct experiments.

Data annotation

The cropped images were annotated with polygon-based instance labels using Labelme. During the budding, growing, and mature phenophases, crown patterns were sufficiently intact, with well-defined boundaries, allowing annotators to label each crown distinctly with a separate instance ID. Crowns that were partially visible at image boundaries and not fully representative were excluded to avoid bias in counting experiments. Annotation of imagery obtained during the leaf-off phenophase is more challenging because extensive leaf drop makes the boundaries between neighboring crowns difficult to discern. Manual annotation under these conditions is prone to subjective bias, making errors hard to avoid. Therefore, only images with lower canopy density and relatively clear crown boundaries were retained. This selection helps maintain annotation consistency and reduces bias introduced by subjective judgment.

Data partitioning

After annotation, the images were split into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:2:1 split. This yielded 3,697, 1,056, and 528 images for the training, validation, and test sets, respectively. This split allows for sufficient data for training and still maintains enough data for the validation and test sets. The split of samples for the phenological stages is shown in Table 1. To better assess the ability of the CTLAM for continual learning, the data split was deliberately made imbalanced, such that only 4.7% of the data in the training split was for defoliation stages.

Methodology

Method overview

To enhance the precision of tree crown segmentation and thereby improve the accuracy of tree counting, this paper proposes the CSAF network, as illustrated in Fig. 2(A). This network primarily comprises five components: feature extraction, BCMM, PMCM, CTLAM, and a post-processing module. During the feature extraction stage, all images within a batch are input into a pre-trained ResNet50 backbone network for multi-scale feature extraction. The extracted features may be categorized as low-level features f2 and mid-level features f3. This paper opts to feed the mid-level features f3 into the BCMM and PMCM modules for targeted enhancement. The primary reason lies in the fact that while the low-level feature f2 contains relatively clear canopy contour information, its semantic expressive capability is limited. Furthermore, it is highly sensitive to texture variations, shading, and background vegetation noise. Further enhancement may amplify irrelevant interference, thereby compromising the semantic extraction of deep-level features. By contrast, the intermediate feature f3 already possesses rudimentary semantic expressive capability while maintaining a certain spatial resolution. Enhancing its contours and suppressing the background facilitates guiding the network to focus on more discriminative structural information. The BCMM and PMCM modules are directly embedded after the f3 feature layer of ResNet50 to enhance intermediate features. The enhanced features are subsequently passed to the subsequent layers of the backbone network, generating high-level features f4 and f5, thereby achieving layer-by-layer refinement of canopy features. CTLAM collects corresponding images and labels into the replay pool based on the current training loss, dynamically replaying them with a forgetting factor to mitigate catastrophic forgetting when the model processes samples from different seasons.

BCMM

In rubber plantation scenes, the difficulty introduced by overlapping canopies is not limited to blurred boundaries. In many cases, branches and leaves occlude the true crown boundary, so the contour becomes locally broken and incomplete. At the same time, leaf textures, branch details, and illumination changes inside the crown often produce strong local responses. These responses do not belong to the true boundary, but they can resemble boundary cues in local regions and interfere with the separation of adjacent crowns. BCMM is designed for this setting, as shown in Fig. 2(B). Rather than recovering contours directly from the original feature, BCMM first suppresses the responses inside the crown that keep disturbing boundary inference, and then models the contour structure that has been disrupted by occlusion.

The backbone feature f3 already contains rich semantic information, yet it still preserves many local variations that are unrelated to the boundary. For contour modeling, the main problem is not only that the true boundary is weak, but also that its responses are mixed with texture responses from inside the crown. If these components are directly passed to the following state-space modeling stage, the model has to aggregate true contour information while handling pseudo-boundary responses at the same time. Under this condition, the structural information that corresponds to the true boundary becomes less salient, and the signal-to-noise ratio of the boundary representation decreases.

BCMM therefore first applies a two-dimensional Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to f3 and maps the feature from the spatial domain to the frequency domain. This step is introduced because internal crown textures and true boundaries may both appear as strong local responses in the spatial domain, whereas they tend to show different patterns after the frequency transformation. The variations caused by leaf textures, branch details, and illumination patches are usually smaller in scale, less regular in orientation, and more scattered in space, so their spectrum contains more randomly distributed high-frequency components. By contrast, although the true crown boundary also carries high-frequency information, it reflects structural changes that extend along the contour and therefore exhibits a more organized pattern in the frequency domain. For BCMM, FFT is not used to simply smooth the feature. The purpose is to make those local responses that are entangled in the spatial domain easier to distinguish in the frequency domain, so that the interference from internal textures can be reduced and the contour-related responses can stand out more clearly. In this sense, the frequency-domain processing improves the signal-to-noise ratio of the information that is used for boundary modeling.

The processed spectrum is then mapped back to the spatial domain through the inverse Fourier transform, producing the feature map f3′. After this step, the pseudo-boundary responses that are caused by internal textures and illumination changes are weakened, while the responses that correspond to the true boundary are retained. The resulting feature map provides a cleaner basis for the contour modeling that follows.

BCMM next employs a state-space model to capture boundary continuity. Specifically, the feature map is unfolded along the spatial dimensions into a one-dimensional feature sequence X∈RB×L×C, where L = H × W. Under this representation, each spatial location in the two-dimensional feature map corresponds to one state unit in the sequence. The resulting representation is more suitable for subsequent feature extraction and helps separate adjacent crowns in overlapping regions.

PMCM

BCMM reduces the interference that pseudo-boundaries inside the crown bring to boundary prediction. But on RT-Set, confusion from background vegetation still remains after this stage. In real images, some weeds and shrubs are not target crowns, but they can still keep strong responses on the feature map and remain mixed with real crowns. Because of this, the network after BCMM does not only need to deal with whether the boundary is clear enough. It also needs to improve the separation between crown regions and background vegetation. We therefore introduce PMCM after BCMM, as shown in Fig. 2(C).

The design of PMCM comes from the sample patterns that we observed in RT-Set. In aerial images, a rubber tree crown usually corresponds to a relatively complete crown region, and its response often shows a continuous distribution in which the center is stronger and the outer area gradually becomes weaker. Background weeds and shrubs can sometimes look similar to tree crowns in color and texture, but their spatial distribution is usually more scattered, and their local structures are often more broken. As a whole, these background regions usually do not show the stable center-led pattern that a real crown tends to have. At this stage, local response strength alone is no longer enough. The network also needs to judge whether the overall spatial pattern of a candidate region is closer to that of a real crown.

We do not use a standard diffusion constraint or an anisotropic diffusion constraint, because these constraints do not focus on the main difficulty in the current task. A diffusion constraint is mainly used to reduce local fluctuations and make the response field smoother. An anisotropic diffusion constraint further tries to preserve clear boundaries during smoothing. These constraints are useful in general segmentation tasks, but the background interference in RT-Set is not mainly isolated noise. Many weed and shrub regions are not targets, but they can still form continuous local responses. Some of them even show relatively complete closed structures. In this case, local smoothing or boundary preservation alone is still not enough to separate real crowns from background vegetation in a stable way. What matters more here is whether the candidate region as a whole shows the spatial pattern in which the response decreases from the center to the periphery.

We introduce a Poisson constraint in PMCM for this reason. Unlike the constraints above, the Poisson equation includes an explicit source term. Because of this, the constraint does not act only on local response regularity. It also acts on the overall spatial distribution of the probability field. In our task, we treat the crown as a structure that is led by its center, and we use the source term to approximate the spatial drive that decreases from the center to the outer area. As a result, the constraint does not examine only whether a candidate region is locally continuous. It also examines whether the overall distribution of that region matches the shape of a real crown. For real crowns, this distribution is usually more consistent with the constraint. For weed and shrub regions, local patch responses may still appear, but these regions usually do not have a stable center, so their probability fields are more likely to deviate from the constraint. The later ablation study on physical constraints shows the same trend. Diffusion-based constraints and anisotropic diffusion constraints both improve the results to some extent, but the Poisson constraint gives the best overall result.

In implementation, PMCM first feeds the enhanced feature map f′′3 from BCMM into a four-layer multilayer perceptron to parameterize a spatial probability field u. We use this structure because, after boundary recovery and background suppression in the previous stage, the later module no longer needs to judge only whether local texture responses are strong. It also needs to estimate whether each spatial position is more consistent with the overall shape of a crown. Compared with direct local convolution, an MLP is more convenient for nonlinear mapping on the high-dimensional semantic feature at each position, and it can learn the probability that this position belongs to the crown region. Since the later stage needs second-order derivatives, we use the Swish activation function in the hidden layers so that the output probability field remains continuous in space.

CTLAM

To address the catastrophic forgetting issue arising from variations in canopy morphology and colour due to the phenological succession of rubber trees, we designed CTLAM, as illustrated in Fig. 2(D). This module establishes a negative feedback replay strategy based on training loss, dynamically adjusting the proportion of historical difficult samples by sensing real-time loss fluctuations of the model across different phenological stages.

CTLAM first established an empirical replay zone to store representative raw data pairs during the training process. Unlike storing feature maps, CTLAM opts to retain the original query image and its corresponding annotation mask, thereby ensuring historical samples can consistently extract the most accurate feature representations through the currently updated network parameters. To balance knowledge retention efficiency within limited computational resources, we set the capacity threshold of the replay buffer to N = 100.

Core Logic of CTLAM with Stability-Driven Top-K Replay

During the dynamic update process in the replay region, a priority replacement strategy according to the training loss is used. During the initial training process, a filling strategy is used, storing the first N samples seen, along with their pairs, in the replay buffer. Once the buffer is full, one-to-one replacement is triggered. The module keeps track of the training loss for the current batch, Lc, and compares it to the minimum loss sample, Lm, stored in the replay region. If Lc>Lm, a replacement process is triggered, replacing the sample with the lowest loss in the pool with the current highest loss sample. This process guarantees that the replay region keeps a pool of the most difficult samples for the segmentation task at any instance.

Results

Experimental setup

This section evaluates CSAF on multiple datasets. The compared methods include general instance segmentation models, such as Cascade Mask R-CNN [27], Swin Transformer [28], Hybrid Task Cascade [29], YOLACT [30], YOLOv5 [31], QueryInst [32], Mask R-CNN [33], and SOLOv2 [34]. We also include methods that were designed for crown segmentation, including DetecTree2 [35], MASK_RCNN_TCDD [36], and Tree Crown Delineator [37], as well as the ViT-based variant of CSAF. In addition, we compare with OpenSeeD [38], OpenWorldSAM [39], and SAM3 [40], which represent recent open-vocabulary segmentation models.

For most baseline models, we keep the training setting as consistent as possible. We use AdamW as the optimizer. The initial learning rate is set to 0.001, and the weight decay is set to 0.0001. All models are trained for 300 epochs. We resize the input images to 640 × 640 and use a fixed-size input setting. During training, we keep random horizontal flipping and use Expand, MinIoURandomCrop, and PhotoMetricDistortion as standard data augmentation.

Some methods are evaluated with their official settings rather than with a fully unified training recipe. Specifically, OpenSeeD, OpenWorldSAM, SAM3, YOLOv5, and YOLACT are adapted and evaluated with the default parameters and public weights from their official implementations, and we do not perform extra tuning on our datasets. CSAF and CSAF (ViT) use the same detection head, the same number of training epochs, and the same inference setting. The only difference between them is the backbone network. Unless otherwise stated, the remaining models are trained and tested under the same data split and are compared with the same evaluation metrics.

All experiments are conducted under the same software and hardware environment. We use Ubuntu 18.04, CUDA Toolkit 11.4, cuDNN 7.4.2, Python 3.9, and PyTorch 1.12.1. The hardware platform consists of an Intel Core i9 processor and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB memory.

In this study, AP(t) denotes the average precision evaluated at a given IoU threshold t.

Evaluation metrics used in this study are intended to capture the performance of the model on the test data from different aspects. The total AP is calculated by taking IoU thresholds from 0.05 increments from 0.5 to 0.95, then taking the mean of the results. This evaluation measure evaluates the performance of the model at different thresholds of overlap and provides a comprehensive evaluation of the total segmentation ability of the model. AP50 is evaluated at IoU threshold 0.5; due to the quite relaxed boundary condition at this threshold, it indicates the basic segmentation ability of the model. Specifically, for the CSAF model, the two enhancement modules (BCMM and PMCM) take as input features of the same layer, each creating five different settings that include Baseline (without any enhancement modules), Input = f2, Input = f3, Input = f4, and Input = f5. As shown in Table 3, the Baseline corresponds to using no feature enhancement. When all features are enhanced uniformly using the enhancement module on f3, CSAF significantly outperforms the Baseline across all key evaluation metrics. Specifically, AP increases from 37.93% to 42.28%, and AP50 rises from 70.52% to 76.63%. In contrast, enhancing the lower-level feature f2 or the higher-level features f4 and f5 results in little or no improvement over the Baseline. This suggests that directly applying the enhancement module to these features may amplify noise or lead to the loss of important information. Overall, feature f3 provides a better balance between spatial detail and semantic information, making it the most suitable input for the augmentation module.

Effectiveness of BCMM

Table 3 lists the results of BCMM and several alternative designs under the same setting. We first examine FFT and SSM separately. Adding FFT alone raises AP from 40.28% to 41.07%, whereas using SSM alone lowers AP to 39.45%. When the two are used together, BCMM gives the best result in this group. This result indicates that the effectiveness of BCMM does not come from either stage alone, but from their sequential cooperation. FFT alone brings only limited improvement, which suggests that suppressing internal interference is helpful but insufficient for recovering contours that have already been broken by occlusion. By contrast, SSM alone performs worse than the Baseline, indicating that direct structural modeling on features still mixed with pseudo-boundary responses is unreliable. The same tendency appears in the other combinations. Bilateral + SSM and BM3D + SSM both outperform SSM alone, yet both remain below BCMM, with Bilateral + SSM being closer to the best result. These results show that preprocessing is necessary before structural modeling, but the difference among preprocessing methods is not determined by denoising strength alone. What also matters is whether preprocessing can suppress internal interference while preserving the contour cues that still correspond to the true crown boundary.

We also compared BCMM with several alternative boundary-enhancement designs, including EPS, BAFP, and EGA. In overlapping-canopy scenes, the difficulty does not arise only from weak boundary responses. The true crown boundary is often interrupted by branches and leaves, while textures, local branch structures, and illumination patches inside the crown continue to interfere with boundary inference. Under this condition, methods that mainly strengthen local boundary responses address only part of the problem. Table 3 shows that EPS, BAFP, and EGA all improve the baseline to some extent, but all three remain below BCMM. EPS mainly improves AP50, while AP75 and APl change little. BAFP performs better on AP75 and APl, but its AP and AP50 are not the best in this group. EGA reaches a relatively high APm, yet this advantage does not extend to overall AP or AP75. By contrast, BCMM maintains an advantage across AP, AP75, and some scale-related metrics. We further replaced SSM with Transformer, LSTM, and Conv1D after FFT. Both FFT + Transformer and FFT + LSTM perform better than the Baseline, but neither exceeds BCMM. This result suggests that the later stage must not only propagate information over a wider range, but also organize contour cues that are weak, spatially scattered, and partially interrupted by occlusion. Conv1D is more confined to local propagation, and Transformer, although capable of global modeling, still falls behind BCMM in this experiment. Taken together, these results show that BCMM is better suited to the current task.

Physics-constraint ablation study of PMCM

As shown in Table 3, different physical constraint formulations lead to different degrees of improvement when the other modules in CSAF are kept unchanged. When no physical constraint is used, the performance drops noticeably compared with the full PMCM, although it still remains better than the original baseline. This suggests that the PMCM structure itself already helps reduce background interference to a certain extent, while the physical constraint further improves the selection of candidate regions.

After introducing the Laplace equation and Total Variation Regularization, the model performance is further improved, indicating that regularizing local responses and suppressing fragmented structures are beneficial for canopy segmentation. Anisotropic diffusion brings additional gains, especially under stricter IoU thresholds, which suggests that preserving stronger local boundaries during the diffusion process is also helpful. Reaction-Diffusion achieves results close to the optimum, implying that introducing additional correction terms into the diffusion process can further improve the discrimination of complex background responses. These results together indicate that local regularization and diffusion-based constraints are useful, but their improvements still mainly come from better local consistency, noise suppression, or boundary preservation. The best overall results are still achieved by the Poisson constraint. This difference is important because the advantage of Poisson in our setting is not simply stronger smoothing or better local edge preservation. Instead, through its explicit source term, it further constrains whether a candidate region follows a center-guided, outward-decaying spatial distribution, which is more consistent with the overall morphology of rubber tree crowns in aerial images. By contrast, background weeds and shrubs may also produce locally continuous or partially closed responses, but they usually lack such a stable center-dominant pattern. Therefore, although other physical constraints also improve the model to some extent, the Poisson formulation is more compatible with the canopy morphology prior used in PMCM.

Learning strategy ablation experiment

In order to validate the robustness of CTLAM in learning complex phenological patterns in rubber trees as well as its learning ability in identifying difficult samples, this section will systematically compare it with current mainstream training methods in academia. For fairness in comparison, all experiments will use the same CSAF structure, with only the logic in scheduling samples differing in the training process.

As shown in Table 3, the results exhibit a clear performance gradient. Ignoring the difficulty of samples across different phenological stages, the basic training method performs relatively poorly across all metrics. By gradually increasing the sample difficulty from easy to difficult during training, a slight improvement is observed in the AP metrics. However, under higher IoU thresholds, the improvement remains limited. Incorporating self-weighted learning and hard sample mining, similar to CTLAM, enhances performance in metrics corresponding to higher IoU thresholds. Employing the meta-learning approach, which updates parameters according to the environment, leads to a marginal overall improvement compared with hard sample mining alone, without requiring explicit sample scheduling. Notably, CTLAM demonstrates substantial gains across all metrics, indicating that solely relying on hard sample mining may result in fluctuations during training due to rapidly changing phenological disturbances.

Comparative experiments

Comparative experiments on the RT-set dataset

On RT-Set, CSAF with ResNet-50 reaches 42.28% AP, 76.63% AP50, 1.34 MAE, and 0.958 R2, which are the best overall results in this dataset. This advantage is still visible when the comparison is restricted to models with similar computational cost. DetecTree2, for example, uses 175.9 G FLOPs, which is close to the 177.4 G FLOPs of CSAF, but its AP is still lower by 4.31 points and its MAE remains much higher (2.41 vs. 1.34). The gap is therefore not limited to segmentation quality; it is also reflected in the stability of the counting results.

The open-vocabulary models show a less favorable pattern on RT-Set. OpenSeeD, OpenWorldSAM, and SAM3 all require more FLOPs, parameters, and GPU memory, but none of them exceeds CSAF on AP, MAE, or R2. Their AP values are 40.36%, 40.89%, and 41.99%, respectively, all below 42.28%, and their MAE values are 2.06, 1.94, and 1.98, all higher than 1.34. Some local metrics are still strong: SAM3 reaches the highest AP75 and APm, while OpenSeeD gives the best APl. But these advantages do not carry over to the main results. RT-Set is difficult mainly because adjacent crowns occlude each other, background vegetation introduces interference, and local contours are often incomplete. In this setting, larger model scale alone does not guarantee better overall segmentation or counting. CSAF performs better because its design is more directly aimed at these structural sources of error.

The visual results are consistent with the quantitative comparison. In Fig. 4(a), several conventional models and the open-vocabulary models produce more false detections in weed regions, and their boundary extraction becomes less stable when the crown contour is weak. Fig. 4(b) shows a similar difference in the defoliation stage. Conventional instance segmentation models often produce incomplete masks, and although the open-vocabulary models can still detect some crowns, their masks remain less complete than those of CSAF and its ViT variant. We provide further discussion of the quantitative results across different phenological stages in Appendix A.

Comparative experiments on the BT-set dataset

BT-Set was collected in Dayangshan Forest Park, Yongjia County, Zhejiang Province, China (28°17′N–28°19′N, 120°26′E−120°28′E), which lies in the mid-subtropical evergreen broad-leaved forest zone. The dataset contains 2430 images of Chinese bayberry tree crowns, which were divided into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 7:2:1, giving 1,701, 486, and 243 images, respectively. In this dataset, the crown body is usually fuller and closer to a spherical shape than in RT-Set. At the same time, the spectral similarity between crowns and understory vegetation remains strong, and shadows under complex illumination still make boundary judgment difficult, especially near the transition between crown and non-target regions [54].

The results on BT-Set are more dispersed than those on RT-Set. No single method leads clearly across all metrics. As shown in Table 4, OpenSeeD reaches the highest AP, at 64.91%, and also gives the highest AP75, at 83.90%. OpenWorldSAM gives the best APm and APl, reaching 64.26% and 74.26%, respectively. Tree Crown Delineator also remains competitive on AP75 and APl, where it reaches 80.26% and 66.64%. CSAF reaches 63.94% AP, which is slightly lower than OpenSeeD, OpenWorldSAM, and DetecTree2, but it still gives 94.89% AP50 and achieves the best counting-related results, with MAE reduced to 0.96 and R2 increased to 0.972. This pattern is consistent with the characteristics of BT-Set. Because the main crown body is more complete here, the gap among methods in basic crown recognition becomes smaller than that on RT-Set. But understory vegetation and local shadows still interfere with boundary judgment and instance separation. As a result, some methods obtain higher AP, AP75, or size-related metrics, while these advantages do not always carry over to counting error or fitting consistency.

The visual results reflect the same tendency. In Fig. 4(c)–(d), some methods are more likely to merge shadows or non-target vegetation into the crown when shadows are heavy or understory weeds are obvious, and this behavior is more visible for Mask R-CNN. QueryInst also shows less stable separation when adjacent crowns are close to each other, with some local regions presenting missing contours or insufficient separation. By contrast, CSAF usually gives more complete masks in these cases, and the boundaries between adjacent individuals remain clearer.

Comparative experiments on the UT-set dataset

UT-Set was collected from urban areas in Lleida, Spain, and covers several typical city scenes, including main roads, residential areas, parks, and dense street blocks. Compared with forest or orchard datasets, this dataset contains smaller crowns, larger species variation, and more frequent occlusion from buildings, parked vehicles, roadside facilities, and surrounding low vegetation. These factors make UT-Set less dependent on large and relatively complete crown regions, and more dependent on whether a model can separate small targets under strong local interference.

The comparison results in Table 4 show that UT-Set does not favor the same models that perform well in forest or orchard scenes. OpenSeeD, OpenWorldSAM, and SAM3 give the highest AP values, reaching 52.12%, 51.22%, and 50.87%, respectively. OpenSeeD also ranks first on AP75, APm, and APl. But these stronger region-level segmentation results do not carry over to the counting-related metrics. None of these models surpasses CSAF on MAE or R2. CSAF reaches 48.31% AP, which is lower than the AP of the three open-vocabulary models, but it still gives 77.03% AP50, reduces MAE to 1.42, and raises R2 to 0.957. Its inference speed also remains much higher, at 32.6 FPS, whereas OpenSeeD, OpenWorldSAM, and SAM3 run at 3.8, 1.6, and 2.4 FPS, respectively. Under this setting, UT-Set makes one difference more visible: a model with higher region-level segmentation scores is not necessarily the one that gives more stable instance-level prediction.

Generalization experiments on the CTF-set dataset

CTF-Set was collected from a temperate mixed-forest region in Québec, Canada, and includes several environmental elements, such as hills, wetlands, and lakes. Broadleaf and conifer species coexist in this region, and most stands are under natural regeneration. The dataset contains 5321 images and covers 24 species from 12 families. Compared with the previous datasets, crown scale, crown shape, and occlusion vary more strongly in this setting, so CTF-Set provides a harder test for transfer to a new forest environment.

We use CTF-Set to evaluate direct transfer to an unseen forest type. All models are tested with the weights trained on RT-Set, and no fine-tuning or hyperparameter adjustment is applied. The results therefore reflect performance after scene shift rather than performance after adaptation. Under this setting, most methods drop noticeably relative to their results on RT-Set. The transfer from rubber plantations to temperate mixed forests is difficult, because species composition, crown structure, and occlusion pattern all change at the same time. DetecTree2 reaches 31.6% AP, Mask R-CNN reaches 30.2%, and many other models fall below 25%.

CSAF loses less performance in this test. Its AP, AP50, AP75, and APl are 35.4%, 76.7%, 27.6%, and 35.5%, respectively, while its MAE and R2 are 1.56 and 0.938. All of these are the best results in the current comparison. Relative to DetecTree2, CSAF gains 3.8 points on AP, 7.0 points on AP50, and 4.0 points on APl. The open-vocabulary models do not show a clear advantage here either. OpenSeeD, OpenWorldSAM, and SAM3 reach 21.8%, 23.0%, and 29.3% AP, respectively, all below CSAF. In this test, larger model scale and open-vocabulary capability do not translate into better transfer results. What matters more is how much performance a model can retain after the shift from RT-Set to CTF-Set, and CSAF remains more stable in this respect.

The visual examples in Fig. 4(g)–(h) show the same tendency. In the mixed-forest environment, crown shapes vary more strongly and occlusion is more complex, so many methods tend to merge adjacent crowns or miss crowns with unusual shapes. CSAF still separates adjacent crowns more reliably and preserves more complete crown responses under these conditions.

Discussion

To test the applicability of CSAF, we carried out tree counting experiments on the RT-Set and CTF-Set datasets. Based on previous experiment results, it has been shown that among the methods being compared, DetecTree2 has relatively high stability. Based on this information, the experiment on tree counting in this paper uses DetecTree2 as the baseline.

Counting evaluation on the RT-set dataset

Single-species plantations are widely used in forest monitoring tasks and thus form an important setting for testing whether a model can produce stable predictions over a long period of time. In this experiment, we perform cross-period counting tasks on the RT-Set dataset to test CSAF's rubber forest counting accuracy. Considering that the original RT-Set images have high spatial resolution but the input size for training is constrained to 640 × 640 pixels, making a direct inference on images leads to a significant drop in model accuracy. To overcome this limitation, a tiling inference approach is adopted. Each image is cut into patches of 1000 × 1000 pixels with a 200-pixel boundary for all sides to avoid undercounting trees in the boundary region of adjacent patches.

As depicted in Fig. 5(A), CSAF has been able to achieve an average counting accuracy of 97.88% on RT-Set, which is significantly higher than the average counting accuracy of 82.33% achieved by DetecTree2. Additionally, CSAF has been able to sustain a high level of counting performance throughout the year-long counting process with least effects of phenological variation. On the contrary, DetecTree2 has been observed to vary significantly at different stages of phenological variation.

As shown in Fig. 5(C d), (C.e), and (C.f), DetecTree2 has very low-quality segmentation results in March 2024. During this phenological phase, there are large ground blank regions with heavy leaf fall. DetecTree2 tended to classify fallen leaves into trees, while CSAF correctly segments each individual tree even under heavy leaf fall interference and low crown clarity of trees in the leaf-off phase. The above results show that CSAF has high accuracy for cross-phenophase monitoring regardless of strong phenological variations.

Counting evaluation on the CTF-set dataset

The crown patterns and spectra of various species of trees are quite different from each other in the case of mixed forests, and this causes heavy demands on the generalization capability of the model. Therefore, we designed cross-species and cross-zone counting tasks on CTF-Set. In this case, the model is not trained on CTF-Set but uses the weights obtained from RT-Set directly without any modifications.

In the case of the mixed forest counting problem, CSAF keeps its high accuracy in all three regions, as depicted in Fig. 5(B). However, the accuracy of the DetecTree2 method fluctuates greatly from one region to another. For example, in region 3, where the trees are relatively larger and closer to each other, the crowns’ boundaries are more difficult to detect. In this region, the accuracy of the DetecTree2 method is reduced to 77%, while CSAF keeps its accuracy above 92%.

As seen from the results of visualization in Fig. 5(D), CSAF is more robust in areas with smaller crowns (Zone 1 and Zone 2), where the majority of crowns are well separated and boundaries are well maintained. Meanwhile, DetecTree2 has a tendency to under-segment these regions and miss some crowns. Of the three zones, Zone 3 poses the most difficult task. Although the size of the zone is smaller, the arrangement of crowns is more irregular, and the crowns are much larger, making them more error-prone for identification and counting. In this case, the performance of DetecTree2 deteriorates. However, CSAF maintains a count accuracy of 92%. On the whole, it is clear from these experiments that CSAF is able to perform large-scale counting tasks effectively. There is no variation in CSAF's results in either pure stands or mixed forests, and it is not affected by the different stages of phenology either. The results are consistent across all species and regions, and this makes CSAF ready for use in practical situations as well.

Counting error analysis

In this section, we provide a detailed analysis of the counting errors observed across the models in the above experiments. This error analysis further clarifies the respective strengths and limitations of the models. Previous studies have provided a relatively systematic discussion of error sources in crown segmentation [55,56]. Related work typically groups these errors into three categories: (1) Missed Crowns, (2) Merged or Fragmented Crowns, and (3) Incorrect Vegetation Segmentation. This categorization also provides a clear basis for the subsequent analysis. In the following analysis, we adopt the same criteria to quantitatively assess the errors.

As shown in Fig. 5(E), the distribution of segmentation errors shows large differences under different ecological and phenological conditions for these two representative datasets. For the RT-Set dataset, the seasonal dynamics have a significant effect on the capability of the model in outlining the boundaries of the crown. The over-segmentation and under-segmentation that occurred in the budding and growing stages were strongly related to the increased density of the canopy and the indistinct boundaries of the crown. Despite being affected by the seasonal dynamics, CSAF has shown more stable control of errors than DetecTree2 in the merged and fragmented crowns.

From the interspecies assessment on the CTF-Set dataset, the error distribution in segmentation showed notable differences. In the heterogeneous forest scenario, most errors were mainly caused by insufficient adaptation to interspecies morphological differences and confusion between non-canopy regions, such as lakeshores and rocky outcrops. DetecTree2 tends to misclassify rocky outcrops as tree crown pixels in Zone 1 and Zone 2, while CSAF occasionally misclassified image boundaries or water bodies as vegetation. These mis-segmentations can be considered a potential trade-off for the models’ robust generalization. It is worth noting that non-target vegetation in a heterogeneous forest tends to be more irregular in shape, providing clearer cues for the PMCM. Consequently, the proposed CSAF outperforms the baseline DetecTree2 in suppressing the influence of non-target vegetation.

Limitations and future directions

Although CSAF generally performs well across the evaluated datasets, it still faces certain difficulties in complex forest-field environments, as indicated by the experimental results and error distributions. The PMCM leverages a physical constraint expressed through the Poisson equation, effectively eliminating irregular structures of weeds, as demonstrated in the analyses above. However, when the structures of the vegetation resemble the diffusion patterns described by the Poisson equation, CSAF's performance declines accordingly. In the RT-Set dataset, the difference in the number of incorrectly segmented vegetation between CSAF and DetecTree2 is relatively small, which is closely related to the morphology of the non-target vegetation. In some regions, non-target vegetation exhibits a circular or ellipsoidal shape, similar to the rubber trees, rather than apparent irregularity or clutter. Even when the physical constraint from the Poisson equation is applied, its effect is weakened because both non-target vegetation and rubber trees share similar characteristics in the equation. When the distribution of non-target vegetation follows the diffusion pattern described in the Poisson equation, the discriminative constraint becomes considerably less effective, and the segmentation performance is affected accordingly. In light of these observations, future research could focus on improving the learnability of the constraint itself. One possible direction is to learn a physical constraint dynamically, allowing the system to automatically select an appropriate form of partial differential equations from a pool of candidates that best fits the observed crown geometries. Alternatively, a meta-constraint could be learned to adapt to the characteristics of the scene, enabling the system to remain flexible in the presence of non-target vegetation with morphology similar to that of tree crowns, thereby ensuring stable segmentation performance in more complex environments.

Practical considerations for real-world applications

The comparison with open-vocabulary models indicates that CSAF does not show an absolute advantage across all scenarios and metrics. Models such as SAM3, OpenSeeD, and OpenWorldSAM already provide strong general-purpose segmentation capability, making them particularly useful for rapid surveys in new areas and for assisted annotation. When species-specific annotations are unavailable, or when the objective is to obtain preliminary masks and rough counts quickly, these methods can serve as low-cost starting tools.

For long-term forest management, however, where tree counts must be accurate, repeatable, and comparable over time, CSAF is better suited as the final counting model. Unlike open-vocabulary models, CSAF can be trained for a fixed target and a well-defined application setting, resulting in a more stable counting tool that can be readily incorporated into recurring tasks such as annual inventories, yield estimation, and management planning. It is also worth noting that, even after fine-tuning, the counting accuracy of the open-vocabulary models remained below that of CSAF. This suggests that, for tree counting in fixed operational scenarios, general segmentation ability alone is insufficient. Models specifically designed for crown segmentation and counting therefore remain necessary and practically valuable.

In practice, the two types of methods can play complementary roles at different stages. Open-vocabulary models are well suited to the initial deployment phase in new areas, for example by supporting manual annotation and helping to build an initial training set. A subset of these outputs can then be corrected by experts and used to train or fine-tune CSAF. After adaptation to the target scene, CSAF can be applied to large-scale inference and fine-grained counting in subsequent survey cycles, providing more consistent statistics for long-term monitoring.

Conclusion

This paper presents CSAF, a model for tree canopy segmentation and counting. To address issues such as overlapping canopies, interference from background vegetation, and seasonal variations, CSAF integrates three key modules. BCMM enhances the intensity of canopy profile features through denoising and state-space modeling. PMCM introduces a Poisson constraint mechanism to efficiently remove interference from irregularly distributed background vegetation. CTLAM uses learning state feedback to dynamically control the proportion and scale of sample replay, mitigating the catastrophic forgetting problem caused by seasonal variations in canopies. In addition, we introduce a new public dataset for rubber tree segmentation and counting, which aims to provide a benchmark for evaluating models’ ability to perform tree canopy segmentation and counting tasks.

Experimental results demonstrate that CSAF performs robustly across all tasks. On the RT-Set dataset, it achieves an AP50 of 76.63%, surpassing the previous best result by 5.35%. In the long-term counting experiment, CSAF maintains high accuracy throughout the year, with an average counting accuracy of 97.88%, exceeding the 82.33% achieved by DetecTree2. In the generalization experiment, it attains an AP50 of 76.71%, and the counting accuracy across all three zones exceeds 92%, demonstrating its effectiveness in unseen forest environments. In summary, the three-module CSAF system provides a robust and reliable solution for crown segmentation and canopy counting in UAV imagery.

In the mature stage, crowns are usually fuller and their outlines are easier to preserve. In the defoliation stage, the canopy becomes sparse, and branches and background regions interfere more strongly with the prediction.

Most models follow this trend.
