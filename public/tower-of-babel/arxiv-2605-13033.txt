Separable surfaces that are critical points of the Dirichlet energy
Rafael López

Abstract
. In this paper, we study surfaces z = φ ⁡ ( x , y ) z=\varphi(x,y) in Euclidean space that satisfy the equation φ x ​ x + φ y ​ y = Λ 2 \varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{yy}=\frac{\Lambda}{2} where Λ ∈ ℝ \Lambda\in\mathbb{R} is a real constant. We classify these surfaces when they are the zero level sets of an implicit equation of the type f ⁡ ( x ) + g ⁡ ( y ) + h ⁡ ( z ) = 0 f(x)+g(y)+h(z)=0 , where f f , g g and h h are smooth functions of one variable. If Λ = 0 \Lambda=0 , we find a large family of surfaces with interesting symmetry properties. However, if Λ ≠ 0 \Lambda\not=0 , we show that the surfaces must be either surfaces of revolution or of the type z = f ⁡ ( x ) + g ⁡ ( y ) z=f(x)+g(y) ; furthermore, explicit parametrizations of these surfaces are obtained.

1.
Introduction and classification

In this paper, we study solutions of the equation

\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{yy}=\frac{\Lambda}{2},

(1)

where
\varphi=\varphi(x,y)
is a smooth function defined in a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a real constant. Solutions
\varphi
of Eq. (
1
) are critical points of the functional

E[\varphi]=\int_{\Omega}|D\varphi|^{2}+\Lambda\int_{\Omega}\varphi.

The first term is the Dirichlet energy of
\varphi
and the second integral is the volume of the graph
z=\varphi(x,y)
. Let
\Sigma
be the graph
z=\varphi(x,y)
viewed as a surface in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If
\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3})
is the unit normal vector to
\Sigma
, then
\nu=(-D\varphi,1)/\sqrt{1+|D\varphi|^{2}}
and thus
\nu_{3}=1/\sqrt{1+|D\varphi|^{2}}
This allows us to express the Dirichlet energy as a functional
\mathcal{F}
defined by

\mathcal{F}(\Sigma):=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\dfrac{1}{\nu_{3}}-\nu_{3}\right)\,d\Sigma,

(2)

where
d\Sigma
is the area element of
\Sigma
. Consequently,
\Sigma
is a critical point of
E
if and only if
\Sigma
is a critical point of the energy (
2
) for all compactly supported, volume-preserving variations of
\Sigma
, where
\Lambda
is a Lagrange multiplier. The functional
\mathcal{F}
defined in (
2
) is of anisotropic type because the integral depends on the unit normal vector
\nu
of
\Sigma
. In general, anisotropic functionals are energies of the type

\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)=\int_{\Sigma}F(\nu)\,d\Sigma,

(3)

where
F\colon U\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a smooth function defined on an open set
U
of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. These energies appear in fluid phenomena when the surface tensions of interfaces depend on the orientation
[
10
]
. From a differential-geometric viewpoint, anisotropic functionals (
3
) have received significant interest, especially regarding problems related to the stability of the surface. We refer the reader to the works of Koiso and Palmer
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
and the references therein.
On the other hand, very recently the author has obtained a full classification of the surfaces that are solutions of (
1
) of ruled type
[
7
]
or of rotational and cyclic type
[
8
]
.

For functionals of type (
3
), a surface
\Sigma
is a critical point for all volume-preserving variations of
\Sigma
if and only if the quantity
\Lambda
, defined by

\Lambda:=2HF-\mbox{div}_{\Sigma}DF,

is constant, where
DF
is the gradient on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
H
is the mean curvature of
\Sigma
. If the functional (
3
) is elliptic, we associate it with the
Wulff shape

\xi(\nu)=DF(\nu)+F(\nu)\nu
, which is a convex surface. The Wulff shape is the unique global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
when a volume constraint is fixed
[
1
,
10
]
. For the Dirichlet energy (
2
) the anisotropic mean curvature coincides with the value
\Lambda
in Eq. (
1
) and the Wulff shape is the paraboloid
z=x^{2}+y^{2}
. On the other hand, the isotropic case corresponds to
F\equiv 1
in (
3
) where
\Lambda
is now the mean curvature
H
of the surface and the Wulff shape is the round sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

An approach to finding solutions of Eq. (
1
) is to consider a separation of variables of the type
\varphi(x,y)=f(x)+g(y)
or
\varphi(x,y)=f(x)g(y)
, where
f
and
g
are smooth functions of one variable. These solutions are easily obtained from (
1
): see also Prop.
1.2
.

In this paper, we address the problem of finding the solutions of (
1
) by separation of variables in full generality. For this purpose, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

A (regular) surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be separable if
\Sigma
can be expressed as

\Sigma=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:f(x)+g(y)+h(z)=0\},

(4)

where
f
,
g
and
h
are smooth functions defined in intervals of
\mathbb{R}
, respectively.

By the regularity of
\Sigma
,
f^{\prime}(x)^{2}+g^{\prime}(y)^{2}+h^{\prime}(z)^{2}\not=0
for every
(x,y,z)\in\Sigma
. The symbol
(^{\prime})
denotes the derivative with respect to the corresponding variable of the function. In such a case, the unit normal vector is
\nu=(f^{\prime},g^{\prime},z^{\prime})/\sqrt{f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}+h^{\prime 2}}
.

For particular choices of functions in (
4
), we obtain special types of separable surfaces.

(1)

Translation surfaces. A translation surface is a surface given by
z=f(x)+g(y)
. These surfaces correspond to (
4
) when
h(z)=-z
. A translation surface
\Sigma
is obtained by the sum of the planar curves
x\mapsto(x,0,f(x))
and
y\mapsto(0,y,g(y))
. A particular case occurs when
f
or
g
is a constant function, in which case,
\Sigma
is a cylindrical surface; that is, a ruled surface whose rulings are parallel straight lines. For example, if
g(y)=y_{0}
, then
\Sigma
is generated by the planar curve
z=f(x)
contained in the
xz
-coordinate plane and the rulings are parallel to the
y
-axis.

(2)

Homothetical surfaces. These surfaces are given by
z=p(x)q(y)
. Here, the choice in (
4
) is
h(z)=-\log z
.

(3)

Rotational surfaces. If
\Sigma
is a rotational surface with respect to the
z
-axis, then
\Sigma
can be expressed as
z=p(x^{2}+y^{2})
. Here
f(x)=x^{2}
and
g(y)=y^{2}
.

Notation convention: To increase readability of the solutions obtained in this paper, we use
a_{i},b_{i},\ldots
to denote arbitrary constants of integration arising in the expressions of solutions of ODEs. We only discuss explicitly the cases in which some of these constants are non-zero.

Surfaces satisfying Eq. (
1
) and being of one of the above three types are obtained by a direct integration. The solutions are described in the following result.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface satisfying (
1
). In the following statements, the roles of the variables
x
and
y
can be interchanged.

(1)

(Translation surface). If
z=f(x)+g(y)
, then

\begin{split}f(x)&=\frac{\Lambda+2m}{4}x^{2}+a_{1}x+a_{2},\\
g(y)&=-\frac{m}{2}y^{2}+b_{1}y+b_{2},\end{split}

(5)

with
m\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

(Homothetical surface). Suppose
z(x,y)=p(x)q(y)
.

(a)

Case
\Lambda=0
. Then
p
and
q
are linear functions or

\begin{split}p(x)&=a_{1}\cos(kx)+a_{2}\sin(kx),\\
q(y)&=b_{1}\cosh(ky)+b_{2}\sinh(ky),\\
\end{split}

(6)

with
k\in\mathbb{R}
,
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}\not=0
and
b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}\not=0
.

(b)

Case
\Lambda\not=0
. Then
q(x)=q_{0}\not=0
is constant and

p(x)=\frac{\Lambda}{4q_{0}}x^{2}+a_{1}x+a_{2}.

(7)

(3)

(Rotational surface). If
z=p(x^{2}+y^{2})
, then

z(x,y)=c_{1}\log(x^{2}+y^{2})+\frac{\Lambda}{8}(x^{2}+y^{2})+c_{2}.

(8)

Remark 1.3
.

(1)

Cylindrical surfaces appear in (
5
) when
m=b_{1}=0
, which coincide with the homothetical surfaces given in (
7
).

(2)

If we take
m=-\Lambda/4
in the translation surface (
5
), then we obtain the rotational surface (
8
) for
c_{1}=0
.

(3)

The Wulff shape corresponds to
c_{1}=0
in (
8
).

(4)

All translation surfaces (
5
), and homothetical surfaces (
6
), (
7
) are entire graphs. Rotational surfaces (
8
) are entire graphs if
c_{1}=0
and graphs on
\mathbb{R}^{2}-\{(0,0)\}
if
c_{1}\not=0
.

This work is also motivated by the isotropic setting, corresponding to
F\equiv 1
in (
3
). The classification of the separable surfaces with constant mean curvature
H
is well known. When
H=0
, there exists a large variety of examples due to Schwarz, Fréchet, Scherk, and Weingarten, among others: see
[
9
, pp. 71-80]
for a description of them. When
H\not=0
, the classification was obtained more recently by T. Hasanis and the author, who proved that the only separable surfaces with non-zero constant mean curvature are surfaces of revolution (Delaunay surfaces)
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we classify all separable surfaces that are critical points of the Dirichlet energy.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a separable surface given by the implicit equation (
4
). If
\Sigma
satisfies (
1
), then
\Sigma
is a surface described in Prop.
1.2
or
\Lambda=0
and the first integrals of the functions
f
,
g
and
h
in (
4
) are given by

\left\{\begin{split}f^{\prime}(x)^{2}&=r\cos(kf(x)-d_{1})+a,\\
g^{\prime}(y)^{2}&=r\cos(kg(y)-d_{2})-a,\\
h^{\prime}(z)^{2}&=c(1+\cos(kh(z)+d_{1}+d_{2}),\end{split}\right.

(9)

or

\left\{\begin{split}f^{\prime}(x)^{2}&=r\cosh(kf(x)-d_{1})+a,\\
g^{\prime}(y)^{2}&=r\cosh(kg(y)-d_{2})-a,\\
h^{\prime}(z)^{2}&=c(1+\cosh(kh(z)+d_{1}+d_{2}),\end{split}\right.

(10)

with
r,k,c\not=0
.

This classification exhibits similarities with the isotropic case. If we distinguish whether
\Lambda
is
0
or not, we see that if
\Lambda=0
, the family of separable surfaces is large, with many examples obtained after fixing the constants of integration in (
9
) and (
10
) (see Sect.
5
). In contrast, if
\Lambda\not=0
, then Thm.
1.4
shows that the only possibilities are those already described in Prop.
1.2
. Taking into account item (1) of Rem.
1.3
, we emphasize this case in the following corollary.

Corollary 1.5
.

If
\Lambda\not=0
, then the surfaces defined in (
5
) and (
8
) are the only separable surfaces satisfying Eq. (
1
).

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Sect.
2
we prove Prop.
1.2
. We prove Thm.
1.4
in two steps, which will be carried out in Sects.
3
and
4
. In the last section (Sect.
5
), we show explicit examples of surfaces obtained via Thm.
1.4
by solving (
9
) and (
10
). These surfaces have interesting symmetry properties, proving in particular that they are doubly periodic surfaces.

2.
Proof of Proposition
1.2

In this section, we prove Prop.
1.2
, the proof of which is almost immediate. See Figs.
1
and
2
.

(1)

If
z(x,y)=f(x)+g(y)
, then Eq. (
1
) becomes
f^{\prime\prime}+g^{\prime\prime}=\Lambda/2
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}
depends only on
x
and
g^{\prime\prime}
on
y
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f^{\prime\prime}-\frac{\Lambda}{2}=m=-g^{\prime\prime}

and the solutions are given in (
5
).

(2)

If
z(x,y)=p(x)q(y)
, then Eq. (
1
) is
p^{\prime\prime}q+pq^{\prime\prime}=\Lambda/2
. Discarding the trivial cases
p=0
or
q=0
where the solution is a horizontal plane, we divide by
pq
, obtaining

\frac{p^{\prime\prime}}{p}+\frac{q^{\prime\prime}}{q}=\frac{\Lambda}{2pq}.

(11)

If
\Lambda=0
, then there exists
m\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\frac{p^{\prime\prime}}{p}=m=-\frac{q^{\prime\prime}}{q}.

If
m=0
, then
p
and
q
are linear functions. If
m=-k^{2}<0
, the solutions are given in (
6
). If
m=k^{2}>0
, we obtain (
6
) again by interchanging the roles of
p
and
q
.

Suppose
\Lambda\not=0
. Differentiating (
11
) with respect to
x
and then with respect to
y
, we obtain
\Lambda p^{\prime}q^{\prime}=0
. Thus
p^{\prime}=0
or
q^{\prime}=0
; that is,
p
or
q
are constant functions. If, for example,
q(x)=q_{0}\not=0
is constant, then (
11
) becomes
p^{\prime\prime}q_{0}=\Lambda/2
and the solution is given in (
7
).

(3)

Let
r=x^{2}+y^{2}
. Then Eq. (
1
) becomes
4rh^{\prime\prime}+4h^{\prime}=\Lambda/2
, whose solution is given in (
8
).

3.
First step of the proof of Theorem
1.4

We consider the general case in the implicit equation (
4
). From now on, we will discard the cases covered by Prop.
1.2
.
If
z=z(x,y)
, differentiating with respect to
x
we have

f^{\prime}+h^{\prime}z_{x}=0
and differentiating again, we arrive at
f^{\prime\prime}+h^{\prime\prime}z_{x}^{2}+h^{\prime}z_{xx}=0
. Doing the same with the variable
y
, equation (
1
) becomes

(f^{\prime\prime}+g^{\prime\prime})h^{\prime 2}+(f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2})h^{\prime\prime}+\frac{\Lambda}{2}h^{\prime 3}=0.

(12)

We will obtain a first integral of the second-order differential equation (
12
), yielding three first-order differential equations (
9
) and (
10
) for the functions
f
,
g
and
h
.

Since the functions
f
,
g
and
h
are not constant, let us introduce the following variables

u=f(x),\quad v=g(y),\quad w=h(z).

Then the implicit equation (
4
) is simply

u+v+w=0.

(13)

Define the functions

X(u)=f^{\prime}(x)^{2},\quad Y(v)=g^{\prime}(y)^{2},\quad Z(w)=h^{\prime}(z)^{2}.

In terms of these new functions, the cases discarded by Prop.
1.2
are:

(1)

When one of the functions
X
,
Y
or
Z
is constant (cylindrical or translation surfaces).

(2)

Z(w)=e^{c_{1}w}
, with
c_{1}\not=0
(homothetical).

(3)

X(u)=a_{1}u+a_{2}u
and
Y(v)=b_{1}v+b_{2}
with
a_{1}=b_{1}
(rotational).

Differentiating the functions
X
,
Y
and
Z
, we have

X^{\prime}(u)=2f^{\prime\prime}(x),\quad Y^{\prime}(v)=2g^{\prime\prime}(y),\quad Z^{\prime}(w)=2h^{\prime\prime}(z).

With this change of variables, equation (
12
) takes the form

(X^{\prime}+Y^{\prime})Z+(X+Y)Z^{\prime}+\Lambda Z^{3/2}=0,

(14)

for all values of
u
,
v
and
w
under the condition (
13
).

We now differentiate Eq. (
14
) with respect to
u
,
v
and
w
. Notice that the variables
u
,
v
and
w
are not independent because of (
13
). Thus, it is not possible, for example, to differentiate the left-hand side of (
14
) with respect to
u
and ensure that it is
0
. However, we will use the following lemma, whose proof is immediate.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Q=Q(u,v,w)
be a smooth function defined in a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If
Q(u,v,w)=0
for any triple of the section
\Omega\cap\Pi
, where
\Pi
is the plane with equation
u+v+w=0
, then on this section we have
Q_{u}=Q_{v}=Q_{w}
, or equivalently,

Q_{u}-Q_{v}=0,\quad Q_{u}-Q_{w}=0,\quad Q_{v}-Q_{w}=0,

(15)

where
Q_{u}
,
Q_{v}
and
Q_{v}
are the partial derivatives of
Q
with respect to
u
,
v
and
w
, respectively.

By applying (
15
), we differentiate (
14
) with respect to
u
and
v
, obtaining

Z\left(X^{\prime\prime}-Y^{\prime\prime}\right)+Z^{\prime}\left(X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime}\right)=0.

(16)

We apply (
15
) again, differentiating with respect to
u
and
v
,

Z\left(X^{(3)}+Y^{(3)}\right)+Z^{\prime}\left(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}\right)=0.

(17)

Viewing Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
) as a linear system in
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
and since
Z\not=0
, the determinant of the coefficients must vanish. This gives

X^{\prime\prime 2}-Y^{\prime\prime 2}-(X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime})(X^{(3)}+Y^{(3)})=0.

(18)

In this equation, the variable
w
does not appear. If we apply (
15
) by differentiating (
18
) with respect to
u
and
w
, then we are differentiating (
18
) with respect to
u
and the equation still holds. The same applies to the variable
v
. Having established this, we only differentiate (
18
) with respect to
u
and then with respect to
v
, obtaining

X^{(4)}Y^{\prime\prime}-Y^{(4)}X^{\prime\prime}=0.

(19)

Since the roles of
X
and
Y
are symmetric, the following arguments will be applied to
X
, and similar reasoning holds for the function
Y
.

The remainder of this section is devoted to the case
X^{\prime\prime}=0
identically in (
19
), while the general case
X^{\prime\prime}\not=0
will be considered in Sect.
4
.

Assume
X^{\prime\prime}=0
. Then
X(u)=a_{1}u+a_{2}
, where
a_{1}\not=0
. Substituting this into (
18
), we have

(Y^{\prime}-a_{1})Y^{\prime\prime\prime}-Y^{\prime\prime 2}=0.

(20)

In order to solve this equation, we separate the cases where
Y^{\prime}-a_{1}=0
identically and
Y^{\prime}-a_{1}\not=0
.

(1)

Case
Y^{\prime}-a_{1}=0
identically. The solution to this equation is
Y(v)=a_{1}v+b_{2}
. But this implies that the surface is rotational because
X
and
Y
are linear functions of their variables and the coefficient of
v
coincides with that of
u
in the expression for
X(u)
. This case was initially discarded.

(2)

Case
Y^{\prime}-a_{1}\not=0
. The solution to (
20
) is

Y(v)=a_{1}v+\frac{1}{b_{1}}e^{b_{1}v+b_{2}}+b_{3},\quad b_{1}\not=0.

Now (
17
) becomes

b_{1}e^{b_{1}v+b_{2}}(Z^{\prime}+b_{1}Z)=0.

Thus
Z^{\prime}+b_{1}Z=0
, whose solution is
Z(w)=c_{1}e^{-b_{1}w}
, with
c_{1}\not=0
. However, this situation corresponds with the homothetical surfaces in Prop.
1.2
, which were also discarded from the beginning. In fact, when

in Prop.
1.2
, the function
Y
is constant, which is not possible here because
Y^{\prime}-a_{1}\not=0
. Continuing with our current argument, and using the value of

, identity (
14
) becomes

c_{1}e^{-b_{1}w}\left(a_{2}b_{1}+b_{1}b_{3}-a_{1}(2+b_{1}w+\Lambda\sqrt{c_{1}}e^{-b_{1}w/2})\right)=0.

Since the functions
\{1,w,e^{-b_{1}w/2}\}
are linearly independent, we deduce that
a_{1}b_{1}=0
, which is a contradiction.

4.
Second step of the proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section, we complete the proof of Thm.
1.4
. Assume
X^{\prime\prime}\not=0
and, by symmetry, we also assume
Y^{\prime\prime}\not=0
. From (
19

m\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\frac{X^{(4)}}{X^{\prime\prime}}=m=\frac{Y^{(4)}}{Y^{\prime\prime}}.

(21)

The discussion depends on the sign of

4.1.
Case

.

We prove that this case is not possible. The solutions to (

) are

\begin{split}X(u)&=a_{1}u^{3}+a_{2}u^{2}+a_{3}u+a_{4},\\
Y(v)&=b_{1}v^{3}+b_{2}v^{2}+b_{3}v+b_{4}.\end{split}

Moreover,
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}\not=0
and
b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}\not=0
to ensure
X^{\prime\prime}\not=0
and
Y^{\prime\prime}\not=0
. Substituting into (
18
), we obtain a polynomial equation of the type

A_{0}+A_{1}u+B_{1}v+A_{2}u^{2}+B_{2}v^{2}=0
, where

\begin{split}A_{2}&=18a_{1}(a_{1}-b_{1}),\\
B_{2}&=18b_{1}(a_{1}-b_{1}),\\
A_{1}&=12a_{2}(a_{1}-b_{1}),\\
B_{1}&=12b_{2}(a_{1}-b_{1}),\\
A_{0}&=4(a_{2}^{2}-b_{2}^{2})-6(a_{1}+b_{1})(a_{3}-b_{3}-b_{4}).\end{split}

From
A_{2}=0
, we have two cases.

(1)

Case
a_{1}=0
. Then
B_{2}=0
implies
b_{1}=0
and from
A_{0}=0
, we have
b_{2}^{2}=a_{2}^{2}
with
a_{2}\not=0
. Thus
b_{2}=\pm a_{2}
and now (

\pm 4a_{2}Z^{\prime}=0
, which is a contradiction because
a_{2}\not=0
and
Z
is not a constant function.

(2)

Case
a_{1}=b_{1}
and
a_{1}\not=0
. Then
A_{0}=0
is written as

a_{2}^{2}-b_{2}^{2}+3a_{1}(b_{4}+b_{3}-a_{3})=0.

Now (
17
) becomes

6a_{1}Z+(a_{2}+b_{2}+3a_{1}(u+v))Z^{\prime}=0.

Since
u+v=-w
, this equation is converted to

6a_{1}Z+(a_{2}+b_{2}-3a_{1}w)Z^{\prime}=0.

Given
a_{1}\not=0
, the solution is

Z(w)=c_{1}(a_{2}+b_{2}-3a_{1}w)^{2},\quad c_{1}\not=0.

Substituting this into (
14
) we obtain a polynomial equation in the variables
u
,
v
and
w
. When we replace
w=-u-v
, this equation can be expressed as

P_{1}+\Lambda(c_{1}(a_{2}+b_{2}+3a_{1}(u+v))^{3/2}=0.

This implies

P_{1}^{2}-\Lambda^{2}(c_{1}(a_{2}+b_{2}+3a_{1}(u+v))^{3}=0.

This identity is a polynomial equation
P(u,v,u^{r}v^{s})=0
with
r+s\leq 8
. Thus, all coefficients must vanish. The coefficient of
u^{8}
is
81a_{1}^{6}c_{1}^{2}
. However,
81a_{1}^{6}c_{1}^{2}=0
is a contradiction because
a_{1},c_{1}\not=0
.

4.2.
Case
m=-k^{2}<0
.

For this value of
m
, the solutions for
X
and
Y
in (
21
) are

X(u)=a_{1}\cos(ku)+a_{2}\sin(ku)+a_{3}u+a_{4},

Y(v)=b_{1}\cos(kv)+b_{2}\sin(kv)+b_{3}v+b_{4}.

Here
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}\not=0
and
b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}\not=0
in order to ensure again that
X^{\prime\prime}\not=0
and
Y^{\prime\prime}\not=0
.
Substituting these into (
18
), we obtain

k\left(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}-b_{1}^{2}-b_{2}^{2}\right)+(b_{3}-a_{3})(a_{1}\sin(ku)-a_{2}\cos(ku)+b_{1}\sin(kv)-b_{2}\cos(kv))=0.

Since the trigonometric functions are linearly independent and
k\not=0
, we deduce

\begin{split}b_{2}(a_{3}-b_{3})&=0,\\
a_{2}(a_{3}-b_{3})&=0,\\
b_{1}(a_{3}-b_{3})&=0,\\
a_{1}(a_{3}-b_{3})&=0,\\
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}-b_{1}^{2}-b_{2}^{2}&=0.\end{split}

It is immediate that
a_{3}-b_{3}=0
because otherwise,
a_{1}=b_{1}=a_{2}=b_{2}=0
, which is not possible. Letting
b_{3}=a_{3}
, the last equation implies
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}=b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}
. Since this number is non-zero, there exist
r\not=0
and
d_{1},d_{2}
such that

\begin{split}&a_{1}=r\cos d_{1},\quad a_{2}=r\sin d_{1},\\
&b_{1}=r\cos d_{2},\quad b_{2}=r\sin d_{2}.\end{split}

Let
v=-u-w
. By computing identity (
17
), we have

\sin(\frac{1}{2}(k(2u+w)-d_{1}+d_{2}))\left(Z^{\prime}\cos(\frac{1}{2}(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))+kZ\sin(\frac{1}{2}(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))\right)=0.

Since
\sin(\frac{1}{2}(k(2u+w)-d_{1}+d_{2}))\not=0
because
u
and
w
are here independent variables, we deduce that the term in parentheses in the above equation must vanish identically. This leads to

\tan(\frac{1}{2}(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))=-\frac{Z^{\prime}}{kZ}.

The solution to this equation is

Z(w)=c_{1}(1+\cos(kw+d_{1}+d_{2})),\quad c_{1}\not=0.

Now (
16
) is trivially
0
and (
14
) becomes
P_{1}+\sqrt{2}\Lambda P_{2}=0
, where

\begin{split}P_{1}&=a_{3}c_{1}\left(2+2\cos(kw+d_{1}+d_{2})+kw\,\sin(kw+d_{1}+d_{2})\right),\\
P_{2}&=(c_{1}(1+\cos(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))^{3/2}.\end{split}

Thus,

P_{1}^{2}-2\Lambda^{2}c_{1}^{3}(1+\cos(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))^{3}=0.

(22)

This equation is a polynomial equation on the functions
w^{n_{1}}\sin(n_{2}(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))\cos(n_{3}(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))
, where
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,
n_{1}\leq 2
and
n_{2},n_{3}\leq 3
. Thus, all coefficients vanish identically. The coefficient of
\cos(3(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))
in (
22
) is
-\frac{1}{2}c_{1}^{3}\Lambda^{2}
. Since
c_{1}\not=0
, we have
\Lambda=0
. As a consequence, the case
\Lambda\not=0
cannot occur. This proves, together with the same conclusion in the case
m=k^{2}>0
below, the situation
\Lambda\not=0
described in Thm.
1.4
, where only the separable surfaces of Prop.
1.2
are possible.

We now continue the reasoning assuming from now on that
\Lambda=0
. The computation of the coefficient of
w\sin(2(kw+d_{1}+d_{2}))
in (
22
) is
2a_{3}^{2}c_{1}^{2}k
. Thus,
a_{3}=0
and, consequently,
b_{3}=0
. Now (
14
) reduces to

4k^{2}c_{1}^{2}(a_{4}+b_{4})^{2}(\sin(2(kw+d_{1}+d_{2})))^{2}=0.

This implies
b_{4}=-a_{4}
. Returning to the previous expressions, we have

\left\{\begin{split}X(u)&=r\cos(ku-d_{1})+a_{4},\\
Y(v)&=r\cos(kv-d_{2})-a_{4},\\
Z(w)&=c_{1}(1+\cos(kw+d_{1}+d_{2})).\end{split}\right.

Substituting these into (
14
) results in a trivial identity. Thus, these are the solutions that we seek, which coincide with (
9
).

4.3.
Case
m=k^{2}>0
.

The arguments are similar to the case
m<0
. We deduce
\Lambda=0
and

\left\{\begin{split}X(u)&=r\cosh(ku-d_{1})+a_{4},\\
Y(v)&=r\cosh(kv-d_{2})-a_{4},\\
Z(w)&=c_{1}(1+\cosh(kw+d_{1}+d_{2})).\end{split}\right.

These solutions coincide with (
10
).

5.
Explicit examples of separable surfaces

In this section, we provide some explicit examples of separable surfaces when
\Lambda=0
by solving the systems (
9
) and (
10
) in Thm.
1.4
. Note that numerous examples of surfaces can be obtained through various choices of the integration constants in both systems. To be precise, we present three examples. In the first two examples, the function
h(z)
is explicitly obtained. When solving the systems (
9
) and (
10
) for the functions
f
and
g
, elliptic integrals of the first kind arise, and the solutions can be expressed using the inverse of these functions. In the third example, we solve explicitly (
10
) for suitable choices of constants, obtaining the parametrization of the surface.

We derive the symmetry properties of these surfaces using the Schwarz reflection principle for harmonic functions. Since
\Lambda=0
, the surface is the graph of a function
\varphi=\varphi(x,y)
which is harmonic on the
xy
-plane. Consequently, we have the following two results, which are analogous to those appearing in the theory of minimal surfaces
[
3
]
.

(1)

If
\Sigma
contains a horizontal line
L
, then
\Sigma
can be extended analytically by
180^{0}
rotations across
L
. Equivalently, if
\Sigma
satisfies Eq. (
1
) for
\Lambda=0
and
L
is a straight line contained in
\Sigma
, then
\Sigma
is symmetric with respect to a
180^{0}
rotation about
L
.

(2)

If
\Sigma
meets a vertical plane
\Pi
orthogonally along a regular curve
C\subset\Sigma\cap\Pi
, then
\Sigma
can be extended analytically by symmetry across
\Pi
. Equivalently, if
\Sigma
satisfies Eq. (
1
) for
\Lambda=0
and
\Pi
is a plane that meets
\Sigma
orthogonally, then
\Sigma
is symmetric under reflection across
\Pi
.

5.1.
First example

We begin with the system (
9
). Let
k=-2
. Take
r=c_{1}=1
and
d_{1}=d_{2}=a=0
. Let
\Sigma_{1}
be the corresponding separable surface defined by the implicit equation (
4
). Then we have

\left\{\begin{split}f^{\prime}(x)^{2}&=\cos(2f(x)),\\
g^{\prime}(y)^{2}&=\cos(2g(y)),\\
h^{\prime}(z)^{2}&=1+\cos(2h(z)).\end{split}\right.

The first equation yields

\int\frac{df}{\sqrt{\cos(2f)}}=\int\frac{df}{\sqrt{1-2(\sin f)^{2}}}=x+\lambda,

with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. For simplicity, let
\lambda=0
. This integral is the elliptic integral of the first kind
F=F(\phi,2)
. Thus,
F(f,2)=x
and, analogously,
F(g,2)=y
. Let
M=M(\phi,2)
be a branch of the inverse function of
F
with
M(0,2)=0
. Thus,

\left\{\begin{split}f(x)&=M(x,2),\\
g(y)&=M(y,2).\end{split}\right.

We now find the function
h=h(z)
. In this case, we find
h
because

\int\frac{dh}{\sqrt{1+\cos(2h)}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tanh^{-1}(\sin(h)).

Thus, up to an additive constant,

h(z)=\sin^{-1}(\tanh(\sqrt{2}z)).

The surface is then defined by the implicit equation:

\Sigma_{1}:M(x,2)+M(y,2)+\sin^{-1}(\tanh(\sqrt{2}z))=0.

(23)

A picture of the surface is shown in Fig.
3
, left. The function
f(x)=M(x,2)
(also
g=g(y)
) is an odd function. Since
M(-x,2)=-M(x,2)
, the straight line
L=\{x,-x,0):x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is contained in
\Sigma
. Therefore
\Sigma_{1}
is invariant under a
180^{0}
rotation about
L
. This allows the surface to be extended by successive reflections. In fact, the surface contains two families of parallel straight lines
\{L_{m},L^{m}:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, all of which are contained in the horizontal plane
z=0
and defined by:

L_{m}:=\{(x,-x+4m,0):x\in\mathbb{R}\},\quad L^{m}:=\{(x,x+4m-2,0):x\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Figure 3.
The surface
\Sigma_{1}
defined in (
23
). Left: a piece of the surface showing a straight line (black) contained in the surface. Right: the same surface after reflection of
180^{0}
degrees across these lines.

Other symmetries of the surface can be obtained by reflections across vertical planes. In the case of the surface (
23
), these planes occur at the curves of
\Sigma
where
f^{\prime}(x)=0
and
g^{\prime}(y)=0
. The first point where the derivative of
f
vanishes is
x_{0}\approx 1.311
. By the symmetry of
f
, the same occurs at
-x_{0}
. Let
C=\Sigma_{1}\cap\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x=\pm x_{0}\}
. Since the normal
\nu
of
\Sigma_{1}
at
(x,y,z)\in\Sigma_{1}
is proportional to
(f^{\prime}(x),g^{\prime}(y),h^{\prime}(z))
, then
\nu
is contained in the vertical planes defined by
x=\pm x_{0}
. This proves that
\Sigma_{1}
meets both planes orthogonally (Fig.
4
, left). Thus the reflections across these planes extend the surface
\Sigma_{1}
(Fig.
4
, right). Similarly, since the function
g
coincides with
f
, the surface
\Sigma_{1}
meets the planes
y=\pm x_{0}
orthogonally and, thus,
\Sigma_{1}
can be also extended by reflections across these planes (Fig.
4
, right).

The above process of reflection across vertical planes can be repeated. These planes are situated at the points where
z
approaches
\pm\infty
. The points
(x,y)
where
z
is
\infty
are
\{((4m+1)x_{0},(4n+1)x_{0}):m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and, analogously, the points where
z
is
-\infty
are
\{((4m-1)x_{0},(4n-1)x_{0}):m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Thus, the vertical planes of symmetry are given by
\{x=(2n-1)x_{0}\}
and
\{y=(2n-1)x_{0}\}
. As a final observation, we point out that the surface is a global graph on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
except for a discrete set points which is invariant under translations of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in two linearly independent directions.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
\Sigma_{1}
is the surface defined in (
23
), then
\Sigma_{1}
is doubly periodic. Moreover,
\Sigma_{1}
is invariant under reflections across the lines
L_{m}
and
L^{m}
and under symmetries with respect to the planes
\{x=(2m-1)x_{0}\}
and
\{y=(2m-1)x_{0}\}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. The surface is a global graph on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
except for a discrete set points.

\Sigma_{1}
defined in (
23
). Left: a piece of the surface showing the intersection curves (black) of
\Sigma_{1}
with the vertical planes of equations
x=\pm x_{0}
. Right: the same surface after reflection across the plane
x=x_{0}
.

5.2.
Second example

For the second example, we take
k=2
in (
10
). Again, let
r=c_{1}=1
and
d_{1}=d_{2}=a=0
. The system to solve is

\left\{\begin{split}f^{\prime}(x)^{2}&=\cosh(2f(x)),\\
g^{\prime}(y)^{2}&=\cosh(2g(y)),\\
h^{\prime}(z)^{2}&=1+\cosh(2h(z)).\end{split}\right.

Again, the function
h
can be explicitly found, obtaining

h(z)=\sinh^{-1}(\tan(\sqrt{2}z)).

For the function
f
(and similarly for
g
), we have

\int\frac{df}{\sqrt{1+2(\sinh f)2}}=x+\lambda.

Let
\lambda=0
. In this case,
-i\,F(if,2)=x
and
-i\,F(ig,2)=y
, and thus:

\left\{\begin{split}f(x)&=i\,M(ix,2),\\
g(y)&=i\,M(iy,2).\end{split}\right.

Let
\Sigma_{2}
be the corresponding surface,

\Sigma_{2}:f(x)+g(y)+\sinh^{-1}(\tan(\sqrt{2}z))=0.

(24)

The function
f=f(x)
is an odd function defined on the interval
(-x_{1},x_{1})
, where
x_{1}\approx 1.31
with the property that
\lim_{x\to\pm x_{1}}f(x)=\mp\infty
. Notice that
x_{1}
is the same value
x_{0}
as the function
f(x)=M(x,2)
for
\Sigma_{1}
. Let
y_{1}=x_{1}
and
z_{1}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}
such that
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})\in\Sigma_{2}

The surface
\Sigma_{2}
meets the cuboid with vertices
(\pm x_{1},\pm y_{1},\pm z_{1})
along six segments (Fig.
5
, left). Only four of them are horizontal, namely,

\begin{split}L_{1}&=\{(-x_{1},y,z_{1}:-y_{1}<y<y_{1}\},\\
L_{2}&=\{(x,-y_{1},z_{1}:-x_{1}<x<x_{1}\},\\
L_{3}&=\{(x_{1},y,-z_{1}:-y_{1}<y<y_{1}\},\\
L_{4}&=\{(x,y_{1},-z_{1}:-x_{1}<x<x_{1}\}.\\
\end{split}

Lines
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are depicted in Fig.
5
, left, as black lines. Thus, using the Schwarz reflection principle, the surface
\Sigma_{2}
can be extended by a rotation of
180^{0}
across
L_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 4
(Fig.
5
, right). Since
\Sigma
can be reflected along two directions in space, then
\Sigma_{2}
is a doubly periodic surface. Notice that although
\Sigma
contains two vertical lines, the reflection across that lines does not imply that the surface can be extended along that direction. Consequently, the surface is a graph on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
except for a discrete set of points.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
\Sigma_{2}
is the surface defined in (
24
), then
\Sigma_{2}
is doubly periodic. The surface is a global graph on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
except for a discrete set points.

5.3.
Third example

We consider Eqs. (
10
) and take
k=2
,
r=a=d_{1}=d_{2}=c_{1}=1
. By a direct integration, we obtain

\left\{\begin{split}f(x)&=2\tanh^{-1}\left(\text{sech}(\frac{1}{2})\tan(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}})+\tanh(\frac{1}{2})\right),\\
g(y)&=\log\left(\frac{e\coth(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}})-\sqrt{e}}{\sqrt{e}-\coth(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}})}\right),\\
h(z)&=\log\left(\tan(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}})\right)-1.\end{split}\right.

We determine the domains of the functions. Since the argument of
\tanh^{-1}
must be between
-1
and
1
, we deduce that the domain of
f
is
(x_{1},x_{2})
with
x_{1}\approx-1.45
and
x_{2}\approx 0.77
. For the function
g
, the argument of the logarithm must be positive, so
y\in(y_{1},\infty)
with
y_{1}\approx 0.995
. Finally,
z\in(0,z_{2})
, with
z_{2}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}
. Moreover, from the expression for
h(z)
and substituting into (
4
), we obtain

\Sigma_{3}:z=z(x,y)=\sqrt{2}\tan^{-1}\left(e^{-f(x)-g(y)+1}\right).

(25)

Since
\lim_{x\to x_{1}}f(x)=-\infty
,
\lim_{x\to x_{2}}f(x)=\infty
and
\lim_{y\to y_{1}}g(y)=\infty
, we conclude that the horizontal lines

\begin{split}L_{1}&=\{(x_{1},y,z_{2}):0<y<\infty\},\\
L_{2}&=\{(x_{2},y,0):0<y<\infty\},\\
L_{3}&=\{(x,y_{1},0):x_{1}<x<x_{2}\},\end{split}

are limit points of
\Sigma_{3}
(Fig.
6
, left). Thus, the reflections of
180^{0}
across
L_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq 3
) extend analytically
\Sigma_{3}
(Fig.
6
, right).

Proposition 5.3
.

If
\Sigma_{3}
is the surface defined in (
25
), then
\Sigma_{2}
is doubly periodic. The surface is a global graph on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
except for a discrete set points.
