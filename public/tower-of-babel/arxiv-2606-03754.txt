Fast TetraBFT: Optimizing Latency Where It Matters
Antonio J. Fernández-Pinto, Manuel Bravo, Gregory Chockler, Alexey Gotsman

Abstract
Unauthenticated Byzantine consensus protocols achieve optimal failure resilience while relying only on authenticated point-to-point channels, not authenticated messages. They are an attractive building block for blockchains that do not mandate symmetric trust assumptions as well as for future post-quantum settings. We consider unauthenticated Byzantine consensus in partially synchronous networks and focus on optimizing its good-case latency – the worst-case time for correct processes to reach a decision under favorable conditions. A recently proposed ForgetIT protocol achieves an optimal good-case latency of 3 3 message delays but employs a highly complex design. We show that this complexity is unnecessary. To this end, we present Fast TetraBFT – an unauthenticated Byzantine consensus protocol that achieves optimal good-case latency by augmenting an existing TetraBFT protocol with a simple fast-path wrapper. Our solution lowers the good-case latency of TetraBFT from 5 5 to 3 3 message delays while preserving its bounded space requirements and low communication complexity.

Introduction

We consider Byzantine consensus in the
partial synchrony
model
[
14
]
,
which approximates the behavior of real networks. In this setting, the system
is guaranteed to eventually reach a
Global Stabilization Time
(
\mathsf{GST}
)
after which message delays are bounded by an unknown constant
\delta
and
process clocks track real time.
Motivated by the blockchain use case, in recent years researchers have developed
a plethora of Byzantine consensus protocols in this model. Most of these are

authenticated
, i.e., they send messages authenticated using cryptographic
signatures. These allow a process
p_{1}
to prove to a process
p_{2}
that a third
process
p_{3}
has sent a given message.
In contrast,
unauthenticated
(aka
information-theoretic
) protocols
rely only on authenticated point-to-point channels and thus disallow the above
pattern.
The minimal assumptions of such protocols make them applicable in a broader
range of settings. These inlcude post-quantum
[
2
]
or computationally constrained
environments as well as blockchains that do not rely on global trust
assumptions, such as Stellar
[
16
,
17
]
and
Ripple
[
7
]
.

Since realistic networks behave synchronously most of the time, the efficiency of
partially synchronous blockchain implementations is largely determined by their performance under stable
network conditions.
In more detail, blockchains often run a sequence of Byzantine consensus
instances, each deciding on the next block in the
chain
[
10
,
16
,
6
]
. A consensus instance proceeds
through a sequence of
views

0,1,2,\ldots
, each with a designated
leader process responsible for driving the protocol towards a decision.
Since in practice the leaders are selected to maximize the chances of correct
behavior (e.g., by penalizing misbehavior via stake slashing), under stable
network conditions decisions are usually reached in view
0
.
For example, data from the Cosmos Hub blockchain
[
1
]
running the
Tendermint protocol
[
10
]
shows that 99.995% of the instances
were decided in view
0
over a seven-day sample period. Thus, a
performance-critical metric for Byzantine consensus protocols is their
good-case latency
–
the worst-case latency to decision if the consensus
instance is started during the synchronous period (after
\mathsf{GST}
) and the
initial leader is correct.

In this paper we consider the problem of optimizing the good-case latency of
unauthenticated Byzantine consensus. Assuming the optimal resilience –

n\geq 3f+1
processes to tolerate
f
Byzantine failures – the lower bound on
the good-case latency is
3\delta

[
4
]
.
The first protocol to match this bound, a signature-free version of
PBFT
[
12
]
, was able to achieve this only at the expense of a
high message complexity –
O(n^{3})
per view. Information-theoretic
HotStuff
[
5
]
and TetraBFT
[
20
]
then lowered the
communication complexity to
O(n^{2})
per view, but only at the expense of a
higher good-case latency –
6\delta
and
5\delta
, respectively. A very recent
proposal, Forget-IT
[
3
]
, achieved both the optimal good-case latency
of
3\delta
and the communication complexity of
O(n^{2})
per
view. Unfortunately, the protocol has a complicated design, in part because it
also minimizes another latency metric, which its authors call
robust
good-case latency
. This is the worst-case latency of a decision measured from
the first time after
\mathsf{GST}
when a correct leader enters a view (whether
0
or
higher).

The rationale for the above metric was to capture latency guarantees under
adversarial conditions, when the protocol is started before
\mathsf{GST}
.
We argue that the metric does not fulfil this function.
In practice, what matters under adversarial conditions is how quickly the protocol recovers after the onset of the synchronous period, i.e., the time to decision measured from
\mathsf{GST}
.
To take a decision in this case, processes need to first exit the views they were in before
\mathsf{GST}
and enter a “good” view with a correct leader.
Processes exit a view when their timers expire, and the duration of these timers is typically orders of magnitude larger than
\delta
.
Thus, optimizing the latency to the decision after entering a good view is irrelevant, since the time to decision after
\mathsf{GST}
will be dominated by timer durations.

Given the above considerations, we set out to find a simpler protocol that achieves the optimal good-case latency and low communication complexity.
Our answer is
Fast TetraBFT
– a protocol with the good-case latency of
3\delta
and the communication complexity of
O(n^{2})
per view.
It furthermore guarantees optimal resilience and optimistic responsiveness
[
19
]
, and can be implemented in bounded space.
It is a wrapper around TetraBFT
[
20
]
that introduces a special code path for view
0
and executes a slightly modified TetraBFT in higher views.
Thus, the protocol improves the good-case latency of TetraBFT while preserving its performance under adversarial conditions.
Our wrapper makes only minimal assumptions about the structure of TetraBFT.
For this reason, we hope that its underlying ideas will also be applicable to lower the good-case latency of future proposals of unauthenticated BFT consensus protocols.

2
System Model

We consider a distributed message-passing system of
n
processes, of which at most
f
can experience Byzantine failures.
As is standard, we assume
n\geq 3f+1
and call a set of
n-f
processes a

quorum
. The system is partially
synchronous
[
14
,
13
]
: for every execution, there exist a

Global Stabilization Time
(
\mathsf{GST}
) and a duration
\delta
such that,
after
\mathsf{GST}
, message delays between every pair of correct processes are bounded
by
\delta
; before
\mathsf{GST}
, messages can be arbitrarily delayed or lost. We also
assume a known upper bound
\Delta
on the maximum value of
\delta
across all
executions, i.e.,
\delta<\Delta

[
15
,
18
]
. Processes are equipped with
hardware clocks that can drift arbitrarily from real time before
\mathsf{GST}
, but do
not drift thereafter. Communication channels are

authenticated

[
8
]
: if a correct process
p_{j}
receives a
message
m
claiming to have been sent by a process
p_{i}
, and
p_{i}
is correct,
then
m
was previously sent by
p_{i}
to
p_{j}
.

We assume an application-specific
\mathsf{valid}()
predicate that indicates whether a
value proposed to consensus is valid
[
11
]
. In Byzantine
consensus, each correct process proposes a valid value and then has to decide on
a value so that: (
Validity
) a correct process decides on a valid value;
(
Agreement
) no two correct processes decide on different values; and
(
Liveness
) every correct process eventually decides on a value.

Processes go through four rounds of voting on the proposal, sending messages
\mathtt{{VOTE1}}
to
\mathtt{{VOTE4}}
(line

–

When a process receives a quorum of matching
\mathtt{{VOTE4}}

A process stores the last votes it issued in variables
\mathsf{V1}

\mathsf{V4}

Fast TetraBFT

Fast TetraBFT runs a special protocol in view
0
(
Figure 3
), which is driven by an
initial leader
.
When this leader is correct and the network is synchronous, the protocol decides within
3\delta
.
Hence, at the beginning of the protocol each process sets a timer for a conservative bound of
3\Delta
(line
3
).
When the timer expires (line
3
), the process switches to TetraBFT, disabling the actions in
Figure 3
and enabling those in
Figure 1
via a flag
\mathsf{started\texttt{\textunderscore}tetra}
.

The initial leader starts Fast TetraBFT by broadcasting its consensus proposal in a
\mathtt{{FAST\texttt{\textunderscore}PROPOSE}}
message (line
3
).
Each process accepts only one such proposal (cf. the
\mathsf{voted}
flag), which it broadcasts in a
\mathtt{{VOTE0}}
message (line
3
).
A process receiving a quorum of
\mathtt{{VOTE0}}
s for the same value broadcasts it in a
\mathtt{{COMMIT}}
message (line
3
), and a process receiving a quorum of matching
\mathtt{{COMMIT}}
s decides (line
3
).
This ensures the good-case latency of
3\delta
.

A process sending a
\mathtt{{COMMIT}}
message for a value
x
agrees to
x
being decided, and thus becomes
locked
on this value.
This is recorded by storing
x
in
\mathsf{val}
and
\mathsf{lock}
(line
3
).
The former variable determines the proposal fed into TetraBFT (line
3
), and is initialized to the process’s own proposal (line
3
).
The latter constrains the proposals that can be made by leaders and accepted by followers in TetraBFT: when
\mathsf{lock}
is set to
x
, any proposals different from
x
are rejected (lines
2
and
2
).
Since view
0
may fail to reach a decision, a process also needs to be able to
unlock
the value
x
it recorded, to allow TetraBFT to decide.
Our main insight is that the process can do this upon receiving
f+1

\mathtt{{VOTE2}}
messages for values different from
x
, either directly or within
\mathtt{{SUGGEST}}
messages (line
1
).

Correctness

Validity
holds trivially, so we now prove
Agreement
. First, we
show correct processes cannot lock different values or decide
different values in view
0
.

Lemma 1
.

If two correct processes lock values
x
and
x^{\prime}
, respectively, then
x=x^{\prime}
.

Proof.

A correct process locks a
value only after receiving a quorum of
n-f

\mathtt{{VOTE0}}
messages for it
(line
3
). Since any two quorums intersect in a correct
process, locking different values
x
and
x^{\prime}
requires some correct process to
send both
\mathtt{{VOTE0}}(x)
and
\mathtt{{VOTE0}}(x^{\prime})
. This cannot happen since a
process only sends a
\mathtt{{VOTE0}}
once (the
\mathsf{voted}
flag at
line
3
).
∎

Lemma 2
.

Any two correct processes that decide in view
0
must decide the same value.

Proof.

A correct process decides a value
x
in view
0
after receiving

\mathtt{{COMMIT}}(x)
from a quorum of
n-f
processes
(line
3
). Out of these processes, at least one must
be correct, and this process locks
x
before sending its message.
The required then follows from Lemma
1
.
∎

The safety of TetraBFT rules out contradictory decisions in views
>0
(we
formalize this in Appendix
A
). It thus remains to prove
agreement between the values decided in view
0
(line
3
)
and those decided in the subsequent views (line
1
).
To this end, we show the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 3
.

If
x
is locked by
f+1
correct processes, then no correct process can
unlock
x
.

Proof.

Assume toward a contradiction that some correct process unlocks
x
. Let
t

be the earliest time when this happens, and let
p_{i}
be the unlocking
process. Then, by the time
t
