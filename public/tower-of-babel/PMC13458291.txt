Assessment of renal allograft cortical perfusion in the early postoperative period using arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Tao Ren, Lejun Fu, Lihua Chen, Nina Hao, Song Liu, Yu Sun, Wen Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe early postoperative period is critical for patients who have undergone renal transplantation. This study aimed to quantify cortical perfusion in renal allografts during this period using arterial spin labeling (ASL) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).MethodsBetween May and December 2014, ASL MRI was performed on 53 patients with renal allografts who were 2 to 4 weeks post-transplantation, as well as on 20 healthy volunteers. Recipients were stratified into two groups according to estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR): those with good allograft function (eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2), and those with impaired allograft function (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2). Renal blood flow (RBF) values were compared across healthy controls and the two recipient groups. The correlation between RBF and eGFR was also evaluated.ResultsMean cortical RBF was 389.9±61.2 mL/100 g/min in healthy controls, 295.0±67.9 mL/100 g/min in recipients with good allograft function, and 195.5±88.9 mL/100 g/min in recipients with impaired allograft function. Statistically significant differences in cortical RBF were observed among the three groups (P<0.001). A moderate positive correlation between eGFR and cortical RBF was identified among renal allograft recipients (R=0.62, P<0.01).ConclusionsRenal cortical perfusion in allografts during the early postoperative period was significantly reduced compared to healthy controls, particularly in cases of impaired allograft function. ASL MRI demonstrates potential as a non-contrast imaging modality for quantifying renal cortical perfusion in the post-transplant setting.Keywords: Arterial spin labeling (ASL), cortical renal blood flow (cortical RBF), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), renal allograft

Introduction

Allogeneic renal transplantation remains the most effective and preferred therapeutic option for patients with end-stage renal disease. Timely detection of renal allograft dysfunction and related postoperative complications is essential, particularly during the early postoperative period, as delayed intervention may accelerate graft deterioration and increase the risk of allograft failure (1).

In clinical practice, renal allograft function is typically evaluated using serum creatinine (SCr) levels, ultrasonographic imaging, and renal biopsy. However, both SCr and ultrasonography are limited by low sensitivity and specificity, whereas renal biopsy is invasive, associated with procedural discomfort, and susceptible to sampling inaccuracies (2).

The measurement of renal perfusion may provide a complementary assessment of renal function (3). Although para-amino hippuric acid plasma clearance is regarded as the reference standard for measuring renal perfusion, its clinical utility is limited due to its invasive nature, extended procedural time, and unsuitability for research settings (4). Alternative imaging techniques, including dynamic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and renal scintigraphy, have also been employed to estimate renal perfusion. However, these modalities require administration of nephrotoxic contrast agents or expose patients to the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, which is of particular concern in patients with renal insufficiency and renal transplant recipients (5).

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) is a non-invasive technique that quantifies tissue perfusion by using blood as an endogenous contrast agent, thereby eliminating the need for exogenous tracers. The method involves the selective magnetic labeling of inflowing arterial blood, generating magnetization that contrasts with adjacent non-labeled reference tissue. Perfusion is quantified by subtracting labeled and unlabeled image sets, which reflects blood exchange within the imaging volume and enables calculation of tissue perfusion values (6,7).

In recent years, ASL has been applied across multiple anatomical regions for the noninvasive assessment of tissue perfusion (8-11). In the field of renal transplantation, ASL has been utilized to assess renal blood flow (RBF) in recipients under various clinical conditions (12-19). However, only a limited number of investigations have concentrated on the early postoperative phase, a critical period characterized by increased susceptibility to complications (20). Early adverse events such as acute rejection (AR) may result in irreversible nephron loss and accelerate allograft failure if not promptly managed.

The present study aimed to quantify renal perfusion in patients with renal allografts during the early postoperative period using a flow-sensitive alternating inversion recovery (FAIR) prepared true fast imaging with steady-state precession (TrueFISP) ASL sequence. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2662/rc).

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. Ethical approval was waived by Tianjin First Central Hospital due to the retrospective nature of the study. Between May and December 2014, a total of 53 patients with renal allograft (36 males and 17 females; mean age: 35.3±10.7 years; age range, 21–57 years) from Tianjin First Central Hospital, and 20 healthy volunteers (13 males and 7 females; mean age: 36.0±13.4 years; age range, 24–58 years) were enrolled. All allograft recipients received standard triple immunosuppressive therapy following transplantation. Ultrasonography was regularly performed prior to the magnetic resonance (MR) examination to screen for renal artery stenosis (21). The MR scans were conducted 2 to 4 weeks post-transplantation.

Volunteers had no documented history of renal disease, cardiovascular disease, or hypertension, and maintained stable SCr levels within the reference range for at least six months prior to imaging. On the day of the MR scan, SCr concentrations were measured in all recipients, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) equation (22). Recipients were categorized into two subgroups according to eGFR levels: 34 recipients (age range, 21–57 years) with good allograft function (eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and 19 recipients (age range, 25–52 years) with impaired allograft function (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m2) (23).

MRI

All MR examinations were conducted using a clinical 3.0T MR scanner (Magnetom Trio; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Participants were positioned supine during imaging, utilizing a six-channel body matrix array coil anteriorly and an integrated spine coil posteriorly. For morphological evaluation of the kidneys, each participant underwent a transverse T1-weighted volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE) sequence with the following parameters: field of view (FOV): 380×380 mm2; echo time (TE)/repetition time (TR)/flip angle: 1.26/4.36 ms /9.0°, slice thickness: 2.5 mm, and 72 sections. A coronal T2-weighted half-Fourier acquisition single-shot turbo spin-echo (HASTE) sequence was also acquired (FOV: 380×285 mm2, TE/TR: 78 ms/2,000 ms, slice thickness: 2.5 mm, and 60 sections).

Quantitative assessment of renal perfusion was conducted using a FAIR-TrueFISP sequence (24). An oblique-sagittal imaging plane was selected to avoid inclusion of major renal feeding vessels, with a single slice obtained at the midpoint of the renal longitudinal axis. Sequence parameters included: TE/TR: 2.24 ms/4.48 ms, flip angle: 70°, voxel size: 2.3×2.3×5.0 mm3, slice thickness: 5.0 mm, inversion time (TI): 1,200 ms, and 12 repetitions. An M0 image without inversion was acquired to determine the equilibrium magnetization.

T1 mapping of the same imaging slice was conducted using a modified Look-Locker inversion recovery (MOLLI) sequence, employing the same spatial resolution as that used in perfusion imaging. Among healthy volunteers, respiratory-triggered acquisitions minimized respiratory motion artifacts. The acquisition time per kidney ranged from 2.5 to 3 minutes, depending on individual respiratory cycles. In recipients of renal allografts, respiratory triggering was not required due to the anatomical placement of the grafts in the iliac fossa, where respiratory motion had minimal influence. The average acquisition time for this group was approximately 2 minutes.

Image analysis

Two radiologists, possessing 15 and 11 years of experience in abdominal MRI respectively, independently interpreted the imaging data while blinded to participants’ clinical information. Image data were processed using a custom-developed algorithm implemented in Matlab (Mathworks, R2010b, Natick, MA, USA) protocol. Renal perfusion was quantified on a pixel-by-pixel basis using a one-compartment kinetic model (25):

The calculation relied on the difference in longitudinal magnetization (∆M) between labeled and control FAIR acquisitions. The perfusion rate (mL/100g/min) was denoted by f, and λ with the blood-tissue water partition coefficient was set at a constant 80 mL/100g. The inversion efficiency α was set at 1.0, M0 represented the tissue equilibrium magnetization per unit mass, and T1 was the longitudinal relaxation time measured using the previously described sequence.

In both the T1 mapping and perfusion maps, between 6 and 10 circular regions of interest (ROIs) of uniform size were manually placed in the upper, middle, and lower segments of the renal cortex. Quantification was limited to the cortical region, with no medullary perfusion and T1 value assessment included in this analysis (19).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). T1 relaxation times and RBF values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) and confidence interval (CI) were calculated to evaluate the reproducibility of the quantitative measurements.

Paired t-tests were applied to assess differences in T1 and RBF values between the right and left kidneys in the healthy control group. The mean values derived from both kidneys were subsequently averaged and used for further group comparisons. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni post hoc testing, was performed to assess differences in T1 and RBF across the comparison groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate the association between RBF and eGFR. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Among healthy controls, no statistically significant differences were observed in T1 relaxation times and RBF values between the right and left kidneys. Corresponding mean cortical RBF values were 388.2±67.8 and 391.6±64.5 mL/100 g/min (t=0.309, P=0.761).

Discussion

The present findings demonstrated the utility of ASL MRI in quantifying renal cortical perfusion in kidney transplant recipients during the early postoperative period. The technique enabled noninvasive assessment of perfusion characteristics across varying levels of graft function.

Recent advancements in MR imaging techniques have enabled non-invasive quantification of renal perfusion using ASL. Prior investigations reported wide variability in the timing of ASL examinations following kidney transplantation, ranging from several days to over two decades, which may influence the comparability of perfusion data across studies (12-19,26). In the present study, ASL examinations were conducted between 2 and 4 weeks post-transplantation, and renal allograft recipients were stratified according to eGFR. In the present study, FAIR-TrueFISP was used for renal perfusion measurement mainly due to its short acquisition time and low sensitivity to respiratory motion, which is more suitable for early post-transplant patients with limited compliance. Although pCASL has become the mainstream ASL technique with high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and efficient labeling, FAIR-TrueFISP still shows good feasibility and reproducibility for cortical perfusion quantification. The main clinical incremental value of this study lies in the focus on the critical early window of 2–4 weeks after transplantation, clarifying the quantitative relationship between cortical perfusion and renal function, thus providing complementary evidence for noninvasive evaluation of early graft function.

Within this cohort, the mean cortical RBF in allografts with good function was lower than that observed in healthy controls. This reduction may be due to the altered regulation of RBF following denervation of the renal allografts and the vasoconstrictive effects of calcineurin inhibitors commonly administered in immunosuppressive regimens (27,28). Allografts with impaired function demonstrated a further significant decline in cortical RBF, the underlying reason may involve capillary damage in the transplanted kidney, decreased glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and altered blood perfusion distribution, leading to reduced cortical RBF during renal function decline. Moreover, a moderate correlation was observed between cortical RBF and eGFR. These findings are consistent with prior reports indicating that renal dysfunction is associated with decreased renal cortical perfusion (12,14,16). Experimental models have shown that ASL is capable of detecting modifications in RBF proportional to the severity of induced ischemia, with these alterations correlating significantly with histopathological damage and renal functional impairment (29). Furthermore, Wang et al. demonstrated a strong positive correlation between cortical RBF measured by ASL and peritubular capillary density (r=0.77; P<0.001), supporting the credibility of ASL as a non-invasive modality for evaluating renal perfusion (30).

Renal cortical perfusion assessed using ASL in previous studies involving healthy individuals yielded RBF values ranging from 208 to 327 mL/100 g/min (19,25,31). In the present investigation, the cortical RBF values obtained in healthy controls were slightly higher. This discrepancy may be due to technical differences in sequence implementation, the application of individualized T1 relaxation time values, and the adoption of a more inclusive perfusion threshold of 600 mL/100 g/min for defining cortical perfusion.

T1 relaxation time, a parameter capable of detecting subtle tissue changes, is known to vary with renal pathological conditions and technical factors (12,32). A recent study reported differences in cortical T1 values among transplanted kidneys with varying levels of function (12). Similarly, in the present cohort, cortical T1 values were higher in allografts with good function compared to healthy controls, and further increased in allografts with impaired function. These findings align with earlier observations indicating that prolonged cortical T1 values are associated with moderate to severe reductions in eGFR. This prolongation may reflect water accumulation in the renal interstitium, secondary to pathophysiological alterations such as interstitial fibrosis, edema, and inflammation (33). Additionally, elevated renal tissue oxygenation has been reported to contribute to reduced T1 values (34). Interstitial injury, often characterized by a decline in peritubular capillary density, may result in renal hypoxia. This hypoxic environment could contribute to the prolonged cortical T1 values observed in renal allografts with impaired function during the early postoperative period.

AR and ATN represent the most clinically significant complications in the early postoperative period following renal transplantation, commonly manifesting as oliguria or anuria. Despite similar clinical presentations, these conditions require distinct therapeutic approaches due to differences in underlying pathophysiology. AR is primarily characterized by interstitial edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and in some cases, fibrinoid necrosis of renal arterioles. In contrast, ATN predominantly involves tubular epithelial cell degeneration, rupture of the basement membrane, renal interstitial edema, and compression of renal arterioles.

RBF values were compared between allografts affected by AR and those with ATN. The results demonstrated a trend toward lower RBF in allografts with AR compared to those with ATN. This observation differs from findings reported by Jiang et al. in which cortical RBF was negatively correlated with peritubular capillaritis scores based on the Banff classification (12,35). However, due to the limited number of cases, statistical analysis was not performed for these comparisons. These findings in AR and ATN subgroups should be considered exploratory and preliminary. No definitive clinical conclusions could be drawn, and this analysis was only hypothesis-generating for future larger-scale studies.

The primary limitation of this study was the absence of histopathological data for all allografts with impaired function, which hindered more precise classification of underlying pathology. Although AR and ATN were histologically confirmed in two and four cases, respectively, the use of eGFR as a grouping criterion introduced heterogeneity in the pathological spectrum among allografts with impaired function. Future investigations should incorporate a larger sample size and include pathological confirmation to enhance diagnostic accuracy. The second limitations included the application of single-slice ASL imaging, which restricted assessment to a limited renal region and did not allow comprehensive evaluation of the entire kidney. The third limitations included the lack of systematic recording of calcineurin inhibitor trough levels, mean arterial pressure, and other hemodynamic parameters (e.g., hydration status) at the time of MRI scanning, which may influence renal perfusion measurements. Furthermore, due to the retrospective design, key physiological parameters (e.g., blood pressure, heart rate) were not systematically recorded, precluding multivariate adjustment. Fourth, this study lacks continuous dynamic monitoring data of SCr before MRI examination, making it impossible to confirm that all subjects had stable renal function during scanning, which may affect the reliability of inter-group comparison.

Conclusions

During the early postoperative period, renal allografts exhibited significantly reduced cortical perfusion compared to healthy controls, with the lowest perfusion values observed in allografts with impaired function. ASL MRI demonstrated potential as a non-contrast-enhanced modality for quantitative assessment of renal cortical perfusion.
