Effectiveness of the BALatrine intervention on soil-transmitted helminth infections in Central Java, Indonesia: a cluster-randomised controlled trial
Authors: Darren J Gray, Budi Laksono, Mary Lorraine Mationg, Salvador Amaral, Johanna Kurscheid, Min Jeong Park, Juniawati Juniawati, Muharsono Muharsono, Sukoto Sukoto, Aldy Aldy, Galuh Wulandari, Sukardi Sukardi, Suharyo Hadisaputro, Léa Indjein, Fasil Wagnew, Matthew Kelly, Callum Lowe, Lee Jones, Gunter Hartel, Susana Vaz Nery, Ricardo Soares-Magalhaes, Kate Halton, James S McCarthy, Archie C A Clements, Donald E Stewart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil-transmitted helminths (STH) remain a global health challenge, particularly in areas lacking improved sanitation. We evaluated the effectiveness of an integrated BALatrine (an improved pit-latrine) intervention to prevent STH in Central Java, Indonesia. We conducted a cluster-randomized controlled trial involving 2155 participants in 8 village clusters between October 2016 and October 2017. Intervention villages received mass drug administration (MDA), the BALatrine, and community hygiene education, while the control group received only MDA. Follow-up stool samples were collected 12 months after baseline. We compared outcomes between study arms using a generalized linear mixed-effect model, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. At baseline, the prevalence of any STH infection was 13.0% (95% CI: 10.7–15.7) in the control group versus 12.4% (95% CI: 9.9–15.3) in the intervention group. At the follow-up, the BALatrine intervention had a significant overall effect on any STH (adjusted OR: 0.22, 95% CI: 0.15–0.32). Stratifying by species also showed a significant effectiveness of 100% for Ascaris lumbricoides and 95% for hookworm, but the reduction in Trichuris trichiura was not statistically significant. This study highlights the potential interventions that focus on sanitation and hygiene, combined with health education and treatment to control and eliminate STH. Introduction

Soil-transmitted helminths (STH) include roundworms (Ascaris lumbricoides), the most prevalent pathogen among the neglected tropical diseases, with an estimated 1 billion infections globally1, whipworms (Trichuris trichiura) and hookworms (Necator americanus, Ancylostoma duodenale and Ancylostoma ceylenicum), each respectively infecting approximately 600 to 800 million individuals. These infections are intimately connected to rural poverty, inadequate sanitation, poor waste management, lack of clean water and poor hygiene, as well as limited access to healthcare and preventive measures through health education2,3. STH infections impact significantly on public health with moderate and heavy intensity infections causing severe morbidity in the world’s poorest communities4.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO)5, children and pregnant women are most at risk of STH infections, among whom they have a particularly negative impact on the health and economic well-being of children6. Hookworm infections are associated with iron-deficiency anemia, particularly among women of reproductive age. Chronic moderate and heavy-intensity STH infection can lead to iron deficiency anemia, anemia of inflammation, malnutrition, intestinal obstruction, chronic pain, exercise intolerance, stunting, wasting, and impaired physical development 7,8.

STH infection is also associated with impaired cognitive function, memory, growth, development, and physical fitness, leading to reduced school enrollment, performance and attendance8–10, together causing substantial health and socioeconomic consequences6,11. Estimates put the global disease burden for STH infections as high as 39 million disability-adjusted life years lost7,12. The World Bank estimates that 16.7 million disability-adjusted life years are lost among children aged 5–14 years due to STH infections 13.

Indonesia, with ideal environmental and socioeconomic conditions for infection, faces a significant burden of STH infections, with an estimated 200 million individuals at risk across 31 provinces14–16. One-fifth or more of the population is estimated to be infected with A. lumbricoides or hookworm, and approximately 12% are infected with T. trichuria17. Prevalence of STH infection up to 80% has been reported in parts of the country where sanitation is poor 18.

The mainstay of STH control is preventive chemotherapy, usually targeting school-age children through school deworming using the benzimidazole anthelminthic drugs albendazole or mebendazole. Although these are effective in achieving morbidity control, they do not prevent re-infection. A number of studies have shown that once treatment is stopped, prevalence returns to pre-treatment levels within 6-18 months19. Therefore, deworming alone is not a sustainable long-term solution because it requires persistent treatment. We hypothesized that the BALatrine intervention (installation of an improved pit-latrine (BALatrine) and systematic hygiene education) incorporated with albendazole mass drug administration would reduce STH transmission, thus making it an effective intervention for STH reinfection in resource-poor environments. Our pilot study showed proof of principle that the BALatrine intervention was effective in preventing STH infections20. We present findings from a cluster randomized trial evaluating the impact of BALatrine interventions on STH infections in Central Java, Indonesia.

Results

A total of 3574 participants living in 1112 households consented and participated in the HELP questionnaire at baseline, of which 3228 individuals provided stool samples (1875 in the control group and 1353 in the intervention group). At follow-up, about 30% of the study participants at baseline did not provide a stool sample, leaving a total of 2155 individuals with complete stool assessments at both survey points. Characteristics of participants with complete versus incomplete stool submissions are presented in Supplementary table 1. Baseline knowledge and behavior on STH

At baseline, the overall knowledge level of study participants across the study indicated that the majority (60.4%) had poor knowledge of STH, 37.7% had average knowledge, and only 1.9% had good knowledge. Knowledge scores were significantly higher in the control villages than in the intervention villages (mean score of 9.7; 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 8.9−9.4 vs 9.1; 95% CI: 9.5−9.9; P = <0.001).

Overall behavior level of study participants showed that 72% of the study participants were categorized as having good self-reported behavior towards STH prevention, 27.2% were categorized as average, and only 0.8% as having poor behavior. Reported behavior scores were higher in the control villages than in intervention villages (mean score of 44.9; 95% CI: 44.6−45.3 vs 43.8; 95% CI:43.4−44.2 7; P = <0.001).

Discussion

Our trial results showed that the BALatrine intervention had a 78% efficacy in preventing STH infection, which is one of the highest reported in the existing literature for sanitation interventions. For instance, the WASH Benefits trials conducted in Kenya and Bangladesh reported modest species-specific reductions in STH infections, with approximately 22% reductions in A. lumbricoides infection and 29–33% reductions in hookworm prevalence, respectively21,22. A systematic review and meta-analysis also reported a 33% reduction in STH infections with sanitation interventions3. More recently, the DeWorm3 trial demonstrated that although preventive chemotherapy substantially reduced the prevalence of STH, interruption of transmission was not consistently achieved, highlighting the need for complementary sanitation and hygiene interventions for sustainable control 23.

The significant reduction in STH infections observed with the BALatrine intervention in our study may be due to important features regarding the intervention components and implementation. First, the process building of the BALatrines by community members themselves, the BALatrine was designed to tackle existing challenges with a focus on cultural appropriateness, using simple, affordable, locally sourced materials to ensure cost-effectiveness, easy maintenance, and adaptability to both wet and dry conditions. Community involvement in the construction is likely to provide us with a sense of ownership, which can result in better adherence to sanitation practices. Second, the addition of comprehensive community health and hygiene education (including latrine construction and maintenance) likely promoted behavior change and increased disease prevention awareness. Adherence to MDA was also high at 89%, and all households in the intervention arm received the full BALatrine intervention package, which may have further contributed to the observed effectiveness. Previous trials have also shown that health education and promotion programs improve individuals’ knowledge of STH and promote positive behavioral change24,25. The substantial impact of the BALatrine intervention highlighted in this trial underscores the WHO 2030 Neglected Tropical Disease Roadmap’s call to integrate WASH interventions with deworming programs, aiming to disrupt transmission cycles and sustainably reduce STH prevalence1. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the diagnostic methods used in this study may have had lower sensitivity for detecting low-intensity infections than molecular methods such as qPCR used in some other trials, which could have influenced the estimated intervention effect.

Even though we did not observe a significant difference in T. trichiura prevalence between the two groups, consistent with findings from previous studies,26–28, this may partly reflect a pharmacological issue, namely, the poor efficacy of albendazole against T. trichiura19, which fails to clear baseline infections and makes assessing the impact of preventive interventions difficult for this species. However, it could be argued that Ascaris might serve as a proxy for T. trichiura due to their similar transmission dynamics. Recent evidence also shows the high efficacy of combining albendazole and ivermectin for treating T. trichiura29, with ivermectin currently considered the WHO essential drug for STH 5.

The use of preventive chemotherapy alone to control helminth infections has raised concerns about the potential development of anthelmintic resistance due to continuous treatment pressure, donor fatigue, and challenges with long-term sustainability18. Addressing these challenges requires an integrated approach that combines pharmacologic treatment, health education and promotion programs, and preventive measures such as improved sanitation and hygiene practices. Such strategies can reduce reinfection, minimize treatment cycles, and give a more sustainable long-term solution for controlling STH30. In addition, improving water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure will remain a key policy priority not only for controlling STH but also for reducing other WASH-related diseases such as schistosomiasis, amebiasis, and gastroenteritis, with broad benefits for public health, societal development, and overall well-being 31.

Our trial has some important limitations. Given the small number of clusters and the relatively high MDA uptake of 89%, the generalizability of the findings to other settings, particularly those with lower treatment coverage, may be limited. The time elapsed between trial completion and publication should also be considered when interpreting the findings, as post-trial research activities were affected by operational disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition, with only a 12-month follow-up, we were unable to evaluate the long-term impact of BALatrine interventions at the community level. Moreover, the effects of BALatrine interventions on participants’ STH knowledge and behavior were not evaluated due to the absence of follow-up assessments. STH infection intensity was not measured, reducing the comprehensiveness of the disease burden assessment and limiting the ability to determine BALatrine’s effectiveness across STH infection severities. Furthermore, we used the fecal flotation technique for the microscopic diagnosis of STH eggs in stool, which has lower sensitivity and specificity than molecular-based methods such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

In conclusion, our BALatrine intervention package can be effectively integrated into future deworming initiatives and existing programs, given its context-specific design and feasibility. Future strategies should also prioritize infection intensity over prevalence as a more relevant indicator for assessing morbidity. Furthermore, targeted research into effective treatment combinations for persistent infections, such as T. trichiura, is pivotal for optimizing intervention outcomes.

Methods

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committees at Griffith University (Ref: 2016/442) and the Australian National University (protocol 2016/442); and also the Semarang City Government Agency for national unity, politics and community protection (133/UN7.4/DIK/AK/2011). The trial is registered with the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12613000523707). After providing thorough information in the local language on the aim and procedures of the study, verbal and written informed consent were obtained from all study participants aged 18 years and above or parents or guardians for those below 18 years old. Written assent was also sought from participants aged 12–17 years.

Study setting, design, and participants

A cluster-randomized controlled trial was undertaken in 8 villages or ‘dusuns’ located in Wonosobo, Central Java Province, Indonesia. The trial was conducted over one year, from October 2016 to October 2017. The intervention and control group each contained four clusters (‘dusuns’) with low baseline levels of household latrine ownership. Stratification was based on topography (mountain: 4 dusuns; hillsides: 4 dusuns) to account for potential differences in environmental conditions, soil characteristics, and sanitation feasibility that may influence the transmission of STH.

Two dusuns within each topography type were randomly selected as the intervention group and the other two as the control group (receiving mass drug administration only). Following the baseline stool survey, all study participants received the albendazole treatment by the local STH control program. In each dusun, a target of 110 households without latrines was randomly selected to yield a fixed cohort of 500 people aged ≥ 2 years, and this cohort was followed up over 12 months.

Study intervention

The study intervention is an integrated model previously described20, comprising mass drug administration with albendazole, a locally constructed household latrine (“BALatrine”), and community hygiene education. The details of each component are discussed in the sections below.

BALatrine (Budi’s Amphibious Latrine)

The BALatrine is a simple squat latrine designed for resource-limited communities, typically constructed by the villagers themselves using inexpensive local materials, at the side of/behind a house. The latrine comprises a 2-meter pit (with optional bamboo/concrete pipe strengthening); a base cover with raised footsteps (which can be used during dry periods); and a flushable U-bend porcelain bowl that may be removable. The full details of the structure, specifications, construction, and materials of the BALatrine have been published elsewhere32. Following the BALatrine construction and ongoing maintenance workshops in each dusun, latrines were constructed for all householders who did not have improved latrines with material support and advice from the researchers. Latrines were constructed during the follow-up period, with construction initiated after the baseline survey and typically completed within one month. Most households constructed their own latrines, with village military officers (Babinsa) assisting families who were unable to build them independently. Implementation was supported by the research team and village authorities to ensure consistent delivery across clusters.

Mass drug administration with albendazole

All residents in both the control and intervention groups received a single dose of albendazole (400 mg/kg) treatment following the provision of stool sample collection. The treatment was administered in coordination with the routine national deworming program and delivered by local health officials. The overall treatment uptake among eligible participants was approximately 89%.

Health education

In addition to the physical intervention (the BALatrine), a health education program related to hygiene and sanitation was implemented. This program was delivered via community meetings in each village in the intervention arm after the baseline survey. A representative from each ‘dusun’ household was invited to the meetings, which were held in the village meeting hall. The dusun-wide meetings were implemented at the start of the project (September 2016) and after the latrine construction in all households (October 2016).

The first meeting was intended for the project launch and mobilization of households and included awareness-raising and provision of general information about parasite infection and the burden of STH. Participants took part in discussions regarding the BALatrine intervention, its construction, maintenance, and use to assist village residents to plan, construct, use and maintain their latrines. Leaflets supporting the meeting and summarizing the health education content discussed were distributed to each household. The second meeting, after latrine construction, was aimed to deliver hygiene and sanitation education to each intervention household.

Study procedures

At baseline, a face-to-face interview questionnaire was administered with the head of the household or another adult who could speak for the household, to collect data on household socio-economic information and access to water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure. All consenting study participants in each household also completed the Helminth Education and Latrine Project (HELP) questionnaire33. After the administration of the HELP questionnaire, participants were provided with one plastic container for fecal samples labeled with their unique identification and the date of distribution. Participants were instructed to deposit samples from their own feces into the container and to return the container immediately to the research team. The fecal flotation method was used to analyse stool samples for the presence of STH eggs. Moreover, the questionnaire and the collection of stool samples were repeated during the follow-up survey.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome measure for the effectiveness of the integrated intervention was infection status, defined as positive infection with any STH species (Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichiuris trichiura, and hookworm) measured at baseline and at follow-up.

Sample size determination

Sample size calculations were performed according to Donner and Klar; with an STH incidence of 8% (typical of communities with ~24% STH prevalence), an intervention efficacy of 30%, and a design effect (relating to paired differences) of 1.5; the study was estimated to achieve 80% power (alpha = 0.05) with a sample size of 4 intervention clusters (8 in total) and 400 participants per cluster at the end of the trial following a predicted annual 10% loss to follow-up. The total study population was 4000.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in duplicate using a Microsoft Access database. All data management and analyses were conducted using SAS Proprietary Software 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Baseline knowledge and behavior scores were calculated as the sum of item responses (maximum scores: 29 for knowledge and 60 for behavior) and categorized into poor, average, and good levels using predefined cut-offs (knowledge: ≤10 poor, 11–18 average, ≥19 good; behavior: ≤20 poor, 21–40 average, ≥40 good). A generalized linear mixed-effect model (GLMM) with a binary outcome and a logit link was used to estimate the intervention’s effect on STH infections. Follow-up infection status as the outcome was modeled separately for any species, A. lumbricoides, T. trichiura, and hookworm, with study group as the primary exposure. The models were additionally adjusted for baseline infection status, age (continuous) and sex. A random intercept for households nested within villages (dusun) was included to account for clustering. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In addition, adjusted predicted prevalences of infection by study group were estimated using least-squares means (estimated marginal means) resulting from back-transforming model estimates to the probability scale using the inverse logit function.
