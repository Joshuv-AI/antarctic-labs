 1. PROJECT INTRODUCTION

Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) and Argonne
National Laboratory (ANL) are jointly conducting the Joining Core Program 3.0: Materials Joining
Solutions toward High Volume Vehicle Manufacturing. The consortium aims to develop innovative
multi-material joining technologies enabled by AI/ML-driven process monitoring and control to address
critical challenges in the manufacturing and assembly of multi-material automotive structures. The
program focuses on developing and demonstrating joining technologies on targeted application
components at TRL 3–4, enabling U.S. automakers to efficiently integrate multiple material
combinations, including advanced high-strength steels, cast and wrought aluminum alloys, and carbon-
fiber composite materials, into cost-effective manufacturing pathways for fuel-efficient vehicles,
particularly next-generation hybrid and electrified platforms.

JCP 3.0 builds on the successful outcomes of the previous two JCP phases. The three national laboratories
will continue to leverage complementary strengths in materials joining science, galvanic corrosion
mitigation, AI and machine learning, computational modeling, and advanced characterization. Together,
these capabilities have enabled significant advances in joining technology development and in the
integration of multi-material automotive structures. This strong foundation positions the JCP 3.0 team to
transition from laboratory-scale innovations toward manufacturing-relevant demonstration and validation.

Thrust 3 is structured to support and accelerate joining technology development in Thrusts 1 and 2
through machine learning, physics-based simulation, and advanced characterization, with the goal of
innovating process development and improving manufacturing robustness.

 1. Machine learning to enable process monitoring and quality control (ORNL)

A central objective of Thrust 3 is to advance AI/ML-enabled inline weld process monitoring and adaptive
control to reliably produce high-quality joints in high-volume manufacturing environments. In
production, process variability and part-to-part uncertainties are unavoidable and often difficult to detect
prior to joining. Factors such as sheet thickness variation, non-flatness, fit-up changes, tool/electrode
wear, and material variation can alter joining conditions within seconds, leading to inconsistent weld
formation and defects [1]. Because traditional quality assurance [2] relies on destructive testing and post-
inspection, it lacks the agility to address these real-time disturbances. To overcome these limitations, we
utilize inline sensing as a real-time diagnostic window. Building on JCP 2.0 foundations, we will leverage
the expansible AI/ML framework to enable smart manufacturing through high-fidelity feature extraction,
real-time diagnostics, and adaptive process control. The objective is to establish a robust, production-
ready architecture that bridges the gap between laboratory innovation and high-volume deployment,
providing the technical scalability required for next-generation vehicle platforms.

 2. Physic-based computational modeling from process to performance (ORNL/PNNL)

Physics-based modeling is leveraged to optimize solid-state and fusion joining for multi-material vehicle
structures and battery enclosures. By simulating the complex thermal-mechanical-metallurgical
phenomena in joining, this effort establishes physics-informed foundations for understanding the complex
relationships governing joint formation in techniques such as friction stir welding, high-velocity joining,
friction self-piercing riveting, and laser welding, etc. A primary objective is addressing critical
challenges, including cracking in cast aluminum and liquid metal embrittlement in Gen III steels—
through predictive and mechanistic insights. By resolving local thermal histories, stress–strain evolution,
and microstructure-sensitive responses, modeling offers a predictive insight to understand defect
formation and supports the design of process windows that mitigate these risks. These simulations not

 1

only guide parameter selection but also enable virtual experimentation to reduce costly trial-and-error
during development.

 3. Advanced characterization (ANL/ORNL/PNNL)

Advanced characterizations, both in situ and ex situ, will be performed at the high-energy synchrotron
facility at APS at ANL, to support joining process development. The effort focuses on laser welding of
multilayer aluminum (Al) and copper (Cu) thin foils, addressing keyhole dynamics and porosity
formation in current collector joints. Challenges such as high electrical resistance, heat generation, and
oxide-layer-induced defects limit joint performance. Using in-situ X-ray imaging and in-operando laser
welding system that interactable to the beamline at ANL, the study will characterize defect formation and
optimize welding parameters for reliable multilayer and multi-material joints. This collaborative effort
with ORNL and General Motors (GM) supports U.S. Department of Energy priorities by advancing
materials characterization, improving battery packaging technologies, and strengthening domestic
manufacturing and energy security.

 2. OBJECTIVES

Thrust 3 integrates physics-informed machine learning, multi-physics modeling, and advanced
characterization to accelerate the development of robust and production-ready joining technologies. By
supporting Thrusts 1 and 2, this effort enables inline quality monitoring, adaptive control, and scalable
process design for TRL 3–4 multi-material joints in automotive structures and battery enclosures. The
specific objectives include
ORNL
 • Advance a unified, physics-informed AI framework for real-time monitoring and closed-loop
 adaptive control across multiple processes, ensuring robustness under realistic manufacturing
 variability.
 • Establish a standardized, multi-modal data foundation through coordinated experiments in Thrust
 1 and 2 to enable high-fidelity ML training and validation for joining process development,
 monitoring, and control.
 • Develop and mature multi-physics modeling capabilities to investigate the fundamental thermal,
 mechanical, metallurgical phenomenon governing joint formation, establishing a mechanistic
 basis for guiding process optimization and addressing material-specific joining challenges.
ANL
 • Leverage advanced in situ and ex situ characterization at APS/ANL to advance understanding and
 control of laser welding in multilayer in multilayer Al and Cu foils, including keyhole dynamics,
 porosity formation mechanisms, and process optimization to minimize defects and ensure reliable
 joint performance, providing guidance to improve battery packaging and manufacturing
 processes.
 3. APPROACH

3.1 PHYSICS-AWARE AI FOR SMART JOINING - ORNL

Thrust 3 leverages three synergistic, complementary approaches of physics-informed machine learning,
multi-physics computational modeling, and advanced characterization to advance the joining technologies
developed in Thrusts 1 and 2, as the flow chart presented in Figure 1. Together, these approaches form a
closed-loop framework that links real-time sensing, mechanistic insight, and predictive capability to
optimize weld quality, process robustness, and structural performance.

Figure 1 Integrated Thrust 3 approach supporting joining technology development in Thrusts 1 and 2.
Physics-informed machine learning, multi-physics computational modeling, and advanced
characterization form three complementary approaches that provide inline monitoring and control
strategies, process and performance guidance, and mechanistic validation to accelerate robust, production-
relevant joining solutions.

The ORNL team focuses on the development of a modular, physics-aware AI framework designed to
accelerate laboratory-scale innovation and bridge the gap toward high-volume manufacturing
deployment. By expanding the unified architecture established for resistance spot welding (RSW) in JCP
2.0 [3, 4], this methodology moves beyond traditional "black-box" AI model by embedding fundamental
joining principles into the model’s architecture and training strategies. This physics-aware approach aims
to provide enhanced interpretability and robustness; by grounding the AI in physical laws, the system can
reliably distinguish between non-physical noise and true process disturbances, a critical requirement for
maintaining accuracy even in the high variability of a production environment. To ensure production
relevance, the approach utilizes a multi-scale data hierarchy dataset that systematically transitions from
fundamental laboratory coupons to controlled variability tests and, ultimately, part-level trials. This
structured strategy allows the AI to learn signal signatures that generalize across diverse materials and
complex geometries. The methodology follows a strategic evolution from inline process monitoring to
autonomous process optimization. By establishing a deterministic foundation in high-accuracy inline
monitoring, the framework currently provides robust Accept/Reject predictions for real-time quality
monitoring. Under JCP 3.0, we are expanding and modularizing this architecture, refining our established
models for RSW while extending the framework to encompass ultrasonic welding (USW) and friction self
piercing riveting (FSPR). This staged progression establishes the necessary technical pathway for root-
cause reasoning and, ultimately, closed-loop adaptive control, ensuring consistent, high-quality joints in
next-generation vehicle assembly.

4.1 MACHINE LEARNING TO ENABLE PROCESS MONITORING AND CONTROL OF
 RSW (ORNL)

In JCP 3.0, we are expanding the physics-informed machine learning (ML) framework to address
production-relevant weld variability and enable robust, inline weld quality monitoring and control. This
effort directly builds on the foundation established in JCP 2.0, where we demonstrated a unified ML
architecture for resistance spot welding (RSW) that achieved >99% accuracy in nugget quality prediction
using a large, controlled GM lab dataset.
To support this transition toward real-world manufacturing conditions, we collaborated with Thrust 2 and
industry collaborators to develop a production-aware dataset. The intial set of dataset generated in FY25

 4

included two representative steel stackups - 1.5 mm HDG DP600 / 1.5 mm HDG DP600 and 1.4 mm
HDG DP590 / 1.5 mm HDG DP600, with baseline welding schedules selected to produce welds which
meets the AWS nugget size requirement of 4 𝑡, with 𝑡 being the minimum thickness of stacking sheets.
We then systematically introduced controlled, representative manufacturing disturbances such as sheet
gap, electrode angle misalignment, and edge weld conditions, to simulate part-to-part manufacturing
uncertainties in high-volume production.

Figure 3 Representative multi-modal inline signals, filtering, and frequency-domain characteristics for an
 example weld. (a) Mechanical signals of load and displacement and (b) electrical signals of current,
 voltage, dynamic resistance, and power shown as raw and filtered signals, illustrating the preprocessing
 applied prior to ML. (c–d) Frequency-domain analysis of the mechanical and electrical signals,

 During each weld, multi-channel high-speed inline signals were acquired, including current, voltage,
dynamic resistance, power, load, and displacement, as shown in Figure 3(a) and (b). This rich dataset
captures detailed electrical and mechanical dynamics across the weld cycle, including indentation, plastic
collapse, electrode motion, and resistance evolution. Frequency-domain analysis in Figure 3(c) and (d)
further reveals signal features such as MFDC switching harmonics (~2 kHz, 4 kHz) and mechanical
vibration modes, which are essential for capturing the physics of weld formation and disturbances.
We build on the JCP 2.0 physics-informed ML architecture shown in Figure 4, which was trained
primarily on the large GM laboratory dataset and is well suited for learning the baseline physics of nugget
formation. In JCP 3.0, this architecture is extended to handle real-world manufacturing uncertainties
through a data-driven yet physics-aware strategy. To assess the transition from lab-controlled conditions
to manufacturing relevance, we trained a unified model on a combined dataset that includes both the GM
lab data and the newly collected process-variability data. Despite the broadened conditions, the model
showed stable and consistent training and testing loss curves (Figure 5), with loss levels comparable to
the lab-only model. This indicates that the physics foundation learned from controlled experiments
transfers well, and that the network can assimilate new signal signatures without degrading overall
performance. Focusing on the variability cases, the unified model produced high-confidence
Accept/Reject predictions and achieved ~99.2% accuracy, consistent with the model’s performance on lab
data. This Year-1 result demonstrates that the unified ML model preserves the physics-driven patterns
learned in the lab while gaining robustness to real production disturbances.

4.2 EXPANDING THE ML FRAMEWORK BEYOND RSW (ORNL)

In FY25, we also extend this physics-informed machine learning framework to additional joining
processes with the goal of creating a unified, physics-informed ML backbone applicable across joining
technologies. This modular architecture links process-specific signals, such as energy input and material
response, directly to underlying physical phenomena like mechanical and metallurgical bonding. By
selecting sensor modalities that reflect these specific mechanisms, the resulting models achieve the
interpretability and robustness required to correlate weld quality with measurable process behavior rather
than black-box correlations.
Expansion to USW: A primary focus of this expansion is addressing the unique challenges of USW,
where joint formation is highly sensitive to the mechanical impedance and resonance of the part
geometry. Changes in part stiffness, fixturing, or sequence (e.g., multiple consecutive welds) can
significantly alter energy delivery and bonding behavior. To tackle this, we integrate machine learning
with process innovation to capture the strong coupling between local stiffness, process energy input, and
material response. In collaboration with Thrusts 1 and 2, we leverage high-fidelity inline signals,
including indentation depth, force, power, and energy, as shown in Figure 7(a), to characterize bond
development and joint performance.
The preliminary model targeting joint peak load has identified indentation depth, absorbed energy, power
peak magnitude, and the timing of the power peak as key drivers of weld strength. The results in Figure
7(c) and (d) show good predictive accuracy across mid-range conditions, targeted data expansion is
underway to mitigate edge-case bias and improve generalization across a broader process space. To
address the dependence of USW on geometry and constraint, we are conducting a structured, multi-scale
dataset that begins with single-joint coupons to isolate bonding physics, moves to multi-joint

 7

configurations to capture load-path interactions, and ultimately scales to production-relevant component
validations reflecting the true geometry and stiffness variability of industrial environments.

4.3 ADVANCED CHARACTERIZATION (ANL)

In FY 2025 the team acquired synchrotron beamtime and working closely with stakeholders, to conduct
in-situ and in-operando laser welding studies on Al - Al systems. During this period stakeholders
collaborated with stakeholders collaborated to extract key insights from the experiments and feed them
into the other Thrusts and to industrial partners, ensuring that the results directly addressed needs related
to manufacturing reliability and process robustness.

 8

Porosity and Melt pool Interaction: Figure 8 shows in-situ synchrotron X-ray imaging of laser-welded
Al foil stacks consisting of 50 ultrasonically pre-welded layers, processed at a P of 500 W and a v of 70
mm/s. The imaging reveals pore formation within the molten pool arising from keyhole instability and
entrapped gas. Once formed, pores are transported by melt flow and may coalesce during their motion
through the liquid metal. As pores approach the oscillating keyhole wall, they can merge with the
keyhole, releasing gas into the vapor-filled cavity, giving the appearance that one pore absorbs another.
From a manufacturing perspective, this pore and keyhole interaction directly influences joint integrity and
process robustness. Unstable keyhole behavior and pore coalescence increase the likelihood of residual
porosity after solidification, leading to elevated electrical resistance, localized heating, and reduced
mechanical durability of current collector joints. These defects can compromise battery performance and
long-term reliability, highlighting the need for controlled keyhole dynamics and optimized processing
windows to ensure consistent, high-quality welds in high-volume battery manufacturing.

Figure 8 A time series radiograph showing in-situ X-ray imaging of laser welded Al foil stacks consisting
 of 50 ultrasonically pre welded layers, processed at a laser power, P = 500 W and a scan speed, v = 70
 mm/s showing pores interaction in-operando. All scale bars are a 150 µm. (Source: ANL)

Keyhole and Pore Interaction: Figure 9 shows in-situ X-ray imaging of laser welding of 50 layers of pre-
ultrasonically welded Al foils, processed at a P of 600 W and a travel speed of 20 mm/s. In these
radiographs, the keyhole exhibits pronounced dynamic fluctuations in thickness. It initially forms as a
narrow cavity, subsequently thickens and becomes highly irregular, with pore formation occurring near
the keyhole walls, before thinning again. This behavior indicates an unstable keyhole, which is
characteristic of laser welding at high power densities. In laser welding, the keyhole is generated by
intense localized vaporization of the metal, producing a vapor cavity that enables deep penetration. Such
keyhole instability promotes repeated collapse and re-opening of the vapor cavity, leading to the
entrapment of metal vapor and gas and the subsequent formation of porosity within the fusion zone.
Consequently, unstable keyhole behavior directly compromises the electrical, mechanical, and thermal
reliability of laser-welded Al battery tab interconnects.

 of 50 ultrasonically pre welded layers, processed at a laser power, P = 500 W and a scan speed, v = 20
  mm/s showing keyhole and pore interaction in-operando. All scale bars are a 150 µm. (Source: ANL)

Burn Through: Figure 10 shows in-situ X-ray imaging of laser welding performed at 700 W and 30
mm/s on a pre ultrasonically welded 50-layer Al foil stack. During laser welding of a 50-layer Al
ultrasonically welded foil stack to a tab, radiography shows a large molten cavity that evolves into an
unstable keyhole. Small bubbles then nucleate, grow, and coalesce until they form larger cavities and
eventually cause burn-through. The sequence suggests that high heat input and keyhole instability,
combined with entrapped air between foils and possible gas release from oxide layers, drive bubble
formation and acceleration. These effects together explain the observed porosity and final burn-through. It
can be hypothesized that thin Al foils heat unevenly, trap gas at oxide layers, and form rapidly growing
bubbles that break through, causing burn through.

 (Source: ANL)

Material Configuration Sample Contact: Figure 11 compares laser welding performed at 500 W and 100
mm/s on 50-layer Al foil stacks that were ultrasonically welded prior to laser processing, ultrasonically
processed prior to welding, and mechanically constrained using tape. The in-situ X-ray radiographs show
that ultrasonic pre-welding increases foil rigidity, making it more difficult for the stack to achieve
uniform contact with the underlying tab compared to non-ultrasonically welded foils, which remain more
malleable. As a result, ultrasonically welded samples exhibit large interfacial gaps within the weld region,
whereas improved conformity and reduced gap formation are observed in the non-ultrasonically welded
configurations.

Next steps: The results obtained from Figure 8 to Figure 11 will be summarized into a process parameter
map that defines the occurrence of key defects (e.g., porosity, lack of fusion, and poor contact) as a
function of laser power and scan speed. This map unifies the findings and establishes a scalable
framework for industrial implementation. Ex situ X-ray computed tomography (CT) will be conducted to
verify and validate the findings obtained from radiographic analysis.

 5. CONCLUSION

In FY25, Thrust 3 advances physics-informed machine learning, computational modeling, and advanced
characterization to support the development of scalable, production-relevant joining technologies.
ORNL team established a physics-informed machine learning framework that moves weld quality
monitoring from lab-scale demonstrations toward production-relevant robustness and cross-process
expansion.
 • Extended the RSW ML foundation toward production robustness by training a unified model on
 combined laboratory and variability datasets. The resulting model maintained stable learning
 behavior and achieved ~99% accuracy in Accept/Reject quality predictions under real-world
 disturbances.

 11

 • Developed a multi-scale, production-aware data strategy, in close collaboration with Thrusts 1
 and 2 and guided by IAB feedback. This strategy spans lab-scale coupon welds, controlled
 variability tests, and ultimately towards part-level trials, creating a scalable, physics-rich dataset
 foundation for robust ML training and transferability across joining processes.
 • Initiated cross-process expansion of the ML framework to USW. Early training results show that
 indentation depth, absorbed energy, and power peak/timing, are key predictors of joint strength,
 establishing a foundation for cross-process ML scalability.
ANL team conducted in-situ and in-operando synchrotron X-ray characterization of laser welding in
multilayer aluminum foil stacks, providing mechanistic insight into weld formation and defect evolution
while generating datasets and insights directly support Thrust 3 and Thrust 2 through data driven
improvements to manufacturing and joining processes.
 • Performed in situ synchrotron X-ray imaging during laser welding to directly observe keyhole
 dynamics, melt pool evolution, porosity formation, and burn-through, providing critical insight
 into the mechanisms governing weld quality in multilayer foil stacks.
 • Investigated how stack, interfacial contact conditions, and pre ultrasonic welding influence
 thermal response and defect evolution during laser welding.
Provided experimental foundation for predictive modeling and future joining applications through
operando datasets connecting process parameters, stack conditions, and defect formation
