Clinical Practice of the Proactive Health Concept in Chronic Disease Management: A Qualitative Study from the Perspective of Neurology Medical Staff
Authors: Xiaoyun Xu, Yanlin Chen, Ying Zhang, Lie Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis qualitative study explores the understanding and clinical practice of the proactive health concept among neurology medical staff in managing chronic neurological disorders.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 15 healthcare professionals from a tertiary hospital in China, and data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method.ResultsFive core themes emerged: (1) education and motivation as core strategies for promoting proactive health; (2) multidimensional factors influencing patient compliance, including cognition, emotion, and family support; (3) the dual role of medical tools and technologies in facilitating and hindering self-management; (4) the nascent state and mixed impact of external support systems such as patient groups and community services; and (5) systemic barriers including time constraints and lack of institutional support. Findings indicate that while medical staff recognize the value of proactive health, its implementation is hindered by individual patient differences, limited clinical resources, and underdeveloped external systems.ConclusionThe study highlights the need for a multi-level support environment involving institutional, policy, societal, and technological dimensions to enable the effective integration of proactive health into chronic disease management.Keywords: proactive health, chronic disease management, neurological disorders, qualitative study, patient education, healthcare professionals

Introduction

Globally, neurological disorders such as stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and chronic pain impose a heavy burden on patients, families, and society due to their high incidence, high disability rates, and prolonged disease course.1,2 Additionally, neurological disorders are often accompanied by complications. For instance, the five most common non-motor complications in Parkinson’s disease patients include sleep disorders, cognitive decline, depressive mood disorders, lower urinary tract symptoms, and chronic constipation.3 The traditional medical model primarily focuses on symptom control and disease management during the post-acute phase, representing a typical “passive-reactive” approach to healthcare.4 Under this model, patients often seek medical intervention only after obvious symptoms appear, while healthcare professionals act as “fixers.” Indeed, without systematic and continuous management, chronic diseases can easily lead to preventable acute episodes or complications. This increases the frequency of emergency visits, not only aggravating the physical, psychological, and economic burden on patients and their families but also imposing ongoing cost pressures on the healthcare system.5

In this context, the concept of “proactive health” has gradually gained prominence. In 2021, China explicitly included “proactive health intervention technologies” as a key component of the Healthy China initiative in both the 14th Five-Year Plan Outline and the Outline of the 14th Five-Year Plan for National Economic and Social Development and the Long-Range Objectives Through the Year 2035.6,7 Building on the systematic integration of health promotion principles, China defines “proactive health” as the sum of a series of social activities aimed at improving the health of all populations across the entire life cycle. Its core lies in controlling health risk factors at the source, creating health value throughout the process, and actively addressing various challenges to population health security.8 The introduction of this concept provides new perspectives and policy directions for the prevention, control, and management of chronic neurological disorders.

As proposed by Ahmed et al, the S.E.L.F 4 Health™ Model advances a proactive health paradigm centered on sleep, exercise, lifestyle, and food, in contrast to the traditional reactive “sick care” model that has proven insufficient to curb the global upward trend of chronic diseases and healthcare costs.9 In contrast to the traditional “passive-reactive” medical model, proactive health emphasizes the transition from “treating illness after it occurs” to “preventing illness before it occurs” through early identification of risk factors, continuous monitoring, and personalized interventions prior to disease onset or progression.

Although the concept of “proactive health” has gained increasing attention at both policy and theoretical levels, there is a lack of in-depth qualitative research on how neurology medical staff understand this concept, what practical strategies they employ, and what systemic barriers they encounter in clinical chronic disease management. Existing studies have predominantly focused on the effectiveness of proactive health technologies or policy advocacy, overlooking the process-oriented knowledge of how frontline clinicians interpret, adapt, and implement this concept in real-world settings. This research gap hinders the effective translation of proactive health from policy rhetoric to clinical practice. Therefore, this study aims to explore the current application, practical strategies, and challenges of implementing the proactive health concept in the management of patients with chronic neurological disorders from the perspective of neurology medical staff, with the goal of providing insights for promoting the effective integration of this model into clinical practice.

Method

This study adopted a descriptive qualitative research design. This design is particularly suitable for exploring healthcare professionals’ perceptions and practical experiences regarding a specific conceptual framework—in this case, the proactive health concept—within their natural clinical context. Without manipulating the study environment, this design allows for an in-depth understanding of participants’ subjective meanings and real-world practices, which closely aligns with the exploratory nature of our research objectives. This study adopted a descriptive qualitative research design within the constructivist paradigm, which assumes that reality is multiple and subjectively constructed, and that knowledge emerges from interactive understanding between researchers and participants.

Study Participants

This study employed purposive sampling to select healthcare professionals from the Neurology Department of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (single-center) as interviewees between January and February 2026. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows:

Inclusion Criteria

Registered physicians or nurses with ≥1 year of continuous frontline clinical experience in the Neurology Department of a tertiary Grade A hospital.

Professional Competence: Possession of an intermediate or higher professional title (eg., attending physician, charge nurse) or recognition by the department head as capable of independently conducting patient diagnosis, treatment, health education, and rehabilitation guidance.

Exclusion Criteria

Role and Experience Limitations

Administrative or research staff not directly involved in patient management during the study period, or those absent from duty for extended periods (eg., >1 month due to sick leave, maternity leave, or further training).

Interference From Other Studies

Healthcare professionals simultaneously participating in other interventional studies that may influence the outcomes of this research.

Communication Barriers

Participants with language expression or communication difficulties preventing successful completion of in-depth interviews.

A total of 15 healthcare professionals were interviewed; thematic information approached saturation after the 12th interview, and the subsequent three interviews only validated existing themes without generating new core themes, indicating that data saturation had been achieved.

Study Content

Data Collection

Basic Participant Information

Gender, age, professional title (eg., physician, nurse, charge nurse, associate chief physician), years of work experience, and role type (eg., clinician, specialist nurse).

Interview Data

Collected through semi-structured interviews, covering 15 core dimensions: guidance direction for patients’ proactive health behaviors; assessment of behavioral execution ability and compliance; effectiveness of medical technology tools; mechanisms of psychological state influence; strategies for patient motivation; methods for building rehabilitation confidence; the role of disease awareness; the value of family caregiving support; utilization of community and peer resources; impact of healthcare policy systems; challenges of multidimensional integration; skill requirements for healthcare professionals; sharing of typical cases; suggestions for institutional support; recommendations for policy optimization.

The 15 core dimensions of the interview guide were developed through a systematic process. First, an initial framework was derived from a comprehensive literature review on proactive health, chronic disease management, and patient self-management behaviors. Second, the framework was refined through three rounds of discussion within our research team, which included two qualitative research experts and two neurology clinical experts (one attending physician and one head nurse). Third, the dimensions were reviewed by an external expert panel consisting of two additional qualitative methodologists and two clinical specialists in neurology, who assessed the content validity and relevance of each dimension to the research objectives. Based on their feedback, two dimensions were merged and one was reworded for clarity, resulting in the final set of 15 core dimensions (See Figure 1).

Interview framework. This semi-structured guide comprises 15 questions across four domains: physical symptom management, psychological adjustment, family/social support, and reflective summary. Probes address daily functional difficulties, emotional trajectories, support access, social reintegration challenges, and patient-generated advice, aiming to comprehensively capture proactive responses and adaptive processes in the post-illness period.

The preliminary version of the semi-structured interview guide was pilot-tested with three neurology healthcare professionals who met the inclusion criteria but were not part of the formal study sample (one physician and two nurses). After each pilot interview, the research team conducted a debriefing session to assess question clarity, comprehensibility, and flow. Participants were asked to identify any ambiguous, leading, or difficult-to-answer questions. Based on the pilot test feedback, we rephrased two questions that were found to be overly broad, reordered three questions to improve logical flow, and added one probe question to better capture patient motivation strategies. The final interview guide was approved by all research team members and the two qualitative methodologists before formal data collection commenced.

Sample Analysis

Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method,10 as follows: repeatedly reviewing transcribed texts and field notes to gain thorough familiarity with the data; identifying key statements relevant to the research theme; coding meaningful information and extracting core concepts; clustering concepts logically to form preliminary themes; defining preliminary themes and interpreting them with original statements; comparing, integrating, and synthesizing related themes to construct a thematic framework; inviting participants to validate the thematic structure, ensuring alignment with their original meanings.Verbatim transcription was used to preserve contextual authenticity.

Quality Control

This study systematically ensured trustworthiness based on Lincoln and Guba’s framework across four dimensions.11 Credibility was achieved through member checking (transcripts verified by participants), peer debriefing, and prolonged engagement by the research team to ensure that findings authentically reflected participants’ perspectives. Transferability was enhanced by providing detailed demographic information and thick descriptions of participant characteristics, enabling readers to judge the applicability of findings to their own contexts. Dependability was established through an audit trail documenting all research decisions and procedures, as well as dual independent coding with cross-checking and consensus to ensure a logical and traceable research process. Confirmability was ensured through researcher reflexivity and triangulation among multiple researchers and expert reviewers, guaranteeing that findings emerged from the data rather than researcher bias.12

Ethics Statement

This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No.: 2026–2028-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection, and all collected data are used solely for the purposes of this research.

Demographic Information of Research Subjects

The demographic analysis of the 15 medical staff participants reveals a predominantly female workforce (91.7%), with ages ranging from 25 to 43 years and a mean age of approximately 31.8 years. The group consists of both doctors (33.3%) and nurses (66.7%), who hold a variety of professional titles from junior to associate senior level, with the most common being junior and resident titles. In terms of education, the majority possess a bachelor’s degree (58.3%), followed by those with a master’s degree (33.3%), and one with a doctorate (8.3%). Their clinical experience varies considerably, spanning from 1 to 18 years, with a mean of 5.3 years; however, two-thirds of the participants have 5 years of experience or less, indicating a relatively early-career cohort (see Table 2).

Demographic Information of Research Subjects

This study conducted in-depth interviews with fifteen neurology medical professionals and employed Colaizzi’s seven-step method for data analysis. The aim was to explore healthcare providers’ understanding of the proactive health concept, their practical experiences, and the challenges encountered. The analysis ultimately distilled the findings into five core themes with corresponding sub-themes, which are elaborated as follows. Theme 3.2 (education and motivation) represents the core strategy driven by healthcare professionals; Theme 3.3 (patient-level factors) and Theme 3.4 (tools and technologies) serve as proximal moderators that influence the effectiveness of this strategy; Theme 3.5 (external support systems) constitutes a distal contextual layer; and Theme 3.6 (systemic barriers) functions as the overarching structural environment that either constrains or enables all other themes.

Healthcare Professionals’ Understanding and Practice of the Proactive Health Concept — Education and Motivation as Core Strategies

Healthcare professionals widely believe that the key to promoting proactive health lies in enhancing patients’ understanding of their diseases and providing effective health education. They emphasize that patients’ accurate comprehension of their conditions is the foundation for motivating proactive behaviors. As one physician (N1) noted, “Patients’ understanding of their disease is extremely important—it directly influences their motivation.” Another physician (N3) added, “Only when patients recognize the harm of poor disease management will they have the desire and need to actively make changes.”

In practice, healthcare professionals tend to adopt personalized communication and education strategies, tailoring their methods to patients’ cognitive levels and cultural backgrounds.

For patients with higher literacy, providing training manuals and verbal explanations is often sufficient for them to follow through seriously. For those with lower literacy or comprehension difficulties, we use simpler language and analogies to make the information more accessible. (N5)

Additionally, sharing success stories has proven to be a common and effective motivational approach, helping patients build confidence through role models. A nurse (N2) shared her experience:

We once had a patient in her 90s who was in poor condition upon admission but eventually walked out of the hospital on her own, recovering remarkably well. Such cases are not only inspiring but also serve as powerful examples.

Multidimensional Factors Influencing Patient Compliance – Cognition, Emotion, and Family as Key Elements

Patient compliance is influenced by multiple factors. The primary challenge lies in patients’ varying levels of self-discipline and cognitive understanding. As noted by one healthcare professional (N1),

Each patient is an individual with different levels of cognition, work backgrounds, and personalities. Ensuring all of them adhere to medical advice is difficult.

Another professional (N9) added candidly, “If they were truly self-disciplined in the first place, they might not have developed the condition that led them here.”

In this context, family support and supervision play an indispensable role. Proactive family members can effectively encourage behavioral changes in patients. As one physician (N7) observed,

Supportive family members discourage unhealthy behaviors. For example, the family of a patient in bed 13 insisted that the aphasic patient say a name before giving them water, thereby encouraging movement and speech.

Additionally, patients’ emotional states significantly impact their motivation for recovery. Negative emotions not only diminish motivation but also lead to adverse physiological effects. As noted by N1, “Especially in depressed patients, inactivity can lead to muscle atrophy. The impact is substantial.”

Medical Tools and Technologies as a Double-Edged Sword — Balancing Convenience and Limitations

At the level of tools and technologies, home medical devices such as blood pressure monitors and glucose meters offer convenience but also come with challenges related to operational accuracy and cultural adaptability. As one healthcare professional (N7) pointed out, their limitations include potential user errors:

Self-operation may lead to inaccuracies—for example, how tightly the cuff is fastened can affect results. Additionally, patients with lower literacy levels might struggle to perform standardized operations even after instruction.

Regarding emerging technologies, healthcare professionals recognize the potential of the internet and artificial intelligence in health science communication but remain cautious about their risks. They acknowledge the powerful reach of self-media platforms as effective channels for future public health education (N1) and believe that acquiring psychological knowledge or utilizing AI for science communication represents a valuable new skill set for medical professionals to develop (N7).

The External Support System: Taking Shape Yet Needing Guidance – Weighing the Pros and Cons of Patient Support Groups and Community Services

Healthcare professionals hold nuanced views regarding external resources. They perceive patient support groups as a double-edged sword, with both potential benefits and drawbacks. While these groups may offer emotional support, they are also seen as prone to disseminating misinformation. As one professional (N1) noted, “Personally, I believe such groups often do more harm than good. Interactions among patients frequently spread negative emotions and incorrect information.”

Simultaneously, community-based chronic disease management services have begun to play a positive role, though their coverage and depth remain limited. Several healthcare professionals acknowledged the value of community services (N2, N3) but emphasized that their effectiveness has yet to fully meet practical needs.

Systemic Challenges and Future Directions — Time Constraints and Lack of Institutional Support

At the systemic level, healthcare professionals widely report that limited time and staffing are major obstacles to implementing proactive health initiatives. Overwhelming workloads make it difficult to deliver detailed health management, as noted by one professional (N4): “The patient volume is simply too high. During busy periods, we can barely manage to complete our tasks, let alone provide meticulous care.”

In response, they have proposed several recommendations at both institutional and policy levels. Key suggestions include:

Establishing a systematic and scalable health education framework, such as organizing patient education sessions and creating instructional or rehabilitation videos with scannable codes (N5, N9).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that healthcare professionals’ core strategies—personalized education, motivational communication, and fostering patient active engagement—are conceptually consistent with the LEARNS health education model.13 Although we did not set out to test this model, its principles emerged during the interpretive phase as a useful framework for making sense of our emergent themes, particularly Theme 3.2 (education and motivation as core strategies) and Theme 3.3 (multidimensional factors influencing compliance).

Recent research collectively corroborates the effectiveness of the LEARNS model in promoting proactive health behaviors across different clinical contexts. Across studies on laryngeal tumor perioperative education, stroke rehabilitation training, and general chronic disease management, the LEARNS-based approach has been shown to consistently improve disease awareness, treatment adherence,14 and patient engagement in recovery.15 They also underscore the significant value of structured, personalized educational interventions.

However, this study further reveals that in practical implementation, healthcare professionals face multiple real-world challenges. On one hand, individual patient differences, emotional states, and levels of family support directly influence the effectiveness of education and behavioral change. On the other hand, healthcare professionals widely report that limited time and insufficient staffing constrain the depth of personalized health management. Moreover, external support systems, while in their nascent stages, remain underdeveloped. For instance, patient support groups may offer emotional assistance but also carry the risk of spreading misinformation. Similarly, community-based chronic disease management services have been initiated but still require expansion in terms of coverage and service depth. These findings indicate that relying solely on educational models or the efforts of individual healthcare professionals is insufficient for the systematic implementation of proactive health management. This convergence with Melzer et al’s findings—where U.S. clinicians similarly identified “competing priorities, cost, and unclear staffing models” as core barriers—suggests that implementation gaps in proactive health are systemic and cross-contextual rather than unique to the Chinese neurology setting.16

In terms of social support systems, previous studies have shown that team leadership, timely responses, and intergroup competition play significant roles in building online communities based on social identity.17 This aligns with the “double-edged sword” effect of external support systems identified in this study. Peer support platforms, such as patient groups, can provide emotional support and foster social identity.18 However, without proper guidance and regulation, they may also spread negative emotions and misinformation.19 Therefore, how to effectively guide and regulate peer support platforms to maximize their positive effects and mitigate potential risks is a critical issue that requires focused attention in the future.

Effective patient education is key to enhancing patient engagement, strengthening self-management capabilities, and improving overall health outcomes.20 This study argues for the establishment of a multi-level and systematic proactive health support environment. At the institutional level, dedicated roles and standardized education systems should be established to alleviate clinical workforce pressures. At the policy level, incentive mechanisms should be optimized to support healthcare professionals in conducting health management work. At the societal level, peer support platforms should be regulated, and community health service capacity should be strengthened. At the technological level, the development and oversight of user-friendly medical tools and high-quality information platforms should be promoted. Only through multi-stakeholder collaboration and systematic advancement can the proactive health concept be effectively translated and sustainably developed in the management of chronic neurological diseases.

Strengths and Limitations

This study holds certain academic and practical value in exploring the understanding and practice of the proactive health concept among neurology healthcare professionals, but it also has several limitations.

The strengths of the study are primarily reflected in its methodological rigor. It strictly adhered to Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method and ensured systematic and credible data analysis and theme development through multiple measures, including dual independent coding, expert guidance, and validation of results via participant feedback. Additionally, the study’s perspective is highly focused, centering on frontline neurology healthcare professionals directly involved in health management. This approach provides an in-depth exploration of their real-world experiences, strategic applications, and challenges in implementing the proactive health concept in clinical practice, offering valuable empirical evidence for understanding the integration of this concept into chronic disease management.

However, the study also has certain limitations. First, the representativeness of the sample is limited. As a single-center qualitative study with a small sample size, all participants were recruited from the same tertiary Grade A hospital. Although purposive sampling was employed and demographic diversity was considered, caution should be exercised when generalizing the findings to healthcare professionals from different levels of medical institutions, regions, or cultural backgrounds. Second, recall and reporting biases may exist. The data were derived from self-reported interviews with healthcare professionals, and their responses could be influenced by social desirability, subjective perceptions, or memory biases. Third, the perspective is not fully comprehensive. The study only collected views from healthcare professionals and did not include the experiences and perspectives of patients and their families. As a result, it does not fully capture the bidirectional dynamics of doctor-patient interactions in proactive health management or potential differences in needs and expectations.

Conclusion

This qualitative study reveals that while neurology healthcare professionals recognize the value of proactive health in chronic disease management, its implementation is hindered by patient-level variability, clinical resource constraints, and underdeveloped external support systems. These barriers, consistent with findings from other healthcare contexts, point to a systemic implementation gap rather than localized deficiencies. To bridge this gap, future participatory or implementation science research should develop concrete operational guidelines based on the multi-level (institutional, policy, societal, technological) framework proposed in this study.
