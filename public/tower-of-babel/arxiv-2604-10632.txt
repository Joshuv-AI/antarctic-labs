Multimodal Dataset Normalization and Perceptual Validation for Music-Taste Correspondences
Matteo Spanio ( spanio@dei.unipd.it )( 0000-0002-2436-7208 ), Valentina Frezzato ( valentina.frezzato.1@studenti.unipd.it ), Antonio Rodà ( roda@dei.unipd.it )( 0000-0001-9921-0590 )

Abstract
Collecting large, aligned cross-modal datasets for music–flavor research is difficult because perceptual experiments are costly and small by design. We address this bottleneck through two complementary experiments. The first tests whether audio–flavor correlations, feature-importance rankings, and latent-factor structure transfer from an experimental soundtracks collection (257 tracks with human annotations) to a large FMA-derived corpus ( ∼ \sim 49,300 segments with synthetic labels). The second validates computational flavor targets—derived from food chemistry via a reproducible pipeline—against human perception in an online listener study (49 participants, 20 tracks). Results from both experiments converge: the quantitative transfer analysis confirms that cross-modal structure is preserved across supervision regimes, and the perceptual evaluation shows significant alignment between computational targets and listener ratings (permutation p < 0.0001 p<0.0001 , Mantel r = 0.45 r=0.45 , Procrustes m 2 = 0.51 m^{2}=0.51 ). Together, these findings support the conclusion that sonic seasoning effects are present in synthetic FMA annotations. We release datasets and companion code to support reproducible cross-modal AI research.

Multimodal pipelines also suffer from inconsistent feature spaces, weak normalization across sources, and limited reproducibility.

We address these issues through two complementary experiments. The first analyzes whether audio–flavor relationships transfer from an experimental soundtracks collection with human annotations to a large FMA-derived corpus (

Methodologically, the work contributes to reproducible multimodal science in three ways: (i) it tests consistency of audio–flavor structure across supervision regimes (human labels vs weak model labels); (ii) it provides an independent perceptual validation of computationally derived targets; and (iii) it links dataset-level statistics to perceptual-level outcomes, supporting stronger external validity claims than single-dataset analyses. These choices are especially relevant for sonic seasoning, where perceptual plausibility and interpretability often outweigh maximizing a single predictive metric, and where transferability across supervision regimes remains a practical bottleneck.

Related Work

2.1
Cross-Modal and Neuroscientific Background

Prior work in multisensory perception shows systematic interactions between auditory cues and gustatory judgments, motivating computational sonic seasoning as a data-driven extension of cross-modal correspondences
[
1
]
.

Classic and contemporary studies converge on robust non-arbitrary mappings between auditory and non-auditory features. Early cross-modal findings, including shape-sound correspondences, established that humans consistently align sensory dimensions across modalities
[
12
]
. Later work showed similar regularities for taste and sound, including pitch-taste and timbre-taste associations
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

Two limitations recur: scale (controlled studies cover limited musical diversity) and representational compatibility (task-specific ratings are hard to propagate across datasets). Without explicit normalization, cross-study comparability remains weak, motivating our target-construction protocol and consistent target semantics.

2.2
Cross-Modal AI and Synthetic/Fused Datasets

Recent AI approaches combine neural models, metadata fusion, and synthetic or weakly supervised labels to scale cross-modal tasks. In music–flavor contexts, this enables larger datasets but introduces normalization and validation challenges that require explicit evaluation protocols
[
2
,
13
,
11
]
.

The trend is consistent with broader multimodal AI: text-to-image and text-to-music systems demonstrate the utility of large heterogeneous corpora but also expose sensitivity to dataset construction choices
[
14
,
15
]
. In affective and emotionally conditioned modeling, multimodal fusion improves robustness, yet depends heavily on aligned labels and modality-specific preprocessing
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

In music information retrieval, data-driven feature extraction and transformer-based audio models have improved representational power for downstream prediction tasks. However, cross-modal tasks remain constrained by supervision quality and target reliability. Prior work has shown feasibility for music-taste prediction
[
2
,
19
]
, but reproducible protocols for normalization and cross-regime validation are still limited.

A related challenge concerns synthetic and fused datasets. Weak labels can greatly increase scale but may introduce regime shifts; transfer analysis is therefore a primary evaluation objective. Synthetic multimodal datasets in vision–language (e.g., CLEVR, GQA, SMMQG) show how controlled generation can scale supervision and stress-test compositional reasoning
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

Overall, the literature suggests a gap between high-capacity model development and robust multimodal dataset methodology. This paper addresses that gap by centering normalization, transfer diagnostics, and perceptual validation in a single integrated workflow, rather than proposing a new model architecture.

3
Methodology

3.1
Data Sources and Harmonization

We jointly analyze: (i) an experimental soundtracks collection (257 tracks) with human flavor annotations aggregated from 22 published studies, (ii) an FMA-extended chunk-level dataset (
\sim 49{,}300
30-second segments) with synthetic flavor annotations, and (iii) FoodDB-derived food chemistry resources
[
23
]
for target construction. All components are mapped to a common five-dimensional taste space (sweet, bitter, sour, salty, spicy).

The FMA flavor labels were generated by an Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST)
[
24
]
fine-tuned on the experimental soundtracks collection, as described in
[
2
]
. The AST was trained on the 257-track corpus; the transfer analysis in Experiment 1 therefore tests whether audio-feature–flavor
structure
(correlation profiles, importance rankings, latent factors) is preserved in model-generated labels, not whether the labels are independently derived. No FMA audio was used during AST training, and no human-annotated tracks appear in the FMA corpus, ruling out direct data leakage.

Both corpora share a common set of 92 audio features extracted with
librosa

[
25
]
from 11 feature families: zero crossing rate (ZCR), chroma (CQT, STFT, CENS), tonnetz, MFCCs (20 coefficients), RMS energy, spectral centroid, spectral bandwidth, spectral contrast (7 sub-bands), and spectral rolloff. For each time-varying feature, mean and standard deviation are computed over the analysis window (frame length 2048, hop length 512). The FMA corpus uses 6-bin chroma representations, yielding 46 base features
\times
2 statistics
=
92 signal features; the experimental collection uses 12-bin chroma, producing 148 features, of which the 92 shared features are used for all cross-corpus comparisons.

3.2
Cross-Modal Transfer Analysis

The first experiment tests whether audio–flavor relationships observed in the experimental soundtracks collection (257 tracks with human annotations) transfer to the FMA-extended corpus (
\sim 49{,}300
segments with synthetic labels). The analysis comprises three components:

1)

Correlation transfer:
we compare Spearman correlation profiles between audio features and flavor dimensions across corpora, measuring sign agreement and rank preservation;

2)

Feature-importance transfer:
we compare bootstrap Random Forest importance rankings (30 iterations, 30 estimators, max depth 8; FMA subsampled to 5,000 for tractability) to assess whether the most explanatory audio families are consistent across supervision regimes;

3)

Latent-structure compatibility:
we use parallel analysis and canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to test whether shared latent factors linking audio and flavor are preserved despite differences in annotation source. CCA is fitted on the full data in each corpus; we note that the resulting canonical correlations may overestimate held-out performance, and report them as descriptive measures of multivariate coupling rather than predictive accuracy estimates.

As an additional probe, we test whether text-side metadata in the FMA corpus (genre, mood tags, free-text descriptions) shows systematic flavor associations, using Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn post-hoc (Bonferroni), mood-tag effect sizes, and TF-IDF clustering. If text-derived patterns align with audio-derived ones, confidence in the dataset representation increases.

3.3
Perceptual Evaluation

The second experiment validates whether computational flavor targets align with human gustatory perception. This requires three preparatory steps—constructing machine-readable flavor vectors from food chemistry, designing an experimental target set, and matching each target to a music stimulus—followed by an online listener study.

3.3.1
From Food Compounds to Taste Vectors

Because the perceptual study requires food-based stimuli with known flavor profiles, we derive computational flavor vectors from FoodDB
[
23
]
. Compound-level concentrations are transformed into taste probabilities via the neural FART classifier
[
26
]
. Food-level vectors are then aggregated with logarithmic weighting (Weber–Fechner-inspired):

Umami is merged into salty to match the five-dimensional experimental design: the two dimensions are highly correlated in FoodDB vectors (Pearson

3.3.2
Target-Set Design and Music Matching

We select 20 foods spanning different regions of flavor space (
Figure
2
), balancing simple single-taste-dominant profiles and complex multi-taste dishes. For each food target vector, we compute Euclidean distance to all annotated FMA chunks and select the minimum-distance track as the stimulus.

3.3.3
Listener Study

49 participants (17 men and 32 women; mean age
=40.9
,
SD=19.2
, range 18–95, median 31) each rated 10 randomly assigned tracks out of 20 on five taste dimensions using a 7-point Likert intensity scale. The study was administered online via PsyToolkit
[
27
]
. Participants evaluated how strongly each track evoked each of the five basic tastes. Most participants used device speakers (
n=35
; headphones:
n=13
; other:
n=1
). Demographic screening included self-reported hearing impairments and taste/smell impairments; participants reporting impairments were not excluded but are flagged in the released data.

3.3.4
Statistical Analysis

Perceptual ratings are z-score normalized per dimension and aggregated into mean perceived vectors
\bar{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
for each track
i
. Alignment between computational targets and perception is assessed through:

1)

a permutation test (10K iterations) on mean Euclidean distance between target and perceived vectors, testing whether the observed distance is smaller than expected by chance;

2)

the Mantel test on distance matrices between target and perceptual spaces;

3)

Procrustes/PROTEST analysis for structural correspondence.

Experimental Setup

Results

5.1
Experiment 1: Cross-Modal Transfer

5.1.1
Audio–Flavor Transfer

The transfer analysis indicates preserved cross-modal trends between corpora. Sign agreement across the top five pairwise feature–flavor associations is high (22/25), meaning that the direction of audio–flavor relationships is largely consistent regardless of whether annotations come from human raters or the AST-based pipeline. First canonical correlations are 0.962 (human-annotated corpus) and 0.910 (FMA). Because CCA was fitted on full data without cross-validation, these values likely overestimate held-out canonical correlations and should be interpreted as descriptive measures of in-sample multivariate coupling rather than predictive performance estimates. The key observation is the relative consistency of coupling strength across supervision regimes, not the absolute magnitudes.

Figure

Each panel corresponds to one taste dimension and plots the Spearman correlation of every audio feature with that flavor in the human-annotated corpus (horizontal axis) against the same correlation in the FMA corpus (vertical axis). Points falling near the diagonal indicate features whose association with the flavor is preserved across supervision regimes; the overall positive trend visible in all five panels confirms that the direction and relative magnitude of audio–flavor relationships are largely maintained. Deviations from the diagonal highlight features whose correlations change under weak labeling, with sourness showing the widest scatter and sweetness the tightest alignment.

5.2.1
Target Construction

The taste-vector construction pipeline produces coherent target profiles across food groups and supports interpretable composition and blending steps for the 20 experimental stimuli.

6
Discussion

The two experiments provide converging evidence that the sonic seasoning effect—systematic audio–flavor correspondences documented in controlled laboratory studies—is preserved in synthetic annotations of the FMA dataset.

Experiment 1 shows that the correlation structure, feature-importance rankings, and latent-factor coupling observed in the human-annotated corpus transfer significantly to the FMA-extended corpus with model-generated labels. A key finding is that spectral families (MFCCs, spectral contrast, spectral rolloff) dominate flavor prediction in both corpora. This is not surprising for FMA weak labels, since they are generated through an AST
[
24
]
pipeline that relies on spectrogram structure. The critical result is not the presence of spectral dependence itself, but its agreement with the experimental soundtracks collection: transfer and rank-preservation statistics support the quality of synthetic annotations and validate weak-label expansion as a practical strategy. We acknowledge that the spectral dominance could partly reflect the AST’s inductive bias rather than fully independent convergence; an ablation restricting features to non-spectral families or using a feature-neutral encoder would help disentangle these factors. Text-side analyses reinforce the overall conclusion: genre effects, mood-dependent contrasts, and coherent lexical clusters indicate that flavor variance is carried across multiple modalities, not just audio.

Experiment 2 provides external validation from the perceptual side. The permutation test, Mantel correlation, and Procrustes analysis all confirm that tracks selected from the FMA corpus via computational flavor matching evoke gustatory perceptions that are systematically aligned with their food-derived targets. Stimuli with salient single-taste profiles show the strongest alignment, consistent with literature reporting more robust cross-modal associations for dominant taste dimensions. The residual mismatch for complex multi-taste stimuli points to optimization opportunities in retrieval distance design and target-vector construction, especially for near-neighbor foods within the same flavor macro-cluster.

Taken together, Experiment 1 establishes that the quantitative audio–flavor structure is transferable across supervision regimes, and Experiment 2 demonstrates that this structure is perceptually grounded. The combination supports the conclusion that synthetic FMA annotations encode genuine cross-modal relationships rather than dataset artifacts. Several extensions would strengthen the evidence: (i) cross-validated CCA and PLS baselines to obtain held-out canonical correlations and predictive
R^{2}
values; (ii) bootstrap confidence intervals and permutation-based null distributions for sign-agreement and feature-rank correlations, providing formal uncertainty quantification; (iii) sub-sampling stability checks on the large corpus and bootstrap resampling of the small corpus to assess robustness; and (iv) alternative similarity metrics (cosine, Mahalanobis) for music–target matching, which may reduce the within-cluster confusions observed in the distance matrix.

The proposed workflow can be adapted to adjacent domains where perceptual labels are expensive and weak labels are abundant. Because the release package includes both large weakly labeled data and the human-annotated experimental collection, future work can benchmark robustness across supervision quality rather than reporting single-regime results.

7
Conclusions

We presented two complementary experiments addressing whether sonic seasoning effects are preserved in synthetic annotations of a large music corpus. Experiment 1 demonstrates that audio–flavor correlations, feature-importance rankings, and latent-factor structure transfer significantly from the experimental soundtracks collection to the FMA-extended dataset with model-generated labels. Experiment 2 validates that tracks retrieved from this corpus via computational flavor matching evoke perceptual responses aligned with food-derived targets in a listener study. Together, these results support the viability of weak-label expansion for cross-modal music–flavor research and provide a reproducible evaluation protocol.

This study has several limitations. First, weak labels in the large corpus are model-derived and may encode systematic biases from upstream AST training; although we rule out direct data leakage, the AST’s reliance on spectrogram structure means that feature-importance overlap could partly reflect the model’s inductive bias. Second, genre and metadata analyses are partly constrained by annotation noise and class imbalance; the large sample size inflates omnibus test statistics, so per-pair effect sizes should guide interpretation. Third, stimulus selection was optimized to minimize distance to food targets based on synthetic labels, which may bias the perceptual test in favor of the hypothesis; adversarial or random selection strategies would provide a stronger baseline.

Despite these constraints, the workflow is designed for transparent iteration. By releasing intermediate artifacts (compound predictions, food-level vectors, processed FMA splits, target vectors, and perceptual ratings), we enable independent replication at each stage. The release includes all code, data, and configuration needed to reconstruct intermediate artifacts—including the exact FMA chunk splits and feature extraction pipeline—and to reproduce all figures and tables. The same protocol can be adapted to other cross-modal tasks where one modality is scarce in high-quality labels while another is abundant but noisier.
