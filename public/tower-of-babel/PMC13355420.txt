Outcome after surgery for meralgia paresthetica – a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mikkel Lammers Haglund, Peter Birkeland, Vagn Eskesen, Markus Harboe Olsen, Ove Ketil Bergdal, Kåre Fugleholm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMeralgia paresthetica may cause chronic pain and sensory disturbances. When conservative treatment fails, surgery with neurolysis or neurectomy is considered, but evidence remains limited.Research questionTo evaluate pain relief, sensory outcomes, and durability after surgical treatment of meralgia paresthetica.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, all meralgia paresthetica patients treated surgically between 2019 and 2024 were included. Outcomes (pain, sensory disturbances and secondary surgery) were selected in accordance with core outcome set recommendations. Pain, measured as changes in NRS, was analyzed with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, between-procedure differences with Mann–Whitney U tests, and time to reoperation with Kaplan–Meier analysis.ResultsA total of 101 patients underwent 128 procedures. Primary neurolysis (n = 102) was associated with a median pain reduction of 6.5 NRS points (p < 0.001). Secondary neurectomy, most often after insufficient effect of neurolysis (n = 18), resulted in a similar cumulative decrease of 7.0 points (p < 0.001). The magnitude of pain reduction did not differ significantly between procedures. At one year, 85.4% remained free from reoperation. Sensory improvement occurred in 64% of patients. Pain reduction occurred in 85.3% of patients.ConclusionSurgery for meralgia paresthetica provides substantial and durable pain relief. Neurolysis is effective as first-line surgery, with reoperations being uncommon and typically occurring within the first postoperative year. Neurectomy did not show superior pain relief and is associated with unavoidable sensory loss.

Introduction

Meralgia paresthetica (MP) arises from impingement of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve at the level of the anterior superior iliac spine, usually where the nerve passes beneath the inguinal ligament, although anatomical variations often occur (Williams and Trzil, 1991), (Harney and Patijn, 2007), (Solomons et al., 2022), (Reuter et al., 2025), (Xu et al., 2019).

Symptoms of MP include chronic or episodic burning, tingling, and pain in the affected area (Harney and Patijn, 2007), (Solomons et al., 2022), (Reuter et al., 2025).

MP is relatively uncommon, with an estimated incidence of up to 40 per 100,000 individuals globally, which likely contributes to the scarcity of larger comparative studies (Parisi et al., 2011), (van et al., 2004).

MP is often treated conservatively, which includes weight loss, looser clothing, avoidance of belts and use of NSAIDs or other analgesics for symptom relief. While corticosteroid injections may provide temporary diagnostic or therapeutic benefit, they are not considered a definitive treatment modality for MP (Williams and Trzil, 1991), (Gomez et al., 2023), (Ahmed et al., 2025).

When conservative treatment fails, surgical options include decompression by neurolysis (NL), neurectomy (NR) or transposition (Son et al., 2012), (Nouraei et al., 2007), (Benezis et al., 2007), (Hanna, 2017). Transposition has not yet gained acceptance as routine treatment.

Although several observational studies suggest a longer-lasting effect of neurectomy (NR), and systematic reviews have reported higher rates of complete or near-complete symptom relief after NR compared with neurolysis (NL), the available evidence remains limited by small, non-randomized, and retrospective study designs (Nouraei et al., 2007), (de et al., 2012), (Emamhadi and Sheikhvatan, 2012), (Lu et al., 2021), (Khalil et al., 2012).

In a recent meta-analysis, Lu et al. reported complete pain relief in 85% of patients after NR compared with 63% after NL and 22% after injection (Lu et al., 2021).

Similarly, the Cochrane review by Khalil et al. found surgical treatment to be beneficial in 88% of patients treated with decompression and 94% of patients treated with NR, while emphasizing the absence of randomized controlled trials (Khalil et al., 2012).

However, NR results in loss of sensibility on the anterolateral thigh, which can be bothersome.

(Nouraei et al., 2007), (de et al., 2012), (Emamhadi and Sheikhvatan, 2012). NL significantly improves symptoms in many patients; however, most previous studies of NL are also small and/or lack long-term follow-up (Benezis et al., 2007), (de et al., 2012), (Emamhadi and Sheikhvatan, 2012), (van et al., 1995), (Schönberg et al., 2023). Recent anatomical studies have described distinct fascial planes surrounding the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve near its pelvic exit, which may have implications for nerve entrapment and surgical decompression. In light of these anatomical insights, LFCN transposition has also been proposed as a nerve-preserving surgical treatment option.

To address these limitations, we conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study including all surgically treated MP patients between 2019 and 2024. Through structured patient interviews, we evaluated long-term pain relief of NL and NR, aiming to clarify their relative effectiveness and durability when applied in a stepwise surgical strategy, to guide future surgical decision-making.

Methods

This study included patients who underwent surgical intervention with either NL or NR for MP at Copenhagen University Hospital – Rigshospitalet, Denmark, between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2024. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies (von Elm et al., 2007).

All procedures were performed by a small team of four neurosurgical consultants. Data compiled from electronic medical records were stored and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). Exclusion criteria were cognitive impairment, language barriers, or non-response despite repeated contact attempts.

Interviews

Follow-up data were collected through structured telephone interviews conducted by a single physician investigator using a predefined and standardized questionnaire. Interviews were performed at varying intervals, depending on the timing of the first procedure. Preoperative pain intensity was retrospectively reported during follow-up interviews.

Outcome measures (pain intensity, sensory disturbances, and need for secondary surgery) were selected in accordance with recently proposed core outcome set (COS) recommendations for meralgia paresthetica and lateral femoral cutaneous neuropathy. Pain intensity was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, range 0–10, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 worst imaginable pain). These outcomes represent core patient-relevant measures and enable better comparability with previous and future studies (Dinh and Wilson, 2024), (Wilson et al., 2026).

Ethics approval and informed consent

This retrospective study was approved by the Legal Department of the Capital Region of Denmark, Rigshospitalet (approval no. P-2024-18092, approved 10 December 2024). In accordance with applicable national legislation and administrative regulations, informed consent was not required, as the study was based exclusively on a retrospective design and the processing of fully anonymized data. Participation in the telephone follow-up was voluntary. No directly or indirectly identifiable personal data are included in this manuscript.

Procedures

At Rigshospitalet, NL was advised as a first-line treatment. In accordance with shared decision making, patients were also offered primary NR. As a policy, re-treatment by neurectomy was not considered until 6 months after the primary decompression. By intraoperative decision, some patients had a second NL. A primary surgery was defined as the first surgical intervention on any side. Five patients underwent surgery on one side at their initial surgery and later the opposite side at a second surgery. Since we treat MP on each side as separate events, each of these five patients contributed two primary surgeries, one for each side.

Procedures were performed under general anesthesia. With the patient in the supine position, a transverse skin incision was made medial to and slightly below the anterior superior iliac spine. The subcutaneous tissue and fascia were incised, and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve was identified, usually in its fascial plane superficial to the sartorius muscle.

The nerve was released proximally by cutting the inguinal ligament until the nerve was decompressed into the preperitoneal space; in NR, a 3–5 cm segment of the nerve was resected, allowing the proximal nerve stump to retract into the abdominal cavity. Hemostasis was achieved, and the wound was closed in standard fashion.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Baseline characteristics are presented as medians with interquartile range (IQR). As neurolysis was the primary surgical approach in the vast majority of patients, treatment effects were evaluated separately for patients undergoing primary neurolysis at first surgery and for patients undergoing secondary neurectomy at reoperation. For patients undergoing a second surgical procedure, pain intensity was reassessed prior to reoperation. In all cases, patients reported pain levels comparable to those recorded prior to the initial neurolysis, reflecting recurrence of symptoms. Accordingly, baseline pain for analyses of the second procedure was defined as the preoperative NRS score prior to the first surgery. Patients treated with alternative procedures were few and were included in descriptive analyses only.

Changes in pain intensity (NRS) were assessed using paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests for pre‐ versus post‐operative comparisons within each procedure. Differences in the magnitude of pain reduction between patients treated with primary neurolysis and those undergoing secondary neurectomy were assessed descriptively using the Mann–Whitney U test. For each comparison, the median difference is reported together with the 95% Hodges–Lehmann confidence interval (HLCI). Time to second surgery was analyzed using a Kaplan-Meier analysis (survival and survminer packages).

Patients with missing data for either time point (pre- or post-surgery) in a given comparison were excluded from that specific analysis. No data imputation was performed. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 101 patients were eligible and included in the study. Five patients underwent bilateral surgery, resulting in 106 primary surgical procedures. NL was performed as the primary procedure in 102 cases (96.2%). In addition, 22 patients underwent a second surgical procedure, most commonly NR (18/22, 81.8%) yielding a total of 128 surgical procedures (Fig. 1). The median age was 55.5 years (46.0-64.0) and 49.1% of patients were female. Time to second surgery

Time to secondary surgery following primary neurolysis is illustrated in Fig. 2. At 1, 2, and 3 years, the Kaplan–Meier estimated probabilities of remaining free from secondary surgery were 85.4% (95% CI: 78.7–92.8%), 78.6% (95% CI: 70.4–87.8%), and 77.1% (95% CI: 68.6–86.6%), respectively. Among patients who underwent reoperation, 14 of 21 (66.7%) underwent a second procedure within the first year following primary surgery, whereas 7 of 21 (33.3%) underwent reoperation after one year. Sensory outcomes

Patient-reported changes in sensory disturbances following primary NL were heterogeneous. Overall, 64% of patients reported improvements in sensory symptoms (31.4% reporting very high improvement, 10.8% high, 13.7% somewhat and 7.8% minor improvement), while 29.4% reported no change and 6.9% did not know. Sensitivity

A sensitivity analysis restricting analyses to one observation per patient, by excluding secondary bilateral procedures, yielded results similar to the primary analyses.

Discussion

We found a significant and mostly sustained reduction in pain following NL. The second procedure (NR) was performed after a median of 9.5 months, suggesting that while revision surgery is uncommon, it tends to occur within the first post-surgical year. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that 85.4% of patients who underwent primary NL remained free from second surgery at one year and 77.1% at 3 years, indicating that the majority of patients experience sustained long-term benefit following primary surgery. This strengthens the clinical relevance of the observed pain relief and patient satisfaction, supporting the durability of the surgical outcomes presented.

Due to the retrospective nature of the study, randomization between surgical interventions was not possible. We found no significant difference in NRS change after surgery between the two procedure types. However, NR was almost exclusively used as a second-line procedure following insufficient or transient effect of NL. Consequently, the observed effect of NR is subject to selection bias, and its true effect may be under- or overestimated. These findings should be interpreted within the context of a stepwise treatment strategy rather than as a direct comparison between procedures. Several prior studies and reviews, including a meta-analysis by Lu et al. (2021) and the Cochrane review by Khalil et al. (2012) have reported higher rates of complete or near-complete symptom relief following neurectomy compared with neurolysis. However, this evidence is based predominantly on non-randomized, retrospective studies, and the potential benefits of neurectomy must be weighed against inevitable sensory loss of the anterolateral thigh.

A recent German national register-based cohort study from 2023 found that decompression was used far more often than neurectomy (Schönberg et al., 2023). Although neuroma formation and deafferentation pain are theoretical concerns after nerve transection, there is limited MP-specific evidence that these complications represent established clinical outcomes after LFCN neurectomy. They were not predefined outcomes in the present cohort and are not included among the core outcomes in the recently proposed core outcome set for lateral femoral cutaneous neuropathy (Dinh and Wilson, 2024), (Wilson et al., 2026). These considerations support a cautious interpretation of neurectomy outcomes and reinforce the rationale for reserving neurectomy for selected patients with insufficient or recurrent symptoms after neurolysis.

LFCN transposition represents another nerve-preserving surgical option for MP (Xu et al., 2019), (Hanna, 2017). However, this technique was not part of the stepwise surgical strategy used at our institution during the study period and could therefore not be evaluated in the present cohort.

We found that patients reported high pain levels on the NRS before surgery and a clear reduction afterwards, both after the first and the second surgical procedure. These results are fully in line with previous studies, supporting the credibility and external validity of our findings (Ataizi et al., 2019), (Morimoto et al., 2018), (Schwaiger et al., 2022), (Kibici et al., 2021).

Importantly, patients who did not experience early recurrence after primary surgery were unlikely to require a later second surgery, suggesting that sustained symptom relief becomes apparent relatively early in the postsurgical course.

When examining sensory disturbances, most patients reported improvement after surgery. This pattern is consistent with previous studies, which likewise describe a high level of sensory improvement after surgery. Overall, our findings support that sensory disturbances tend to improve in the majority of patients after NL, in line with the existing literature (Reuter et al., 2025), (Nouraei et al., 2007), (Malessy et al., 2019).

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the comparatively large cohort, including 101 patients undergoing 128 procedures, making it larger than most previously published series (Benezis et al., 2007), (Ataizi et al., 2019), (Morimoto et al., 2018), (Schwaiger et al., 2022), (Kibici et al., 2021). Another strength is the single-center design with all procedures performed by a limited number of experienced peripheral nerve consultants, ensuring consistency of surgical technique. Furthermore, the use of time-to-event analysis allowed a nuanced assessment of risk of reoperation over time. Limitations such as recall bias and selection bias are inherent to retrospective interview-based designs. Preoperative pain intensity was retrospectively reported during follow-up interviews, which may have introduced recall bias. However, presurgical NRS values corresponded closely to previous reports, supporting reliability. The small number of NR cases reflects current clinical practice and does not weaken the overall conclusion.

Conclusion

This large single-center retrospective cohort study provides robust evidence that surgery for MP results in sustained long-term pain relief in the majority of patients. NL as a primary surgical strategy is effective and durable for most patients, the majority remaining free from a second surgery during follow-up. When reoperation is required, it most often occurs within the first post-surgical year. As a secondary procedure, NR did not demonstrate superior pain reduction compared with NL and is associated with inevitable sensory deficits. Based on these findings, NL should remain the preferred first-line surgical option for refractory MP. Larger prospective and randomized studies are needed to further refine patient selection and optimize treatment algorithms.
