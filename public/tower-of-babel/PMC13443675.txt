Metal-dependent inhibition of germination and seedling growth in two onion cultivars under in vitro conditions
Authors: Ecem Kara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeavy metal contamination can reduce seed germination and early seedling growth. However, plant responses may change depending on the metal and cultivar. This study examined the effects of copper (Cu), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), nickel (Ni), and cobalt (Co) on two onion (Allium cepa L.) cultivars, Gence and Karbeyazı, under in vitro conditions. Seeds were cultured on Murashige and Skoog medium containing 0, 100, 300, or 500 µM of each metal. Germination rate, mean germination time, germination index, shoot and root length, and fresh and dry biomass were recorded. Cultivar, dose, and their interaction significantly affected most traits. In general, higher metal doses reduced germination performance, root growth, and biomass accumulation. Cu and Cd decreased germination rate from 77.50 to 54.00% and from 80.00 to 52.67%, respectively. Root length was markedly inhibited at the highest metal doses, with Cu, Pb, and Co showing pronounced reductions based on relative inhibition values. Ni showed a different pattern. It increased final germination at low to medium doses but reduced germination vigor. Overall, Gence maintained higher final germination, whereas Karbeyazı better preserved root-related traits. These results show that early onion responses to heavy metals are both metal- and cultivar-dependent.Keywords: Allium cepa, Cultivar tolerance, Germination index, Heavy metal stress, Seedling growthSubject terms: Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Heavy metal pollution is a severe environmental problem in agricultural soils. Trace amounts of certain metals are necessary for the growth and metabolism of plants. However, they may be toxic at concentrations above the tolerance limits of plants1,2. Heavy metal pollution can inhibit plant growth, reduce productivity, and threaten soil quality and food safety3–5.

Industrial activities, mining, vehicle emissions and agricultural inputs such as fertilizers, herbicides and pesticides may introduce heavy metals into agricultural soils. Persistence and possible accumulation in soil are important risks for crop production6,7. The extent of this risk depends on the type of metal, its concentration, the soil conditions and the plant species3–5,8. Therefore, controlled screening studies using sensitive early-growth traits are useful for evaluating plant responses to metal stress9,10.

Plants respond differently to heavy metal exposure. These responses may also vary among genotypes of the same species8,11,12. Heavy metal toxicity can disrupt plant growth by affecting physiological and biochemical processes. Common symptoms include reduced seed germination, inhibited root and shoot growth, lower biomass accumulation, chlorophyll degradation, and oxidative damage5,7,13,14. Seed germination and root elongation are among the most sensitive early indicators of metal toxicity. For this reason, they are often used to evaluate stress responses during the first stages of plant development.

Onion (Allium cepa L.) is an important vegetable crop and a widely used model plant in toxicity studies. Its roots are sensitive to environmental contaminants, and early responses can be easily monitored through germination, root growth, and biomass traits. Previous studies using Allium plants have shown that metal stress can reduce germination, root development, and biomass. These effects may also be associated with cellular and cytogenetic damage9,10,15. Therefore, A. cepa provides a practical system for evaluating the phytotoxic effects of heavy metals under controlled conditions.

Although heavy metal toxicity has been studied in A. cepa, comparative information on the early responses of different onion cultivars to several metals under the same in vitro conditions remains limited. The commercially used Gence and Karbeyazı cultivars were selected to compare metal-specific toxicity and cultivar-dependent tolerance using germination dynamics, root growth, and biomass traits together. In particular, final germination percentage alone may not fully explain early stress response. Mean germination time, germination index, root growth, and biomass traits should also be evaluated together.

In this study, the early responses of two onion cultivars, Gence and Karbeyazı, to copper (Cu), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), nickel (Ni), and cobalt (Co) were comparatively evaluated under in vitro conditions. Four concentrations of each metal were tested: 0, 100, 300, and 500 µM. The study was designed to identify how metal type, dose and cultivar affect germination dynamics, root and shoot growth and biomass traits.

Results

In general, increased concentrations affected germination dynamics and early seedling growth of the two onion cultivars across the five metals. The magnitude and direction of these responses were dependent upon metal type, dose and cultivar.

Overall comparison among metals

Based on relative reduction values, Cu, Pb, and Co showed pronounced inhibitory effects on root elongation at 500 µM. Cu and Cd also reduced germination and seedling growth in a dose-dependent manner. Ni differed from the other metals because low to medium doses increased final germination. However, this increase was not accompanied by a higher germination index. Across treatments, Gence generally maintained higher final germination, whereas Karbeyazı had higher values for several root-related traits. This pattern shows that cultivar response depended on the parameter used for evaluation. Based on the tested concentrations, the first clear inhibitory responses were generally observed at 100 µM for germination index and biomass-related traits, whereas 300–500 µM represented the most sensitive range for root growth inhibition. At 500 µM, Cu, Pb, and Co showed pronounced inhibitory effects on root-related parameters based on relative reduction values, while Ni displayed a non-linear response by increasing final germination at low to medium doses but reducing germination vigor and root performance.

To provide a clearer cross-metal comparison, relative inhibition values were calculated using the control value of each metal treatment as the reference. The heat map suggested that the strongest reductions were generally associated with germination index, root length and biomass-related traits, especially at higher metal concentrations (Fig. 1).

Relative root length inhibition provided a descriptive grouping of metal effects at the highest tested concentration. Discussion

Early onion responses to heavy metal stress varied according to metal type, concentration, and cultivar. Although growth inhibition under heavy metal stress is an expected response, the magnitude of inhibition and the affected traits differed among the tested metals. The differences between final germination, germination index, root growth, and biomass traits indicate that early stress responses should be interpreted using multiple parameters rather than final germination percentage alone.

Environmental stress impacts one of the initial phases of seed germination. The heavy metal treatments generally lowered germination-related traits in the present study. The germination index gave more information than the final percentage of germination because it considers both the occurrence and the time of germination. A seed may eventually germinate, but delayed or weak germination still reflects a reduced early performance under stress. Similar reductions in germination and germination rate index have been reported in Allium cepa and other plant species under heavy metal stress10,14,16.

Root growth was one of the most sensitive traits in this study. Increasing metal doses generally reduced root length and root biomass. This response was expected, because roots are the first organs exposed to metal-containing media. The inhibition of root growth may be related to mechanisms previously reported in Allium, including reduced cell division, cellular damage, and cytogenetic abnormalities10,17. However, these mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study. When the tested concentrations were considered together, 100 µM appeared to be the first effective threshold for reductions in germination vigor, particularly in the germination index, whereas 300–500 µM represented the critical range for root growth and biomass inhibition. Among the tested metals, Pb and Co caused the most pronounced reductions in root-related traits, indicating that early root development was highly sensitive to these metals. Similar dose-dependent reductions in seedling growth under in vitro heavy metal stress have also been reported in squash and arugula18,19. The stronger inhibition of root traits may be associated with direct exposure of the root meristem to metal-containing medium, reduced cell division, impaired water and nutrient uptake, and oxidative stress. In contrast, Ni showed a non-linear response, as low to medium concentrations increased final germination but did not improve germination index or root growth. Therefore, final germination percentage alone was insufficient to define metal tolerance; germination index, root length, and biomass traits should be evaluated together. Since cellular and biochemical mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study, these findings should be interpreted mainly at the morphological and biomass level.

The response to Ni differed from the other metals. Low to medium Ni doses increased final germination percentage, especially compared with the control. However, this increase was not supported by a higher germination index. The highest Ni dose also reduced root growth and biomass. Therefore, the final germination percentage may be an inadequate reflection of Ni response. The decrease in germination index indicates lower or slower germination despite increased final germination. This pattern supports the need to evaluate germination index, root growth and biomass together. Previous studies have also suggested that the effect of Ni may depend on concentration, plant species and developmental stage20,21. This pattern might be indicative of a hormetic-type response where low to moderate levels of stress induce some early responses, but higher concentrations are detrimental to seedling vigor and growth. Low Ni levels may be beneficial in the short term for completion of germination, since Ni is required in trace quantities for some enzymatic processes. Germination index, root length or biomass traits showed no stimulation, suggesting that final germination alone might overestimate tolerance. Also, better maintenance of root related traits in Karbeyazı may be linked to cultivar specific differences in early root sensitivity or stress avoidance capacity.

Among the metals tested, Co and Pb appeared to cause the strongest reductions in root-related traits at the highest dose. Pb reduced both germination performance and seedling growth, especially root length. This agrees with studies reporting that Pb stress can inhibit root development and damage cellular processes in Allium root tissues17. Co also caused marked reductions in germination index, root length, and biomass. Similar inhibitory effects of Co on physiological traits in A. cepa have been reported previously10,22.

Cu and Cd also reduced early seedling growth, but their response patterns were not identical. Cu caused a clear decrease in germination rate, shoot length, root length, and biomass at higher doses. The temporary increase in root length at the lowest Cu dose suggests that low-level exposure may not always produce an immediate inhibitory response. This response may reflect a possible hormetic-type effect, as Cu is an essential micronutrient at low concentrations, whereas higher Cu levels can inhibit root elongation and biomass accumulation. However, this effect disappeared at higher concentrations. Cd also reduced germination and seedling growth, especially at the highest dose. These results are consistent with reports showing that Cu and Cd can impair germination, root development, and early growth in Allium and other species14,23,24.

The two cultivars showed different response patterns under heavy metal stress. Gence generally maintained higher final germination, whereas Karbeyazı had higher values for several root-related traits, including root length and root fresh or dry weight in some treatments. This difference indicates that cultivar response may change according to the trait used for evaluation. Therefore, final germination percentage alone may not be sufficient to describe cultivar performance under metal stress.

These findings suggest that early-stage heavy metal screening should include more than final germination percentage. Germination index, root growth, and biomass traits provide additional information about stress response. This is especially important when metals produce different response patterns, as observed for Ni. The combined use of these traits may help distinguish cultivar responses under controlled metal stress conditions. Previous studies have shown that heavy metal-induced growth inhibition may be accompanied by changes in protein responses, chlorophyll content, antioxidant enzyme activity, metabolite balance, and metal accumulation in plant tissues25–28. The present study was conducted under in vitro conditions and focused only on early developmental stages. Responses under soil or field conditions may differ from those observed in vitro because soil-related factors can influence metal availability and plant responses. Therefore, the results should be considered as a controlled preliminary screening rather than a direct field-based tolerance assessment. Further studies under soil or greenhouse conditions would be useful to confirm whether these early responses are maintained during later growth stages. Even so, the present findings show that onion responses to heavy metals are both metal- and cultivar-dependent. They also show that root-related traits and germination index are useful indicators for evaluating early phytotoxicity in onion.

Methods

The study was conducted at the Plant Tissue Culture Laboratory of the Faculty of Agricultural Sciences and Technology at Sivas Science and Technology University. Two commercial onion cultivars, Gence and Karbeyazı were used as plant material.

Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium was used as the basal culture medium29. The medium was supplemented with 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose as a carbon source and solidified with 8 g L⁻¹ agar. Four concentrations of each heavy metal were tested: 0, 100, 300, and 500 µM. The tested metals were copper (Cu), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), nickel (Ni), and cobalt (Co). Stock solutions were prepared in sterile distilled water and added to the medium to obtain the final concentrations.

The pH of the medium was adjusted to 5.8 before sterilization. The medium was sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C and 1.2 atm for 15 min. Heavy metal treatments were prepared separately for each metal and concentration.

Onion seeds were surface-sterilized under aseptic conditions. Seeds were immersed in 20% commercial sodium hypochlorite solution for 15 min in a laminar flow cabinet. They were then rinsed 4–5 times with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were transferred to the prepared culture media.

Cultures were incubated at 25 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of 3000 lx. Germination was recorded daily during the experiment. Final germination and seedling growth measurements were taken after 21 days of culture.

At the end of the experiment, shoot and root lengths were measured in millimetres using a digital caliper. Shoot and root fresh weights were recorded after removing surface moisture with filter paper. Samples were weighed using an analytical balance. For dry weight measurements, shoot and root samples were dried at 55 °C until they reached constant weight, usually for 48–72 h, and then weighed again32.

The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design with four replications. Each treatment included 20 Petri dishes, with five seeds per Petri dish. Each treatment consisted of four replications, with five Petri dishes per replication, and the replication mean was used as the statistical unit in the analysis.

Data were analysed using JMP statistical software. Each metal was analysed separately. For each metal, cultivar, dose, and cultivar × dose interaction were considered as fixed factors. Data were subjected to two-way analysis of variance. When the ANOVA indicated significant differences, means were separated using the least significant difference test at P < 0.05. Percentage data were converted to proportions before analysis. The assumptions of ANOVA were checked before mean comparison. Results are presented as treatment means.

For cultivar and treatment comparisons, relative response (%) was calculated as (treatment value/control value) × 100, and relative growth reduction (%) was calculated as [(control value − treatment value)/control value] × 100.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that early responses of onion to heavy metal stress were dependent on metal and cultivar under controlled in vitro conditions. In general, higher metal levels reduced germination vigour, root growth and biomass accumulation. According to the tested concentrations, 100 µM was the first inhibitory threshold mainly for germination index and biomass-related traits, whereas 300–500 µM was the critical range for root growth inhibition. Gence had generally higher final germination, but many root-related traits were better maintained in Karbeyazı. These results suggest that final germination percentage alone is not adequate to assess the early heavy metal response in onion and that the germination index, root length and biomass traits should be assessed in conjunction. Future studies including physiological, biochemical, cytogenetic, greenhouse, and soil-based evaluations would be useful to confirm these early responses and to better understand the mechanisms underlying cultivar-dependent tolerance.
