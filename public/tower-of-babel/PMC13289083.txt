Association of ischaemic stroke/TIA with subsequent oral anticoagulant adherence in patients with atrial fibrillation: a scoping review
Authors: Aashiq Ahamed Shukkoor, Ricky D Turgeon, Mary A De Vera, Mark Harrison, Nimmy Elizabeth George, Peter S Loewen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the association and potential causal relationship between ischaemic stroke/transient ischaemic attack (TIA) and oral anticoagulant (OAC) adherence using Bradford Hill’s criteria and to determine the factors influencing OAC adherence following ischaemic stroke in atrial fibrillation (AF) patients.DesignWe conducted a scoping review.Data sourcesMEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science from inception up to March 2026.ResultsThe analysis included 43 studies on OAC adherence across different phases: initiation (n=1), implementation (n=15), implementation and discontinuation (n=7) and persistence (n=20). Of these, 26 studies showed a positive association between ischaemic stroke history and OAC adherence while one showed a negative association. The evaluation of the body of evidence suggests a mixed association between ischaemic stroke and subsequent OAC adherence. While causality is not definitively established due to the observational nature of the included studies, they fulfil several of the classic Bradford Hill criteria, including temporality, consistency, biological gradient, coherence and plausibility. Factors like age, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer, heart failure, hypertension, myocardial infarction, anaemia, dementia, smoking, prior warfarin use, education level, stroke severity and recurrence were associated with decreased OAC adherence, whereas experiencing a first stroke was associated with increased adherence.ConclusionsExisting evidence shows mixed associations between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence and causality also remains inconclusive. There are multiple factors that modify this relationship. Further observational studies using causality methods are needed to definitively confirm this relationship.Keywords: Anticoagulation, Stroke, Cardiology

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a complex cardiac arrhythmia, affecting more than 33 million people globally and between 500 000 and one million individuals in Canada.1 2 AF increases the risk of ischaemic stroke, transient ischaemic attack (TIA), systemic embolism, death and making it a major driver of the global cardiovascular epidemic in the 21st century.3,5 AF-related ischaemic strokes have higher mortality rates, a greater risk of recurrence, increased severity and worse neurological outcomes compared with non-AF related ischaemic strokes.6,9

Oral anticoagulants (OACs), which include vitamin K antagonists (VKAs, typically warfarin) and direct OACs (DOACs, such as apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban and rivaroxaban) reduce the ischaemic stroke risk by approximately 66%.10,14 However, the effectiveness of OAC is influenced by adherence, which can be affected by factors such as socioeconomic status-related, condition-related, therapy-related, patient-related and healthcare system-related factors.15 Previous systematic reviews showed that lower adherence to OAC is associated with ischaemic stroke.16,18 Such ischaemic strokes can negatively impact subsequent OAC adherence, which might be due to factors like the number of ischaemic strokes experienced by a patient, neurological impairments, change in OAC class after an ischaemic stroke, financial strain and lack of family support.19 20 On the other hand, ischaemic stroke can also highlight the risks of non-adherence, reinforcing the importance of OAC therapy to prevent recurrent ischaemic stroke.

Although the importance of OAC adherence for secondary prevention is well known, there is no comprehensive synthesis of the existing evidence describing adherence specifically after an ischaemic stroke or TIA among patients with AF. Furthermore, it is unclear whether any studies have formally examined the potential causal relationship between ischaemic stroke and subsequent OAC adherence or which factors might influence this relationship. To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a scoping review to map and summarise the available literature. Our specific objectives were: (1) to synthesise the evidence on association between ischaemic stroke/TIA and OAC adherence in patients with AF, (2) to assess whether any studies have evaluated a potential causal relationship between ischaemic stroke/TIA and subsequent OAC adherence and (3) to gather the factors that significantly impact adherence in this context.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a scoping review following the framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley and the guidelines provided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for scoping reviews.21 22 The reporting of the review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA) standards.23 We intended to explore the existing evidence regarding the relationship between a history of stroke and subsequent adherence to OAC in patients with AF. The research question was framed using the JBI ‘population, concept and context’ strategy.22 The population of interest was patients with AF, the concept was ischaemic stroke or TIA, and the context was OAC adherence. The primary research question was: ‘What evidence exists about the association between ischaemic stroke/TIA and subsequent OAC adherence in patients with AF?’. The secondary research question was ‘Is there evidence suggesting a potential causal relationship between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence?’.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies involving patients aged 18 years and older with ischaemic stroke/TIA related to AF who were prescribed OAC. Studies that focused on ischaemic stroke/TIA as a primary exposure or included them as variables in their analysis were eligible for inclusion. We also included studies that used self-reported adherence scales as well as objective measures of adherence based on pharmacy dispensations, such as the proportion of days covered (PDC) or medication possession ratio (MPR). Only studies that addressed the implementation and persistence phases of OAC adherence were considered. There were no restrictions on the setting, country or study quality. The search was limited to English-language publications due to feasibility constraints and lack of resources for translation of non-English studies. No restrictions on publication date were applied; all records available from database inception to March 2026 were considered. We limited the review to observational studies because ischaemic stroke is a naturally occurring event that cannot be ethically assigned in a randomised trial. Observational designs are therefore necessary to examine their impact on medication adherence.

We excluded position papers, commentaries, opinion articles, conference proceedings, critical appraisals, pilot studies and editorial papers. We also excluded studies focusing only on OAC adherence after a haemorrhagic stroke in AF patients, because AF does not cause haemorrhagic stroke; rather, haemorrhagic stroke is usually an adverse event associated with OAC use.

Literature sources and search strategy

A literature search was conducted using the MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science databases, covering the period from their inception to March 2026. An academic librarian assisted in developing the search strategy (online supplemental 1, table A1 to table A4), which used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and relevant search terms, combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR) to refine the results. Duplicate studies were identified and removed using Covidence. Two reviewers (AAS and NEG) independently screened titles and abstracts. After the removal of duplicates, the selected studies progressed to a full-text review to determine eligibility based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies or disagreements between the two reviewers regarding study eligibility were resolved through careful discussion and re-examination of the study. A third reviewer (PSL) was available to arbitrate if consensus could not be reached, although this was not required. The reference lists of the included studies were reviewed to identify additional potential studies.

Data extraction and analyses

Data from the included articles were extracted by one reviewer (AAS) and entered into Microsoft Excel. A second reviewer (NEG) verified the accuracy of the extracted data. The extracted data included the study title, authors, country of origin, study design, publication year, study data year, type of data, population, age, sample size, type of OAC, its adherence measurement, type of outcome variable, adherence phase, whether ischaemic stroke/TIA was the primary interest of exposure and findings (online supplemental file 2).

To assess whether the observed association between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence may reflect a causal relationship, we applied the Bradford Hill’s (BH) criteria as a qualitative framework. The criteria included temporality, strength of association, consistency, biological plausibility, coherence, biological gradient, specificity, analogy and experimental evidence.24 For each criterion, we reviewed the findings of all included studies and relevant supporting literature. As we aimed to map evidence rather than grading causality per study, each criterion was evaluated across the overall body of evidence rather than for individual studies. A judgement of ‘Yes’ (criterion supported), ‘No’ (criterion not supported) or ‘Not available’ (insufficient evidence) was recorded. We did not assign numeric scores or weightings to the criteria. Application of the BH criteria was conducted by one reviewer (AAS) and verified by a second reviewer (NEG). Any discrepancies in judgement were resolved using the same consensus-based approach described for study selection.

Implementation phase

One studied the initiation and implementation phase,59 15 explored only the implementation phase,25 26 28 33 34 39 41 45 48 50 51 55 56 64 67 and seven explored the implementation and discontinuation phases.29 42 46 54 57 58 62 Of these, 17 studies used PDC or MPR to assess OAC adherence,2628 29 33 39 41 42 45 46 50 54,59 64 while four relied on self-reported adherence tools such as Adherence to Refills and Medication Scale, and Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-4 and MMAS-8).25 48 51 62 Across these studies, 13 studies showed a positive association between stroke/TIA and OAC adherence, with PDC/MPR≥80% (figure 2).3339 41 42 45 48 50 54 56,59 64 For instance, Salmasi et al observed a 6.7% increase in VKA or DOAC PDC in patients with a stroke (OR: 1.067, 95% CI 1.032 to 1.103, p<0.05).58 Similarly, Kim et al reported a 22% increase in DOAC adherence among AF patients with ischaemic stroke (OR: 1.22, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.29, p<0.001).45 Two studies analysed OAC adherence trajectories and found that AF patients who had stroke/TIA were more likely to adhere to OAC.41 57 Salmasi et al found that patients with ischaemic stroke or TIA were more likely to not decline and discontinue (OR: 0.50, 95% CI 0.40, 0.62), to not rapidly decline and partially recover (OR: 0.74, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.90), and also to not slowly decline and discontinue (OR: 0.64, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.88) OAC. In contrast, one study reported a negative association between stroke/TIA and OAC adherence. The remaining eight studies showed no association between stroke/TIA and OAC adherence.25 26 28 29 34 46 51 62 Of these, four used self-reported adherence measures (online supplemental file 1, table A6).25 34 51 62

Persistence phase

The persistence phase was examined in 20 studies.2730,32 35 Of these, 13 studies showed that stroke is associated with higher persistence of OAC (positive association), and none reported a negative association. Dhamane et al found that AF patients with an ischaemic stroke or TIA were less likely to be non-persistent to OAC compared with those without stroke or TIA (HR: 0.90, 95% CI 0.89 to 0.91, p<0.001 for stroke; HR: 0.96, 95% CI 0.95 to 0.97, p<0.001 for TIA).38 Additionally, a registry-based study demonstrated a reduced risk of non-persistence to OAC (HR: 0.63, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.81).65 Three studies examined the OAC persistence over time.31 36 61 Collings et al reported that patients with stroke had a lower risk of non-persistence to OACs (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban and dabigatran) compared with patients without stroke (HR: 0.83, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.91).36 They also found that patients with stroke were less likely to be non-persistent during the first 100 days and after 100 days, respectively, compared with patients without stroke or TIA (HR: 0.82, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.91, and HR: 0.86, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.99, respectively).36 Similarly, Spivey et al reported that patients with stroke or TIA had a lower risk of non-persistence to warfarin (>7 days and >14 days, HR: 0.70, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.73, and HR: 0.70, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.73, respectively) compared with patients without stroke or TIA.61

On the other hand, seven studies found no significant association between stroke/TIA and OAC persistence.27 30 35 37 47 53 63 Annavarupu et al reported that ischaemic stroke or TIA was not associated with non-persistence over 1 year among patients using dabigatran and warfarin (HR: 0.91, 95% CI 0.71 to 1.17, and HR: 1.11, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.42, respectively).27 Similarly, Kozieł et al showed that patients with stroke were not associated with 1 year persistence of warfarin, apixaban, dabigatran and rivaroxaban (HR: 0.96, 95% CI 0.86 to 1.08).47

Almost two-thirds of the studies examining the persistence phase showed a positive association between stroke and OAC persistence.

In sensitivity analysis limited to studies with ischaemic stroke as the primary exposure, seven studies demonstrated a positive association with OAC adherence,31 39 48 55 59 62 64 two reported no association25 56 and one did not assess or report the association.67

Temporality

This is a crucial element in assessing causality, as it requires that the cause must precede the effect in time, helping to establish a potential causal link rather than a mere association.24 All 10 studies with ischaemic stroke as primary exposure adhered to the temporality criteria. For instance, Duong et al observed an increase in OAC adherence after an index stroke event, with the proportion of patients achieving a PDC ≥80 rising from 55% to 85%.39 In contrast, Pham Nguyen et al found a decline in adherence, with the proportion of individuals meeting the PDC ≥80% decreasing from 76.2% to 67.2% after hospitalisation for ischaemic stroke or thromboembolism.55

Consistency and strength of association

The association between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence was not consistent across the studies with ischaemic stroke as the primary exposure. Looking into the overall studies, out of 43 studies, 26 reported a positive association, indicating improved adherence after ischaemic stroke, while one study found a negative association, one didn’t report association and 15 showed no association. The criteria for consistency and strength were not met in studies including ischaemic stroke as the primary exposure (n=9) or as a covariate (n=33).

Biological gradient

This refers to a dose-response relationship, where increasing exposure (stroke) corresponds with a stronger effect on the outcome (OAC adherence).24 In our context this can be quantified in two ways, stroke severity and number of stroke events. Three studies defined stroke severity using the modified Ranking Scale (mRS) and the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale.25 59 64 Shah et al found that patients with severe stroke are less likely to adhere to OAC therapy compared with patients with mild stroke (OR: 0.25; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.62).59 And, Vibo et al found that patients who experienced their first ever stroke were more likely to be adherent to OAC compared with patients with recurrent strokes (OR: 2.27, 95% CI 1.12 to 4.61).64

Biological plausibility and coherence

Ischaemic stroke can increase OAC adherence due to increased risk of recurrent stroke and fear of death, or when caregivers are involved in medication management. Vibo et al found that patients who experienced a first-ever ischaemic stroke were significantly more adherent to OAC than those with recurrent ischaemic stroke (OR: 2.27, 95% CI 1.12 to 4.61).64 Additionally, Polymeris et al reported that among ischaemic stroke patients, those who were fully adherent to OAC were more likely to have a caregiver involved in medication management (53.2% vs 18.9%, p<0.001).56

Experiment and analogy

No experimental studies were identified, as our inclusion criteria were limited to observational designs. We infer that experimental studies are unlikely in this context, given that randomising individuals to experience an ischaemic stroke is not ethically feasible. As such, the evidence synthesised here is exclusively based on observational studies.

Specificity

Regarding specificity, evidence was limited. Most of the studies did not evaluate whether ischaemic stroke was uniquely associated with changes in OAC adherence.

Positively associated factors

A study reporting unadjusted analyses found that OAC adherent patients were more likely to use antithrombotic and other cardiovascular medications, have a caregiver and experience polypharmacy compared with non-adherent patients.56 In adjusted analyses, patients who experienced their first ever stroke were more likely to be adherent to OAC compared with patients with recurrent strokes.

Negatively associated factors

On the other hand, studies using univariate analysis identified factors that were negatively associated with OAC adherence. Ischaemic stroke patients who were younger, male, had a tertiary education, smoked (either as former or current), had a history of cancer, had used VKAs previously, and had an ischaemic lesion during the index hospitalisation were less likely to adhere to OAC therapy. Additionally, a lower CHA2DS2-VASc score, fewer prior ischaemic strokes, a history of heart failure (HF) and polypharmacy were also associated with poor adherence.25 62 In adjusted analyses, factors such as dementia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cancer, chronic HF, hypertension, myocardial infarction and anaemia, alcohol use, higher CHA2DS2-VASc score, age of 65, severe stroke were more likely to be non-persistent with warfarin.31

Overall, factors influencing adherence to OAC after an ischaemic stroke in AF patients are multifaceted, with both positive and negative associations identified in various studies. Understanding these factors is crucial for improving patient adherence and optimising outcomes.

Discussion

In this scoping review, we synthesised the evidence from 43 studies evaluating the association between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence among AF patients. Overall, most studies focusing on implementation phase found that a stroke or TIA was associated with better OAC adherence in AF patients, especially when measured with objective endpoints like PDC/MPR. However, self-reported adherence measures showed mixed results, with no significant association in some studies. We also found that a history of dementia, COPD, cancer, HF, hypertension, myocardial infarction, anaemia, alcohol use, severe stroke, age, lower educational attainment were associated with lower OAC adherence in patients with ischaemic stroke, as was OAC use prior to the index stroke event. While experiencing a first ischaemic stroke was positively associated with adherence compared with those with recurrent strokes.

Although much of the existing evidence originates from high-income countries, this review aimed to synthesise findings across all available settings.25,3336 Overall, the evidence leaned towards a positive association, with 26 studies reporting that ischaemic stroke was positively associated with OAC adherence. However, 15 studies showed no association and one study reported a negative association. This inconsistency underscores the methodological heterogeneity across the literature, including differences in study designs, adherence measurement methods, populations, addressing bias and analytic approaches, all of which likely contributed to these mixed findings. When we examined the specific phases of adherence, the evidence suggests that an ischaemic stroke tends to be associated with improved OAC adherence, both during the implementation and persistence phases. In the implementation phase, this pattern was most consistently observed in studies that relied on PDC or MPR to measure OAC adherence, which likely provides a more accurate estimate of adherence than self-reported tools. Similarly, in the persistence phase, many large cohort studies found that patients with a stroke were less likely to discontinue OAC over time, pointing to a sustained impact of the index event on adherence. These findings contrast with a meta-analysis of 63 studies showing that overall medication adherence and persistence rates are often low among patients with a prior stroke.68 Taken together, our results align with the idea that a major clinical event can serve as a ‘teachable moment’,’ motivating patients to engage more actively in their treatment, although this effect may not be uniform across all medication classes or populations.

Moving one step ahead, we also examined the association between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence in AF patients using BH’s criteria to further evaluate causation. To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review using the application of BH’s criteria to examine the causation between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence in AF patients. When evaluating the included studies against BH’s criteria for causality, the evidence was mixed. Temporality was consistently satisfied, as all ten studies (ischaemic stroke as a primary exposure of interest) ensured that ischaemic stroke preceded any observed changes in OAC adherence. This criterion is essential to distinguish a possible causal relationship from simple association.24 However, consistency and strength of association were less clear. Although many studies reported improved adherence following stroke, a substantial proportion showed no association and a negative association, suggesting that the relationship may be influenced by other factors and does not fulfil the criteria of consistency. The evidence for a biological gradient was also inconsistent; while one study suggested that stroke severity diminished adherence, others did not observe a clear dose-response pattern. A plausible reason is that the relationship between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence may not always follow a simple gradient and could be influenced by other factors, such as cognitive, physical impairments or patients’ beliefs about OAC therapy.19 20 In addition, the criteria of plausibility and coherence were met, while the experiment was not applicable. Based on this assessment, the plausible hypothesis is that ischaemic stroke might have a causal effect on OAC adherence.

Many patient-related, condition-related, socioeconomic-related and therapy-related factors were identified by the included studies as significant factors that influence adherence after ischaemic stroke. These factors were reported to have a significant impact on adherence after stroke by a few studies. Due to the lack of patient self-reported outcome studies, we could not capture why their adherence may change with a stroke event. While administrative data may allow for proxy measures such as continuity of care, they generally lack detailed information on patients’ beliefs and knowledge about stroke and medications, as well as the patient–provider relationships and social support, which are commonly reported among individuals with stroke.69

Limitations

Despite the comprehensive nature of this review, there are notable limitations. First, very few studies specifically examined ischaemic stroke as the primary exposure of interest when assessing OAC adherence. In many cases, stroke was treated as a covariate within multivariable models rather than the main exposure. Interpreting the coefficients of such adjustment variables as meaningful associations can lead to misleading inferences, thereby limiting our ability to fully understand how ischaemic stroke influences subsequent changes in OAC adherence.70 Second, we did not conduct subgroup analyses by country or healthcare system, which may influence OAC adherence through factors such as medication cost and access. Relatedly, we were unable to assess differences in primary care systems or relational continuity of care across study settings, as these factors were not consistently reported. These contextual differences may play an important role in adherence following stroke and warrant further investigation. Third, the restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language bias and resulted in the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages. Fourth, some included studies were derived from overlapping cohorts, which may limit the independence of the evidence and could lead to overrepresentation of certain populations. Finally, although our findings consistently point to an association between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence, it is important to note that the studies included were all observational. It is ethically not possible to conduct an RCT with ischaemic stroke as an exposure. Therefore, in our further efforts to examine the association and potential causal relationship between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence, we should focus on cohort studies employing robust methods like Inverse Probability Treatment Weighting that can emulate the conditions of an RCT.71 This approach will help us better understand the relationship between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence. And also, to quantify the potential effect of behavioural change/post-stroke care on OAC adherence.

Conclusions

This scoping review shows that the association between ischaemic stroke/TIA and OAC adherence in patients AF varies across studies, with some reporting improved adherence post-stroke and others showing no change or declines depending on factors such as study design, population and adherence measurement. Factors such as the severity of the ischaemic stroke, prior experience with anticoagulant therapy and the presence of comorbidities may modify this relationship. Although the assessment of causality revealed inconsistent associations overall, the evidence supports key criteria, including temporality, biological plausibility and coherence, suggesting that ischaemic stroke may have a causal impact on adherence. To clarify this relationship, further studies employing robust causal inference methods are needed to establish a definitive causal link between ischaemic stroke and OAC adherence.
