Multi-Headed Transformer Architectures as Time-dependent Wasserstein Gradient Flows
Alex Massucco Leonardo Del Grande Marcello Carioni, [15pt] Christoph Brune Carola-Bibiane Schönlieb

Abstract
In recent years, transformer architectures have revolutionized the field of language processing, opening the door to previously unforeseen possibilities. However, from a theoretical point of view, the mathematical models proposed in the literature often lack direct contact with the actual architectures and depend on strong simplifying assumptions. In this paper, we reduce this gap by modelling the data flow in multi-headed transformer architectures as time-dependent gradient flows for a suitable interaction energy capturing the design of the attention mechanism. The explicit dependence on time allows us to consider different weights for each head and for each layer, without imposing constraints on the initialization method. Moreover, we prove that, under a suitable integrability assumption on the evolution of the weights, each element of the ω \omega -limit set of the gradient flows is a stationary point of the interaction energy at a limiting weight distribution. Finally, we analyse the stability of the gradient flows considering perturbations of both the initial data and the weights. Specifically, on the one hand, we study the robustness of the proposed models with respect to noisy inputs, establishing a continuous dependence of the gradient flows on the initial data and uniqueness of the flows. On the other hand, we prove the Γ \Gamma -convergence of the perturbed interaction energy to the unperturbed one, leading to the convergence of the corresponding gradient flows. We complement these theoretical results with numerical experiments that confirm the predicted energy-dissipation identity and clarify the asymptotic behavior of the dynamics in both the autonomous-like (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) and the genuinely non-autonomous (oscillating-weights) regimes.

Introduction

Since their introduction
[
56
]
, transformers architectures have rapidly emerged as a leading paradigm in machine learning, achieving state-of-art performance in many data-science tasks. These include astonishing development of Large Language Models (such as GPT
[
41
]
, Gemini
[
17
]
, Deepseek
[
36
]
), together with advances in machine translation
[
58
]
, computer vision
[
28
,
14
,
39
]
, speech and audio recognition
[
3
,
40
]
, protein and molecular modeling
[
31
]
, drug discovery
[
52
]
, weather forecasting
[
35
]
, and applications in health care
[
54
]
and finance
[
23
]
.
A central feature that has distinguished transformers from the other architectures is the presence and the choice of the self-attention mechanism
[
56
,
34
,
45
,
51
,
29
,
42
,
9
]
, which enables each input element to share and integrate information from distant parts of the data efficiently.

The remarkable success of transformer architectures raises fundamental questions about the underlying structural properties that drive their effectiveness and the role of the self-attention mechanism. A rigorous mathematical analysis of their behavior and inductive biases can yield valuable insights into how architectural modifications influence performance in real-world applications.
Such an understanding may also clarify key features of transformers, including depth heterogeneity
[
63
]
, the emergence of token clustering
[
20
]
, stability properties
[
61
]
, and physical interpretability
[
18
]
.

Motivated by these questions, a growing body of work has recently advanced the mathematical theory of transformers by interpreting their behavior as gradient flows minimizing suitable interaction energies
[
21
,
8
,
9
,
45
]
.
Inspired by the seminal analysis in
[
21
]
, the inference process of a transformer can be viewed as a particle interaction system, in which each particle represents a token evolving in time according to a dynamics governed by the transformer parameter matrices.
This observation has laid the groundwork for a mean-field theory of transformers, in which the particle interaction system is reinterpreted as a flow of time-dependent probability measures, each describing the evolving distribution of tokens.
This mean-field viewpoint has provided a rigorous framework to study the clustering behavior of transformers
[
10
,
20
,
8
,
33
]
. In particular, it has been shown that the output measure tends to concentrate around a small number of tokens, with this concentration bias depending on the structural properties of the weight matrices of the transformer.
Beyond clustering phenomena, this theoretical framework has also sparked growing interest in controllability and approximation properties of transformers
[
22
]
.
Moreover, it has been observed in
[
21
]
and rigorously analyzed in
[
8
]
that the mean-field transformer dynamics can be described by a Wasserstein gradient flow of a suitable interaction energy. This observation has made it possible to employ the powerful and well-established framework of Wasserstein gradient flows
[
2
]
to rigorously explain several empirically observed features of transformer dynamics, such as energy dissipation and clustering.

Despite resting on solid theoretical foundations, existing analysis rely on rather idealized assumptions regarding the structure of the attention matrix and the time-independence of the weight parameters. In particular, prior works have primarily focused on the single-head setting with weight matrices that remain fixed throughout the evolution.
Operating under these assumptions is mathematically convenient but misaligned with modern architectures. In practice, parameters vary across both heads and layers, so the effective attention interaction changes with depth. In a continuous-depth formulation, this leads naturally to a genuinely non-autonomous vector field. By contrast, the autonomous regime is precisely the one in which clustering and concentration results are usually derived. Moreover, recent works (see e.g.
[
53
]
) provide numerical evidence that learned time-dependent weight dynamics can invalidate the autonomous approximation and may prevent the onset of clustering. This suggests that clustering is not a universal feature of deep transformer dynamics and may even be undesirable in autoregressive next-token prediction settings
[
53
]
. In addition to that, the stability properties of transformers, as well as their interplay with layer normalization and multi-head aggregation, are more faithfully described within non-autonomous frameworks, where the weights are allowed to vary both across heads and over depth
[
61
,
32
,
59
]
.
Finally, time-dependent models are more suited to capture underlying physical structures, symmetries, energy landscapes and complex dynamics in transformer inference
[
43
,
30
]
.
These observations motivate the central goal of this paper:
to bridge the gap between the autonomous mean-field/Wasserstein gradient flow theory and the non-autonomous, multi-head architectures used in practice
. Concretely, we generalize the mean-field transformer framework by incorporating multi-head attention in a way that includes single head (and empirical head) models as a special case, and allowing parameters to evolve with depth via a time-dependent parameter distribution. We then analyze the resulting model from the perspective of non-autonomous Wasserstein gradient flows, developing a framework that accounts for the explicit time dependence of the interaction energy and incorporates its time derivative into energy balance relations describing the dynamics.
This maintains the explanatory power of the Wasserstein gradient flow perspective, while aligning the model with the depth heterogeneity that is intrinsic to modern transformers.

1.1
Mathematical framework and main results

In mathematical terms we
deviate from
[
21
]
and
[
8
]
by considering multi-head transformer dynamics with time-dependent weights that is described by the following dynamical system for the tokens
x_{i}
, here written in the case of the softmax self-attention mechanism:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}(t)=P_{x_{i}(t)}^{\perp}\left(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{e^{\langle Q(\theta_{t}^{(h)})x_{i},K(\theta_{t}^{(h)})x_{j}\rangle}}{\sum_{p=1}^{n}e^{\langle Q(\theta_{t}^{(h)})x_{i},K(\theta_{t}^{(h)})x_{p}\rangle}}V(\theta^{(h)})x_{j}\right),

(1)

where
H
is the number of heads,
Q(\theta_{t}^{(h)}),K(\theta_{t}^{(h)}),V(\theta_{t}^{(h)})
are the time-dependent and head-dependent matrices of
queries
,
keys
and
values
depending on the transformer parameter
\theta_{t}^{(h)}
for each head
h
and
P^{\perp}
is the projection on the tangent space of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(encoding the layer normalization).
We then consider the mean-field limit of the previous dynamical system both in the heads
\theta^{(h)}
and in the tokens
x_{i}
. This is achieved by representing the tokens as a time-dependent probability measures
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
and the transformer parameters
\theta^{(h)}
as time-dependent probability measures
t\mapsto\Theta_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
.
This recovers the multi-head formulation when
\Theta
is an empirical probability measure and it reduces to the autonomous setting when
\Theta
does not depend on time.
This leads to a mean-field transformer dynamics described by the following PDE (Transformer PDE):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mu_{t}}+\operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left(\mu_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{V}[\mu_{t}](x;\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right)=0

(2)

where
\mathcal{V}[\mu_{t}]
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}[\mu_{t}](x;\theta)=P_{x}^{\perp}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\frac{e^{\langle Q(\theta)x,K(\theta)y\rangle}}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}e^{\langle Q(\theta)x,K(\theta)y^{\prime}\rangle}d\mu_{t}(y^{\prime})}V(\theta)y\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(y)\right).

(3)

We choose to model
\Theta_{t}
as the solution of a stochastic differential equation
\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta}_{t}=f(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
for a suitable choice of
f
and
g
. This allows to model random initialization strategies as well as noise injection throughout the layers.
We refer to Section
2
and Section
2.2
for a precise mathematical description of our models, the assumptions we use and the generalization to other self-attention mechanisms.

With the goal of developing a Wasserstein gradient flow analysis of the previously described model, following and generalizing
[
8
]
, we consider a weighted Wasserstein distance
{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot;\Theta)
where the non-linear mobility
m
reflects the layer normalization. In the infinite-head regime, the corresponding kinetic energy is obtained by averaging, with respect to the time-dependent parameter distribution
\Theta_{t}
, the contributions of weight-dependent velocity fields. A key point of our analysis is that this weighted distance is equivalent to the classical
2
-Wasserstein distance. This allows us to use the compactness, lower-semicontinuity, and stability tools of optimal transport while working in a geometry adapted to the normalized attention dynamics.

To understand time-dependent gradient flows, that is the ultimate goal of our paper, the first step in the analysis is the characterization of absolutely continuous curves with respect to
W_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot;\Theta)
. This is already one of the points where the present time-dependent, multi-head setting differs from the single-head autonomous framework. Indeed, in the multi-head regime, the velocity field depends not only on the token variable
x
, but also on the parameter variable
\theta
. However, the evolution of the token distribution only depends on the velocity obtained after averaging over the parameter distribution
\Theta_{t}
.

The cost of producing a given averaged velocity is determined by the different mobilities associated with the parameters
\theta
. More precisely, a given averaged velocity
\bar{v}
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
admits infinitely many representations as a
\Theta_{t}
-average of
\theta
-dependent velocities; among these, the one minimizing the kinetic cost, weighted pointwise in
x
by
m_{\mu_{t}}(x,\theta)
, is selected by a constrained quadratic minimization. Solving it explicitly via Lagrange multipliers (see Lemma
3.8
) yields the effective scalar mobility at
x
:

b^{\Theta_{t}}_{\mu}(x):=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{m_{\mu_{t}}(x,\theta)}\,d\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right)^{-1},

that is the
\Theta_{t}
-weighted harmonic mean of the mobilities associated with the different heads. Its qualitative behavior reflects a typical feature of harmonic means:
b_{\mu_{t}}^{\Theta_{t}}(x)
is small, and transport at
x
correspondingly cheap, as soon as a non-negligible fraction of heads, weighted by
\Theta_{t}
, admits a low mobility
m_{\mu_{t}}(x,\theta)
at
x
. In this way,
b_{\mu_{t}}^{\Theta_{t}}
describes the local transport geometry seen by the token distribution after averaging over the head parameters. In particular, the dependence on the individual weights is not lost, but is encoded in a single scalar mobility factor that, at each point
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, determines the energetic cost of moving mass; in the single-head case
\Theta_{t}=\delta_{\bar{\theta}}
, it collapses to
b_{\mu_{t}}^{\Theta_{t}}(x)=m_{\mu_{t}}(x,\bar{\theta})
, recovering the framework of
[
8
]
. This effective mobility is the key object that allows us to describe the multi-head weighted geometry as a Wasserstein-type geometry on the token space, while still retaining the influence of the time-dependent parameter distribution.

Building on such analysis and taking inspiration from
[
16
,
44
]
we then define Wasserstein gradient flows of the time-dependent interaction energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mu,t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)

(4)

through a minimizing movement approach with the goal of relating such time-dependent gradient flow to solutions of the transformer PDE. In particular for a suitable choice of
\mathscr{K}
this recovers (
2
) in the case
Q^{T}K=V
. More precisely, we show that minimizing movements are solutions of the Transformer PDE and viceversa solutions of the Transformer PDE are curves of maximal slope for the (time-dependent) interaction energy, again defined taking into account that the interaction energy depends explicitly on time
[
16
,
44
]
(see Definition
3.3
for a precise definition).
Such characterization is again crucially based on the characterization of the metric derivative obtained by considering the local transport geometry seen through the effective mobility
b^{\Theta_{t}}_{\mu}(x)
and allows to derive a corresponding energy-dissipation identity (EDI) for solutions of the transformer PDE.

We then investigate the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the transformer PDE, exploiting the fact that they are curves of maximal slope for the time-dependent energy
\mathcal{E}(\cdot,t)
. In contrast to
[
8
,
21
]
, for a general time-dependent family
t\mapsto\Theta_{t}
one cannot expect convergence to stationary states, especially when
\Theta_{t}
exhibits oscillatory behavior. Nevertheless, if
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Theta_{t}^{\prime}|\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
,
then part of the asymptotic behavior of the autonomous setting can still be recovered. More precisely, the
\omega
-limit set is non-empty, and each of its elements is a stationary point of a limit energy associated with a convergent subsequence
(\Theta_{t_{n}})_{n}
.

We also study the stability of the transformer dynamics with respect to perturbations both of the initial token distribution and of the time-dependent family of probability measures
\Theta_{t}
representing the weights. In particular, we show that stability with respect to the initialization is governed by the quantity

M_{\theta}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\|D(\theta)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{\tau}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
.
This highlights a head- and layer-dependent normalization of the form

z\mapsto\frac{z}{t\sqrt{H}}
,
where
H
denotes the number of heads, which keeps
M_{\theta}(t)
independent of the network depth.

Finally, we complement the theoretical analysis with numerical experiments on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
that confirm the predicted dissipation and stability behavior of the dynamics. Two qualitatively different regimes are considered: an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) evolution of the weights, which satisfies the integrability assumption needed for our long-time results, and a deterministic oscillating evolution, which does not. In the OU regime, the token cloud is observed to relax to a stationary configuration consistent with Theorem
4.16
, and the strong upper gradient is shown to decay at the predicted rate
\mathcal{O}(H^{-1})
in the number of heads. Instead, in the oscillating regime, neither relaxation nor decay of the gradient is observed, illustrating the sharpness of the integrability assumption. In both cases the discrete energy identity (
147
), which is a numerical analogue of (
116
), is verified along the simulated trajectories.

1.2
Summary of main contributions and plan of the paper

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

Mean field model for multi-head time-dependent transformers.
We introduce a measure-valued parameterization
\Theta_{t}
that recovers single-head and multi-head formulations as special cases, and we allow these weights to vary with time, matching the non-autonomous structure of practical transformers. In particular, starting from a Transformer ODE model we will recover its mean field formulation, called Transformer PDE. This is carried out in Section
2
.

•

Weighted Wasserstein geometries and existence of minimizing movements.

We introduce nonlinear anisotropic mobilities induced by the attention mechanism and, generalizing
[
8
]
, we define the corresponding weighted Wasserstein distance
{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot;\Theta)
for time-dependent weights
\Theta_{t}
. We prove that
{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot;\Theta)
is equivalent to the classical
{\mathrm{W}}_{2}
, and we construct Wasserstein gradient flows solutions for the time-dependent energy
\mathcal{E}(\cdot,t)
. A central step here is the characterization of the metric derivative in terms of the effective mobility
b^{\Theta_{t}}_{\mu}(x)
introduced above, namely the
\Theta_{t}
-weighted harmonic mean of
m_{\mu}(x,\cdot)
. This is carried out in Section
3
and provides the geometric and analytical foundations used throughout the paper.

•

A gradient flow characterization for the Transformer PDE.

We show that solutions to the constructed gradient flows solve the mean-field continuity equation (
2
). Conversely, we prove that weak solutions of the associated continuity equation are curves of maximal slope for
\mathcal{E}(\cdot,t)
with respect to
\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot;\Theta)
. Furthermore, we study the long-time behavior of the system, showing that if the oscillations of
\Theta_{t}
remain suitably controlled as
t\to+\infty
, then the
\omega
-limit set is non-empty and is contained in the union of the sets of stationary points associated with the cluster points of
\Theta_{t}
.
This is carried out in Section
4
.

•

Robustness and stability for transformer inference dynamics.

We establish a Grönwall-type estimate that controls the distance between trajectories corresponding to different initial data, justifying layer-dependent normalizations, and to perturbations
f^{\varepsilon},g^{\varepsilon}
of the weight-initialization functions
f,g
. This is carried out in Section
5
.

•

Numerical validation.
We derive a discrete analogue (
147
) of the energy-dissipation identity (
116
) for a projected Euler / Euler–Maruyama scheme of the coupled token–weight system, and use it to numerically corroborate the predicted long-time behavior on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Monte Carlo simulations in the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck regime quantitatively confirm the
\mathcal{O}(H^{-1})
decay of the strong upper gradient in the number of heads
H
and the relaxation of tokens to a stationary configuration predicted by Theorem
4.16
; the oscillating-weights regime, in which the underlying integrability assumption fails, exhibits no such decay and no persistent clustering, providing numerical evidence for the necessity of the assumption. This is carried out in Section
6
.

From Transformer ODE to Transformer PDE

2.1
Multi-head transformers as time-dependent particle interacting systems

Let us fix
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
n,d\geqslant 1
, and consider a set of tokens (or particles)
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, for every
h=1,\dots,H
with
H\geqslant 1
, we set
\theta^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\tilde{\theta}=(\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(H)})\in\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
,
\alpha:=HN
.
Then, we define the
generalized multi-headed attention vector field

\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\mathcal{A}(x_{i};\tilde{\theta}):=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(\theta^{(h)})B_{ij}(\theta^{(h)}),

(5)

where, for every
h\in[1,H]
,
B_{ij}(\theta^{(h)})=B(x_{i},x_{j},\theta^{(h)})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
selects the directions along which the attention head operates and
A_{ij}(\theta^{(h)})=A(x_{i},x_{j},\theta^{(h)})\in\mathbb{R}
is the
attention matrix
given by

A_{ij}(\theta^{(h)}):=\frac{\sigma(x_{i},x_{j},\theta^{(h)})}{\mathcal{N}_{i}(\theta^{(h)})},

(6)

with
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{N}_{i}(\theta^{(h)})=\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\theta^{(h)})\in\mathbb{R}
is a normalization factor. A common example of the above mechanism is the
softmax self-attention
, in which, for every
\theta

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{i}(\theta):=\sum_{p=1}^{n}\sigma(x_{i},x_{p},\theta),\qquad\sigma(x_{i},x_{j};\theta)=e^{\langle Q(\theta)x_{i},K(\theta)x_{j}\rangle},\qquad B_{ij}(\theta)=V(\theta)x_{j},

(7)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard Euclidean scalar product, and
Q(\theta)
,
K(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
,
V(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are respectively the
queries
, the
keys
and the
values
weights matrices. Moreover, to simplify the notation, it is often defined
D(\theta):=Q^{\top}(\theta)K(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, so that (
5
) reads as

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{softmax}}(x_{i};\tilde{\theta}):=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{e^{\langle x_{i},D(\theta^{(h)})x_{j}\rangle}}{\sum_{p=1}^{n}e^{\langle x_{i},D(\theta^{(h)})x_{p}\rangle}}V(\theta^{(h)})x_{j}.

(8)

Heuristically, this mechanism creates a linear combination of the tokens weighted by the components in the rows of the matrix
A
, which we can interpret as the probability vectors encoding the (nonlinear) weighted coupling of a token with every other particle in the considered set. For a more comprehensive analysis of several other (less frequent) attention mechanisms, we refer, for instance, to
[
9
]
.

Employing (
5
) and following
[
21
]
, we can define a first simple example of a multi-headed transformer model as

x_{i}^{\ell}=x_{i}^{\ell-1}+hP_{x_{i}^{\ell-1}}^{\perp}\mathcal{A}(x^{\ell-1}_{i};\tilde{\theta}),\qquad i\in[1,n],\,\ell\in[1,L],\,h>0,

where
P_{x}^{\perp}y:=y-\langle y,x\rangle x
is the orthogonal projector on the tangent space of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. The choice to include this projector (that is also used in
[
8
,
21
]
) is justified by the strong empirical performance of architectures that normalize hidden states at each layer
[
55
]
. In particular, the recently adopted normalization RMSNorm
[
60
]
scales each token to unit norm, effectively projecting representations onto
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
after each layer.

The above update formula can be interpreted (see for instance
[
21
]
) as an
h
-step of an explicit Euler scheme, discretizing the following ODE

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x}_{i}(t)=P_{x_{i}(t)}^{\perp}\mathcal{A}(x_{i}(t);\tilde{\theta}),\qquad i\in[1,n],\\
x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{0},\end{array}\right.

(9)

where the time
t\in[0,T]
,
T>0
, embeds the continuous layer evolution. We will refer to this as the
Transformer ODE
.

The model presented is actually quite different from the architectures used in practice. In order to reduce this gap, we will allow the weight vector
\tilde{\theta}
to depend on the time. We consider the following multi-headed fully time-dependent transformer model

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x}_{i}(t)=P_{x_{i}(t)}^{\perp}\left(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(\theta^{(h)}_{t})B_{ij}(\theta^{(h)}_{t})\right),\qquad i\in[1,n]\\
\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta}_{t}=f(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{0},\,\tilde{\theta}_{0}=\theta^{0},\end{array}\right.

(10)

where
W_{t}
is a
\xi
-dimensional Brownian motion and
f:\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{\alpha\times\xi}
are given by

\displaystyle f(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)=\Big(f_{1}(\theta^{(1)}_{t},t),\,\dots,\,f_{H}(\theta^{(H)}_{t},t)\Big),\qquad g(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)=\Big(g_{1}(\theta_{t}^{(1)},t),\,\dots,\,g_{H}(\theta_{t}^{(H)},t)\Big),

(11)

satisfying that
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\,L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha};\,\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}))
,
g\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\,L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha};\,\mathbb{R}^{\alpha\times\xi}))
and also that

f_{h}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\,L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\theta};\,\mathbb{R}^{N}))
,
g_{h}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\,L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\,\mathbb{R}^{N\times\xi}))
for every
h\in[1,H]
.

We emphasize that we have modelled the weights evolution as the solution of the SDE
\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta}_{t}=f(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(\tilde{\theta}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
.
This can be mathematically modelled as an Itô process and includes a wide class of initialization techniques involving random sampling.
Finally, we underline that all the arguments we will present in the following can be generalized to any Lévy process with some additional technicalities involving its generator.

2.2
A mean-field formulation for tokens and heads

Now, we will give an equivalent mean-field interpretation of the previous transformer model. Let us consider the empirical measures
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\Theta_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
given by

\mu_{t}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}(t)}\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta_{t}:=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\delta_{\theta_{t}^{(h)}},

where
\delta_{x_{i}(t)}
is a Dirac delta concentrated at the token
x_{i}(t)
. Then, recalling (
6
), and noticing that
P^{\perp}_{x}
is linear in
y
, the first equation of (
10
) can be rewritten as

\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{V}[\mu_{t}](x_{i}(t);\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta),

(12)

where, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, we denote by
\mathcal{V}[\mu]:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\rightarrow\mathrm{T}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
the velocity field given by

\mathcal{V}[\mu](x;\theta):=P_{x}^{\perp}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}A(x,y,\theta)B(x,y;\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right).

(13)

Following
[
8
,
21
]
, for every function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times(0,+\infty))
, by the chain rule, it holds

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\partial_{t}\varphi(x,t)+\Big\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi(x,t),\dot{x}(t)\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x),

(14)

where
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
is the Riemannian gradient on the submanifold
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
induced by the metric associated with the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given by

\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi(x):=P_{x}^{\perp}(\nabla\varphi(x)),

with
\nabla
indicating the usual gradient in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, by (
14
) and (
12
), we get the following weak continuity equation

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\partial_{t}\varphi(x,t)+\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi(x,t),\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{V}\left[\mu_{t}\right](x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t=0,\,\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times(0,+\infty)).

(15)

Finally, considering the second equation in (
10
), since the SDE coefficients
f
and
g
depend only on
\tilde{\theta}_{t}
and
t
, applying Itô’s formula yields the following weak formulation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\partial_{t}\psi(\theta,t)

\displaystyle+\Big\langle\nabla_{\theta}\psi(\theta,t),f(\theta,t)\Big\rangle-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\psi(\theta,t):G(\theta,t)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}t=0,\,\forall\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\times(0,+\infty)),

(16)

where
:
indicates the Frobenius inner product,
G(\theta,t)=\frac{1}{2}g(\theta,t)g(\theta,t)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\alpha\times\alpha}
.
Therefore, for some initial distributions
\mu^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\Theta^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
, we can rewrite system (
10
) as (a weak formulation of)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle{\partial_{t}{\mu_{t}}+\operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left(\mu_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{V}[\mu_{t}](x;\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right)=0,}\\
\displaystyle{\partial_{t}{\Theta_{t}}(\theta)+\operatorname{div}_{\theta}\Big(\Theta_{t}(\theta)f(\theta,t)\Big)-\operatorname{div}_{\theta}\Big(\operatorname{div}_{\theta}(\Theta_{t}(\theta)G(\theta,t))\Big)=0,}\\
\displaystyle{\mu_{0}=\mu^{0},\,\Theta_{0}=\Theta^{0}.}\end{array}\right.

We will refer to the system (
2.2
) as the
Transformer PDE
.
We also emphasize that the solution of the equation governing the flow of
\Theta_{t}
in (
2.2
) can be obtained
a priori
since
f
and
g
are given.

3
Wasserstein Time-dependent Minimizing Movements

In this section, we employ a minimizing movement construction to derive Wasserstein (weighted) time-dependent gradient flows for the interaction energy defined as follows:

\mathcal{E}(\mu,t)=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}_{:=\mathscr{K}[\mu](x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta),

(20)

where
\Theta_{t}
is a solution of the second equation in (
2.2
).
We make the following assumptions on the kernel
\mathscr{K}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

(
K1
)

\theta\mapsto\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)
is supported in a compact set contained in
\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

(
K2
)

\mathscr{K}(\cdot,\cdot,\theta)\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
,
\mathscr{K}(x,y,\cdot)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

(
K3
)

\mathscr{K}
is symmetric in the first two entries, that is
\mathscr{K}(x,y,\cdot)=\mathscr{K}(y,x,\cdot)
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

We emphasize that the explicit dependency on time in the definition of
\mathcal{E}
is expressed through the distribution
\Theta_{t}
provided by the second equation in the Transformer PDE (
2.2
).

Following
[
21
,
8
]
the Wasserstein gradient flow will be defined with respect to a weighted Wasserstein distance defined using non-linear mobilities needed to deal with the normalized attention. For this reason, in this section, we first define the weighted metric setting specifying the assumptions required for the non-linear mobility. Then, since
\mathcal{E}
is a time-dependent functional, we adapt some definitions from
[
2
]
to work in this scenario, following closely the theory developed in
[
16
,
44
]
. Finally, we will prove the existence of minimizing movements for
\mathcal{E}
, providing the first building block for the next discussion.

3.1
Time-dependent Gradient Flows

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and let

E:X\times[0,T]\to(-\infty,+\infty],\qquad T>0,

be such that for every
x\in X
, the map
t\mapsto E(x,t)
is locally absolutely continuous.

Definition 3.1

(Metric derivative)
.

For any absolutely continuous curve
v:[0,T]\rightarrow X
,
T>0
, we define the
metric derivative
of
v
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
as

\left|\dot{v}\right|(t)=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{d(v(t+h),v(t))}{|h|}\,.

(21)

It is exactly the minimal function
g\in

L^{1}([0,T])
satisfying the inequality

d(v(s),v(r))\leqslant\int_{s}^{r}g(t)\,dt\quad\forall\,0\leqslant s\leqslant r\leqslant T\,.

(22)

Definition 3.2

(Strong upper gradient)
.

A family of functions
\mathcal{G}_{t}:X\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
with
t\in[0,T]
is a
strong upper gradient
for
E\colon X\times[0,T]\to(-\infty,+\infty]
if, for every absolutely continuous curve
v:[0,T]\rightarrow X
, the map
t\mapsto\mathcal{G}_{t}(v(t))
is Borel on
[0,T]
and it holds

\left|E(v(r),r)-E(v(s),s)-\int_{s}^{r}\partial_{t}E(v(t),t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leqslant\int_{s}^{r}\mathcal{G}_{t}(v(t))\left|\dot{v}\right|(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\forall\,0\leqslant s\leqslant r\leqslant T.

(23)

Definition 3.3

(Time-dependent curves of maximal slope)
.

A locally absolutely continuous
u\colon[0,T]\to X
is a
curve of maximal slope
with respect to a family of functions
\mathcal{G}_{t}\colon X\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
,
t\in[0,T]
, if
t\mapsto\mathcal{G}_{t}(u(t))
is Borel on
[0,T]
,
t\mapsto E(u(t),t)
is absolutely continuous and, for all
0\leqslant s\leqslant r\leqslant T
, the following energy–dissipation inequality holds

E\!\left(u(r),r\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{r}|\dot{u}|^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{r}\mathcal{G}_{t}^{2}\!\left(u(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\;\leqslant\;E\!\left(u(s),s\right)+\int_{s}^{r}\partial_{t}E\!\left(u(t),t\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(24)

When equality holds in (
24
) for every
0\leqslant s\leqslant r\leqslant T
we say that
u
satisfies the energy–dissipation identity (EDI).

3.2
Weighted Wasserstein distance

Let us consider a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
with tangent bundle
\mathrm{T}\mathcal{M}
, and let
T>0
. Then, for every couple of measures
\mu_{0}
,
\mu_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
, we can define the classical Wasserstein distance through its dynamical (or Benamou-Brenier
[
5
]
) formulation as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T}):=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|v_{t}(x)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t:(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}((0,T);\mu_{0}\rightarrow\mu_{T})\right\},

(25)

where

\mathrm{CE}((0,T);\nu\rightarrow\eta):=\{(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}(0,T):\mu_{0}=\nu,\mu_{T}=\eta\}

and
\mathrm{CE}(0,T)
is the set of distributional solutions of the continuity equation on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{CE}(0,T):=\biggl\{(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{AC}([0,T];\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}))\times\Gamma([0,T]\times\mathcal{M};\mathrm{T}\mathcal{M}):

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}_{t}=\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu_{t}v_{t}),

(26)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|v_{t}\|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty\biggr\}.

Here,
\Gamma([0,T]\times\mathcal{M},\mathrm{T}\mathcal{M})
is the set of measurable vector fields
v:[0,T]\times\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathrm{T}\mathcal{M}
such that
v_{t}(x)\in\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
for every
x
. Moreover, the continuity equation
\dot{\mu}_{t}=\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu_{t}v_{t})
is intended distributionally, that is

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathcal{M}}\partial_{t}\phi(x,t)+\langle\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\phi(x,t),\,v_{t}(x)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t=0

(27)

for all test functions
\phi\in C_{c}^{1}\big((0,T)\times\mathcal{M}\big)
.

{\mathrm{W}}_{2}
is an actual distance on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
, so we will call a Wasserstein space the couple
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}),{\mathrm{W}}_{2})
. We briefly recall in the following lemma the fundamental properties of this space (see for instance
[
57
,
47
]
for a more detailed analysis).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},d)
be a Riemannian manifold. Then, the following holds:

(i)

Lower boundedness and metricity.
for every fixed
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
the map
\mu\mapsto{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
is finite and bounded from below on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
.

(ii)

Narrow lower semicontinuity.
Suppose
\mu_{n},\nu_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
satisfy

\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu,\qquad\nu_{n}\rightharpoonup\nu\quad\text{narrowly in }\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}),

and

\sup_{n}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d(x,x_{0})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)<\infty,\qquad\sup_{n}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d(y,x_{0})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}(y)<\infty

for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then,
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
and

{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n}).

In particular, for a fixed
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
, if
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
narrowly and
\sup_{n}\int d(x,x_{0})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}<\infty
, then
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
and
\mu\mapsto{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
is narrowly lower semicontinuous at
\mu
.

(iii)

Narrow compactness of
{\mathrm{W}}_{2}
-bounded sets.
Let
A\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
be
{\mathrm{W}}_{2}
-bounded, i.e., there exists
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
and
r<\infty
such that
{\mathrm{W}}_{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})\leqslant r
for every
\mu\in A
. Then:

(a)

A
has uniformly bounded moment:

\sup_{\mu\in A}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d(x,x_{0})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty\quad\text{for any }x_{0}\in\mathcal{M};

(b)

A
is tight, hence relatively compact for the narrow topology by Prokhorov’s theorem. In particular, every sequence in
A
admits a narrowly convergent subsequence with limit in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
.

As highlighted in
[
21
]
, because of the presence of the normalization factor
\mathcal{N}
, it is not possible to describe (
10
) as the gradient flow of a functional in a classical Wasserstein space. In order to overcome this issue, following
[
8
]
, we will embed
\mathcal{N}
directly in the underlying metric considering a
non-linear anisotropic mobility

m:\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
as

m_{\mu}(x,\theta)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\mathfrak{M}(x,y,\theta)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(y),

(28)

where
\mathfrak{M}:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
.
In the following, we will assume
\mathfrak{M}
in (
28
) satisfying
(K2)
and the following assumption:

(
M
)

there exists
c,\bar{c}>0
such that
c\leqslant\mathfrak{M}(x,y,\theta)\leqslant\bar{c}
for every
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
.

Let us now call
\pi_{1}:(p,q)\mapsto p
,
\pi_{2}:(p,q)\mapsto q
the projections on the first and second component, respectively, and
\iota:p\mapsto(p,\boldsymbol{0})
the inclusion map such that
\pi_{1}\circ\iota=\mathrm{Id}
. We are now ready to define a Wasserstein distance weighted by the mobility
m
defined in (
28
).

Definition 3.5
.

For any
t\mapsto\Xi_{t}\in AC^{2}\!\big((0,T);\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})\big)
, we define the
weighted Wasserstein distance

\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi)
between
\mu_{0},\mu_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T};\Xi):=\inf\Big\{\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m_{\mu}(x,\theta)\|\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\|^{2}

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t:

(29)

\displaystyle(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}((0,T);\mu_{0}\rightarrow\mu_{T})\Big\},

(30)

where

\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}((0,T);\nu\rightarrow\eta):=\{(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T):\mu_{0}=\nu,\mu_{T}=\eta\}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T):=\biggl\{(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in

\displaystyle\mathrm{AC}(0,T;\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}))\times\Gamma([0,T]\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha},\mathrm{T}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})):

(31)

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}_{t}(x)=\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}^{x}\biggl(\mu_{t}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,d\Xi_{t}(\theta)\biggr),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathcal{M}}\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta)\biggr\|\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty\biggr\}.

Note that we consider vector fields
v_{t}\in\Gamma([0,T]\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha},\mathrm{T}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}))
that map between the product manifold
\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
and its tangent bundle. This will be useful to modify the metric depending on the weights. Due to the action of the projection
\pi_{1}
, the continuity equation above can be interpreted distributionally in
\mathcal{M}
as in (
27
). The semicolon notation emphasizes that
\Xi

enters as a
parameter
: the distance

\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi)
is a metric on

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
for each fixed choice of
\Xi
. We note that, when

\Xi=\delta_{\bar{\theta}}
is a Dirac mass, the definition reduces to
the classical weighted Wasserstein distance of

[
13
,
8
]
with mobility

m_{\mu}(x,\bar{\theta})
.

A wider analysis of weighted Wasserstein distances can be found in
[
13
]
, where such objects were first studied for
\mu_{0},\mu_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Remark 3.6
.

We first observe that for any couple
(\mu,v)\in\mathrm{CE}(0,T)
, the vector field defined as
\tilde{v}_{t}(x,\theta)=\iota(v_{t}(x))
for every
\theta
is such that
(\mu,\tilde{v})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)
. In particular, this implies that
\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)\neq\emptyset
.
Moreover, we emphasize that the choice
\mathfrak{M}\equiv 1
trivially satisfies
(K2)
,
(M)
and, in this case, if we restrict the minimization problem to vector fields of the form
\tilde{v}_{t}(x,\theta)=\iota(v_{t}(x))

we immediately recover the classical Wasserstein distance
\mathrm{W}_{2}
.

Thanks to
(K2)
and
(M)
, we can adapt the arguments presented in
[
8
]
and conclude the existence of a couple
(\mu,v)\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)
for which the infimum in (
29
) is actually attained. Moreover, it can also be proven that
\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi)
is an actual distance on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
.

To conclude, we remark that the introduction of the multi-headed
architecture does not allow for a trivial generalization of the
argument presented in
[
8
]
to prove the
equivalence of
\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi)
and

\mathrm{W}_{2}
and the consequent completeness of

(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}),\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi))
.
We then explicitly prove these properties in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathrm{W}_{2}
and
\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi)
be
as in (
25
) and (
29
), respectively.
Then, there exist two positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that

C_{1}\,\mathrm{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\mu,\nu;\Xi)\leqslant C_{2}\,\mathrm{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu),\qquad\forall\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}).

(32)

Moreover,

(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}),\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi))

is complete.

Proof.

By assumption
(M)
there exist
constants
0<c\leqslant\bar{c}<\infty
such that

c\leqslant m_{\mu}(x,\theta)\leqslant\bar{c}\qquad\forall\,\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}),\;(x,\theta)\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}.

(33)

Step 1: lower bound.

Let

(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}((0,T);\mu_{0}\to\mu_{T})
.
Define the averaged velocity

\bar{v}_{t}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta).

By the definition of
\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)
, this is well-defined
for a.e.
(x,t)
and satisfies

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\bar{v}_{t}(x)\|\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
.
Since the continuity equation in
\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)
reads

\dot{\mu}_{t}=\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}^{x}(\mu_{t}\,\bar{v}_{t})
,
we have

(\mu_{t},\bar{v}_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}((0,T);\mu_{0}\to\mu_{T})
.
By the lower bound
m_{\mu}\geqslant c
and Jensen’s inequality
(since
\Xi_{t}
is a probability measure and
\|\cdot\|^{2}
is
convex), we get:

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m_{\mu_{t}}\|\pi_{1}(v_{t})\|^{2}\,d\Xi_{t}\,d\mu_{t}

\displaystyle\geqslant c\int_{\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\|\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\|^{2}\,d\Xi_{t}(\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant c\int_{\mathcal{M}}\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta)\bigg\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=c\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\bar{v}_{t}(x)\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x).

Integrating over
[0,T]
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\!\iint_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m_{\mu_{t}}\|\pi_{1}(v_{t})\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}t\geqslant c\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\bar{v}_{t}\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}t\geqslant c\,\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T}),

where the last inequality holds because

\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}
is the infimum over all pairs in

\mathrm{CE}
and
(\mu_{t},\bar{v}_{t})
is one such pair.
Since this holds for every

(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}((0,T);\mu_{0}\to\mu_{T})
,
taking the infimum yields

\mathrm{W}_{m,2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T};\Xi)\geqslant c\,\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T})
.

Step 2: upper bound.

Let
(\mu_{t},w_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}((0,T);\mu_{0}\to\mu_{T})
be a pair
such that

\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|w_{t}(x)\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

Define
v_{t}(x,\theta):=\iota(w_{t}(x))
for every
\theta
, so
that
\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))=w_{t}(x)
is independent of
\theta
.
Since
\Xi_{t}
is a probability measure, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta)=w_{t}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta)=w_{t}(x),

so the continuity equation

\dot{\mu}_{t}=\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}^{x}(\mu_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t})\,d\Xi_{t})
reduces to

\dot{\mu}_{t}=\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}^{x}(\mu_{t}\,w_{t})
, which
holds by assumption. The integrability condition in

\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}
is similarly inherited from
\mathrm{CE}
. Therefore,

(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}((0,T);\mu_{0}\to\mu_{T})
.
For this
\theta
-constant test field, the upper bound

m_{\mu}\leqslant\bar{c}
gives

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m_{\mu_{t}}(x,\theta)\,\|\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|w_{t}(x)\|^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m_{\mu_{t}}(x,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\Xi_{t}(\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)

(34)

\displaystyle\leqslant\bar{c}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|w_{t}(x)\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x).

(35)

Integrating over
[0,T]
and taking the infimum over

(\mu_{t},w_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}((0,T);\mu_{0}\to\mu_{T})
yields

\mathrm{W}_{m,2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T};\Xi)\leqslant\bar{c}\,\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T}).

Combining Steps 1 and 2,

c\,\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T})\leqslant\mathrm{W}_{m,2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T};\Xi)\leqslant\bar{c}\,\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T}),

which gives (
32
) with

C_{1}:=\sqrt{c}
and
C_{2}:=\sqrt{\bar{c}}
. The completeness of

(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}),\mathrm{W}_{m,2}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\Xi))
follows
immediately from the completeness of

(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}),\mathrm{W}_{2})
and the equivalence
(
32
).

∎

The following lemma extends
[
8

\tau=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T\}
, and set the mesh size

|\tau|:=\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}(t_{k}-t_{k-1}).

By Lemma
3.10
, for any
u_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
we set
u_{\tau}^{0}:=u_{0}
and construct
(u_{\tau}^{k})_{k=1}^{N}
recursively by minimizing (
57
) with
\nu=u_{\tau}^{k-1}
and
t=t_{k}
. Then, the piecewise constant interpolation
u_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is well defined by

u_{\tau}(t)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{0},\qquad t=0,\\
&u_{\tau}^{k},\qquad t\in(t_{k-1},t_{k}],\quad k=1,\ldots,N.\end{aligned}\right.

(58)

As for standard minimizing movements, we can prove that for
|\tau|\rightarrow 0
the constructed sequence converges (up to subsequence) to an absolutely continuous limit curve.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
T>0
and let
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of partitions of
[0,T]
with
|\tau_{n}|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then, there exists a (non-relabeled) subsequence
(u_{\tau_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a limit curve
u\in AC^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}))
such that

u_{\tau_{n}}(t)\rightharpoonup u(t)

(59)

narrowly in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

By minimality of
u_{\tau}^{k}
with respect to (
57
), it follows that

\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{2|\tau|}+\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t_{k})\leqslant\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k-1},t_{k}).

Then, summing over
k=1,\ldots,N
, gives

\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{N},T)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{2|\tau|}\leqslant\mathcal{E}(u_{0},0)+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left[\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t_{k})\right].

(60)

Using the fundamental theorem of calculus on each
\left[t_{k},t_{k+1}\right]
, the definition of
\mathcal{E}
in (
20
), and Fubini, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigl[\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t_{k})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(t)(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(t)(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}t.

(62)

Therefore, by the triangle inequality, the weak formulation of the
\Theta_{t}
-dynamics (
2.2
), and
(K1)
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t_{k})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right)\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(x)\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

(63)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\iiint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}|\nabla_{\theta}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)|\,|f(\theta,t)|\,+

\displaystyle+\,\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)|\,|g(\theta,t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(x)\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Big(\|f\|_{L^{1}([0,T];\,L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}))}\,+\,\|g\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\,L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}))}^{2}\Big)=:C_{fg},

where
C
depends only on
\sup_{(x,y,\theta)}\left|\nabla_{\theta}\mathscr{K}\right|
and
\sup_{(x,y,\theta)}\left|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\mathscr{K}\right|
, which are finite by assumptions
(K1)
and
(K2)
.

Setting then
N=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n|\tau|\leqslant T\}
, the previous inequality implies

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\zeta_{\tau}^{2}(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\mathcal{E}(u_{0},0)-\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}(T),T)+C_{fg},

(64)

where

\zeta_{\tau}(t):=\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{2}\left(u_{\tau}(t),u_{\tau}(t-|\tau|)\right)}{|\tau|},

(65)

with the convention
u_{\tau}(t-|\tau|)=u_{0}
when
t-|\tau|\leqslant 0
. Since
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is compact and
\mathcal{E}(\cdot,T)
is bounded from below, we have

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\zeta_{\tau}^{2}(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\bar{C},

that is
\left(\zeta_{\tau}\right)
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T)
. Hence, up to a (non-relabeled) subsequence,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\tau}\rightharpoonup\zeta\quad\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(0,T)

(66)

for some
\zeta\in L^{2}(0,T)
with
\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\zeta^{2}\leqslant\bar{C}
. Because
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is compact,
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is narrowly compact. In particular,

\mathcal{U}_{T}:=\{u_{\tau}^{k}:\text{$|\tau|>0$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$ s.t. $k|\tau|\leqslant T$}\}

(67)

is narrowly relatively compact. Moreover, for any
0\leqslant s\leqslant r\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{|\tau|\rightarrow 0}{\mathrm{W}}_{2}(u_{\tau}(r),u_{\tau}(s))

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{|\tau|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{j=\left\lfloor\frac{s}{|\tau|}\right\rfloor}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{|\tau|}\right\rfloor}\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{2}(u_{\tau}^{j},u_{\tau}^{j-1})}{|\tau|}|\tau|\leqslant\limsup_{|\tau|\rightarrow 0}\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{|\tau|}\right\rfloor|\tau|}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{|\tau|}\right\rfloor|\tau|}\zeta_{\tau}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{s}^{r}\zeta(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\left(\int_{0}^{T}\zeta^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,|r-s|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{2\bar{C}}\,|r-s|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence, we can apply the refined version of Ascoli–Arzelà Theorem
[
2
, Prop. 3.3.1]
, obtaining the desired convergence.
∎

Similarly to the construction made in
the classical Wasserstein setting, we now define time-dependent minimizing

\mathrm{W}_{m,2}
by

u_{\tau}^{0}:=u_{0},\qquad u_{\tau}^{k}\in\operatorname*{argmin}_{u\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\left\{\mathcal{E}(u,t_{k})+\frac{1}{2\tau}\,\mathrm{W}_{m,2}^{2}(u,u_{\tau}^{k-1})\right\},\quad k\geqslant 1.

(68)

We denote by

u_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
the
piecewise constant interpolation of the discrete sequence

(u_{\tau}^{k})_{k}
. Relying on the equivalence of the Wasserstein distances in Proposition
3.7
, the existence of minimizing movements follows similarly to
Theorem
3.11
. In particular, there exists a subsequence
(u_{\tau_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a limit curve
u\in AC^{2}([0,T];(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\mathrm{W}_{m,2}))
such that

u_{\tau_{n}}(t)\rightharpoonup u(t)\qquad\text{narrowly in }\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\quad\text{for every }t\in[0,T].

(69)

4
Gradient flow characterization of Transformers PDE

In this section, we provide a full characterization of the flows satisfying (
10
). We begin by establishing an interchanging flow estimate, which allows us to prove that the gradient flows of
\mathcal{E}
are tangent to
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}^{x}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)
and satisfy a suitable continuity equation. We then demonstrate that specific choices of
\mathscr{K}
recover the input-output flow of a transformer architecture with unnormalized attention
\sigma(x,y,\theta)B(x,y,\theta)
(Corollary
4.8
). Furthermore, leveraging the weighted Wasserstein defined in Section
3.2
, we derive (
2.2
). Conversely, we establish that solutions to (
2.2
) satisfy the energy-dissipation identity (
24
), thereby completing the characterization. Finally, we analyze the long-time behavior of these gradient flows and validate the theory with numerical experiments.

4.1
Interchanging Flows

Let us consider a complete metric space
(X,d)
and introduce the so-called
interchanging flows estimate

[
37
]
. Heuristically, this method is based on the idea that the dissipation of a functional along the gradient flow of another functional equals the dissipation of the second functional along the gradient flow of the first one.

We start by recalling the following definitions and properties. We denote
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathscr{M}):=\{\mu\in X:\mathscr{M}(\mu)<\infty\}
.

Definition 4.1
.

[
37
, Eq. 3.15]

Let
\mathscr{M}:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper and lower semicontinuous functional and fix
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\mathscr{M}

generates a
\beta
-flow
if there exists a continuous semigroup
S_{s}^{\mathscr{M}}:\operatorname{Dom}(\mathscr{M})\rightarrow\operatorname{Dom}(\mathscr{M})
,
s\geqslant 0
, such that, for every
\mu,\nu\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathscr{M})
and every
s\geqslant 0
, the following evolution variational inequality (EVI) holds:

\underset{h\searrow 0}{\limsup}\frac{d^{2}\left(S_{s+h}^{\mathscr{M}}(\mu),\nu\right)-d^{2}\left(S_{s}^{\mathscr{M}}(\mu),\nu\right)}{2h}+\frac{\beta}{2}d^{2}\left(S_{s}^{\mathscr{M}}(\mu),\nu\right)\leqslant\mathscr{M}(\nu)-\mathscr{M}\left(S_{s}^{\mathscr{M}}(\mu)\right).

(70)

Remark 4.2
.

We refer to
S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}}
as the semigroup associated to the flow generated by
\mathscr{M}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle S_{s+h}^{\mathscr{M}}=S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}}\circ S_{s}^{\mathscr{M}},\quad\lim_{h\searrow 0}S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}}(\mu)=\mu\quad\forall\mu\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathscr{M}),s\geqslant 0.

(71)

We note that the case
\beta>0
corresponds to contractive flows with rate
\beta
, while
\beta=0
is the standard EVI formulation for gradient flows of 0-convex functionals.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathscr{M}:X\to(-\infty,+\infty]
generate a
\beta
–flow with semigroup
(S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}})_{h\geqslant 0}
as in Definition
4.1
. Let
\mathscr{N}:X\times[0,\infty)\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be proper and l.s.c. in the first variable, and assume

\operatorname{Dom}(\mathscr{N}(\cdot,t))\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\mathscr{M})
for every
t\geqslant 0
.
For a fixed
t\geqslant 0
, the
dissipation of
\mathscr{N}
along the
\beta
–flow at
\mu
is

\mathfrak{D}_{\mathscr{M}}\mathscr{N}(\mu,t):=\limsup_{h\searrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{N}(\mu,t)-\mathscr{N}\!\big(S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}}(\mu),\,t\big)}{h},

(72)

for every
\mu\in\Dom(\mathscr{N}(\cdot,t))
.

We can then prove the following result, which is simply the adapted version of
[
37
, Theorem 3.2]
in our time-dependent case for
(X,d)
being
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\mathrm{W}_{2})
. The proof follows exactly as the proof of
[
37
, Equation 3.21]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be as in (
20
). Fix
u_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and let
\left\{u_{\tau}^{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the minimizing movement sequence generated by recursively minimizing (
57
) with
\nu=u_{\tau}^{k-1}
. For each
t\in[0,T]
, let
\mathscr{M}_{t}:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
generate a
\beta
-flow with semigroup
(S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}_{t}})_{h\geqslant 0}
. Then, for every
k\geqslant 1
and
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\mathfrak{D}_{\mathscr{M}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t)+\beta\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{2|\tau|}\leqslant\frac{\mathscr{M}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k-1})-\mathscr{M}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k})}{|\tau|}.

(73)

Proof.

Fix
k
and
t
. By the minimality of the sequence
\{u_{\tau}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, for every
h>0
it holds

\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t)-\mathcal{E}(S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}_{t}}(u_{\tau}^{k}),t)\leqslant\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}_{t}}(u_{\tau}^{k}),u_{\tau}^{k-1})-\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{2|\tau|}.

(74)

Dividing by
h
, adding
\frac{\beta}{2|\tau|}d^{2}\left(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1}\right)
to both sides and taking the
\limsup_{h\searrow 0}
we get

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\mathscr{M}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t)+\beta\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{2|\tau|}\leqslant\frac{1}{|\tau|}\limsup_{h\searrow 0}\Bigg(\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}_{t}}(u_{\tau}^{k}),u_{\tau}^{k-1})-\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{2h}+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})\Bigg).

(75)

By the EVI (
70
) for
\mathscr{M}_{t}
with
\mu=u_{\tau}^{k}
and
\nu=u_{\tau}^{k-1}
, we also have

\limsup_{h\searrow 0}\left[\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}\left(S_{h}^{\mathscr{M}_{t}}(u_{\tau}^{k}),u_{\tau}^{k-1}\right)-\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{2h}+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})\right]\leqslant\mathscr{M}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k-1})-\mathscr{M}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k})

(76)

implying the thesis.
∎

4.2
Gradient flows as solutions of the continuity equation

We aim now to exploit the general theory presented in the previous paragraph in order to characterize the gradient flows obtained above as weak solutions of a suitable continuity equation. To this end we introduce a class of linear functionals along which we will estimate the dissipation of
\mathcal{E}
.

Proposition 4.5

(
[
2
, Theorems 11.2.1, 11.2.3]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{g})
be a smooth Riemannian manifold and let
\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}
denote the Riemannian gradient on
\mathcal{M}
induced by
\mathfrak{g}
. Fix
t\in(0,T)
and let
\varphi_{t}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
. Assume that

\beta\;\geqslant\;\big\|\nabla^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\varphi_{t}\big\|_{\infty},

where
\nabla^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\varphi_{t}
is the Riemannian Hessian and the norm is the operator norm on tangent spaces.
Then, for any
t\in(0,T)
, the functional
\mathscr{V}_{t}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathscr{V}_{t}(\nu):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\varphi_{t}(x)\,d\nu(x)

(77)

generates a
(-\beta)
-flow in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
and, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
, the associated semigroup is given by
S_{s}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(\mu)=(F_{s})_{\#}\mu
,
s\geqslant 0
, where
F_{s}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
solves

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}F_{s}(x)=-\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\varphi_{t}(F_{s}(x)),\\
&F_{0}(x)=x.\end{aligned}\right.

(78)

Relying on the previous proposition and on the minimizing–movement construction from the previous section, we prove that the continuity equation (
79
) admits at least one weak solution for arbitrary initial data. In particular, we first establish the result for the classical Wasserstein distance in the following proposition and then extend it in Theorem
4.7
to the weighted case with a similar argument.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be as in (
20
). Then, for every initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, there exists at least one weak solution
u\in AC^{2}([0,T];(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),{\mathrm{W}}_{2}))
to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u_{t}(x)=\operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}^{x}\left(u_{t}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u](x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right),\\
&\lim_{t\searrow 0}u_{t}(x)=u_{0}(x),\end{aligned}\right.

(79)

where
\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u](x,\theta):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}^{x}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(y)
.

Proof.

Given a test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, for every
t\in(0,T)
, let
\mathscr{V}_{t}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
be as in Proposition
4.5
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Exploiting the definition of push-forward,
(K1)
,
(K3)
, and dominated convergence, for every
s\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{E}(S_{s+h}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(\mu),t)-\mathcal{E}(S_{s}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(\mu),t)}{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\Big(\mathscr{K}(F_{s+h}(x),F_{s+h}(y),\theta)

(80)

\displaystyle-\mathscr{K}(F_{s}(x),F_{s}(y),\theta)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)

\displaystyle\underset{h\to 0}{\to}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu](F_{s}(x),\theta),\partial_{s}F_{s}(x)\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu](F_{s}(x),\theta),\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}(F_{s}(x))\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

In order to complete this proof, we aim to show that the limit function
u
obtained in Theorem
3.11
is also a weak solution of (
79
). To this end, let us consider
u_{\tau}(t)
as in (
58
) and
t\in(t_{k-1},t_{k}]
. Then, recalling Definition
4.3
and setting
s=0
in (
80
), it holds

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\mathscr{V}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}(t),t)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{h\searrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau}^{k},t)-\mathcal{E}(S_{h}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(u_{\tau}^{k}),t)}{h}=\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{\tau}^{k}],\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}^{k}.

Applying the flow–interchange estimate (Lemma
4.4
) to the pair

\big(\mathcal{E}(\cdot,t),\mathscr{V}_{t}\big)
and using (
80
) at
s=0
, we obtain,
for every
t\in(t_{k-1},t_{k}]
,

-\frac{\beta}{2}{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})+|\tau|\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{\tau}^{k}],\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}^{k}\leqslant\mathscr{V}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k-1})-\mathscr{V}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k}).

Repeating the same passages for
-\mathscr{V}_{t}
, we get

-\frac{\beta}{2}{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})-|\tau|\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{\tau}^{k}],\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}^{k}\leqslant\mathscr{V}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k})-\mathscr{V}_{t}(u_{\tau}^{k-1}),

so that, the following estimate holds

-\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{|\tau|}\leqslant\frac{1}{|\tau|}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}(u_{\tau}^{k}-u_{\tau}^{k-1})+\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{\tau}^{k}],\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}^{k}\leqslant\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{k},u_{\tau}^{k-1})}{|\tau|}.

Then, integrating in time and by a change of variables, since

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{\varphi_{t}-\varphi_{t+|\tau|}}{|\tau|}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{\tau}(t)],\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leqslant\frac{\beta|\tau|}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\zeta_{\tau}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\zeta_{\tau}(t):=\mathrm{W}_{2}\!\big(u_{\tau}(t),u_{\tau}(t-|\tau|)\big)/|\tau|
as in (

Finally, letting

3.11

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\varphi_{t}-\varphi_{t+|\tau|}}{|\tau|}\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(t)\to-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\partial_{t}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}u(t),\qquad\frac{\beta|\tau|}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\zeta_{\tau}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0,

and

\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{\tau}],\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}u_{\tau}(t)\to\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u],\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}u(t),

for almost every

, which imply the thesis.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

m_{\mu}

&\lim_{t\searrow 0}u_{t}(x)=u_{0}(x).\end{aligned}\right.

(81)

Proof.

By Proposition

,

\mathrm{W}_{m,2}

\mathrm{W}_{2}
.
Hence, up to different constants, all the estimates from

such that
u_{\tau}(t)\rightharpoonup u(t)
narrowly for
every
t\in[0,T]
. We prove that
u
satisfies the
distributional formulation of
(
81
).

We observe that the weighted distance
\mathrm{W}_{m,2}

can be obtained by considering

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
endowed with the
degenerate metric

\mathfrak{L}^{m}:=\mathfrak{g}^{m}\circ(\iota\circ\pi_{1},\iota\circ\pi_{1})
as follows.

First, we introduce the non-degenerate Riemannian metric

\mathfrak{g}^{m}
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
induced by
the inner product

\mathfrak{g}^{m}(v(x,\theta),w(x,\theta))=m(x,\theta)\,\langle v(x,\theta),w(x,\theta)\rangle,\qquad\forall\,v,w\in\mathrm{T}_{(x,\theta)}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}),

(82)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard product
Riemannian metric on

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
.
Denoting by
\nabla^{m}
the Riemannian gradient associated with

\mathfrak{g}^{m}
, by definition, for every

\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
and every

w\in\mathrm{T}_{(x,\theta)}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
,
it holds

\mathfrak{g}^{m}(\nabla^{m}\phi(x,\theta),w(x,\theta))=\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\phi(x,\theta),w(x,\theta)\rangle.

(83)

Thus, by (
82
),

\nabla^{m}\phi(x,\theta)=\frac{1}{m(x,\theta)}\,\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\phi(x,\theta).

(84)

We now pass to the degenerate metric

\mathfrak{L}^{m}
,
which maintains only the
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
-component:

\mathfrak{L}^{m}_{(x,\theta)}(v,w)=m(x,\theta)\,\langle\pi_{1}(v),\pi_{1}(w)\rangle,\qquad\forall\,v,w\in\mathrm{T}_{(x,\theta)}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}).

(85)

We define
\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi(x,\theta)
as the unique
vector in
\mathrm{T}_{(x,\theta)}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
satisfying

\pi_{2}\bigl(\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi(x,\theta)\bigr)=0

(86)

and

\mathfrak{L}^{m}\bigl(\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi(x,\theta),w\bigr)=\bigl\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\phi(x,\theta),w\bigr\rangle

for every

w\in\mathrm{T}_{(x,\theta)}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})

such that
\pi_{2}(w)=0
. The condition (
86
) is the same horizontality condition appearing in Lemma
3.8
, that is
\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi
has no component along the parameter direction
\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
.
We claim that

\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi(x,\theta)=(\iota\circ\pi_{1})(\nabla^{m}\phi(x,\theta))=\iota\biggl(\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\phi(x,\theta)}{m(x,\theta)}\biggr).

(87)

Indeed, let
w
be such that
\pi_{2}(w)=0
. By
(
85
) and (
84
), we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}^{m}\bigl((\iota\circ\pi_{1})(\nabla^{m}\phi),w\bigr)

\displaystyle=m(x,\theta)\,\bigl\langle\pi_{1}(\nabla^{m}\phi),\pi_{1}(w)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\phi,\pi_{1}(w)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\phi,w\bigr\rangle,

where in the last step we used
\pi_{2}(w)=0
.
Moreover,

\pi_{2}\bigl((\iota\circ\pi_{1})(\nabla^{m}\phi)\bigr)=0.

Therefore
(\iota\circ\pi_{1})(\nabla^{m}\phi)
satisfies the defining
properties of
\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi
, proving
(
87
).
Now, define

\mathscr{V}_{t}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})\to\mathbb{R}

by

\mathscr{V}_{t}(\mu\times\Xi):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\phi_{t}(x,\theta)\,d(\mu\times\Xi)(x,\theta).

By Proposition
4.5
,

\mathscr{V}_{t}
generates a flow on

(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha},\mathfrak{L}^{m})
whose
associated semigroup is given by

\mathscr{S}_{s}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(\mu\times\Xi):=(\mathscr{F}_{s})_{\#}(\mu\times\Xi),\qquad\forall\,s\geqslant 0,

where

\mathscr{F}_{s}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\to\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}

solves

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}\mathscr{F}_{s}(x,\theta)=-\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi_{t}(\mathscr{F}_{s}(x,\theta))=-\iota\biggl(\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}(\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta))}{m_{\mu}(\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta),\theta)}\biggr),\\
&\mathscr{F}_{0}(x,\theta)=(x,\theta).\end{aligned}\right.

(88)

Letting

\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}:=\pi_{1}\circ\mathscr{F}_{s}

and

\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(2)}:=\pi_{2}\circ\mathscr{F}_{s}
,
(
86
) implies that the
\theta
-component is
constant along the flow, hence

\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(2)}=\mathrm{Id},\qquad\forall\,s\geqslant 0.

(89)

Let us consider now the time-independent interaction energy
\mathscr{E}
of the form

\mathscr{E}(\mu\times\Xi)=\iint_{(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})\times(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})}\mathfrak{K}(x,y,\theta,\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}(\mu(x)\times\Xi(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}(\mu(y)\times\Xi(\theta^{\prime})).

(90)

Moreover, for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
, let
\ell_{\theta}
be the reproducing kernel of
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha};\Xi)
. Then, calling
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{\alpha}}
the standard dot product in
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha};\Xi)
and choosing
\mathfrak{K}(x,y,\theta,\theta^{\prime})
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathfrak{K}(x,y,\theta,\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta^{\prime}):=\left\langle\mathscr{K}(x,y,\frac{\theta+\theta^{\prime}}{2}),\ell_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime})\right\rangle_{H^{\alpha}}=\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta),\qquad\forall(x,y,\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha},

we have that
\mathscr{E}
coincides with the time-dependent interaction energy
\mathcal{E}
introduced in (
20
). In addition, for every fixed
t\in[0,T]
, let us consider
\varphi_{t}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and set
\phi_{t}(x,\theta)=\varphi_{t}(\pi_{1}(x,\theta))=\varphi_{t}(x)
and
\Xi=\Theta_{t}
.

Then, arguing as in (
80
) and recalling (
89
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathscr{E}(\mathscr{S}_{s+h}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(\mu\times\Theta))-\mathscr{E}(\mathscr{S}_{s}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(\mu\times\Theta))}{h}=\frac{1}{2}\iiint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\left\langle\mathscr{K}\left(\mathscr{F}_{s+h}^{(1)}(x,\theta),\mathscr{F}_{s+h}^{(1)}(y,\theta^{\prime}),\frac{\theta+\theta^{\prime}}{2}\right)\,\right.+

(91)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-\,\mathscr{K}\left(\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta),\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(y,\theta^{\prime}),\frac{\theta+\theta^{\prime}}{2}\right),\ell_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime})\right\rangle_{H^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)

(92)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\underset{h\to 0}{\to}\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\Big\langle\iota\Big(\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu](\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta),\theta)\Big),\partial_{s}\mathscr{F}_{s}(x,\theta)\Big\rangle_{d+\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(93)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,=-\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\left\langle\iota\Big(\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu](\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta),\theta)\Big),\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\varphi_{t}(\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta))\right\rangle_{d+\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(94)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,=-\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\left\langle\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu](\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta),\theta)}{m_{\mu}(\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta),\theta)},\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}(\mathscr{F}_{s}^{(1)}(x,\theta))\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(95)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{d+\alpha}
is the standard dot product in
\mathbb{R}^{d+\alpha}
and in the last equality we exploited (
87
).
Thus, since

\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}
depends only on
x
, we can exchange the order of integration and
write the dissipation of
\mathcal{E}
along
\mathscr{V}_{t}
as

\mathfrak{D}_{\mathscr{V}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(\mu,t):=-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}s}\bigg|_{s=0}\mathscr{E}(\mathscr{S}_{s}^{\mathscr{V}_{t}}(\mu\times\Theta_{t}))=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\biggl\langle\mathcal{V}[\mu,\Theta_{t}](x),\,\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\varphi_{t}(x)\biggr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(96)

where

\mathcal{V}[\mu,\Theta_{t}](x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu](x,\theta)}{m_{\mu}(x,\theta)}\,d\Theta_{t}(\theta).

(97)

Finally, applying the same flow–interchange argument as in Proposition
4.6
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

To conclude, from the above result, we can recover the transformer model considered in (
10
) as follows.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let us consider the same setting as in Theorem
4.7
. Then, any gradient flow
u
of
\mathcal{E}
is a solution of (
2.2
) with a general velocity field (attention mechanism) given by

\mathcal{V}[\mu](x,\theta)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}^{x}\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)}{m_{u}(x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(y).

(98)

Moreover, choosing

\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)=\mathit{\Sigma}(\mathfrak{B}(x,y,\Theta)),

(99)

with
\mathfrak{B}(x,y,\Theta):\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
of class
C^{1}
in
x
and
\Sigma\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, allows us to recover (
13
) with

A(x,y,\theta)=\frac{\mathit{\Sigma}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{B}(x,y,\theta))}{m_{u}(x,\theta)},\qquad B(x,y,\theta)=\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}^{x}\mathfrak{B}(x,y,\theta),

(100)

where
\mathit{\Sigma}^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
\mathit{\Sigma}
.

It is worth noting that choosing

\mathit{\Sigma}(s)=e^{s},\qquad\mathfrak{B}(x,y,\theta)=\langle x,D(\theta)y\rangle,\qquad m_{u}(x,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}e^{\langle x,D(\theta)y\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(y),

(101)

where

, thanks to Corollary
4.8
, we recover
the continuous version of the softmax self-attention (
8
) with

(in accordance with

[
8
]
).

4.3
Solutions to the continuity equation as curves of maximal slopes

In the previous section we proved that the generalized minimizing movements obtained minimizing
\mathcal{E}
are solutions to (
81
). We now establish the converse, showing that the solutions to (
81
) are curves of maximal slope for
\mathcal{E}
.

Theorem
4.7
produced a gradient

\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}\phi
on the product manifold

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
, which is a vector field on the full product space and
depends pointwise on both
x
and
\theta
. To analyse the dynamics on

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
alone, however, we need a notion of gradient that has been averaged
out in the
\theta
-direction against
\Theta_{t}
. This is the content of
Lemma
4.10
below: we lift the pointwise metric
\mathfrak{L}^{m}

to an
L^{2}
-inner product on
\theta
-parametrized tangent vectors at fixed
x
,
and then descend to
T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
via Riesz duality.

Lemma 4.10

(Degenerate gradient on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{L}^{m}
be the degenerate metric on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}

defined in (
85
), let
\Xi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
, and let

f\colon\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable. Then,
\mathfrak{L}^{m}

together with
\Xi
induces a vector field

\nabla^{m}f\colon\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathrm{T}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
given by

(\nabla^{m}f)(x):=b^{\Xi}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x,\theta)}{m(x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta),

(102)

where
b^{\Xi}(x):=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{m(x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta)\right)^{-1}
is the nonlinear effective mobility in (
36
) when
m=m_{\mu}
.

Proof.

Consider the weighted Bochner space

\mathbf{H}_{x}:=L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha},\Xi;\,\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\big),\qquad\langle U,W\rangle_{\mathbf{H}_{x}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m(x,\theta)\,\langle U(\theta),W(\theta)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta),

(103)

whose elements are square-integrable maps
U\colon\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\to\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
;
we refer to
[
12
, Ch. II]
for an introduction to Bochner spaces. By assumption
(M)
, the weight
m(x,\theta)
is bounded above and away from zero, hence
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{H}_{x}}
is an inner product and
\mathbf{H}_{x}
a Hilbert space. The inner product on
\mathbf{H}_{x}
is the
\Xi
-average of the
pointwise metric
\mathfrak{L}^{m}
from Theorem
4.7

fiberwise in
x
: for
U,W\in\mathbf{H}_{x}
, the inclusion

\iota\colon\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{T}_{(x,\theta)}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
,

\iota(v)=(v,0)
, satisfies
\pi_{1}\circ\iota=\mathrm{Id}
, so the
definition (
85
) of
\mathfrak{L}^{m}
yields

\mathfrak{L}^{m}_{(x,\theta)}\bigl(\iota(U(\theta)),\iota(W(\theta))\bigr)=m(x,\theta)\,\langle U(\theta),W(\theta)\rangle,

(104)

hence

\langle U,W\rangle_{\mathbf{H}_{x}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\mathfrak{L}^{m}_{(x,\theta)}\bigl(\iota(U(\theta)),\iota(W(\theta))\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta).

(105)

Now, consider the bounded linear map

\mathfrak{P}\colon\mathbf{H}_{x}\to\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\qquad\mathfrak{P}(U):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}U(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta),

which assigns to each
\theta
-parametrized vector field its
\Xi
-average.

\mathfrak{P}
is surjective, so the quotient

\mathbf{H}_{x}/\ker\mathfrak{P}
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, with the
quotient norm realized by orthogonal projection onto
(\ker\mathfrak{P})^{\perp}
.
Concretely, this gives the induced norm on
\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

\|v_{x}\|_{m}^{2}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathbf{H}_{x}\\
\mathfrak{P}(U)=v_{x}\end{subarray}}\|U\|_{\mathbf{H}_{x}}^{2},\qquad\forall v_{x}\in\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(106)

The minimization problem (
106
) can be solved explicitly. By
the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz (or, equivalently, by Lagrange multipliers),
the unique minimizer is

V_{x}^{v}(\theta)=b^{\Xi}(x)\frac{v_{x}}{m(x,\theta)},

(107)

and substituting back gives

\|v_{x}\|_{m}^{2}=b^{\Xi}(x)\|v_{x}\|^{2}.

(108)

We use the gradient
\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}f
from
Theorem
4.7
, recalling that the
computation leading to (
87
) applies verbatim to any
differentiable
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
and gives

\pi_{1}\bigl(\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}f\bigr)(x,\theta)=\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(x,\theta)}{m(x,\theta)}.

(109)

The map
v_{x}\mapsto\left\langle\pi_{1}\left(\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}f\right),V_{x}^{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{H}_{x}}
is linear on
\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, since
v_{x}\mapsto V_{x}^{v}
is linear by (
107
). Therefore, by the
Riesz representation theorem there exists a unique

(\nabla^{m}f)(x)\in\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that

\big\langle(\nabla^{m}f)(x),\,v_{x}\big\rangle=\big\langle\pi_{1}(\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}f),\,V_{x}^{v}\big\rangle_{\mathbf{H}_{x}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m(x,\theta)\,\big\langle\pi_{1}(\nabla^{\mathfrak{L}^{m}}f)(x,\theta),\,V_{x}^{v}(\theta)\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta)\quad\forall\,v_{x}\in\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(110)

Plugging (
109
) and (
107
) into the right-hand
side and simplifying, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\langle(\nabla^{m}f)(x),v_{x}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}m(x,\theta)\,\Big\langle\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x,\theta)}{m(x,\theta)},\,b^{\Xi}(x)\frac{v_{x}}{m(x,\theta)}\Big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta)

\displaystyle=b^{\Xi}(x)\Big\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x,\theta)}{m(x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\Xi(\theta),\;v_{x}\Big\rangle,

and (
102
) follows.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

The construction of Lemma
4.10
also induces, fiberwise in
x
, an inner product on
\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\langle v_{x},w_{x}\rangle_{x,m}:=b^{\Xi}(x)\langle v_{x},w_{x}\rangle,\qquad v_{x},w_{x}\in\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathbb{S}^{d-1},

(111)

namely the Euclidean inner product rescaled by the harmonic mean of
m(x,\cdot)
with respect to
\Xi
. It naturally appears here because we are minimizing a quadratic cost subject to a linear averaging constraint, and it agrees with the harmonic mean rescaling of Lemma
3.8
; in the single-head case
\Xi=\delta_{\bar{\theta}}
,
b^{\Xi}(x)=m(x,\bar{\theta})
and the construction recovers the metric structure of
[
8
]
.

Throughout the rest of the paper,
\nabla^{m}f
denotes the descended gradient on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
defined in (
102
); this should not be confused with the Riemannian gradient on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
from (
84
).

Lemma 4.12
.

Let us consider
T>0
and
\mathcal{E}
as in (
20
). Then,
\mathscr{G}_{t}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\mu\mapsto\mathscr{G}_{t}(\mu):=\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\mu}(x)\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu](x,\theta)}{m_{\mu}(x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\forall t\in[0,T]

(112)

is a strong upper gradient for
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

Let
(\mu_{\tau})_{\tau\in[0,T]}\in\mathrm{AC}([0,T];\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}))
. By Lemma
3.8
with
\Xi_{\tau}\equiv\Theta_{\tau}
, there exists a Borel field
(v_{\tau})_{\tau\in(0,T)}
such that
(\mu,v)\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)
and, for a.e.
\tau\in(0,T)
,

|\dot{\mu}|(\tau)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\mu_{\tau}}(x)\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{\tau}(x,\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{\tau}(\theta)\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\tau}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(113)

Set
\bar{v}_{\tau}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{\tau}(x,\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{\tau}(\theta)
, so
(\mu_{\tau},\bar{v}_{\tau})\in\mathrm{CE}(0,T)
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For
0\leqslant s\leqslant r\leqslant T
, decompose the increment of
\mathcal{E}
along the diagonal
(\mu_{\tau},\tau)
,

\mathcal{E}(\mu_{r},r)-\mathcal{E}(\mu_{s},s)=\int_{s}^{r}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\tau},\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{s}^{r}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\tau},t)\bigg|_{t=\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(114)

By Lemma
4.10
, applied with
\Xi=\Theta_{\tau}
and
f=\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}]
, the descended gradient
\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}]
satisfies

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\tau},t)\bigg|_{t=\tau}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\langle(\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}])(x),\bar{v}_{\tau}(x)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\tau}(x).

(115)

If we rewrite the integrand of (
115
) as

\left\langle b_{\mu_{\tau}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(x)(\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}])(x),\;b_{\mu_{\tau}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(x)\bar{v}_{\tau}(x)\right\rangle,

then Cauchy–Schwarz pointwise in
x
, followed by Cauchy–Schwarz in
L^{2}(\mu_{\tau})
, gives

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\tau},t)\bigg|_{t=\tau}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\mu_{\tau}}^{-1}(x)\|(\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}])(x)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\tau}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\mu_{\tau}}(x)\|\bar{v}_{\tau}(x)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\tau}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{G}_{\tau}(\mu_{\tau})\,|\dot{\mu}|(\tau),

where the last equality uses, on one hand, the explicit formula (
102
) of Lemma
4.10
for
\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}]
, which gives

b_{\mu_{\tau}}^{-1}(x)\|(\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}])(x)\|^{2}=b_{\mu_{\tau}}(x)\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{\tau}]}{m_{\mu_{\tau}}}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{\tau}\right\|^{2},

together with (
112
), and on the other hand (
113
). Substituting into (
114
), we obtain

\left|\mathcal{E}(\mu_{r},r)-\mathcal{E}(\mu_{s},s)-\int_{s}^{r}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\tau},\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right|\leqslant\int_{s}^{r}\mathscr{G}_{\tau}(\mu_{\tau})\,|\dot{\mu}|(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\qquad\forall\,0\leqslant s\leqslant r\leqslant T.

By Definition
3.2
, we conclude.
∎

Finally, we are ready to prove the following characterization result.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let us consider
\mu\in\mathrm{AC}(0,T;\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}))
and
v_{t}\in\mathrm{T}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha})
such that
(\mu_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)
. Then, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mu_{T},T)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{E}(\mu_{0},0)-\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{t},t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

(116)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\mu_{t}}(x)\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\mu_{t}}(x)\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{t}](x,\theta)}{m_{\mu_{t}}(x,\theta)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t\;\geqslant\;0.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
\mu

\mu
is a curve of maximal slope for
\mathcal{E}
if and only if it solves (

).

Proof.

Set
\bar{v}_{t}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)
, so

on

(117)

By Cauchy–Schwarz applied with the splitting

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\!\!\bigl\langle\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{t}],\bar{v}_{t}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle\geqslant-\Bigl(\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\!\!b_{\mu_{t}}^{-1}\|\nabla^{m}\mathscr{K}[\mu_{t}]\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\!\!b_{\mu_{t}}\|\bar{v}_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\Bigr)^{1/2}

Substituting into (

4.4
Long-time behavior

We aim to study the limiting behavior of the gradient flows constructed in this section as
t
diverges.

Let us start by recalling the following definitions.

Definition 4.14

(
[
24
]
, Def. 2.32)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be any compact Riemannian manifold. Then, for any
\nu\in\mathrm{AC}([0,\infty);\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}))
, we define its
\omega

\omega_{\nu}:=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}):\text{$\exists t_{n}\nearrow\infty$ such that $\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu_{t_{n}}=\mu$ in $\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})$}\}.

(119)

Since the strong upper gradient
\mathscr{G}_{t}
defined in Lemma

depends on time through the distribution
\Theta_{t}
, the notion of stationarity for
\omega
-limit points is tied to the asymptotic behavior of
\Theta_{t}
. To make this dependence explicit, we introduce the following.

Definition 4.15

(
\bar{\Theta}
-stationary point)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and let
\mathscr{G}_{t}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M})\to[0,\infty]
be a strong upper gradient for
\mathcal{E}(\cdot,t)
for each

is a

-stationary point
of

and a subsequence

\nu_{t_{n}}\rightharpoonup\mu\quad\text{in }\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}),\qquad\Theta_{t_{n}}\rightharpoonup\bar{\Theta}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathscr{G}_{t_{n}}(\nu_{t_{n}})\to 0.

(120)

We also denote by

\Omega(\Theta):=\bigl\{\bar{\Theta}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}):\exists\,s_{n}\nearrow\infty,\;\Theta_{s_{n}}\rightharpoonup\bar{\Theta}\bigr\}

the set of narrow cluster points of the weight trajectory
(\Theta_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. By
(K1)
,

is supported in a common compact set for all

by Prokhorov’s theorem.

We have then all the elements to prove the following result.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be as in (

), and let

be a solution of (

Then,

and for every

\bar{\Theta}\in\Omega(\Theta)
such that
\varphi\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{G}}^{\bar{\Theta}}
. In particular, it holds

\omega_{u}\;\subseteq\;\bigcup_{\bar{\Theta}\in\Omega(\Theta)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{G}}^{\bar{\Theta}}.

(122)

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is compact,
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

\omega_{u}\neq\emptyset
.

Since
u
solves (

(123)

Defining

so

is non-increasing. Moreover, by
(K1)
–
(K2)
and compactness of

so

is bounded from below.
Hence

, and
(

) gives, for every fixed

\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{t+s}|\dot{u}|^{2}(\tau)\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{t+s}\mathscr{G}_{\tau}^{2}(u_{\tau})\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau=F(t)-F(t+s)\underset{t\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

By the explicit form of the velocity field in (

and

(126)

Now fix

and let

with

narrowly. By (

Remark 4.17
.

We emphasize that the weight dynamics

enters Theorem

5
Robustness and stability

In this section we aim to investigate two important stability properties. First, we concentrate on proving the
robustness
of the considered models with respect to input perturbations, from which we will deduce also their uniqueness. Informally, considering the common application of transformers to natural language models, this property corresponds to a good response of our architecture when fed with imperfect prompts (misspells, syntax or grammar errors). Second, we tackle the more subtle problem of
perturbing the weights initialization strategy. On the one hand, the continuous
dependence on the weights distribution provides interesting insights on the regularity
of the parameter landscape. On the other hand, recalling the mean-field nature of the
considered setup, this
stability
result allows us to interpret the multi-headed
transformers used in practice as finite approximations of our continuous theory.

5.1
Robustness under input perturbation

We consider now a perturbation of the initial data
u_{0}^{\eta}\in B_{\eta}(u_{0})
,
\eta\geqslant 0
, where
B_{\eta}(u_{0})
denotes the
\mathrm{W}_{2}
-ball of radius
\eta
around
u_{0}
. The following holds.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
u_{t}^{\eta}
and
u_{t}
be gradient flows of
\mathcal{E}
with initial data

u_{0}^{\eta}
and
u_{0}
, respectively. Then, there exist two positive constants
C_{1}
,

C_{2}
such that the following estimate holds:

{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\leqslant C_{1}\,{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(u_{0}^{\eta},u_{0})\,e^{C_{2}t},\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

(127)

Proof.

Since
u_{t}^{\eta}
and
u_{t}
are gradient flows of
\mathcal{E}
, by Theorem
4.7
there exist Borel velocity fields
v_{t}^{\eta},v_{t}\in\Gamma([0,T]\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha};\mathrm{T}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}))
with
(u_{t}^{\eta},v_{t}^{\eta}),(u_{t},v_{t})\in\mathrm{CE}_{\theta}(0,T)
and

\pi_{1}(v_{t}^{\eta}(x,\theta))=\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{t}^{\eta}](x,\theta)}{m_{u_{t}^{\eta}}(x,\theta)},\qquad\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))=\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{t}](x,\theta)}{m_{u_{t}}(x,\theta)}.

Let
\Pi(a,b)
denote the set of optimal transport plans between
a,b\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. For every
\gamma\in\Pi(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})
, adapting
[
2
, Theorem 8.4.7]
to
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})=2\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\biggl\langle x-y,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\bigl(\pi_{1}(v_{t}^{\eta}(x,\theta))-\pi_{1}(v_{t}(y,\theta))\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\biggr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),

(128)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Now, we estimate
\|\pi_{1}(v_{t}^{\eta}(x,\theta))-\pi_{1}(v_{t}(y,\theta))\|
. By Theorem
4.7
and (
28
), we can write

\displaystyle\|\pi_{1}(v_{t}^{\eta}(x,\theta))-\pi_{1}(v_{t}(y,\theta))\|

(129)

\displaystyle=\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(x,z,\theta)}{m_{u_{t}^{\eta}}(x,\theta)}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}^{\eta}(z)-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(y,z,\theta)}{m_{u_{t}}(y,\theta)}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(z)\biggr\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\underbrace{\frac{1}{m_{u_{t}^{\eta}}(x,\theta)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(x,z,\theta)-\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(y,z,\theta)\|\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}^{\eta}(z)}_{=:I_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\biggl|\frac{1}{m_{u_{t}^{\eta}}(x,\theta)}-\frac{1}{m_{u_{t}}(y,\theta)}\biggr|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(y,z,\theta)\|\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(z)}_{=:I_{2}}.

For
I_{1}
, by the
C^{1,1}
-regularity of
\mathscr{K}
in
(K2)
and the
lower bound
m_{\mu}\geqslant c>0
from
(M)
, we immediately get

I_{1}\leqslant C\|x-y\|.

(130)

For
I_{2}
, by
(M)
, we have

\biggl|\frac{1}{m_{u_{t}^{\eta}}(x,\theta)}-\frac{1}{m_{u_{t}}(y,\theta)}\biggr|\leqslant\frac{|m_{u_{t}^{\eta}}(x,\theta)-m_{u_{t}}(y,\theta)|}{c^{2}}.

(131)

Recalling
m_{\mu}(x,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}M(x,z,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
and adding and subtracting
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}M(y,z,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}^{\eta}(z)
, we get

|m_{u_{t}^{\eta}}(x,\theta)-m_{u_{t}}(y,\theta)|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|M(x,z,\theta)-M(y,z,\theta)|\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}^{\eta}(z)+\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}M(y,z,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}(u_{t}^{\eta}-u_{t})(z)\biggr|.

(132)

The first term is bounded by
C\|x-y\|
by the Lipschitz regularity of
M(\cdot,z,\theta)
inherited from
(K2)
. For the second term, since

is Lipschitz with constant
C
by
(K2)
, the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality gives

\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}M(y,z,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}(u_{t}^{\eta}-u_{t})(z)\biggr|\leqslant C\,\mathrm{W}_{1}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\leqslant C\,\mathrm{W}_{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t}).

Combining with (
131
) and using
\int\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{K}(y,z,\theta)\|\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(z)\leqslant C
by

, we obtain

I_{2}\leqslant C\bigl(\|x-y\|+\mathrm{W}_{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\bigr).

(133)

Putting (

\|\pi_{1}(v_{t}^{\eta}(x,\theta))-\pi_{1}(v_{t}(y,\theta))\|\leqslant C\bigl(\|x-y\|+\mathrm{W}_{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\bigr).

(134)

Substituting (

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|x-y\|\bigl(\|x-y\|+\mathrm{W}_{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=2C\,\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})+2C\,\mathrm{W}_{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\iint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|x-y\|\,\,\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle\leqslant C\,\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t}),

where we used

since

-plan.
Finally, by Grönwall’s lemma, we obtain

{\mathrm{W}}_{2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\leqslant{\mathrm{W}}_{2}(u_{0}^{\eta},u_{0})\,e^{Ct},

and

in Proposition

∎

Remark 5.2
.

Let us fix
q\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and consider
\mathscr{K}
of the form

\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)=q(\langle x,D(\theta)y\rangle).

(135)

Then, we get the refined estimate

{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(u_{t}^{\eta},u_{t})\leqslant C_{1}{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(u_{0}^{\eta},u_{0})e^{C_{2}M_{\theta}(t)}

where

We point out that, considering an

so that the expansion is independent of the network depth. The above normalization is standard in several numerical algorithms aiming to stabilize the training of the networks of the models (see for instance
[
25
,

,

]
). Thus, Theorem

theoretically supports common regularization techniques enhancing the robustness of the models such as weight decay and

-norm control.

with initial datum

.

Proof.

The result immediately follows by letting

in (

\mu^{\varepsilon}_{t}\times\Theta_{t}^{\varepsilon}\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\rightharpoonup}\mu_{t}\times\Theta_{t}\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,T].

(139)

(i)
Recalling (

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},t)
can be written as the integral of

\mathscr{K}(x,y,\theta)
against the product measure

\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\Theta_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. The result follows from
(

) and the lower semicontinuity property for narrowly converging measures (see for instance
[

, Lemma 5.1.7]
).

(ii)
By a direct computation from (

\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},t)
depends on
f^{\varepsilon}
,
g^{\varepsilon}
, and
\Theta_{t}^{\varepsilon}
through the terms
\int\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathscr{K},f^{\varepsilon}\rangle\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}^{\varepsilon}
and
\int\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\mathscr{K}:g^{\varepsilon}\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. By
(S1)
and
(S2)
, combined with the boundedness of
\nabla_{\theta}\mathscr{K}
and

from

\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},t)\to\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{t},t)
for a.e.

(iii)
By
(K2)
and

\mathscr{G}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{2}

We are then ready to state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let us consider the same setting as in Lemma
5.4
. Let
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
be a gradient flow of
\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}
with initial datum
u_{0}
. Then, there exists
u\in AC^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}))
such that

u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{t}\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(140)

narrowly in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Moreover,
u
is a gradient flow of
\mathcal{E}
with initial datum
u_{0}
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into two steps: in the first one, we show (
140
); the second is devoted to proving that
u
is a gradient flow of
\mathcal{E}
.

Step 1.
Let us first observe that, since
u^{\varepsilon}\in AC^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}))
, we have

{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(u_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})\leqslant\int_{s}^{t}|\dot{u}^{\varepsilon}|(r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

(141)

Moreover, recalling that
u^{\varepsilon}
is a gradient flow, by Lemma
3.8
and Theorem
4.7
, it holds

|\dot{u}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{u_{r}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}(x)\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{r}^{\varepsilon}](x,\theta)}{m_{u_{r}^{\varepsilon}}(x,\theta)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{r}^{\varepsilon}(\theta)\biggr\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{r}^{\varepsilon}(x)\leqslant C,

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
, thanks to
(K1)
,
(K2)
, and
(M)
. Then, by Hölder inequality, we get

{\mathrm{W}}_{m,2}(u_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})\leqslant(t-s)^{1/2}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|\dot{u}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C|t-s|^{1/2}.

Finally, by compactness of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and Prokhorov’s theorem, the family
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0,\,t\in[0,T]}
is narrowly relatively compact in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
; the Hölder estimate gives equicontinuity in
t
. Hence (
140
) follows by the refined Ascoli–Arzelà theorem
[
2
, Prop. 3.3.1]
.

Step 2.

Since
u^{\varepsilon}
is a curve of maximal slope for

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}
, by Theorem
4.13
it
satisfies the energy-dissipation identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(u_{0},0)-\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(u_{T}^{\varepsilon},T)=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(u_{t}^{\varepsilon},t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\dot{u}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(\mathscr{G}_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{2}(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,\,\mathrm{d}t.

(142)

We take
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}
on both sides. For the left-hand side, since

u_{0}^{\varepsilon}=u_{0}
for every
\varepsilon>0
and

\Theta_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\Theta_{0}
by
(S1)
, we have

\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(u_{0},0)\to\mathcal{E}(u_{0},0)
. Moreover, by

(i)
in Lemma
5.4
, we have

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}-\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(u_{T}^{\varepsilon},T)\leqslant-\mathcal{E}(u_{T},T),

so that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bigl[\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(u_{0},0)-\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}(u_{T}^{\varepsilon},T)\bigr]\leqslant\mathcal{E}(u_{0},0)-\mathcal{E}(u_{T},T).

(143)

For the right-hand side, reasoning as in the proof of
(iii)
of Lemma
5.4
, we get

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}|\dot{u}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}(t)\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{u_{t}}(x)\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\biggr\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x),

(144)

where
\pi_{1}(v_{t}):=\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{t}]/m_{u_{t}}
.

Since the left-hand side and right-hand side of (
142
) are equal for each

\varepsilon
, combining (
143
) with (
144
),

(ii)
,
(iii)
in Lemma
5.4
and Fatou’s lemma, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{0},0)-\mathcal{E}(u_{T},T)

\displaystyle\geqslant-\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(u_{t},t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

(145)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{u_{t}}(x)\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\biggr\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{u_{t}}(x)\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{t}](x,\theta)}{m_{u_{t}}(x,\theta)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\biggr\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t.

Recalling now (
118
)
and (
117
), we get that
\mathcal{E}(u_{0},0)-\mathcal{E}(u_{T},T)
is also a lower bound for the right-hand side of the previous inequality, which implies that all the above inequalities are in fact equalities.
In particular, equality in (
145
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{T},T)-\mathcal{E}(u_{0},0)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(u_{t},t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{u_{t}}(x)\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\pi_{1}(v_{t}(x,\theta))\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\biggr\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{u_{t}}(x)\biggl\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{D}\mathscr{K}[u_{t}](x,\theta)}{m_{u_{t}}(x,\theta)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t}(\theta)\biggr\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t=0,

and the result follows from the last claim of Theorem
4.13
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Lemma
5.4
and Theorem
5.5
are a reformulation
of
[
48
, Theorem 2]
in our setting. The key observation is that
Step 2 reverses the proof of Theorem
4.13
: the EDI
for
u^{\varepsilon}
is combined with the
\Gamma
-liminf properties

(i)
–
(iii)
to recover the EDI for

, from which the gradient

-limsup property (recovery sequence) is

\Theta_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\Theta_{0}

Moreover, since the measure valued formulation recovers the multi-head model when

it is natural to approximate a general parameter distribution

chosen such that

and

then Theorem

yields convergence of the corresponding gradient flows to the gradient flow

Ornstein–Uhlenbeck weights.

Oscillating weights.

We now consider the purely deterministic case

f(D,t)=F(t)-D,\qquad g(D,t)=0,

where
F(t)
is a time-periodic symmetric matrix. Each weight matrix evolves according to
\dot{D}_{t}^{(h)}=F(t)-D_{t}^{(h)}

D_{t}^{(h)}=e^{-t}D_{0}^{(h)}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}F(s)\,\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad h=1,\dots,H.

Because
F
is periodic and non-decaying,
D_{t}^{(h)}
is attracted toward a time-periodic orbit; in particular,
\dot{D}_{t}^{(h)}=F(t)-D_{t}^{(h)}
oscillates around a strictly positive value and does not decay to zero. Since
\mathcal{E}(u_{t},t)
inherits this periodicity through
D_{t}^{(h)}
, the integrand
\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(u_{t},t)
also oscillates without a definite sign, and its running integral grows without bound:

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{E}(u_{\tau},\tau)\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau=+\infty.

Assumption (
121
) is therefore violated, Theorem
4.16

\omega_{u}\subseteq\bigcup_{\bar{\Theta}\in\Omega(\Theta)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{G}}^{\bar{\Theta}}
may fail. Correspondingly, the metric slope
\mathscr{G}_{t}^{2}
receives a persistent non-zero contribution from the mean-velocity term: since
\dot{D}_{t}^{(h)}
does not vanish,
\bar{V}_{i}(t)\not\to 0
and bound (
150

7
Conclusions and Future Directions

In this work we generalized previous mean-field analyses of fixed-weight transformers to multi-headed, depth-dependent architectures, narrowing the gap with the architectures used in practice. Indeed, by modelling layer-wise parameter variation through a time-dependent probability measure
\Theta_{t}
, we formulated inference dynamics as a genuinely time-dependent Wasserstein gradient flow. A central theoretical contribution is the derivation of the effective scalar mobility
b^{\Theta_{t}}_{\mu}(x)
, the
\Theta_{t}
-weighted harmonic mean of the head-dependent mobilities, which compresses the multi-head transport geometry into a single scalar factor on the token space. This framework rigorously introduces the Transformer PDE and extends the energetic perspective of attention mechanisms to dynamic regimes in which the interaction energy
\mathcal{E}(\mu,t)
carries an explicit time dependence.

We also established that the structural evolution of the weights intrinsically governs the macroscopic behaviour of the token distribution. When the parameter dynamics satisfy a suitable integrability condition, as shown by Theorem
4.16
and illustrated by the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck simulations of Section
6
, the token distribution relaxes to a stationary configuration and the strong upper gradient decays at rate
\mathcal{O}(H^{-1})
. Conversely, sustained oscillations in
\Theta_{t}
obstruct this relaxation, providing numerical evidence that token clustering is not an inescapable artefact of deep attention but a phenomenon controlled by the parameter dynamics. Furthermore, we investigated the stability of our models with respect to perturbations of the initial data and of the weight distribution, obtaining a Grönwall-type estimate for the former and a
\Gamma
-convergence result for the latter; together these provide a mathematical justification for common architectural initialization techniques such as weight decay and
L^{2}
-norm control.

7.1
Future Research Directions

The rigorous connection between the Transformer PDE and non-autonomous gradient flows opens several paths for characterising the geometry of stationary points. A natural next step is a broader classification of the
\omega
-limit sets, and the investigation of how head multiplicity shapes the underlying energy landscape. In particular, one may ask whether specific spectral properties of the
\Theta_{t}
-averaged attention matrices dictate the number and spatial distribution of limiting clusters on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. A range of quantitative results on clustering, synchronization, and metastability in the autonomous regime have recently been obtained (see for instance
[
1
,
7
,
10
,
11
]
); extending such estimates to the time-dependent, multi-head setting developed here is a natural next step, and would in turn shed light on the emergence of distinct token sub-populations and on sharp bounds on the expressivity of a given attention configuration.

Additionally, the time-dependent interaction energy
\mathcal{E}(\mu,t)
introduced here provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for designing novel neural architectures, rather than merely analyzing existing ones. Within our framework both the parameter measure
\Theta_{t}
and the energy
\mathcal{E}
itself can be treated as design objects: one may formulate optimal control problems over
\Theta_{t}
, or modify
\mathcal{E}
by adding regularizing terms. A natural example is the addition of an entropic penalty
\varepsilon\int\mu\log\mu
, whose Wasserstein gradient flow yields a McKean–Vlasov equation on the sphere, that is, an interaction dynamics with built-in diffusion of the tokens. The stationary states, bifurcation structure, and clustering dynamics of such noisy transformer models, and more generally of the weakly interacting diffusions underlying them, have recently attracted considerable attention (see e.g.
[
4
,
15
,
19
,
50
]
and references therein); the time-dependent formulation developed here provides a natural setting in which to design and analyze architectures of this kind that retain the dissipation and robustness properties established in this paper. The variational viewpoint further suggests building energy-stable transformer layers via structure-preserving discretizations of the resulting gradient flow, such as JKO-type minimizing-movement schemes that perform a time-discrete minimization of the chosen energy directly. Such physically motivated architectures would inherently resist token collapse, promote feature separation, and achieve robustness by design, offering a principled alternative to the regularization strategies currently prevalent in the literature.
