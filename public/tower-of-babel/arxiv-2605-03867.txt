Geometric Perspective on Concentration Phenomena in Frame Theory
Samuel Ballas, Ferhat Karabatman, Tom Needham

Abstract
Parseval and equal-norm frames play a fundamental role in frame theory and signal processing. In this work, we prove non-asymptotic concentration bounds showing that random equal-norm frames are nearly Parseval with high probability, and that random Parseval frames are nearly equal-norm with high probability. Our proofs are geometric in nature, and rely on general measure concentration principles in Riemannian manifolds. As an application, we obtain a novel probabilistic upper bound for the Paulsen problem.



1
Introduction

Frames provide redundant systems of vectors for representing signals in Hilbert spaces.
Unlike bases, frames allow stable reconstruction while permitting redundancy, a feature
that makes them particularly useful in signal processing, data transmission, and related
areas
[
10
,
9
,
6
,
17
]
. In finite dimensions, a frame is simply a spanning family of vectors

(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and its redundancy is reflected in the fact that
one may have
n>d
. This redundancy is not merely a formal feature: it plays a central
role in the stability of reconstruction, especially when the transmitted coefficients are
affected by noise or erasures.

A central class of frames is given by
Parseval frames
.
For such frames, the
frame operator
, or the map
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

v\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle v,x_{i}\rangle x_{i},

is the identity map. The interpretation is that, in this case, the signal
v
can be reconstructed from its sequence of linear measurements
(\langle v,x_{i}\rangle)_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in a particularly
simple and stable manner. Equal-norm tight frames, where
\|x_{i}\|=\|x_{j}\|
for all
i,j
, are also especially important because they
distribute the importance of the linear measurements uniformly. This balance is closely connected
to robustness under coefficient losses and to optimality properties in reconstruction
problems. Several foundational results illustrate the importance of these structures. Goyal et
al.
[
13
]
showed that, under an additive noise model, the mean squared
error is minimized precisely by tight frames. Subsequent works further emphasized the
role of tight and equal-norm tight frames in reconstruction problems involving noise and
erasures. For instance, Casazza and Kovačević
[
8
]
and
Holmes and Paulsen
[
16
]
showed that equal-norm tight
frames enjoy strong optimality properties in erasure settings. Taken together, these
results indicate that both tightness and equal distribution of vector norms are essential
features for stable and robust signal representation.

In light of the above, frames which are both Parseval and equal-norm are desirable in applications. Unfortunately, the space of equal-norm Parseval frames forms a complicated subvariety of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
which is hard to sample directly
[
1
,
11
,
5
,
25
,
23
]
. On the other hand, spaces of frames which are only constrained to be Parseval or to be equal-norm (i.e., only satisfying one of the desired conditions) are easy to sample, as they can be identified with Stiefel manifolds or products of spheres, respectively. The goal of this paper is to address the sampling problem from a measure concentration perspective, addressing the natural question: if one samples vectors satisfying one of the desired conditions (e.g., the equal-norm condition), how likely is it that the resulting system is close to satisfying the other condition (e.g., the Parseval condition)? In other words, to what extent are
the frame-theoretic structures that are optimal for reconstruction typical within natural
random ensembles? Explicit quantitative answers to this question appear to be limited,
especially outside the setting of independent subgaussian models and asymptotic results; see, for example,

[
14
,
2
,
21
]
.

In this work, we address the questions above from the perspective of measure concentration on Riemannian manifolds (see
[
20
,
30
]
). Our main contributions are as follows:

Concentration on Parseval Frames.
In Section
2
, we consider random equal-norm frames, considered as i.i.d. samples from the uniform measure on a sphere. We show in Theorem
2.1
that random samples are close to being Parseval with high probability, when the number of vectors in the frame is large. The proof relies on classical matrix concentration inequalities and measure concentration properties of the sphere. Similar techniques allow us to establish concentration on Parseval frames for frames whose vector norms are not fully prescribed, but only bounded above (Theorem
2.5
). The tightness of these concentration bounds is explored via numerical experiments, included in the appendix.

Concentration on equal-norm Frames.
We take the dual perspective in Section
3
, and consider concentration properties of random Parseval frames, considered as samples from a symmetric measure on the Stiefel manifold. We show in Theorem
3.1
that these frames are nearly equal-norm with high probability, assuming that the number of frame vectors is sufficiently large. The proof is more technical than the sphere case, and requires some estimates for geodesic distance and Ricci curvature of the Stiefel manifold. We also include numerical experiments illustrating this concentration result in the appendix.

Application to the Paulsen Problem.
We give an application of our sphere concentration result to the Paulsen problem
[
3
,
7
,
4
,
18
,
15
]
. Roughly, the Paulsen problem seeks a uniform bound on the (squared) distance from a frame which is
\varepsilon
-nearly
equal-norm and Parseval (in a certain, precise sense) to the subvariety of
exactly
equal-norm Parseval frames. In Theorem
4.3
, we derive an
O(\varepsilon^{2}d)
bound which holds with high probability for random nearly equal-norm and Parseval frames. Our bound is tighter than any of the deterministic bounds in the existing literature. In fact, we show in Proposition
4.9
that any deterministic bound can be at best on the order of
O(\varepsilon d)
; this claim has been made in the literature, but to our knowledge no formal proof has appeared previously. This shows, in particular, that an optimal determinstic bound and our probabilistic bound are of different orders. Our proof is based on an extension of an example appearing in
[
27
]
.

2
Concentration of Parseval Frames

In this section, we establish concentration results for random vectors drawn from appropriately scaled spheres and balls. Our main results show that random frames drawn from these spaces are close to being Parseval with high probability, with respect to both the operator and Frobenius norms. We begin with a review of basic terminology and notation from frame theory.

2.1
Frame Theoretic Concepts and Notation

Throughout the paper, we consider
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as an inner product space, endowed with its standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm
\|\cdot\|
. The adjoint (i.e., transpose) of a linear map
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to the standard inner products will be denoted by
T^{\ast}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

A finite sequence
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of vectors
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a
frame
if it forms a spanning set for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that, for the sake of convenience, we consider a frame to be an ordered tuple of vectors (rather than an unordered set, which is an alternative convention).

For any finite sequence of vectors
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, we have the associated
analysis operator

T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

T(x)=(\langle x,x_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle x,x_{n}\rangle)
, and its adjoint, the
synthesis operator

T^{\ast}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T^{\ast}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}
. The
frame operator
is
S=T^{*}T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we write as a sum of outer products
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\ast}
. In the context of signal processing, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is considered as a signal, application of the analysis operator amounts to taking linear measurements encoded by the sequence, and application of the synthesis operator amounts to reconstructing a signal from these measurements. A simple linear algebra exercise shows that a sequence is a frame if and only if its frame operator
S
is invertible.

For frames in finite-dimensional spaces, there are certain natural classes of frames which are of particular interest. First, it is especially convenient when the frame operator is not just invertible, but is actually the identity map; in this case, the frame is said to be
Parseval
. Second, one may wish for the vectors in the frame to be
equal-norm
. If a frame
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is both Parseval and equal-norm, a simple calculation shows that the norm must be given by
\|x_{i}\|^{2}=\frac{d}{n}
.

In short, the results of this paper address the following question: if one of the desired properties above is enforced (e.g., the equal-norm condition), how likely is it that a random frame is close to satisfying the other property (e.g., Parseval)?

2.2
Concentration for Spheres

We begin by considering concentration properties for equal-norm frames. We use the notation
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{R}
for the
(d-1)
-dimensional sphere of radius
R>0
, considered as a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We endow the sphere with the Riemannian metric induced from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and consider it as a measure space with normalized Riemannian volume; i.e., with the uniform probability measure.

We consider concentration properties framed in terms of operator norms. For a linear operator on Euclidean space
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we use
\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}
to denote its operator norm and
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}
to denote its Frobenius norm. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 2.1

(Concentration over the Sphere)
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a set of independent random vectors, drawn from the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\sqrt{d/n}}
, and let
S
be the associated frame operator. Then, for every
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\;\leq\;2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{\,2d-2+\tfrac{2\varepsilon(d-1)}{3}\,}\right).

and

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2d^{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(d-1)n\varepsilon^{2}}{8d^{4}}\right).

In the language of frame theory, this result has the following interpretation: for fixed
d
, a random equal-norm frame consisting of
n
vectors is arbitrarily close to being Parseval with overwhelmingly high probability, provided
n
is sufficiently large. This is concretely quantified in Corollary
2.8
below.

We now present the main tools that will be used in the proof of the theorem. The operator norm result uses a classical concentration result for random matrices, which will be the key ingredient in our analysis:

Theorem 2.2

(Matrix Bernstein Inequality
[
29
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
Y
be a random self-adjoint
d\times d
matrix such that
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
and
\|Y\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq A
almost surely, for some
A>0
. Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be i.i.d. copies of
Y
. Then, for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2d\cdot\exp\!\left(\frac{-{\varepsilon}^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+A\varepsilon/3}\right),\qquad\text{where}\quad\sigma^{2}\coloneqq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

To establish concentration estimates in the Frobenius norm, we need to lift the concentration of Lipschitz functions from a single sphere to a product of spheres. For this purpose, we use the logarithmic Sobolev inequality on the sphere together with its tensorization on product spaces, and the Herbst argument, which yields concentration results for Lipschitz functions under the product measure.

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider the product space
X:=\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{R}\bigr)^{n}
, equipped with the product probability measure
\mu:=\sigma_{R}^{\otimes n}
, where
\sigma_{R}
denotes the uniform probability measure on

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{R}
. The associated geodesic product distance is

d_{X}(x,y):=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}}(x_{i},y_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
are points in
X
and
d_{\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}}
denotes the geodesic distance on

\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}
. Then every
L
-Lipschitz function

F:X\to\mathbb{R}
, with respect to
d_{X}
, satisfies, for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|F-\mathbb{E}[F]|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)t^{2}}{2R^{2}L^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Let
\sigma
denote the uniform probability measure on the unit sphere

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. By the logarithmic Sobolev inequality on the sphere

[
20
, p. 94]
, for every smooth function

g:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathrm{Ent}_{\sigma}(g^{2})\leq\frac{2}{d-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla g\|^{2}\,d\sigma.

Here, for a nonnegative integrable function
h
,

\mathrm{Ent}_{\sigma}(h):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}h\log h\,d\sigma-\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}h\,d\sigma\right)\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}h\,d\sigma\right).

We first transfer this inequality to the scaled sphere

\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}
. Let
f:\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth and
define
g(u):=f(Ru)
where
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. If
T:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}
is given by
T(u)=Ru
, then

T_{\#}\sigma=\sigma_{R}
. Hence
\mathrm{Ent}_{\sigma_{R}}(f^{2})=\mathrm{Ent}_{\sigma}(g^{2})
. Moreover, under the scaling
u\mapsto Ru
, the gradients satisfy
\|\nabla g\|(u)=R\,\|\nabla f\|(Ru)
. Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla g\|^{2}\,d\sigma=R^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}}\|\nabla f\|^{2}\,d\sigma_{R}.

It follows that

\mathrm{Ent}_{\sigma_{R}}(f^{2})\leq\frac{2R^{2}}{d-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}}\|\nabla f\|^{2}\,d\sigma_{R}.

Now consider the product space
X=(\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1})^{n}
with product measure
\mu=\sigma_{R}^{\otimes n}
. The factors of the product metric are orthogonal, thus, for a tangent vector
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in T_{x}X
, we have

\|v\|_{T_{x}X}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|v_{i}\|_{T_{x_{i}}\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}}^{2}.

Consequently, for a smooth function
G:X\to\mathbb{R}
,

\|\nabla G\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{i}G\|^{2},

where
\nabla_{i}G
denotes the gradient in the
i
-th coordinate.

By the tensorization property of the logarithmic Sobolev inequality

[
20
, Corollary 5.7]
, the product measure
\mu

satisfies

\mathrm{Ent}_{\mu}(G^{2})\leq\frac{2R^{2}}{d-1}\int_{X}\|\nabla G\|^{2}\,d\mu

for every smooth function
G:X\to\mathbb{R}
.

The geodesic distance induced by this product Riemannian structure is precisely

d_{X}(x,y)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\mathbb{S}_{R}^{d-1}}(x_{i},y_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

This is the natural distance associated with the identity
\|\nabla G\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{i}G\|^{2}

appearing in the tensorized logarithmic Sobolev inequality.

By the Herbst argument, equivalently by Theorem 5.3 in

[
20
]
, every
1
-Lipschitz function

G:X\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to this geodesic product distance satisfies

\mu\left(G-\mathbb{E}[G]\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)t^{2}}{2R^{2}}\right),\qquad t\geq 0.

Now let
F:X\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{X}
, and define
G:=\frac{F}{L}
. Then
G
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{X}
. Applying the preceding
estimate to
G
, we get

\mu\left(\frac{F-\mathbb{E}[F]}{L}\geq\frac{t}{L}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)t^{2}}{2R^{2}L^{2}}\right),

or equivalently,

\mu\left(F-\mathbb{E}[F]\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)t^{2}}{2R^{2}L^{2}}\right).

Combining this result with the analogous one for
-F
, two one-sided estimates yields

\mu\left(|F-\mathbb{E}[F]|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)t^{2}}{2R^{2}L^{2}}\right),

as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
v=(v^{(1)},\ldots,v^{(d)})
be a random vector drawn uniformly from the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\sqrt{d/n}}
. By rotational invariance,

\mathbb{E}[v^{(i)}v^{(j)}]=0\quad\text{for }i\neq j,\qquad\mathbb{E}[(v^{(i)})^{2}]=\frac{1}{n},\qquad\text{and hence}\hskip 5.69046pt\mathbb{E}[vv^{*}]=\frac{1}{n}I_{d}.

Let
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be independent random vectors with the same distribution as
v
, and define the frame operator
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{*}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[S]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{i}^{*}]=n\cdot\frac{1}{n}I_{d}=I_{d}.

Define
Y_{i}:=x_{i}x_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{n}I_{d}
. Then
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]=0
. Since
\|x_{i}\|^{2}=d/n
, the matrix
x_{i}x_{i}^{*}
has eigenvalues
d/n
and
0
(with multiplicity
d-1
). Hence
Y_{i}
has eigenvalues
\frac{d-1}{n}
and
-\frac{1}{n}
(with multiplicity
d-1
), so for
d\geq 2
,

\|Y_{i}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac{d-1}{n}.

Moreover, a direct computation shows that

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac{d-1}{n}.

Applying Theorem
2.2
yields the desired bound for the operator norm.

We now turn to concentration in the Frobenius norm. Define

f:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\sqrt{d/n}}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
by

f(v)=vv^{*}
, and for each
(j,k)
let
f_{jk}(v):=v^{(j)}v^{(k)}
. A direct computation shows that
\nabla f_{jk}(v)=v^{(k)}e_{j}+v^{(j)}e_{k}
, and
\|\nabla f_{jk}(v)\|\leq 2\|v\|\leq 2\sqrt{d/n}
. It is known that, on spheres, the geodesic distance is greater than the Euclidean distance. Thus the Lipschitz constant with respect to the Euclidean distance is also a Lipschitz constant with respect to the geodesic distance. Thus, we can say
f_{jk}
is Lipschitz with
\mathrm{Lip}(f_{jk})\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}
.

Now consider the product space
X:=\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\sqrt{d/n}}\right)^{n}
equipped with the product probability measure
\mu:=\sigma_{\sqrt{d/n}}^{\otimes n}
, and geodesic product distance,
d_{X}
, described in Lemma
2.3
.

For each
(j,k)
, define
F_{jk}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{jk}(x_{i})
. Then the frame operator is
S=[F_{jk}]_{j,k=1}^{d}
. So,

\mathbb{E}[F_{jk}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[x_{i}^{(j)}x_{i}^{(k)}]=\delta_{jk},\qquad\text{and therefore}\quad\mathbb{E}[S]=I_{d}.

We now estimate the Lipschitz constant of
F_{jk}
with respect to
d_{X}
. By the standard product rule for Lipschitz constants,

\mathrm{Lip}(F_{jk})\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Lip}(f_{jk})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Using the bound above,

\mathrm{Lip}(F_{jk})\leq\left(n\cdot\frac{4d}{n}\right)^{1/2}=2\sqrt{d}.

Applying Lemma
2.3
with
R=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}
and
L=2\sqrt{d}
to the function
F_{jk}
, we obtain, for every
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|F_{jk}-\mathbb{E}[F_{jk}]|\geq t\bigr)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)t^{2}}{2R^{2}L^{2}}\right).

Substituting
R^{2}=d/n
and
L^{2}=4d
, this becomes

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|F_{jk}-\mathbb{E}[F_{jk}]|\geq t\bigr)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)n\,t^{2}}{8d^{2}}\right).

Now suppose that
\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\varepsilon
. Since
\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\left(F_{jk}-\mathbb{E}[F_{jk}]\right)^{2}
, there exists at least one pair
(j,k)
such that
|F_{jk}-\mathbb{E}[F_{jk}]|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{d}
. Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\left(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(|F_{jk}-\mathbb{E}[F_{jk}]|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\right).

Using the previous estimate with
t=\varepsilon/d
, and taking a union bound
over all
d^{2}
pairs
(j,k)
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2d^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)n\varepsilon^{2}}{8d^{4}}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The same approach extends beyond the uniform sphere measure. In general, one may define

Y_{i}:=x_{i}x_{i}^{*}-\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{i}^{*}],

so that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]=0
. Thus, the concentration result in Theorem
2.2
applies as long as the random vectors
x_{i}
satisfy
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{i}^{*}]=\alpha I_{d}
for some
\alpha>0
. In particular, this includes distributions supported on the sphere or the ball that are isotropic after appropriate normalization.

2.3
Concentration for Euclidean Balls

The next main result is a concentration result for frames whose vectors are drawn from Euclidean balls. We use
\mathbb{B}^{d}_{R}
to denote the closed Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
R>0
. This is treated as a metric measure space with Euclidean metric and uniform probability measure. The main result of this subsection is as follows.

Theorem 2.5

(Concentration in the Ball)
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a set of independent random vectors, drawn from the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{B}_{\sqrt{\frac{d+2}{n}}}^{d}
and let
S
be the associated frame operator. Then, for every
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|S-I_{d}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\;\leq\;2d\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2d+2+\frac{2\varepsilon(d+1)}{3}}\right).

and

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\;\leq\;d^{2}\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{cn\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{3}}\right).

where
c
is an absolute constant.

Remark 2.6
.

Let us point out that the Frobenius concentration bound involves an unspecified absolute constant
c
. While we were able to specify constants in all other concentration results in the paper, we were unable to do so in this one case.

The proof uses an approach similar to that of the concentration result on the sphere, but requires some additional tools.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
v
be a random vector uniformly distributed on the Euclidean ball
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}[vv^{*}]=\frac{r^{2}}{d+2}\,I_{d}.

In particular, choosing
r=\sqrt{\frac{d+2}{n}}
ensures that
\mathbb{E}[vv^{*}]=\frac{1}{n}I_{d}
.

Proof.

Since the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}
is rotationally invariant, the matrix

\mathbb{E}[vv^{*}]
must be a scalar multiple of the identity.
In particular, all diagonal entries are equal, and we have
\mathbb{E}[v_{i}^{2}]=\frac{1}{d}\,\mathbb{E}[\|v\|^{2}]
. Let

k=\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[vv^{*}]\bigr)=\frac{1}{d}\,\mathbb{E}[\|v\|^{2}].

Then

\mathbb{E}[vv^{*}]=kI_{d}.

To compute
k
, note that

k=\frac{1}{d\,\operatorname{Vol}(\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d})}\int_{\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}}\|v\|^{2}\,dV.

Using polar coordinates
(\rho,\theta)
, where
\|v\|=\rho
and

dV=\rho^{d-1}\,d\rho\,d\theta
, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}}\|v\|^{2}\,dV=\int_{0}^{r}\int_{S^{d-1}}\rho^{2}\rho^{d-1}\,d\theta\,d\rho=\left(\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{d+1}\,d\rho\right)\operatorname{Vol}(S^{d-1})=\frac{r^{d+2}}{d+2}\operatorname{Vol}(S^{d-1}).

On the other hand, the volume of
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}
is given by

\operatorname{Vol}(\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d})=\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{d-1}\,d\rho\cdot\operatorname{Vol}(S^{d-1})=\frac{r^{d}}{d}\operatorname{Vol}(S^{d-1}).

Substituting these expressions yields

k=\frac{\frac{r^{d+2}}{d+2}\operatorname{Vol}(S^{d-1})}{d\cdot\frac{r^{d}}{d}\operatorname{Vol}(S^{d-1})}=\frac{r^{2}}{d+2}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[vv^{*}]=\frac{r^{2}}{d+2}\,I_{d}.

Choosing
r=\sqrt{\frac{d+2}{n}}
makes the expected value
\frac{1}{n}I_{d}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

For the concentration result in the operator norm, we proceed exactly as in the corresponding part of the proof of Theorem
2.1
. By Lemma
2.7
, the radius of the ball must be
\sqrt{(d+2)/n}
in order to ensure that
\mathbb{E}[S]=I_{d}
.

For the Frobenius norm, the concentration result for a single vector in the ball is provided by
[
20
, Proposition 2.9]
. We then follow the same steps as in the Frobenius norm part of the proof of Theorem
2.1
to obtain the result in the ball setting.
∎

We conclude this section with a corollary that quantifies our concentration in terms of increasing
n
(the number of vectors in the frames). The proof follows immediately from the main theorems.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, for both the sphere and ball models, there exists a constant
C_{d,\varepsilon}
(depending on dimension
d
and tolerance
\varepsilon
) such that if

n\;\geq\;C_{d,\varepsilon}\,\log\!\left(\frac{2d}{\delta}\right),

then

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\varepsilon\bigr)\leq\delta.

In particular:

•

To guarantee a confidence level of
95\%
, it suffices to take

n\;\geq\;C_{d,\varepsilon}\,\log(40d).

•

To guarantee a confidence level of
99\%
, it suffices to take

n\;\geq\;C_{d,\varepsilon}\,\log(200d).

Here one may take

C_{d,\varepsilon}=\frac{2d-2+\frac{2\varepsilon(d-1)}{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}

for the sphere model, and

C_{d,\varepsilon}=\frac{2d+2+\frac{2\varepsilon(d+1)}{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}

for the ball model.

An analogous statement holds for the Frobenius norm.

3
Concentration of Equal-Norm Frames

In the previous section, we showed that random collections of equal-norm vectors concentrate around Parseval frames. In this section, we prove a complementary result: random Parseval frames concentrate around equal-norm frames. More precisely, we show that if a Parseval frame is chosen at random, then the norms of its frame vectors are close to the common norm of an equal-norm Parseval frame, namely
\sqrt{d/n}
, with high probability.

A Parseval frame
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be identified with a matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
whose rows are the vectors
x_{i}
. Such a matrix thus satisfies the condition
U^{*}U=I_{d}
, so that Parseval frames correspond to the points of the
Stiefel manifold

\mathrm{St}(n,d)\coloneqq\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\mid U^{\ast}U=I_{d}\}.

(1)

We note that the correspondence between Parseval frames and the Stiefel manifold has appeared previously in the frame theory literature, e.g.,
[
11
,
24
]
. Throughout this section, we equip
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
with its canonical Riemannian metric (described in the following subsection) and the associated normalized Riemannian volume measure, which we refer to as the
symmetric measure
.

We can now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.1

(Concentration over the Stiefel Manifold)
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a random Parseval frame in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, drawn from the symmetric measure. Let
\varepsilon>0
and assume that
n
is sufficiently large so that
n\geq e^{d}
and

3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}<\varepsilon.

Then we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists\,i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\;\text{such that}\;\bigl|\,\|x_{i}\|-\sqrt{d/n}\,\bigr|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{(n-2)\left(\varepsilon-3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}\right)^{2}}{8}\right).

Remark 3.2
.

The theorem shows that random Parseval frames concentrate on frames whose norms are all
\varepsilon
-close to
\sqrt{d/n}
, in an additive sense. Frequently in frame theory, an alternative notion of
\varepsilon
-nearly equal-norm frames, based on relative error, is considered. This notion is considered in Section
4
, in relation to the Paulsen problem.

In analogy with Corollary
2.8
, the theorem immediately yields a quantification of concentration in
n
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
and
\varepsilon\geq 6\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}
, if

n\geq 2+\frac{32\ln(20)}{\varepsilon^{2}},

then the conclusion of Theorem
3.1
holds with confidence at least
95\%
. If

n\geq 2+\frac{32\ln(100)}{\varepsilon^{2}},

then the conclusion holds with confidence at least
99\%
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is geometric. It relies on a concentration inequality on manifolds together with two key ingredients: an upper bound for the expectation of a suitable Lipschitz function, and a lower bound on the Ricci curvature of
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
M
be a compact, connected Riemannian manifold with
\mathrm{Ric}\geq\kappa>0
, and let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz with constant
\mathrm{Lip}(f)
. If
X
is distributed according to the normalized Riemannian volume measure on
M
, then for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(f(X)-\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\geq t\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\kappa t^{2}}{2\,\mathrm{Lip}(f)^{2}}\right).

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3.1
, which is more technical than the proofs of the concentration results presented in the previous section. We note that a concentration result can be derived somewhat straightforwardly from Theorem
3.4
and a union bound, but that our proof of Theorem
3.1
avoids a union bound argument, resulting in stronger concentration—see Remark
3.11
for details.

3.1
Geometric Ingredients

Since the main result follows directly from Theorem
3.4
, we need two geometric ingredients: the geodesic distance and the Ricci curvature for the Stiefel manifold. For the geodesic distance, it is enough for our purposes to understand its relationship with the Frobenius norm. Indeed, we will define a function whose Lipschitz constant can be estimated easily with respect to the Frobenius norm, and the comparison between the Frobenius norm and the geodesic distance will then allow us to obtain the required Lipschitz constant. For the Ricci curvature, it will be sufficient to establish an appropriate lower bound.

Let us recall some basic geometric structures of the Stiefel manifold; a standard reference for details is
[
12
]
. The tangent space at
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,d)
is given by

T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,d)=\left\{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\;\middle|\;U^{*}\Delta+\Delta^{*}U=0\right\}.

Equivalently, any tangent vector
\Delta\in T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,d)
can be expressed in the form

\Delta=UA+U_{\perp}B,

where
A
is a skew-symmetric
d\times d
matrix,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-d)\times d}
is an arbitrary matrix, and
U_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-d)}
is an orthonormal basis for the complement of the span of the columns of
U
. The canonical Riemannian metric is defined at
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,d)
as the map

\displaystyle T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,d)\times T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,d)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Delta_{1}^{*}\left(I_{n}-\frac{1}{2}UU^{*}\right)\Delta_{2}\right).

By general principles, this Riemannian metric yields a volume measure, which we normalize to define the
symmetric measure
, used in the statement of Theorem
3.1
.
We use
d_{C}
to denote the geodesic distance with respect to the canonical metric. As stated above, to deduce that certain functions are Lipschitz with respect to
d_{C}
, the following bound in terms of Frobenius distance will suffice.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathrm{St}(n,d)
,

d_{C}(U_{1},U_{2})\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\|U_{1}-U_{2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Proof.

By
[
22
]
,

d_{C}(U_{1},U_{2})\geq\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}}\cdot 2\sqrt{d}\,\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\|U_{1}-U_{2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}{2\sqrt{d}}\right).

The result follows, since
\arcsin(x)\geq x
for
x\in[0,1]
.
∎

Next, we derive a lower bound on Ricci curvature.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\xi\in T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,d)
be a unit tangent vector with respect to the canonical metric. If
n>d+1
, then

\mathrm{Ric}(\xi,\xi)\geq\frac{n}{2}-1.

Proof.

Write
\xi=UA+U_{\perp}B
, where
A
is skew-symmetric and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-d)\times d}
. The explicit formula for the Ricci curvature under the canonical metric, obtained from
[
26
]
, gives

\mathrm{Ric}(\xi,\xi)=\left(\frac{2-d}{4}+\frac{d-n}{4}\right)\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})+\left(\frac{1-d}{2}+(n-2)\right)\mathrm{Tr}(B^{*}B).

Since
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=-\|A\|^{2}
, this becomes

\mathrm{Ric}(\xi,\xi)=\frac{n-2}{4}\|A\|^{2}+\left(n-\frac{d}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right)\|B\|^{2}.

Because
\xi
is a unit vector with respect to the canonical metric,

\frac{1}{2}\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}=1.

Optimizing under this constraint yields

\mathrm{Ric}(\xi,\xi)\geq\min\!\left\{\frac{n}{2}-1,\,n-\frac{d}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right\}.

The result follows if
n>d+1
.
∎

3.2
Analytic Ingredients

We now introduce the function that will be used in the proof of Theorem
3.4
. We will estimate its Lipschitz constant and compute its expected value. For
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, define

f(G):=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|g_{i}\|,

where
g_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the
i
-th row of
G
. When restricted to
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
, this function records the maximum row norm of the corresponding Parseval frame.

We first estimate
\mathbb{E}[f(U)]
, where
U
is drawn from the symmetric measure on
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
. Since the row norms of
U
are not independent, we compare
U
to a Gaussian matrix using the following result of Tropp.

Theorem 3.7

(
[
28
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
U
be a random element of
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
drawn from the symmetric measure, and let
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a random matrix with independent
\mathcal{N}(0,n^{-1})
entries. For each nonnegative, sublinear, convex function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and each weakly increasing, convex function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\,\Phi\!\bigl(f(U)\bigr)\leq\mathbb{E}\,\Phi\!\left(\left(1+\frac{d}{2n}\right)f(G)\right).

We are now ready to bound the expected value.

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume
n\geq e^{d}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[f(U)]\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}.

Proof.

Take
\Phi
to be the identity function in Theorem
3.7
, which is weakly increasing and convex. The function
f
is nonnegative, convex, and sublinear on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, hence

\mathbb{E}[f(U)]\leq\left(1+\frac{d}{2n}\right)\mathbb{E}[f(G)].

We now compute the expected value of
f(G)
. This follows from a straightforward calculation, since the vectors are independent and the distribution of each vector can be identified explicitly.
\|g_{i}\|^{2}\sim\frac{1}{n}\chi_{d}^{2}
since each component of
g_{i}
has
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{n})
.

Since
f(G)\geq 0
, we use the standard identity

\mathbb{E}[f(G)]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(f(G)>t)\,dt.

Moreover, by independence,

\mathbb{P}(f(G)>t)=1-\left(\mathbb{P}(\|g_{i}\|\leq t)\right)^{n}=1-\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\chi_{d}^{2}\leq t^{2}\right)\right)^{n}=1-\left(\mathbb{P}(\chi_{d}^{2}\leq nt^{2})\right)^{n}.

Using
1-x^{n}\leq n(1-x)
for
x\in[0,1]
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(f(G)>t)\leq n\,\mathbb{P}(\chi_{d}^{2}\geq nt^{2}).

Hence, for every
A>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(G)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{A}\mathbb{P}(f(G)>t)\,dt+\int_{A}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(f(G)>t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{A}1\,dt+\int_{A}^{\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(\chi_{d}^{2}\geq nt^{2})\,dt

\displaystyle=A+n\int_{A}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\chi_{d}^{2}\geq nt^{2})\,dt.

Now apply Laurent–Massart’s inequality
[
19
, Lemma 1]
: if
X\sim\chi_{d}^{2}
, then for all
x\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X-d\geq 2\sqrt{dx}+2x\right)\leq e^{-x}.

Solving
2\sqrt{dx}+2x=nt^{2}-d
for
x
yields

x=\frac{\left(\sqrt{2nt^{2}-d}-\sqrt{d}\right)^{2}}{4},

and therefore

\mathbb{P}(\chi_{d}^{2}\geq nt^{2})\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\left(\sqrt{2nt^{2}-d}-\sqrt{d}\right)^{2}}{4}\right).

Substituting this into the previous inequality and performing the change of variables

r=\frac{\sqrt{2nt^{2}-d}-\sqrt{d}}{2}

gives

\mathbb{E}[f(G)]\leq A+\int_{B}^{\infty}\frac{2r+\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\frac{(2r+\sqrt{d})^{2}+d}{2n}}}\,e^{-r^{2}}\,dr,\qquad\text{where}\hskip 5.69046ptA=\sqrt{\frac{(2B+\sqrt{d})^{2}+d}{2n}}.

Bounding the prefactor by
\sqrt{2n}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[f(G)]\leq A+\sqrt{2n}\int_{B}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}}\,dr.

Using

\int_{B}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}}\,dr\leq\frac{e^{-B^{2}}}{2B},\qquad B>0,

we conclude that

\mathbb{E}[f(G)]\leq\sqrt{\frac{(2B+\sqrt{d})^{2}+d}{2n}}+\sqrt{2n}\,\frac{e^{-B^{2}}}{2B}.

Choosing
B=\sqrt{\ln n}
yields

\mathbb{E}[f(G)]\leq\sqrt{\frac{4\ln n+4\sqrt{d\ln n}+2d}{2n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n\ln n}}.

Since
n\geq e^{d}
and
d\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(U)]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{d}{2n}\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\ln n+4\sqrt{d\ln n}+2d}{2n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n\ln n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\ln n+4\ln n+2\ln n}{2n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2nd}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{5\ln n}{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2nd}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\left(\sqrt{5}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}.

This gives the claimed result.
∎

We next compute a Lipschitz constant for
f
on
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
.

Proposition 3.9
.

The restriction of
f
to
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
is
\sqrt{2}
-Lipschitz with respect to the geodesic distance
d_{C}
.

Proof.

Let
U,V\in\mathrm{St}(n,d)
, and let
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
denote their
i
-th rows. Then

|f(U)-f(V)|=\left|\max_{i}\|u_{i}\|-\max_{i}\|v_{i}\|\right|\leq\max_{i}\left|\|u_{i}\|-\|v_{i}\|\right|\leq\max_{i}\|u_{i}-v_{i}\|.

Therefore,

|f(U)-f(V)|\leq\bigl\|(\|u_{1}-v_{1}\|,\dots,\|u_{n}-v_{n}\|)\bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\bigl\|(\|u_{1}-v_{1}\|,\dots,\|u_{n}-v_{n}\|)\bigr\|_{2}=\|U-V\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Thus
f
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the Frobenius norm. Applying Lemma
3.5
yields the result.
∎

3.3
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We are now prepared to prove the main theorem of this section.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,d)
be the matrix associated with the random Parseval frame, and let
u_{i}
denote its rows. Since

3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}<\varepsilon\qquad\text{and}\qquad n\geq e^{d},

we have
\varepsilon>\sqrt{d/n}
. Therefore,

\left|\|u_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right|\geq\varepsilon

can only occur when

\|u_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\geq\varepsilon
,
because the alternative inequality

\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}-\|u_{i}\|\geq\varepsilon
would force

\|u_{i}\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}-\varepsilon<0
, which is impossible. Hence

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists\,i\text{ such that }\left|\|u_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|u_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\geq\varepsilon\right).

Since
f(U)=\max_{i}\|u_{i}\|
, this equals

\mathbb{P}\!\left(f(U)-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\geq\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(f(U)-\mathbb{E}[f(U)]\geq\varepsilon-\bigl(\mathbb{E}[f(U)]-\sqrt{\tfrac{d}{n}}\bigr)\right).

By Proposition
3.8
,
\mathbb{E}[f(U)]\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}},
and therefore

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists\,i\text{ such that }\left|\|u_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(f(U)-\mathbb{E}[f(U)]\geq\varepsilon-3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}\right).

By Proposition
3.9
,
f
is
\sqrt{2}
-Lipschitz. Moreover, by Lemma
3.6
, the Ricci curvature of
\mathrm{St}(n,d)
under the canonical metric is bounded below by
\frac{n}{2}-1
. Applying Theorem
3.4
with

\kappa=\frac{n}{2}-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{Lip}(f)=\sqrt{2}

gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(f(U)-\mathbb{E}[f(U)]\geq t\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{(n-2)t^{2}}{8}\right),\qquad t\geq 0.

Finally, substituting

t=\varepsilon-3\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}},

which is nonnegative by assumption, yields the claim.
∎

In the proof above, we employed an explicit upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[f(G)]
under the assumption that
n
is sufficiently large.
In principle, one could obtain a sharper estimate by using the exact
expression for
\mathbb{E}[f(G)]
, which involves the incomplete gamma function and the gamma function. Since our goal was to state a clean two-sided formula, and keep the inequality as simple as possible, we used an upper bound for
\mathbb{E}[f(G)]
. For completeness, we record below the concentration result obtained when the exact expression is used.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a random Parseval frame in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, drawn from the symmetric measure, and let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Then, for all sufficiently large
n
satisfying
K<\varepsilon
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\|x_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{(n-2)(\varepsilon-K)^{2}}{8}\right),

where

K\coloneqq\left(1+\frac{d}{2n}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\left(\dfrac{\gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2},y\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\right)^{n}}{\sqrt{y}}\,dy-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}.

Although this formula provides a sharper result, we chose to state the previous version instead. The reason is that the constant
K
appearing here is rather complicated, whereas in the previous formulation we replace it by a simpler upper bound that can be stated more transparently. If
n\geq d/\varepsilon^{2}
, the one-sided condition appearing in the probability estimate in Proposition
3.10
is equivalent to the corresponding two-sided condition.
In Section
A
, we compare the theoretical predictions
of Proposition
3.10
with empirical results obtained
from numerical experiments.

Remark 3.11
.

An alternative concentration estimate can be obtained by applying Theorem
3.4
to each coordinate separately and then using a union bound. Define

g_{i}:\mathrm{St}(n,d)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad g_{i}(U)=e_{i}^{*}\operatorname{diag}(UU^{*}),

so that
g_{i}(U)=\|u_{i}\|^{2}
. Then
\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(U)]=d/n
, and
g_{i}
is
2\sqrt{2}
-Lipschitz with respect to the geodesic distance. Applying Theorem
3.4
and summing over
i
yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists\,i,\;\left|\|u_{i}\|^{2}-\frac{d}{n}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2n\exp\!\left(-\frac{(n-2)\varepsilon^{2}}{32}\right).

This bound is valid, but weaker than Theorem
3.1
because of the extra factor
n
produced by the union bound.

4
Application to the Paulsen Problem

In this section, we present an application of Theorem
2.1
to the
Paulsen problem
, a major open problem in finite-dimensional frame theory
[
3
,
7
,
4
,
18
,
15
]
, which we now recall. We call
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
an
\varepsilon
-nearly Parseval frame
if
\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\varepsilon
, and an

\varepsilon
-nearly equal-norm frame
if the squared norms of its frame vectors satisfy

(1-\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}\leq\|x_{i}\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We write
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
for the class of frames that are both
\varepsilon
-nearly equal-norm and
\varepsilon
-nearly Parseval, and
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}
for the class of (exactly) equal-norm Parseval frames in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Problem 4.1

(Paulsen Problem
[
3
]
)
.

Find the simplest function
f(d,n,\varepsilon)
satisfying, for all
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
, the bound

\inf_{(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}}d\big((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\big)^{2}\leq f(d,n,\varepsilon),

where the distance is

d\big((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\big)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-y_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The existence of such a function
f(d,n,\varepsilon)
is attributed to Hadwin
by Bodmann and Casazza
[
3
]
.
Subsequent work focused on expressing a bounding function in the cleanest possible form and, in particular, without explicit dependence on
n
.
In
[
18
]
, Kwok et al. were the first to obtain a bound depending only on
d
and
\varepsilon
; more precisely, they proved a bound of order
O(\varepsilon d^{13/2})
.
Subsequently, Hamilton and Moitra obtained the following result, which will be useful for our purposes.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
15
]
)
.

For
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
,

\inf_{(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}}d\big((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\big)^{2}\leq 20\varepsilon d^{2}.

At present, this is the best general bound for the Paulsen problem in the literature. On the other hand, it was shown in
[
7
]
that any such bound must necessarily be at least of order
\varepsilon^{2}d
. Our main result is the following. In the statement, we refer to a
uniform measure
on
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
, which is defined precisely in the following subsection—see (
3
).

Theorem 4.3

(Concentration for the Paulsen Problem)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and let
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be chosen uniformly at random from
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
. Then the bound

\inf_{(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}}d\big((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\big)^{2}\leq(\sqrt{20}+\sqrt{2})^{2}\varepsilon^{2}d

(2)

holds with probability at least

1-\frac{2d\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{n\varepsilon^{4}}{2d^{3}}\right)}{1-2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{6{\varepsilon}^{2}n}{5(d+2)^{2}}\right)}.

The next (immediate) corollary quantifies the success probability in terms of
n
.

Corollary 4.4
.

A random
\varepsilon
-nearly equal norm and Parseval frame in
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
satisfies the Paulsen bound (
2
) with probability
95\%
if

n\;\geq\;\frac{2d^{3}\ln(80d)}{\varepsilon^{4}},

and with probability
99\%
if

n\;\geq\;\frac{2d^{3}\ln(400d)}{\varepsilon^{4}}.

The theorem is proven in the next subsection, after setting up necessary preliminary results. In Section
4.2
, we clarify the distinction between the best possible deterministic scaling and the high-probability scaling obtained here.

4.1
Proof of Theorem
4.3

Let us begin by setting up some relevant notation. Consider the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
consisting of all vectors whose squared norms lie between
(1-\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}
and
(1+\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}
.
This set can be viewed as a thin spherical shell in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We denote it by

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\coloneqq\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\;(1-\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}\leq\|v\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}\right\}.

Let
\mu
denote the uniform probability measure on

\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon}
, and consider the product measure

\mu^{\otimes n}
on
(\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon})^{n}
.
Our primary set of interest is
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}\subset(\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon})^{n}
. Since
\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\big((x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}\big)>0
, the corresponding conditional probabilities are well-defined. Let
\nu
denote the conditional probability measure, i.e.,

\nu(\,\cdot\,)\coloneqq\mu^{\otimes n}(\,\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}).

(3)

Moreover,
\nu
is precisely the normalized restriction of the uniform product measure
to
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
; in particular, it is again uniform on
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
. This is the measure on
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
that is used in Theorem
4.3
.

The proof of Theorem
4.3
consists of two separate parts. In the first part, we compute the relevant probability, and in the second part, we derive the upper bound. Since the proof is rather lengthy, we first establish some auxiliary results that will be needed in the argument.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
v
be a random vector uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\|v\|^{2}=\frac{d}{n}\cdot\frac{d}{d+2}\cdot\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{\frac{d+2}{2}}-(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{d+2}{2}}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{\frac{d}{2}}-(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\leq\frac{d}{n}\cdot\frac{d}{d+2}\cdot\left(1+\varepsilon+\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{d}\right).

Proof.

By the definition of the expected value, we have

\mathbb{E}\|v\|^{2}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon}}\|v\|^{2}\,dv}{\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon}}dv}.

Using polar coordinates
v=r\omega
with
\omega\in S^{d-1}
and
dv=r^{d-1}\,dr\,d\omega
, and writing

a=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}(1-\varepsilon)},\qquad b=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon)},

we obtain

\mathbb{E}\|v\|^{2}=\frac{\int_{a}^{b}r^{2}\,r^{d-1}\,dr}{\int_{a}^{b}r^{d-1}\,dr}=\frac{d}{d+2}\cdot\frac{b^{d+2}-a^{d+2}}{b^{d}-a^{d}}.

Substituting
a,b
yields

\mathbb{E}\|v\|^{2}=\frac{d}{n}\cdot\frac{d}{d+2}\cdot\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{\frac{d+2}{2}}-(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{d+2}{2}}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{\frac{d}{2}}-(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{d}{2}}},

(4)

and this proves the equality part of the lemma.

Since the expression (
4
) is complicated, we now derive a simpler upper bound. Let
k=d/2
,
\alpha=1+\varepsilon
and
\beta=1-\varepsilon
where
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Define

L\coloneqq\frac{\alpha^{k+1}-\beta^{k+1}}{\alpha^{k}-\beta^{k}}.

Observe that
\alpha^{k+1}-\beta^{k+1}=\alpha(\alpha^{k}-\beta^{k})+\beta^{k}(\alpha-\beta)
, hence

L=\alpha+\frac{\beta^{k}(\alpha-\beta)}{\alpha^{k}-\beta^{k}}=\alpha+\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{k}-1}.

Now

\alpha-\beta=2\varepsilon,\qquad\frac{\alpha}{\beta}=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}=1+u,\quad\text{where}\quad u=\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}>0.

Then

\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{k}-1=(1+u)^{k}-1\geq ku\quad\text{by Bernoulli's inequality.}

Therefore,

\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{k}-1}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{k\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{k}.

Thus

L\leq\alpha+\frac{1-\varepsilon}{k}.

This implies the inequality in the lemma.
∎

Using Lemma
4.5
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be i.i.d. sampled from
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon}
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\big((x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}\big)>1-2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{24{\varepsilon}^{2}n}{(d+2)(1+\varepsilon)\left(3(d+2)(1+\varepsilon)+4d\varepsilon\right)}\right).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\big((x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\text{ is $\varepsilon$–nearly Parseval}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\varepsilon\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}>\varepsilon\Big)

Proceeding as in the sphere and ball cases, we invoke
Theorem
2.2
, the Matrix Bernstein inequality. Define
Y_{i}\coloneqq x_{i}x_{i}^{*}-\mathbb{E}\!\left[x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\right]
,
so that, clearly
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]=0
, and
\mathbb{E}\!\left[x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\right]=(\mathbb{E}\|v\|^{2}/d)I_{d}
by the rotation invariant distribution. Next, we estimate the parameters in Theorem
2.2
.

First, we need to find
R
, an upper bound for
\|Y_{i}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. Since
\|x_{i}\|^{2}\leq\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon)
for all

x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\|x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\|x_{i}\|^{2}\leq\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|Y_{i}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|x_{i}x_{i}^{*}-\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\|v\|^{2}}{d}\right)I_{d}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon)-\frac{\mathbb{E}\|v\|^{2}}{d}\leq\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon),

so we can take the last term as
R
.

Now we derive an expression for the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
. We compute

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]=n\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i}\|^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{*}-2\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\|x_{i}\|^{2}}{d}\right)x_{i}x_{i}^{*}+\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\|x_{i}\|^{2}}{d}\right)^{2}I_{d}\right]

\displaystyle=n\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i}\|^{4}\right]}{d}-\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}\|x_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{2}}{d^{2}}\right)I_{d}.

Taking operator norms and using

\|x_{i}\|^{2}\leq\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon)
, we obtain

\sigma^{2}\coloneqq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq n\left\|\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{i}\|^{4}\right]}{d}I_{d}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq n\cdot\frac{\big(\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon)\big)^{2}}{d}=\frac{d}{n}(1+\varepsilon)^{2}.

Therefore, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\|S-MI_{d}\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}>t\Big)\leq 2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{3t^{2}n}{6d(1+\varepsilon)^{2}+2d(1+\varepsilon)t}\right),

where
M\coloneqq n\left(\mathbb{E}[\|v\|^{2}]/d\right)
. By the triangle inequality for the operator norm, if

\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}-\|I_{d}-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}>t,

then

\|S-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}>t.

Hence, the probability of the first event is bounded by the probability of
the second, and the previous tail bound also applies to

\mathbb{P}\Big(\|S-I_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}>t+\|I_{d}-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\Big).

Since our aim is an
\varepsilon
–nearly Parseval statement, we set

\varepsilon=t+\|I_{d}-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}},\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad t=\varepsilon-\|I_{d}-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

To use the bound we must have
t>0
, so it remains to ensure

\varepsilon-\|I_{d}-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\varepsilon-|1-M|>0.

By Lemma
4.5
, we know the exact value and an upper bound for
M

M=\frac{d}{d+2}\cdot\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{\frac{d+2}{2}}-(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{d+2}{2}}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{\frac{d}{2}}-(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\leq\frac{d}{d+2}\cdot\left(1+\varepsilon+\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{d}\right).

Viewing
M
as a function of
d
, one checks that it is increasing in
d
and
satisfies
M=1
at
d=2
. Hence, for
d\geq 2
we have
M\geq 1
, and therefore

\|I_{d}-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=|1-M|=M-1.

In particular,

t=\varepsilon-\|I_{d}-MI_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\varepsilon-(M-1)=\varepsilon+1-M\geq\frac{4\varepsilon}{d+2}>0.

Substituting all of the parameters we have obtained into
Proposition
2.2
yields the claimed result.

∎

We now have all the necessary ingredients to prove
Theorem
4.3
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

We define the radial projection map

P:\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\longrightarrow\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\quad\text{by}\quad P(v)=\frac{\sqrt{d/n}}{\|v\|}\,v.

This map rescales each vector in the shell onto the sphere of radius
\sqrt{d/n}
. Since
\mu
is rotation-invariant and
P(Qv)=QP(v)
for every orthogonal transformation
Q\in O(d)
, it follows that the pushforward measure
P_{\#}\mu
is also rotation-invariant on the sphere
\sqrt{d/n}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. By uniqueness of the normalized Haar measure on the sphere, we conclude that
P_{\#}\mu
is precisely the uniform probability measure on
\sqrt{d/n}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

The strategy for the rest of the proof is sketched as follows. After selecting
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
, we show that the projected vectors

(P(x_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}
are, with high probability,

\varepsilon^{2}/d
–nearly Parseval.
Since they also have equal-norm with
\|P(x_{i})\|=\sqrt{d/n}
, they belong to
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon^{2}/d}
, so that we can apply the result of Hamilton and Moitra
(Theorem
4.2
),
we obtain a sharper upper bound.
Moreover, we show that
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is close to

(P(x_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}
.
We will then apply the triangle inequality to conclude the proof.

We begin by determining the relevant probability.
For this purpose, we introduce some notation and definitions.

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\left\{\text{$\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d}$–nearly Parseval frames
with vectors in }\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right\}.

We are interested in the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\in\mathcal{A}\Big)
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
corresponds exactly to the

\varepsilon
–nearly Parseval condition in
\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\bigr)^{n}
, this equals

\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\in\mathcal{A}\;\Big|\;(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\text{ is $\varepsilon$–nearly Parseval}\Big).

Using complements, we write

=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\notin\mathcal{A}\;\Big|\;(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\text{ is $\varepsilon$–nearly Parseval}\Big).

By the definition of conditional probability,

=1-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\notin\mathcal{A}\;\text{ and }\;(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\text{ are $\varepsilon$–nearly Parseval}\Big)}{\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\text{ is $\varepsilon$–nearly Parseval}\Big)}.

Finally, we obtain the lower bound

\geq 1-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\notin\mathcal{A}\Big)}{\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\text{ is $\varepsilon$–nearly Parseval}\Big)}.

Since
P_{\#}\mu
is the uniform probability measure on

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\sqrt{d/n}}
, the vectors

P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n})
are i.i.d. uniform on the sphere.
Hence

\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\notin\mathcal{A}\Big)

is exactly the probability in Theorem
2.1

with
\varepsilon
replaced by
\varepsilon^{2}/d
.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\notin\mathcal{A}\Big)\leq 2d\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{n\varepsilon^{4}}{2d\!\left(d^{2}-d+\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}(d-1)}{3}\right)}\right).

By Proposition
4.6
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\Big((x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\text{ is $\varepsilon$–nearly Parseval}\Big)>1-2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{24{\varepsilon}^{2}n}{(d+2)(1+\varepsilon)\left(3(d+2)(1+\varepsilon)+4d\varepsilon\right)}\right)

Consequently, we obtain the following lower bound for the main probability:

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\in\mathcal{A}\Big)\geq 1-\frac{2d\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{n\varepsilon^{4}}{2d\,\!\left(d^{2}-d+\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}(d-1)}{3}\right)}\right)}{1-2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{24{\varepsilon}^{2}n}{(d+2)(1+\varepsilon)\left(3(d+2)(1+\varepsilon)+4d\varepsilon\right)}\right)}

After simplifying the expression in this probability bound,
we may write the following alternative lower bound.

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\Big((P(x_{1}),\dots,P(x_{n}))\in\mathcal{A}\Big)\geq 1-\frac{2d\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{n\varepsilon^{4}}{2d^{3}}\right)}{1-2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{6{\varepsilon}^{2}n}{5(d+2)^{2}}\right)}

We have now completed the analysis of the probability term appearing
in the theorem. In particular, for a randomly chosen

(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
, we have obtained a lower bound on the probability that
(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
with
z_{i}=Px_{i}
forms an
\varepsilon^{2}/d
–nearly Parseval frame. Moreover, since each
z_{i}
satisfies
\|z_{i}\|=\sqrt{d/n}
, the vectors automatically have equal-norm. Consequently,

(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
belongs to

\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon^{2}/d}
with at least the same probability. In this setting, applying
Theorem
4.2
of Hamilton and Moitra yields

\inf_{(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}}d\!\left((y_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\right)^{2}\;\leq\;20\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d}d^{2}=20\varepsilon^{2}d.

At this point, our main focus is to estimate
d\!\left((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\right)^{2}
. We have

x_{i}-z_{i}=x_{i}\!\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\,\frac{1}{\|x_{i}\|}\right),

so that

\|x_{i}-z_{i}\|^{2}=\|x_{i}\|^{2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\,\frac{1}{\|x_{i}\|}\right)^{2}=\left(\|x_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)^{2}.

Summing over
i
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-z_{i}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\|x_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)^{2}.

Note that

\|x_{i}\|\in\left[\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sqrt{1-\varepsilon},\,\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\right],

which implies

\left|\|x_{i}\|-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\left(\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\right),

yielding

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-z_{i}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d}{n}\left(\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{2}=d\left(\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{2}.

Next, observe that

\left(\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{2}=2-2\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{2}}<2-2(1-\varepsilon^{2})=2\varepsilon^{2},

which implies

d\!\left((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-z_{i}\|^{2}\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}d.

By using triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}}d\!\left((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\!\left((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\right)+\inf_{(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,n}}d\!\left((y_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon^{2}d}+\sqrt{20\varepsilon^{2}d}=\left(\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{20}\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}d}.

After squaring both sides, we obtain the result.
∎

It was shown by Cahill and Casazza
[
4
]
that the Paulsen problem is equivalent to the
projection problem
in operator theory. Using this equivalence, we have the following probabilistic result for the projection problem. The proof follows directly from
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
and Theorem
4.3
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the standard basis vectors. Let
P
be a random projection to a
d
-dimensional subspace, sampled under the condition that

(1-\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}\leq\|Pe_{i}\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n},

for all
i
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the standard norm. Then there exists a projection
Q
with
\|Qe_{i}\|^{2}=\frac{d}{n}
, for all
i
, satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Pe_{i}-Qe_{i}\|^{2}\leq 4\left(\sqrt{20}+\sqrt{2}\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}d

with high probability, provided
n
is sufficiently large.

Remark 4.8
.

After completing this work, we became aware of related results in Ramachandran’s dissertation
[
27
]
. In the average-case analysis therein, it is shown that a frame consisting of independent random unit vectors is, with high probability,
\varepsilon-
doubly balanced and lies within squared distance
O(\varepsilon^{2})
of a doubly balanced frame (The doubly balanced case is simply the equal-norm Parseval frame case scaled by
1/\sqrt{d}
, which is why his results there are missing
d
.). We note three differences from our setting. First, the sampling models differ: we draw uniformly from the set of
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
for prescribed
\varepsilon
, whereas in
[
27
]
the parameter
\varepsilon
is not free but is determined by
d
and
n
through concentration of measure. Second, the results in
[
27
]
require a structural pseudorandomness condition on the input, which is verified probabilistically for random unit vectors but is not guaranteed for an arbitrary
\varepsilon
-doubly balanced frame. Third, the bounds in
[
27
]
involve unspecified universal constants at every stage—in the threshold on
n
, the failure probability, and the distance bound itself—whereas our results are fully explicit and numerically transparent for every given
\varepsilon,d,
and sufficiently large
n
.

4.2
On the Optimal Paulsen Bound

The goal of this subsection is to clarify the theoretical order of an optimal bound
f(\varepsilon,d,n)
in the Paulsen problem. As was mentioned above, it was shown in
[
7
]
, via a family of examples, that the Paulsen problem must at least be on the order of
\varepsilon^{2}d
. On the other hand, quoting from
[
4
]
: “For all examples we know at this time, we have

f(\varepsilon,d,n)\leq 16\varepsilon d
.”
The papers
[
18
]
and
[
15
]

state that the optimal theoretical bound in the Paulsen problem must be at least of order
\varepsilon d
, both citing
[
4
]
as the source. However, as far as we can tell, there is no such theoretical bound established in that paper, or anywhere else in the literature. Our goal is to clarify the situation by proving:

Proposition 4.9
.

If a bound
f(\varepsilon,d,n)
for the Paulsen problem is a polynomial in
\varepsilon
and
d
, then it must be of order at least
O(\varepsilon d)
.

Remark 4.10
.

Theorem
4.3
gives a bound of the form
O(\varepsilon^{2}d)
. Since the theorem is probabilistic, this does not contradict the proposition.

The proof is via a family of examples, heavily borrowing ideas from the following example from Ramachandran’s thesis
[
27
]
.

Example 4.11

(
[
27
, Example A.1.1]
)
.

For a fixed
\theta
which is small enough, consider the synthesis operator
U
where the columns represent a frame:

U\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2\theta)&\cos(2\theta)&0&0\\
\sin(2\theta)&-\sin(2\theta)&1&1\end{bmatrix}.

The corresponding frame of
U
is in
\mathcal{F}_{2,4}^{\varepsilon}
where
\varepsilon\leq 4\sin^{2}\theta
.
The closest equal-norm Parseval frame (ENPF) to
U
has synthesis operator

V\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\theta)&\cos(-\theta)&\sin(-\theta)&\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)&\sin(-\theta)&\cos(-\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{bmatrix},

and the squared distance is
\|U-V\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\sin^{2}\theta\geq\varepsilon/4
.

By using this example, we generate the next one.

Example 4.12
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and let
d=2k
and
n=4k
. Let
U_{k}
and
V_{k}
be the following matrices

U_{k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}U&0&\cdots&0\\
0&U&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&U\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}V&0&\cdots&0\\
0&V&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&V\end{bmatrix}

where
U
and
V
are the matrices defined in the previous example. Let
\varepsilon
be the same number from the previous example.

Lemma 4.13
.

The frames represented by
U_{k}
and
V_{k}
belong to
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}
, respectively.

Proof.

The frame vectors represented by
U_{k}
have the same nonzero components as those of
U
,
with additional zero entries. In particular, each column of
U_{k}
has the same norm as the
corresponding column of
U
, so the equal-norm condition is preserved. Moreover,

U_{k}U_{k}^{*}
is a block diagonal matrix with each diagonal block equal to
UU^{*}
, and
therefore has the same eigenvalues as
UU^{*}
, each with multiplicity
k
. Since
U

represents an
\varepsilon
-nearly equal-norm Parseval frame, all eigenvalues of
UU^{*}
lie
between
1-\varepsilon
and
1+\varepsilon
, and the same holds for
U_{k}U_{k}^{*}
.
Hence
U_{k}
represents a frame in
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}^{\varepsilon}
. The same argument
applied to
V
and
V_{k}
shows that
V_{k}
represents a frame in
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}
.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
with
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}=1/2
, and let
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{d}\in\mathbb{R}

with
b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+\cdots+b_{d}^{2}=1/2
. Then the expression

Y=(a_{1}-b_{1})^{2}+(a_{2}-b_{2})^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{d}b_{i}^{2}

is minimized only when
b_{3}=\cdots=b_{d}=0
.

Proof.

This result follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
∎

Lemma 4.15
.

The frame represented by
V_{k}
is the closest ENPF to the frame represented by
U_{k}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the synthesis operator of an arbitrary frame in
\mathcal{F}_{2k,4k}
. We decompose
H
as

H=H^{\mathrm{diag}}+H^{\mathrm{off}}
, where
H^{\mathrm{diag}}
retains the

2\times 4
diagonal blocks of
H
and sets all other entries to zero, and
H^{\mathrm{off}}

retains the remaining entries and sets the diagonal blocks to zero. Since
U_{k}
is block diagonal,

U_{k}-H^{\mathrm{diag}}
and
H^{\mathrm{off}}
have disjoint support, and therefore

\|U_{k}-H\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\|U_{k}-H^{\mathrm{diag}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|H^{\mathrm{off}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Denote the columns of
U_{k}
by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{4k}
and the columns of
H
by
h_{1},\ldots,h_{4k}
.
For each
i
, write
h_{i}=h_{i}^{\prime}+h_{i}^{\prime\prime}
, where
h_{i}^{\prime}
is the part supported on the diagonal
block containing
f_{i}
and
h_{i}^{\prime\prime}
is the remaining part. Since
\|f_{i}\|^{2}=\|h_{i}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}
and

\|h_{i}\|^{2}=\|h_{i}^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|h_{i}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}
, we have

\|U_{k}-H\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{4k}\left(\|f_{i}-h_{i}^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|h_{i}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}\right).

Each summand has the form treated in Lemma
4.14
: the nonzero components of
f_{i}

play the role of
(a_{1},a_{2})
and the components of
h_{i}
play the role of
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})
,
all with fixed squared norm
\frac{1}{2}
. By the lemma, each summand is minimized only when
h_{i}^{\prime\prime}=0
.
Therefore the minimizer
H
must be block diagonal.

Since
H
is block diagonal and represents an equal-norm Parseval frame, each
2\times 4

diagonal block
H_{i}
of
H
itself represents an ENPF. Therefore

\|U_{k}-H\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|H_{i}-U\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq k\,\|V-U\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

where the inequality follows from the fact that the frame represented by
V
is the closest ENPF to the frame represented by
U
. This lower bound is attained when

H=V_{k}
, so the frame represented by
V_{k}
is the closest ENPF to the
frame represented by
U_{k}
.
∎

Combining the above lemmas yields:

Proposition 4.16
.

The minimum squared distance from the frame represented by
U_{k}
to
\mathcal{F}_{d,n}
satisfies

\|V_{k}-U_{k}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=k\,\|V-U\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq k\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{4}=\frac{d\,\varepsilon}{8}.

In particular, for any
\varepsilon
-nearly ENPF in the regime
d=2k
,
n=4k
, the distance to the closest ENPF grows linearly in
\varepsilon
, showing that the bound of order
\varepsilon^{2}d
cannot hold uniformly over all

\varepsilon
-nearly ENPFs.

This family of examples therefore proves Proposition
4.9
.
