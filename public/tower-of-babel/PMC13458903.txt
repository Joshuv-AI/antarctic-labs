Beyond Wall Thickness
Authors: Wei Jin, Yili Chen, Qing Zhang, Daoquan Peng, Zexuan Wu, Jiayu Bu, Yong Huo, Junbo Ge, Yugang Dong, Jiefu Yang
Published: 2026

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), defined echocardiographically by a maximal end-diastolic wall thickness of >12 mm in men or >11 mm in women, is among the most frequent structural cardiac abnormalities encountered in clinical practice.1 Its etiological spectrum is remarkably broad: While systemic hypertension accounts for over 40% of cases, the differential diagnosis encompasses hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), cardiac amyloidosis, Fabry disease, and a variety of other heritable or infiltrative disorders.2 A critical and underappreciated pitfall is that wall thickness alone is an unreliable guide to the underlying etiology. Morphological severity does not reliably distinguish between expected hemodynamic adaptation and progressive infiltrative or storage processes. Consequently, when severe LVH occurs in the absence of proportionate hypertension or valvular heart disease, the clinical consequences of misattribution are substantial. Accurate differentiation demands a shift away from isolated anatomical parameters toward a broader principle of phenotype-guided diagnosis, systematically integrating clinical, electrocardiographic (ECG), and imaging “red flags” to unmask the specific underlying pathophysiology.

The therapeutic imperative for accurate phenotypic and etiological diagnosis has never been greater. Disease-modifying therapies now exist for obstructive HCM (mavacamten), transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (ATTR-CA; tafamidis), and Fabry disease (enzyme replacement and chaperone therapy), each with demonstrated outcome benefit when initiated promptly.3, 4, 5 However, diagnostic delays for hereditary cardiomyopathies remain substantial. Globally, the mean delay from symptom onset to diagnosis averages 5 to 6 years for ATTR-CA and exceeds 13 years for Fabry disease.6,7 Similar delays are anticipated in Asian populations because of limited specialist access. These conditions are increasingly recognized in the region, with hereditary ATTR-CA affecting an estimated 18.9 to 74.9 per 100,000 individuals in China8 and Fabry disease occurring in approximately 1 in 100,000 people globally.5

To bridge this diagnostic gap, we propose a structured, 2-step clinical pathway designed to translate phenotypic pattern recognition into early etiological diagnosis across primary to tertiary care settings.

Two-Step Diagnostic Framework

We recommend a pragmatic, 2-step approach stratified by clinical setting and diagnostic complexity (Figure 1).

A 2-Step Phenotype-Guided Clinical Pathway for Left Ventricular Hypertrophy Diagnosis

(Top) Step 1: frontline assessment (primary care setting). Initial screening focuses on systematic phenotyping to separate common hypertensive LVH from suspicious cardiomyopathies. Key components include a detailed 3-generation family history, multisystem physical examination for extracardiac red flags (eg, carpal tunnel syndrome, neuropathy, or skin lesions), and standard 12-lead ECG to detect “voltage-mass discordance.” Standard echocardiography at this stage serves to quantify wall thickness and identify gross structural abnormalities. (Bottom) Step 2: advanced evaluation (specialized centers). For patients with unexplained LVH or phenotypic “red flags,” referral to specialized centers is imperative for definitive etiological differentiation. This stage uses advanced speckle-tracking echocardiography (GLS bull's-eye mapping), CMR for tissue characterization (T1 mapping and LGE), bone-avid tracer scintigraphy/radionuclide scintigraphy (99mTc-PYP, 99ᵐTc-DPD, or 99ᵐTc-HMDP) to confirm ATTR-CA, and targeted biochemical/genetic testing. Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for inconclusive noninvasive evaluations. Step 1: frontline assessment

All patients with suspected LVH should undergo a detailed clinical history, including a 3-generation family tree and systematic inquiry into extracardiac manifestations (eg, peripheral neuropathy, renal dysfunction, angiokeratomas, carpal tunnel syndrome, and acroparesthesia). Within this frontline work-up, the 12-lead ECG provides critical phenotypic clues: high QRS voltage with repolarization abnormalities typically reflects hypertensive heart disease or sarcomeric HCM. Conversely, low QRS voltage or a pseudoinfarct pattern strongly suggests cardiac amyloidosis, particularly when discordant with echocardiographic wall thickness (voltage-to-mass discordance).9 However, the absence of this discordance, noted in a substantial proportion of cases, does not preclude infiltration.9 Deep inferolateral or anterior T-wave inversions should prompt evaluation for apical HCM, which is frequently missed on standard parasternal echo views. Meanwhile, a short PR interval or pre-excitation implies metabolic or storage etiologies (eg, Fabry disease, Danon disease, PRKAG2 cardiomyopathy). Crucially, around 5% to 10% of sarcomeric HCM cases present with a normal ECG,10 emphasizing that an unremarkable tracing does not rule out underlying myopathy.

Echocardiography should systematically document the distribution of wall thickening (symmetric vs asymmetric, basal vs midventricular vs apical), right ventricular and valvular involvement, papillary muscle hypertrophy, and diastolic filling patterns. Because parasternal off-axis acquisition can artificially overestimate myocardial thickness, obtaining multiple orthogonal views and pursuing cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) confirmation in equivocal cases are essential.

Step 2: advanced evaluation at specialized centers

When initial assessments reveal diagnostic uncertainty or findings suspicious for a rare cardiomyopathy, prompt referral to a specialized center is imperative. Advanced echocardiography should incorporate global longitudinal strain (GLS) bull's-eye mapping and comprehensive provocation maneuvers. Techniques such as the Valsalva maneuver, standing, postexercise echocardiographic imaging, and pharmacologic stress testing are necessary to unmask resting or provocable left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. This evaluation should be complemented by CMR featuring native T1 mapping and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE).10 Targeted biochemical and radionuclide investigations (eg, serum α-galactosidase A activity, serum and urine immunofixation electrophoresis, serum-free light chains, and bone-avid tracer scintigraphy) are then deployed based on the suspected phenotype. Genetic testing and endomyocardial biopsy are reserved for cases in which noninvasive investigations remain inconclusive.

Reading the Phenotype: Multimodality Imaging for Etiological Differentiation

The central thesis of phenotype-guided diagnosis is that the pattern of LVH, rather than its absolute magnitude, provides the decisive etiological signal. Reliable differentiation among the 3 most clinically consequential nonhypertensive causes requires systematic multimodality imaging (Figure 2).

•Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: The HCM phenotype is dominated by asymmetric septal hypertrophy, systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve, resting or provocable LVOT obstruction, and impaired GLS, whose regional distribution reflects the underlying pattern of hypertrophy. In the most common septal morphologies, longitudinal strain impairment is typically most pronounced at hypertrophied segments; in apical HCM, dysfunction predominates at apical levels. This distinction underscores the value of bull's-eye mapping for phenotype-specific interpretation rather than application of a single regional template across all HCM subtypes. CMR precisely delineates wall thickness, identifies apical aneurysms, and characterizes the spectrum of myocardial fibrosis through LGE. The LGE pattern in HCM is heterogeneous: Fibrosis characteristically appears as patchy intramural enhancement within hypertrophied segments and at RV insertion points. In advanced or “burned-out” HCM, extensive LGE may involve multiple left ventricular segments, including regions without overt residual hypertrophy given wall-thickness regression. This finding carries particular prognostic weight: An LGE burden of ≥15% of left ventricular mass confers an approximately 2-fold increase in the risk of sudden cardiac death, independent of LGE location or pattern.10 Sarcomeric genetic testing is recommended in all HCM probands to facilitate cascade family screening.

•Cardiac amyloidosis: The amyloid phenotype demands a strict diagnostic algorithm. Echocardiographic red flags include symmetric biventricular and valvular thickening, biatrial enlargement, and restrictive diastolic dysfunction. The GLS “apical sparing” pattern, characterized by markedly impaired longitudinal strain at the basal and midsegments with relatively preserved apical function, is highly specific for amyloid infiltration. CMR reveals diffuse subendocardial LGE and markedly elevated native T1 and extracellular volume values. Bone-avid tracer scintigraphy, including 99ᵐTc-PYP as well as alternative validated tracers such as 99ᵐTc-DPD and 99ᵐTc-HMDP where available, plays a central role in detecting TTR cardiac infiltration. However, because grade 2 to 3 myocardial uptake may occasionally occur in AL cardiac amyloidosis (AL-CA), serum and urine immunofixation electrophoresis and serum free light-chain testing must be performed to exclude monoclonal proteins before establishing a noninvasive diagnosis of ATTR-CA. ATTR-CA can be confirmed noninvasively only when grade 2 to 3 cardiac uptake is present and monoclonal proteins are absent.9

•Fabry disease: This phenotype is characterized by concentric LVH with prominent papillary muscle hypertrophy and selectively impaired basal inferolateral GLS. CMR uniquely demonstrates low native T1 values, reflecting intramyocardial glycosphingolipid accumulation. This finding is essentially pathognomonic among LVH mimics and is typically accompanied by midwall LGE confined to the inferolateral wall.10 The combination of low native T1, a short PR interval on ECG, and extracardiac features should rapidly trigger plasma α-galactosidase A activity measurement and GLA gene sequencing.

From Phenotype to Therapy: Why Diagnosis Determines Outcome

Phenotypic diagnosis is not merely an academic exercise; it is the direct precondition for effective treatment. Each major cardiomyopathic etiology of LVH now has approved disease-modifying therapy: mavacamten for obstructive HCM,3 tafamidis for ATTR-CA (with RNA-targeting therapies including patisiran and vutrisiran demonstrating additional benefit in ATTRv amyloidosis with cardiac involvement),4,9 and enzyme replacement or chaperone therapy (migalastat) for eligible Fabry disease variants.5 Furthermore, conventional therapies may be actively harmful if applied to the wrong phenotype: Vasodilators and high-dose diuretics risk hemodynamic collapse in obstructive HCM and amyloidosis, digoxin is hazardous in amyloidosis, and negative chronotropes require extreme caution in Fabry disease patients with conduction abnormalities. Delayed or inaccurate diagnosis therefore exposes patients to actively harmful therapeutic choices.

Future Directions

Three priority areas are needed to advance phenotype-guided LVH diagnosis in Asia. First, artificial intelligence–assisted echocardiographic tools trained to detect phenotypic signatures (eg, apical sparing, asymmetric septal thickness) at the point of care could extend expert-level pattern recognition to primary clinics where specialist access is limited, directly addressing the step 1 bottleneck. Second, normative CMR T1 mapping reference values specific to Asian populations remain inadequately defined. Establishing robust regional databases is crucial to distinguish Fabry disease and amyloidosis from sarcomeric HCM and hypertensive heart disease, specifically by identifying both the low-T1 signature of Fabry disease and the characteristically elevated native T1 values of cardiac amyloidosis against an appropriate baseline. Third, multicenter prospective registries enrolling unselected patients with LVH across Asia are needed to quantify disease prevalence, validate the clinical impact of structured diagnostic pathways, and identify ethnic-specific diagnostic thresholds.

Conclusions

LVH is a phenotype, not a final diagnosis. Wall thickness is the starting point rather than the endpoint of clinical evaluation. By reading the full phenotypic signature encoded in ECG voltage-mass relationships, echocardiographic strain maps, and CMR tissue characterization, clinicians can move reliably from pattern recognition to precise etiological diagnosis. The structured 2-step pathway proposed here provides a practical, scalable framework for achieving this goal, enabling the timely initiation of therapies that fundamentally alter the natural history of these diseases across the spectrum of Asian health care settings.
