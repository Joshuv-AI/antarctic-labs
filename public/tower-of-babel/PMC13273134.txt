Metabolic engineering of Saccharomyces cerevisiae for co-production of ergothioneine, salidroside and gadusol
Authors: Leyi Zhang, Sen Xiao, Daoan Wang, Ping Zhang, Ruirui Xu, Litao Hu, Zhen Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ergothioneine (EGT), salidroside and gadusol are high-value cosmetic ingredients valued for their moisturizing, skin-brightening and ultraviolet (UV)-protective properties. Leveraging Saccharomyces cerevisia, a GRAS organism already widely used in cosmetics, we engineered a single strain for their simultaneous co-production. We first identified the 5-histidylcysteine sulfoxide synthase domain reaction as rate-limiting and addressed the poor solubility of ergothioneine biosynthesis protein 1 (EGT1) by screening heterologous homologs and optimizing EGT1 through solubility-enhancing fusions and structure-guided stabilization, increasing EGT titer in shake flask from 34 to 132 mg L−1. Multicopy integration of the optimized pathway into the ribosomal DNA (rDNA) locus further boosted EGT production to 690 mg L−1 in a 5-L bioreactor. Subsequently, biosynthetic pathways for salidroside and gadusol were introduced, yielding a tri-product strain that achieved final titers of 1329 mg L−1 EGT, 224 mg L−1 salidroside and 556 mg L−1 gadusol in fed-batch fermentation. This work establishes a multifunctional yeast cell factory capable of simultaneous co-production of multiple premium cosmetic actives, offering a scalable and sustainable platform for next-generation ingredient development.

Introduction

Ergothioneine (EGT), salidroside and gadusol are structurally distinct natural bioactive compounds with broad applications in food, cosmetics and pharmaceuticas [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. EGT is a sulfur-containing histidine derivative with potent antioxidant activity and has recently garnered attention as a potential longevity-associated micronutrient [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Salidroside, the principal active constituent of Rhodiola species [10], is widely utilized in functional health products and cosmetic formulations due to its anti-inflammatory and skin-protective properties [11,12]. Gadusol, a naturally occurring mycosporine-like compound originally identified in fish eggs, functions as an endogenous ultraviolet (UV)-absorbing agent and represents a promising candidate for eco-friendly, biodegradable sunscreens and photoprotective cosmetics [[13], [14], [15]]. The growing market demand for these high-value molecules has spurred interest in developing sustainable, scalable and cost-effective production platforms.

Conventional production methods, including extraction from plants or animals [[16], [17], [18], [19]] and chemical synthesis [20], are often limited by low yield, high cost and environmental pollution. In contrast, microbial biosynthesis utilizes renewable carbon sources to achieve green, efficient and highly selective targeted production, positioning it as a promising sustainable alternative pathway. To date, individual biosynthetic pathways for EGT, salidroside and gadusol have been successfully reconstructed in various microbial hosts, including Escherichia coli [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]], Corynebacterium glutamicum [27,28] and yeast [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. However, nearly all prior metabolic engineering efforts have focused on single-product synthesis, while integrated strategies for the stable, simultaneous co-production of multiple high-value actives remain largely unexplored.

From an industrial biotechnology perspective, the co-production of multiple products within a single microbial host can significantly enhance process economics by integrating metabolic networks, optimizing carbon flux distribution and reducing downstream processing costs [34,35]. For instance, engineered Bacillus subtilis strains for uridine and acetoin co-production and E. coli strains for succinate and polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) co-production achieved higher substrate conversion efficiency and lower byproduct formation by balancing precursor supply and regulating redox cofactors [36,37]. In the cosmetic field, multi-component formulations often elicit synergistic effects in formulations, providing the skin with comprehensive protection against oxidative stress, photoaging and inflammation [38,39]. This underscores the significant value of developing integrated microbial platforms capable of simultaneously producing multiple functional ingredients within a single fermentation cycle.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae holds unique advantages in this regard: it not only serves as an efficient heterologous production chassis, but also yields a fermentation broth rich in amino acids, peptides and other skin-beneficial metabolites that can be directly utilized as premium cosmetic ingredients [40,41]. Recent studies have further validated its immense potential for multi-product biosynthesis by enabling the co-production of diverse terpenoids in engineered yeast [42].

Here, we report a multifunctional S. cerevisiae cell factory capable of stable tri-product co-production of EGT, salidroside and gadusol. We first optimized EGT biosynthesis by screening ergothioneine biosynthesis protein 1 (EGT1) and protein 2 (EGT2) homologs and identified EGT1 from Neurospora crassa (NcEGT1) and EGT2 from Trichoderma reesei (TrEGT2) as the optimal pair. EGT biosynthesis was further enhanced through N-terminal glutathione S-transferase (GST) fusion engineering, targeted stability-improving mutations and the optimization of the 5-histidylcysteine sulfoxide synthase domain of EGT1. To improve genetic stability, multicopy chromosomal integration was employed to replace plasmid-based expression, enabling robust production in fed-batch fermentation. On this chassis, heterologous biosynthetic pathways for salidroside and gadusol were subsequently introduced, resulting in a single yeast strain capable of stable tri-product biosynthesis. This work establishes a scalable, integrated platform for the sustainable biomanufacturing of high-value cosmetic and nutraceutical ingredients, thereby providing new insights for the development of next-generation multifunctional bioactive formulations.

Material and methods

Strains and plasmids

In this study, all plasmids are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The plasmids were propagated in the E. coli strain JM109. All S. cerevisiae strain were derived from CEN.PK2–1C (Mata; ura3-52, trp1-289, leu2-3_112, his3Δ1; MAL2-8c; SUC2). Detailed information about these strains is available in Supplementary Table 2. Gene editing was performed using the Cre/loxP recombination system. The plasmids pMHyLp-his, pMHyLp-trp, pMHyLp-leu and pY26-TEF1p-ura-Cre required for Cre/loxP-mediated gene editing were obtained from laboratory stocks. All exogenous genes incorporated into the yeast were synthesized by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China) and codon-optimized for efficient expression in S. cerevisiae (Supplementary Table 3).

Reagents

In this study, all primers were synthesized by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China). DNA-related procedures, including purification, gel extraction and plasmid isolation, were performed using kits acquired from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). PrimeStar DNA polymerase from TaKaRa (Dalian, China) were utilized. For PCR validation, we utilized Ex Taq® DNA polymerase, sourced from TaKaRa (Dalian, China). Additionally, all chemicals employed in the study were procured from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Media

In this study, E. coli strains were selected and cultivated on Lysogeny Broth (LB) agar plates containing 100 mg L−1 ampicillin (Amp). Subsequent liquid cultures were also grown in LB medium (10 g L−1 tryptone, 5 g L−1 yeast extract and 10 g L−1 NaCl), supplemented with the same concentration of Amp. Yeast competent cells were grown in YPD medium (20 g L−1 tryptone, 10 g L−1 yeast extract and 20 g L−1 glucose). Recombinant yeast strains carrying pESC plasmids were cultured in medium supplemented with 200 mg L−1 geneticin (G418). For solid cultivation, YPD agar medium was prepared by adding 15 g L−1 agar.

Auxotrophic selection was performed using synthetic dropout (SD) medium containing 3.4 g L−1 yeast nitrogen base (YNB), 10 g L−1 ammonium sulfate and 20 g L−1 glucose, supplemented with the required amino acids (histidine, leucine, tryptophan and uracil) at final concentrations of 50 mg L−1 each.

To eliminate the URA3 marker plasmid, YPD medium was supplemented with 1 g L−1 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA).

Plasmids construction

Two plasmids (pY26, pESC) were used for constructing gene expression cassettes. All relative primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 4. For this amplification, we used the genomic DNA of S. cerevisiae as the template for all native elements, including promoters, genes and terminators. Codon-optimized Ncegt1, Cpegt1, Tregt1, Gfegt1, Ncegt2, Cpegt2, UGT, EEVS and MT-Ox genes were synthesized by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China). The corresponding DNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

The promoters PGAL1 and PGAL10 were amplified from the S. cerevisiae genome, while the vector backbone was amplified from plasmid pESC. The purified DNA fragments were assembled into the pESC vector using Gibson Assembly to construct recombinant plasmids harboring heterologous gene expression cassettes.

Strain construction

We employed an integrated strain engineering strategy involving plasmid transformation, the ribosomal DNA (rDNA) multicopy integration and Cre/loxP-mediated genome editing for targeted gene overexpression and deletion, following established methodologies. Initially, to achieve heterologous gene expression, the parental S. cerevisiae strain CEN.PK2–1C was transformed with centromeric plasmids. The base EGT-producing strain, designated E1, was constructed by introducing plasmid pESC-NcTr, which carries G418 and Amp resistance and expresses the EGT synthase genes Ncegt1 and Tregt2. To enhance enzymatic activity, a second centromeric plasmid, pY26-1 (TRP1 marker), was subsequently introduced. For stable and high-level expression, the optimized synthase gene expression cassette was chromosomally integrated into the rDNA locus via Cre/loxP-mediated homologous recombination, yielding a multicopy engineering strain with improved genetic stability and product titer.

Gene integration and marker recycling were performed using the Cre/loxP system. The target gene expression cassette and a loxP-flanked selectable marker were cloned into an integration vector with ∼1000 bp homology arms. The linearized vector was transformed into yeast via the lithium acetate method, and transformants were selected on auxotrophic plates. Positive colonies were randomly picked for shake-flask fermentation.

For marker recycling, the Cre expression plasmid (pY26-TEF1p-ura-Cre) was introduced. Cre expression led to recombination between the loxP sites, excising the marker. Marker loss was verified on 5-FOA plates (for URA3), and the Cre plasmid was cured by serial passaging, yielding marker-free engineered strains.

Flask cultivation

Three biological replicates of yeast colonies were pre-cultured in 2 mL of YPD and incubated overnight at 30 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The pre-cultures were then inoculated at 2% (v/v) into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of YPD. After 24 h of cultivation, galactose was added to a final concentration of 2% (w/v) to induce target gene expression. The cultures were continuously incubated for 96h at 30 °C with agitation at 220 rpm.

Fed-batch fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor

A single colony from a YPD agar plate was inoculated into 5 mL YPD medium and incubated overnight at 30 °C and 220 rpm. The seed culture was then inoculated at 2% (v/v) into 50 mL of YPD medium in a 250 mL shake flask and cultivated for 12 h under the same conditions. The resulting culture was inoculated into a 5-L bioreactor to achieve an initial OD600 of 2.

Fermentation was performed at 30 °C with an initial agitation speed of 300 rpm. The pH was maintained at 5.5 using automatic addition of ammonia water and 30% phosphoric acid. The aeration rate was maintained at 2 vvm, while the agitation speed was adjusted within the range of 300–1200 rpm to keep the dissolved oxygen level at approximately 30% in the fermentation system. When the initial carbon source in the fermentation system is depleted, fed-batch supplementation commences at an initial rate of 12 mL h−1. The feed solution consisted of 600 g L−1 glucose, 60 g L−1 yeast extract, 1% (v/v) trace element solution and 1.2% (v/v) vitamin solution. After 24 h of cultivation, galactose was added to a final concentration of 2% (w/v) to induce target gene expression. Simultaneously, the amino acid solution was supplemented into the fermentation system at a flow rate of 5 mL h−1. Using an M-100 biosensor analyzer (Sieman Technology, China) to measure the concentrations of glucose and ethanol in the fermentation supernatant.

Trace element solution: 15 g EDTA, 10.2 g ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.5 g MnCl2·4H2O, 0.5 g CuSO4, 0.86 g CoCl2·6H2O, 0.56 g Na2MoO4·2H2O, 3.84 g CaCl2·2H2O and 5.12 g FeSO4·7H2O per liter. The solution was sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min and stored at 4 °C.

Vitamin solution: 1 g thiamine hydrochloride (VB1), 1 g niacin (VB3), 1 g calcium pantothenate (VB5), 1 g pyridoxine (VB6), 0.05 g biotin (VB7), 25 g myo-inositol (VB8) and 0.2 g p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) per liter. The solution was sterilized by filtration and stored at 4 °C.

Amino acid solution: 10 g histidine, 10 g methionine, 10 g cysteine per liter. The solution was sterile-filtered and stored at 4 °C.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis

To analyze the expression of the recombinant protein, the S. cerevisiae cells were first subjected to cell fragmentation. A 1 mL aliquot of the fermentation broth was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm to harvest the cells. The resulting pellet was washed three times with sterile water, resuspended in an equal volume of water, and then disrupted using glass beads (diameter: 0.5 mm). Cell disruption was performed in an Automatic Sample Fast Grinder (Shanghai Jing Xin, China) for 40 cycles, each consisting of oscillation frequency 70 Hz and a 20 s run followed by a 30 s pause. After disruption, a small aliquot of the lysate was examined under an optical microscope to confirm that the majority of cells had been lysed. The lysate was then separated by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min into soluble and insoluble crude extracts. These crude extracts were then mixed with LDS Sample Buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. For protein separation, sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was conducted on 10% Bis-Tris gels using MES running buffer at a constant voltage of 150 V. The gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue dye and the image was captured using a Gel Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA).

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry methods

For qualitative analysis of gadusol, the quantification of gadusol was performed using a high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry methods (LC-MS) system equipped with a photodiode array detector and a mass spectrometer. The liquid chromatography conditions were as follows: a BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm) was used; the mobile phase consisted of water containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (Phase A) and acetonitrile (Phase B). A gradient elution program was applied: 0–4.0 min, 100% A; 4.0–4.5 min, 30% B; 4.5–5.0 min, 80% B; 5.0–5.5 min, 100% B; and 5.5–10.0 min, 100% A for column re-equilibration. The column temperature was maintained at 45 °C, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL min−1 and an injection volume of 5 μL. The detection wavelength was set at 268 nm. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using an electrospray ionization source operating in negative ion mode. The key parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage 3.0 kV, cone voltage 20 V, source temperature 100 °C and desolvation temperature 400 °C. The detection method for EGT was performed according to Yukiko Horie et al. [43]. The detection method for salidroside was performed according to Chen et al. [44].

High-performance liquid chromatography methods

After fermentation, the cultures were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 2 min to separate the supernatant and pellet. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane for EGT and salidroside analysis. The pellet was resuspended in the original culture volume and subjected to bead-beating with glass beads for cell disruption. The resulting lysate supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and then used for the detection of EGT and gadusol. EGT, salidroside and gadusol were determined using an Agilent 1200 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with a C18 column (Thermo Scientific Hypersil GOLD AQ, 250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm).

EGT concentrations were determined using a UV detector set at 257 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 1% methanol (v/v) in water (aqueous phase) and pure methanol (organic phase). The flow rate was 0.7 mL min−1, the injection volume was 5 μL and the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Each run lasted 20 min.

Salidroside concentrations were determined using a UV detector set at 275 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 20% methanol (v/v) in water (aqueous phase) and pure methanol (organic phase). The flow rate was 0.6 mL min−1, the injection volume was 5 μL and the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Each run lasted 20 min.

Gadusol concentrations were determined using a UV detector set at 268 nm. Mobile phase A was acetonitrile and Mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid (v/v); the flow rate was 0.3 mL min−1. The gradient profile was as follows: a linear gradient from 1% A to 90% A (0-10 min), then a linear gradient from 90% A to 100% A (10-20 min), finally return to 1% A for 5 min. The injection volume was 5 μL and the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Each run lasted 25 min.

A calibration curve was generated using EGT, salidroside, gadusol standard solutions at concentrations of 5, 10, 25, 50, 100 and 200 mg L−1. The peak areas obtained from HPLC analysis were plotted against the corresponding concentrations to establish a linear standard curve. The R2 coefficient for the calibration curve was higher than 0.999. At least three biological replicates were analyzed for each strain.

Results and discussion

Screening and identification of optimal EGT1/EGT2 enzyme combinations for EGT biosynthesis in S. cerevisiae

The biosynthesis of EGT in eukaryotes is catalyzed by two essential enzymes: EGT1 and EGT2 [45]. EGT1 catalyzes the methylation of l-histidine to give hercynine (HER), followed by its coupling with cysteine to form hercynylcysteine sulfoxide (Cys-HER). EGT2 catalyzes the conversion of Cys-HER to 2-(hydroxysulfanyl)hercynine [46] (Fig. 1A). Therefore, we systematically evaluated EGT1 and EGT2 homologs from N. crassa (Nc), T. reesei (Tr), Claviceps purpurea (Cp) and Grifola frondosa (Gf) (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1). The combinatorial screening results indicated that strain E1 exhibited the optimal performance (Fig. 1B), achieving a total EGT titer of 34 mg L−1 (including 19 mg L−1 intracellular and 15 mg L−1 extracellular). EGT production was further confirmed by LC-MS (Fig. 1C). Screening of EGT synthases showed CpEGT1 and GfEGT1 were inactive, while NcEGT1 outperformed TrEGT1. Among EGT2 homologs, TrEGT2 and CpEGT2 showed higher activity. Taken together, these results suggest differential host compatibility. The optimal NcEGT1-TrEGT2 pairing benefited from metabolic channeling, preventing intermediate accumulation. This study highlights that optimizing both host compatibility and inter-enzyme coordination is key to constructing efficient yeast pathways.

Improving NcEGT1 soluble expression enhances EGT production

Although strain E1 was capable of producing EGT, its titer remained suboptimal. Previous studies have indicated that the expression of NcEGT1 may serve as a primary bottleneck limiting the synthesis flux [47]. To identify the key limitations in EGT production, we analyzed the expression levels of NcEGT1 and TrEGT2 by SDS-PAGE. Analysis revealed that NcEGT1 was insoluble and expressed at a low level (Fig. 2A). To enhance the soluble expression of NcEGT1 in S. cerevisiae, we evaluated four fusion tags, including thioredoxin A (TrxA), maltose-binding protein (MBP), GST and N-utilization substance protein A (NusA) [48]. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that only the GST fusion tag improved the solubility of NcEGT1 (Fig. 2B). Correspondingly, strain E18 expressing NcEGT1 with the GST fusion (GST-NcEGT1) produced 60 mg L−1 of EGT, reaching 1.8 times the yield of the tag-free control (Fig. 2C). In contrast, the NusA and MBP tags provided only moderate improvements in EGT yield, whereas the TrxA tag showed no detectable enhancement. These results indicate that the GST fusion facilitates the soluble expression of NcEGT1, enabling proper folding and functional expression of the enzyme in vivo, thereby substantially increasing EGT biosynthesis efficiency.

Subsequently, to further improve the stability and solubility of the NcEGT1, computer-aided mutagenesis design was performed using the ProteinMPNN-DDG algorithm [49]. Nineteen amino acid residues predicted to potentially enhance protein stability were selected for site-directed mutagenesis (Supplementary Table 5). Among the constructed variants, a 33% increase in EGT production was observed in mutant E26 (GST-NcEGT1A458P) over that of E18 (GST-NcEGT1) (Fig. 2D). To further improve enzyme performance, the five most beneficial single mutation sites (excluding A458P) were individually combined with the A458P mutation in the E26 strain to generate a series of double mutants (Fig. 2E). Subsequent shake-flask fermentation demonstrated that strain E43 (GST-NcEGT1A458P/S745P) showed higher EGT yields than the single mutant, achieving the EGT titer of 89 mg L−1 (Fig. 2F). We speculate that the A458P and S745P mutations may have a synergistic effect, further enhancing the soluble expression level of NcEGT1 by jointly improving its conformational stability. The above studies demonstrate that the low solubility of NcEGT1 is a key bottleneck limiting EGT biosynthesis efficiency in yeast.

Enhancing EGT production by identifying and engineering the rate-limiting 5-histidylcysteine sulfoxide synthase domain of NcEGT1

NcEGT1 is a bifunctional enzyme comprising a methyltransferase domain and a 5-histidylcysteine sulfoxide synthase domain [47] (Fig. 3A and B). To identify the rate-limiting step in EGT biosynthesis, we individually overexpressed the methyltransferase domain fragment of NcEGT1 (residues 1-357, named TNC1), 5-histidylcysteine sulfoxide synthase domain fragment of NcEGT1 (residues 358-878, named TNC2) and TrEGT2 based on the E43 strain (Fig. 3A). EGT titer analysis revealed that only TNC2 overexpression enhanced EGT production, reaching a titer of 106 mg L−1, whereas overexpression of TNC1 and TrEGT2 did not improve EGT titer (Fig. 3C). In a previous study, Zhang et al. [47] reported that specific mutations in TNC2 (G2S/D109A, F478I, F478V and L447D) could modulate its enzymatic activity. They noted that residues F478 and L447 are located near the catalytic pocket, suggesting these sites may serve as key functional residues. They further attributed the enhanced enzymatic activity to a potential interaction between the mutated residues serine in position 2 and alanine in position 109 [47]. To investigate these mutations, we systematically introduced the corresponding mutations into TNC2. The results showed that only the mutant E50 (TNC2G2S/D109A) markedly increased EGT production, reaching a titer of 132 mg L−1, which represents a 25% improvement over the E45 strain (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings suggest that the G2S/D109A mutation enhances EGT production by improving TNC2 catalytic efficiency, further confirming that the TNC2-catalyzed step is rate-limiting in the EGT biosynthetic pathway. The failure of F478V, F478I, and L447D mutations to further increase EGT titers may be attributed to intrinsic differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic systems in protein folding mechanisms and intracellular microenvironment. Collectively, these findings highlight that targeted alleviation of the TNC2-mediated rate-limiting step is key to enhancing EGT production.

Multicopy pathway integration for stable EGT synthesis in S. cerevisiae

Based on strain E50, we restored the URA3 gene in situ and knocked out the genes GAL1, GAL7 and GAL10, which encode galactokinase, galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase and UDP-glucose 4-epimerase, respectively [50]. Knockout of these genes blocks galactose metabolism and enhances induction efficiency, yielding the engineered strain E50-1. In fed-batch fermentation, EGT titer remained low at 115 mg L−1 (Supplementary Fig. 3A), which we attribute to the genetic instability and antibiotic dependence of the dual-plasmid system.

To overcome these limitations and establish a stable and high-efficiency EGT synthesis platform, we turned to a multicopy chromosomal integration strategy targeting the rDNA locus (Fig. 4A). We first integrated the GAL1/10 promoter-driven expression cassettes for GST-Ncegt1A458P/S745P and Tregt2 (Gene 1) into the rDNA locus via multicopy integration. Transformants were randomly selected from selective plates for fermentation validation, yielding strain E68, which achieved an EGT titer of 50 mg L−1 (Fig. 4B). However, EGT production varied among transformants, with some strains showing no detectable levels. This variation likely arises from two factors: random multicopy integration at the rDNA locus, leading to copy number differences; and incomplete integration of the gene expression cassette during homologous recombination, resulting in failed target gene expression. Building on E68 strain, we further performed multicopy integration of the GAL1/10 promoter-driven expression cassettes for TNC2G2S/D109A (Gene 2) to strengthen the biosynthesis of the intermediate CYS-HER. Among the resulting transformants, strain E100 exhibited the highest EGT titer of 124 mg L−1 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 4), a level similar to that observed in plasmid-based expression systems. Subsequently, by restoring URA3 and knocking out galactose-utilization genes, engineered strain E103 was obtained. We first achieved independent salidroside and gadusol synthesis in S. cerevisiae, verifying the feasibility of their heterologous expression. Salidroside is synthesized via a regio-specific uridine 5′-diphospho-glucosyltransferase (UGT)-catalyzed tyrosol glycosylation [51] (Fig. 5A). Expression of a codon-optimized UGT from Rhodiola rosea in strain E104 supported both salidroside production and its extracellular secretion, achieving a titer of 45 mg L−1 in shake flasks and 596 mg L−1 in a 5-L bioreacter (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. 5A), demonstrating functional heterologous expression of UGT and efficient secretory capacity for salidroside. Gadusol synthesis requires two key enzymes, 2-epi-5-epi-valiolone synthase (EEVS) and MT-Ox (for methylation and oxidation) [52] (Fig. 5A). EEVS converts sedoheptulose-7-phosphate (S7P) to 2-epi-5-epi-valiolone, which is further transformed into gadusol by MT-Ox. Co-expression of these two enzymes in strain E105, followed by shake-flask fermentation and LC-MS analysis, confirmed intracellular gadusol synthesis in yeast, achieving a titer of 355 mg L−1 in shake flasks and 592 mg L−1 in a 5-L bioreacter (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. 5B).

Starting from the strain E103, we first introduced the salidroside biosynthetic pathway to establish a dual-product co-production system for both EGT and salidroside. In shake-flask fermentation, this system produced 39 mg L−1 of EGT and 14 mg L−1 of salidroside (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, the gadusol pathway was integrated to enable triple-product co-production. Under shake-flask conditions, the triple-producer strain yielded 31 mg L−1 of EGT, 33 mg L−1 of salidroside and 114 mg L−1 of gadusol (Fig. 5D). Compared with the single-product strain E103, EGT titer decreased in the triple-producer, likely due to limited carbon and precursor supply in the flask. Interestingly, during subsequent fed-batch fermentation, EGT titer exceeded that of E103. After 96 h of fermentation, the titers of the three target compounds reached 1329 mg L−1 of EGT, 224 mg L−1 of salidroside and 556 mg L−1 of gadusol (Fig. 5E). We propose that under more ample nutrient and precursor availability, the introduction of the salidroside and gadusol pathways may have rewired the central carbon metabolism, redistributing metabolic flux and thereby promoting enhanced accumulation of EGT. Subsequent work will first focus on increasing the production yields of individual compounds, and then, based on the co-production platform established in this study, rationally regulate the production ratios of the three compounds to meet the specific requirements of different application scenarios. Achieving this precise regulation will broaden the applicability of the platform and provide a generalizable strategy for the customized production of multifunctional natural product combinations.

Conclusions

This study developed an engineered S. cerevisiae strain capable of simultaneously producing EGT, salidroside and gadusol. Screening of various EGT synthases identified the combination of NcEGT1 and TrEGT2 as the optimal enzyme pair. However, SDS-PAGE analysis indicated that poor solubility and low expression of NcEGT1 limited EGT production. To address this, protein engineering strategies—including fusion with solubility tags, stabilizing mutations and domain optimization—were employed to enhance soluble expression of NcEGT1. Further investigation revealed that the reaction catalyzed by the 5-histidylcysteine sulfoxide synthase domain was rate-limiting and overexpression of its engineered mutant TNC2G2S/D109A increased overall EGT titer to 132 mg L−1, representing a 2.9-fold improvement over the parental strain E1. Subsequently, the optimized genes were genomically integrated in multiple copies to improve genetic stability, achieving an EGT yield of 690 mg L−1 in a 5-L bioreactor. Building on this EGT chassis, heterologous pathways for salidroside and gadusol were introduced. The final triple-product co-producing strain achieved titers of 1329 mg L−1 EGT, 224 mg L−1 salidroside and 556 mg L−1 gadusol. This work establishes the stable co-production of three high-value natural products in a single yeast strain, demonstrating the feasibility of integrated multi-pathway engineering and providing a scalable platform and generalizable strategy for efficient biosynthesis of multifunctional natural product combinations.
