Decomposing wealth-based inequality in modern reversible contraceptive utilisation in India: evidence from national family health survey, 2019-21
Authors: Puja Das, Tanmoy Ghosh, Pradip Chouhan, Ankita Zaveri, Apurba Sarkar, Salim Mandal, Avijit Roy, Margubur Rahaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThough modern contraceptive prevalence has increased significantly across all wealth quintiles, a substantial gap in the utilisation of modern reversible contraceptives persists among women in lower socioeconomic groups. Nevertheless, very few studies have explored the same. This study assesses the magnitude and underlying factors of wealth-based inequalities in the utilisation of MRC. The study included 202,954 currently married women who are not currently pregnant, are not sterilised, and need family planning, from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey, 2019-21. To address the study objectives, descriptive statistics, bivariate chi-square significance test, and Erreygers concentration index were employed. The results demonstrate that the level of MRC use varies from 41.93% among the poorest to 56.86% among the richest. Particularly, a pro-rich inequality was found in the utilisation of MRC (CI = 0.113), with the pro-rich pattern being more pronounced for short-acting (CI = 0.093) than for long-acting (CI = 0.020) reversible contraceptives. Additionally, pro-rich inequality in MRC is attributed to no media exposure (31.80%), the east region (16.71%), urban residence (14.39%), higher education level (13.15%), ≥ 3 children (8.64%), and the ≥ 35 age group (3.50%). Hence, the study findings endorse scaling up media exposure and strengthening outreach programmes, as well as informed choices through community support in the eastern region, as pivotal for achieving equity in reproductive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27987-w.Keywords: Modern reversible contraceptive use, Wealth-based inequality, Erreygers concentration index, NFHS, India

Introduction

Universal access to safe and effective contraception is an essential pillar of reproductive rights [1]. Family planning not only helps individuals and couples to prevent unwanted pregnancies, enable birth spacing and achieve their desired family size, but also reduces the risk of maternal mortality, unsafe abortions, and adverse birth outcomes [1–3]. Globally, the scenario of reproductive health of women has undergone a transformative shift with an increase in the use of modern methods of contraception from 663 million to 851 million in the last two decades [4]. However, these proportions were unevenly distributed across and within countries [4]. Socioeconomic inequalities still influence access to and use of modern contraception, particularly those associated with household wealth [5]. Women from the poorer households were much less likely to adopt modern reversible methods of contraception than their wealthier counterparts. This may be because women in lower-income households face multiple barriers, such as poor access to good-quality health care, lack of information or awareness, and financial difficulties [6]. As a result, these barriers restrict the advancement of both universal health coverage and reproductive health equity.

Permanent methods of contraception, primarily female sterilization remains persistently high in many developing nations [7]. However, the use of reversible methods of contraception, such as oral pills, injectables, and intrauterine devices (IUDs), remains disproportionately concentrated among women from the wealthier families [8]. Evidence from the previous studies further shows that women in the poorest families often face limited contraceptive choices in the public health care facilities, particularly in the low- and middle-income countries [2, 8–10].

The report of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) shows that inequality in the overall contraceptive use has declined. However, the disparity in the use of modern reversible methods has remained persistently high [11, 12]. This situation is particularly observed in the rural and marginalised urban settings [13]. In many low and middle-income families, sterilisation is often seen as a one-time solution to financial pressures [2]. At the same time, the use of modern reversible methods of contraception continues to be limited due to misconceptions about their side effects and future fertility outcomes [14]. Higher educational attainment and household decision-making power are also strongly correlated with the use of MRCs [15].

Prior multi-country studies [8, 16] examined socio-economic inequality, particularly regarding contraceptive choices and modern contraceptive use. Likewise, studies [5, 12] in India documented socio-economic inequality in access to modern contraceptives using data from the fourth round of the National Family Health Survey, and a few [8, 17, 18] have been conducted at a smaller scale that cannot be applied to a large population and thereby have limited statistical power. While a wealth-differential-based Indian study [2] articulated a contraceptive method-specific degree of inequality, without excluding currently pregnant women, which might affect the actual extent of inequality, it also did not document the decomposition analysis. In particular, there is a significant paucity of studies exploring wealth-based inequality, particularly in modern reversible contraceptives. Thus, the present study findings enrich the literature in two ways- firstly, measuring the extent of wealth-based inequality in MRC by restricting the sample to currently non-pregnant, non-sterilised, currently married women who want to either space or limit their next childbearing using the latest round of NFHS. Secondly, assessing the factors associated with wealth-based inequality in MRC is which is essential for designing targeted, equity-oriented family planning interventions. Moreover, by providing nationally representative evidence, this study aims to inform policies and programmes to reduce these disparities and also strengthen equitable access to modern family planning services across India.

Methods

Data

The latest round of the National Family and Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019-21) [19] was utilised for this study to ensure that the findings reflect the most recent demographic and health conditions in India. The detailed datasets are also available in the public domain (https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm). Since 1992, NFHS has provided information on various health aspects of women, children, maternal, and men through five consecutive rounds, viz. NFHS-1 (1992-93), NFHS-2 (1998-99), NFHS-3 (2005-06), NFHS-4 (2015-16), and NFHS-5 (2019-21). Under the stewardship of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), the survey employed a two-stage stratified sampling design across the states and union territories of India.

Population

The latest round of NFHS (2019-21) covers approximately 724,115 women. However, 202,954 currently married women who are not currently pregnant, are not sterilised, and need family planning were included in this study. (Fig. 1).

Variables

Outcome variable

In the present study, modern reversible contraceptive use was the outcome variable. For assessing this, women were asked whether they were using any method for spacing or averting pregnancy (dichotomised as v312 ‘current contraceptive method). Hence, women responding using pills, IUD, injections, diaphragm, male condom, LAM, female condom, foam or jelly, emergency contraception, standard days method, and other modern methods were dichotomised as 1. In contrast, those using none of those above methods or using traditional methods were dichotomised as 0. Similarly, women responding using pills, diaphragm, male condom, LAM, female condom, foam or jelly, emergency contraception, standard days method, and other modern methods were considered as short-acting reversible methods users, whereas those responding using IUD, injections were considered as long-acting reversible methods users [20, 21].

Predictor variable

Age group (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, ≥ 35); residence (Urban, rural), number of living children (≤ 2 children, ≥ 3 children), level of education (No education, primary, secondary, higher), religion (Non-Muslim, Muslim), social group (General, OBC, SC/ST, unknown), mass media exposure (No, partial, high), and geographical region (South: Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Lakshadweep, Puducherry, Tamil Nadu, Telangana; central: Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh; east: Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal; north-east: Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura; west: Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra; north: Chandigarh, Delhi, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh, Punjab, Rajasthan, Uttarakhand), distance to health facility (Not a big problem, big problem, no problem) were included as predictor variables.

Statistical analysis

Firstly, the study assessed the socio-demographic characteristics using descriptive statistics and weighted percentages to analyse the distribution of respondents. Secondly, the association between modern reversible contraceptive use and the background characteristics of study participants were explored through a bivariate chi-square significance test. Thirdly, wealth quintile-based inequality in modern reversible contraceptive (MRC) use was assessed using the weighted Erreygers Corrected concentration index (ECI) involving probability weight, which is appropriate for bounded binary outcomes and satisfies key properties such as mirror and level independence [22]. The household wealth index was used as the ranking variable to order individuals from poorest to richest.

Concentration curve and concentration index

A concentration curve shows how a health outcome is distributed across different income groups. It compares the cumulative share of the health variable (such as use of contraception) with the cumulative share of the sample population, ranked from the poorest to the richest based on a household wealth Index.

The concentration index (C) measures inequality by quantifying how far the curve lies from the line of equality (a 45-degree line indicating perfect equality) [23]. In simple terms, it is equal to twice the area between the concentration curve and the line of equality. A positive value signifies prevalent use among women from economically well-off families, while a negative value signifies prevalent use among women from impoverished families. The concentration index reflects both how strongly the health outcome is linked to wealth and how unevenly it is distributed. It also indicates the direction of inequality, that is, whether the health outcome is more common among the poor or the rich [24].

Since modern reversible contraceptive utilisation is a binary variable with a limited range (coded as 0 and 1), the standard concentration index may yield biased estimates [22]. To address these, Erreygers’ concentration index was used, which is more suitable for binary (yes/no) health outcomes and measures inequality in absolute terms [22, 25]. Decomposition of the concentration index

To examine the contribution of socio-demographic factors to observed inequality, a decomposition analysis of the Erreygers concentration index (ECI) was performed. The Erreygers concentration index involves two components: the explained portion (the sum of the individual contributions to the determinants) and the unexplained portion (the component). However, the decomposition depicts the marginal effect, coefficient, elasticity, concentration index, and the absolute and per cent contributions of each factor to total wealth-based inequality. A generalised linear model with a binomial family and probit link function was fitted to estimate marginal effects for each explanatory variable. The coefficient indicates the relationship between MRC use and each explanatory variable. Elasticities were calculated as the product of the marginal effect and the mean of each determinant, indicating how changes in explanatory variables relate to changes in MRC use. The concentration index depicts how determinants are distributed across the ranking variable (i.e. wealth quintile). The absolute contribution of each determinant to inequality was obtained by multiplying its elasticity by its concentration index, and the percentage contribution was derived by dividing the absolute contribution by the overall ECI. The residual component, representing unexplained inequality, was computed as the difference between the total ECI and the sum of the explained contributions.

To assess the robustness of the findings, Wagstaff’s normalized concentration index was also estimated for MRC use [26]. In addition, inequality was separately estimated for spacing methods (SAR) and limiting methods (LAR) using the Erreygers index. All analyses were conducted using Stata (version 17.0). Additionally, the mean VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) for all explanatory variables is 1.18, indicating no severe multicollinearity that may distort the analysis. Approximately 48.77% of the currently married women were using modern reversible contraception, which varied significantly by respondents’ socio-economic characteristics. For instance, the percentage share of using modern reversible contraception increases significantly with age up to 34 years. The use of modern reversible contraception was found to be significantly higher among women who resided in urban regions (53.9%), had up to two children (50.75%), had completed higher education (55.34%), belonged to the Muslim religion (51.55%), and had high media exposure (31.55%). Women who belong to the general category (52.58%) or those whose social group was not identified (60.86%) had a higher percentage of using modern reversible contraception. Additionally, a higher prevalence was also observed among women residing in the northern region (47.57%) and among those reporting no problem with the distance to the health facility (50.42%). (Table 1).

For instance, the level of modern reversible contraceptive use varied by nearly 15% points, from 41.93% among the poorest to 56.86% among the richest.

The weighted Erreygers normalised concentration index of 0.113, with a standard error of 0.0025 (p < 0.001), indicates a significant wealth-quintile-related disparity in the use of modern reversible contraception among currently married women. The positive value signifies that modern reversible contraception use is disproportionately concentrated among women from the richest households, reflecting a pro-rich inequality where women from the richest families were more likely to use modern reversible contraception compared to those from the poorest families. Similarly, the concentration curve lies below the line of equality which imparted that modern reversible contraception use is disproportionately concentrated among women from the richest households. A decomposition analysis based on weighted Erreygers normalised concentration index was performed to break down the overall wealth-quintile-related inequality in the use of modern reversible contraception, using selected explanatory variables to assess their contribution to the observed inequality (Table 2). In this study, the combination of variables included in this model explained 72.02% of the wealth index-related inequalities in the use of modern reversible contraception, while the remaining 27.98% remained unexplained and could not be accounted for by the factors analysed. Of this, nearly one-third (31.80%) of pro-rich inequality in modern reversible contraceptives is explained by mass media exposure. In particular, women with no media exposure accounted for 31.80% of the observed inequality. The east region and urban residence also turned out to be significant contributors with shares of 16.71% and 14.39%, respectively. Likewise, significant contributors to pro-rich inequality were higher education level (13.15%), ≥ 3 living children (8.64%), and ≥ 35 years of age (3.50%).

Discussion

Research showed women from the poorest wealth quintile disproportionately grapple with unwanted pregnancy [27], unsafe abortion [28], short birth interval [29] and associated birth outcomes [30]. As per the Guttmacher report, 2019, addressing the demand for family planning with modern methods can drop unintended pregnancies by 68%, unsafe abortion by 72% and maternal deaths by 62%, respectively, in LMICs. Additionally, modern reversible contraceptive is also positively linked with birth spacing and preventing sexually transmitted diseases [1]. However, a growing body of literature has shown lower use of modern reversible contraceptives among economically disadvantaged women in India [2, 5, 31, 32], and elsewhere [9, 33–35]. Amidst several initiatives towards achieving universal access to sexual and reproductive health-care services (SDG 3.7), persistent inequality in the utilisation of MRC pinpoints the urgency to address the needs of family planning of underprivileged women with MRC. Therefore, the study aimed to depict wealth-based inequality in modern reversible contraceptive use in India. The study found that 48.77% of women used modern reversible methods, with use of both short-acting and long-acting reversible methods disproportionately concentrated among the richest women. This pro-rich inequality aligns with statistically significant concentration indices of 0.113 (p < 0.001) for modern reversible methods (SARC: 0.093, p < 0.001; LARC: 0.020, p < 0.001), implying the existence of health inequalities that disfavour poor women. However, it might be explained on the grounds of the legacy of the predominant practice of sterilisation among underprivileged women [36]. Additionally, underprivileged households often relied on public health facilities where inconsistent supply of contraceptives, inadequate staffing, infrastructure, and poor healthcare experiences are common [17, 37–39]. This is very alarming in the Indian context, where more than two-thirds of women rely on public healthcare facilities for modern methods [19]. Therefore, strengthening the public healthcare infrastructure is critical to bring equity in MRC utilisation. Moreover, limited access and autonomy in contraceptive decisions among underprivileged women also impede the use of MRC [40, 41].

Apparently, the coefficients for the no media exposure, east region, and ≥ 3 children were negatively associated with MRC utilisation. Unlike those variables, coefficients for higher education, urban setting, and 35 and above age group were positively associated with MRC utilisation.

Notably, the study reveals significant geographical differences in wealth-based inequality in MRC use, which aligned with prior studies in India [2, 5] and could be attributed to socio-economic, cultural and infrastructural factors. For instance, a negative CI value for the eastern region indicates a lower concentration of wealthier households in that region, which could be linked to restricting access to MRC due to associated costs and eventually reliance upon subsidised services. Previous study [42] have also demonstrated marked health care inequality in the respective region. For instance, the east region exhibited a low rate of safe delivery, where the rate ranged from 20.4% among the poorest to 87.9% among the richest, marking a widespread wealth inequality in the region. Considering this, Mission Parivar Vikas, which was launched to expand the basket of choices by providing free injectables, pills, and financial incentives for contraception shots, needs to scale up coverage, as other districts not covered under the scheme are grappling with inadequate infrastructure [43]. Additionally, no mass media exposure was a significant contributor to pro-rich inequality in modern reversible contraceptives. It signifies a structural failure rooted in inadequate access or gender-based restrictions in media access among the poorest women, which led to an information gap that eventually compounds the service access gap. Additionally, several studies [41, 44] showed that partial/high media exposure amplifies MRC utilisation, as media exposure is positively linked to scaling knowledge and awareness and to dispelling misinformation about modern reversible contraceptives.

Likewise, women having three or more children were negatively associated with MRC use and were in tune with studies in India [41, 45] and elsewhere [34, 46]. This might be credited to undergoing sterilisation upon fulfilling fertility goals, as they intend to take no more risk of conception.

In harmony with prior studies [44, 47, 48], higher education level was found to be positively associated with MRC utilisation. In particular, higher education level accounts for 13.15% of wealth-based inequality. It might be explained in the following ways. Firstly, educated women tend to delay marriage until a later age, which usually shortens their reproductive window [49, 50]. Secondly, educated women are often well-informed about the benefits and efficacy of modern reversible methods and have greater autonomy in contraceptive decision-making [51, 52]. Similarly, urban residence was positively associated with the use of MRC and was consistent with several studies [12, 44]. The prevalent practice among urban dwellers might be credited to the effective role of community resource centres [53], logistical advantages [54], and greater access to family planning services [55].

Consistent with prior studies [40, 56, 57], women aged 35 and above were found to be negatively associated with MRC use. It might be due to declining fertility [51]and adopting sterilisation [58], which align directly with a low median age at first birth (21 years) and a low median age at sterilisation (26 years) in India [19]. However, the findings contradict a previous study in India [12], which depicted women aged 35 or above had twofold greater odds of using short-acting and long-acting reversible contraceptives.

Policy implications

The present study has the following policy implications. Firstly, the predominant role of no media exposure, which magnifies wealth-related inequality, is disproportionately concentrated among the poorest women. The findings suggest Mission Parivar Vikas needs to extend the interpersonal communication strategy through ASHA workers, particularly in the districts of east region, where the concentration of poor women is disproportionately large (CI: -0.1926). Secondly, low MRC use among socio-economically marginalised groups (the poorest women, particularly those in rural settings) needs to be addressed through tailored community outreach by community health workers (ASHA) during the postnatal home visits and subsidised access to MRC. Additionally, improving the availability of contraceptive commodities at primary health facilities and sub-centres, coupled with frontline counselling by community health workers, can enhance the level of MRC use.

Limitations

The limitations of the present study are as follows. Firstly, cross-sectional data do not allow for a causal relation. Secondly, self-reported data may be subject to recall bias. Finally, the lack of adequate data does not allow to capture the enabling role of cultural and social norms regarding modern reversible contraceptive use.

Conclusion

This study highlighted a persistent wealth gap in the use of modern reversible methods (0.090), with a particularly sharp disparity for short-acting methods (0.094). The decomposition analysis further showed that no media exposure, the eastern region, having three or more children, higher education, urban setting, and the 35-and-above age group were major contributors to wealth-based inequality in MRC use. To address the gaps, measures such as scaling up media exposure, targeted district intervention, promoting interpersonal communication strategies through ASHA workers, subsidised access to modern reversible contraceptives in socio-economically disadvantaged regions (east region, rural setting) need to be implemented.
