Type I to type II Boston keratoprosthesis exchange: a report of two patients
Authors: Lourdes Salgueiro Tielas, Michael Bednar, Ramez Haddadin, James Chodosh, Thomas H. Dohlman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo report two cases of Boston Keratoprosthesis (BKPro) type I failure in patients with severe ocular surface disease and to describe the successful replacement of the BKPro type 1 with a BKPro type II, highlighting clinical indications, surgical considerations, and postoperative outcomes. This is a case report of two patients with a history of complex ocular surface pathology—one with Stevens-Johnson Syndrome and the other with bilateral chemical burns—both of whom had previously undergone type I BKPro implantation and subsequently developed complications including corneal melt and backplate exposure. Clinical presentation, surgical intervention, and follow-up are detailed. A literature review was conducted to identify previously reported cases and recommendations regarding type I BKPro replacement with type II BKPro.Case presentationBoth patients underwent successful explantation of the type I BKPro and implantation of a type II BKPro in a combined procedure with pars plana vitrectomy. In one case, corneal melt around the type 1 BKPro developed in the setting of Stevens-Johnson Syndrome prompting replacement with a type 2 BKPro. In the second case, dacryoadenectomy may have contributed to ocular surface instability and corneal melt. Visual acuity improved in both cases postoperatively, with stable device positioning and no signs of infection or retinal complications at last follow-up.ConclusionsTransitioning from type I BKPro to type II is a feasible and effective strategy in patients with severe ocular surface disease and BKPro failure due to corneal melt in the setting of history of Stevens Johnson Syndrome and chemical injury.

Background

The Boston keratoprosthesis (BKPro) is an effective therapeutic option for patients with severe corneal blindness who are not candidates for conventional corneal transplantation and is available in two versions, the type I and the type II [1]. The type I BKPro is the most widely used device globally and is primarily indicated for patients with multiple failed corneal grafts or those in whom a standard corneal transplant is unlikely to be successful, such as in the setting of corneal neovascularization or limbal stem cell deficiency. In contrast, the type II BKPro is a modified version of the type I that features an anterior extension intended to protrude through a full tarsorrhaphy; it is specifically designed for patients with severe ocular surface disease characterized by dryness, keratinization, conjunctival scarring, and abnormal eyelid function. These features are often associated with systemic autoimmune conditions such as Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP), and ocular chemical burns [2–6].

Although the type I BKPro is the first-line keratoprosthesis in most cases, long-term complications such as corneal melting, device extrusion, and infection may necessitate surgical revision [1, 6]. In patients with underlying cicatrizing ocular surface disorders who have a type I BKPro, these complications may ultimately warrant a transition to a type II BKPro, which offers enhanced protection and stability in the context of severe ocular surface disease [4–5].

While there is existing literature that makes reference to patients who had a type I BKPro exchanged for a type II BKPro, no clinical details on such patients have been reported [4–7]. In this report, we present two cases in which patients initially implanted with a type I BKPro later required replacement with a type II BKPro. We describe the clinical course and rationale for replacement, surgical approach, and postoperative outcomes, highlighting the feasibility and therapeutic value of this transition in patients with complex ocular surface disease.

Case 1

A 76-year-old male presented to the Cornea Clinic for evaluation. His systemic history was notable for SJS, resulting in a complex ophthalmic history in both eyes (OU). In 1980, he underwent reconstruction of both upper eyelids with mucous membrane grafts (MMG). His right eye (OD) had a history of multiple failed penetrating keratoplasties (PK) with no light perception (NLP) vision status post Gunderson flap. A type I BKPro was implanted in 2011 in his left eye (OS) but had to be explanted and replaced by a therapeutic (PK) due to a fungal infection. In 2012, a new type I BKPro was implanted, along with an Ahmed glaucoma valve (AGV) to manage intraocular pressure (IOP). Despite this intervention, IOP remained uncontrolled, necessitating the addition of a non-valved tube shunt in 2014.

In 2024, the patient was referred to the Cornea Service at Massachusetts Eye and Ear (MEE) for corneal melt OS. At presentation, his topical treatment was moxifloxacin once daily, polymyxin B twice daily, timolol once daily, and brimonidine three times daily. Oral treatment included doxycycline 100 mg and acetazolamide 500 mg once daily. He was not receiving any systemic immunosuppressive therapy at the time as he had a history of gastrointestinal bleed and reported symptoms of mania and insomnia from oral prednisone used to treat SJS in the past.

During the consultation, the patient reported no ocular pain or changes. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) OS was hand motion (HM), and IOP by palpation was approximately 10–15 mmHg. Slit-lamp examination revealed palpebral keratinization and symblepharon in the fornices. The type I BKPro was well positioned but showed melting of the carrier cornea from 6 to 12 o’clock, with a negative Seidel test and retroprosthetic membrane (RPM) (Fig. 1). There was no overlying carrier cornea in this area with exposed polymethyl methacrylate backplate, as demonstrated by anterior segment optical coherence tomography (Fig. 2). Leak was likely prevented by the RPM growth posterior to backplate, and the patient was known to be aphakic. B-scan ultrasonography revealed an attached retina.

Given these findings, the decision was made to explant the type I BKPro and implant a type II Boston BKPro OS in a combined procedure with the Retina Service, which included pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) and intravitreal triamcinolone injection. During this combined procedure, the supertemporal AGV and inferonasal non-valved tube shunt were left in situ. Following the PPV, periocular injections of vancomycin, tobramycin, and triamcinolone were given away from the glaucoma valve. Prednisone and mycophenolate mofetil were initiated starting one week pre-operatively and continued for seven weeks post-operatively. The patient was hospitalized due to hypertension attributed to the mycophenolate mofetil but was able to complete an 8-week course of immunosuppression. At 48 h postoperatively, the patient’s visual acuity was counting fingers (CF). Four months post-operatively the patient underwent revision of his tarsorrhaphy with tarsal thinning due to occlusion of the BKPro optic with overriding upper eyelid tissue despite regular cleaning of the BKPro optic. Following this second procedure, the patient reported subjective improvement in vision, including increased perception of light and clarity. Six months post-operatively, visual acuity was CF and IOP was approximately 8 to 12 mmHg by palpation. (Fig. 3).

Case 2

This is a 69-year-old male patient with a history of chemical burns and a complex surgical history OU. He underwent multiple type II BKPros due to device extrusion and infection OD, ultimately leading to NLP vision ten years after placement of the first type II BKPro. In the left eye, the patient received a type I BKPro in 2006. OS had an AGV placed supertemporal prior to this BKPro, so no concomitant glaucoma intervention was necessary. Post-operatively, due to phimosis of the eyelid skin covering the device optic, multiple eyelid skin revisions with mitomycin C were performed. In 2018, due to recurrence of this complication and visually significant scratches on the type I BKPro optic, a type I BKPro exchange was performed. During this procedure, the supertemporal tube was flushed with balanced salt solution and a cyclophotocoagulation transscleral with diode laser was performed. Following this BKPro, the patient remained stable aside from recurrent skin phimosis which progressed over the patient’s entire ocular surface and encroached on the optic. This required periodic skin revisions to clear the visual axis. It was hypothesized that the patient’s continued tear production was contributing, so a complete dacryoadenectomy was performed [7]. Vision during this period remained between 20/200 and 20/400.

One month following dacryoadenectomy, the patient presented with complaints of ocular pain, eyelid inflammation, and increased discharge OS. On examination, BCVA was CF at 3 feet. Clinical evaluation revealed erythema and soft edema with mild induration at the supertemporal margin of the upper eyelid. Slit-lamp examination showed a type I BKPro in place with supertemporal retraction of the overlying skin clear of the optic, and exposure of the backplate due to carrier cornea melting with positive Seidel test (Fig. 4). Microbiological cultures returned negative for bacterial and fungal growth. B-scan ultrasonography ruled out retinal or choroidal detachments.

Considering these findings, the decision was made to explant the type I BKPro and place a type II BKPro with a concomitant PPV. This patient received the same periocular injections described in case 1. The surgical procedure was completed without complication and the AGV was left in situ.

At 72 h postoperatively, the patient’s visual acuity was HM, improving to 20/300 with pinhole correction. The type II BKPro was well positioned, with good skin apposition. One month after surgery, BCVA improved to 20/125, with the type II BKPro with good skin apposition. At ten months post-operatively, VA was 20/200 with the type II BKPro well positioned, no signs of infection, and an attached retina (Fig. 5). This patient was not on immunosuppression pre- or post-operatively.

Discussion and conclusions

The type I BKPro remains the most widely used artificial cornea, particularly in patients with multiple failed PKs [1]. However, outcomes in eyes with severe cicatrizing ocular surface disease are less predictable. Alexander et al. reported significantly higher rates of corneal stromal necrosis (59% vs. 8%, P < 0.001) and corneal epithelial defects (59% vs. 24%, P < 0.001) in patients with SJS undergoing type I BKPro implantation compared with other indications. These differences reflect the chronic inflammatory and keratinizing surface environment characteristic of this disease and led to significantly higher rates of device failures (0.306 failures per eye year vs. 0.068, P < 0.001) [2]. Similarly, Salvador-Culla et al. demonstrated that in severe chemical burns, persistent ocular surface instability contributes to a high risk of corneal melt (30.9%) [3]. These findings provide important context for the clinical courses described in our two patients, who both developed progressive carrier corneal melting with backplate exposure despite long-standing type I device retention.

In such high-risk ocular environments, progressive melt often reflects not only focal tissue breakdown but a fundamentally hostile surface microenvironment. In the first case, longstanding SJS with keratinization, symblepharon, and absence of systemic immunosuppression likely contributed to chronic inflammatory stress on the carrier cornea. In second case, repeated eyelid revisions and subsequent tear film alteration following dacryoadenectomy may have further destabilized the surface. In both cases, backplate exposure and Seidel positivity raised concern for impending extrusion or infectious complications, prompting consideration of surgical conversion.

Conversion from type I to type II BKPro is a potential progression in cases where the ocular surface environment proves unsuitable for maintaining the type I device. To date, there is limited published literature addressing this specific transition. Prior publications describing type II BKPro at MEE include patients who received a type II BKPro after a type I, however the clinical details of those cases were not described [4–8]. Here, our two cases are described in detail and highlight that this approach is feasible and can result in anatomical stability and visual improvement, even after complications.

Alternative approaches to prolong type I retention in severe ocular surface disease have been described. Mahuvakar et al. reported use of conjunctival flaps to enhance surface coverage and delay failure in chemical burns [9]. Arboleda et al. described en bloc minor salivary gland transplantation combined with MMG to improve lubrication and surface stability prior to type I BKPro implantation in SJS [10]. Similarly, buccal mucosal grafting has been used as an overlay technique to provide coverage in eyes with severe ocular surface compromise undergoing type I BKPro surgery [11–12]. While such strategies may be effective in selected cases, our patients demonstrated advanced cicatricial changes with backplate exposure, limiting the likelihood that additional surface coverage procedures would change the long-term prognosis. These cases suggest that once melt progresses beyond a certain threshold, conversion offers the most definitive solution.

Surgical management of the type II BKPro requires careful multidisciplinary planning. Coordination with the Retina Service allowed for simultaneous posterior segment evaluation and intervention via pars plana vitrectomy. We believe that by removing vitreous and any membranes/scar tissue, PPV reduces the risk of posterior segment complications including retinal detachment, particularly in the setting of BKPro replacement and in type II BKPro eyes which have limited posterior visualization both pre- and post-operatively. Both patients had a history of glaucoma requiring prior drainage device (GDD) placement and ongoing medical therapy. At the time of type II BKPro implantation, these GDDs were left in situ. Postoperatively, both patients have maintained stable IOP. Depending on a patient’s glaucoma history, placement or revision of a concomitant GDD may be required.

One postoperative challenge encountered in the first case was progressive overhang of upper eyelid skin partially obscuring the optic. This required surgical revision of the tarsorrhaphy with tarsal thinning, which led to an improvement in visual quality. We believe that this palpebral thickening may be related to the previously placed MGG placed in the upper eyelid decades earlier. This observation raises the possibility that such grafts, which are valuable for ocular surface reconstruction, increase lid thickness and may also predispose to abnormal tissue remodeling or hypertrophy, particularly in patients with severe ocular surface disease undergoing type II BKPro implantation. MMGs remain a first-line treatment to improve the ocular surface in SJS [13]. Surgeons considering a type II BKPro may need to consider lid thickness and the need to excise or debulk prior MMGs. Surgeons may also consider either avoiding MMGs or attempting to place the thinnest graft possible in patients who may be considered for a type II BKPro in the future. In either instance, the MMG may impact lid-optic interactions and necessitate additional surgical revisions.

In the second case, tear production may have influenced disease progression. While in general tear production is protective for type I BKPros, this patient’s entire cornea was covered by skin, obviating the need for a wet ocular surface. Epidermal growth factor and other mediators are known to be present in the tears [7]. The presence of these factors in the tears may have been responsible for the progressive phimosis occluding the patient’s optic and this is the reason dacryoadenectomy was performed. Intraglandular botulinum toxin injection may have been a reversible alternative, administered transcutaneously [14]. However, given the limited visualization of the left lacrimal gland and his history of a successful right-sided dacryoadenectomy in the setting of a type II BKPro, left-sided dacryoadenectomy was performed. Following dacryoadenectomy, the combination of altered tear composition and iatrogenic aqueous tear deficiency may have contributed to carrier corneal compromise and melt surrounding the type I device. While causation cannot be definitively established, this case underscores the delicate balance between managing perioptic tissue proliferation and preserving sufficient surface lubrication in eyes with complex cicatricial disease.

Together, these cases demonstrate that transition from type I to type II BKPro is a viable and effective strategy in select patients with end-stage cicatrizing ocular surface disease who develop progressive type I failure. Early recognition of melt and timely surgical conversion may prevent complications such as complete device extrusion or endophthalmitis. Careful patient selection, interdisciplinary surgical coordination, and vigilant postoperative monitoring remain critical to optimizing long-term anatomical and functional outcomes.
