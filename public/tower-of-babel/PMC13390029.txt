Predicting Aquatic Species Sensitivity Distributions Using Machine Learning in a Regulatory Setting
Authors: Jordi Minnema, Markus Viljanen, Emiel Rorije, Willie J.G.M. Peijnenburg, Pim N. H. Wassenaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The assessment of chemical risks is increasingly challenged by insufficient toxicological data available for the large number of marketed substances. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) offer promising avenues to bridge these data gaps through model-based predictions. However, the integration of these methodologies into risk assessment practices remains limited, largely due to issues surrounding (regulatory) trust in the model outcomes. We developed an ML model to predict the ecotoxicity of chemicals across a broad range of aquatic species. These predictions are utilized to construct Species Sensitivity Distributions (SSDs), which constitute a key tool in environmental risk assessment. Trust of risk assessors in the SSDs is essential for use in chemical risk assessment, especially for modeled SSDs. Hence, besides quantitative performance evaluation, we also performed an extensive qualitative validation encompassing aspects such as interpretability and transparency. However, to truly advance the development of ML models for risk assessment, it is crucial to foster interdisciplinary collaboration to enhance the applicability of these technologies within regulatory frameworks. Therefore, this study emphasizes the importance of regulatory acceptance when developing new ML models for SSD predictions. We highlight future opportunities for ML in SSD prediction, while also addressing the challenges of qualitative model validation. As such, this work aims to stimulate the discussion on advancing in silico methodologies beyond the current state of the art and bridging the gap between the technological efforts made in the field of AI and the regulatory needs of chemical risk assessors.

Introduction

Chemical hazard assessment is commonly performed on the basis of well-defined protocols in which all the steps, starting with data generation and culminating in risk limits, are explicitly defined in detail. − Data generation is a cumbersome process which is costly and time-consuming, while typically requiring animal testing for hazard assessment. It also needs to be realized that man and the environment are potentially exposed to thousands of chemicals, while technological advancements result in strongly increasing numbers of newly developed compounds, some of which of increasing complexity in terms of chemical functionalities and associated potential risks. Thereupon, given the trend of reducing the time-to-market of novel materials and products, regulators and regulatory agencies are being faced with the increasing challenge of doing swift and at the same time thorough risk assessment of chemicals while being handicapped by a lack of reliable fate and effect data for suites of chemicals of widely differing composition. It is estimated that reliable and regulatory compliant data is lacking for about two-thirds of all chemicals being marketed, despite decades of efforts in development of standardized test protocols.

One application that particularly faces the challenge of insufficient ecotoxicological data is the generation of species-sensitivity distributions (SSDs). SSDs have become key tools in environmental risk assessment and can be used to derive chemical concentrations for which at least 95% of the species does not suffer effects, thereby protecting the functioning of ecosystems. As can be seen in table R.10–4 of the REACH Guidance, the SSD as a method to derive a Predicted No Effect Concentration (PNEC) for use in ecotoxicological risk assessment is considered the most reliable, having the smallest required additional assessment factor. However, since ecotoxicological data of various different species from several taxonomic groups is required to reliably derive such SSDs, the application of SSDs in regulatory risk assessment remains limited.

At the same time, we are currently facing an enormous advance of novel machine learning (ML)-based technologies that have the potential to revolutionize not only society at large but also chemical risk assessment. In terms of environmental hazard assessment, ML provides the unique opportunity to reveal the wealth of information that is enclosed in the available data on hazard endpoints of chemical substances. The general paradigm of “similar” chemicals sharing similar fate and toxicity profiles potentially allows ML to disentangle qualitative and quantitative patterns of toxicity across hitherto hidden information on chemicals. Various examples of successful development of in silico ML models for toxicity prediction have been published recently. − It is obvious that the field of ML-based predictive toxicology will advance further in the near future, and it is also obvious that several new tools will be made available for, among others, regulators to advance and speed up chemical risk assessment. In exploiting these new tools for the purpose of making informed regulatory decisions, it is essential that regulators as well as additional stakeholders gain trust in the model output and are willing to accept the outcomes.

By now, we have reached the point where models need to be made applicable for regulators and other stakeholders to stimulate their regulatory application. For this purpose, it is not only important for the model to generate accurate predictions on the chemicals in the applicability domain, but the model also needs to be interpretable in terms of the predictions, and transparent in data curation and applied training procedures. Ideally, risk assessors and computational modelers should work together to dynamically build trust into ML models to facilitate their regulatory acceptance.

In view of these considerations, the objective of this study was to develop an in silico model to generate species sensitivity distributions (SSDs) for aquatic toxicity of organic chemicals in regulatory settings. Specifically, an ML model was trained to generate predictions of the toxicity of chemicals for a large set of aquatic species, which are subsequently cumulatively aligned in an SSD. The focus of this study was not to solely optimize the quantitative performance of the model, but also to consider various aspects that may affect trust in the model for risk assessment applications. As such, besides the quantitative validation of the model, qualitative validation and specific model design choices are given a prominent role in this contribution in an effort to bridge the gap between computational modelers and risk assessors.

In the following sections, additional background information on the regulatory use of SSDs is given. In addition, we provide further details on the ML model that was developed, and we describe general aspects that can influence trust in ML models for chemical risk assessment. In Section , we demonstrate both the quantitative and qualitative validation of the predictive model. In addition, we elaborate on the advantages and drawbacks of the model and highlight key elements that may aid regulatory use of the model. This paper concludes with various open research questions and future perspectives on the development and use of ML models by risk assessors to derive SSDs for chemical risk assessment. While the primary objective of this study is not to develop a fully optimized predictive model, but rather to explore design choices that facilitate regulatory acceptance, we recognize the importance of situating model performance within the broader landscape of existing approaches. Therefore, where relevant, we provide limited contextual comparisons to alternative modeling strategies to illustrate the potential positioning of the proposed framework.

Background on Species Sensitivity Distribution

As stated above, the hazards associated with the presence of chemicals in ecosystems are primarily determined on the basis of ecotoxicity data typically generated in the laboratory. To facilitate the generation of reliable and useful data for relevant endpoints and organisms, ecotoxicity tests are commonly performed according to internationally accepted test protocols published by bodies like the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). Results of acute tests are usually expressed as the concentration associated with 50% lethality (LC50) of the test organisms or 50% of effect (EC50) for sublethal effects such as algae population growth rate. Chronic tests are typically used to derive a no-observed effect concentration (NOEC), which is the lowest exposure concentration at which no adverse effect is observed, or an effect concentration which is considered equivalent to nonrelevant changes in performance (e.g., EC10). Results from acute and chronic tests can both be used to derive ecotoxicological risk limits, which are generally referred to as the Predicted No Effect Concentration (PNEC). PNECs may be derived for short-term peak exposure or intermittent release, , and for chronic exposure, the latter of which is preferably based on chronic data. Depending on the amount and type of data available, laboratory-based PNECs can be derived using a deterministic method by applying an assessment factor on the lowest credible test result, or by using a probabilistic approach by applying SSDs. An SSD represents the cumulative frequency distribution of results of toxicity testing of individual (different) species (Figure ) from which a concentration is derived protecting x% of the species from adverse effects. In most regulatory contexts, the Hazardous Concentration for 5% of the species (HC5) is used, and an assessment factor is applied to account for any remaining uncertainty relating to e.g., the quality of the data set, diversity and representativeness of the taxonomic groups, statistical uncertainties, and evidence from field data. ,, SSDs are used to derive environmentally safe levels of chemicals for prospective regulatory risk assessment and quality standard setting. In addition, the SSD concept allows for diagnostic assessment of the fraction of species potentially affected given a certain (environmental) exposure concentration.

The application of SSDs in regulatory risk assessment is currently hampered by the lack of sufficient experimental data for a suite of biological species, since most European regulatory frameworks typically require an SSD to contain ecotoxicity data of at least 10 different aquatic species (preferably 15) covering at least 8 taxonomic groups. , This lack of experimental data may potentially be solved by using ML-based predictions. Various research groups have reported on in silico models suited for this purpose − and various types of models have been developed, as summarized in Wassenaar et al. In the present study, we developed an ML-based in silico model to generate SSDs, and particularly evaluated its use and reliability for regulatory settings.

Methods

Data Collection and Curation

In the present study, a large data set was acquired to train a ML model to predict aquatic ecotoxicity values and derive an SSD. To this end, the ECOTOX Knowledgebase was used as a starting point to obtain ecotoxicity data for a wide variety of chemicals and species. In the following subsections, we describe the curation and processing of the ECOTOX Knowledgebase, the translation of chemical, species and test characteristics into machine-readable features (i.e., feature representation), and the acquisition of the additional features.

ECOTOX Data

The ECOTOX Knowledgebase is one of the largest public databases containing experimental ecotoxicity values. Chemicals that are used in the experiments are identified through their Chemical Abstract Service (CAS) number, and organisms used in the experiments are identified through their Latin species name. For each experiment, the corresponding test duration is also reported.

The ECOTOX Knowledgebase was downloaded on 15–03–2023, and several curation and filtering steps were performed. First, the data set was filtered such that only data of aquatic organisms were included, while making sure that the data were obtained in laboratory settings and via aquatic exposure. Data were excluded if exposure concentrations were not expressed as a concentration or if they were not convertible to a concentration, i.e., a concentration expressed in units of mass per volume of water. Subsequently, the units of the remaining exposure concentrations were converted into mg/L. Any exposure concentration higher than three times the predicted water solubility was deemed unrealistic and thus excluded from the data set. Data were excluded if no exposure duration was reported, if the exposure duration was not expressed in units of time, if exposure duration was shorter than observed duration (i.e., evaluation of postexposure effects). Furthermore, data were excluded if the exposure duration was shorter than 1 h, which effectively results in the exclusion of bacteria tests. This filtering step also resulted in the exclusion of many studies that did not primarily focus on general aquatic toxicity, such as studies targeting highly specific endpoints. Toxicity data were included if they were expressed as a lethal concentration 50% (LC50), no observed effect concentration (NOEC), or effect concentration 50% (EC50). If the EC50 described the effect of mortality, the endpoint was considered as LC50. EC50 and NOEC effect types were limited to growth, development, morphology and reproduction effects. Toxicity data obtained for chemicals that did not pass the QSAR-ready chemical curation workflow were excluded as well. This includes metals or chemicals containing metal-ions, and multicomponent substances. In addition, toxicity data for ambiguous species, without a clear description of the species level (e.g., fish, instead of rainbow trout), as well as fungi, and protozoa were excluded. All data processing and data curation steps were performed using an R-script that is publicly accessible through Github (https://github.com/rivm-syso/predictive_toxicology).

Feature Representation

Three different types of features were used for model development: chemical, species and test-related features. As described before, all chemicals in the curated ECOTOX data set were characterized by a CAS number. These CAS numbers were used to couple them to a Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) representation of the chemical. These SMILES representations were standardized and neutralized using the QSAR-ready workflow described by Mansouri et al. The QSAR-ready SMILES representation of each chemical was used to create 1024-bit morgan fingerprints (ECFP6), and the 166-bit Molecular ACCess System (MACCS) fingerprints using rcdk. These molecular fingerprints encode the SMILES as a bit-string of ‘0′s and ‘1′s, representing the presence and absence of specific chemical fragments.

In addition to the molecular fingerprints, 16 structural descriptors (e.g., number of aromatic atoms, number of rotatable bonds) and 9 physicochemical properties (e.g., octanol–water partition coefficient, water solubility) were acquired for each chemical by using OPERA (v2.9). Note that OPERA uses the open-source PaDEL-Descriptor tool that in turn utilizes the Chemistry Development Kit (CDK). All structural and physicochemical descriptors were modeled as continuous numeric variables.

Furthermore, various mode of action classifications for eco and/or human toxicity were used as input for the model which were predicted for each chemical by using five profilers in the OECD QSAR toolbox (v4.5) (https://qsartoolbox.org/): (1) Acute aquatic toxicity MOA by OASIS, (2) Acute aquatic toxicity classification by Verhaar (Modified), (3) Aquatic toxicity classification by ECOSAR, (4) Toxic hazard classification by Cramer and (5) iSafeRat Mechanisms of toxic Action profiler. The mode of action classifications 1 and 2 are categorical variables, whereas the mode of action classifications 3, 4, and 5 were converted into binary descriptors. For this conversion, each profiler result was mapped to a vector with length equal to the total number of unique results generated by the profiler. All elements of the vector are set to 0 except for the index corresponding to the given profiler results, which are set to 1. Thus, a separate descriptor was created for each unique profiler result, that was 1 if that result was the output of the given profiler and 0 if the result was not given.

Organisms in the data set were represented as one categorical variable with 1310 categories that each represent a unique species.

The exposure duration was also included as a descriptor. However, the absolute exposure duration does not reflect differences in how time affects the development of various organisms. For example, water fleas usually have shorter lives than fish. Consequently, an exposure duration of a few days may be considered chronic exposure for some species, whereas it may be considered acute exposure to others. In risk assessment, reference acute and chronic exposure durations are therefore defined for each type of organism in various standardized OECD tests (OECD 2025). In this study, we used reference durations to normalize the absolute exposure duration as follows harmonizedduration=exposuredurationreferenceduration

Thus, a harmonized duration of 1 represents an exposure duration that is equal to the reference duration. If a chronic test is considered, then the exposure duration was normalized using the reference chronic duration. Similarly, if an acute test is considered, then the exposure duration was normalized using the reference acute duration. All NOECs were considered as chronic and EC50 and LC50 as acute. The used reference durations are shown in Supporting Information S.1.

Finally, two additional categorical descriptors were used to define the endpoint (NOEC, LC50 or EC50) and the effect measured (mortality, growth, morphology, development, and reproduction).

Data Description

The resulting data set consisted of 1343 descriptors and 67197 unique toxicity measurements. Furthermore, the data set contained 2702 unique chemicals based on QSAR-ready SMILES and 1310 unique species. The majority of toxicity values refer to LC50 (n = 52262), followed by NOECs (n = 11901) and EC50 (n = 3034). The majority of measurements involved standard test species according to the ECOTOX Knowledgebase (n = 44653).

Model Description and SSD Generation

In the present study, we used the eXtreme Gradient boosting (XGBoost) model to predict the ecotoxicity of chemicals. The choice of using the XGBoost model was based on both its performance and simplicity. The XGBoost algorithm is similar to a random forest in that it learns an ensemble of decision trees. However, instead of performing a random bootstrap of the data, the XGBoost algorithm uses the so-called boosting technique, where a weak decision tree is improved by combining it with other weak decision trees to generate a collectively stronger model. The XGBoost model was implemented in Python using the package xgb. The learning rate was tuned and set at 0.1 and the number of trees was set at 1135 (see Section ).

The model was trained to predict aquatic ecotoxicity values based on the data described in Section . After training, the model can be used to predict aquatic ecotoxicity values and generate SSDs. To generate SSDs, aquatic ecotoxicity values need to be predicted for a large number of species. Although a total of 1310 unique species were included in the training data set, for many of these species no standardized test guidelines are available. The absence of standardized test guidelines could influence the variability in test results for these species as a wider variety of experimental test setups could have been applied. We therefore selected only those species that were marked as standardized test species in the ECOTOX Knowledgebase for generating SSDs. This resulted in the inclusion of 128 standardized test species in the predicted SSD to assess acute toxicity endpoints, i.e., LC50, whereas 84 standardized test species were included in the predicted SSD for chronic endpoints, i.e., NOEC. It must be noted that the generation of SSDs was done for LC50 and NOEC data. EC50 values were used for training, but not for prediction because of the limited size of the data set.

As such, the generated SSDs are directly calculated from the selected predicted toxicity values for 128 species for the acute LC50 endpoint and 84 for the chronic NOEC endpoint. Within the SSDs, the predicted toxicity values at a harmonized duration of 1 are included. In addition, for the chronic SSD, the lowest NOEC effect, i.e., the most conservative, of the four predicted effects (growth, morphology, development and reproduction) is included. Typically, SSDs are generated by fitting a log-normal distribution to the available data. However, since sufficient species data are used, we decided to generate the SSD solely based on the point predictions without fitting a specific distribution. The predicted toxicities were ordered based on their values and a cumulative distribution, i.e., the SSD, was created. To derive an HC x value from this SSD, a linear interpolation was performed between the predicted toxicities of the two species closest to the x-value.

Hyper-Parameter Optimization and Model Validation

For model optimization, the data were first split into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%), where the test set contained only species-chemical combinations that are not present in the training set. Then, a 10-fold cross-validation was performed on the training set, and a grid search was performed to find the optimal hyper-parameters. With this procedure, the training data were split up in 10 folds, where 9 folds were used for training, and the last fold was used for validation. This is repeated 10 times such that all data points are part of testing exactly once. Specifically, learning rates of 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, and 3 were applied, and the number of trees was varied between 1 and 1500 in steps of 1. The learning rate and the number of trees were chosen such that they resulted in the lowest root mean squared error (RMSE) on the left-out validation sets. This resulted in a learning rate of 0.1 and 1135 trees.

The performance of the predictive model was evaluated using quantitative and qualitative tools. Quantitative validation of the model was performed by training the model on the entire training set and evaluating its performance on the left-out test set using the RMSE. However, this test set only contains species-chemical combinations that are not found in the test set. With this validation setting (setting 1), it is possible that a given chemical is in both the training and test set if different species were tested. This validation setting would correspond with a situation where toxicity measurements are available for a chemical in some species, but a toxicity prediction would need to be generated for a species that has not been tested before. An additional validation (setting 2) was performed where a new train (80%) and test (20%) split was performed, but where the test set only contains chemicals that are not present in the training set. The second validation setting thus corresponds to a situation where toxicity predictions would need to be done for fully new chemicals. For this second validation setting, the model was retrained on the training set while keeping the same optimized hyper-parameters, and the RMSE on the test set was calculated.

Although these quantitative metrics are extremely helpful to compare models and their performances, it remains difficult to interpret these values and assess the implications of the model uncertainties on practical cases in chemical risk assessment. , As we used these predicted toxicity measurements to generate SSDs, an additional qualitative validation was also performed to aid risk assessors in evaluating the added value of the model. In this qualitative validation, we considered three main aspects. First, we implemented an approach to assess whether a particular chemical falls within the applicability domain of the model, which provides the model user with crucial information when interpreting the predicted output. More information on the criteria used to determine the applicability domain is provided in the Supporting Information S.2. Second, we compared the predicted SSDs to existing SSDs that had been derived from data-rich chemicals. Finally, a demonstration session was organized to allow risk assessors to become more familiar with the model and provide feedback on modeling choices. For this interactive session, a demonstrator tool was developed with a graphical user interface so that risk assessors could run the model themselves and get a better feeling of the model’s behavior and predictions. Furthermore, this session allowed the model developers to guide the risk assessors when using the model and explain the underlying modeling principles when the risk assessors requested clarification. The modeling scripts and functions used to implement the ML model described in this study are publicly accessible on Github (https://github.com/rivm-syso/predictive_toxicology).

Results and Discussion

An ML model was developed to predict aquatic toxicity values and construct SSDs. Quantitative and qualitative performance evaluation are both essential when validating predictive models for use in chemical risk assessment. In fact, the development of predictive models is increasingly shifting toward building trust, as exemplified in recent guidelines and recommendations, including the QSAR validation guidance from the OECD and recommendations. , As our predictive model is specifically developed for regulatory use, we aimed to create a modeling pipeline that reflects the current practice of environmental risk assessment. Accordingly, model development was focused on regulatory application and therefore evaluation of the model should not only include general statistics on quantitative performance but also focus on qualitative performance in terms of interpretability and transparency which are related to the design and choices underlying the model. Aspects related to the model performance as well as model design choices are elaborated in the next sections.

Model Performance

Based on the quantitative validation (see Section ), the model achieved an RMSE of 0.98 in the first validation setting, i.e., new chemical-species pairs, and an RMSE of 1.05 in the second validation setting, i.e., new chemicals. Since the logarithm of the measured toxicity was taken as the predicted endpoint, the model predictions were thus on average 1 order of magnitude off from the experimental measurements. In comparison: a NULL model which simply predicts the mean toxicity across all species and chemicals, resulted in an RMSE of 1.79, and would explain none (0%) of the variability due to chemical structure and species differences. To provide additional context, the estimated variability typically present in experimental LC50 data in the ECOTOX Knowledgebase is estimated to be an RMSE of 0.54. This variability thus reflects other than chemical structure and species differences. As such variability cannot be captured with the model, the RMSE of 0.54 would therefore approximate a hypothetical perfect model that explains all (100%) variability due to chemical structure and species differences. Translating the RMSE to variability explained for the different validation settings would then give the following interpretation: In validation setting 1 the model can explain 76.8% of the variability due to species differences, and in validation setting 2 the model can explain 71.8% of the variability due to both chemical structure and species differences.

To further analyze the performance of the model, we compared predictions generated with the newly developed model with two types of experimental SSDs: (1) those that can be derived from the experimental data in the ECOTOX Knowledgebase that was used for training the model (see Section ), and (2) formal SSDs that have been used to derive environmental risk limits or quality standards. To this end, fluoranthene and imidacloprid were used as examples, representing two very different chemicals with different modes of action. In this example, we predicted a chronic endpoint, i.e., NOEC.

The differences between the predicted SSD and the experimental SSD may be due to several reasons. The first reason is the difference between the exposure durations used for the experimental data points and the harmonized duration of 1 that is used for the predictions. In addition, for some species multiple experimental test results are available (with varying exposure durations and/or effect measures), for which the lowest concentration or a geometric mean is used in experimental SSDs. In contrast, the predictive model always uses the whole range as input, and only plots one predicted effect (i.e., the most conservative predicted effect at a harmonized duration of 1). An example is the available test results for fluoranthene with Daphnia magna in the ECOTOX Knowledgebase, for which NOECs of 1.4, 17, and 35.3 μg/L are observed (Table ), and the geometric mean is plotted in the SSD (Figure A). For the formal SSD, even more data on Daphnia magna were available, and the most conservative effect value of 1.4 μg/L was selected for the SSD. It should be noted that some of the experimental tests for fluoranthene, and particularly the more sensitive tests, are conducted under UV exposure. Under these conditions, fluoranthene may exert photoinduced toxicity, which is a different mode-of-action than narcosis toxicity which occurs without UV-exposure. Data on photoinduced toxicity are not widely included in the ECOTOX Knowledgebase, and likely insufficient to be identified by the predictive model. As such, the experimental SSDs are likely more conservative due to the inclusion of a wider variety of tests and test conditions, whereas the predicted SSDs are potentially more uniform and equal in their direct comparison between species for nonspecific mode-of-actions. Therefore, predicted SSDs are likely less reliable for chemicals that can exert such specific modes of action.

Imidacloprid

Considering the SSDs of imidacloprid (Figure B), an important observation is the rather large difference between the predicted and experimental SSDs. More specifically, over 50% of the species used in the experimental SSDs (both based on ECOTOX data and formal SSDs) are more sensitive than the most sensitive species in the predicted SSD. An explanation for the differences between predicted and experimental SSDs may be the limited number of chemicals in our data set with a similar specific mode-of-action as imidacloprid. As imidacloprid is an insecticide, several taxonomic groups are expected to be much more sensitive to imidacloprid than to nonspecifically acting chemicals. Furthermore, literature data indicate that large differences in sensitivity exist between insect orders, relating to its specific mode of action via irreversible binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. − Despite the inclusion of mode-of-action features in the model, this may have hampered the ability of the model to predict toxicities for such specifically acting chemicals. Besides the reasoning that the model is likely insufficiently capable of acknowledging the contribution of this specific mode-of-action to the toxicity toward some taxonomic groups, it can also be observed that the experimental SSDs have a different taxonomic group representation compared to the predicted SSD. In the formal SSD over 50% of the species are insects, whereas the predicted SSD only includes 4.8% of insects. This difference in species representation also influences the differences between experimental and predicted SSDs.

Altogether, these two examples demonstrate that direct comparison of experimental SSDs and predicted SSDs remains challenging. Experimental SSDs are created using a lot of expert knowledge and evolving insights. This expert knowledge entails, for example, the selection of species, the interpretation and selection of test conditions, the inclusion of biological effects, and ultimately the selection of relevant data points for SSDs, which could focus on more sensitive taxa. Such expert knowledge and evolving insights are hard, if not impossible, to fully capture in a predictive model. Furthermore, in the case of imidacloprid, increasing awareness of its impact on aquatic ecosystems has also led to the generation of more ecotoxicity data on particularly sensitive species groups such as mayflies, leading to a relatively high representation of sensitive species in the experimental data set. If information is available that a chemical has a very specific mode-of-action that leads to very specific species toxicity, it is much more likely that predicted SSDs will deviate strongly from SSDs derived by environmental risk assessors taking into account these specifics. Imidacloprid is a typical example of a substance where these issues play a role. Model performance thus needs to be improved to fully substitute experimental data for regulatory decision making. Nevertheless, this does not mean that predictive models are useless. The added benefit lies especially in using expert knowledge to interpret the model outcomes. Extensive collaboration between computational modelers and risk assessors is thus essential when the model is applied to a specific chemical/species combination.

Although direct benchmarking against alternative SSD modeling approaches remains challenging due to the scarcity of standardized reference data sets, it is informative to position the observed model performance within the context of existing studies. For example, previously reported machine learning and regression-based approaches for ecotoxicity prediction typically report prediction errors in a similar order of magnitude, depending on endpoint and data curation strategies. The RMSE values obtained in this study (0.98–1.05) therefore suggest that the model performs within the range expected for current in silico approaches, while offering additional advantages in terms of transparency and alignment with regulatory workflows. Nevertheless, more systematic benchmarking against alternative methods remains an important area for future work.

Model Design and Use

Besides the extensive quantitative validation of the model, this study aimed to explore model features that influence how a risk assessor evaluates the reliability of its outcomes. In the following subsections, we highlight key modeling features that may play an important role in this aspect. Furthermore, we discuss the applicability of the model in practice, including the advantages and challenges faced when applying such a predictive model.

Modeling Choices

Several modeling design choices were made during model development with the aim of stimulating regulatory application. These choices have been made at various parts of the machine learning pipeline, such as data collection and curation, model algorithm, and performance validation.

A good example of such a choice is the inclusion of a harmonized exposure duration in this study. To date, ML models for ecotoxicological risk assessment have generally been trained using the absolute exposure duration as a descriptor. However, due to differences in the life stage and lifetime of biota, time affects some organisms differently than others. For example, an exposure duration of 4 days is typically considered acute for fish, whereas it can be considered chronic for algae. To account for these differences, we introduced the harmonized exposure duration as a descriptor to train the model in this study (see Supporting Information S.1). In this approach, we applied general reference exposure durations that were extrapolated to entire taxonomic groups. This method reduces variance by taking species-specific life cycles into account. However, it is not perfect as it does not eliminate all sources of uncertainty. For example, this approach does not correct for differences in test set-ups across life cycle stages. For instance, embryo toxicity tests conducted over short exposure durations may, in fact, represent (sub)­chronic exposures. Additionally, it remains uncertain whether reference durations taken from species-specific guidelines can be extrapolated to other species belonging to the same taxonomic group. Currently, the used reference durations mainly influence the predicted species effects that are plotted in the SSD (i.e., at a harmonized duration of 1). Depending on the use case, these plotted effects could easily be adjusted to other durations based on the user’s preferences. Furthermore, depending on the scope and applicability of the model, different choices could be made that align closely to regulatory settings or biological relevance. For instance, it could also be questioned whether reference durations derived from test guidelines are selected solely based on biological considerations, as most likely also practical considerations are involved (e.g., considering working weeks of 5 days). For more biologically realistic reference durations, the use of species life cycle information might be more appropriate, such as information from AddMyPet, fishbase.org, or more specific dynamic energy budget parameters. − Accordingly, the curation of the input data should be tailored to the intended scope and applicability of the model.

Another example of a modeling choice was the chosen algorithm. A relatively simple and straightforward algorithm was used with the Gradient Boosting algorithm. Although more complex models, such as a (deep) neural network, may perform better than a relatively simple Gradient Boosting algorithm in terms of quantitative performance statistics, complex models are generally harder to interpret and more difficult to use in practical settings.

Our model design was also an important choice that was tailored to current risk assessment practices. The developed model predicts the toxicity of a wide variety of species and plots the predicted effect concentrations of selected species and exposure duration in an SSD, which is similar to how risk assessors gather toxicity information for individual species. Although alternative model designs were also possible, such as directly predicting an HC x value or predicting the parameters of a log-normal distribution (μ and σ), such approaches would result in a loss of species-specific toxicity information.

Besides choices that directly relate to technical modeling aspects, choices were also made to aid and help the interpretation of the model outcomes. One of the choices that we made in the current study was to include four different endpoints when predicting NOECs, i.e., growth, development, morphology, and reproduction. In regulatory practice, risk assessors typically combine several effect types in a single SSD and choose the most critical effect concentration in the case that multiple effect measurements for a single species are available. Accordingly, we applied this approach also in the formation of the predicted SSDs based on the predicted toxicity values.

The above-mentioned model design choices may not be the best decisions for all regulatory applications. For different regulatory applications and frameworks, different decisions could be made. Most important for modelers is to be transparent about the underlying assumptions and decisions related to data collection and curation, model algorithm, and performance validation.

Model Application

To apply a predictive model in regulatory settings, trialability and interpretability of the outcomes is essential. To this end, a simple, user-friendly graphical user interface was built to run the model and generate SSD predictions for a variety of chemicals. Figure shows the model interface and the predictions. An interactive session was organized with a selected group of expert risk assessors to demonstrate the model interface and test the model on several chemicals, including fluoranthene and imidacloprid (see Section ).

Overview of the simple user interface of the model that has been developed to discuss and evaluate the performance with risk assessors.

The graphical user interface was extremely helpful to visualize the model predictions and show the resulting SSDs, as it facilitates the interpretation of the predicted SSD and its usefulness in practical settings. An important observation was that risk assessors primarily evaluated the model based on comparisons with existing experimental data points. If a toxicity prediction for a single species differs more than 1 order of magnitude from the experimental data, or a different species sensitivity order is observed, risk assessors tend to doubt the reliability of the model. The potential uncertainty and variation in experimental biological test results, which can also cover various orders of magnitude, , is often not considered within this direct comparison. Model predictions may therefore also serve to critically examine experimental data and highlight potential anomalies.

Another aspect that relates to the interpretability of the predicted SSD is the approach that was implemented to assess the applicability domain of the developed model. Visual and textual information is provided to the user as to whether the chemical descriptors of the target chemical are sufficiently similar to those of the chemicals in the training data (so-called descriptive similarity, “Is the model able to make a prediction with a certainty comparable to the training dataset?”). Additionally, we determined the quantitative model performance for chemicals in the training data that are similar to the target chemical (so-called predictive similarity, “What is the uncertainty in the prediction for comparable chemicals?”). For more details and for more information on the applicability domain used, see Supporting Information S.2. This is comparable to other approaches , and helps the user to estimate whether the model is likely to perform well for the target chemical. It must be emphasized that the applicability domain implemented in this study was used as an example to demonstrate the variety of considerations that should be made when defining the applicability domain of a model. Various approaches on determining an applicability domain have been reported in literature, − and the most informative method may depend on the use case.

An interesting outcome of the demonstration session was that risk assessors considered the predictive model to be particularly useful as a screening tool in scenarios where very little to no data is available for a chemical. This is mainly justified by the fact that a potential decision on predicted data is more informative than no data at all. Nevertheless, risk assessors also noted that the model application in regulatory decisions would require more experience and practical validation, including success stories. Validation of the model outcomes with formal SSDs of chemicals with less-specific mode of actions is therefore recommended. Furthermore, an interesting avenue for further research would be to apply transfer learning. With transfer learning, a generic model can be trained on the large bulk of the data, after which it is refined on the data of chemicals with specific modes of action.

As such, it would be recommended to apply the model in upcoming risk assessments, in parallel to current regulatory practices, to gain more experience on its usability. Furthermore, risk assessors noted that a potential improvement would be to allow further customization of the output, as this would aid their interpretation of the generated SSD. Examples of such customization are the selection of certain taxonomic groups, the plotting of experimental data provided by the user, and providing a log-normal fit through the data.

In a general sense, feedback provided by risk assessors is indispensable and should be considered when developing new models or new model versions. The points raised above also highlight the relevance of qualitative model validation, as this could never have been captured by solely considering quantitative validation.

Future Perspectives

We are currently witnessing a significant advancement in the development and application of novel ML technologies. In the context of chemical risk assessment, ML offers a unique opportunity to extract valuable insights from the available data on the environmental fate and hazard-related endpoints of chemical substances without the need of performing additional (animal) testing. The overarching paradigm that chemically similar substances tend to exhibit comparable toxicity patterns enables the potential to uncover qualitative and quantitative toxicity profiles across diverse chemical entities, including chemicals without any toxicity data. The opportunities offered by ML thus facilitate more accurate and efficient retrospective and especially prospective risk assessments, with one of the key advantages of predictive modeling being its ability to make predictions across broad ranges of organisms and chemicals.

The promise of ML-based models as developed in this study lies chiefly in their ability to contribute to the identification of chemicals that pose increased toxicity risks to aquatic ecosystems. A critical area requiring deeper examination concerns the capacity of the model to accurately account for chemicals with specific modes of action. It cannot be presupposed that these models, as currently developed, adequately encompass all possible modes of action. This leads potentially to an underestimation of ecological impacts from chemicals with targeted biological effects. However, when models are rigorously validated and specifically acting chemicals are thoroughly incorporated, the ecological realism of chemical risk assessments could be markedly enhanced. This is particularly true when ML-based SSDs are utilized in the calculation of the Potentially Affected Fraction (PAF), which quantitatively integrates the cumulative impacts of all chemicals present at a given location. Historically, PAF calculations have suffered from incompleteness, often due to insufficient data.

Moreover, ecological realism is further augmented by employing these models inverselyto identify the specific taxonomic groups most sensitive to certain chemicals or combinations of chemicals within an ecosystem. This approach not only strengthens ecological risk assessments but also aids in pinpointing vulnerable species, guiding targeted protective measures.

For future SSD modeling approaches it would be particularly interesting to consider uncertainty and variability in the model output and provide an understanding of the contribution of each type of underlying variation, such as the variation within chemical-species pairs, uncertainties that arise from interspecies extrapolation, or uncertainties of the species representation in the SSD. Such additional information could stimulate regulatory discussion and decision-making on the acceptability of the interspecies extrapolation and SSD modeling.

In a broader perspective, the newly developed predictive model provides the option of direct application in materials innovation at the earliest possible stage of substance/material development. The early development stages, i.e., the lower Technological Readiness Levels (TRLs), are typified by a lack of fate and effects data of chemicals that exist primarily on the drawing table. Predictive modeling provides the unique opportunity of early stage identification of chemicals that are safe by design while expected by innovators to bear specific functionalities suited for specific future applications.

All in all, the adoption and acceptance of predictive models by innovators, regulators, and other stakeholders is paramount. A critical prerequisite in this regard is open and transparent communication, coupled with an acknowledgment that some degree of uncertainty will always accompany model predictions. It is inherently evident that ML models of the type discussed in this contribution are often perceived as “black boxes”, requiring stakeholders to place their trust in them. To foster greater confidence, these models should not only be accessible and user-friendly but also as streamlined and interpretable as possible. Transparency regarding the underlying data and the methodologies employed in data curation can play a pivotal role in mitigating concerns about predictive uncertainty. In fact, such openness may prove more influential in overcoming regulatory hesitations than the uncertainties themselvesparticularly in the case of chemicals of concern, where legislative actions must be substantiated with robust and credible evidence. Moreover, besides stimulating transparency and accessibility, it is essential to perform a comprehensive comparison with existing alternative modeling approaches. Such benchmarking would allow for a more systematic evaluation of predictive performance and provide clearer guidance for regulatory adoption. In the present study, we deliberately limited such comparisons in order to focus on model design aspects that influence trust and interpretability. However, future work should explicitly evaluate the proposed framework against established SSD modeling approaches and alternative in silico toxicity prediction methods to further substantiate its added value.

Conclusions

This paper illustrates the advantages and challenges of an ML-based approach to predict SSDs, particularly in the relation to trust in such predictions, which is needed for regulatory acceptance of ML approaches. The model developed in this study can be used as a screening tool for chemicals with no data at all but is in its current form insufficient to fully substitute experimental data for regulatory decision making.

It can be concluded that it is very challenging to align a predictive model to current regulatory practices, particularly due to variation in terms of data availability in endpoints, test conditions, test organisms etc. Often, only a few selected species are tested, or very unique test set-ups are conducted based on expert knowledge and regulatory decisions. Due to this induced variation, the predictive model is also affected, where certain chemicals, species or specific test-conditions are under-represented and accordingly misinterpreted, like chemicals with specific modes of actions. It can be concluded that the quantitative and qualitative validations of model predictions are very important to build trust for further regulatory acceptance, but they are not necessarily straightforward. There is no definitive right or wrong, and the choice of model should depend on its intended purpose. To further stimulate the developments and inclusions of ML-based models in regulatory practices, closer collaboration and interaction between risk assessors and computational modelers is therefore required.
