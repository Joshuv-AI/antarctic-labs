Policy Gradient Learning for Distributionally Robust Markov Decision Processes under Wasserstein Ambiguity
Yadh Hafsi, Samy Mekkaoui, Huyên Pham, Kaixin Yan

Abstract
We study finite-horizon Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) under distributional uncertainty in the transition kernels and develop a policy-gradient framework for Wasserstein distributionally robust control. Ambiguity is modeled by state-action dependent Wasserstein balls around nominal transition kernels, leading to a max-min control problem over randomized policies and admissible transition laws. Since the worst-case transition law depends implicitly on the policy parameters, the usual policy-gradient argument does not apply. We address this difficulty by using a Wasserstein dual reformulation of the robust Bellman recursion and analyzing its directional differentiability. This yields an explicit recursive characterization of the robust policy gradient. Building on this characterization, we propose a robust actor-critic algorithm and illustrate its behavior on discrete and continuous benchmark examples.

1. Introduction

In many reinforcement learning and control problems, the transition law of the system is not known exactly. Even when a nominal model is available, the system may later be tested or deployed in an environment that differs from the one used for calibration. Different data sources, market conditions, or operating regimes may induce different probability measures on trajectories. As a result, a policy that performs well under one reference model may perform poorly when the underlying dynamics change. This motivates robust reinforcement learning, whose goal is to construct policies that perform well under a family of plausible transition laws rather than under a single nominal model.

This issue appears naturally in many applications. In inventory control and supply chain management, demand distributions are difficult to estimate precisely and may vary over time because of seasonality, structural change, or external shocks. Robust and distributionally robust methods have therefore been used to hedge against ambiguity in demand models; see for example
6
,
30
,
15
,
19
. In portfolio optimization,
market dynamics shift across regimes, with changes in volatility, correlations, and return
distributions that may occur at different time scales. A strategy calibrated to one regime may break
down after a regime shift. This has led to distributionally robust formulations under Wasserstein ambiguity
8
, as well as robust continuous-time control under model uncertainty and adaptive learning
12
. In all these settings, uncertainty acts through the law of the controlled state process. In our framework, this uncertainty is represented by set-valued transition kernels
{\cal P}_{t}(x,a)
, and robust optimization over these sets leads to policies that are more stable under model misspecification.

We study finite-horizon robust Markov decision processes with randomized policies and set-valued transition kernels. Our main focus is on ambiguity sets defined around reference transition laws. This setting is attractive because it models local distributional perturbations in a flexible and interpretable way. At the same time, it creates a basic difficulty for policy optimization. Under general ambiguity classes, such as Wasserstein or Kullback–Leibler balls, the worst-case transition kernel is typically not available in closed form. It is selected adversarially according to the value function generated by the current policy, so changing the policy can also change the worst-case model. This makes direct differentiation of the robust Bellman recursion difficult, and generally intractable without strong regularity assumptions on the optimizer (see Remark
3.3
).

Related work.

There are two main lines of literature behind our approach: robust Markov decision processes (MDPs) and distributionally robust optimization (DRO). On the robust MDPs side, the foundational papers
17
and
27
proved robust dynamic programming under rectangular ambiguity sets, showing that the Bellman recursion remains tractable when uncertainty separates across state–action pairs. Since then, robust MDPs have been studied under broader forms of model uncertainty (see
23
for example). MDPs under general model uncertainty are studied in
25
. Policy gradient methods for robust MDPs have recently been developed in
31
, while robust Q-learning under Wasserstein ambiguity is studied in
26
. In a related direction,
13
propose an actor–critic algorithm for robust risk-aware reinforcement learning under Wasserstein ambiguity, deriving policy gradients via the quantile representation of distortion risk measures.

On the distributionally robust optimization side, a general convex framework and tractable reformulations are given in
33
. For Wasserstein DRO, dual reformulations and performance guarantees were developed in
24
, while structural properties of worst-case distributions were analyzed in
16
. The duality result most relevant for our work is
10
, which gives a scalar dual representation for Wasserstein DRO through optimal transport. Related links between Wasserstein robustness and regularization in statistical learning are established in
9
. Sensitivity of robust values with respect to the Wasserstein radius is studied in
3
, with related results under financial constraints in
29
, and dynamic robustness on path space in
14
.

The closest related work is
32
, which studies policy gradient methods for robust MDPs under
R
-contamination sets. In that setting, the robust Bellman operator associated with contamination sets admits a closed-form expression. This makes the policy gradient derivation relatively direct. Under more general types of ambiguity like Wasserstein or Kullback-Leibler ambiguity, however,
the worst-case transition kernel depends on the value function through an optimization problem, and
differentiating through it would require regularity conditions on the optimal kernel that do not hold
in general (see Remark
3.3
). We instead work with the dual formulation
of
10
, which avoids differentiating the optimal kernel directly. Our framework also differs from the robust Q-learning algorithm of
26
, which operates in the infinite-horizon discounted setting with deterministic policies and tabular updates. By contrast, our actor–critic approach handles finite-horizon problems, optimizes over randomized parametric policies via gradient ascent and accommodates continuous state spaces through function approximation. Additionally, our numerical results show that the method remains effective even for large values of
\varepsilon
.

Our main contributions.

We derive directional policy-gradient formulas for finite-horizon robust MDPs
with state–action dependent Wasserstein ambiguity sets. Our approach combines the robust dynamic programming recursion with a Wasserstein dual
representation, thereby avoiding differentiation of the implicit worst-case
transition kernel. Since the robust Bellman operator is generally nonsmooth, our main result is a one-sided directional derivative formula, which reduces to a vector-valued policy-gradient recursion under uniqueness of the dual multiplier and inner transport selector.

More precisely, our contributions are as follows.

•

Robust Bellman and Wasserstein dual formulation.

We formulate the finite-horizon distributionally robust control problem
under state–action dependent ambiguity sets and establish the corresponding
robust dynamic programming recursion. For Wasserstein ambiguity sets, we use
the dual representation of Wasserstein DRO to rewrite the robust Bellman
operator as an optimization over a scalar dual multiplier.

•

Directional policy-gradient representation.

We prove continuity and directional differentiability of the robust value
functions with respect to the policy parameter
(Propositions
3.7
and

3.14
). We then derive explicit
one-sided directional derivative formulas for the Wasserstein robust
Bellman operator and the associated value recursion
(Theorem
3.15
and Corollary
3.1
).
When the dual multiplier and the inner transport selector are unique, these
directional formulas simplify to a vector-valued robust policy-gradient
recursion.

•

Robust actor–critic algorithm.

We propose an actor–critic implementation based on the dual robust Bellman
recursion and the derived gradient formulas. The critic approximates the
robust value function, while the actor is updated using the corresponding
robust policy-gradient recursion. We illustrate the method on inventory
control, coin-toss, multi-armed bandit, and linear-quadratic control
benchmarks, covering both finite and continuous state-space settings.

Outline of the paper.

The paper is organized as follows: Section
2

3

Remark 2.4

In our setting, define

\mathcal{P}_{l}^{0}:=\left\{\mathbb{P}_{l}:\mathcal{X}\times A\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):\mathbb{P}_{l}\text{ is Borel-measurable and }\mathbb{P}_{l}(x,a,\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}_{l}(x,a)\text{ for all }(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A\right\}.

We say that the ambiguity set is rectangular if
\mathcal{B}=\prod_{l\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{P}_{l}^{0}.
Rectangularity means that admissible transition kernels can be selected
separately at each time, with no additional coupling constraints across stages.
This product structure is standard in robust MDPs and is the structural condition
that makes dynamic programming possible. In particular, for any function
\Phi:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\inf_{(\mathbb{P}_{l})_{l\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathcal{B}}\Phi((\mathbb{P}_{l})_{l\in\mathcal{T}})=\inf_{\mathbb{P}_{T-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{T-1}^{0}}\cdots\inf_{\mathbb{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{0}}\Phi((\mathbb{P}_{l})_{l\in\mathcal{T}}).

This identity follows from the Cartesian-product structure. In the dynamic
programming proof, it is combined with measurable selection to reduce the robust optimization problem to a sequence of one-step minimizations.

3.2. Duality results

In this framework, the problem (

\displaystyle{\cal X}\ni x\mapsto V(x):=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\sup}\penalty\ \underset{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal B}_{x,\pi^{\theta}}}{\inf}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[R(S)\big].

Assumption 3.2

Assume the following holds:

(i)

For each

Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})

.
Let

(3.6)

Note that we no longer optimize over the full admissible policy

\Pi
. Instead, we restrict attention to the parametric family

. The gradient results characterize local

within this family. They do not imply global

Remark 3.3

We recall that our objective is to obtain a tractable representation of the
derivative of the robust performance criterion
J(\theta)
defined in
(
3.6
). The main difficulty is that the worst-case transition
selector depends on the policy parameter
\theta
through the value function.

Indeed, for
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the dynamic programming recursion can be written as

V_{t}^{\theta}(x)=\int_{A}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[f(t,x,a,x^{\prime})+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(x^{\prime})\right]\mathbb{P}_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\pi^{\theta}_{t}(x,a)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}a).

A direct differentiation of this expression would require differentiability, in
a suitable weak sense, of the selector

\theta\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a,\cdot)
, together with domination conditions
allowing the derivative to pass through the integrals. Such regularity is
generally difficult to verify, since
\mathbb{P}_{t}^{*,\theta}
is defined implicitly as
an optimizer of the local robust problem and depends on

V_{t+1}^{\theta}
.

This prevents a straightforward backward induction proof of differentiability
for
V_{t}^{\theta}
. We therefore introduce a dual formulation, which avoids
differentiating the worst-case transition selector directly while still yielding
a tractable characterization of the sensitivity of
V_{t}^{\theta}
and

J(\theta)
.

We now specialize to the Wasserstein ambiguity sets defined in
(
3.1
). To tackle these ambiguity sets, we use the duality results from
10
. We first introduce the auxiliary quantities needed
for this dual formulation.

Proposition 3.4

(Theorem 1,
10
)

Let
c:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\ni(x,y)\mapsto c(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert^{p}
and
\lambda\geq 0
. We define the operator
{\cal F}^{\lambda}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}(V)(x):=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{V(y)-\lambda c(x,y)\right\},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

(3.7)

Then, the following duality result holds for any
V\in C(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbb{P}\in\mathbb{B}^{\varepsilon,q}(\mathbb{P}^{0})}{\inf}\penalty\ \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[V(X)\big]=\underset{\lambda\geq 0}{\sup}\penalty\ \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{0}}\big[-{\cal F}^{\lambda}(-V)(X)\big]-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda,

(3.8)

where
X\sim\mathbb{P}^{0}
.

The duality result (
3.8
) reduces the optimization problem from a set of probability measures to a one-dimensional optimization over the dual variable
\lambda
.

Remark 3.5

Note that the DPP defined in (

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} V_{T}^{\theta}(x)&=g(x),\\
V_{t}^{\theta}(x)&=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\theta}_{t}(x,\cdot)}\Big[\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)}\big[-{\cal F}^{\lambda}(-f(t,x,a,\cdot)-V^{\theta}_{t+1}(\cdot))(X)\big]-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda\Big)\Big],\penalty\ t=T-1,\dots,0.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.9)

Remark 3.6

The operator
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
may also be approximated by operator-learning
methods. For example, Theorem 4.5 in

shows that, under
compactness assumptions on
\mathcal{X}
and on the relevant class of input

can be approximated uniformly over

\lambda\in[0,\Lambda]
. More precisely, if

is compact in the supremum norm,
then for every

there exist integers

and parameters

such that

This suggests a possible offline approximation of the robust Bellman operator in

the theoretical results below.

3.3. Measurability of selectors

The Wasserstein dual formulation involves two optimization problems: a pointwise
minimization over the transported next state
y
and a scalar maximization over
the dual multiplier
\lambda
. We first isolate the optimizer correspondences
that will be used in the policy-gradient analysis.

For
t\in\mathcal{T}
,
\theta\in\Theta
,
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
,

x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, and
\lambda\geq 0
, define

Y_{t,\theta,\lambda}^{*}(x,a;x^{\prime}):=\underset{y\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathrm{argmin\,}}\left\{f(t,x,a,y)+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(y)+\lambda c(x^{\prime},y)\right\}.

The corresponding scalar dual objective is

F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(t,x,a,y)+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(y)+\lambda c(x^{\prime},y)\right\}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda,

and the set of optimal dual multipliers is

\Lambda_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a):=\underset{\lambda\geq 0}{\mathrm{argmax\,}}F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a).

Here
\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)
is the nominal transition law. Thus, after fixing

(x,a)
, the Wasserstein dual formulation replaces the optimization over
admissible next-state distributions by a scalar optimization over
\lambda
and
a pointwise transport problem in
y
. The sets

Y_{t,\theta,\lambda}^{*}(x,a;x^{\prime})
and
\Lambda_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a)
encode the
corresponding optimizers and determine the sensitivity of the robust Bellman
operator.

The results below focus on measurability, which is sufficient for the
directional derivative formulas to be well-defined. Sufficient conditions for
uniqueness of the relevant optimizers are given in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 3.8

Assume that Assumption
2.5
holds, that

c(x,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and that
\varepsilon>0
. Then, for every

(t,\theta,x,a)\in\mathcal{T}\times\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\times A
,

F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a)\to-\infty\qquad\text{as }\lambda\to\infty.

Consequently, the supremum over
\lambda\geq 0
is attained on a compact
interval. More precisely, if the value functions
V_{t+1}^{\theta}
are uniformly
bounded over
(t,\theta)
, then there exists
\Lambda<+\infty
, independent of

(t,\theta,x,a)
, such that

\Lambda_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a)=\underset{\lambda\in[0,\Lambda]}{\mathrm{argmax\,}}F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a).

Proof.

By Assumption
2.5
and compactness of

\mathcal{X}
and
A
, the functions
f
and
g
are bounded. Since the horizon
is finite, the robust Bellman recursion implies that the value functions

V_{s}^{\theta}
are uniformly bounded. Hence there exists
C<+\infty
such that
|f(t,x,a,x^{\prime})+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(x^{\prime})|\leq C
for all
(t,\theta,x,a,x^{\prime})\in[0,T]\times\Theta\times{\cal X}\times A\times{\cal X}
. For any
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, the admissible choice
y=x^{\prime}
gives

\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(t,x,a,y)+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(y)+\lambda c(x^{\prime},y)\right\}\leq f(t,x,a,x^{\prime})+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(x^{\prime}),

because
c(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})=0
. Therefore,

F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a)\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[f(t,x,a,x^{\prime})+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(x^{\prime})\right]\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda\leq C-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda.

Since
\varepsilon>0
, this implies

F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a)\to-\infty\qquad\text{as }\lambda\to\infty.

Moreover, by the same boundedness, there exists
C_{0}<+\infty
such that
F_{t}^{\theta}(0;x,a)\geq-C_{0}

for all
(t,\theta,x,a)\in[0,T]\times\Theta\times{\cal X}\times A
. Choose
\Lambda>0
such that

Then, for every
\lambda\geq\Lambda
,

F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a)\leq C-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda\leq C-\varepsilon^{q}\Lambda<-C_{0}\leq F_{t}^{\theta}(0;x,a).

Thus no maximizer lies in
[\Lambda,\infty)
. By continuity of

\lambda\mapsto F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a)
, the maximum is attained
on
[0,\Lambda]
, and

\Lambda_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a)=\underset{\lambda\in[0,\Lambda]}{\mathrm{argmax\,}}F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a).

∎

Such compact reductions are a standard consequence of Wasserstein dual
formulations in compact settings. Here, compactness of
\mathcal{X}
makes the
transport cost bounded, while the term
-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda
forces the dual
objective to
-\infty
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Therefore the maximization over

\lambda\geq 0
can be restricted to a compact interval. This is consistent with
the Wasserstein DRO duality framework used in

10
,
24
,
16

Proposition 3.9

and

Remark 3.11

Measurability ensures that expressions involving selected optimizers are
well-defined. Uniqueness, however, is not required for the one-sided directional
formulas derived later. When the inner or dual optimizer is set-valued, the
derivative is computed over the corresponding active set. Thus set-valued optimizers are compatible with
directional differentiability, although they need not yield a single
vector-valued gradient.

Remark 3.12

Whenever the optimizer is unique, the corresponding selector is continuous, and
uniformly continuous on compact parameter sets by Berge’s maximum theorem

[
2
, Theorem 17.31]
. Continuity of the objective alone gives hemicontinuity of the optimizer
correspondence, but not necessarily a continuous selector. For example, for

\ell(p,y)=py
on
[-1,1]\times[-1,1]
,

\underset{y\in[-1,1]}{\mathrm{argmin\,}}\ell(p,y)=\begin{cases}\{-1\},&p>0,\\
[-1,1],&p=0,\\
\{1\},&p<0,\end{cases}

so no continuous selector exists. Without uniqueness, one should keep the
set-valued directional formula or impose an explicit selection rule.

3.4. Policy gradient representation

We now establish the regularity properties of the robust value functions needed to differentiate the robust performance criterion with respect to the policy parameter. The next step is to quantify how the robust value functions vary with the policy parameter.
Since the dependence on
\theta
enters both through the policy
\pi^{\theta}
and through the
dual robust Bellman operator, we work with directional derivatives in
\theta
.
We recall the notion of directional differentiability that will be used to state our regularity results.

Definition 3.13

Let
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open, let
f:O\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
\theta\in\operatorname{int}(O)
. For a direction
r\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the right and left directional derivatives of
f
at
\theta
in direction
r
are defined, whenever the limits exist, by

D_{\theta}^{+}f(\theta)[r]:=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{f(\theta+hr)-f(\theta)}{h},\quad D_{\theta}^{-}f(\theta)[r]:=\lim_{h\uparrow 0}\frac{f(\theta+hr)-f(\theta)}{h}.

When the side is clear from the context,
D_{\theta}
denotes the corresponding
one-sided directional derivative. Statements involving
D_{\theta}
are to be read separately for the right derivative
D_{\theta}^{+}
and for the left derivative
D_{\theta}^{-}
.

In what follows, all directional derivatives with respect to
\theta
are taken at points

\theta\in\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
. If
\theta\in\partial\Theta
, the same
statements are understood only for feasible directions
r
, i.e. directions for
which
\theta+hr\in\Theta
for all sufficiently small one-sided
h
.

Proposition 3.14

Suppose Assumptions
2.5
and

3.2

\theta\in\Theta
,

See Appendix
B.3
.

The content of the policy gradient therefore reduces entirely to characterizing

for each

the backward recursion in Corollary

with respect to

so

Since
G_{t}^{\theta}

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{-\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}(U_{h})+\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}(U_{0})}{h}-\frac{-\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}(\widetilde{U}_{h})+\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}(U_{0})}{h}\bigg\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\|U_{h}-\widetilde{U}_{h}\|_{\infty}}{|h|}=\frac{\|V^{\theta+hr}_{t+1}-(V^{\theta}_{t+1}+hH)\|_{\infty}}{|h|}\xrightarrow[h\downarrow 0]{}0.

(3.17)

(3.18)

Proof.

Recall from Remark
3.5

,

Remark 3.16

(Finite state–action spaces)

When
\mathcal{X}
and
A
are finite, the compactness and measurability
conditions used above hold automatically. Every function is measurable and
continuous, nonempty-valued correspondences admit measurable selectors by

sums. The local equicontinuity condition in Proposition

also holds automatically, since every
family of functions on a finite set is equicontinuous. Finiteness does not remove possible nonsmoothness when the inner minimizer or

one-sided directional derivative of Theorem

. A classical
gradient formula is recovered only if the active inner minimizer

and the active dual maximizer

are singletons, or if all active minimizers and
maximizers give the same directional value.

3.5. A vector-valued gradient representation

The directional derivative obtained in the previous section involves the dual
optimizer set and the inner minimizer set. In general, these sets may contain several
elements, so the resulting expression need not be linear in the direction

.
To obtain a linear gradient representation, it is enough to impose uniqueness of
both selectors. Under these uniqueness conditions, the max–min structure in the
directional Danskin formula disappears. This is made precise in the lemma below.

Discussion on the uniqueness assumptions.

Lemma
3.17
requires uniqueness of two selectors. The first one is
the inner minimizer

Y^{*}_{t,\theta,\lambda}(x,a;x^{\prime}).

This uniqueness is easy to obtain under standard convexity assumptions as discussed in Section
C
. The dual maximizer may be set-valued.

If the inner minimizer is unique and the
envelope theorem applies, then

\partial_{\lambda}F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a)=R_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,\lambda)-\varepsilon^{q},

where

R_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)}\left[\|X-y_{t}^{*}(x,a,X,\lambda,\theta)\|^{q}\right].

Thus, if
\lambda\mapsto R_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,\lambda)
is continuous, strictly
decreasing on
(0,\Lambda)
, and satisfies

R_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,0^{+})>\varepsilon^{q}>R_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,\Lambda^{-}),

then there is a unique

\lambda_{t}^{*}(x,a,\theta)\in(0,\Lambda)

such that

R_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,\lambda_{t}^{*})=\varepsilon^{q}.

Since
F_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,\cdot)
is concave, this point is the unique maximizer.

We can check this setting in smooth quadratic models. For example, when
q=2
,
set

H(y):=f(t,x,a,y)+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(y).

If
H
is
C^{2}
and convex, and if the minimizer

y_{\lambda}(x^{\prime}):=y_{t}^{*}(x,a,x^{\prime},\lambda,\theta)

is interior, then

\nabla H(y_{\lambda})+2\lambda(y_{\lambda}-x^{\prime})=0.

Differentiating in
\lambda
gives

\partial_{\lambda}y_{\lambda}=2\big(\nabla^{2}H(y_{\lambda})+2\lambda I\big)^{-1}(x^{\prime}-y_{\lambda}),

and therefore

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\|x^{\prime}-y_{\lambda}\|^{2}=-4(x^{\prime}-y_{\lambda})^{\top}\big(\nabla^{2}H(y_{\lambda})+2\lambda I\big)^{-1}(x^{\prime}-y_{\lambda})\leq 0.

The inequality is strict when
x^{\prime}\neq y_{\lambda}
. Hence, the map

\lambda\mapsto R_{t}^{\theta}(x,a,\lambda)

is strictly decreasing.

In the Linear Quadratic (LQ) case (see
4
for example),
H
is quadratic, so the
first-order condition is linear and the scalar dual problem can be checked
directly. The gradient-valued recursion of Lemma
3.17
is then
appropriate when the dual maximizer is unique and interior.

Remark 4.1

Note that:

1.

While the present algorithm is formulated for Wasserstein ambiguity sets, the robust actor-critic framework extends to any family
\{{\cal B}(\mathbb{P}^{0}(x,a,\cdot))\}_{x,a}
of convex, weakly compact uncertainty sets for which strong duality holds and the worst-case measure
\mathbb{P}^{*,\theta}\in\operatorname{arg\,inf}_{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal B}(\mathbb{P}^{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
is attained. Under these conditions, the algorithm requires modification only in two places:
(i)
the dual representation used to evaluate the robust Bellman target in the critic step, and
(ii)
the identification of
\mathbb{P}^{*,\theta}
in the robust policy gradient
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)

which follows from an envelope Theorem argument. All remaining components are independent of the geometry of the ambiguity set.

2.

The introduction of parametric families

V_{\psi,t}:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R},\quad U_{\xi,t}:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}},

intended to approximate
V_{t}^{\theta}(x)
and
\nabla_{\theta}V_{t}^{\theta}(x)
respectively, is not an intrinsic feature of the algorithm but rather a practical necessity in large or continuous state spaces. In settings where
{\cal X}
is finite and of moderate size, such as the numerical experiments below, both quantities can be computed exactly by direct tabulation, rendering the approximation step superfluous. Function approximation should therefore be understood as a scalability device, and all theoretical guarantees of the algorithm apply without modification in the tabular case.

4.2.1

-1

4.2.2
Supply chain

We also consider a finite-horizon inventory control problem with state space

\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,\dots,n\},
where
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
denotes the on-hand
inventory at time
t\in{\cal T}
. The action space is
A=\{0,1,\dots,n\},

where
A_{t}\in A
denotes the order quantity placed at time
t
. Given state-action pair

(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
, the post-order inventory is

\bar{x}=\min\{n,\,x+a\}\in\mathcal{X}
. Demand
D_{t}
is drawn from the reference
distribution
D_{t}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\big(\{0,1,\dots,n\}\big),
and the next inventory
evolves as

X_{t+1}=\max\{0,\,\bar{x}-D_{t}\}\in\mathcal{X}.

The one-step cost is

\ell(x,a,d)=h\,(\bar{x}-d)_{+}+p\,(d-\bar{x})_{+}+k\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{a>0\}},\quad\forall(x,a,d)\in\mathcal{X}\times A\times\{0,\dots,n\},

h>0
is the holding-cost coefficient,
p>0
is the shortage-cost coefficient, and

k>0
is a fixed ordering cost. The reward is the negative cost,

r(x,a,d)=-\ell(x,a,d),\quad\forall(x,a,d)\in\mathcal{X}\times A\times\{0,\dots,n\}.

Since the actor–critic interface is written in terms of
(x,a,x^{\prime})
, we equivalently work with the conditional expected reward given the realized next-state inventory level
x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}
. Concretely, we set

f(t,x,a,x^{\prime})=-\,\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{0,\dots,n\}}\big[\ell(x,a,D)\,\big|\,X_{t+1}=x^{\prime}\big],\quad g(x)=0,\quad\forall(t,x,a,x^{\prime})\in{\cal T}\times{\cal X}\times A\times{\cal X}.

c(x,y)=|x-y|
for all
(x,y)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal X}
. The model coefficients are: maximal on-hand inventory
n
, horizon
T
, Wasserstein radius
\varepsilon\geq 0
, holding cost
h>0
, shortage cost
p>0
, and fixed ordering cost
k>0
. In the numerical experiments, we take
n=10
,
\varepsilon=1
,
T=5
,
h=1
,
p=3
, and
k=2

\pi^{\theta}
as a time-dependent tabular softmax policy

\pi^{\theta}_{t}(x,a)=\frac{\exp(\theta_{t,x,a})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}\exp(\theta_{t,x,a^{\prime}})},\quad\forall(t,x,a)\in{\cal T}\times{\cal X}\times A,

and illustrate the greedy action

4.2.3
Robust Linear-Quadratic Control

We consider a finite-horizon linear-quadratic control problem under distributional
uncertainty on the noise. A closely related penalized robust LQ problem admits an explicit Riccati
solution. We use it as a structural sanity check for the learned policy. This benchmark falls outside the compact state-action framework of the theory and serves to test the method in a standard control setting.

The state dynamics are

X_{t+1}=AX_{t}+Bu_{t}+\Xi w_{t},\quad X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t=0,\dots,T-1,

(4.1)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
,
\Xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
,

u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control, and
w_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a random disturbance with
distribution
\mu_{t}\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Under the reference model, each
w_{t}
is drawn
i.i.d. from an empirical distribution

\nu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\hat{w}^{(i)}}

constructed from samples

\{\hat{w}^{(1)},\dots,\hat{w}^{(N)}\}
. The running and terminal costs are

f(t,x,u,x^{\prime})=x^{\top}Qx+u^{\top}Ru,\quad g(x)=x^{\top}P_{T}x,\quad\forall(t,x,u,x^{\prime})\in{\cal T}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

with
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
Q\succeq 0
(state penalty);
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
R\succ 0
(control penalty); and
P_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
P_{T}\succeq 0
(terminal penalty). The remaining coefficients of the model are the dynamics matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
,
\Xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
, the number of empirical samples
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the Wasserstein radius
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and the penalty parameter
\lambda>0
used in the Riccati benchmark of Proposition
4.2
.

The agent distrusts the nominal distribution and solves the robust minimax problem

V_{t}(x)\;=\;\underset{(\pi_{s})_{s\in\mathcal{T}_{t}}}{\inf}\penalty\ \underset{(\gamma_{s})_{s\in\mathcal{T}_{t}}}{\sup}\;\mathbb{E}^{\pi,\gamma}\left[\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\bigl(X_{s}^{\top}Q\,X_{s}+u_{s}^{\top}R\,u_{s}\bigr)+X_{T}^{\top}P_{T}\,X_{T}\right],

(4.2)

subject to (
4.1
), where the controller chooses feedback policies

u_{t}=\pi_{t}(X_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the adversary chooses, at each stage, a distribution

\gamma_{t}(X_{t},u_{t})\in{\cal P}\bigl(\{\hat{w}^{(1)},\dots,\hat{w}^{(N)}\}\bigr)

for the noise
w_{t}
, constrained by

W_{q}\bigl(\gamma_{t}(X_{t},u_{t}),\;\nu\bigr)\;\leq\;\varepsilon,\quad t=0,\dots,T{-}1.

(4.3)

For reference, we compare against the Wasserstein penalty framework
of
34
and
18
,
which solves a closely related but different problem. Starting from the same empirical
nominal
\nu
, their adversary transports each atom to any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

(not just the
N
existing atoms), and the robust value is defined as

\inf_{\pi}\;\sup_{\gamma}\;\mathbb{E}^{\pi,\gamma}\left[X_{T}^{\top}P_{T}\,X_{T}+\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\bigl(X_{s}^{\top}Q\,X_{s}+u_{s}^{\top}R\,u_{s}-\lambda\,W_{q}(\gamma_{s},\nu)^{p}\bigr)\;\middle|\;X_{0}=x\right],

(4.4)

where
\lambda>0
is a fixed parameter. In this penalized LQ formulation, the quadratic structure is preserved by the Riccati recursion, so the value function remains quadratic at each stage. The supremum over

\gamma_{t}\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
admits a closed-form solution, yielding the Riccati
recursion of Proposition
4.2

Assumption C.1

is compact, so the minimum is
attained. Since the objective is strictly convex on the convex set

\mathcal{X}
, the minimizer is unique. Hence

Y_{t,\theta,\lambda}^{*}(x,a;x^{\prime})
is a singleton for every
\lambda>0
.
∎

Remark C.4

For the Euclidean Wasserstein cost
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{q}
, the map

y\mapsto c(x,y)
is strictly convex whenever
q>1
. In particular, for
q=2
,
it is strongly convex. Hence Corollary
C.3
applies
at every positive dual maximizer. If
q=1
, strict convexity generally fails,
and uniqueness of the inner selector must come from additional strict convexity
of
y\mapsto f(t,x,a,y)+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(y)
.

C.2. Uniqueness of the dual selector

Fix
t\in\mathcal{T}
,
\theta\in\Theta
, and

(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
. Throughout this subsection, we assume that the results of the previous subsection
give uniqueness of the inner selector on
(0,\Lambda]
.

Lemma C.5

\Lambda_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a)
is a singleton if and only if
there do not exist
\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}
such that

[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]\subseteq\Lambda_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a).

Proof.

If the maximizer is unique, the claim is immediate. Conversely, suppose

\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}
are two maximizers. Since
\lambda\mapsto F_{t}^{\theta}(\lambda;x,a)
is concave on

[0,\Lambda]
and both endpoints attain the maximum, every point of

[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
also attains the maximum. Hence the maximizer set
contains a nontrivial interval.
∎

Proposition C.6

Assume that, for every
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}
in
(0,\Lambda]
,

\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}\left(x,a,\left\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}:y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{0}}(x,a;x^{\prime})\neq y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{1}}(x,a;x^{\prime})\right\}\right)>0.

Then
F_{t}^{\theta}(\cdot;x,a)
is strictly concave on
(0,\Lambda]
. In particular, it has at
most one maximizer on
(0,\Lambda]
.

Proof.

For fixed
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, the inner value

\lambda\mapsto\phi_{x^{\prime}}(\lambda):=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(t,x,a,y)+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(y)+\lambda c(x^{\prime},y)\right\}

is concave, as an infimum of affine functions of
\lambda
. Fix
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}
in
(0,\Lambda]
,
s\in(0,1)
, and

\lambda_{s}=(1-s)\lambda_{0}+s\lambda_{1}
. If equality holds in

\phi_{x^{\prime}}(\lambda_{s})\geq(1-s)\phi_{x^{\prime}}(\lambda_{0})+s\phi_{x^{\prime}}(\lambda_{1}),

then the minimizer at
\lambda_{s}
is also a minimizer at both
\lambda_{0}
and

\lambda_{1}
. Since the inner minimizer is unique on
(0,\Lambda]
, this implies

y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{0}}(x,a;x^{\prime})=y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{1}}(x,a;x^{\prime}).

Thus the inequality is strict on the set where the two selectors differ. Integrating with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)
gives strict concavity of

\lambda\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi_{x^{\prime}}(\lambda)\,\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}),

because the strict inequality holds on a set of positive

\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)
-mass by assumption. The affine term
-\varepsilon^{q}\lambda
does not
affect strict concavity, so
F_{t}^{\theta}(\cdot;x,a)
is strictly concave on

(0,\Lambda]
.
∎

Proposition C.7

Define

H^{\theta}(t,x,a,y):=f(t,x,a,y)+V_{t+1}^{\theta}(y).

Suppose that:

(i)

Assumptions
C.1
hold, i.e,
y\mapsto H^{\theta}(t,x,a,y)
is convex on
\mathcal{X}
and has a unique
minimizer

\underset{y\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathrm{argmin\,}}H^{\theta}(t,x,a,y)=\{\bar{y}(t,x,a,\theta)\}.

(ii)

y\mapsto H^{\theta}(t,x,a,y)\in C^{1}(\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{X}))
.

(iii)

For every
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and every
\lambda\in(0,\Lambda]
, we have that

y_{t,\theta,\lambda}(x,a;x^{\prime})\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{X})
.

(iv)

\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)
is non-atomic:

\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,y)=0,\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{X}.

Then, for every
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}
in
(0,\Lambda]
,

\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}\left(x,a,\left\{x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}:y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{0}}(x,a;x^{\prime})\neq y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{1}}(x,a;x^{\prime})\right\}\right)>0.

More precisely,

\left\{x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}:y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{0}}(x,a;x^{\prime})=y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{1}}(x,a;x^{\prime})\right\}\subseteq\{\bar{y}(t,x,a,\theta)\}.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}
in
(0,\Lambda]
and suppose that, for some
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{0}}(x,a;x^{\prime})=y_{t,\theta,\lambda_{1}}(x,a;x^{\prime})=:y.

By assumption,
y\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{X})
. The first-order optimality
conditions give

\nabla H^{\theta}(t,x,a,y)+\lambda_{i}\nabla_{y}\|X-y\|^{q}=0,\quad i=\{0,1\}.

Subtracting yields

(\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1})\nabla_{y}\|x^{\prime}-y\|^{q}=0.

Since
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}
,

\nabla_{y}\|x^{\prime}-y\|^{q}=0.

For
q>1
, this is equivalent to
y=x^{\prime}
. Hence
x^{\prime}=y
. Returning to the first-order
condition gives

\nabla H^{\theta}(t,x,a,x^{\prime})=0.

Since
y\mapsto H^{\theta}(t,x,a,y)
is convex,
x^{\prime}
minimizes
y\mapsto H^{\theta}(t,x,a,y)
. By
uniqueness of the minimizer,
x^{\prime}=\bar{y}(t,x,a,\theta).

Therefore the equality set is contained in

\{\bar{y}(t,x,a,\theta)\}
. By non-atomicity, this singleton has zero

\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)
-mass. Hence the set where the two selectors differ has positive mass.
∎

Remark C.8

Assumption
C.1
(ii) is stated directly for
readability. It can be verified from more explicit smoothness conditions: for
instance, if
g\in C^{1}(\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{X}))
,

(x,y)\mapsto f(t,x,a,y)
is
C^{1}
, the inner and dual optimizers are unique and
interior, and differentiation can be passed through the transition and action
integrals, then an envelope-theorem argument propagates
C^{1}
regularity
backward through the Bellman recursion. We omit these stronger conditions to
keep the presentation concise.

Corollary C.9

Assume the conditions of Proposition
C.7
.
Then
F_{t}^{\theta}(\cdot;x,a)
is strictly concave on
(0,\Lambda]
. Consequently, if the
dual maximizer belongs to
(0,\Lambda]
, then
\Lambda_{t}^{*,\theta}(x,a)
is a singleton.

Proof.

The movement condition in
Proposition
C.6
follows from
Proposition
C.7
. Hence

F_{t}^{\theta}(\cdot;x,a)
is strictly concave on
(0,\Lambda]
. A strictly concave function
has at most one maximizer on a convex set.
∎

Remark C.10

Strict concavity is only sufficient, not necessary, for uniqueness of the dual
selector. Since
F_{t}^{\theta}(\cdot;x,a)
is concave, uniqueness is equivalent to
the absence of a nontrivial flat interval of maximizers, as stated in
Lemma
C.5
.

D.1. Robust self-exciting Multi-armed bandits

D.1.1
Self-exciting bandit

We next consider a self-exciting bandit, which extends the static robust
bandit rule to a finite-horizon Markovian setting. In a standard robust
bandit, the reward law of each arm
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
is uncertain and
belongs to a Wasserstein ball around a nominal law

\mathbb{P}_{j}^{0}\in{\cal P}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. For the
1
-Wasserstein distance on
\mathbb{R}
,
the worst-case mean reward of arm
j
is

\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in{\cal P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}):\,{\cal W}_{1}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P}_{j}^{0})\leq\varepsilon_{j}}\mathbb{E}_{R\sim\mathbb{Q}}[R]=\mu_{j}^{0}-\varepsilon_{j},\qquad\mu_{j}^{0}:=\mathbb{E}_{R\sim\mathbb{P}_{j}^{0}}[R],

where
\varepsilon_{j}>0
is the ambiguity radius. Thus the static robust decision
selects an arm maximizing
\mu_{j}^{0}-\varepsilon_{j}
.

We now introduce a dynamic version in which the success probability of an arm
depends on the previous outcome. Let
K\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
be the number of arms,

K_{\sup}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
the maximal stake, and
T\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
the horizon,
with time index
t\in{\cal T}:=\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
. The state is

x_{t}=(m_{t},b_{t})\in{\cal X},\qquad{\cal X}=\{-K_{\sup},\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,K_{\sup}\}\times\{1,\ldots,K\},

where
m_{t}\in\{-K_{\sup},\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,K_{\sup}\}
is the signed outcome
of the previous investment and
b_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
is the arm played in the
previous round. The action is

a_{t}=(k_{t},j_{t})\in A,\qquad A=\{1,\ldots,K_{\sup}\}\times\{1,\ldots,K\},

where
k_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,K_{\sup}\}
is the stake and

j_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
is the selected arm.

Let
p_{j}\in(0,1)
denote the base success probability of arm

j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. Given
x_{t}=(m_{t},b_{t})\in{\cal X}
and

a_{t}=(k_{t},j_{t})\in A
, the success probability is

\tilde{p}(x_{t},a_{t})=p_{j_{t}}+\lambda\,\operatorname{sign}(m_{t})\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{b_{t}=j_{t}\}},

where
\lambda>0
is the excitation parameter. We assume
0<\lambda<\min_{j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}\{p_{j},1-p_{j}\},
so that
\tilde{p}(x_{t},a_{t})\in(0,1)
for all
(x_{t},a_{t})\in{\cal X}\times A
. The next state is generated by

\xi_{t}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\tilde{p}(x_{t},a_{t})),\qquad m_{t+1}=k_{t}(2\xi_{t}-1),\qquad b_{t+1}=j_{t},

where
\xi_{t}\in\{0,1\}
is the success indicator. Thus

x_{t+1}=(m_{t+1},b_{t+1})\in{\cal X}
. The one-step reward is the signed payoff

f(t,x,a,x^{\prime})=m^{\prime},\qquad g(x)=0,\quad\forall(t,x,a,x^{\prime})\in{\cal T}\times{\cal X}\times A\times{\cal X},

where
x^{\prime}=(m^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in{\cal X}
is the next state. Hence a successful play with stake

k_{t}
gives reward
+k_{t}
, while an unsuccessful play gives reward
-k_{t}
.

For the robust formulation, the reference transition kernel

\mathbb{P}^{0}_{t}(x,a,\cdot)\in{\cal P}({\cal X})
is the kernel induced by the nominal probabilities

\tilde{p}(x,a)
. The agent evaluates each policy under the worst-case transition
kernel within a Wasserstein ball of radius
\varepsilon>0
around

\mathbb{P}_{t}^{0}(x,a,\cdot)
. As in the previous finite-state examples, we parametrize
the policy by a time-dependent tabular softmax,

\pi_{t}^{\theta}(x,a)=\frac{\exp(\theta_{t,x,a})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}\exp(\theta_{t,x,a^{\prime}})},\quad\forall(t,x,a)\in{\cal T}\times{\cal X}\times A,

where

\theta=(\theta_{t,x,a})_{(t,x,a)\in{\cal T}\times{\cal X}\times A}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\cal T}|\times|{\cal X}|\times|A|}.

For illustration, we report the greedy action

a_{t}^{*}(x)=\underset{a\in A}{\mathrm{argmax\,}}\pi_{t}^{\theta}(x,a),
for all
(t,x)\in{\cal T}\times{\cal X},

extracted from the learned stochastic policy. Figure
5
compares
the greedy actions learned by Algorithm
1
with the
exact robust solution. The exact solution is computed by backward induction:
starting from the terminal condition, we sweep backward in time and, at each
state, evaluate the robust Bellman operator over all feasible actions.

t=0
for
\varepsilon=0.3

.

(m,b)
: the agent predominantly
selects arm
