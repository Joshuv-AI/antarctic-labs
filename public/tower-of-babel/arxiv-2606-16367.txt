Segregated solutions of a degenerate cross-diffusion system with drifts
Filippo Santambrogio, Simon M. Schulz

Abstract
. We prove the global existence of segregated weak solutions of a one-dimensional degenerate cross-diffusion system with independent drifts, which is endowed with a Wasserstein gradient flow structure. We argue by a Lagrangian formulation written in terms of the (pseudo-)inverse for the cumulative mass function of the sum of the species, which we solve by a Minimising Movement Scheme in the setting of L 2 ∩ B ​ V loc L^{2}\cap BV_{\mathrm{loc}} . This Lagrangian problem gives rise to a parabolic PDE similar to a p p -Laplace equation, with typical range p ∈ ( − ∞ , 1 ) p\in(-\infty,1) . We employ monotonicity methods à la Minty–Browder to obtain strong convergence and pass to the limit τ → 0 \tau\to 0 in the time-step of the discrete scheme. Our contribution simultaneously treats all porous medium degeneracies, the log-entropy, and fast diffusions of index α ∈ ( 1 3 , 1 ) \alpha\in(\frac{1}{3},1) , thereby complementing the recent results [ 31 , 46 , 47 , 51 ] , and the prior work [ 42 ] .

1.
Introduction

We study the following cross-diffusion system in one spatial dimension
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\varrho=\partial_{x}\big(\varrho\partial_{x}(f^{\prime}(\varrho+\mu)+V)\big),\\
&\partial_{t}\mu=\partial_{x}\big(\mu\partial_{x}(f^{\prime}(\varrho+\mu)+W)\big),\end{aligned}\right.

where the terms
V,W
are given Lipschitz drifts depending only on
x
, and
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function of the sum. In the sequel, we prove the existence of
segregated
solutions
\varrho,\mu
belonging to the space of probability measures
i.e.

\varrho,\mu
are mutually singular; more precisely, they will be absolutely continuous measures such that
\varrho(x)\mu(x)=0

\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
x
,
cf.
(
1.8
). Our strategy relies on a Lagrangian formulation of the problem via the pseudo-inverse of the cumulative mass function for the sum
S=\varrho+\mu
, which itself satisfies

(1.2)

\partial_{t}S=\partial_{x}\big(S\partial_{x}(f^{\prime}(S))\big)+\partial_{x}\big(\varrho\partial_{x}V+\mu\partial_{x}W\big).

1.1.
Context and novelty

In this portion of the paper, we give a brief overview of cross-diffusion systems and outline the “state-of-the-art” for system (
1.1
).

1.1.1.
Background on cross-diffusion systems

Systems of advection-diffusion equations arise naturally when studying collective behaviour in the
biological and social sciences. Instances of such systems can be found in the modelling of multiple chemotactic populations in competition for nutrient
[
24
,
32
]
, tumour growth
[
40
,
13
]
, population biology
[
26
,
35
]
,
neural networks
[
39
]
, and semiconductors
[
23
,
45
]
.

One of the most pertinent features of cross-diffusion systems is their relevance in describing cell sorting. This is a reorganisation process in which cells of different categories regroup into subregions with clearly defined boundaries
[
41
,
50
]
; thereby giving rise to segregation effects. This biological phenomenon is related to the inhibition/activation of growth whenever two populations occupy the same environment
[
9
]
. These considerations make the study of segregated solutions of cross-diffusion systems a central theme, both for theory and applications.

A complete well-posedness theory for cross-diffusion systems involving transport terms is currently out of reach. This is partly due to the lack of maximum principles, which means that the classical parabolic theory
[
3
,
43
]
no longer applies. Furthermore, the aforementioned segregation effects between species naturally lead to the formation of boundaries and sharp interfaces, which make the analysis of such equations delicate. This is exacerbated by the presence of degenerate
diffusions, which cause a loss of strict parabolicity and can give rise to singularities
[
48
]
. In turn, one often tailors the analysis to low-regularity settings which can accommodate for the presence of jumps and discontinuities. And indeed, when the drift terms in (
1.1
) are replaced with reaction terms, an existence
theory was obtained in a one-dimensional
BV
setting in
[
20
]
; this setting was also highlighted in
[
14
,
15
]
, while for the Sobolev setting we refer to
e.g.

[
2
,
8
,
22
,
21
,
37
]
. However, it is fair to say that, while the functional framework of
BV
seems suitably general for analysis, it does not exploit any special structure of the cross-diffusion system at hand.

Some cross-diffusion systems are intrinsically endowed with a gradient flow structure, meaning that they can be recast as the dynamic time-evolution of a minimisation problem associated to a particular energy functional. For instance, the system (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

(1.3)

\partial_{t}\varrho=\partial_{x}\left(\varrho\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\mathrm{E}}{\delta\varrho}\right),\quad\partial_{t}\mu=\partial_{x}\left(\mu\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\mathrm{E}}{\delta\mu}\right),

where
\frac{\delta\mathrm{E}}{\delta\varrho}
and
\frac{\delta\mathrm{E}}{\delta\mu}
denote the first variation of
\mathrm{E}
with respect to its two variables
\varrho
and
\mu
, respectively, where the energy
\mathrm{E}
is given by

(1.4)

\mathrm{E}[\varrho,\mu]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\varrho+\mu)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\varrho V+\mu W\big)\,\mathrm{d}x.

While there exists ample literature concerning the well-posedness of Wasserstein gradient flows
[
5
,
49
]
in the case of single population densities, these results do not generally apply to the case of systems such as (
1.3
), due to the lack of
\lambda
-geodesic convexity (unless the system is actually diagonal, thanks to a clever construction in
[
6
]
which shows necessary conditions for
\lambda
-geodesic convexity in
W_{2}\times W_{2}
). Nevertheless, this gradient flow approach to cross-diffusion systems was successfully employed in
[
29
,
28
]
for systems of non-local interaction equations with
two species and non-symmetric cross-interactions, and was first used in a system with cross-diffusion terms
but no transport terms in
[
44
]
. Other results focus on diagonal diffusion and
bounded domains
[
18
,
19
]
, small cross-diffusion
[
1
,
2
]
, convergence to equilibrium
[
6
]
, or triangular structures
[
25
,
27
]
. In some cases, one may interpret the system of equations at hand as a perturbation of a gradient flow associated with a convex functional
[
12
,
30
]
, which sometimes makes them amenable to the boundedness-by-entropy method
[
16
,
38
]
; see also
[
17
]
for a non-local version involving infinitely many species. All things considered, despite the simple gradient flow structure of (
1.1
), there is still much to discover concerning the behaviour of this system of equations.

1.1.2.
State-of-the-art for system (
1.1
)

The simplest case of (
1.1
) arises when
\partial_{x}V=\partial_{x}W=0
, which makes the system of equations purely diffusive. We can then compute, for

(1.5)

\mathrm{F}[S]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(S)\,\mathrm{d}x,

the first variation as
\frac{\delta\mathrm{F}}{\delta S}[S]=f^{\prime}(S)
. In this instance of the problem, the evolution of the sum
S
can be recast as the closed-form gradient flow:

(1.6)

\partial_{t}S=\partial_{x}(S\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\mathrm{F}}{\delta S}[S]),

and existence for (
1.6
) follows directly from the well-established Wasserstein gradient flow theory
[
5
]
. Once existence of
S
is known, one can go back to the individual equations for
\varrho,\mu
and treat them as independent linear scalar conservation laws:
\partial_{t}\varrho=\partial_{x}(\varrho\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S))
and similarly for
\mu
. Since the density
S
that is obtained as a solution to (
1.6
) is sufficiently smooth, this linear equation is well-posed, which allows to obtain existence of
\varrho
and
\mu
and also to verify that the
\varrho,\mu
obtained in this manner satisfy
\varrho+\mu=S
. This purely diffusive problem and its segregating effects had already been studied in
[
7
]
;
cf.
system (1.6) therein. When the drifts are not null, and not identical, the situation is more intricate.

A first general existence result for system (
1.1
) was given in
[
42
]
, which involved very restrictive assumptions on the ordering of the potentials
V,W
and on the initial configuration so as to obtain segregated solutions for all times. The problem then stayed with no progress for some years, until the next breakthrough, obtained in
[
47
]
, where global existence to (
1.1
) with periodic boundary conditions, general Lipschitz potentials
V,W
, and a broad class of initial data was shown for the specific case
f(s)=s\log s-s
. The recent contribution
[
31
]
then improved the result of
[
47
]
, and proved existence of periodic solutions for a relatively broad class of (“totally mixed”) initial data with
f(s)\sim s^{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
; the case
\alpha=1
coincides precisely with the choice of
f
in
[
47
]
. Both of these recent contributions employ a formulation of the problem where the main quantities that are studied are the sum
\varrho+\mu
and the quotient
\varrho/\mu
. Therein, the authors obtain

BV
estimates for the logarithm of the quotient
\varrho/\mu
and deduce suitable bounds on
\varrho
and
\mu
from this and
H^{1}
bounds on functions of
S
. We highlight that in one dimension,
BV
functions are
L^{\infty}
, and that the boundedness from above and below of this logarithm corresponds to the case of
totally mixed solutions
, with overlapping supports. This structure condition was then relaxed in
[
46
]
(relying on the ratio of each density with respect to the sum, instead of using the logarithm). A much more general existence result was subsequently obtained in
[
51
]
using a compensated compactness approach; this contribution can accommodate for all choices of fast diffusion, porous medium degeneracy, and the log-entropy.

1.1.3.
Novelty

The focus of the present manuscript is not to obtain as general an existence result as possible, but rather to study the segregation dynamics encoded in (
1.1
) using variational techniques. In particular, we are concerned with the
preservation of segregation
from segregated initial data (which was not studied in the aforementioned works
[
31
,
46
,
47
,
51
]
). This is a natural case to consider from a thermodynamic/energy-minimising perspective, since mixing is often energetically less favourable than staying segregated. Our contribution is closer in spirit to that of
[
42
]
, which is the only other work to specifically study segregated solutions, but the result contained in the present paper is much stronger than that of
[
42
]
as we allow for arbitrary segregated initial data and general drifts. That is to say, we do not require all the mass of
\varrho
to be supported to the left of
\mu
for example, and we allow part of the support of
\varrho
to be squeezed between two components of the support of
\mu
. In terms of the the drifts, we do not require anymore their orientation to be such that it favours segregation. As a byproduct of our approach, which entails a Lagrangian formulation (see §
1.2.2
), we end up solving a parabolic
p
-Laplace problem with typical range of exponent
p\in(-\infty,1)
by considering a gradient flow in the setting
L^{2}\cap BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
, which is novel in and of itself.

1.2.
Set-up

We describe our approach and the novel results contained herein in more detail. Firstly, we outline which segregation property will be preserved in the solution we build.

1.2.1.
Segregation

This work is concerned with (
1.1
) where one assumes a particular segregated structure on the solution. To explain this notion, we introduce the cumulative distribution function for the sum
S_{t}=S(t,\cdot)=\varrho(t,\cdot)+\mu(t,\cdot)
, denoted by
F_{t}
:

(1.7)

F_{t}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}S_{t}(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.

We study solutions which are such that, for
A\subseteq[0,2]
a given measurable subset satisfying
\mathscr{L}(A)=1=\mathscr{L}(A^{c})
, it holds

(1.8)

\varrho_{t}(x)=S_{t}(x)\mathds{1}_{A}(F_{t}(x)),\qquad\mu_{t}(x)=S_{t}(x)\mathds{1}_{A^{c}}(F_{t}(x));

where
\varrho_{t}=\varrho(t,\cdot),\mu_{t}=\mu(t,\cdot)\in\mathrm{Prob}(\mathbb{R})
. It follows that
F_{t}
takes values in
[0,2]
, and furthermore, by writing the formal change of variables
y=F_{t}(x)
,
i.e.
\,\mathrm{d}y=S_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, it holds

1=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varrho_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathds{1}_{A}(F_{t}(x))S_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{[0,2]}\mathds{1}_{A}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\mathscr{L}(A),

and similarly for
A^{c}
; thus the requirement
\mathscr{L}(A)=1=\mathscr{L}(A^{c})
is necessary.

The condition (
1.8
) imposes that the solutions are
segregated
in the sense:

\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\,\varrho_{t}(x)>0\}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\,\mu_{t}(x)>0\}=\varnothing\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

Morally speaking, the structure condition (
1.8
) means that the supports of
\varrho_{t},\mu_{t}
have a fixed
ordering
, which does not change in time; however, the supports themselves do evolve in time. Note that the case studied in
[
42
]
corresponds to the case
A=[0,1]
(with additional assumptions on
V
and
W
).

1.2.2.
Strategy

With the segregated formulation of §
1.2.1
, the final term of (
1.2
) equals

(1.9)

\partial_{x}\big(\varrho_{t}(x)\partial_{x}V(x)+\mu_{t}(x)\partial_{x}W(x)\big)=\partial_{x}\big(S_{t}(x)b(F_{t}(x),x)\big),

where we used (
1.8
), and where

(1.10)

b(y,s):=\mathds{1}_{A}(y)\partial_{x}V(s)+\mathds{1}_{A^{c}}(y)\partial_{x}W(s);

note that, for
V,W
Lipschitz continuous, it holds
b\in L^{\infty}([0,2]\times\mathbb{R})
. It then follows that the entire system (
1.1
) effectively collapses into the single equation

(1.11)

\partial_{t}(\partial_{x}F_{t})=\partial_{x}\big(\partial_{x}F_{t}\cdot\partial_{x}(f^{\prime}(\partial_{x}F_{t}))\big)+\partial_{x}\big(\partial_{x}F_{t}\cdot b(F_{t},x)\big),

and we have that
\varrho_{t},\mu_{t}
given by (
1.8
) are solutions of the original system (
1.1
).

The equation (
1.11
) is not in a particularly nice form, which motivates us to consider the equation satisfied by its (pseudo)-inverse function
u_{t}=F_{t}^{-1}
. As shown in §
4.1
, it turns out that the equation for this inverse—which we call the
Lagrangian reformulation
—has a familiar structure; in formal non-divergence form, it may be written

(1.12)

\partial_{t}u_{t}=f^{\prime\prime}(\frac{1}{\partial_{y}u_{t}})\frac{\partial_{yy}u_{t}}{(\partial_{y}u_{t})^{3}}-b(y,u_{t}),\qquad\qquad y\in[0,2],

with
b
given by (
1.10
). For
f(s)\sim s^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>0
, (with a negative sign if
\alpha<1
so as to have a convex function
f
) one can see that the leading-order terms in (
1.12
) formally correspond to a
parabolic
p
-Laplace equation
, but with
p=1-\alpha\in(-\infty,1)
. This formulation enables us to harness powerful monotonicity methods
à la
Minty–Browder. Furthermore, one can show that (
1.12
) is endowed with an
L^{2}
-gradient flow structure (
cf.
§
4.2
). Our approach is therefore to solve (
1.12
) by means of Minimising Movement Scheme (
cf.
§
5
–§
6
), and to then deduce existence to (
1.11
)—and hence (
1.1
)—by translating back into the original coordinates.

2.
Functional Framework

In this section, we state some functional analytic results on the space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
of locally finite measures; in the sequel, we study variational problems over the space
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
.

Definition 2.1

(The space
C_{c}((0,2))
and its dual)
.

We recall the space of continuous functions of compact support
C_{c}((0,2))=\{\varphi\in C([0,2]):\,\supp\varphi\subset(0,2)\}
,
equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}([0,2])}
. The dual
(C_{c}((0,2)))^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}((0,2))
is the space of signed measures on
(0,2)
, equipped with the total variation norm
\|\nu\|_{\mathcal{M}((0,2))}=|\nu|((0,2))
, where
|\nu|=\nu^{+}+\nu^{-}
and
\nu^{\pm}
are nonnegative measures in
\mathcal{M}((0,2))^{2}
, singular to each other (i.e. concentrated on two disjoint sets), such that
\nu=\nu^{+}-\nu^{-}
.

In the sequel, we will often work with measures which belong to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
,
i.e.
locally finite measures on
(0,2)
: when we write
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
we mean
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(K)=(C(K))^{\prime}
for every compact subset
K\subset(0,2)
. Often these measures will be non-negative: we write
\nu\geq 0
if, for all compact subsets
K\subset(0,2)
, it holds
\nu(K)\geq 0
.
Equivalently,
\nu\geq 0
if, for all non-negative
\varphi\in C_{c}((0,2))
, it holds
\int_{0}^{2}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\geq 0
; note that the integral is well-defined for
\varphi\in C_{c}((0,2))
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
.

We recall the following weak-* compactness result for locally finite measures; its proof is standard, and follows by compact exhaustion of
(0,2)
and a diagonal argument.

Theorem 2.2

(Alaoglu’s Theorem for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
)
.

Let
\{\nu_{n}\}_{n}
be a sequence belonging to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
for which it holds

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\nu_{n}\|_{\mathcal{M}([y_{0},y_{1}])}\leq C_{y_{0},y_{1}}
for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
. Then, there exists a subsequence
\{\nu_{\sigma(n)}\}_{n}
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
such that
\int_{0}^{2}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\sigma(n)}\to\int_{0}^{2}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\nu
for all
\varphi\in C_{c}((0,2))
; we write
\nu_{\sigma(n)}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\nu
weakly-* in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
.

Exactly as it happens for finite signed measures, each
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
can be uniquely decomposed (
Jordan decomposition
) as
\nu=\nu^{+}-\nu^{-}
where
\nu^{+},\nu^{-}\geq 0
are singular to each other. Moreover, every
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
is also decomposed uniquely into its
absolutely continuous and singular parts
, denoted
(\nu_{a},\nu_{s})\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))\times\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
,

(2.1)

\nu^{+}=\nu_{a}\!\cdot\!\mathscr{L}+\nu_{s}.

Note that, in the above, when
\nu\geq 0
then
\nu_{a}\geq 0

\mathscr{L}
-a.e. and
\nu_{s}\geq 0
.

Definition 2.3

(The space
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
)
.

We define the space
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
to be the subset of
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
composed of those functions whose distributional derivative is a locally finite measure on
(0,2)
,
i.e.
,

BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2)):=\Big\{v\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2)):\,\partial_{y}v\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))\Big\}.

For
v\in BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
we write
v^{\prime}=(\partial_{y}v)_{a}
in the decomposition (
2.1
),
i.e.
,

(2.2)

\partial_{y}v=v^{\prime}\!\cdot\!\mathscr{L}+(\partial_{y}v)_{s}.

As a consequence of the compactness results for locally bounded measures, we note that a compactness result for sequences of functions in
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
is also available:
if
\{v_{n}\}_{n}
is a sequence belonging to
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
for which it holds
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|v_{n}\|_{TV([y_{0},y_{1}])}\leq C_{y_{0},y_{1}}
for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
, there exists a subsequence
\{v_{\sigma(n)}\}_{n}
and
v\in BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
such that
\partial_{y}v_{\sigma(n)}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\partial_{y}v
weakly-* in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|v_{\sigma(n)}-v\|_{L^{1}([y_{0},y_{1}])}=0
for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
; we write
v_{\sigma(n)}\to v
strongly in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
.

Remark 2.4

(Precise representative in
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
)
.

Recall from
[
4
, Theorem 3.28 and p.139]
that, for all
u\in BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
, there exists a unique right-continuous function
u^{r}
defined everywhere in
(0,2)
which satisfies
u^{r}=u

\mathscr{L}
-a.e.; we call
u^{r}
the (right-continuous)
precise representative
for
u
. For this representative, it holds

u^{r}(y_{1})-u^{r}(y_{0})=\partial_{y}u([y_{0},y_{1}))\qquad\forall[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2).

Throughout the paper, we always identify
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
functions with a precise representative which, by arbitrary convention, can be chosen to be the right-continuous one (but all results would also work if choosing the left-continuous one, or the average of the right-continuous and of the left-continuous, or any other reasonable precise representative). Moreover, we use the shorthand notation
\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\partial_{y}u
to denote
\partial_{y}u([y_{0},y_{1}))
, whence the following version of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
is satisfied:

(2.3)

u(y_{1})-u(y_{0})=\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\partial_{y}u\qquad\forall[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2).

This clarification removes all ambiguity concerning the evaluation of the integral on the right-hand side when either of the endpoints are atoms for the measure
\partial_{y}u
.

Finally, we introduce the function space in which we will conduct the bulk of our analysis.

Definition 2.5

(The space
X
)
.

We define the function space
X:=L^{2}([0,2])\cap BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
. For a sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n}\subset X
, we say it is
bounded in
X
if
\sup_{n}\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}<\infty
and if, for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
, it holds
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|v_{n}\|_{TV([y_{0},y_{1}])}\leq C_{y_{0},y_{1}}<\infty
. For a sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n}\subset X
, we say that
v_{n}
converges to
v
in
X
if
||v_{n}-v||_{L^{1}([0,2])}\to 0
and
v_{n}
is bounded in
X
.

We observe that bounded sets in
X
are precompact for this convergence. Indeed, the
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
bound provides strong
L^{1}
compactness on each interval
[y_{0},y_{1}]
by Helly’s Theorem
[
4
, Theorem 3.23]
and hence, up to a subsequence,
\mathscr{L}
-a.e. convergence, and the
L^{2}
bound on the whole interval
[0,2]
provides equi-integrability of
v_{n}
, which transforms (via an easy application of Egoroff’s theorem) the
\mathscr{L}
-a.e. convergence into strong
L^{1}
convergence. We also observe that when a sequence
v_{n}
converges to
v
in
X
, then we also have
v_{n}\to v
in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
and
v_{n}\to v
in
L^{p}([0,2])
for every
p<2
.

3.
Main Results

We give our notion of solution, list the assumptions on
f
in (
1.1
), and state our main results.

Definition 3.1

(Segregated weak solution)
.

We say that a pair of curves
(\varrho_{t},\mu_{t})
valued in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{Prob}(\mathbb{R})
is a
segregated weak solution
of (
1.1
) if
\varrho_{t},\mu_{t}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
t
, there exists a measurable subset
A\subseteq[0,2]
such that (
1.8
) holds,
\sqrt{\varrho_{t}+\mu_{t}}\partial_{x}(f^{\prime}(\varrho_{t}+\mu_{t}))\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, and for all
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, it holds for
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
t>0
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varrho_{t}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varrho_{t}\partial_{x}\big(f^{\prime}(\varrho_{t}+\mu_{t})+V\big)\partial_{x}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{t}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{t}\partial_{x}\big(f^{\prime}(\varrho_{t}+\mu_{t})+W\big)\partial_{x}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.

In what follows, we will assume that
f\in C([0,\infty))\cap C^{2}((0,\infty))
is strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
and
f(0)=0
. Associated with
f
, we define the function
\tilde{f}
through

(3.2)

\tilde{f}(s):=sf(1/s)\qquad\text{for all }s>0.

Remark 3.2

(Properties of
\tilde{f}
)
.

We compute the derivatives of
\tilde{f}
and obtain the explicit formulas

(3.3)

\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s)=f(1/s)-\frac{1}{s}f^{\prime}(1/s),\qquad\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{s^{3}}f^{\prime\prime}(1/s).

Since (for a
C^{2}
function) strict convexity is equivalent to the second derivative not vanishing on any interval, it follows from the previous expression for
\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}
that
\tilde{f}
is also strictly convex. From
f(0)=0
and the strict convexity of
f
we deduce
f^{\prime}(s)s>f(s)-f(0)=f(s)
, whence

(3.4)

\tilde{f}^{\prime}<0,

and hence
\tilde{f}
is a strictly decreasing function. Moreover, we observe that we have

(3.5)

\lim_{s\to\infty}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s)=\lim_{s\to 0}\big(f(s)-sf^{\prime}(s)\big)=0.

In computing this limit, we use
f(0)=0
and
\lim_{s\to 0}sf^{\prime}(s)\leq 0
. The latter is justified by the fact that, if
f^{\prime}
is unbounded close to
0^{+}
, then it can only be negative (as a consequence of convexity). This implies
\liminf_{s\to\infty}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s)\geq 0
which, together with
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s)<0
, proves (
3.5
).

The function
\tilde{f}
could be bounded or not in a neighbourhood of
s=0
but, in any case, it admits a (possibly infinite) limit for
s\to 0^{+}
due to its monotonicity. We then extend
\tilde{f}
to
[0,+\infty)
by continuity, accepting the possible value
\tilde{f}(0)=+\infty
.

Our assumptions on
f
are listed below.

Assumption 3.3
.

We assume
f\in C([0,\infty))\cap C^{2}((0,\infty))
is strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
and
f(0)=0
, and we assume that
f
and
\tilde{f}
satisfy
the following two conditions:

(3.6)

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s)=-\infty,

and, for some positive constant
C
and some
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{3},1)
, the lower bound

(3.7)

f(s)\geq-Cs^{\alpha}\qquad\forall s\geq 0.

We remark that we
do not impose
a quantitative convexity assumption such as
f^{\prime\prime}>0
a.e. (even for
f\in C^{2}
strictly convex, its second derivative
f^{\prime\prime}
is allowed to vanish on a set of positive Lebesgue measure, just not on an interval). Furthermore, note that the condition (
3.6
) is weaker than superlinearity,
i.e.

(3.8)

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{f(s)}{s}=+\infty,

which follows from the fact that the superlinearity of
f
is equivalent to

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\tilde{f}(s)=+\infty
.

Remark 3.4

(Admissible
f
)
.

Assumption
3.3
allows for any
f
of porous medium type
f(s)=s^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>1
, the log-entropy
f(s)=s\log s-s
, and a large class of fast diffusions
f(s)=-s^{\alpha}
for
\frac{1}{3}<\alpha<1
. The reason for imposing
\alpha>\frac{1}{3}
is connected with the choice of the
L^{2}
space that we will use later and is standard whenever considering gradient flows in
W_{2}
: equations of fast diffusion type in
W_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be considered as far as the exponent of the diffusion
\alpha
satisfies
\alpha>\frac{d}{d+2}
, which is required for finiteness of the second moment of the Barenblatt solutions.

Our first main theorem is concerned with the global existence of weak segregated solution of the cross-diffusion system (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.5

(Global existence of segregated solutions)
.

Let
f
satisfy Assumption
3.3
,
V,W\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, let
\varrho_{0},\mu_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{Prob}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be given initial data, and assume the finiteness of the energy
\mathrm{F}[S_{0}]
, where
S_{0}:=\varrho_{0}+\mu_{0}
. Then, there exists
(\varrho_{t},\mu_{t})
a segregated weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
, and the initial data is achieved in the sense
\varrho_{t}\to\varrho_{0}
and
\mu_{t}\to\mu_{0}
in
W_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

As mentioned, the conclusion of Theorem
3.5
follows from a corresponding result for a Lagrangian reformulation (
cf.
(
1.12
) and §
4
), which is encapsulated in our second main theorem.

Theorem 3.6

(Lagrangian problem)
.

Let
u_{0}\in X
satisfy
\partial_{y}u_{0}\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
and
\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(u_{0}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty.
Let

be given by (
1.10
) with
V,W\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathscr{L}(A)=1
. Let

satisfying

, and

(3.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime}))-b(y,u)\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~in }(0,\infty)\times[0,2],\\
&u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|u(t,\cdot)-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}=0
,
u^{\prime}
as per (
2.2

\essinf_{[0,2]}u^{\prime}(t,\cdot)>0
for
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.

. Moreover, for
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.

the the measure

is concentrated on the set where the function
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime})

We remark that only the absolutely continuous part

of the derivative
\partial_{y}u
appears in the equation (

). Uniqueness for (

) can be obtained under certain semiconvexity conditions on

; this lies outside the scope of this paper, as we do not require it to solve (
1.1
).

Remark 3.7

(Comments on the main results)
.

In this paper we study the cross-diffusion system (
1.1
) on the whole real line
\mathbb{R}
, but it would have been possible to instead consider a bounded interval
[a,b]
, with no-flux boundary conditions. This would have simplified some parts of the analysis, in particular since we would consider (in the Lagrangian reformulation) non-decreasing functions valued into
[a,b]
, which are automatically globally
BV
. This would have avoided the use of locally finite measures and
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
functions, and our functional setting would have been endowed with strong compactness in
L^{2}
(and not only
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
),
cf.
the topology on the space
X
of Definition
2.5
. The restriction
\alpha>\frac{1}{3}
in the assumption (
3.7
) is also related to bounding the second moment of the mass distribution
S=\varrho+\mu
, or equivalently the
L^{2}
norm of

, and could be removed when working on

[a,b]
. On the other hand, the extra difficulty in the bounded domain case lies in the boundary conditions in the Lagrangian formulation, where it would not be possible to prove the Dirichlet boundary values
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime})|_{y=0}=\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime})|_{y=2}=0
.

4.
Lagrangian Reformulation

In this section we rewrite the system (
1.1
) in Lagrangian form, by arguing via
u_{t}
the inverse function of the cumulative density function
F_{t}
introduced in (
1.7
). Section §
4.1
contains heuristic arguments to give context to the reader before presenting the rigorous definitions (§
4.2
–§
4.3
) needed for the analysis of the Lagrangian problem in §
5
–§
6
.

4.1.
Formal Lagrangian reformulation

Recall the cumulative mass function
F_{t}
for the sum
S_{t}
as defined in (
1.7
). Let us assume for the time being that
F_{t}
is strictly increasing, such that it admits a well-defined inverse function
u_{t}:[0,2]\to\mathbb{R}
which is also strictly increasing. Our objective is to rewrite the equation (
1.2
) as an equation for
u_{t}

i.e.
(
1.12
); this is what we mean by
Lagrangian reformulation
. All manipulations in this portion of the manuscript are formal, and for the purposes of exposition; in what follows, the Eulerian coordinate
x
and the Lagrangian coordinate
y
are linked via
y=F_{t}(x)
and
x=u_{t}(y)
.

Notice that (
1.7
) formally implies
\partial_{x}F_{t}(x)=S_{t}(x)
, which, using also

(4.1)

F_{t}(u_{t}(y))=y,

implies, by differentiating with respect to
y
and using the formula (
1.7
),

(4.2)

S_{t}(u_{t}(y))=\partial_{x}F_{t}(u_{t}(y))=\frac{1}{\partial_{y}u_{t}(y)},

and taking a further derivative with respect to
y
yields
\partial_{x}S_{t}(u_{t}(y))=-\frac{\partial_{yy}u_{t}(y)}{\partial_{y}u_{t}(y)^{3}}
. Using (
1.7
), we take the primitive of (
1.2
) and, using (
1.9
), we get
\partial_{t}F_{t}=S_{t}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S_{t})+S_{t}b

i.e.
,

(4.3)

\partial_{t}F_{t}(u_{t}(y))=S_{t}(u_{t}(y))\bigg[-f^{\prime\prime}\big(\frac{1}{\partial_{y}u_{t}(y)}\big)\frac{\partial_{yy}u_{t}(y)}{(\partial_{y}u_{t}(y))^{3}}+b(y,u_{t}(y))\bigg].

By (
4.1
) and differentiating in
t
, we get
\partial_{t}F_{t}(u_{t}(y))+\partial_{x}F_{t}(u_{t}(y))\partial_{t}u_{t}(y)=0
. We deduce
\partial_{t}F_{t}(u_{t}(y))=-S_{t}(u_{t}(y))\partial_{t}u_{t}(y).
Substituting into (
4.3
) and cancelling by
S_{t}(u_{t}(y))
yields the formal non-divergence equation (
1.12
),
i.e.
,
\partial_{t}u_{t}=f^{\prime\prime}(\frac{1}{\partial_{y}u_{t}})\frac{\partial_{yy}u_{t}}{(\partial_{y}u_{t})^{3}}-b(y,u_{t})
.

Recalling the definition (
3.2
) for
\tilde{f}
and the computations (
3.3
), this final equation can be recast in the divergence form
\partial_{t}u_{t}=\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(\partial_{y}u_{t}))-b(y,u_{t})
; as one can see in equation (
3.9
), a a distinction between
\partial_{y}u
and
u^{\prime}
has to be made, and will be made in the next section, where we also rewrite this divergence-form equation as an
L^{2}
-gradient flow.

4.2.
Gradient flow formulation of the Lagrangian problem

In §
4.2.1
, we define the functionals over the spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
and

needed for our analysis. §

links this with the underlying

-gradient flow structure of the equation obtained in §

4.2.1.
Defining the functionals

For what follows, we recall the function
\tilde{f}
in (
3.2
). This function is now used to define an entropy-like functional.

Definition 4.1

(Functionals
\mathrm{Ent}
and
\mathscr{F}
)
.

For all
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
, with
\nu_{a}
as per (
2.1
), we define

(4.4)

\mathrm{Ent}[\nu]:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &+\infty\qquad&\text{if }\nu^{-}\neq 0,\\
&\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(\nu_{a}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y\qquad&\text{if }\nu^{-}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

We then define the functional
\mathscr{F}[v]:=\mathrm{Ent}[\partial_{y}v]
for all
v\in BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
.

Note that the functional
\mathrm{Ent}
is a local lower semicontinuous functional on measures (in the sense of the theory of lower semicontinuous functionals in the space of measures, see
[
10
,
11
]
), and the fact that the singular part of
\nu
does not appear in the functional is due to the condition
\lim_{s\to\infty}\tilde{f}(s)/{s}=\lim_{s\to\infty}f(1/s)=f(0)=0
(see,
e.g.
,
[
49
, Chapter 7]
).

Next, we introduce the functional
\mathscr{E}
corresponding to
\mathrm{E}
in (
1.4
).

Definition 4.2

(Functional
\mathscr{E}
)
.

For all
v\in X
and
b\in L^{\infty}([0,2]\!\times\!\mathbb{R})
, we define

(4.5)

\mathscr{E}[v]:=\mathscr{F}[v]+\int_{0}^{2}B(y,v(y))\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
B(y,s):=\int_{0}^{s}b(y,s^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}
is a primitive for
b
in the second variable.

Remark 4.3

(Control of
B
)
.

Note that the assumption
b\in L^{\infty}([0,2]\!\times\!\mathbb{R})
—which follows from (
1.10
) when
\partial_{x}V,\partial_{x}W\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
—implies

(4.6)

|B(y,s_{1})-B(y,s_{2})|\leq\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}|s_{1}-s_{2}|\qquad\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

4.2.2.
Formal
L^{2}
-gradient flow structure

By performing the usual formal computation
0=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\delta}|_{\delta=0}\mathscr{E}[v+\delta\varphi]
for
\varphi
a smooth test function (assuming formally that
\partial_{y}v+\delta\partial_{y}\varphi\geq 0
,
cf.
Lemma
5.2
and Proposition
5.3
), we obtain that the first variation of
\mathscr{E}
is

(4.7)

\mathscr{E}^{\prime}[v]=b(y,v)-\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime})),

with the notation
v^{\prime}
of Definition
2.3
. Note that in this Hilbert setting we prefer to use the notation
\mathscr{E}^{\prime}
rather than the notation with
\frac{\delta}{\delta\varrho}
that we used in the space of measures, but the meaning is still the first variation of the functional.

Using (
3.3
), equation (
1.12
) is formally equivalent to

(4.8)

\partial_{t}u=-\mathscr{E}^{\prime}[u],

which is (up to identifying singular parts of the derivative) precisely the divergence-form equation written at the end of §
4.1
,
cf.
(
3.9
). We shall therefore prove the existence of a solution of this Lagrangian reformulation (
cf.
Theorem

) by means of a Minimising Movement Scheme with respect to the functional

(see §
5
–§
6
).

The next section contain the properties required on the functionals
\mathscr{E}
,
\mathscr{F}
,

for our later analysis; namely lower semicontinuity, convexity, and boundedness from below.

4.3.
Properties of the functional
\mathscr{E}

We begin with the following lemma concerned with the convexity of
\mathscr{F}
and its boundedness from below.

Lemma 4.4

(Convexity of
\mathscr{F}
and lower bound)
.

The functional
\mathscr{F}
is convex and there exist positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depending only on
\alpha
in (
3.7
) such that

(4.9)

\mathscr{F}[u]\geq-C_{1}\int_{0}^{2}u^{\prime}(y)^{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}y\geq-C_{2}\Big(1+\|u\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\Big)\qquad\forall u\in X.

Moreover,
\tilde{f}(u^{\prime})_{-}\in L^{1}([0,2])
for all
u\in X
, where
\tilde{f}(u^{\prime})_{-}
is the negative part of
\tilde{f}(u^{\prime})
.

In order to prove the above, we first need two technical lemmas (Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
).

Lemma 4.5

(
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
and
L^{\infty}_{loc}
estimate for
L^{2}
monotonic functions)
.

Let
v\in X
be such that
\partial_{y}v\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
. Then, for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subseteq[0,2]
we have the pointwise bounds

(4.10)

-\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}([0,y_{0}])}}{\sqrt{y_{0}}}\leq v(y)\leq\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}([y_{1},2])}}{\sqrt{2-y_{1}}}\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~}y\in(y_{0},y_{1}).

Consequently, given any compact subset
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
, we have the
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
-estimate
:

(4.11)

\|v\|_{TV([y_{0},y_{1}])}\leq\|v\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-y_{1}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{y_{0}}}\Big).

We remark that the assumption
\partial_{y}v\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
above implies immediately that the derivative is a locally finite measure, and hence
v\in BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
; the real content of the result are the estimates (
4.10
)–(
4.11
). This result is needed to obtain uniform estimates on the minimising sequences in the proof of Proposition
5.1
(when applying the direct method), since the functional
\mathscr{F}
is
a priori
not coercive.

Proof.

We begin with the upper bound. Note that
v
is an increasing function on
(0,2)
. We then distinguish two cases: if
v(y_{1})<0
the desired inequality is automatically satisfied since
v(y)\leq v(y_{1})<0
for all
y<y_{1}
. If instead
v(y_{1})\geq 0
, we use the fact that for
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
y\in(y_{1},2)
we have
v(y)\geq v(y_{1})
. Squaring and integrating in
y
over
(y_{1},2)
yields

(2-y_{1})v(y_{1})^{2}\leq\int_{y_{1}}^{2}v(y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y=\|v\|_{L^{2}([y_{1},2])}^{2}.

This provides the upper bound

v(y_{1})\leq\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}([y_{1},2])}}{\sqrt{2-y_{1}}}

and, again by monotonicity, the claim. The proof of the lower bound is similar. For the total variation estimate, we just need to use the equality
||v||_{TV([y_{0},y_{1}])}=v(y_{1})-v(y_{0})
, which is satisfied whenever
v
is non-decreasing; recall that when evaluating pointwise values of
v\in BV_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
we identify it with its precise representative given in Remark
2.4
.
∎

The next result provides a useful lower bound; its proof relies on Fenchel’s inequality.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\alpha
be as in (
3.7
),
0<\beta<(1-\alpha)^{-1}-\frac{3}{2}
, and set

(4.12)

h(y):=\big(y(2-y)\big)^{\frac{1}{2}+\beta},

which satisfies
\int_{0}^{2}h(y)^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty
and
h\in H^{1}_{0}([0,2])
. Then, there exists a positive constant
C
depending only on
\alpha,\beta
, such that for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
it holds for all
u\in X

(4.13)

\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\!\tilde{f}(u^{\prime})_{-}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C\bigg(\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([y_{0},y_{1}])}\|u\|_{L^{2}([y_{0},y_{1}])}+\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\!h(y)^{-\frac{1\!-\!\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\big[h(y)u(y)\big]^{y_{1}}_{y_{0}}\bigg).

Proof.

Using the bound (
3.7
) and
\tilde{f}(s)=sf(1/s)
, there exists
C>0
such that

(4.14)

\tilde{f}(u^{\prime}(y))_{-}\leq Cu^{\prime}(y)^{1-\alpha}\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~}(0,2).

We begin by showing that, for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
,

(4.15)

-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}u^{\prime}(y)^{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}h(y)^{-\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\geq-\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}u^{\prime}(y)h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

To see this, recall from Fenchel’s inequality that, for all
x\in(0,\infty)
and
z\in(-\infty,0)
, it holds

-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}x^{1-\alpha}+\frac{\alpha}{{1-\alpha}}(-z)^{-\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}}\geq xz,

where we recognise
\frac{\alpha}{{1-\alpha}}(-z)^{-\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}}=\sup\{xz+\frac{1}{{1-\alpha}}x^{{1-\alpha}}:x\in(0,\infty)\}
as the convex conjugate of the function
x\mapsto-\frac{1}{{1-\alpha}}x^{1-\alpha}
; the inequality (
4.15
) is then obtained directly from the above by setting
z=-h(y)
for
h(y)\geq 0
and
x=u^{\prime}(y)
, and integrating over the interval
[y_{0},y_{1}]
. Then, since
h\geq 0
and
\partial_{y}u\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
, the final term in (
4.15
) is estimated as

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}u^{\prime}(y)h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}h(y)\,\partial_{y}u(y)=-\!\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}u(y)\partial_{y}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\!+\!\big[h(y_{1})u(y_{1})\!-\!h(y_{0})u(y_{0})\big],

whence, by returning to (
4.15
), it holds

(4.16)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}u^{\prime}(y)^{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}h(y)^{-\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq-\|u\|_{L^{2}([y_{0},y_{1}])}\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([y_{0},y_{1}])}\!+\!\big[h(y_{0})u(y_{0})\!-\!h(y_{1})u(y_{1})\big].

Note that we have
0\leq\int_{0}^{2}h(y)^{-\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C\int_{0}^{1}y^{-(\frac{1}{2}+\beta)\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty
, as a consequence of the condition on
\beta
, which implies
-(\frac{1}{2}+\beta)\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}>-1
. Moreover, we have
\partial_{y}h\in L^{2}([0,2])
since
|\partial_{y}h|^{2}
behaves as
(y(2-y))^{2\beta-1}
, which is integrable for
\beta>0
.
∎

We are ready to prove Lemma
4.4
.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Since the convexity of the functional is straightforward, we are only concerned with the lower bound (
4.9
). Note that if the condition
\partial_{y}u\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
is not satisfied, then
\mathscr{F}[u]=+\infty
by Definition
4.1
, and the inequality is trivially satisfied. Henceforth, we assume
\partial_{y}u\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
, and we use (
4.13
) with
y_{0}=\varepsilon
and
y_{1}=2-\varepsilon
for an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. We obtain

(4.17)

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{2-\varepsilon}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime})_{-}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C\bigg(\!\|u\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\!+\!\!\int_{\varepsilon}^{2-\varepsilon}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!h(y)^{-\frac{{1-\alpha}}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\!+\!h(2\!-\!\varepsilon)u(2\!-\!\varepsilon)\!-\!h(\varepsilon)u(\varepsilon)\!\bigg).

Since
\partial_{y}u\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
, the assumptions of Lemma
4.5
hold, thus
|u(y)|\leq C(\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-y}})
for some
C>0
depending only on
\|u\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}
. This implies
h(\varepsilon)u(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and, then

(4.18)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime})_{-}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C\Big(1+\|u\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\Big)<+\infty.

This allows to prove at the same time (
4.9
) and
\tilde{f}(u^{\prime})_{-}\in L^{1}([0,2])
.
∎

Lemma 4.7

(Properties of
\mathscr{E}
)
.

The functional
\mathscr{E}:X\to\mathbb{R}
of Definition
4.2
is proper and

(4.19)

\mathscr{E}[v]\geq-C\Big(1+\|v\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\Big)\qquad\text{for all }v\in X,

where the positive constant
C
depends only on
\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}
and the exponent
\alpha
in (
3.7
). Furthermore, if
\{v_{n}\}_{n}
is a sequence converging to
v
in
X
, then

\mathscr{E}[v]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{E}[v_{n}]
and

(4.20)

\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\qquad\forall[y_{0},y_{1}]\subseteq[0,2].

Proof.

1.
Estimate and proper functional
. The estimate (
4.19
) follows immediately from the lower bound (
4.9
) and the estimate (
4.6
) on the drift term along with an application of Hölder’s inequality. It is clear that
\mathscr{E}
is proper since, for instance, the linear function
v_{*}(y)=y
for all
y\in[0,2]
belongs to the space
X
, and it holds

\mathscr{E}[v_{*}]=\int_{0}^{2}f(1)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{2}B(y,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq 2\big(f(1)+\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}\big)<\infty.

2.
Lower semicontinuity
. Note that
B
is continuous in its second argument and has at most linear growth. From the strong convergence
v_{n}\to v
in
L^{1}([0,2])
we deduce

(4.21)

\int_{0}^{2}B(y,v)\,\mathrm{d}y=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{2}B(y,v_{n})\,\mathrm{d}y.

Denoting
v^{\prime},v_{n}^{\prime}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
the absolutely continuous parts of the derivatives as in (
2.2
), we obtain the lower semicontinuity:

(4.22)

\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y,

on any compact subset
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
since the
BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
bound on
v_{n}
implies weak-* convergence of
\partial_{y}v_{n}
to
\partial_{y}v
on
[y_{0},y_{1}]
and the functional
\mathrm{Ent}
is lower semicontinuous for this convergence.

We have therefore verified (
4.20
) for all
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
, and it remains to verify it for
[y_{0},y_{1}]=[0,2]
. Using (
4.13
) with lower endpoint
0
and upper endpoint
y_{0}\in(0,1)
, noting that
v(0)h(0)=0
with
h
as prescribed by (
4.12
), we get

(4.23)

\int_{0}^{y_{0}}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\geq-C\bigg(\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([0,y_{0}])}+\int_{0}^{y_{0}}h(y)^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{y_{0}}}h(y_{0})\bigg),

where we also used that
v_{n}(y_{0})\geq-\frac{\sup_{n}\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}}{\sqrt{y_{0}}}
for
y_{0}\in(0,1)
from Lemma
4.5
. Analogously, for
1<y_{1}<2
, since
u(2)h(2)=0
, we have

(4.24)

\int_{y_{1}}^{2}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\geq-C\bigg(\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([y_{1},2])}+\int_{y_{1}}^{2}h(y)^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-y_{1}}}h(y_{1})\bigg),

where we also used that
v_{n}(y_{1})\leq\frac{\sup_{n}\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}}{\sqrt{2-y_{1}}}
for
y_{1}\in(1,2)
from Lemma
4.5
. As such,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{y_{0}}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{y_{1}}^{2}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y-C\bigg(\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([0,y_{0}])}+\int_{0}^{y_{0}}h(y)^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{y_{0}}}h(y_{0})\bigg)

\displaystyle-C\bigg(\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([y_{1},2])}+\int_{y_{1}}^{2}h(y)^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-y_{1}}}h(y_{1})\bigg),

for all
0<y_{0}<1<y_{1}<2
. By taking the
\liminf_{n}
on both sides and using (
4.22
), we get

(4.25)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\geq

\displaystyle\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\!-\!C\bigg(\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([0,y_{0}])}\!+\!\int_{0}^{y_{0}}\!\!h(y)^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\!+\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{y_{0}}}h(y_{0})\bigg)

\displaystyle-C\bigg(\|\partial_{y}h\|_{L^{2}([y_{1},2])}+\int_{y_{1}}^{2}h(y)^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-y_{1}}}h(y_{1})\bigg).

We let
y_{0}\to 0^{+}
and
y_{1}\to 2^{-}
in (
4.25
). Recall from the final part of Lemma
4.4
that the negative part
\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})_{-}
is integrable, whence
\int_{y_{0}}^{y_{1}}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y
tends to
\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y
as
y_{0}\to 0
and
y_{1}\to 1
; note that this integral may assume the value
+\infty
, but it is bounded from below by Lemma
4.4
. Hence, using the integrability of
h
and
\partial_{y}h
from Lemma
4.6
, we get
\liminf_{n}\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y
. Using (
4.21
), the proof of (
4.20
) is complete.
∎

We now record the monotonicity properties of
\tilde{f}^{\prime}
which are key to passing to the limit in the time-step
\tau\to 0
in §
6
to prove existence to the Lagrangian reformulation.

Lemma 4.8

(Quantified strict convexity of
\tilde{f}
)
.

Recall the function
\tilde{f}
defined in (
3.2
).
Then there exists a non-negative continuous function
\omega:[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
satisfying
\omega(s_{1},s_{2})=0
if and only if
s_{1}=s_{2}
, and

(4.26)

\tilde{f}(s_{1})-\tilde{f}(s_{2})-(s_{1}-s_{2})\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s_{2})\geq\omega(s_{1},s_{2})\qquad\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in(0,\infty).

Furthermore, for all fixed
s_{1}\in(0,\infty)
,
\omega
admits the limits

(4.27)

0<\lim_{s_{2}\to 0}\omega(s_{1},s_{2})\leq\infty,\qquad 0<\lim_{s_{2}\to\infty}\omega(s_{1},s_{2})\leq\infty.

Proof.

Let
s_{1},s_{2}\in(0,\infty)
. We define the quantity
\omega
by

(4.28)

\tilde{f}(s_{1})-\tilde{f}(s_{2})-(s_{1}-s_{2})\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s_{2})=\int^{s_{1}}_{s_{2}}\int_{s_{2}}^{r}\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}r=:\omega(s_{1},s_{2}).

This formula guarantees the continuity of
\omega
on
[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
using the integrability of
\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}
on all compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
, and shows that
\omega
vanishes on the diagonal. Also, this expression clearly provides
\omega(s_{1},s_{2})>0
for all
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
because of the strict convexity of
\tilde{f}
, which implies that
\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}
does not vanish on any interval; hence there exists a point in the interval
(s_{2},r)
where
\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}
is strictly positive, and by continuity of
\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}
a small neighbourhood on which this holds, giving rise to a strictly positive integral. Similarly, we obtain for the same reason
\lim_{s_{2}\to 0}\omega(s_{1},s_{2})>0
and
\lim_{s_{2}\to\infty}\omega(s_{1},s_{2})>0
. ∎

5.
Minimising Movement Scheme for the Lagrangian Problem

In order to show existence to (
3.9
), we proceed by means of an implicit Euler (Minimising Movement) scheme discretised with respect to the time variable. Our result is the following.

Proposition 5.1

(Implicit Euler time-stepping scheme)
.

Let
u_{0}\in X
be such that
\mathscr{F}[u_{0}]
is finite,
T>0
be arbitrary,
\tau>0
, and
N:=\lceil T/\tau\rceil
. For
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, define the functionals

(5.1)

\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]:=\frac{1}{2\tau}\|v-v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}^{2}+\mathscr{E}[v],

with
v_{0}:=u_{0}
, and
v_{k}\in\argmin\big\{\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]:v\in X\big\}
. Then, the minimisers
\{v_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset X
are well-defined, and satisfy
\partial_{y}v_{k}\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
and
\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y<+\infty
.

Proof.

We emphasise at the start of the proof that
\tau
and
k
are fixed throughout. By the estimate (
4.9
), the functional
\mathscr{E}^{k}
satisfies

\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]\geq\frac{1}{2\tau}\|v-v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}-2C\big(1+\|v\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\big).

It follows that
\mathscr{E}^{k}
is bounded from below and coercive in terms of the
L^{2}
norm.

We now argue by the direct method of the calculus of variations. By definition of the infimum, there exists a minimising sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
:

\mathscr{E}^{k}[v_{n}]<+\infty,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{E}^{k}[v_{n}]=\inf\big\{\mathscr{E}^{k}[w]:w\in X\big\}=:M^{k}>-\infty.

The aforementioned coercivity implies that the sequence
v_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}([0,2])
. Recall also
\partial_{y}v_{n}\geq 0
for all
n
, and these two facts together imply (by (
4.11
)) that
v_{n}
is bounded in
X
, and hence compact for the convergence in
X
. The lower semicontinuity result of Lemma
4.7
and the weak lower semicontinuity of the
L^{2}
norm imply the existence of a minimiser
v\in X
for the functional
\mathscr{E}^{k}
, which is such that
\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y
is finite and satisfies
(\partial_{y}v)_{s}\geq 0
in the sense
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
and
v^{\prime}\geq 0

\mathscr{L}
-a.e. in [0,2]. We set
v_{k}=v
.
∎

In fact, a stronger lower bound is available than simply
\partial_{y}v_{k}\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
for minimisers
v_{k}
of
\mathscr{E}^{k}
. This is encapsulated in the next lemma, and is fundamental in obtaining the Euler–Lagrange equation associated to
\mathscr{E}^{k}
,
cf.
Proposition
5.3
.

Lemma 5.2

(Strictly positive lower bound on
v_{k}^{\prime}
)
.

Assume the conditions of Proposition
5.1
hold, and let
\mathscr{E}^{k}
be as per (
5.1
) and
v_{k}\in\argmin\{\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]:v\in X\}
. Then, it holds

(5.2)

\essinf_{[0,2]}v_{k}^{\prime}>0,

where we use the notation
v_{k}^{\prime}
of Definition
2.3
.

Proof.

For ease of notation we omit the subscript
k
from the minimiser
v_{k}
of
\mathscr{E}^{k}
in this proof. Suppose for contradiction that (
5.2
) does not hold,
i.e.
, we suppose

(5.3)

\essinf_{[0,2]}v^{\prime}=0.

Our strategy is now to construct a competitor
\tilde{v}\in X
such that
\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]<\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]
, which contradicts the minimality of
v
in the class
X
; we explain the underlying idea in the lines that follow. By (
3.6
),
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime})
explodes as
v^{\prime}
approaches the value zero and, by the contradiction hypothesis (
5.3
),
v^{\prime}
takes arbitrarily small values on sets of positive measure. We therefore build a competitor
\tilde{v}
, modifiying some small values of
v^{\prime}
into larger values, so that
\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(\tilde{v}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y
is much less than
\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y
and such that the gain in this term overcomes the possible loss in the other terms of the functional
\mathscr{E}^{k}
.

1.
Construction of a competitor
. For all
\varepsilon>0
, we define the competitor

(5.4)

\tilde{v}(y):=v(y)+\int_{0}^{y}(2\varepsilon-v^{\prime}(z))\mathds{1}_{\{0\leq v^{\prime}(z)<\varepsilon\}}\,\mathrm{d}z\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~}y\in[0,2];

we omit the explicit dependence on
\varepsilon
to avoid heavy notation. Since the integrand is bounded by
2\varepsilon
, the integral is well-defined.

Moreover,
\tilde{v}\in X
for all choices of
\varepsilon>0
and, by differentiating (
5.4
) distributionally,

\partial_{y}\tilde{v}=\partial_{y}v+(2\varepsilon-v^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{\{0\leq v^{\prime}<\varepsilon\}}\geq 0,

where the final inequality holds in the sense
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
, and follows directly from
\partial_{y}v\geq 0
. By taking the absolutely continous part of the measure above, which is a linear operation,

\displaystyle\tilde{v}^{\prime}=v^{\prime}+(2\varepsilon-v^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{\{0<v^{\prime}<\varepsilon\}}=v^{\prime}\mathds{1}_{\{v^{\prime}\geq\varepsilon\}}+2\varepsilon\mathds{1}_{\{0\leq v^{\prime}<\varepsilon\}}\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~in }[0,2],

from which it follows from Definition
4.1
of the functional
\mathscr{F}
that

(5.5)

\mathscr{F}[\tilde{v}]=\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{\{v^{\prime}\geq\varepsilon\}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\tilde{f}(2\varepsilon)m(\varepsilon),

where we have used the shorthand notation
m(\varepsilon):=\mathscr{L}(\{0\leq v^{\prime}<\varepsilon\})
. Note that the contradiction hypothesis (
5.3
) implies
m(\varepsilon)>0
for all
\varepsilon>0.

2.
Estimates on competitor
. We compute the functional value for the competitor
\tilde{v}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]-\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau}\|\tilde{v}-v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}-\frac{1}{2\tau}\|v-v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{2}\Big(B(y,\tilde{v}(y))\!-\!B(y,v(y))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y\!+\!\mathscr{F}[\tilde{v}]-\mathscr{F}[v].

By expanding the
L^{2}
-inner product, denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, and using the Lipschitz bound (
4.6
) for
B
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]

\displaystyle-\!\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]\!-\!(\mathscr{F}[\tilde{v}]-\mathscr{F}[v])\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau}\Big|\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}\!-\!\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}\!-\!2\langle\tilde{v}\!-\!v,v_{k-1}\rangle\Big|+{\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}}\|\tilde{v}-v\|_{L^{1}([0,2])}.

Returning to (
5.4
), we note the pointwise estimate
|\tilde{v}(y)-v(y)|\leq 2\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)
for
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
y
, and

\displaystyle\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+2\langle v,\tilde{v}-v\rangle+\|\tilde{v}-v\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])},

from which we get
|\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}-\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}|\leq 4\sqrt{2}\|v\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)+8\varepsilon^{2}m(\varepsilon)^{2}
. In turn,

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]\!-\!\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]\!-\!(\mathscr{F}[\tilde{v}]-\mathscr{F}[v])\bigg|\leq C\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)\big(1+\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)\big),

where the positive constant
C
depends on
\|v\|_{L^{2}},\|v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}},\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}
,
\tau
, but is independent of
\varepsilon
. Meanwhile, by (
5.5
), we also have
\mathscr{F}[\tilde{v}]-\mathscr{F}[v]=-\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{\{0\leq v^{\prime}<\varepsilon\}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\tilde{f}(2\varepsilon)m(\varepsilon)
so that the previous inequality implies the upper bound

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]-\!\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{\{0\leq v^{\prime}<\varepsilon\}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\tilde{f}(2\varepsilon)m(\varepsilon)+C\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)\big(1+\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)\big);

the above holds for all choices of
\varepsilon>0
, with
C
independent of
\varepsilon
.

3.
Competitor beats minimiser
. Our aim is to show that the contribution from the negative integral term in (
5.6
) dominates, so that we have the strict inequality
\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]<\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]
.

As
\tilde{f}
is strictly decreasing (Remark
3.2
), it holds
\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{\{0\leq v^{\prime}<\varepsilon\}}\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\tilde{f}(\varepsilon)m(\varepsilon)
, whence

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]\!+\!m(\varepsilon)\Big[-\tilde{f}(\varepsilon)+\tilde{f}(2\varepsilon)+C\varepsilon\big(1+\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)\big)\Big]\qquad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,

and because of the hypothesis (
5.3
), it suffices to show that the term in the square brackets is strictly negative for some choice of
\varepsilon
; indeed, recall that the quantity
m(\varepsilon)
is strictly positive for all choices of
\varepsilon>0
. This quantity is also bounded above by
m(\varepsilon)\leq\mathscr{L}([0,2])=2
. It follows that, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have
1+\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)\leq 3
, and (
5.7
) yields

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{k}[\tilde{v}]

\displaystyle<\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]+\varepsilon m(\varepsilon)\Big[\frac{\tilde{f}(2\varepsilon)-\tilde{f}(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}+3C\Big]\qquad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1).

Meanwhile, the convexity of
\tilde{f}
gives

\frac{\tilde{f}(2\varepsilon)-\tilde{f}(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{f}^{\prime}(2\varepsilon)\to-\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
by (
3.6
). Thus, by choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, we contradict the minimality of
v
in
X
.
∎

The strictly positive lower bound on
v_{k}^{\prime}
from the previous lemma allows us to compute the Euler–Lagrange equation satisfied by the minimiser
v_{k}
; this is the content of the next result, which relies crucially on the aforementioned strictly positive lower bound on the derivative.

Proposition 5.3

(Discrete-time Euler–Lagrange)
.

The minimisers
\{v_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset X
of Proposition
5.1
satisfy
\essinf_{[0,2]}v_{k}^{\prime}>0
,
(\partial_{y}v_{k})_{s}\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
,
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\in H^{1}([0,2])
, and

(5.9)

\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}=\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))-b(y,v_{k}),

with
v_{k}^{\prime}
as per (
2.2
). For some positive universal constant
C
we also have

(5.10)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\|_{H^{1}([0,2])}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\big\|\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}\big\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}+\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big).

Proof.

The proof is divided into three steps.

1.
Euler–Lagrange equation in duality with
C^{1}([0,2])
. Let
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,2])
. Recall from Lemma
5.2
that it holds
\ell:=\essinf_{[0,2]}v_{k}^{\prime}>0
. In turn, for all
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(5.11)

|\delta|<\delta_{*}:=\frac{\ell}{(1+\|\partial_{y}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})},

it holds, using the notation (
2.2
),
\partial_{y}(v_{k}+\delta\varphi)=\partial_{y}v_{k}+\delta\partial_{y}\varphi\geq\ell_{\varphi}\!\cdot\!\mathscr{L}>0
, where
\ell_{\varphi}:=\ell(1-\frac{\|\partial_{y}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}}{1+\|\partial_{y}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}})
. Thus, for all
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,2])
and
\delta
satisfying (
5.11
), it holds

\mathscr{E}^{k}[v_{k}+\delta\varphi]=\frac{1}{2\tau}\|v_{k}+\delta\varphi-v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}^{2}+\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime}+\delta\partial_{y}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{2}B(y,v_{k}+\delta\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}y.

The minimality of
v_{k}
in the class
X\ni v_{k}+\delta\varphi
implies
\mathscr{E}^{k}[v_{k}]\leq\mathscr{E}^{k}[v_{k}+\delta\varphi]
, whence

(5.12)

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{0}^{2}\Big(\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime}+\delta\partial_{y}\varphi)-\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{2}\Big(B(y,v_{k}+\delta\varphi)-B(y,v_{k})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\tau}\int_{0}^{2}\Big(|v_{k}+\delta\varphi-v_{k-1}|^{2}-|v_{k}-v_{k-1}|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Choosing
\delta\in(0,\delta_{*})
and dividing by
\delta
, and then letting
\delta\to 0^{+}
, we get

(5.13)

0\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{2}\frac{\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime}+\delta\partial_{y}\varphi)-\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime})}{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{2}\Big(b(y,v_{k})+\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}\Big)\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y.

To evaluate the first term on the right-hand side, we use

\Big|\frac{\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime}+\delta\partial_{y}\varphi)-\tilde{f}(v_{k}^{\prime})}{\delta}\Big|\leq\sup_{s\geq\ell_{\varphi}}|\tilde{f}^{\prime}(s)|\cdot\|\partial_{y}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}=-\tilde{f}^{\prime}(\ell_{\varphi})\|\partial_{y}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.},

where we used that
\tilde{f}^{\prime}\leq 0
and
\tilde{f}^{\prime}
is increasing (
cf.
Remark
3.2
) to obtain the final inequality. In turn, by returning to (
5.13
) and applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we get

0\leq\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\partial_{y}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{2}\Big(b(y,v_{k})+\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}\Big)\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y\qquad\forall\varphi\in C^{1}([0,2]).

Replacing
\varphi
with
-\varphi
we finally obtain
equality for all
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,2])
, which proves (
5.9
) in duality with any
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,2])
.

2.
L^{2}
-estimate on
\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))
. Directly from (
5.9
), we obtain in the sense of distributions
\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))=\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}-b
, which shows that
\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))
belongs to
L^{2}([0,2])
with

(5.14)

\|\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq\Big(\big\|\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}\big\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}+\sqrt{2}\|b\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big).

3.
L^{1}
-estimate on
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})
. In order to obtain a full bound in
H^{1}
on
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})
we will also derive an
L^{1}
-estimate on it. To this end, we test the equation with
\varphi(y)=y
, for which
\partial_{y}\varphi=1
(note
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,2])
), and obtain (using
\tilde{f}^{\prime}\leq 0
)

\|\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\|_{L^{1}}=\int_{0}^{2}-\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{2}y\Big(\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}+b(y,v_{k})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Then, by the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality, we
also deduce a bound on the whole
H^{1}
norm of
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})
.
Now that we know that we have
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\in H^{1}
, the equation holds in weak form in duality with less smooth functions
\varphi
, and also in strong form as an aquality a.e. with the (
L^{2}
) distributional derivative of
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})
.
∎

Remark 5.4

(Improved weak formulation)
.

Choosing to test (
5.9
) with
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}((0,2))
, using the
H^{1}
-bound on
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})
obtained in (
5.10
), and integrating by parts, we deduce

(5.15)

\int_{0}^{2}\varphi\bigg(\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}+b(y,v_{k})-\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

Moreover, due to the density of
C^{1}_{c}((0,2))
in
L^{2}([0,2])
, the weak formulation (
5.15
) holds for all
\varphi\in L^{2}([0,2])
, and in fact the Fundamental Lemma of the calculus of variations implies

(5.16)

\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}=\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))-b(y,v_{k})\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~in }[0,2].

Corollary 5.5

(
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})
vanishes at the endpoints)
.

It holds
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\in H^{1}_{0}([0,2])
, i.e.

(5.17)

\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})|_{y=0}=\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})|_{y=2}=0.

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
5.3
that the Euler–Lagrange equation (
5.9
) holds in duality with
H^{1}([0,2])
. In particular, for all
r>0
, we define the truncation

\varphi_{r}(y)=\min\{\frac{y}{r},1\}-1,\qquad y\in[0,2].

Note that
\partial_{y}\varphi_{r}=\frac{1}{r}\mathds{1}_{\{y<r\}}
in the sense of distributions, whence
\varphi_{r}\in H^{1}([0,2])
for all
r>0
. Moreover we have
|\varphi_{r}|\leq 1
. By inserting
\varphi_{r}
into the weak formulation of (
5.9
), we get

(5.18)

\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{y}{r}-1\right)\Big(\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}+b(y,v_{k})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y=-\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y.

The left-hand side in (
5.18
) is controlled as follows:

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{r}\underbrace{\left(\frac{y}{r}-1\right)}_{\in[-1,0]}\Big(\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}+b(y,v_{k})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{r}\Big|\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}+b(y,v_{k})\Big|\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C\sqrt{r}.

Returning to (
5.18
) and letting
r\to 0
(keeping
k,\tau
fixed), an application of the Dominated Convergence Theorem and Lebesgue’s Differentiation Theorem imply

\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})|_{y=0}=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

An analogous procedure yields
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})|_{y=2}=0
by considering instead
\varphi_{r}(2-y)
.
∎

We conclude this section with the proof of a particular optimality condition which is needed in the sequel. Morally speaking, this says that
(\partial_{y}v_{k})_{s}
and
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})
have disjoint supports.

Lemma 5.6

(Optimality condition on the singular part of the derivative)
.

Let
v_{k}\in X
be a minimiser of
\mathscr{E}^{k}
provided by Proposition
5.1
. Then, we have

\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime})=0

(\partial_{y}v_{k})_{s}
-a.e.

Proof.

For clarity of presentation, in this proof, we omit the
k
subscript in our notation for
v_{k}
the minimiser of
\mathscr{E}^{k}
.

1.
Defining
w
such that
\partial_{y}w=(\partial_{y}v)_{s}
. Fix any Lebesgue point
y_{0}\in(0,2)
of
v
, and define

(5.19)

w:=v-\int_{y_{0}}^{y}v^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\in X.

It follows directly directly from the above that

(5.20)

\partial_{y}w=\partial_{y}v-v^{\prime}\!\cdot\!\mathscr{L}=(\partial_{y}v)_{s}\geq 0,\qquad w^{\prime}=0\quad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.},

where the inequalities hold in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
. In the lines that follow, we verify
w\in X
.

For
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
y\in(0,2)
, the non-negativity of the measure
(\partial_{y}v)_{s}
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
and
v^{\prime}\geq 0

\mathscr{L}
-a.e. (
cf.
Proposition
5.3
) imply

0\leq\int_{y_{0}}^{y}v^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{y_{0}}^{y}v^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{y_{0}}^{y}\,(\partial_{y}v)_{s}(s)=\int_{y_{0}}^{y}\,\partial_{y}v(s)=v(y)-v(y_{0}),

where we used the identification with the precise representative from Remark
2.4
and the formula (
2.3
). Hence, returning to (
5.19
), we have
0\leq|w(y)|\leq 2|v(y)|+|v(y_{0})|

\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
y\in(0,2)
.
As such, by integrating the previous inequality in
y
we get
\int_{0}^{2}|w(y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq 4|v(y_{0})|^{2}+8\int_{0}^{2}|v(y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y
. Hence
w\in L^{2}([0,2])
, and it follows that
w\in X
, as required.

2.
Variations along
w
. Our next objective is to show

(5.21)

\int_{0}^{2}\Big(b(y,v_{k})+\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}\Big)w\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

We do this by computing variations along
w
. Using (
5.20
), for all
|\delta|<1
, we have

(5.22)

\partial_{y}(v+\delta w)\geq 0\,\text{in }\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2)),\qquad(v+\delta w)^{\prime}=v^{\prime}.

The minimality of
v
in
X\ni(v+\delta w)
implies
\mathscr{E}^{k}[v]\leq\mathscr{E}^{k}[v+\delta w]
, from which we deduce
(
5.21
).

3.
Conclusion
. The strong form (
5.16
) of the Euler–Lagrange equation allows to test with
w
without needing to approximate by mollification or cut-off. Multiplying (
5.16
) with
w
and integrating, we get

(5.23)

0=-\int_{0}^{2}w\Big(\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{\tau}+b(y,v_{k})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{2}w\partial_{y}(-\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\prime}))\,\mathrm{d}y.

We would like to deduce (by integration by parts) from (
5.23
) that we have
\int\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k})\partial_{y}w=0
, which would give the claim. Yet, the difficulty comes from the fact that
\partial_{y}w
is not necessarily a finite measure (the boundary term should formally disappear because
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k})\in H^{1}_{0}
). Actually, using
\partial_{y}w\geq 0
and
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k})\leq 0
, it would be enough to obtain
\int\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k})\partial_{y}w\geq 0

The conclusion is obtained using the following facts:
w
is an
L^{2}
function, and
\partial_{y}w
is a positive measure;
\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k})
is a non-positive
H^{1}_{0}
function, which can be approximated strongly in
H^{1}_{0}
(and hence uniformly) by a sequence of functions
\varphi_{m}\in C^{1}_{c}
with
\varphi_{m}\leq 0
. We then write

\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k})\,\partial_{y}w\geq\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{0}^{2}\varphi_{m}\,\partial_{y}w=-\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{0}^{2}w\partial_{y}\varphi_{m}\,\mathrm{d}y=-\int_{0}^{2}w\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k}))\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

where the first inequality above comes from Fatou’s lemma, the first equality is an integration by part on a compact subset of
(0,2)
, the next one comes from
w\in L^{2}
and the strong
L^{2}
convergence of
\partial_{y}\varphi_{m}
to
\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{k}))
and the last is (
5.23
).
∎

6.
Solution of the Lagrangian Problem

In this section, we prove the main well-posedness result for the Lagrangian problem (
cf.
Theorem
3.6
). The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in X
be such that
\mathscr{F}[u_{0}]
is finite, and
T>0
. Then, there exists
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;X)\cap H^{1}([0,T];L^{2}([0,2]))
satisfying
\partial_{y}u(t,\cdot)\geq 0
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
and
0\leq-\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime})\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}([0,2]))
, and the equation

(6.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=\partial_{y}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u^{\prime}))-b(y,u),\\
&u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where the initial data is achieved in the sense
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|u(t,\cdot)-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}=0
, and with
u^{\prime}
as per (
2.2
). Moreover, for
\mathscr{L}
-a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, there exists
c_{t}
such that

(6.2)

\essinf_{[0,2]}u^{\prime}(t,\cdot)\geq c_{t}>0.

We will prove Proposition
6.1
via a sequence of lemmas. Firstly, we prove uniform estimates for the sequence of piecewise constant time-interpolations
\{u_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
. The solution of (
6.1
) provided by Proposition
6.1
will be obtained as the limit as
\tau\to 0^{+}
of the sequence
\{u_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
.

Lemma 6.2

(Properties of interpolations)
.

Let
u_{0}\in X
be such that
\mathscr{F}[u_{0}]
is finite,
0<\tau<1
,
T>0
, and
N:=\lceil T/\tau\rceil
. For
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, let the functionals
\mathscr{E}^{k}
and their minimisers
v_{k}
be as in Proposition
5.1
. For all
t\in[0,T]
, define the piecewise constant interpolation

(6.3)

u_{\tau}(t,\cdot):=v_{k},\qquad\text{for all }t\in((k-1)\tau,k\tau],\quad k\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}.

Then,
\partial_{y}u_{\tau}\geq 0
,
u_{\tau}(0,\cdot)=u_{0}
, and for some positive
C=C(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}},\mathscr{E}[u_{0}],\|b\|_{L^{\infty}},T)
independent of
\tau
, it holds the equicontinuity estimate
:

(6.4)

\|u_{\tau}(t,\cdot)-u_{\tau}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq C\big(\sqrt{|t-s|}+\sqrt{\tau}\big)\qquad\text{a.e.~}t,s\in[0,T],

(
in particular, the functions
u_{\tau}(t,\cdot)
are bounded in
L^{2}([0,2])
)

and the uniform estimates
:

(6.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2}\tilde{f}(u_{\tau}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C,\,\|u_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}([0,2])}\leq C,\,\|\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u_{\tau}^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}([0,2])}\leq C,

with
u^{\prime}_{\tau}
as per (
2.2
). Moreover, for all compact subsets
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subset(0,2)
, we have

(6.6)

\|u_{\tau}(t,\cdot)\|_{TV([y_{0},y_{1}])}\leq C_{y_{0},y_{1}},

where
C_{y_{0},y_{1}}=C_{y_{0},y_{1}}(T,y_{0},y_{1},\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}},\mathscr{E}[u_{0}],\|b\|_{L^{\infty}})
is independent of
\tau
. Also, we have

(6.7)

\tilde{f}^{\prime}(u_{\tau}^{\prime})=0\quad(\partial_{y}u_{\tau})_{s}\text{-a.e.,}\quad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~}t.

Proof.

Since
\partial_{y}v_{k}\geq 0
in the sense
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2))
for all
k
, direct computation yields:

(6.8)

0\leq\partial_{y}u_{\tau}(t,\cdot)\quad\text{in }\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,2)),\qquad\mathscr{L}\text{-a.e.~}t\in[0,T].

By construction,
u_{\tau}(0,\cdot)=v_{0}=u_{0}\in X
and
\mathscr{E}[u_{0}]\in\mathbb{R}
. Equality (
6.7
) follows from definition (
6.3
) and Lemma
5.6
for
v_{k}^{\prime}
. The rest of the proof deals with the uniform-in-
\tau
estimates.

1.
Estimate on discrete time-derivative
. The minimality of each
v_{k}
implies
\mathscr{E}^{k}[v_{k}]\leq\mathscr{E}^{k}[v_{k-1}]
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
i.e.
, it holds

(6.9)

\frac{1}{2\tau}\|v_{k}-v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}+\mathscr{E}[v_{k}]\leq\mathscr{E}[v_{k-1}]\qquad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,N\},

from which we deduce
\|v_{k}-v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq 2\tau(\mathscr{E}[v_{k-1}]-\mathscr{E}[v_{k}])
, and summing over
k
yields

(6.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|v_{k}-v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq 2\tau(\mathscr{E}[v_{0}]-\mathscr{E}[v_{N}])\leq 2\tau\mathscr{E}[u_{0}]+\tau C\big(1+\|v_{N}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\big),

where we used the lower bound (
4.19
). By the triangle inequality
\|v_{N}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|v_{k}-v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}
, and thus by returning to (
6.10
), we deduce

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\!\|v_{k}\!-\!v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}

\displaystyle\leq\!C\tau\Big(\mathscr{E}[u_{0}]+\!1\!+\!\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\Big)\!+\!C\tau\!\!\sum_{k=1}^{N}\!\|v_{k}\!-\!v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}

\displaystyle\leq\!C\tau\Big(\mathscr{E}[u_{0}]\!+\!1\!+\!\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\Big)\!+\!\frac{C^{2}}{2}N\tau^{2}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\!\sum_{k=1}^{N}\!\|v_{k}\!-\!v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}^{2},

where we used Young’s inequality in the final sum. In turn, using also that
N\tau\leq T+1
since
N=\lceil T/\tau\rceil
, we absorb the final term into the left-hand side, and we find

(6.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|v_{k}\!-\!v_{k-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq C_{0}\tau,

for some positive
C_{0}=C_{0}(T,\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}},\|b\|_{L^{\infty}},\mathscr{E}[u_{0}])
independent of the time-step
\tau
.

2.
Equicontinuity in time
. We rewrite the previous estimate (
6.11
) in terms of the interpolation (
6.3
). Let
0<t<s<T
such that
t\in((n-1)\tau,n\tau]
and
s\in((m-1)\tau,m\tau]
;
i.e.

n<m
. Then, by the triangle inequality, it holds

\|u_{\tau}(t,\cdot)-u_{\tau}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}\|v_{k}-v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq\bigg(\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}\|v_{k}-v_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}|n-m|^{\frac{1}{2}},

where we applied the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality; note that the above also holds if
n=m
, since in this case the left-hand side is zero. We note that
|n-m|\leq\frac{|t-s|}{\tau}+1
by our choice of
t,s
and thus, by (
6.11
), we deduce
\|u_{\tau}(t,\cdot)-u_{\tau}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq C_{0}(\sqrt{|t-s|}+\sqrt{\tau})
, where we recall
C_{0}
is independent of the time-step
\tau
. We have proved (
6.4
).

3.
Uniform
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{y}
-bound on
u_{\tau}
. Using the triangle inequality as before,

\displaystyle\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|v_{j}-v_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}+\sqrt{N}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|v_{j}-v_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}+\sqrt{C_{0}N\tau},

where we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
6.11
). Using again
N\tau\leq T+1
, we get that there exists a positive
C_{1}=C_{1}(T,\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}},\|b\|_{L^{\infty}},\mathscr{E}[u_{0}])
independent of
\tau
such that

(6.12)

\displaystyle\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}([0,2])}\leq C_{1}\quad\forall k\implies\|u_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}([0,2]))}\leq C_{1}.

4.
Estimates on the functional
. By dropping the positive contribution from the
L^{2}
bound in (
6.9
), we get
\mathscr{E}[v_{k}]\leq\mathscr{E}[v_{k-1}]
for all
k
, and thus
\mathscr{E}[v_{k}]\leq\mathscr{E}[u_{0}]
. It then follows from the Lipschitz estimate (
4.6
) on
B
and Jensen’s inequality that

Lemma 6.5

(Condition on the singular part of the derivative)
