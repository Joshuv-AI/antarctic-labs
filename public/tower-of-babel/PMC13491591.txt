Metagenomic insights into regional gut microbiota variation of invasive Spodoptera frugiperda across the Gaoligong Mountains
Authors: Jin-Gui Bi, You-Hui Wang, Pei-Kan Li, Qing Liu, Xiang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe invasive pest Spodoptera frugiperda poses a potential threat to the ecological security of western Yunnan, using the Gaoligong Mountains as an important cross-border corridor and overwintering site. However, the potential role of gut microbiota in the local adaptation of S. frugiperda during its invasion remains poorly understood.MethodsAdult populations were monitored using sex pheromone traps, and metagenomic sequencing was performed on larval gut microbiota from different regions of the Gaoligong Mountains. The gut microbial composition and functional potential were analyzed, with specific focus on the microbial traits potentially associated with host adaptation and invasion.ResultsS. frugiperda populations persisted year-round in the Gaoligong Mountains, with adult activity peaking from January to May. Microbial diversity was highest in southern samples. Enterococcus, typically dominant in S. frugiperda, displayed low abundance in the central and northern regions. In contrast, Providencia emerged as the dominant genus specifically at the Pianma site (PM, along the China-Myanmar border), where the gut microbiota exhibited higher abundance of site-specific functional genes compared to other regions. These genes encoded proteins including type 1 subunit membrane proteins and outer membrane-targeting proteins. Additionally, the PM samples showed a higher relative abundance of genes K07345, K07347, and K15125. Functional annotation highlighted a strong potential for vancomycin degradation and an enrichment of diverse antimicrobial resistance-associated genes, with adeL being the most abundant.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that the PM area may represent an important gateway or a priority monitoring site for the transboundary invasion of S. frugiperda, underscoring the urgency of strengthening local management of invasive pests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05259-2.

Background

The fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda, FAW) is a typical transboundary migratory agricultural pest characterized by high reproductive capacity, a broad host range, and rapid dispersal ability, posing an increasingly serious threat to global food security [1, 2]. Since its invasion of Yunnan, China, in 2019, FAW management has relied heavily on chemical pesticides [3, 4]; however, the consequent development of insecticide resistance has emerged as a major challenge to current control efforts. Traditional investigations into resistance mechanisms have mainly focused on target-site mutations and detoxification enzyme systems within the insect itself [5]. In contrast, recent studies have indicated that the gut microbial community of insects acts as a critical “symbiotic partner” in mediating host adaptation [6]. At present, chemical control remains a major strategy for FAW management. Although effective, the long-term and excessive application of insecticides has resulted in resistance of FAW to key active ingredients, including diamides and pyrethroids [7].

To address the challenges posed by FAW invasion to agricultural production in China, elucidating the mechanisms underlying pesticide detoxification—particularly the potential role of gut microbiota—is important for the development of novel control technologies [8, 9]. As an essential component of pest physiology, resistance-associated traits harbored by the gut microbiota may influence FAW susceptibility to chemical pesticides, thereby affecting the efficacy of control measures [10]. Studies have shown that resident microorganisms can promote insect fitness and protect insect hosts against pathogens [11, 12]. Furthermore, previous research has revealed significant differences in the gut microbial community structure of FAW populations from different geographical regions or feeding on different host plants. These variations have been associated with differences in tolerance to specific pesticides, such as chlorantraniliprole and pyrethroids. For instance, gut bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Bacillus have been reported to directly metabolize or degrade pesticide molecules, thereby reducing their effective concentration within the gut [13]. Concurrently, the gut microbial community may indirectly modulate the host response to pesticide stress through regulation of immune status.

Additionally, metabolites produced by the gut microbial community may function as signaling molecules that activate or enhance the activity of insect detoxification enzyme systems—such as P450 monooxygenases [14], carboxylesterases (CarE), and glutathione S-transferases (GSTs)—in the hepatopancreas, thereby contributing to a synergistic “microbiota-host” detoxification effect [7, 15]. Currently, the “antibiotic-mediated microbiota depletion and re-colonization” approach is widely applied for functional verification, often combined with 16S rRNA sequencing and metagenomics to identify key gut functional strains [16]. Relevant studies have established gut microbiota compositional profiles of FAW populations across China and monitored their susceptibility to commonly used pesticides, providing important foundational data for subsequent investigations into the mechanisms by which gut microbiota may mediate insecticide resistance in this pest [15]. These studies also complement the findings of the present work, highlighting the potential significance of the gut microbiota in FAW adaptive invasion and providing a basis for the future development of targeted control strategies [17].

Spanning an immense altitudinal gradient from 210 to 5,128 m, the Gaoligong Mountains harbor a heterogeneous mosaic of climates and vegetation [18], providing diverse insect habitats [19] and earning recognition as the “World’s Gene Bank for Species”. In this unique context, insect gut microbiota may exhibit high genetic diversity and environmental adaptability [20], potentially contributing to the evolution of more complex insecticide resistance-associated traits [21, 22]. Moreover, as a key ecological corridor connecting Southeast and South Asia, the Gaoligong Mountains are a critical hub for transboundary insect invasions. Breaching this barrier would threaten agriculture in southwestern China and beyond [23]. Consequently, investigating how gut microbiota may mediate the spread of the invasive pest FAW in western Yunnan is essential for reinforcing regional ecological security. Given the high migratory capacity of FAW adults, we first used pheromone-based monitoring to track population dynamics and identify infestation hotspots. We then collected larvae from these areas and performed metagenomic sequencing of their gut microbiota. Integrating adult monitoring data with larval gut metagenomic profiles allowed us to examine whether regional microbial variation was associated with FAW occurrence patterns and potential local adaptation during invasion.

Collection and dissection of FAW larvae in the Gaoligong Mountains

After collection, the larvae were transported to the laboratory for gut dissection and microbiota extraction. Prior to dissection, collected FAW larvae were surface-sterilized to eliminate external microbial contamination by immersion in 75% ethanol for 90 s, followed by three consecutive rinses with sterile water. Gut dissection was performed aseptically in an ultraclean workbench. Using sterile forceps, the entire gut of each FAW larva was carefully isolated in a sterile Petri dish [24, 25]. For subsequent experiments, each sample treatment consisted of 10 pooled individual guts, transferred into sterile 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. DNA extraction and Illumina sequencing of gut microbiota from FAW larvae

Genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using the HiPure Stool DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and integrity of the extracted DNA were subsequently assessed using two complementary approaches: NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for quantitative evaluation and agarose gel electrophoresis for qualitative assessment of fragment integrity [6]. For library construction, qualified genomic DNA was first fragmented to a target size of about 350 bp. The fragmented DNA was then subjected to a series of standard library preparation procedures, including end repair, 3’dA-tailing, and adapter ligation, using the NEBNext ® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, USA) following the manufacturer’s optimized protocol. DNA fragments ranging from 300 to 400 bp were selectively enriched by PCR. After amplification, PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) to remove primer dimers and other non-target fragments. Prior to sequencing, the constructed libraries underwent rigorous quality control. Their size distribution was analyzed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and library concentration was accurately quantified using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR). Finally, paired-end (PE150) high-throughput sequencing was performed on qualified libraries using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform [26].

Bioinformatics analysis of gut microbiota from FAW larvae

Quality control

Raw sequencing data generated from the Illumina platform were subjected to stringent quality filtering using FASTP (v 0.18.0) to ensure data reliability. Reads containing ≥ 10% ambiguous nucleotides (N), reads with ≥ 50% of bases having a Phred quality score ≤ 20, and reads aligned to barcode or adapter sequences were removed. Following quality control, the resulting high-quality clean reads were retained for subsequent genome assembly [27].

Assembly, gene prediction, and construction of a non-redundant gene catalogue

Clean reads from each sample were independently assembled de novo using MEGAHIT (v 1.2.9; k-mer range: 27–127, step: 10 bp; minimum contig length: 500 bp) to generate sample-specific contigs. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from assembled contigs longer than 500 bp using MetaGeneMark (v 3.38) [28]. Predicted genes with lengths ≥ 300 bp from all samples were pooled and clustered to construct a non-redundant gene catalogue using CD-HIT (v 4.6), with a sequence identity threshold of 95% and an alignment coverage threshold of 90%. Clean reads were subsequently mapped back to the predicted genes using Bowtie2 (v 2.3.5.1; default end-to-end alignment parameters) to quantify read counts per gene. To assign reads to their most likely source genes [29], Pathoscape (v 2.0.7) was employed to perform Bayesian re-assignment of aligned reads, resolving multi-mapped reads and improving taxonomic assignment based on alignment quality and genome coverage; subsequently, low-abundance genes with read counts < 2 were filtered out to generate the final non-redundant gene catalogue, and relative gene abundance was calculated for each sample. Boxplots illustrating the distribution of gene numbers across experimental groups were visualized using the ggplot2 package in the R programming environment (R Core Team, 2023) [13].

Taxonomic annotation and diversity analysis

Taxonomic annotation of the non-redundant gene catalogue was performed using a two-step sequence alignment strategy to improve annotation accuracy. First, all non-redundant genes were aligned against reference databases using DIAMOND (v 0.9.25) with a stringent e-value cutoff of 1 × 10–5 to remove low-confidence matches. Subsequently, species-level annotation was performed using Kaiju by aligning clean reads against the Non-Redundant Protein (NR) database of NCBI (e-value < 1 × 10–5), which includes bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses. α-diversity indices including Chao1, ACE, Shannon, and Simpson indices were calculated in R to characterize within-sample microbial diversity. Intergroup comparisons of α-diversity indices were performed using non-parametric statistical methods, specifically the Kruskal–Wallis test for overall differences among multiple groups [30].

For β-diversity analysis, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using the gmodels package in R to reduce the dimensionality of the microbial community data and visualize clustering patterns among samples. Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS), which is more suitable for ecological community datasets, was conducted using the vegan package in R, and the resulting ordination plots were visualized with ggplot2 [31]. To statistically evaluate differences in microbial community structure among groups, Adonis (PERMANOVA) and ANOSIM analyses were performed using the vegan package in R [32].

Function annotation

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Carbohydrate-Active enZYmes (CAZy), and Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) were used for functional annotation of gut microbiota-related genes with e-value < 1 × 10–5 [30, 33]. Non-redundant genes were additionally annotated using the BacMet-Scan (v 1.1, with e-value < 1 × 10–5) against the Antibacterial Biocide and Metal Resistance Genes Database (BacMet, v 2.0). Functional annotations associated with CARD were interpreted as antimicrobial resistance-related or potential xenobiotic tolerance-associated genes. Heatmaps of functional abundance were generated using the pheatmap package in R. Bar plots of functional abundance were generated using the ggplot2 and circos visualizations were generated using the circlize package in R [34].

Statistical analysis

Among-groups Venn analysis was performed using the Venn Diagram package in R. Comparisons of species and functional abundance among groups were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) in the vegan package of R. Differentially enriched KEGG pathways were identified according to reporter scores calculated from the Z-scores of individual KEGG Orthologs (KOs). Where applicable, p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software version 3.3.1 [35].

Results

Regional variation in adult population dynamics of FAW in the Gaoligong Mountains

The adult population dynamics of FAW in the Gaoligong Mountains exhibited distinct annual patterns. Monthly trapping data revealed a multi-peak distribution, with the highest activity occurring from January to May and relatively low abundance from June to September (Fig. 2A). A marked population increase was observed in March, whereas the lowest abundance occurred in September. Although FAW did not complete a full annual breeding cycle across all regions of the Gaoligong Mountains, its population dynamics consistently followed a regional pattern characterized by a major peak between January and May (Fig. 2B). Geographically, population density was highest in the southern regions, intermediate in the central regions, and lowest in the northern regions. Notably, the most severe outbreak occurred in LL in March 2023, followed by an outbreak in PM in April 2023. Metagenomic sequencing data of FAW larval gut microbiota

Metagenomic sequencing of FAW larval gut microbiota was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Detailed statistics of raw and clean reads after filtering and quality control are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. A total of 1,800,257,150 raw reads were generated from all gut microbiota samples. Following stringent filtering and quality control including removal of low-quality reads, adapter sequences, and contaminating reads, 1,795,367,116 high-quality clean reads were retained, corresponding to a data retention rate of about 99.73%. This high retention rate indicated the reliability and overall quality of the sequencing data. The clean reads were evenly distributed across individual FAW samples, ranging from 60,727,970 to 72,692,646 reads per sample, meeting the requirements for subsequent microbial community and functional analyses.

Discussion

Regional occurrence dynamics of FAW adults in the Gaoligong Mountains

The Gaoligong Mountains in southwestern Yunnan represent an important region where FAW first invaded China and currently exhibits year-round population activity [13]. Our field surveys revealed distinct spatial patterns in adult FAW occurrence with population gradually decreasing from south to north. To explore potential associations between gut microbiota and FAW environmental adaptation, the gut microbiota composition of larvae from nine sampling sites across the southern, central, and northern regions of the Gaoligong Mountains was analyzed based on adult occurrence dynamics. The results revealed marked regional specificity in microbial community structure, suggesting possible associations with invasion establishment, dispersal, and environmental adaptation of this pest.

Regional differences in gut microbiota composition of FAW larvae

At the phylum level, the dominant taxa including Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and unclassified viruses were consistent across all regions, which agree with the general characteristics of gut microbial communities reported in Lepidopteran insects [36]. However, their relative abundances varied substantially among regions. The southern regions exhibited a markedly higher abundance of Firmicutes, whereas the central and northern regions showed relatively similar phylum-level community structures, suggesting the presence of a potential geographic gradient in gut microbiota composition.

Regional differences became more pronounced at the genus level. Enterococcus, a well-recognized dominant genus in the FAW gut according to previous studies [21, 37–42], was highly abundant only in southern samples, with relative abundances ranging from 18.2 to 25.7%. Enterococcus has been reported to support host growth and development under nutrient-limited dietary conditions [36]. In contrast, FAW populations from the central and northern regions were dominated by Sphingobacteriaceae_noname, and the relative abundance of Enterococcus was below 5%. This discrepancy suggests the presence of region-specific microbial profiles in the central and northern areas of the Gaoligong Mountains. Nevertheless, several studies have demonstrated that Enterococcus abundance may vary according to the host plant species consumed by the FAW [43], and these microbes may exhibit increased activity during the egg stage, early larval stages, and the pre-oviposition period in females [24].

Specificity of the gut microbiota structure of FAW larvae at the PM sampling site

The PM sampling site, located near the China–Myanmar border in the central region of the Gaoligong Mountains, exhibited a distinctive gut microbiota structure. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria predominated, accounting for more than 60% of the relative abundance, which was about twice the average level observed at other central-region sampling sites.

At the genus level, one of the most notable finding of this study was the distinct microbial composition detected at the PM sampling site. Providencia, a genus rarely reported as dominant in the FAW gut, represented the most abundant genus at this site, accounting for 32.1% of the relative abundance. Previous studies have shown that Providencia can produce various degradative enzymes [44], and may participate in the metabolism of host plant secondary compounds [45, 46]. Therefore, the high relative abundance of Providencia at PM may reflect a site-specific microbial signature associated with local environmental selection, host plant conditions, or cross-border population movement. However, its functional role requires experimental validation.

Regional functional characteristics of gut microbiota in FAW larvae

KEGG functional annotation revealed substantial differences in the predicted metabolic pathways of gut microbiota among FAW populations from different regions of the Gaoligong Mountains. Southern samples exhibited relatively high abundances of genes associated with starch, sucrose, and galactose metabolism, which may be related to carbohydrate-rich host plants present in the southern regions [18]. This finding suggests that the gut microbiota may facilitate efficient decomposition and utilization of carbohydrate-rich plant resources. In contrast, central and northern samples exhibited relatively higher abundances of genes associated with amino acid and lipid metabolism [47], possibly reflecting adaptation to local host plants with relatively higher protein and lipid content. The PM site displayed the most distinct metabolic profile, with elevated abundances of genes associated with purine and pyrimidine metabolism (KEGG pathways ko00230 and ko00240). These pathways are involved in nucleic acid synthesis, energy metabolism, and antimicrobial-related metabolic processes [16, 25]. The enrichment of these pathways suggests that the gut microbiota at the PM site may possess enhanced metabolic flexibility for utilizing nucleotides and related compounds as nitrogen, carbon, or energy sources.

CAZy database analysis further highlighted regional variation in carbohydrate-active enzyme profiles. GH families GH13 and GH32 were most abundant in southern samples and are known to exhibit catalytic activity toward complex carbohydrates such as starch and fructan. These enzymes may facilitate nutrient assimilation and may also contribute to degradation of carbohydrate-conjugated plant secondary metabolites, thereby reducing phytotoxic effects [20, 48]. The PM site harbored the largest repertoire of unique CAZy families, including GH102, GH104, and GT44. GH102 and GH104 are associated with hydrolysis of chitin-containing polysaccharides, and may contribute to decomposition of insect exoskeletons or fungal cell walls as supplementary nutrients sources [49]. In contrast, GT44 is involved in glycoside biosynthesis and may participate in enzymatic modification of xenobiotic compounds. Additionally, PM samples showed elevated abundances of auxiliary activity (AA) families AA0 and AA4 [50], which are associated with redox reactions that may help alleviate oxidative stress induced by environmental factors.

Antimicrobial resistance potential of the gut microbiota in FAW larvae at the PM sampling site

CARD database analysis further indicated enrichment of antimicrobial resistance-associated genes in the FAW population at the PM site. A total of 111 unique genes were annotated in PM samples, compared with 2 genes in BZL and 19 genes in LL, representing a substantially higher diversity of annotated antimicrobial resistance-associated genes at the PM site. Among the identified genes, macB exhibits the highest relative abundance and is associated with functional potential for macrolide antibiotic resistance [51]. In addition, PM-site samples were enriched in other canonical antimicrobial resistance-associated genes, including TxR and msbA. The relatively high abundance of these genes may suggest enhanced microbial capacity for xenobiotic transport and stress response within the gut environment [33]. High-abundance antimicrobial resistance-associated genes previously reported in FAW populations from Guizhou, such as vanRC, and efrA [21], were also detected in the present study; notably, efrA showed relatively high abundance in samples from the southern regions of the Gaoligong Mountains.

Implications for pest management and limitations of the present study

Based on annual FAW outbreak dynamics in the Gaoligong Mountains, this study analyzed larval gut microbiota across different regions, and provides baseline information that may support for invasion monitoring and pest management efforts. The PM site, located within an important cross-border corridor and characterized by relatively high abundances of Providencia and antimicrobial resistance-associated genes, may represent an important monitoring and management priority area. Potential management strategies may include enhanced cross-border surveillance, evaluation of pesticide application strategies targeting efflux-associated tolerance mechanisms, and exploration of microbiota-based ecological regulation approaches to reduce colonization by potentially adaptive microbial taxa.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is based on metagenomic sequencing and therefore reveals microbial composition and predicted functional potential rather than direct gene activity or causal mechanisms. Second, although the PM site showed a distinct microbial profile and enrichment of antimicrobial resistance/xenobiotic tolerance-associated genes, these findings do not prove that gut microbes directly enhance pesticide tolerance or invasion success. Third, host plant, season, pesticide exposure history, and local environmental conditions may also influence gut microbiota composition. Future studies should combine strain isolation, microbiota depletion and re-colonization, pesticide bioassays, detoxification enzyme assays, qPCR validation, metatranscriptomics, and host transcriptome analysis to verify the functional contribution of PM-associated microbes.

Conclusion

FAW populations occurred throughout the year in the Gaoligong Mountains of western Yunnan, with clear regional variation in adult abundance and larval gut microbiota. The PM border site displayed a distinct microbial profile, characterized by relatively high abundances of Providencia and antimicrobial resistance/xenobiotic tolerance-associated genes. These metagenomic patterns suggest that PM may represent a priority monitoring site for transboundary FAW movement and local adaptation. However, because the present findings are correlative, further functional validation is required to determine whether PM-associated microbes directly influence pesticide tolerance, host adaptation, or invasion success. Overall, this study provides useful baseline evidence for regional FAW surveillance and microbiota-informed pest-management strategies in the Gaoligong Mountains.
