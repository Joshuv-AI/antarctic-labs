Climate change adaptation strategies as a pathway to gender empowerment in East Shewa Zone, Ethiopia
Authors: Amanuel Berhanu Bonso, Abayineh Amare Woldeamanuel, Teferi Tolera Engura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change threatens agricultural productivity and rural livelihoods, particularly among smallholder farmers in East Africa. In Ethiopia, rural women play critical roles in natural resource management; however, structural social, economic, and institutional barriers limit their adaptive capacity and reinforce gender inequalities. Despite growing attention to climate change adaptation strategies (CCAS), empirical evidence on its role in promoting gender empowerment remains limited. Therefore, this research aims at generating location-specific data on the contribution of CCAS to gender empowerment in the East Shewa Zone of Ethiopia. A multistage sampling procedure was employed to select 446 sample households. Data were collected through household survey, focus group discussion and key informant interviews across four districts and eight kebeles. Gender empowerment outcomes were assessed using a Gender Empowerment Index for Climate Change Adaptation Strategies (GEI-CCAS), covering social, economic, political, and agricultural dimensions, alongside a Gender Parity Index (GPI). Both descriptive and econometric model (binary logit model) were used for data analysis. The findings revealed that farmers implemented 22 climate adaptation strategies, with mixed cropping (91%) and crop calendar adjustment (89%) being the most common. Households adopting climate change adaptation strategies achieved higher empowerment outcomes than non-adopters, with greater involvement in decision-making, livelihoods, and leadership. Binary logistic analysis results indicated that, among male-headed households, gender empowerment increased with education level, livestock ownership, extension contact, annual income, training participation, and involvement in local institutions, while it decreased with greater market distance. For women-headed households, empowerment was positively influenced by education, extension access, income, training, cooperative membership, institutional participation, and irrigation access, whereas crop failure had a negative effect. Strengthening gender-responsive adaptation policies can contribute to achieving SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 5 (Gender Equality), SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities), and SDG 13 (Climate Action). The study recommends gender-responsive climate adaptation policies that enhance women’s access to resources, climate-smart technologies, institutional services, and leadership opportunities, thereby promoting gender equality and strengthening resilience to climate change.Keywords: Climate change adaptation, Gender empowerment, Gender equality, EthiopiaSubject terms: Development studies, Environmental studies, Social policy, Sociology

Introduction

Climate change has emerged as a global pressing phenomenon, with agriculture among the most vulnerable sectors1,2. It leads to events such as floods and droughts, which cause a reduction in global agricultural productivity3. Rural women are critical participants in improving food and nutrition security, as they are key players in land management and other resources such as water4. However, research conducted in Sub-Saharan African countries shows that male farmers have higher agricultural productivity than females due to unequal access to and control over agricultural inputs5.

Sub-Saharan Africa ranks sixth globally with an overall gender parity score of 68.0%. The region places fifth in Economic Participation and Opportunity (67.5%), reflecting a 4.8%-point improvement since 2006, and eighth in Educational Attainment (85.6%), up by 5.2% points, largely due to improved enrolment parity. Women surpass men in tertiary enrolment, consistent with global trends. In Political Empowerment, the region ranks fifth with a score of 22.2%; while it initially scored zero for years with a female head of state, this indicator reached 3.2% in 2025. Women now account for 40.2% of ministerial positions and 37.7% of parliamentary seats6.

This gender inequality reduces rural women’s engagement in household decisions and limits their voice in wider society in Ethiopia7. Ethiopia ranks 75th in 2025, improving four places from 2024 with a modest overall improvement of 0.1% points. However, the estimated earned income score declined from 66.2% to 55.7%, reducing the Economic Participation and Opportunity score by 3.4% points5,6. Women face gender-related constraints associated with customary laws that favor men, the absence of equal opportunities, and limited access to production inputs and resources, which lead to reduced agricultural production and productivity8. Consequently, women lag behind men due to the persistent gender gap in access to, control over, and use of productive resources and inputs8,9. The substantial gap in access to and control over agricultural inputs and limited engagement in household decision-making also negatively influence women’s potential adaptation to climate change10, even though women are key agents in natural resource conservation and coping strategies8.

In the context of climate change adaptation, women farmers face several constraints in adopting climate-smart agricultural practices on an equal basis with their male counterparts, affecting the benefits of men and women differently7,8. Evidence from some developing countries reports the potential of climate-smart agriculture to empower women, enhance gender equality, and shows how gender empowerment differs between adaptation users and non-users4,11. Thus, understanding the role of climate change adaptation in promoting gender empowerment and equality has important policy implications for reducing the gender gap. Several previous studies found that climate change adaptation measures (minimum tillage, use of organic manure, and growing drought-resistant varieties) vary across zones12,13 and involve different agricultural management practices and crop types14. Thus, there is scant literature on inequalities in adaptation to climate change8,11.

Nowadays, East Shewa Zone has long suffered from climate change vulnerability and has been targeted with emergency interventions in response to food insecurity through the Safety Net Programme. This situation of complex and prolonged crisis has affected the lives and livelihoods of thousands of people, particularly female-headed households in the study area15. This may help to clearly explore and take a broader view of how female-headed households are empowered in choosing different climate change adaptation options in the study area.

To assess gender parity and gender empowerment, the Gender Empowerment Index for Climate Change Adaptation Strategies (GEI-CCAS) and the Gender Parity Index (GPI) were formulated based on11, which cover different pathways to address gender empowerment while confronting climate change, in line with political, social, economic, and agricultural pillars. Previous studies in Ethiopia and related contexts have addressed gender and climate change adaptation, but important gaps remain regarding gender empowerment and equality outcomes16] and [17. focused on gender-responsive development and policy mainstreaming but did not assess whether adaptation interventions improve women’s empowerment or decision-making power.

Similarly18, examined climate-smart agriculture, livelihoods, and gender dimensions, but mainly focused on adoption and productivity rather than gender transformation, agency, or leadership. Studies by19,20, and21 focused on adaptation determinants but overlooked empowerment outcomes such as women’s participation in decisions and control over resources. Likewise22, , and23 addressed resource access, irrigation, and productivity but paid limited attention to whether adaptation promotes gender equality and social transformation. Studies by24,25, highlighted gender inequalities in adaptation but provided limited localized evidence.

However, existing literature largely overlooks whether climate change adaptation fosters sustainable gender empowerment in terms of women’s agency, leadership, and transformed gender relations. Empirical evidence from the East Shewa Zone also remains limited despite high climate vulnerability. To address this gap, this study applies a gender empowerment index to assess how adaptation strategies influence gender equality and empowerment at the household level. It further contributes a novel, context-specific index and provides the first quantitative evidence from East Shewa linking climate adaptation to gender parity.

Therefore, this study aims to bridge these gaps by providing empirical evidence from the East Shewa Zone of Ethiopia. Specifically, the study addresses the following questions: (1) What gender disparities exist in access to climate adaptation resources and services among rural households? (2) To what extent do climate change adaptation strategies (CCAS) contribute to gender empowerment and gender parity? (3) What are the major climate change adaptation strategies practiced by male- and women-headed households? and (4) Which socio-economic, institutional, and resource-related factors determine gender empowerment in climate adaptation among male- and women-headed households? Strengthening gender-responsive and gender-transformative adaptation policies can contribute to SDG 5 (Gender Equality) by enhancing women’s empowerment, equitable access to resources, and participation in decision-making, and SDG 13 (Climate Action) by improving resilience and adaptive capacity to climate change. The findings are intended to guide the development of targeted, integrated, and gender-sensitive policies that strengthen institutional support and, in turn, enhance the climate resilience of smallholder farming systems.

Theoretical framework of the study

This study is theoretically grounded in the Women’s Empowerment Theory developed by26 and supported by the Gender and Development (GAD) Theory advanced by scholars such as27. These theories provide a strong framework for explaining how climate change adaptation strategies can promote gender empowerment and gender equality among rural households. Climate change adaptation is not only an environmental response mechanism but also a socio-economic and institutional process that influences access to resources, participation in decision-making, adaptive capacity, and livelihood opportunities. In rural areas such as East Shewa Zone, where women often face unequal access to productive resources and institutional support, adaptation interventions may either reinforce or reduce existing gender inequalities.

Women’s Empowerment Theory, developed by26, serves as the principal theoretical foundation of the study. Kabeer defines empowerment as the expansion of people’s ability to make strategic life choices. The theory emphasizes three key dimensions: resources, agency, and achievements. Resources include access to land, credit, education, agricultural technologies, and climate information; agency refers to participation in decision-making and control over livelihood choices; while achievements involve improved income, livelihood security, leadership participation, and social recognition. The theory is particularly relevant because climate adaptation strategies such as climate-smart agriculture, irrigation, livelihood diversification, extension services, and climate information systems can strengthen women’s productive capacity, resilience, and decision-making power. The study also draws insights from the Capability Approach developed by28, which emphasizes expanding individuals’ freedoms and capabilities through improved access to knowledge, technologies, and institutional support.

The study is further supported by Gender and Development (GAD) Theory, developed by scholars such as27. GAD Theory explains how socially constructed gender relations create inequalities in access to resources, opportunities, and decision-making power. The theory argues that sustainable development requires equal participation and equitable benefits for both men and women. This perspective is highly relevant to the current study because women’s vulnerability and adaptive capacity are often shaped by unequal gender relations, including limited access to land, agricultural inputs, extension services, credit, and climate information. Consequently, adaptation strategies that promote equitable access to resources, inclusive participation, and gender-sensitive policies can reduce gender disparities and improve women’s socio-economic status.

Overall, the two theories complement each other in explaining the relationship between climate change adaptation and gender empowerment. Women’s Empowerment Theory by26 focuses on access to resources, agency, and achievements at the individual and household levels, while GAD Theory by27 highlights the broader structural and institutional gender inequalities that shape adaptation benefits. Together, these theories provide a comprehensive framework for analyzing whether climate change adaptation strategies promote gender empowerment and gender equality in East Shewa Zone.

Research methodology

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in the East Shewa Zone, located in the Oromia regional state of Ethiopia. The East Shewa Zone occupies a central position within the Oromia region and has geographical coordinates ranging from 7º33’ 50” to 9º 08’ 56” N latitude and 38º24’ 10” to 40º05’ 34” E longitude. According to data from the East Shewa Zone Administrative Office15, the total area of the zone is 9178km2. It falls within the tropical zone and has an elongated shape due to its location within the rift system. The Zone shares physical boundaries with three other regional states of Ethiopia: Sidama, Afar, and Amhara. Specifically, it is bordered to the southeast by Afar, to the north by Amhara National Regional State, to the southeast by Arsi Zone, to the west by Southwest Shewa Zone, and to the south by West Arsi Zone. The shortest border is with Afar National Regional State, while the longest border is with Arsi Zone.

The Zone is relatively narrow along its northern and southern borders. According to the population projection report by the Central Statistical Agency29, the total population of the East Shewa Zone was 3,356,342, consisting of 1,696,350 men and 1,659,992 women. This results in a crude population density of 124 individuals per square kilometer. The zone has a total of 309,726 households, with an average household size of 4.38 persons and 296,342 housing units. The three largest ethnic groups in the Zone are the Oromo (84.06%), the Amhara (5.39%), and the Gurage (3.82%), while other ethnic groups make up 6.73% of the population. The primary language spoken by 79.15% of the population is Oromo, followed by Amharic at 14.29% and Gurage at 2.64%. The remaining 3.92% speak various other primary languages. In terms of religious affiliation, the majority of inhabitants identify as Ethiopian Orthodox Christians (39.33%), followed by Muslims (31.18%), Protestants (23.4%), and those adhering to traditional beliefs (5.08%)26.

Research design and sampling technique

The research design for this study was a cross-sectional survey incorporating both qualitative and quantitative components to capture their complementary strengths. Cross-sectional studies collect data at a single point in time from a sample, providing a snapshot of population characteristics and behaviors30. A multi-stage sampling procedure was employed to select study households. In the first stage, four districts, Fantale, Boset, Adamitulu Jidokombolcha, and Adama, were purposively selected from the 11 districts in the East Shewa Zone due to their high exposure to recurrent drought and erratic rainfall. Due to this, those districts are now targeted as beneficiaries of the Safety Net Programme in the zone15.

In the second stage, eight kebeles (the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia), two from each district, were randomly selected, as almost all kebeles were relatively similar in their socio-economic context. Finally, 446 households were randomly selected from the selected kebeles using probability proportional to size. The sample size was determined using Cochran’s31 formula, which yielded a minimum requirement of 365 households. To account for potential non-response and incomplete questionnaires, the sample size was increased by 22.2%, resulting in a final sample of 446 households. Adjusting the sample size beyond the minimum requirement is methodologically justified, as it helps maintain adequate statistical power and representativeness despite possible non-response. In addition, larger sample sizes reduce sampling error, enhance parameter stability, and improve the accuracy and reliability of statistical inference32. Furthermore, larger samples are recommended in survey design to strengthen the validity of estimates and ensure more reliable population inference, particularly in heterogeneous populations33.

Therefore, the final sample size enhances the overall accuracy and generalizability of the study findings. Regarding the distribution of sample respondents across kebeles, in Adama District (Humo Facasa and Ceka Alem Tena), 122 households were selected, including 85 male-headed and 37 female-headed households. Similarly, in Boset District (Bose Dache and Buta Dalecha), 106 households were selected, comprising 74 male-headed and 32 female-headed households. Likewise, in Fantale District (Gola and Elalana Kereri), 107 households were selected, of which 75 were male-headed and 32 female-headed households. In Adami Tulu Jildo Kombolcha District (Abine Garmama and Aluto), 111 households were selected, comprising 77 male-headed and 34 female-headed households. Overall, 446 households (311 male-headed and 135 female-headed) were proportionally selected from the four districts, representing both male- and female-headed households.

Data for this study were collected from both primary and secondary sources. The primary data were collected using the Kobo Collect Toolbox, an open-access digital platform administered through smartphones. Primary data were gathered from sampled households across the eight kebeles. The main data collection instruments included structured household survey questionnaires, focus group discussions (FGDs), and key informant interviews (KIIs). A structured questionnaire was used to collect detailed information on household demographics, socioeconomic conditions, institutional factors, and psychological factors. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and subsequently translated into Afan Oromo (local language) to ensure clarity and contextual understanding. A pilot survey was conducted prior to full deployment to test the questionnaire design and to familiarize enumerators with the data collection process.

In parallel with the household survey, six focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted, each consisting of 10 participants, giving a total of 60 participants. Three FGDs were held with male participants and three with female participants to ensure gender balance. Participants were purposively selected based on their farming experience and knowledge of local climate change impacts, with emphasis on experienced and knowledgeable community members, particularly elders. The FGDs were designed to generate in-depth qualitative information to complement the survey findings. The FGDs were facilitated by a team of three trained moderators who received one day of training on participatory research methods, including village mapping, and conducted the discussions in the local language to ensure effective communication.

In addition, 34 key informants were interviewed to triangulate and enrich the quantitative findings. These included 6 experts at the zonal level, 12 at the district level, and 16 at the kebele level (including kebele administrators and development agents). The key informants were purposively selected based on their professional roles and relevance to climate change adaptation and agricultural development in the study area, ensuring they had appropriate knowledge and experience for the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants to ensure voluntary participation. Furthermore, secondary data were collected from both published and unpublished sources through a systematic literature search using databases such as Google Scholar, Scopus, and Web of Science with keywords related to climate change adaptation, gender empowerment, and Ethiopia. Only peer-reviewed articles, books, and reputable institutional reports published mainly within the last 5–7 years were included, while irrelevant, non-peer-reviewed, or outdated sources were excluded.

Construction of the gender empowerment and gender parity index

This study aims to establish the interconnection between gender empowerment indicators and climate change adaptation strategies. Despite the growing interest in its measurement and research, measuring gender empowerment remains a major challenge. To assess gender empowerment, empirical studies often begin with the empowerment definition provided by26, who defined gender empowerment as the process of increasing the ability of individuals, particularly women and marginalized genders, to make strategic life choices and to participate fully in social, economic, and political life.

Researchers and development practitioners attempt to determine what influences this ability and to identify the key domains of gender empowerment for measurement purposes34. The choice of domains—legal, social, economic, political, and psychological—depends on the local context or the topic of analysis35. Thus, several studies36–38 have attempted to measure women’s empowerment using a combination of different domains, considering its multidimensional nature. However, recent research has increasingly applied multidimensional Gender Empowerment Indices aligned with specific research objectives. The Women’s Empowerment in Agriculture Index (WEAI), a survey-based tool developed to measure gender empowerment in agriculture11,39, is one widely used example.

As mentioned above, the Gender Empowerment Index for Climate Change Adaptation Strategies (GEI-CCAS) was developed based on the guidelines of the Women’s Empowerment in Agriculture Index (WEAI) proposed by11,39 and). In this study, the GEI-CCAS focused on four dimensions of gender empowerment: social, economic, political, and agricultural, addressing the participation and decision-making power of both women and men. These dimensions were further categorized into several indices and sub-indicators.

Table 1 presents the four pillars and their respective indicators. To derive the Gender Empowerment Index for Climate Change Adaptation (GEI-CCAS), weights were assigned to each pillar based on the focus group discussions (FGDs) conducted during the survey. Equal weights (0.1) were assigned to the social, political, economic, and agricultural pillars because there was no empirically robust or statistically validated evidence to justify prioritizing one pillar over another. In multi-criteria assessment, equal weighting is widely recognized as a scientifically acceptable and methodologically transparent approach when objective information for differential weighting is unavailable40,41. Moreover, the four pillars were considered highly interconnected and collectively influential in shaping community resilience and sustainable development; therefore, assigning unequal weights could introduce subjectivity and analytical bias. Consequently, equal weighting was adopted to ensure neutrality, consistency, and balanced representation of all dimensions within the assessment framework.

To assess changes in the participation of women and men, a questionnaire using a five-point Likert scale was prepared and coded as follows: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree. The responses collected through the Likert scale were subsequently rescaled into three categories for analysis: (1) improved (agree and strongly agree), (2) unchanged (neutral), and (3) reduced (strongly disagree and disagree). These grouped indicators were used to assess changes in the participation of men and women in climate change adaptation and non-climate change adaptation activities across the four pillars in East Shewa Zone. The categorized scales were then used to construct the Gender Empowerment Index for Climate Change Adaptation Strategies (GEI-CCAS).

Consequently, responses indicating “agree” and “strongly agree” were coded as 1, while the remaining three response categories were recoded as 0. The GEI-CCAS was then derived as the weighted sum of the four pillars, and a threshold value of 0.8 was adopted as the benchmark for achieving an acceptable level of empowerment11,39. Similarly, the Gender Inequality Index (GII), which reflects disparities between adult men and women across the different empowerment pillars within each household, was developed based on the approach proposed by39. The index was calculated by subtracting the women’s GII score from the men’s GII score, and the resulting values were classified into three categories. Values greater than 0.1 were categorized as indicating inequality favoring men, values less than − 0.1 were categorized as indicating inequality favoring women, and values between − 0.1 and 0.1 were considered to indicate relative equality between men and women.

Adaptation index

To assess household’s adaptation strategies to climate change, adaptation practices currently employed in the study area were identified through an extensive review of literature and focus group discussions (FGDs) with male and female farmers. Although farmers implemented multiple adaptation practices to cope with climate change, these practices varied in their effectiveness and contribution to reducing climate-related risks and vulnerabilities. Therefore, a Climate Change Adaptation Index (AI) was constructed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to capture the relative importance of each adaptation practice. PCA is a multivariate statistical technique widely used to reduce a large set of correlated variables into a smaller number of uncorrelated components while retaining most of the variation in the original data42,43. Accordingly, households were categorized as adopters or non-adopters based on the grand mean adaptation score. A household whose score was higher than the grand mean was classified as an “adopter,” whereas a household with a score equal to or below the grand mean was classified as a “non-adopter”45. Subsequently, a dummy variable was created from the adaptation index, taking a value of 1 for adopters and 0 otherwise. The mean threshold is widely used in adoption studies because PCA-based indices are relative measures that rank households by adaptation intensity rather than providing an absolute benchmark. Thus, households with an adaptation index above the sample mean were classified as adopters, while those at or below the mean were non-adopters, indicating lower adaptation engagement44,45. The sample mean provides an objective and statistically consistent cutoff for distinguishing above- and below-average adaptation performance. This binary transformation also enables the use of logistic regression models commonly applied in adoption and climate change studies with dichotomous outcomes46,47.

Result and discussions

Descriptive analysis of respondents

Table 2 reveals the summary of descriptive analyses of household characteristics in the study area. There were statistically significant differences between male-headed households (MH-HHs) and women-headed households (WH-HHs) in terms of several variables, including household education level, family size, livestock holdings, land size, frequency of agricultural extension contact, distance from the nearest market, annual income, access to credit services, access to weather information, participation in climate-related training, experience of crop failure, land tenure security, participation in PSNP, access to climate-smart agricultural technologies, participation in local decision-making institutions, participation in income-generating activities, and access to irrigation services. In the focus group discussions, participants stated that participation in local decision-making institutions, including kebele meetings, farmer associations, water user committees, natural resource management groups, and indigenous organizations such as Iddir and Equb, plays a vital role in empowering women for climate change adaptation in East Shewa Zone. These platforms enable women to access climate information, contribute to community decisions, and benefit from social support during climate-related shocks. However, their participation remains constrained by cultural norms, heavy domestic responsibilities, limited educational opportunities, and male-dominated leadership structures, which weaken their influence in decision-making processes.

Climate change adaptation strategies adopters by gender of households

In this study, households were categorized using a dummy variable based on the adoption index (1 if the value was greater than the mean and 0 otherwise) (Eq. 4). Accordingly, 233 male-headed and 81 female-headed households were categorized as adopters of climate change adaptation strategies, whereas 78 male-headed and 54 female-headed households were categorized as non-adopters.

Table 2 clearly reveals a noticeable gender gap in climate change adaptation, highlighting that adaptation is not only determined by exposure to climate risks but also by the capacity to respond effectively. Overall, out of the total 446 households, 70.4% are adopters of climate change adaptation strategies, while 29.6% remain non-adopters, indicating a moderate but uneven level of adaptation uptake in the study area. A closer examination shows that male-headed households account for the majority of adopters. Out of 311 male-headed households, 75% (233 households) have adopted climate change adaptation strategies, while only 25% (78 households) have not. This indicates that male-headed households are relatively better positioned to respond to climate shocks, likely due to improved access to productive resources such as land, income, extension services, and climate-related information, which enhance their adaptive capacity. In contrast, female-headed households demonstrate lower adoption levels. Among the 135 female-headed households, only 60% (81 households) are adopters, while a relatively higher 40% (54 households) are non-adopters. This suggests that female-headed households face persistent structural constraints, including limited access to resources, information, and institutional support, which hinder their ability to effectively implement climate change adaptation strategies.

Overall, the findings imply that adaptation is higher where resource access and support systems are stronger, and lower where such constraints are more pronounced. Male-headed households therefore appear more resilient and better able to adapt to climate change, while female-headed households remain comparatively more vulnerable and less engaged in adaptation practices.

Gender-based climate change adaptation practices in the study area

Smallholder farmers in East Shewa Zone have long relied on proactive adaptation strategies within their traditional farming systems to manage increasing climate risk. These locally rooted practices demonstrate strong resilience to environmental variability. However, as seen across East Africa, many farmers still prioritize short-term coping over long-term adaptation due to institutional barriers and limited access to affordable resources such as credit, extension services, and improved inputs. Despite these challenges, agriculture remains central to adaptation, as maintaining crop productivity is vital for household food security and livelihoods. Table 4 presents 22 adaptation strategies adopted by male- and female-headed households, showing both shared practices and gender-based differences. Mixed cropping (91%) and adjusting cropping calendars (89%) were the most common, reflecting traditional risk-spreading methods used to cope with rainfall variability. Early-maturing and drought-tolerant crops are also widely used, especially among male-headed households (91% and 84% female-headed), securing harvests before dry spells in a warming climate. Gender differences emerged in specific strategies. Female-headed households favour sustainable land management practices such as agroforestry (56%) and improved seeds and fertilizers (55%), which are lower in cost and align with women’s roles in food production and land stewardship.

Gender empowerment index (GEI-CCAS)

The gender empowerment index for men and women in CCAS and non-CCAS are presented in Figs. 2 and 3. Thus, more men in the climate change adaptation were empowered, with some men scoring GEI greater than 0.8 and above compared to men in non-CCAS. The recoding of Likert-scale responses into binary values and the construction of a weighted composite index (GEI-CCAS) is justified by established multidimensional empowerment measurement approaches, particularly the39 methodology, which transforms ordinal responses into adequacy indicators to enable aggregation across multiple empowerment domains. This approach ensures comparability, reduces measurement complexity, and captures empowerment as a multidimensional construct rather than a single variable. The use of a threshold (0.8) is also justified under standard cut-off practices in empowerment and capability-based indices, where a minimum score is required to classify individuals or households as empowered, consistent with applied climate-gender studies such as11. The situation was similar for women in the CCAS where more women were empowered than those in the non-CCAS. Many women in the CCAS scored GEI more than 0.8 and equal to one, while a significant number of women in the non-CCAS was not empowered with zero GEI (Figs. 2 and 3).

This finding indicates that participation of both men and women in climate change adaptation strategies (CCAS) contributes positively to gender empowerment. Although empirical evidence on this relationship remains limited, several studies provide partial support for this result. For instance, climate-smart agriculture and related adaptation practices have been shown to enhance livelihood outcomes and empowerment among rural households50,51. Similarly, gender-responsive agricultural interventions have been associated with reduced gender gaps and improved productivity outcomes in farming systems23,52.

However, the present finding contrasts with8, who reported that participation in climate-smart agriculture acts as a catalyst for gender empowerment in East Africa, suggesting a stronger and more direct empowerment effect than observed in this study. Likewise11, found in the Indian context that women’s empowerment through climate-smart village approaches not only reduces gender inequality but also enhances agricultural productivity. The inconsistency may be attributed to contextual differences in institutional support, resource access, and sociocultural norms across study areas.

When the gender empowerment index (GEI) is disaggregated into its four pillars, the results show that women participating in CCAS are more empowered across all domains compared to women in non-CCAS households. Among these, the economic pillar emerges as the most influential driver of women’s empowerment, followed by agricultural and social domains. This is consistent with evidence that economic participation is a key pathway through which climate adaptation strengthens women’s agency and decision-making power34,53.

In contrast, women in non-CCAS households show relatively higher empowerment only in the social domain compared to other pillars, indicating limited transformation in economic and agricultural dimensions. For men, both CCAS and non-CCAS groups demonstrate higher empowerment in the economic pillar compared to other domains, suggesting persistent gendered patterns in resource control and livelihood engagement. These findings align with37, who emphasize that climate adaptation outcomes are shaped by existing gendered structures that may reproduce or transform inequalities depending on context.

Overall, the distribution of GEI across the four domains (Figs. 2 and 3) reinforces the argument that climate adaptation can be an important entry point for gender empowerment, but its effects are uneven across gender and empowerment dimensions. Recent literature further supports this view by highlighting that addressing structural inequalities is essential for ensuring that climate adaptation translates into inclusive empowerment outcomes7,35,54. Research conducted in Ethiopia supports the finding that participation in climate change adaptation strategies (CCAS) improves women’s gender parity and household decision-making status. For instance8, found that households engaged in climate-smart agriculture in Ethiopia and Kenya achieved significantly higher levels of women’s empowerment and gender parity compared to non-participating households, with women gaining stronger involvement in agricultural production and household decision-making. Similarly10, reported that intra-household gender disparities significantly reduce climate change adaptation capacity, while households with stronger women’s participation demonstrate more balanced gender relations and improved adaptation outcomes in Ethiopia. In the same line23, showed that reducing gender gaps in agricultural productivity is closely linked with improved women’s access to resources and decision-making power, which reinforces the present finding that CCAS households show higher parity than non-CCAS households.

Evidence from broader Sub-Saharan Africa and global South contexts further strengthens this result11. found that climate-smart village interventions enhance women’s participation in farm decisions and improve household welfare and gender relations. Similarly34,37, emphasize that gender-responsive climate adaptation strategies promote women’s empowerment, strengthen agency, and contribute to climate justice and equality outcomes54. also highlight that integrating gender perspectives into climate adaptation frameworks in Ethiopia improves women’s adaptive capacity and decision-making roles. Likewise4,53, found that gender-responsive agricultural and climate policies are essential for ensuring equitable participation and empowerment outcomes.

However, some evidence suggests that improvements in parity may not always reflect full empowerment7. argue that gender inequality can persist in climate adaptation systems even when participation increases, meaning that equality in outcomes may mask underlying structural gaps. Similarly35, emphasize that broader socio-economic inequalities, such as education gaps, still constrain women’s climate resilience. Overall, the evidence consistently shows that CCAS participation enhances women’s empowerment and gender parity, although deeper structural inequalities may persist in some contexts.

Factors affecting gender empowerment in climate change

This study used multiple linear regression model to analyze the factors affecting gender empowerment in climate change. Prior to the main data analysis, multicollinearity was assessed. All explanatory variables had Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values below 10, with an average VIF of 1.19, and correlation coefficients (CC) below 0.07. These results indicate that multicollinearity is not a concern. In addition, the model demonstrates a good overall fit, as the log-likelihood chi-square statistic is significant at the 1% level, confirming strong explanatory power. In addition, a comprehensive assessment was performed to evaluate the model’s fitness, and the coefficient of determination (R²) served as a key metric to gauge the model’s ability to explain variations in the dependent variable. Ranging between 0 and 1, the coefficient of determination provides crucial insights: a low R² value, approaching 0, indicates limited explanatory power, whereas a value closer to 1 suggests that the independent variables encompass almost all necessary information for accurately predicting the dependent variable.

Seven variables were found to significantly influence gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies among male-headed households, while eight variables significantly affected women-headed households. For male-headed households, gender empowerment increased with education level, livestock holding, extension contact, annual income, participation in training, and participation in local decision-making, whereas it decreased with market distance. In women-headed households, gender empowerment increased with education level, extension contact, annual income, participation in training, cooperative membership, participation in local decision-making, and access to irrigation, while it decreased with crop failure experienced during the last ten years. These findings indicate that socio-economic characteristics, institutional support services, and access to productive resources play an important role in shaping gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies. The results further suggest that the determinants of empowerment vary between male- and women-headed households, implying the need for gender-responsive and targeted policy interventions to strengthen adaptive capacity, resource access, and inclusive participation in climate adaptation practices (Table 6).

Education level of household heads

It was hypothesized that the education level of household heads would positively influence gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because education enhances awareness, access to information, and decision-making capacity related to climate adaptation practices. The result reveals that education level positively and significantly influences gender empowerment among both male-headed and women-headed households at 10% significance level (p < 0.1). The odds ratio indicates that the likelihood of gender empowerment increases by a factor of 1.23 for male-headed households and 2.26 for women-headed households as the education level increases by one grade. This implies that educated household heads are more likely to understand and adopt climate adaptation strategies that promote inclusive participation and empowerment. The possible explanation is that education improves access to climate-related information, analytical skills, and confidence in engaging with adaptation programs and community institutions. This finding is consistent with the study by55, which reported that education enhances women’s participation and empowerment in agricultural value chains by improving access to information and productive opportunities. Similarly51, found that education positively influences awareness and adoption of climate-smart agricultural practices in Ethiopia. However, this finding contradicts the argument by56, who noted that education alone may not guarantee women’s empowerment where strong socio-cultural and institutional barriers continue to limit women’s access to productive resources and decision-making power.

Livestock holding

It was hypothesized that livestock holding would positively affect gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because livestock assets improve household economic stability and adaptive capacity. The finding shows that livestock holding positively and significantly influences gender empowerment among male-headed households at 10% significance level (p < 0.1). The odds ratio indicates that the likelihood of gender empowerment increases by a factor of 1.27 as livestock holding increases by one unit. This suggests that households with larger livestock assets are better positioned to diversify livelihoods and support adaptation measures that empower household members, including women. Livestock ownership may also strengthen financial resilience during climate-related shocks. This finding is in line with57, who found that strengthening household productive assets such as livestock contributes to women’s empowerment through improved financial and social capital in rural Ethiopia. However53, argued that livestock ownership does not always empower women because men often control large livestock assets and income generated from them, limiting women’s decision-making authority.

Extension contact

It was hypothesized that access to extension services would positively influence gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies since extension agents provide technical advice, climate information, and institutional support. The result confirms that extension contact positively and significantly affects gender empowerment among male-headed households at 10% significance level (p < 0.1) and women-headed households at 5% significance level (P < 0.05). The odds ratio shows that the probability of gender empowerment increases by a factor of 1.61 for male-headed households and 3.28 for women-headed households with increased extension contact. This finding suggests that frequent interaction with extension agents enhances awareness and adoption of climate adaptation practices while improving women’s participation in agricultural and household decision-making processes. This finding agrees with50, who reported that climate-smart agricultural innovations and extension support significantly improved women’s livelihoods and adaptive capacity in Ethiopia. Likewise53, underscore that inclusive extension systems are critical for achieving gender-responsive climate-resilient agriculture. Nevertheless, some studies, such as56, observed that extension services in many African countries remain male-biased, often excluding women farmers from accessing information and training opportunities.

Market distance

It was hypothesized that market distance would negatively affect gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because longer distances limit access to markets, information, and economic opportunities. The result indicates that market distance negatively and significantly influences gender empowerment among male-headed households at 1% significance level (p < 0.01). The odds ratio reveals that the likelihood of gender empowerment decreases by a factor of 0.97 as market distance increases by one kilometer. This implies that households located far from markets face greater challenges in accessing climate adaptation inputs, information, and income-generating opportunities, which may reduce empowerment outcomes. This finding is consistent with58, who found that access to markets and diversified agricultural systems improves women’s economic participation and empowerment. However59, argued that even where market access exists, unequal control over income and mobility restrictions may continue to constrain women’s empowerment.

Annual income

It was hypothesized that annual income would positively influence gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because higher household income enhances the capacity to invest in climate adaptation measures, access productive resources, and participate in socio-economic activities. The result shows that annual income positively and significantly influences gender empowerment among both male-headed and women-headed households at 1% significance level (p < 0.01). The odds ratio indicates that the likelihood of gender empowerment increases by a factor close to 1.00 for both male-headed and women-headed households as annual income increases by one unit. Although the odds ratio appears approximately equal to one due to the large measurement scale of income, the positive coefficient and strong statistical significance confirm that increases in household income improve the probability of gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies. The possible explanation is that households with better financial capacity are more capable of accessing climate adaptation technologies, agricultural inputs, training opportunities, and diversified livelihood activities, which enhance women’s participation and adaptive capacity. This finding is supported by4, which indicated that improving women’s economic opportunities and access to financial resources strengthens climate resilience and empowerment outcomes. Conversely60, argued that increased agricultural income does not always translate into empowerment because women may experience heavier workloads and limited control over generated income.

Training participation

It was hypothesized that participation in training would positively affect gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because training enhances knowledge, technical skills, and awareness regarding climate adaptation practices. The finding reveals that training positively and significantly influences gender empowerment among male-headed households at 1% significance level (p < 0.01) and women-headed households at 10% significance level (p < 0.1). The odds ratio indicates that the likelihood of gender empowerment increases by a factor of 4.07 for male-headed households and 7.88 for women-headed households among those who participated in training. This suggests that climate-related training programs play a vital role in strengthening awareness, confidence, and participation in adaptation activities, particularly among women. This result agrees with19, who found that agricultural innovation training improves women’s empowerment and participation in productive activities. Similarly53, emphasized that gender-responsive training programs are essential for socially inclusive climate-resilient agriculture. However, some studies indicate that training programs may unintentionally exclude women due to poor scheduling, mobility constraints, and gender-insensitive approaches.

Cooperative membership

It was hypothesized that cooperative membership would positively influence gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because cooperatives facilitate access to information, credit, social networks, and collective action. The result indicates that cooperative membership positively and significantly affects gender empowerment among women-headed households at 5% significance level (p < 0.05). The odds ratio shows that women-headed households who are cooperative members are 9.21 times more likely to experience gender empowerment compared to non-members. This may be because cooperatives create opportunities for women to access resources, participate in decision-making, and strengthen their social and economic capacities in adapting to climate change. This finding is consistent with55, who reported that collective organizations and agricultural value chains enhance women’s agency and economic participation. Similarly59, emphasized that women-led cooperatives are critical for building resilience and climate adaptation capacity. However4, cautioned that when climate adaptation interventions become economically profitable, men may increasingly dominate control over cooperative resources and benefits, thereby limiting women’s long-term empowerment outcomes (Table 6).

Crop failure experience

It was hypothesized that crop failure would negatively influence gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because repeated production losses reduce household resilience and economic capacity. The result confirms that crop failure during the last ten years negatively and significantly affects gender empowerment among women-headed households at 5% significance level (p < 0.05). The odds ratio indicates that the probability of gender empowerment decreases by a factor of 0.01 for households experiencing crop failure. This finding suggests that recurrent crop losses severely undermine the adaptive capacity and economic independence of women-headed households, thereby limiting their participation in climate adaptation activities. This finding supports the report by4, which showed that climate-related shocks disproportionately reduce women’s income and increase their vulnerability in rural areas. Likewise61, found that droughts and climate extremes widen gender inequalities in labor participation and resilience.

Participation in local decision-making

It was hypothesized that participation in local decision-making would positively affect gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because involvement in community decisions enhances confidence, representation, and access to local institutions. The result reveals that participation in local decision-making positively and significantly influences gender empowerment among male-headed households and women-headed households at 10% significance level (p < 0.1). The odds ratio indicates that the likelihood of gender empowerment increases by a factor of 2.21 for male-headed households and 28.46 for women-headed households among those participating in local decision-making processes. This suggests that community participation substantially strengthens women’s voice, influence, and access to adaptation-related opportunities and resources. This finding is supported by53, who emphasized that agency and leadership are essential dimensions of gender-inclusive climate-resilient agriculture. Similarly57, reported that women’s participation in local institutions strengthens voice, collective action, and empowerment in rural Ethiopia. In contrast56, argued that deeply rooted patriarchal norms often restrict women’s effective participation in community decision-making despite formal inclusion efforts.

The FGDs also indicated that participation in local decision-making plays a vital role in empowering women in climate adaptation strategies, as women who actively participate in community meetings and local institutions are more likely to access climate-related information, adaptation programs, and communal resources. Participants reported that involvement in local decision-making enhances women’s confidence, leadership skills, and bargaining power within households and communities. However, traditional gender norms, limited public participation opportunities, and heavy domestic workloads were identified as major constraints limiting women’s effective participation in local governance and adaptation planning processes.

Access to irrigation

It was hypothesized that access to irrigation would positively influence gender empowerment in climate adaptation strategies because irrigation enhances agricultural productivity and reduces vulnerability to rainfall variability. The finding shows that access to irrigation positively and significantly affects gender empowerment among women-headed households at 10% significance level (p < 0.1). The odds ratio indicates that women-headed households with access to irrigation are 7.47 times more likely to experience gender empowerment compared to those without irrigation access. This may be attributed to the role of irrigation in improving food security, income generation, and women’s participation in productive agricultural activities under changing climatic conditions. This finding is in agreement with58, who reported that diversified and irrigated agricultural production systems improve women’s economic opportunities and empowerment. Likewise50, found that climate-smart agricultural innovations, including irrigation practices, improve women’s livelihoods and resilience in Ethiopia. However4, highlighted that irrigation and climate adaptation interventions may sometimes reinforce existing gender inequalities when men dominate ownership of irrigated land, technologies, and market benefits (Table 6).

FGD participants explained that irrigation practices related to fruit and vegetable production particularly benefit women more than large-scale cereal production such as wheat cultivation. Participants noted that women are more actively engaged in irrigated fruit and vegetable farming because these activities are conducted on smaller plots, require intensive management, and provide frequent income for household consumption and local market sales. The income generated from vegetable and fruit production was reported to improve women’s economic independence, household decision-making power, and food security. In contrast, irrigated wheat production was described as being largely dominated by men due to larger land requirements, mechanization, and market control. However, inadequate irrigation infrastructure, unequal access to water resources, and financial limitations in acquiring irrigation technologies were highlighted as major challenges particularly affecting women-headed households.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study examined the role of climate change adaptation strategies in promoting gender empowerment and gender equality among rural households in East Shewa Zone, Ethiopia. The findings reveal that participation in climate adaptation strategies significantly improves empowerment outcomes across social, economic, political, and agricultural domains. Households adopting climate adaptation practices demonstrated better participation in decision-making, improved access to productive resources, enhanced livelihood opportunities, and stronger adaptive capacity compared to non-adopters. However, substantial gender disparities persist in access to education, land, livestock, extension services, climate information, irrigation, and financial resources, which continue to limit women’s participation in climate-resilient agriculture. Male-headed households generally exhibited higher adaptation rates due to relatively better access to productive assets and institutional support, whereas female-headed households faced greater structural and socio-economic barriers.

The binary logistic regression results further confirmed that education level, extension contact, annual income, participation in climate-related training, and involvement in local decision-making institutions positively and significantly influence gender empowerment among both male- and women-headed households. In addition, livestock holding significantly improved empowerment among male-headed households, while cooperative membership and access to irrigation positively enhanced empowerment among women-headed households. Conversely, market distance negatively affected empowerment among male-headed households, whereas recurrent crop failure significantly reduced empowerment among women-headed households. These findings suggest that socio-economic conditions, institutional support, and access to productive resources are key determinants of gender empowerment in climate adaptation.

Despite existing constraints, women in adapting households achieved higher empowerment levels, particularly in economic and agricultural domains, and many attained parity or even exceeded men in several empowerment indicators. Adaptation activities such as agroforestry, mixed cropping, irrigation-based fruit and vegetable production, cooperative participation, and climate-related training strengthened women’s income generation, leadership participation, resilience to climate shocks, and household decision-making power.

Therefore, climate change adaptation should be viewed not only as a mechanism for reducing climate vulnerability but also as an important pathway for advancing gender empowerment and social inclusion. Accordingly, government institutions, development partners, and non-governmental organizations should strengthen gender-responsive and gender-transformative adaptation policies that improve women’s access to productive resources, climate-smart technologies, extension services, irrigation, credit facilities, and market opportunities. Expanding gender-sensitive training programs, improving access to climate information systems, strengthening rural infrastructure and social protection programs, and promoting women’s participation in local institutions and cooperatives are essential to enhance adaptive capacity and resilience. Ultimately, the findings demonstrate that climate adaptation can serve as a powerful pathway for gender equality when policies intentionally address the structural barriers limiting women’s access to resources, information, and decision-making opportunities.

Limitation of the study and future research

This study provides important insights into the relationship between climate change adaptation and gender empowerment in East Shewa Zone. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish long-term causal relationships between adaptation strategies and empowerment outcomes. Second, the study relied on self-reported survey data, which may be affected by recall bias and social desirability bias. Third, although the study considered major socio-economic and institutional factors, it did not fully capture intra-household power relations, cultural norms, and intersectional factors such as age, wealth status, and youth differences. Therefore future research should employ longitudinal and mixed-method approaches to better understand the long-term effects of climate change adaptation on gender empowerment. Comparative studies across different agro-ecological and socio-cultural settings are also recommended to validate and strengthen the Gender Empowerment Index for Climate Change Adaptation (GEI-CCAS). Furthermore, future studies should give greater attention to intra-household differences and intersectional inequalities influencing adaptation and empowerment outcomes.
