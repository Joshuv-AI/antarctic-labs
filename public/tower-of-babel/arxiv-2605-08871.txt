Rennala MVR : Improved Time Complexity for Parallel Stochastic Optimization via Momentum-Based Variance Reduction
Zhirayr Tovmasyan, Artavazd Maranjyan, Peter Richtárik

Abstract
Large-scale machine learning models are trained on clusters of machines that exhibit heterogeneous performance due to hardware variability, network delays, and system-level instabilities. In such environments, time complexity rather than iteration complexity becomes the relevant performance metric for optimization algorithms. Recent work by 31 established the first time complexity analysis for parallel first-order stochastic optimization, proposing Rennala SGD as a time-optimal method for smooth nonconvex optimization. However, Rennala SGD is fundamentally a modification of SGD , and variance reduction techniques are known to improve the iteration complexity of SGD . In this work, we investigate whether variance reduction can also improve time complexity in heterogeneous systems. We show that, under a mean-squared smoothness assumption, variance reduction can improve time complexity in relevant parameter regimes. To this end, we propose Rennala MVR , a variance-reduced extension of Rennala SGD based on momentum-based variance reduction, and analyze its oracle and time complexity. We establish lower bounds for time complexity under these assumptions. On a stochastic quadratic benchmark, experiments with the exact method support the theory, while neural-network experiments with a practical inexact variant show similar empirical gains over Rennala SGD .

1
Introduction

Modern machine learning models have grown to a scale that makes training on a single machine impractical.
As a result, training is performed in distributed environments spanning multiple machines with heterogeneous computation speeds, communication delays, and system variability.
In such settings, the efficiency of optimization algorithms is determined not only by the number of iterations required for convergence, but also by their time complexity, which is critically affected by system-level heterogeneity.

Traditionally, optimization methods for training machine learning models have been analyzed through the lens of iteration complexity, which measures how many algorithmic steps are needed to reach a target accuracy.
While this metric has been instrumental for understanding algorithmic efficiency in idealized settings, it becomes insufficient in distributed and heterogeneous environments.
In practice, two methods with similar iteration complexity can exhibit vastly different runtimes due to idle time, synchronization overhead, and other system-level bottlenecks.
This mismatch has motivated a shift toward time complexity as a more faithful measure of algorithmic performance in real-world distributed systems.

Recent work by
31
derived the first lower bounds on time complexity for parallel first-order methods with smooth nonconvex losses.
They matched this lower bound with a method called
Rennala SGD
, which is a modification of classical stochastic gradient descent (
SGD
)
(
26
)
.
This line of work highlights a fundamental principle: achieving optimal performance in distributed systems often requires redesigning classical algorithms to account for system heterogeneity.

Beyond
SGD
,
variance reduction
techniques have been shown to substantially improve optimization efficiency in the nonconvex regime under iteration complexity analysis.
Methods such as
STORM

(
6
)
(which uses momentum variance reduction (
MVR
)) or
SNVRG

(
36
)
reduce gradient noise and achieve stronger convergence guarantees than vanilla
SGD
under additional assumptions.
However, despite their strong theoretical and empirical advantages, the time complexity of variance-reduced methods in heterogeneous distributed systems remains unexplored.

This raises a natural question: if variance-reduced methods outperform
SGD
in terms of iteration complexity, can they also achieve better time complexity, and how should they be modified to do so?

In this work, we initiate a systematic study of time complexity for distributed variance-reduced optimization.
We propose
Rennala MVR
, a variance-reduced extension of
Rennala SGD
, and show that it achieves better time complexity under the stronger mean-squared smoothness assumption (
2.4
).

Problem Setup

We consider the nonconvex optimization problem

\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(x)\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(x;\xi)\right]\right\},

(1)

where
f(x;\xi)
is the loss function evaluated on a data sample
\xi
drawn from distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and the model is parameterized by
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d
denoting the parameter dimensionality.

We consider a distributed learning setting with
n
workers, where each worker
i
has access to the same data distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
This setting is common in data centers with either unified memory or uniformly partitioned data.
Following the
fixed computation model

(
22
)
, we formalize the heterogeneous computation times as follows:

Assumption 2.1

(Fixed Computation Model)
.

Each worker
i
requires
\tau_{i}
seconds to compute one stochastic gradient
\nabla f(x;\xi)
.
Without loss of generality, we assume

0<\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{n}.

We assume instantaneous communication (zero latency) between workers and the server in both directions.
This is the standard modeling assumption in prior work on time complexity for distributed stochastic optimization
(
22
;
13
;
31
;
20
)
, and we adopt it here in order to make a direct comparison with these results.

We stress that this is a modeling simplification rather than a claim that communication is negligible in practice.
Explicitly modeling communication costs leads to a substantially richer theoretical problem, since one must then also specify which communication-reduction mechanisms are allowed, such as compression, quantization, sparsification, or local updates.
These questions are important, but largely orthogonal to the present paper, whose goal is to understand how variance reduction affects time complexity under heterogeneous worker speeds within the standard theoretical model.

2.1
Assumptions

We make the following standard assumptions:

Assumption 2.2

(Lower boundedness)
.

There exists
f^{*}>-\infty
such that
f(x)\geq f^{*}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We define
\Delta\coloneqq f(x^{0})-f^{*},
where
x^{0}
is the starting point of the optimization methods.

Assumption 2.3
.

For every
\xi
, the function
f(x;\xi)
is differentiable with respect to its first argument
x
.
Moreover, the stochastic gradients are unbiased and have bounded variance
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
, that is,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\nabla f(x;\xi)\right]=\nabla f(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Assumption 2.4

(Mean-squared smoothness)
.

There exists
\bar{L}>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)\|^{2}\right]\leq\bar{L}^{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

This assumption is stronger than classical
L
–smoothness of
f
, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5

(Proof in
Section

B.1
)
.

Mean-squared smoothness (
2.4
) implies that
f
is
\bar{L}
–smooth, i.e.

\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|\leq\bar{L}\left\|x-y\right\|,\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~.

Mean-squared smoothness also implies expected similarity, a centered gradient-difference bound closely related to the notion of Hessian variance introduced by
27
.

Lemma 2.6

(Proof in
Section

B.2
)
.

Mean-squared smoothness (
2.4
) implies expected similarity with the same constant
\bar{L}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)-(\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y))\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\bar{L}^{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2},\quad\forall\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Under these assumptions, our objective is to find an
\varepsilon
–stationary point: a (possibly random) point
x
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}]\leq\varepsilon
.

3
Background and Motivation

Under Assumptions
2.2
and
2.3
, combined with
L
–smoothness of
f
(instead of
2.4
), the
SGD
method with iterations
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k})
and i.i.d.
\xi^{k}\sim\mathcal{D}
achieves optimal oracle complexity
(
10
;
4
)
of
\mathrm{O}(\nicefrac{{L\Delta}}{{\varepsilon}}+\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}L\Delta}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})
.
Oracle complexity measures the total number of stochastic gradient evaluations; on a single machine, this corresponds to the iteration complexity.

However, achieving time-optimal performance with
n
parallel machines requires effectively parallelizing
SGD
.

31
addressed this question by proposing the
Rennala SGD
method, which is a minibatch extension of
SGD
.
Instead of computing a single gradient,
Rennala SGD
collects
B
gradients and takes a step using their average.
The key to achieving optimal time complexity is distributing the batch of size
B
across all available machines and computing it asynchronously (as quickly as possible).
By setting
B=\max\{1,\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}}}{{\varepsilon}}\}
,
Rennala SGD
attains the lower bound on time complexity.
Importantly, the oracle complexity—the total number of gradient evaluations—remains
\mathrm{O}(\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}L\Delta}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})
, matching the lower bound up to a constant factor (see the last column in
Table

1
).

When we additionally invoke
2.4
, which is stronger than
L
–smoothness of
f
(
Lemma

2.5
), we can achieve better oracle complexity on a single machine using variance reduction methods such as
STORM

(
6
)
or
SNVRG

(
36
)
.
These methods improve the oracle complexity to
\mathrm{O}(\nicefrac{{\bar{L}\Delta\sigma}}{{\varepsilon^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}}+\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}}}{{\varepsilon}})
and are optimal
(
4
)
.

This motivates our central question: does time complexity also improve under the stronger
2.4
, and which method can we use to surpass
Rennala SGD
’s time complexity?
We focus on the
STORM
algorithm
(
6
)
and try to make use of its
MVR
technique because it has favorable properties and is easier to parallelize across multiple machines.

4.1
Algorithm Description

The algorithm begins by obtaining an initial gradient estimator
g^{0}
(for example, a minibatch average computed at
x^{0}
).
At iteration
k
, the server first updates the model via

x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\gamma g^{k}
,
and then ensures that the workers have access to the pair
(x^{k},x^{k+1})
.
In practice, it suffices to broadcast only the new point
x^{k+1}
since
x^{k}
was broadcast in the previous iteration and can be cached by the workers.

Workers operate asynchronously and continuously.
Each time a worker samples
\xi
, it computes the gradient pair

\bigl(\nabla f(x^{k};\xi),\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi)\bigr)

and sends this pair to the server.
Thus, one arrival to the server consists of
two
stochastic gradients corresponding to the same sample
\xi
.
The server collects the first
B
such arrivals, that is,
B
gradient pairs, and forms the sums
g^{-}
and
g^{+}
from the gradients evaluated at
x^{k}
and
x^{k+1}
, respectively.
Equivalently, each iteration uses
B
gradient pairs, or
2B
stochastic gradient evaluations.

Once these
B
arrivals have been collected, the server constructs the next estimator as

g^{k+1}=\frac{g^{+}}{B}+(1-p)\left(g^{k}-\frac{g^{-}}{B}\right),

and then proceeds to the next iteration.

4.2
Connection to Rennala SGD

When
p=1
, the update simplifies to

g^{k+1}=\nicefrac{{g^{+}}}{{B}}
,
so the method reduces to minibatch
SGD
as a special case, using only gradients at the current point
x^{k+1}
.
In this regime, the additional computation of gradients at
x^{k}
(i.e., the
g^{-}
term) is unnecessary.
If we therefore modify the worker routine to compute only
\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi)
, the resulting asynchronous minibatch
SGD
implementation coincides with
Rennala SGD

(
31
)
.

5.2
can be written more compactly as

T_{{\color[rgb]{0.35,0.35,0.35}\small\sf MVR}}=\mathrm{O}\left(\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)\cdot T\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)+T\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

Comparison with Rennala SGD.

For
Rennala SGD
, the corresponding time complexity is

T_{{\color[rgb]{0.35,0.35,0.35}\small\sf SGD}}=\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{L\Delta}{\varepsilon}\cdot T\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

The key distinction is the per-iteration waiting time.
Each iteration of
Rennala MVR
waits for roughly
\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}
gradient pairs and therefore costs on the order of

T(\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}})
time, whereas each iteration of
Rennala SGD
costs

T(\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}}}{{\varepsilon}})
, which is always at least as large.
This smaller per-iteration time is the main source of potential speedups for
Rennala MVR
.

On the other hand, the dominant iteration count for
Rennala MVR
(for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
) scales as

\nicefrac{{\bar{L}\Delta}}{{\varepsilon}}
, which can be larger than the
\nicefrac{{L\Delta}}{{\varepsilon}}
scaling of
Rennala SGD
since
L\leq\bar{L}
.
Consequently, when
\bar{L}=\mathrm{O}(L)
, the reduction in per-iteration time dominates and
Rennala MVR
enjoys an improved overall time complexity compared to
Rennala SGD
.

Comparison with the lower bound.

We next relate
Theorem

5.2
to the time lower bound proved in the following section.
The lower bound takes the form (
Theorem

6.7
)

\displaystyle T=\Omega\left(\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1\right)\cdot T\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)~.

First, note that the implied oracle complexity (recall: the number of stochastic gradient evaluations) is

\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1\right)\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\bar{L}\Delta\sigma}{\varepsilon^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}~,

which matches the oracle complexity of
Rennala MVR
from
Theorem

5.1
(and
Table

1
).
In other words,
Rennala MVR
computes the minimal number of stochastic gradients up to constant factors.

The remaining gap is a batching/synchronization issue.
The lower bound corresponds to producing gradients in batches of size
\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}}}{{\varepsilon}}
, while
Rennala MVR
uses smaller batches of size
\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}
.
Smaller batches may require more frequent synchronization (i.e., more frequent termination of ongoing gradient computations), which can increase the elapsed time even when the total number of computed gradients is optimal.

Despite this gap in general, the upper bound can match the lower bound in regimes where

T\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\approx\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\cdot T\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)~.

For example, this occurs in the homogeneous worker case with
n=\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}
and
\tau_{i}=\tau
for all
i\in[n]
.

Overall, the fact that the current time upper bound for
Rennala MVR
does not match the lower bound highlights a key distinction between variance-reduced methods and plain
SGD
under heterogeneous compute-time models: optimal oracle complexity does not automatically imply optimal time complexity.
Closing this gap appears to require a method that can operate with much larger effective batch sizes (on the order of
\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}}}{{\varepsilon}}
) while preserving the desired convergence rate.
Within the current proof approach and stepsize restrictions, it is unclear how to achieve this via a simple modification of
Rennala MVR
, which suggests that fundamentally different algorithmic ideas may be needed.
This issue is discussed in more detail in
Appendix

C
.

6
Lower Bound on Time Complexity

In this section we formalize a framework for proving
lower bounds
on the time required to find an
\varepsilon
–stationary point in stochastic nonconvex optimization with heterogeneous parallel workers.
Following
31
, we specify function class
\mathcal{F}
, an oracle class
\mathcal{O}
, and an algorithm class
\mathcal{A}
together with a time-based interaction protocol.
We then define a minimax notion of time complexity and state our lower bound.

Compared to the protocol in
31
, our setting requires one additional feature: the algorithm may request
variable batch sizes
up to a maximum of
B
points per interaction (in particular, our
Rennala MVR
(
Algorithm

1
) asks for two stochastic gradients).

6.2
Oracle Model

Each oracle
O_{i}
has an internal state encoding whether it is idle or busy and, if busy, which batch it is currently processing.
We represent the state as

s_{i}=(s_{t},s_{X},s_{b},s_{q})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}\times\{0,\dots,B\}\times\{0,1\},

where
s_{q}=0
indicates that the oracle is idle, and
s_{q}=1
indicates that it is busy computing a batch of size
s_{b}
started at time
s_{t}
for the stored query tuple
s_{X}
(with the convention that
(s_{X})_{j}=0
for
j>s_{b}
).

Formally, define

\displaystyle O_{\tau_{i}(\cdot),B}^{f}

\displaystyle:~\underbrace{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}_{\text{time}}\times\underbrace{\{1,\dots,B\}}_{\text{batch size}}\times\underbrace{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}}_{\text{points}}\times\underbrace{(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}\times\{0,1,\dots,B\}\times\{0,1\})}_{\text{input state}}\times\underbrace{\mathcal{D}_{i}}_{\text{randomness}}

\displaystyle\;\to~\underbrace{(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}\times\{0,1,\dots,B\}\times\{0,1\})}_{\text{output state}}\times\underbrace{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}}_{\text{gradients}}.

It is defined by

\displaystyle O_{\tau_{i}(\cdot),B}^{f}(t,b,X,(s_{t},s_{X},s_{b},s_{q}),\xi)=\begin{cases}((t,X,b,1),\ 0),&\text{if }s_{q}=0,\\
((s_{t},s_{X},s_{b},1),\ 0),&\text{if }s_{q}=1\text{ and }t<s_{t}+\tau_{i}(s_{b}),\\
((0,0,0,0),\ G),&\text{if }s_{q}=1\text{ and }t\geq s_{t}+\tau_{i}(s_{b}),\end{cases}

(5)

where
0
denotes the all-zero element of
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}
, and
G\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}
is given by

G_{j}=\begin{cases}\nabla f((s_{X})_{j};\xi),&j\leq s_{b},\\
0,&j>s_{b}.\end{cases}

Thus, if the oracle is idle, it starts computing the gradients for the submitted batch (of size
b
) and becomes busy.
While busy, it returns the all-zero tuple until the computation finishes.
Once
t
reaches the completion time
s_{t}+\tau_{i}(s_{b})
, the oracle returns the stored batch gradients and resets to idle.
In particular, each completed reply uses a
single
random sample
\xi\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}
shared across the
s_{b}
gradients in that reply.

Relative to
31
, our oracle model differs in two ways.
First, we allow
B>1
(variable batch sizes).
Second, the runtime is governed by a
batch-time function

\tau_{i}(\cdot)
rather than a constant:
\tau_{i}(s_{b})
is the time required for worker
i
to compute
s_{b}
stochastic gradients.

We impose the following natural monotonicity property.

Assumption 6.1

(Batch time)
.

For each oracle
i\in[n]
, the batch-time function
\tau_{i}(\cdot)
is nondecreasing: for any
1\leq k\leq\ell
,
\tau_{i}(k)\leq\tau_{i}(\ell)
.

6.3
Algorithm Class

At each interaction, the algorithm can use all previously received replies
\{G^{1},\dots,G^{k}\}
to select the next oracle, the request time, the batch size, and the query points.
We also impose the natural constraint
t^{k+1}\geq t^{k}
(the algorithm cannot query in the past).

Definition 6.2

(Algorithm class)
.

An algorithm
A=\{A^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of mappings such that for each
k\geq 0
,

A^{k}:\bigl((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}\bigr)^{k}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times[n]\times\{1,\dots,B\}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B},

and if
(t^{k+1},\cdot)=A^{k}(G^{1},\dots,G^{k})
and
(t^{k},\cdot)=A^{k-1}(G^{1},\dots,G^{k-1})
, then
t^{k+1}\geq t^{k}
.
Moreover, if
A^{k}(\cdot)=(t^{k+1},i^{k+1},b^{k},X^{k})
, then
X^{k}=(x^{k,1},\dots,x^{k,B})
satisfies
x^{k,j}=0
for all
j>b^{k}
.
We denote the class of all such algorithms by
\mathcal{A}
.

As in
31
, we restrict attention to zero-respecting algorithms.

Definition 6.3

(Zero-respecting)
.

Consider an execution of Protocol
2
.
For each interaction
r\geq 0
, let

(t^{r+1},i^{r+1},b^{r},X^{r})

be the algorithm output, where

X^{r}=(x^{(r,1)},\dots,x^{(r,B)})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}.

Let

G_{\mathrm{comp}}^{r}=(g^{(r,1)},\dots,g^{(r,B)})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{B}

denote the gradient tuple eventually returned when the batch submitted at interaction
r
is completed, with the convention
g^{(r,k)}=0
for all
k>b^{r}
.
The algorithm is
zero-respecting
if for all
r\geq 0
and all
k\in[B]
,

\operatorname{support}\bigl(x^{(r,k)}\bigr)\subseteq\bigcup_{s<r}\ \bigcup_{k^{\prime}\in[B]}\operatorname{support}\bigl(g^{(s,k^{\prime})}\bigr).

We denote the class of all zero-respecting algorithms by
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{zr}}
.

6.4
Function and Oracle Classes

We define the following.

Definition 6.4

(Function class
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta,\bar{L}}

.

We define
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta,\bar{L}}
as the set of all
\bar{L}
–smooth functions

such that

Definition 6.5

(Oracle class)
.

For any

\mathcal{O}_{\tau_{1}(\cdot),\dots,\tau_{n}(\cdot)}^{\sigma^{2},\bar{L},B}(f)

and distributions
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for all

\nabla f(\cdot;\xi)
are unbiased and
\sigma^{2}

\bar{L}

The oracle

6.5

Let
\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{F}_{\Delta,\bar{L}}]
denote the set of all probability distributions over
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta,\bar{L}}
.
We define the minimax time complexity as follows.

Definition 6.6

For a given batch-budget
B
and problem parameters
(\Delta,\bar{L},\sigma^{2})

by which a zero-respecting algorithm can ensure an

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{time}}(B,\Delta,\bar{L},\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle S_{t}\coloneqq\{\,(r,k)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times[B]\mid 1\leq k\leq b^{r},\ c^{r}\leq t\,\}.

Neural Network Experiments

We also evaluate the practical behavior of

Unlike the quadratic benchmark in

The motivation is practical: while the present network is small, for larger models the exact variant introduces additional runtime overhead, whereas the inexact variant reuses previous stochastic-gradient information and is therefore computationally more attractive.

We additionally introduce a scaling parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=x^{k}-\gamma g^{k},

\displaystyle g^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-p)g^{k}+p\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k})+{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.5781,0.0547}\alpha}(1-p)\left(\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k})-\nabla f(x^{k};{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.5781,0.0547}\xi^{k-1}})\right).

\displaystyle g^{k+1}

This modification is motivated by the fact that pure momentum-based variance reduction often behaves poorly in neural-network training, whereas introducing a small correction coefficient can substantially improve stability and empirical performance, as also observed in MARS and related follow-up work.

8
Conclusion

This work initiates time complexity analysis for variance-reduced methods in heterogeneous parallel stochastic optimization.
Focusing on the
MVR
mechanism in
STORM

(
6
)
, and working under the stronger mean-squared smoothness assumption, we proposed
Rennala MVR
and established its iteration, oracle, and time complexity guarantees.
In particular, we showed that variance reduction can improve not only oracle complexity, but also time complexity in heterogeneous parallel environments, yielding improved guarantees over
Rennala SGD

(
31
)
, the previously time-optimal method in the standard smoothness regime, in relevant parameter regimes.

More broadly, our results highlight a key conceptual message: in parallel stochastic optimization, oracle complexity is not sufficient to understand performance.
Indeed, methods based on momentum-based variance reduction are known to be oracle-optimal in the single-worker setting under stronger smoothness assumptions
(
6
;
4
)
; however, as our results and
Table

1
show, this does not automatically translate into time optimality in the parallel heterogeneous-worker setting.
Time complexity behavior also depends on how optimization progress interacts with the worker-time model and the batch-size/time tradeoff.
In this sense, a central novelty of the paper lies in the time complexity theory needed to understand this mechanism in the heterogeneous parallel regime.

To support this perspective, we also established a new lower bound on the achievable time complexity under the same model.
Together with our upper and lower bounds, this provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first theoretical understanding of the time complexity behavior of momentum-based variance reduction in heterogeneous distributed systems.
At the same time, a gap between the current upper and lower bounds remains.
Closing this gap appears to be nontrivial, and likely requires fundamentally different ideas rather than a routine extension of existing variance-reduction analyses.

Appendix A
Time Complexity Under Arbitrarily Varying Worker Speeds

The fixed-computation model in
2.1
assumes that every worker processes gradients at a constant rate.
This assumption is often too restrictive in practice: worker speeds may change over time due to interruptions, network interference, or temporary hardware slowdowns.
To capture such effects, we now replace the constants
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with time-varying computation rates.

A.1
Universal Computation Model

We adopt the universal computation model of
33
.

Assumption A.1

(Universal Computation Model)
.

For each worker
i\in[n]
, there exists a nonnegative function

p_{i}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

that is continuous almost everywhere.
For any
0\leq T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, the number of stochastic gradients completed by worker
i
during the interval
[T_{1},T_{2}]
is

N_{i}(T_{1},T_{2})\coloneqq\left\lfloor\int_{T_{1}}^{T_{2}}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor.

Moreover,
A.1
contains
2.1
as a special case: if
p_{i}(s)=1/\tau_{i}
for all
s\geq 0
, then

N_{i}(T_{1},T_{2})=\left\lfloor\frac{T_{2}-T_{1}}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor.

A.2
Time Complexity in the Universal Model

Unlike the fixed-time model, the universal model does not generally lead to a closed-form expression for the total time.
The natural guarantee is therefore implicit and expressed through the times at which enough gradients have been completed to finish the initialization and each subsequent iteration.
For the universal-model upper bound, we assume that at each iteration boundary all workers are effectively reset to the idle state, so unfinished computations from the previous iteration do not contribute to the next one.

Theorem A.2

(Time Complexity Under the Universal Computation Model)
.

Assume the setup and parameter choices of
Theorem

5.1
, and define

K_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{24\Delta\bar{L}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil.

Let

\displaystyle B_{0}=\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,\quad B=\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil,

and define the completion times
\{T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
by

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\left\{t\geq 0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor\int_{0}^{t}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor\geq B_{0}\right\},

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\left\{t\geq T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k-1}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\int_{T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k-1}}^{t}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor\geq B\right\},\qquad k\geq 1.

Then
Rennala MVR
(
Algorithm

1
) returns an
\varepsilon
–stationary point within at most
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{K_{\varepsilon}}
seconds.

Proof.

By
Theorem

5.1
, under the stated parameter choice,
Rennala MVR
reaches an
\varepsilon
–stationary point after at most
K_{\varepsilon}
iterations.
It therefore remains to upper bound the time needed to complete the initialization and these
K_{\varepsilon}
iterations.

By definition of
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{0}
, by time
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{0}
the workers have collectively produced at least
B_{0}
stochastic gradients at
x^{0}
.
Hence the initialization step used to construct
g^{0}
is completed no later than
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{0}
.

Now fix
k\geq 1
and suppose that iteration
k
starts at time
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k-1}
.
Over the interval
[T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k-1},t]
, worker
i
can complete

\left\lfloor\int_{T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k-1}}^{t}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor

stochastic gradients.
One arrival in
Rennala MVR
at iteration
k
is a complete pair

\left(\nabla f(x^{k-1};\xi),\nabla f(x^{k};\xi)\right),

and therefore requires two stochastic-gradient computations on the same worker.
Consequently, by time
t
, worker
i
can contribute at least

\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\int_{T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k-1}}^{t}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor

complete gradient pairs.
Summing over workers, the server has received at least

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\int_{T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k-1}}^{t}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor

pairs by time
t
.
By the definition of
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k}
, this quantity is at least
B
when
t=T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k}
.
Hence iteration
k
finishes no later than
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{k}
.

Applying the above argument inductively for
k=1,\dots,K_{\varepsilon}
, we conclude that all
K_{\varepsilon}
iterations finish by time
T_{\mathrm{MVR}}^{K_{\varepsilon}}
.
Combining this with
Theorem

5.1
proves the claim.
∎

Comparison with Rennala SGD.

The corresponding universal-model bound for
Rennala SGD
follows from
33
, specialized to our notation.
In the regime
\varepsilon<\sigma^{2}
considered in
Theorem

5.1
,
Rennala SGD
reaches an
\varepsilon
–stationary point within
T_{\mathrm{SGD}}^{K_{\mathrm{SGD}}}
seconds, where

K_{\mathrm{SGD}}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{24L\Delta}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil

and

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{SGD}}^{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{SGD}}^{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\left\{t\geq T_{\mathrm{SGD}}^{k-1}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor\int_{T_{\mathrm{SGD}}^{k-1}}^{t}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor\geq\left\lceil\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\right\},\qquad k\geq 1.

Thus, the universal model preserves the same qualitative trade-off as the fixed-time model.
Each iteration of
Rennala MVR
waits for
B=\Theta(\sigma/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
complete pairs, whereas each iteration of
Rennala SGD
waits for
\Theta(\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon)
stochastic gradients.
For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the number of stochastic gradients that must be completed before each
Rennala MVR
update is therefore asymptotically smaller.
The trade-off is that
Rennala MVR
requires
K_{\varepsilon}=\Theta(\bar{L}\Delta/\varepsilon+\sigma/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
update rounds, whereas
Rennala SGD
requires
K_{\mathrm{SGD}}=\Theta(L\Delta/\varepsilon)
rounds.
Since the universal-model bounds are implicit, no unconditional ordering between the two total times follows without additional structure on the rate functions
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Nevertheless, whenever
\bar{L}=\mathrm{O}(L)
and the reduction in per-round waiting time dominates, the same mechanism that improves the fixed-time bound can also favor
Rennala MVR
under time-varying worker speeds.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemmas

This appendix provides complete proofs of all lemmas referenced in the main text, including both those stated explicitly and auxiliary results needed for the theorem proofs.

B.1
Proof of
Lemma

2.5

Let us first restate the lemma.

Lemma
2.5
.

Mean-squared smoothness (
2.4
) implies smoothness of
f
with constant
\bar{L}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|\leq\bar{L}\left\|x-y\right\|,\quad\forall\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~.

Proof.

Using Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|^{2}=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)\right]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\bar{L}^{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}~.

Hence, taking square roots,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq\bar{L}\|x-y\|~.

∎

B.2
Proof of
Lemma

2.6

Again restating the lemma first.

Lemma
2.6
.

Mean-squared smoothness (
2.4
) implies expected similarity with the same constant
\bar{L}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)-(\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y))\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\bar{L}^{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2},\quad\forall\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

This follows by doing some simple algebra

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)-(\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y))\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)\|^{2}\right]-\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)\|^{2}\right]\leq\bar{L}^{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

∎

B.3
Expected Similarity and Bounded Variance for the Minibatch Case

We now prove another lemma that will be used in the iteration complexity analysis.

Lemma B.1
.

The following bounds hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})+\nabla f(x^{k})-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bar{L}^{2}}{B}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}~,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{B}~.

Proof.

Using
Lemma

2.6
and independence of
\xi^{k,j}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})+\nabla f(x^{k})-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{B}E\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})+\nabla f(x^{k})-\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\bar{L}^{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}=\frac{\bar{L}^{2}}{B}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}~.

Similarly, using
2.3
and independence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\sigma^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{B}~.

∎

Appendix C
Why the Current Analysis Cannot Close the Time-Complexity Gap

Our time lower bound (cf.
Theorem

6.7
) implies, up to universal constants, a requirement of the form

m_{\mathrm{time}}(B,\Delta,\bar{L},\sigma^{2})\;\geq\;\Omega\left(\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1\right)T\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right),

(6)

where

T(B)=\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}^{-1}\right)^{-1}(B+m)

is an upper bound on the time needed to obtain
B
stochastic gradients asynchronously.
On the other hand, the time upper bound we obtain in this paper for
Rennala MVR
has the schematic structure

T_{\mathrm{up}}\lesssim T(B_{0})+K\,T(B),

(7)

where
B_{0}
is the initialization batch size,
B
is the per-iteration batch size, and
K
is the number of server updates (iterations).
If one aims to align (
7
) with the lower-bound scaling (
6
)
uniformly over heterogeneous systems
(within our timing model), then the structure of
T(\cdot)
can force a specific scaling of
(B,K)
; a simple way to see this is to consider the homogeneous case
\tau_{i}\equiv\tau
, for which

\displaystyle T(B)=\min_{m\in[n]}\frac{\tau(B+m)}{m}=\frac{\tau(B+n)}{n}=\frac{\tau}{n}B+\tau~.

In particular, even in this “linear” regime,
T(B)
has a nonzero per-round overhead
\tau
, and hence

K\,T(B)=\frac{\tau}{n}KB+\tau K,

while

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1\right)\,T\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)=\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1\right)\left(\frac{\tau}{n}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}+\tau\right)=\frac{\tau}{n}\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}+\tau\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1\right).

Thus, matching the lower-bound scaling in this example forces the additive contributions to be of the same order, which yields

K\asymp\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+1~,

(8)

and then matching the leading linear-in-
B
terms yields

B\asymp\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}

(with
B_{0}
of the same order as well).
However, our iteration-complexity bound (
11
) shows that (
8
) cannot be achieved by
Rennala MVR
under the stepsize restriction (
10
) used in our analysis.
Let us enforce the batching suggested by the lower bound and set

B_{0}=B=c\,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}

for a sufficiently large constant
c
.
Then (
11
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2\Delta}{\gamma K}+\frac{2p\sigma^{2}}{B}+\frac{1}{pK}\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{B_{0}}~.

With
B=B_{0}=\nicefrac{{c\sigma^{2}}}{{\varepsilon}}
, the noise terms become

\frac{2p\sigma^{2}}{B}=\frac{2p}{c}\,\varepsilon

and

\frac{1}{pK}\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{B_{0}}=\frac{2}{cpK}\,\varepsilon~.

By choosing
c
large enough (and, say, any constant
p\leq 1
), these two terms can be made
\leq\varepsilon/3
provided
K\gtrsim 1/p
.
The remaining requirement comes from the descent term
\frac{2\Delta}{\gamma K}
.
Crucially, the stepsize constraint (
10
) enforces
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(1/\bar{L})
even when
B
is as large as
\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon
(the factor
(1-p)/\sqrt{pB}
only makes
\gamma

smaller
).
Therefore, to make

\frac{2\Delta}{\gamma K}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}~,

we must have

K\geq\Omega\!\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\varepsilon}\right).

(9)

In the high-noise regime
\sigma>\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, the lower-bound-implied target

K\asymp\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}

is
smaller
than
\nicefrac{{\bar{L}\Delta}}{{\varepsilon}}
by a factor
\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}
.
Hence, (
9
) rules out the simultaneous choice (
8
) within our current upper-bound analysis.
In summary, while the lower bound (
6
) suggests that optimal time would be attained by producing gradients in batches of size
\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon
and performing only

\Theta\left(\bar{L}\Delta/(\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon})\right)

update rounds, the smoothness-limited descent mechanism behind (
11
) forces
\Omega(\nicefrac{{\bar{L}\Delta}}{{\varepsilon}})
update rounds (under (
10
)) regardless of how large we take
B
.
This creates a gap relative to (
6
) for the particular
Rennala MVR
analysis developed in this paper, which explains why our time upper bound does not match the lower bound in full generality.
Although one can argue that the remaining gap may be due to our proof technique, we believe the bounds are reasonably tight: in particular, when we ignore the timing model and count only oracle queries, our analysis recovers the optimal oracle complexity of
MVR
.

Appendix D
Proofs of Upper Bounds

In this section, we prove the upper bounds on the iteration and time complexity.

D.1
Proof of
Theorem

5.1

We start with the iteration complexity.

Theorem
5.1

(Iteration Complexity of
Algorithm

1
)
.

Let Assumptions
2.2
,
2.3
, and
2.4
hold.
Fix parameters
p\in(0,1]
, integers
B_{0}\geq 1
and
B\geq 1
.
Consider
Algorithm

1
with the modification that the initialization
g^{0}
uses an unbiased estimate of
\nabla f(x^{0})
using a minibatch of size
B_{0}
, while for all
k\geq 0
the iteration-
k
minibatch has size
B
.
Choose the stepsize

\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2\bar{L}\left(1+\frac{1-p}{\sqrt{pB}}\right)}~.

(10)

Then for any
K\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2\Delta}{\gamma K}+\frac{2p\sigma^{2}}{B}+\frac{1}{pK}\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{B_{0}},

(11)

where
\Delta\coloneqq f(x^{0})-f^{*}
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and assume
\varepsilon<\sigma^{2}
and
\varepsilon<2\bar{L}\Delta
.
Choose

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{4\bar{L}},\quad p\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sigma},\quad B_{0}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,\quad B\coloneqq\;\left\lceil\frac{6p\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil,

(12)

then the iterates of
Algorithm

1
satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]\ \leq\ \varepsilon~,

for

\displaystyle K\geq\frac{24\Delta\bar{L}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}~.

Proof.

Since
2.4
implies that
f
is
\bar{L}
–smooth (see
Lemma

2.5
), the standard smoothness inequality (e.g.,
(
23
)
) gives

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})+\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x^{k})+\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),-\gamma g^{k}\rangle+\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x^{k})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x^{k})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}-\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\right)\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}.

Subtract
f^{*}
and take
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\cdot\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]
, where
\mathcal{F}_{k}\coloneqq\sigma(x^{0},\ldots,x^{k},g^{0},\ldots,g^{k})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(x^{k+1})-f^{*}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})-f^{*}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}-\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\right)\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}.

We need to control the variance term above.
So, let us study
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|g^{k+1}-\nabla f(x^{k+1})\right\|^{2}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]
.
Using bias–variance decomposition:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|g^{k+1}-\nabla f(x^{k+1})\right\|^{2}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})+(1-p)\left(g^{k}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})\right)\right\|^{2}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})+(1-p)\left(\nabla f(x^{k})-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})\right)\right\|^{2}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Bigg\|p\Bigg(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+(1-p)\Bigg(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})+\nabla f(x^{k})-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})\Bigg)\Bigg\|^{2}\ \Bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq(1-p)^{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}+2p^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})\right\|^{2}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2(1-p)^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi^{k,j})-\nabla f(x^{k+1})+\nabla f(x^{k})-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla f(x^{k};\xi^{k,j})\right\|^{2}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right].

Using
Lemma

B.1
, we get

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|g^{k+1}-\nabla f(x^{k+1})\right\|^{2}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leq(1-p)^{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}+\frac{2p^{2}\sigma^{2}}{B}+\frac{2(1-p)^{2}\bar{L}^{2}}{B}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}.

Define the Lyapunov function:

\Psi_{k}=f(x^{k})-f^{*}+\frac{\gamma}{2p}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}.

We have:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\Psi_{k+1}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})-f^{*}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}-\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\right)\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\gamma}{2p}\left((1-p)^{2}\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}+\frac{2p^{2}\sigma^{2}}{B}+\frac{2(1-p)^{2}\bar{L}^{2}}{B}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f(x^{k})-f^{*}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}-\frac{\bar{L}}{2}-\frac{\gamma(1-p)^{2}\bar{L}^{2}}{pB}\right)\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\gamma}{2p}\left(p+(1-p)^{2}\right)\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma p\sigma^{2}}{B}~.

Using
p+(1-p)^{2}\leq 1
, we get

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\Psi_{k+1}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leq\Psi_{k}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}-\frac{\bar{L}}{2}-\frac{\gamma(1-p)^{2}\bar{L}^{2}}{pB}\right)\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma p\sigma^{2}}{B}~.

Next to ensure that the coefficient before
\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}
is non-negative, it is sufficient to have

\frac{1}{4\gamma}\geq\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{4\gamma}\geq\frac{\gamma(1-p)^{2}\bar{L}^{2}}{pB}~,

which is equivalent to

\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2\bar{L}}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2\bar{L}\frac{1-p}{\sqrt{pB}}}~.

Hence, taking
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2\bar{L}\left(1+\frac{1-p}{\sqrt{pB}}\right)}
ensures

\frac{1}{2\gamma}-\frac{\bar{L}}{2}-\frac{\gamma(1-p)^{2}\bar{L}^{2}}{pB}\geq 0
.
Thus, we obtained

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\Psi_{k+1}\ \right|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Psi_{k}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma p\sigma^{2}}{B}~.

Taking full expectation and telescoping from
k=0
to
K-1
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]-\frac{\gamma p\sigma^{2}}{B}\right)\leq\Delta+\frac{\gamma}{2p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g^{0}-\nabla f(x^{0})\right\|^{2}\right].

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]\;\leq\;\frac{2\Delta}{\gamma K}\;+\;\frac{2p\sigma^{2}}{B}\;+\;\frac{1}{pK}\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{B_{0}}~.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
We assume
\varepsilon<\sigma^{2}
.
Otherwise, we are in the low-noise regime, hence we can set
p=1
and use
SGD
with minibatch size
B
, which reaches the target accuracy in
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\varepsilon}\right)
stochastic gradients.
Also assume
\varepsilon<2\bar{L}\Delta
.
Otherwise, since
\|\nabla f(x^{0})\|^{2}\leq 2\bar{L}\Delta\leq\varepsilon
, the initial point
x^{0}
is already
\varepsilon
–stationary.

Recall that we have established

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\Delta}{\gamma K}\;+\;\frac{2p\sigma^{2}}{B}\;+\;\frac{1}{pK}\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{B_{0}}~.

To make sure the right hand side is bounded by
\varepsilon
, we bound each term on the right-hand side by
\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{3}}
.

We choose the initialization batch size

B_{0}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,

(13)

which gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{pK}\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{B_{0}}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{pK}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{3}~.

To ensure this term is at most
\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{3}}
, we require

K\;\geq\;\frac{1}{p}~.

(14)

We set

B\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{6p\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,

(15)

which directly ensures

\displaystyle\frac{2p\sigma^{2}}{B}\;\leq\;\frac{\varepsilon}{3}~.

We choose

p\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sigma}~,

(16)

Substituting (
16
) into (
15
) yields

\displaystyle B=\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil.

(17)

Moreover, this choice of
p
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{\sqrt{pB}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-p}{\sqrt{p\cdot 6p\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1-p)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}~.

The above bound ensures that the step size

\gamma\coloneqq\frac{1}{4\bar{L}}

(18)

satisfies the required condition, since

\displaystyle 2\bar{L}\!\left(1+\frac{1-p}{\sqrt{pB}}\right)\;\leq\;2\bar{L}\!\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\right)\;<\;4\bar{L}~.

With
\gamma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4\bar{L}}}
, bounding the first term by
\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{3}}
requires

K\;\geq\;\frac{24\Delta\bar{L}}{\varepsilon}~.

(19)

Combining with (
14
) and recalling
p=\nicefrac{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}{{\sigma}}
, we take

K\;\geq\;\frac{24\Delta\bar{L}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}~.

(20)

The parameter choices (
13
), (
17
), (
16
), (
18
), and (
20
) together guarantee

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\right]\;\leq\;\varepsilon~.

∎

D.2
Proof of
Theorem

5.2

Before proving the theorem, we first prove a simple lemma.
A result of this type was used in
(
31
, Theorem 7.5)
; here we provide a simpler proof.

Lemma D.1
.

Suppose we have
n
workers with computation times as in
2.1
.
Let
B\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
.
Consider any iteration boundary at which the server starts collecting new stochastic gradients.
Then, the time needed to collect
B
stochastic gradients is at most

2T(B)=2\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B+m)~.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
. For worker
i
, in the worst case the worker finishes and sends a gradient just before the iteration starts.
Therefore, the first interval of length
\tau_{i}
after the iteration starts may not produce a
new
gradient usable in this iteration.
Hence, within
t
seconds from the iteration start, worker
i
can contribute at least

n_{i}(t)\coloneqq\max\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{t-\tau_{i}}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor,0\right\}=\max\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor-1,0\right\}

new gradients.
Set

\displaystyle j^{*}=\argmin_{j\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B+j)~,

then, since
\max\{u,0\}\geq u
, we have
n_{i}(t)\geq\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor-1
, and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}n_{i}(t)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{j^{*}}n_{i}(t)\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{j^{*}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor-1\right)\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{j^{*}}\left(\frac{t}{\tau_{i}}-2\right)\;=\;t\sum_{i=1}^{j^{*}}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}-2j^{*},

where we used
\lfloor a\rfloor\geq a-1
for all
a\geq 0
.
Thus, if
t
satisfies

t\sum_{i=1}^{j^{*}}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}-2j^{*}\;\geq\;B,

then by time
t
the server can collect at least
B
gradients from workers
1,\ldots,j^{*}
.
Choosing

t=2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j^{*}}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B+j^{*})

makes the left-hand side equal to
2B\geq B
, concluding the proof.
∎

Theorem
5.2

(Time Complexity of
Algorithm

1
)
.

Assume the setup of
Theorem

5.1
and our distributed time model:
there are
n
workers with per-sample computation times
0<\tau_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{n}
.
Assume that on worker
i
, computing the pair
\bigl(\nabla f(x^{k};\xi),\nabla f(x^{k+1};\xi)\bigr)
takes

(21)

seconds, and each iteration (collecting
B

4\cdot\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B+m)

(22)

seconds.

In particular, with the parameter choice of
Theorem

5.1
,
Algorithm

1
returns an
\varepsilon
–stationary point within

T_{\mathrm{MVR}}\;\leq\;2\cdot\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B_{0}+m)\;+\;4K\cdot\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B+m)

(23)

seconds, where

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\frac{6\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil,

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\geq\frac{24\Delta\bar{L}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}~.

Thus,

T_{{\color[rgb]{0.35,0.35,0.35}\small\sf MVR}}=\mathcal{O}\Bigg(\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{\varepsilon}\right)\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+m\right)+\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}+m\right)\Bigg)~.

The initialization bound follows from

D.1
applied with

and computation times
\tau_{i}
.

seconds.
Applying
Lemma

D.1
with computation times

gives that one iteration takes at most

\displaystyle 2\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B+m)=4\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}(B+m).

iteration times yields the desired bound.

Appendix E
Proofs of Lower Bound

We start by introducing several definitions and notations that will be used throughout this section.

For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\operatorname{support}(x)\coloneqq\{i\in[d]\mid x_{i}\neq 0\}
and
x_{\geq i}\coloneqq(x_{i},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-i+1}
.
For
\alpha\in[0,1)
define the
progress

\operatorname{prog}_{\alpha}(x)\coloneqq\max\{\,i\in\{0,1,\dots,d\}\mid|x_{i}|>\alpha\,\},\qquad\text{with }~x_{0}\equiv 1~.

(24)

Our goal is to construct functions and stochastic oracles such that, under zero-respecting algorithms, each completed oracle reply can activate at most one new coordinate.

Definition E.1

(First-order zero-chain)
.

A differentiable function
F:\mathbb{R}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is a (first-order) zero-chain if, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
,

\operatorname{prog}_{0}\bigl(\nabla F(x)\bigr)\ \leq\ \operatorname{prog}_{0}(x)+1.

(25)

Intuition.

In the noiseless case
g(x,\xi)\equiv\nabla F(x)
, (
25
) implies that a zero-respecting
algorithm can reveal at most one new coordinate per completed oracle reply, so its progress is at most linear
in the number of replies.

Definition E.2

(Probability-
p
zero-chain)
.

A stochastic mapping
g:\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{T}
is a probability-
p
zero-chain if, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}\!\left(\operatorname{prog}_{0}\bigl(g(x,\xi)\bigr)=\operatorname{prog}_{\tfrac{1}{4}}(x)+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq p,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\xi}\!\left(\operatorname{prog}_{0}\bigl(g(x,\xi)\bigr)>\operatorname{prog}_{\tfrac{1}{4}}(x)+1\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(27)

Setup

We use the same deterministic chain function as in the works by
31
and
4
.
Define
F_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{T}\!\to\mathbb{R}
by

F_{T}(x)\ \coloneqq-\Psi(1)\,\Phi(x_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{T}\Bigl(\Psi(-x_{i-1})\,\Phi(-x_{i})\ -\ \Psi(x_{i-1})\,\Phi(x_{i})\Bigr),

where

\displaystyle\Psi(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\\[3.0pt]
\exp\!\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{(2t-1)^{2}}\Bigr),&t>\tfrac{1}{2},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Phi(t)\ =\ \sqrt{e}\ \int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}}\,d\tau.

Lemma E.3

(Properties of
F_{T}
, cf. Lemma 2)
.

There are absolute constants
\Delta_{0}=12
,
\ell_{1}=152
,
\gamma_{\infty}=23
such that:

1.

F_{T}(0)-\inf_{x}F_{T}(x)\leq\Delta_{0}\cdot T
.

2.

\nabla F_{T}
is
\ell_{1}
–Lipschitz in
\ell_{1}
.

3.

For all
x
,
\|\nabla F_{T}(x)\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma_{\infty}
.

4.

\operatorname{prog}_{0}\!\bigl(\nabla F_{T}(x)\bigr)\ \leq\ \operatorname{prog}_{1/2}(x)+1
.

5.

If
\operatorname{prog}_{1}(x)<T
, then
\|\nabla F_{T}(x)\|\ \geq\ \bigl|\nabla_{\operatorname{prog}_{0}(x)+1}F_{T}(x)\bigr|\ >\ 1
.

Next we define the estimator. Let
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth, non-decreasing, and Lipschitz, with

\Gamma(t)=0\ \text{ for }t\leq\tfrac{1}{4},\qquad\Gamma(t)=1\ \text{ for }t\geq\tfrac{1}{2}.

(28)

Define, for each
i
,

\displaystyle\Theta_{i}(x)\coloneqq\Gamma\!\left(1-\biggl\|\Gamma\bigl(|x_{\geq i}|\bigr)\biggr\|_{2}\right)

(29)

so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\{i>\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)\}\ \leq\ \Theta_{i}(x)\ \leq\ \mathbf{1}\{i>\operatorname{prog}_{1/2}(x)\}.

(30)

A concrete choice is obtained by the “integrated bump”:

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=\frac{\int_{1/4}^{t}\Lambda(\tau)\,d\tau}{\int_{1/4}^{1/2}\Lambda(\tau^{\prime})\,d\tau^{\prime}},\quad\Lambda(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\ \text{or}\ t\geq\tfrac{1}{2},\\[4.0pt]
\exp\!\Bigl(-\dfrac{1}{100\,(t-\tfrac{1}{4})\,(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)}\Bigr),&\tfrac{1}{4}<t<\tfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

(31)

This
\Gamma
satisfies:
\Gamma\in C^{\infty}
,
0\leq\Gamma^{\prime}(t)\leq 6
, and
|\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq 128
.

Define the smoothed estimator

\displaystyle\bigl[\bar{g}_{T}(x,\xi)\bigr]_{i}\ \coloneqq\ \nabla_{i}F_{T}(x)\,\nu_{i}(x,\xi),\qquad\nu_{i}(x,\xi)\coloneqq 1+\Theta_{i}(x)\Bigl(\frac{\xi}{p}-1\Bigr),\quad\xi\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p).

(32)

Lemma E.4

(Mean-squared smooth estimator (Lemma 4 in
4
))
.

\bar{g}_{T}
is a probability-
p
zero-chain, is unbiased for
\nabla F_{T}
, and there exist constants
\varsigma=23
and
\bar{\ell}_{1}=328
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl\|\bar{g}_{T}(x,z)-\nabla F_{T}(x)\bigr\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varsigma^{2}\,\frac{1-p}{p},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl\|\bar{g}_{T}(x,z)-\bar{g}_{T}(y,z)\bigr\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bar{\ell}_{1}^{\,2}}{p}\,\|x-y\|^{2}.

(39)

to ensure

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\ \inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\ \|\nabla f(x^{(r,k)})\|^{2}\ \right]\leq\varepsilon.

As in
4
we consider the cases

\frac{\bar{L}\Delta\sqrt{p}}{2\Delta_{0}\bar{\ell}_{1}\varepsilon}\geq 5

and

\frac{\bar{L}\Delta\sqrt{p}}{2\Delta_{0}\bar{\ell}_{1}\varepsilon}<5

separately.
In the former case, using
\lfloor x\rfloor/2-1\geq x/4
, we get

\displaystyle t\

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{24}\ \frac{\bar{L}\Delta\sqrt{p}}{8\Delta_{0}\bar{\ell}_{1}\varepsilon}\ \min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{p}+m\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{24}\ \frac{\bar{L}\Delta\min\{\frac{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\varsigma}{\sigma},\,1\}}{8\Delta_{0}\bar{\ell}_{1}\varepsilon}\ \min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\varepsilon\varsigma^{2}}+m\right).

Choosing
c^{\prime}=1/(40\bar{\ell}_{1}\Delta_{0})
implies
\varepsilon\leq\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{8}
, hence the conditions of Lemma
E.5
hold, and combining both bounds yields the desired result.

In the latter case,

\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\frac{\bar{L}\Delta\sqrt{p}}{2\Delta_{0}\bar{\ell}_{1}\varepsilon}\right\rfloor-1\right)\leq 1,

and having

\varepsilon<\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{40\bar{\ell}_{1}\Delta_{0}}

precludes the option
p=1
.
Hence, the right-hand side in (
39
) is smaller than the lower bound in
Lemma

E.5
up to a universal constant.
This completes the proof.
∎

Here we prove the lemma that was used in the proof above.

Lemma E.5
.

Assume
\varepsilon\leq\nicefrac{{\bar{L}\Delta}}{{8}}
.
Consider Protocol
2
with
n
workers and batch-time functions

\{\tau_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfying Assumption
6.1
.
Write
\tau_{i}\coloneqq\tau_{i}(1)
and assume

0<\tau_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{n}
.
Then there exist functions
\{f_{s}\}_{s\in\{-1,+1\}}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\Delta,\bar{L}}
and, for each
s\in\{-1,+1\}
,
a collection of oracles and distributions
((O_{1},\dots,O_{n}),(\mathcal{D}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{n}))\in\mathcal{O}_{\tau_{1}(\cdot),\dots,\tau_{n}(\cdot)}^{\sigma^{2},\bar{L},B}(f_{s})

such that for every algorithm
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{A}
run under the protocol,
for any time
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\max_{s\in\{-1,+1\}}\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[\ \inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\ \|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(r,k)})\|^{2}\ \right]\;\geq\;\min\!\left\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{64\,N(t)},\ \frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{8}\right\},

(40)

where
\mathbb{E}_{s}
denotes expectation under instance
s
,
S_{t}
is the set of query-point indices whose corresponding gradients have been returned by time
t
, and

\displaystyle N(t)\ \coloneqq\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor,

(41)

with the convention
\sigma^{2}/(64\,N(t))\coloneqq+\infty
when
N(t)=0
.
Consequently, if
t
satisfies
N(t)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{64\varepsilon}
, then

\displaystyle\max_{s\in\{-1,+1\}}\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[\ \inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\ \|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(r,k)})\|^{2}\ \right]\;\geq\;\varepsilon.

(42)

In particular, any algorithm satisfying

\max_{s}\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[\inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(r,k)})\|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon

must have

\displaystyle t\ \geq\ c_{0}\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}+m\right),

(43)

for some absolute constant
c_{0}
.

Proof.

The argument is the same as
4
, with the sample budget

T
replaced by the maximal number of independent oracle draws available by time
t
, namely
N(t)
.

Fix
r\in\bigl(0,\sqrt{2\Delta/\bar{L}}\bigr)
and define, for
s\in\{-1,+1\}
,

\displaystyle P_{\xi}^{\,s}\ \coloneqq\ \mathcal{N}\!\left(rs,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\bar{L}^{2}}\right),\qquad f(x,\xi)\ \coloneqq\ \frac{\bar{L}}{2}\Bigl(\|x\|^{2}-2\xi x_{1}+r^{2}\Bigr),\qquad f_{s}(x)\ \coloneqq\ \mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim P_{\xi}^{\,s}}[f(x,\xi)].

Let
\theta_{s}\coloneqq(rs,0,\dots,0)
. Then

\displaystyle f_{s}(x)=\frac{\bar{L}}{2}\|x-\theta_{s}\|^{2},

so
f_{s}
is
\bar{L}
–smooth and

f_{s}(0)-\inf_{x}f_{s}(x)=\frac{\bar{L}}{2}r^{2}\leq\Delta
, hence
f_{s}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta,\bar{L}}
.

For each worker
i\in[n]
, set
\mathcal{D}_{i}\coloneqq P_{\xi}^{\,s}
and let the stochastic gradient mapping be

\nabla f(x;\xi)\coloneqq\nabla_{x}f(x,\xi)=\bar{L}(x-\xi e_{1})
, so that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\left[\nabla f(x;\xi)\right]=\nabla f_{s}(x),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f_{s}(x)\right\|^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(y;\xi)\right\|^{2}\right]=\bar{L}^{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}.

Thus, the corresponding oracles
O_{i}=O_{\tau_{i}(\cdot),B}^{f}
(defined in (
5
))
belong to
\mathcal{O}_{\tau_{1}(\cdot),\dots,\tau_{n}(\cdot)}^{\sigma^{2},\bar{L},B}(f_{s})
.

In (
5
), each
completed
reply uses a single sample
\xi\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}
shared across the
entire returned batch.
Hence, a completed reply contributes at most one independent sample, regardless of the chosen batch size

b\leq B
. Since
\tau_{i}(\cdot)
is nondecreasing (Assumption
6.1
), using
b>1
cannot increase the number of independent samples received by time
t
and can only increase completion times.
Therefore, for the purpose of lower bounding the obtainable information by time
t
, we may restrict attention to unit batches and work with
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}(1)
, which yields the sample budget
N(t)
in (
41
).

Let
S
be uniform on
\{-1,+1\}
, indicating which instance is selected.
Conditioned on
S=s
, each completed reply reveals one independent draw from
P_{\xi}^{\,s}
(indeed, from any returned gradient

g=\bar{L}(x-\xi e_{1})
we recover
\xi=x_{1}-g_{1}/\bar{L}
exactly).
By time
t
, worker
i
can complete at most

\lfloor t/\tau_{i}\rfloor
such replies, hence the algorithm can receive at most
N(t)
independent samples.

Define, for
s\in\{-1,+1\}
,

A_{s}\ \coloneqq\ \inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\ \left\|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(r,k)})\right\|.

Define

\displaystyle\hat{S}\ \coloneqq\ \argmin_{s^{\prime}\in\{-1,+1\}}A_{s^{\prime}},\quad\text{with ties broken arbitrarily.}

(44)

If
\hat{S}\neq S
, then
A_{\hat{S}}\leq A_{S}
, and hence

\displaystyle 2A_{S}\geq\ A_{1}+A_{-1}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\|\nabla f_{1}(x)\|+\|\nabla f_{-1}(x)\|\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{L}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\|x-\theta_{1}\|+\|x-\theta_{-1}\|\right)\geq\bar{L}\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{-1}\|=2r\bar{L}~.

Hence,
A_{S}\geq r\bar{L}
whenever
\hat{S}\neq S
, and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A_{S}\right]\ \geq\ r\bar{L}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}\neq S),

(45)

where
\mathbb{P}
is over the randomness of
S
, the oracle, and the algorithm.

Write
\mathbb{P}_{s}
for the law of the information available by time
t
under instance
s
.
Any estimator of
S
based on this information has error at least

\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}\neq S)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\|\mathbb{P}_{1}-\mathbb{P}_{-1}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\Bigr).

By Pinsker’s inequality,

\|\mathbb{P}_{1}-\mathbb{P}_{-1}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\ \leq\ \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{1}\|\mathbb{P}_{-1})}~.

Since the transcript by time
t
is a measurable function of at most
N(t)
i.i.d. samples

\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N(t)}
with
\xi_{j}\sim P_{\xi}^{\,s}
under instance
s
, the data-processing inequality yields

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{1}\|\mathbb{P}_{-1})\ \leq\ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left((P_{\xi}^{\,1})^{\otimes N(t)}\,\big\|\,(P_{\xi}^{\,-1})^{\otimes N(t)}\right)=N(t)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\xi}^{\,1}\|P_{\xi}^{\,-1})~.

Moreover,

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\xi}^{\,1}\|P_{\xi}^{\,-1})=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\mathcal{N}\!\left(r,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\bar{L}^{2}}\right)\Big\|\ \mathcal{N}\left(-r,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\bar{L}^{2}}\right)\right)=\frac{2r^{2}\bar{L}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}~.

Thus,

\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}\neq S)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{r\bar{L}\sqrt{N(t)}}{\sigma}\right),

and by (
45
),

\mathbb{E}[A_{S}]\ \geq\ \frac{r\bar{L}}{2}\left(1-\frac{r\bar{L}\sqrt{N(t)}}{\sigma}\right).

Now set

r\ \coloneqq\ \min\left\{\frac{\sigma}{2\bar{L}\sqrt{N(t)}},\ \sqrt{\frac{2\Delta}{\bar{L}}}\right\},

interpreting
\sigma/(2\bar{L}\sqrt{N(t)})\coloneqq+\infty
when
N(t)=0
.
Then
r\leq\sqrt{2\Delta/\bar{L}}
ensures
f_{s}(0)-\inf f_{s}\leq\Delta
, and

r\leq\sigma/(2\bar{L}\sqrt{N(t)})
makes the parenthesis at least
1/2
, so

\mathbb{E}[A_{S}]\ \geq\ \frac{r\bar{L}}{4}=\min\!\left\{\frac{\sigma}{8\sqrt{N(t)}},\ \sqrt{\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{8}}\right\}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[A_{S}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}\mathbb{E}_{s}[A_{s}]
, we get

\max_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[\inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(r,k)})\|\right]\ \geq\ \min\!\left\{\frac{\sigma}{8\sqrt{N(t)}},\ \sqrt{\frac{\bar{L}\Delta}{8}}\right\}.

Finally, for each
s
, Jensen’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[\inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(r,k)})\|^{2}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[A_{s}^{2}\right]\ \geq\ \left({\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[A_{s}\right]\right)^{2},

and squaring the previous bound gives (
40
). The implication (
42
) follows
immediately from
\varepsilon\leq\nicefrac{{\bar{L}\Delta}}{{8}}
and
N(t)\leq\sigma^{2}/(64\varepsilon)
.

It remains to obtain (
43
). Set
S_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\sigma^{2}/(64\varepsilon)
.
If
S_{\varepsilon}<\frac{1}{4}
, then
\varepsilon>\sigma^{2}/16
and any algorithm with

\max_{s}\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[\inf_{(r,k)\in S_{t}}\|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(r,k)})\|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon

must have
S_{t}\neq\emptyset
, hence
t\geq\tau_{1}
.
Moreover,

\frac{1}{384}\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}+m\right)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{384}\,\tau_{1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}+1\right)\ <\ \tau_{1},

so (
43
) holds.

Assume now that
S_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{1}{4}
.
Define
\tau_{n+1}\coloneqq\infty
and

j_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\ \coloneqq\ \inf\left\{m\in[n]\,\middle|\,S_{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}<\tau_{m+1}\right\}.

Define

t_{1}\ \coloneqq\ S_{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1},\qquad t_{2}\ \coloneqq\ \min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(S_{\varepsilon}+m\right).

By
(
31
, Lemma D.7)
(applied with
S=S_{\varepsilon}
), we have
t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq 6t_{1}
.
Moreover,
t_{1}<\tau_{j_{\varepsilon}^{\star}+1}
, and hence
\lfloor t_{1}/\tau_{i}\rfloor=0
for all
i\geq j_{\varepsilon}^{\star}+1
, so

N(t_{1})=\sum_{i=1}^{j_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\left\lfloor\frac{t_{1}}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\frac{t_{1}}{\tau_{i}}=t_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{j_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}=S_{\varepsilon}.

Since
N(\cdot)
is nondecreasing, for any
t\leq t_{2}/6
we have
t\leq t_{1}
and thus

N(t)\leq N(t_{1})\leq S_{\varepsilon}
.
Therefore, any algorithm with
\max_{s}\mathbb{E}_{s}[\inf_{k\in S_{t}}\|\nabla f_{s}(x^{(k)})\|^{2}]\leq\varepsilon

must satisfy
t\geq t_{2}/6
, i.e.

t\ \geq\ \frac{1}{6}\min_{m\in[n]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{64\varepsilon}+m\right).

Finally, since
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{64\varepsilon}+m\geq\frac{1}{64}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}+m\right)
for all
m\in[n]
, we obtain (
43
).
∎
