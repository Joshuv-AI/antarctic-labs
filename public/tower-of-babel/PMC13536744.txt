Effect of collection time and reported cigarettes per day on exhaled carbon monoxide: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Perry N Willette, Karina C Chen, Tanaia L Botts, David R Botts, Gal Cohen, Jed E Rose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch studies that include adults who smoke conventional combustible tobacco cigarettes (cigarettes) commonly use exhaled carbon monoxide (eCO) as a biomarker of cigarette usage. eCO is regularly used as an inclusion or exclusion criterion. Multiple studies have examined the time course of eCO levels after acute use of cigarettes. Published studies have verified the relationship between the reported number of cigarettes smoked per day (CPD) and eCO levels. One article suggested that collection time of day may induce variations in eCO elimination. Researchers can improve recruitment if there is confidence that collection time does not significantly affect eCO.MethodsWe analyzed data from eight separate studies conducted between 2019 and 2024 that collected eCO levels from adults who were not abstinent from smoking, after they reported their usual consumption patterns of cigarettes, to determine whether collection time or CPD had a significant effect on eCO levels. Data analyzed included eCO levels, collection times of eCO levels, and reported CPD smoked.ResultsIn the aggregate data, collection time did not result in significant changes in eCO levels (p = 0.87), as assessed throughout typical clinical hours (08:00 to 18:00). In contrast, and as expected, eCO levels were significantly impacted by CPD (p < 0.001).ConclusionThis finding verifies that screening eCO levels may be obtained throughout the day while minimally impacting eCO levels, allowing for greater flexibility in scheduling individuals for participation in research studies which require evaluation of smoking status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12954-026-01494-y.Keywords: Exhaled carbon monoxide, Collection time, Screening, Tobacco, Smoking

Introduction

Research on smoking cessation and harm reduction continues to be extremely important, as the effects associated with tobacco use present an enormous burden on the United States (US) and globally. According to the National Health Interview Survey (2022 highlights), 49.2 million US adults (nearly 1 in 5 adults) reported current tobacco product use (1). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that cigarette smoking kills over 480,000 Americans each year, with yearly healthcare costs of over $240 billion and an additional $372 billion in lost productivity (2). The use of exhaled Carbon Monoxide (eCO) for verification of current smoking status provides a fast, simple, inexpensive and noninvasive method for ensuring proper recruitment and enrollment of current conventional combustible tobacco cigarette (cigarette) consumers for research studies (3). eCO is also a vital tool for assessing smoking status in studies that require abstinence from cigarette use (4). Recruitment and enrollment of adults who truly smoke cigarettes are crucial to ensuring accurate and reproducible results. Equally important is verification of abstinence when conducting studies to assess the effect of an intervention, absent systemic alterations known to be caused by recent cigarette smoking.

Validation studies have focused on comparing eCO values with the number of cigarettes smoked and the time between smoking a cigarette and eCO collection. These studies clearly revealed a statistical relationship between the reported number of cigarettes smoked per day and the recorded eCO values (3). These studies have also shown the effect of time from last cigarette inhalation to collection time, verifying an elimination half-life of eCO of approximately 2–6 h (3, 4). Common factors that may affect eCO levels include characteristics of cigarette use (such as the intensity of inhalation for each puff, the duration of each puff inhalation, and the number of puffs inhaled per cigarette smoked). Other factors that can influence eCO levels include sex, pulmonary ventilatory status, and physical exercise, all of which can cause variations in carbon monoxide elimination (5). One article suggested that the time of day may induce variations in carbon monoxide elimination, implying a potential effect on measured eCO levels (5). Despite known absorption/elimination kinetics, there exists uncertainty regarding the appropriate or optimal eCO collection time. eCO levels will change depending on the timing of the first cigarette smoked upon awakening, the total number of cigarettes smoked in the previous day, the amount of time spent sleeping, time intervals between each cigarette smoked during active periods, differences in elimination half-lives (as noted above), and environmental factors such as high vehicular traffic or indoor exposure (4). Studies that evaluate the actual time of day for eCO collection, irrespective of time since last cigarette use, are lacking.

Researchers often question whether it is best to screen light consumers of cigarettes (less than 10 cigarettes daily) in the afternoon versus the morning in an attempt to avoid misclassifying an eligible light consumer of cigarettes as non-consumers of cigarettes. Similarly, questions often arise regarding heavy consumers of cigarette (more than 25 cigarettes daily) and whether they should be screened and have their eCO levels measured in the afternoon versus early in the morning to improve the inclusion of eligible participants for research based on stated criteria. Prior to this analysis, evaluation of collection time versus eCO levels during periods of nonabstinence, correlated with reported CPD smoked, were limited if not lacking. This information is important to allow for proper scheduling of potential study participants for screening and follow-up sampling prior to, during, and after interventions. The time of initial screening is important as it will guide the time for follow-up eCO sampling after enrollment to ensure that sampling is consistent with baseline observations for true comparisons after interventions (no effect based on the time of sampling).

Methods

Data collection

Data entered via electronic case report forms and recorded on paper sources were gathered from the screening sessions of eight research studies performed at Rose Research Center (RRC). All of these studies were performed after Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and were conducted in accordance with the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH), local and federal laws, and Good Clinical Practices (GCP). The collected deidentified data included the reported cigarettes per day (CPD), the measured eCO value, and the time of sampling for the eCO reading, as well as demographics and body mass index (when available). After obtaining informed consent, all participants in these studies verified the normal use of their cigarettes prior to any screening procedures. eCO levels were obtained by trained Clinical Research Assistants/Coordinators per RRC Standard Operating Procedures using the Vitalograph® BreathCO monitoring device (Vitalograph® Medical, Ireland; model number 29750) (6). All the devices utilized in these studies had been calibrated according to the manufacturer's instructions prior to use.

Data analysis

Single-variable regression analyses were performed with eCO as the dependent variable and with CPD or collection time as the independent variable. Polynomial regressions were evaluated, with either linear or quadratic regression models derived based on backward elimination (retaining only significant terms with p values < 0.05). Subgroup analysis was also utilized to evaluate trends in eCO and collection time based on the reported number of CPD smoked (less than 5 CPD, less than 10 CPD, greater than or equal to 10 CPD, 10–14 CPD, 15–30 CPD, greater than 20 CPD, and greater than 30 CPD). Pertinent results are reported below.

Results

Participants

A total of 756 individual data sets (which included eCO, reported CPD, and collection time) were obtained from individuals who participated in eight research studies performed at RRC from 2019 to 2024. Each data set represents information from unique participants for each study. A limited number of participants may have been enrolled in multiple studies. The participants in these studies were recruited from the Raleigh/Durham and Charlotte areas of North Carolina. The average number of CPD reported by participants was 17.3 (SD 8.0), and the average eCO concentration was 21.5 parts per million (ppm) (SD 11.1). All eCO levels were obtained between 08:00 and 18:00. The average collection time was 11:36 am (SD 1:48 h).

Self-reported CPD significantly impacted eCO readings

Single-variable polynomial regression analysis, evaluating eCO as the dependent variable and CPD as the independent variable, was performed on the entire data set irrespective of the collection time (Fig. 1). This analysis demonstrated a statistically significant effect of CPD on eCO (p < 0.001). This is consistent with previously published findings, with an increase in measured eCO values associated with increasing values of reported CPD. The mean eCO measures maximally asymptoted at 35.6 CPD, suggesting titration in participants who smoked over 35 CPD.

Figure 2 provides a more granular view of the distribution of eCO measurements, as segmented by self-reported CPD. For people who reported smoking 1–9 CPD, the median eCO readings were 11 to 20 ppm, and 89% of the readings were ≤ 20 ppm. Notably, 11% had measurements less than 7 ppm, indicating that in some studies, these participants might have been rejected for a study which utilized an eCO level of at least 10 ppm as an inclusion criteria. In contrast, for people who reported smoking 16 or more CPD, the median eCO readings were 21–30 ppm.

Collection time did not impact eCO readings

As indicated in Fig. 3, across the aggregated data set, eCO readings were not significantly correlated with collection time (p = 0.999). The slope of the best fit line for the eCO values was nearly zero (1 part per billion per hour).

eCO versus collection time (eCO levels do not correlated with collection time, linear regression slope = 0.001 ppm/hour). eCO (exhaled carbon monoxide), ppm (parts per million)

Subgroup analysis, which was based on reported CPD, was conducted to determine whether there might be a relationship between the time of collection and eCO depending on the reported CPD intensity, which might have been obscured in the overall analysis (Fig. 4). In particular, we were interested in whether the eCO levels of people who smoked less than 10 CPD might be impacted by collection time.

Right panel: eCO vs. time of day for people who reported smoking 1 to 9 CPD

For participants who reported smoking less than 10 CPD, the slope of the linear regression analysis (coefficient) was not significant, as was the case for the full group (p = 0.87). However, there was a trend towards a reduction of 0.34 ppm per hour. Similarly, for the participants who reported the heaviest smoking rate of > 20 CPD, there was a statistically significant trend (p = 0.04) toward an increase in CO of 1.05 ppm per hour during the course of the day (the predicted eCO increased from 23.9 ppm at 9 am to 27.0 ppm at noon; see supplement Figure S1).

Discussion

The findings from this analysis validate previous research that reported CPD has a statistically significant effect on measured eCO levels. This reassures both the validity of this analysis and the support of previously reported results. While the CPD is a significant predictor of eCO measurements, the data demonstrate that the collection time does not have a large impact on eCO measurements, allowing for increased flexibility in scheduling both for screening and subsequent measurements, depending on the impact of small changes in eCO on the research question being studied. This holds true for studies that evaluate participants to ensure that they are truly cigarette consumers, and likely for those studies that verify abstinence before or after interventions. Further studies that specifically evaluate collection time and eCO levels for people who have ceased smoking could further validate this conclusion.

Subgroup analysis revealed lower eCO levels in the afternoon for participants who reported less than 10 CPD, with a potential rate of change of 1 ppm decrease over an approximately 3-h period (slope of change of − 0.344 ppm/hour per Fig. 3 and Table 1). Subgroup analysis for reported CPD greater than or equal to 10 CPD did not reveal a similar trend. In the aggregate data, although there may be an expectation that morning eCO values would have been lower than afternoon eCO values, this was not evident in the analysis, with the possible exception of participants smoking more than 2 packs per day (> 20 CPD). Two factors could plausibly account for why morning eCO values are not generally lower than they are later in the day: 1) individuals with moderate or high dependence on cigarettes are likely to begin smoking within 1 h of awakening, and 2) heavy cigarette consumption throughout the day can lead to a high evening eCO level, which is only partially eliminated overnight. The half-life of eCO is expected to increase during periods of low activity.

Factors which may affect any study using eCO include both recall bias and reporting bias for reported CPD. Additionally, participants for these studies were recruited from an area concentrated around the Raleigh/Durham and Charlotte, North Carolina region. This could affect the generalization of any conclusions, especially considering environmental factors such as air pollution in various global geographic areas. Participants were screened after they reported their “usual” daily smoking pattern, irrespective of the time since last cigarette or their sleeping patterns (day shift workers versus night shift workers). Inclusion/Exclusion criteria did not include any mention of shift work for any of the studies included in this analysis. This analysis also did not evaluate or address possible sex effects.

In summary, depending on the research question being evaluated, study designers may feel confident and comfortable in providing scheduling time flexibility for eCO collection times to meet the availability of individuals who are not abstinent from cigarettes.
