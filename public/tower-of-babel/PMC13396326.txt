Digital Motor Outcomes Capture Upper Limb and Mild Stage Impairment in Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia
Authors: Malin Schulze, Dominik Hermle, Robin Schubert, Pascal Barallon, Winfried Ilg, Rebecca Schüle, Ralf Reilmann, Matthis Synofzik, Andreas Traschütz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDigital motor outcomes may surpass the sensitivity of clinician-reported outcomes in hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP), particularly in cases of mild upper limb impairment and early disease stages. We thus validated a trial-ready quantitative motor (Q-Motor) assessment of rapid alternating limb movement tasks against clinician-reported, patient-focused, and performance outcomes in HSP.MethodsExploratory single-center cross-sectional assessment in 41 patients with different HSP genotypes (age: 48 ± 14 years), with speeded foot tapping, diadochokinesia, and finger tapping measured by a high-resolution force transducer. Validation comprised discrimination from 48 age- and sex-matched controls; correlations to the Spastic Paraplegia Rating Scale (SPRS; mean: 18.6 ± 9.1), Friedreich Ataxia Rating Scale- Activities of Daily Living (FARS-ADL), and Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT); and stratification by functional stage (FARS stage: mild/moderate/severe = ambulatory/walking aid/wheelchair).ResultsFoot tapping best discriminated patients with HSP from controls (e.g., frequency: area under the curve [AUC] = 0.94–0.96), and particularly captured HSP severity and functional impairment by measures of foot elevation and cumulative tap force (across all measures: |rhoSPRS|= 0.33–0.59; |rhoADL|= 0.35–0.56). Speed measures of diadochokinesia and finger tapping captured functional impairment (|rhoADL|= 0.32–0.60) and impaired dexterity (|rho9HPT |= 0.53–0.77) in the upper limbs of patients with HSP—even in the mild stage, and with slowed finger tapping even in patients without upper limb pyramidal signs. Foot tapping measures were most sensitive in discriminating mild-stage impairment (AUC = 0.72–0.95) and predominantly changed between mild and moderate HSP, while upper limb measures of diadochokinesia and finger tapping predominantly changed from moderate to severe HSP.ConclusionQ-Motor captures motor impairment in HSP, even in the upper limbs and mild disease stages, and should be further evaluated as a possible trial outcome by longitudinal validation in genotypically stratified cohorts and mapping to patient experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00950-y.Keywords: Hereditary spastic paraplegia, Digital motor outcome, Upper limb, Dexterity, Q-Motor

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Clinician-reported outcomes for hereditary spastic paraplegias (HSP) lack sensitivity to slowly progressive motor changes, limiting their usefulness to capture treatment effects in upcoming trials.Hypothesizing that digital motor outcomes may achieve better sensitivity, this study validated a trial-ready quantitative motor (Q-Motor) assessment of rapid alternating limb movement tasks against clinician-reported, patient-focused, and performance outcomes in HSP. What was learned from the study? Digital measures of foot tapping, diadochokinesia, and finger tapping capture HSP severity across clinician-rated, patient-focused, and performance outcomes—even in the upper limbs of patients without manifest clinical signs, and in the mildest disease stages most relevant for trials.Q-Motor captures motor impairment in HSP, even in the upper limbs and mild disease stages, and should be further evaluated as a possible trial outcome by longitudinal validation in distinct and mapping to patient experience.

Introduction

Hereditary spastic paraplegias (HSP) are a large group of neurodegenerative diseases with progressive length-dependent degeneration of the corticospinal tract, leading to spasticity, slowing of movement, and paresis [1]. After the identification of more than 90 genetic forms of HSP, and the increasing understanding of the underlying molecular processes and pathogenic pathways, first HSP subtypes are now entering the stage of clinical trials for disease-modifying treatments [2–4]. However, such trials are currently limited by the available clinical outcome assessments (COAs), which have insufficient sensitivity to change in the face of slow disease progression and small trial populations inherent to rare neurological diseases [5–7].

With their objectivity, reliability, and granularity, digital motor outcomes promise better sensitivity than clinical outcomes and are thus increasingly implemented—even as trial endpoints—in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, or hereditary ataxias [8–11]. However, the development and implementation of digital motor outcomes in HSP remain at an early stage [12–14]. One promising approach could be the digital assessment of rapid alternating limb movements, which is widely applied in other neurodegenerative diseases [15–17], and could also be sensitive to the pyramidal slowing of movement in HSP. If measured by a force sensor, such digital motor tasks may also capture paresis and difficulties of force control in HSP, as has been shown by grip force tasks in other diseases with distinct upper and/or lower motor neuron damage [18, 19].

A particular challenge for digital motor assessment in HSP is the mild clinical impairment in the upper limbs and in early stages of the disease [1, 2]. Upper limb involvement is suggested by hyperreflexia, spasticity, and/or weakness, as well as by neurophysiological studies in many patients with HSP [2, 20–22], and may impair dexterity [23, 24]. However, upper limb involvement is not generally assessed in HSP rating scales [25], and has never been systematically explored as a digital outcome domain in HSP. In the early stages of HSP—most relevant for future disease-modifying therapies—patients mainly present with spastic gait, while paresis is often minimal or absent in the clinical examination [2]. Nevertheless, impaired foot elevation has been detectable by digital gait analysis even in patients who are presymptomatic with spastic paraplegia type 4 (SPG4) [12]. Thus, digital assessment of rapid foot movements could also be sensitive to motor impairment in early stages of HSP.

With the hypothesis that digital assessment of rapid alternating limb movements can capture slowing and weakness in HSP, this exploratory study conducted a single-center cross-sectional validation of a trial-proven quantitative motor (Q-Motor) battery with a speeded foot tapping (pedomotography), pronation/supination (diadochomotography), and finger tapping (digitomotography) task [9, 10, 16]. Overall, we show that digital measures in all three tapping tasks discriminate a large cohort of cross-genotype patients with HSP from matched controls, and capture HSP severity in clinician-rated, patient-focused, and/or performance outcomes, even in patients without clinically manifest upper limb involvement and in the trial-relevant mild disease stage.

Methods

Cohort and Clinical Outcome Assessment

Participants were recruited from consecutive patients presenting to the HSP clinic of the University Hospital Tübingen between February and September 2020. Inclusion criteria were a clinical or genetic diagnosis of HSP—irrespective of disease stage, clinical upper limb involvement, or additional multisystemic involvement (i.e., pure or complex HSP; see Supplement 1 for list of diagnoses). Clinical assessments included demographic information, handedness according to the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [26], and severity of HSP as measured by (i) the Spastic Paraplegia Rating Scale (SPRS) as clinician-reported outcome (ClinRO) [25]; (ii) the functional stage and Activities of Daily Living part of the Friedreich Ataxia Rating Scale (FARS stage and FARS-ADL) [27] by interview as patient-focused outcome (PRO); and (iii) the Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT, Rolyan version) as performance outcome (PerfO) [28], calculated as the average duration across two trials in the dominant and non-dominant hand, respectively. The SPRS is the most widely used disease-specific ClinRO for HSP, consisting of 13 items rating the severity of impaired functional mobility, lower-limb weakness and contractures, pain, and bladder dysfunction (total score: 0 [normal] to 52 [most severe]) [25]. The FARS-ADL comprises nine items rating functional impairment in activities of daily living including speech, cutting food/handling utensils, dressing, hygiene, falling, walking, sitting, and impaired swallowing and bladder function [27]. The 9HPT is a measure of manual dexterity, assessing the time to complete a standardized peg placement/removal task [29]. Healthy controls, who were matched with patients according to age and sex, were recruited among staff members, students, and patients’ company, and have previously also been used for validation in hereditary ataxia [30]. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Medical Faculty of the University of Tübingen (423/2019BO1), and all subjects provided written informed consent.

Digital Motor Assessment

All participants were assessed with Q-Motor (George-Huntington-Institute and QuantiMedis, Münster, Germany) [16], comprising three tasks measured by force sensor: speeded foot tapping (pedomotography), diadochokinesia (diadochomotography), and finger tapping (digitomotography; Fig. 1A). All three tasks required speeded tapping in a 10 s period, which was captured by a pre-calibrated force transducer at a sampling frequency of 350 Hz. Digital measures were extracted based on the time-force series of the tapping sequence after centralized quality control and using automated algorithms independent from study design, patient recruitment/assessment, and blinded to clinical information. These algorithms provided measures of maximum tap force (Fmax [N]), tapping frequency [taps per second, Hz], and—for each inter-tap or tap segment—the inter-onset interval (IOI [s]), inter-tap interval (ITI [s]), inter-peak interval (IPI [s]), and tap duration (TD [s]; Fig. 1B), as previously described [16]. The tap segment (i.e., the period of the motion cycle during which the limb touches the sensor) was further analyzed at higher resolution with novel digital measures that quantify the cumulative area under the tap force curve over the entire duration of the tap (FAUC [Ns]), and measures that separate the duration of its rising edge (Trise [s]) and falling edge (Tfall [s]) before and after the maximum tap force, thus for example isolating foot elevation during foot tapping as a distinct subsegment of the tap (Fig. 1C). For all measures, the respective mean, standard deviation (STD), and coefficient of variation (CV; STD/mean) were first calculated based on their within-trial variability, and then averaged across three repeated trials on the dominant and non-dominant side. For each tapping task, this digital motor analysis thus provided 2 × 25 measures that systematically quantified motor impairment across five conceptual movement features: (i) tapping speed, (ii) tap duration, (iii) inter-tap variability, (iv) tap variability, and (v) measures of force control. Six measures in each task were predefined as primary exploratory outcomes: tapping frequency, tap duration (TD; mean and STD), variability between taps (IOI; STD), and cumulative tap force (FAUC; mean and STD). Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated between the first and third trial, and were excellent for measures based on within-trial means (ICC: 0.87–0.99) and expectedly lower for measures based on within-trial variability (ICC: 0.13–0.95, Supplement 2).

Statistical Analysis

Construct validity of digital measures for motor impairment in HSP was explored by receiver operating characteristics for the discrimination between patients with HSP and matched healthy controls (known-groups validity) [31], and by correlation with disease severity (convergent validity) as measured by the SPRS (ClinRO), FARS-ADL (PRO), and 9HPT (PerfO) in complementary regulatory outcome domains [30]. Based on FARS stage, the cohort was further divided into a functionally meaningful mild (≤2.5 points, i.e., independent ambulation, n = 22), moderate (3–4 points, i.e., patients requiring walking aids, n = 13), and severe (≥4.5 points, i.e., patients requiring a wheelchair, n = 6) subgroup. This stratification allowed for distinct validation in the trial-relevant early/mild disease stage and the ability to analyze whether digital measures discriminated between disease stages of HSP, thus determining approximate meaningful score regions [32]. For further analysis, patients were categorized into subgroups (i) without upper limb pyramidal signs, defined as hyperreflexia, spasticity, or weakness, to determine sensitivity at subclinical stages (n = 12), and (ii) without signs of additional ataxia (n = 30). Non-parametric Spearman correlation and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (for comparisons between severity levels) were applied in these analyses to account for non-normal distributions. Correlation and discrimination analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons in this exploratory study because digital measures were not independent within and across the five conceptual movement features, and regarding bimanual testing [30]. However, confirmatory results based on Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted correlations of the primary exploratory outcomes are provided as supplementary material.

All descriptive and statistical tests were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and visualized with GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Demographic and clinical data were compared between groups using t tests for continuous data and Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± standard deviation or as median [interquartile range].

Results

Patient Characteristics and Assessment

Digital motor assessment was performed in 41 patients with HSP (HSP; 20 women; age: 48 ± 14 years, range: 17–76), and in 48 sex- and age-matched healthy controls (CON; 33 women, Fisher’s exact test: p = 0.083; age: 44 ± 18 years, range: 18–88, t test: p = 0.336; Table 1). The genetic cause of HSP was identified in 27/41 patients, while the remainder received a clinical diagnosis of simple or complex HSP after comprehensive diagnostic work-up (see Supplement 1 for list of patients and diagnoses). The HSP cohort was predominantly right-handed (38/41) and representative for a wide range of onset ages (25 ± 19 years, range: 1–60), disease durations (24 ± 17 years, range: 3–65), and disease severities as determined by the SPRS (19 ± 9, range: 3–38) or FARS-ADL (9 ± 5, range: 0–20). Stratified by FARS stage, 22 (54%) patients had independent ambulation (≤ 2.5, “mild HSP”), 13 (32%) patients depended on walking aids (3–4, “moderate HSP”), and 6 (15%) patients required a wheelchair (≥ 4.5, “severe HSP”). Digital motor assessment could be completed in all except one patient with severe HSP (HSP12; SPRS: 38 points) who could not perform foot tapping. In another patient (HSP11), finger tapping was completed but discarded as outlier due to severe hand atrophy and paresis, likely unrelated to HSP.

Tap Segment Measures of Foot Tapping Capture Lower Limb Impairment Most Effectively in HSP

Digital measures across all three tasks discriminated between HSP and matched controls and correlated with clinical severity in the SPRS and functional impairment in the FARS-ADL (Table 2; see Supplement 2 for complete list of measures; Supplement 3 for representative plots; Supplement 4 for 95% confidence intervals; and Supplement 5 for adjustment for multiple comparisons of 96 correlations of primary exploratory outcomes). The best discrimination of HSP from controls was achieved by foot tapping measures (e.g., by lower frequency [ndom]: area under the curve (AUC) = 0.96), consistent with the clinical lower limb predominance of HSP. Correlations with SPRS and FARS-ADL were highest for the tap duration (mean TD [dom]: rhoSPRS = 0.53, p = 0.001, rhoADL = 0.49, p = 0.001), while they were absent for other primary exploratory outcomes, i.e., mean cumulative tap force and variability measures based on the STD (Table 2). However, both SPRS and FARS-ADL were correlated with variability measures based on the CV (i.e., STD/mean), with even the highest observed correlations for novel measures capturing the foot elevation phase of the motion cycle (CV Tfall [ndom]: rhoSPRS = −0.59, p < 0.001; rhoADL = −0.51, p = 0.001) and the cumulative tap force (CV FAUC [dom]: rhoSPRS = −0.52, p = 0.001; rhoADL = −0.56, p < 0.001). Unlike the CV of the tap duration or inter-tap variability, the negative correlations of the CV of the cumulative tap force with the SPRS and FARS-ADL were not explained by systematic changes of the respective mean or STD, as those were not correlated with HSP severity (Table 2), and it was obtained despite very strong positive correlations between the mean and STD that would counteract their use as a composite ratio (rho > 0.9, p < 0.001, Supplement 6).

Diadochokinesia and Finger Tapping Capture Upper Limb Motor Impairment in HSP

Completion of the 9HPT took significantly longer in patients with HSP than in matched controls (26.8 ± 9.4 vs. 20.5 ± 3.6; t test; p < 0.001), even in the mild stage (24.8 ± 8.0, p = 0.003), indicating the presence of impaired dexterity in the study cohort. Although digital upper limb measures showed greater overlap between HSP and healthy controls than foot tapping measures, in particular speed measures still provided moderate discrimination (frequency [dom], AUC = 0.70–0.71). Slower performance in diadochokinesia and finger tapping, e.g., lower tapping frequency, were associated with greater HSP severity across all outcome domains, particularly with the 9HPT as PerfO (diadochokinesia: rho9HPT = −0.72; finger tapping: rho9HPT = −0.76; both p < 0.001; Table 2). Correlations with speed measures of finger tapping were stronger in the dominant hand (see Supplement 2 for complete list of measures), and robust in the subcohort of 12 patients without upper limb pyramidal signs (Supplement 7). Beyond speed, the mean tap duration and the mean cumulative tap force as primary exploratory outcomes were correlated to impairment in each SPRS, FARS-ADL, and 9HPT in the diadochokinesia task (e.g., mean TD [dom]: rhoSPRS = 0.37, rhoADL = 0.42, rho9HPT = 0.61, all p < 0.018), while correlations were limited to the 9HPT in the finger tapping task (mean TD [dom]: rho9HPT = 0.60, p < 0.001). Unlike foot tapping, CV measures in both upper limb tasks were not correlated with HSP severity (Supplement 2). Overall, qualitatively similar findings were obtained for diadochokinesia and finger tapping in a subgroup of 30 patients without additional signs of ataxia that could contribute to motor impairment unrelated to pyramidal tract degeneration. The level of clinical severity observed in this subgroup was similar to that seen in the full cohort (n = 30; SPRS: 19±9 (3–38); FARS-ADL: 9±5 (0–20); 9HPT: 25.2±8.7 (17.9–63.6); Supplement 8).

Digital Tapping Measures are Sensitive to Mild Motor Impairment

To explore whether the sensitivity to motor impairment of the digital tapping measures would be sufficient for trial-relevant early stages of HSP, further validation was performed in a subcohort of 22 patients with mild HSP as defined by a FARS stage ≤ 2.5 (i.e., independent ambulation; Table 1). Discrimination between mild HSP and matched controls by foot tapping was comparable to the full cohort, with optimal discrimination by tapping speed (frequency [ndom]: AUC = 0.94) and the duration of foot elevation (mean Tfall [ndom]: AUC = 0.95). Both measures also correlated with clinician-reported disease severity (rhoSPRS = −0.60 and 0.53, respectively, p = 0.003 and 0.012; Table 3; see Supplement 9 for complete list of measures). However, only the CV of the cumulative tap force during foot tapping correlated with the FARS-ADL and thus reflected functional impairment also in the early disease stage (CV FAUC [dom]: rhoADL = −0.45; p = 0.037). In the upper limbs, slower speed of diadochokinesia and finger tapping as well as longer tap durations during diadochokinesia were correlated with impairment in FARS-ADL and 9HPT also in mild HSP (rhoADL = 0.44–0.69, rho9HPT = 0.49–0.77, all p < 0.043). None of the correlations between the SPRS and upper limb measures in the full cohort could be replicated in the subcohort with mild HSP (Table 3; see Supplement 9 for all measures).

Optimal Discrimination Between Meaningful HSP Stages Depends on Motor Task and Measure

To determine approximate meaningful score regions and to further characterize the discriminatory power of the digital tapping measures between meaningful disease stages, the cohort was split into three groups of mild, moderate, and severe HSP (FARS stage: ≤ 2.5/3–4/ ≥ 4.5; n = 22/13/6; see Table 1 for characteristics). Overall, the meaningful score regions differed in 48/75 measures across all three tapping tasks (Kruskal–Wallis test, all p < 0.047; Supplement 10). Optimal discrimination between mild versus moderate and moderate versus severe HSP depended on the respective motor task and measure (Fig. 2). In foot tapping, for example, tap duration differed only between mild versus moderate HSP (TD [dom]: 0.14 [0.13] vs. 0.30 [0.27] s; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.017). In turn, the moderate and severe stage of HSP differed only in the variability of timing between successive foot taps relative to the mean (e.g., CV IOI [ndom]: 0.15 [0.08] vs. 0.07 [0.02], p = 0.013). In the upper limb domain, tapping measures were not consistently different between mild and moderate HSP. However, measures of both upper limb tasks consistently differed between the moderate and severe stage of HSP, for example, in speed of diadochokinesia (e.g., frequency [dom]: 2.96 [0.83] vs. 1.70 [1.06] Hz, p = 0.004) or speed of finger tapping (e.g., frequency [ndom]: 4.46 [0.97] vs. 2.83 [2.79] Hz, p = 0.009).

Discussion

To explore digital motor outcomes for HSP, the present study validated the trial-ready Q-Motor battery of rapid alternating limb movement tasks in a large cross-sectional and cross-genotype HSP cohort. Overall, this exploratory study demonstrated that lower limb measures of foot tapping as well as upper limb measures of diadochokinesia and finger tapping can capture HSP severity across clinician-rated (SPRS), patient-focused (FARS-ADL), and/or performance outcomes (9HPT)—even in the upper limbs and in the most trial-relevant mild disease stage.

Digital motor assessment of speeded foot tapping best discriminated patients with HSP from matched controls, and best reflected clinician-reported disease severity as rated by the SPRS, particularly with digital measures quantifying slower tapping speed and longer tap durations. This was expected given the lower limb predominance of HSP, with leg weakness and spasticity as clinical hallmarks [1]. Correlations with the SPRS, but also with functional impairment as rated by the FARS-ADL, were highest for the novel high-resolution measures focusing on the tap segment of the foot tapping cycle, in particular the foot elevation phase and the integral of the cumulative tap force. The detectable prolongation of foot elevation and its correlation with the FARS-ADL probably reflects impairment of dorsiflexion and foot clearance, which is a key feature of HSP [14, 33, 34], and associated with gait impairment and falls [13, 35, 36]. The cumulative tap force measure is a composite measure that reflects both impaired force control and pyramidal slowing, as the latter prolongs the tap duration and thus expands the time-force curves during foot tapping. Particularly high correlations with the SPRS and FARS-ADL were obtained for exploratory foot tapping measures based on the CV (i.e., STD/mean)—with counterintuitive negative correlations suggesting reduced variability in more severe HSP. For the duration of the taps and the interval between taps, this apparent reduction was the result of the prolongation of the mean relative to the STD, which was not correlated to clinical severity. Given their better interpretability, the mean would thus be preferred over the CV as a digital measure for a trial. In contrast, a reduced CV appeared to be a genuine measure of HSP severity for the cumulative tap force, for which neither its mean nor its STD alone were correlated with the SPRS or FARS-ADL. This requires further investigation to enhance interpretability for regulatory contexts, but we speculate that this reduced variability reflects pathological constraints of the motor system in spastic paresis—by increased muscle tone, co-contraction, and/or reduced descending modulation—that may effectively “freeze” degrees of freedom, leading to more stereotyped and less variable motor output despite impaired function [37, 38].

Both upper limb motor tasks, i.e., speeded diadochokinesia and finger tapping, also captured upper limb impairment in our HSP cohort. Speed measures of diadochokinesia and finger tapping were slower in HSP than in controls, and correlated with motor impairment measured by the SPRS, functional impairment measured by the FARS-ADL, and impaired manual dexterity measured by the 9HPT. Notably, upper limb motor impairment was independently shown by worse 9HPT performance in patients with HSP than in matched controls—even in patients with mild HSP, although upper limb involvement is not generally considered prevalent in HSP [1]. This upper limb motor impairment was not conveyed by concurrent ataxia, as substantiated by robust findings in our control analysis of patients with HSP, but without clinical signs of ataxia. Digital upper limb measures of diadochokinesia and finger tapping also consistently correlated with the FARS-ADL and/or 9HPT even in the subgroup of patients with HSP without clinical signs of upper limb pyramidal involvement (e.g., spasticity or hyperreflexia). This suggests that digital motor assessment may capture upper limb involvement in HSP that is neither covered by widely used clinical rating scales (e.g., SPRS) nor evident in standard clinical examinations. Further studies are needed to determine whether this reflects a limitation of clinical scales with a lower limb focus, or whether indeed this upper limb involvement can only be captured with highly sensitive sensors, but not clinically. The diadochokinesia and finger tapping task capture this mild upper limb involvement with slower overall speed measures, and in particular also with measures that quantify only the duration or the cumulative force of the tap. Digital capture of this tap segment of the rapid alternating limb movement, which requires a rapid switch from agonist to antagonist muscle, may be particularly sensitive to pyramidal tract degeneration, for example, due to detrimental effects of co-contraction of agonist and antagonist, and the loss of supraspinal descending inputs facilitating reciprocal spinal inhibition [39–41].

Digital measures in all three tapping tasks captured motor impairment in the mild stage of HSP, as defined by independent walking (FARS stage ≤ 2.5). Correlations between HSP severity in the SPRS and measures of foot tapping speed and tap duration were at least as strong in mild HSP as for the broad severity range covered by the full cohort, and discrimination of patients with mild HSP from matched controls by the speed of foot tapping was the same (e.g., tapping frequency [ndom]: AUC = 0.94). Notably, discrimination between HSP and matched controls was overall highest based on the duration of the foot elevation in mild HSP (e.g., mean Tfall [ndom]: AUC = 0.95), suggesting that digital assessment of foot dorsiflexion could be the most sensitive motor outcome in early-stage HSP. Further studies are required to explore whether this digital foot tapping measure could capture even earlier—i.e., presymptomatic or prodromal—stages of HSP, given that impaired foot dorsiflexion has been detectable by digital gait analysis in prodromal stages of SPG4 [12]. In the upper limb domain, digital measures of diadochokinesia and finger tapping also showed moderate-to-strong correlations with the FARS-ADL and 9HPT in the mild HSP stage, although the overlap with matched controls was larger and correlations with the SPRS were lacking. The lack of correlation between SPRS and digital upper limb (but not lower limb) measures in mild HSP warrants additional studies with in-depth upper limb assessments, as the mobility and lower limb focus of the SPRS does not allow us to disentangle the presence and severity of concurrent upper and lower limb involvement [25]. In turn, however, the distinct correlation of the SPRS with digital lower but not upper limb measures underlines that our tapping tasks captured specific impairment in the respective lower and upper limb motor domain, and not just overall HSP severity as an unspecific underlying construct.

Our comparison of digital measures across mild, moderate, and severe HSP provided their respective range of values in these relevant disease stages. These ranges correspond approximately to meaningful score regions, which were recently endorsed for regulatory outcome qualification [42]. Our comparison between these disease stages showed that the most discriminative measure depends on the specific disease stage—even within the same motor task. Foot tap duration differed most between mild and moderate HSP stages (as defined by independent vs. dependent walking), thus extending its optimal discrimination of mild HSP from matched controls into the discrimination of mild and moderate HSP severity. In contrast, measures of variability relative to the mean interval between foot taps (e.g., CV IOI) differed most between moderate and severe HSP, i.e., after patients become wheelchair-bound. In the upper limb domain, digital measures of diadochokinesia and finger tapping did not appear to differ between mild and moderate HSP stages. However, given their correlations with the SPRS, FARS-ADL, and/or 9HPT at the same time—even in the subgroup of patients with mild HSP—this may be due to the stratification of severity by the FARS stage, which is a mobility-centered anchor that may not resolve variable upper limb motor involvement [27]. In contrast, upper limb measures consistently differed between moderate and severe HSP. For clinical trials in patients with advanced HSP who already lost their independent ambulation, digital limb tapping analysis may thus still be feasible and provide sensitive outcomes when digital gait analysis can no longer be performed [12, 13].

This exploratory cross-sectional study included patients affected by HSP with a broad range of disease characteristics and severities, and analyzed the convergent validity of digital tapping measures against a clinician-reported (SPRS), patient-focused (FARS-ADL), and performance outcome (9HPT) of HSP severity. This enabled validation in a large and representative HSP cohort and across key regulatory outcome domains, but also has several limitations. First, our study did not conduct longitudinal follow-ups or short-term re-assessments necessary to determine test–retest reliability and minimal detectable change thresholds. However, our preliminary within-task analysis (ICC between first and last repetition) indicated good to excellent test–retest reliability for many measures. In genetic ataxia cohorts, this within-task test–retest reliability has been robust in 2-week re-assessments, and eventually yielded minimal detectable change thresholds sufficient to detect longitudinal change [30, 43]. Second, our cohort included multiple genotypes and patients with simple and complex HSP. This cross-genotype approach aimed for generic validation in HSP, including ultra-rare HSP forms for which separate validation would never be possible. Nevertheless, validation in genotypically stratified cohorts is now required to characterize the metrics of our digital tapping measures for specific diseases, and thus trial scenarios. To control variable HSP phenotypes that could affect motor performance, we excluded one patient with unusual upper limb atrophy, and performed subgroup analyses in patients without upper limb pyramidal signs and signs of ataxia. Key findings of our study were robust in these subgroups, yet sensitivity for “pure” HSP remains to be confirmed in genotypically stratified cohorts with simple HSP. On the other hand, given that HSPs are multisystemic (often with concurrent neuropathy, ataxia, cognitive impairment, and/or extrapyramidal involvement) [6] and even overlapping with other disease entities (e.g., genetic ataxias or neuropathies) [44], validation of “pure” HSP will not be sufficient for future trial scenarios. Rather, our digital measures must be validated in genotypically stratified cohorts with overlapping yet clinically controlled motor phenotypes—for example, as currently ongoing in the spastic ataxias SPG7 and ARSACS [45]. Third, the subgroup of non-ambulatory patients in the severe disease stage included a relatively small number of participants (n = 6), which may limit the generalizability of the findings and conclusions about ceiling effects. Larger studies including more patients at severe HSP stages are thus required to validate, confirm, and extend our present results—in particular, because our digital assessment of limb movement would be particularly suitable for trials in non-ambulatory patients, in whom digital gait analysis is no longer possible. Fourth, although the SPRS, FASR-ADL, and 9HPT present different constructs and regulatory outcome domains, their correlations with digital measures should be interpreted in light of partial construct overlap. In particular, both the 9HPT and several digital finger tapping tasks are based on speed and dexterity. Future longitudinal validation in natural history studies comparing patients with HSP and controls will be required to determine to what extent digital measures reflect disease-specific progression versus more general motor performance characteristics. Lastly, correlations with the FARS-ADL are insufficient to understand the patient meaningfulness of our digital measures, a key requirement for regulatory outcome qualification [46]. For example, correlations with the FARS-ADL indicated that for foot tapping, measures of force control—but not speed or tap duration—captured functional impairment in mild HSP, while the strength of their discrimination from controls and their correlation with the SPRS was inverse. Implementation of patient-reported outcomes aligned with patient-meaningful aspects of health and concepts of interest [47, 48], and/or collection of qualitative patient input by interviews could help to better map the relevance of our digital tapping measures to patient experience [49].

Conclusion

This exploratory study demonstrates that digital motor assessment of rapid alternating limb movements can capture motor impairment in HSP across clinician-reported, patient-focused, and performance outcomes of disease severity, particularly also in the upper limb domain and in trial-relevant mild disease stages. Thus, after implementation in biomarker studies in Huntington’s disease [50, 51], and recent longitudinal validation in genetic ataxias [43], the respective digital tapping measures could also be promising digital motor outcomes for HSP—warranting further evaluation in early-stage patients and genotypically stratified HSP cohorts. Specifically, the next validation steps before trial adoption include validation of test–retest reliability to determine minimal detectable change thresholds, longitudinal validation to determine sensitivity to change, and correlation with and anchoring in patient-reported outcomes to determine patient meaningfulness and minimal clinically important difference. Some of the upper limb measures not requiring high-resolution capture of force trajectories could be device-agnostic and promising also for smartphone-based remote assessment—either for use in clinical trials or as mobile health application for use in clinical care [52]. For multi-center assessments, the availability of Q-Motor at more than 150 sites worldwide and its prior regulatory acceptance as a trial endpoint [9, 10, 53] would allow rapid implementation and evaluation as an exploratory endpoint in clinical trials.
