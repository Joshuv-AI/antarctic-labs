Generalized BChS Model with Group Interactions: Shift in the Critical Point and Mean-Field Ising Universality
Amit Pradhan

Abstract
We introduce a generalized version of the Biswas–Chatterjee–Sen (BChS) model Biswas with group interactions of size q q , extending the original pairwise interaction dynamics. Within a mean field framework, we derive an exact expression for the critical noise p c ​ ( q ) p_{c}(q) , showing that it increases monotonically with q q and approaches 1 / 2 1/2 in the large q q limit, consistent with a Gaussian approximation. Despite this shift in the phase boundary, the critical behavior remains unchanged across all q q : the order parameter scales as ( p c ​ ( q ) − p ) 1 / 2 (p_{c}(q)-p)^{1/2} and the relaxation timescale diverges as | p − p c ​ ( q ) | − 1 |p-p_{c}(q)|^{-1} , identical to the original BChS model Biswas . Finite size scaling of the Binder cumulant, order parameter, and its fluctuations confirm that the system belongs to the mean field Ising universality class for all q q . Our results demonstrate that higher-order interactions modify the location of the transition without altering its universality class.



I
Introduction

The study of collective behavior in social systems has attracted significant attention within the framework of statistical physics. In particular, opinion dynamics models aim to understand how individual interactions can lead to consensus or coexistence of different opinions. Several models have been proposed over the years, and comprehensive reviews of this field can be found in Refs.
Castellano
;
Galam
;
Sen
;
Perc
;
Jusup
;
Toscani
, including recent overviews of kinetic exchange approaches to socio-economic systems.

A widely studied class of models is based on kinetic exchange dynamics, where opinions evolve through pairwise interactions in which individuals partially adopt the opinions of others during interactions. This approach was introduced in the context of opinion formation by Lallouache et al.
Mehdi
. It was subsequently generalized to include individual conviction and influencing ability through two parameters
Parongama
. An exact solution of a discrete version of the model, exhibiting an active–absorbing phase transition and incorporating multi-agent interactions, was later obtained in Ref.
Soumyajyoti
. A detailed overview of the developments in kinetic exchange models is given in Ref.
Bikash
;
soumyajyoti
.

Building on this framework, the Biswas–Chatterjee–Sen (BChS) model was introduced in Ref.
Biswas
. This model exhibits a noise driven order–disorder phase transition, and its critical behavior has been analyzed within the same framework
Biswas
. More generally, it has been shown that a broad class of kinetic exchange opinion models with noise exhibit mean field Ising universality
Sudip
.

Subsequent works have explored various extensions of the BChS model, including studies in higher dimensions
Arnab
, and modifications of the dynamical rules such as the inclusion of additional opinion states and stochastic switching mechanisms
Kathakali
;
kathakali
. Further developments have examined dynamical features such as virtual walks and temporal evolution
Katha
;
Surajit
, as well as interactions between multiple groups
Suchecki
.

In addition, several studies have explored the behavior of the BChS model on complex networks and heterogeneous structures
Nuno
;
Raquel
;
David
;
Lima
;
Oliveira
;
Sousa
, highlighting the robustness of the phase transition and its universality properties under different interaction topologies. However, a systematic understanding of how the size of interacting groups affects the location of the phase transition and its universality class is still lacking.

However, most of these studies are based on pairwise interactions, where opinions are updated through interactions between two individuals at a time. In contrast, real social interactions often occur in groups, where multiple individuals interact simultaneously and influence each other collectively. Recent developments in the study of temporal and higher-order networks have emphasized the importance of such group interactions, showing that they can qualitatively change the collective dynamics
Holme
;
Iacopini
.

Motivated by these observations, we introduce a generalized version of the Biswas–Chatterjee–Sen (BChS) model in which interactions occur within groups of size
q
, rather than through pairwise exchanges. Within a mean field framework, we analytically derive the phase boundary separating ordered and disordered states and show that increasing the group size enhances the stability of the ordered phase, leading to a systematic increase of the critical noise

p_{c}(q)
. In the limit of large
q
, the critical point approaches
1/2
, consistent with a Gaussian approximation.

Despite the shift in the transition point, the critical behavior remains unchanged: the system continues to exhibit mean field Ising universality, indicating that higher-order interactions modify the location of the transition without altering its universality class.

II
Model and dynamics

We consider a generalized version of the Biswas–Chatterjee–Sen (BChS) model in which interactions extend beyond pairwise exchanges to involve a group of
q
neighbors. The parameter
q
controls the size of the interacting group and varies in the range
1\leq q\leq N-1
, where
N
is the system size.

In the limiting case
q=1
, the model reduces to the original BChS model introduced in Ref.
Biswas
. At the other extreme, the case
q=N-1
corresponds to the fully connected (mean field) limit, where each agent interacts with all other agents in the system.

Each agent
i
carries a discrete opinion
O_{i}(t)\in\{-1,0,+1\}
. At each time step, an agent
i
is selected at random, and a group of
q
neighbors is chosen randomly from the population. The agent experiences an effective social influence from this group given by

\eta_{i}=\text{sgn}\left(\mu_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{q}O_{k}\right).

(1)

Here
\sum_{k=1}^{q}O_{k}
represents the total opinion of the selected group, and
\eta_{i}
is the resulting net influence acting on agent
i
. The interaction parameter
\mu_{i}
determines the nature of the interaction and is defined as

\mu_{i}=\begin{cases}\;\;+1,&\text{with probability }1-p,\\[4.0pt]
-\,1,&\text{with probability }p,\end{cases}

(2)

where
p
is the disorder parameter. A positive value
\mu_{i}=+1
corresponds to a reinforcing interaction, where the agent tends to align with the group influence, while
\mu_{i}=-1
represents an opposing interaction, leading the agent to react against the group.

The opinion of the selected agent is updated according to

O_{i}(t+1)=\text{sgn}[O_{i}(t)+\eta_{i}].

(3)

III
Mean field theory and phase structure

III.1
Rate equations

In this subsection, we derive the mean field rate equations governing the evolution of the system.
Within the mean-field approximation, spatial correlations between agents are neglected, and the
q
neighbors of a given agent are assumed to be chosen randomly from the population.

The state of the system is characterized by the fractions
f_{+}
,
f_{-}
, and
f_{0}
, corresponding to opinions
+1
,
-1
, and
0
, respectively, with the normalization condition
f_{+}+f_{-}+f_{0}=1
.

Consider a randomly selected group of
q
agents. Let
n_{+}
,
n_{-}
, and
n_{0}
denote the number of agents in this group with opinion
+1
,
-1
, and
0
, respectively, such that

n_{+}+n_{-}+n_{0}=q.

(4)

Under the mean field assumption, the probability of observing a given configuration (
n_{+},n_{-},n_{0}
) is given by the multinomial distribution

\mathcal{P}(n_{+},n_{-},n_{0})=\frac{q!}{n_{+}!n_{-}!n_{0}!}f_{+}^{n_{+}}f_{-}^{n_{-}}f_{0}^{n_{0}}.

(5)

The total opinion of the group is

S=\sum_{k=1}^{q}O_{k}=n_{+}-n_{-}.

(6)

The effective social influence acting on the agent is
\eta=\text{sgn}(\mu S)
. Therefore, the probabilities of the three possible outcomes
\eta=+1,-1,0
can be written as

\begin{split}P(\eta=+1)=P_{+}=(1-p)P(S>0)+pP(S<0),\\
P(\eta=-1)=P_{-}=(1-p)P(S<0)+pP(S>0),\\
P(\eta=0)=P_{0}=P(S=0).\end{split}

(7)

Here,

\begin{split}P(S>0)=\sum_{n_{+}>n_{-}}\mathcal{P}(n_{+},n_{-},n_{0}),\\
P(S<0)=\sum_{n_{+}<n_{-}}\mathcal{P}(n_{+},n_{-},n_{0}),\\
P(S=0)=\sum_{n_{+}=n_{-}}\mathcal{P}(n_{+},n_{-},n_{0}).\end{split}

(8)

Using the update rule [Eq. (
3
)], one can enumerate all allowed transitions between the three opinion states. The transition rates are expressed in terms of
P_{+}
and
P_{-}
as follows :

\begin{split}\omega_{+1\to 0}=P_{-},\quad\omega_{+1\to-1}=0,\\
\omega_{-1\to 0}=P_{+},\quad\omega_{-1\to+1}=0,\\
\omega_{0\to+1}=P_{+},\quad\omega_{0\to-1}=P_{-}.\end{split}

(9)

Using the above transition rates, the time evolution of the fractions
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
can be written as

\begin{split}\frac{df_{+}}{dt}=-f_{+}P_{-}+f_{0}P_{+},\\
\frac{df_{-}}{dt}=-f_{-}P_{+}+f_{0}P_{-}.\end{split}

(10)

To simplify the analysis, it is convenient to express the dynamics in terms of the order parameter
O=f_{+}-f_{-}
and the activity
s=f_{+}+f_{-}
. After straightforward algebra, one obtains

\frac{dO}{dt}=\left(1-\frac{s}{2}\right)(P_{+}-P_{-})-\frac{O}{2}(P_{+}+P_{-}),

(11)

\frac{ds}{dt}=\left(1-\frac{3s}{2}\right)(P_{+}+P_{-})+\frac{O}{2}(P_{+}-P_{-}).

(12)

III.2
Fixed points and phase boundary

The stationary behavior of the system is governed by the fixed points of the coupled evolution equations for the order parameter
O
[Eq. (
11
)] and activity
s
[Eq.
12
)]. In this subsection, we first analyze the stability of the disordered fixed point and determine the critical line
p_{c}(q)
, and then examine the existence of symmetry broken ordered solutions.

Disordered fixed point and its stability :

The disordered fixed point can be obtained by setting
ds/dt=0
in Eq. (
12
) with
O=0
. Solving this equation, one finds
s^{*}=2/3
, which immediately implies
f_{+}^{*}=f_{-}^{*}=f_{0}^{*}=1/3
. Therefore the nontrivial disordered fixed point of the system is given by
(O^{*},s^{*})=(0,2/3)
.

To analyze the stability of this fixed point, we linearize the coupled evolution equations for
O
and
s
[Eq. (
11
),(
12
)] around
(O^{*},s^{*})
. This leads to the Jacobian matrix evaluated at the fixed point.

A straightforward calculation shows that the Jacobian matrix is diagonal at
(O^{*},s^{*})
, with eigenvalues
\lambda_{s}=-1,\lambda_{O}
, corresponding to perturbations along the
s
and
O
directions, respectively.

Since
\lambda_{s}<0
, deviations in the
s
direction always decay. Therefore the stability of the disordered fixed point is completely determined by the sign of
\lambda_{O}
, which is obtained by linearizing the evolution equation of
O
near this point.

To do that, we begin by computing
P_{+}-P_{-}
in the vicinity of
(O^{*},s^{*})=(0,2/3)
. starting from the definition used in Eq. (
7
) and (
8
) ,

\begin{split}P_{+}-P_{-}=(1-2p)\bigg[\sum_{n_{+}>n_{-}}\mathcal{P}(n_{+},n_{-},n_{0})\\
-\sum_{n_{+}<n_{-}}\mathcal{P}(n_{+},n_{-},n_{0})\bigg].\end{split}

(13)

We combine the two sums into a single expression

P_{+}-P_{-}=(1-2p)\sum_{n_{+},n_{-}}\text{sgn}(n_{+}-n_{-})\mathcal{P}(n_{+},n_{-},n_{0}).

(14)

Using
f_{+}=(s+O)/2
,
f_{-}=(s-O)/2
and
f_{0}=1-s
we write

f_{+}^{n_{+}}f_{-}^{n_{-}}=\left(\frac{s+O}{2}\right)^{n_{+}}\left(\frac{s-O}{2}\right)^{n_{-}}.

(15)

Expanding this expression for small
O
, we obtain

f_{+}^{n_{+}}f_{-}^{n_{-}}=\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^{n_{+}+n_{-}}\left[1+\frac{O}{s}(n_{+}-n_{-})+\mathcal{O}(O^{2})\right].

(16)

Substituting this into the expression for
P_{+}-P_{-}
, the zeroth order contribution cancels due to symmetry, and the leading contribution becomes linear in
O

\begin{split}P_{+}-P_{-}=\frac{(1-2p)O}{s}\sum_{n_{+},n_{-}}\text{sgn}(n_{+}-n_{-})(n_{+}-n_{-})\\
\frac{q!}{n_{+}!n_{-}!n_{0}!}\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^{n_{+}+n_{-}}(1-s)^{n_{0}}.\end{split}

(17)

Evaluating at the fixed point
s=2/3
,

P_{+}-P_{-}=\frac{3}{2}(1-2p)O\sum_{n_{+},n_{-}}\frac{q!}{n_{+}!n_{-}!n_{0}!}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{q}|n_{+}-n_{-}|.

(18)

We define

\chi_{q}=\frac{3}{2}\sum_{n_{+},n_{-}}\frac{q!}{n_{+}!n_{-}!n_{0}!}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{q}|n_{+}-n_{-}|,

(19)

so that

P_{+}-P_{-}=(1-2p)\chi_{q}O.

(20)

The quantity
\chi_{q}
characterizes the average response of a group of size
q
to a small perturbation in the order parameter.

We also use the identity

P_{+}+P_{-}=1-P_{0},

(21)

where

P_{0}=\sum_{n_{+}=n_{-}}\frac{q!}{n_{+}!n_{-}!n_{0}!}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{q},

(22)

is the probability that the total influence vanishes.

Substituting these results into the evolution equation of
O
[Eq. (
11
)] and evaluating at
s=2/3
, we obatin

\frac{dO}{dt}=\left[\frac{2}{3}(1-2p)\chi_{q}-\frac{1}{2}(1-P_{0})\right]O.

(23)

Thus near the disordered fixed point,

\frac{dO}{dt}=\lambda_{O}O,

(24)

where

\lambda_{O}=\frac{2}{3}(1-2p)\chi_{q}-\frac{1}{2}(1-P_{0}).

(25)

The stability of the disordered fixed point is determined by the sign of
\lambda_{O}
. If
\lambda_{O}<0
, both eigenvalues (
\lambda_{s}
and
\lambda_{O}
) are negative, and the disordered fixed point is stable. On the other hand, if
\lambda_{O}>0
, the fixed point becomes a saddle point, unstable along the
O
direction.

We will show later that this condition
\lambda_{O}>0
is precisely the regime where two nontrivial ordered fixed points emerge and become stable.

The order-disorder transition point is obtained from the marginal condition
\lambda_{O}=0
, which yields

p_{c}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{3}{4}\frac{(1-P_{0})}{\chi_{q}}\right].

(26)

where
\chi_{q}
and
P_{0}
can be found from Eq. (
19
) and (
22
) respectively.

Eq. (
26
) provided an explicit expression for the phase boundary
p_{c}(q)
, which constitutes the central analytical results of this work. We now turn to the existence of symmetry broken steady states characterized by a non zero order.

Ordered fixed points :

The ordered fixed points
(\pm O^{*},s^{*})
are obtained by setting the right hand sides of Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
) to zero with
O\neq 0
.

From Eq. (
11
), we obtain

O^{*}=2\left(1-\frac{s^{*}}{2}\right)\frac{P_{+}-P_{-}}{P_{+}+P_{-}}.

(27)

Substituting this into Eq. (
12
), the steady state condition for
s^{*}
becomes

\left(\frac{3s^{*}}{2}-1\right)(P_{+}+P_{-})^{2}=\left(1-\frac{s^{*}}{2}\right)(P_{+}-P_{-})^{2}.

(28)

For general
q
, one has
P_{+}+P_{-}=1-P_{0}
, while
P_{+}-P_{-}
depends on the group statistics. Solving Eqs. (
27
)-(
28
) self consistently determines the ordered state
(\pm O^{*},s^{*})
.

From these equations, one can eliminate
P_{+}-P_{-}
to obtain

({O^{*}})^{2}=4\left(1-\frac{s^{*}}{2}\right)\left(\frac{3s^{*}}{2}-1\right),

(29)

which implies that a physical ordered solution exists only when
s^{*}>2/3
. To connect this condition with the stability of the disordered fixed point, we use Eq. (
27
) to write

(1-2p)\chi_{q}=\frac{1-P_{0}}{2\left(1-\frac{s^{*}}{2}\right)}.

(30)

Substituting this into the expression of
\lambda_{O}
in Eq. (
25
), one obtains

\lambda_{O}=(1-P_{0})\frac{\left(\frac{3s^{*}}{2}-1\right)}{6\left(1-\frac{s^{*}}{2}\right)}.

(31)

Since
1-P_{0}>0
and
1-\frac{s^{*}}{2}>0
, it follows that

s^{*}>\frac{2}{3}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lambda_{O}>0,

(32)

i.e., the ordered state exists only when
p<p_{c}(q)
, where the disordered fixed point
(0,2/3)
becomes unstable (a saddle point).

For
q=1
, the transition probabilities simplify to

P_{+}-P_{-}=(1-2p)O,\quad P_{+}+P_{-}=s.

(33)

Using these in Eq. (
27
) and (
28
), one obtains

s^{*}=\frac{1-2p}{1-p},\quad(O^{*})^{2}=\frac{1-4p}{(1-p)^{2}}.

(34)

Thus, the existing condition of the ordered phase becomes

\lambda_{O}=\frac{1-4p}{3}>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad p<p_{c}=\frac{1}{4},

(35)

in agreement with the original model
Biswas
.

The general expression of the critical point obtained in Eq. (
26
) can now be verified for small values of
q
. For
q=1
, one finds
\chi_{1}=1
,
P_{0}=1/3
, which immediately gives
p_{c}(1)=1/4
, in agreement with the original model
Biswas
. Similarly, for
q=2
, using
\chi_{2}=4/3,P_{0}=1/3
, we obtain
p_{c}(2)=5/16
. The Binder cumulant curves for different system sizes also intersect at a value consistent with
p_{c}(2)=5/16
(see Fig.
1
), confirming the analytical prediction.

Extending this to higher group sizes, for
q=3
and
q=4
, using
P_{0}=7/27,19/81
and
\chi_{3}=5/3,\chi_{4}=52/27
, one obtains
p_{c}(3)=1/3\approx 0.33
and
p_{c}(4)=73/208\approx 0.351
.

Binder cumulant
U(p,N)
constructed from the moments of the order parameter
O
, as a function of
p
is plotted for different system sizes
N
with
q=2
. The curves intersect at a value consistent with
p_{c}(2)=5/16
, in agreement with the analytical prediction from Eq. (
26
).

These results clearly show that the critical point increases with
q
, indicating that larger interaction groups enhance the stability of the ordered phase. This behavior can be understood physically: increasing
q
effectively leads to an averaging over a larger number of interacting agents, thereby suppressing fluctuations and stabilizing the ordered state. This trend is also reflected in Fig.
2
, where the averaged order parameter
\langle O\rangle
is plotted as a function of
p
for different values of
q
, showing that the transition point shifts to higher values of
p
as
q

increases.

Ensemble averaged order parameter
\langle O\rangle
as a function of the noise parameter
p
is plotted for different group sizes
q
with system size
N=1024
. The vanishing of
\langle O\rangle
marks the transition point, which shifts to higher values of
p
as
q
increases, demonstrating that larger interaction group stabilize the ordered phase. For large
q
, the critical point approaches
p_{c}(q)\to 1/2
, and in particular, for
q=500
, we find
p_{c}(500)\approx 1/2
.

III.3
Large
\bm{q}
limit : Gaussian Approximation

For large values of
q
, the exact expressions of
\chi_{q}
[Eq. (
19
)] and
P_{0}
[Eq. (
22
)] involves sums over an exponentially large number of configurations and are therefore not convenient for analytical treatment. In this limit, a simpler description can be obtained using the central limit theorem.

We note that the relevant stochastic quantity is the total opinion of the group
S
, defined in Eq. (
6
), which is a sum of independent and identically distributed random variables taking values
+1,-1
and
0
with probabilities
f_{+},f_{-}
and
f_{0}
, respectively.

For large
q
,
S
approaches a Gaussian random variable with mean and variance given by

\langle S\rangle=qO,\quad\text{Var}(S)=qs.

(36)

Thus the distribution of
S
can be written as

P(S)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi qs}}\exp\left[-\frac{(S-qO)^{2}}{2qs}\right].

(37)

Using Eq. (
7
), one finds

P_{+}-P_{-}=(1-2p)[P(S>0)-P(S<0)].

(38)

Evaluating this expression using the Gaussian distribution and introducing the scaled variable
x=(S-qO)/\sqrt{qs}
, we obtain

P(S>0)-P(S<0)=\text{erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{q}{2s}}O\right).

(39)

Therefore,

P_{+}-P_{-}=(1-2p)\text{erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{q}{2s}}O\right).

(40)

To analyze the stability of the disordered fixed point
(O^{*},s^{*})=(0,2/3)
, we expand Eq. (
40
) for small values of
O
. Using the leading order behavior of the error function, we obtain

P_{+}-P_{-}\approx(1-2p)\sqrt{\frac{2q}{\pi s}}O=(1-2p)\chi_{q}O,

(41)

where the effective response function of the group

\chi_{q}=\sqrt{\frac{2q}{\pi s}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\chi_{q}=\sqrt{\frac{3q}{\pi}}\quad\text{at}\quad s^{*}=\frac{2}{3}.

(42)

Similarly the probability of a neutral group is

P_{0}=P(S=0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi qs}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad P_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi q}}\quad\text{at}\quad s^{*}=\frac{2}{3}.

(43)

Substituting these large
q
expressions into Eq. (
26
), we obtain the critical point for large
q
as

p_{c}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{3}{4}\frac{\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi q}}\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{3q}{\pi}}}\right].

(44)

Thus,

p_{c}(q)\to\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{as}\quad q\to\infty.

(45)

To validate the accuracy of the asymptotic expression in Eq. (
44
), we numerically compute the critical point
p_{c}(q)
using the exact mean field formulation in Eq. (
26
) and compare it with large
q
approximation. As shown in Fig.
3
, the two results are in excellent agreement for sufficiently large
q
, with an complete overlap observed for
q\gtrsim 10
. This demonstrates that the central limit (Gaussian) approximation provides an accurate description of the system behavior already at moderate values of
q
. The convergence of the two curves further confirms that
p_{c}(q)\to 1/2
in the large
q
limit.

Finally we emphasize that the expression in Eq. (
44
) has been derived within a central limit (Gaussian) approximation and is therefore valid only for large values of
q
. For small
q
, the discrete nature of the opinion variables becomes important, and deviations from the asymptotic form arise, as the Gaussian approximation no longer captures the underlying discrete fluctuations accurately.

critical noise
p_{c}(q)
is plotted as a function of group size
q
. Red line points correspond to the exact numerical evaluation based on Eq. (
26
), while black line points represent the asymptotic expression given by Eq. (
44
). The two datasets converge rapidly with increasing
q
, becoming indistinguishable for

, confirming the validity of the Gaussian approximation in the large

limit.

IV
Critical Behavior and Universality

To characterize the nature of the phase transition, we analyze the critical behavior of the model within the mean-field framework. In particular, we derive the scaling behavior of the order parameter near the critical point
p=p_{c}(q)
to extract the associated critical exponent
\beta
. We also examine the relaxation dynamics in the vicinity of the transition to determine the divergence of the characteristic timescale and obtain the exponent
\nu z
. These results allow us to assess the universality class of the model and compare it with that of the
original BChS dynamics.

IV.1
Order parameter scaling near
p_{c}(q)

To determine the behavior of the order parameter near the transition, we analyze the ordered solution in the vicinity of the critical point
p\to p_{c}(q)
. Close to the transition, the ordered fixed point satisfies

s^{*}=\frac{2}{3}+\delta,\quad\delta\ll 1.

(46)

Using Eq. (
29
), and expanding for small
\delta
, we obtain to leading order

(O^{*})^{2}\approx 4\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad O^{*}\sim\sqrt{\delta}.

(47)

To relate
\delta
with the distance from criticality, we use Eq. (
30
). Expanding around
s^{*}=2/3
and comparing with its value at the critical point, one finds that

\delta=\frac{32}{9}\frac{\chi_{q}}{1-P_{0}}(p_{c}-p)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta\propto(p_{c}-p).

(48)

Combining the above results, we obtain the scaling behavior of the order parameter near the transition

O^{*}\sim(p_{c}-p)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(49)

independent of
q
, indicating mean field Ising criticality, identical to that of the original BChS model
Biswas
.

IV.2
Divergence of the relaxation timescale

To quantify how fast the system returns to the disordered fixed point, we consider a small deviation of the order parameter from
O^{*}=0
, i.e.,

O(t)=\epsilon(t),\quad\epsilon\ll 1.

(50)

From Eq. (
24
), one obtains

\frac{d\epsilon}{dt}=\lambda_{O}\epsilon,

(51)

where
\lambda_{O}<0
in the disordered phase. The solution is
\epsilon(t)=\epsilon(0)e^{\lambda_{O}t}
, which defines the relaxation timescale as

\tau=\frac{1}{|\lambda_{O}|},

(52)

where
\lambda_{O}
is defined in Eq. (
25
).

Near the critical point
p_{c}(q)
, expanding
\lambda_{O}
to leading order in
p-p_{c}(q)
yields

\lambda_{O}\approx-\frac{4}{3}\chi_{q}(p-p_{c}(q))\sim(p-p_{c}(q)).

(53)

As a result, the relaxation timescale diverges as

\tau\sim|p-p_{c}(q)|^{-1}.

(54)

Thus the associated critical exponent is
\nu z=1
, independent of
q
, indicating mean field Ising universality of the dynamical scaling, consistent with the behavior of the original BChS model
Biswas
. These analytical predictions for the critical exponents will be tested numerically using finite-size scaling in Sec. V.

V
Numerical estimation of critical exponents using Finite-size scaling

To verify the analytical predictions for the critical behavior, we perform a finite-size scaling (FSS) analysis of the model. In Sec. IV, we obtained the mean-field critical exponents
\beta=1/2
and
\nu z=1
. Here, we independently estimate the static critical exponents
\beta
,
\nu
, and
\gamma
from numerical simulations using standard FSS techniques.

The Binder cumulant is defined as

U(p,N)=1-\frac{\langle O^{4}\rangle}{3\langle O^{2}\rangle^{2}},

(55)

and the susceptibility (fluctuation of the order parameter) as

\chi_{O}=N\left(\langle O^{2}\rangle-\langle O\rangle^{2}\right),

(56)

where
O
denotes the order parameter and
\langle.\rangle
represents averaging over different realizations.

Near the critical point
p_{c}(q)
, these quantities are expected to obey the standard FSS forms :

\begin{split}U(p,N)=\mathcal{F}\left[(p-p_{c}(q))N^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\right],\\
\chi_{O}(p,N)=N^{\frac{\gamma}{\nu}}\mathcal{G}\left[(p-p_{c}(q))N^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\right],\\
\langle O\rangle(p,N)=N^{-\frac{\beta}{\nu}}\mathcal{H}\left[(p-p_{c}(q))N^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\right].\end{split}

(57)

4
shows the Binder cumulant
U(p,N)
as a function of
p
for different system sizes for
q=10
. The curves intersect at
p_{c}(10)\approx 0.396
, and a good data collapse is obtained using
1/\nu=1/2
, indicating
\nu=2
.

In Fig.
5
, we present the steady state order parameter
\langle O\rangle
. The data collapse is achieved for
\beta/\nu=1/4
and
1/\nu=1/2
, yielding
\beta=1/2
.

Finally, Fig.
6
shows the susceptibility
\chi_{O}
, which collapses for
\gamma/\nu=1/2
and
1/\nu=1/2
, giving
\gamma=1
.

These results confirm that the critical exponents
\beta=1/2,\nu=2,
and
\gamma=1
are independent of
q
and consistent with the mean field Ising universality class. Although the plots are shown here for
q=10
, identical scaling is observed for other values of
q
.

We conclude with the remark that the estimated values of the critical exponents
\beta
and
\gamma
are consistent with the mean field Ising values. Interpreting
\nu
as
\nu^{\prime}d
, where
d
is the effective dimension in this long range interacting system, and taking
d=4
as in the original model
Biswas
and in small world like networks
Hong
;
Chatterjee
, we obtain
\nu=\nu^{\prime}d=2
(with
\nu^{\prime}=1/2
), consistent with the mean field Ising universality class.

VI
Summary and Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced and analyzed a generalized version of the Biswas–Chatterjee–Sen (BChS) model incorporating group interactions of size
q
. Within a mean field framework, we derived the phase structure of the model and obtained an explicit expression for the critical point
p_{c}(q)
, demonstrating its systematic dependence on the group size. In particular, we showed that
p_{c}(q)\to 1/2
in the large
q
limit, consistent with a central limit (Gaussian) approximation.

We further investigated the critical behavior of the model and found that the order parameter exhibits mean field scaling with exponent
\beta=1/2
, while the relaxation dynamics is characterized by
\nu z=1
. These analytical predictions were tested through numerical simulations using finite-size scaling. From the scaling of the Binder cumulant, order parameter, and susceptibility, we independently estimated the critical exponents
\beta
,
\nu
, and
\gamma
, and found them to be consistent with the mean field Ising universality class, as observed in the original model
Biswas
.

Taken together, these results show that although increasing the interaction group size shifts the location of the phase transition, it does not alter the underlying scaling behavior of the system. These findings highlight that changes in interaction range or structure do not necessarily lead to new critical behavior in kinetic exchange models.
