Use of ultrasound for early detection of ICU patients at risk of acquiring swallowing disorders: EIDAR, a prospective diagnostic study protocol
Authors: Jose Arturo Pineda, Carlos Díaz, Céline Plasse, Juan Manuel Ruiz de la Fuente, Christian Duvillard, Alexis Bozorg-Grayeli, Christelle Pennecot, Marie Labruyère, Pascal Andreu, Jean-Baptiste Roudaut, Alicia Taha, Adrien Morariu-Patrichi, Marine Jacquier, Amadou-Khalilou Sow, Isabelle Fournel, Jean-Pierre Quenot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostextubation swallowing disorders (SD) are common in the intensive care unit (ICU) and are associated with severe complications, including aspiration pneumonia, a three-fold increase in reintubation risk and higher mortality. While fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) and videofluoroscopy are gold standards for diagnosis, they are often impractical or impossible to perform on intubated patients. The use of ultrasound offers a non-invasive, bedside alternative to evaluate the musculoskeletal structures involved in swallowing. The Echographie Identifier les troubles de Déglutition Acquis en Réanimation (EIDAR) study aims to evaluate the diagnostic performance of pre-extubation ultrasound in identifying patients at risk of SD following mechanical ventilation.Methods and analysisThis prospective, monocentric diagnostic study conducted at the Dijon University Hospital ICU will include 100 adult patients ventilated for ≥48 hours. The primary outcome is the presence of SD, defined as a Penetration-Aspiration Scale score >2 during a FEES procedure performed 3 to 24 hours postextubation and independently assessed by an otolaryngologist blinded to index test results. Pre-extubation cervical ultrasound (Index Test) will be performed within 3 hours prior to extubation and measure hyoid bone ascension (primary variable of interest), geniohyoid muscle surface area and digastric muscle cross-sectional area. The diagnostic performance of cervical ultrasonographic parameters will be assessed using their discriminative capacity via a receiver operating characteristic curve. The feasibility of the ultrasound procedure in a critical care setting will also be assessed.Ethics and disseminationThe study received a favourable opinion from the independent ethics committee CPP Ouest III and is registered with the French health authority ANSM (national agency on safety in medicine and health products). It is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Participants or their proxies provide free and informed oral consent. Results will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed medical journals and presented at international conferences.Trial registration numbersRCB 2023-A00461-44 and NCT05922085

Background

Swallowing disorders (SD) or dysphagia correspond to difficulties in swallowing, specifically the transfer of food from the oral cavity to the stomach. A 2010 literature review estimated the incidence rate of SD after extubation to be between 3% and 62%.1 This difference in results is explained by the great heterogeneity of the studies and their design. In a more recent study involving 933 intensive care unit (ICU) patients,2 systematic screening for SD after extubation showed an incidence of 12.4% with a median intubation duration of 0.7 days.

The various causes of SD are known: neurological disorders, structural damage, drug-induced or toxic effects, presbyphagia (age-related swallowing changes) or phagophobia (fear of swallowing).3 Conversely, the aetiology of SD after extubation appears less clear and is considered multifactorial.4

Concerning risk factors for SD, age,58 tube feeding9 and neurological pathologies911 have been suggested, but the main risk factor for SD after extubation is the duration of intubation and mechanical ventilation (MV). Indeed, these disorders appear more pronounced after MV of 48 hours or more,9 12 and patients with SD have a mean MV duration increased by between 2 and 6 days, depending on the pathology5 9 11 13 14 with a 25% increased risk of SD per day of intubation.15 Regarding age, the incidence of SD after extubation in older patients is 52% compared with 36% in the control group in a cohort study that consecutively included 84 patients, with delayed resolution of SD in older patients.16

These SDs have a significant impact on patients as they are associated with a threefold higher risk of aspiration pneumonia.13 They are also associated with a prolonged length of stay in the ICU and a prolonged hospital stay of 4.32 days,11 as well as an increased mortality rate,17 with the risk of death for dysphagic patients multiplied by 2.7 at 28 days of hospitalisation and by 2.9 at 90 days of hospitalisation.2 In the long term, several studies show a prevalence of malnutrition superior to 75% in dysphagic patients compared with patients without SD18 and a decreased quality of life, for example, with a 23.6% increase in the sensation of fatigue and a 36.7% greater fear of choking or contracting pneumonia.19

In the ICU, SDs are also critically important. The presence of moderate to severe dysphagia (assessed by a score between 1 and 4 on the Dysphagia Outcome and Severity Scale)20 is associated with a 38% increased risk of reintubation.13 This reintubation occurs in more than half of cases less than 24 hours after extubation, and it is even earlier if caused by aspiration or excessive pulmonary secretions (21 hours vs 31 hours).21

In patients who have suffered a stroke, the early detection of SD is associated with a 68% reduction in the relative risk of aspiration pneumonia,22 and therefore, a reduction in complications (hospitalisation, drug consumption, etc). This suggests that a rapid and reliable assessment of SD in at-risk patients, particularly intubated patients, is necessary and should be more systematised. In the ICU, it is common practice to assess SD at least 24 hours after extubation. An earlier assessment would be relevant to allow for the rapid implementation of preventative measures.

Currently, videofluoroscopy (VFSS) and fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) are considered the gold standard examinations for SD assessment.2325 The FEES procedure has shown results similar to those of VFSS in the assessment of SD using the Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS),26 a scale for evaluating SD originally created and validated for VFSS.27 However, these examinations are difficult to perform on an intubated and ventilated patient. The difficulty of moving the patient, sometimes with unstable haemodynamics, makes performing FEES extremely complicated and VFSS impossible. Furthermore, VFSS involves radiation, and the FEES procedure remains a relatively invasive and slightly painful examination for the patient.

While clinical assessment of SDs has been widely described in the literature, no assessment has demonstrated sufficient reliability, often having a very low sensitivity.28 Tests such as the Functional Oral Intake Scale29 or the Gugging Swallowing Screen30 have been created and validated but primarily concern post-stroke patients with a neurological context different from the majority of patients hospitalised in the ICU.

In the ICU and before extubation, no consensus exists regarding the assessment of SD in intubated patients. Ultrasonographic examination of swallowing in intubated patients may be a solution to improve its assessment. Although ultrasound in the assessment of SD has been the subject of numerous studies,31 it has never been evaluated in intubated patients in the ICU. It allows for a qualitative and quantitative assessment of the structures involved in swallowing, particularly in mobility, the volumes of the different swallowing structures or the swallowing kinematics.

There are no real medical contraindications to ultrasound, as ultrasound waves, unlike the X-rays used in standard radiography, are harmless. Only a few hazards may hinder the proper execution of the examination, such as an agitated or an obese patient.32

Several parameters measurable by ultrasound appear in the literature in connection with SD. The most common is the measurement of hyoid bone ascension. This ascension is concomitant with laryngeal ascension during the pharyngeal phase. It causes the epiglottis to tip backwards, which, by folding over the laryngeal opening, protects the airways. The measurement of hyoid bone displacement has shown good intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) greater than 0.8. Its ICC compared with VFSS is also greater than 0.8.33 The difference in length of the geniohyoid (GH) muscle (due to its attachments to the mandible and the hyoid bone) at rest and during swallowing also allows this ascension to be quantified and has shown excellent intraobserver reproducibility (ICC of 0.94) and moderate interobserver reproducibility (ICC of 0.68).34

For the digastric muscle, its ultrasonographic measurement is very close to its measurement by MRI, which remains the gold standard examination, with a strong correlation between these two measurements (r>0.77).35 Its intraobserver reproducibility is excellent (ICC of 0.91),36 and its interobserver reproducibility seems good (ICC of 0.84),37 although this study was conducted on a small number of individuals. A decrease in its resting cross-sectional area (79.3 mm2 vs 70.4 mm2) is correlated with SD in older people.38

Furthermore, there is a growing interest in ultrasonographic muscle assessment in the ICU. One of the most studied muscles in the ICU is the quadriceps femoris (QF). Different studies have assessed muscle loss during the ICU stay, quantifying the decrease in QF thickness with the ultrasound scanner.39 40 Muscle atrophy occurs rapidly with a 30% reduction in the thickness of the vastus intermedius (VI), the thickness of the rectus femoris (RF) and its cross-sectional area after 10 days of ICU admission.40 The measurement of QF thickness (RF+VI) has shown excellent interobserver reproducibility in critical care units (ICC >0.9), regardless of the observer’s experience.41 42

Despite these numerous studies, no study has evaluated the application of ultrasound as a method for assessing SD in the ICU. The interest of this project is to propose a new tool for detecting SD, adapted to the intubated ICU population, usable routinely at the patient’s bedside unlike VFSS and FEES, and with better reliability than routine clinical examinations. We hypothesise that an ultrasonographic assessment of hyoid bone mobility before extubation could be a valuable parameter for early identification of patients at risk of presenting with SD after extubation.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a prospective diagnostic study conducted at the Dijon University Hospital aiming to estimate the diagnostic performance of various ultrasonographic parameters, performed within 3 hours prior to extubation, for the purpose of early identification of patients at risk of SD after extubation. Sample size

In the absence of reference data in the literature, we hypothesise that the ultrasonographic assessment of hyoid bone elevation will have a discriminative capacity of 75% for identifying patients at risk of SD after extubation. Considering this area under the curve of 75% (corresponding to the minimum threshold recommended for the clinical use of a biomarker,43 an alpha risk of 5% and a precision of 20%), 92 analysable patients are required. To account for non-analysable patients, 100 patients will be included.

Procedures

Screening, informed consent (see supplementary file) and enrolment

Eligible patients will be identified daily by the physiotherapist in the department, who will be informed by the medical team of extubations planned within the next 24 hours online supplemental file 1. An anonymous register of eligible, non-included patients will be maintained by the department’s research team to verify the representativeness of the included patients compared with the overall population of patients admitted to the ICU. If the patient fulfils the inclusion and non-inclusion criteria, the physiotherapist will inform them or their trusted person about the study and present it to them. If the patient is able to consent, their free, informed, and oral consent will be obtained by the physiotherapist 3 to 24 hours prior to extubation. If the patient is unable to consent and is accompanied by their trusted person (or next of kin), the trusted person may provide their consent for the patient’s participation in the study. The patient’s oral consent (continuation consent) will be sought and obtained as soon as their condition allows.

Pre-extubation assessment: H–3 to H0 (appendix 2)

This visit will take place in the patient’s room, within 3 hours prior to extubation (H0) online supplemental appendix 2. It will be performed by the departmental physiotherapist. A member of the departmental care team will be present to time the ultrasonographic examination. To ensure the integrity of the blinding process, the physiotherapist remains blinded to the results of the gold standard (FEES), performed 3 to 24 hours later. Measurement bias is mitigated by this temporal separation and the use of objective, quantitative anatomical landmarks. Furthermore, measurements are standardised through prerequisite operator training to ensure consistency.

Once the ultrasonographic assessment is complete, the physiotherapist will proceed to evaluate the patient’s global muscle strength using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale.46 These results, once entered into the e-CRF, will not be accessible to the intensivists in the department.

Extubation (H0)

The patient’s extubation will be performed in accordance with the recommendations of the relevant learnt societies (Société française d’anesthésie et de réanimation) which have been incorporated into a departmental protocol. In the event of acute respiratory failure following extubation, FEES will be contraindicated. Should a patient be reintubated before FEES can be performed, their follow-up will be prolonged, and a new ultrasonographic examination must take place prior to the subsequent extubation.

Postextubation assessment: H+3 to H+24 (appendix 3)

SD will be assessed using FEES within 3 to 24 hours following extubation online supplemental appendix 3. An Ambu single-use ‘slim’ fibreoptic endoscope, with a length of 300 mm, a distal tip diameter of 3.5 mm, a 130° viewing angle, will be used and connected to the Ambu aView 2 Advance Full HD screen. For this examination, the patient’s bed will be in a chair-bed or seated position with a slight cervical spine flexion to protect the airways. The patient will not be anaesthetised to avoid disturbing the sensitivity of the aerodigestive tract.47 The food required for the assessment will be prepared in advance by the nursing team and mixed with Methylene Blue for better visualisation during the examination.

Phase 1: Initial visual assessment

Initially, the endoscope will be introduced through one of the patient’s nostrils. Observation will then proceed by placing the tip of the endoscope in the nasopharynx and then at the inferior border of the soft palate. The doctor may first visualise anatomical or structural abnormalities suggesting SD (eg, vocal cord paralysis or lesion, significant localised oedema, important salivary stasis).48 The motility of the soft palate, pharynx, and larynx during a dry swallow may also be assessed. The doctor will observe and record whether SDs are present, indicated, for example, by a pharynx that leaves the larynx ‘too clearly visible’ during the cycle and/or salivary residue on the walls of the larynx.47 The possibility of recording the examination during the FEES test will allow for the evaluation of the concordance between the parameters assessed by the otolaryngologist and the intensivist.

Phase 2: Swallowing assessment

Next, the assessment will be conducted based on 3 teaspoons of a thick liquid (such as puréed fruit/veg) administered by the physiotherapist. Each swallow of each bolus will be assessed.

If the PAS score for the thick liquid is ≤2, the evaluation will continue with 3 teaspoons of still water, and the PAS score associated with this consistency will be recorded. See figure 2 for the procedures relevant to the swallowing assessment.

The result of the evaluation and any potential Serious Adverse Events will be entered into the electronic case report form (e-CRF) by the intensivist. The video of the examination will be recorded in its entirety on the screen’s memory and exported at the end of the examination for the evaluation of concordance between the otolaryngologist and the intensivist. The swallowing assessment is to be performed by the intensivist and the results evaluated independently by the otolaryngologist, both blinded from the ultrasonographic assessment. The study concludes at the end of the patient’s stay in the ICU.

Data management

The data will be entered into an e-CRF specifically developed for this study using a Clinical Data Management System (CDMS–CleanWeb). All data will be captured anonymously in the CleanWeb secured and dedicated database, consistent with the recommendations of the MR01 reference method of the CNIL (Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés). The names and addresses of participants will never be recorded in an unencrypted form. Only the first letter of the surname and the first letter of the given name will be recorded followed by a serial inclusion number. Using automatic checks, in cases with missing or inconsistent data, requests for correction will be sent to the participating centre via the CDMS. If corrections are necessary, they will be made directly using the CDMS. At the end of the study, a blind review of the data will be performed and may result in additional queries. All queries should be resolved before the final database closure.

Statistical analysis

The evaluation of the diagnostic performance of the ultrasonographic criteria will include all patients who meet the inclusion criteria, have no non-inclusion criteria, and for whom both examinations (ultrasound and FEES) have been performed and are interpretable.

The evaluation of the relationship between SDs and global muscle strength/QF muscle mass will include all patients who meet the inclusion criteria, have no non-inclusion criteria, have an interpretable FEES result, and for whom the results of the MRC scale and the quadriceps muscle ultrasound are available.

The evaluation of the feasibility of performing the ultrasound in ICU patients before extubation will include all eligible patients who have undergone the ultrasound, whether or not they also underwent endoscopy.

A descriptive analysis of the patient characteristics and their ICU stay will be performed and reported as percentages for qualitative variables, and as mean with its SD or median with its IQR depending on the distribution.

The diagnostic performance of the ultrasonographic parameters before extubation for the early detection of SD (globally, mild and severe) will be assessed by estimating the discriminative capacity via Receiver Operating Characteristic curves, which will be compared using DeLong’s test. The threshold providing the best compromise between sensitivity and specificity will be determined, prioritising sensitivity. A list of other possible thresholds for which sensitivity is good and specificity is acceptable will be presented. For all these thresholds, we will present indicators such as sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values. To evaluate the added value of combining multiple variables, a multivariable model will be developed. Its performance will be assessed and compared with individual predictors. In addition, Decision Curve Analysis will then be performed to evaluate the clinical utility. Finally, to account for potential physiological variations postextubation, a sensitivity analysis will be performed to assess the robustness of the diagnostic results relative to the time elapsed between extubation and the FEES assessment.

Muscle strength, quadriceps ultrasonographic measurements and cough strength will be described based on the presence or absence of SD as mean and standard deviation (or median and IQR depending on the data distribution) and compared between groups using a Student’s t-test or non-parametric tests depending on the conditions of application. The concordance between the parameters evaluated during FEES will be assessed using a kappa coefficient for qualitative variables and an ICC for quantitative variables. The following analyses will be performed concerning the feasibility of the ultrasound:

The proportion of ultrasounds performed (number of ultrasounds performed/number of eligible extubated patients) will be presented with its 95% CI.

Discussion: Methodological strengths and limitations

The EIDAR study design prioritises clinical feasibility and real-world applicability in the ICU environment while maintaining high standards of internal validity. Methodological limitations include the absence of a second observer for ultrasonographic reproducibility; however, this is addressed through a highly standardised acquisition protocol using fixed anatomical landmarks and prerequisite training.

Furthermore, while the 3–24-hour window for the reference standard (FEES) introduces potential physiological heterogeneity, the robustness of the results will be formally evaluated via sensitivity analyses. Finally, potential operator bias is mitigated by the strictly quantitative nature of the measurements and the blinding of the physiotherapist to the subsequent FEES results. This combination of pragmatic inclusion and rigorous analysis ensures that the findings will be both robust and applicable to routine clinical practice.

Ethics and dissemination

The protocol received a favourable opinion from the independent review committee CPP Ouest III on 04/05/2023, approval number RCB 2023-A00461-44. The planning and conduct of this study are governed by French and European laws (Law No. 2012-300 as amended 16/6/2016). The study will be conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the recommendations of the Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

In accordance with Article L1121-1 of the French Public Health Code (Code de la Santé Publique), this study constitutes category 2 research involving the human person, on the grounds that it is an interventional research study involving only minimal risks and constraints, the list of which is defined by order of the Minister of Health. As a category 2 research intervention, an external trial monitoring committee is not mandatory per French law.

Informed consent requirements are discussed in the Procedures section. The computer file used to conduct this research will be subject to a commitment to comply with the CNIL, pursuant to the French Data Protection Act (Law No. 78-17 of 6/1/1978) and the General Data Protection Regulation.

Throughout the duration of the research and at its conclusion, the data collected on the individuals participating in the research and transmitted to the Sponsor by the investigators (or any other specialised personnel) will be anonymised. Under no circumstances will the clear names of the concerned individuals or their contact details be displayed. The correspondence list between the inclusion number and nominative information, which will not be computerised, will be kept securely in the department participating in the study and destroyed at the end of the study.

The final study report will be written in collaboration with the study methodologists. The results of the study, whatever they may be, will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.
