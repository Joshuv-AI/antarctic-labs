Context

Vital signs monitoring is an important component of clinical practice in acute care settings. In the UK, the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) is the system recommended for use.1 The NEWS2 is calculated based on six vital signs and physiological parameters, namely: respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, level of consciousness and temperature.1 Evidence suggests that using the Early Warning Scores correlates positively to reduced mortality and serious adverse events.2 However, nurses juggle competing demands that affect their adherence to the recommended vital signs monitoring frequency per established protocol.3 This study aimed to explore how nursing teams carry out vital sign observations while conceptualising which types of work deprioritise their performance of this task.

Methods

The authors performed a non-participant observational study with qualitative content analysis of the observational data. The study was conducted in 16 randomly selected adult acute care wards across four hospitals in southern England. The authors adapted a validated tool, the Quality of Interactions tool4 for data collection. Data collection comprised four 2-hour daytime sessions per ward.

Findings

The results from the study indicated eight categories of reasons why vital sign observations are delayed and interrupted: bundled care, proximity-related activities, collaborative care, patient inaccessible or unavailable, requests for and responses to time-sensitive activities, limited context available, fixed care and lower staff availability. From these categories, four temporal status types were created and conceptualised, namely: fixed routines, flexible care, time-critical activities and continuous organisational work. It was suggested that vital signs assessments were treated as flexible care that is fitted around fixed care, bundled with flexible care and delayed in favour of time-critical activities and continuous organisational work.

Commentary

This study focused on the conceptualisation of the reasons for interruptions and delays to vital signs observations, as experienced by dayshift nurses working in acute inpatient care settings.5 The authors used a robust qualitative analysis methodology to analyse the data from the direct observation made during the study. Findings from the study provide a deeper understanding of missed vital signs observations within the lens of temporal status, as it was determined that nurses rank vital signs monitoring lower on the scale of priority. This finding relates to the concept of time scarcity discussed by Jones,6 a consequence of which is unfinished nursing care because certain tasks are prioritised over others. To mitigate this, nurses improve their time management skills by learning to complete vital sign monitoring with as many tasks as possible within a set time duration, a phenomenon that the authors conceptualised as bundled care.6 While bundling of care may lead to improved time efficiency, it sets vital sign monitoring as a form of ‘flexible care’ which could lead to non-adherence to prescribed protocols. The perception of vital sign monitoring as a form of flexible care could be a precursor to its lower prioritisation regardless of its clinical priority. Neglect of vital sign monitoring poses a significant risk to the clinician and the patient. The proposal by the authors on ‘planning vital signs monitoring off peak care periods’ (eg, early morning and late evenings) could contribute to protocol adherence, but the unpredictable nature of the hospital environment may limit the viability of this approach.
