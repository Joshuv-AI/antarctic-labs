Cross-frequency coordination in a hippocampo–cortical circuit during probabilistic reversal learning
Authors: Alejandro Aguilera, Nelson Espinosa, Mauricio Caneo, Guillermo Lazcano, Ariel Lara-Vasquez, Pablo Fuentealba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Learning under uncertainty requires detecting latent changes in environmental contingencies and flexibly adjusting choice strategy. To examine hippocampo–cortical circuit dynamics during probabilistic choice behavior, we trained rats on a two-armed bandit task with uncued reward reversals while recording local field potentials from dorsal hippocampus (CA1d), lateral entorhinal cortex (LEC), and prefrontal cortex (PFC). Performance improved across sessions, shifting from outcome-reactive exploration to exploitation, quantified using a model-free index. Mixed-effects modeling identified session-averaged cortical synchrony in theta and fast-gamma bands as a negative performance marker. Theta and gamma oscillations were coordinated, with CA1d theta coupled with fast-gamma in PFC, and modulated slow- and fast-gamma bursts in LEC, selectively increasing at goal-reaching. Neuronal spiking showed pathway-specific phase-locking, with theta-dependent spike timing across hippocampo-cortical pairs and reciprocal slow-gamma phase-locking most evident between CA1d and LEC. Distributed spiking was modulated during goal approach, with sustained peri-goal ramping in PFC putative single units, whereas hippocampal and entorhinal populations showed transient suppression around nosepoke onset. These results identify a session-averaged LEC–PFC connectivity marker of task performance and dissociate it from hippocampal theta mechanisms organizing cortical gamma bursts and spike timing during peri-goal processing, providing a circuit-level framework for adaptive decision-making under uncertainty.

Introduction

Effective decision-making under uncertainty requires the ability to detect hidden changes in the environment and update behavioral strategies accordingly1,2. This challenge is exemplified by probabilistic reversal learning tasks, such as the two-armed bandit paradigm3,4, in which agents must continuously arbitrate between exploring alternative options and exploiting known rewards as reward contingencies shift unpredictably5,6. Resolving this exploration–exploitation tradeoff is central to flexible behavior, and uncovering the circuit-level mechanisms that support it remains a major subject in systems neuroscience7–9.

Converging evidence implicates a distributed hippocampo–cortical network, centered on the prefrontal cortex (PFC), in guiding adaptive strategy selection10–12. Within this circuit, the dorsal CA1 (CA1d) region of the hippocampus, the lateral entorhinal cortex (LEC), and medial PFC form an interconnected circuit that integrates contextual, mnemonic, and executive information to guide decision-making. Indeed, PFC maintains internal representations of latent task states and supports behavioral regulation under uncertainty13–15. CA1d provides contextual information and supports latent state inference, particularly when external cues are ambiguous16,17. LEC, in turn, encodes local option identity and recent outcome history, delivering structured content to both hippocampus and cortex18–20. These regions are anatomically linked through bidirectional projections, forming a closed-loop anatomical substrate for iterative learning and credit assignment21–26.

A prevailing hypothesis is that frequency-specific oscillations coordinate communication within hippocampo–cortical circuits, with cross-frequency interactions providing a particularly informative framework for studying flexible learning because they capture hierarchical coordination across temporal and spatial scales27,28. While power and phase synchrony describe activity within a single frequency band, cross-frequency coupling reflects how slower rhythms organize faster population activity that carries local computations27,29. In this framework, slow oscillations such as theta waves (4–10 Hz) regulate the timing of gamma rhythms (30–100 Hz) and neuronal firing, coordinating information exchange between distributed regions while preserving local processing dynamics30. Theta rhythms align excitability and structure interregional communication, whereas gamma rhythms support local neuronal processing and population-level computation31,32. Accordingly, cross-frequency interactions, in particular theta-phase/gamma-amplitude coupling, provide a mechanism through which large-scale network states dynamically modulate local information processing depending on task demands. This hierarchical organization is especially relevant for learning under uncertainty, where circuits must integrate contextual information, recent outcomes, and action policies across multiple timescales33,34, offering a mechanistic readout of how distributed networks route information during adaptive decision making. Although prior work has demonstrated increased theta synchrony between hippocampus and prefrontal cortex during working memory, spatial navigation, and memory-guided decision-making35–37, it remains unclear how these cross-frequency dynamics specifically support learning in uncertain environments.

Here, we address this question by recording local field potentials and unit activity simultaneously from CA1d, LEC, and PFC as rats learned a self-paced two-armed bandit task with uncued reversals. We quantified strategy at the session level using a model-free index derived from simple behavioral patterns following wins and losses, and used linear mixed models to identify neurophysiological metrics associated with task performance and session progression38,39.

We identified circuit dynamics underlying decision-making and strategy adaptation. Across recording sessions, behavior shifted from outcome-reactive exploration to an exploitation-biased strategy, which was linked to changes in cortico-cortical connectivity, particularly LEC–PFC theta–gamma coupling. Moreover, hippocampal theta oscillations, particularly in CA1d, structured cortical gamma bursts and spike timing across the circuit, supporting the organization of temporal windows for processing. These results provide insights into how hippocampo–cortical circuits dynamically reconfigure to support adaptive decision-making under uncertainty. This framework offers insight into how distributed systems communicate to resolve uncertainty and adapt behavior, and helps to explain the functional role of theta–gamma interactions in mesoscale coordination.

Results

Session-dependent strategy shifts in a probabilistic reversal learning task

We trained rats on a two-armed bandit task, a commonly used probabilistic serial reversal learning task5,6, in which animals made self-directed binary choices to maximize reward collection across trials (Fig. 1A). Within each block (27–30 trials), reward probabilities were fixed for the two arms (0.83:0.12), and blocks switched without explicit cues (Fig. S1, Table S1); because outcomes were stochastic, transitions could only be inferred by trial-and-error40. Animals acquired the task, as reward rate increased across sessions (P = 5.3 × 10−10; Fig. 1B). Importantly, these effects remained significant after controlling for mean running speed and trial count per session (Table S2), indicating that they are not explained by changes in speed or differences in trial count. To further characterize behavioral adaptation to contingency reversals, we aligned trials to block reversal and examined the probability of selecting the newly rewarded arm across subsequent trials. Following each reversal, animals initially persisted in selecting the previously rewarded option, producing a transient drop in optimal choice probability. However, over the following trials, animals progressively shifted toward the newly high-probability arm, indicating detection of the contingency change and gradual policy updating under stochastic feedback (Fig. S1). Throughout the manuscript, we distinguish between reversal adaptation within a block and performance improvement across training experience. Reversal adaptation refers to the trial-by-trial adjustment of choices following an uncued contingency switch, whereas session progression refers to changes observed across recording days. Given that the maze was fixed within the behavioral room relative to distal visual cues, we tested whether animals exhibited systematic lateral choice biases. At the animal level, the signed probability of choosing the left option did not differ from chance (median P(left) = 0.601; Wilcoxon signed-rank test vs. 0.5: P = 0.3). A generalized linear mixed-effects model accounting for repeated measurements across sessions similarly revealed no significant signed leftward bias in choice behavior (P = 0.25). However, because signed left/right preferences could cancel across animals or sessions, we additionally quantified absolute lateral bias at the session level as |P(left) − 0.5 | . This measure directly captures the magnitude of lateral preference independently of whether a session was biased leftward or rightward, and therefore addresses the possibility that signed side preferences could cancel at the group level. Across 72 sessions, the median absolute bias was 0.111, corresponding to an approximately 11% deviation from equal left/right choice probability, and was significantly greater than zero (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 2.4 × 10−13). This effect remained significant when averaged across animals (median = 0.134; P = 0.0039). Thus, animals showed measurable session-level lateral biases, although these were not expressed as a consistent leftward or rightward preference across the cohort. Because reward contingencies reversed across blocks, such biases could influence local choice patterns but could not by themselves explain task performance across reversals. We therefore interpret the behavioral categories as operational session-level choice-persistence regimes and acknowledge that they may include contributions from lateralized or reward-independent spatial strategies.

We next assessed behavioral strategies during decision-making across recording sessions. Over successive sessions, rats became increasingly likely to repeat a rewarded choice (Fig. 1C) and more tolerant to an isolated unrewarded trial (Fig. 1D). To summarize choice persistence in a model-free way, we defined a Persistence Index (PI) from stay behavior after rewarded and isolated unrewarded outcomes (PI = 0.5 × [P(S | W) + P(S | L)], Fig. S2). PI captures generalized choice stability across outcome valence, without assuming that persistence is always strategically beneficial. In this probabilistic task, persistence after isolated omissions on the high-probability arm can prevent overreaction to stochastic non-reward, but the same tendency can be maladaptive immediately after a true reversal if it delays switching away from the previously rewarded side. Thus, PI should be interpreted as a descriptive session-level measure of choice stability, not as a direct index of optimality or trial-by-trial reversal adaptation (Fig. 1E). Using distribution-guided thresholds (Fig. S2), we classified individual sessions into exploitative, exploratory, or mixed regimes. Sessions were classified as exploitative when PI > 0.60 and P(S | W) > 0.70, exploratory when PI < 0.50 and P(S | W) < 0.60, and mixed otherwise. This classification was intended as an operational description of session-level choice-persistence regimes, not as definitive evidence that animals used a single cognitive strategy throughout each session. We use the term “training experience” to refer to changes associated with session progression across recording days. This should be distinguished from reversal adaptation, which refers to trial-by-trial adjustment after an uncued contingency switch. The increase in P(stay|loss) across sessions does not imply that animals became more outcome-reactive after losses. Rather, it indicates increased persistence after isolated unrewarded outcomes. In a probabilistic task, such persistence can protect against overreacting to stochastic omissions on the high-probability arm, although the same tendency can delay switching immediately after true reversals. To ensure that results were not dependent on precise boundary placement, we verified that modest shifts of the thresholds (P(S | W) and PI) did not alter the overall classification structure or the general behavioral effects reported (Fig. S2). When assessing the temporal evolution of strategies, we found that the prevalence of exploitative sessions increased across session progression while exploratory sessions declined (Fig. 1F).

We then modeled behavioral performance with a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) predicting reward rate from session and strategy (Table S3). To account for repeated measurements across animals and sessions, we used a GLMM with a binomial response (rewarded vs unrewarded trials) and a logit link function. In this model, training session and behavioral strategy were included as fixed effects, while animal identity was included as a random effect to account for between-subject variability and repeated observations across sessions. This approach allows estimation of population-level effects while appropriately modeling within-animal dependence in the behavioral data. The model revealed a significant positive effect of session on reward rate (β = 0.126, P = 3.4 × 10−6), meaning that animals improved task performance over time. Relative to exploratory sessions, exploitative sessions showed higher reward rate (β = 0.184, P = 0.0039). Consistent with the model, session-level reward rate was higher during exploitative than exploratory sessions (58.4 ± 6.3% vs 49.9 ± 5.0%, respectively, P = 2.3 × 10−7; Fig. 1G). A paired comparison across rats confirmed this result (Wilcoxon signed-rank, P = 8 × 10−8; Fig. S3).

Session structure differed by strategy, as exploitative sessions had higher trial count (P = 1.6 × 10−4) and shorter trial durations (P = 5.2 × 10−7) (Fig. S3). Moreover, locomotion was slower under the exploratory strategy relative to exploitative sessions (P = 8.8 × 10−6; Fig. S3). Exploratory sessions also tended to include longer trial durations. Because the classification relies on recent-outcome measures, including P(S | W) and PI, longer intervals between choices could weaken the influence of the immediately preceding outcome and thereby contribute to reduced persistence. Thus, reduced persistence in these sessions may reflect a combination of exploratory sampling, weaker task engagement, longer outcome-to-choice intervals, and reduced use of recent outcome history. Importantly, applying a stricter trial-duration cutoff produced qualitatively similar results (Fig. S2), and the principal neural associations remained significant after controlling for trial counts and locomotor speed (Table S2). We therefore interpret the exploratory category conservatively and do not assume that it reflects exploration alone. Together, these results indicate a behavioral transition from outcome-reactive exploration in early sessions to an exploitation-biased strategy that tolerates isolated unrewarded trials and yields higher reward in later sessions.

Session-averaged cortico-cortical connectivity as a marker of task performance

Neural recordings were performed during the same behavioral sessions analyzed in the previous section, allowing neural activity to be directly related to behavioral performance and strategy classification across the recording timeline. To examine circuit dynamics during probabilistic task performance, we simultaneously recorded local field potentials from the PFC, LEC, and CA1d area of the hippocampus (Fig. 2A, Fig. S4). Power spectral density across the task revealed canonical frequency signatures for each region. CA1d exhibited prominent theta oscillations (4–10 Hz), consistent with known patterns during spatial navigation and active exploration41,42. PFC showed dominant delta-band activity (2–4 Hz) with a broad gamma shoulder (30–100 Hz), and a minor theta component43–45. Coordinated oscillations have been proposed as a mechanism for long-range communication between distributed neural circuits49, a well-established framework that is consistent with circuit-level metrics we monitor here. To test whether functional connectivity within the hippocampo-cortical circuit is structured by rhythmic activity, we used the weighted phase-lag index (wPLI) to quantify frequency-specific coupling between pairs of recorded regions50,51. As a first approach, we computed the spectra across the entire behavioral session, which revealed distinct global interaction profiles (Fig. 2C). The cortico-cortical pair (LEC–PFC) showed robust coupling in the delta–theta range (2–10 Hz), with an additional minor peak in the fast-gamma (70–110 Hz) band. CA1d–PFC connectivity was dominated by narrow-band theta coupling, with weaker coherence in the beta (10–20 Hz) range. Conversely, CA1d–LEC interactions spanned a broader theta range and uniquely expressed slow-gamma (20–50 Hz) coupling alongside beta synchrony. Importantly, there was no evident difference in the session-level inter-regional coupling across strategies in either theta or gamma frequency bands (Fig. S5). These patterns indicate that theta-range coordination is a shared motif across hippocampo-cortical circuits, while distinct fast- and slow-gamma components differentiate specific pathways, thus suggesting that parallel frequency channels may support specialized forms of functional communication during probabilistic task performance, as has been previously documented52,53.

We next asked whether these rhythms supported directional interactions. Using frequency-resolved, non-parametric Granger causality computed per session and averaged across animals, we observed distinct asymmetries in the recorded regions. For the cortico-cortical pair (LEC–PFC), theta-band (peak at ~6 Hz) Granger-predicted directed interaction was stronger in the PFC → LEC direction than LEC → PFC, with smaller components in the gamma band (Fig. 2D). For the hippocampo-cortical pairs, PFC→CA1d showed a similar theta peak (~6 Hz) with weak gamma components, whereas the reverse CA1d→PFC direction expressed a sharper, faster theta peak (~8 Hz) with negligible gamma contribution (Fig. 2E). The LEC–CA1d pair was dominated by a prominent CA1d→LEC theta directional coupling (~8 Hz), several-fold larger than the reverse, with modest gamma coupling (Fig. 2F). These spectra align with the previous synchrony findings, highlighting the theta band as a common carrier of long-range coordination while revealing pathway-specific directionality. That is, theta oscillations dominated across pairs, whereas gamma effects were modest and pair-specific, thus suggesting theta and gamma frequencies as the principal channels of hippocampo-cortical communication during task performance.

To determine whether large-scale neural coordination predicted behavioral performance, we analyzed session-level relationships between neural connectivity metrics and reward rate using GLMMs. In these models, session reward rate served as the response variable and therefore provided the direct session-level measure of task performance. Neural connectivity metrics, including frequency-specific synchrony and Granger-based directional coupling, were included as fixed predictors. Session number was included as a covariate to account for the overall progression of training experience across recording days, and behavioral persistence category was included to account for differences in session-level choice-persistence regime. We use session number as an index of training experience, not as a precise measure of trial-by-trial reversal learning. Animal identity was included as a random effect to account for repeated observations within subjects (Table S4). This modeling framework allowed us to test whether functional connectivity explained additional variance in performance beyond behavioral factors alone39,54. Testing of fixed effects within the mixed model showed that only the cortico-cortical pair LEC–PFC was associated with reward rate, specifically theta (P = 0.019, FDR-controlled) and gamma (P = 0.019, FDR-controlled) frequencies. Neither hippocampo-cortical pairs (CA1d–LEC, CA1d–PFC) nor Granger causality reached significant effects (Table S4). To represent the relation between regional synchrony and task performance, we plotted the residualized values to illustrate the partial effect for LEC–PFC wPLI in the theta (Fig. 2G) and gamma (Fig. 2H) bands and reward rate. In both oscillatory bands the slope was negative, indicating that, controlling for session and strategy, sessions with stronger LEC–PFC synchrony exhibited lower reward rate, consistent with the linear model estimates. Because these metrics were estimated across the entire session, we refer to them as session-averaged cortico-cortical coupling. To test whether this association could be explained by differences in locomotor speed or session structure, we performed additional mixed-effects analyses including mean running speed and the number of valid trials per session as covariates. Most LEC–PFC connectivity measures showed no significant relationship with mean running speed or trial count across sessions. One exception was a modest association between mean running speed and fast-gamma LEC–PFC wPLI (P = 0.049; Table S5). Importantly, the negative association between session-averaged LEC–PFC synchrony and reward rate remained significant after including mean running speed and trial count as covariates, indicating that the principal connectivity–performance relationship was not explained by locomotor speed or session structure. Thus, the observed connectivity effect cannot be explained by differences in movement speed or trial count across sessions. Taken together, these data point to a previously unreported connectivity mechanism in which cortico-cortical (LEC–PFC) theta-gamma coupling operates as a consistent negative marker of behavioral performance.

Hippocampal theta organizes entorhinal and prefrontal gamma bursts and spike timing during probabilistic choice behavior

Although hippocampo–cortical connectivity was not directly associated with reward rate at the session scale (Table S4), Granger causality indicated prominent theta-band directionality from CA1d to cortical targets. Because hippocampal theta has been shown to organize cortical gamma oscillations and spike timing in behaving rodents29,30,34,52,55, we asked whether this established organizational principle was expressed in the CA1d–LEC–PFC circuit during probabilistic choice behavior (Fig. 3A). Cross-region phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) showed that CA1d theta phase modulated gamma amplitude in both LEC and PFC, yielding significant PAC maps (Fig. 3B) and biasing the phase distribution of cortical gamma bursts, including LEC fast gamma, PFC fast gamma, and LEC slow gamma (Fig. 3C–E). We next compared candidate theta sources for cortical gamma modulation. In LEC, PAC strength did not differ between CA1d-theta→LEC-fast-gamma and PFC-theta→LEC-fast-gamma (P = 0.469; Fig. 3F), suggesting that both hippocampal and cortical theta provide comparable temporal reference signals for LEC fast-gamma. In contrast, in PFC, CA1d-theta→PFC-fast-gamma coupling, although significant, was weaker than LEC-theta→PFC-fast-gamma coupling (P = 0.0312; Fig. 3G), consistent with stronger cortico–cortical influence on PFC gamma timing. Finally, hippocampal theta also organized slow-gamma activity in LEC, with stronger CA1d-theta→LEC-slow-gamma coupling than PFC-theta→LEC-slow-gamma coupling (P = 0.0156; Fig. 3H). Together, these results indicate that CA1d theta provides a reliable temporal scaffold for cortical gamma bursts, with hippocampal coupling most prominent for LEC slow-gamma and cortico–cortical coupling more prominent for PFC fast-gamma.

Hippocampo–cortical theta–gamma cross-modulation was reliable across training experience, as it did not vary significantly with session progression or behavioral persistence category (Table S6). Directional coupling, however, changed across session progression, particularly in the hippocampo–entorhinal pathway. CA1d→LEC theta directional coupling increased with session number (β = 0.136, P = 0.019), whereas the reverse LEC→CA1d direction remained stable (P = 0.278). In slow gamma, Granger causality increased bidirectionally across session progression (CA1d→LEC, β = 0.119, P = 0.014; LEC→CA1d, β = 0.107, P = 0.002; Table S7). We interpret these effects as changes associated with training experience rather than as direct measures of trial-by-trial reversal adaptation. The main contribution of this analysis is the dissociation between hippocampo–entorhinal temporal organization and the session-averaged cortical performance marker. CA1d–LEC theta directionality and theta–gamma coupling were robust and changed with session progression. However, these measures did not explain session-to-session reward-rate variance once LEC–PFC coupling and behavioral persistence category were accounted for. Furthermore, we tested whether session duration influenced these neural metrics using GLMMs (Table S8). Most relationships between session duration and the neural metrics examined were not significant. The only exception was a positive association between LEC–PFC theta synchrony and session duration (Fig. S6). Because wPLI was computed from LFP cross-spectral estimates rather than spike counts, firing rate does not directly determine wPLI detectability. Nevertheless, session duration and firing rate may reflect behavioral-state differences across sessions. We therefore included session duration as an additional covariate in the reward-rate model. The negative association between LEC–PFC theta synchrony and reward rate remained significant after this control (Table S8), indicating that the principal connectivity–performance relationship was not explained by session length.

Together, these findings indicate that hippocampal theta waves enable temporal windows that bias cortical gamma bursts, consistent with a regulatory role over cortico-cortical dynamics. Importantly, adding PAC to the reward rate model did not improve association beyond session and connectivity (Table S9), implying that inter-regional theta–gamma coordination is mechanistic but not a session-level marker of task performance. Taken together, these data suggest that theta-gated gamma coordination appears to coexist with cortico-cortical coupling, but it does not add predictive value for reward rate once training session and connectivity are controlled.

We also examined slow-gamma spike timing in the CA1d–LEC pathway because this pathway showed bidirectional slow-gamma directional coupling. CA1d slow-gamma phase biased LEC unit timing (P = 6.6 × 10−5), and LEC slow-gamma phase biased CA1d unit timing (P = 8.9 × 10−7). These findings extend the LFP-based CA1d–LEC coupling results by showing that reciprocal slow-gamma coordination is also reflected in spike timing within the hippocampo–entorhinal pathway. Together, the phase-locking analyses show that gamma bursts and neuronal spiking are organized within pathway-specific temporal windows during probabilistic choice behavior, particularly in the CA1d–LEC pathway. These observations support the main conclusion that hippocampal theta- and gamma-dependent timing organizes entorhinal and prefrontal circuit activity, while remaining distinct from the session-averaged LEC–PFC connectivity marker associated with reward rate. Thus, the novelty here lies in the task- and pathway-specific demonstration that CA1d–LEC and CA1d–PFC spike timing is organized during probabilistic choice behavior, with particularly prominent hippocampo–entorhinal phase-locking, rather than in the general observation that oscillatory phase can bias neuronal discharge.

Peri-goal neuronal spiking shows prominent prefrontal ramping and hippocampo–entorhinal suppression

Latency-sorted heatmaps revealed robust, region-specific modulation. In PFC, modulation was dominated by activation (134/232 units, 57.8%), forming the clearest sustained population ramp, which began before nosepoke onset and remained elevated afterward. This is a pattern consistent with prior reports of prefrontal ramping activity33,49, but here expressed during unsignaled probabilistic choice in a reversal learning task. Average traces showed a stable separation between activated and inhibited populations during the goal-reaching interval, consistent with sustained engagement of PFC neurons around choice execution (Fig. 5D). In LEC, the balance reversed (56/184 activated, 30.4%), although a subset of units showed brief activation during goal approach, the population response was dominated by inhibition around nosepoke onset, followed by gradual recovery (Fig. 5E). CA1d showed the strongest population suppression (51/171 activated, 29.8% activated), with a sharp trough centered on nosepoke onset and gradual recovery thereafter, despite brief activation in a subset of units (Fig. 5F). Direct comparison of population spiking confirmed that CA1d exhibited a stronger suppression upon goal-reaching, whereas LEC and PFC remained closer to baseline levels (Fig. S8). Across regions, the latency-sorted discharge profiles indicate a consistent temporal sequence, in which a cortical build-up led by PFC precedes the nosepoke, a briefer overlapping suppression in LEC, and a pronounced hippocampal suppression tightly locked to goal-reaching.

Because interneurons and principal neurons can differ substantially in firing properties and oscillatory coupling, we performed an additional control analysis to assess whether the observed peri-goal firing patterns were driven by inclusion of high firing-rate units potentially corresponding to fast-spiking interneurons. Although definitive cell-type classification was not feasible in the present dataset, examination of spike waveform features and firing-rate distributions revealed partial segregation consistent with putative interneuron- and principal neuron-like profiles (Fig. S9). We therefore repeated the peri-goal analyses after conservatively excluding high firing-rate units using maximum mean firing-rate thresholds of 30 Hz and 20 Hz. In both cases, the principal regional response patterns remained qualitatively unchanged, PFC units retained sustained peri-goal activation, whereas LEC and CA1d populations continued to exhibit transient suppression around goal-reaching (Fig. S9). These results indicate that the reported population-level firing dynamics are not driven by a small subset of high firing-rate units.

Cross-regional theta–gamma coupling has been proposed as a routing mechanism in cortical networks27,56. We therefore examined how peri-goal hippocampal theta interacts with cortical gamma bursts when trial information must be integrated and updated. Phase–amplitude coupling revealed a noticeable increase in hippocampo–cortical coordination upon goal-reaching, whereas cortico–cortical coupling remained comparatively stable. Indeed, CA1d theta phase dynamically biased slow-gamma bursts in LEC, with coupling rising during goal-approach, peaking shortly after goal-reaching, and then slowly decaying toward baseline, while PFC theta→LEC slow-gamma coupling remained stable over the same interval (Fig. 5G). Likewise, CA1d theta phase tightly controlled fast-gamma bursts in both LEC (Fig. 5H) and PFC (Fig. 5I), following a similar temporal profile to the CA1d theta→LEC slow-gamma interaction. Moreover, we quantified these dynamics at the single-animal level. For each rat, we computed the variance of theta–gamma MI across the peri-goal interval, providing an index of how strongly coupling fluctuated over time rather than its average strength. In every case, cross-frequency coupling was more volatile for hippocampo-cortical pathways than for cortico-cortical connections (Fig. 5J–L). This pattern indicates that hippocampal theta oscillations transiently organize cortical gamma activity around goal-reaching, even though this time-locked gating does not itself improve prediction of performance at the broader session-level.

To test whether peri-goal spiking suppression and theta–gamma modulation could be explained by locomotor slowing alone, we analyzed neuronal activity and theta–gamma modulation during spontaneous stopping events occurring outside nosepoke execution at the reward port. These events provided a control for reductions in running speed outside the reward port, although they did not reproduce all motor components of the nosepoke period, such as licking or reward-port engagement. Unlike nosepoke-aligned events, spontaneous stops did not elicit robust regional firing-rate suppression or increased modulation index volatility in any region (Fig. S8), suggesting that locomotor slowing alone is insufficient to explain the full peri-goal activity profile.

Finally, to assess in more detail the peri-goal spiking dynamics in relation to locomotor behavior, we first computed the zero-lag Pearson correlation between population firing rate and instantaneous running speed across the peri-goal interval (Fig. 6). For each region, firing rates of activated and inhibited units were normalized and correlated with the mean speed profile. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing (trial-wise time shuffling within session). In PFC, activated units were negatively correlated with speed (r = −0.48, P = 2 × 10−4), whereas inhibited units were strongly positively correlated (r = 0.87, P = 2 × 10−4; Fig. 6A). In LEC, both populations covaried with speed in the zero-lag correlation analysis (activated: r = 0.35, P = 2 × 10−4; inhibited: r = 0.82, P = 2 × 10−4; Fig. 6B). A similar pattern was observed in CA1d, where inhibited units closely tracked speed (r = 0.84, P = 2 × 10−4⁻⁴), while activated units showed only a weak association (r = 0.14, P = 8 × 10−3; Fig. 6C). Cross-correlation functions were computed between population firing and speed, and significance was assessed for the maximal absolute coefficient relative to a surrogate distribution. Taken together, these findings suggest that goal-reaching is accompanied by a coordinated reconfiguration of circuit activity, with prefrontal ensembles ramping and remaining engaged around the goal, while hippocampal and entorhinal populations undergo a pronounced suppression temporally aligned with locomotor slowing. This peri-goal state reflects structured temporal coupling between movement dynamics and network activity rather than a purely kinematic effect.

Finally, to determine whether these peri-goal dynamics were specifically driven by contingency transitions, we performed a reversal-aligned control analysis. For each block, we compared the first five trials following an uncued reversal (transition phase) with the last five trials of the same block (stable phase). As a behavioral validation of these operational windows, reward proportion was lower during transition trials than stable trials across the pooled dataset and in most individual animals (Fisher’s exact test, P = 3 × 10−11; signed-rank test across animals, P = 0.0039; Fig. S10). We quantified both the magnitude of peri-nosepoke firing-rate suppression and the peak peri-goal theta–gamma modulation index for each condition (Fig. S10). We found that neither firing-rate suppression nor modulation index volatility differed significantly between transition and stable trials across regions (P > 0.05, Fig. S10). Moreover, the temporal profiles of suppression and oscillatory coupling were qualitatively similar in early and late block trials. These results indicate that, at the resolution of this coarse early-versus-late comparison, peri-goal firing-rate suppression and theta–gamma modulation were not detectably restricted to the earliest post-reversal trials. This analysis does not exclude finer modulation by trial-by-trial reversal adaptation, recent outcome history, accumulated evidence, or latent contingency beliefs.

Discussion

We have studied how a hippocampo–cortical circuit linking CA1d, LEC and PFC supports learning under uncertainty in a probabilistic reversal task. Behaviorally, there was a gradual shift across sessions from outcome-reactive exploration toward an exploitation-biased strategy that tolerated isolated unrewarded trials and produced higher reward rates. Taking advantage of simultaneous recordings across the three regions, we then used mixed-effects models to identify a neural marker of task performance at the session-level and dissociate it from others tied to specific behavioral events. Three main conclusions emerge in this study. First, session-averaged cortico–cortical synchrony in the theta and fast–gamma bands is a marker of task performance57,58, as stronger LEC–PFC coupling consistently associates with lower reward rate after controlling for session and strategy. Second, hippocampal theta shows directional coupling with cortical gamma bursts and spike timing55,59, particularly in the hippocampo-entorhinal pathway. Third, during the peri-goal interval the circuit displays a state in which prefrontal activity ramps60 while hippocampal and entorhinal populations are suppressed61, temporally aligned with locomotor deceleration.

Regarding the behavioral task, tolerance to isolated losses may reflect adaptive inference about environmental volatility. However, we interpret the strategy index as a reflection of the average persistence across positive and negative outcomes, and not as evidence of higher-order meta-learning about successive losses. Indeed, in our task, trial outcomes followed a Bernoulli distribution (either rewarderd or unrewarded), and were conditionally independent on the chosen arm. Thus, consecutive losses could occur stochastically even without a contingency change. Adaptive performance therefore requires graded evidence accumulation rather than deterministic switching after fixed loss counts2,4.

Importantly, interpreting behavioral performance in probabilistic reversal tasks requires considering the constraints imposed by stochastic reward delivery. In our case, the high-value arm delivered reward with probability 0.83, meaning that unrewarded outcomes occurred with probability 0.17, even when animals selected the optimal option. Under such conditions, consecutive losses can occur despite unchanged contingencies (e.g., two omissions with probability 0.17² ≈ 2.9%), making simple loss-counting strategies unreliable. Adaptive behavior in stochastic environments therefore requires gradual evidence integration rather than switching after individual unrewarded trials2. Consequently, session-level reward rate is constrained by three factors: stochastic reward omissions, transient adaptation periods following unsignaled reversals, and continued exploration under uncertainty. Normative analyses of stochastic bandit tasks predict that these factors prevent reward rate from approaching the nominal reward probability4,62. Consistent with this framework, rodent studies using probabilistic bandit paradigms typically report reward rates well below the nominal reward probability due to exploration and reversal costs6,63. Thus, reward rates in the 50–60% range observed here are consistent with adaptive probabilistic learning rather than suboptimal task acquisition. In addition, running speed showed a negative association with reward rate in the control mixed-effects model (Table S2). Because exploratory sessions tended to involve slower locomotion and lower performance, this relationship likely reflects differences in behavioral regimes rather than a direct causal effect of locomotor speed on decision accuracy. The exploratory label should be interpreted operationally. These sessions were defined by low P(S | W) and low PI, indicating a reduced tendency to repeat the same choice after recent outcomes. Such behavior is compatible with exploratory sampling, but it does not by itself prove a specific cognitive exploration strategy. In particular, low persistence could also arise from reward-independent spatial strategies, including alternation, side preferences, reduced task engagement, or weaker use of recent outcome history. We therefore use these categories as descriptive session-level behavioral regimes rather than as direct evidence for a single underlying decision process.

Regarding neural connectivity, one significant result is the negative relation between session-averaged theta-gamma LEC–PFC synchrony and session-level reward rate64. Here, we use the term ‘session-averaged’ in an operational sense to denote neural metrics computed over long, non–event-locked windows spanning many trials and therefore captured a session-level coupling regime rather than transient peri-event dynamics. This terminology describes how the metric was computed and should not be taken to imply that coupling was invariant across all behavioral epochs within a session. When the principal frequency bands and pairwise connections were included in the behavioral model, only theta and fast–gamma connectivity in the cortico–cortical pair survived as significant effects, and higher coupling was consistently associated with poorer performance. This effect persisted after accounting for session and strategy, and was absent in hippocampo–cortical connections and in Granger directionality. These findings argue against the simple idea that stronger synchrony necessarily improves performance. Instead, higher session-averaged LEC–PFC coupling was associated with lower session-level reward rate after accounting for session progression and behavioral persistence category. This association may reflect a cortical network state linked to reduced flexibility, increased uncertainty, greater task difficulty, or compensatory engagement during less efficient behavioral regimes. However, because the present analyses do not directly measure trial-by-trial contingency inference, we interpret LEC–PFC synchrony as a session-level performance-associated marker rather than as direct evidence for reduced flexibility during reversals59,64.

The negative relationship between LEC–PFC synchrony and reward rate should be interpreted cautiously. Although increased cortico–cortical coupling could reflect stabilization of an ongoing decision policy, alternative explanations are also possible65,66. For example, stronger synchrony may emerge during periods of increased uncertainty, cognitive effort, or task difficulty. Because connectivity measures were summarized at the session level, the present analyses cannot distinguish whether increased coupling reflects maladaptive stabilization of behavior or compensatory network engagement during challenging decision regimes. Here, we define a marker as a metric that predicts task performance while controlling for session and strategy. In this context, session-averaged LEC–PFC synchrony operates as a marker of reward rate, rather than as a direct mechanism driving performance improvement.

In contrast, the hippocampal theta rhythm showed a clear directional coupling on entorhinal and prefrontal dynamics that changed across session progression33,67,68. Granger analysis revealed a prominent CA1d→LEC theta directional coupling that increased across sessions, while the reverse LEC→CA1d directional coupling remained invariant. Slow–gamma interactions were specific for this pathway and became bidirectional across session progression, consistent with a progressive strengthening of hippocampo–entorhinal dialogue. Cross-frequency coupling further showed that CA1d theta robustly phased both slow– and fast-gamma bursts in LEC and modulated fast–gamma bursts in PFC, and these effects were stable across strategies. Together, these results support a model in which hippocampal theta provides temporally precise windows for entorhinal and prefrontal processing30,55,69, particularly as animals become more familiar with the task structure. Importantly, however, adding PAC metrics to the reward rate model did not improve association beyond LEC–PFC connectivity and session. Thus, theta–gamma coordination appears as a mechanistic scaffold for organizing cortical computations rather than as an additional session–scale marker of task performance.

Our results show a dissociation between the cross-cortical coupling marker of reward rate and the hippocampal theta mechanism for temporal organization. Within this framework, the entorhinal pathway plays a special role, although it is not the only route linking hippocampal and prefrontal circuits. LEC is positioned to contribute information about local option identity and recent outcomes to hippocampal processing20 and to interact functionally with prefrontal networks70. Direct hippocampo–prefrontal projections also exist, with the strongest projections arising from ventral/intermediate hippocampal regions; recently described dorsal CA1–PFC projections provide an additional, but comparatively smaller, anatomical route71. Thus, our emphasis on LEC should be interpreted as highlighting a relevant hippocampo–entorhinal–prefrontal pathway in the present recordings, not as excluding direct CA1–PFC communication. Our data suggest that LEC participates in both sides of the dissociation. On one hand, its long–range coupling with PFC provides the session-averaged synchrony that tracks performance72. On the other hand, LEC gamma bursts are strongly phase-modulated by CA1d theta and display strong theta–gamma coupling69,73, especially in the slow–gamma band where Granger directional coupling is bidirectional. This position in the circuit is ideally suited for LEC to act as a conditional gate, so that when strongly synchronized with PFC it may stabilize a current belief about the environment, whereas when driven by CA1d theta it may be more engaged in updating value estimates or contextual representations.

Also, our peri–goal analyses further clarified how these mechanisms operate at the lower level of neuronal spiking populations. Aligning unit activity to the nose–poke revealed a clear segregation of dynamics across the circuit. In PFC, the majority of units classified as activated showed a sustained ramp that began shortly before goal–reaching and remained elevated afterward, while inhibited units decreased firing in parallel with locomotor slowing. Several studies have shown ramping activity in PFC during behaviorally relevant moments, like choice-selection or target-approaching33,74–77. In LEC and CA1d, inhibition dominated, as both regions exhibited a sharp suppression of activity during peri-goal intervals, followed by a slow recovery, consistent with a transient reset of hippocampo–entorhinal activity during outcome evaluation78,79. These neural activity patterns are consistent with a model in which prefrontal populations remain engaged in representing the chosen option and outcome evaluation, while hippocampal and entorhinal ensembles briefly disengage and likely reset.

This interpretation is further refined by quantifying the temporal relationship between peri–goal neuronal spiking and locomotor speed. Inhibited populations in CA1d and LEC showed strong coupling to the deceleration profile, and cross-correlation analyses revealed that peak correlation occurred at negative lags by several hundred milliseconds, suggesting that suppression preceded changes in speed. These anticipatory relationships indicate that peri-goal suppression is not merely a passive reflection of slowing but is temporally structured around the deceleration phase. In contrast, the activated PFC ensemble exhibited sustained ramping whose temporal profile extended beyond the period of peak deceleration, indicating that prefrontal activity cannot be fully reduced to locomotor dynamics alone. Thus, peri-goal activity reflects coordinated circuit reconfiguration embedded within, but not fully explained by, movement-related changes. In this situation, prefrontal neurons are likely to encode goal-approach74,80 and outcome-assessment14,81, in a manner that is partially decoupled from locomotor kinematics. Together with the oscillatory data, these findings support a model in which hippocampo-entorhinal ensembles are temporally associated with upcoming deceleration and state change, while PFC maintains a higher–order representation of choice and expected outcome82.

These results also have implications for theoretical accounts of credit assignment in recurrent circuits. For example, reinforcement learning models with latent states require that outcome information be routed back to the representations that generated the choice62,83,84. The relationship between hippocampo–cortical coordination and probabilistic task performance can be interpreted within the framework of reinforcement-learning models of probabilistic decision making. In this task, states can be understood as the animal’s current belief about the task context (defined by recent outcomes and the inferred probability that each arm is currently rewarded) whereas actions correspond to selecting the left or right goal port. Credit assignment therefore involves updating the estimated value of the chosen action following probabilistic outcomes. From this perspective, the observed LEC–PFC synchrony may reflect the stabilization of mappings between entorhinal representations of task context and prefrontal action policies, potentially influencing how outcome information updates value estimates. Strong cortico–cortical synchrony may therefore correspond to periods in which the current policy is maintained despite stochastic losses, whereas reduced synchrony during exploratory regimes may reflect increased flexibility in updating value estimates under uncertainty. Although the present analyses cannot directly measure computational variables such as prediction errors or belief updates, the circuit dynamics observed here are broadly consistent with reinforcement-learning accounts in which hippocampal and prefrontal networks coordinate the integration of outcome history to guide adaptive decision policies. The session-averaged LEC–PFC coupling that associates with performance may reflect the current credit assignment configuration of the cortical network, with strong synchrony corresponding to a more rigid mapping between states and actions. In parallel, hippocampal theta–gamma coordination, especially in the CA1d–LEC pathway, may provide temporal windows that bind together entorhinal content and hippocampal state representations, enabling efficient updating when contingencies change. The peri–goal suppression and anticipatory coupling to deceleration suggest that these updates are embedded within finely-tuned transitions in motor and cognitive states. Further experiments will be required to test this model, which currently fall beyond the scope of the current study.

Several limitations of the present work should be acknowledged. First, the behavioral categories used here should be interpreted as operational regimes of session-level choice persistence rather than as direct evidence for fixed decision strategies. Low persistence cannot be uniquely assigned to cognitive exploration: reduced P(S | W) and reduced PI may reflect exploratory sampling, but they may also arise from local spatial strategies, reduced engagement, longer trial intervals, or weaker use of recent outcome history. Conversely, high persistence may support performance when isolated omissions occur on the high-probability arm, but may impair adaptation immediately after true reversals. In addition, although animals did not show a consistent signed leftward or rightward preference across the cohort, the absolute-bias analysis revealed measurable lateral choice biases within sessions. Such biases could contribute to local choice patterns, especially in exploratory sessions. However, because reward contingencies reversed across blocks, a fixed side preference would not consistently improve reward rate and cannot by itself account for the session-level connectivity–performance relationship.

Second, behavioral strategies were defined at the session level, whereas neural dynamics were analyzed at finer temporal scales. Accordingly, the oscillatory and spiking patterns reported here should be interpreted as session-level and peri-event correlates of probabilistic task performance rather than as direct neural readouts of trial-by-trial value updating, accumulated evidence, latent belief, or optimal policy. Similarly, the early-versus-late block comparison should be interpreted cautiously. Although reward proportion was lower during the first post-reversal trials than during late-block trials, confirming that these windows captured behaviorally distinct phases, the first post-reversal trials do not necessarily encompass the full reversal-adaptation period. In a probabilistic reversal task, the number of trials required to infer a new contingency is variable because unrewarded outcomes on the high-probability arm and rewarded outcomes on the low-probability arm can occur by chance. Thus, this analysis tests only whether peri-goal firing suppression and theta–gamma modulation are grossly restricted to the earliest post-reversal period. A more complete account of reversal learning will require trial-by-trial computational estimates of accumulated evidence, latent belief, or prediction error.

Third, the spontaneous-stop control should be interpreted within its intended scope. It addresses whether reductions in running speed alone can reproduce the peri-goal firing-rate pattern, but it does not control for licking, reward-port engagement, or the detailed acceleration/deceleration structure of the nosepoke event. Therefore, we do not interpret this analysis as excluding all motor or consummatory contributions to peri-goal activity. Rather, it indicates that locomotor slowing alone does not account for the full regional pattern observed during goal-reaching.

Fourth, the analyses are correlational and rely on mesoscale electrophysiological recordings. Demonstrating causality will require targeted manipulations of specific frequency bands or anatomical pathways, for example closed-loop perturbations of CA1d theta or LEC–PFC synchrony. In addition, although the recordings were sufficient to isolate units for peri-goal analyses, they did not provide enough independent waveform dimensions to classify units reliably as putative principal neurons or interneurons across CA1d, LEC, and PFC. Therefore, the activated and inhibited populations should be interpreted as response-defined groups rather than cell-type-specific populations. Future studies using tetrodes, high-density probes, or optogenetic tagging will be required to determine how principal neurons and interneurons separately contribute to these peri-goal dynamics.

Finally, all electrophysiological recordings were obtained from the right hemisphere to maintain consistency across animals. Although hippocampo–cortical circuits are generally considered bilaterally organized, hemispheric asymmetries may affect some cognitive functions; therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as characterizing right-hemisphere dynamics, and future studies will be required to determine whether similar coordination patterns occur bilaterally.

Despite these caveats, the present findings advance our understanding of how hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and prefrontal cortex jointly support learning under uncertainty. By separating a cortical synchrony marker of performance from a hippocampal theta mechanism that organizes cortical gamma bursts and spike timing, and by showing how these processes unfold around goal–reaching and locomotor transitions, we provide a circuit–level framework for how latent state inference, strategy adaptation and motor control are integrated within a hippocampo–cortical circuit.

Methods

Animals

Nine adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (postnatal day 40–60; 300–350 g) were obtained from the Center for Innovation on Biomedical Experimental Models (CIBEM; https://cibem.bio.puc.cl/) at the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile. Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled room (22 ± 1 °C) on a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on 07:00) with food and water ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the Scientific Ethical Committee for the Care of Animals and the Environment (CEC-CAA; protocol 240701014). All methods complied with relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Habituation

Upon arrival from the animal facility, rats were acclimated to the housing room for three days. This was followed by daily handling by the experimenter for an additional 3–5 days to reduce stress and facilitate task engagement. Animals were then habituated to the recording environment by being placed in the recording room and on the maze apparatus for 30–60 min per day for several days (typically 2–4 days). During this phase, animals freely explored the maze and learned the basic navigation sequence (start box → stem → goal ports → return).

Pre-surgical task training

After habituation, animals entered a pre-surgical training phase in which they performed the probabilistic two-armed bandit task without implants. The goal of this phase was to verify sustained task engagement and reliable maze performance before stereotaxic surgery. Eligibility for implantation was based on consistent task participation and completion of multiple blocks within a single session (typically ≥2 blocks within ~20 min). This criterion served as a practical benchmark of stable engagement rather than a strict numerical cutoff, and ensured that animals were likely to resume task performance following post-surgical recovery. Animals that failed to show sustained task engagement (e.g., prolonged immobility or grooming, failure to initiate trials) were not advanced to surgery.

Stereotaxic surgery

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% for induction, 1.5–2% for maintenance) and secured in a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting). Body temperature was held at 35–37 °C with a homeothermic blanket, and animals received hourly glucosaline (0.9% NaCl, 2.5% dextrose, 1 mL). After a scalp incision, three ~1 mm craniotomies were made in the right hemisphere at predetermined coordinates (all in mm): CA1d, −4.0 AP, 2.0 ML, 2.4 DV; LEC, −6.8 AP, 6.3 ML, 7.4 DV; PFC, 2.5 AP, 0.4 ML, 4.2 DV. Two additional craniotomies anterior to bregma accommodated ground and reference electrodes, and four more (two contralateral parietal, one ipsilateral parietal, one posterior to lambda) secured the implant with screws. The dura was removed at the target sites, cortical surfaces were kept moist with mineral oil, and electrodes were inserted carefully. Craniotomies were sealed with silicone elastomer or wax, and the microdrive was fixed with dental acrylic. Postoperative care consisted of daily subcutaneous enrofloxacin (5 mg/kg) and meloxicam (2 mg/kg) for three days. Animals recovered for at least seven days before recordings.

Behavioral task

Rats performed a self-paced modified two-armed bandit task in a T-maze with return4,6. The behavioral apparatus was custom-built by our group. Each goal port contained an infrared beam used to detect nosepoke entry and a reward spout positioned inside the port. Nosepoke onset was defined as interruption of the infrared beam. On rewarded trials, this event immediately triggered a pump that delivered 300 µL of 10% sucrose solution through the spout. The delay between nosepoke detection and reward delivery was determined by the pump-response latency and was approximately 100 ms. On unrewarded trials, the pump was not triggered and no explicit error cue was presented; the animal experienced only reward omission. Nosepoke duration was substantial (median 3.5 s, IQR 2.3–5.3), about 20% of the total trial duration. Animals were first trained on the task until stable performance was achieved. Neural recordings were then conducted during subsequent behavioral sessions, which correspond to the sessions shown in Fig. 1, allowing simultaneous analysis of behavioral performance and circuit dynamics across the same experimental timeline. Animals were maintained under restricted water access (50–60% of daily water intake by weight) to promote task engagement. Body weight was monitored daily and maintained above 85% of the expected age-matched growth curve throughout the experimental period. Each session consisted of multiple unsignaled blocks, and each block comprised 27–30 trials. Blocks were defined as consecutive trials with constant reward probabilities assigned to each goal port. Block transitions were not explicitly cued. Each session began with the animal positioned in the start box. Animals exited the start box, traversed the central stem, and selected one of two goal ports. Trial duration was defined as the interval between consecutive nosepoke events. After each nosepoke, reward delivery (or omission) occurred probabilistically, and the animal returned to the start box before initiating the next traversal. Reward outcomes were generated as independent Bernoulli draws conditional on the chosen arm; no pseudo-random constraints were imposed on the ordering of rewarded or unrewarded trials. Within each block, one side was designated as the high-probability option (P = 0.83), while the alternative side delivered reward with lower probability (P = 0.12). The arm assigned as high-probability in the first block of each session was selected independently of the contingency of the previous session. Reward probabilities were conditional on the chosen side and therefore did not sum to 1, as only one option was selected per trial. Outcomes were stochastic; thus, unrewarded trials could occur even when the high-probability option was selected (with 0.17 probability), and rewarded trials could occasionally occur on the low-probability side (with 0.12 probability). An “isolated unrewarded trial” refers to an unrewarded outcome that is not preceded by another unrewarded trial on the same arm. In this probabilistic task, unrewarded trials on the high-probability arm arise from stochastic outcome delivery (17% omission probability) and are therefore not considered behavioral errors. Rather, they represent probabilistic omissions conditional on the chosen arm, not mistakes in action selection. Session duration varied naturally depending on engagement, with a typical duration of approximately 50 min (Fig. S6) and typically included multiple within-session contingency reversals (i.e., blocks, Fig. S1). Sessions ended when animals disengaged from the task, operationally defined as a sustained absence of trial initiation, accompanied by consistent immobility or grooming.

Session classification

Trial durations varied across sessions and occasionally reached 100 s (Fig. S11), although the majority of trials were substantially shorter (median = 17.5 s; IQR = 12.4–24.6 s). The 100 s cutoff was chosen conservatively to exclude clear disengagement episodes while retaining valid exploratory laps. Only a small fraction of trials exceeded this threshold (0.8%, 94 out of 11,920) and most of these long trials were contributed by a single animal (AL29, 61 out of 94). To ensure that our results were not driven by this outlier, we performed a control analysis. We excluded this animal entirely and recomputed the main behavioral and neural metrics. Under these conditions, results remained stable, animals acquired the task over time (reward rate, β = 0.052, P = 1.2 × 10−10), cortical connectivity remained a significant negative predictor of behavioral performance (wPLI PFC-LEC, theta: β = −0.098, P = 0.0014; gamma: β = −0.109, P = 0.0075), and global firing rates were unchanged (LEC: P = 1; PFC: P = 0.81; CA1d: P = 1). This control indicates that the reported effects are robust to both the inclusion of long-duration trials and to inter-animal variability in trial structure. Importantly, restricting the analyses to shorter trials did not qualitatively alter the results (Fig. S11), indicating that the findings are not driven by long or irregular trials. Thresholds used to classify sessions into exploitative, mixed, and exploratory regimes were empirically derived from the joint distribution of behavioral metrics, based on visual inspection of the clustering structure formed by the two metrics used for classification: persistence index (PI) and P(stay|win). Specifically, after computing these measures for all sessions, we examined their two-dimensional distribution across the dataset and identified regions corresponding to qualitatively distinct behavioral regimes. Sessions with high persistence following rewarded trials and high tolerance of isolated losses clustered in the upper-right region of this space and were classified as exploitative, whereas sessions with low persistence and low loss tolerance clustered in the lower-left region and were classified as exploratory. Sessions falling between these regimes were classified as mixed. The numerical thresholds (exploitative: PI > 0.60 and P(stay|win) > 0.70; exploratory: PI < 0.50 and P(stay|win) < 0.60) were therefore chosen to separate these visually apparent regimes in the empirical distribution rather than to produce balanced group sizes. Importantly, the resulting classification was robust to small changes in threshold values and was not iteratively tuned to achieve a specific proportion of sessions in each category (Fig. S2).

Recording implant assembly

The recording microdrive was designed in Autodesk Fusion and 3D-printed on an F410 printer. A custom microdrive carried 16 or 32 tungsten, standard tapered-tip electrodes (MicroProbes, USA; 5 MΩ), positioned to target the regions of interest according to a rat stereotaxic atlas. Each electrode was wired to an EIB-36 PCB (36 channels, including ground and reference). Ground and reference leads were connected to stainless-steel skull screws placed during surgery, and the entire assembly was enclosed in copper mesh to minimize electrical noise.

Electrophysiological recordings

After recovery, electrophysiological recordings were performed for 10 consecutive days during the light phase inside a Faraday-shielded enclosure. Rats were placed on the task maze for roughly 60–min sessions. The EIB-36 board was connected via a 16- or 32-channel headstage (Intan Technologies) to an Intan RHD amplifier, with one electrode used as the reference. Video, synchronized to the amplifier clock, assisted brain-state identification. Signals were sampled at 20 kHz with RHX Data Acquisition (Intan Technologies) and converted to MATLAB using the LAN toolbox for analysis.

Weighted phase-lag index (wPLI)

Phase synchronization between pairs of LFP signals was quantified using the debiased weighted phase-lag index (wPLI), a measure of consistent non-zero phase-lag coupling that reduces spurious synchrony arising from volume conduction and common reference effects (39). Continuous LFP signals were segmented into non-overlapping 5-s windows. Spectral estimates were computed for each window using the FieldTrip toolbox, and phase synchrony was quantified from the resulting cross-spectral density. Connectivity estimates were obtained using the debiased formulation of wPLI, which corrects for sample-size–dependent bias. Connectivity metrics were computed using neural activity recorded during the entire behavioral session, including stem traversal, goal approach, nosepoke execution, and return trajectories. Periods outside active task engagement (e.g., immobility or inter-trial intervals) were minimal and were included in the analysis. Because reliable estimation of phase-based connectivity and cross-frequency coupling requires sufficiently long data segments, these metrics were computed over extended time windows spanning entire sessions rather than individual trials.

Granger causality

Directional interactions between recorded brain regions during maze sessions were quantified using frequency-resolved Granger causality (GC)51. Importantly, GC quantifies directed temporal predictability between signals and does not imply mechanistic causation. Each recorded region was normalized to place channels on a comparable scale. For each unique pair of recorded areas, signals were locally detrended to reduce slow fluctuations before causal analysis. GC was estimated in the frequency domain, yielding directed GC spectra for both directions of each area pair.

Cross-frequency phase–amplitude coupling

Cross-frequency interactions were quantified using phase–amplitude coupling (PAC), assessed via comodulograms. Analyses were performed on LFP signals. Temporal padding was applied to minimize edge-related artifacts. Signals were locally detrended prior to filtering. Low-frequency phase signals were obtained by band-pass filtering the LFP into overlapping phase-frequency bands spanning 1–17 Hz (2-Hz bandwidth, 1-Hz steps), followed by extraction of instantaneous phase via the Hilbert transform. High-frequency amplitude signals were obtained by band-pass filtering the same LFP into overlapping amplitude-frequency bands spanning 20–170 Hz (4-Hz bandwidth, 5-Hz steps), followed by extraction of the instantaneous amplitude envelope. θ bands followed regional definitions (PFC/LEC: 6–10 Hz; CA1d: 4–11 Hz). No post hoc minimum-amplitude threshold was applied to exclude phase samples. We avoided amplitude thresholding because hard cutoffs can preferentially select high-power epochs, alter the behavioral composition of the analyzed samples, and introduce unequal sample counts across sessions or pathways. In addition, the analyzed periods consisted primarily of active maze behavior, during which animals were moving through the maze and hippocampal theta was prominent (Fig. 2A). Phase–amplitude coupling was quantified using the modulation index (MI), computed by comparing the empirical distribution of high-frequency amplitude across phase bins to a uniform distribution. Phase was discretized into 18 equally spaced bins spanning −π to π radians (−3.14 to 3.14). For each phase–amplitude frequency pair, the MI was computed, yielding a two-dimensional comodulogram describing coupling strength across frequency pairs. Given the data-length requirements of phase–amplitude coupling estimation, analyses were conducted on extended continuous segments (i.e., session-level data) rather than on individual trials to ensure robust and statistically stable estimates. Statistical significance of PAC was assessed using a surrogate-based null distribution constructed independently for each session. Surrogate comodulograms were generated using a circular time-shifting procedure that preserves the spectral and temporal structure of the phase and amplitude signals while disrupting their temporal alignment. Specifically, the high-frequency amplitude envelope was circularly shifted by a random temporal offset relative to the low-frequency phase signal, while the phase time series was left unchanged. To avoid trivial correlations arising from short temporal shifts, the minimum allowed shift for each amplitude-frequency band was determined from the temporal autocorrelation structure of the amplitude envelope. The minimum shift was defined as the first lag at which the autocorrelation dropped below 1/e, indicating approximate temporal decorrelation. 100 surrogate realizations were generated per session to form a session-specific null distribution of comodulograms.

Phase–amplitude coupling maps

Group-level inference on PAC was performed in two stages. First, for each animal, session-level comodulograms were aggregated into a single animal-level observed comodulogram by averaging across sessions. In parallel, an animal-level null distribution was generated by repeatedly resampling one surrogate comodulogram from each session and averaging these across sessions. From this null distribution, the mean and standard deviation at each phase–amplitude frequency pair were estimated, and an animal-level z-scored comodulogram was computed by comparing observed values to the null expectation. Second, group-level inference was performed across animals. For each phase–amplitude frequency pair, the distribution of animal-level z-scores was tested against zero using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, assessing whether PAC values were consistently greater than expected under the null. The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons across the full phase–amplitude frequency grid using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. The resulting q-value map defines significant regions of PAC at the group level (q < 0.05) and is visualized as −log₁₀(q).

Histology

After the recording protocol finished, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% induction, 1.5–2% maintenance), and electrolytic lesions (30 µA for 20 s, negative polarity) marked electrode locations. After 48 h of recovery, animals were anesthetized to a surgical plane with ketamine (200 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and euthanized by transcardial perfusion with 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were postfixed overnight, transferred to PBS-azide, and sectioned coronally using a vibratome (World Precision Instruments, USA). Sections were Nissl-stained and examined with a Nikon Eclipse CI-L microscope to verify electrode placements.

Spike sorting

Extracellular signals were band-pass filtered (600–5000 Hz), and waveforms with negative or positive peaks crossing a fixed voltage threshold were extracted. Spike sorting was performed with Kilosort2 (https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort), and clusters were distinguished using principal-component features (Fig. S9). Putative single units were then classified as “activated” or “inhibited” based on the relative magnitude of their maximal positive versus negative peri-nosepoke firing-rate deviations. Firing rates were first Z-scored relative to non-nosepoke maze activity. Units were classified as “activated” when the absolute value of the maximal positive deviation exceeded that of the maximal negative deviation ( | Z|max > |Z|min), and as “inhibited” when the opposite was true ( | Z|min > |Z|max).

Linear mixed-effects models

We used linear and generalized linear mixed-effects models to relate session-level behavioral performance and neural metrics to session progression and decision strategy. Behavioral performance was quantified as session reward rate, modeled as the number of rewarded trials relative to the total number of valid trials using binomial generalized linear mixed-effects models with a logit link. To ensure consistency across longitudinal analyses, session-related effects include both a random intercept and a random slope for session by animal (1 + Session | Animal). This specification allows session-related effects to vary across animals and accounts for repeated measurements within subjects. Neural metrics were averaged at the session level and included functional connectivity (wPLI), directional interactions (spectral Granger causality), and cross-frequency coordination (phase–amplitude coupling), depending on the specific analysis. When connectivity metrics were adjusted for band-limited power, residualization was performed independently within each session by linearly regressing the connectivity measure against the corresponding band-limited power values, and the resulting residuals were used as predictors in subsequent models. Fixed effects included within-animal z-scored session, behavioral strategy, and, when appropriate, neural predictors or band-limited power covariates. All models included a random intercept and a random slope for session for each animal (1 + Session | Animal), allowing session-related effects to vary across animals and accounting for repeated measurements within subjects. Classification of sessions showed that “exploratory” sessions were characterized by fewer completed trials and longer trial durations than “exploitative” sessions, reflecting slower and less efficient behavior during early training stages. To evaluate whether these behavioral differences could account for the reported training effects, we first included trial number and mean running speed as covariates in a control mixed-effects model (Table S2). Although both variables were significantly associated with reward rate, their inclusion did not attenuate or eliminate the effect of training session. This indicates that improvements across sessions cannot be explained solely by differences in trial count or locomotor speed. Because these behavioral covariates did not account for the training effect, and to avoid unnecessary over-parameterization of subsequent neural models, they were not retained in later GLMMs focused on connectivity and phase–amplitude coupling metrics. Fixed effects were evaluated using Wald tests derived from the fitted mixed-effects models. Multiple comparisons across neural predictors were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Statistics and reproducibility

Because phase–amplitude coupling measures can vary substantially across animals due to factors such as electrode placement, local circuit engagement, and behavioral state, modulation indices were normalized within each animal (Z-scoring) prior to statistical analysis. This procedure reduces between-subject scaling differences and emphasizes relative modulation changes across behavioral conditions within each animal. Group-level comparisons were then evaluated using paired nonparametric tests, and statistical significance was assessed after correction for multiple comparisons.

Mixed-effects models were used to account for the hierarchical structure of the dataset (sessions nested within animals), allowing population-level inference while controlling for within-animal dependence. Group differences with a single independent factor were tested with one-way ANOVA when assumptions were met (normality, homogeneity of variance); if normality was violated, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test. For repeated observations, we applied repeated-measures ANOVA when residuals were normally distributed; when only two repeated levels were compared, we used a paired t-test, and if normality was violated we used a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (2 levels) or Friedman test (≥3 levels). Post hoc pairwise comparisons following any omnibus ANOVA were Bonferroni-adjusted. Linear associations between two continuous, normally distributed variables were evaluated with Pearson’s r; otherwise we used Spearman’s ρ. Statistical significance was set at two-tailed α = 0.05. All fitting and statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks).

Speed–spiking correlations

To test whether neuronal population activity tracked changes in running speed around the nosepoke (Fig. 6), we computed Pearson correlations between the average peri-nosepoke speed trace and the average peri-nosepoke firing-rate trace for each neuronal subpopulation (activated and inhibited units within each area). Statistical significance was assessed using a permutation approach. We generated surrogate distributions by randomly shuffling the temporal order of the peri-nosepoke speed trace (5,000 permutations), thereby breaking its alignment with neuronal activity while preserving its overall distribution. For each permutation, the correlation was recomputed. Two-sided p-values were calculated as the proportion of surrogate correlations that were equal to or larger in magnitude than the observed correlation. For cross-correlation analyses (lead–lag relationships), surrogate distributions were generated by circularly shifting the speed trace by a random amount (2000 iterations), preserving its temporal structure while disrupting its alignment with neuronal activity. The significance of peak cross-correlation values was assessed relative to this surrogate distribution.
