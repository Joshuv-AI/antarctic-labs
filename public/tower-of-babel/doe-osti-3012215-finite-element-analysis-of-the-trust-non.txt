Abstract
This paper builds on prior work conducted within the Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL)
Testbeds to Reduce Uncertainty in Simulations and Tests (TRUST) program. Specifically, it
builds on finite element (FE) modeling efforts for the TRUST program’s Nonlinear Dynamics
(ND) testbed. Historically, the FE model for the ND testbed has exclusively utilized a Lanczos
eigensolver that linearly extracts the system’s natural frequencies. This paper investigates the
Abaqus 2024’s explicit dynamic solver, which implements a central difference explicit solver.
The central difference method used in Abaqus can capture nonlinear material responses in
structural dynamic simulations making it suitable for the ND FE model. The ND testbed is a
metal and foam cylindrical stack-up in which the goal is to characterize the dynamic response of
the testbed due to foam thickness, density, and preload. The existing ND testbed uses two pieces
of SX358 cellular silicone foam, which have a highly nonlinear stress-strain relationship, to act
as springs for the central block of aluminum diagnosed by six accelerometers. The foam
specimens in the assembly are mechanically characterized via a quasistatic load cycling test, then
assembled into the ND testbed for dynamic testing. For the FE model the load cycling test is
used to bound the range of mechanical response with the first load and last unload stress strain
curves. Improving the finite element (FE) model of the testbed has potential applications for
structural health monitoring as it may be a method for simulating damage in structural foam in
the assembly. Damage is defined as a change in stiffness that affects the system’s performance.
Modeling the bounds of the foams’ mechanical response encapsulates the changes in stiffness
that alter the ND assembly’s response via damage. Results from comparing the frequency
response functions (FRFs) of foams with first load cycle and last unload cycle foam material
models show an average decrease of 0.064 g/N root mean square across all modeled tests.

Background and Introduction
Many structures utilize foams in situ. Seats in cars, packaging, and helmets have foam as a
material to help control the structural dynamics of a system under varying dynamic compressive
loads. Accurately characterizing the dynamics of foams is essential for developing predictive
capabilities that can be used to inform the degradation of performance for foams in structural
applications. Predictive modeling capabilities have the potential application to structural health
monitoring (SHM) as a way of simulating all stages of a structure’s life. From first
implementation to removal from service, modeling can inform when and how system stiffness

 2

changes. Changes in stiffness that affect the system’s performance from here on will be referred
to as damage.

Cellular silicone foams characteristically portray nonlinear elastic stress strain behaviors. Figure
1 shows how different stages of foam compression create its stress strain response. Cellular
silicone foams require a large strain (up to 90%) material model that accounts for nonlinear
elastic mechanical behavior. Additional features may include energy dissipation, and stress
softening effects [1]. Within Abaqus, hyperfoam is a material model that can easily capture
nonlinear elastic behavior. Hyperfoam can be implemented by inputting compressive stress strain
data into Abaqus, and Abaqus will do a Savitsky-Golay filtering method followed by least-
squares fit to the stress strain curve to form a hyperfoam material model [1].

 Figure 1: Example compressive stress strain curve of foam [1].

The Abaqus hyperfoam material is relevant because the finite element (FE) model for the
Testbeds to Reduce Uncertainty for Simulation and Testing (TRUST) Nonlinear Dynamics (ND)
testbed utilizes hyperfoam as its foam material model exclusively. The hyperfoam material
model is prescribed to the two SX358 cellular silicone foam samples in the assembly. The
TRUST program at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) aims to design, test, analyze, and
quantify uncertainty using low-complexity testbeds that isolate an engineering feature of interest.
This generally provides engineers with improved predictive modeling capabilities through
reduction of uncertainties. SHM benefits from the work in TRUST by improving damage
identification and modeling of complex systems either through test or simulation.

The ND testbed was used in 2022 to investigate the first peak frequencies of the ND assembly
for foam specimens with varying thickness, density, and compressive preloads [2]. A FE model
was used in addition to experimental data to investigate the usage of a hyperfoam material model

 3

in 2D and 3D simulations. These simulations began with an implicit preload analysis step that
carried into a modal frequency extraction step. A random response step was then used to apply
white noise excitation so that the model’s first axial mode could be extracted from the main peak
in the frequency response functions (FRF) in post processing [2].
In 2023, both the experimental setup and FE model were updated [4]. For the experiment, base
excitation was maintained, but instead of mounting the assembly to a shaker table it was
suspended via bungee cords and excited with a rod coupled to a shaker at the base. The updated
test setup is shown by Figure 3 and more thoroughly described in [4]. Sine dwell tests where
dynamic excitation was held at the peak excitation frequency were conducted on the assembly.
The purpose of the sine dwell tests was to study the changes in dynamic properties with varying
input. Regarding the FE model, updates to boundary conditions and model execution automation
were made. These improvements more accurately represented the new experimental setup and
provided relevant updates to moving the model into a repository with a WAVES [3] workflow.
As an aside, WAVES is a computational science and engineering workflow automation tool that
enables reproducible workflows and parametric studies. With the FE model, parameter studies of
axial first mode vs foam thickness, and axial first mode vs effective modulus at 20% strain were
conducted. The results showed correlation between each independent parameter and first axial
frequency. These studies helped justify the use of a reduced order model to more efficiently
handle system uncertainty [4].

This capstone project builds on the previous year’s TRUST efforts by combining improved
dynamic modeling and SHM to help enhance a robust, predictive capability by meeting the
following deliverables:

 o Modify existing finite element model (FEM) of the TRUST Nonlinear Dynamics
 testbed to allow for variation in the compliant material model and geometry, the
 initial compressive preload, and the testbed boundary conditions.

 o Perform modal analysis using the FE model from 0 – 2000 Hz to obtain preliminary
 estimates of the testbed frequencies and mode shapes.

 o Perform an explicit dynamic analysis using the FE model to map the applied
 experimental test profile (sine sweep, chirp, random, etc.) to the model.

 o Compare frequency response function (FRF) from the model to those obtained in the
 experiments. Determine if either are revealing nonlinear behaviors in the testbed.

Historically the FE model for the ND testbed has executed the following Abaqus analysis steps:

 1. Preload foam to desired loading or strain condition.
 2. Modal frequency extraction up to 3200 Hz.
 3. Random response with power spectral density (PSD) from test input applied.

 4

The modal frequency extraction step is a linear procedure that solves the classic eigenvalue
problem setup below (equation 1) with a Lanczos eigensolver [1].

                                    (−𝜔𝜔2 [𝑀𝑀] + [𝐾𝐾]){𝜙𝜙} = 0                                     (1)

This method fails to account for time varying properties such as dynamic softening or stiffening
of a material that may occur during a dynamic test. Consequently, materials like SX358 foam,
which have nonlinear stress strain relationships, are linearized about their current state when
modal frequency extraction is done. Moving to a nonlinear solver enables dynamic responses
affected by time varying properties to be more accurately modeled.
Abaqus Explicit dynamics step utilizes a central difference, direct integration method paired with
lumped, diagonal mass matrices to solve for displacement at the next increment using
displacement, velocity and acceleration at previous increments [1]. A simpler explanation of this
kind of solver is that it is an explicit solver. The basics of the central difference method are given
by Chopra’s overview of the central difference method and how it is adapted for nonlinear
problems [5]. Summarizing Dokainish and Subbaraj, limitations and benefits of explicit solvers
are that they require smaller time steps for stability, which typically leads to more data points to
solve for. Smaller step increments are the main reason why explicit solvers are best suited for
shorter duration inertial problems, like structural responses from blast or impact loading.
However, these smaller increments are solved much quicker than any implicit method. The main
benefit of an explicit solver is that it, “…enables one to evaluate the material physics which can
be used to generate new internal stresses for use in the solution at the next step. Also, very
complex material models can be easily incorporated using this approach.” [5]. These references
highlight the potential limitations and benefits associated with using the Abaqus central
difference explicit solver into the ND FE model.

Experimental Data Processing
Two types of tests were performed on the foam specimens in previous years’ work. The data for
these experiments were stored in a LANL owned experiment database. These experimental
results are important because they were used in the FE model to improve connection between
modeling and testing.

The first type of test was a quasistatic load cycle test, and the experimental setup is shown in
Figure 2. A single load cycle where foams were compressed past 40% strain and then
uncompressed was repeated three times, so that time dependent load, compression, gap closure,
stress, and strain data could be recorded. A machine compliance correction was also applied to
the datasets to remove the noise introduced by the test apparatus. The purpose of the load cycling
test was to use the data for characterizing the material’s mechanical behavior. An example of the
data gathered in a single test is illustrated by Figure 4.

The second test was a dynamic sine dwell test and is shown in Figure 3. The foam specimens
from the first test were assembled into the ND testbed and subjected to dynamic loading. The
dwell frequency centered at the testbed’s first natural frequency for the purpose of extracting
damping values from the assembly. In total, the 2023 ND experiments recorded 15 dynamic
tests (sine dwell) with 30 foam specimens i.e. two per dynamic test.

narrowed down to 7 for modeling because the 7 selected met two criteria. The foam specimens

To improve the testbed model, the material properties of the foam specimens used in each
dynamic test were linked to the FE model. The machine compliance corrected data for the load
cycles was further processed to be input for calibrated hyperfoam material models in Abaqus
2024. The peaks of the load cycles were identified, then each cycle was split apart. The first load
and last unload stress strain curves, shown in Figure 5, were extracted from the data and
smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter. The subsequent uniaxial stress strain curves were then
written to input files starting at 10% compressive strain. Starting the compressive strains at 10%
allows the hyperfoam fit to focus on higher strain values more relevant to the experimental setup,
which used a nominal 30% strain for both specimens during assembly. How Abaqus executes
this fitting process is thoroughly detailed by [1], [2].

Separating the data into the first cycle load and last cycle unload stress strain curves create
bounds for the material’s changing compressive stiffness. The start of the foam’s stiffness is
represented as the first load cycle. Conversely, the foam’s stiffness after the onset of damage is
represented by the last cycle unload, see Figure 5.

Modeling Approach
The ND testbed is modeled with a FE model and analytical model; results from both models are
compared to test data.

The FE model for the ND testbed is modeled in Abaqus 2024 and is illustrated by Figure 6. The
model has eight accelerometers modeled as cubes with fictitious densities that create a total mass
the same as the accelerometer it is representing. In addition, an elastic modulus of stainless steel
was assigned to each accelerometer to give it stiffness. Accurate stiffness for the accelerometers
is not important for the scope of this project. Six accelerometers are on the center mass (1-6) and
two on the base (7,8). Previous TRUST reports [2], [4] go into more detail on the material
properties and modeling for each component.

An undamped single degree of freedom (SDOF) model was also used to calculate natural
frequencies of foams for each dynamic test. The analytical model utilized the same compressive
stress strain data, thicknesses, and mass of the central block with accelerometers that the FE
model used to calculate cyclic frequencies. The analytical SDOF model helped to check the
accuracy of FE model.

 8

Finite Element Model
Changes made to the model and WAVES workflow centered on linking the test data for foam
load cycling to usable Abaqus material models. Foam thickness and density for each specimen
were added as parameterized values in the model for the dynamic tests being simulated. As a
result, for each dynamic test run simulated, independent of analysis step or boundary conditions,
the experimentally recorded density, thickness, first load, and last unload stress strain curves for
each foam specimen (top and bottom) were used as parameters in the model.

The material models in the FE model do not include damage parameters, energy dissipation
mechanisms, or temperature effects for the foams i.e. Mullins effect, viscoelasticity, or
temperature dependencies. These behaviors are assumed to be bounded by the first load and last
unload cycle hyperfoam models.

Boundary Conditions
Three different boundary conditions for the FE model were implemented and can be visualized
by Figure 6. The first boundary condition was the fixed base condition that was then used as base
excitation in a random response step. This boundary condition is what was used in years past and
showed very little to no impact on results when changed to a smaller fixed base boundary
condition to simulate the addition of a stinger [4].

The next boundary condition was the free-free (no boundary condition) case used for the free
vibration response.

The third boundary condition was fixed at the top surface of the top nut. The bottom nut’s bottom
surface was kinematically coupled to a reference node below the assembly. For this boundary
condition, the top nut constraint enforces zero displacement for the entire assembly.

Loading
There were 3 different loading schemes applied to the ND model after an initial preload was
complete. The initial preload was changed from a concentrated load on the threaded rod to an
Abaqus shrink fit step, which resolves the interference and forces the foam into the confined
space. This change was made because the concentrated load cause elements in the threaded rod
to invert, which made Abaqus crash when trying to handoff the model from the implicit solver to
the explicit solver. The shrink fit step allows the foam to be strained without inverting any
elements, making it possible to quickly solve the preload step in the implicit solver then handoff
the prestresses state to the explicit solver.

An Abaqus shrink fit step typically is used to resolve components in contact [1]. Essentially, the
components are strained throughout the analysis step so that by the end, the interference is
resolved and there is residual stress left over in the components equivalent to what strain was
needed to deform the interfering parts. This analysis step was parameterized so different initial
strains on the foam could be used by changing component locations to force shrink fits resulting
in different strain states. However, the dynamic experiments being simulated all specified a 30%
nominal strain condition, so the analyses were all conducted at a 30% nominal strain at the end
of the initial preload step.
From the initial preload step, three different analyses were conducted. First, a random response
simulation from a modal frequency extraction. The random response step uses a PSD applied as
a base excitation, and the magnitude of the FRF could be computed using the following equation:

The second load case applied a ramp function up to 20 g’s acceleration over 6.25 ms to the base
of the ND assembly and was then allowed to freely vibrate. The integrated acceleration responses
at each accelerometer’s nodes were averaged, so a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) on the net

under free vibration.

The third load case mapped the experimental inputs for each sine dwell test to the FE model. The
sine dwell tests each ran to 55 s and were sampled at 3200 Hz, Figure 7 illustrates a single
dynamic test run’s input force spectrogram . The duration and sampling frequency of each test
input produces around 176,000 data points. An input signal this long would be too many points
to try to simulate a response, so reduction in the driving force time history needed to be
completed. The purpose of reducing the input force time is to reduce computational costs of
simulating each dynamic test with an explicit solver. To shorten the input, the spectrogram of
each input was taken to visualize the frequency content vs time of the input (see Figure 7). In

seeing that the signals were stationary, a 2s long window was arbitrarily taken towards the end of
the test to get higher input forces. The PSD of the full-time history and shortened time history of
the force were taken and overlayed on each other to see if the shortened version still maintained
the same frequency content as the entire signal. This signal reduction workflow is illustrated by
Figure 8. For all tests, both signals’ PSD aligned suggesting no loss in frequency content by
using a shortened input.

Each shortened input was then brought into the Abaqus model as an amplitude time history to be
applied to the reference node coupled to the bottom nut during the explicit simulation. Exciting a
reference node coupled to the bottom nut surface uniformly distributes the forcing amplitude
history onto the assembly.

For the seven chosen dynamic test runs that were conducted in 2023 an explicit impulse loaded /
free vibration model, experimental data, modal / random response model, and analytical model
were compared to one another by extracting the first peak frequency from each response. The
responses plotted in Figure 9 are not all derived consistently, but the frequency at the peak value

frequency of the system.
For the impulse loaded model, and the experimental response the fast Fourier transform (FFT) of
the response acceleration was taken and plotted against frequency. Meanwhile, for the modal
model the FRF was derived from equation 2 and overlayed on Figure 9. The natural frequency
values computed by the analytical model were plotted on Figure 9 as vertical lines with a gray
area in between to represent the bounds of the natural frequency for different foam conditions.
The natural frequency calculated by the experimentalist [4] was added to the figure as the

Explicit Solver FRF Comparisons
Figure 10 shows the comparison of the magnitudes of the FRFs of the simulated loading cycles
with experimental data. Peaks and valleys at higher frequencies do appear to become apparent
for both first cycle load and last cycle unload material model. One apparent difference in the
material models is that the first cycle load model is consistently at higher magnitudes than the
last cycle unload model. This result is highlighted by comparing root mean squared (RMS)
values of the first load cycle material model’s FRF with the last cycle unload’s FRF RMS values.
The first cycle load FRFs were on average 0.064 g/N greater than the last cycle unload FRFs,
with no cases where the last cycle unload FRF RMS was larger. This result is likely from the
differences in material compressive stiffnesses impacting the FRF magnitudes, which shows how
the response function of a foam experiencing damage would change under more extensive
dynamic loading.
Figures 11 and 12 show the results by taking the Short Time Fast Fourier Transform (STFFT) of
the accelerometers responses averaged and plotted as a spectrogram for each dynamic test run.
The first load cycle dynamic test run spectrograms are like the last cycle unload spectrograms in
that they show how neither response varies with time. However, in overlaying the experimental

peak frequency as a red dotted line on each plot, it is apparent that the acceleration responses at
the peak frequencies are all larger in magnitude for both first load and last unload materials.

Conclusion
Overall, from the work done for this capstone the following deliverables were satisfied.

 o Modify existing finite element model (FEM) of the TRUST Nonlinear Dynamics
 testbed to allow for variation in the compliant material model and geometry, the
 initial compressive preload, and the testbed boundary conditions.

 17

 o Perform modal analysis using the FE model from 0 – 2000 Hz to obtain preliminary
 estimates of the testbed frequencies and mode shapes.

 o Perform an explicit dynamic analysis using the FE model to map the applied
 experimental test profile (sine sweep, chirp, random, etc.) to the model.

 o Compare frequency response function (FRF) from the model to those obtained in the
 experiments. Determine if either are revealing nonlinear behaviors in the testbed.

From the analysis, it was learned that the first axial frequencies can best be approximated with a
linear single degree of freedom model. The modal and explicit solvers perform similarly when
extracting peak frequencies from the acceleration responses. Comparing the FRF’s of the test
loaded explicit solver did reveal higher frequency features in the FRF’s, and a difference in RMS
FRF magnitudes between material models implemented. However, more work is needed to more
definitively characterize nonlinearities in the system.

Spectrograms of acceleration responses averaged across accelerometers 1 to 6 show higher
magnitudes at the first mode, but these frequencies did not shift with time for either the first load
or last unload material model. This indicates that the model is not capturing the hysteresis of the
material over time as the assembly is dynamically loaded and is thereby limited to bounding the
behavior with two material models.

Future work on the FE model may be to improve understanding of other mechanisms in the
assembly that could be contributing to the inaccuracies of preload in the foam. Damage
parameters and energy dissipation mechanisms of the foam should be implemented into the
material model. i.e. Mullins effect, and viscoelasticity. Incorporating Mullins effect would
eliminate the need to model two different materials as bounds for material hysteresis, and
viscoelasticity would help capture strain rate dependent loading. Higher fidelity nonlinearity
checks to better quantify differences in pristine and damaged materials may be an improvement
on the explicit dynamic solver implementation. Lastly, exploration of modeling environmental
factors, like temperature changes, and how they impact dynamic performance may be
worthwhile to investigate.
To conclude, this capstone project was an exciting opportunity to exercise concepts and skills
learned in all my classes at UCSD on a practical real-world project.
