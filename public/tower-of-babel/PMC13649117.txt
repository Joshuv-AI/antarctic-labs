Immunopeptidomics reveals conserved and abundant Francisella tularensis proteins as immunogenic CD4 T cell antigens in mice

Pavlina Laskova, Stanislava Porkertova, Ondrej Ballek, Lucie Balonova, Jana Pavloskova, Ales Neuwirth, Ivona Pavkova, Ivo Fabrik, Pavol Lukac, Luca Vannucci, Dominik Filipp, Jiri Stulik, Marek Link, Jason A Carlyon

Abstract

Human tularemia is caused by infection with the intracellular bacterium Francisella tularensis. The ability of the bacterium to cause a severe pneumonic form of the disease has led to concerns in the field of biodefense and efforts to develop a vaccine. F. tularensis LVS provides partial protection against fully virulent strains, with CD4+ T cells playing a significant role in this protection, however the recognized bacterial antigens remain largely unknown. To identify these antigens, we screened MHC class II peptides presented by infected dendritic cells using mass spectrometry-based immunopeptidomics. We identified 57 MHC-II peptides from 23 different F. tularensis LVS proteins. Most of these proteins were highly expressed in the bacterial proteome. Subsequent screening for IFN-γ responses identified ten peptides as immunogenic epitopes of CD4+ T cells following immunization of mice with F. tularensis LVS, thereby revealing previously unknown specificity and breadth of the induced immune responses. Five epitopes, derived from the antigens FTL_1678, DacD, DnaK, FTL_1498, and MinD, represented the dominant epitopes that elicited a response in at least 80% of immunizations. Finally, a peptide vaccine containing these epitopes proved to be immunogenic in mice and contributed to the early control of bacterial replication following challenge with the virulent F. tularensis FSC200 strain, although this alone was insufficient to ensure complete protection. Using immunopeptidomics, we have thus identified several immunogenic CD4+ T cell epitopes and antigens of F. tularensis LVS. Beyond their potential role in the protective immune response, the identified epitopes may also enable the development of tools for characterizing antigen-specific CD4+ T cells in vaccination studies or T cell biology research.
The establishment of effective T cell-mediated immunity is essential for the elimination of infections caused by intracellular bacterial pathogens. Identifying bacterial targets for T cells is important for determining the spectrum and specificity of immune responses, developing diagnostic tools, and designing vaccines. For the intracellular bacterium Francisella tularensis, the causative agent of tularemia, knowledge of the antigens and epitopes recognized by T cells is limited. In this study, we use immunopeptidomics to identify bacterial peptides presented by MHC class II from infected dendritic cells and a mouse model of tularemia to evaluate their immunogenicity. We uncovered several immunogenic epitopes and antigens of CD4+ T cells in the F. tularensis LVS strain and evaluated the protective potential of these epitopes against infection with the virulent F. tularensis FSC200 strain. Our study provides new insights into the specificity, dominance, and breadth of CD4+ T cell immune responses induced by F. tularensis LVS.

Introduction

Francisella tularensis is a Gram-negative intracellular bacterium and the etiologic agent of the zoonotic disease tularemia. Human tularemia, caused by F. tularensis subsp. tularensis and holarctica, poses a health problem primarily in endemic areas and during occasional outbreaks. Although it is not a very common type of lethal infection, early diagnosis and antibiotic treatment are essential to overcome the infection without consequences. The natural reservoirs of the infection are primarily hares and small rodents, and transmission occurs through direct contact with infected animals, or via vectors such as ticks, mosquitoes, and biting flies []. F. tularensis subsp. tularensis is a CDC-designated Tier 1 select agent. Its low infectious dose and high mortality in the pneumonic form brought this pathogen to biodefence concerns and consequently to efforts of vaccine development []. The live vaccine strain (LVS) of F. tularensis subsp. holarctica is the most advanced vaccine candidate and the only one with human clinical data []. However, LVS remains in the investigational stage because the genetic basis of its attenuation is not fully defined, and it also provides only limited protection against aerosol infection by the most virulent strains of subsp. tularensis. Despite these limitations, studies using LVS have yielded key insights into protective immune responses against F. tularensis [,]. In parallel, mouse models have been widely used to study tularemia pathogenesis, as they resemble human infection in many respects. Like humans, mice can become infected by various routes and develop disseminated disease with granulomatous lesions. Mice can also be successfully vaccinated with LVS, but protection is limited against high-dose challenge with F. tularensis subsp. tularensis and wanes over time. However, significant differences exist between mouse models and human infection, particularly regarding virulence and lethality. Strains of F. tularensis subsp. tularensis, subsp. holarctica, and subsp. novicida are fatal to mice by various routes of infection; for the first two subspecies, a dose of as few as 4–10 bacteria is sufficient to cause death [,]. In contrast, infections with subsp. holarctica are rarely fatal in humans, and subsp. novicida is considered largely non-virulent in immunocompetent individuals [].

F. tularensis primarily infects phagocytic cells and, after a brief stay in the phagosome, multiplies in the nutrient-rich cytosol and spreads throughout the host []. As with other intracellular bacteria, the T cell-mediated immune response plays a key role in protecting against F. tularensis infection []. Although this has been shown mainly in mice, persistent T cell proliferative responses and cytokine responses have also been reported in patients with tularemia or in persons vaccinated with LVS [,]. In mice, αβ T cells were essential for survival and bacterial clearance, but the relative importance of each subset varies depending on the vaccine strain, challenge strain, and route of infection used. For the LVS strain, CD4+ or CD8+ T cells alone were sufficient to resolve primary intradermal infection and subsequent lethal intraperitoneal challenge []. MAIT (mucosa-associated invariant T) cells also supported mice survival following intranasal inoculation with LVS but were unable to clear the bacteria in the absence of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells []. Both T cell subsets were essential for providing protection against infection with different strains of F. tularensis subsp. tularensis in mice that had been immunized with the LVS strain under various conditions [–]. The ability of immune lymphocytes or individual populations of CD4+, CD8+, and CD4-CD8- T cells to suppress LVS growth in macrophages was also confirmed in vitro []. Further studies have shown that the efficacy of T cell-mediated control of intracellular Francisella growth reflects the protective potential of the vaccine and that the greater protection provided by the live attenuated SCHU S4-ΔclpB vaccine compared to LVS can be attributed to more effective T cell activation [–].

Although CD8+ and CD4+ T cells play a key role in protecting against F. tularensis infection, the Francisella antigens and epitopes targeted by these cells have not been precisely defined, and only a few studies have addressed this issue. In mice, genome-wide prediction of MHC (major histocompatibility complex) class I peptides and their subsequent screening led to the identification of 82 Francisella CD8+ T cell epitopes [,]. The CD4+ T cell epitopes of Tul4 (hereafter referred to as LpnA), GroEL, DnaK, IglB, IglC, and 50S ribosomal protein L3 were identified through screening of T cell hybridoma reactivity against a panel of recombinant proteins [–]. It was found that the LpnA epitope is recognized during both the acute and memory phases of primary LVS infection [], whereas the DnaK, GroEL, and IglB epitopes were insignificant, subdominant epitopes []. In humans, T cell immune response to F. tularensis may involve a broad range of antigens. The 15 HLA-I and 24 HLA-II epitopes stimulated IFN-γ secretion in PBMCs of patients who previously had tularemia []. Although no single epitope appeared immunodominant, HLA-II epitopes from IglD, chitinase family 18 protein, and release factor glutamine methyltransferase were recognized in more than 43% of subjects. The three T cell stimulating peptides of LpnA were also identified in humans with previous exposure to F. tularensis []. Recently, ten bacterial HLA-I peptides have been identified from LVS infected human monocyte-derived dendritic cells, but these peptides were not evaluated for immunogenicity [].

Mass spectrometry (MS)-based immunopeptidomics has enabled the identification of peptides bound to MHC class I and II proteins that are generated during the processing and presentation of both endogenous and foreign antigens. It is based on the isolation of MHC-peptide complexes using allele- or pan-specific monoclonal antibodies, the purification of antigenic peptides, and sequencing via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), followed by bioinformatics analysis [,]. Continuous development of the technology have enabled its translational applications [] including those in the field of vaccine research []. With a focus on bacterial pathogens, immunopeptidomics has been used to discover novel T cell antigens with a protective potential for Chlamydia species [,], Mycobacterium species [,], Salmonella enterica [] or Listeria monocytogenes [,].

Given the importance of F. tularensis LVS in studies of protective immunity and the role of T cell-mediated immunity in eliminating the infection, we decided to identify Francisella antigens that are recognized by T cells, specifically CD4+ T cells, and that could thus be involved in the protective immune response. To achieve this goal, we used immunopeptidomics to identify bacterial peptides presented by MHC-II molecules of infected bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). For infection, we used the F. tularensis LVS strain or a mutant strain with a deletion in the gene encoding disulfide oxidoreductase (LVSΔdsbA) []. Deletion of this gene in various Francisella strains results in bacteria with impaired escape from phagosomes and intracellular replication, prolonged residence in vacuoles bearing markers of the lysosomal degradation pathway, and the ability to activate BMDCs [–], suggesting that it may interact more strongly with the antigen processing pathway []. In our study, we identified 57 Francisella peptides from 23 different proteins, which originated mainly from a highly abundant fraction of bacterial proteome. Most of these proteins were previously unknown MHC-II antigens. We further validated the identified bacterial peptides as epitopes of CD4+ T cells in a mouse model of tularemia. Ten peptide epitopes exhibited immunogenicity following primary infection with F. tularensis LVS, with five of them representing dominant epitopes. Finally, the protective potential of the proposed peptide vaccine against infection with the F. tularensis FSC200 strain was tested.

Results

Bacterial MHC-II peptides represent rare and low abundant population of the immunopeptidome

To identify bacterial peptides presented by MHC class II molecules, we analyzed the immunopeptidome of BMDCs infected with the F. tularensis LVS strain or the LVSΔdsbA strain. During the initial setup of infection, we assessed bacterial growth within cells, as well as the effects on viability and the expression of surface markers. Although both strains replicated in BMDCs, the growth of the LVSΔdsbA strain was significantly lower than that of the LVS strain, with average logarithmic increases in colony-forming units of 0.64 and 2.94, respectively, after 22 hours (Fig A in ). The viability of BMDCs typically ranged from 50-65% 20–24 h postinfection when a multiplicity of infection of 5 was used. BMDCs upregulated the surface activation marker CD86 20–24 h after infection with either Francisella strain, but we were unable to detect differences in MHC-II expression between infected and uninfected cells (Fig A in ).

For immunopeptidomics, replicates of infection with both Francisella strains as well as control samples of lipopolysaccharide-stimulated BMDCs were prepared, and MHC-II associated peptides were identified by LC-MS/MS after immunoprecipitation of I-Ab complexes from cell lysates. The identified MHC-II peptidomes are based on the two main datasets (infected and control samples) and the two small-scale data (only LVS-infected samples) (). In the main datasets, we identified 5,570 and 5,227 mouse MHC-II peptides with an intensity range spanning four orders of magnitude and, importantly, 47 and 39 putative Francisella peptides, respectively. Bacterial peptides accounted for approximately 0.8% of all identifications and 0.1% of the total peptide intensity in individual samples (), making them rare and low-abundance species in the MHC-II peptidome of infected BMDCs. Given the complexity of identifying non-specifically cleaved peptides and the low intensity of many bacterial peptides, which can lead to spectra with incomplete fragment ion series or fragments from co-isolated peaks, we performed manual curation of the identifications. To assess the specificity of bacterial peptide identifications, we utilized peptide extracted ion chromatograms (XICs), as these traces should be detected in infected but not uninfected control samples (, ). As a result, we excluded two bacterial peptides with low ion scores and five identifications for which peptide-spectrum matches were assigned in control samples, leading to 79 confirmed peptides. For 73 of these peptides, XICs were detected only in infected samples. Although interferences from control samples were observed in the XICs of the remaining 6 peptides, their nonspecific nature and low intensity justified acceptance of peptide identification. We thus identified 79 confident Francisella peptides, representing 57 non-redundant sequences and 24 distinct sequence clusters. The sequence determination of selected representatives from each cluster was further supported by spectral similarity analysis. High spectral similarity was obtained for 20 peptides (PCC ranging from 0.83 to 1) for which synthetic peptide spectra were available (Fig B in ), while moderate similarity was obtained for 3 peptides (PCC 0.46-0.76) for which spectra were predicted (Fig C in ). In the LVSΔdsbA strain, we identified more bacterial peptides presented on MHC-II than in the LVS strain (), suggesting improved antigen presentation. Interestingly, based on a matching of peptide features, we were able to detect many of these peptides in the immunopeptidome of LVS as well. This indicates that the peptide repertoire overlaps to a significant extent and that the differences are primarily quantitative, but further experiments would be needed to confirm this.

A total of 57 different Francisella peptides were identified (). As shown in , most of these peptides were 16–17 amino acids in length, consistent with the length distribution of endogenous mouse peptides and characteristic of MHC-II associated peptides []. 80% and 7% of the bacterial peptides were predicted to be strong and weak I-Ab binding peptides using NetMHCIIpan binding thresholds of 1% and 5%, respectively (). Similarly, 64% and 22% of the endogenous peptides were classified into these categories. The binding motif derived from the bacterial peptide sequences () revealed a preference for proline at position P4 and was similar to the binding motif found in endogenous peptides () or previously published for this allele []. The bacterial peptides exhibited similar biochemical properties to endogenous peptides, suggesting that they were generated in the BMDC antigen processing and presentation pathway and thus represent genuine MHC class II presented peptides.

Epitope regions are conserved across Francisella strains and display low sequence homology to eukaryotic proteins

Fifty-seven unique Francisella peptides were assigned to 24 epitope regions derived from 23 different antigens (). The term “epitope region” refers to the part of the protein from which these peptides, often detected as overlapping N- or C-terminal sequence variants, originated. Thirteen of these epitope regions were thus characterized by a single peptide, while the others were characterized by two or more peptides. The conservation of peptide sequences from F. tularensis LVS was analyzed in other strains of this bacterium to determine the possibility of cross-reactivity. The genomes of various F. tularensis strains are highly similar at the nucleotide level [], and the sequences of most peptides were indeed identical in the subsp. holarctica strains FSC200 and OSU18, the subsp. tularensis strain SCHU S4, and the subsp. novicida strain U112 (, ). Only a few peptides contained a single amino acid substitution, and the epitope region 63TAATSAAAPKVNLAEEISG81 of the AccB protein contained an additional insertion of three amino acids in its homologue from strain U112. None of these sequence differences, whether located outside or within the binding core, had a significant effect on the predicted binding of these mutated peptides to I-Ab compared to the LVS peptides (Table A in ). Importantly, the bacterial peptides showed either no or minimal sequence homology with the mouse peptides. Only three epitope regions showed sequence homology of 75%, 64%, and 62%, with up to five amino acid mismatches allowed in their sequences (). This is a prerequisite for eliciting a T-cell response, as highly homologous bacterial peptides may be suboptimally recognized by naïve T cells []. The identified bacterial peptides thus have the potential to induce specific T cell reactivity in mice and to exhibit cross-reactivity in vaccination and challenge studies using heterologous Francisella strains.

Francisella MHC-II antigens

Twenty-three antigens were identified as sources of Francisella MHC-II peptides, and all of them except the DnaK chaperone protein, were defined by a single epitope region (). Of these antigens, only DnaK, IglC (intracellular growth locus subunit C), and FopA1 have been described to date as antigens for CD4+ T cells. It is interesting to note that while the identified epitope 53IEGGEDVTKADSATAAASVIR73 of IglC was identical to a previously described epitope [], this was not the case for the DnaK epitopes 591KALEEAFAPIAQKAYAEQ608 and 629VVDADFEDVEDDKK642, as these epitopes originated from different regions of the protein than those previously described []. In contrast, our analysis did not lead to the identification of peptides from the conserved hypothetical lipoprotein (LpnA), which contains a characterized CD4+ T cell epitope []. To find functional associations of identified Francisella MHC-II antigens, we annotated them in the Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG) [] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [] databases. The largest group of proteins, containing the ribosomal proteins RpsJ, RplQ, RplA and MetG, was annotated in the COG category translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis (). Several proteins were also involved in metabolic processes and were divided into the categories of lipid transport and metabolism (FabF, FabD, AccB; fatty acid biosynthesis pathway), energy production and conversion (SucC, Mdh; citrate cycle), and amino acid transport and metabolism (Ggt, GlyA; cyanoamino acid metabolism pathway). Three proteins (FTL_1678, DacD, FopA1) were classified into the cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis category. Orthologs of these proteins have been identified as MHC class II antigens in other bacterial pathogens, such as Chlamydia [,] and Mycobacteria species [,], Salmonella enterica [], Listeria monocytogenes [], and Bordetella pertussis [], and some of them have been found to be immunogenic. In summary, we identified 20 novel MHC class II antigens of Francisella whose functional profiles suggested involvement in translation, lipid metabolism, and cell wall biogenesis.

Francisella MHC-II antigens originate from a highly abundant fraction of the bacterial proteome

We performed a proteomic analysis of the LVS and LVSΔdsbA strains to determine whether there was a relationship between the discovered MHC-II antigens and the proteome. We ranked the 1,131 identified proteins () by intensity to express their relative abundance in the proteome. MHC-II antigens were enriched in the high-abundance fraction of the proteome (), and this observed pattern was statistically significant (). To identify changes induced by the dsbA gene deletion, we compared the proteomes of LVSΔdsbA and LVS using label-free quantitation. In this analysis, we confirmed the deletion of the dsbA gene and detected several proteins with regulated expression, but none of them belonged to the MHC-II antigens, which in fact lay close to the line indicating equal expression in both strains (). Regarding the bacterial proteome, we have thus demonstrated that the identified Francisella antigens are highly expressed and that the availability of these antigens is the same in both strains used for infection.

Francisella MHC-II peptides elicit time-dependent reactivity in immune splenocytes and lymph nodes

Sublethal infection with F. tularensis LVS induces protective immunity against lethal challenge with homologous or heterologous Francisella strains, involving various populations of αβ T cells, including CD4+ T cells, and the cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α [,]. In the following experiments, we therefore sought to determine whether any of the MHC-II peptides identified in vitro represent an immunogenic target for CD4+ T cells. For this purpose, C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously immunized with LVS, immune lymphocytes were isolated and stimulated ex vivo with corresponding synthetic peptides. IFN-γ production was measured as a marker of CD4+ T cell activation. Twenty-one bacterial peptides (), representing 20 different epitope regions, were selected for synthesis. This selection was based exclusively on data from mass spectrometry, and in cases where multiple peptides were identified for a given epitope region, the most frequently sequenced or most abundant peptide was selected.

For initial testing, whole-cell suspensions from spleens of immunized mice were stimulated with peptides arranged in three different pools (subset of 15 peptides, Table B in ) and the number of IFN-γ-producing cells was measured by ELISpot. Importantly, peptide pools (PP) elicited a statistically significant response in immune splenocytes collected 4 weeks postimmunization (p.i.) (Fig D in ). In the following experiments, splenocytes were isolated at 3, 4, and 5 weeks after immunization and stimulated with PPs to gain insight into dynamics of the response (). The highest number of IFN-γ-producing cells was detected 3 weeks p.i., followed by a gradual decrease with time after immunization. Regardless of the time interval examined, a general order of reactivity of PP-1 ≈ PP-2 < PP-3 was observed, suggesting that PP-3 is most effective in stimulating IFN-γ-producing cells. No bacteria were detected in the spleens and lymph nodes (LN) at 3 weeks p.i. as determined by CFU counting of plated organ suspensions. To verify that CD4+ T cells were the responding subpopulation, peptide stimulations were performed in cocultures of purified CD4+ T cells and BMDCs, which were used as antigen presenting cells. These experiments confirmed the production of IFN-γ by CD4+ T cells and revealed similar PPs reactivity profiles as in whole splenocytes (). IFN-γ-producing cells provoked by peptide stimulation were detected in immune cells only (splenocytes or CD4+ T cells), and not in cells from control PBS-receiving mice (, ), verifying that the measured responses were specific and due to immunization with LVS. These experiments were performed with PP-3 that showed the most significant and reproducible responses in previous assays. Significant accumulation of IFN-γ as well as TNF-α was also confirmed in the supernatants of immune splenocytes stimulated with PP-3 by ELISA (, ). The temporal profile of immune responses was further evaluated in whole-cell suspensions and in CD4+ T cells from LNs of immunized mice. Except for lower response levels, the hierarchy and reactivity profiles of PPs were similar to those observed in the spleen (Fig D in ). In conclusion, all PPs elicited IFN-γ secretion in both LVS-immune splenocytes and LNs. This secretion was highest at 3 weeks p.i. and was mediated by CD4+ T cells. The PP-3 stimulated the most IFN-γ-producing cells under all conditions tested, indicating that it contains the most potent stimulatory peptide(s).

Identification of immunogenic CD4+ T cell epitopes of F. tularensis LVS

Based on the results obtained with PPs, the potential of individual Francisella peptides to induce IFN-γ-producing cells in LVS-immune lymphocytes was examined. Spleen lymphocytes were collected 3 weeks after immunization unless otherwise stated. The screening was performed using whole splenocytes or isolated CD4+ T cells in coculture with naïve B cells. B cells were used instead of BMDCs in all subsequent assays because they showed lower background levels of IFN-γ while maintaining a specific response and thus increased the sensitivity of the ELISpot measurements (Fig E in ). Net reactivity of individual peptides (peptide - control induced IFN-γ-producing cells) measured in splenocytes or CD4+ T cell cocultures for 4 independent immunizations are shown in and , respectively. Representative data for each cell type are provided in Fig F in . In splenocytes, 7 peptides induced a statistically significant stimulation of IFN-γ-producing cells in at least one of the immunizations. These peptides, along with 3 others, also induced reactivity in CD4+ T cell cocultures. Selected reactive peptides were subsequently assayed with lymphocytes from control mice that received PBS. No significant induction of IFN-γ-producing cells was detected in either splenocytes () or CD4+ T cell cocultures (), confirming that the reactivity of the peptides with immune lymphocytes is specific and a consequence of immunization with the LVS. In conclusion, the ability of the peptides to stimulate both splenocytes and CD4+ T cells confirms their role as immunogenic epitopes of CD4+ T lymphocytes in vivo.

A total of 10 peptides were identified as immunogenic epitopes of CD4+ T cells in at least one of the 9–10 immunizations performed (, ). Based on the frequency of statistically significant response as a criterion, we divided the peptides into three groups- nonimmunogenic (never positive), minor epitopes (positive in at least one, but max. in 50% of immunizations), and major epitopes (positive in more than 50% of immunizations) []. Using this definition, we found 5 minor and 5 major CD4+ T cell epitopes of Francisella LVS. Minor epitopes are FTL_1042-VD-17 (VTIAYKGTTPVIAYEDD), FabF-IQ-20 (IGMGGFAAARALSTRNDDPQ), RpsJ-TE-16 (TGAQVKGPIPLPVRKE), RplA-VG-17 (VAVFAKGPAADAAKEAG), and RplQ-IE-16 (IELVDRPQVEEAADEE). For major epitopes, the general order of reactivity was FTL_1678-YK-16 (YPSDFGYSNPDSIRAK)> DacD-RK-17 (RSYIYNFPEAYKVYDDK)> DnaK-AE-16 (ALEEAFAPIAQKAYAE) ≈ FTL_1498-KE-15 (KINTTNAPQAIGSYE) ≈ MinD-IR-16 (IIVTNPEVSSVRDSDR). The reactivity of individual peptides also explains the hierarchy of reactivity among peptide pools, as the peptides FTL_1498-KE-15 and FTL_1042-VD-17, the peptide MinD-IR-16, FabF-IQ-20 and RpsJ-TE-16, and the peptides FTL_1678-YK-16 and DnaK-AE-16 were components of PP-1, PP-2, and PP-3. In our tests, we further confirmed the previously characterized peptide epitope LpnA-RD-14 (RLQWQAPEGSKCHD) [] as a significant epitope of F. tularensis LVS. Compared to LpnA-RD-14, the reactivity of FTL_1678-YK-16 was superior, and the epitopes DacD-RK-17, DnaK-AE-16, and FTL_1498-KE-15 induced IFN-γ at least as effectively, confirming the significance of the newly discovered epitopes.

Finally, the phenotype of antigen-specific CD4+ T cells was determined by FACS using a mixture of the most reactive peptides (PP-4) (Table B in ). As expected, and as confirmed by the ELISpot assay, PP-4 induced strong IFN-γ secretion only in LVS-immune CD4+ T cells (). FACS analysis supported this finding by showing that specifically CD4+ T cells isolated from the spleen of LVS-immunized mice respond to PP-4 by producing IFN-γ (). Further characterization revealed that the main source of IFN-γ is predominantly the effector CD44+ CD62L- subpopulation of CD4+ T cells (, Fig G in ). By analyzing whole spleen, we also showed that, compared to PBS-control mice, LVS infection did not affect the frequency of different CD4+ T cell subpopulations (naïve CD44- CD62L+ vs. central memory CD44+ CD62L+ vs. effector T cells CD44+ CD62L-) in splenocytes, which were the source of CD4+ T cells for all subsequent coculture assays (Fig G in ).

A vaccine containing Francisella peptide epitopes is immunogenic in mice

In the next experiments, we evaluated the immunogenicity of the peptide antigens themselves, which is a prerequisite for assessing their functional potential. Mice were therefore immunized with a peptide vaccine containing five major peptide epitopes of Francisella (FTL_1498-KE-15, MinD-IR-16, FTL_1678-YK-16, DnaK-AE-16, DacD-RK-17) (). Since these peptides are CD4+ T cell epitopes 15–17 amino acids in length, we analyzed the induced cellular response as an indicator of successful immunization. In lymphocytes from the spleen () or lymph nodes (Fig H in ) of immunized mice, restimulation with each peptide led to a statistically significant number of IFN-γ-producing cells, and none of the peptides dominated this response. This reactivity was specific and resulted from immunization with the peptide vaccine, as no significant responses were detected in lymphocytes from control mice that received the same adjuvant but without peptides (). It should be noted that some immunized mice exhibited low but statistically significant reactivity or increased reactivity to a single peptide (Fig I in ), which led to variability in responses. Interestingly, the IFN-γ response to selected peptides in splenocytes from vaccinated mice was, on average, twenty times higher than in mice immunized with F. tularensis LVS (), suggesting quantitative differences in the induced T cell responses. Furthermore, the peptide vaccine induced a Th1-type response, as evidenced by the significant production of not only IFN-γ but also IL-2 and TNF-α following peptide stimulation of splenic lymphocytes (-). Overall, we provide evidence that the selected Francisella peptide epitopes are immunogenic and that the peptide vaccine induced a strong polyepitope cellular response.

Protective efficacy of peptide vaccine against F. tularensis FSC200 infection

After the peptide vaccine induced a Th1-type cellular immune response, we tested its protective potential against infection with the F. tularensis subsp. holarctica FSC200 strain. This strain is highly virulent to mice infected by any route, and the lethal dose after subcutaneous inoculation is < 5 bacteria []. The subcutaneous route of infection, which is more significant in terms of public health risks than in terms of biological defense threats associated with pulmonary infection, was used in the study for reasons of biological consistency, since the screening of immunogenic T cell epitopes was based on a subcutaneous infection model. The five peptide epitopes contained in the vaccine also have conserved sequences in the FSC200 strain, which is essential for their recognition during infection and for triggering an immune response. Four weeks after the start of vaccination, mice from the control group and mice vaccinated with the peptide vaccine were subcutaneously infected with 53 CFU of F. tularensis FSC200, and the course of the infection was monitored (). The pattern of weight loss differed significantly between the vaccinated and control mice, even though the differences at individual time points were not statistically significant. The vaccinated mice thus exhibited a more gradual decline in weight than the control mice, suggesting a slower progression of the infection (). Vaccinated mice also showed a slight delay in mortality (days 6–9 postinfection, median survival 7 days) compared to control mice (days 6–7 postinfection, median survival 6 days), although this difference was not statistically significant (). Consistent with these trends, peptide vaccination reduced the bacterial burden in organs, as evidenced by a decreased bacterial count in both the spleen and liver on day 4 postinfection (, ). In conclusion, immunization of mice with the peptide vaccine induced cellular immunity against T cell peptide epitopes, which contributed to early control of the bacterial load and reduced disease severity, although this alone was insufficient to provide full protection against lethal infection with the virulent FSC200 strain.

Discussion

The induction of T cell-mediated immunity is essential for overcoming infections caused by intracellular bacterial pathogens. This applies to primary infection with F. tularensis LVS and is of critical importance in secondary infection with the subsp. tularensis strain, where any alterations in T cell subpopulations lead to failure of vaccine-induced immunity [–]. On the other hand, the vaccine’s ability to elicit a more potent overall T cell response [–], or to induce antigen-specific CD8+ or CD4+ T cells [,], enhances its protective effect. Although the key role of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in protection has been clearly defined, the Francisella antigens and epitopes recognized by these cells have not yet been well characterized.

The contribution of CD4+ T cells to protection against F. tularensis infection appears to be just as important as that of CD8+ T cells [,], despite the cytosolic lifestyle of the bacterium. Our goal was therefore to identify Francisella antigens that are recognized by CD4+ T cells in a mouse model of tularemia and that could play a protective role. To achieve this, we applied MS-based immunopeptidomics to identify bacterial peptides presented by MHC-II molecules of BMDCs previously infected with F. tularensis LVS or the LVSΔdsbA strain. Our analysis yielded 57 unique Francisella peptides originating from 24 epitope regions of the 23 different proteins. The characteristics of bacterial peptides, such as sequence length distribution, predicted binding to I-Ab, and binding motif, were similar to those of endogenous peptides, confirming that they were generated as a result of antigen processing and presentation in BMDCs. Interestingly, the length distribution of bacterial peptides was slightly shifted toward longer peptides compared to endogenous peptides. A similar pattern has been described for HLA-II peptides in activated human dendritic cells and B cells []. We hypothesize that longer bacterial peptides may reflect infection-associated activation of BMDCs, whereas shorter endogenous peptides likely originate predominantly from uninfected cells. Although we initially began our experiments with F. tularensis LVS, the altered intracellular phenotype of the LVSΔdsbA strain led us to include it in the study, as we hypothesized that it might interact more strongly with the MHC class II antigen processing pathway. Furthermore, it has been shown that the LVS strain suppresses MHC-II surface expression in macrophages [], though we did not observe this in BMDCs. The use of the LVSΔdsbA strain indeed helped us identify more bacterial MHC-II peptides, and we demonstrated that this cannot be attributed to changes in antigen availability at the proteome level. Bacterial peptides were detected in the MHC-II peptidome of infected BMDCs as rare and low-abundance compounds. The number of BMDCs used for immunoprecipitation was a critical parameter in our experiments for identifying a larger number of immunopeptides. However, improving the sensitivity of the workflow in terms of both sample preparation and LC-MS/MS analysis would allow for a more in-depth characterization of the immunopeptidome and the identification of a broader spectrum of bacterial peptides, which, in our opinion, is not yet complete.

We identified 23 antigens, of which only IglC, DnaK, and FopA1 had previously been described as MHC-II antigens in F. tularensis. Based on their functional annotation, the proteins FTL_1678, DacD, and FopA1 were classified under the COG category “cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis.” These proteins are therefore accessible not only for presentation on MHC-II molecules and recognition by T cell receptors, but also for interaction with B cell receptors and the induction of an antibody response. Most of the other proteins were associated with functional categories related to translation, nutrient acquisition, and energy production, which are essential for the sustained replication of Francisella in the cytosol []. We also demonstrated that the identified Francisella MHC-II antigens originate from a highly abundant fraction of its proteome. A similar finding has been reported for MHC-II antigens of M. tuberculosis []. This likely reflects the limited sensitivity of the immunopeptidomic approach, which favors the detection of peptides from more abundant proteins. However, many highly abundant proteins were not detected as MHC-II antigens, confirming that other parameters, such as peptide binding to the MHC-II molecule or the accessibility of the epitope region within the protein structure, also play a role in determining the outcome of antigen processing and presentation [].

Francisella MHC-II peptides were identified using an in vitro infection model. However, the spectrum of presented antigens may vary during in vivo infection due to changes in the bacterial proteome profile or the type of antigen-presenting cells. We therefore used a mouse model of LVS-induced tularemia to investigate the immunogenicity of the identified peptides. In LVS-immune lymphocytes from the spleen or LNs, IFN-γ-producing cells were induced after stimulation with mixtures of bacterial peptides, and we demonstrated that this response was mediated by CD4+ T cells and was specific for immune lymphocytes. Regardless of the type of lymphocytes or time interval examined, a general order of reactivity of PP-1 ≈ PP-2 < PP-3 was observed, indicating that the PP-3 contains a potent stimulatory peptide(s). This was later confirmed, and two highly reactive peptides, FTL_1678-YK-16 (YPSDFGYSNPDSIRAK) and DnaK-AE-16 (ALEEAFAPIAQKAYAE), were found to be responsible for this reactivity. PP-3 also induced significant production of IFN-γ and TNF-α in the culture supernatant of immune splenocytes, suggesting that these antigen-specific T lymphocytes produce key cytokines involved in protection against infection []. Using PPs, we demonstrated that the highest number of IFN-γ-producing cells was detected 3 weeks p.i., followed by a gradual decline in the response in the fourth and fifth weeks after immunization. At these time intervals, the onset of the T lymphocyte contraction phase is likely due to limited antigen availability []. Indeed, no bacteria were detected in the spleen and LNs as early as 3 weeks after immunization.

Based on further screening, we identified 10 peptides, derived from an equal number of antigens, as immunogenic epitopes of CD4+ T cells in F. tularensis LVS. The reactivity of these peptides exhibited a similar profile in both splenocytes and CD4+ T cells, was specific to LVS-immune lymphocytes, and was primarily mediated by the effector subpopulation of CD4+ T cells. We classified the discovered Francisella immunogenic epitopes into minor and major epitopes based on the frequency with which they were recognized across immunizations. Similar to the case of VACV CD8+ T cell epitopes [], we observed a positive correlation between the strength of the immune response and the frequency of recognition across immunizations. Thus, only a few immunizations were sufficient to detect the major epitopes that exhibited the highest reactivity or a very stable response in our experiments. Conversely, a greater number of immunizations is required to detect even less significant epitopes, some of which exhibited reactivity only rarely.

Immunization with F. tularensis LVS induces a protective immune response against infection with virulent strains of F. tularensis [,]. The high reactivity and frequency of recognition make the major epitopes and their source antigens interesting targets for evaluating their role in protection. The epitopes of the FTL_1678, DacD, and FTL_1498 antigens thus elicited a response in all immunizations, the DnaK epitope in 90%, and the MinD epitope in 80% of immunizations. At the same time, the FTL_1678 epitope was superior, and the DacD, DnaK, and FTL_1498 epitopes were at least as effective as the LpnA epitope in inducing IFN-γ-producing cells. A comparison with LpnA is appropriate, as it is a well-known antigen for B cells and T cells in both mice [] and humans [], and has already been evaluated as a component of subunit vaccines [–]. The FTL_1678 (LD-carboxypeptidase) and DacD antigens are involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis, envelope integrity, and virulence of F. tularensis [–], making them attractive antigens for immunological intervention from a functional perspective as well. Interestingly, the DacD homolog in Bordetella pertussis has been found to be an immunodominant antigen of human CD4+ T cells []. The functions of the other major antigens are not yet known. FTL_1498 has so far been described as a secreted protein [], and MinD as a virulence factor []. Of the identified minor antigens, only FTL_1042 (peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase) has been tested as a vaccine component to date []. Furthermore, epitopes of the ribosomal proteins RplA, RpsJ, and RplQ elicited a response in 10–20% of immunizations and thus represent subdominant CD4+ T cell epitopes in F. tularensis LVS. Ribosomal proteins from other bacterial pathogens have previously been identified as CD4+ T cell antigens, but their reactivity depended on the specific pathogen [,]. Regarding the DnaK, IglC, and FopA1 antigens, which have been evaluated as components of subunit vaccines in several studies [,,,,], only the DnaK epitope showed immunogenicity in our study. It is worth noting that the FopA1 peptide elicited an immune response in 20% of immunizations but did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons.

The functional potential of the identified peptide epitopes was ultimately evaluated under conditions of F. tularensis infection. In these experiments, we successfully immunized mice with a peptide vaccine containing five major peptide epitopes of F. tularensis LVS, which elicited a cellular immune response involving IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the induced immune response contributed to early control of the bacterial load and reduced disease severity following infection with the virulent F. tularensis FSC200 strain, although this alone was insufficient to provide full protection. From the perspective of immunity mediated by antigen-specific CD4+ T cells, we are considering two possible causes of the limited efficacy of vaccine-induced immunity. First, the protective potential of all five epitopes may not be fully exploited during infection. Although these epitopes are conserved in the virulent FSC200 strain, they may not all be effectively presented during infection and therefore remain unrecognized. Second, and this is perhaps the decisive reason, F. tularensis FSC200 is highly virulent in mice [], and protection mediated solely by CD4+ T cells may not be sufficient on its own, as has been demonstrated in studies with virulent strains of subsp. tularensis [–]. It must be acknowledged that mice are highly susceptible to infection with F. tularensis. For this reason, Fisher 344 rats are currently considered a more predictive and suitable animal model for evaluating vaccine efficacy [,,]. Although T cell epitopes may differ among MHC alleles, the antigens entering the MHC pathway may be similar across different host species; therefore, their immunogenic and protective potential could be evaluated in a more suitable animal model in future studies.

In summary, using MS-based immunopeptidomics, we identified MHC class II-presented peptides from F. tularensis LVS and confirmed that these are immunogenic epitopes for CD4+ T cells in a mouse model of tularemia. We demonstrated that CD4+ T cell responses were elicited against various bacterial epitopes, thereby revealing the previously unknown breadth and specificity of T cell responses induced by F. tularensis LVS. Several epitopes capable of eliciting a strong CD4+ T cell response were discovered, and their protective potential in a peptide vaccine was evaluated. The identified epitopes may also enable the development of tools for characterizing antigen-specific CD4+ T cells in vaccination studies or T cell biology research.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experiments using mice were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Care and Use at the Military Faculty of Medicine, University of Defence (Czech Republic), under project No. 5/21 (4284/2021-MZE-18134).

Bacteria

F. tularensis subsp. holarctica LVS (strain FSC155), subsp. holarctica strain FSC200 (both provided by Prof. Forsberg, Sweden), and deletion mutant on dsbA gene in the LVS strain [] (LVSΔdsbA) were grown and harvested within a BioSafety Level 2 containment facility. The bacteria were thawed from a frozen stock and cultured on McLeod agar supplemented with bovine hemoglobin (#212392) and IsoVitaleX (#211876, both from Becton Dickinson) at 37°C for 24 hours. The LVS and LVSΔdsbA strains were subsequently passaged onto a new agar plate and cultured overnight under the same conditions. On the day of infection of mice or BMDCs, the bacteria were resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (#D8537, Sigma-Aldrich) and serially diluted to the desired inoculum dose. The actual number of bacteria was determined by plating serial dilutions and enumerating colony-forming units (CFU). For proteomics, following the initial 24 h culture on McLeod agar, the bacteria were inoculated in Chamberlain medium and grown at 37°C to OD600 of 0.7.

Mice

Specific-pathogen-free 6–8 week-old female C57BL/6J mice (Velaz, CZ) were housed under conventional conditions with ad libitum access to food and water and were allowed to acclimatize for at least 14 days before starting experiments. At the indicated time points, animals were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation.

Generation and infection of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs)

Femoral and tibial bone marrow cells were isolated from female C57BL/6 mice and BMDCs were generated as previously described [] with minor modifications. Approximately 2 × 107 bone marrow cells were plated in a 15-cm tissue culture dish containing 20 ml RPMI-1640 medium (#R8758) supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS) (#F4135), 50 U/ml penicillin and 50 μg/ml streptomycin (#P0781, all from Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were incubated overnight at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Next, non-adherent cells were transferred to a new dish and cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 5% v/v supernatant from Ag8653 cells transfected with cDNA encoding murine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor. Cells were then passaged every 2–3 days and on day 9 of culture the suspension cells were harvested for experiments. The 1 × 108 BMDCs in 25 ml of RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS were infected with F. tularensis LVS or the LVSΔdsbA strain at a multiplicity of infection of 5 (bacteria: cells). Infection was carried out at 37°C in a 50 ml Falcon tube for 30 minutes with gentle shaking. After this, 2 × 107 cells were transferred to a 15-cm Petri dish with an additional 15 ml of RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and incubated for 22–24 h under standard conditions. The cells were collected by gentle scraping with a cell scraper, washed with PBS and stored as a dry pellet at -80°C for immunopeptidomics. In some experiments, control cells were treated with 0.5 μg/ml lipopolysaccharide from E. coli O55:B5 (#L6529, Sigma-Aldrich).

Generation of immunoaffinity beads

Monoclonal antibody Y-3P (ATCC HB-183) against mouse I-Ab was purified from the supernatant of hybridoma cells (Moravian-Biotechnology, CZ) and used for construction of immunoaffinity beads. The 3 mg of antibody was bound to 1 ml of beads of Protein G Agarose Fast Flow (#MILL16–266, Millipore) by rotating the suspension in Econo-Column (#7371007, Bio-Rad) for 2 h at 4°C. To cross-link the antibody to beads, 20 mM dimethyl pimelimidate (#D8388-1G, Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.2 M triethanolamine pH 8.5 (#90279, Sigma-Aldrich) was used for 1 h at room temperature. The reaction was quenched by incubation with 0.1 M ethanolamine pH 8.2 (#398136, Sigma-Aldrich) for 90 min. Finally, following washing with 0.2 M acetic acid (#5.43808.0100, Merck), the immunoaffinity beads were stored in 50 mM Tris pH 7.6 (#T6066, Sigma-Aldrich), 150 mM NaCl (#73575, Fluka) at 4°C until use. The success of individual steps in the procedure was checked by SDS-PAGE.

Isolation of MHC-II complexes and associated peptides

The protocol was adopted from previously described procedures [,] with modifications. Frozen pellets of 3–9 × 107 BMDCs were briefly thawed, resuspended in 1.5 ml of lysis buffer and rotated at 4°C for 1 h to solubilize. Lysis buffer contained 1% w/v CHAPS (#28300, Pierce), 50 mM Tris pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM iodoacetamide (#I6125, Sigma-Aldrich), 5 mM EDTA (#E5134, Sigma-Aldrich) and protease inhibitors- 5 μg/ml aprotinin, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, 5 μg/ml pepstatin A (#78432, #78435, #78436, Pierce) and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (#10837091001, Roche). Cell lysates were then cleared by two rounds of centrifugation at 16,000 g for 15 min at 4°C and further precleared by incubation with 0.1 ml Protein G beads for 1 h. To capture the MHC-II-peptide complexes, the precleared lysates were rotated with 0.1 ml immunoaffinity beads at 4°C overnight. Next day, the immunoaffinity beads were transferred to empty Macro SpinColumn (5 μm frit, #74–3820, Harvard Apparatus) and washed with 0.8 ml 0.1% CHAPS, 150 mM NaCl in 50 mM Tris pH 7.6, with 1.5 ml 450 mM NaCl in 50 mM Tris pH 7.6 and with 1.5 ml 50 mM Tris pH 7.6 buffer. Finally, the beads were eluted with 0.5 ml 10% v/v acetic acid. The eluate was incubated for 10 min at 65°C and the MHC-II peptides were isolated using a two-step solid phase extraction. The eluate was loaded first on 15 mg of equilibrated PolySulfoethyl A resin (#BMSE1203, PolyLC) packed in Macro SpinColumn. The column was then washed sequentially with 15 mM ammonium phosphate pH 2.7 (#09709, Fluka) containing 30% or 15% acetonitrile (ACN) (#1.00030.2500, Merck) and the peptides were released with 0.27 M KCl (#60128, Fluka), 15 mM ammonium phosphate pH 2.7, 15% ACN, while retaining the MHC class II chains on column. Following dilution to 5% ACN with 0.15% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) (#80457, Sigma-Aldrich), the peptides were loaded on Empore C18-SD 4 mm/1 ml column (#66871-U, Sigma-Aldrich), washed with 5% methanol (#1.06007.2500, Merck), 0.1% TFA and eluted using 0.2 ml of 65% methanol, 0.1% TFA. The dried peptides (Concentrator Plus, Eppendorf) were stored at -20°C until analysis. In some experiments, immunoaffinity purification was monitored by SDS-PAGE with immunodetection using anti-I-Ak β-chain rabbit serum (generous gift from Paul A. Roche, NIH, Bethesda, MD) as a primary detection antibody.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Samples were analyzed using an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system interfaced to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer via a Nanospray Flex ion source (all instruments from Thermo Fisher Scientific). For MHC-II peptidomics, peptides were dissolved in 2% ACN, 0.1% TFA and 30–50% of the sample volume was loaded onto PepMap100 C18, 3 μm, 0.075 × 20-mm trap column (#164535, Thermo Scientific). The peptides were separated at 0.25 μl/min and 40°C on a PepMap RSLC C18, 2 μm, 0.075 × 250-mm column (#164536, Thermo Scientific) using conditions as follow: 4% B for initial 7 min, 4–32% B in 93 min, 32–55% B in 23 min, wash with 90% B for 10 min and re-equilibration at 4% B for 21 min, with 0.1% FA (#56302, Fluka) in water as phase A and 80% ACN, 0.1% FA as phase B. Positive ion full scan MS spectra from 350-1,200 m/z range were acquired on a 1 × 106 target ion population in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 70,000. Precursor ions with charge state range 2–6, intensity threshold of 3 × 104 and not fragmented during previous 30 s were admitted for higher-energy collisional dissociation. Tandem mass spectra were acquired at resolution of 35,000 and with other parameters set to: 1 × 105 for AGC target, 170 ms for maximum ion time, 1.6 m/z for quadrupole isolation window, 27 for normalized collision energy, and m/z 140 for fixed first mass. For proteomics, approx. 0.3 μg of peptides were loaded onto Symmetry C18, 5 μm, 0.18 × 20-mm trap column (#186008821, Waters) and separated at 0.25 μl/min and 45°C on a Peptide BEH C18, 1.7 μm, 0.075 × 250-mm column (#186008795, Waters) using conditions as follow: 6% B for initial 7 min, 6–42% B in 90 min, 42–60% B in 18 min, wash with 90% B for 15 min and re-equilibration at 6% B for 26 min. Full scan MS spectra from 350-1,650 m/z range with a resolution of 70,000 were acquired in the Orbitrap and precursor ions with charge state range 2–5 and not fragmented during previous 25 s were admitted for HCD. Tandem mass spectra were acquired at resolution of 17,500 and with other parameters set to: 1 × 105 for AGC target, 100 ms for maximum ion time, 1.6 m/z for quadrupole isolation window and 27 for normalized collision energy.

Sample preparation for proteomic analysis

LVS and LVSΔdsbA bacteria, grown in quadruplicate, were harvested from Chamberlain culture medium at an OD600 of 0.7 by centrifugation and processed as previously described [] with minor modifications. The pellets were washed, dissolved in 2% w/v sodium deoxycholate (SDC) (#D5670, Sigma-Aldrich), and lysed at 70°C for 5 min. After incubation with 0.1 unit/ml of benzonase endonuclease (#E1014, Millipore) at room temperature for 30 min to hydrolyze nucleic acids, the lysates were clarified by centrifugation for 10 min at 14,000 g. Total protein was determined by bicinchoninic acid assay (#BCA1, Sigma-Aldrich). An aliquot of 50 μg was dissolved in 2% SDC, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (#PN09830, Sigma-Aldrich) and the proteins were reduced with 4 mM dithiothreitol (#D5545, Sigma-Aldrich) at 60°C for 30 min and alkylated with 12 mM iodoacetamide (#I6125, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The excess of iodoacetamide was quenched by addition of dithiothreitol, the concentration of SDC adjusted to 0.5% with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 1 μg of rLys-C and 1 μg of trypsin (#V167A and #V511A, Promega) were added for overnight digestion at 37°C. After digestion, the mixtures were acidified with hydrochloric acid and the precipitated deoxycholic acid was removed by extraction into ethyl-acetate (#34858, Sigma-Aldrich). The samples were then cleaned on Empore C18-SD column and eluted peptides were dried and stored at -20°C until analysis.

Peptides identification

Peptides and proteins were identified using the Proteome Discoverer v2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the integrated search tool Mascot v2.4.1 (Matrix Science). Mass spectra from immunopeptidomic analyses were searched using a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm, a fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, methionine oxidation as a dynamic modification, and protein digestion without enzyme restriction. Tandem mass spectra were deisotopized (IMP-MS2 spectrum processor) before searching to reduce their complexity. The database contained sequences from Mus musculus (55,021 entries, Uniprot), F. tularensis subsp. holarctica strain LVS (1,678 entries, Uniprot), and general contaminants (256 entries). Detection, alignment, and matching of peptide features across samples were enabled with default settings. Searches were performed against target and decoy databases and re-evaluated with Percolator v3.02.1 []. Identifications at the peptide-spectrum match level, peptide group level, and protein level meeting a percolator q-value threshold of 0.01 were considered for further analysis. In addition to the q-value, peptide ion scores and XICs in infected and uninfected control samples were also considered in the manual validation of bacterial peptide identification. The identified contaminants and class II peptides from serum proteins of the culture medium were filtered out from the data. For proteomics, a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm, a fragment mass tolerance of 0.025 Da, trypsin cleavage specificity (unlimited by proline), and up to two missed cleavage sites were used to search mass spectra in the sequence database (F. tularensis LVS appended with general contaminants). Oxidation (M), deamidation (NQ), carbamidomethyl (N-terminus), Gln to pyro-Glu (N-term Q), and carbamidomethyl (C) were used as dynamic and static modifications. Identifications were controlled at an FDR of 0.01. For comparative analysis of LVS and LVSΔdsbA strains, samples were normalized to total peptide amount and the obtained protein ratios were evaluated for differential expression using a background-based t-test with FDR control at 0.05. Quantification of at least two peptides and a 20% change were used as additional criteria. To estimate the relative abundance of proteins in the proteome, the protein intensity used for scoring was calculated as the average intensity of the three most abundant peptides for a given protein [] and its median from four replicates was used.

Bioinformatics

To verify the identified bacterial peptide sequences, spectral similarity analysis was performed using the Universal Spectrum Explorer tool []. A mass tolerance of 15 ppm was used for the annotation of b- and y-fragments and for peak matching. To characterize the MHC-II peptidome, the identified peptides (unique sequences) were analyzed for several properties. Predictions of peptide binding to the I-Ab allele were performed using the NetMHCIIpan-4.3 [] program with default settings and eluted ligand prediction mode. GibbsCluster-2.0 [] was used to generate the binding motif for peptides with one cluster, or 1–2 clusters allowed for bacterial and mouse peptides, respectively. The sequence motif length was 9, trash cluster was enabled, and other parameters were left at default values. Peptides of F. tularensis LVS were also evaluated for sequence conservation in different Francisella strains using BLASTP [] in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [] database and for their homology to mouse peptides using the PEPMatch tool [] hosted on the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB). For functional annotation of F. tularensis LVS proteins, the FTL gene names were converted to the corresponding FTT gene names in F. tularensis subsp. tularensis SCHU S4 before submitting them to the Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG) [] and KEGG databases. The proteomics data were deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository [].

Synthetic peptides

Peptides were commercially synthesized with a purity greater than 90% and supplied as lyophilized TFA salts (ProImmune, UK). Peptides were dissolved in sterile PBS to obtain theoretical 1 mM stock solutions and stored at -40°C. The actual concentration of peptides was determined from their peak areas after LC-UV analysis in combination with sequence-specific extinction coefficients []. For this purpose, the peptides were analyzed on a Poroshell 120 SB-C18 column, 2.1 × 100 mm (#685775–902, Agilent) using a mobile phase of water-ACN-0.1% TFA and monitored at 205 nm (Alliance 2695, Waters). The peptide Angiotensin I (#A9650, Sigma-Aldrich) served as a standard, the concentration of which was determined from the absorbance at 280 nm and ε 1290 M-1 cm-1 of tyrosine using a NanoPhotometer (Implen). Then, working solutions of the peptides were prepared with a concentration of 0.2 mM and stored in aliquots at -40°C. Synthetic peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using Q Exactive to confirm their sequences and obtain reference spectra for spectral similarity analysis.

Immunization of mice

Mice aged 8–11 weeks, age-matched in the experiment, were infected subcutaneously in the flank with 3 × 104 CFU of F. tularensis LVS diluted in 0.2 ml PBS. Spleens and lymph nodes were harvested 3–5 weeks later, and lymphocytes were prepared for screening of T cell reactivity. Control mice received sterile PBS alone.

To evaluate the immunogenicity of T cell epitopes, a peptide vaccine containing five Francisella epitopes (FTL_1498-KE-15, MinD-IR-16, FTL_1678-YK-16, DnaK-AE-16, DacD-RK-17) was formulated and immunization was performed according to a previously described procedure []. Mice aged 13–16 weeks were primed with 25 μg of each peptide emulsified in Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (CFA), which consisted of Incomplete Freund’s Adjuvant (#F5506, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 5 mg/ml of heat-inactivated Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra (#231141, BD Difco). The emulsion was prepared by mixing equal volumes of CFA and peptides dissolved in PBS using two glass syringes connected by a capillary. The emulsion (total 0.2 ml) was administered subcutaneously into two dorsal injection sites per mouse. A booster injection (0.2 ml) was delivered intraperitoneally 14 days later, containing 25 μg of each peptide, 50 μg of high molecular weight poly(I:C) (#tlrl-pic, InvivoGen), and 100 μg of anti-CD40 antibody (clone FGK4.5/FGK45, #BE0016–2, BioXcell). Control mice received the same formulations but without peptides.

Protective study

The vaccinated and control mice were treated as described above. Fourteen days after the booster injection, mice were given a lethal dose of 53 CFU of F. tularensis subsp. holarctica strain FSC200 (in 0.2 ml PBS) subcutaneously in the flank and monitored for weight loss and survival. To determine bacterial burden, spleens and livers were aseptically isolated 4 days after infection and homogenized through a 70 μm mesh filter (#93070, SPL Life Sciences) in 2 ml PBS for spleen and 4 ml PBS for liver to form single cell suspensions. The suspensions were then serially diluted and 50 μl of each dilution was plated in triplicate on McLeod agar plates supplemented with penicillin (100 units/ml). Colonies were counted after one week of incubation at 37°C and bacterial load was expressed as CFU per gram of organ.

Preparation of lymphocytes

Spleens were aseptically removed from mice and homogenized through a 40 μm mesh filter (#93040, SPL Life Sciences) in 2 ml of PBS per spleen. Cells were then pooled from 3-4 mice (LVS immunization experiments) or left separate (peptide immunization experiments), and erythrocytes were lysed with ACK Lysing Buffer (#A1049201, Gibco). Following filtration through a 40 μm filter, the single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were either used in ELISpot, ELISA, and FACS assays or subjected to CD4+ T cell purification. This was performed by negative selection via magnetic column using a CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (#130-104-454, Miltenyi Biotec) according to manufacturer ́s instructions. In some experiments, lymph nodes (mandibular, axillary and subiliac) were also isolated and processed as above, except for ACK treatment, and lymphocytes were used in ELISpot and FACS assays directly or after enrichment of CD4+ T cells. Purified B cells, which were used in cocultures, were obtained from splenocytes suspension (no ACK-treatment) of syngeneic age-matched naïve mice by negative selection separation using the EasySep Mouse B Cell Isolation Kit (#19854, Stemcell Technologies) following manufacturer ́s instructions. The purity of isolated cell populations was verified by flow cytometry and the viability of lymphocytes entering the subsequent assays was > 90%.

ELISpot

IFN-γ production of peptide-stimulated lymphocytes was detected using an ELISpot Plus-HRP (#3321–4HPW-10) or ELISpot Flex-HRP (#3321-2H, both from Mabtech) in conjunction with MultiScreenHTS IP Filter Plate (#MSIPS4W10, Millipore) according to manufacturer’s instructions. For the assay, lymphocytes were resuspended in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin (50 U/ml and 50 μg/ml, respectively). For stimulation of whole-cell suspensions from spleens and lymph nodes, 5 × 105 cells in 200 μl were plated per well and 5 μl of peptide pools (Table B in ) or 10 μl of individual peptides were added to reach a final concentration of 5 or 10 μM, respectively. The cells were then cultured under stable conditions at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 20–22 h. After washing according to the manufacturer’s instructions, the spots were developed by adding the HRP substrate tetramethylbenzidine (#3651-10, Mabtech), after which the plates were washed with deionized water and allowed to air-dry. Cytokine secretion in mice immunized with the peptide vaccine was determined similarly except that due to the unknown magnitude of the response, two different cell numbers were plated, ranging from 1-5 × 105 cells. IL-2 and TNF-α secretion was also determined from these lymphocytes (ELISpot Flex #3441-2H and #3511-2H, Mabtech). For coculture-based assays, 2 × 105 purified B cells in a volume of 100 μl were plated per well and allowed to settle down at the bottom of the well for 30 min in an incubator. Then, peptides were added as described above and immediately thereafter a 3 × 105 purified CD4+ T cells in 100 μl was carefully added. In initial experiments, BMDCs at seeding density of 3 × 104 cells/well were used in cocultures as antigen presenting cells instead of B cells. Subsequent cultivation conditions, plate washing and spot-development steps were the same as described above for whole-cell suspensions. In an assay, four wells were usually used per peptide and 4–6 wells for unstimulated control cells. In addition, peptide RLQWQAPEGSKCHD of Francisella LpnA (FTL_0421) was used as a positive peptide []. Tryptic peptide YAIAVNDLGTEYVHR of mouse Glutaminase (Gls, acc. D3Z7P3) which was predicted as I-Ab non-binder or Ovalbumin (323–339) peptide (#O1641, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a negative peptide. Finally, the wells containing 4 μg/ml of Leucoagglutinin (PHA-L) (#L4144, Sigma-Aldrich) served as technical controls in the assays. Plates were scanned and the number of spot-forming cells was determined using an ImmunoSpot S6 Analyzer (Cellular Technology Limited) (assays in -, and Fig D in ) or ASTOR 2 (Mabtech) (assays in -, , -, and Figs E, F, H, I in ).

FACS analysis and intracellular IFN-γ staining

To measure intracellular IFN-γ production, 3 × 104 BMDCs or 2 × 105 isolated B cells (preparation described above) were supplemented with 5 μM final concentration of peptide pools and cocultured with 3 × 105 CD4+ T cells in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C for 24 h. CD4+ T cells were isolated from LNs or spleens of control or immunized mice. Brefeldin A (#B7450, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to a final concentration of 5 μg/ml for the last 4 h of incubation. Cells were then stained for the desired surface markers and fixed according to the manufacturer's protocol using an Intracellular Fixation and Permeabilization Buffer Set (#88-8824-00, eBioscience) followed by staining with anti-IFN-γ antibody. Measurement was performed using a BD FACSymphony A5 Cell Analyzer and analysis was performed with FlowJo software (Becton, Dickinson and Company). Antibodies against CD4 (#100453), CD44 (#103032), TCRβ (#109220), B220 (#103227), MHC-II (#116507), IFN-γ (#505810, all from Biolegend), CD62L (#741230, BD biosciences) and fixative cell viability Dye 506 (#65-0866-18, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used.

ELISA

Single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were plated in clear flat-bottom 96-well MaxiSorp plates (#442404, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at amount of 8 × 105 cells per well, stimulated with 5 μM peptide pools and cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 48 h. The concentrations of IFN-γ and TNF-α in supernatants were quantified using Mouse DuoSet ELISA (#DY485–05 and #DY410–05, Bio-Techne, R&D Systems) with absorbance reading at 450 nm using a Synergy H1 Reader (BioTek Instruments).

Statistical analysis

Data visualization and analyses were performed in Prism v9.5.1. (GraphPad Software). The types of tests used to assess statistical significance of the data are described in the figure legends. For ELISpot assays of individual biological experiments, SFCs of peptide- or PPs-stimulated cells were compared with unstimulated cells (background) using one-way ANOVA followed by multiple testing correction. The net average peptide reactivity was obtained by subtracting the average background reactivity. The number of SFCs in peptide-immune lymphocytes was normalized to 1.5 × 105 cells.
