Tailoring implementation strategies for acute coronary syndrome quality improvement program at primary care level in east, west, and central China
Authors: Can Liu, Yixuan Wu, Zongbin Wang, Siwei Xie, Zhi-Jie Zheng, Shuduo Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) remains a leading contributor to cardiovascular disease burden in China, and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is the most severe ACS. To improve early identification and timely treatment of STEMI patients, the chest pain unit (CPU) program was established to strengthen referral pathways to qualified facilities. This study explores key barriers and facilitators to the CPU implementation and proposes context-specific strategies to optimize its delivery and scale-up. We conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews in three purposively selected, representative counties across eastern, central, and western China. A total of 61 key informants from 36 township hospitals participated in the study. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and thematically coded guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.0 using Atlas.ti 9. Implementation strategies were mapped and refined using Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change. We identified 46 barriers and 50 facilitators, spanning all five domains of CFIR. Technical deficiencies, residents’ lack of health-seeking awareness, financial difficulties, inefficient awareness campaign, and limited professional knowledge are respectively the most significant barriers for the five domains. We developed a three-pronged strategy framework, including innovation optimization, external empowerment, and internal improvement, to inform future practice. Accordingly, the most urgent strategies encompass enhancing technical capacity, expanding financing mechanisms, empowering communities, implementing mass media campaigns, strengthening patient adherence through structured follow-up, and providing continuous practical training. We recommend that the proposed strategies should be taken into full consideration to facilitate timely detection and intervention of ACS in the primary healthcare context.

This qualitative study is the first process evaluation of an ongoing national quality improvement program for acute coronary syndromes (ACS) in a primary care setting.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of global death (Kaptoge et al. 2019). The incidence of it has been increasing rapidly over the past 10 years (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) 2022). With an aging population, China is confronted with escalating CVD burdens. Acute coronary syndromes (ACS), the major contributor of CVD deaths, stands as the primary cause of mortality among both urban and rural populations in China (Zhou et al. 2019, National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases and The Writing Committee of the Report on Cardiovascular Health and Diseases in China 2025). ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is the most severe and time-sensitive ACS (Yildiz et al. 2021). Presently, percutaneous coronary intervention plays a vital role in STEMI treatment, and the treatment timeliness significantly impacts patient outcomes (Ozaki et al. 2022). Previous studies have found that the emergency response time in rural areas of China significantly surpassed the urban counterparts, primarily resulting from insufficient emergency resources, personnel scarcity, and inadequate inter-agency collaboration, which led to a higher mortality rate for ACS, especially for STEMI in rural settings (Feng et al. 2016, Xia et al. 2019).

Diverse interventions have been implemented in the primary care context to respond to the challenges and improve the quality of ACS care (Ruiz-Pérez et al. 2019). However, the patient outcomes of most interventions remained suboptimal although some quality improvement efforts were beneficial (Bhagwat et al. 2016, Thompson et al. 2019). Many interventions fail to achieve their original goals for organizational change and outcome improvement due to various barriers from individual level (such as resistance to change), organizational level (such as cultural inertia and weak leadership), and health system level (such as inadequate infrastructure) (Ferlie and Shortell 2001, Zhou et al. 2022). Previous research has suggested that the translation from project into practical outcomes is more likely to succeed when informed by an assessment of the possible facilitators and barriers (Grimshaw et al. 2012). Implementation science, which provides the mechanism about how the interventions affect outcomes by formative evaluations, plays an important role in the success of quality improvement programs (Bauer et al. 2015). Yet, limited studies have investigated the influencing factors within the implementation process of the quality improvement programs focusing on the primary ACS care.

In a rural context, prehospital and in-hospital delays tend to be longer owing to the lower level of health literacy among rural residents, the limited medical resources, and the lack of professional skills of primary medical staff (Wang et al. 2020a, 2020b, Hu et al. 2022). In 2016, the Chinese Cardiovascular Association (CCA) launched the Chest Pain Center (CPC) in hospitals above Grade II with the primary goal of reducing system-level delays in the management of STEMI patients through process optimization, the establishment of green channels, and regional networking. In March 2020, the chest pain unit (CPU) was launched to further the establishment of regional network for the diagnosis and transfer of STEMI patients in primary healthcare (PHC) settings, and thus better the patient care outcomes of STEMI. This program encourages public PHC institutions that undertake the initial management of STEMI cases (manage ≥10 acute chest pain patients annually) and have established routine referral relationship with nearby CPC to undergo the CPU accreditation process. The accreditation has four dimensions: (i) organizational structure and responsibility assignment, (ii) equipment configuration and treatment process, (iii) collaboration mechanism and public education, and (iv) training and assessment mechanism (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1). Building on this initiative, the CCA introduced the electrocardiogram (ECG) Network Program to further strengthen early diagnosis of STEMI in 2021. The program provides PHC institutions with wearable ECG devices which are easier to operate and the results of which could be uploaded online and diagnosed by doctors in the higher-level hospitals. The CPU program, synergizing with the ECG Network Program, contributes to the building of a regional collaborative emergency service system for ACS, especially for STEMI. At present, more than 7000 CPUs have been launched in China (China Heart House and Chest Pain Professional Committee of National Clinical Research Center for Interventional Medicine 2024).

Despite the widespread establishment of the CPU program, there have been few studies investigating the enablers and barriers to the program implementation. This study therefore aims to identify facilitators and barriers in the operational process, and further provide optimization strategies for future better implementation of the program.

Methods

Study design and site

This qualitative study employs a multistage cluster sampling design to select CPUs in China. In the first stage, the national CPU headquarters and project experts jointly selected three representative counties—Laixi (eastern), Gong’an (central), and Leishan (western)—through a purposive sampling process informed by expert consultation and strategic considerations. In the second stage, all township health centers within each selected county were included, resulting in a total of 36 CPUs for in-depth investigation (Supplementary Table S2). Counties were selected based on several factors, including the varying levels of CPU development, regional disparities across the eastern, central, and western areas, geographical and environmental features, and the availability of health resources. The final selection was determined through structured expert discussions involving members of the China CPU headquarters and the project implementation team. The chosen locations include Laixi City in Qingdao City, Shandong Province, Gong'an County in Jingzhou City, Hubei Province, and Leishan County in Qiandongnan Miao and Dong Autonomous Prefecture, Guizhou Province. All three are county-level administrative regions characterized by predominantly rural populations, and represent different levels of socioeconomic development and degree of urbanization (Supplementary Figure S2). These three areas encompass the multifaceted rural landscape in China, and could enhance the generalizability of the research findings.

Study participants and sampling

We conducted semi-structured key informant interviews with both CPU directors and CPU coordinators from August 2023 to February 2024. The CPU director, who holds a key decision-making role, and the CPU coordinator, who plays a transactional and operational role, were invited to explore potential obstacles, enabling factors, and possible solutions in the implementation of the CPU program. Interview guides were tailored according to the roles of the participants: for CPU directors, the focus was on the organizational context, implementation processes, evaluation, and feedback mechanisms; for healthcare providers, the emphasis was placed on their understanding of the program, behavior changes, implementation fidelity, and sustainability. Prior to the interviews, all participants provided written informed consent, including permission for recording.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews. All interviews were conducted face-to-face by trained qualitative members (S.Z. and Z.W.) and were recorded by note-takers. The interview guide was developed based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), and the guide’s final version was produced after full discussion among the interview team and consultation for experts (Supplementary Text S1). Prior to the formal interviews, all interview guides underwent pilot testing and refinement. The survey was administered in 36 PHC institutions with CPUs, with 61 key informants participating in the interviews, and the response rate was 84.7%. The length of each interview was approximately 15–90 min. The interviews were digitally recorded and conducted in Chinese to ensure effective communication, with key messages confirmed with participants to prevent misunderstandings.

Data analysis

All recordings were transcribed verbatim for analysis. ATLAS.ti 9.0 was used for theme coding. A well-trained author (C.L.) analyzed the transcripts and used a hybrid thematic analysis approach to code the data guided by CFIR. CFIR, a well-established theoretical framework in implementation science, comprises five dimensions and 48 constructs (Supplementary Table S3) (Damschroder et al. 2022). To ensure interrater agreement, a second coder (S.Z.) reviewed all the codes and the disagreements were fully discussed among the coding team (C.L., S.Z., Z.W., and Y.W.) until consensus was reached. After initial coding, the codes were then refined and consolidated to be more clear and concise. The codes were translated into English and back-translated into Chinese to ensure conceptual consistency. The final codes are context-specific themes that were inductively derived from the data and deductively mapped onto the 48 CFIR constructs. Multiple barriers or facilitators could correspond to a single construct, capturing different dimensions of the construct in China’s PHC setting.

This study adopted the CFIR-ERIC matching tool as a reference to inform strategy design. Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) presents 73 implementation strategies categorized into nine thematic clusters, and the matching tool links CFIR constructs to multiple evidence-based, potentially relevant strategies (Powell et al. 2015). For each barrier, we entered it into the CFIR-ERIC matching tool and selected the five strategies that are most strongly recommended. Then we arranged group discussions and expert consultations to assess the alignment of candidate strategies with the identified constructs and their feasibility within China’s PHC settings. Based on these discussions, the most appropriate strategies were selected. When the ERIC strategies did not adequately address the context-specific challenges identified in the data, additional tailored strategies were developed through the same consultation process. Existing operational practices within the CPU program identified as facilitators were retained and formalized as recommended strategies. The final strategies were further grouped according to their level of implementation and summarized into a recommended framework. The research team reached a consensus for the final version of the strategies. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist was used to assess the study design, analysis, and findings (Supplementary Table S4) (Tong et al. 2007).

Results

A total of 61 PHC workers (average age: 42 (±7.85) years, 21 female participants) were invited for interviews. Most participants (n = 53) had attained an education of college or above, and others only received secondary education. Six of them were village doctors, 35 were the medical staff from township health centers, and 20 of them were the directors of the township health centers. More than half of the participants have worked in their positions for more than 10 years (Table 1).

The facilitators and barriers to implementing CPU in China.

Innovation domain

Ten facilitators and nine barriers were found in the innovation domain. They majorly cover three themes: wearable ECG devices, innovation design and complexity, innovation cost.

Wearable ECG devices

Compared to the ECG machines commonly used in clinical diagnosis, the wearable ECG devices were easier to operate, and the results could be transmitted to the upper-level hospitals directly, which allowed for remote consultations.

However, deficiencies of the devices were frequently reported, such as being susceptible to interference, like patients’ body shape (PHW0801, Table 2), and its inconsistent data upload terminals that might lead to delays of information communication (PHW1701, Table 2).

Design and complexity

The CPU program was designed by the government and proved efficient by online data, which facilitated the implementers’ trust in it. Its protocols are comprehensive with detailed steps to follow, and include training and incentives to improve implementers’ capability and motivations as well. Taking into consideration the limited resources in PHC settings, the program was also designed with a relatively low technical threshold for implementation. Specifically, physicians were only required to identify suspected chest pain cases, and promptly report and transfer the patients to the nearest CPC. Nevertheless, the online training included in the protocols was reported to have limited effectiveness in improving clinicians’ practical skills because it lacked supervision and hands-on training (PHW0802, Table 2). In addition, PHC workers also expressed concerns that the lack of coordinated medication allocation system and the incomplete emergency service coverage might cause medication waste and treatment delays.

Participants recognized the critical role of data reporting in documenting symptom onset times and medical response times for patients. However, the process was noted as “confusing” and “time-consuming.” Informants reported that the data reporting website has missing options, and that it was hard to keep the required timestamps when saving the patients (PHW1401, Table 2).

Innovation cost

The medical equipment and medications for chest pain diagnosis and treatment were partially covered by the government and other organizations like the Qingdao Red Cross (PHW1402, Table 2), but informants stated that the high personnel expenses and cost of medical resources still increased the financial burden on PHC institutions (PHW2402, Table 2).

Outer setting domain

There were 11 facilitators and 17 barriers identified in this domain. Three themes emerged under this domain: local conditions and attitudes, partnerships and connections, policies and financing.

Local conditions and attitudes

We found that the geographical situation varies greatly in different areas. Approximately one fifth of the included CPUs were close to their corresponding CPCs; the participants from one institution mentioned that the high private vehicle ownership and efficient transportation network in their local community benefited timely transfers. However, eight in twenty-six institutions expressed their concerns that the long distance between CPUs and CPCs, and between CPUs and some remote villages might increase patients’ risk of dying during the transport.

The proportion of older adults living alone in the communities was generally high, and their health-seeking awareness was inadequate, so they sometimes might fail to seek help from the hospital or PHC institutions in time. One PHC worker informant also mentioned that villagers had low faith in hospitals and doctors, so they did not adhere to the treatment. In addition, PHC workers reported that the local funeral customs of some villages hindered patients’ emergency care seeking (PHW0501, Table 2).

More than half of the PHC institutions had difficulty meeting the required number of chest pain cases, an indicator of operational capacity and clinical readiness within the CPU accreditation process. Besides, the interviewees also reported that the prevalence of medical bill evasion increased their financial burden (PHW2501, Table 2).

Partnerships and connections

Medical consortiums were forged between PHC institutions and CPCs to enhance PHC’s diagnosis and treatment capabilities and to facilitate timely transfers (PHW0301, Table 2). Village doctors, the frontline providers for the early identification and referral of STEMI patients, were familiar with the residents’ medical histories due to their close relationships with the community.

However, participants stated that it was challenging to organize the locals for health education without the engagement of the village committees. Additionally, PHC clinicians also expressed their desire for more frequent personnel rotation within medical consortiums, which would give them the opportunities to learn from specialists (PHW1201, Table 2). Besides, time lags were occasionally observed in CPU–CPC communication, which could lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment.

Policies and financing

Policy support is critical for the long-term operation of CPU. According to the directors of CPUs, the tiered healthcare delivery system mandated by China’s medical reform increased patients’ willingness to visit PHC institutions for mild ailments, boosting their financial incomes.

However, interviewees stated that the COVID-19 pandemic diverted the medical resources, which deprioritized the CPU implementation. Additionally, the absence of direct funding and the government reimbursement delays are common concerns in CPUs. Participants noted that the required examinations and medicines were not fully covered by insurance, which increased the economic burden on patients. Also, the PHC workers mentioned that the substandard remuneration and inadequate staff quotas contributed to staff shortages in PHC institutions (PHW2101, Table 2).

Inner setting domain

There were 17 facilitators and 13 barriers found in the inner setting domain. There are three emerging themes under this domain: structural characteristics and resources; implementation and administration; training and learning.

Structural characteristics and resources

Although the majority of PHC facilities had the basic resources for CPU operations, they commonly faced a lack of funding due to low operating revenue, which resulted in inadequate medication supplies and a lack of up-to-date equipment. In terms of the work infrastructure, informants stated that the staffing shortages and excessive non-clinical workloads were common challenges confronted by PHC institutions (PHW0101, Table 2).

Implementation and administration

Recognizing the high fatality rate of STEMI, PHC clinicians reported they had tension to change the situation and to improve the survival rates, which was in alignment with the purpose of the CPU program. More than half of the participating institutions showed a high ambition for self-improvement and capacity building, motivating them to implement the program (PHW1301, Table 2). Also, the close and effective collaboration within the PHC facilities made it possible to share information quickly inside a facility. However, there were still some challenges existing in terms of implementation and administration.

PHC workers reported that although the ongoing work process fit with the emergency care process, it was still inadequate for recording the required timestamps (PHW1001, Table 2). Additionally, most PHC institutions gave high priority to the CPU implementation, but there were still some participating institutions that showed low priority due to the lack of leadership support or funding. Besides, despite the fact that the incentive mechanism included in CPU protocols was beneficial, PHC workers pointed out that it was not fully enforced in some institutions and there was no linkage between pay and performance, which sometimes neutralized its effect (PHW0901, Table 2).

Training and learning

Regular training was conducted under the guidance of the upper-level hospitals (PHW0701, Table 2). This is a facilitator of the implementation and the PHC workers noted that it instructed them on how to react when encountering an STEMI patient. However, they also mentioned some shortcomings of the current training, including the lack of hands-on clinical training, and high training thresholds that posed challenges for clinicians with limited knowledge on ACS (PHW1302, Table 2).

Individual domain

Two facilitators and three barriers were identified in this domain. A commonly reported barrier was the PHC workers’ insufficient professional skills, which could not support them to provide appropriate care for patients with STEMI, such as ECG interpretation and defibrillation. Therefore, they had needs for enhancing their competency in recognizing and managing chest pain. In addition, they also hoped that implementing the CPU program could help improve their earnings, their career satisfaction, and the institutional prestige, which gave them motivations.

Nevertheless, it was reported that some PHC workers showed low motivation due to work pressure, insufficient awareness of the severity of ACS, especially STEMI, and age-related factors (PHW1202, Table 2).

Implementation process domain

Ten facilitators and four barriers were identified in this domain. Three themes emerged from these facilitators and barriers: planning and preparing, implementing and adapting, reflecting and evaluating.

Planning and preparing

Most PHC institutions made full preparation according to the requirements of CPU, including conducting training, updating equipment and establishing a team for CPU implementation (PHW2601, Table 2). However, some participants acknowledged that they didn’t fully understand the accreditation standards and procedural requirements of the CPU program, and thus did not have a clear plan for the implementation.

Implementing and adapting

In compliance with the CPU protocols, PHC facilities set up priority pathway for emergencies, which facilitated the STEMI patients to gain timely treatment. They also improved their workflows and staff allocation in order to fit with the CPU implementation. Additionally, some facilities optimized informed consent process to save the treatment time (PHW1002, Table 2).

However, many PHC workers mentioned that the nighttime responses were slower than daytime, because only a few clinicians were on the night shift, and they sometimes failed to react as swiftly as they did in the daytime.

After the chest pain patients were successfully saved and discharged from the hospital, CPUs would arrange regular follow-up by phone calls to prevent relapses. They also conducted regular awareness campaigns among villages. However, although some campaigns were diversified by the help of social media, others were reported to have a poor effect.

Reflecting and evaluating

The CPU leaders were required to participate in joint meetings held by the upper-level hospitals, which provided feedback for the overall performance of CPU implementation, and thus helped make improvements accordingly (PHW2101, Table 2). However, the interviewees noted that they rarely received specific feedback regarding the quality of data reporting, and expressed their hopes to receive more specific guidance in this regard, so they could know whether the reported data was qualified (PHW1403, Table 2).

This study developed five strategies to optimize the innovation. Of these strategies, two were learned from the facilitators, including promoting adaptability and developing a formal implementation blueprint, which respectively echoed the facilitators that CPU was easy to promote and it had comprehensive and easy-to-follow protocols. Another three strategies were majorly developed to respond to the barriers. Specifically, technical improvement was intended to solve the technical defects of the wearable ECG devices and the data reporting website. Using data warehousing techniques was aimed at easing the burden of cumbersome non-clinical work and promote data sharing. Organizing clinician implementation team meetings by the innovation designers could promote implementers’ understanding on the innovation and implementation.

We produced eight strategies to empower the implementation through the external environment. Of these strategies, two were drawn from the facilitators. The government and the Qingdao Red Cross provided some equipment and medications to CPUs, which was a good example for funding and contracting for clinical innovation. Increasing demand echoed the competitive pressures and level-improvement pressures faced by the PHC institutions. Another five strategies were developed to solve the barriers. The policy-level support strategy was proposed to promote adjustments to relevant reimbursement and insurance policies, thereby alleviating the financial burden on PHC institutions resulting from limitations in the existing coverage policies. Promoting network weaving would improve the cooperation and communication across facilities. Promoting transport network weaving could also shorten the geographical distance and promote transportation. Community empowerment strategy and mass media campaign suggest that CPUs should collaborate with local communities and use mass media for public education, which could improve their awareness and compliance. Also, intervention with patients to enhance uptake and adherence, such as more frequent follow-up, is beneficial in improving patient compliance and may help prevent bill evasion as well. Additionally, accessing new funding could respond to financial challenges commonly faced by the PHC institutions.

This study designed ten strategies aiming at improving the internal environment. Of them, four were concluded from the facilitators, including changing physical structure and equipment, mandate change, tailor strategies, and identifying implementers’ needs. They echoed PHC institutions’ basic equipment construction, leadership, and their initiatives to implement the innovation. Another six strategies were to respond to the barriers. Conducting local consensus discussions is helpful for implementers to recognize the importance of CPU implementation, which could mitigate the impact of COVID-19 pandemic on medical system. Developing disincentives and altering organizational incentive structure, such as introducing salary-based incentives and linking incentives to performance appraisal, were designed to meet the challenge of staff shortage and the implementers’ lack of motivation. Improving workflow was developed to respond to challenges such as delayed nighttime response and inaccurate timestamps documentation and to alleviate the non-clinical workloads. Audit and feedback respond to challenges caused by the lack of performance assessment, especially in the data reporting process. Conducting ongoing hands-on training was to meet the needs of the implementers who have difficulties in understanding theoretical learning materials for self-improvement.

Discussion

This qualitative study identified the facilitators and barriers to the implementation of CPU in three representative provinces in China with CFIR. Facilitated by the CFIR-ERIC matching tool, this study generated concrete strategies for improving the CPU implementation to inform future practice. The wearable ECG devices being prone to interference, the missing options and undefined entries of the data reporting website, inadequate health-seeking awareness among residents, lack of chest pain cases and financial support, staffing shortage, deficient incentive systems, insufficient medical resources, lack of hands-on clinical training, and inefficient awareness campaigns are the major barriers. On the other hand, the innovation was majorly facilitated by easy operation of the wearable ECG devices, medical consortiums with higher-level hospitals, the corporate culture of prioritizing self-improvement and capacity building, high priority, universal commitment to improving STEMI survival rates, and regular training.

Beyond the major barriers and facilitators identified, several themes were observed to recur across more than one CFIR domain. In particular, financial constraints were reflected in high costs associated with the innovation, limited operating revenue, insufficient insurance reimbursement, and the absence of dedicated funding. These limitations restricted equipment renewal, affected staffing levels and remuneration systems, and even weakened incentive structures. In addition, gaps in technological infrastructure included problems in the wearable ECG devices and the data reporting website, and the absence of a big data analytics infrastructure and medication allocation system. These barriers also hindered the implementation efficiency of the CPU program.

The consistency of these findings with those from the implementation of the CPC program reinforces the robustness of the present results and highlights their potential to inform evidence-based policies and resource allocation strategies aimed at strengthening CPU implementation nationwide (Zhou et al. 2022). Based on the investigated facilitators and barriers, this study developed a three-pronged strategy framework including innovation optimization, external empowerment, and internal improvement to inform future practice.

Innovation optimization

Innovation optimization was repeatedly emphasized by respondents as critical components for enhancing the functionability of the CPU program.

Technological stability and smooth inter-institutional data sharing are two important elements to ensure the early diagnosis and timely treatment in PHC settings. However, technical barriers, such as unreliable wearable ECG devices and data reporting websites, and the lack of a data integrating system, impeded the promotion of the CPU program in resource-constrained settings. The technical defects underscore the necessity of constant technical support and improvement. Literature also proves the important role that effective and timely technical assistance play in promoting the smooth integration of health technologies into the workflow and enhancing the implementation success of relevant innovations (Taboada et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2025b). Furthermore, the adoption of data warehousing techniques can serve as a strategic solution to promote efficient data sharing and reduce repetitive work. By integrating data from heterogeneous sources into a database designed for efficient querying and analysis, and converting them to a standardized format, it could also support data interoperability by enabling consistent data structures across institutions and thereby facilitate the coordination of medical resources (Ferlie and Shortell 2001, Solodovnikova et al. 2015). This is in alignment with the practice in the USA, where researchers used the data warehousing techniques to promote the resource allocation and policy making in community health care (Berndt et al. 2003).

Interviewees mentioned that having difficulties in understanding the requirements of CPU hindered the fidelity of implementation. Previous studies indicate that organizing clinician implementation team meeting regularly is important to consistently include medical staff into the key implementation team (Sperber et al. 2019). In the USA, this strategy was introduced for knowledge sharing and fidelity maintaining in implementing the Appointment-Based Model for community pharmacies (Herbert et al. 2022). Therefore, this study suggests innovation designers organize clinician implementation team meeting to clarify the requirements and thus consolidate clinicians’ confidence and commitment for the innovation implementation.

The adaptability and an actionable blueprint are important premises for the successful implementation. The CPU program demonstrates high adaptability, primarily due to its low technical threshold, which allows PHC institutions with limited resources and heavy clinical workload to have the capacity to adopt it. Similar facilitators have also been documented in other programs implemented in the PHC context, where the program was tailored and included into the routine works, and thus exhibited high adaptability, promoting the scaling up of the program (Ndejjo et al. 2020). Previous studies have indicated that a well-designed implementation blueprint can also promote adaptability by providing detailed information about the preparation the practitioners should make and the specific procedures to follow (Powell et al. 2019). The protocols of CPU program are such one that has offered guides for PHC workers to enhance readiness and implementation, and thus promoted its replicability and scale-up across different sites. Therefore, it is advisable to preserve the low technical threshold and adhere to a clear implementation blueprint in CPU’s future scaling-up and the design of similar programs.

External empowerment

Insights from the interviews reveal that external empowerment is crucial for the successful implementation of CPU. Participants highlighted patients’ limited knowledge and awareness about STEMI symptoms and severity as key barriers that resulted in patient delay in seeking treatment. Consistent with this finding, previous studies also identified prehospital delay due to patients’ lack of awareness as a critical factor that contributed to STEMI mortality (Zhou et al. 2022). The strategies of mass media campaigns, intervention with patients to enhance adherence, and community empowerment are aimed to respond to these barriers. Using mass media such as short videos could broaden the reach of the awareness campaigns. Intervention with patients, such as regular follow-up, could increase patients’ adherence to the therapy. The community empowerment strategy attempts to involve the community committees in the organization of awareness campaigns to enhance residents’ engagement. The positive outcomes of the strategy, such as boosting individual’s involvement and volunteerism, have been reported in previous studies (World Health Organization 2003).

This study proposed government policies and financial support strategies to cope with the financial challenges universally confronted by the PHC institutions. In our findings, financial problems were closely associated with reimbursement delays, low operating revenue, and the absence of governmental financial support, which restricted equipment renewal and staffing capacity. Therefore, policy-level adjustments in reimbursement mechanisms and funding arrangements are critical to facilitate sustainable implementation. Similar strategies were also put forward in CPC implementation, where researchers suggested that financial support and policy support could provide CPCs with an adequate foundation of equipment and personnel (Zhou et al. 2022). Although direct financial subsidies for CPU implementation were limited, essential equipment and medications were partly supported by the government and non-profit organizations, which helped alleviate implementation burdens.

Notably, although most identified factors were consistently observed across the three provinces, geographical variations were evident in the level of local policy and organizational support, which influenced implementers’ motivation. For example, while all three provinces had policies to encourage the CPU implementation, Hubei incorporated CPU development into the “323” Health Initiative, providing stronger endorsement and thereby strengthening local institutional commitment. This suggests that differences in policy prioritization can influence implementers’ engagement. Similar approaches could be adopted in other regions to promote implementation.

Additionally, respondents pointed out that the loose collaboration with and long distance from the upper-level hospitals resulted in delayed diagnoses and transfers. Promoting network weaving strategy, highlighting the cooperation network strengthening as well as the transportation network building, was proposed to cope with these challenges. Studies in the Netherlands have shown that building a network of partnerships within care pathways could ensure smooth patient transfer (Te Loo et al. 2024). Improving the local transportation infrastructure could further facilitate the transfer between CPUs and CPCs.

External competition could foster an enabling environment to implement the innovation. According to the informants, the pressure from competing with peer hospitals encouraged the implementation, by which they could enhance their capabilities and attract more patient to choose them. In the USA, researchers made similar findings that market pressure from competing organizations could influence hospital systems’ decisions to adopt innovations (Comella and Elwy 2025).

Internal improvement

This study found that internal improvement should be implemented in terms of training, workflow, incentive systems, and feedback systems. Interviewees identified the lack of professional skills as a major barrier to the CPU program, which is consistent with other quality improvement studies, where regular training is recommended to alleviate the problems (Ye et al. 2022). However, despite the existing training program, the PHC workers stated that the online theoretical instruction still led to inadequate learning outcomes, particularly in practical diagnosis and emergency care. Therefore, this study recommends that on-site hands-on training be offered to the medical staff to achieve implementation success. Additionally, we found that delays in treatment delivery and transfer are more likely to happen in the evening and at night. This finding aligns with evidence from Canada showing that evening transport was associated with significantly longer time to initiate transfer (Myers and Nolan 2021). To address this problem, the strategy of improving the workflow proposes that the PHC institutions could adjust their staff schedules to increase nighttime coverage, in order to ensure timely and high-quality treatment for the evening patients.

Participants reported that financial incentives and performance-based incentives were promoting factors to encourage the uptake of innovation implementation. Similar findings have also been documented in other studies. For example, Wang et al. identified the absence of clear reward and punishment policies as a barrier to implementation and suggested that performance-linked incentives could enhance staff engagement (Wang et al. 2025a). However, the existing incentive system was not linked to performance or salary, and some doctors remained unmotivated, although the incentives were working well. This suggests that incentives alone may be insufficient. Hence, we suggest that CPUs integrate disincentives with positive incentives and establish a salary-and-performance-based evaluation system to motivate implementers to prioritize the program. While such mechanisms should be tailored across PHC institutions according to their financial and staffing conditions, their effective implementation also requires policy guidance and resource support from the outer setting to ensure sustainability.

Besides, this study proposed to establish an audit and feedback system in the data reporting process to improve the quality of records and enhance implementers’ understanding toward relative requirements. Similar strategy could be found in other quality improvement studies, which have illustrated that a well-established audit and feedback system could improve professional practice and implementation outcomes (Ye et al. 2022). Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, many PHC institutions attached more significance to infectious disease prevention and control, resulting in low priority for CPU. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that the pandemic led to the reallocation of health system resources toward COVID-19 response, disrupting routine service delivery and reducing follow-up care for chronic conditions (Fekadu et al. 2021, Okereke et al. 2021). Thus, the conducting local consensus discussions strategy was developed to help relevant stakeholders recognize the importance of STEMI control and thus prioritize the CPU implementation.

Internal prioritization and improvement could enhance motivation and facilitate innovation implementation. Leaders emphasizing the importance of CPU and expressing their determination to have it implemented could elevate the priority of it in the institutions. Previous research also found that such leadership engagement often accompanies incentive funding and personnel training, which is crucial to the successful implementation of the program (Zhou et al. 2022). Participants reported that through the training provided by the CPU program, they can also have their professional skills improved and thus gain a sense of honor from their career. These greatly motivated them. Therefore, the strategy of identifying implementers’ needs recommends to connect the innovation with their own needs to promote their motivations. Similar facilitators are also reported in other quality improvement programs. For example, in Uganda, community health workers were motivated by increased professional knowledge acquired from training, and thus showed great commitment into the program (Ndejjo et al. 2020). Additionally, improving the physical structures based on the requirements of the innovation and tailoring strategies to make the innovation fit with the workflow and actual situation are also important. For instance, some institutions performed the informed consent process during transport to save time. Previous study has indicated the crucial role of shortening the time of obtaining informed consent play in achieving optimal clinical outcomes for STEMI patients (Zhou et al. 2022).

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the interviewees only included PHC workers. We did not collect the viewpoints of patients, government officials, policymakers, and academic experts, which may limit the comprehensiveness of our findings. Second, this study adopted a qualitative method, which could not indicate a causal relationship. Third, the use of a purposive, non-random sampling strategy, while necessary to capture facilitators and barriers of diverse contexts, indicates that our study sites and participants may not be fully representative of all primary care settings. Future studies could include multi-perspective points of view, and utilize randomized sampling and quantitative methods to supplement the findings of this study and evaluate the effects of the strategies on chest pain patients’ outcomes.

Conclusion

Utilizing the CFIR-ERIC tool, this study identified 46 barriers and 49 facilitators in the CPU implementation in China. A three-pronged strategic framework, consisting of 23 strategies across innovation optimization, internal improvement, and external empowerment, was developed to leverage the facilitators and address the identified barriers in Chinese PHC settings. Technical deficiencies, financial difficulties, and residents’ lack of awareness for health-seeking are the three most significant barriers to the implementation of the CPU program. Technical deficiencies and financial difficulties are also cross-cutting themes that require special attention. Therefore, technical improvement, access new funding, community empowerment, mass media campaigns, and intervention with patients to enhance adherence are crucial for the improvement of the CPU implementation. These findings and strategies may be applicable to other low- and middle-income countries pursuing decentralized emergency care reforms, especially those with under-resourced primary care networks.
