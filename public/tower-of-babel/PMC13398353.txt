Methodological concerns on the predictive performance evaluation of anthropometric indices
Authors: Xiaoyan Zhang, Wenya Han
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Pezeshki et al. 1 , which evaluated the predictive performance of 19 traditional and novel anthropometric indices for diabetes and hypertension in an Iranian population at risk for cardiovascular disease. The study addresses a critical gap in identifying optimal anthropometric tools for metabolic risk screening in resource‐constrained settings, providing valuable comparative data. However, we have several methodological concerns that potentially threaten the validity of the conclusions.

First, there is a critical flaw in the statistical comparison of AUC values across anthropometric indices, which undermines the core conclusion that certain indices outperform BMI. The authors claimed the Deurenberg formula significantly outperformed BMI in predicting diabetes in both genders and noted superior performance of BRI, WHT.5R, and WHtR in females (table 3). Yet, they only applied a Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons in the initial significance threshold (set to 0.0025) but failed to use appropriate statistical tests for pairwise AUC comparisons. As established in methodological guidelines, simple visual or magnitude comparisons of AUC values without formal tests (e.g., DeLong's test) cannot confirm significant differences between ROC curves 2 . This oversight leads to unreliable claims of ‘superior predictive performance’, as the observed AUC discrepancies may be due to random variation rather than true differences. To rectify this, the authors should employ DeLong's test for pairwise comparisons of AUCs, adjusting for multiple testing to validate which indices truly outperform BMI. A thorough assessment of such methodological issues is a core component of modern tools for evaluating prediction model studies 3 .

Second, the study suffers from inappropriate handling of collinearity among highly correlated anthropometric indices, resulting in misleading interpretations of ‘independent predictive value’. Table 4 shows substantial correlation between most indices (ICC = 0.84 for males and 0.83 for females), with indices like BRI, WHtR, and WHT.5R all derived from waist circumference and height. Despite this, the authors used conventional multivariate logistic regression to assess independent predictive value, which is unstable in the presence of high collinearity (variance inflation factor, VIF, not reported). High collinearity can distort regression coefficients, inflate standard errors, and lead to spurious conclusions about the independent utility of individual indices. This issue is particularly problematic for the claim that BRI, WHT.5R, and WHtR have independent predictive value for diabetes in females. A more appropriate approach would be to first assess collinearity using VIF, then employ dimension reduction techniques (e.g., principal component analysis) or penalized regression (LASSO) to identify indices with genuine independent predictive power while accounting for inter‐index correlations.

In summary, addressing the improper AUC comparison methods and unaccounted collinearity is crucial for validating the study's conclusions regarding the superiority and independence of specific anthropometric indices. Correcting these issues will ensure reliable interpretation of the evidence, preventing the adoption of suboptimal indices in clinical screening protocols and guiding more rigorous future research on anthropometric risk prediction.
