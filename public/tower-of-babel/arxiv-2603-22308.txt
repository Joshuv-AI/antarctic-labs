Infinitesimals inside the Familiar Field of Complex Numbers
Todor D. Todorov

Abstract
Abstract : We show that the field of complex numbers ℂ \mathbb{C} contains non-zero infinitesimals by observing that ℂ \mathbb{C} contains non-Archimedean subfields. Our observation is based on an old theorem in algebra due to E. Steinitz, discussed in the article in detail. The presence of infinitesimals in ℂ \mathbb{C} was surprise to the author and might be surprise to the readers as well, since ℂ \mathbb{C} is commonly defined in terms of the field of reals ℝ \mathbb{R} , which is Archimedean. An additional intrigue arises from the fact that ℝ \mathbb{R} was historically introduced in 19-th century (by Dedekind, Cauchy and others) exactly to make infinitesimals in Leibniz-Newton infinitesimal calculus redundant. It seems that mathematics will never get rid of infinitesimals completely - they are all around us whether we like it or not. In the last section of the article we explain how our result fits to analysis, both standard and non-standard. With examples from history of calculus as well of first-class recent achievements in analysis we try to convince the reader that presence of infinitesimals in analysis simplifies its formal language and improves its efficiency. The motivations of our research is related to an attempt to simplify the properties of particular algebras of generalized functions of Colombeau’s type, shortly discussed in the text.



Keywords
: Totally ordered field; non-Archimedean field; real closed field; algebraically closed field; Artin-Schreier theorem; Steinitz theorem; Erdös, Gillman, Henriksen theorem; Puiseux-Newton series; Levi-Civitá series; Hahn series; Robinson’s non-standard numbers; Robinson’s asymptotic numbers.

We can only speculate why mathematicians, who have been familiar with Steinitz theorem for a long time, have failed to notice the presence of infinitesimals in
\mathbb{C}
. Technically speaking, a such observation was possible soon after the publication of Steinitz theorem
[
33
]
in 1910, since the non-Archimedean field
\mathbb{R}\{t\}
of Puiseux-Newton series was used by Newton in the distant 1736. And actually, no more is needed to make our observation. One possible explanation (shortly hinted in the abstract) is that infinitesimals have been out of fashion in modern mathematics for a long time. A student majoring mathematics nowadays might very well have never heard about the concept of infinitesimal. Why? The reason is rooted in history of calculus. In attempt to explain this, let us recall how Leibniz derived, say, the formula
\frac{d}{dx}(x^{3})=3x^{2}
. By “definition”
\frac{d}{dx}(x^{3})
is what the following algorithm produces:

Step 1:
Let
x
be a “fixed ordinary quantity” (a fixed real number in modern language) and
dx\not=0
be “non-zero quantity” (a variable taking non-zero values in modern language). We calculate:

\frac{(x+dx)^{3}-x^{3}}{dx}=\frac{x^{3}+3x^{2}dx+3xdx^{2}+dx^{3}-x^{3}}{dx}=3x^{2}+3xdx+dx^{2}.

Step 2:
Let
dx=0
and the result is:
\frac{d}{dx}(x^{3})=3x^{2}
.

Notice that modern computers nowadays calculate symbolically derivatives of real functions following similar algorithms (Shamseddine, Berz
[
32
]
). From the point of view of pure mathematics, however, there is clearly
lack of consistency
between Step 1 (
dx\not=0
) and Step 2 (
dx=0
), which might be phrased even as a
infinitesimal paradox
: “
dx\not=0
and
dx=0
”. To avoid this paradox, Leibniz (Newton, Euler and others) introduced the concept of non-zero infinitesimals;
dx
must be a non-zero infinitesimal, which allows dropping the term
3xdx+dx^{2}
, while keeping
3x^{2}
on the right hand side of the above formula. Persistent doubts however, in the existence of non-zero infinitesimals (as a tool to avoid the above paradox), led historically to a
movement against infinitesimals, which gradually resulted to a purge of infinitesimals from mathematics
(Hall &Todorov
[
11
]
). The movement started in 19-th century by Bolzano, Weirstrass and others and it is still lingering today in spite of the advent of non-standard analysis (Robinson
[
29
]
) at the middle of 20-th century. For those interested in the pre-history of infinitesimals in the context of Reformation, Counter-Reformation and English Civil War, we recommend the excellent book by Alexander
[
1
]
.

While dealing with infinitesimals in
\mathbb{C}
we are facing the following
philosophical alternative
:

1.

Infinitesimals exist in
\mathbb{C}
regardless whether we realized it or not - very much like microbes swimming in a drop of water - invisible for the naked eye. Perhaps, the absolute value
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{R}}
(with non-Archimedean field
\mathcal{R}
) is our “microscope” under which the infinitesimal become “visible”?

or

2.

\mathbb{C}
is free of infinitesmals (as it should be). We (humans) create infinitesimals in
\mathbb{C}
by artificially embedding a non-Archimedean fields into
\mathbb{C}
?

As we know too well, mathematics is not equipped to answer any philosophical questions and we are not going to do that. Still, we somehow prefer to believe that infinitesimals do, indeed, exist in
\mathbb{C}
(like microbes in a drop of water) and become observable only for those who want to observe them.

2
Set-Theoretical Framework

Our set-theoretical framework is the usual ZFC (Zermelo-Frankel Axioms with Axiom of Choice) along with the GCH (Generalized Continuum Hypothesis) in the form of
2^{\kappa}=\kappa_{+}
for all cardinal numbers
\kappa
(or equivalently,
2^{\aleph_{\alpha}}=\aleph_{\alpha+1}
for all ordinals
\alpha
). Here
\kappa_{+}
stands for the successor of
\kappa
. In particular case of
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
, GCH reduces to CH (Continuum Hypothesis), which can be written in the form
\mathfrak{c}=\aleph_{1}
, since
2^{\aleph_{0}}=\mathfrak{c}
. Here
\aleph_{0}={\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{N})
and
\mathfrak{c}={\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{R})
. Due to the assumption of axiom of choice (AC) we have the formula
\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}=\max\{\kappa_{1},\,\kappa_{2}\}
for every two cardinal numbers
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
, assuming in addition that at least one of them is infinite.

Actually, we do not need GCH (neither CH) for our main observation - that
\mathbb{C}
contains non-zero infinitesimals. We impose GCH mostly for the sake of convenience - to be able to establish an isomorphism between some of the fields in Examples
6.1
and to calculate more easily their cardinalities. However, we try to localize the dependence (if any) of our results on GCH or CH (or even on AC) whenever possible. Otherwise, no particular expertise in set theory is expected from the reader.

3
Preliminaries: Ordered Fields

For convenience of the reader we present a list of basic definitions and facts about totally ordered fields. For more detailed expositions we refer to Lang
[
13
]
,  Hunderford
[
12
]
and Waerden
[
43
]
. A reader, who is familiar with the topic, might jump directly to the next section.

1.

As usual, we denote
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}(i)
the sets of natural, rational, real and complex numbers, respectively, and we also use
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. If
S
is a set, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(S)
the power set of
S
.

2.

If
\mathbb{K}
is a totally ordered field (an ordered field) and
x\in\mathbb{K}
, we let
|x|=\max\{-x,x\}
, where
\max
is relative to the order on
\mathbb{K}
. We say that

(a)

x
is
finite
if
|x|\leq n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(b)

x
is
infinitesimal
if
|x|<1/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(c)

x
is
infinitely large
if
n<|x|
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Recall that every totally ordered field contains a copy of the field of rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
. Thus both
n
and
1/n
are in
\mathbb{K}
(justifying the above definition).

3.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{L}
the set of the finite, infinitesimal and infinitely large numbers in
\mathbb{K}
, respectively. We also denote by
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{I}
the group of units of the ring
\mathcal{F}
. We observe that
\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{K}
,
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{F}
. Moreover, if
x\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
, then
x\in\mathcal{I}

{\rm{\,iff\;}}
1/x\in\mathcal{L}
. We sometimes write
x\approx y
instead of
x-y\in\mathcal{I}
.

4.

A totally ordered field
\mathbb{K}
is called
Archimedean
if
\mathcal{L}=\varnothing
. The latter is equivalent to either
\mathcal{I}=\{0\}
, or
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{K}
. Consequently,
\mathbb{K}
is
non-Archimedean

{\rm{\,iff\;}}
either of the following holds:
\mathcal{L}\not=\varnothing
,
\mathcal{I}\not=\{0\}
, or
\mathcal{F}\not=\mathbb{K}
.

5.

A totally ordered field
\mathbb{K}
is
algebraically saturated
if every nested sequence of open intervals in
\mathbb{K}
has a non-empty intersection. More generally,
\mathbb{K}
is
algebraically
\kappa_{+}
-saturated
for some infinite cardinal
\kappa
if every family of open intervals in
\mathbb{K}
of cardinality
\kappa
with the finite intersection property has a non-empty intersection. Moreover,
\mathbb{K}
is
fully saturated
if it is
\kappa_{+}
-saturated for
{\kappa_{+}}={\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{K})
.

6.

In addition to what was mentioned above, a totally ordered field
\mathbb{K}
is
Archimedean
if and only if
\mathbb{K}
is an ordered subfield (can be embedded as such) of the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, every totally ordered field
\mathbb{K}
which contains a proper copy of
\mathbb{R}
(as an ordered subfield) is
non-Archimedean
. On the other hand, it is clear that an algebraically saturated ordered field must be non-Archimedean. Perhaps, the simplest example of a non-saturated non-Archimedean field, which does not contain a copy of
\mathbb{R}
, is given by the field
\mathbb{Q}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{Q}[t]}{\mathbb{Q}[t]}
of rational functions in one variable with rational coefficients. First, we embed
\mathbb{Q}
into
\mathbb{Q}(t)
by
q\mapsto q\,t^{0}
and convert
\mathbb{Q}(t)
into a totally ordered field by ordering the ring of polynomials
\mathbb{Q}[t]
: A non-zero polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
is positive if the coefficient in front of the lowest power of
t
in
P
is positive. For example,
2t^{2}-100t^{3}>0
, because
2>0
. We observe that
t,\,t^{2},\;2t^{2}-100t^{3},\;,\dots
are
positive infinitesimals
. In particular,
t
is a positive infinitesimal, because
\frac{1}{n}-t>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Similarly,

t^{-1},\;t^{-2}
,
\frac{1}{2t^{2}-100t^{3}},\dots
are
positive infinitely large
elements of
\mathbb{Q}(t)
.

7.

A field
\mathcal{R}
is
formally real
(or
orderable
) if
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}=0
always implies
x_{1}=x_{2}=\dots=x_{n}=0
. The fields
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{Q}(t)
are formally real, while
\mathbb{C}
is not.

8.

A formally real field
\mathcal{R}
is
real closed
if

(a)

For every
a\in\mathcal{R}
either
a
, or
-a
has a square root.

(b)

Every polynomial
P\in\mathcal{R}[x]
(with coefficients in
\mathcal{R}
) of
odd degree
has a root in
\mathcal{R}
.

9.

\mathbb{R}
is a real closed field, but
\mathbb{Q}
is not. Similarly,
\mathbb{Q}(t)
is not real closed. More examples of real closed fields (Archimedean and non-Archimedean alike) appear later in the article (Examples
6.1
).

10.

Every real closed field
\mathcal{R}
is a
totally ordered field
under the unique ordering:
x\geq 0
in
\mathcal{R}
if
x=y^{2}
for some
y\in\mathcal{R}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{R}
is a field of zero-characteristic in the sense that
\mathcal{R}
contains
\mathbb{Q}
as a subfield.

11.

If
\mathcal{R}
is a real closed field, then
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{I}
is an Archimedean real closed field. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a subfield of
\mathcal{R}
, which is a maximal subring of
\mathcal{F}
(a field of representatives). The existence of
\mathcal{A}
is guaranteed by AC. Then
\mathcal{A}
is an Archimedean real closed field as isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{I}
. We refer to
\mathcal{A}
as an
Archimedean part of

\mathcal{R}
and sometimes use the notation
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{R})
or
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{R}}
, since
\mathcal{R}
determines
\mathcal{A}
uniquely up to isomorphism. Consequently,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}(i)
are subfields of
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
, respectively. Notice that if
\mathcal{R}
is Archimedean, then
\mathcal{A}(i)=\mathcal{R}(i)
, since
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{R}
. If, however,
\mathcal{R}
a non-Archimedean, then the field extension
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}
must be transcendental hence,
\mathcal{A}\subsetneqq\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{A}(i)\subsetneqq\mathcal{R}(i)
.

12.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a real closed field and
\mathcal{R}(i)
obtained by adjoining

to
\mathcal{R}
. We define
\mathcal{R}
-absolute value
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathcal{R}(i)
by the familiar formula
|x+iy|_{\mathcal{R}}=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, where
x,y\in\mathcal{R}
. We observe that for the range of the absolute value we have
{\rm{ran}}(|\cdot|_{\mathcal{R}})=\{x\in\mathcal{R}:x\geq 0\}
. It is clear that
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{R}}
possesses the usual properties of absolute value. Moreover,
|x|_{\mathcal{R}}=\max_{\mathcal{R}}\{-x,x\}
for all
x\in\mathcal{R}
, where
\max_{\mathcal{R}}
is relative to the order in
\mathcal{R}
. The topology on
\mathcal{R}(i)
determined by
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{R}}
coincides with the product topology on
\mathcal{R}(i)
inherited from the order topology on
\mathcal{R}
. For more details we refer to Section
9
.

13.

We have
|z|_{\mathcal{R}}=|z|
for all
z\in\mathcal{A}(i)
, where
\mathcal{A}
as an
Archimedean part of

\mathcal{R}
and
|\cdot|
stands for the usual absolute value in
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover,
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{R}}
clearly reduces to the usual absolute value on
\mathbb{C}
in the particular case
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}
.

14.

In contrast to the above, recall that a field
\mathbb{K}
is
algebraically closed
if every non-constant polynomial
P\in\mathbb{K}[x]
(with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
) has at least one root in
\mathbb{K}
. The field

\mathbb{C}
is algebraically closed, but
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{Q}(t)
are not.

15.

Let
\mathcal{R}
is a formally real field. Then
\mathcal{R}
is
real closed
if and only if
\mathcal{R}(i)
is
algebraically closed
(Artin and Schreier
[
2
]
).

16.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a subfield of
\mathbb{K}
and suppose that field extension
\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}
is transcendental. A subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{K}
is called a
transcendence basis
of
\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}
if:
(a)
\mathcal{B}
is algebraically independent over
\mathbb{F}
; (b) The field extension

\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{B})
is algebraic. The existence of
\mathcal{B}
is guaranteed by Zorn lemma. The cardinality of
\mathcal{B}
is called
transcendence degree
of
\mathbb{K}\!:\!\mathbb{F}
and usually denoted by
{\rm trd}(\mathbb{K}\!\!:\!\!\mathbb{F})
, i.e.
{\rm trd}(\mathbb{K}\!\!:\!\!\mathbb{F})={\rm{card}\,}\,\mathcal{B}
(Hunderford
[
12
]
). Moreover, we have the formula:

(1)

{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{K})=\max\{\aleph_{0},\,{\rm{card}\,}\,\mathbb{F},\,{\rm trd}(\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F})\}.

Indeed,
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{K})={\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{B}))
, since the field extension
\mathbb{K}\!:\!\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{B})
is algebraic by the definition of
\mathcal{B}
. On the other hand,
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{B}))=\max\{\aleph_{0},\,{\rm{card}\,}\,\mathbb{F},\,{\rm{card}\,}\,\mathcal{B}\}
by the definition of
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{B})
, since
\mathcal{B}
is algebraically independent over
\mathbb{F}
.

17.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be the
prime subfield
of
\mathbb{K}
(the smallest subfield of
\mathbb{K}
containing the unit of
\mathbb{K}
) and suppose, as before, that the field extension
\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}
is transcendental. In this case the transcendence degree
{\rm trd}(\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F})
is called

of
\mathbb{K}
and denoted, simply, by

4
Isomorphic Fields: Steinitz Theorem

We recall two classical theorems in algebra about isomorphisms between algebraically closed and real closed fields. We also present several consequences from Steinitz theorem, which will lead us eventually to our main result in the next section. A reader who is familiar with Steinitz theorem might skip this section and jump to the next one.

4.1 Theorem

(Steinitz)
.

Every two algebraically closed fields of the same characteristic and the same absolute transcendence degree are isomorphic.

Proof.

For the original result, we refer to (Steinitz
[
33
]
, p.125). For a more recent presentation we also mention (Hunderford
[
12
]
, Ch.VI, Thm.1.12, p.317).
∎

In some cases we can eliminate the concept of
absolute transcendence degree
from the discussion.

4.2 Corollary

(Uncountable Cardinality)
.

All algebraically closed fields of the same characteristics and the same uncountable cardinality are mutually isomorphic.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
4.1
, because the absolute transcendence degree coincides with the cardinality of the field for uncountable fields. Indeed, let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field with
{\rm{card}\,}\,\mathbb{K}>\aleph_{0}
and let
\mathbb{F}
stands for its prime subfield. We observe that the formula (
1
) at the end of Section
3
reduces to
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{K})=\max\{\aleph_{0},\,{\rm trd}\,\mathbb{K}\}
,
since
{\rm{card}\,}\,\mathbb{F}\leq\aleph_{0}
and
{\rm trd}(\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F})={\rm trd}\,\mathbb{K}
, because
\mathbb{F}
is the prime subfield of
\mathbb{K}
. On the other hand, the above formula implies
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{K})={\rm trd}\,\mathbb{K}
(as desired), since
{\rm{card}\,}\,\mathbb{K}>\aleph_{0}
by assumption.
∎

The above corollary justifies the following “algebraic definition” of the field
\mathbb{C}
. Notice that the field of reals
\mathbb{R}
participates only through its cardinality,
\mathfrak{c}
; the fact that
\mathbb{R}
is a Dedekind complete topological field is not even mentioned in the definition. (In contrast, compare with Section
9
).

4.3 Definition

(Algebraic Definition of
\mathbb{C}
)
.

\mathbb{C}
is (by definition) an algebraically closed field of zero-characteristic and cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
.

4.4 Corollary

(Two Fields)
.

The fields
\mathcal{R}_{1}(i)
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}(i)
are isomorphic for every two real closed fields
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
such that
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R}_{1})={\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R}_{2})>\aleph_{0}
. In particular,
\mathcal{R}(i)
and
\mathbb{C}
are isomorphic for every real closed field
\mathcal{R}
(Archimedean or not) of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
. Moreover, if
\sigma:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}
is an isomorphism, then
\sigma(q)=q
for all
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\sigma(i)=i
or
\sigma(i)=-i
. Consequently,
\sigma(x+iy)=\sigma(x)\pm i\sigma(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{R}
, depending on
\sigma(i)=\pm i
, respectively.

Proof.

An immediate consequence of Corollary
4.2
taking into account that both
\mathcal{R}_{1}(i)
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}(i)
are algebraically closed fields by Artin-Schreier theorem (Section
3
) and both fields are of zero characteristic (since
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
are of zero characteristic). Moreover,
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R}_{1}(i))={\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R}_{2}(i))
, because
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R}_{n}(i))={\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
,
n=1,2
. In particular,
\mathcal{R}(i)
and
\mathbb{C}
are isomorphic, because
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}(i)
and
\mathbb{R}
is a real closed field. Finally,
\sigma(i)=\pm i
follows from the fact that
\sigma(i)
is one of the two solutions of
x^{2}+1=0
in
\mathbb{C}
.
∎

The next theorem is a counterpart of Steinitz theorem for real closed fields.

4.5 Theorem

(Erdös, Gillman, Henriksen)
.

Every two real closed fully saturated fields
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
of the same uncountable cardinality are isomorphic.

Proof.

We refer to (Erdös, Gillman and Henriksen
[
8
]
, Thm.2.1, p.543). We should mention that this result requires GCH (in addition to the more conventional ZFC).
∎

5
Infinitesimals in Algebra

Our main result below has
the character of a
mathematical observation
and it is a straightforward consequence of what was discussed in Section
4
. Our framework is purely algebraic (Definition
4.3
). The discussion of
\mathbb{C}
from the point of view of analysis will be postpone for Section
9
.

5.1 Theorem

(Main Result)
.

\mathbb{C}
contains a non-Archimedean totally ordered subfield
\mathcal{R}
. Consequently, the non-zero infinitesimals in
\mathcal{R}
are also elements of
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{R}
is a non-Archimedean real closed field of
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R})=\mathfrak{c}
. The existence of such a field is left to Examples
6.1
in the next section. By Corollary
4.4
, the fields
\mathcal{R}(i)
and
\mathbb{C}
are isomorphic. Let
\sigma:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}
be any isomorphism between them. The subfield
\sigma[\mathcal{R}]
of
\mathbb{C}
is also real closed field. Moreover,
\sigma[\mathcal{R}]
is also non-Archimedean, because the algebraic operations in a real closed field determines uniquely the order. Indeed, let
x
be a positive infinitesimal in
\mathcal{R}
, i.e.
0<x<1/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus
0<\sigma(x)<1/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
as well, since
\sigma(1/n)=1/n
. The latter means that
\sigma(x)
is a positive infinitesimal in
\sigma[\mathcal{R}]
. Clearly,
\sigma(x)\in\mathbb{C}
.
∎

5.2 Definition

(Infinitesimals in
\mathbb{C}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a non-Archimedean real closed field of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
and
\sigma:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}
be a field isomorphism. We define the absolute value
|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}:\mathbb{C}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
, (with range
{\rm{ran}}(|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]})=\{\sigma(\mathcal{R}):x\in\mathcal{R},x\geq 0\}
) by the formula
|\sigma(x+iy|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}=\sigma(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{R}
. For the sets of the finite, infinitesimal, finite, but non-infinitesimal and infinitely large elements of
\mathbb{C}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{\sigma(x+iy)\in\mathbb{C}:\;\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\leq n\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\{\sigma(x+iy)\in\mathbb{C}:\;\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}<1/n\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{I}=\{\sigma(x+iy)\in\mathbb{C}:\;1/n\leq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\leq n\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\{\sigma(x+iy)\in\mathbb{C}:\;n<\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

respectively, where we have taken advantage of the fact that
\sigma(q)=q
for all
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.

5.3 Theorem

(Algebraic vs. Transcendental)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a non-Archimedean real closed field of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
(as above). Let
\mathbb{A}
denote the field of the real algebraic numbers and
\mathbb{A}(i)
stand for the field of all algebraic numbers. Then:

fnum@T-enumi(i)(i)

\mathbb{A}(i)\setminus\{0\}\subset\mathcal{U}
. Consequently, a non-zero algebraic number cannot be neither infinitesimal, nor an infinitely large element of
\mathbb{C}
(whatever is the choice of
\mathcal{R}
and
\sigma
).

fnum@T-enumi(ii)(ii)

Non-zero infinitesimals and infinitely large numbers in
\mathbb{C}
are all transcendental, i.e.
\mathcal{I}\setminus\{0\}\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{A}(i)
and
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{A}(i)
.

fnum@T-enumi(iii)(iii)

Let
z\in\mathbb{A}(i)
and
dx\in\mathcal{I}
. Then
z+dx\in\mathbb{A}(i)
if and only if
dx=0
.

fnum@T-enumi(iv)(iv)

For every non-Archimedean real closed field
\mathcal{R}
of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
and every transcendental number
z\in\mathbb{C}
there exists an isomorphism
\sigma_{1}:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}
such that
\sigma_{1}(z)\notin\mathcal{U}
. Consequently, one of the two numbers in the set
\{\sigma_{1}(z),\;1/\sigma_{1}(z)\}
is a non-zero infinitesimal and the other is an infinitely large element of
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

(i)

Suppose
z\in\mathbb{A}(i)\setminus\{0\}
. We have
P(z)=0
for some polynomial
P(t)=q_{0}t^{d}+q_{1}t^{d-1}+\dots+q_{d}
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 1
, and
q_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
k=0,1,\dots,d
, since
\mathbb{A}(i)
is an algebraically closed field. Thus
|z|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}\leq 1+\frac{d}{|q_{0}|}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|q_{k}|
, where we have taken into account that
|q|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}=|q|
for all
q\in\mathbb{Q}
. The latter implies
|z|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}\leq n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1+\frac{d}{|q_{0}|}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|q_{k}|\leq n
. Thus
z\in\mathcal{F}
. Similarly,
1/z\in\mathcal{F}
(since
1/z\in\mathbb{A}(i)\setminus\{0\}
). Hence,
z\in\mathcal{U}
as required.

(ii)

is equivalent to (i).

(iii)

Let
z+dx\in\mathbb{A}(i)
and suppose (seeking a contradiction) that
dx\not=0
. Thus
dx=z+dx-z\in\mathbb{A}(i)\setminus\{0\}
implying
dx\in\mathcal{U}
by (i), contradicting the assumption that
dx\in\mathcal{I}
. Conversely,
dx=0
implies
z+dx\in\mathbb{A}(i)
(trivially), because
z\in\mathbb{A}(i)
by assumption.

(iv)

Let
\sigma:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}
be an isomorphism,
x
be a non-zero infinitesimal in
\mathcal{R}
and
\sigma(x)
be the corresponding non-zero infinitesimal in
\mathbb{C}
(as in the proof of Theorem
5.1
). Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
be a transcendental number. We extend
\{\sigma(x)\}
and
\{z\}
to two transcendence bases
\mathcal{B}_{x}:=\{\sigma(x)\}\cup\mathbb{B}_{x}
and
\mathcal{B}_{z}:=\{z\}\cup\mathbb{B}_{z}
, respectively, of the same field extension
\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{Q}
(Section
3
). Let
\varphi:\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{x})\mapsto\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{z})
denote the isomorphism defined by
\varphi(\sigma(x))=z
and
\varphi[\mathcal{B}_{x}]=\mathcal{B}_{z}
. Since
\mathbb{C}\!:\!\mathbb{Q}
is a Galois extension,
\varphi
admits an extension to an automorphism
\widehat{\varphi}:\mathbb{C}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
(Hunderford
[
12
]
, Ch.VI) and/or (Milne
[
22
]
, Ch.7); and we still have
\widehat{\varphi}(\sigma(x))=z
. Now, the isomorphism
\sigma_{1}:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}
, defined by the formula
\sigma_{1}=\widehat{\varphi}\circ\sigma
, is what we are looking for, because
z=\sigma_{1}(x)
is a non-zero infinitesimal.

∎

The “luxury” for having infinitesimals in
\mathbb{C}
(as any luxury in life or science) is not “for free”. This is the content of the following remark.

5.4 Remark

(Price to Pay)
.

Let
\sigma:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}
be an isomorphism, where
\mathcal{R}
is a non-Archimedean real closed field of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
(Theorem
5.1
) and assume, in addition, that
\mathcal{R}
contains a copy of
\mathbb{R}
. (The fields:
\mathbb{R}\{t\}
,
\mathbb{R}\langle t\rangle
,
\mathbb{R}[t]
,
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
,
{}^{*}\!\mathbb{A}
and
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}
in Example
6.1
satisfy these assumptions.) Notice that the field extension
\mathbb{C}:\sigma[\mathbb{R}]
is transcendental (since
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}
is transcendental), in contrast to
\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}
, which is algebraic. Consequently,
there is no automorphism

\Phi:\mathbb{C}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
such that
\Phi[\mathbb{R}]=\sigma[\mathbb{R}]
, because both field extensions are Galois extensions (Hunderford
[
12
]
, Ch.VI) and/or (Milne
[
22
]
, Ch.7).

7
Uniqueness up to Isomorphism

Some of the numerous fields listed in Examples

are actually, identical or isomorphic under some additional assumptions. For example, if

is a finite, but non-infinitesimal element of

\mathcal{M}_{\rho_{1}}=\mathcal{M}_{\rho_{2}}

\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{1}}=\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{2}}

\mathcal{M}_{\rho_{1}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\rho_{2}}
are convex rings. In this case
{}^{\rho_{1}}\mathbb{R}
and
{}^{\rho_{2}}\mathbb{R}
are not just isomorphic, but identical. In this section we impose CH in addition to ZFC (Section

and

and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\{t\}\subset\mathbb{R}\langle t^{\mathbb{R}}\rangle\subset\mathbb{R}[t^{\mathbb{R}}]\subset{{}^{*}\mathbb{R}}[t^{\mathbb{R}}]\hookrightarrow{{}^{*}\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\{t\}\subset\mathbb{R}\langle t^{\mathbb{R}}\rangle\subset\mathbb{R}[t^{\mathbb{R}}]\hookrightarrow{{}^{*}\mathbb{R}}\subset{{}^{*}\mathbb{R}}[t^{\mathbb{R}}],

where
\subset
stands for “subfield” (hence, “subset”) and
\hookrightarrow
stands for “field embedding”.

8
\kappa
-Complex Numbers

We generalize the result of Theorem
5.1
and show that every field
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
of
\kappa
-complex numbers (defined below) contains non-zero infinitesimals.

8.1 Definition

(
\kappa
-Complex Numbers)
.

Let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal and let
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
be an algebraically closed field of zero characteristic and cardinality
\kappa_{+}
. We refer to the elements of
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
as
\kappa
-complex numbers.

In the next theorem we show the existence of
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
for every infinite cardinal
\kappa
. For the concept of
\kappa_{+}
-good free ultrafilter
(used below) we refer to Chang & Keisler
[
5
]
and for an exposition on the topic we also mention (Lindstrøm
[
16
]
, Appendix).

8.2 Theorem

(Generalized Fundamental Theorem of Algebra)
.

For every infinite cardinal
\kappa
there exists a unique, up to isomorphism, algebraically closed field of zero-characteristic and cardinality
\kappa_{+}
. Consequently
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
exists for every infinite cardinal
\kappa
.

Proof.

\mathbb{R}
is a real closed field, because every polynomial with real coefficients is a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
hence, if the degree of such a polynomial is odd, its graph must intersect the
x
-axis at least once. Thus
\mathbb{R}(i)
is an algebraically closed field of zero characteristic by Artin-Schreier
[
2
]
theorem - the so called fundamental theorem of algebra. Next, let
I
is a set of cardinality
\kappa
and
\mathcal{V}
be a
\kappa_{+}
-good free ultrafilter on
I
. Let
\mathbb{R}^{I}/\!\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
stand for the corresponding non-standard extension of
\mathbb{R}
. Thus (whether
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
or
\kappa>\aleph_{0}
) the field
\mathbb{R}^{I}/\!\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
is real closed by transfer principle (Davis
[
7
]
, p.28), since
\mathbb{R}
is real closed and
\mathbb{R}^{I}/\!\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
is a non-standard extension of
\mathbb{R}
. Also,
\mathbb{R}^{I}/\!\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
is of cardinality
\kappa_{+}
, because the ultrafilter
\mathcal{V}
is
\kappa
-good by assumption. It is customary to use the simpler notation
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
(along with
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{R})=\kappa_{+}
) instead of
\mathbb{R}^{I}/\!\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
. Using this notation,
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}(i)
must be algebraically closed field of zero characteristic by Artin-Schreier theorem and
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}(i)
is of cardinality
\kappa_{+}
, since
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{R})=\kappa_{+}
. These properties of
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}(i)
determines
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}(i)
uniquely, up to isomorphism, by Corollary
4.2
applied for characteristic zero. In particular,
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}(i)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
proving the existence of
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
.
∎

8.3 Remark

(Countable Fields)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}(i)
denote the field of the algebraic numbers in
\mathbb{C}
(here
\mathbb{A}
stands for the field of the real algebraic numbers). The field
\mathbb{A}(i)
is algebraically closed, of zero characteristic and
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{A}(i))=\aleph_{0}
. Notice however, that the countable fields are excluded in the above theorem; the reason is that they do not satisfy the condition for “uniqueness up to isomorphism”; two countable algebraically closed fields of zero characteristic might or might not be isomorphic.

8.4 Theorem
.

Each of the fields
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
contains non-zero infinitesimals in the sense that
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
contains a non-Archimedean subfields.

Proof.

We already know that
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
contains non-zero infinitesimals in the case
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
, since
\mathbb{C}_{\aleph_{0}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
(Section
5
). In the case
\kappa>\aleph_{0}
we observe that
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{R}(i)
for every real closed field
\mathcal{R}
of cardinality
\kappa_{+}
. Hence,
\mathcal{R}
must be non-Archimedean as totally ordered field of cardinality greater than
\mathfrak{c}
. Thus
\mathcal{R}
can be embedded as a subfield of
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
supplying
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
with non-zero infinitesimals.
∎

8.5 Remark

(Warning about Notation)
.

The asterisk notation (like any notation in mathematics) is less than perfect. For example, the fields
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}/\!\!\sim_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{I}\!\!/\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
are certainly non-isomorphic in the case
{\rm{card}\,}(I)>\aleph_{0}
. Indeed, we have
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}/\!\!\sim_{\mathcal{U}})=\mathfrak{c}
, while
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{R}^{I}/\!\!\sim_{\mathcal{V}})=\kappa_{+}>\mathfrak{c}
, where
\kappa={\rm{card}\,}(I)
. Still we usually use the same notation,
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
for both. The same hold for
{}^{*}\mathbb{C}
and the corresponding fields of asymptotic numbers
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{R}
and
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}
. For example, we have
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{C})={\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{c}
in (Oberguggenberger & Todorov
[
24
]
) and
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{C})={\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{c}_{+}
in (Todorov & Vernaeve
[
37
]
). To avoid misunderstanding, the readers is adviced to pay attention to the choice of the saturation (hence, cardinality) of
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
usually specified at the very beginning of the text under reading.

9
Infinitesimals in Analysis

So far we discuss the infinitesimals in
\mathbb{C}
(Definition
4.3
) and infinitesimals in
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
(Section
8
) mostly from algebraic point of view. In our last section we supply
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
hence, also
\mathbb{C}
, with different, non-homeomorphic in general, topologies and shortly discuss standard, non-standard and non-standard asymptotic analysis in comparison.

9.1 Definition

(Topology on
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
)
.

Let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal and
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
stand for the field of
\kappa
-compex numbers (Definition
8.1
). Let
\mathcal{R}
be a real closed field of cardinality
\kappa_{+}
and
\sigma:\mathcal{R}(i)\mapsto\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
stand for a field isomorphism (Section
8
).

1.

We define the absolute value
|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}:\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}\mapsto\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
, by
|\sigma(x+iy|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}=\sigma(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{R}
and supply
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
with the topology generated by
|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
. We denote the corresponding topological field by
(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]})
.

2.

In the particular case
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
we shall write simply,
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]})
, instead of
(\mathbb{C}_{\aleph_{0}},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]})

(since
\mathbb{C}_{\aleph_{0}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
and the latter determines
\mathbb{C}
uniquely up to isomorphism - Definition
4.3
).

3.

We say that
z\in\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
(in particular,
z\in\mathbb{C}
) is an
infinitesimal relative to the absolute value

|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
if
0\leq|z|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}<1/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Similarly,
z

is finite relative to

|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
if
|z|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}\leq n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
z

is infinitely large relative to

|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
if
n<|z|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The absolute value
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{R}}
determines on
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
the product topology inherited from the order topology on
\sigma[\mathcal{R}]
. In addition,
|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
can be used for localizing the infinitesimals within
\mathbb{C}
.

9.2 Lemma

(Infinitesimals Relative to the Absolute Value)
.

\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
is free of non-zero infinitesimals relative to
|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
if and only if
\mathcal{R}
is an Archimedean field if and only if
\sigma[\mathcal{R}]
is an Archimedean field if and only if
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
.

Proof.

For the range of the absolute value we have
{\rm{ran}}(|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]})=\{x\in\sigma[\mathcal{R}]:x\geq 0\}
. Thus
{\rm{ran}}(|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]})
is free of positive infinitesimals
{\rm{\,iff\;}}
\sigma[\mathcal{R}]
is free of non-zero infinitesimals
{\rm{\,iff\;}}
\sigma[\mathcal{R}]

is an Archimedean field
{\rm{\,iff\;}}
\mathcal{R}
is an Archimedean field. Finally, the latter is equivalent to
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
, since
{\rm{card}\,}(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa})=\aleph_{1}=\mathfrak{c}={\rm{card}\,}(\mathcal{R})
(Section
2
).

∎

9.3 Example
.

We go through the examples in Examples
6.1
and their counterparts in Section
8
.

1.

\mathbb{C}
is
free of non-zero infinitesimals
(hence, free of infinitely large elements)
relative to

|\cdot|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
in the particular cases
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is a transcendental real number (Examples
6.1
).

2.

(\mathbb{C},\;{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathbb{R}]})
, commonly denoted, simply by
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}(i)
, is the only topological field among those in the form
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]})
, which is a Banach algebra. The latter follows from the fact that
\mathbb{R}
hence,
\sigma[\mathbb{R}]
, is Dedekind complete (
\sup
-complete).

3.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be any of the real closed fields in Examples
6.1
except the fields
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}
(mentioned above). Then
\mathbb{C}

does contain non-zero infinitesimals
(hence, infinitely large elements)
relative to

|\!\cdot\!|_{\sigma[\mathcal{R}]}
(the fields
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}
are the only Archimedean fields in Examples
6.1
).

4.

Let
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
be the non-standard extension of
\mathbb{R}
of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
(Examples
6.1
). The topological field
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
is commonly denoted by
{}^{*}\mathbb{C}
with the restriction
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{c}
or simply, by
{{}^{*}\mathbb{R}}(i)
, where
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{R})=\mathfrak{c}
. It is the only topological field among those in Examples
6.1
, which is
\mathfrak{c}
-saturated in the sense that every nested sequence
of open disks
(more generally,
of internal sets
) of
\mathbb{C}
relative to
{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]}
has non-empty intersection (Lindstrøm
[
16
]
, Loeb
[
17
]
). More generally, let
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
be a field of
\kappa
-complex numbers of cardinality
\kappa_{+}
(Definition
8.1
). Then the topological field
(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
, is commonly denoted also by
{}^{*}\mathbb{C}
with the restriction
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{C})=\kappa_{+}
or simply, by
{{}^{*}\mathbb{R}}(i)
, where
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{R})=\kappa_{+}
(Remark
8.5
). The field
{}^{*}\mathbb{C}=(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
is
\kappa_{+}
-saturated in the sense that every family of cardinality
\kappa

of open disks
(and more generally,
of internal sets
) of
\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}
relative to

with the finite intersection property has non-empty intersection (Lindstrøm
[

]
, Loeb
[

]
).

5.

Let
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{R}
stand for Robinson’s field of asymptotic numbers of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
(Examples
6.1
). The topological field
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]})
is commonly denoted by
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}
with the restriction
{\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{c}
or equivalently, by
{{}^{\rho}\mathbb{R}}(i)
, where
{\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{R})=\mathfrak{c}
. The field
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}
is non-saturated, but it is Cantor complete in the sense that every nested sequence of closed disks of
\mathbb{C}
relative to
{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]}
has non-empty intersection (Todorov &Wolf
[
35
]
). Similarly, the topological field
(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]})
is commonly denoted also by
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}
with the restriction
{\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{C})=\kappa_{+}
or equivalently, by
{{}^{\rho}\mathbb{R}}(i)
, where
{\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{R})=\kappa_{+}
. As before,
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}
is Cantor complete, but non-saturated.

6.

As we already know, from purely algebraic point of view we have the isomorphism
{\mathbb{C}_{\kappa}}\cong{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}\cong{{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}}
in the case
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{C})={\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{C})=\kappa_{+}
. In particular,
{\mathbb{C}}\cong{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}\cong{{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}}
in the case
{\rm{card}\,}(^{*}\mathbb{C})={\rm{card}\,}(^{\rho}\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{c}
. In contrast, the topological fields
(\mathbb{C},\;{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathbb{R}]})
,
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
and
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]})
are
pairwise non-homeomorphic
. Indeed,
(\mathbb{C},\;{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathbb{R}]})
is Dedekind complete (sup-complete) unlike the other two, which are not (because they both contain non-zero infinitesimals relative to their absolute values). On the other hand, the last two fields are also non-homeomorphic, because
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
is saturated unlike
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]})
, which is non-saturated. Similarly, the topological fields
(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
and
(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]})
are non-homeomorphic, because the first is saturated and the second is not. These topological fields behave quite differently in complex and functional analysis, which is the topic of the next remark.

9.4 Remark

(Standard vs. Non-Standard Analysis)
.

1.

As we know, the topological field
(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathbb{R}]})
is the common framework of (standard)
complex and functional analysis
. Its success is due to the fact that
\mathbb{R}
is Dedekind complete hence,
\mathbb{C}
is a Banach algebra.

2.

The field
{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}=(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
and its “larger” counterpart
{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}=(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
are the framework of
non-standard complex and non-standard functional analysis
(Davis
[
7
]
, Lindstrøm
[
16
]
, Loeb
[
17
]
). Due to the Transfer Principle (Davis
[
7
]
, Ch.1, Thm.7.3), every result of standard analysis (expressed in terms of the field
\mathbb{C}=(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[\mathbb{R}]})
) can be also expressed equivalently in the terms of the fields
{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}=(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
or
{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}=(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{*}\mathbb{R}]})
, i.e. in the language of non-standard analysis. In addition, the non-standard version of the statements
are simpler
than their standard counterpart in the sense that they involve less quantifiers (Cavalcante
[
4
]
). Here is a typical example: Let
c
be an accumulating point of
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and
f:D\mapsto\mathbb{C}
be a complex-valued function. Let
L\in\mathbb{C}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(3)

\displaystyle(\forall dx\in\mathcal{I}(^{*}\mathbb{R}))(c+dx\in{{}^{*}\!D}\Rightarrow{{}^{*}\!f}(c+dx)-L\in\mathcal{I}(^{*}\mathbb{R})),

(4)

\displaystyle(\forall\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+})(\exists\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+})(\forall z\in D)(|z-c|<\gamma\Rightarrow|f(z)-L|<\varepsilon),

(5)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\mapsto c}f(z)=L.

Here
\mathcal{I}(^{*}\mathbb{R})
stands for the set of infinitesimals in
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
and
{}^{*}\!D
and
{}^{*}\!f
are the non-standard extension of
D
and
f
, respectively.

3.

Readers with interest to history of calculus might notice that (
3
) very much resembles a criterion for convergence in the old Leibniz/Euler infinitesimal calculus. This especially become obvious if we drop the asterisks in front of
{}^{*}\mathbb{R},{{}^{*}\!D}
and
f
and verbalize the statement in language of 17-19 centuries mathematics. We should be not surprise that the calculus was first invented in terms of infinitesimals requiring a single quantifier (rather than three non-commuting ones).

4.

A more recent example of taking advantage of the simplicity of non-standard methods offers the
Bernstein–Robinson
[
3
]
solution of the open invariant subspace problem for polynomially compact operators on Hilbert space
(for a presentation see also Davis
[
7
]
, Ch.5, §3). A standard solution was produced a decay later (Lomonosov
[
18
]
).

5.

Another example of efficiency of non-standard methods comes from the recent
solution of the Hilbert fifth problem for local groups
(Goldbring
[
9
]
). As far as we can tell, a standard solution of this problem has not been produced so far. As in the previous example, the reducing of the numbers of quantifiers (compared with those in the earlier attempts to solve the same problem by standard methods) plays a decisive role.

6.

As we noted already every statement in standard analysis has an equivalent counterpart in non-standard analysis. The converse however, is not always true: there are results in non-standard analysis without counterpart in standard analysis. For example, Dirac delta-functional appears as a pointwise function
\delta:{{}^{*}\mathbb{R}}\mapsto{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}
within an algebra of generalized function of Colombeau type (Vernaeve
[
42
]
). The latter is impossible in standard analysis, since
\delta
takes infinitely large values for some infinitesimal numbers in
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
. Similar result appears in non-standard asymptotic analysis discussed below. Let us recall that an associative commutative differential algebra
G
is called
algebras of generalized functions of Colombeau type
if
G
contains a copy of Schwartz distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
(as a differential vector subspace) and such that the product in
G
reduces to the usual point-wise product on the ring of
C^{\infty}
-functions in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
(Colombeau
[
6
]
). Every such algebra
solves the problem of multiplication of Schwartz distributions
, since
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
itself does not admit associative product (Schwartz
[
31
]
).

7.

The field
{{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}}=(\mathbb{C},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]})
and its “larger” counterpart
{{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}}=(\mathbb{C}_{\kappa},\,{|\!\cdot\!|}_{\sigma[^{\rho}\mathbb{R}]})
are the framework of
non-standard asymptotic analysis
(Robinson
[
30
]
, Lightstone & Robinson
[
15
]
). As mentioned already, the field
{}^{\rho}\mathbb{C}
had beed used in the role of
fields of scalars of algebras of generalized functions of Colombeau type
(Oberguggenberger &Todorov
[
24
]
, Todorov &Vernaeve
[
37
]
, Todorov
[
38
]
-
[
41
]
).

9.5 Remark

(Summary)
.

We show that even the familiar (and widely popular) field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
contains non-zero infinitesimals. We try to convince the reader that infinitesimals are not only unavoidable in modern mathematics, but they are, actually, very useful for simplifying mathematical reasoning by reducing the numbers of quantifiers compared with those in standard mathematics; just like infinitesimals were spectacularly successful (and perhaps, unavoidable) in creation of calculus long time ago. The simplicity of non-standard methods is especially noticeable in the presence of plenty of infinitesimals - the so called
saturation
.
