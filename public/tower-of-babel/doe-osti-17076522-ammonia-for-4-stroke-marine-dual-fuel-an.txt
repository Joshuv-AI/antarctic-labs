 ABSTRACT

This report presents research and development efforts focused on ammonia as an alternative fuel for
marine engines, specifically targeting four-stroke dual-fuel and gas engine retrofits and new designs. The
project, jointly funded by the U.S. Department of Transportation Maritime Administration and the U.S.
Department of Energy, addresses technical barriers related to ammonia combustion, emissions, materials
compatibility, and safety. Experimental work was conducted using a modified Cummins diesel engine
and flow reactor systems to characterize performance, emissions, and catalyst behavior. Key findings
include strategies for minimizing NOx, N2O, and NH3 emissions, achieving diesel-like thermal
efficiencies, and optimizing fuel injection and emissions control systems. The research supports
MARAD’s mission by providing industry stakeholders with data, publications, and presentations that
inform the adoption of ammonia as a marine fuel, particularly for inland and coastal vessels. Results have
been disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, open datasets, and extensive presentations,
advancing the state of knowledge and enabling further technology development for ammonia-capable
marine engines

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

1.1 Motivation

Ammonia has gained significant interest as an alternative fuel for marine vessels, but there is a lack of
information in the open literature on performance and emissions resulting from the use of ammonia in
compression ignition (CI) engines. Barriers to consuming ammonia (NH3) as a fuel in marine diesel
engines include (1) less favorable ignition energy and flame speed relative to conventional fuels; (2)
emissions challenges, including potentially high NH3, NOx, and N2O emissions; (3) materials
compatibility issues with NH3 contributing to fuel delivery and handling challenges; and (4) the human
and environmental toxicity concerns with NH3 exposure, which require additional engineering controls
and handling procedures compared with other fuels. The “immediately dangerous to life and health”
concentration for anhydrous NH3 is 300 ppm, and fatal exposures to humans at 5,000 to 10,000 ppm have
been reported.

Retrofit solutions for dual-fuel NH3 combustion in CI engines are being explored to help accelerate the
introduction of alternative fuels for these long-lived assets. In this dual-fuel retrofit strategy, the direct
injection (DI) fuel system for diesel fuel (or other compression ignition fuel) is used to initiate
combustion of a premixed charge of ammonia in air with the ammonia being port fuel injected (PFI).

A jointly funded project between the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) Maritime Administration
(MARAD) and U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation
(CMEI) was initiated at ORNL to conduct research and development to improve utilization of ammonia
by advancing the science and understanding through combustion strategy development and emissions
characterization and emissions controls integration.

The overall objectives of this work were to develop combustion strategies and emission control solutions
to enable industry to use ammonia in marine engine retrofits and to inform the design of next-generation
ammonia-capable marine engines. Specific objectives of this work were to:

• Characterize and minimize emissions with high ammonia/diesel substitution ratios
• Measure emissions, and minimize NOx with commercially viable emissions control solutions
• Quantify efficiency differences across different ammonia combustion strategies
• Improve engine performance with ammonia fuel including optimization of fuel injection strategies

 2. BENEFITS TO FUNDING SPONSOR’S MISSION

2.1 Supporting MARAD’s Mission Through R&D

This project supports MARAD’s mission to foster, promote, and develop the U.S. maritime industry in
support of the nation’s economic and security needs. As the federal agency responsible for America’s
waterborne transportation system, MARAD advances the technical, operational, environmental, safety,
and national-security aspects of maritime transportation infrastructure.

The project addresses industry questions regarding low technology-readiness-level (TRL) ammonia
combustion approaches by generating experimental data using a research engine. These results help
maritime stakeholders understand both the potential benefits and technical challenges associated with
ammonia as a marine fuel, enabling them to assess whether further technology development and eventual
commercial deployment may be justified.

Much of the currently available ammonia-combustion literature is focused on large ocean-going vessels
powered by slow-speed, two-stroke engines. In contrast, this research uses a high-speed, four-stroke
engine to investigate ammonia-fuel opportunities and challenges relevant to auxiliary engines and smaller
propulsion systems. These applications are particularly important for inland and coastal vessels operating
in the United States.

To further support industry and the maritime community, the results of this research have been
disseminated through a series of publications, presentations and datasets that are openly available and
summarized further in Sections 3.2, 3.3, and 3.4. In addition, the dual-fuel ammonia research platform
was leveraged to support a collaboration with the University of Tennessee—ORNL Innovation Institute
on the use of spiking neural networks to control advanced dual-fuel combustion concepts, and data sets
shared with Argonne National Laboratory and Clemson University resulted in joint publications.

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED

3.1 Summary of Technical Work Performed

The outputs of this research and development project were aimed at filling in gaps in the literature for
premixed ammonia combustion and project and generating validation data sets at small scale that can be
applied to all scales of engines and datasets and publications. The output was reported to the public
domain as much as possible and is detailed in Sections 3.2 for peer reviewed publication, 3.3 for data sets
and reports, and 3.4 for presentations.

The engine experiments were conducted on a Cummins 6.7L ISB diesel engine which was converted to
single cylinder operation. This is a 250 HP version of the engine that has a 20:1 compression ratio and a
cylinder bore of 107mm. The research platform was modified for dual-fuel operation with purpose-built
ammonia PFI injectors and the ability to port inject hydrogen via a mass flow controller. Several
emissions control devices were evaluated on the single-cylinder research platform. Gaseous emissions
were measured using a Fourier Transfer Infrared (FTIR) analyzer, a paramagnetic oxygen analyzer, and a
flame ionization detector, and particulate matter (soot) was measured using an AVL Micro Soot Sensor.

An automated flow reactor system was used to evaluate the performance of two different ammonia
decomposition catalyst formulations to inform the hydrogen-enhanced dual-fuel ammonia experiments on
engine to bound what could be generated in an integrated system.

In addition, the findings from a Hazard and Operability Study for the Ammonia Fuel Systems at the
National Transportation Research Center were published as an ORNL report to share that analysis with
the community.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

4.1 Key Findings and Conclusions from Project

This project identified technical barriers and challenges for implementation of dual-fuel ammonia retrofits
for 4-stroke compression ignition engines and through a series of experimental and modeling
investigations, helped fill in critical gaps in the knowledge base, and advanced the state of the art for dual-
fuel ammonia engine research.

Some key findings from this work that have been disseminated through publications, presentations and
data sets include:

 • Increased understanding of air to fuel ratio & injection strategy effects on combustion and
 emissions behavior
 • Diesel-like thermal efficiencies achieved at moderate to high loads + thermodynamic analysis
 completed
 • Demonstrated reductions of N2O possible with richer late-pilot and early-pilot conditions
 • Initial SCR results show potential for simultaneous reductions of NOx and NH3
 • Temperatures > 420°C are needed to achieve > 10% H2 Yields for Ru-based Catalyst
 • Bio-pilot experiments completed with renewable diesel and biodiesel
 • Cu-SCR aids in simultaneous reductions of NH3 and NOx reductions up at the expense of
 increased N2O
 • Additional heat input needed for Ni-based decomposition catalyst for generating on-board H2
 • H2 enhancement allows greater NH3 utilization, but effect is small compared to λ and comes at
 the expense of adverse NOx and N2O effects
 • Soot reduction with premixed NH3 dual-fuel combustion was not proportional to 90–96%
 reduction in diesel fueling
 • Collaboration with ANL: CFD insights on in-cylinder process (role of pilot ignition, flame
 propagation, & wall quenching)
