Prevalence of oral recurrent aphthous stomatitis and its association with stress among a sample of Egyptian dental students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Christine Raouf George Mikhail, Marwa Hassan Mostafa, Fatma E. A. Hassanein, Eman D. El Desouky, Nourhan Abdullah Abdelkhalek, Mai Abdelhalim Hamouda, Rehab Ahmed Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) is one of the most common inflammatory ulcerative disorders that impact the oral mucosa. The etiology of aphthous ulcers is complex and multifaceted; however, emotional stress is considered one of the primary risk factors contributing to their development in younger populations.ObjectivesThis study aimed to determine the prevalence of RAS among a sample of dental students, its correlation with their stress levels, and to identify possible risk factors linked to these oral ulcers.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 4th level dental students at King Salman International University using a standardized questionnaire evaluating medical and dental histories, personal habits, as well as oral hygiene practices including tooth brushing frequency and clinically relevant data regarding oral diseases, particularly RAS, alongside the 10- item Perceived Stress Scale.ResultsOne hundred twenty-eight out of 150 students participated, 68.8% experienced RAS with females exhibiting a significantly lower likelihood of developing aphthous ulcers compared to males (OR = 0.233, p = 0.015). Daily toothbrushing was unexpectedly associated with a markedly increased risk (OR = 9.634, p < 0.001), suggesting a potential underlying mechanism warranting further investigation. Additionally, a family history of recurrent ulcers emerged as the strongest predictor (OR = 34.394, p < 0.001), highlighting a possible genetic predisposition. While poor oral hygiene demonstrated a high odds ratio (OR = 11.224), the association was not statistically significant (p = 0.059), possibly due to wide variability in the data. Similarly, high and moderate stress levels exhibited elevated odds ratios (OR = 8.716 and OR = 6.519, respectively), but their associations did not show statistical significance (p = 0.056 and p = 0.061).ConclusionsThis research underscored a notable prevalence of RAS among dental students. It also identified familial history of aphthous stomatitis, presence of other dental issues, and regular daily toothbrushing as the three primary risk factors for aphthous lesions. Stress levels did not show a significant association with RAS lesions among dental students. Given that RAS is a multifactorial condition, further investigation into additional intervening factors is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13005-026-00626-w.

Background

Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) is among the most prevalent inflammatory ulcerative conditions affecting the oral mucosa [1]. The term “aphthous” originates from the Greek word “aphtha,” which translates to ulceration [2]. The global prevalence of oral ulcers stands at 4%, with 25% of these being aphthous ulcers, making them the most frequently occurring type of oral ulcer [3].

RAS primarily manifests in non-keratinized regions such as the lips, tongue, buccal mucosa, and soft palate. Typically, these ulcers are painful, shallow, round lesions characterized by an erythematous halo and a yellowish-gray slough covering them [1]. According to their clinical characteristics, recurrent aphthous ulcerations are classified into three types: minor aphthae (also known as Mikulicz’s aphthae), major aphthae (referred to as Sutton’s aphthae), and herpetiform aphthae [3]. At times, these ulcers can be so excruciating that they disrupt speech, chewing, or swallowing. In summary, they can significantly impede daily activities and diminish the quality of life [4].

In the existing literature, numerous factors have been identified as potential causes of RAS, including genetics, nutrition, local trauma, allergies, endocrine disorders, alterations in oral microbiota (disruption of flora), and cessation of smoking. However, the precise cause remains largely unknown [5–7]. It is widely believed that RAS is provoked by stress and anxiety [8].

Anxiety and stress are thought to have a significant connection to the onset of RAS, as indicated by various studies [9, 10]. Additionally, elevated levels of salivary cortisol have been associated with the development of RAS [11]. Some research has proposed that the menstrual cycle may play a crucial role in the occurrence of ulcers in female patients. Nevertheless, there is insufficient reliable data regarding the relationship between RAS and the pre-menstrual phase, pregnancy, or menopause [9].

In studies examining various ethnic groups with differing socio-economic statuses, the incidence rate ranges from 5% to 66% [12, 13]. Lifestyle encompasses a blend of needs and desires shaped by influences such as culture, family, social groups, and class. An unhealthy lifestyle, characterized by smoking, stress, poor diet, and insufficient physical activity, poses significant risks to human life [14]. Each lifestyle-related factor can profoundly impact the development of oral aphthous lesions. Stress plays a crucial role in the emergence of aphthous ulcers, contributing to their formation through various mechanisms, including trauma to the oral soft tissues resulting from habits like cheek and lip biting [12]. Some researchers have identified a link between aphthous ulcers and psychological factors, including anxiety, suppression of anger and rage, occupational stressors, and other stress-related factors. Numerous studies have explored the negative relationship between smoking and RAS. Nicotine and its metabolites are known to reduce levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, Interleukin 1, and Interleukin 6, while simultaneously increasing levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines like Interleukin 10 [8, 13]. Another aspect of lifestyle is oral hygiene. Research indicates that using dental floss and a toothbrush for a minimum of 2 min, 2 to 3 times daily, can help prevent the occurrence of aphthous ulcers [15].

Furthermore, a study conducted in 2023 by Zulkaidah revealed that 45.16% of people use removable dentures. 45.16% of respondents said they washed their dentures three times a day. Up to 67.74% of respondents wore their dentures constantly without taking them out. 45.16% of the cheek and lip mucosa is the area of RAS that is most commonly impacted. Up to 61.29% of those surveyed used self-medication as a form of treatment for RAS [16].

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) is one of the most common psychological tools for assessing the perception of stress. It evaluates the extent to which various situations in an individual’s life are considered stressful [17].

Prevalence studies are essential for practical purposes, including assessing the level of morbidity and the “disease burden” within a population affected by a non-fatal condition. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, there is a lack of data regarding RAS in the general population of young adolescents, especially among students at a particular institution [11]. Moreover, these recurrent painful ulcers in the oral mucosa often lead to discomfort, making it difficult to eat, speak, and swallow, thereby significantly impacting an individual’s quality of life [18].

Consequently, the aims of this study are to ascertain the prevalence of oral recurrent aphthous stomatitis in a cohort of dental students, examine its relationship with their stress levels, and identify potential risk factors associated with these oral ulcers.

Methods and subjects

Adhering to STROBE statement checklist in reporting observational cross-sectional study.

Study design

An observational cross-sectional study.

Ethical approval

Was obtained from the Fayoum University Supreme Committee for Scientific Research Ethics (FU-SCSRE)(RHDIRB30012022001). The objectives of the study and its potential benefits were thoroughly explained to each participant, with a strong emphasis on the confidentiality of the data collected. Prior to enrollment in the study, each participant was required to sign an informed consent form. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki regarding Medical Research involving Human Subjects.

Setting

The study was conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry, King Salman International University, located in South Sinai, Egypt.

Participants

Using convenient sampling technique, 128 out of 150 dental students representing the total number of 4th year dental students at the Faculty of Dentistry, King Salman International University. Participants were selected during a two-month period, spanning from March to April, 2025. Only students who agreed to take part in the research were included and received paper based questionnaire, those who resort to any type of addicts, those with severe systemic diseases or those taking medications such as steroids, which suppress the immune system, and who refused to complete the questionnaire and clinical examination were excluded. The students ‘grades weren’t impacted by either agreeing or refusing to enter the study.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on the total number of students from 4th academic year, as well as previous research conducted by Verma et al., 2023 [19]. Using power 80% and 5% significance level with 99% CI and population size 150 ; 119 participants were required. Sample size is calculated using Epi Info.

Survey development

Data collection was carried out using a standardized questionnaire and clinical examination was done to each enrolled student by two expert academics specialized in Oral Medicine and Oral Diagnosis at the same time, but were blinded to the findings of each of them and Kappa values were calculated to determine the level of agreement (minor vs. major aphthous ulcer) which was found to be 0.9 to ensure inter-examiner reliability. The questionnaire was subjected to pretesting to assess both face and content validity.

Content and face validity were established through an expert panel review consisting of four specialists in [Oral Medicine, Oral Pathology, Periodontology & Prosthodontics]. Based on their feedback regarding item relevance and technical accuracy, minor adjustments were made to the phrasing of three items to improve clarity. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted with a small group of 20 students to evaluate the instrument’s clarity, comprehensiveness, and the time required for completion. The pilot participants confirmed that the items were easily understood and the total response time was acceptable.

The questionnaire consisted of two sets of questions. The first set included questions related to demographic data and clinically significant information regarding RAS (i.e., prior history, family history of RAS, date of the last ulcer, duration of the ulcer, frequency of occurrence, whether the ulcer was painful, count of ulcers observed in each episode, location of occurrence, medication required, associated conditions, and types of stress) as recommended by Verma et al. in 2023 [19].

Assessment of perceived stress levels

The second set of questionnaires comprised the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) which has undergone validation for its test-retest reliability, Cronbach’s alpha, and factorial validity across various populations and languages [20–22]. PSS-10 consists of ten items, with six items addressing the negative factor and four addressing the positive factor. The items are straightforward and inquire about an individual’s feelings and thoughts over the past month. Each question asks how often the person felt or thought in a certain manner. The response options are organized on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from zero (never) to four (always). The total score on the PSS-10, which can vary between 0 and 40 points, is derived from the sum of the scores of all items. Higher scores signify a greater level of perceived stress [23] .

The overall stress scores obtained were analyzed for individuals with and without recurrent oral ulcers. Additionally, a comparison of the stress scores was conducted among all participants. The questionnaire was prepared in the English language and uploaded as supplementary file.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 23 for Windows, Armonk, NY, USA. Quantitative variables were presented using mean and standard deviation (SD) while qualitative variables were presented using frequency and percentages. Association between aphthous ulcer occurrence and risk indicators were tested using independent t test and Pearson Chi Square. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to assess various risk indicators on the likelihood of an aphthous occurring. Adjusted odds ratio and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. All tests were two tailed and the significance level was set at p value at 0.05.

Results

Out of 150 students representing the 4th level dental students, 128 students accepted to participate in the present study with age range 21 – 23 years old. Among the participants, a gender distribution of 44 females and 84 males was observed, with a majority of 65.6% being male. Medical history unveiled that the majority were medically free, despite 21.9% of them reported psychiatric disorders with 26 students indicated stress and anxiety, while only 2 students reported depression. Concerning dental care, regular dental visits were common 48.4%, and 89.1% had diagnosed oral or dental problems, notably recurrent oral aphthous ulcer was the most prevalent 68.8% followed by gingivitis 28.1%. Previous dental treatment was reported by 48.4% of participants. 64 dental students had moderate oral hygiene status, 58 students had good oral hygiene while, only 6 suffered from poor or bad oral hygiene. The majority indicated regular tooth brushing with a regular frequency of twice/day, only 38 students were brushing their teeth irregularly and two students don’t care at all.

Sixty-eight point eight percent suffered from RAS, out of which 28.1% indicated positive family history of recurrent oral aphthous ulcers. Among these 88 students, 88.6% had their ulcers associated with stressful situations particularly exams. 59.1% reported a frequency of more than or almost three times per year while the rest 40.9% reported a frequency of less than 3 times/year. The majority 59.1% noticed a single ulcer during each episode, whereas 31.8% had two ulcers and 9.1% had three ulcers with two mucosal surfaces involved during each episode. Although, all of them indicated that the ulcers were painful, 84.1% did not use any medications for their ulcers, while 15.9% had applied topical analgesic agents. The most common area of occurrence was the labial mucosa 75%, followed by the ventral surface of tongue & floor of mouth 13.6%, 11.4% on buccal mucosa and vestibule (Table 1).

Among those with aphthous ulcers, 100% reported having dental problems, compared to only 65% in the unaffected group (p < 0.001). A family history of ulcers was present in 38.6% of affected individuals, significantly higher than the 5% in the unaffected group (p < 0.001). Daily toothbrushing was reported by 81.8% of those with ulcers, compared to 55% in the unaffected group (p = 0.001). Regular dental visits were more frequent among affected individuals (54.5%) than among those without ulcers (35%) (p = 0.040). Additionally, psychiatric disorders were reported by 22.7% of those with ulcers versus 20% in the unaffected group, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.729). Smoking was more common among those with ulcers (6.8%) compared to 0% in the unaffected group (p = 0.091), while mouth breathing was reported by 13.6% of affected individuals and 10% of the unaffected group (p = 0.564). Stress levels showed no significant differences, with high stress reported by 25% in both groups, moderate stress by 70.5% of affected individuals and 65% of the unaffected, and low stress by 4.5% and 10%, respectively (p = 0.490) (Table 3).

The regression analysis assessing the effect of risk indicators on the occurrence of aphthous ulcers revealed several significant associations. Females exhibited a significantly lower likelihood of developing aphthous ulcers compared to males (OR = 0.233, p = 0.015). Daily toothbrushing was unexpectedly associated with a markedly increased risk (OR = 9.634, p < 0.001), suggesting a potential underlying mechanism warranting further investigation. Additionally, a family history of recurrent ulcers emerged as the strongest predictor (OR = 34.394, p < 0.001), highlighting a possible genetic predisposition. While poor oral hygiene demonstrated a high odds ratio (OR = 11.224), the association was not statistically significant (p = 0.059), possibly due to wide variability in the data. Similarly, high and moderate stress levels exhibited elevated odds ratios (OR = 8.716 and OR = 6.519, respectively), but their associations did not show statistical significance (p = 0.056 and p = 0.061). Conversely, age, psychiatric disorders, and regular dental visits did not show significant associations with aphthous ulcer occurrence (Table 4).

Discussion

RAS is a widespread oral condition characterized by the repeated emergence of painful ulcers on the oral mucosa. These recurrent painful ulcers in the oral mucosa often lead to discomfort, making it difficult to eat, speak, and swallow, thereby significantly impacting an individual’s quality of life [18]. While RAS are influenced by multiple factors, emotional stress stands out as a critical risk factor for their development in young individuals [19] .

The present study aimed to determine the prevalence of RAS among 4th year dental students, its correlation with their stress levels, and to identify possible risk factors linked to these oral ulcers.

The current research revealed an incidence of RAS at 68.8% within our sample, which is lower than the findings of Eman et al., who reported a prevalence of RAS at 81% among dental students [24], Additionally, the study by Wan Ahmad Kamil et al. indicated a prevalence of 90.3% among UiTM Dental Students in Malaysia [11], and a prevalence of 83.3% was noted in dental students at Al-Azhar University in Egypt [25], In contrast, other studies conducted among dental students in India [26], Iran [27], Afghanistan [28], and Saudi Arabia [10] reported prevalence rates of 60%, 40.2%, 30%, and 21.7%, respectively, all of which are lower than the incidence found in our study. This outcome implies that RAU is significantly prevalent among dental students. It is important to approach a comparison of the current findings with those from previous studies with caution due to variations in study design, sample size, and geographic location.

Regarding the site of RAS occurrence, the most common was the labial mucosa (77.3%), followed by the ventral surface of tongue (13.6%), (18.2%) on buccal mucosa and vestibule, and (2.3%) on the floor of mouth. These results align with a study carried out by Manoj et al., which indicated that RAS was found on the labial mucosa at a prevalence of 78.2% [29]. Furthermore, Eman aga et al. indicated that the labial mucosa was the most impacted area at 40.7%, succeeded by the buccal mucosa at 35.8%, and other regions within the oral cavity at 14.8% [24]. These findings closely align with previous research that suggested the buccal mucosa and lips are the most affected areas, as these two regions are more vulnerable to damage from biting the lips and cheeks [4, 25, 27].

The majority of students (68.8%) experienced moderate stress, while 25% reported high stress levels. A smaller proportion (6.3%) had low stress levels. The overall mean stress score was 23.09 ± 5.89, reflecting a moderate level of stress across the whole sample. This observation can be partially attributed to the fact that dental students tend to experience higher levels of stress compared to those in other professional courses. The academic pressure they face, which involves tackling challenging assignments, contributes significantly to their stress levels. Additionally, parents and educators place considerable pressure on students to excel, compelling them to work tirelessly and creating a stressful atmosphere. Some students exhibit resistance to change, struggling to accept the necessity of relocating from their hometowns to pursue higher education, which often leads to feelings of homesickness. Many find it burdensome to step outside their comfort zones in order to realize their aspirations, rendering them susceptible to stress. Furthermore, poor time management is another contributing factor to the stress experienced by college students. A common sign of stress among students is inadequate sleep patterns. Students consistently seem to be under stress due to the anxiety surrounding upcoming exams or the pressure to complete assigned tasks.

Among those with aphthous ulcers, 100% reported having dental problems, compared to only 65% in the unaffected group (p < 0.001), indicating a positive correlation between the presence of RAS among the study participants and the incidence of caries, gingivitis, and periodontal diseases. This can be partially attributed to the effects of painful RAS on the oral mucosa, which causes discomfort and hinders the ability to eat, speak, swallow, and even uphold adequate oral hygiene, thereby considerably affecting an individual’s quality of life, including their oral health [18].

A family history of ulcers was present in 38.6% of affected individuals, significantly higher than the 5% in the unaffected group (p < 0.001). This discovery aligns with research conducted by Basirat et al., who identified a notable correlation between the occurrence of RAS and the existence of a family history of this condition (p=0.01). This finding is also consistent with earlier studies. [13, 30, 31]. Different DNA polymorphisms and alterations in cytokine metabolism, such as ILs, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, represent genetic risk factors that can significantly influence individuals’ vulnerability to RAS [32].

In our study, stress levels showed no significant differences between the RAS affected group and those unaffected, with high stress reported by 25% in both groups, moderate stress by 70.5% of affected individuals and 65% of the unaffected, and low stress by 4.5% and 10%, respectively (p = 0.490). This finding is consistent with the research carried out by Pedersen, who evaluated psychological stress in patients with recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) and identified no relationship between psychological life stress and recurrent RAS [33] Furthermore, Karaer et al. found no notable difference in levels of depression and anxiety between the two groups, one with RAS and the other without [34]. Similarly, Mirzaie et al. employed the Beck Anxiety and Depression Inventory to assess anxiety levels and reported no significant difference in anxiety and depression between the two groups, those with RAS and those without [35].

Contrary to the findings of Dhopte et al., [36] who utilized a modified perceived stress scale (PSS-10), it was shown that elevated levels of stress, anxiety, and depression are present in students with a history of recurrent aphthous ulcers (RAU). Additionally, research conducted by Safi et al. [37] at Khyber College of Dentistry revealed that anxiety and depression are the predominant risk factors among dental students.

Nonetheless, Huling et al. indicated that experiencing a stressful life event nearly tripled the chances of having an RAS episode (OR = 2.72; 95% CI = 2.04 3.62) [8]. Psychological stress initiates immunoregulatory responses by increasing the leukocyte count at sites of inflammation; this occurrence is often observed during the onset of RAS. The pathophysiological impacts of stress on patients are not uniform, particularly due to the complex and dynamic mechanisms that affect individuals in different ways. Similarly, a single patient may exhibit varying degrees of symptoms associated with the same type of emotional stress [38].

Stress was not a major contributing factor, even though our study took place during the end of the semester and exam period. This could be partially attributed to the natural beauty of the faculty campus location, as well as the various physical activities organized by the faculty to help students cope with stress.

Evaluating the impact of risk indicators on the incidence of aphthous ulcers uncovered several notable correlations. Females demonstrated a considerably reduced probability of developing aphthous ulcers in comparison to males (OR = 0.233, p = 0.015), which was unexpectedly contrary to many earlier studies indicating that women were more prone than men to experience recurrent aphthous stomatitis, including those by Wan Ahmad Kamil et al. [11], Rathod et al., [4], Eman aga et al., [24] and Al-Johani [10]. Also, this finding contradicts the research conducted by Noujeim et al., which indicated a minor gender preference for females (59.7%) [39]; additionally, other investigations have reported a greater occurrence of recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) in females [40]. Furthermore, Kumar et al. observed that the incidence of RAS was higher in females (50.86%) than in males (49.14%) [41] .

Daily toothbrushing was unexpectedly associated with a markedly increased risk (OR = 9.634, p < 0.001). This can be elucidated by the fact that toothbrushing is an advised daily activity that contributes to the maintenance of oral health. Nevertheless, if executed incorrectly, it may result in the deterioration of tooth structure and harm to soft tissues. Additionally, a family history of recurrent ulcers emerged as the strongest predictor (OR = 34.394, p < 0.001), highlighting a possible genetic predisposition as mentioned before.

The study is constrained by a limited sample size, the inflexibility of closed-ended questions, and the data gathered being somewhat reliant on the participants’ retrospective recollections. Furthermore, the findings do not yield definitive evidence regarding whether the frequency of ulcers has escalated, become more enduring, or intensified in terms of pain after the transition to dental school compared to a previous educational context. Additionally, the research lacks comprehensive data on the dietary habits of students during periods of stress, which could be attributed to our significant concern regarding the relationship between recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) and stress, with a strong emphasis on the prevalence of RAS and associated risk factors. We recommend undertaking further research that encompasses a larger sample size and employs open-ended questions in the designed questionnaire. Moreover, additional studies should explore the connection between the dietary practices of college students and stressful circumstances.

Conclusions

This research underscored a notable prevalence of RAS among dental students. It also identified common risk factors linked to the occurrence of RAS, establishing a significant correlation between a familial history of aphthous stomatitis, the presence of other dental issues, and regular daily toothbrushing as the three primary risk factors for aphthous lesions. However, stress levels did not show a significant association with these lesions. Given that aphthous stomatitis is a multifactorial condition, further investigation into additional intervening factors is warranted.
