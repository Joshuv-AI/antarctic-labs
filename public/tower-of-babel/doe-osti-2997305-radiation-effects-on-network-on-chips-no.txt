ABSTRACT
This document details the findings from the FY25 RAD-Tech LDRD titled “Radiation Effects
on NoC (Network on Chips).” We utilized the Versal FPGA from AMD as an exemplar
platform for NoC. We conducted two radiation tests, one at Texas A&M University (TAMU)
in June 2025 and another at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) in August 2025.
These experiments showed that radiation could upset the NoC and that it experiences a
variety of failures, that we could detect those upsets, and provided data that was used to
calculate failure cross-sections. Additionally, we discovered a high- current mode in the
adaptable intelligence engine (AIE) on the Versal. This paper presents the methodology for
these tests, conclusions, and potential future work.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This project was motivated by State-of-the-Art (SOTA) technology that incorporates
Network on Chips (NOC) for efficient data communication across the various computer
kernels. For example, on the AMD Versal Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGA), an NoC
has been incorporated for fast data communication from the programmable logic and
other computer kernels (processing system, adaptable intelligence engines, etc.). The
radiation effects on the legacy technology of this FPGA, such as the programmable logic,
are well understood, and established methods exist to measure cross-sections when new
families/generations are released; however, newly incorporated technologies, such as the
NoC, are not fully understood and could introduce new failure points into the mission
space.
Through a combination of experimental research, data analysis, and computational
modeling, we aimed to better understand strategic radiation effects on NoC, which will
continue to appear in SOTA components. We tested the NoC on the AMD Versal in heavy
ions and 43 MeV protons at Texas A&M University and heavy ions at Lawrence Berkeley
Laboratories. The results from these experiments showed us that easy-to-implement
mitigation methods, such as error correction codes (already implemented in the Versal
NoC), can be effective in mitigating most radiation-induced upsets. We further hypothesize
that standard data packet transmission protocols can also increase reliability, as long as a
mission can tolerate the occasional data packet needing to be resent. Moreover, we
discovered a high-current (but not single-event latchup) event involving the adaptable
intelligence engines (that are not part of the NoC) on the Versal AI. Events like this could
potentially negatively affect missions planning to utilize the Versal AI chip.
This work will benefit future missions incorporating chips with NoCs such as those being
considered for space and hypersonic missions.

1. SCOPE
This report presents the findings of the Radiation Effects on Network on Chips (NoC)
Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project. This effort was conducted
by Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) to determine the failure rates and mechanisms of
emerging NoC technology. As a premier research and development institution, SNL is
committed to advancing scientific knowledge and technological innovation to meet the
challenges faced by our nation and the global community.
This project was motivated by System on Chips (SoC) continuing to incorporate more
technology to increase computer power and connecting computer kernels with a high-
bandwidth capable NoC for efficient data movement across the chip. For example, the
latest generation of AMD Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGA) include an NoC for data
movement between the PL and other computer kernels. This presents a new opportunity
for radiation-effects research, as the radiation effects on legacy technology, such as the PL,
are well understood. However, newly incorporated technology, such as the NoC, is not fully
understood and could introduce new failure points into the mission space. Through a
combination of experimental research, data analysis, and computational modeling, we
aimed to better understand strategic radiation effects on NoC, which will continue to
appear in future SOTA components. We can use these results to better understand
potential failures on NoCs that will be deployed in harsh radiation environments. We
anticipate that components containing an NoC will become more common in Space and
Hypersonic missions as well as missile defense and future ND missions.
Due to time and budget constraints, we chose the AMD Versal as an exemplar platform. We
anticipated that this platform would be representative of NoCs across a variety of chips.
The NoC is separate from the Programmable Logic (PL) (e.g., it is a hard block) and will be a
good exemplar of the radiation behavior of NoCs in general.
In the following sections, we will outline the methodology employed, present the results
obtained, and discuss the implications of our findings. This report aims to provide a
comprehensive overview of our research efforts.
We now better understand general NoC vulnerabilities across all chips and have
considered potential strategies on how to mitigate (specific mitigation strategies can be
researched as part of a follow-on LDRD) and better understand how radiation impacts data
integrity in SOTA NoC and control (packet arbitration, quality of service).

1.1. Background
NoCs are a newer technology that enables fast and efficient data communication on SOTA
SoCs (ex: AMD Versal, Intel Stratix). These components are used to enable space and
hypersonic systems. Both mission spaces plan to heavily utilize Integrated Circuits (ICs)
with NoCs, because of their high bandwidth demands (using advanced sensors and
algorithms). We want to make sure we are not incurring unknown radiation risk by using

 9

these new architectures in these mission areas, but if we are, we want to study these
failure mechanisms, so mitigations can be created.
To the author’s best knowledge, the general radiation response of NoCs is unknown. This
complicated technology could have multiple failure mechanisms, such as data corruption,
packet misrouting (or dropping) and Quality of Service (QoS) disruptions. There is also the
possibility of other failures that cannot be predicted at this time.
A previous research experiment [1] studied the Versal NoC, but only about 10% of the NoC
endpoints were utilized. The bandwidth utilization and QoS constraints from that study are
unknown. This study found no errors in the NoC under neutron irradiation. We hypothesize
that by more fully utilizing the NoC, we will see upsets.

2. FIRMWARE DESIGN
We used two types of design for this test. The first was a PL-only design. This design
exercised the NoC with only PL endpoints (packet switches are included in all designs as
they are internal to the NoC architecture). This type of design was used for both the Versal
Prime (no AIE) and the Versal AI. Additionally, this design had a low and high bandwidth
variation. The low bandwidth variation of the design was only tested at the Texas A&M
cyclotron.
The second type of design was PL plus other endpoints. These additional endpoints
included the AIE, DDR, and processing system (PS). The following sections will include
additional details about both types of designs.

2.1.1. Firmware/Test Script Development
A total of three firmware images were created and tested, with one created for the Versal
Prime device and two created for the Versal AI Core device. The details of each image for
testing are in Section 2.1.1.1 and Section 2.1.1.2.

2.1.1.1. Versal Prime & Versal AI Core Programmable Logic-only images
These images solely exercised the Programmable Logic NoC endpoints on the Versal Prime
and Versal AI Core devices, respectively. In this design, 28 of the 56 PL NoC endpoints were
exercised, with 14 endpoints being NMUs (NoC input endpoints) and 14 endpoints being
NSUs (NoC output endpoints). NoC packet switches (NPS) were also utilized to connect
NMUs to NSUs through the generated network. Depending on the path length, as little as
two, or as many as 13, packet switches could be used in a path. Each PL NoC endpoint
received a constraint of providing 6000MB/s of both read and write bandwidth,
encouraging the tool to create an optimal NoC routing configuration. Each NMU was fed by
an AXI Traffic Generator IP Core, which generated reads and writes to be sent through the
NoC endpoints. Each Traffic Generator IP core included a CSV file, which told it what data
should be written to which address. Each traffic generator wrote 8192 words at ascending

 10

addresses, then read the 8192 words back. It then switched to a different address space
and did the same operation again. This set of four transactions (write, read, write, read)
was looped indefinitely for the duration of the test. A Block-RAM memory was connected to
the other side of each NSU to provide a memory space that received the read and write
transactions. Because each traffic generator was connected to two Block-RAM memories,
the generated NoC network was non-trivial. A non-trivial NoC network configuration was
desired since it created longer paths that were both more representative of actual designs
that used the NoC on Versal devices and generated more error data to be analyzed.
Pictures of the connection matrix and generated NoC network for each device are shown in
Figure 1 and Figure 2.

2.1.1.2. Versal AI Core Programmable Logic and AI Engine image
This image contains the same PL as the above images, while also running an AI Engine
application that necessitated sending information from the AI Engines to and from the DDR
Memory through the NoC. The programmable-logic and AI Engine applications ran
independently of one another, with the shared commonality of using the same NoC paths
to transmit their information.

An increment function implemented in the AI Engines retrieved data from DDR memory,
incremented the values received by one address, and wrote the data back to DDR memory
at a different address. The AI Engine application was kept basic to reduce the chance of
radiation-induced errors affecting the measurement of NoC reliability. Four instances of
this application were instantiated on the device, with each using two NoC endpoints,
totaling eight AI-Engine NoC NSU endpoints. To avoid routing issues on the NoC due to the
high-bandwidth requirements of the programmable logic endpoints, these endpoints were
set to have a low-bandwidth requirement (100MB/s for reads and writes). A DDR NoC
endpoint was also used for the AI Engines to interact with DDR memory.

A host application on the processing system of the device drove the AI Engine transactions.
It ran the AI Engine application indefinitely, inverting the source and destination DDR
memory pointers to run the increment function continuously. Because the program data of
this application was retrieved from DDR memory, NoC endpoints connecting the
processing system and DDR were also created in the design. A single NoC connection from
the processing system to the AI Engines was also used to program and send commands to
the AI Engines. A picture of the routed NoC network is shown in Figure 3.

AI Engine NoC connections were at the top of the board (eight NSU connections for the
application, one NMU connection for programming and commands from the processing

2.1.1.3. Error Readout Script
In the NoC architecture, every component, such as the NMU (NoC Master Unit), NSU (NoC
Slave Unit), NPS (NoC Packet Switch), and DDR endpoint, was equipped with a set of
registers that monitored the health of the respective components. A critical register within
this set was the Interrupt Status Register (ISR), which captured specific error information
pertinent to the operation of the NoC components. Within the ISR register, different bits
corresponded to different errors, as seen in Figure 4.

 Figure 4. ISR register errors

 13

To allow for continuous monitoring during testing, a TCL script was written to iteratively
poll the ISR registers across the NoC. The script was designed to detect any raised flags
within the ISR register, subsequently identifying the specific error type and its location, and
clearing the error before resuming the polling. All of the errors detected during the test
were logged into an error dump file for further post-test analysis.
Because every Versal board has different addresses for components and a different
number of components, a corresponding CSV detailing the associated addresses for every
NoC site was used in the TCL script. Depending on the specific hardware utilized in the
test—either the XCVM1802 or XCVC1902—the script dynamically loads the appropriate
addresses from the respective CSV file.
Upon detecting an error indicated by a raised field within the ISR register, the script unlocks
the PCSR lock register and writes a value of 1 to clear the error flag (the PCSR has a write-1-
to-clear protocol). Clearing errors ensures that errors are counted only once and are
cleared upon detection, thereby enabling continuous operation without redundant error
logging.

2.1.1.4. Image Programming/Verification
The generated .PDI files for each image were programmed through JTAG using Vivado.
Programmable logic functionality was verified through debug modules in the image (Virtual
I/O and Integrated Logic Analyzer blocks) while AI Engine function was verified through the
debug functionality within Vitis.

2.1.2. Firmware Changes
Between the two radiation tests at LBNL and TAMU, various upgrades were made to the
firmware tested. The first upgrade was the inclusion of the Versal AI Core in the tests,
opposed to testing just the Versal Prime device. Including the Versal AI Core in the tests
allowed different NoC endpoints to be tested, such as those used for DDR connections and
AI Engine connections.
Although the Versal Prime device was used in both tests, the TAMU Versal Prime image
contained a few differences from the LBNL Versal Prime image. The main difference was
the complexity of the paths through the NoC tested. As previously mentioned, the NoC
configuration on the Versal Prime tested at LBNL had input endpoints connected to
multiple output end points to increase the complexity of the generated network. The TAMU
Versal Prime image contained 14 non-intersecting point-to-point connections, with each
NMU connecting to exactly one NSU through two NPSs. The simplified design was used at
TAMU mainly as a proof-of-concept for testing the NoC under radiation and discovering if it
was possible to see errors on the device.

 14

2.1.2.1. Updated Register Readout Script
The script at TAMU read errors in the registers. Errors became persistent, spamming the
console and making it difficult to discern in real-time how the test was behaving.
The new script counted the errors and then cleared them. At the end it reported a total.
This made it much easier to assess how the test was going and how long it needed to run.
Although there were some issues with clearing error registers, situations with stuck
registers were identifiable in the raw data and removed before results were generated.

3. RADIATION TESTING PLAN

 Figure 5. Versal DUT Board

3.1. NoC Test

3.1.1.     Goals
The main goal of both tests was to ascertain the upset rate of the NoC under various
conditions. The design was set up such that different errors could be observed and for the
NoC to be observed under a variety of different conditions.
This allowed us to determine the types of failures and the failure rate of the Network on
Chip (NoC), determine if other constraints, such as bandwidth and Quality of Service (QoS)
affected the failure rate, and determine if there were differences in cross-section into/from
the various types of endpoints (PL, AIE, PS, etc.)

 15

3.1.2.   Method
The methodology for this test was to expose the firmware design(s) to different LETs. The
board (Figure 5) was set up in the beam path and air, degraders, and ion cocktails were
used to vary the LET. The chip was programmed and continuously read back with a script
that monitored for a variety of errors during the beam test. The readback data was stored
and then analyzed post-test to determine cross-sections and draw conclusions.

3.1.3. Test Scripts
A total of three firmware images were created and tested, with one created for the Versal
Prime device and two created for the Versal AI Core device. The builds differed in their
target platform as well as the NoC endpoints included in the test firmware. Two builds
were created for the Versal Prime and AI Core devices, which only tested NoC endpoints in
the programmable logic. The third build for the Versal AI Core included AI Engine
endpoints, along with necessary supporting processing system and DDR endpoints.
Each design was tested with a script run on the host computer that detected any errors on
the NoC that may have arisen. The script iterated through all NoC endpoints continuously
while reading the status register of each endpoint. If an error was detected in one of the
status registers, it was logged on the host computer through JTAG and the status register
was cleared to once again become available to log new errors.

3.3.1. PreVUE Board
Sandia custom boards were designed and verified for the Versal tests. The board is

A daughter card was designed to provide power to all of the different rails. The board is pin

3.3.2. Power Channels
The board broke out many different power planes. All of the transceiver power rails (e.g.,
GTY) were grounded for the radiation test (as they were not used). Seven channels were
powered for the test:
 • VCC_INT - .8V – Nominal current draw for the AI test was about 8A

 18

 • 1.2V – Nominal current draw was about 60 mA
 • 3.3V – Nominal current draw was about 440 mA
 • 2.5V – Nominal current draw was about 50 mA
 • 1.8V – Nominal current draw was about 28.6 mA
 • 1.5V – Nominal current draw was about 1 A
 • .8V – This powered all the .8V rails on the Versal that were not VCC_INT. Nominal
 current draw on the Versal AI was about 5 A.
For designs without the AIE (all designs on the Versal Prime and then designs without AIE
on the Versal AI), no special powering sequence was needed.
For designs with AIE, a special programming sequence was needed. If the part failed to
program, the part would draw 20+A of current until reset or reconfigured. Programming
the part required Keysight Power channels to be ganged together. For the test, two
channels were ganged together for a total power delivery capability of 40A. To successfully
program, the current limit needed to be set to at least 30A and the part overvolted, from
.8V to up to 1V. The overvolting required seemed to be dependent on both cable length
and part temperature at time of programming. Once the part was successfully
programmed, the rail could be returned to its proper voltage of .8V. Due to time limitations,
the need for an overvoltage was not investigated further.

3.3.3. Power Supplies
Two 4-channel Keysight Power supplies were needed to provide power for the rails. When
the AIE was utilized, a third KeySight power supply was also used. These were all connected
to a network for monitoring during the test.

3.3.4. FPGA Support Equipment
A SmartLynq+ or a Xilinx Platform Cable USB II handled JTAG port communication with the
DUT. This was be connected to a network switch or directly via USB to the host computer.
These devices handled part configuration and readback during the test.

3.3.5. Network Switch
A standard network switch was used to handle network/TCP based communication from
the host computer to the power supplies and SmartLynq+

3.3.6. Host Computer
A host computer with the correct version of the Xilinx/Vivado/Vitis tools was used. This ran
custom scripts to program and readback the device state (CRAM/BRAM and NoC registers).

4. TEST RESULTS
The NoC was initially tested at Texas A&M University Cyclotron in College Station, TX in June
of 2025. The goal of this initial test was to buy down some risk and answer generic
questions, such as if the NoC could be upset (based on the results of [1]) and if overheating
would be a concern. The results of this test are detailed below. The part was tested in
Nitrogen and Helium using the 25 A MeV beamline. The part was also tested in 43 MeV
protons. This experiment tested two designs, a high-bandwidth operating at a frequency
between 6.4-9.6 Gbps and a low-bandwidth design operating at 3.2 Gbps.

4.1. TAMU Results

4.1.1. Overheating
One of the main concerns was the potential for the device to overheat when fully
exercising the NoC. For this reason, the chip was not thinned for this test. The device did
not show heating issues when running the NoC without a heatsink, with the temperature
shown in Figure 8. The internal temperature monitor (readout through the Vivado
hardware manager) only showed a max temperature reading of ~50C.

 Figure 8. Infrared Camera monitoring heat of Versal

4.2. LETs and Target Fluences

4.2.1. LBNL Experimental LET Calculation
The heavy ion beam software at LBNL does not come with native LET calculation for varying
air gaps and silicon thicknesses like the TAMU facility has. The team performed our own
calculations using two simulation software codes: SRIM and MCNP6.3. SRIM is a 1D Monte
Carlo code that calculates stopping power and range of ions in matter (SRIM) while MCNP is
a general purpose 3D Monte Carlo radiation transport code developed by Los Alamos

 21

National Laboratory. LET calculations in SRIM were performed by calculating the -dE/dx for
each material slab increment until the target silicon sensitive volume was reached. For
example, the 0.002 inch mylar shield reduces the initial energy in a linear way for
respective ions and this new reduced energy is fed into SRIM as the initial energy for the
next slab. This continues until the sensitive silicon volume is reached—where the LET is
simply recorded as reported by SRIM. In MCNP, a pseudo-1D calculation was constructed
that fully integrated all the materials (instead of chaining 1D calculations into each other)
and the LET was calculated by dividing the heating tally (F6 [MeV/mg]) by the fluence tally
(F4 [#/cm2]) for the respective ion source. Table 1 shows an example of the calculated LETs
for a 5cm air gap experimental setup. The MCNP calculation became increasingly divergent
from SRIM as the heavy ion increased in mass, even refusing to tally a result due to
insufficient range for the three heaviest ions. It is not known why this is, but because the
team definitively recorded upsets at these higher ions, we assess SRIM’s estimation as
more correct.

6. CONCLUSIONS & FUTURE WORK
We were able to successfully upset the NoC in the two experiments we conducted. We
observed many different varieties of failures, showing that the NoC is indeed susceptible to
radiation-induced upsets. While there are a variety of failure modes, one of the most
common types we observed were 1-bit correctable ECC errors. This suggests that for NoCs
on other architectures, adding ECC to the data may be a good first-step mitigation to
prevent many radiation-induced upsets.
Furthermore, by analyzing the raw data, it appears that many other common errors include
parity mismatches (i.e., non-correctable ECC data errors) and packet misrouting. The
authors theorize that these could be resolved by adding a packet transmission protocol on
top of the data (e.g., JAS, AURORA, etc.), or any protocol that could add ECC and verify
receipt at the intended destination. On an ECC or misroute failure, the data could simply be
requested again. We theorize that this would mitigate against most failures present in the
NoC. (This assumes that the given mission can tolerate temporary disruptions in data
arrival).
We did observe a burst of errors that did not clear (e.g., routing and parity). It’s unclear
whether this was due to an inability to clear the error registry on a read or if some more
permanent type of error occurred. Since this was discovered in our August 2025 test, we
did not have the opportunity to further examine this behavior. An exploration or further
analysis of this data should be considered for future work.
Another area for future work is the exploration of the high-current event discovered in the
AIE of the Versal AI. While this work showed that this event was not a single event latchup

 31

(SEL), many other questions remain unanswered. Future work can study whether this event
is limited to version 1 AIEs, if this event is destructive, and if the AIEs remain operable
through this event.
