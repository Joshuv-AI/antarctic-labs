A lightweight improved YOLOv11 framework for intracranial hemorrhage detection
Authors: Yunchang Zheng, Mingzhe Du, Qing Chang, Jianbo Wu, Leyi Han, Ziqi An, Yunlong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely and accurate detection of intracranial hemorrhage in CT images is essential for emergency intervention and for reducing disability and mortality. Although deep learning-based object detectors have shown considerable promise for computer-aided intracranial hemorrhage detection, their performance in CT-based detection settings remains constrained by subtle lesion characteristics, insufficient multi-scale feature representation, class imbalance, and complex background interference. In addition, many existing models still struggle to balance detection precision with computational efficiency, which limits their suitability for real-time diagnosis. To overcome these limitations, we propose a lightweight YOLOv11-based framework for intracranial hemorrhage detection. It improves feature extraction, feature fusion, and lesion localization while maintaining practical inference efficiency. Within this framework, RepStem is adopted to replace the original downsampling convolution for stronger early lesion representation, the FDPN-DASI joint feature optimization module is introduced to improve multi-scale hematoma aggregation and suppress irrelevant background responses, and the original C2PSA block is replaced with cascaded C2CGA attention to better capture small hemorrhages and ambiguous boundaries. Experiments on the BHX dataset show that the proposed method outperforms the YOLOv11 baseline and achieves competitive overall performance among the compared detectors. Compared with YOLOv11, our method improves precision and recall by 3.9% and 3.4%, respectively, while increasing mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 3.7% and 4.6%. Despite these precision gains, the model remains lightweight, containing only 2.65 million parameters and achieving 864.86 FPS, suggesting its potential for CT-based computer-aided detection of intracranial hemorrhage.Keywords: Intracranial hemorrhage, YOLOv11, Object detectionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is an acute neurological disorder caused by bleeding within the cranial cavity. According to the location of bleeding, it can be classified into epidural, subdural, subarachnoid, intraventricular, and intraparenchymal hemorrhage1,2. These subtypes differ in etiology, imaging appearance, clinical manifestation, and prognosis, which makes timely diagnosis clinically important. In severe cases, ICH may lead to hematoma expansion, secondary brain injury, permanent neurological dysfunction, or death. Epidemiological studies have reported that the global incidence of spontaneous ICH is approximately 24.6 per 100,000 person-years3. Therefore, rapid and accurate identification of hemorrhagic lesions is essential for emergency treatment and prognosis assessment.

When patients with ICH arrive at the emergency department, brain computed tomography (CT) is performed to confirm the diagnosis, enabling subsequent medical or surgical treatment4. Radiologists diagnose ICH by reviewing frame-by-frame CT scans, which is a tedious and time-consuming task, with the risk of missing the diagnosis. Furthermore, the increasing number of frames in modern CT imaging adds to the workload of radiologists. Meanwhile, the task of detecting ICH in these images can be a matter of life or death5. In this way, if there is a lack of availability of experienced radiologists or clinicians, an automated system capable of helping in the diagnosis process can accelerate treatment and improve outcomes4.

ML algorithm, with its outstanding advantages of automatic prediction and classification, has increasingly prominent application value in the medical field, and has become the focus of current clinical and scientific research6. In the application practice of algorithms in the medical field, expert system algorithms used to occupy the mainstream position for a long time. These algorithms rely on clinical diagnosis and treatment experience to set core parameters, and have distinct adaptability to specific scenes7. With the development of deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been increasingly applied to medical image analysis8,9. Unlike conventional machine learning methods that often rely on manually designed features, CNNs can automatically extract hierarchical representations from images, which makes them more suitable for complex visual tasks such as lesion detection.

In deep learning-based image analysis, object recognition is a core task. If the model can accurately locate the position of the target object in the image, this kind of model is defined as object detection (OD) model10. In the past decade, the technical performance of the target detection model has been steadily improving. Among them, the single-stage detector has become a research and application hotspot in recent years with continuous technical optimization and long-term leading detection performance, and the YOLO model is a typical representative of this kind of detector11,12.

Recent progress in computer vision has provided new technical support for automated intracranial hemorrhage detection13. To address limitations of traditional methods and CNNs in CT intracranial hemorrhage-specifically multi-bleed classification and computational latency-Lakshmi-Prasanna-Kothala et al. proposed YOLOv5x-GCB, which employs Ghost convolution to form a C3Ghost module for model lightweighting and improved heterogeneous hemorrhage localization efficiency14. V. Vidhya et al. further introduced YOLOv5s CAM, building on YOLOv5s with multi-window preprocessing to produce three-channel inputs, embedding ECA and CBAM cascade attention modules in the neck fusion layer to enhance small hematoma detection, and replacing SPP with SPPF to accelerate inference and expand multiscale receptive fields, while using data splitting and cross-validation to ensure generalization15.

To address the challenge of accurately segmenting and quantifying irregularly shaped and multi-instance hematomas in intracranial hemorrhage volume assessment, Wang Haoran proposed the YOLO-HV model. Based on the YOLOv8n seg architecture, the model improves multi-instance segmentation by integrating a CoordAttention-enhanced backbone, replacing C2f with GDConv in the neck, adopting an LGND detection head, and applying joint lookup cross-layer matching optimization16. Additionally, the second convolution proposed by Abdesselam Ferdi et al. exhibits nonlinear characteristics, thereby enhancing its expressive capability17.

To tackle three major issues in intracranial hemorrhage detection-non-brain tissue interference, inaccurate subtype recognition, and redundant detections-Dingyuan Hu et al. proposed a cascaded architecture: an improved AlexNet first filters out non-brain images; a modified YOLOv8 employing grouped spatial convolutions, a VoV-GSCSP module, and an enhanced detection head raises subtype detection precision; and a post-processing module removes redundant bounding boxes. Experiments show the modified YOLOv8 achieved a mean average precision (mAP50:95) of 68.5%, a 2.3% improvement over the baseline18.

In medical object detection, Zhang Keyuan et al. combined YOLOv11 with EfficientNet to propose YOLO-MEDX, which integrates attention mechanisms and lightweight convolutions to better distinguish lesions from background noise19. Abdul Moiz Qarni et al. developed a real-time MRI detection and segmentation system based on YOLOv11, integrating a CSPDarkNet backbone, an SPPF neck, and an anchor-free detection head; on 1960 samples it achieved 97.6% precision, 99.2% recall, and 99.46% mAP@50 segmentation IoU20. These advances provide critical technical support for deploying AI-based intracranial hemorrhage detection systems.

To address the challenges of small lesion detection and complex CT backgrounds, we develop an enhanced YOLOv11 framework for intracranial hemorrhage detection, focusing on optimizing the algorithm to improve the target detection precision and recall rate, while reducing the missed detection rate, so as to achieve real-time and effective application across scenarios. The main contribution of this study lies in integrating and adapting these module designs within a YOLOv11-based framework for intracranial hemorrhage detection, so that their cooperation is better matched to the characteristics of hemorrhagic CT images as follows: (1) RepStem improves early feature preservation for small and low-contrast lesions. (2) FDPN-DASI enhances bidirectional multi-scale fusion and suppresses background interference. (3) C2CGA improves fine-grained feature modeling for small lesions and ambiguous boundaries.

These three components work together across the detection pipeline, covering early lesion feature preservation, multi-scale feature fusion in the neck, and decoupled prediction in the detection head. Earlier modules provide reliable feature representations, while later modules further refine small lesions and subtle structural details, allowing each part to play a distinct role with limited structural redundancy. The combined architecture exhibits strong task specificity for intracranial hemorrhage CT detection, precisely addressing weak imaging features, small lesions, and strong background; compared with other lightweight backbones, conventional fusion schemes, and generic attention modules, it offers the advantages of re-parameterized inference without extra overhead, bidirectional fusion and dynamic attention tuned for small-lesion detection, and cascaded attention for efficient decoupling and improved detection precision, maximizing lesion feature extraction and localization precision while retaining model lightweightness.

The experimental part reports the dataset, evaluation criteria, comparison with existing detectors, and ablation results. The final section gives the conclusion and discusses the remaining limitations. The last part synthesizes the key findings and summarizes the contribution of this study to the field of medical imaging detection of intracranial hemorrhage.

Related work

YOLOv11 is adopted as the baseline model in this study because it provides a favorable balance between detection accuracy, computational efficiency, and deployment flexibility. As an improved member of the YOLO series, YOLOv11 maintains the advantages of one-stage detection and further optimizes feature extraction and multi-scale feature fusion. These characteristics make it suitable for real-time object detection tasks and provide a solid foundation for subsequent model improvement.

In this study, YOLOv11 is used as the baseline model for enhancement and optimization. As shown in Fig. 1, YOLOv11 follows the typical backbone–neck–head architecture. The backbone is responsible for extracting hierarchical image features from the input image, while the neck performs multi-scale feature fusion through top-down and bottom-up information interaction. The detection head receives the fused feature maps and generates the final classification and localization predictions. This framework strengthens spatial and semantic feature representation for objects at different scales while maintaining a favorable balance between detection performance and inference efficiency.

Network architecture diagram of YOLOv11.

The backbone network of YOLOv11 follows the CSP-style design philosophy of previous YOLO models and introduces C3k2 modules as the main feature extraction components. Compared with the C2f module used in YOLOv8, C3k2 adopts a more compact CSP-based bottleneck design, which helps reduce computational redundancy while maintaining effective feature representation. This structure improves feature reuse and gradient propagation, making the backbone more suitable for extracting multi-level visual features. In addition, the SPPF module is retained to perform efficient multi-scale pooling, enlarge the receptive field, and strengthen high-level semantic representation at the output of the backbone.

The neck serves as an intermediate feature fusion component between the backbone and the detection head. In YOLOv11, multi-scale feature fusion is performed through a top-down and bottom-up aggregation pathway, where upsampling, concatenation, downsampling, and C3k2-based blocks are used to integrate features from different semantic levels. At the end of the backbone, the SPPF and C2PSA modules are introduced to enhance high-level contextual representation and spatial feature modeling. Unlike simply replacing the neck with a BiFPN structure, YOLOv11 mainly refines the original YOLO-style feature aggregation design to improve feature interaction while maintaining computational efficiency.

The detection head is responsible for generating the final classification and localization outputs. YOLOv11 generally follows the decoupled detection head design inherited from YOLOv8, in which classification and bounding-box regression are processed by separate branches. This structure helps the model learn category-related and localization-related features more effectively, thereby improving the overall detection performance.

Methods

Improved YOLOv11 detection algorithm

YOLO is a representative single-stage detector that performs object localization and classification in one forward pass. Its efficient inference process makes it suitable for CT-based lesion detection, where rapid analysis is important for emergency diagnosis21. The newly proposed YOLOv11 algorithm has carried out a number of optimizations in terms of network structure, training process, and inference procedure, and has significant advantages of low latency and high precision. However, intracranial hemorrhage detection in CT images remains challenging because hemorrhagic lesions often differ greatly in size, shape, and location. Small lesions may occupy only a limited number of pixels and are easily weakened after repeated downsampling, whereas large or irregular hematomas require sufficient spatial and semantic information for accurate localization. Therefore, if the network cannot effectively preserve fine details and integrate features from different levels, missed detections and inaccurate boundaries may occur. To address this challenge, we have improved the YOLOv11 algorithm, and the improved structure diagram is shown in Fig. 2. The specific improvements are as follows: The backbone front uses a RepStem22 reparameterization module to replace the original two consecutive downsampling convolutions, achieving 4× downsampling in a single step while preserving subsequent native backbone feature-extraction layers; the neck adopts a bidirectional Feature Pyramid Network embedding FDPN-DASI23 dynamic adaptive spatial attention, replacing the native unidirectional FPN; the detection head replaces the original parallel multi-head attention with the cascaded attention mechanism C2CGA24,25; the output layer performs localization, classification, and confidence prediction of intracranial hemorrhage lesions.

Aiming at the challenges of high requirements for model inference speed and feature extraction precision in the analysis of intracranial hemorrhage medical images, this module aims to replace the traditional inefficient dense convolution, and reduce memory access while completing input down-sampling and feature extraction cost, so as to satisfy real-time clinical deployment requirements, and ensure that the model can be efficiently deployed on both mobile and desktop. C2CGA

Accurate detection of intracranial hemorrhage is challenging when lesion morphology is irregular, lesion size varies markedly, and weak hemorrhagic foci appear together with indistinct boundaries. In such cases, the model must preserve subtle local responses while maintaining sufficient cross-scale feature interaction. In the original YOLOv11 framework, the C2PSA module adopts a standard self-attention strategy. Although MHSA is effective for contextual modeling, its performance in this scenario is limited by the fact that weak responses from small hemorrhagic regions and boundary-sensitive details are easily diluted during feature aggregation. This reduces the model’s ability to describe fine-grained lesion structures. Therefore, this study introduces a cascaded group attention mechanism and builds the C2CGA module to enhance local-detail modeling and improve the detection of micro-lesions as well as hematoma regions with blurred boundaries.

C2CGA optimizes multi-scale feature fusion through cascade attenuation mechanism, and strengthens the interaction between large-scale hematoma and small-scale micro-bleeding point features; The self-attention mechanism within each population can improve the ability to extract local details of the lesion (such as hematoma edge texture, weak gray-scale features of micro-bleeding points), while information interaction across different groups helps the module capture broader contextual cues from brain tissues, thereby improving the representation of hemorrhagic lesions.

Experiment results and analysis

Datasets and preprocessing

This study employs the publicly available Brain Hemorrhage Extended (BHX) dataset26 to evaluate the proposed model. The dataset contains 15,560 non-contrast head CT slices annotated by professional radiologists.The intracranial hemorrhages in the BHX dataset are categorized into six distinct subtypes: intraparenchymal, subarachnoid, intraventricular, epidural, subdural, and chronic subdural hematoma.

The dataset was split at the patient level to prevent data leakage, with all slices from the same patient assigned to a single subset. The training, validation, and test sets were divided in an 8:1:1 ratio, yielding 12,448, 1,556, and 1,556 images, respectively. This ensured proportional representation of relatively rare hemorrhage subtypes across all subsets. Table 1 presents representative samples from the dataset.

Experimental setting

The proposed enhanced architecture and all baseline models were implemented on a GPU-accelerated computing platform. In terms of hardware configuration, this study used an NVIDIA RTX 4090D GPU with 24 GB of video memory and a high-performance Xeon CPU. This hardware platform provides sufficient memory capacity and computing power for training models with an input resolution of 640 × 640 and for supporting complex feature extraction and fusion modules. At the software level, PyTorch 2.2.1 and CUDA 12.1 were used to ensure efficient GPU acceleration and stable model execution.

All models were trained under the same experimental settings to ensure a fair comparison. The input image size was set to 640 × 640, and the batch size was set to 16. Each model was trained for 100 epochs using the SGD optimizer. The initial learning rate was 0.01, the final learning rate factor was 0.01, the momentum was 0.937, and the weight decay was 0.0005. Pretrained weights were used for model initialization. Automatic mixed precision training was adopted to reduce memory consumption and improve training efficiency. For all experiments, the random seed was fixed to 0, and deterministic training was enabled to improve reproducibility. To further verify the repeatability of the training and evaluation procedure, each model was trained and evaluated five times under the same experimental protocol. The final results were reported as the average values over the five repeated runs.

Considering that hemorrhagic lesions in CT images are often small, low-contrast, and boundary-ambiguous, relatively controlled data augmentation strategies were used. Specifically, HSV-based intensity perturbation was applied with , , and . Geometric augmentation included random translation with a maximum ratio of 0.1, random scaling with a scale factor of 0.5, and horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5. Mosaic augmentation was enabled with a probability of 1.0 during training and was disabled in the last 10 epochs to reduce the influence of strong augmentation during the final convergence stage. Vertical flipping, rotation, shear, perspective transformation, MixUp, and Copy-Paste were not used, since these operations may alter the anatomical structure or spatial distribution of hemorrhagic lesions.

The above experimental configuration ensures that the proposed model and all baseline detectors are trained and evaluated under a consistent protocol. This setting improves the fairness and reproducibility of the comparison experiments and provides a stable basis for evaluating model accuracy, complexity, and inference efficiency on the BHX dataset.

In comparison with existing state-of-the-art models, our method achieves an mAP@0.5 of 0.928, surpassing Hyper-yolo (0.923), indicating better coarse-grained detection precision. While our mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.640 is marginally below Hyper-yolo (0.652), the F1 score reaches 0.884, which is slightly higher than Hyper-yolo (0.882), indicating a favorable balance between precision and recall. Compared to RTDETR, our method achieves higher mAP@0.5 and F1 values, while maintaining a close mAP@0.5:0.95 result. Against Gold-yolo, our method achieves higher reported mAP values with lower parameter count and computational cost, while Gold-yolo shows a higher inference speed.

Regarding model complexity and inference efficiency, our method maintains a favorable trade-off between detection accuracy and model complexity. The total parameter count is 2.65M, comparable to YOLOv11’s 2.58M (an increase of only approximately 2.7%), yet it achieves a notable increase in mAP@0.5 from 0.891 to 0.928, indicating progressive enhancement in detection performance while preserving lightweight architecture. The single-module results first show that each component contributes to the baseline model, but the improvement tendency is not the same. After introducing C2CGA alone, precision, recall, and mAP@0.5 increase by 0.2%, 0.8%, and 1.2%, respectively. This suggests that the cascaded attention structure has a positive effect on the representation of small lesions and ambiguous boundaries. When RepStem is used alone, the precision gain reaches 2.5%, while recall and mAP@0.5 increase by 1.7% and 1.4%, respectively. This result is consistent with the role of RepStem in strengthening early feature extraction, since small hemorrhagic regions are easily weakened during the initial downsampling process. FDPN-DASI produces a 1.8% improvement in precision, a 3.3% improvement in recall, and a 2.7% improvement in mAP@0.5. Compared with the other two single modules, its effect on recall is more evident, which reflects the importance of multi-scale feature fusion for lesions with large differences in size and shape.

The combination results further reveal the trade-off among Precision, Recall, and inference speed. When RepStem is combined with C2CGA, the model benefits from both early feature preservation and subsequent attention refinement. Compared with the baseline, recall increases by 1.5% and mAP@0.5 increases by 2.5%, which indicates that the combination improves localization quality, although its Recall does not exceed that of RepStem alone. This means that more complete shallow features can provide a better basis for later fine-grained feature modeling. On this basis, FDPN-DASI further strengthens the interaction between low-level spatial details and high-level semantic information, making the model more capable of handling scale variation and blurred lesion margins. Therefore, the improvement brought by the combined modules is not only caused by adding more structures, but also by the complementary roles of the three components in different stages of feature processing.

After RepStem, FDPN-DASI, and C2CGA are integrated into the baseline, the final model achieves the highest Precision and mAP@0.5, while maintaining competitive Recall and practical inference efficiency. Compared with YOLOv11, precision, recall, and mAP@0.5 are improved by 3.9%, 3.4%, and 3.7%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 10, the proposed model keeps a strong performance on the main accuracy indicators while maintaining a parameter count of 2.65 M. Although the parameter count is slightly higher than that of the baseline, the model still retains practical real-time inference capability.

Overall, the ablation results suggest that RepStem, FDPN-DASI, and C2CGA play complementary roles in early lesion preservation, multi-scale feature aggregation, and fine-grained refinement. Their joint use does not maximize every individual metric, but provides the most balanced configuration for intracranial hemorrhage detection in this study.

Conclusion

To address missed detection of micro-hemorrhages, insufficient multi-scale lesion representation, and background-induced false positives in CT-based intracranial hemorrhage detection, this study proposed an optimized YOLOv11-based framework. The proposed method integrates improvements in early feature extraction, multi-scale feature fusion, and attention-based feature modeling to enhance lesion detection under complex imaging conditions.

Experimental results on the BHX dataset show that, compared with the baseline YOLOv11, the proposed method improves precision and recall by 3.9% and 3.4%, respectively. In addition, F1 score, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95 increase by 3.7%, 3.7%, and 4.6%, respectively. The proposed framework also achieves competitive performance relative to several mainstream detectors while maintaining a lightweight design. These results suggest that the proposed framework is a competitive candidate for CT-based computer-aided intracranial hemorrhage detection under the current experimental setting. However, broader practical deployment still requires external validation on multi-center and cross-device datasets. However, further validation on external datasets, multi-center data, and cross-device clinical scenarios is still needed before broader practical deployment.

Limitations of this research include: (1) The experiments were conducted only on the BHX dataset, and external validation on independent multi-center and cross-device datasets has not yet been performed. Therefore, the generalization ability of the model under different scanners, acquisition protocols, reconstruction settings, and patient populations remains to be further verified; (2) The current analysis is mainly based on overall detection metrics. More detailed subtype-level evaluation, especially for rare categories such as epidural hemorrhage, low-contrast hemorrhages, and blurred-boundary lesions, has not been fully conducted; (3) Fine-grained analyses remain insufficient, including failure-case analysis, subtype confusion analysis, confidence-threshold and IoU-threshold sensitivity analysis, calibration evaluation, and small-lesion subgroup analysis, such as micro-hemorrhages smaller than 50 pixels. In addition, although the proposed modules improve overall detection performance, their computational overhead and practical performance gains have not been fully quantified. In addition, more fine-grained analyses, such as detailed failure-case evaluation, calibration-related analysis, and repeated-run statistical validation, have not yet been fully incorporated in the current study.

Future work will focus on external validation using multi-center and cross-device CT datasets, more detailed subtype-level and difficult-case analyses, and clinically oriented evaluation such as scan-level aggregation, false positives per scan, threshold calibration, and sensitivity analysis. In addition, we will further conduct systematic quantitative analyses of the proposed modules, including their computational cost, inference latency, parameter efficiency, grouping strategy, and practical contribution to lesion detection. These efforts may help improve the robustness, interpretability, and deployment potential of the proposed framework, and support its future development as a more reliable computer-aided tool for intracranial hemorrhage CT detection.
