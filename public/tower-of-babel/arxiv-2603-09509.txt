Backward problem for a degenerate viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation: stability and numerical identification
S. E. Chorfi, A. Habbal, M. Jahid, L. Maniar, A. Ratnani

Abstract
. This work is devoted to the analysis of the backward problem for a viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation with degenerate diffusion and a general Hamiltonian that is not necessarily quadratic. First, we focus on linear degenerate parabolic equations in the nondivergence setting. We prove the conditional stability of the backward problem using Carleman estimates. Then, by a linearization technique, we prove similar results for the nonlinear viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation. Regarding numerical identification, we first investigate the linear degenerate equation with noisy data using the adjoint state method, combined with a Conjugate Gradient algorithm, to solve the associated minimization problem. Finally, the numerical identification for the nonlinear viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation is investigated by the Van Cittert iteration. Numerical tests are presented to show the performance of the proposed algorithms.

1.
Introduction

Degenerate viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equations naturally arise in a range of applications, including optimal control, front propagation, and mean-field game theory, where the diffusion coefficient may vanish or become singular in certain parts of the domain. Backward problems, which involve recovering past states of a system from final-time data, are particularly challenging in this degenerate setting due to the loss of regularity in addition to ill-posedness. Such problems are relevant in many areas of applied science and engineering, including quantum mechanics, stochastic control, game theory, and finance; see, e.g.,
[
12
]
and the references therein. Despite their importance, backward problems for degenerate viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equations have received limited attention in the literature, motivating the present study.

This work investigates the stability of the backward problem and numerical identification for a degenerate Viscous Hamilton-Jacobi (VHJ for short) equation in the one-dimensional case. Throughout the paper, we consider the following notations

\Omega:=(0,1),\qquad Q:=\Omega\times(0,T),\qquad\Sigma:=\left\{0,1\right\}\times(0,T),

where
T>0
is the terminal time.

To motivate our study, let us start with the simple viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}(x,t)-a(x)u_{xx}(x,t)+\frac{1}{q}|u_{x}(x,t)|^{q}=0,&&(x,t)\in Q,\\
&u(x,t)=0,\hskip 184.9429pt&&(x,t)\in\Sigma,\\
&u(x,0)=f(x),&&x\in\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where we assume hereafter that
q\geqslant 1
and the diffusion coefficient degenerates (i.e., vanishes) at the endpoints
x=0
and
x=1
. More precisely, we introduce the following assumption:

Assumption I.

(i)

a\in C([0,1])
such that
a(x)>0
for all
x\in\Omega
;

(ii)

a(0)=a(1)=0
.

Backward problem.

We seek to determine unknown initial or intermediate states from the final measurement in equations such as (
1.1
), that is, for
t_{0}\in[0,T)
, recover
u(\cdot,t_{0})
from given measured data of
u(\cdot,T)
. The aim of this paper is twofold:

(i)

Theoretical: prove conditional stability by Carleman estimates.

(ii)

Numerical: design efficient algorithms for numerical identification.

The solution to the VHJ equation (
1.1
) can be interpreted, by the Dynamic Programming Principle, as the value function associated with a stochastic optimal control problem (see e.g.
[
8
,
44
]
), i.e.,

u(x,t)=\sup_{\alpha_{t}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left\{f(X_{t})\mathds{1}_{t<\tau_{x}}+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{x}}c_{q}|\alpha_{s}|^{\frac{q}{q-1}}ds\right\},

assuming here
q>1
for simplicity, where
\mathbb{E}_{x}
denotes the conditional expectation by the random event
\{X_{0}=x\},

c_{q}
serves as a normalization factor and
X_{t}
is the controlled process governed by the stochastic differential equation

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dX_{t}=\alpha_{t}dt+\sigma(X_{t})dB_{t},\\
&X_{0}=x\in\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

with
B_{t}
is a one-dimensional standard Brownian motion (on a complete filtered probability space),
(\alpha_{s})_{s\geqslant 0}
is a control belonging to a set
\mathcal{A}
of admissible processes, and
\tau_{x}
denotes the exit time from
\Omega
:

\tau_{x}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon\;X_{t}\notin\Omega\right\}.

The diffusion coefficient
\sigma
in (
1.2
) can vanish at some points, and so can
a(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)
. An interesting model is given by
a(x)=x^{\mu}(1-x)^{\nu},\;\mu,\nu>0
, which is, for instance, related to the Wright-Fisher diffusion process used in population genetics. See
[
38
, Examples, p. 48]
and also
[
40
, Ch. VIII, §8]
for multidimensional models. The VHJ equation also appears in some physical models of surface growth where it is linked to the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation for
q=2
, see, e.g.,
[
33
]
.

Regarding the existing literature on the VHJ equation in the nondegenerate case, several works have investigated the well-posedness in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{N},
either in the classical sense; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
9
]
and
[
10
]
, or in the sense of distributions (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
). In the case of a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},
under appropriate assumptions on the initial and boundary data, the solution exists on a time interval
[0,T^{*})
. Moreover, as
t\to T^{*},
the gradient blows up at the boundary while the solution is bounded (see
[
29
,
41
]
).

Degenerate parabolic operators, whether in divergence or nondivergence form, have been the subject of numerous studies in the literature. Many problems in physics, biology, and economics naturally involve them. For controllability problems, we refer to
[
15
]
where the authors proved the null controllability for degenerate parabolic equations in nondivergence form in one dimension. Then they extended this result to the semilinear problem. In
[
3
]
, the null controllability of degenerate linear and semilinear parabolic equations in divergence form has been proven. The authors in
[
14
]
have studied the regional null controllability of semilinear degenerate parabolic equations with nonlinearities involving the first derivative. In this case, the classical null controllability results are invalid due to degeneracy. In
[
2
]
, the degenerate semilinear system was investigated with two distinct degeneracies,
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. They have proven the null controllability results using linearization. Moreover, in
[
7
]
, the authors study the numerical null controllability of a degenerate and singular parabolic problem in divergence form. We also mention some results on stability estimates for inverse problems. In
[
16
]
, the Lipschitz stability for an inverse source problem using Carleman estimates has been established. Furthermore, in
[
45
]
, the authors studied the determination of source terms in a degenerate parabolic equation from the knowledge of a locally distributed observation. The authors of
[
13
]
have proven the Lipschitz stability for an inverse problem of linear degenerate parabolic systems with one force in the interior degeneracy case.

Moreover, backward problems for nondegenerate (uniformly elliptic) parabolic equations have been widely investigated in numerous papers; we refer to
[
35
]
and the references therein for a method based on Carleman estimates, and to
[
47
]
for a survey, particularly for nondegenerate equations. For degenerate equations, we refer to
[
34
]
for Carleman estimates. Recently, in
[
17
]
, a degenerate parabolic equation in divergence form has been studied for higher dimensions by the Carleman estimates method. We emphasize that the backward problems we consider for degenerate viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equations are relevant to the study of degenerate mean-field game systems; see, e.g., the recent work
[
24
]
. As for degenerate problems from time-integral measurements, we refer to
[
32
]
and the cited bibliography. Recently, special attention has been devoted to backward problems (inverse design) for VHJ equations; we refer to
[
28
,
25
]
for first-order HJ equations. In
[
26
]
, a related problem has been studied, taking into account localizations. The recent paper
[
27
]
investigates the reachable set for a multi-dimensional case. However, the literature on backward degenerate problems in nondivergence settings and second-order VHJ equations is scarce, and most of the works impose strong assumptions on the degenerate coefficient, which entails limited applicability.

In this paper, we first prove some conditional stability estimates for corresponding backward degenerate problems using Carleman estimates. Furthermore, we investigate the numerical identification of initial data from noisy final-time data. To this purpose, we start with the linear part of the VHJ equation, extending the methodology used in
[
31
]
. We utilize the adjoint-state methodology with a gradient-type iteration to approximate the solution. Among recent studies, the paper
[
23
]
studies the identification of initial temperatures in the heat equation with dynamic boundary conditions. In the same spirit, we can refer to
[
6
]
for theoretical and numerical analysis of a backward problem involving the identification of the initial data in a 2D degenerate parabolic equation. We also refer to
[
30
]
and
[
1
]
, where a similar approach was applied to inverse source problems. For more information, we recommend the excellent book
[
31
]
that contains more details about this approach. Moreover, we consider the numerical identification of initial data for the nonlinear VHJ equation using Van Cittert’s iteration, known in image reconstruction. The latter was employed for the first time in
[
22
]
for backward problems for linear and nonlinear parabolic equations with nondegenerate diffusion. Here, we extend the existing analysis to a viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation with degenerate diffusion.

The following sections of this paper are organized as follows. In Section
2
, we establish conditional stability for a linear degenerate equation in a nondivergence setting via Carleman estimates. In Section
3
, we prove the conditional stability for a nonlinear VHJ equation following a similar strategy. Then Section
4
is devoted to identifying the initial data for a linearized equation using the adjoint methodology combined with a Conjugate Gradient algorithm. Section
5
presents the algorithmic implementation of the gradient formula used to numerically identify the initial data in degenerate parabolic equations. For the nonlinear viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation, the initial datum is recovered through Van Cittert’s iterative method. Finally, the conclusion and final comments will be mentioned in Section
6
.

2.
Linear degenerate parabolic equation

In this section, we will prove a Carleman estimate for the linear part of the VHJ equation (
1.1
). This will be the key ingredient to tackle the nonlinear VHJ equation.

First, we succinctly review key results on well-posedness and regularity for degenerate parabolic equations in the nondivergence setting. We refer to
[
15
]
for more details.

2.1.
Degenerate equations in the nondivergence setting

2.1.1.
Wellposedness

We consider the following degenerate parabolic equation

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}(x,t)-a(x)u_{xx}(x,t)=G(x,t),&&\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&u(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&u(x,0)=f(x),&&\;\quad x\in\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Let us consider the natural Hilbert spaces to study (
2.1
):

L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(0,1)\;|\;\|u\|_{\frac{1}{a}}<\infty\right\},\;\;\|u\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}\frac{1}{a}dx;

H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)=L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\cap H^{1}_{0}(0,1),\;\;\|u\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}=\|u\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2};

H^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)=\left\{u\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\;|\;au_{xx}\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right\},\;\;\|u\|_{2,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}=\|u\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}au_{xx}^{2}dx.

Then, we consider the linear unbounded operator
(A,D(A))
defined by

Au=au_{xx},\qquad u\in D(A):=H^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1).

Additionally, the following Green’s formula holds:

Lemma 2.1
.

Given
(u,v)\in H^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\times H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}u_{xx}vdx=-\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}v_{x}dx.

Next, we recall the semigroup generation by
(A,D(A))
. We refer to
[
43
]
for the terminology.

Theorem 2.2
.

The operator
(A,D(A))
is self-adjoint and m-dissipative

in
L_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}(0,1)
. It generates then an analytic
C_{0}
-semigroup on
L_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}(0,1).

Consequently, the following well-posedness and regularity result holds.

Theorem 2.3
.

For all
G\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))
and
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
, there is a unique weak solution
u\in C([0,T],L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))
of (
2.1
). Moreover, if
f\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
, then

u\in H^{1}\left(0,T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\cap C\left([0,T];H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right),

and there exists a positive constant
C
(independent of
(u_{0},G)
) such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)}+\int_{0}^{1}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)}dt\leqslant C\left(\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)}+\|G\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(Q)}\right).

2.1.2.
Carleman estimate

Now, we prove a new Carleman estimate that will be useful to prove the stability of the backward problem of the linear part of the viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
2.1
). We adopt the weight function in
[
17
]
defined by

(2.2)

\varphi(t)=e^{\lambda t},\;\;t>0,

where
\lambda>0
is a sufficiently large parameter. The weight function
\varphi
is simple and independent of
x
, which makes it effective for backward problems associated with degenerate equations.

Lemma 2.4
.

There is a constant
\lambda_{0}>0
so that for any
\lambda>\lambda_{0},
there exists a constant
s_{0}(\lambda)>0
and a positive constant
C
such that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|u_{xx}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|u_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{Q}s\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+Cs\left(s\lambda\varphi(T)\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi(T)}

\displaystyle+Cs\left(s\lambda\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s}

for all large
s\geq s_{0}(\lambda)
, where
u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\bigcap H^{1}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)
is the solution of (
2.1
).

Proof.

Set

Lu:=u_{t}-a(x)u_{xx}(x,t),\qquad w=e^{s\varphi}u,\qquad Pw=e^{s\varphi}L\left(e^{-s\varphi}w\right)=e^{s\varphi}G.

We have

e^{s\varphi}\left(e^{-s\varphi}w\right)_{t}=w_{t}-s\lambda\varphi w,\qquad e^{s\varphi}a(x)\left(we^{-s\varphi}\right)_{xx}=a(x)w_{xx}.

Then

Pw=e^{s\varphi}L\left(e^{-s\varphi}w\right)=P_{-}w+P_{+}w=e^{s\varphi}G,

where

P_{-}w=w_{t},\qquad P_{+}w=-s\lambda\varphi w-a(x)w_{xx}.

By taking the norm
\|\cdot\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))}
in the previous equality, we obtain

\displaystyle\|e^{s\varphi}G\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))}=\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w_{t}|^{2}dxdt+2\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}w_{t}\left(-s\lambda\varphi w-a(x)w_{xx}\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left|-s\lambda\varphi w-a(x)w_{xx}\right|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w_{t}|^{2}dxdt-2\int_{Q}w_{t}w_{xx}dxdt+2\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}w_{t}(-s\lambda\varphi)wdxdt

\displaystyle:=\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w_{t}|^{2}dxdt+J_{1}+J_{2}.

Hence

\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt\geqslant J_{1}+J_{2},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w_{t}|^{2}dxdt\leqslant\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt-J_{1}-J_{2},

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|P_{+}w|^{2}dxdt\leqslant\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt-J_{1}-J_{2}.

We assume that
s>1
and
\lambda>1.

Using the boundary conditions and integration by parts gives

(2.5)

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}(w_{x}^{2})_{t}dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left[|w_{x}(T,x)|^{2}-|w_{x}(0,x)|^{2}\right]dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\left[|w_{x}(T,x)|^{2}+|w_{x}(0,x)|^{2}\right]dx.

On the other hand,

(2.6)

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=-s\lambda\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(w^{2}\right)_{t}\varphi\,dxdt

\displaystyle=s\lambda^{2}\int_{Q}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}\,dxdt-s\lambda\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\varphi(T)|w(x,T)|^{2}-|w(x,0)|^{2}\right)dx.

Then

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|e^{s\varphi}G\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))}

\displaystyle\geqslant s\lambda^{2}\int_{Q}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}\,dxdt

\displaystyle-s\lambda\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\varphi(T)|w(x,T)|^{2}+|w(x,0)|^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(|w_{x}(x,T)|^{2}+|w_{x}(x,0)|^{2}\right)\,dx.

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(Pw\right)w\,dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}w_{t}w\,dxdt-\int_{Q}s\lambda\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}\,dxdt

\displaystyle-\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(a(x)w_{xx}\right)w\,dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}w_{t}w\,dxdt-\int_{Q}s\lambda\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}\,dxdt-\int_{Q}w_{xx}w\,dxdt

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

\displaystyle\left|I_{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}(w^{2})_{t}\,dxdt\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left[|w(x,t)|^{2}\right]_{t=0}^{t=T}\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(|w(x,T)|^{2}+|w(x,0)|^{2}\right)\,dx.

Next

|I_{2}|=\left|-\int_{Q}s\lambda\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}\,dxdt\right|\leq\int_{Q}s\lambda\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}\,dxdt,

and

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=-\int_{Q}w_{xx}wdxdt=\int_{Q}|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt.

Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\lambda\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(Pw\right)wdxdt

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{Q}\lambda|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt-\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\lambda}{2a(x)}\left(|w(x,T)|^{2}+|w(x,0)|^{2}\right)\,dx.

Moreover

\displaystyle\left|\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}\lambda(Pw)wdxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|Pw|^{2}dxdxdt+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w|^{2}dxdt.

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{Q}|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w|^{2}dxdt+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(|w(x,T)|^{2}+|w(x,0)|^{2}\right)\,dx.

We use (
2.7
) to estimate the first term on the right-hand side. Then

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{Q}|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt\leqslant C_{3}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+C_{3}\int_{Q}\lambda^{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+C_{3}s\lambda\left(\varphi(T)\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|w(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)+C_{3}\left(\|w_{x}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|w_{x}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\right).

Adding (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\lambda\int_{Q}|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{4}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+C_{4}\int_{Q}\lambda^{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\quad+C_{4}s\lambda\left(\varphi(T)\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|w(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+C_{4}\left(\|w_{x}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|w_{x}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\right).

Given that
\varphi(t)=e^{\lambda t}\geqslant 1
, by choosing
s>0
sufficiently large, the second term on the right-hand side can be absorbed into the left-hand side. Then,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\lambda\int_{Q}|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt\leqslant C_{4}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+C_{4}s\lambda\left(\varphi(T)\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|w(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+C_{4}\left(\|w_{x}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|w_{x}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).

We now focus on estimating
|w_{t}|^{2}
. Since we have
u=e^{-s\varphi}w,

u_{t}=-s\lambda\varphi e^{-s\varphi}w+e^{-s\varphi}w_{t},
and

\frac{1}{s\varphi a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\leqslant 2s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}+\frac{2}{s\varphi a(x)}|w_{t}|^{2}.

Let
\epsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
be an arbitrary parameter to be chosen. We have
\frac{1}{s\varphi}=\frac{1}{se^{\lambda t}}\leq\frac{1}{s}\leq\frac{1}{2}
for
s\geqslant 2.
Hence, for all
s>0
and
\lambda>0
large enough, it follows by (
2.4
) that

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\frac{\epsilon}{s\varphi a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dxdt\leqslant 2\epsilon\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}+2\epsilon\int_{Q}\frac{1}{s\varphi a(x)}|w_{t}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\epsilon\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\epsilon\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|w_{t}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\epsilon\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\epsilon\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt+\epsilon\left(-J_{1}-J_{2}\right).

By applying (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we obtain

(2.10)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\frac{\epsilon}{s\varphi a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dxdt\leqslant\epsilon\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\epsilon\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\left(|w_{x}(x,T)|^{2}+|w_{x}(x,0)|^{2}\right)dx+\epsilon s\lambda\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\varphi(T)|w(x,T)|^{2}+|w(x,0)|^{2}\right)dx.

Adding (
2.9
) to (
2.10
) yields

(2.11)

\begin{split}&\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\int_{Q}\lambda|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt+\int_{Q}\frac{\epsilon}{s\varphi a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dxdt\\
&\leqslant C_{5}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+2\epsilon\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt\\
&+C_{5}s\lambda\left(\varphi(T)\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|w(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)\\
&\quad+C_{5}\left(\|w_{x}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|w_{x}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\right).\end{split}

Next, we will estimate
a(x)w_{xx}^{2}
. We have
a(x)w_{xx}=-P_{+}w-s\lambda\varphi w.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s\varphi}a(x)|w_{xx}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\frac{1}{s\varphi a(x)}|P_{+}w|^{2}+2\frac{s\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}|w|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{a(x)}|P_{+}w|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}+2\frac{s\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}|w|^{2}.

Therefore, for all large
s>0
and
\lambda>0,
we have

(2.12)

\begin{split}\int_{Q}\epsilon\frac{1}{s\varphi}a(x)|w_{xx}|^{2}dxdt&\leqslant\int_{Q}\epsilon\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt-\epsilon(J_{1}+J_{2})+\int_{Q}2\epsilon\frac{s\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}|w|^{2}dxdt\\
&\leq\int_{Q}\epsilon\frac{1}{a(x)}|G|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt+\int_{Q}\epsilon\frac{s\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}|w|^{2}dxdt\\
&+C\int_{0}^{1}\epsilon\left(|w_{x}(x,T)|^{2}+|w_{x}(x,0)|^{2}\right)dx\\
&+\epsilon s\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\varphi(T)|w(x,T)|^{2}+|w(x,0)|^{2}\right)dx.\end{split}

Adding (
2.11
) to (
2.12
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt+\int_{Q}\epsilon\frac{1}{s\varphi}a(x)|w_{xx}|^{2}dxdt\\
&+\int_{Q}\lambda|w_{x}|^{2}dxdt+\int_{Q}\frac{\epsilon}{s\varphi a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dxdt\\
&\leqslant C_{5}\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+4\epsilon\int_{Q}s\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}w^{2}dxdt\\
&\quad+C_{5}s\lambda\left(\varphi(T)\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|w(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)\\
&\quad+C_{5}\left(\|w_{x}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|w_{x}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).\end{split}

We set
v=\varphi^{\frac{1}{2}}u
. Then
v_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\varphi^{\frac{1}{2}}u+\varphi^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{t}
, and so

(2.13)

\displaystyle v_{t}-a(x)v_{xx}=\varphi^{\frac{1}{2}}(u_{t}-a(x)u_{xx})+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\varphi^{\frac{1}{2}}u=\varphi^{\frac{1}{2}}G+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\varphi^{\frac{1}{2}}u.

Therefore, we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{Q}\frac{1}{\varphi}\left(a(x)|v_{xx}|^{2}+\frac{1}{a(x)}|v_{t}|^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi}dxdt\\
&+\int_{Q}\lambda s|v_{x}|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt+\int_{Q}s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}v^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt\\
&\leqslant C\int_{Q}s\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+C\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{s\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}u^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt\\
&\quad+Cs\left(s\lambda\varphi(T)\|v(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|v(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi(T)}\\
&\quad+Cs\left(s\lambda\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s}.\end{split}

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\varphi|u_{t}|^{2}\leq 2|v_{t}|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\varphi|u|^{2},\qquad\varphi|u_{xx}|^{2}=|v_{xx}|^{2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|u_{xx}|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\varphi}\left(\frac{2}{a(x)}|v_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|v_{xx}|^{2}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|u_{xx}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|u_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\varphi}\left(\frac{2}{a(x)}|v_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|v_{xx}|^{2}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|u_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\frac{1}{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|v_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|v_{xx}|^{2}\right)+s\lambda|v_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}|v|^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}.

Applying (
5.1
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|u_{xx}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|u_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi}dxdt\\
&\leqslant C\int_{Q}\frac{1}{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|v_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|v_{xx}|^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi}dxdt+\int_{Q}s\lambda|v_{x}|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\\
&\quad+\int_{Q}\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}|v|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt+\int_{Q}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt\\
&\leqslant C\int_{Q}s\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+C\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q}\frac{s\lambda^{2}\varphi}{a(x)}u^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt\\
&\quad+Cs\left(s\lambda\varphi(T)\|v(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|v(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi(T)}\\
&\quad+Cs\left(s\lambda\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s}+\int_{Q}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt.\end{split}

For sufficiently large values of
s>0
, we can absorb the second term and the last term to the right-hand side, and we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|u_{xx}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|u_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{Q}s\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}|Ge^{s\varphi}|^{2}dxdt+Cs\left(s\lambda\varphi(T)\|v(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|v(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi(T)}

\displaystyle\quad+Cs\left(s\lambda\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}+\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)e^{2s}.

∎

2.1.3.
Stability of the backward problem

Now, we prove Hölder stability for past states
u(\cdot,t_{0})
when
0<t_{0}<T
. Note that this idea was first used in
[
17
]
for degenerate equations in divergence form.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\bigcap H^{1}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)
satisfy (
2.1
) and assume that

\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}\leq M_{1}

for an arbitrarily fixed constant
M_{1}>0
. There are positive constants
C>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
depending on
t_{0}
and
M_{1}
satisfying

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant C\left(\|u(\cdot,T)\|^{\theta}_{1,\frac{1}{a}}+\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}\right).

Proof.

Using the Carleman estimate (
2.3
) and the fact that
\varphi(t_{0})\leq\varphi(t)
, for
t_{0}\leq t\leq T
, yields

\displaystyle e^{2s\varphi(t_{0})}\int_{(0,1)\times(t_{0},T)}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}+s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}u^{2}\right)dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant Cs^{2}\lambda\varphi(T)\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{2s\varphi(T)}+Cs^{2}\lambda M_{1}^{2}e^{2s}.

For
\lambda>0
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)\times(t_{0},T)}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{t}|^{2}+s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}u^{2}\right)dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant Cs^{2}\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{2s(\varphi(T)-\varphi(t_{0}))}+Cs^{2}M_{1}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})},

with
\alpha(t_{0}):=\varphi(t_{0})-1
and the constant
C
depends on
T
and
\lambda
. Hence,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|u_{t}\|_{L^{2}(t_{0},T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))}^{2}\leqslant Cs^{2}D_{0}^{2}e^{2s(\varphi(T)-\varphi(t_{0})}+Cs^{2}M_{1}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})},

where
D_{0}:=\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}.

Since we have

u(x,t_{0})=\int_{T}^{t_{0}}u_{t}(x,t)dt+u(x,T)\qquad\forall x\in(0,1),

there exists a constant
C_{6}>0
such that for all
t_{0}\in(0,T)
we have

(2.16)

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant C_{6}\|u_{t}\|_{L^{2}(t_{0},T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))}^{2}+C_{6}\|u(\cdot,T)\|^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}.

Using (
2.15
) to estimate the first term on the right-hand side, along with the fact that
\varphi(T)>1,
we obtain

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{6}\left(Cs^{2}D_{0}^{2}e^{2s(\varphi(T)-\varphi(t_{0})}+Cs^{2}M_{1}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}\right)+C_{6}\|u(\cdot,T)\|^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{7}s^{2}D_{0}^{2}e^{2s\varphi(T)}+C_{7}s^{2}M_{1}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{7}D_{0}^{2}e^{3s\varphi(T)}+C_{7}M_{1}^{2}e^{-s\alpha(t_{0})}

for all
s>s_{0}
and all
t_{0}\in(0,T)
, and this yields

(2.17)

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant C_{8}D_{0}^{2}e^{3s\varphi(T)}+C_{8}M_{1}^{2}e^{-s\alpha(t_{0})}\;\;\;\text{for all }\;\;s>0,

with
C_{8}=C_{7}e^{6s_{0}\varphi(T)}.

To obtain the desired result, we study two possible cases.

•

If
M_{1}\leqslant D_{0}
, taking
s=0
, the inequality (
2.17
) yields

\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leq 2C_{8}D_{0}^{2}.

•

If
M_{1}>D_{0}
, we choose
s>0
such that

D_{0}^{2}e^{3s\varphi(T)}=M_{1}^{2}e^{-s\alpha(t_{0})},

which yields

s=\frac{2}{3\varphi(T)+\alpha(t_{0})}\log\frac{M_{1}}{D_{0}}>0.

Then,

\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant 2C_{8}M_{1}^{\frac{6\varphi(T)}{3\varphi(T)+\alpha(t_{0})}}D_{0}^{\frac{2\alpha(t_{0})}{3\varphi(T)+\alpha(t_{0})}}.

Thus, taking
\theta:=\frac{\alpha(t_{0})}{3\varphi(T)+\alpha(t_{0})}\in(0,1)
concludes the proof.

∎

Next, we prove logarithmic stability for initial states, i.e., when
t_{0}=0
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
u,u_{t},u_{tt}\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\bigcap H^{1}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)
satisfy (
2.1
) such that

\|u_{tt}(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant M_{1}

for an arbitrarily fixed constant
M_{1}>0
and any
0<\alpha<1
, there is a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant C\left(\log\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-\alpha},

where
D:=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{2}\|\partial_{t}^{k}u(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}
is sufficiently small.

Proof.

The proof is based on the proof of the previous theorem (Case
0<t_{0}<T
) and

u(x,0)=\int_{T}^{0}u_{t}(x,t)dt+u(x,T),\;\;x\in(0,1).

Setting
z=u_{tt}
, we have

(2.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &z_{t}(x,t)-a(x)z_{xx}(x,t)=G_{tt}(x,t)&&\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&z(x,t)=0&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&z(x,0)=z_{0}(x)&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Using the Carleman estimate (
2.3
) and the fact that
\varphi(t_{0})\leq\varphi(t)
for
t_{0}\leq t\leq T
, we obtain

\displaystyle e^{2s\varphi(t_{0})}\int_{(0,1)\times(t_{0},T)}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|z_{t}|^{2}+s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}z^{2}\right)dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant Cs^{2}\lambda\varphi(T)\|z(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{2s\varphi(T)}+Cs^{2}\lambda\|z(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{2s},

which implies for
\lambda>0
sufficiently large and
s\geqslant s_{0}

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)\times(t_{0},T)}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{ttt}|^{2}+s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}|u_{tt}|^{2}\right)dxdt\leqslant

\displaystyle Cs^{2}\|u_{tt}(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{2s(\varphi(T)-\varphi(t_{0})}

\displaystyle+Cs^{2}\|u_{tt}(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})},

with
\alpha(t_{0}):=\varphi(t_{0})-1
and the constant
C
depends on
T
and
\lambda
. Hence,

(2.19)

\displaystyle\|u_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(t_{0},T,L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))}\leqslant C\|u_{tt}(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{2s\varphi(T)}+C\|u_{tt}(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}

for all
0<t_{0}<T.
Since

u_{t}(x,t_{0})=\int_{T}^{t_{0}}u_{tt}(x,t)dt+u_{t}(x,T),\;\;\;x\in(0,1),

we have

\displaystyle\|u_{t}(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant C\|u_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(t_{0},T,L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1))}^{2}+C\|u_{t}(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}.

Using (
2.19
) to estimate the first term on the right-hand side, one obtains

(2.20)

\displaystyle\|u_{t}(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant

\displaystyle C\|u_{tt}(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{2s\varphi(T)}+C\|u_{tt}(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}+C\|u_{t}(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}D^{2}e^{2s\varphi(T)}+C_{1}M_{1}^{2}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}

for all
s\geqslant s_{0}
and
0<t_{0}<T.
Here, we used
\|u_{t}(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant D^{2}\leqslant CD^{2}e^{2s\varphi(T)}
and
\|u_{tt}(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant D^{2}
.
Since

u(x,0)=\int_{T}^{0}u_{t}(x,t_{0})dt_{0}+u(x,T),\;\;x\in(0,1),

we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}|u(x,0)|^{2}dx\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left|\int_{T}^{0}u_{t}(x,t_{0})dt_{0}\right|^{2}dx+2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}|u(x,T)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{t}(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}dt_{0}+C\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}.

Using (
2.20
) to estimate the first term on the right-hand side, we obtain

\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant C_{1}D^{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{2s\varphi(T)}dt_{0}+C_{1}M_{1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}dt_{0}+C\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}.

Since
\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant D^{2},
then

(2.21)

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant C_{1}D^{2}e^{C_{2}s}+C_{1}M_{1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}dt_{0}.

Using the change of variable
\tau=\alpha(t_{0})=e^{\lambda t_{0}}-1
, we have

\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2s\alpha(t_{0})}dt_{0}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2s\tau}\frac{1}{1+\tau}d\tau\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[\frac{e^{-2s\tau}}{2s}\right]_{\tau=e^{\lambda T}-1}^{\tau=0}\leqslant\frac{1}{2s\lambda}.

Hence, by (
2.21
), we obtain

(2.22)

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\leqslant C_{3}D^{2}e^{C_{2}s}+\frac{C_{3}}{s}M_{1}^{2}

for
s\geqslant s_{0}.

Setting
C^{\prime}=C_{3}e^{C_{2}s_{0}}
, we obtain (
2.22
) for all
s>0
by replacing
s:=s+s_{0}
.
Supposing
D<1
and setting

s=\left(\log\frac{1}{D}\right)^{\alpha}>0
,
with
0<\alpha<1
, we have

\displaystyle e^{C_{2}s}D^{2}=\exp\left(-2\left(\log\frac{1}{D}\right)+C_{2}\left(\log\frac{1}{D}\right)^{\alpha}\right).

Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{4}>0
such that

e^{-2\xi+C_{2}\xi^{\alpha}}\leqslant\frac{C_{4}}{\xi^{\alpha}}\;\;\;\text{for all}\;\;\xi>0.

Then, we have

e^{C_{2}s}D^{2}\leqslant C_{4}\left(\log\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-\alpha},

and

C_{3}D^{2}e^{C_{2}s}+\frac{C_{3}}{s}M_{1}^{2}\leqslant C^{\prime}C_{4}\left(\log\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-\alpha}+C_{3}M_{1}^{2}\left(\log\frac{1}{D}\right)^{-\alpha}.

Thus, we have the result.
∎

3.
Degenerate viscous Hamilton–Jacobi equation

Now, we arrive at the main result for a degenerate viscous Hamilton–Jacobi equation with a general Hamiltonian and lower-order terms. Consider the following nonlinear degenerate equation

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &u_{t}(x,t)-a(x)u_{xx}(x,t)+\dfrac{b(x,t)}{q}|u_{x}(x,t)|^{q}+d(x,t)u_{x}(x,t)+c(x,t)u(x,t)=G(x,t),\;\;\;\text{ in }Q,\\
&u(x,t)=0,\hskip 184.9429pt(x,t)\in\Sigma,\end{aligned}

where
b,c,d\in L^{\infty}(Q)
,
q\geqslant 1
and the degenerate coefficient
a(x)
satisfies
Assumption I
.

Remark 3.1
.

It should be emphasized that we consider conditional stability for (
3.1
) with a general Hamiltonian in terms of
q\geqslant 1
, whereas similar works only consider a quadratic Hamiltonian with
q=2
; see e.g.,
[
36
]
for a mean field game system.

Next, we state a result of Hölder stability when
0<t_{0}<T
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
u,v\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\bigcap H^{1}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)
satisfy (
3.1
) and

\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant M_{1},\;\;\;\|v(\cdot,0)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant M_{1}

for an arbitrarily fixed constant
M_{1}>0
. We assume one of the following assumptions:

Case 1:
there exist constants
C,K>0
such that

|b(x,t)|+|d(x,t)|\leqslant C\sqrt{a(x)},\quad\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leqslant K,\;\;\;\|v_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leqslant K;

Case 2:

b\in L^{\infty}(Q)
and there exists a constant
K>0
such that

\left\|\dfrac{|u_{x}|^{q-1}}{\sqrt{a}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leqslant K,\qquad\left\|\dfrac{|v_{x}|^{q-1}}{\sqrt{a}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leqslant K,\qquad|d(x,t)|\leqslant C\sqrt{a(x)}.

Then there exists a constant
C>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
depending on
t_{0}
and
M_{1}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{0})-v(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant C\left(\|u(\cdot,T)-v(\cdot,T)\|^{\theta}_{1,\frac{1}{a}}+\|u(\cdot,T)-v(\cdot,T)\|_{1,\frac{1}{a}}\right)

Proof.

Setting
y=u-v
and
r(x)=\frac{1}{q}(|u_{x}|^{q}-|v_{x}|^{q})
, from (
3.1
) we obtain

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y_{t}(x,t)+a(x)y_{xx}(x,t)=-d(x,t)y_{x}-c(x,t)y-b(x,t)r(x)\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&y(x,t)=0\hskip 241.84842pt\text{ on }\Sigma,\end{aligned}\right.

Case 1:
Using the following key inequality

(3.3)

|a^{q}-b^{q}|\leqslant q\left(a^{q-1}+b^{q-1}\right)|a-b|,

for all
q\geqslant 1,\;\;a,b\geqslant 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle|r(x)|=\frac{1}{q}\left\|u_{x}|^{q}-|v_{x}|^{q}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(|u_{x}|^{q-1}+|v_{x}|^{q-1}\right)(|u_{x}|-|v_{x}|)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(|u_{x}|^{q-1}+|v_{x}|^{q-1}\right)|y_{x}|

\displaystyle\leqslant C|y_{x}|.

Then we apply lemma (
5.1
) to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|y_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|y_{xx}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|y_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{Q}s\varphi\frac{1}{a(x)}|b(x,t)r(x)+d(x,t)y_{x}+c(x,t)y|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dxdt

and

. The first term of the right-hand side can be absorbed, and by choosing

and
\lambda>0
very large, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{a(x)}|y_{t}|^{2}+a(x)|y_{xx}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|y_{x}|^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}\varphi^{2}}{a(x)}|u|^{2}\right)e^{2s\varphi}dxdt

2.5
can complete the proof.

Case 2:

then we apply Lemma
2.4

4.
Identification for linear degenerate parabolic problem

We consider the following degenerate parabolic equation

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}(x,t)-a(x)u_{xx}(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&u(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&u(x,0)=f(x),&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Recall that the backward problem consists of the determination of
f=u(\cdot,0)
from the final state data
u_{T}(x)=u(x,T).
For a given
u(\cdot,0)=f\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
, the parabolic problem (
4.1
) will be referred to as the direct problem.

The input-output operator
\Psi
is defined as follows

\Psi(f)(x)=u(x,T;f),\qquad x\in(0,1).

Therefore, the backward problem can be written as an operator equation as follows

\displaystyle\Psi(f)=u_{T},\qquad f\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)

Lemma 4.1
.

The input-output operator

\Psi\;:\;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\ni f\longmapsto u(\cdot,T;f)\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1).

is not surjective.

Proof.

To derive a contradiction, let’s assume that
\Psi
is surjective. Let
g\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\backslash H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1).
Then, there exists an element
f\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
such that
\Psi(f)=g,
which implies
u(\cdot,T)=g,
where
u
is a solution of (
4.1
). However, this contradicts the fact that
u(\cdot,T)\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
(see Theorem
2.3
).
∎

From Lemma
4.1
, we conclude that the backward problem is ill-posed. Moreover, we define a quasi-solution of this inverse problem as a minimizer of the functional
J
defined by

\displaystyle J(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}|u(x,T;f)-u^{\delta}_{T}(x)|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\|u(\cdot,T;f)-u^{\delta}_{T}\|^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}},\;\;f\in W,

where
u^{\delta}_{T}
is a noisy data of
u_{T}
such that
\|u_{T}-u^{\delta}_{T}\|_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant\delta
for
\delta\geqslant 0
, and

W=\left\{f\in L_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}(0,1)\,:\;\;\|f\|_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant M\right\}

is the set of admissible initial data of size
M
(fixed positive constant). Obviously,
W
is a bounded, closed and convex subset of
L_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}(0,1).

Due to the ill-posedness of the backward problem, one usually uses a regularization approach by introducing the Tikhonov functional

J_{\epsilon}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\|u(\cdot,T;f)-u^{\delta}_{T}\|^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|f\|^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}},\qquad f\in W,

where
\epsilon>0
is the regularizing parameter.

In the following lemma, we establish a conditional stability estimate using the logarithmic convexity method; see, e.g.,
[
42
]
, and
[
21
]
for a survey.

Lemma 4.2
.

The solution of (
4.1
) satisfies

(4.2)

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant M^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{\frac{t}{T}}\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],

for all
f\in W.

Proof.

Let
f\neq 0
, otherwise the estimate is trivial.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}

\displaystyle=2(u_{t}(\cdot,t),u(\cdot,t))_{\frac{1}{a}}

\displaystyle=2(au_{xx}(\cdot,t),u(\cdot,t))_{\frac{1}{a}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}u_{xx}udx

\displaystyle=-2\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}^{2}dx

and

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-4\int_{0}^{1}(u_{t})_{x}u_{x}dx

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{1}u_{t}u_{xx}dx

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{1}a(u_{xx})^{2}dx

\displaystyle=4\|au_{xx}\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-4.26773pt\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}-

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\left(\|au_{xx}\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}-(au_{xx}(\cdot,t),u(\cdot,t))_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)\geqslant 0.

Since,

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\log\left(\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)=\frac{\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}-\left(\frac{d}{dt}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{4}}\geqslant 0,

\log\left(\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)
is a convex function of
t
, which gives

\log\left(\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)\leqslant\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)\log\left(\|u(\cdot,0)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right)+\frac{t}{T}\log\left(\|u(\cdot,T)\|_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right).

Thus, we deduce (
4.2
).
∎

4.1.
Fréchet differentiability of the Tikhonov functional

We recall the following definition:

Definition 4.3
.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
. A function
u
is a weak solution of (
4.1
) if

u\in C\left([0,T];L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)

and satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}u(x,T)v(x,T)dx-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}u(x,0)v(x,0)dx-\int_{Q}\frac{1}{a(x)}u(x,t)v_{t}(x,t)dxdt

\displaystyle=-\int_{Q}u_{x}(x,t)v_{x}(x,t)dxdt

for all
v\in H^{1}\left(0,T;L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{1}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)\right).

Lemma 4.4
.

Denote by
u(x,t;f)
the weak solution of the parabolic degenerate problem (
4.1
) corresponding to the unknown initial datum
f\in W.
Then functional
J
is Fréchet differentiable at
f
and the Fréchet gradient is given by

(4.3)

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(f)(x)=\psi(x,0;f)\qquad\text{for a.e. }\quad x\in(0,1),

where
\psi(x,t;f)
is the weak solution of the adjoint problem

(4.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\psi_{t}(x,t)=-a(x)\psi_{xx}(x,t),&&\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&\psi=0,&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&\psi(x,T)=u(x,T;f)-u^{\delta}_{T}(x),&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

Let
f,\,f+\delta f\in W
be given initial data and
u(x,t;f),\,u(x,t;f+\delta f)
be corresponding solutions of (
4.1
). Then

\delta u(x,t;f)=u(x,t;f+\delta f)-u(x,t,f)

is the weak solution of the following system

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta u_{t}(x,t)=a(x)\delta u_{xx}(x,t),&&\quad\text{ in }Q\\
&\delta u(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&\delta u(x,0)=\delta f(x),&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Let us calculate the increment
\delta J(f)=J(f+\delta f)-J(f)
of functional (
2.4
)

\displaystyle\delta J(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|u(\cdot,T;f+\delta f)-u^{\delta}_{T}\|_{\frac{1}{a}}-\frac{1}{2}\|u(\cdot,T;f)-u_{T}^{\delta}\|_{\frac{1}{a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left[(u(x,T;f+\delta f)-u^{\delta}_{T}(x))^{2}-(u(x,T;f)-u^{\delta}_{T}(x))^{2}\right].

Using the identity

\frac{1}{2}\left[(r-t)^{2}-(s-t)^{2}\right]=(s-t)(r-s)+\frac{1}{2}(r-s)^{2},\;r,s,t\in\mathbb{R},

we obtain

\displaystyle\delta J(f)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left[u(x,T;f)-u^{\delta}_{T}(x)\right]\delta u(x,T;f)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left[\delta u(x,T;f)\right]^{2}dx.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left[u(x,T;f)-u^{\delta}_{T}(x)\right]\delta u(x,T;f)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\psi(x,T)\delta u(x,T)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\psi(x,t)\delta u(x,t)\right)_{t}dt\right\}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\psi(x,0)\delta u(x,0)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\psi_{t}\delta u+\psi\delta u_{t}\right)dtdx+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\psi(x,0)\delta u(x,0)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(-a(x)\psi_{xx}\delta u+\psi a(x)\delta u_{xx}\right)dtdx+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\psi(x,0)\delta u(x,0)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\psi_{xx}\delta u+\psi\delta u_{xx}\right)dtdx+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\psi(x,0)\delta u(x,0)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\psi(x,0)\delta u(x,0)dx.

This implies

\displaystyle\delta J(f)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\psi(x,0)\delta u(x,0)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\delta u(x,T;f)\right]^{2}dx

\displaystyle=(J^{\prime}(f),\delta f)_{\frac{1}{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left[\delta u(x,T;f)\right]^{2}dx.

Using the result of Theorem
2.3
yields

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left[\delta u(x,T;f)\right]^{2}dx\leqslant||\delta f||_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}.

Hence,

\delta J(f)=(J^{\prime}(f),\delta f)+o\left(||\delta f||_{\frac{1}{a}}^{2}\right).

∎

In optimization, it is classical that the Lipschitz continuity of the Fréchet gradient
J^{\prime}
yields fast numerical results. This is proven in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

The Fréchet derivative
J^{\prime}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L=1
, i.e.,

||J^{\prime}(f+\delta f)-J^{\prime}(f)||_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant||\delta f||_{\frac{1}{a}}\qquad\forall f,\,f+\delta f\in W.

Proof.

By definition

||J^{\prime}(f+\delta f)-J^{\prime}(f)||_{\frac{1}{a}}=||\delta\psi(\cdot,0;f)||_{\frac{1}{a}},

where

\delta\psi(x,t;f)=\psi(x,t;f+\delta f)-\psi(x,t;f)
solves

(4.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta\psi_{t}(x,t)=-a(x)\delta\psi_{xx}(x,t),&&\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&\delta\psi(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&\delta\psi(x,T)=\delta u(x,T;f),&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

and
\delta u(x,t;f)
is the weak solution of the auxiliary problem

(4.7)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta u_{t}(x,t)=a(x)\delta u_{xx}(x,t),&&\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&\delta u(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&\delta u(x,0)=\delta f(x),&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

We use the energy identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta\psi(x,t)\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta\psi\delta\psi_{t}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta\psi a(x)\delta\psi_{xx}dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\delta\psi\delta\psi_{xx}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\delta\psi_{x})^{2}dx\geqslant 0.

Integrating on
[0,T]
and using
\delta\psi(x,T;f)=\delta u(x,T;f)
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta\psi(x,0;f)\right)^{2}dx\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta\psi(x,T;f)\right)^{2}dx=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta u(x,T;f)\right)^{2}dx.

Next, we employ the following equality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta u(x,t)\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta u\delta u_{t}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta ua(x)\delta u_{xx}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\delta u\delta u_{xx}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}(\delta u_{x})^{2}dx\leqslant 0.

Integrating on
[0,T]
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta u(x,T;f)\right)^{2}dx\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta u(x,0;f)\right)^{2}dx=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}|\delta f|^{2}dx.

The above estimates yield

||\delta\psi(\cdot,0;f)||_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant||\delta f||_{\frac{1}{a}}.

∎

We introduce the Landweber iteration given by

(4.8)

\displaystyle f_{n+1}=f_{n}-\alpha_{n}J^{\prime}(f_{n}),\;n=0,1,2,\dots

where
f_{0}
is a given initial iteration in
W
and
\alpha_{n}
is the iteration parameter defined as follows

g_{n}(\alpha_{n}):=\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}g_{n}(\alpha),\;g_{n}(\alpha):=J(f_{n}-\alpha J^{\prime}(f_{n})),\,n=0,1,2\dots

Remark 4.6
.

Some remarks are in order:

(i)

to show the monotonicity and convergence of the sequence

4.2.
Existence and uniqueness

In this section, we will study the existence and uniqueness of the quasi-solution using the calculus of variations.

Lemma 4.8
.

For
J\in C^{1}(W)
, the following equality holds

\langle J^{\prime}(f+\delta f)-J^{\prime}(f),\delta f\rangle_{\frac{1}{a}}=\|\delta u(\cdot,T,f)\|^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}\qquad\forall f,\,f+\delta f\in W.

Proof.

Let
\delta\psi
be the solution of (
4.6
). Using gradient formula (
4.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle J^{\prime}(f+\delta f)-J^{\prime}(f),\delta f\rangle_{\frac{1}{a}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta\psi(\cdot,0)\delta u(\cdot,0)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta\psi(x,t)\delta u(x,t)\right)_{t}dt\right\}dx+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta\psi(x,T)\delta u(x,T)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(\delta\psi_{t}\delta u+\delta\psi\delta u_{t}\right)dtdx+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta\psi(x,T)\delta u(x,T)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{a(x)}\left(-a(x)\delta\psi_{xx}\delta u+\delta\psi a(x)\delta u_{xx}\right)dtdx+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta\psi(x,T)\delta u(x,T)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\delta\psi_{xx}\delta u+\delta\psi\delta u_{xx}\right)dtdx+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta u(x,T)\delta u(x,T)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{a(x)}\delta u(x,T)^{2}dx.

5.
Numerical identification for Degenerate viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation

In this section, we develop some numerical algorithms to reconstruct the unknown initial data from the measured final state.
First, we use the Conjugate Gradient (CG) method for the linear equation, while for the nonlinear viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation, we employ Van Cittert’s iteration, which turned out to be more suitable for handling the nonlinearity.

5.1.
Linear degenerate parabolic equation

We consider the following degenerate parabolic equation

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}(x,t)-a(x)u_{xx}(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ in }Q\\
&u(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
&u(x,0)=f(x),&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

5.1.1.
Discretization of the problem

We approximate the solution of the equation (
5.1
) using the finite difference method. We discretize the space-time domain
[0,1]\times[0,T]
into a uniform grid
x_{j}=jdx
for

, with a spatial step size

and
t_{n}=ndt
for

with a time step size

. We denote by

the approximate solution at node
x_{j}
at time
t_{n},
and
a_{j}=a(x_{j})
. The first-order and second-order derivatives of
u
in (
5.1
) are approximated by:

\partial_{t}u(x_{j},t_{n})\approx\frac{u_{j}^{n+1}-u_{j}^{n}}{dt}

and

\partial_{xx}u(x_{j},t_{n})\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{u_{j+1}^{n+1}-2u_{j}^{n+1}+u_{j-1}^{n+1}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{u_{j+1}^{n}-2u_{j}^{n}+u_{j-1}^{n}}{dx^{2}}\right).

Then we consider the Crank–Nicolson discretization to obtain:

\frac{u_{j}^{n+1}-u_{j}^{n}}{dt}=\frac{a_{j}}{2}\left(\frac{u_{j+1}^{n+1}-2u_{j}^{n+1}+u_{j-1}^{n+1}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{u_{j+1}^{n}-2u_{j}^{n}+u_{j-1}^{n}}{dx^{2}}\right).

This numerical scheme can be written as follows

-\gamma_{j}u_{j-1}^{n+1}+(1+2\gamma_{j})u_{j}^{n+1}-\gamma_{j}u_{j+1}^{n+1}=\gamma_{j}u_{j-1}^{n}+(1-2\gamma_{j})u_{j}^{n}+\gamma_{j}u^{n}_{j+1},

where
\gamma_{j}=\dfrac{a_{j}dt}{2dx^{2}}.
Then, we obtain the following algebraic equation

Au^{n+1}=Bu^{n},\quad n=0,\cdots,N_{t},

where
u^{n}=(u^{n}_{1},u_{2}^{n},\cdots,u_{N_{x}}^{n})^{t}
, and

A=\begin{bmatrix}1+2\gamma_{1}&-\gamma_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
-\gamma_{2}&1+2\gamma_{2}&-\gamma_{2}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&-\gamma_{3}&1+2\gamma_{3}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-\gamma_{N-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&-\gamma_{N}&1+2\gamma_{N}\end{bmatrix},

B=\begin{bmatrix}1-2\gamma_{1}&\gamma_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
\gamma_{2}&1-2\gamma_{2}&\gamma_{2}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\gamma_{3}&1-2\gamma_{3}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\gamma_{N-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&\gamma_{N}&1-2\gamma_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

Degenerate viscous Hamilton–Jacobi equation

Now we aim to determine the initial datum
f(x)

(5.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}-a(x)u_{xx}+\dfrac{b}{q}|u_{x}|^{q}+du_{x}+cu=0,&&\quad\text{ in }Q,\\
&u(x,t)=0,&&\quad\text{ in }\Sigma,\\
&u(x,0)=f(x),&&\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
b,c,d\in L^{\infty}(Q)

, and the degenerate coefficient

satisfies
Assumption I
.

As before, let us introduce the input-output operator

\Psi(f)(x)=u(x,T;f),\;x\in(0,1),

solves (

\displaystyle\Psi(f)=u_{T},\qquad f\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1).

5.2.1.

Following
[

[

]
given by

where
\gamma>0
is a relaxation parameter and
f_{1}(x)=\gamma u^{\delta}_{T}
is the first iterate. The Van Cittert iteration is simple to implement. It was used in image restoration, and it is useful in applications such as astronomy, medical imaging, etc. We refer to
[
37
, Chapter 4]
for more details. This iteration has been adapted for some 2D nonlinear backward parabolic problems in
[

]

In practice, measurements always contain noise; instead of

such that

\|u_{T}-u_{T}^{\delta}\|_{\frac{1}{a}}\leqslant\delta\qquad\text{for}\;\delta\geqslant 0.

Since the problem is ill-posed in general, a small perturbation in data can

cause large errors in the solution. To tackle this challenge, the regularization was implemented by stopping the iterations as soon as the value of the residual error becomes just below the noise level; see, e.g.,
[

19

20

39
]

where

6.
Conclusion and final comments

The backward problem of determining unknown initial data in degenerate viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equations from final-time measurements is of significant interest in various applied science applications. While models with nondegenerate diffusion coefficients have been extensively studied within well-established theoretical frameworks, much less attention has been given to viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equations involving degenerate diffusion coefficients. In such cases, both the theory and numerical methods remain at an early stage due to several substantial challenges.

First, the degeneracy of the diffusion coefficient makes the well-posedness and regularity of the viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equations significantly more subtle. This issue has been successfully addressed in our paper by working in appropriately weighted
L^{2}
spaces and using norms that account for the degeneracy. On the numerical side, solving degenerate equations is more delicate, as it requires refined schemes for both discretization and integration. In our setting, the boundary degeneracy, combined with Dirichlet boundary conditions, allowed us to overcome these difficulties. However, the case of interior degeneracy or Neumann boundary conditions would be a worthwhile direction for further investigation.

Regarding the backward problem associated with the nonlinear viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
3.1
), we have proven the stability estimate (Theorem
3.2
) for the Hamiltonian exponent
q\geq 1
thanks to inequality (
3.3
). Therefore, it is natural to ask whether one can prove similar stability results for the case
0<q<1
.

Finally, our analysis has been carried out for the one-dimensional interval
(0,1)
in the weighted space
L^{2}_{\frac{1}{a}}(0,1)
, where a nondivergence form operator has been studied naturally. Extending our results to a multi-dimensional domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\geq 2
is an integer) remains an interesting direction for future research. The Carleman estimate approach we developed is promising, but establishing the appropriate functional framework and conducting the numerical analysis in higher dimensions are more subtle.
