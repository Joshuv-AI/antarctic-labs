A Restart-Free Accelerated Algorithm for Non-Convex Minimization: Continuous and Discrete Analysis
Kansei Ushiyama, Shun Sato

Abstract
We propose two novel first-order methods for minimizing nonconvex functions with Lipschitz-continuous gradients and Hessians. These algorithms attain an ε \varepsilon -approximate first-order stationary point in O ( ε − 7 / 4 ) \mathrm{O}\left\lparen\varepsilon^{-7/4}\right\rparen function and gradient evaluations, without using ε \varepsilon as an input parameter. While existing methods rely on restart mechanisms to achieve this complexity, our methods do not. Consequently, the first algorithm enjoys a simple implementation, making its last iterate differentiable with respect to the initial point. By estimating the Lipschitz constants adaptively, we develop the second algorithm that does not require prior knowledge of the constants. This algorithm exhibits better numerical performance than existing parameter-free methods for certain problems, which can be attributed to its restart-free design. Both algorithms are derived by discretizing a newly introduced continuous-time model represented by an ordinary differential equation, and their continuous- and discrete-time convergence analyses proceed in a parallel manner under the Performance Estimation Problem framework.



keywords
Nonconvex optimization, Momentum methods, Ordinary differential equations, Performance estimation problem, Differentiable algorithm

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the smooth nonconvex optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),

(2)

where
f
is twice differentiable and lower bounded.
In addition, we assume that
f
has Lipschitz continuous gradient and Hessian.
Our goal is to find an
\varepsilon
-first-order stationary point, i.e., a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq\varepsilon
, using only first-order oracle evaluations of
f(x)
and
\nabla f(x)
at given points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Such first-order methods are particularly effective for large-scale problems, such as training large machine learning models, since they do not require Hessian information, whose computation is often costly.

For functions
f
with only Lipschitz continuous gradients, gradient descent achieves the optimal complexity
\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\varepsilon^{-2}\right\rparen
for finding an
\varepsilon
-stationary point (cf.
41
).
Recently, under the additional assumption that the Hessian is Lipschitz continuous, several first-order methods have achieved the improved complexity bound
\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\varepsilon^{-7/4}\right\rparen

34
;
36
;
37
, which was shown to be optimal in
65
(see
Section

1.2
for a detailed review).
Along this line of research, we propose two first-order methods that attain this optimal complexity.

Existing methods mainly rely on
restart strategies
to guarantee fast convergence.
While such strategies are widely used in optimization algorithms for both convex and nonconvex settings, they have several drawbacks.
Typically, when a restart occurs, internal variables of the algorithm, such as the velocity, are reset to their initial values.
This procedure requires conditional “if” statements on internal variables, causing discontinuous behavior between the “if” and “else” branches.
Consequently, when the algorithm’s output is viewed as a function of the initial point, restart strategies render this function non-differentiable.
This non-differentiability hinders the use of
differentiable algorithms
, which can otherwise be incorporated as subroutines within outer functions while maintaining overall differentiability (see
Section

1.2
).
Another drawback is that restarts discard information accumulated by the algorithm.
Although this process theoretically improves the worst-case complexity, it may result in excessive information loss.
This motivates the search for milder strategies that are more natural in practice while preserving the same improved worst-case convergence rate.

In the proposed algorithms, we introduce an alternative strategy, called
velocity control
, instead of restart.
In this strategy, the velocity is reduced, rather than reset to zero, when it exceeds a certain threshold.

In the first proposed algorithm, this deceleration is implemented smoothly with respect to the pre-controlled velocity, making the last iterate of the algorithm differentiable with respect to the initial value and the hyperparameters.
The convergence guarantee, however, is stated for the best iterate, as is common in nonconvex optimization.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first differentiable algorithm that attains an
\varepsilon
-stationary point with oracle complexity
\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\varepsilon^{-7/4}\right\rparen
.

The second algorithm is parameter-free; it does not require any prior knowledge of
L
,
M
, or
\varepsilon
, although its velocity control is implemented discontinuously with respect to the pre-controlled velocity.
This algorithm exhibits faster numerical convergence for certain functions than existing parameter-free methods with complexity
\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\varepsilon^{-7/4}\right\rparen

36
;
37
.
This improvement may be attributed to two factors: first, existing methods rely on restart strategies, whereas ours employs velocity control, in which the velocity is not reset to zero; and second, existing methods estimate the local Lipschitz constant
M
rather conservatively, whereas ours adapts it more flexibly.

Both proposed algorithms are derived by discretizing the newly introduced ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{6}{7}\frac{\alpha}{t^{1/7}}\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0,\qquad x(0)=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\dot{x}(0)=0,

(3)

where
\alpha>0
.
This ODE can be interpreted as the motion of a mass point moving in the potential field
f
under time-decaying friction.
We first analyze the convergence rate of this ODE using the Performance Estimation Problem (PEP) framework (see
Section

1.2
).
Subsequently, we analyze the complexity of the proposed algorithms in parallel with the analysis of the ODE.
Hessian-free inequalities (cf.
36
) enable us to exploit the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla^{2}f
in the analysis of first-order methods, even though
\nabla^{2}f
does not explicitly appear in the algorithms.

Main contributions

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

•

We propose a new continuous-time model ODE (
3
) for nonconvex minimization and prove the convergence rate

\min_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x}(t))\right\|\leq\mathrm{O}\left\lparen T^{-4/7}\right\rparen

(4)

for some weighted average
\bar{x}
of the trajectory.

•

By discretizing the ODE (
3

2
).
Both algorithms achieve the convergence rate

\min_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x}_{t})\right\|\leq\mathrm{O}\left\lparen T^{-4/7}\right\rparen

(5)

for some output
\bar{x}_{t}

\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\varepsilon^{-7/4}\right\rparen
.
Instead of relying on restart mechanisms, these algorithms employ a velocity-control mechanism, which reduces the velocity without resetting it to zero.

–

The first is a differentiable algorithm, which enables differentiation of the last iterate with respect to the initial value and the hyperparameters of the algorithm. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first differentiable algorithm with oracle complexity
\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\varepsilon^{-7/4}\right\rparen
.

–

The second is a parameter-free algorithm, which does not require any prior knowledge of
f
. Numerical experiments show that, for some problems, this method outperforms existing parameter-free methods.

Differentiable algorithms

A
differentiable algorithm
is an optimization routine whose output is differentiable, via automatic differentiation, with respect to its inputs and hyperparameters.
Such algorithms can be embedded as subroutines inside larger models, enabling outer-level objectives to be optimized end-to-end by backpropagation.
Incorporating them into learning systems has been reported to improve performance across hyperparameter tuning and meta-learning
3
;
20
, adversarial training
38
, unrolled sparse coding such as LISTA
22
, and inverse problems via unfolded proximal/primal-dual methods
63
.

Continuous-time algorithms

Continuous-time algorithms are models of optimization methods represented by ODEs.
A classical example is gradient descent, which can be modeled by the gradient flow
\dot{x}=-\nabla f(x)
.
For momentum methods, a continuous-time model first appeared in
46
, where the heavy-ball method and its ODE counterpart

\ddot{x}(t)+\beta\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0

(6)

were considered.
In
51
, Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method (AGM)
40
was modeled by the vanishing-damping ODE

\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{3}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0,

(7)

whose convergence rate corresponds well to that of its discrete counterpart:
for convex
f
, the solution
x(t)
of this ODE satisfies
f(x(t))-f^{\star}=\mathrm{O}\left\lparen 1/t^{2}\right\rparen
.
This formulation was later extended to accelerated mirror descent
33
.
In
50
, AGM was modeled in another way as a high-resolution ODE involving a Hessian-velocity product.
This formulation enables the derivation of the convergence rate for the gradient norm as
\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|=\mathrm{O}\left\lparen 1/t^{3/2}\right\rparen
.
For
\mu
-strongly convex functions, the ODE (
6
) with
\beta=2\sqrt{\mu}
corresponds to a variant of AGM for strongly convex functions
41
, achieving the rate
f(x(t))-f^{\star}=\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{\mu}t}\right\rparen

61
.

For nonconvex problems with
M
-Lipschitz continuous Hessian, the heavy-ball ODE (
6
) was studied in
43
.
They showed that choosing the damping coefficient
\beta=(3M)^{2/7}(\Delta_{f}/T)^{2/7}
, where
\Delta_{f}=f(x_{0})-\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)
, leads to efficient convergence; specifically, for a weighted average
\bar{x}(t)
of the trajectory, it holds that
\min_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla f(\bar{x}(t))\|=\mathrm{O}\left\lparen T^{-4/7}\right\rparen
.
However, this ODE requires prior knowledge of the terminal time
T
, and the convergence guarantee is valid only at that specific
T
.

Our ODE (
3
) was inspired by the above ODE, replacing the damping coefficient
\Theta(1/T^{1/7})
with
\Theta(1/t^{1/7})
, where
t
denotes the current time.
This modification eliminates the need to specify the terminal time
T
in advance.

By discretizing continuous-time models, one can derive implementable optimization algorithms.
However, although systematic approaches to rate-preserving discretization have been studied to some extent (e.g.,
59
), such techniques are generally specific to each ODE.
Accordingly, this paper adopts a specialized discretization tailored to (
3
).

Analysis strategy: Lyapunov functions vs. PEP

The standard approach to analyzing both discrete- and continuous-time algorithms relies on
Lyapunov functions
(also known as
potential functions
), which often explicitly characterize their convergence rates (cf.
19
).
However, identifying appropriate Lyapunov functions is largely heuristic.
Several studies have explored automated methods for constructing such functions
57
;
55
;
58
;
52
;
39
;
27
.

The
Performance Estimation Problem
(PEP), originally proposed in
17
, provides a systematic framework for analyzing and designing first-order methods in convex optimization.
This framework formulates the convergence rate at a fixed iteration
K
as an optimization problem over the function space.
A typical formulation is:

\displaystyle\maximize_{f,\,x^{(0)},\dots,x^{(K)}}

\displaystyle\quad f(x^{(K)})-f^{\star},

(8)

\displaystyle\subjectto

\displaystyle\quad f\text{ is convex and $L$-smooth},

(9)

\displaystyle\quad x^{(k)}\text{ is generated by a first-order method},

(10)

\displaystyle\quad\text{conditions on the initial data}.

(11)

By upper bounding this problem, one can obtain the worst-case convergence rate of the method.
The main challenge is that
f
ranges over an infinite-dimensional function space.
Nevertheless, the problem can be reformulated as a finite-dimensional semidefinite program (SDP) using convex interpolation theory
54
, enabling computer-assisted numerical analyses, with open-source libraries available in
53
;
21
, as well as rigorous analytical convergence studies based on the PEP framework.

Several exactly optimal methods have been derived using the PEP framework,
including the Optimized Gradient Method (OGM)
17
;
28
,
the Information-Theoretic Exact Method (ITEM)
56
,
and OptISTA
24
.
In addition, many other optimal or efficient algorithms have been developed,
and tight worst-case convergence analyses have been obtained within the PEP framework;
see, e.g.,
18
;
31
;
35
;
45
;
30
;
64
;
29
;
13
;
16
;
11
;
15
;

;
14
;
4
;

.

A continuous-time version of PEP for analyzing ODE models was first proposed in
32
,
followed by an alternative formulation in
60
,
which we extend and apply to the nonconvex setting in this paper.

Hessian-free inequalities

Our analysis is based on the following Hessian-free inequalities, introduced in
36
, which exploit the Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian through inequalities that do not explicitly involve the Hessian.
As in
36
;
37
, these inequalities enable us to develop fast Hessian-free methods for minimizing functions
f
with Lipschitz continuous
\nabla^{2}f
.

Proposition 1

(Discrete Hessian-free inequality
(
36
, Lemma 3.1)
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable function with
M
-Lipschitz continuous Hessian. Then, for any
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}z_{i}\right\rparen-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\nabla f\left\lparen z_{i}\right\rparen\right\|\leq\frac{M}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\left\|z_{i}-z_{j}\right\|^{2}.

(12)

Proposition 2

(
(
36
, Lemma 3.2)
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable function with
M
-Lipschitz continuous Hessian. Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

f(x)-f(y)\leq\frac{1}{2}\langle{\nabla f(x)+\nabla f(y)},{x-y}\rangle+\frac{M}{12}\left\|x-y\right\|^{3}.

(13)

Proposition 3

(Continuous Hessian-free inequality
(
43
, Lemma 1)
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable function with
M
-Lipschitz continuous Hessian. Then, for any
z\colon[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
w\colon[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\int_{0}^{t}w(s)\mathrm{d}s=1
, it holds that

\left\|\nabla f\left\lparen\int_{0}^{t}w(s)z(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\rparen-\int_{0}^{t}w(s)\nabla f\left\lparen z(s)\right\rparen\mathrm{d}s\right\|\leq\frac{M}{2}\int_{A}w(s_{1})w(s_{2})\left\|z(s_{1})-z(s_{2})\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}s_{2},

(14)

where
A=\{\,{(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ |\ {0\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq t}\,\}
.

In
5
, it was shown that the converse of
Proposition

1
holds; that is, if (
12
) holds for a differentiable function
f
, then
f
is twice differentiable with
M
-Lipschitz continuous Hessian.

Continuous-time convergence analysis

In this section, we derive a convergence rate for ODE (
3
) using a continuous-time version of PEP.
Specifically, we establish an asymptotic convergence rate for

\min_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x}(t))\right\|
,
where
\bar{x}
is a moving average of the solution
x(t)
.
It is defined by
\bar{x}(0)=x(0)
and, for

\bar{x}(t)\coloneqq\int_{t/2}^{t}w_{t}(\tau)x(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\quad\text{with}\quad w_{t}\colon[t/2,t]\ni\tau\mapsto\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\tau^{6/7}}}{\int_{t/2}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha s^{6/7}}\mathrm{d}s}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

(15)

We consider this averaged trajectory because the Hessian-free inequality (
14

allows us to control the gradient at an average point through the average of gradients
along the trajectory and a quadratic error term.
The weight is motivated by the relation

\frac{1}{w_{t}(\tau)}\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{t}(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{6\alpha}{7\tau^{1/7}}\eqqcolon a_{\tau},

(16)

which exhibits a connection to the damping coefficient

This relation plays a crucial role in the proof of

5

Theorem 4
.

Let

\frac{7}{6}\left\lparen 3\sqrt{\alpha R}+\frac{7^{4}MR}{2\cdot 6^{4}\alpha^{3}}\right\rparen T^{-4/7}+\mathrm{O}\left\lparen T^{-11/7}\right\rparen.

Proof.

We begin with the following elementary bound.
For any fixed constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and any
T\geq\varepsilon_{0}

\min_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla f\left\lparen\bar{x}(t)\right\rparen\right\|\leq\min_{\varepsilon_{0}\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla f\left\lparen\bar{x}(t)\right\rparen\right\|\leq\frac{1}{T-\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{\varepsilon_{0}}^{T}\left\|\nabla f\left\lparen\bar{x}(t)\right\rparen\right\|\mathrm{d}t.

(19)

Since
\varepsilon_{0}
is independent of

Therefore, it suffices to prove that

\int_{\varepsilon_{0}}^{T}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x}(t))\right\|\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{O}\left\lparen T^{3/7}\right\rparen.

(20)

We analyze this quantity using a continuous-time version of PEP:

\displaystyle\maximize_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,\\
x\in C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\phi\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R})\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\varepsilon_{0}}^{T}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x}(t))\right\|\mathrm{d}t,

(21)

\displaystyle\subjectto

\displaystyle\quad f\text{ is twice differentiable and }\nabla^{2}f\text{ is }M\text{-Lipschitz continuous},

(22)

\displaystyle\quad\phi(t)=f\left\lparen x(t)\right\rparen-f(x(T)),

(23)

\displaystyle\quad x\text{ is a solution of the ODE }\eqref{ODE},

(24)

\displaystyle\quad\phi(0)\leq R,\quad\dot{x}(0)=0.

\framebox{PEP}\leq\framebox{relaxed problem}\leq\framebox{Lagrange dual problem}\leq\framebox{value at a feasible dual solution}.

by an integral involving
\|\dot{x}(t)\|
, which is much easier to treat.
This conversion is straightforward if one ignores the tightness of the coefficients of

\|\dot{x}(t)\|
and
\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}
.
However, establishing the desired convergence rate requires sufficiently tight estimates of these coefficients.
Since this estimate involves a technical calculation, the proof is deferred to
Section

7
.

The second relaxation replaces the two constraints

\phi(t)=f(x(t))-f(x(T))
and ODE (
3
)
with alternative constraints that eliminate
f
from the optimization variables.
We focus on the time evolution of
\phi(t)
, into which the information from ODE (
3
)
can be incorporated as follows:

\dot{\phi}(t)=\langle{\nabla f\left\lparen x(t)\right\rparen},{\dot{x}(t)}\rangle=-\left\langle{\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{6}{7}\frac{\alpha}{t^{1/7}}\dot{x}(t)},{\dot{x}(t)}\right\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}-\frac{6\alpha}{7t^{1/7}}\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}.

(28)

We retain this equation as a new constraint, together with the terminal condition

\phi(T)=0
.

The resulting relaxed PEP is

\displaystyle\maximize_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{\,{C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\ |\ {\dot{x}(0)=0}\,\},\\
\phi\in\{\,{C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}\ |\ {\phi(T)=0}\,\}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\quad\int_{{\varepsilon_{0}}/2}^{T}\left\lparen\frac{3\alpha}{t^{1/7}}\|\dot{x}(t)\|+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}M}{6^{4}\alpha^{2}}t^{1/7}T^{1/7}\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}\right\rparen\mathrm{d}t

(29)

\displaystyle\subjectto

\displaystyle\quad\dot{\phi}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{6\alpha}{7t^{1/7}}\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}=0,

(30)

\displaystyle\quad\phi(0)\leq R.

(31)

Step 2: Dual problem

We next derive the Lagrange dual problem to obtain a further upper bound by weak duality.
Let the dual variables be
\lambda\in C^{1}([0,T])
and
\eta\geq 0
.
The Lagrange function
\mathcal{L}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,\phi;\lambda,\eta)

\displaystyle=(\lambda(0)-\eta)\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\lambda}(t)\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t

(32)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\lambda(T)\|\dot{x}(T)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\lambda}(t)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{{\varepsilon_{0}}/2}\frac{6\alpha}{7t^{1/7}}\lambda(t)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

(33)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{{\varepsilon_{0}}/2}^{T}\left[\left\lparen\frac{6\alpha}{7t^{1/7}}\lambda(t)-\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}M}{6^{4}\alpha^{2}}t^{1/7}T^{1/7}\right\rparen\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}-\frac{3\alpha}{t^{1/7}}\|\dot{x}(t)\|\right]\mathrm{d}t+\eta R.

(34)

Thus, the dual problem reads

\minimize_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in C^{1}([0,T]),\\
\eta\geq 0\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{\,{C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\ |\ {\dot{x}(0)=0}\,\},\\
\phi\in\{\,{C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}\ |\ {\phi(T)=0}\,\}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}(x,\phi;\lambda,\eta).

(35)

For
\max_{x,\phi}\mathcal{L}
to be finite, the first term requires

\eta=\lambda(0)
, the second term requires
\lambda(t)
to be constant,
and the third term requires
\lambda(T)\geq 0
.
Thus, slightly abusing notation by writing
\lambda=\lambda(0)
,
and discarding the nonpositive third and fifth terms, we obtain the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\lambda\geq 0}

\displaystyle\quad\max_{x}\bigg\{\int_{{\varepsilon_{0}}/2}^{T}\bigg[-\left\lparen\frac{6\alpha}{7t^{1/7}}\lambda-\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}M}{6^{4}\alpha^{2}}t^{1/7}T^{1/7}\right\rparen\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{3\alpha}{t^{1/7}}\|\dot{x}(t)\|\bigg]\mathrm{d}t\bigg\}+\lambda R.

(36)

The maximized functional above depends on
x
only through
\|\dot{x}(t)\|
.
Hence, the maximization over
x
can be reduced to a pointwise maximization over

\|\dot{x}(t)\|
.

Step 3: Feasible solution

Finally, we evaluate the dual objective at the feasible choice

\lambda=\left\lparen C+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}}{6^{4}}\right\rparen\frac{7M}{6\alpha^{3}}T^{3/7},

(37)

where
C>0
is a constant to be optimized later.
This choice balances the maximization term and the
\lambda R
term in (
36
).
With this choice of
\lambda
, and using
t\leq T
in the integral, the coefficient of

\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\left\lparen\left\lparen C+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}}{6^{4}}\right\rparen\frac{M}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{T^{3/7}}{t^{1/7}}-\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}M}{6^{4}\alpha^{2}}t^{1/7}T^{1/7}\right\rparen

(38)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{M}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{T^{3/7}}{t^{1/7}}\left\lparen C+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}}{6^{4}}\left\lparen 1-\frac{t^{2/7}}{T^{2/7}}\right\rparen\right\rparen\leq-\frac{CM}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{T^{3/7}}{t^{1/7}}.

(39)

Therefore, the objective function in (
36
) is bounded above by

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon_{0}/2}^{T}\max_{u\geq 0}\bigg\{-\frac{CM}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{T^{3/7}}{t^{1/7}}u^{2}+\frac{3\alpha}{t^{1/7}}u\bigg\}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{7M}{6\alpha^{3}}\left\lparen C+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}}{6^{4}}\right\rparen T^{3/7}R

(40)

\displaystyle=\int_{\varepsilon_{0}/2}^{T}\frac{\displaystyle\left\lparen\frac{3\alpha}{t^{1/7}}\right\rparen^{2}}{\displaystyle 4\frac{CM}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{T^{3/7}}{t^{1/7}}}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{7M}{6\alpha^{3}}\left\lparen C+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}}{6^{4}}\right\rparen T^{3/7}R

(41)

\displaystyle=\int_{\varepsilon_{0}/2}^{T}\frac{3^{2}\alpha^{4}}{4CMT^{3/7}}\frac{1}{t^{1/7}}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{7M}{6\alpha^{3}}\left\lparen C+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}}{6^{4}}\right\rparen T^{3/7}R

(42)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7}{6}\left\lparen\frac{3^{2}\alpha^{4}}{4CM}+\frac{M}{\alpha^{3}}\left\lparen C+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}}{6^{4}}\right\rparen R\right\rparen T^{3/7}.

(43)

Choosing
C
to minimize the right-hand side gives the upper bound

\frac{7}{6}\left\lparen 3\sqrt{\alpha R}+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}MR}{6^{4}\alpha^{3}}\right\rparen T^{3/7}

(44)

for (
21
).

Combining this estimate with the initial averaging argument and using the fact that

\varepsilon_{0}
is independent of
T
, we obtain

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x}(t))\right\|\leq\frac{1}{T-\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{\varepsilon_{0}}^{T}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x}(t))\right\|\mathrm{d}t

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7}{6}\left\lparen 3\sqrt{\alpha R}+\frac{2\cdot 7^{4}MR}{6^{4}\alpha^{3}}\right\rparen T^{-4/7}+\mathrm{O}\left\lparen T^{-11/7}\right\rparen.

(46)

This completes the proof.
∎

3
Discretization with velocity control

In this section, we first introduce an update scheme and observe that it can be regarded as a discretization of ODE (
3
) in
Section

3.1
.
We then describe the proposed algorithms based on this discretization with a velocity-control strategy in
Section

3.2
.
This discretization allows us to conduct a convergence analysis in parallel with the continuous-time setting.
We first present a differentiable algorithm and then introduce a parameter-free algorithm by incorporating an adaptation process.

3.1
Discretization

We now discretize the ODE (
3
) and derive optimization algorithms.
Hereafter, let
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denote the iteration counter, which we refer to as time.
The notation for the discrete-time weight
w_{t}(\tau)
and the damping coefficient
a_{\tau}
, defined below, overrides that of their continuous-time counterparts.
Define
v_{t}\coloneqq x_{t}-x_{t-1}
.
Starting from an initial point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we discretize the ODE as

(2-\hat{r}_{t+1}+a_{t+1})\frac{v_{t+1}}{h_{t+1}^{2}}=\hat{r}_{t}\frac{2+a_{t+1}}{2+a_{t}}\frac{v_{t}}{h_{t+1}^{2}}-\nabla f(x_{t}),\qquad v_{1}=0,

(47)

where
\hat{r}_{t}\in[0,1]
and the step size
h_{t}>0
are defined in the algorithms below.

Averaging

For iteration
t\geq 2
, we define the weight
w_{t}(\tau)
for
t_{0}\leq\tau<t
and the output
\bar{x}_{t}
by

\displaystyle w_{t}(\tau)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\tau^{6/7}}}{\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha s^{6/7}}},\qquad\bar{x}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{\tau=t_{0}}^{t-1}w_{t}(\tau)x_{\tau},

(48)

where
t_{0}
is defined for
t\geq 2
by

t_{0}\coloneqq 2^{i-1}\quad\text{if}\quad 2^{i}\leq t<2^{i+1}\quad(i=1,2,\ldots).

(49)

Although the definition of
\bar{x}_{t}
appears to require the history

\{x_{\tau}\}_{t_{0}\leq\tau<t}
, the choice of
t_{0}
enables us to compute

\bar{x}_{t}
recursively without storing the entire history, as detailed in
Section

9
.
The averaging window
[t_{0},t-1]
is connected to the continuous-time counterpart
[t/2,t]
in the sense that, for
t\geq 2
, it holds that
t/4<t_{0}\leq t/2
.

Damping coefficient

The damping coefficient is defined as

\displaystyle a_{\tau}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{w_{t}(\tau-1)}\frac{w_{t}(\tau)-w_{t}(\tau-1)}{1}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\tau^{6/7}}-\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(\tau-1)^{6/7}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(\tau-1)^{6/7}}}.

(50)

This definition is connected to the continuous-time damping coefficient through (
15
).

The next lemma shows that
a_{\tau}
approximates the damping coefficient of ODE (
3
).

Lemma 6
.

It holds that

a_{\tau}\geq\frac{6\alpha}{7\tau^{1/7}},\quad\text{and}\quad a_{\tau}=\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\tau^{-1/7}\right\rparen.

(51)

Proof.

Since
(1-x)^{6/7}\leq 1-\frac{6}{7}x
holds for
x\leq 1
,

a_{\tau}=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(\tau^{6/7}-(\tau-1)^{6/7})}-1\geq\alpha(\tau^{6/7}-(\tau-1)^{6/7})=\alpha\tau^{6/7}\left\lparen 1-\left\lparen 1-\frac{1}{\tau}\right\rparen^{6/7}\right\rparen\geq\frac{6\alpha}{7\tau^{1/7}}.

(52)

These inequalities are asymptotically tight, and hence
a_{\tau}=\mathrm{O}\left\lparen\tau^{-1/7}\right\rparen
.
∎

Correspondence to ODE

We observe that scheme (
47
) can be regarded as a discretization of (
3
) when
\hat{r}_{t}=1
.
Consider a fixed step size
h_{t}=h
.
For large
t
, we use the approximations

a_{t}\simeq\frac{6\alpha}{7t^{1/7}}
and

\frac{2+a_{t+1}}{2+a_{t}}\simeq 1
.
Then, scheme (
47
) reads

\frac{x_{t}-2x_{t-1}+x_{t-2}}{h^{2}}+\frac{6}{7}\frac{\alpha h^{-6/7}}{(th)^{1/7}}\frac{x_{t}-x_{t-1}}{h}+\nabla f(x_{t-1})=0.

(53)

Here,
(x_{t}-x_{t-1})/{h}
and

(x_{t}-2x_{t-1}+x_{t-2})/{h^{2}}
can be interpreted as discretizations of

\dot{x}(th)
and
\ddot{x}(th)
, respectively.
Thus, this scheme can be regarded as a discretization of ODE (
3
), except for the discrepancy between
\alpha
in ODE (
3
) and
\alpha h^{-6/7}
in the above equation.

This discrepancy arises because the definition of
a_{t}
does not incorporate the step size
h
.
If we instead defined

a_{t}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(th)^{6/7}}-\mathrm{e}^{\alpha((t-1)h)^{6/7}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha((t-1)h)^{6/7}}},

(54)

then we would have the approximation

, resolving the discrepancy.
Although this modified definition of
a_{t}
involving
h
is more natural than (
50

varies over iterations.
In the subsequent analysis, we estimate quantities involving
a_{t}
and
w_{t}
in a manner parallel to the continuous-time analysis.
Such estimates would become difficult if the varying step sizes were incorporated into

and
w_{t}
.

6
Discussions

Optimal damping for each function class

For continuous-time models of the form

\ddot{x}+\beta(t)\dot{x}+\nabla f(x)=0,

the optimal damping coefficient
\beta
depends on the convexity of the objective function.
For strongly convex and convex functions, the optimal damping is
\beta(t)=\Theta(1)
and
\beta(t)=\Theta(1/t)
, respectively. In this work, we revealed that for nonconvex functions, the optimal damping is
\beta(t)=\Theta(1/t^{1/7})
. These decay rates are not monotone with respect to the strength of convexity, which is somewhat surprising.

For
\mu
-strongly convex and convex functions, the Information-Theoretic Exact Method
56
achieves the optimal convergence rates for both function classes, and the damping coefficient of its corresponding ODE interpolates between
\Theta(1)
and
\Theta(1/t)
through the parameter
\mu
.
It would be an interesting direction for future research to develop an optimal method that simultaneously covers strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex functions.

Last iterate vs. best iterate

Algorithm

1
is differentiable with respect to the last iterate, whereas the convergence rate in
Theorem

7
is stated in terms of the best iterate or an average of gradients.
This gap should be resolved, although deriving last-iterate convergence rates for nonconvex functions is difficult even for gradient descent.

Optimal choice of
\alpha
and
r

The proposed algorithms have two hyperparameters,
\alpha
and
r
.
In the numerical experiments, we observed that
\alpha=0.1
and
r=0.5
work well.
By minimizing the constant factors in the convergence rates in
Theorem

7
and
Theorem

8
, one can obtain reasonable parameter choices, although these constants are not tight.
How to choose these parameters optimally remains to be investigated.

Cost of adapting
L

In the adaptive algorithm
Algorithm

2
, the Lipschitz constant
L
is estimated so that the inequality used in the proof is guaranteed for all possible next points
x_{t}^{1}
,
x_{t}^{r}
, and
x_{t}^{0}
.
This requires three times as many evaluations of
f
as the estimation procedure in adaptive gradient descent.
Developing a more efficient adaptation process is necessary to improve the practical performance of the algorithm.

(237)

and

where we applied the inequalities

\displaystyle\quad\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}(t-1)^{1/7}(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(t-1)^{6/7}}-\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(t-1)^{6/7}/2}).

(241)

Lemma 24

(Corresponding to

)

Also, by

9
Computing the output

The proposed algorithms can be implemented directly from the definition of
\bar{x}_{t}
in (
48
), but their implementation can be made more memory-efficient by computing
\bar{x}_{t}
recursively.
For
t\geq 2
, the algorithms maintain

X_{t}=\alpha t^{-1/7}\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t^{6/7}}\sum_{\tau=t_{0}}^{t-1}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\tau^{6/7}}x_{\tau},\quad\text{and}\quad A_{t}=\alpha t^{-1/7}\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t^{6/7}}\sum_{\tau=t_{0}}^{t-1}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\tau^{6/7}},

(304)

so that the output can be computed as

\bar{x}_{t}=X_{t}/A_{t}
.

Note that
\bar{x}_{t}
does not involve
x_{t}
, which is determined only at iteration
t+1
in
Algorithm

2
.
The factor
\alpha t^{-1/7}\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t^{6/7}}
is introduced to keep
X_{t}
and
A_{t}
bounded, which is essential for practical implementation.
This factor is motivated by
Lemma
