NATPS: Nonadiabatic Transition Path Sampling Using Time-Reversible MASH Dynamics
Xiran Yang, Madlen Maria Reiner, Brigitta Bachmair, Leticia González, Johannes C. B. Dietschreit, Christoph Dellago

Abstract
Rare nonadiabatic events play a central role in photochemistry but remain difficult to simulate because excited-state dynamics is computationally demanding and often stochastic. Here we introduce a deterministic and time-reversible implementation of nonadiabatic dynamics that enables the application of transition path sampling (TPS) to excited-state processes. Our approach builds on the Mapping Approach to Surface Hopping (MASH) and establishes the conditions required for path ensemble sampling, in particular time reversibility and detailed balance. Combining this dynamics with the TPS framework yields a new method, termed nonadiabatic transition path sampling (NATPS). Using a model system of electronically coupled potential energy surfaces, we demonstrate that NATPS efficiently generates ensembles of reactive trajectories and provides mechanistic insight into nonadiabatic pathways. Compared with brute-force trajectory simulations and forward-flux sampling approaches, NATPS substantially reduces the computational effort required to obtain reactive trajectories.

Introduction

Nonadiabatic events such as photoinduced ring opening, electronically driven isomerization, or internal conversion at conical intersections play a central role in modern photochemistry and molecular photophysics
[
9
,
30
]
.
Although the nonadiabatic transitions themselves typically occur on femtosecond timescales
[
10
]
, the overall kinetics of excited-state processes may span many orders of magnitude.
Access to conical intersections or other electronically coupled regions can require thermally activated motion, diffusion through complex phase-space landscapes, or escape from long-lived excited-state minima
[
15
,
10
]
.
Examples of such slow photoinduced processes include electron transfer
[
28
]
, (pre)dissociation
[
21
,
36
]
, photoisomerization
[
19
,
23
,
24
]
, and intersystem crossing
[
32
]
.
Consequently, many photochemical reactions constitute kinetically rare events despite their ultrafast microscopic dynamics.

At the same time, the mixed quantum-classical simulation methods commonly used to study these processes
in silico
are computationally demanding.
This makes it highly desirable to employ enhanced-sampling techniques capable of extracting statistically meaningful information from a limited number of trajectory time steps.

Trajectory-based enhanced sampling methods such as transition path sampling (TPS)
[
11
,
6
,
12
,
13
]
, transition interface sampling (TIS)
[
42
,
31
,
41
]
, and forward flux sampling (FFS)
[
4
,
2
,
1
,
3
]
provide powerful tools for the mechanistic characterization of rare events.
However, the application of such path-ensemble methods to electronically nonadiabatic dynamics has long remained challenging.
Path sampling techniques that generate both forward and backward trajectories require the underlying dynamics to be time reversible, to conserve phase-space volume, and to satisfy detailed balance in order to define a consistent trajectory probability measure.
Standard mixed quantum-classical approaches generally do not fulfill these conditions
[
38
]
.

In particular, fewest-switches surface hopping (FSSH)
[
39
]
introduces stochastic switching rules for discrete electronic transitions that break microscopic reversibility and violate detailed balance
[
38
]
.
Nevertheless, progress has recently been made toward rare-event sampling of nonadiabatic dynamics.
The earliest attempt to reconcile the apparent time irreversibility of surface hopping with transition path sampling was introduced by Sherman and Corcelli
[
37
]
, who generated backward segments using a surrogate, history-independent hopping scheme and recovered the correct path probability by reweighting through forward retracing of the nuclear trajectory to reconstruct the true fewest-switches probabilities.
In addition, Reiner
et al.

[
34
]
developed a nonadiabatic extension of forward flux sampling (NAFFS), which is feasible because FFS only requires forward trajectory propagation and therefore places less stringent constraints on the underlying dynamics.
More generally, recent work has focused on identifying nonadiabatic dynamics approaches that preserve the statistical-mechanical structure required for path sampling.

In this contribution, we focus on the Mapping Approach to Surface Hopping (MASH)
[
27
]
.
MASH provides a framework for nonadiabatic dynamics that fulfills detailed balance
[
5
]
and time reversibility
[
18
]
.
By representing the electronic degrees of freedom through a continuous spin vector coupled to classical nuclei, MASH yields smooth, deterministic equations of motion for the combined nuclear–electronic phase space.
In this work, we develop a transition path sampling framework for nonadiabatic dynamics based on MASH.

2.1
Mapping Approach to Surface Hopping (MASH)

Assuming the system can be represented by two coupled electronic states with the energy function

E(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\vec{S})=\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{N})^{\top}
are the generalized nuclear coordinates and

\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{N})^{\top}
the conjugate nuclear momenta for a system with
N
degrees of freedom.

\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})
denotes the average, state-independent potential, while

V_{z}(\mathbf{q})
represents the difference between the two electronic potential energy surfaces (PESs) (see Appendix
A
).

S_{z}
is the
z
-component of the auxiliary spin vector
\vec{S}=(S_{x},S_{y},S_{z})^{\top}
.
[
35
,
18
]

Using the associated Fokker-Planck equation (Appendix
A.4
), it can be shown that the canonical Boltzmann distribution

\rho(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\vec{S})\propto\exp\!\left[-\beta\!\left(\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})\right)\right]\,\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)

(2)

is stationary under the MASH dynamics.
Here
\beta=1/k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
denotes the Boltzmann constant and
T
the absolute temperature.
The projection of the full Boltzmann distribution onto the
z
-axis of the spin vector gives the marginal probability density along
S_{z}
, which is uniform for each hemisphere (Appendix
A.3
)
[
35
]
.
The free energy difference between the upper and lower electronic states can then be obtained by direct integration of the respective regions of the marginal distribution
[
14
]
.
These central equations have previously appeared
[
5
]
, however, to our knowledge, the detailed and mathematically rigorous derivation we provide in Appendix
A
has been missing.

For the application of MASH within transition path sampling (TPS)
[
11
,
7
]
, the underlying dynamics must additionally satisfy time reversibility.
In Appendix
C
we show that both the nuclear degrees of freedom and the extended spin vector conserve phase-space volume under the MASH equations of motion.

Under time reversal, the phase-space variables transform according to their parity.
For the nuclear coordinates and momenta, this corresponds to the usual classical transformation, positions remain unchanged and momenta invert their sign.
The components of the auxiliary spin vector exhibit a different symmetry.
The
x
- and
z
-components behave as position-like variables, while the
y
-component changes sign under time reversal (see Appendix
B
for a detailed derivation).
Using these parity relations, it can be shown that the MASH dynamics obey detailed balance (Appendix
D
).

In practice, most nonadiabatic dynamics codes propagate the coefficients
c_{i}
of the electronic wavefunction,

\ket{\Psi(t)}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}},

(3)

rather than the spin vector directly.
For a two-state system, the coefficients are related to the spin-vector components through the mapping
[
22
]

S_{x}=2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right]\ ,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad S_{y}=2\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right]\ ,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad S_{z}=\lvert c_{+}\rvert^{2}-\lvert c_{-}\rvert^{2}\ ,

(4)

where
c_{+}
and
c_{-}
denote the coefficients of the upper and lower electronic states, respectively.
It can be shown that propagations of coefficients
\mathbf{c}
and spin-vector
\vec{S}
are equivalent (Appendix
E
and
F
).

Applying the time-reversal transformation of the spin-vector components to these relations leads to the corresponding symmetry for the electronic coefficients (see Appendix
G
),

c_{i}\rightarrow c_{i}^{*}.

(5)

2.2
Transition Path Sampling

Transition path sampling provides a general framework for sampling rare reactive trajectories without prior knowledge of the reaction coordinate.
[
6
,
12
,
13
,
16
]

The idea of sampling directly in path space rather than configuration space was first introduced by Dellago and co-workers
[
11
]
.
For a trajectory
X
of length
L
, the path probability distribution
P
is defined as
[
20
]

P[X(L)]:=\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0})\prod_{i=0}^{L-1}P(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\to\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i+1}),

(6)

where
\mathbf{\Gamma}=(\mathbf{q}^{\top},\mathbf{p}^{\top},S_{x},S_{y},S_{z})^{\top}
a point in the extended phase space,
\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0})
is the equilibrium probability of the initial phase-space point
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}
, and
P(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\to\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i+1})
is the short-time transition probabilities determined by the underlying dynamical propagator.
The conditional path probability for trajectories that start in basin
A
and end in basin
B
is then given by

P_{AB}[X(L)]=Z_{AB}^{-1}H_{AB}[X(L)]P[X(L)]\ ,

(7)

where
H_{AB}[X(L)]
is only unity for a transition path
X
, i.e., if the first configuration of the trajectory lies in basin
A
and the last in basin
B
, while no other configurations are in
A
or
B
, and zero otherwise
[
20
]
.

Z_{AB}
is the normalization factor.
Trajectories in this restricted path ensemble are sampled using a Monte Carlo (MC) random walk in path space
[
8
]
.
Given an existing path
X^{(o)}
, a new path
X^{(n)}
is proposed and accepted according to the Metropolis-Hastings criterion.
If (i) the dynamical propagator satisfies microscopic reversibility and (ii) the path-generation procedure obeys detailed balance, the acceptance probability is

P_{\text{acc}}(X^{(o)}\to X^{(n)})=h_{A}(\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{0})\,h_{B}(\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{L_{n}})\,\text{min}\left\{1,\frac{L_{o}}{L_{n}}\right\},

(8)

where
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{0}
and
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{L_{n}}
denote the first and last configurations of the proposed trajectory.

New trajectories are generated using a shooting algorithm.
At each MC step, a configuration is randomly selected from the current trajectory and perturbed.
The perturbation typically involves modifying the velocity associated with the selected configuration.
The specific velocity perturbation determines the statistical ensemble sampled by the path ensemble. For instance, sampling the microcanonical (NVE) ensemble requires that the kinetic energy of the selected configuration remains unchanged.
In one-dimensional systems, this restriction limits the velocity modification to a mere sign change, which significantly reduces the diversity of sampled trajectories.
In contrast, sampling the canonical (NVT) ensemble allows the velocity to fluctuate according to the Maxwell-Boltzmann (MB) distribution at a given temperature.

To ensure detailed balance of the path generation, new velocities
v^{\prime}
are obtained using the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
[
40
]
perturbation scheme,

v^{\prime}=\alpha v+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\,\Delta v,

(9)

where
v
is the current velocity,
\Delta v
is a random variable drawn from the MB velocity distribution at temperature
T
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a predefined mixing parameter.
In this work, we fix
\alpha
at
0.9
.

After selecting and perturbing a configuration
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\tau}
, the system is propagated both forward and backward in time from this configuration to generate a new trajectory.
The resulting path is accepted or rejected according to Eq. (
8
).
If the proposed path is rejected, the previous trajectory is retained in the ensemble to maintain the correct path probability distribution.
This shooting procedure is repeated until the desired number of trajectories has been collected.

2.3
Local Diabatization

Within the MASH framework, the nuclear degrees of freedom are propagated using the velocity-Verlet algorithm.
The electronic coefficients are evolved using the local diabatization (LD) scheme
[
33
]
, after which they are mapped onto the components of the spin vector
\vec{S}
.
The propagation of the electronic coefficient vector is given by

\mathbf{c}(t+\Delta t)=\mathbf{T}^{-1}e^{-\frac{i}{2\hbar}(\mathbf{E}(t)+\tilde{\mathbf{H}}(t+\Delta t))\Delta t}\mathbf{c}(t)\ ,

(10)

where
\mathbf{T}
is the Löwdin-orthogonalized overlap matrix between the adiabatic basis at times
t
and
t+\Delta t
:

\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\qquad\mathrm{with}\qquad[\mathbf{S}]_{ij}=\braket{\psi_{i}(t)|\psi_{j}(t+\Delta t)}\ ,

(11)

with
\psi_{i}(t)
denoting the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}(q(t))
.
The locally diabatic Hamiltonian is constructed from the diagonal adiabatic energy matrix
\mathbf{E}(t+\Delta t)
according to

\tilde{\mathbf{H}}(t+\Delta t)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{E}(t+\Delta t)\mathbf{T}^{-1}.

(12)

In the absence of surface hops, the velocity-Verlet integrator guarantees time-reversible nuclear dynamics.
However, in conventional surface-hopping implementations, a change of the electronic state is only detected after completion of a nuclear time step.
As a result, the potential energy surface governing the nuclear propagation during a hopping event depends on the direction of time (forward vs. backward).
This asymmetry breaks time-reversal symmetry, even though the underlying MASH equations of motion are microscopically reversible.

To resolve this asymmetry, we adopt the piecewise continuous approach developed by Geuther and Richardson
[
18
]
.
In this approach, potential hopping events are detected before the nuclear propagation step is completed.
A line-search algorithm is used to determine the precise time at which the hop occurs.
The system is then propagated with a reduced time step up to the hopping event, where the velocity rescaling or reflection is performed, followed by the propagation for the remainder of the original time step.
This approach ensures that the nuclei traverse the correct potential energy surfaces regardless of time direction, thus restoring full time-reversibility.
This method assumes that at most one hop occurs per time step.
That this variable time step implementation is crucial for time-reversibility is shown with numerical experiments in Appendix
H
.

Consecutive paths in the ensemble exhibit significant correlation, as each new trajectory is generated from a modification of a previous one.

This particular shape has two root causes.
First, the transition time is asymmetrically bounded.
There exists a minimum time imposed by the distance between the two basins and the kinetic energy of the system, but there is no strict upper bound except for the maximum path length used in the simulation.
Second, there are two mechanistically different pathways, adiabatic and nonadiabatic, present in the TPS ensemble.
The nonadiabatic paths are typically slower, as they are trapped in the excited state for some time before connecting the two basins, which are strictly located in the ground state.
In contrast, at the lower temperature of

Finally, TPS simulations also provide direct mechanistic insight into the location of nonadiabatic transitions.

In the low temperature range (up to 2000 K), the mean transition time decreases rapidly as
T
increases, reflecting the larger kinetic energy available for barrier crossing.
However, this trend weakens significantly at higher temperatures.
Simultaneously, the variance of the transition time increases and closely follows the growth in the mean number of hops.
To understand this behavior, we separate trajectories into purely adiabatic paths and paths involving surface hops (Fig.
4
e).

Adiabatic trajectories shorten steadily with temperature, as expected for increasingly ballistic motion.
In contrast, nonadiabatic trajectories show only a weak temperature dependence.
This behavior originates from excited-state trapping: once a trajectory hops to the excited state, the time required to return to the ground state is primarily governed by the electronic coupling rather than the nuclear momentum.
As a result, the excited-state lifetime becomes the dominant contribution to the total transition time, producing an effective kinetic plateau.

Spatial structure of the path ensemble.

The influence of temperature is also visible in the spatial statistics of the TPS ensemble.
Figure

At low temperatures, hops occur predominantly near the avoided crossing where the energy gap is smallest.
As the temperature increases, larger nuclear momenta allow hops farther away from the crossing seam, leading to a gradual broadening of the hopping distribution.
This trend is also reflected in the state-resolved path densities shown in Fig.
5

Higher temperatures promote trajectories that explore larger regions of the excited-state surface before returning to the ground state.

Effect of electronic coupling.

We next examine the influence of the electronic coupling
V_{c}
, fixing the temperature at
10,000
K to ensure sufficient sampling of nonadiabatic paths.
Reducing
V_{c}
lowers the adiabatic energy gap and increases the nonadiabatic coupling, thereby promoting surface hops.
Conversely, increasing
V_{c}
suppresses nonadiabatic transitions.
Fig.
4

V_{c}
.

While increasing temperature and decreasing
V_{c}
both promote hopping events, they do so through fundamentally different mechanisms.
Temperature increases the available nuclear kinetic energy, whereas the coupling strength directly controls the electronic transition probability.
This distinction is reflected in the hopping distributions shown in Fig.
5

The temperature-induced broadening of the hopping distribution is gradual, whereas variations in
V_{c}
lead to much stronger changes because the spatial localization of the nonadiabatic coupling depends directly on the electronic Hamiltonian.
Since the nonadiabatic coupling magnitude is sharply peaked near the crossing seam for small
V_{c}
(for details see Appendix
I
), we obtain highly localized hopping regions, while larger couplings smear out the hopping distribution over a wider spatial range.

4
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a nonadiabatic transition path sampling framework (NATPS) based on the mapping approach to surface hopping (MASH).
By implementing a time-reversible propagation scheme for the MASH dynamics, we obtain a deterministic nonadiabatic dynamics method that satisfies detailed balance and is therefore compatible with trajectory-based path sampling algorithms.
This allows the application of transition path sampling methods that rely on backward propagation of partial trajectories, such as transition interface sampling, which are not generally accessible with conventional stochastic surface-hopping dynamics.

We demonstrated the NATPS approach using a one-dimensional model of two coupled diabatic potentials.
The resulting path ensembles reproduce the expected mechanistic features of the system, including the distribution of hopping geometries near the avoided crossing and the presence of distinct adiabatic and nonadiabatic reaction pathways.
Analysis of the path statistics further shows that NATPS efficiently generates statistically independent reactive trajectories and provides direct access to mechanistic observables.
In addition, we find that variations in temperature primarily affect the nuclear kinetic energy and thereby the frequency of surface hops, while changes in the electronic coupling modify the spatial localization and probability of nonadiabatic transitions.

Overall, NATPS offers a practical framework for studying rare nonadiabatic transitions using path sampling techniques.
Because the method is based on deterministic dynamics that preserves the statistical-mechanical structure required for trajectory sampling, it provides a natural route toward applying advanced path ensemble methods to electronically excited-state processes in more complex molecular systems.
Future work will focus on the implementation in nonadiabatic dynamics packages such as SHARC
[
25
,
26
]
and on applying NATPS to multidimensional molecular systems and combining it with interface-based sampling techniques
[
42
,
41
]
for the calculation of nonadiabatic rate constants.

Appendix A
Invariant Distribution of MASH

To find the stationary distribution generated by the Mapping Approach to Surface Hopping (MASH), we consider a two-state system with the energy function
E
,

E(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\vec{S})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})

(A-1)

with
2N+3
degrees of freedom, representing the positions
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{N})^{\top}
, conjugated momenta
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{N})^{\top}
, masses
m_{i}
, and the spin vector
\vec{S}
,

\vec{S}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{x}\\
S_{y}\\
S_{z}\end{pmatrix}

The sign of
S_{z}
indicates whether the spin vector
\vec{S}
is in the northern
(+)
or southern
(-)
hemisphere of the Bloch sphere.
The hemispheres correspond to the upper and lower potential energy surfaces (PESs),
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
, which are two different electronic states of the system, e.g. ground and excited state.

The state–independent potential
\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})
is taken to be the average of the two states

\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{V_{-}(\mathbf{q})+V_{+}(\mathbf{q})}{2}

(A-2)

and the difference-potential
V_{z}(\mathbf{q})
is

V_{z}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{V_{+}(\mathbf{q})-V_{-}(\mathbf{q})}{2}

(A-3)

hence, the potential energy gap between upper and lower PESs corresponds to
2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})
.
Using Eq. (
A-2
) and Eq. (
A-3
) the ground and excited state potentials can be written as

\displaystyle V_{-}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})-V_{z}(\mathbf{q})

(A-4)

\displaystyle V_{+}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})

(A-5)

A.1
Equations of motion in phase space

The equations of motion in phase space (i.e., for positions
q_{i}
and conjugated momenta
p_{i}
) in the MASH approach are given as

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}

(A-6)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bar{F}_{i}(\mathbf{q})+F^{\mathrm{MASH}}_{i}(\mathbf{q},\vec{S})

(A-7)

with mass
m
, where
\bar{F}
is the average force induced by the state-independent potential
\bar{V}
,

\bar{F}_{i}(\mathbf{q})=-\frac{\partial\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial q_{i}}

(A-8)

and
F^{\mathrm{MASH}}
(cf. Eq. (8b) in
[
5
]
) includes the derivative of the difference-potential
V_{z}
as well as a nonadiabatic coupling term

F^{\mathrm{MASH}}_{i}(\mathbf{q},\vec{S})=-\frac{\partial V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial q_{i}}\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})+4V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\,S_{x}\,\delta(S_{z})

(A-9)

with
d_{i}(\mathbf{q})
being the nonadiabatic coupling vector.
The delta function
\delta(S_{z})
activates the last term on the right hand side when the spin vector
\vec{S}
crosses the equator of the Bloch sphere, i.e., the hemisphere changes from northern to southern or vice versa.

If the system is coupled to a heat bath, the equations of motion become

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}

(A-10)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial q_{i}}-\frac{\partial V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial q_{i}}\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})+4V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,d_{i}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\,\delta(S_{z})-\frac{\gamma}{m_{i}}p_{i}+\mathcal{F}_{i}(t)

(A-11)

where a friction term with friction constant
\gamma
and random force
\mathcal{F}_{i}(t)
are introduced.
The random force fulfills the fluctuation dissipation theorem, such that

\langle\mathcal{F}_{i}(t)\,\mathcal{F}_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2\gamma k_{B}T\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(A-12)

for times
t
and
t^{\prime}
with Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
, where
T
is the absolute temperature of the bath.

A.2
Equations of motion for the spin vector

The equations of motion for the spin vector
\vec{S}
are

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{x}

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\,\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\right)S_{z}-2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,S_{y}

(A-13)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{y}

\displaystyle=2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,S_{x}

(A-14)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{z}

\displaystyle=-2\left(\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\,\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\right)S_{x}

(A-15)

They can be re-written in vector form as

\dot{\vec{S}}=\vec{H}\times\vec{S},\qquad\vec{H}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
2\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\\
2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\end{pmatrix}

(A-16)

with the cross product structure

\vec{H}\times\vec{S}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{x}\\
H_{y}\\
H_{z}\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}S_{x}\\
S_{y}\\
S_{z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{y}S_{z}-H_{z}S_{y}\\
H_{z}S_{x}-H_{x}S_{z}\\
H_{x}S_{y}-H_{y}S_{x}\end{pmatrix}

(A-17)

Here
\vec{H}
can be interpreted as an effective magnetic field.
Since
\dot{\vec{S}}\perp\vec{S}
, the magnitude of
\vec{S}
is conserved,

\frac{d}{dt}|\vec{S}|=0

Notice that the heat bath does not couple to the virtual spin degrees of freedom.
We can interpret the motion of the spin vector as a precession around the axis
\vec{H}
, where
H_{y}
describes the nonadiabatic coupling and the energy difference between ground and excited state enters via
V_{z}
(see Eq. (
A-3
)).

A.3
Stationary Distribution

We would like to prove that the stationary distribution
of this dynamics is the distribution

\rho(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\vec{S})\propto\exp\!\left[-\beta\!\left(\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})\right)\right]\,\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)

(A-18)

with the first term stemming from the Boltzmann distribution and the delta function confining the virtual spin vector to the surface of the Bloch sphere.

\beta\equiv 1/k_{B}T
is the inverse temperature.

The distribution on the Bloch sphere is

\displaystyle\rho(\vec{S})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\,\rho(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\vec{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\,\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right]\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\exp\left[-\beta\left(\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})\right)\right]\,\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)

(A-19)

where
Q
is the partition function. We denote the configuration integral for the full two-state system as

\frac{1}{Z}=\frac{1}{Q}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\,\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right]

(A-20)

and introduce
Z_{\alpha}
as the integral over only PES
V_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\{+,-\}
,

\displaystyle Z_{+}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\exp\left[-\beta\left(\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\right)\right]

(A-21)

\displaystyle Z_{-}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\exp\left[-\beta\left(\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})-V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\right)\right]

(A-22)

For the case
S_{z}>0
, i.e., the spin vector on the northern (excited state) hemisphere, we get

\rho(\vec{S})=\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\exp\left[-\beta\left(\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\right)\right]\,\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)=\frac{Z_{+}}{Z}\,\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)

(A-23)

and accordingly, for the case
S_{z}<0
, i.e., the spin vector on the southern (ground state) hemisphere,

\rho(\vec{S})=\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\exp\left[-\beta\left(\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})-V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\right)\right]\,\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)=\frac{Z_{-}}{Z}\,\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)

(A-24)

Eqs. (
A-23
) and (
A-24
) show that the probability density of
\vec{S}
is uniform on each hemisphere.
The relative population of the northern (excited state) and the southern (ground state) hemisphere is

\frac{P_{+}}{P_{-}}=\frac{Z_{+}}{Z_{-}}=\frac{\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\;e^{-\beta V_{+}(\mathbf{q})}}{\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\;e^{-\beta V_{-}(\mathbf{q})}}

(A-25)

by using eqs. (
A-4
) and (
A-5
). Using the difference potential
V_{z}
(see Eq. (
A-3
)), the relative population of the hemispheres is proportional to the exponentiated free energy difference
\Delta F

\frac{P_{+}}{P_{-}}=e^{-\beta\Delta F}\ .

(A-26)

We can rewrite the ratio Eq. (
A-25
) as ensemble averages

\langle\dots\rangle_{\bar{V}}=\frac{\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\;e^{-\beta\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})}\dots}{\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\;e^{-\beta\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})}}

over the state-independent potential
\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})
(cf. Eq. (
A-2

\frac{P_{+}}{P_{-}}=\frac{Z_{+}}{Z_{-}}=\frac{\langle e^{-\beta(V_{+}(\mathbf{q})-\bar{V}(\mathbf{q}))}\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{\langle e^{-\beta(V_{-}(\mathbf{q})-\bar{V}(\mathbf{q}))}\rangle_{\bar{V}}}

(A-27)

Using eqs. (
A-4
) and (
A-5
) this becomes (cf. Eq. (
A-25
))

\frac{P_{+}}{P_{-}}=\frac{\left\langle e^{-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{\left\langle e^{\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}

(A-28)

The probability of being in one particular state is

\displaystyle\frac{P_{+}}{P_{+}+P_{-}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{P_{+}+P_{-}}{P_{+}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\langle e^{-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\rangle_{\bar{V}}+\langle e^{+\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{\langle e^{-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\rangle_{\bar{V}}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\langle 2\cosh(\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q}))\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{\langle e^{-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\rangle_{\bar{V}}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\frac{P_{+}}{P_{+}+P_{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle e^{-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{2\,\left\langle\cosh(\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q}))\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}

(A-29)

and thus

\displaystyle\frac{P_{-}}{P_{+}+P_{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle e^{\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{2\,\left\langle\cosh(\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q}))\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}

(A-30)

Hence, the probability density as a function of only
S_{z}
is

\rho(S_{z})=\frac{\left\langle e^{-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})}\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{2\left\langle\cosh\!\left(\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\right)\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}

(A-31)

Eq. (
A-31
) is the identical result as obtained by Amati
et al.
in Eq. (26b) of
[
5
]
(J. Chem. Phys. 159, 214114 (2023)).

For the free energy difference between the two states, one obtains

\Delta F=-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ln\left(\frac{\left\langle e^{-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}{\left\langle e^{\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})}\right\rangle_{\bar{V}}}\right)

(A-32)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\ g_{0}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\frac{\partial\ \delta(|\vec{S}|-1)}{\partial|\vec{S}|}\frac{\partial|\vec{S}|}{\partial S_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=g_{0}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\frac{\partial\ \delta(|\vec{S}|-1)}{\partial|\vec{S}|}\sum_{\alpha}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\frac{\partial|\vec{S}|}{\partial S_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=g_{0}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\frac{\partial\ \delta(|\vec{S}|-1)}{\partial|\vec{S}|}\frac{1}{|\vec{S}|}\sum_{\alpha}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\ S_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=g_{0}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\frac{\partial\ \delta(|\vec{S}|-1)}{\partial|\vec{S}|}\frac{1}{|\vec{S}|}\left[(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})\cdot\vec{S}\right]

But
(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})\perp\vec{S}
such that

(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})\cdot\vec{S}=0

(A-52)

Thus

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=0

(A-53)

This holds for any distribution
\rho
depending only on
|\vec{S}|
.
Therefore, away from the equator, the RHS = 0, and
\rho
is stationary.

A.4.2
Case “at the equator” (
S_{z}=0
)

At the equator, all previously considered terms cancel, but we get two extra contributions:

\text{RHS}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}4V_{z}(\mathbf{q})d_{i}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\,\delta(S_{z})\,\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_{i}}-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial S_{\alpha}}

(A-54)

From what we have derived in the previous subsection we can write the first term as

-\sum_{i=1}^{N}4V_{z}(\mathbf{q})d_{i}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\,\delta(S_{z})\,\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_{i}}=\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}4V_{z}(\mathbf{q})d_{i}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\,\delta(S_{z})\,\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\rho

Inspecting the derivative of the probability density function with respect to the components of the spin vector, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q}\exp\left[-\beta\left(\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})\right)\right]\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{\alpha}}\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{Q}\exp\left[-\beta\left(\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})\right)\right]\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)\left(-\beta V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})}{\partial S_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q}\exp\left[-\beta\left(\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})\right)\right]\frac{\partial\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)}{\partial|\vec{S}|}\frac{S_{\alpha}}{|\vec{S}|}

\displaystyle-\beta\rho V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})}{\partial S_{z}}\delta_{\alpha z}

The first term gives a contribution
(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})\cdot\vec{S}=0
as before.
Only the second term warrants further inspection:

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=-\beta\rho V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\ (\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{z}\ \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})}{\partial S_{z}}

Using

\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{z}}\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})=2\delta(S_{z}),

and

(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{z}=-2S_{x}\sum_{i}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial S_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-2\beta\rho V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\delta(S_{z})\left(-2S_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\right)

\displaystyle=4\beta\rho V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\right).

Hence we get for the entire RHS:

\displaystyle\text{RHS}=

\displaystyle\ 4\beta\rho V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\left(\sum_{i}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\right)-4\beta\rho V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\left(\sum_{i}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 0

Hence, we have shown that
\rho
(Eq. (
A-18
) or (
A-47
)) is the stationary solution of the Fokker-Planck equation (Eq. (
A-46
)).

B.1
MASH equations of motion

Here we repeat the equations of motion of MASH dynamics for an easier overview:

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\bar{V}}{\partial q_{i}}-\frac{\partial V_{z}}{\partial q_{i}}\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})+4V_{z}(\mathbf{q})d_{i}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\delta(S_{z})-\frac{\gamma}{m_{i}}p_{i}+\mathcal{F}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{S}}

\displaystyle=\vec{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\times\vec{S},\qquad\vec{H}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{S}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}S_{z}-2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{y}\\
2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\\
-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}S_{x}\end{pmatrix}

B.2
Interpretation of time reversal

In configuration space, we want trajectories to retrace when momenta are inverted.
Thus, any variable that appears “like a coordinate” should not change sign under time reversal.

For that to happen,
S_{z}
should retrace its trajectory as well.
Since it appears in the potential energy and in the force, it should not change sign upon time inversion.
It behaves like a "position-like" variable.

The equation of motion for
S_{z}
is

\dot{S}_{z}=-q\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\,S_{x}

So if the momenta are inverted,
\dot{S}_{z}
changes sign (it needs to do that for the trajectory to go back in time) only if
S_{x}
does not change its sign.
Therefore,
S_{x}
is a position-like (sign does not flip) variable as well.

For the right hand side of the equation of motion of
S_{x}
to invert sign:

\dot{S}_{x}=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})p_{i}\,S_{z}-V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{y}

S_{y}
needs to be a momentum-like variable,
i.e.
,
S_{y}
changes sign if we move back in time.

This is consistent with the equation for
S_{y}
:

\dot{S}_{y}=2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}

The RHS does
not
change sign under time reversal, as required for the equation of motion of a momentum-like variable.

B.3
Summary of Time Inversion Transformations

\begin{array}[]{l c r}q_{i}&\rightarrow&q_{i}\\
p_{i}&\rightarrow&-p_{i}\\
\\
S_{x}&\rightarrow&S_{x}\\
S_{y}&\rightarrow&-S_{y}\\
S_{z}&\rightarrow&S_{z}\end{array}

Appendix C
Phase space volume conservation (for
\gamma=0
)

The volume in
(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\vec{S})
–space (or
\mathbf{\Gamma}
-space) is conserved if

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}{\partial q_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\dot{p}_{i}}{\partial p_{i}}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\frac{\partial\dot{S}_{\alpha}}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=0

(A-55)

Since

\dot{q}_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}

(cf. Eq. (
A-10
)) we have

\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}{\partial q_{i}}=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{m}\right)=0

Next, with

\dot{p}_{i}=-\frac{\partial\bar{V}}{\partial q_{i}}-\frac{\partial V_{z}}{\partial q_{i}}\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})

which contains no dependence on
p_{i}
, we get

\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\dot{p}_{i}}{\partial p_{i}}=0

Thus, the dynamics in
(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})
conserve phase-space volume.

Now, we consider the spin part:

\dot{S}_{\alpha}=(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}

such that

\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\dot{S}_{\alpha}}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{\alpha}}\left((\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}\right)

Using the fully antisymmetric tensor
\epsilon

(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}=\sum_{\rho,\nu}\epsilon_{\alpha\rho\nu}H_{\rho}S_{\nu},

we can expand the derivative as

\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{\alpha}}(\vec{H}\times\vec{S})_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha,\rho,\nu}\epsilon_{\alpha\rho\nu}H_{\rho}\frac{\partial S_{\nu}}{\partial S_{\alpha}}

Since

\frac{\partial S_{\nu}}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=\delta_{\alpha\nu},

we get

\sum_{\alpha,\rho,\nu}\epsilon_{\alpha\rho\nu}H_{\rho}\delta_{\alpha\nu}=\sum_{\alpha,\rho}\epsilon_{\alpha\rho\alpha}H_{\rho}=0

because
\epsilon_{\alpha\rho\alpha}=0
.
Thus,

\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\dot{S}_{\alpha}}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=0.

And we can conclude that

\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}{\partial q_{i}}+\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\dot{p}_{i}}{\partial p_{i}}+\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\dot{S}_{\alpha}}{\partial S_{\alpha}}=0,

which means that the
phase space volume is conserved
.

Appendix D
Detailed Balance

Let
\mathbf{\Gamma}
be the state vector and
\bar{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
its inverted counterpart.

\mathbf{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\
q_{i}\\
\vdots\\
p_{i}\\
\vdots\\
S_{x}\\
S_{y}\\
S_{z}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bar{\mathbf{\Gamma}}=\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\
q_{i}\\
\vdots\\
-p_{i}\\
\vdots\\
S_{x}\\
-S_{y}\\
S_{z}\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
q_{i},S_{x},S_{z}
are even under time reversal, and
p_{i},S_{y}
are odd.

The condition for detailed balance is

\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma_{1}})\,P(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\to\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2})=\rho(\bar{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{2})\,P(\bar{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{2}\to\bar{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{1})

(A-56)

with probability distribution
\rho
and transition rates
P
.
As a reminder, the stationary distribution is

\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=Q^{-1}\exp\!\left[-\beta\left(\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\bar{V}(\mathbf{q})+V_{z}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})\right)\right]\delta(|\vec{S}|-1)

and the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Gamma_{i}}\left[\mu_{i}(\mathbf{\Gamma},t)f(\mathbf{\Gamma},t)\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X_{i}\partial X_{j}}\left[D_{ij}f(\mathbf{\Gamma},t)\right]\ ,

with drift vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and diffusion matrix
\mathbf{D}
defined in Eq. (
A-35
) and (
A-40
), respectively.

D.1
Time-reversal parity operator

Introduce
\varepsilon_{i}
such that

\varepsilon_{i}=\begin{cases}+1,&\Gamma_{i}\text{ even under time reversal},\\
-1,&\Gamma_{i}\text{ odd under time reversal}.\end{cases}

and

\bar{\mathbf{\Gamma}}=\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}\Gamma_{1}\\
\varepsilon_{2}\Gamma_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\varepsilon_{m}\Gamma_{m}\end{pmatrix}.

Using the parity operator the detailed balance expression becomes

\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1})\,p(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}\to\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2})=\rho(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2})\,p(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2}\to\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}).

(A-57)

For our probability density function
\rho
we have

\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=\rho(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\ ,

which means that the stationary distribution is invariant under time reversal.

D.2
Conditions for Detailed Balance

The following conditions are necessary and sufficient for detailed balance
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}\mu_{i}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\,\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})+\sum_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Gamma_{j}}\left(2D_{ij}\,\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})\right)

(A-58)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}\,D_{ij}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=D_{ij}(\mathbf{\Gamma})

(A-59)

D.3
Check condition Eq. (
A-58
)

As a reminder the elements of the time-reversal parity operator are:

\varepsilon_{i}=\begin{cases}+1,&i=1,\dots,N\quad\text{(positions: even)}\\
-1,&i=N+1,\dots,2N\quad\text{(momenta: odd)}\\
+1,&i=2N+1,2N+3\quad\text{(spin $S_{x},S_{z}$ even)}\\
-1,&i=2N+2\quad\text{(spin $S_{y}$ odd)}\end{cases}

D.3.1
Case 1: positions (
i=1,\dots,N
,
\varepsilon_{i}=1
)

For the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
corresponding to the position indices, we have

\mu_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\quad\text{such\ that}\quad\varepsilon_{i}\mu_{i}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\ .

For these indices, diffusion does not contribute anything:

\sum_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Gamma_{j}}(D_{ij}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma}))=0

Thus

\varepsilon_{i}\mu_{i}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma}),

and the condition is satisfied for coordinates.

D.3.2
Case 2: momenta (
i=N+1,\dots,2N
,
\varepsilon_{i}=-1
)

For these indices, the drift vector has the values

\mu_{i}=-\frac{\partial\bar{V}}{\partial q_{i-N}}-\frac{\partial V_{z}}{\partial q_{i-N}}\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})+4V_{z}d_{i-N}S_{x}\delta(S_{z})-\frac{\gamma}{m_{i-N}}p_{i-N}

Under time reversal of the vector, we obtain

\varepsilon_{i}\mu_{i}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\left(-\frac{\partial\bar{V}}{\partial q_{i-N}}-\frac{\partial V_{z}}{\partial q_{i-N}}\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})+4V_{z}d_{i-N}S_{x}\delta(S_{z})+\frac{\gamma}{m_{i-N}}p_{i-N}\right)\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})

and

-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\left(-\frac{\partial\bar{V}}{\partial q_{i-N}}-\frac{\partial V_{z}}{\partial q_{i-N}}\mathrm{sign}(S_{z})+4V_{z}d_{i-N}S_{x}\delta(S_{z})-\frac{\gamma}{m_{i-N}}p_{i-N}\right)\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})

such that

\varepsilon_{i}\mu_{i}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})+\mu_{i}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-2\gamma\frac{p_{i-N}}{m_{i-N}}\ \rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})

As a reminder, the elements of the diffusion matrix are:

D_{ij}=\begin{cases}\gamma k_{B}T\,\delta_{ij},&j=N+1,\dots,2N\\
0,&\text{else}\end{cases}

The derivative of the diffusion term in Eq. (
A-58
) for the momenta indices is therefore

\sum_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Gamma_{j}}\left(2D_{ij}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})\right)=2\gamma k_{B}T\sum_{j=N+1}^{2N}\frac{\partial\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})}{\partial p_{j-N}}\delta_{ij}=-2\beta\gamma k_{B}T\frac{p_{i-N}}{m_{i-N}}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-2\gamma\frac{p_{i-N}}{m_{i-N}}\ \rho(\mathbf{\Gamma}).

Hence, condition Eq. (
A-58
) is also fulfilled for the momenta.

D.3.3
Case 3: Spin components (
i=2N+1
,
2N+2
,
2N+3
)

For spin degrees of freedom we have
D_{ij}=0
(cf. Eq. (
A-40
).
Thus we only must check:

\varepsilon_{i}\mu_{i}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})

For the left hand side, we have

\varepsilon_{i}\mu_{i}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=\begin{pmatrix}1\dot{S}_{x}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\\
-1\dot{S}_{y}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\\
1\dot{S}_{z}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})\end{pmatrix}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=\begin{pmatrix}-2\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}S_{z}+2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{y}\\
-2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\\
+2\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}S_{x}\end{pmatrix}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})

and for the right hand side

-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\begin{pmatrix}1\dot{S}_{x}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\\
1\dot{S}_{y}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\\
1\dot{S}_{z}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\end{pmatrix}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})=-\begin{pmatrix}2\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}S_{z}-2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{y}\\
+2V_{z}(\mathbf{q})S_{x}\\
-2\sum_{i}d_{i}(\mathbf{q})\frac{p_{i}}{m_{i}}S_{x}\end{pmatrix}\rho(\mathbf{\Gamma})

Thus, the condition holds for all spin degrees of freedom as well.

D.4
Check condition Eq. (
A-59
)

For the diffusion matrix, we have:

\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}\,D_{ij}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})=D_{ij}(\mathbf{\Gamma})

This is true, because only the diagonal elements for the momentum indices are non-zero and constant.

D=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\gamma k_{B}T\mathbb{I}_{n}&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

In other words, the nonzero entries only for momenta mean that

•

indices
i,j\in\{N+1,\dots,2N\}
,

•

for these:
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{j}=-1
,

•

thus
\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}=+1
.

Such that for the momentum indices we have

\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}D_{ij}(\varepsilon\mathbf{\Gamma})=D_{ij}(\mathbf{\Gamma}).

and everywhere else
D_{ij}=0
, so the condition holds trivially.
Thus, condition Eq. (
A-59
) holds as well.

Hence, MASH dynamics satisfies detailed balance.

Appendix E
Relation of the dynamics of
\vec{S}
to that of
c_{+}
and
c_{-}

The wave function of a two-state system, can be written as

\ket{\Psi}=c_{-}\ket{\psi_{-}(\mathbf{q})}+c_{+}\ket{\psi_{+}(\mathbf{q})}\ ,

(A-60)

where the first term denotes the ground state and the second one denotes the excited state.
In the adiabatic representation, the Schrödinger equation using the energy impression from Eq. (
A-1
) leads to the following equations of motion for the coefficients
c_{-}
and
c_{+}

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{-}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}c_{-}V_{-}(\mathbf{q})-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{q})c_{+}

(A-61)

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{+}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}c_{+}V_{+}(\mathbf{q})+\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{q})c_{-}

(A-62)

where
\mathbf{F}=\bra{\psi_{-}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\ket{\psi_{+}}
is the nonadiabatic coupling element.
Note that
\mathbf{F}=-\mathbf{F}^{*}
.

In order to connect the equations of motion for the coefficients with those of the spin vector, the elements of the spin vector are related to the coefficients by:

\displaystyle S_{x}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}\left[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right]

(A-63)

\displaystyle S_{y}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Im}\left[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right]

(A-64)

\displaystyle S_{z}

\displaystyle=|c_{+}|^{2}-|c_{-}|^{2}

(A-65)

Taking the time derivatives

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{x}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{Re}\left[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right]=2\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\left(c_{+}^{*}c_{-}+(c_{+}^{*}c_{-})^{*}\right)}{2}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(c_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}c_{-}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}+\dot{c}_{+}c-^{*}+c_{+}\dot{c}_{-}^{*}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}+\left(\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}\right)^{*}}{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}\left[\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}\right]

(A-66)

Similarly,

\dot{S}_{y}=2\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{Im}\left[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right]=2\mathrm{Im}\left[\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}\right]

(A-67)

and

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(|c_{+}|^{2}-|c_{-}|^{2}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(c_{+}^{*}c_{+}-c_{-}^{*}c_{-}\right)

\displaystyle=\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{+}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{+}-\dot{c}_{-}^{*}c_{-}-c_{-}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}\ .

(A-68)

Next, we will evaluate the mixed products of coefficients and their time derivatives,
and use the assumption that
\mathbf{F}
is real:

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}c_{+}V_{+}+\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{-}\right)^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}c_{-}V_{-}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{+}c_{+}^{*}c_{-}+\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{-}^{*}c_{-}-\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{-}c_{+}^{*}c_{-}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{+}^{*}c_{+}

Using Eq. (
A-3
) we obtain

\dot{c}_{+}^{*}c_{-}+c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}=\frac{2i}{\hbar}V_{z}(q)c_{+}^{*}c_{-}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\left(|c_{+}|^{2}-|c_{-}|^{2}\right)

(A-69)

The other products are

\displaystyle c_{+}^{*}\dot{c}_{+}=c_{+}^{*}\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}c_{+}V_{+}+\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{-}\right)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{+}c_{+}^{*}c_{+}+\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{+}^{*}c_{-}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{+}|c_{+}|^{2}+\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{+}^{*}c_{-}

and

\displaystyle c_{-}^{*}\dot{c}_{-}=c_{-}^{*}\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}c_{-}V_{-}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{+}\right)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{-}c_{-}^{*}c_{-}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{-}^{*}c_{+}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{-}|c_{-}|^{2}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ c_{-}^{*}c_{+}

Plugging the results into the expressions for
\vec{S}
(cf. eqs. (
A-63
), (
A-64
) and (
A-65
)), one obtains:

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{x}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}\left[\frac{2i}{\hbar}V_{z}(q)c_{+}^{*}c_{-}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ \left(|c_{+}|^{2}-|c_{-}|^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=4\frac{V_{z}}{\hbar}\mathrm{Re}\left(ic_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right)-2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ S_{z}=-2\frac{V_{z}}{\hbar}2\mathrm{Im}\left(c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right)-2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ S_{z}

\displaystyle=-2\frac{V_{z}}{\hbar}S_{y}-2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ S_{z}

and

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{y}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{2i}{\hbar}V_{z}(q)c_{+}^{*}c_{-}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ \left(|c_{+}|^{2}-|c_{-}|^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{2i}{\hbar}V_{z}(q)c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right]=2\frac{V_{z}}{\hbar}\mathrm{Re}\left(c_{+}^{*}c_{-}\right)=2\frac{V_{z}}{\hbar}S_{x}

and

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{z}=2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ \mathrm{Re}(c_{+}^{*}c_{-})+2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ \mathrm{Re}(c_{+}^{*}c_{-})=2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ 2\mathrm{Re}(c_{+}^{*}c_{-})=2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ S_{x}

Thus, we arrive at the equations of motion

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2V_{z}(q)}{\hbar}S_{y}-2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ S_{z}

(A-70)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{2V_{z}(q)}{\hbar}S_{x}

(A-71)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{z}

\displaystyle=2\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{F}\ S_{x}

(A-72)

Eqs. (
A-70
), (
A-71
) and (
A-72
) are identical to the equations of motion from Mannouch
et al.

[
27
]
. This means that, alternatively to propagating the spin vector, one can use the coefficients
c_{+}
and
c_{-}
instead.

Appendix F
Reflection of
\vec{S}
at frustrated transition

If there is a transition from the southern to the northern hemisphere of the Bloch sphere (or vice versa), or in the language of surface hopping a “hop", and the kinetic energy
K
is insufficient,
K<2V_{z}
, the hop is frustrated.
In this case, the Bloch vector
\vec{S}
is reflected at the equator.
We assume a reflection takes place between time steps
t
and
t+1
.
To the first (linear) approximation, the vector
\vec{S}(t+\Delta t)
is obtained by reflecting
\vec{S}^{\prime}(t+\Delta t)
, i.e., the vector calculated without reflection on the equator:

\displaystyle S_{x}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=S_{x}^{\prime}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle S_{y}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=S_{y}^{\prime}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle S_{z}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=-S_{z}^{\prime}(t+\Delta t)

Since we do not run the dynamics in
\vec{S}
, but rather in the coefficients
c_{+}
and
c_{-}
, we need to implement the reflection in the coefficients
c_{+}
and
c_{-}
.
We write
c_{+}^{\prime}
and
c_{-}^{\prime}
— the coefficients before the reflection — as

\displaystyle c_{+}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=r_{+}e^{i\varphi_{+}}

\displaystyle c_{-}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=r_{-}e^{i\varphi_{-}}

The reflection is performed by exchanging the norms

\displaystyle c_{+}

\displaystyle=r_{-}e^{i\varphi_{+}}=c_{+}^{\prime}\frac{r_{-}}{r_{+}}

\displaystyle c_{-}

\displaystyle=r_{+}e^{i\varphi_{-}}=c_{-}^{\prime}\frac{r_{+}}{r_{-}}\ .

This changes
\vec{S}
in the following way:

\displaystyle S_{x}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}]=2\mathrm{Re}\left[c_{+}^{\prime*}\frac{r_{-}}{r_{+}}c_{-}^{\prime}\frac{r_{+}}{r_{-}}\right]=2\mathrm{Re}[c_{+}^{\prime*}c_{-}^{\prime}]=S_{x}^{\prime}

(A-73)

\displaystyle S_{y}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Im}[c_{+}^{*}c_{-}]=2\mathrm{Im}\left[]c_{+}^{\prime*}\frac{r_{-}}{r_{+}}c_{-}^{\prime}\frac{r_{+}}{r_{-}}\right]=2\mathrm{Im}[c_{+}^{\prime*}c_{-}^{\prime}]=S_{y}^{\prime}

(A-74)

\displaystyle S_{z}

\displaystyle=|c_{+}|^{2}-|c_{-}|^{2}=r_{-}^{2}-r_{+}^{2}=-(r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2})=-S_{z}^{\prime}

(A-75)

Hence, exchanging the norms
r_{+}
and
r_{-}
leaves
S_{x}
and
S_{y}
invariant, but changes the sign of
S_{z}
, exactly as intended.

In the reversible implementation of the MASH dynamics used in this work, however, whether a hop is frustrated can be known exactly when the spin vector crosses the equator. Therefore, the need to reflect
S_{z}
as a electronic variable readjustment vanishes. Once the nonadiabatic coupling vector component of velocity is reflected,
\dot{S}_{z}
is inverted automatically, and this reflects the spin vector off the equator.

Appendix G
Inversion of Coefficients

The following derivation is true for two or multiple pairwise-coupled electronic states.
The total electronic wave function is written as a linear combination of electronic basis states
\ket{\psi_{i}}
:

\ket{\Psi(t)}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}}\,.

(A-76)

While the electronic wave function spans all states, the classical nuclei evolve at each time step on a single
active
potential energy surface.
In the unSMASH
[
22
]
formulation of MASH, a generalization that treats more than two electronic states, the coupling between the active state and each of the remaining states are represented by
N_{s}-1
Bloch spheres, where
N_{s}
is the total number of electronic states.

For each Bloch sphere, the corresponding spin vector
\vec{S}
can be obtained directly from the electronic amplitudes:

\displaystyle S_{x}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*}c_{\lambda}\right],

(A-77)

\displaystyle S_{y}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left[c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*}c_{\lambda}\right],

(A-78)

\displaystyle S_{z}

\displaystyle=\lvert c_{\mathrm{act}}\rvert^{2}-\lvert c_{\lambda}\rvert^{2}\,,

(A-79)

where
c_{\mathrm{act}}
denotes the coefficient of the active state and
c_{\lambda}
that of the coupled state.
As shown in Section
B
, under time inversion the components
S_{x}
and
S_{z}
remain unchanged, whereas
S_{y}
changes sign.

Because most nonadiabatic dynamics engines propagate only the coefficients
c_{i}
, time inversion of the Bloch sphere must be implemented through a transformation of the coefficients instead.
Complex conjugation of all coefficients produces exactly the required transformation:

c_{i}\;\longrightarrow\;c_{i}^{*}\ ,

(A-80)

for all
i
.

G.1
Proof

Each electronic coefficient can be written as

c_{i}=\mathrm{Re}[c_{i}]+i\,\mathrm{Im}[c_{i}]\,,

so that its complex conjugate is

c_{i}^{*}=\mathrm{Re}[c_{i}]-i\,\mathrm{Im}[c_{i}]\,.

The mixed product entering the Bloch-sphere components is

c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*}c_{\lambda}=\mathrm{Re}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Re}[c_{\lambda}]+\mathrm{Im}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Im}[c_{\lambda}]-i\,\mathrm{Im}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Re}[c_{\lambda}]+i\,\mathrm{Re}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Im}[c_{\lambda}]\,.

We now examine the three components individually.

S_{x}
:

Since

S_{x}(c_{\mathrm{act}},c_{\lambda})\propto\mathrm{Re}\!\left[c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*}c_{\lambda}\right]=\mathrm{Re}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Re}[c_{\lambda}]+\mathrm{Im}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Im}[c_{\lambda}],

complex conjugation of all coefficients leaves
S_{x}
invariant:

\mathrm{Re}\!\left[(c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*})^{*}(c_{\lambda}^{*})\right]=\mathrm{Re}[c_{\mathrm{act}}c_{\lambda}^{*}]=\mathrm{Re}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Re}[c_{\lambda}]+\mathrm{Im}[c_{\mathrm{act}}]\,\mathrm{Im}[c_{\lambda}]=\mathrm{Re}\!\left[c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*}c_{\lambda}\right]\propto S_{x}(c_{\mathrm{act}},c_{\lambda})\,.

S_{z}
:

Because

S_{z}(c_{\mathrm{act}},c_{\lambda})=\lvert c_{\mathrm{act}}\rvert^{2}-\lvert c_{\lambda}\rvert^{2},

and the modulus is invariant under complex conjugation,

S_{z}(c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*},c_{\lambda}^{*})=\lvert c_{\mathrm{act}}\rvert^{2}-\lvert c_{\lambda}\rvert^{2}=S_{z}(c_{\mathrm{act}},c_{\lambda})\,.

S_{y}
:

For the imaginary component we have

S_{y}(c_{\mathrm{act}},c_{\lambda})=2\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left[c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*}c_{\lambda}\right].

Under conjugation,

\mathrm{Im}\!\left[(c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*})^{*}c_{\lambda}^{*}\right]=\mathrm{Im}[c_{\mathrm{act}}c_{\lambda}^{*}]=-\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left[c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*}c_{\lambda}\right],

which yields

S_{y}(c_{\mathrm{act}}^{*},c_{\lambda}^{*})=-\,S_{y}(c_{\mathrm{act}},c_{\lambda})\,.

Together, these relations demonstrate that complex conjugation of all
coefficients produces the correct time-inversion transformation of the
spin vector
\vec{S}
.

\mathbf{c}=(1.0,0.0)^{\top}

v=-0.5

(ii) moderate kinetic energy (
v=-0.05
a.u.), producing fewer hops;
and (iii) low kinetic energy (
v=-0.002
a.u.), resulting in purely adiabatic dynamics.
Trajectories without hops are perfectly reversible for both implementations.
However, for trajectories involving electronic transitions, the naive velocity-Verlet implementation accumulates significant deviations after forward–backward propagation, as summarized in Table

In contrast, simulations employing the line-search procedure remain reversible up to machine precision.

N

N

For reference, the initial coordinate is

Appendix I
Analytical Properties of the Model System

The model system used in this work consists of two coupled harmonic diabatic potentials (see Eq. (
13
) in the main text).
Owing to the simple analytic form of the Hamiltonian, all quantities required for the dynamical simulations can be obtained analytically.

I.1
Adiabatic Energies

Diagonalization of the diabatic Hamiltonian yields the adiabatic energies

E_{\pm}(q)=\left[q^{2}+1\pm\sqrt{4q^{2}+\left(\frac{V_{c}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}}\right]\epsilon\ ,

(A-81)

where the symbols
+
and
-
denote the excited and ground electronic states, respectively.
The two adiabatic surfaces form an avoided crossing centered at
q=0
, where the energy gap between the states is minimal.

I.2
Nonadiabatic Coupling

The nonadiabatic coupling between the two adiabatic states is defined as

d_{-+}(q)=\frac{\braket{\psi_{-}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{H}}{\mathrm{d}q}|\psi_{+}}}{E_{+}-E_{-}}\ .

(A-82)

For the present model this quantity can be evaluated analytically, yielding

|d_{-+}(q)|=\frac{\epsilon|V_{c}|}{4\epsilon q^{2}+V_{c}^{2}}.

(A-83)

This expression shows that the nonadiabatic coupling is maximal at the avoided crossing (
q=0
) and decays with increasing distance from the crossing seam.

I.3
Dependence on the Electronic Coupling

The spatial localization of the nonadiabatic coupling depends strongly on the magnitude of the off-diagonal coupling parameter
V_{c}
.
For small
V_{c}
, Eq. (
A-83
) becomes sharply peaked near
q=0
, leading to highly localized coupling between the two adiabatic states.
In this regime, population transfer between the states occurs predominantly in the immediate vicinity of the crossing seam.

As
V_{c}
increases, the coupling becomes progressively broader in space.
The rate of change of the coupling magnitude with respect to
V_{c}
is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}|d_{-+}|}{\mathrm{d}V_{c}}=\frac{\epsilon(4\epsilon q^{2}+V_{c}^{2})-2\epsilon V_{c}^{2}}{(4\epsilon q^{2}+V_{c}^{2})^{2}}.

(A-84)

Because this dependence is approximately inverse quadratic in
V_{c}
, increasing the coupling parameter rapidly reduces the localization of the nonadiabatic coupling around the crossing seam.
Consequently, electronic population transfer can occur over a wider range of nuclear coordinates.

Even when choosing a much lower target temperature of
1000

Rarely are longer paths sampled.
This shows that by discarding the first 15% of all paths, we ensure that the path ensemble statistics are done under full thermal equilibrium.
