Abstract
Introduction: The increasing clinical recognition of autism spectrum disorder has improved access to diagnosis, but it
has also increased the risk of automatically attributing social withdrawal, interpersonal discomfort, and sensory
hypersensitivity to autism. Objective: To review, from a clinical and psychopathological perspective, the differential
diagnosis between autism spectrum disorder and social anxiety disorder, emphasizing sensory processing, anxious
dissociation, social cognition, camouflaging, and comorbidities. Method: Narrative review conducted according to
SANRA (Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles) recommendations, based on a targeted search of
PubMed/MEDLINE, clinical guidelines, institutional sources, scientific publisher databases, and reference works in
psychiatry, psychopathology, and neurodevelopment. Search terms were combined around autism spectrum disorder,
social anxiety disorder, social phobia, sensory processing, camouflaging, intolerance of uncertainty, alexithymia,
differential diagnosis, and psychopathology. Results: Social anxiety disorder is mainly organized around fear of
negative evaluation, anticipatory shame, hypervigilance, and avoidance. Autism involves persistent
neurodevelopmental changes in social reciprocity, pragmatic communication, behavioral flexibility, and sensory
processing. Similar symptoms, such as avoiding parties, reduced eye contact, or distress with noise, may arise from
different mechanisms. Recent studies highlight intolerance of uncertainty, sensory hypersensitivity, alexithymia,
emotion regulation, and social camouflaging as factors of clinical overlap. Conclusion: Differential diagnosis requires
longitudinal developmental history, psychopathological examination, assessment of social cognition, family or school
information, and clinical/pharmacological review. Standardized instruments should support, but not replace, clinical
formulation. The main contribution is to propose a mechanistic and hierarchical framework to avoid rapid
equivalences between nonspecific signs and diagnostic categories.
Keywords: Autism Spectrum Disorder; Anxiety Disorders; Phobia Social; Diagnosis Differential; Psychopathology.

1. Introduction
The contemporary recognition of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has decisively changed psychiatric practice. Many
patients previously described merely as “eccentric,” “shy,” “rigid,” “difficult,” “avoidant,” or “antisocial” have come to be
understood through a more appropriate neurodevelopmental framework, with clinical, educational, family, and identity-related
benefits. This advance should be preserved. However, this same broadening of social awareness about neurodivergence has
brought a side effect: the tendency to convert any social or sensory discomfort into an immediate suspicion of ASD.
In clinical practice, it is increasingly common for adolescents and adults to seek evaluation reporting discomfort with
crowded environments, difficulty with interaction, social fatigue, preference for being alone, discomfort with noise, avoidance
of parties, distress during public presentations, or a sense of “not fitting in.” Such complaints can, indeed, occur in ASD.
However, they may also result from social anxiety disorder, temperamental shyness, depression, avoidant personality
disorder, dissociative phenomena, trauma, obsessive disorders, medication effects, akathisia, clinical alterations, or affectiverelational organizations that express themselves through withdrawal and interpersonal instability.
The risk lies not in recognizing ASD, but in impoverishing clinical reasoning through an excess of quick
equivalences. Avoiding people is not synonymous with a primary deficit in social reciprocity. Disliking noise is not, by itself, a

opposition, cultural embarrassment, or simple temperament.
Differential diagnosis, therefore, requires shifting the focus from observable behavior to the psychopathological
mechanism that produces it. The decisive question is not merely whether the patient avoids social interactions, but whether
they avoid them out of fear of negative evaluation, sensory overload, neurodevelopmental difficulty with reciprocity, rigidity
and unpredictability, anxious derealization, affective-relational instability, or a combination of these mechanisms.
This article proposes a narrative, clinical, and psychopathological review of the differential diagnosis between social
phobia/social anxiety disorder and ASD. Its specific contribution lies not in presenting a new diagnostic category, nor in
replacing already established guidelines, but in integrating scattered findings on social anxiety, sensory processing,
camouflaging, social cognition, anxious dissociation, comorbidities, and mimicking clinical conditions into a practical script
for differential formulation. The objective of this study was to review, from a clinical and psychopathological perspective, the
differential diagnosis between autism spectrum disorder and social anxiety disorder, emphasizing sensory processing, anxious
dissociation, social cognition, camouflaging, and comorbidities. The goal is to offer psychiatrists and mental health
professionals a reasoning model that connects diagnostic criteria, recent literature, the psychopathological tradition of
development, and therapeutic implications.

2. Methodology
A qualitative study (Risemberg et al., 2026b; Pereira et al., 2018) was conducted as a literature review (Snyder,
2019)—specifically, a narrative literature review (Ogassawara et al., 2025; Fernandes, Vieira & Castelhano, 2023).
This manuscript was structured as a narrative review. Its methodological design was guided by SANRA (Scale for the
Assessment of Narrative Review Articles), an instrument proposed for assessing the quality of narrative and non-systematic
reviews, which recommends making explicit the importance of the topic, the review's objectives, the literature search strategy,
the referencing of information, scientific reasoning, and the presentation of data relevant to the object analyzed (Baethge et al.,
2019). The clinical, integrative, and non-systematic nature of the article was therefore preserved, while an effort was made to
make the search, selection, and synthesis pathway transparent.
The guiding question was: which clinical and psychopathological elements help differentiate autism spectrum disorder
and social anxiety disorder in adolescents and adults, especially when there are complaints of social discomfort, sensory
hypersensitivity, camouflaging, anxious dissociation, or comorbidities?
The importance of this review stems from the growing frequency, in psychiatric practice, of clinical presentations in
which nonspecific signs, such as social avoidance, gaze aversion, noise intolerance, and a sense of interpersonal inadequacy,
may be quickly attributed to autism without sufficient analysis of the underlying psychopathological mechanisms.
The search was conducted in a targeted manner between June 4 and 6, 2026, consulting PubMed/MEDLINE,
scientific publisher databases, diagnostic documents, clinical guidelines, and institutional sources, including materials from the
National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, as well as books and chapters on psychopathology, neurodevelopment, and
clinical psychiatry considered relevant to the differential formulation.
The strategy combined terms in English and Portuguese related to “autism spectrum disorder,” “transtorno do espectro
autista,” “social anxiety disorder,” “transtorno de ansiedade social,” “social phobia,” “fobia social,” “sensory overresponsivity,” “sensory processing,” “processamento sensorial,” “camouflaging,” “camuflagem,” “intolerance of uncertainty,”

The operational inclusion criteria were: direct relevance to the guiding question; contribution to clinical differentiation

Redundant texts, publications with no direct relation to differential diagnosis, materials without substantive clinical
contribution, studies whose main focus did not help distinguish psychopathological or neurodevelopmental mechanisms,
opinion pieces without sufficient conceptual or empirical grounding, and publications focused on epidemiological or
therapeutic outcomes without direct applicability to the objective of this review were excluded. At the end of the process, 24

formulation.
The selection process took place in three stages. In the first, titles, abstracts, guidelines, and institutional materials
were examined for thematic relevance.
In the second, potentially relevant texts were assessed for their clinical usefulness in differentiating mechanisms of
social avoidance, sensory overload, fear of negative evaluation, alterations in social reciprocity, camouflaging, anxious
dissociation, and comorbidities.
In the third, references were organized into interpretive axes corresponding to the main domains of the narrative
synthesis: social anxiety, ASD, sensory processing, social cognition, camouflaging, mimicking conditions, diagnostic
assessment, and therapeutic implications.
The final choice of references was justified by their direct contribution to formulating a clinical and
psychopathological matrix applicable to the psychiatric interview.
Thus, priority was given to studies and guidelines that allowed understanding not merely the presence of similar
behaviors, but the mechanisms that produce them. As this is a narrative review guided by SANRA, there was no intent to
perform a meta-analysis, estimate aggregate prevalence, apply a formal risk-of-bias assessment, or execute a systematic
protocol with independent double selection.
The results are presented as a critical, clinical synthesis, organized by psychopathological axes and practical
implications for diagnostic assessment. The intended originality is clinical-integrative: to transform available evidence and
concepts into a decision-making script applicable to psychiatric practice, especially in scenarios of overlap between ASD,
social anxiety, and nonspecific sensory complaints.

3. Results and Discussion
Social anxiety disorder: fear of negative evaluation and affective avoidance
Social anxiety disorder, traditionally called social phobia, is defined by marked fear or anxiety in situations in which
the individual is exposed to possible evaluation by others. Its phenomenological core is the fear of acting inappropriately,
showing signs of anxiety, being humiliated, rejected, ridiculed, or negatively evaluated. Social situations are avoided or
endured with intense distress, with persistence, functional impairment, and disproportion relative to the sociocultural context
(Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], 2016).

In this presentation, avoidance is predominantly affective. The patient does not necessarily avoid because they fail to
understand the logic of the interaction, but because they anticipate shame, criticism, exposure, or rejection. In many cases,
social competence is relatively preserved but becomes blocked by anxiety. When anxiety decreases, through familiarity,
treatment, a secure bond, or judicious use of psychotherapeutic strategies, social performance can improve markedly.
This relative preservation of social capacity, even if inhibited, is a clinical finding of great value. Social anxiety
disorder tends to be persistent, frequently comorbid with depression, other anxiety disorders, and substance use, and can
compromise relationships, school life, professional performance, and quality of life (National Institute for Health and Care
Excellence [NICE], 2013).
In children and adolescents, it may manifest as crying, freezing, situational mutism, irritability, dependence on
familiar adults, or refusal of social exposure.
In adults, it may take more discreet forms, with avoidance of meetings, difficulty speaking on the phone, avoidance of
eating in public, over-preparation, post-event rumination, and a chronic sense of inadequacy.
The psychopathology of social anxiety involves hypervigilance for signs of criticism, self-focused attention,
monitoring of bodily symptoms, catastrophic anticipation, and avoidance as short-term relief. Thus, the anxious patient may
say they “don't like people,” when, more precisely formulated, they fear being seen, judged, or embarrassed. The statement “I
don't like people” can be a verbal defense against the pain of wanting connection while fearing failure in it.
Autism spectrum disorder: neurodevelopment, social reciprocity, and restricted patterns
ASD is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent difficulties in social communication and social
interaction, associated with restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities. These alterations must be
present since the developmental period, even though they may become fully evident only when social demands exceed
adaptive capacities or when compensatory strategies are no longer sufficient (NICE, 2012).
In adults, masking and social compensation strategies can reduce the external visibility of autistic traits, at the cost of
exhaustion, anxiety, and psychological distress, which makes the longitudinal history even more important (Hull et al., 2021).
The social axis of ASD is not reduced to avoidance. It involves qualitative changes in socioemotional reciprocity,
nonverbal communication, language pragmatics, inference of mental states, contextual adjustment, and relationship
maintenance. The literature on theory of mind and social cognition, on both psychological and neuropsychiatric grounds, is
central to understanding why some autistic patients may appear “uninterested,” “naïve,” “literal,” “monologic,” “rigid,” or “out
of step” with interaction, even when they desire connection (Caixeta et al., 2023).
In ASD, social discomfort can arise through multiple pathways. It may stem from difficulty interpreting implicit
social cues, keeping up with rapid exchanges, tolerating unpredictability, sustaining eye contact, modulating body language,
adapting interests to the interlocutor, or coping with sensorially intense environments.
Unlike primary social anxiety, the problem is not merely fear of negative evaluation, although that fear may coexist.
The problem may be the very neurodevelopmental architecture of interaction itself.
Assessment should seek longitudinal data: language development, symbolic play, early reciprocity, food selectivity,
rigidity, intense interests, sensory reactions since childhood, peer relationships, school history, family adaptation, routine
patterns, and modes of emotional regulation. Classic and contemporary works on child psychopathology and child psychiatry
emphasize that a child's or adolescent's symptoms must be interpreted within their maturational, family, and relational history,
not as isolated signs (Ajuriaguerra & Marcelli, 1998; M. Caixeta & L. Caixeta, 2019).

The zone of overlap: when similar behaviors express different mechanisms
The overlap between ASD and social anxiety is real. People with ASD show increased rates of anxiety, including
social anxiety, and individuals with social anxiety may exhibit subclinical autistic traits or social difficulties secondary to
prolonged avoidance.
Recent reviews indicate that social anxiety in autistic people may stem from deficits in social competence, difficulties
interpreting nonverbal cues, maintaining eye contact, emotional recognition, intolerance of uncertainty, and a preference for
predictability (Montaser et al., 2023).
In autistic adults, a recent empirical model suggests that intolerance of uncertainty and difficulties in emotional
regulation play a central role in anxiety, while alexithymia and differences in sensory processing contribute directly or
indirectly to its clinical expression (Riedelbauch et al., 2024).
In autistic and neurotypical adolescents with comparable levels of social anxiety, the literature points to relevant
associations among social anxiety, intolerance of uncertainty, alexithymia, emotional regulation, sensory hypersensitivity, and
interoception, reinforcing that transdiagnostic mechanisms may cross categorical boundaries (Pickard et al., 2020).
Furthermore, a recent study comparing autistic adults with people with social anxiety disorder indicates that social
camouflaging should not be reduced to mere role-playing, since it relates to anxious distress, compensatory strategies, and
subjective exhaustion (Pyszkowska, 2025).
Studies and reviews specifically focused on social anxiety in autism reinforce that comorbidity is clinically frequent,
can generate additional functional impairment, and requires careful assessment to differentiate secondary/comorbid anxiety
from core neurodevelopmental manifestations (Maddox & White, 2015; Spain et al., 2018).
However, behavioral similarity does not authorize diagnostic equivalence. An eye-tracking study in adolescents with
social anxiety disorder showed an interesting dissociation: higher autistic traits were associated with slower orientation toward
the eyes, while social anxiety symptoms were associated with faster orientation away from the eyes after fixation (Kleberg et
al., 2017).
Clinically, two patients may both avoid eye contact, but one does so due to difficulty with spontaneous social
orientation or overload from ocular stimulation, while another does so due to perceived threat and fear of evaluation. This
distinction illustrates the value of mechanistic reasoning. The behavior “doesn't make eye contact” is merely the surface.
Beneath it, diverse mechanisms may exist: low social salience, limbic hyperarousal, shame, trauma, opposition, depression,
bodily estrangement, cultural rigidity, or a conscious attempt to appear “normal.”

Sensory processing: multimodal filtering, hypervigilance, and the danger of diagnostic shortcuts
Sensory processing occupies a special place in this discussion. Hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory stimuli and
unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment are part of the current diagnostic criteria for ASD. Many autistic patients
describe common sounds as intrusive, lights as painful, fabrics as intolerable, smells as invasive, and environments with
multiple stimuli as exhausting.
The expression “multimodal sensory filtering” is clinically useful for describing the difficulty of integrating and
modulating auditory, visual, tactile, proprioceptive, interoceptive, and social stimuli.
However, sensory processing is not synonymous with ASD. Green and Ben-Sasson (2010) proposed three models for
the relationship between anxiety and sensory over-responsivity in autism: anxiety could amplify sensory reactivity; overresponsivity could generate anxiety; or both could share common factors or diagnostic overlap.
Reviews on sensory interventions also note that sensory over-responsivity, defined as an intense negative reaction to
or avoidance of sensory stimuli, may be associated with anxiety, depression, insomnia, and family burden, but is not exclusive
to ASD, and can occur in other disorders and in typical development (Yuan et al., 2022).
Clinically, the anxious patient may “not like noise” because they are hyperalert, tense, sleeping poorly, anticipating
threat, with autonomic symptoms and low tolerance for unpredictability.
The patient with ASD, in turn, may present sensory discomfort that is more primary, early-onset, multimodal,
relatively independent of fear of evaluation, and frequently associated with rigidity, selectivity, overload-related crises, and a
need for predictability.

Shyness, avoidant traits, and personality: not all inhibition is spectrum
Shyness is a common temperamental trait and, by itself, does not constitute a disorder. Many shy people desire
contact, function well in family and friendship relationships, and experience distress only in situations of heightened exposure.
The boundary with social anxiety disorder is crossed when there is persistent distress, functional impairment, and
marked avoidance. The boundary with ASD requires a different kind of analysis: the presence of qualitative changes in
reciprocity, social communication, and restricted patterns since development.
Avoidant personality traits add complexity. The individual may organize their life around the expectation of rejection,
inadequacy, and inferiority, avoiding bonds out of fear of criticism. In this case, the avoidance has an affective-relational core,
not a neurodevelopmental one.
The history usually reveals an intense desire for acceptance, sensitivity to rejection, self-deprecation, and
interpersonal suffering. Although it may coexist with ASD, this pattern should not be automatically confused with autism.
Clinical psychiatry and psychopathology teach that diagnosis is not a mere counting of symptoms, but an
interpretation of how the symptom is constituted over time, in the body, in the relationship, and in culture (M. Caixeta, 2004;
Caixeta et al., 2022).
Two patients who isolate themselves may do so for radically different reasons. One seeks to reduce sensory overload;
another avoids shame; another is depressed; another presents paranoid ideation; another has akathisia; another uses isolation as
relational protest; another has a combination of these factors.
Anxious dissociation, hysteriform phenomena, and relational manifestations
Another point frequently confused with ASD is anxious dissociation. At peaks of anxiety, some patients report
derealization, depersonalization, a sense of estrangement, freezing, distancing from the body, altered perception of the
environment, and the impression of “leaving oneself.” When these phenomena are fleeting, situational, associated with fear,
panic, or social exposure, and not accompanied by persistent disorganization of reciprocity since childhood, they suggest an
anxious/dissociative mechanism.
Different is the field of more hysteriform, relational, or borderline manifestations, in which the symptom may be
linked to theatricality, aggressiveness, sexuality, frustration, relational disputes, bodily dramatization, interpersonal oscillation,
and intense affective charge.
The older language of psychopathology described such presentations as more “limbic” or affective-relational, in the
sense that the symptom's energy is organized around the bond, the interpersonal scene, and affective regulation.
Although contemporary terminology must be used carefully to avoid stigma, the clinical distinction remains useful:
there are forms of suffering that express themselves socially but are not primarily deficits of autistic social cognition, nor pure
social phobia. This differentiation has therapeutic consequences. Anxious dissociation may improve with anxiety management,
psychoeducation, grounding techniques, cognitive-behavioral therapy, gradual exposure, and appropriate pharmacological
treatment when indicated. Intense relational manifestations may require psychotherapy focused on emotional regulation,
mentalization, attachment, and interpersonal patterns.
ASD, in turn, requires communicational, environmental, and psychoeducational adaptations and, when necessary,
treatment of comorbidities. Confusing these axes can lead to ineffective interventions.

Recent changes in withdrawal, restlessness, intolerance to stimuli, irritability, insomnia, difficulty concentrating,

example, can be experienced as intense anxiety, motor restlessness, and subjective distress, and can occur with various
psychotropic drugs, including antipsychotics and, in some cases, antidepressants (Salem et al., 2017).
Assessment should investigate the use and dosage of antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, stimulants,
psychoactive substances, caffeine, decongestants, corticosteroids, and other agents capable of altering sleep, anxiety, or
activation.
In patients using lithium, valproate, or other mood stabilizers, adverse effects, interactions, serum levels when
applicable, and the appropriateness of the clinical indication must be considered. Historical response to medications can inform
hypotheses but should never define the diagnosis in a simplistic way. One should not conclude that “responding to an
anxiolytic” proves social anxiety, or that “improving with a mood stabilizer” proves ASD. Pharmacology offers clues; it does
not replace psychopathology.
It is also prudent to assess nutritional and metabolic conditions. Deficiencies of iron, vitamin B12, folate, and vitamin
D, as well as thyroid alterations, anemia, sleep deprivation, chronic pain, epilepsy, autoimmune diseases, intoxications,
hypervitaminoses, and neurocognitive conditions can alter energy, mood, attention, sensory tolerance, and social functioning.

Comorbidity: when the question is not “one or the other”
Differential diagnosis should not be turned into an artificial choice between ASD and social anxiety.
Many autistic patients develop secondary social anxiety from repeated experiences of interpersonal failure, bullying,
rejection, camouflaging effort, social unpredictability, and exhaustion. In these cases, fear of negative evaluation is real and
clinically treatable, but it occurs on top of a neurodevelopmental matrix.
On the other hand, patients with severe social anxiety may appear autistic due to an impoverished social repertoire,
chronic avoidance, defensive rigidity, and limited interpersonal experience.
The review literature on social anxiety in ASD describes an association with worse social functioning, lower social
motivation, and greater clinical impact, reinforcing that diagnosis must recognize both overlap and the possibility of
comorbidity (Spain et al., 2018).

The right question may be: is there a predominant mechanism, or are there two mechanisms in comorbidity? ASD
does not confer immunity against social anxiety; social anxiety does not exclude ASD. Clinical formulation must recognize
layers.
One patient may present sensory hypersensitivity since childhood, pragmatic difficulties, and restricted interests, and
additionally develop fear of judgment during adolescence. Another may have no history of neurodevelopmental alterations but
present intense social anxiety, derealization in exposure situations, and sensory complaints secondary to hypervigilance.
Assessment of comorbidity should include interviews with family members when possible, school records, developmental
milestones, functioning in low-anxiety settings, direct observation, standardized instruments, and longitudinal analysis.
Scales and tests are useful but should not be used as diagnostic machines. In camouflaged patients, especially women
and high-functioning adults, the absence of obvious signs does not exclude ASD.
Conversely, elevated scores on screening instruments may reflect anxiety, trauma, depression, obsessiveness, or
prolonged isolation. Therefore, the diagnostic hypothesis must remain revisable, especially when the main complaint is
formulated late in adult life or arises after periods of stress, isolation, occupational changes, or increased social demand.
Practical proposal for diagnostic reasoning
A useful clinical model is to organize the interview around axes. First, investigate temporality: since when has the
patient been this way? Second, ask about the subjective mechanism: what do they fear, feel, or try to avoid? Third, assess
social reciprocity: is there understanding of cues, conversational turn-taking, inference of mental states, pragmatic flexibility?
Fourth, map sensory processing: which stimuli, since when, in which contexts, with what impact? Fifth, look for restricted,
repetitive patterns, routines, fixed interests, and the need for predictability. Sixth, review the body, sleep, medications, and
tests. Seventh, integrate family, school, culture, and life trajectory. Table 4 below organizes useful interview questions and

The final formulation should be descriptive and hierarchical. Rather than simply recording “ASD versus social
phobia,” one could write: “Patient with an early history of sensory selectivity and rigidity, persistent pragmatic difficulties, and
social anxiety secondary to experiences of rejection”; or “Patient with no robust evidence of neurodevelopmental alterations,
with preserved social competence outside of evaluative situations, intense fear of humiliation, and situational anxious
derealization”; or even “Patient with multifactorial social avoidance, under investigation, requiring exclusion of akathisia and
iron/B12 deficiency.”
The practical contribution of this synthesis is to shift differential diagnosis from a static comparison of symptoms
toward a formulation by mechanisms. Rather than asking only whether a given behavior occurs, the proposed script asks since
when it occurs, in which contexts it appears, what subjective experience accompanies it, what avoidant function it serves, what
developmental data support it, and what clinical or pharmacological hypotheses might mimic it. This organization adds to the
existing literature a map for immediate clinical use, particularly useful when the patient arrives at the consultation already
identified with a diagnostic hypothesis but without yet having a sufficient longitudinal reconstruction.
Therapeutic implications
Diagnostic distinction matters because it guides treatment. In primary social anxiety, specific psychological
interventions, especially cognitive-behavioral therapy with exposure, belief restructuring, reduction of safety behaviors,
training in external attention, and management of rumination, play a central role.
Pharmacological treatment may be indicated depending on severity, comorbidities, patient preference, and medical
assessment. In ASD, the plan should include psychoeducation, environmental adaptations, predictability, sensory management,
support for pragmatic communication, family interventions, school or occupational support, and treatment of comorbidities
(Caixeta et al., 2024; Lord et al., 2018).
The goal is not to “normalize” the patient, but to reduce suffering, expand autonomy, and adjust environmental
demands to neurodevelopmental characteristics. When there is comorbid social anxiety in an autistic person, CBT can be
useful, but it often requires adaptations: concrete language, predictability, attention to interests, work on real social skills,
sensory regulation, and graded exposure that does not confuse anxious desensitization with violation of sensory limits.
Recent exploratory evidence suggests that CBT for social anxiety in autistic adults can reduce anxious symptoms
without necessarily increasing reports of camouflaging, a clinically relevant point for differentiating anxiety treatment from the
imposition of social masking (Roisenberg et al., 2025).
In anxious dissociative phenomena, psychoeducation, grounding techniques, breathing management, sleep hygiene,
caffeine reduction, and anxiety treatment can be decisive. In affective-relational presentations, intervention should include
emotional regulation, mentalization, attachment patterns, and management of impulsivity.
When akathisia, medication-induced activation, or an organic condition is suspected, the first step in treatment is to
recognize the possible cause and revise the clinical plan.
Contemporary practice also requires care with identity-related diagnoses. For some patients, receiving an ASD
diagnosis organizes a life narrative and reduces guilt. For others, a premature diagnosis can freeze therapeutic possibilities,
obscure treatable anxiety, reinforce avoidance, or neglect clinical conditions.
A recent review on differential diagnosis of ASD in adults reinforces that assessment should integrate developmental
history, functionality, comorbidities, psychiatric differential diagnosis, and clinical judgment, avoiding both underdiagnosis
and excessive attribution of nonspecific symptoms to the autism spectrum (Carroll et al., 2025). The ethical commitment is not
to hastily confirm or deny identities, but to offer an honest, longitudinal, and revisable formulation.

4. Conclusion
The boundary between social phobia and ASD is one of the most delicate areas of contemporary psychiatry, and the
contribution of this article is to propose a clinical synthesis that helps transform nonspecific signs into hierarchical, testable
hypotheses throughout assessment. The broadening recognition of autism was necessary and beneficial, but it does not
eliminate the obligation to think psychopathologically. Social avoidance, discomfort with noise, shyness, isolation, and
interpersonal exhaustion are nonspecific signs. They may belong to ASD, to social anxiety, to avoidant traits, to dissociative
phenomena, to relational organizations, to depression, to medication effects, to organic conditions, or to combinations of these
axes. Differential diagnosis must shift from appearance to mechanism. In ASD, avoidance is frequently linked to
neurodevelopment, social reciprocity, social cognition, rigidity, predictability, and sensory processing. In social anxiety, it is
organized around fear of negative evaluation, shame, hypervigilance, and avoidant relief. In practice, many patients present
overlap, and the best formulation may be dimensional and combined. Thinking well matters more than labeling quickly.
Good psychiatry does not ask only “which criterion was met?” but “what history produced this symptom, in which
body, in which brain, in which family, in which culture, at which point in development, and under which treatment?” For
clinical practice, the central recommendation is to formulate hypotheses in layers, make the predominant mechanism explicit,
recognize comorbidities, record diagnostic uncertainties, and revise the formulation as new longitudinal data emerge. It is at
this point that diagnosis stops being a label and becomes, once again, a clinical act.
