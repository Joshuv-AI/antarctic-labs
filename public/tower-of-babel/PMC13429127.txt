Epidemiological Characteristics and Antimicrobial Resistance of Prototheca Isolated in China During 2002–2024
Authors: Rong Bai, Lili Wang, Chuang Liu, Teng Li, Zhiyong Shao, Jie Chen, Jian Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRecently, Prototheca has emerged as a global threat to human and animal health. This study aims to characterize the epidemiological features and antimicrobial resistance profiles of Prototheca isolates in China from 2002 to 2024, thereby providing critical insights for the management and control of protothecosis.MethodsDuring 2002–2024, a total of 93 Prototheca isolates were collected from human, animals and environment in China. The strain identification, in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility test, and phylogenetic reconstruction were performed.ResultsAmong all 93 Prototheca isolates, azole antifungals such as itraconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole exhibited potent in vitro activity, with MIC50 values of 1 μg/mL. Isavuconazole demonstrated superior in vitro activity, yielding geometric mean (GM) MIC and MIC50 of 0.09 μg/mL and 0.06 μg/mL, respectively. Similarly, amphotericin B showed strong activity, with MIC50 of 0.5 μg/mL. Among antibacterial agents, levofloxacin, tigecycline, and gentamicin showed modest activity. Isavuconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole exhibited significantly greater activity against P. ciferrii than against P. bovis and P. wickerhamii. P. wickerhamii isolates constituted the predominant clade, which exhibited a highly complex branching topology.DiscussionAmphotericin B and isavuconazole exhibit superior anti-Protothecal activity against isolates from both human and bovine mastitis; however, significant inter-species differences in drug susceptibility exist.

Introduction

The global emergence of Prototheca infections poses an escalating threat to both human and animal health. Prototheca are achlorophyllous, unicellular microalgae (Chlorellaceae, Chlorophyta) that have lost photosynthetic capability and adopted saprophytic or parasitic lifestyles. Ubiquitous in soil, water, and organic matter, these opportunistic pathogens cause protothecosis in vertebrates.1,2

Current taxonomy, based on the mitochondrial CYTB gene, coding for cytochrome b, a component of the respiratory chain bc1 complex, recognizes 18 Prototheca species, of which six are pathogenic to humans and animals: P. wickerhamii, P. ciferrii, P. blaschkeae, P. cutis, P. miyajii, and P. bovis.3–7 Human protothecosis manifests as cutaneous/soft tissue lesions, olecranon bursitis, or disseminated infections. Cutaneous forms account for approximately 50% of cases, while systemic dissemination—predominantly affecting immunocompromised individuals—carries a grim prognosis with mortality exceeding 50%.8 Risk factors include immunosuppressive therapy, diabetes, malignancy, and invasive procedures such as catheterization.9 Infections are typically exogenous, resulting from traumatic inoculation with contaminated environmental sources.10 Furthermore, bovine mastitis—defined as the inflammation of the mammary gland—is the most prevalent and devastating disease affecting dairy cattle worldwide, P. bovis is associated with this condition, which leads to reduced milk production.11,12

Despite the lack of standardized treatment guidelines, amphotericin B (AMB) and its liposomal formulations remain the cornerstone of therapy, serving as first-line agents globally. Although effective, their use is often limited by toxicity. Second-line options, including azoles (eg, itraconazole, voriconazole), exhibit variable efficacy due to significant inter-strain susceptibility differences.13 Studies exploring the antimicrobial susceptibility of Prototheca species are limited, predominantly focusing on strains of animal or environmental origin.14

This study aims to analyze the in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility and molecular biological characteristics of Prototheca in China, providing a scientific basis for the prevention and control of protothecosis. The strains used were isolated from China by the Protothecosis Science Popularization and Monitoring Consortium (PSPMC) during 2002 to 2024.

Materials and Methods

Collection of Prototheca Strains

In this retrospective study, we analyzed a total of 93 Prototheca strains consecutively collected by the Protothecosis Science Popularization and Monitoring Consortium (PSPMC) between 2002 and 2024. The collection comprised 76 clinical isolates obtained from human patients across various hospitals in China, derived from specimens including skin tissue, feces, sputum, cerebrospinal fluid, ascites, and blood. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the prolonged storage duration, demographic data (such as age and gender) were incomplete or unavailable for a subset of patients. Additionally, 17 environmental and veterinary isolates were collected from cases of bovine mastitis and cow-barn surroundings in China.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai East Hospital, Shanghai, China (Approval No. 2024YS-274). As this is an in vitro study on Prototheca, it did not involve any human participants or animal subjects, nor did it collect or use any personally identifiable information. The strains used were isolated from clinical samples obtained from the hospital laboratory, as well as from environments associated with bovine mastitis and cow barns.

Strain Isolation and Identification

Isolated strains were inoculated onto Sabouraud Dextrose Agar and cultured at 35°C for 2–3 days. Identification was performed using the EXS3000 matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (Zybio, China). To ensure accurate identification, CYTB gene sequencing was performed for all isolates. Primers for PCR amplification and sequencing targeted a partial region of the CYTB gene: CYTB-F: 5′-GYGTWGAACAYATTATGAGAG-3′, CYTB-R: 5′-WACCCATAARAARTACCATTCWGG-3′. To improve primer universality across different species and strains, degenerate bases were incorporated into the aforementioned primers, with Y, R, and W representing C/T, A/G, and A/T, respectively. The CYTB gene sequences of the strains in this study were compared with corresponding sequences retrieved from the GenBank database.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

The antifungal susceptibility test for Prototheca via Sensititre® CMC1JHY kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the amphotericin B microbroth dilution method (Bio-Kont, Wenzhou, China) was carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The echinocandins (anidulafungin, caspofungin, and micafungin), azoles (isavuconazole, voriconazole, fluconazole, itraconazole, and posaconazole), amphotericin B and 5-fluorocytosine were tested. And 26 antimicrobials were used, including amikacin, levofloxacin, tigecycline, gentamicin, penicillin, cefoxitin, oxacillin, vancomycin, teicoplanin, azithromycin, erythromycin, clindamycin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, rifampin, linezolid, nitrofurantoin, ampicillin, ampicillin/sulbactam, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefazolin, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, cefoperazone/sulbactam, chloramphenicol, and meropenem. The inoculum was prepared to a final concentration of (1.5~8) × 103 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined by broth microdilution following the manufacturer’s instructions. It was visually determined as the lowest concentration of an antifungal agent that substantially inhibited growth of the organism. The susceptibility plates were incubated at 35°C for 72 hours. Candida parapsilosis ATCC22019 and Candida krusei ATCC6258 were used as quality control and reference strains.

Alignment and Phylogenetic Reconstruction

Multiple Sequence Alignment and Quality Control

The reference sequences were obtained from GenBank. The selected sequences include Gene ID 42265515 for Prototheca bovis (strain SAG 2021) and Gene ID 36486143 for Prototheca ciferrii (strain SAG 2063). The raw nucleotide sequences of the CYTB gene from both isolates collected and reference strains were subjected to multiple sequence alignment (MSA) using MAFFT (v7.505). To ensure the highest alignment accuracy, particularly across variable regions of the mitochondrial marker, the L-INS-i strategy (a highly accurate iterative refinement method with local pairwise alignment information) was employed. The alignment parameters were set to default values (gap opening penalty: 1.53; offset value: 0.123).

Sequence Trimming and Phylogenetic Filtering

To eliminate poorly aligned positions, highly divergent regions, and gap-rich sites that could potentially introduce systematic errors or phylogenetic noise, the resulting alignment was automatically trimmed using trimAl (v1.4.1). The -automated1 heuristic method was applied, which dynamically optimizes the balance between gap score and single-column similarity thresholds specifically for Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic reconstruction. This rigorous filtering process resulted in a highly reliable, trimmed alignment block with a final dataset length of {620} bp containing only high-quality phylogenetically informative sites.

Evolutionary Model Selection

To determine the optimal nucleotide substitution model for the dataset, model selection was performed within the PhyloSuite (v1.2.3) platform using ModelFinder (or the integrated model selection tool in IQ-TREE). Based on the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the General Time Reversible (GTR) model with a CAT approximation of rate variation among sites ({GTR} + {CAT}) was selected as the best-fit model for describing the evolutionary dynamics of the CYTB gene partition.

Phylogenetic Tree Reconstruction (Approximately Maximum Likelihood)

Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using FastTree (v2.1.11), which computes approximately Maximum Likelihood trees with high computational efficiency and reliability. The tree search was executed under the {GTR} + {CAT} substitution model, incorporating 20 distinct rate categories to model rate variation across sites. To evaluate the statistical support and robustness of the resulting node topologies, Shimodaira-Hasegawa (SH)-like local support values were calculated using 1,000 resamples. Branches with SH-like support values lower than 0.50 (or 50%) were collapsed to focus on biologically meaningful phylogenetic clusters.

Tree Visualization and Annotation

The final optimal tree in Newick format was exported and uploaded to the online platform Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL v6.8) for advanced visualization, rooting, and formatting. The tree was rooted at the midpoint, and the isolates were color-coded according to their biological origins: Red for human clinical isolates, Blue for bovine mastitis isolates, and Green for environmental or dairy farm surroundings isolates. All font sizes, branch lines, and support labels were adjusted to high-resolution vector formats (300{DPI}minimum) to ensure maximum legibility and reproducibility.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA). The results are presented as the geometric mean MIC (GM MIC), MIC50, and MIC90. The MIC50 and MIC90 were defined as the minimum concentrations of drug required to inhibit the growth of 50% and 90% of the isolates, respectively. Non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were employed to determine overall significance, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for post-hoc analysis. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Epidemiology of Prototheca

A total of 93 strains of Prototheca were collected during 2002 to 2024 in China. Seventy‑six Prototheca isolates were collected from humans, with demographic data available for 49 cases. Among these, 53.1% were females (Table 1). Prototheca was mainly detected in people aged 41–60 (n = 16, 32.7%) and 61–80 (n = 20, 40.8%) years old. The Prototheca isolated from clinical infection included P. wickerhamii, P. bovis, P. zopfii, P. ciferrii and P. miyajii, accounting for 73.7%, 6.6%, 3.9%, 14.5% and 1.3%, respectively, with the largest number of strains collected in East China (n = 52), followed by South China (n = 15), Southwest Region (n = 7), North China (n = 1), and Central China (n = 1). The most common site of isolation for the strain was skin tissue (82.9%), followed by feces, cerebrospinal fluid, blood, ascites, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, lymph node tissue, etc. A total of 17 non-human source Prototheca strains were identified (Table 1). About 82.4% of the isolates came from raw milk, and 17.6% of the isolates came from dairy farm. Antimicrobial Susceptibility Profiles

The in vitro activity of different antimicrobial agents against Prototheca varies significantly (Table 2). Of the 93 Prototheca isolates, azole antifungals, including itraconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole, exhibited potent in vitro activity, with MIC50 values of 1 μg/mL and MIC90 values ranging from 2 μg/mL (itraconazole and posaconazole) to 4 μg/mL (voriconazole). Isavuconazole demonstrated superior in vitro activity, yielding geometric mean (GM) MIC and MIC50 values of 0.09 μg/mL and 0.06 μg/mL, respectively. Similarly, amphotericin B showed strong activity, with MIC50 of 0.5 μg/mL. In contrast, fluconazole, 5-fluorocytosine, and the echinocandins (caspofungin, anidulafungin, and micafungin) displayed limited activity, with MIC50 values of 256, 32, 4, 8, and 8 μg/mL, respectively. Among antibacterial agents, levofloxacin, tigecycline, and gentamicin showed modest activity (MIC50 of 16, 8, and 8 μg/mL, respectively), whereas azithromycin, vancomycin, and ceftriaxone were largely ineffective. And no significant differences in antimicrobial susceptibility were observed between human and non-human isolates, or between cutaneous and non-cutaneous isolates. Susceptibility Profiles of Different Species

Susceptibility profiles of effective agents across different species are detailed in Figure 2. Isavuconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole exhibited significantly greater activity against P. ciferrii than against P. bovis, P. wickerhamii, and P. zopfii (P < 0.05), with corresponding MIC50 values for P. ciferrii being 0.03, 0.5, and 0.5 μg/mL, respectively. Similarly, itraconazole was significantly more active against P. ciferrii (MIC50 0.25 μg/mL) than against P. bovis and P. wickerhamii (MIC50 1 μg/mL; P < 0.05). The activity of isavuconazole and voriconazole against P. bovis was significantly lower compared to P. wickerhamii (MIC50 0.06 and 1 μg/mL, respectively). The AMB demonstrated consistent activity against all four Prototheca species, with uniform MIC50 values of 0.5 μg/mL. Furthermore, tigecycline, gentamicin, and amikacin showed significantly higher potency against P. ciferrii than against P. bovis and P. wickerhamii (P < 0.05). Phylogenetic Reconstruction

The phylogenetic analysis unveiled distinct genetic divergence and species-specific clustering patterns among Prototheca isolates. As illustrated in Figure 3, all isolates were clearly segregated into several major monophyletic clades. Notably, P. wickerhamii isolates constituted the predominant clade, encompassing the vast majority of strains and characterized by a highly complex branching architecture reflecting substantial intraspecific genetic heterogeneity. The CYTB gene-based typing effectively discriminated among P. zopfii, P. ciferrii, and P. bovis, assigning each to a distinct cluster. Discussion

To date, over 200 cases of human protothecosis have been documented in the literature.15,16 Clinically, the disease manifests primarily as cutaneous lesions (66%), olecranon bursitis (15%), and disseminated or systemic infections (19%).15 No cases of direct human-to-human transmission have been reported. Infection can occur via contact with environmental sources through traumatic inoculation or insect bites.17 Potential sources of Prototheca include soil, trees, grass, sewage, animals, animal feces, food, etc.

In this study, the main infection site of Prototheca is skin tissue, followed by feces. Gastrointestinal protothecosis was first reported in 2018 by Sari et al,18 describing an 8-year-old girl with a CARD9 deficiency who presented with abdominal pain and hematochezia. The causative agent, identified as P. zopfii by PCR-RFLP, was successfully treated with amphotericin B (AMB) for 5 days, leading to the resolution of hematochezia. Notably, two isolates in our study were recovered from blood: one P. wickerhamii and one P. miyajii. Disseminated cases of protothecosis are unusual, occurring predominantly in immunocompromised hosts.19,20

The lack of standardized diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines of Prototheca poses a significant clinical challenge. Current management is largely empirical, with poor predictability of efficacy. This dilemma is largely attributed to the intrinsic resistance of Prototheca species to conventional antimicrobial agents, coupled with the poor correlation between in vitro susceptibility testing and clinical outcomes.13,21,22 Based on limited case data, AMB and its liposomal formulations are considered the backbone of therapy for severe or systemic infections, often combined with azoles.

Our findings indicate that itraconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole exhibit favorable anti-Prototheca activity, yielding identical MIC50 values of 1 μg/mL in human source isolates. Compared to clinical isolates tested by Proskurnicka et al (MIC50 = 4 μg/mL), the MIC50 values in our study were 2 dilution steps lower. The MIC range for amphotericin B was 0.25–1 μg/mL, consistent with previous reports.23,24 AMB is currently a first-line agent for systemic protothecosis, achieving an overall treatment success rate of 77%, compared to 67% (fluconazole), 66% (itraconazole), and 55% (ketoconazole).8 Nevertheless, a major drawback of AMB is its high cellular toxicity, which may lead to severe hepatorenal failure.25 Isavuconazole, a novel triazole agent approved for invasive aspergillosis and mucormycosis,20 demonstrated potent in vitro activity against Prototheca isolates from human, bovine, and dairy farm sources in this study (MIC range: 0.015–1 μg/mL), suggesting its potential for treating protothecosis. 5-fluorocytosine exhibited no activity, and fluconazole resistance was prevalent—findings consistent with previous reports.26 Echinocandins are generally ineffective against Prototheca due to the structural composition of their cell walls. Unlike fungal β-1,3-glucan, the Prototheca cell wall is primarily composed of polysaccharides linked by β-1,4-glycosidic bonds, rendering echinocandins ineffective.27 Notably, there was no significant distinction in drug susceptibility patterns based on the source (human vs bovine) or the clinical manifestation (cutaneous vs non-cutaneous).

Interestingly, levofloxacin, gentamicin and tigecycline showed certain in vitro activity against Prototheca. For instance, Shahan et al reported that Prototheca exhibited sensitivity to gentamicin, with MIC values ranging from 0.3 to 0.9 μg/mL. While these in vitro MICs fall entirely within the achievable therapeutic serum concentration range (4–10 μg/mL), it is crucial to emphasize that these observations are strictly preliminary. Despite this in vitro susceptibility, documented clinical treatment failures have occurred following the administration of gentamicin.28 Further in vivo and clinical studies are required to validate whether these agents offer any true therapeutic benefit. Inter-species analysis revealed that azoles—including isavuconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole, and voriconazole—exhibited significantly lower activity against P. bovis than against P. ciferrii. This corroborates prior findings that P. ciferrii was generally more susceptible to the majority of azoles, such as efinaconazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole, and ravuconazole, compared to P. bovis.23 This higher susceptibility in P. ciferrii may be attributed to species-specific variations in Erg11p binding affinity or membrane permeability. Further studies are warranted. And tigecycline, gentamicin, and amikacin showed significantly higher potency against P. ciferrii than against P. bovis and P. wickerhamii.

Different strains were distributed across distinct clusters, and P. wickerhamii isolates constituted the predominant clade, which exhibited a highly complex branching topology reflecting substantial intraspecific genetic heterogeneity among isolates from both human and bovine mastitis. Nevertheless, we observed that certain isolates from different sources (eg, patients, food, and the environment) clustered into tight sub-clades, even sharing identical or highly similar sequence types across provinces. This suggests the potential for widespread regional dissemination of specific clones.

Genomic advancements are transforming Prototheca research by establishing whole-genome analysis as a pivotal means to decipher the molecular basis of drug metabolism and resistance. These insights are expected to catalyze the development of novel anti-Prototheca therapies.29–31 Concurrently, a range of innovative agents—including antimicrobial peptides and metal nanoparticles—have emerged as promising candidates, diversifying the pipeline for anti-Prototheca drug discovery.32–34

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and long-term strain storage resulted in incomplete demographic data; future prospective studies with rigorous data collection are needed to address this. Furthermore, the limited sample size for P. zopfii may restrict statistical power, underscoring the necessity of larger-scale multicenter investigations. Finally, the lack of correlation between in vitro susceptibility and clinical outcomes limits the prediction of therapeutic efficacy.

In conclusion, amphotericin B and isavuconazole demonstrated notable in vitro anti-Prototheca activity against both human and bovine mastitis isolates. Also, significant inter-species variability in drug susceptibility was observed. Given the limited clinical data and inconsistent treatment outcomes reported in the literature, these in vitro findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence. This study highlights the critical importance of species-specific susceptibility patterns and underscores the necessity of individualized therapy guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing, which may inform future surveillance and treatment strategies.
