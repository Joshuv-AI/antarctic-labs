Determinants of early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding: a qualitative meta-synthesis
Authors: Jiaqi Lv, Yunhao Wang, Jiaqi Li, Chunling Xia, Jun Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast milk is recognized as the ideal source of nutrition for infants; however, the widespread early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding within the first six months postpartum constitutes a major public health challenge. Despite clear recommendations from the World Health Organization and strong maternal intentions to exclusively breastfeed, complex factors continue to lead to involuntary interruption, and it is estimated that only 44% of infants worldwide are exclusively breastfed. This unplanned cessation not only deprives infants of irreplaceable nutritional and immunological benefits and increases the risk of morbidity, but also negatively affects maternal psychological well-being and results in substantial economic losses. Current evidence on the determinants of this interruption remains scattered across single-region studies, with a lack of cross-cultural and systematic synthesis of qualitative findings. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically review and meta-synthesize global qualitative evidence to identify the multidimensional determinants of early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding, thereby providing an evidence base for developing multilevel and effective intervention strategies.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase for publications from March 6, 2021 to March 6, 2026. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research, and the JBI meta-aggregation approach was used to summarize and synthesize the findings.ResultsA total of 33 studies were included, from which 252 findings were extracted. These findings were grouped into 10 categories and further synthesized into three integrated findings: mothers turning to supplementary feeding amid feeding uncertainty and physical and psychological burden; insufficient social support systems weakening the continuation of exclusive breastfeeding; and sociocultural factors, infrastructure gaps, and the commercial power of formula milk jointly normalizing supplementary feeding.ConclusionsEarly interruption of exclusive breastfeeding is influenced by multilevel factors involving mothers, families and communities, healthcare services, work systems, sociocultural contexts, and commercial marketing. Future efforts should move beyond mother-focused health education and establish comprehensive support strategies covering families, communities, healthcare institutions, workplaces, and policy settings to reduce avoidable interruption of exclusive breastfeeding.Trial registrationPROSPERO Number CRD420251179108.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28067-9.Keywords: Exclusive breastfeeding, Interruption, Determinants, Meta-synthesis

Introduction

Breast milk is widely recognized as the ideal and safest natural food for infants. It provides comprehensive nutrition, enhances immune function, reduces the risk of infant infection and mortality, and promotes infant growth, development, and long-term health [1–4]. For mothers, breastfeeding can reduce the risk of chronic diseases such as ovarian cancer, breast cancer, type 2 diabetes, and hypertensive heart disease [5, 6]. Breastfeeding provides unique and irreplaceable benefits for maternal and child health. Improving early-life population health and reducing health inequalities is an important public health strategy [7, 8].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends initiating breastfeeding within the first hour of birth and be exclusively breastfed for the first six months of life (without no other foods or liquids are provided, including water) [9]. However, many infants do not receive optimal breastfeeding. Globally, only approximately 44% of infants aged 0–6 months are exclusively breastfed, and a considerable proportion of mother–infant dyads face interruption of exclusive breastfeeding within the first six months postpartum [9–11]. Although most mothers initially intend to exclusively breastfeed, they are forced to stop and introduce formula milk or other substitutes for various reasons.

For infants under six months of age, especially preterm infants, the nutritional and immunological support provided by breast milk cannot be replaced by any substitute. Premature interruption of exclusive breastfeeding may increase infant morbidity and mortality [12]. At the same time, failing to achieve one’s intended feeding goals can cause mothers considerable frustration, anxiety, and self-blame, undermine their confidence to continue any form of breastfeeding, and hinder the establishment of a positive parent–child relationship [13]. In addition, studies have shown that, because breastfeeding recommendations are not followed and formula or other substitutes are introduced early, the additional health and human development benefits of breastfeeding are not realized, leading to global economic losses exceeding US$300 billion annually [8]. Therefore, identifying and intervening in the risk factors that lead to early interruption is urgently needed to improve exclusive breastfeeding rates and maternal and infant health outcomes.

Although research on the determinants of early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding has increased, existing studies are often confined to single countries or regions and lack cross-cultural and cross-regional systematic integration. The determinants of early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding may vary substantially across cultural backgrounds, social customs, and healthcare systems. At present, there is a lack of comprehensive qualitative evidence synthesis that integrates multicultural perspectives and systematically identifies common and context-specific determinants. Interruption of exclusive breastfeeding is the result of multiple interacting factors [14], yet the existing evidence mainly consists of scattered qualitative studies or quantitative cross-sectional surveys, which may not fully or deeply capture the nature of the phenomenon. Therefore, this study systematically searched global qualitative studies and conducted a meta-synthesis of findings related to postpartum interruption of exclusive breastfeeding to systematically analyze its determinants and provide an evidence base for developing layered and diversified intervention strategies.

Methods

The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251179108). This qualitative evidence synthesis was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA statement and the ENTREQ guideline for enhancing transparency in qualitative evidence synthesis. Data extraction and synthesis were guided by the JBI meta-aggregation methodology. This qualitative meta-synthesis was conducted to aggregate evidence on the determinants of early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding during the first six months postpartum. Specifically, this review aimed to answer the following questions: What factors contribute to the early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding?

Search strategy

We searched multiple databases, including PubMed, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase. The publication period searched ranged from March 6, 2021 to March 6, 2026. A combination of medical subject headings and free-text terms was used. Search terms included “breastfeeding”, “interruption”, “cessation”, “discontinuation”, “qualitative research”, “phenomenology”, “grounded theory”, and “ethnography”. The detailed search strategy is provided in Additional file 1.

Study selection

EndNote 20 was used to remove duplicate records. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, two systematically trained researchers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review. The two researchers cross-checked the results throughout the screening process, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

Quality assessment

Two trained researchers independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research [15]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher. The checklist contains 10 items, each rated as “yes”, “no”, or “unclear”. Studies fully meeting the criteria were rated grade A, those partially meeting the criteria were rated grade B, and those not meeting the criteria were rated grade C.

Data extraction and synthesis

Two trained researchers used a predefined form to extract data from the included studies, including author, year, country, study design, data collection methods, participants, phenomenon of interest, main findings, study findings, and illustrative quotations. The information was verified by a third researcher.

The JBI meta-aggregation approach was used to analyze, summarize, and synthesize the qualitative findings. Researchers trained in qualitative research repeatedly read, analyzed, and compared the included studies and interpreted each study finding. Similar findings were aggregated into categories, which were then synthesized into integrated findings.

Results

Search results

A total of 1,823 records were identified. After removing duplicates, 1,207 records remained. Following title/abstract screening and full-text assessment, 33 studies were ultimately included. The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Characteristics and quality of the included studies

A total of 33 studies were included [16–48], comprising 28 qualitative studies [16, 17, 19, 20, 22–36, 40–48] and 5 mixed-methods studies [18, 21, 37–39]. The study locations covered Asia [21, 30, 33, 39, 41, 42, 44, 48], Africa [16, 23, 24, 26, 27, 29, 35, 40], Europe [19, 28, 31, 32, 36, 43, 46], South America [25, 38, 47], North America [18, 34, 37, 45], Central America [17], the Caribbean [22], and Oceania [20]. Participants included mothers, fathers, other family members, healthcare providers, policymakers, and other stakeholders. The basic characteristics of the included studies are presented in Additional file 1.

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Among the 33 included studies, 1 was rated as grade A and 32 as grade B, indicating generally good methodological quality. Most studies performed well in terms of congruity among the research question, methodology, data collection, data analysis, and interpretation of findings, as well as representation of participants’ views, ethical approval, and the evidentiary basis for conclusions. Methodological limitations were mainly concentrated in the reporting of congruity between philosophical stance and methodology, the researchers’ cultural or theoretical positioning, and reflexive reporting on the interaction between the researcher and the research process, as reflected by “unclear” or “no” ratings for Q1, Q6, and Q7. Meta-synthesis results

Synthesized finding 1: Mothers turn to supplementary feeding amid feeding uncertainty and physical and psychological burden

Difficulties in judging breast milk adequacy and infant satiety cues

Many mothers reported difficulty judging milk volume and infant satiety cues, which caused considerable anxiety; their doubts about whether breast milk was sufficient were alleviated only after formula supplementation [34]. Perceived insufficient milk was an important reason for premature interruption of exclusive breastfeeding. Infant crying, apparent appetite, restless sleep, frequent feeding, and weight gain below expectations were often interpreted by mothers as signals of insufficient milk or poor milk quality, leading to supplementation with formula or complementary foods [16, 21, 22, 25, 26, 28, 29, 32, 34, 36, 47]. When the infant’s condition improved after formula or complementary foods were introduced, mothers perceived this as confirming their previous suspicion of insufficient milk [35, 38, 40], and formula or complementary foods were even used functionally as convenient tools to soothe crying infants [35, 37, 44].

“One night I got up and I was feeding him, he drank and drank. So, I put him down [to sleep] but even though his [diapers] were dry and I hushed him, he wouldn’t fall asleep. To me this is because his belly is not full; so that is why I had to buy the formula” [22].

“I did let him latch on. I just felt like he wasn’t getting enough so I gave him the bottle. I was thinking I should breastfeed for a minute but then I’m one of the ones that thinks that the baby is not getting enough, so I was like, I want the baby to eat so I will just go ahead and give him this formula” [34].

“They give us problems, they just cry and cry, but if you feed them, they are alright, they don’t bother you and cry incessantly” [35].

Misconceptions about exclusive breastfeeding and infant and young child feeding practices

Erroneous personal beliefs also drove decisions to introduce supplementary feeding. Some mothers did not know what exclusive breastfeeding meant [40], whereas others, even when aware of professional recommendations, still considered their own intuition more reliable than hospital guidance and adhered to personal theories such as early introduction of complementary foods to help infants adapt to eating and the belief that infants need water [16, 18, 19, 29, 41, 44]. Some mothers also believed that breast milk alone was insufficient to satisfy infants or could even cause constipation [23, 30, 35, 40, 47], while their awareness of the potential harms of formula milk was limited [45, 47].

“We, as older people, we drink water when we are hot because it makes us to feel good. And the kid too, sometimes they are hot, and they need water to cool down. So why does the doctor always say we should not be giving the kids water? I think it is not okay” [18].

“Breastmilk is water and water does not satisfy, it is just that it is colored, and is milk. Alone it will never satisfy the baby. You will always need to also feed the child solid food on a side” [23].

Maternal and infant physiological difficulties weaken mothers’ ability to maintain exclusive breastfeeding

Breast problems were the main physiological reason prompting mothers to introduce formula or complementary foods. Cracked nipples, bleeding, mastitis, and severe pain during breastfeeding made it difficult for mothers to continue exclusive breastfeeding [20, 21, 24, 32, 36, 38, 46, 48]. A lack of supportive information further exacerbated these problems. Faced with such difficulties, mothers often lacked effective knowledge and solutions and could only rely on complementary foods or formula to cope [21, 26, 36, 37, 40, 44, 47]. Fatigue and sleep deprivation also constituted barriers; frequent nighttime breastfeeding exhausted mothers and prompted them to use formula feeding to gain rest [46]. In addition, short birth intervals could leave mothers insufficiently recovered, affect milk production, or require them to breastfeed two children simultaneously [42]. Some mothers also mentioned neonatal jaundice as a reason for switching to formula feeding [21, 41].

“My baby was on phototherapy. When I went to feed him every 45 minutes on the third day, he cried shortly after each feeding. The nurse called me whenever he cried and asked me whether I was feeding him enough milk and had enough milk for him. I was in pain from the wound and also had a fever. I couldn’t sleep and decided to switch to formula feeding. That’s why I started supplementing with formula. Later, I think it is fine to give him formula milk if I don’t have enough breastmilk” [21].

Psychological exhaustion and constrained autonomy

At the psychological level, many mothers had weak motivation and low self-efficacy; once difficulties arose, they tended to give up rather than seek technical adjustments or assistance [41, 46]. Social and family expectations of the image of a “good mother” placed mothers under pressure to succeed at breastfeeding. Constant anxiety that their milk might run out created ongoing psychological strain, leaving them overwhelmed and ultimately leading to premature cessation of breastfeeding [32, 36]. Conversely, some women prioritized their personal well-being over breastfeeding. After pregnancy and childbirth had already occupied their bodies and lives, they were no longer willing to continue sacrificing their personal lives, perceiving breastfeeding as restrictive and preventing them from resuming social activities and freedom to go out [23, 24, 37, 46, 47]. In addition, some women had limited bodily autonomy and viewed the breast only as an organ for satisfying a partner’s sexual needs rather than as a part of their own body under their control; this narrow perception of breast function also weakened their autonomy in continuing breastfeeding [34].

“Well, it wasn’t that I physically could not cope with his demand, but psychologically I couldn’t cope any longer and I couldn’t cope with thinking anymore of what am I going to do when I run out of milk and well I was a bit upset about this” [32].

“You can’t do anything because (of) this child, you are (like) a walking . fridge” [24].

Synthesized finding 2: Insufficient social support systems weaken the continuation of exclusive breastfeeding

Insufficient family support and pressure from elders

On the one hand, exclusive breastfeeding conflicted with feeding traditions maintained in some families, making mothers more vulnerable to discouragement when difficulties arose. Previous family feeding experiences influenced mothers’ judgments about the feasibility of exclusive breastfeeding and made formula feeding seem familiar and acceptable. On the other hand, family members, especially mothers, mothers-in-law, grandmothers, and other female elders, were important influences on infant feeding decisions. Their limited understanding of the importance of exclusive breastfeeding often led them to recommend formula or other complementary foods based on their own childcare experiences, traditional beliefs, or interpretations of infant crying. Some mothers were more inclined to follow elders’ advice than healthcare professionals’ recommendations and chose mixed feeding; even when they accepted healthcare professionals’ advice on exclusive breastfeeding, they compromised because of dependence on elders’ care, pressure from co-residence, intergenerational authority, or the desire to avoid conflict [17, 21, 23–26, 29, 31, 35, 42, 46, 48].

“My mother-in-law and my mother told me that I had to give him formula because breast milk does not fill him and that I had to give him water. But the paediatrician said the opposite. They insisted so much that I gave him water before six months. I did it because I wanted to avoid conflicts” [17].

In addition, fathers rarely participated in breastfeeding or insisted on mixed feeding, and mothers’ sacrifices and efforts to maintain breastfeeding were often not understood or were even opposed by family members, weakening their motivation to continue breastfeeding [40, 46, 47]. Without anyone to share responsibilities, some mothers were forced to assume multiple roles involving childcare, housework, and work, struggling across competing responsibilities and becoming physically and mentally exhausted. Structural factors such as unstable family relationships and single parenthood further intensified the absence of support [25, 28, 37, 45]. Families with multiple children also posed challenges: mothers found it difficult to attend to the emotional needs of several children while breastfeeding, as each child required maternal companionship and attention, making exclusive breastfeeding difficult to sustain in terms of time and energy [25].

“What made me switch to formula all the way was cause when I got home.I was by myself and trying to clean and take care of myself and take care of him and pump my breasts and all that stuff it was just too much” [45].

“If you do not have the support of your partner and you have a person next to you who continues doing a bit their own thing, without getting involved, then even worse because you have to continue doing everything you did before plus breastfeeding” [46].

Negative community influences

The feeding practices of other mothers in the community had a clear modeling effect. Some mothers imitated others after seeing them introduce formula [18, 41, 48]. In addition, negative comments and persuasion from relatives and friends interfered with mothers’ efforts to continue exclusive breastfeeding [35, 44, 46].

“After some time, they were mixing the milk in the freezer with formula. So, the reason they were giving them that [was because] the breast milk was losing some of its nutrition, so they needed to add some formula. So, after I left the hospital, I started doing that too, mixing the formula and the breast milk” [18].

Work, employment, and economic pressure

Although many mothers understood exclusive breastfeeding recommendations, unstable employment, lack of maternity leave, insufficient time, and economic pressure forced many women to return to work early after childbirth, making direct breastfeeding difficult to continue [20, 23, 25, 27, 31, 37, 46]. Infants therefore had to be cared for by substitute feeders, and when reliable caregivers or sufficient stored breast milk were unavailable at home, the introduction of formula or complementary foods became a common coping strategy [16, 22, 30, 34, 39, 44, 47]. Meanwhile, many workplaces did not provide breastfeeding-friendly environments, lacking basic facilities such as private pumping spaces and refrigerators; some workplaces even showed severe discrimination against breastfeeding, making it difficult for working women to pump milk and maintain exclusive breastfeeding [24]. Heavy workloads and a lack of reasonable and flexible breastfeeding time or lactation breaks were another prominent issue. Mothers were unable to pump on time, resulting in breast engorgement and reduced milk production, which further weakened their ability to continue breastfeeding [21, 23, 33, 38, 41, 46]. For women in informal or highly mobile employment, continued direct breastfeeding was even more difficult. They were often unable to return home during work to breastfeed and found it difficult to pump, store, and arrange substitute feeding [39].

“Because her mother left (to work) since morning. Breast milk is only in the morning and afternoon. At midday, when the breast milk (stockpile) run out, then (I gave her) water” [30].

“My boss told me straight up, ‘Please don’t think you are going to get time off to do your lady boob things’, and I was like, ‘thanks’” [24].

“I can’t keep going back to my house to feed my child breastmilk every time he/she is hungry while I am vending, I have to get him/her habituated to ‘upar ka doodh’” [39].

Insufficient healthcare support and inconsistent advice

Healthcare providers paid insufficient attention to and inadequately identified breastfeeding problems. Antenatal and postnatal education often remained limited to repeating the benefits of breastfeeding or providing general advice, and failed to detect problems arising during breastfeeding and offer targeted solutions in a timely manner. This may have partly resulted from healthcare providers’ own lack of breastfeeding support knowledge and skills, making it difficult for them to provide effective help to mothers [28, 43, 47]. At the same time, inconsistent advice among healthcare providers occurred from time to time, further increasing mothers’ confusion and unease [43]. As authorities in mothers’ eyes, some healthcare providers treated formula or complementary foods as a convenient solution to problems [25, 38, 41, 47]; in more serious cases, formula was recommended or provided for profit, directly violating the International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes [43].

“I felt really stupid because she told me all sorts of things that I already knew” [28].

“The information is not provided correctly . the technique of how the baby had to latch on, for example, was never explained to us, so that made the baby suck air, so that it would not fill up, it would fill up with gas. So, the easiest thing the doctor could do was to prescribe formula ‘Give her formula, it’s much easier’” [47].

“The agreement for commercialisation of breast milk substitutes is not complied with, it is almost always violated in all centres. The professionals themselves do not comply with it and there is no follow-up to see who complies and who does not, and there is no repercussion for those who do not comply” [43].

Synthesized finding 3: Sociocultural factors, infrastructure, and the commercial power of formula jointly normalize supplementary feeding

Social climate and gaps in the support infrastructure chain

A social climate favoring formula feeding and intolerance toward breastfeeding in public places coexisted, placing breastfeeding mothers in a difficult position. On the one hand, they were expected to meet the standard of being a “good mother” by breastfeeding; on the other hand, they experienced shame and exclusion when breastfeeding in public [37, 38, 45, 46]. In addition, long-standing social scrutiny of women’s appearance drew mothers into pressure to return to their pre-pregnancy bodies, while women who fully devoted themselves to breastfeeding clearly did not fit this standard [46].

“Others have insinuated it’s dirty or nasty” [37].

For women who objectively had insufficient milk and whose difficulties could not be resolved, the lack of alternative support systems such as human milk banks made formula milk the only feasible option [47]. Existing programs and institutions intended to support women also had limitations. For working women, childcare centers could have served as sites to support breastfeeding; however, many childcare centers refused to accept breast milk because of hygiene and safety concerns, restricting the continuation of breastfeeding [25]. In some public assistance programs, high accessibility of formula may also promote unnecessary supplementation. When mothers can easily obtain formula while breastfeeding support is insufficient, formula is more likely to become the default option [37, 45].

“Even if he went to a nursery, they don’t give him mother’s milk, even if you take it there from home they won’t give because of concerns about hygiene, and the fact there are other children and people there” [25].

“Formula feeding cost more but thank God we got WIC. Cause if it wasn’t for WIC, I would probably try to stick to more breastfeeding” [45].

Influence of formula milk commercial marketing

In the information environment, breastfeeding promotion was often overly idealized and failed to address mothers’ real difficulties, leaving mothers without realistic expectations of actual challenges and making them feel misunderstood or even blamed when difficulties arose [43, 46]. At the same time, formula milk marketing was pervasive. In addition to traditional advertising, some “mom bloggers” influenced other mothers’ feeding decisions by drawing on their identities as mothers [48]. In commercial promotion, formula milk fully catered to mothers’ psychological needs in difficult situations and was portrayed as an alternative that could transfer childcare responsibilities, balance work and childcare, and be used conveniently when going out. Its rapid preparation, visible feeding volume, and easy availability were also emphasized as ways to reduce uncertainty and anxiety about breastfeeding [24, 25, 28, 32, 33, 36, 43–47]. However, while commercial marketing emphasized these conveniences, it avoided discussing the potential harms of formula milk, making it difficult for mothers to make decisions based on complete information. Unlike breastfeeding promotion, which emphasizes maternal effort and persistence, formula marketing focuses more on offering mothers an apparently simple path that involves less pain and uncertainty, making mothers facing difficulties more susceptible to its appeal and more likely to discontinue exclusive breastfeeding.

“When she wakes up in the middle of the night and she wants a bottle.my boyfriend can wake up and make her a bottle.so I can get some rest” [45].

“I think it is convenient to use artificial milk as a supplement to breast milk when I must cope with the limited production of milk by my body. It’s a gift for a working woman like me” [33].

Discussion

Feeding uncertainty underlying perceived insufficient milk

This study found that perceived insufficient milk and misconceptions about feeding are important reasons for early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding. Existing health education is disconnected from real feeding contexts: much education overemphasizes the benefits of breast milk for infants while neglecting the practical challenges of breastfeeding, leaving mothers with an overly simplified understanding of breastfeeding [49]. When mothers face complex and uncertain feeding situations, they lack a cognitive framework that helps them interpret infant behavior, judge whether intake is adequate, and manage common problems; they are unable to distinguish whether crying, frequent feeding, night waking, or prolonged sucking represent normal developmental behaviors, temporary supply–demand regulation, or objectively inadequate feeding.

Therefore, healthcare providers should offer a concrete and practical set of assessment methods, such as judging whether infant intake is adequate by combining urine output, stool patterns, weight change, swallowing, effective sucking, and mental responsiveness, rather than relying solely on crying, sleep duration, or breast fullness. At the same time, mothers should be informed about the normal range of cluster feeding, night waking, short and frequent sucking, and episodic crying; the reasons why no additional substances are needed and the potential harms of supplementation should be explained; and clear guidance should be provided on when professional help is needed. For mothers who express anxiety about milk supply during pregnancy or hospitalization, focused assessment and individualized guidance should be provided before discharge to interrupt the pathway from uncertainty to premature cessation.

Early postpartum breastfeeding difficulties and support gaps

More than 70% of women experience breastfeeding difficulties, including breast pain, cracked nipples, mastitis, and infant refusal of the breast, most of which occur within the first postpartum month [50]. However, postpartum breastfeeding support is usually brief and lacks comprehensive assessment of breastfeeding and of women’s transition into motherhood [49]. This mismatch in timing means that mothers are most lacking in professional support precisely when they need it most. This may explain why simply increasing breastfeeding initiation during hospitalization does not necessarily translate into sustained exclusive breastfeeding. Successful latch during hospitalization, brief direct breastfeeding, or health education cannot ensure that mothers will be able to independently manage breast pain, perceived insufficient milk, infant crying, and other problems after returning home. Without sufficient assessment before discharge and continuous follow-up after discharge, breastfeeding problems may shift from technical issues that could be corrected early into life crises borne by mothers alone.

The impact of breastfeeding pain and fatigue on mothers is not only physiological. Some mothers continue breastfeeding despite pain while also bearing the pressure that they must persist; when they can no longer continue, they often experience pronounced guilt. This suggests that, in the early postpartum period, breastfeeding is not only a feeding behavior but also a highly emotional and moralized maternal practice. If healthcare providers and family members emphasize persistence without responding to pain, fatigue, and anxiety, giving up is not merely a change in feeding mode but also an escape from the ongoing pressure of self-blame.

At the other end of this pathway, some women, after the bodily demands of pregnancy and childbirth, wish to regain social life and personal autonomy and are no longer willing to devote all of their body and time to infant care. Choosing to shorten or stop exclusive breastfeeding in order to prioritize personal well-being should not itself be judged. What truly needs to be examined is why, in a society lacking flexible work arrangements, public breastfeeding facilities, and substantive sharing of caregiving responsibilities, mothers are forced to make an either-or choice between self-care and maintaining breastfeeding.

This suggests that nursing practice should treat early postpartum breastfeeding difficulties as problems that can be screened, intervened upon, and followed up, rather than waiting for mothers to seek help. Before discharge, at least one complete breastfeeding observation should be conducted, including breastfeeding skills and knowledge, infant latch, sucking and swallowing function, maternal breast pain and injury scores, maternal concerns about milk supply, and confidence in continuing exclusive breastfeeding. For nipple pain, latch difficulties, early signs of mastitis, infant refusal of the breast, or jaundice, mothers’ awareness of modifiable barriers should be improved, and clear management pathways and referral criteria should be established to prevent mothers from directly using formula as a solution because of a lack of professional support [50]. At the same time, healthcare providers should use non-judgmental communication and allow mothers to express fatigue, pain, and ambivalence. In addition, breastfeeding support should not be limited to single-session education or a single postpartum time point. A continuum of support from pregnancy through hospitalization and postnatal follow-up should be established, with partners and other primary family caregivers included [51, 52]. Home visits can serve as an important means of connecting healthcare institutions with family contexts and providing breastfeeding technical guidance and emotional support [51]. In specific implementation, motivational interviewing, theory of planned behavior-based interventions, and cognitive behavioral therapy may be considered to enhance maternal self-efficacy [52].

Coordination and tensions in family and community support

This study found that family members, especially grandmothers and partners, have important influences on early postpartum feeding decisions. At the same time, the feeding practices of other mothers in the community have a clear modeling effect, and negative comments and persuasion from relatives and friends further interfere with mothers’ efforts to maintain exclusive breastfeeding. Mothers do not make decisions independently between professional knowledge and personal intention; rather, they make choices in an environment where family experience, community examples, and medical advice compete with one another.

Partner support plays an important role in breastfeeding support. However, due to cultural beliefs, lack of knowledge, insufficient leave, and other reasons, fathers’ participation in breastfeeding is generally inadequate in practice [53]. It should be clarified, however, that fathers’ participation does not necessarily translate into effective support. Especially in traditionally patriarchal societies, some seemingly positive forms of support, such as enthusiastically offering advice, emphasizing the importance of breastfeeding, and frequently being present and attentive, may unintentionally convey to mothers the message that “you cannot do this well on your own” or create a form of surveillance-like pressure associated with earlier cessation. Support can truly become a safeguard for exclusive breastfeeding rather than a burden only when it is based on sensitive awareness of mothers’ needs and respect for their autonomy [54].

Studies have shown that the knowledge level of primary caregivers is as important as that of mothers, and that the average collective knowledge level of the family is key to the feeding approach [55]. In rural areas and multigenerational households, grandmothers account for more than half of primary family caregivers and often serve as the actual main force in childcare with considerable influence [56]. However, grandmothers are not inherently opposed to breastfeeding; some are willing to learn and become nodes for disseminating information in the community [57]. They should therefore be regarded as important targets of health education. Their caregiving experience should be respected, and evidence-based knowledge should be provided so that they can shift from transmitters of traditional experience to collaborators in evidence-based support. At the same time, family-oriented education should not remain only at the level of recognizing benefits; it should also focus on concrete and practical skills, such as assisting with correct breastfeeding positioning, preparing nutritious meals for mothers, and sharing housework. At the community level, community nurses, peer support groups, and mothers who have successfully breastfed can be used to share experiences, while unverified experiential information and blind modeling of formula use should be avoided.

Breaks and reconstruction in healthcare-system breastfeeding support

This synthesis found that the healthcare system still has clear deficiencies in early postpartum breastfeeding support, mainly reflected in insufficient attention, limited counseling quality, and weak institutional regulation. Mothers expect qualified, individualized, consistent, and respectful counseling services and hope that partners can be included in breastfeeding support [58]. However, in reality, breastfeeding support is often constrained by staff shortages, heavy workloads, lack of funding, and inadequate space [59]. As a result, support services are not only insufficiently accessible, but even when obtained, their quality often fails to meet mothers’ needs. Breastfeeding support tends to remain superficial and lacks in-depth assessment and feedback, making it difficult to identify or respond to mothers’ difficulties and questions during breastfeeding, and preventing mothers from receiving effective confirmation of their own breastfeeding ability. This forces many mothers to seek support from private lactation consultants at their own expense, exacerbating breastfeeding inequities associated with economic disparities [60].

There are also clear gaps in the education of professionals themselves. Mechanisms of lactation regulation, common infant behaviors, and soothing strategies are rarely included in training curricula, and clear guidelines are lacking. Consequently, when faced with breastfeeding difficulties, healthcare providers often lack the professional skills to identify and solve problems and may even hold the same misconceptions as parents [61]. In addition, implementation and regulation of the International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes by hospitals and governments remain insufficient. Some healthcare professionals and public assistance programs apply overly loose review of formula use, allowing large amounts of formula supplementation without clear medical indications.

Therefore, repair of the healthcare system needs to proceed collaboratively across multiple levels. Breastfeeding support skills should be incorporated into core training for healthcare providers so that they can not only educate mothers about the benefits of breastfeeding but also solve mothers’ practical problems. Lactation consultants and community health workers can be incorporated into the breastfeeding support system to reduce the workload of clinical healthcare providers and expand the accessibility of professional support [62]. At the same time, before recommending formula supplementation, healthcare institutions should first conduct a comprehensive assessment of the mother and infant and record the specific reasons. For mother–infant dyads with medical indications requiring short-term supplementation, strategies to maintain lactation and return to exclusive breastfeeding should be provided simultaneously to prevent temporary supplementation from developing into long-term mixed feeding. Public assistance programs may establish feeding assessment and counseling procedures before formula provision and require documentation of medical indications or professional assessment opinions when necessary.

Restrictions imposed by work systems on breastfeeding

Our findings show that many mothers interrupted exclusive breastfeeding because they returned to work. As increasing numbers of women enter the workforce, they face the need to balance work and maternal responsibilities, and workplace parenting policies are crucial for breastfeeding. In work environments that provide maternity leave or childcare services and support breastfeeding, women are significantly more likely to continue breastfeeding after returning to work [63]. However, only 54 countries globally provide at least 18 weeks of maternity leave, and many countries have lactation breaks but lack corresponding workplace policies and regulations on breastfeeding facilities [64]. Workplace protections for breastfeeding are therefore generally insufficient, and mothers still need to travel back and forth to feed their infants. Busy work schedules, non-office environments, and shift work further squeeze the space for breastfeeding. Workplace culture is also an important factor; particularly in team-based work systems, breastfeeding mothers often feel pressure from colleagues and worry about being perceived as using work time or reducing work engagement [63]. For women in informal employment, the situation is even more severe. Because of their labor status, they are often excluded from legal protections and therefore face crises such as loss of income and unemployment after childbirth, leaving them unable to stay at home on leave to breastfeed [65].

Effective workplace support requires combined interventions across multiple levels, such as paid maternity leave, fixed pumping time, and private and hygienic pumping spaces [51]. For women in informal employment, cash assistance, community childcare, flexible follow-up, and primary care services can help compensate for the limited coverage of traditional labor protections [66]. At the same time, return-to-work planning should be included as a routine component of postnatal follow-up, with guidance provided on pumping frequency, storage methods, substitute feeding arrangements, and communication with childcare providers. Childcare institutions should establish standardized procedures for receiving, storing, and feeding expressed breast milk so that mothers can continue breastfeeding after returning to work.

Sociocultural contradictions and the influence of formula milk commercial marketing

This synthesis found that deeply rooted traditional beliefs unsupportive of exclusive breastfeeding persist in some regions. At the same time, breastfeeding is constrained by contradictory social attitudes: on the one hand, mothers are subjected to moral pressure under the standard that a good mother should breastfeed; on the other hand, breastfeeding in public is implicitly or explicitly rejected. When mothers cannot breastfeed conveniently and naturally in public, going out, socializing, and participating in public life come into conflict with breastfeeding. For young mothers, breastfeeding may also conflict with social expectations regarding body recovery, appearance management, social freedom, and intimate relationships. Thus, formula is not only a feeding substitute but also a tool through which mothers maintain public life and personal image.

Responses to this issue should not stop at adding mother-and-baby rooms and other physical facilities; breastfeeding needs to become a visible and freely accommodated part of everyday urban life [67]. Dedicated breastfeeding spaces may unintentionally convey the implicit message that breastfeeding should be isolated and hidden, thereby reinforcing a stigmatized public breastfeeding environment. When society offers only hidden solutions without simultaneously challenging the cultural sexualization of breasts, what truly needs correction is not the absence of facilities but the social norm that excludes breastfeeding from public space. Only when breastfeeding is no longer automatically excluded from public life can it shift from an individual maternal responsibility to a public health issue collectively supported by society. During the transition period before sociocultural change can occur rapidly, however, providing convenient and safe breastfeeding spaces remains necessary to protect breastfeeding mothers’ basic dignity and right to go out. In addition, for communities with traditional feeding customs, community health workers, elder-involved education, and peer support should be used to foster dialogue between evidence-based knowledge and local cultural experience, rather than simply negating traditional experience.

In response to the many breastfeeding problems and barriers described above, formula milk seems to offer a perfect solution; however, behind this appearance of convenience lies a carefully designed commercial logic. Formula marketing frames normal infant behavior as a problem requiring intervention [68] and packages formula as a solution for promoting sleep, enhancing immunity, supporting growth, or liberating mothers [69]. Some marketing distorts breastfeeding advocacy into a form of moral coercion against women’s autonomy and then presents formula as a tool that supports women’s freedom of choice, enables partners to participate in feeding, and frees mothers from sole feeding responsibility. It creates a false opposition between breastfeeding and women’s liberation and uses the language of female empowerment to package commercial interests [70], redirecting mothers’ legitimate pursuit of autonomy and rest toward consumer choices rather than structural demands such as paid maternity leave, breastfeeding-friendly workplaces, and fair sharing of childcare responsibilities.

Social media provides mothers with a platform for peer support and experience sharing, but it also offers formula companies a more concealed channel for marketing. Some mom bloggers and other influencers publish paid promotional content for formula while presenting it as authentic sharing, and algorithms precisely push such content to pregnant women and mothers. Commercial promotion thus appears in the form of personal experience, making it difficult for mothers to distinguish [70]. For mothers who are experiencing feeding anxiety, knowledge deficits, or fragile motivation, such seemingly close and trustworthy content may influence decisions more easily than traditional advertising. Current regulatory measures have not yet effectively addressed this issue; platform rules are inconsistent, disclosure mechanisms are incomplete, and significant gaps remain in regulatory enforcement [71].

Therefore, promoting exclusive breastfeeding requires responding to mothers’ real difficulties, especially defective systems and policies [72], while also regulating commercial marketing. Only when breastfeeding support can provide solutions as concrete, practical, and anxiety-reducing as formula does will mothers not naturally turn to product-based solutions when difficulties arise. Breastfeeding advocacy should also guard against becoming moral pressure; it should both respect mothers’ right to make choices based on full information and remain alert to commercial marketing that undermines breastfeeding in the name of supporting women.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the included studies were highly heterogeneous in terms of region and cultural background. The studies covered Asia, Africa, Europe, North America, South America, and Oceania. Countries and regions differed substantially in maternity leave policies, healthcare service models, family structures, grandparental involvement in caregiving, public breastfeeding environments, formula availability, and infant feeding cultures. These differences enabled this study to understand common determinants of early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding across diverse contexts, but they may also affect the consistency of the synthesized findings. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted and applied in light of specific cultural norms, healthcare systems, and social policy contexts. In addition, the thickness of evidence varied across themes. In this study, themes such as perceived insufficient milk, misinterpretation of infant behavior, breast pain, pressure from family elders, inadequate healthcare support, and difficulties returning to work appeared repeatedly across multiple studies and were relatively well supported. By contrast, themes such as insufficient human milk banks, childcare institutions refusing to accept breast milk, formula accessibility through public assistance programs, and digital marketing, although highly relevant in practice, appeared less frequently in the included studies and were partly derived from a small number of studies. Therefore, these findings can serve as important clues for future interventions and research, but their generalizability and magnitude of influence require further verification in additional primary studies. Some primary studies also had insufficient methodological reporting. The quality appraisal showed that the included studies were generally of good quality, with most performing well in terms of research question, data collection, data analysis, ethical approval, and representation of participants’ voices. However, some studies did not adequately state the researchers’ cultural or theoretical positioning and rarely reflected on the interaction between the researchers and the research process. In qualitative research, insufficient reporting of researcher positioning and reflexivity may affect the transparency of data interpretation and the credibility of meta-synthesis findings. Finally, this study used a qualitative meta-synthesis approach, which can provide an in-depth understanding of the experiences, contexts, and mechanisms underlying early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding, but cannot determine the relative importance of each determinant or establish causal relationships among factors. For example, this study can show that perceived insufficient milk, family pressure, inadequate healthcare support, and work-system constraints often co-occur in the process of exclusive breastfeeding interruption, but it cannot determine which factor has the greatest effect or the temporal sequence among factors. Future research could build theoretical models based on the findings of this study and further verify the pathways among different factors through longitudinal, mixed-methods, or intervention studies. Future work should also focus on evaluating the actual effects of interventions such as early postpartum proactive follow-up, family-based education, integration of lactation consultants, combined workplace support, childcare-institution breast milk support, and regulation of digital formula marketing.

Conclusion

This review used the JBI meta-aggregation approach to synthesize qualitative evidence on the determinants of early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding. The findings indicate that early interruption of exclusive breastfeeding results from the combined effects of maternal individual difficulties and insufficient external support. Perceived insufficient milk, breastfeeding-related physical and psychological burden, family and community influences, gaps in healthcare support, work and childcare constraints, sociocultural environments, and formula marketing jointly drive the occurrence and normalization of supplementary feeding. Future interventions should move beyond mother-centered education and develop multilevel support strategies involving families, communities, healthcare systems, workplaces, and policy regulation to reduce avoidable interruption of exclusive breastfeeding.
