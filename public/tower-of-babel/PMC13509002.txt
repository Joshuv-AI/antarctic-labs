Element-Specific Sensitivity of Lanthanoid Ions to Chiral Crystal Fields in K3[Ln2(NO3)9] Probed by 4f–4f Circular Dichroism
Authors: Yusuke Inomata, Chihiro Mochizuki, Tetsuya Kida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For chiral inorganic crystals composed of achiral components, chiral crystal fields around metal ions are expected to play a key role in the emergence of chiroptical properties. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra can serve as useful probes of such chiral crystal fields. Here, we report the optical resolution of K3[Ln2(NO3)9], a family of chiral potassium lanthanoid nitrates, and systematically investigate chiral crystal fields associated with 4f electrons in the solid state. The K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series (Ln = La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd) crystallizes in the chiral space groups P4132 and P4332, and both enantiomorphs are obtained by spontaneous resolution. Among the series, K3[Pr2(NO3)9], K3[Nd2(NO3)9], K3[Sm2(NO3)9], and K3[Eu2(NO3)9] show sharp CD bands arising from 4f–4f transitions, demonstrating the presence of chiral crystal fields around lanthanoid ions in a fully inorganic environment. The distorted, noncentrosymmetric coordination environment around the lanthanoid ion partially relaxes the Laporte-forbidden nature of 4f–4f transitions, leading to enhanced absorption and CD intensities. Notably, Sm3+ exhibits the largest absolute CD anisotropy factor (|g CD|), suggesting that it is particularly sensitive to the chiral crystal field among the lanthanoid ions investigated.

Introduction

Lanthanoid ions exhibit sharp absorption and emission spectra arising from 4f–4f transitions because the closed-shell 5s and 5p electrons shield the 4f electrons. The chiroptical properties of lanthanoid ions have attracted considerable attention owing to their characteristic electronic circular dichroism (CD) and circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) associated with 4f electrons. − However, the electronic properties of lanthanoid ions are less susceptible to modulation by external ligands or changes in the chemical environment because of the distinctive inner-shell nature of the 4f orbitals. In molecular complexes, the precise design of chiral ligands enables the expression of chiroptical properties by creating chiral environments around lanthanoid ions. ,, Recently, intrinsically chiral inorganic crystals have attracted considerable attention owing to their potential for solid-state chiroptical properties. − Although chiral inorganic crystals can be composed solely of achiral ions, their crystal structures lack rotoinversion symmetry, enabling the emergence of chiroptical properties. Chiral crystal fields around metal ions are believed to be the origin of these properties. Therefore, experimentally confirming such crystal fields in fully inorganic crystals is essential to understanding how the arrangement of achiral ions gives rise to chiral optical responses.

CD spectroscopy is an analytical method that probes the chiral crystal field spectroscopically. In a chiral coordination environment, the electronic absorption arising from the relaxation of parity-forbidden 4f–4f transitions increases and, simultaneously, sharp CD signals appear, providing evidence for the presence of a chiral field around the lanthanoid ions. , CD spectra arising from 4f–4f transitions have been intensively investigated for lanthanoid complexes in solution, for example, chiral Yb3+ complexes, ,,− cesium tetrakis (+)-3-heptafluorobutyrylcamphorate (hfbc) Ln­(iii) complexes and [Ln­(PW11O39)2]11– anions. Although solid-state CD spectra associated with 4f–4f transitions have been reported less frequently than those in solution, pioneering studies on Na3[Ln­(ODA)3]·2NaClO4·6H2O (ODA: oxidiacetate ligand, ―OOCCH2OCH2COO―) − demonstrated that such spectra can arise in crystals composed of achiral organic ligands and lanthanoid ions. For chiral inorganic crystals, a limited number of examples have been reported, including Pr3+ and Ho3+-doped LaBGeO5, K3[Pr2(NO3)9] and Nd-doped Bi12SiO20. Although these studies suggest the presence of chiral crystal fields around lanthanoid ions in inorganic crystals, systematic investigations remain necessary to elucidate their element-dependent characteristics.

A 3[Ln2(NO3)9] is a double nitrate composed of an alkali-metal ion (A) and a trivalent lanthanoid ion (Ln). Although it is constructed from achiral ANO3 and Ln­(NO3)3 components, it crystallizes in the chiral space groups P4132 and P4332. , Previous studies have demonstrated that the lanthanoid site in A 3[Ln2(NO3)9] can accommodate various lanthanoid ions, and La, Ce, Pr, Nd, and Sm have been successfully incorporated into the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] framework. ,− We also reported the crystallization of K3[Eu2(NO3)9], which demonstrated that bulk inorganic crystals can exhibit CPL arising from the chiral coordination environment around lanthanoid ions. To investigate the chiroptical properties of such systems, optical resolution is required. Among various resolution methods, spontaneous resolution is the simplest approach to obtain enantiopure compounds because it does not require any external chiral source. In this process, both enantiomorphs crystallize separately, yielding optically pure crystals, as exemplified by Pasteur’s salt (sodium ammonium tartrate). − However, spontaneous resolution is relatively rare, occurring in fewer than 4–5% of crystalline compounds. − Despite this low occurrence rate, several molecular crystals obtained through spontaneous resolution have been investigated to elucidate 4f-electron-related chiroptical properties in the solid state. − ,− Although our previous CPL result on K3[Eu2(NO3)9] suggested the enantioenrichment of the crystals, it had not been confirmed whether all members of the isostructural K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series could be obtained as enantioenriched crystals. Although observations of CD and CPL spectra can confirm enantioenrichment, CD can also be applied to nonluminescent compounds. A previous study reported that K3[Pr2(NO3)9] crystallized a the eutectic melt of nitrates and exhibited a CD spectrum at a low temperature (77 K). Although crystallographic data and the corresponding mirror-image spectrum were not reported, the observed CD response is consistent with the formation of enantioenriched crystals. Nevertheless, the spontaneous resolution of other K3[Ln2(NO3)9] crystals has not been explicitly reported in subsequent studies and their optical resolution has therefore not been established. Therefore, the lanthanoid-dependent nature of chiral crystal fields in this series remains unclear.

Lanthanoid ions can serve as chiral probes because the 4f electrons scarcely participate in chemical bonding, yet remain sensitive to the symmetry of the surrounding electric field, resulting in sharp spectral lines that are largely independent of the chemical environment. For example, previous studies have employed the CD spectra of Yb3+ ions to probe interactions with biomolecules. , The sensitivity of lanthanoid ions to chiral environments has been theoretically established by Richardson. Although lanthanoid-dependent sensitivity has been comprehensively examined using CD spectra measured across the lanthanoid series for solution-phase complexes such as CsLn­(hfbc)4 and THP Ln­(III) complexes (THP = 1,4,7,10-tetrakis­(2-hydroxypropyl)-1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane), comparable studies in inorganic chiral crystals, particularly K3[Ln2(NO3)9], remain limited. As noted above, lanthanoid ions in K3[Ln2(NO3)9] occupy well-defined, structurally identical coordination sites across the series, making this system suitable for systematically analyzing the sensitivity of lanthanoid ions to chiral environments.

In this work, we performed the optical resolution of the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series (Ln = La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd) to investigate the sensitivity of lanthanoid ions to the chiral crystal field using CD spectra arising from 4f–4f transitions. By determining the crystal structures and varying the lanthanoid ion across this isostructural series, we examined the correlation between the dissymmetric coordination environment and the resulting CD properties.

Experimental Section

Reagents

All reagents were used without further purification. Lanthanum­(III) nitrate hexahydrate, cerium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate, praseodymium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate, neodymium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate, samarium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate, europium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate, gadolinium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate, and potassium nitrate (KNO3) were purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Industries.

Crystallization of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] (Ln = La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd)

Single crystals of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] were obtained by the slow evaporation of an aqueous solution of KNO3 and the corresponding lanthanoid nitrate. KNO3 (1.52 g, 15 mmol) and lanthanoid nitrate (10 mmol) were added to water (3 mL) in a 10 mL beaker (PYREX, Corning) and the solution was stirred to completely dissolve KNO3 and lanthanoid nitrate. Then, the stirring bar was removed and the aqueous solution of KNO3 and lanthanoid nitrate was placed in an incubator at 80 °C. The crystallization took 2 to 3 days. The single crystals of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] were collected from the beaker and the crystals were dried in an electric oven (80 °C). The single crystals were kept in a desiccator since they were hygroscopic. The typical crystal size was approximately 1 cm.

Optical Resolution of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] by Polarized-Light Microscopy

Left- and right-handed single crystals were manually separated based on the color differences observed using a stereomicroscope equipped with polarizing plates (Stemi 305 cam W Stand K EDU, ZEISS).

Single Crystal X-ray Diffraction Analysis

The obtained single crystals were cut into small pieces for Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) measurements and mounted on microloops with silicon oil. SCXRD data were collected at 293 K using a Rigaku XtaLAB mini II diffractometer equipped with a HyPix-Bantam 2D semiconductor X-ray detector. Diffraction data were measured using ω scans with graphite-monochromatized Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å). Data collection, initial indexing and processing of data were conducted using the CrysAlisPRO software. The structure was solved with the SHELXT solution program using intrinsic phasing and by using OLEX2 as the graphical interface. The model was refined with SHELXL using full-matrix least-squares minimization on F2. Analytical numerical absorption correction was performed using a multifaceted crystal model using spherical harmonics, implemented in SCALE3 ABSPACK scaling algorithm. The crystal structure was drawn by VESTA.

Powder X-ray Diffraction Analysis

Powder XRD (PXRD) patterns were obtained using a MiniFlex600 (Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation. Due to the hygroscopic nature of the sample, it was placed in an air-sensitive sample holder for the measurements.

Circular Dichroism Measurements

Circular dichroism (CD) measurements were conducted with a CD spectrometer (JASCO J-820) equipped with an integrating sphere. Single crystals whose handedness had been determined by polarized-light microscopy were ground using an agate mortar. For powder CD measurements, single crystals exhibiting a uniform optical response were selected by polarized-light microscopy before grinding. The powdered samples were placed in the sample cell with a quartz glass window. A background correction was performed using BaSO4. CD spectra were recorded with the simultaneous observation of absorption spectra at room temperature.

Results and Discussion

Optical Resolution of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] (Ln = La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd)

K3[Ln2(NO3)9] was crystallized from aqueous solutions containing stoichiometric amounts of KNO3 and lanthanoid nitrate. We first examined the crystallization of K3[Ce2(NO3)9], as previous studies have reported successful crystal growth by evaporation from aqueous solution. If a crystal is optically active, this property can be directly verified using polarized light microscopy. Figure b–d show stereoscopic microscope images of a K3[Ce2(NO3)9] single crystal observed by polarized light microscopy. Under crossed Nicols, the K3[Ce2(NO3)9] crystal uniformly appeared light blue (Figure c). In general, a cubic crystal observed under crossed Nicols displays a completely dark image only when it is optically inactive (achiral). Therefore, the image in Figure c provides direct evidence of the optical activity of K3[Ce2(NO3)9]. Upon rotating the analyzer counterclockwise from the crossed-Nicols position (approximately −20°), the lower part of the crystal became brighter, while the upper part turned deep blue. Conversely, rotating the analyzer clockwise (approximately +20°) produced the opposite contrast. This behavior indicates that the lower region of the crystal is dextrorotatory (d-K3[Ce2(NO3)9]), whereas the upper region is levorotatory (l-K3[Ce2(NO3)9]). These observations demonstrate that d-K3[Ce2(NO3)9] and l-K3[Ce2(NO3)9] crystallize separately, indicating that spontaneous resolution occurs during the crystallization of K3[Ce2(NO3)9], resulting in enantiopure chiral crystals. For subsequent measurements, crystals of each handedness were carefully collected while avoiding interfacial regions between crystals, because even a minor fraction of the opposite enantiomorph could partially cancel the CD signals.

The crystal structures of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] belong to two chiral space groups, P4132 and P4332. We investigated which space group corresponds to the dextrorotatory crystal (d-K3[Ce2(NO3)9]) and the levorotatory crystal (l-K3[Ce2(NO3)9]). The crystals were trimmed, and the resulting fragments were analyzed by SCXRD to determine the absolute structures of K3[Ce2(NO3)9]. SCXRD analysis revealed that l-K3[Ce2(NO3)9] crystallizes in the P4132 space group, whereas d-K3[Ce2(NO3)9] adopts the P4332 space group. Figure e–h show the crystal structures of K3[Ce2(NO3)9] as thermal ellipsoid plots. When viewed along the [111] direction, the two enantiomorphs exhibit chiral propeller-like arrangements (Figure g and h). In these crystal structures, the lanthanoid ions are located on a 3-fold axis, and the least-squares planes of the coordinating nitrate ligands are not parallel to this axis. Therefore, a chiral center is present at the lanthanoid ion. Further structural details are discussed below.

Other K3[Ln2(NO3)9] compounds were also crystallized from aqueous solutions containing mixtures of potassium nitrate and the corresponding lanthanoid nitrates. Both l- K3[Ln2(NO3)9] and d-K3[Ln2(NO3)9] were separately obtained through spontaneous resolution (Figure a–g). Although most previous studies reported crystal growth from eutectic melts at elevated temperatures, all K3[Ln2(NO3)9] compounds in the present study were successfully crystallized from aqueous solutions. Polarized light microscopy revealed characteristic light–dark contrast changes in the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] crystals, allowing the determination of their optical rotation.

Coordination Environment of Lanthanoid Ions

The coordination environments of lanthanoid ions in K3[Ln2(NO3)9] were examined by SCXRD. Figure a shows the coordination environment of the lanthanoid ions. Each lanthanoid ion is coordinated by six NO3 – ligands, forming a distorted icosahedral Ln­(NO3)6 unit. Two crystallographically distinct nitrogen atoms are labeled in Figure a, and the oxygen atoms coordinated to the lanthanoid ion are classified into four types (O1, O2, O3, and O4). The Ln–O bond lengths corresponding to these four oxygen atoms (Ln1–O1, Ln1–O2, Ln1–O3, and Ln1–O4) were inequivalent (Figure b), indicating distortion of the icosahedral coordination geometry. For example, the Ce–O bond distances in K3[Ce2(NO3)9] ranged from 2.586 to 2.763 Å. The Ln–O bond lengths decreased with increasing atomic number, reflecting the lanthanoid contraction. Figure c shows selected bond angles between the lanthanoid ion (Ln1) and oxygen atoms (O1, O2, O3, and O4). The ideal central angle of a regular icosahedron is 63.43°. However, in the Ln­(NO3)6 units, most of the O–Ln–O bond angles between nearest-neighbor oxygen atoms deviated significantly from this ideal value, ranging from approximately 47 to 75°. Consequently, the Ln­(NO3)6 coordination units form distorted icosahedra that adopt a noncentrosymmetric C 3 geometry.

Electronic Absorption and Circular Dichroism Spectra of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] in the 4f–4f Transitions

To investigate the chiroptical properties of K3[Ln2(NO3)9], electronic absorption and CD spectra were recorded. Figure a–d show the absorption and CD spectra corresponding to the 4f–4f transitions of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] for lanthanoid ions that exhibit observable 4f–4f transitions (Ln = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Eu). The spectra of the other compounds, as well as the full spectral range, are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). K3[Pr2(NO3)9], K3[Nd2(NO3)9], K3[Sm2(NO3)9], and K3[Eu2(NO3)9] exhibited sharp absorption bands in the visible region, which are characteristic of 4f–4f transitions. These bands arise from the partial relaxation of the Laporte selection rule caused by distorted, noncentrosymmetric coordination environments.

Absorption and CD spectra of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] in the 4f–4f transition region. Ln = (a) Pr, (b) Nd, (c) Sm, and (d) Eu, and (e–h) the corresponding CD anisotropy factors (g CD).

Among the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series, K3[Pr2(NO3)9], K3[Nd2(NO3)9], K3[Sm2(NO3)9], and K3[Eu2(NO3)9] showed sharp mirror-image CD signals in the visible region associated with the 4f–4f transitions (Figure ). Among these compounds, only the CD spectrum of K3[Pr2(NO3)9] has previously been reported. The CD spectra arising from the 4f–4f transitions observed in this study were in good agreement with the previously reported low-temperature CD spectrum measured at 77 K. As noted in the Introduction, however, the relationship between the observed CD spectrum and the corresponding crystal structure had not been clarified in the previous study. Based on the SCXRD analyses and the CD spectra obtained in the present work, the space group of K3[Pr2(NO3)9] can now be unambiguously assigned to P4332. Although the spectral positions of K3[Ln2(NO3)9] were close to those previously reported for lanthanoid complexes in solution, the shapes and signs of the CD spectra were partially inverted. ,, This difference can be attributed to variations in the point-group symmetry of the coordination environments. In contrast to UV–vis absorption spectra, individual CD bands corresponding to a single absorption transition were clearly split. For example, distinct splitting was observed in the CD spectrum (559 and 562 nm) associated with the 6H5/2 → 4G5/2 transition of K3[Sm2(NO3)9] (Figure c). The observed splitting of approximately 3 nm in the CD spectrum corresponded to an energy separation of about 100 cm–1, which was consistent with typical Stark splitting induced by the crystal field in lanthanoid ions. , While UV–vis absorption spectra are broadened by crystal-field effects, CD spectra provide more distinct information through sign changes and band splitting, serving as a more sensitive probe of the local chiral crystal field. Thus, these results demonstrated the presence of chiral crystal fields in the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series.

For the other compounds, La3+ has no 4f electrons, and therefore K3[La2(NO3)9] did not exhibit CD signals arising from 4f–4f transitions. In the case of K3[Ce2(NO3)9], CD activity was observed in the 4f–5d transitions, which are dominant for Ce3+. By contrast, K3[Gd2(NO3)9] did not show clear CD features associated with 4f–4f transitions because Gd3+ has zero orbital angular momentum (L = 0) in its ground state and is therefore much less affected by chiral crystal field perturbations.

Although the 4f–4f transitions of K3[Pr2(NO3)9], K3[Nd2(NO3)9], and K3[Eu2(NO3)9] were predominantly classified as DIII-type transitions according to Richardson’s classification, K3[Sm2(NO3)9] exhibited larger |g CD| values than the other compounds because its absorption spectrum includes DII-type transitions. The observed order of |g CD| magnitudes across the lanthanoid series therefore indicates that the assignment of the 4f–4f CD transitions in K3[Ln2(NO3)9] is consistent with the theoretical classification. Notably, the largest |g CD| value was observed for K3[Sm2(NO3)9] in the 6H5/2 → 4G5/2 transition, demonstrating that this transition is the most CD-sensitive among the 4f–4f transitions investigated in the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series. These results reveal element-dependent sensitivity to chiral crystal fields in the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series.

Origin of the 4f–4f Circular Dichroism in K3[Ln2(NO3)9]

The absorption and CD intensities of the 4f–4f transitions were enhanced as demonstrated by the structure–optical property relationships observed across the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series. In general, 4f–4f transitions are parity-forbidden, making direct optical excitation of the 4f electrons inefficient. Consequently, excitation of lanthanoid ions is commonly achieved via energy transfer from antenna ligands. However, they can become partially allowed when dissymmetric crystal or ligand fields are introduced around lanthanoid ions. , The increase in absorption originates from an enhancement of the electric transition dipole moment caused by 4f–5d parity mixing induced by an odd-parity crystal field. , Such dissymmetric fields in K3[Ln2(NO3)9] therefore enable direct optical excitation of the 4f–4f transitions. Indeed, our previous work revealed that K3[Eu2(NO3)9] exhibits circularly polarized luminescence upon direct excitation of a 4f–4f transition. The CD intensity is governed by the rotatory strength (R), which is proportional to the electric ( μ ) and magnetic transition dipole moments ( m ) (ΔA ∝ R ∝ | μ || m |cos θ). , In K3[Ln2(NO3)9], the coordination environment of the lanthanoid ions lacks an inversion center, allowing odd-parity crystal fields to induce nonzero electric transition dipole moments. In general, magnetic-dipole transitions are allowed for lanthanoid ions. As a result, the rotatory strength increases through interference between the electric and magnetic transition dipole moments, giving rise to observable CD signals. According to eq , when the absorbance is small but nonzero, the absolute CD anisotropy factor (|g CD|) becomes large. Thus, although the CD intensity itself is governed by the rotatory strength, a relatively small electric transition dipole moment can give a large |g CD| value because the absorbance is proportional to the square of the electric transition dipole moment (A ∝ | μ |2). , Consequently, Sm3+ exhibited the largest |g CD| value, consistent with the weak absorption of the 6H5/2 → 4G5/2 transition.

Structural analysis of the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series revealed that each lanthanoid ion and its nearest-neighbor atoms form an intrinsically chiral crystal field. This chiral crystal field induces distinct CD signals in the 4f–4f transitions, demonstrating that Cotton effects can arise purely from crystalline symmetry, even in the absence of chiral ligands. Thus, all-inorganic chiral crystals can exhibit pronounced CD spectra originating from lanthanoid 4f–4f transitions. This study established a clear correspondence between the CD spectral features and the crystal structures in the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we successfully achieved the spontaneous resolution of the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series (Ln = La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd) and systematically investigated their chiroptical properties. All compounds crystallized in the chiral space groups P4132 and P4332 through spontaneous resolution and exhibited mirror-image CD signals. Among the series, K3[Pr2(NO3)9], K3[Nd2(NO3)9], K3[Sm2(NO3)9], and K3[Eu2(NO3)9] showed sharp CD bands arising from 4f–4f transitions. The absorption and CD intensities of these transitions increased in the chiral coordination environment, indicating that the nominally forbidden 4f–4f transitions became partially allowed through dissymmetric crystal fields around the lanthanoid ions. Because the g CD values reflect the ratio of CD intensity to absorbance, they provide a quantitative measure of the sensitivity of each lanthanoid ion to the chiral environment. Among the ions investigated, Sm3+ was the most sensitive to the chiral crystal field. These results demonstrate that intrinsically chiral inorganic crystals can generate chiral crystal fields around lanthanoid ions. We hope that this work will stimulate further development of the chemistry associated with 4f–4f excitation and that the K3[Ln2(NO3)9] series will serve as a useful model system for exploring the chiral chemistry of inorganic crystals.
