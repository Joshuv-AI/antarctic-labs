Analysis of deep-resequencing data of 984 soybean accessions reveals structural variations underlying agronomic traits
Authors: Jie Li, Wenhao Yue, Guangqi He, Yubin Zou, Weidong Wang, James Whelan, Junmei Wang, Xianzhong Feng, Huixia Shou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic structural variants (SVs) are major sources of genetic variation and have profound impacts on phenotypic traits. However, their functional effects remain largely unexplored in soybean. Here, we resequence 940 soybean accessions. Together with 44 publicly available datasets, we identify 602,281 SVs. Using a graph-based genome, we detect an additional 58,760 presence/absence variations (PAVs) that broadly affect gene expression. Population genomic analyses reveal that SVs serve as a core driving force for soybean domestication and improvement. Integrating SVs with QTLs for oil and protein content, and performing GWAS on 27 traits, we identify key functional SVs. These include transposable element insertions altering seed coat color, multiple insertions within a cytochrome P450 gene modifying flower and hypocotyl color, and a GmMATE1 deletion enhancing seed size. Together, our study establishes a comprehensive SV map of soybean, offering a valuable resource for dissecting the genetic basis of complex traits to accelerate molecular breeding.Subject terms: Structural variation, Agricultural genetics, Plant breeding, Comparative genomics

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is the most valuable cultivated legume crop, with a global market value projected to rise from approximately USD 155 billion in 2023 to USD 278 billion by 20311. It serves as a premier source of plant protein and oil, accounting for more than half of global oilseed production and a quarter of the global protein supply, making it the cornerstone of both human diets and livestock feed2,3. Cultivated soybean is thought to have been domesticated from its wild progenitor (Glycine soja Sieb. and Zucc.) in East Asia approximately 5000 years ago, before its subsequent spread and extensive global cultivation4. To ensure food security for a growing global population, developing high-yielding and nutritionally superior soybean varieties poses both a challenge and an opportunity for plant breeders3,5. Therefore, fully dissecting genetic variation underlying important agronomic traits is a prerequisite for unlocking the latent genetic potential of soybean, serving as a critical means to accelerate yield improvements and cultivar development. However, realizing this potential requires comprehensive genomic characterization of the vast germplasm resources available. Soybean harbors extensive genetic diversity, with over 100,000 accessions conserved worldwide3,6, yet most remain genomically uncharacterized.

Although genome-wide association studies (GWAS) based on single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertion/deletion (InDels) have identified loci linked to important agronomic traits in soybean7–11, many phenotypic variations still remain unexplained. Structural variations (SVs) are an important source of genomic variations in a variety of crop species12. To this end, mining superior genomic variations provides an opportunity for accelerating soybean breeding. SVs, including insertion (INS), deletion (DEL), duplication (DUP), inversion (INV), and translocation (TRA), account for large-scale genomic alterations that can profoundly influence gene function13,14 and act as crucial drivers of phenotypic diversity. SVs play crucial roles in shaping traits across a wide range of plant species, such as fruit morphology in tomato15, fruit shape in peach16–18, asexual reproduction in citrus and grapevine19,20, seed size and weight in Brassica napus21, interspecific reproductive isolation in cotton22, and divergence between Asian and European pears23. In soybean, a copy number variation (CNV) at the rhg1 (resistance to H. glycines) locus confers nematode resistance via increasing expression of a set of dissimilar genes in a repeated multigene segment24. Large-effect variants are pivotal drivers of genetic improvement in molecular breeding. Notable examples include Mao125, where a retrotransposon insertion in the promoter boosts yield, and PH1326, where an insertion reduces plant height. More recently, the assembly of 26 soybean genomes has linked multiple SVs to key agronomic traits, including an inverted repeat of the chalcone synthase (CHS) gene cluster that underlies the transition from a black to a yellow seed coat27–29.

Despite these advances, systematically genotyping SVs remains challenging. A previous study assessed SVs in 547 soybean accessions, uncovering a multitude of traits associated with these genetic alterations30. However, many of the significant associations observed in that study were found to be driven by SNPs or InDels rather than SVs themselves. This limitation largely arises because SVs are often embedded in repetitive, complex genomic regions, and large-scale efforts to resolve and phase them remain scarce31. Furthermore, since sufficient sequencing depth is crucial for detecting SVs, the insufficient depth in previous studies limited SV discovery. Consequently, large-scale SV characterization across populations requires improvement to fully elucidate the genetic architecture of some key traits31.

In this study, we resequence 940 soybean accessions. Together with 44 publicly available datasets, we construct a comprehensive genetic variation map focusing on resequencing-based structural variants (reseq-SVs). We build a graph pan-genome by integrating 56 high-quality assembled genomes to capture presence/absence variations (PAVs). Using this resource, we analyze domestication signatures through reseq-SV selective sweeps and perform GWAS for 27 agronomic traits. This analysis incorporated reseq-SVs, PAVs, SNPs, and InDels to identify important genomic variants associated with seed coat color, flower color, and seed weight for functional validation. These results establish a genomic framework that advances the understanding of soybean diversity and serves as a resource for molecular breeding.

Results

Structural variants drive soybean population divergence

We generated deep whole-genome resequencing data for a globally representative collection of 984 soybean accessions, comprising 97 wild soybeans (G. soja), 480 landraces, and 407 cultivated varieties. Among these, 940 accessions were newly sequenced in this study, while 44 were previously published8,32,33 (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1). The dataset yielded 283.6 billion paired-end reads (about 40 Tb) with 97.1% average genome coverage (95.3–98.2%) and 42.7x average sequencing depth (30.6–79.3x) (Supplementary Data 1). Using a combination of four SV-calling tools, we detected 602,281 reseq-SVs, including 147,459 DELs, 27,660 DUPs, 145,258 INVs, 57 INSs and 281,847 TRAs (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 2). In addition, we identified a high-quality set of 5,690,318 SNPs and 1,365,266 InDels genome-wide.

Phylogenetic tree analysis categorized the 984 soybean accessions into 8 distinct clades with clear geographical association (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 3). Wild soybeans formed a genetically distinct cluster widely distributed across the core origin region (Group 1, Fig. 1B and Supplementary Data 3), providing the genetic basis for subsequent domestication and dissemination. This distinct clustering supports a single domestication origin for cultivated soybeans, consistent with previous studies11,33,34. Both reseq-SV-based and SNP-based principal component analyses (PCA) confirmed higher genetic diversity in wild soybeans than in landraces and cultivars (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 4A–C and Supplementary Data 4). However, reseq-SV PCA captured markedly greater genetic divergence and showed a clearer separation between wild and cultivated soybeans than SNP-based PCA, indicating that reseq-SVs are the principal driver of species diversity and capture more effective evolutionary signals (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). Furthermore, the nucleotide diversity (π) based on reseq-SVs exhibited a progressive decline during domestication, from 2.82 × 10⁻⁵ in wild populations to 1.93 × 10⁻⁵ in landraces (a 1.46-fold reduction) and 1.72 × 10⁻⁵ in cultivars (a 1.64-fold reduction). SNPs exhibited a similar but smaller magnitude of decline (1.04- and 1.18-fold, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 4D, E).

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis revealed distinct decay patterns between variant types. Reseq-SV-based LD decayed rapidly (to r² = 0.085 within 0.5 kb) across all populations, and slightly slower in cultivars (1.4 kb), highlighting the genetic diversity captured by reseq-SVs (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Data 5). In contrast, SNP-based LD decayed much more slowly, reaching half its maximum value (r² = 0.3) at 18.7 kb. Notable variation was observed across subpopulations, with wild populations ( ~ 5.1 kb) decaying faster than domesticated groups (29.1 kb in landraces and 32.5 kb in cultivars), consistent with the previous studies11 (Supplementary Fig. 5A). While all groups had low LD value, wild soybeans exhibited the fastest decay rate and possessed higher genetic diversity based on reseq-SV data (Fig. 1D). Cultivated groups exhibited a hierarchical LD pattern: CHN VI (highest LD) > CHN V > North America (NA) > CHN IV > CHN III > CHN II > CHN I > Japan&Korea (CHN I–VI denote the ecological divisions of China, as shown in Fig. 1A) (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 5B). This inversely correlated with genetic diversity, implying divergent selection pressures across ecological populations. Overall, while both variant types show consistent broad trends, reseq-SVs capture evolutionary signals with considerably higher sensitivity.

Given that reseq-SVs frequently disrupt gene structure and exert disproportionately large functional effects compared to SNPs due to their larger genomic footprints, we further characterized their genomic landscape in detail. We observed that wild soybeans harbored a significantly higher number of reseq-SVs compared to landraces and cultivars (Supplementary Fig. 6A), suggesting that wild soybeans potentially possess higher SV diversity. A total of 92% of the reseq-SVs (559,453/602,281) were captured in just 800 randomly selected accessions, indicating near-saturation detection and confirming the comprehensiveness of our dataset (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Regarding allele frequency, 77.5% of reseq-SVs represented rare variants (135,812 reseq-SVs with a Minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1%) (Supplementary Data 2), implying that the majority of reseq-SVs were specific to individual soybean accessions. Furthermore, 52.4% of all reseq-SVs overlapped with transposon elements (TEs) (7.05% with intact TEs), strongly suggesting that TEs, mostly affected by epigenetic modifications35, are a primary driver of genomic structural changes (Supplementary Fig. 6C).

The length distributions of reseq-SVs show that most DELs peaked at 50 – 200 bp and 1 – 100 kb, while DUPs clustered between 10 – 100 kb (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, we found that most reseq-SVs occurred in noncoding regions: 53.5% were intergenic, 11.7% intronic, 15.4% upstream, and 13.5% downstream of genes. About 4.7% of reseq-SVs overlapped with exons, a substantial proportion considering that exons constitute only 6.1% of the genome (Fig. 1G). Regarding exonic reseq-SVs, non-frameshift deletions represented the most abundant reseq-SV class (75.17%), followed by frameshift substitutions (15.19%) and non-frameshift substitutions (9.36%) (Fig. 1G). Among these, the dominance of non-frameshift events suggests that these reseq-SVs frequently preserved the reading frame. We also investigated the distribution of reseq-SVs relative to gene regions. A total of 54,175 out of 55,436 genes (97.7%) were associated with at least one reseq-SV.

Graph-based pan-genome reveals extensive PAVs in soybean

We integrated 56 previously sequenced genomes (7 wild, 11 landraces, and 38 cultivated soybeans) (Supplementary Data 6). Benchmarking with universal single-copy orthologs (BUSCO) revealed that a mean of 99.58% of the 1614 Embryophyta single-copy genes were fully assembled in 56 genomes, with an average N50 of 50.81 Mb (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 6), confirming their high completeness. Subsequently, we performed a gene family analysis to clarify the diversity and evolutionary relationships within this species and to reveal their differentiation patterns. Orthologous clustering classified all genes into 94,730 families. To resolve the phylogenetic relationships among Glycine species, we constructed a species tree using 2108 single-copy genes. We found that wild soybeans clustered into a distinct basal branch, confirming their earlier evolutionary origin (Supplementary Fig. 8A). As the number of genomes analyzed rose, the pangenome size reached a plateau and stabilized at 49 genomes (growth rate < 0.1%), whereas the core gene families showed a commensurate decrease (Fig. 2A). This pattern reflects that the pan-genome captures the vast majority of the genetic content of the soybean species, indicating the high representativeness of these 56 soybean genomes (Fig. 2B). At the gene level, core genes exhibited significantly greater average lengths for exons, genes, and CDS regions, as well as higher copy numbers, compared to dispensable genes (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, Ka/Ks ratios were significantly higher in dispensable genes than in core genes (Fig. 2C). Pfam domains were highly enriched in core (89.8%) and softcore (79.9%) genes, contrasting sharply with the lower proportions observed in dispensable (47.6%) and private (37.2%) genes (Supplementary Fig. 8B). Collectively, these findings indicate that core genes are more functional conserved than dispensable genes.

Using these high-quality genomes, we constructed a graph-based pan-genome and identified 58,760 PAVs. These PAVs consisted of 24,249 (41.27%) DELs and 34,511 (58.73%) INSs (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 4). Of these, only 4523 overlapped with reseq-SVs, while 54,237 served as a crucial supplement by capturing complex variants inaccessible to short reads. Furthermore, we found that most of the PAVs were concentrated in the range of 100 to 200 base pairs (Supplementary Fig. 9). Similar to the patterns of core and dispensable gene families (Fig. 2A), the nonredundant PAV set increased with sample size before leveling off. Concurrently, the number of shared PAVs declined, ultimately reaching zero across all samples (Fig. 2E). The number of PAVs was significantly higher in wild soybeans than in cultivated and landrace varieties (Fig. 2F). To further understand their potential functional impact, we functionally annotated all pan-genome PAVs. These variants were predominantly located in intergenic regions (57.9%), followed by promoter regions (13.8%) and exonic regions (3.8%) (Fig. 2G). Collectively, our comprehensive interrogation of 984 resequencing datasets integrated SNPs, InDels, PAVs, and reseq-SVs to delineate a high-resolution, multi-scale genomic variation landscape, capturing the full spectrum of genetic diversity from single-nucleotide polymorphisms to complex structural rearrangements (Supplementary Fig. 10).

PAVs affect gene expression and phenotypic traits

To further characterize these PAVs, we examined their density relative to gene structures. While the majority of PAVs were located in intergenic regions, a closer examination of PAV density around genes revealed a distinct gradient. Specifically, PAV density was lowest within gene bodies and increased with distance from the gene into flanking regions (Fig. 3A), suggesting that PAVs affecting regulatory sequences are under less stringent selection pressure than those disrupting coding sequences. Given that exonic PAVs directly disrupt gene function while promoter PAVs alter gene expression, we intersected the gene sets associated with PAVs in these two regions. We identified 7679 genes harboring promoter PAVs and 2810 genes harboring exonic PAVs, yielding an overlap of 1021 genes (Fig. 3B). The majority of these overlapping genes exhibited tissue-specific or high expression patterns that likely dictate distinct plant phenotypes; alternatively, they were characterized by low expression levels or complete transcriptional silencing across the whole plant (Fig. 3C). Ultimately, this extreme expression divergence suggests that genes bearing both regulatory and coding PAVs serve as pivotal nodes for phenotypic plasticity, either enhancing specific traits or undergoing functional loss.

To dissect the independent contributions of each variant type beyond this overlapping subset, we separately characterized the broader functional impacts of promoter and exonic PAVs. Functioning as cis-regulatory variants, promoter PAVs primarily modulate gene expression. Our analyses revealed that these promoter PAVs shape a multilayered regulatory variation network, encompassing epigenetic modifications, RNA processing, metabolic regulation, and environmental responses, which collectively provide a rich reservoir of regulatory variation for plant adaptive evolution (Fig. 3D). In contrast to promoter PAVs, exonic PAVs are significantly enriched in genes underlying fundamental biological processes, particularly those involved in chromatin remodeling, histone modification, and cell cycle regulation (Fig. 3E). By disrupting gene function in pathways related to genomic stability, cell division, and epigenetic regulation, these exonic PAVs act as key drivers of plant phenotypic variation. We selected SoyZH13_15G048502 and SoyZH13_12G099200 to examine the impact of upstream deletions (Fig. 3F, G). Dual-luciferase assays showed that 551-bp and 2298-bp deletions significantly reduced LUC/REN ratios, indicating a clear downregulation of gene expression. This demonstrated that the deleted sequences are essential for maintaining normal expression levels (Fig. 3F, G).

To further investigate the effects of PAVs on traits, we conducted a PAV-GWAS analysis. This approach revealed significant loci for 100-seed weight and leaf shape that were missed by SNP-, InDel- and reseq-SV-based GWAS. For 100-seed weight, we identified a 142-bp deletion in the 3’ UTR region of a candidate gene. This gene is highly expressed during early seed development, a pattern consistent with the known QTLs for seed weight 24-3, 29-5, 36-5, 50-13 (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 11A), suggesting it may play a crucial role in determining seed weight. For leaf shape, we identified a 245-bp promoter deletion that perfectly co-localizes with known leaf morphology QTLs (leaflet shape 7-2, leaflet length 10-3, and leaflet width 7-1). The associated gene shows leaf-specific expression, strongly indicating it is a key regulator of leaf morphology (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Fig. 11B).

Structural variants drive domestication and improvement

To evaluate how reseq-SVs drove adaptive divergence during soybean domestication (from wild soybeans to landraces) and subsequent improvement (from landraces to cultivars), we analyzed reseq-SV frequency shifts as proxies for selection pressure. We identified 150,166 reseq-SVs with significant frequency differences between wild and cultivated soybeans (Supplementary Data 7). Among these, 13,696 reseq-SVs showed positive selection during domestication (enriched in landraces), while 117,238 underwent negative selection (Fig. 4A, B and Supplementary Data 7). Similar trends emerged during the improvement process, with 9748 reseq-SVs positively selected and 23,768 negatively selected (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Data 7). We found that reseq-SV selection trends were often consistent across both phases (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Data 7), suggesting persistent adaptive pressures.

Genes detected by two or more selection signatures (FST, π, and XP-CLR) were defined as selected genes. Using reseq-SV data, 3579 domestication-related genes (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 13A) and 2114 improvement-related genes (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 13B) were identified, highlighting widespread reseq-SV-driven adaptation. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis linked domestication genes to embryonic development, redox homeostasis, glycosinolate catabolic process, regulation of cell size and fatty acid beta-oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 13C), while improvement genes governed cotyledon development, vascular patterning, fatty acid transport, leaf morphogenesis and embryo development (Supplementary Fig. 13D). Parallel SNP-based analysis yielded 4,171 domestication related genes (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 14A) and 1411 improvement related genes (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 14B). GO enrichment revealed that the domesticated genes identified by SNP were mainly enriched in seed maturation and positive regulation of hormone metabolic processes (Supplementary Fig. 14C), while the improvement genes were mainly enriched in flavonoid biosynthetic processes and cellular ion homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 14D). We constructed a genome-wide selection landscape based on reseq-SV data (Supplementary Fig. 15), revealing pronounced differentiation and diversity loss in domesticated lineages compared to wild soybeans. Only 1875 domestication genes (Fig. 4C) and 663 improvement genes (Fig. 4D) overlapped between reseq-SV- and SNP-derived datasets, indicating that these genetic variants may act through different regulatory mechanisms.

Using XP-CLR36, we detected 244 domestication-associated and 229 improvement-associated selective sweeps (Fig. 4E, F and Supplementary Data 8, 9). Among the 473 selection scans, we found 146 domestication and 131 improvement sweeps located within known oil quantitative trait locus (QTLs) regions (Fig. 4E, F, Supplementary Data 10, 11), and 108 domestication selections and 105 improvement selections within known protein QTLs (Supplementary Fig. 16). Reseq-SV-specific QTLs include 15 oil-related (solid box area in Fig. 4G) and 11 protein-related QTLs (solid box area in Fig. 4H) associated with domestication, alongside 25 oil-related (dashed box area in Fig. 4G) and 8 protein-related (dashed box area in Fig. 4H) undergoing improvement selection, which were undetectable via SNPs. Among these, there were 6 domestication-related genes and 8 improvement-associated loci overlapping with validated functional genes (e.g., VAMP721d, NDR1b, PT7, CHR5, E1, SDP1-3, and Rj2/Rfg1) (Fig. 4E, F and Supplementary Data 12). We found that the intervals of these genes were frequently associated with multiple DELs (Supplementary Data 12). We then used SoyZH13_03G101400 as a case study. This gene contains a 2199-bp deletion in its promoter (the domestication locus DEL2199) (Fig. 4I), the frequency of which dropped stepwise from 71.1% in wild soybean to 14.6% in landraces, and finally to 8.1% in cultivars (Fig. 4J). Our findings reveal how reseq-SVs have shaped key agronomic traits during soybean evolution, and demonstrate that reseq-SV-based analyses can uncover functionally important genes overlooked by SNP-based approaches.

Dissecting soybean agronomic traits using reseq-SV GWAS

To comprehensively dissect the genetic basis of agronomic traits in soybean, we conducted GWAS using reseq-SVs and PAVs along with SNPs and InDels to identify significantly associated loci. Our analysis encompassed 27 agronomic and morphological traits (Supplementary Data 13 and Supplementary Fig. 17). We found that single loci often influence multiple traits, explaining the phenotypic correlations. For example, increased oil content often coincided with reduced protein content, while 100-seed weight showed strong associations with oil content, seed length, width, and thickness, suggesting that they might be genetically co-regulated (Supplementary Fig. 17). The most significant association signals showed substantial overlap between SNP- and InDel-based GWAS analyses (Supplementary Figs. 18–44). Among the 676 non-redundant reseq-SVs identified, 50% co-localized with SNP-GWAS signals (Supplementary Data 14). However, reseq-SV GWAS also identified 338 trait-associated reseq-SVs in genomic regions that SNP-GWAS failed to capture. This complementarity aligns with biological expectations. While SNPs typically exhibit sharper association peaks due to their high density, SVs often exert stronger functional effects, uncovering genetic signals inaccessible to SNP-based approaches and thus providing direction for identifying causal variants. Our results recapitulated known trait-associated loci7, including leaf shape (ln) (Supplementary Fig. 45), plant height (Dt1) (Supplementary Fig. 46), and seed coat color (I) (Supplementary Fig. 18), confirming the reliability of our data. Although significant progress has been made, the current catalog of SNP-based associations likely represents only a fraction of the genetic variations. Integrating multiple variant types enhanced causal variant detection. InDels, PAVs, and reseq-SVs provided complementary insights to SNPs, particularly in characterizing large-scale structural changes and complex variations, thereby aiding in pinpointing candidate genes. Below, we present case studies demonstrating gene discovery through GWAS of reseq-SVs.

TE insertions in the CHS cluster affect yellow seed coat color

Consistent with known phenotypic diversity, our population exhibited distinct seed coat color distributions. While wild accessions were exclusively black, landraces exhibited multiple colors (yellow, green, black, brown), and modern cultivars were predominantly yellow (Fig. 5A). Previous studies showed that an INV at the I locus rearranged the subtilisin fragment and CHS cluster, triggering silencing of CHS expression and resulting in a yellow seed phenotype37. However, we observed intact INV sequences in some yellow-seeded accessions in comparative genomics analysis (Supplementary Fig. 47), suggesting that the INV polymorphisms may not be the only causal factor of the seed coat color changes. In addition, we detected strong association signals for seed coat color on chromosome 8 using reseq-SV GWAS, co-localizing with the characterized CHS gene cluster (Fig. 5B). A total of three reseq-SV signals were detected in the CHS region, including a 259-bp intronic deletion in SoyZH13_08G104701 (CHS6), and two CHS-proximal deletions flanked by intact terminal inverted repeat (TIR) transposons (termed TE1 and TE2) (Fig. 5C, D), which were confirmed by PCR amplification in our soybean accessions (Supplementary Fig. 48A). Haplotype analysis revealed that these deletion alleles are strongly associated with dark seed coats ( > 92.41%, black and brown), whereas reference genotypes mostly exhibited light seed coats ( > 75.71%, yellow and green) (Fig. 5E). To better elucidate the role of the deletion alleles in soybean domestication, we compared their frequencies across wild soybeans, landraces, and cultivars. The results revealed a clear frequency differentiation among these groups, indicating their potential involvement in soybean domestication (Supplementary Fig. 48B).

To preserve genomic stability, TEs are often silenced by epigenetic modification, such as DNA methylation, in plants, which could affect the neighboring gene expression38–40. We hypothesized that TE insertions within CHS clusters affect DNA methylation and subsequently alter CHS expression and seed coat colors. To verify the hypothesis, we analyzed the previous methylation data41 of soybean with yellow (TE1 insertion) or black seed colors (TE1 deletion), and observed significantly increased methylation levels in yellow seed color accessions (Fig. 5F, Supplementary Fig. 49 and Supplementary Table 2). Consistently, McrBC-qRT-PCR analysis in our soybean accessions revealed increased methylation near CHS1/2/3 along with TE1 insertions (Fig. 5G, H and Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, the three reseq-SVs defined seven haplotypes within the CHS cluster, and the insertions of TE1 and TE2 significantly decreased the transcriptional levels of CHS1/2/3 (Fig. 5I, Supplementary Fig. 50 and Supplementary Table 2). These results demonstrate that during soybean domestication, TE insertions in the CHS cluster drove black-to-yellow seed coat transitions by recruiting methylation-mediated silencing of CHS genes.

GmW1 pleiotropically regulates flower and hypocotyl pigmentation

Through a multi-variant genome-wide association study (Multi-variant GWAS) integrating SNPs, InDels, reseq-SVs and PAVs, we identified a major locus associated with flower and hypocotyl color. Soybean flower color and hypocotyl color are known to be a pair of coupled traits. Accessions with white flowers usually accompanied by green hypocotyls, while purple flowers are typically associated with purple hypocotyls (Fig. 6A). SNP-, InDel-, reseq-SV- and PAV-GWAS analyses all pinpointed the same significantly associated locus (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. 22, 23). Through InDel-GWAS, we identified the most significant locus containing frameshift insertions resulting in the premature termination of SoyZH13_13G057501 (GmW1) (Fig. 6B), a gene ecoding flavanone 3-hydroxylase encoding gene (F3'5’H) previously implicated in flower pigmentation42,43. Our study identified multiple variants at this locus, including several small insertions (3 bp, 1 bp, 12 bp, and 8 bp) detected by InDel-GWAS, as well as a 29 bp insertion revealed by comparative genomic analysis (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 51A, B). Collectively, these insertions result in the premature termination of translation. The GmW1 locus resides within a strong LD block, reinforcing its role as a pleiotropic hub (Fig. 6B). PCR amplification confirmed the site and the presence of the total 53-bp insertion (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. 51C). Among the accessions analyzed, 353 accessions carried the insertion, 96.6% of which had white flowers. In contrast, 556 reference-genotype accessions, 97.3% of which had purple flowers, with the small remaining proportion likely influenced by modifying factors or environmental noise (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. 52).Consistent with these phenotypes, anthocyanin content was significantly higher in purple-flowered plants than in white-flowered plants (Fig. 6E). Haplotype analysis classified 54 haplotypes into two major groups, Hap1 and Hap2, based on phylogenetic clustering, with Hap1 associated with purple flowers and Hap2 with white flowers (Supplementary Fig. 53A). The proportion of the purple flower color phenotypes among wild soybeans, landraces, and cultivars accessions were 99%, 58.4% and 58%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 53B). This trend suggests that the white flower color was gradually favored during artificial selection, reflecting potential breeding preferences or adaptive advantages in cultivated varieties.

To validate the functional role of SoyZH13_13G057501 (GmW1) in controlling flower color, transcriptome analysis of 8 purple-flowered and 8 white-flowered varieties was performed using published data44. The results showed that the expression level of the SoyZH13_13G057501 was significantly higher in purple-flowered varieties than in white-flowered varieties (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, introducing GmW1 into white-flowered W82 restored purple flower pigmentation, accompanied by elevated anthocyanin content (Fig. 6G-I). Vanillin staining showed significant enhancement of anthocyanin in the petals of GmW1-overexpressing lines (Fig. 6H, I). In summary, the frameshift insertions in GmW1 could be the causal variants driving the transition from purple to white in flower color by truncating the enzyme, disrupting anthocyanin biosynthesis, and abolishing hypocotyl pigmentation.

Deletion of a detoxification protein GmMATE1 increased seed size

Reseq-SV GWAS identified a seed weight-associated locus on chromosome 2 that was consistently observed across multiple years and environments but not detected in previous SNP/InDel-based GWAS or QTL studies (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 54). Further characterization revealed that a 2652-bp deletion resulted in the complete loss of SoyZH13_02G084000 (GmMATE1), which encodes a detoxification efflux carrier/multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (DTX/MATE) detoxification protein (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. 55). The deletion correlated with increased 100-seed weight across wild soybeans, landraces, and cultivars, indicating that GmMATE1 play a negative role in regulating seed weight (Fig. 7C). Genotyping of diverse soybean varieties by PCR confirmed that the large deletion significantly increased seed length, width, thickness, and 100-seed weight (Fig. 7D, E), without significantly altering oil or protein content (Supplementary Fig. 56). The clear link between genotype and phenotype strongly supports our findings in natural populations.

To further confirm the physiological role of GmMATE1 in regulating seed weight, we phenotyped an ethyl methane sulfonate (EMS)-induced Gmmate1 mutant derived from iSoybean45, which carries a splice-site mutation in the fifth exon that led to loss of gene function (Supplementary Fig. 57). The Gmmate1 mutant displayed more vigorous growth than the W82 control at the 7-week stage, characterized by larger leaves and pods. However, the number of pods per plant was decreased (Supplementary Fig. 58). In addition, the Gmmate1 mutant exhibited a prolonged growth period, along with significantly larger pods and seeds (Supplementary Figs. 59, 60). Consequently, the Gmmate1 mutant showed significant increases in seed length, width, and thickness, and 100-seed weight (Fig. 7F, G). Consistent with the natural deletion haplotype, the Gmmate1 EMS mutant exhibited a comparable increased in seed weight (Fig. 7D vs 7F). This phenotypic convergence, arising from two entirely distinct molecular lesions, provided robust and mutually reinforcing genetic evidence confirming the role of GmMATE1 in seed size regulation.

To complement these functional findings with natural variation evidence, we analyzed GmMATE1 haplotypes and found GmMATE1Hap2 predominantly present among accession originating from lower-latitude regions (Fig. 7H). The haplotype mirrored the mutant phenotype through its association with both a prolonged growth period (Fig. 7I) and larger seeds (Supplementary Fig. 61A). GmMATE1Hap2 was mainly distributed in the Yangtze River region of China (Fig. 7J), a vegetable soybean production zone. This suggests that the GmMATE1Hap2 may have been historically maintained, perhaps due to local adaptation or mild human preference, due to its association with larger seeds, a key trait for fresh vegetable soybean. However, population genetic analysis indicated that this haplotype had not undergone strong artificial selection during domestication or improvement (Supplementary Fig. 61B). Its frequency showed only a minor increase from wild soybeans (4.12%) to landraces (13.78%) and cultivars (11.82%) (Fig. 7K). Taken together, GmMATE1Hap2 represents a valuable yet underutilized genetic resource. Its preferential enrichment in China III (CHN III) ecological zones (Supplementary Fig. 61C) further underscores its potential for breeding programs aimed at developing large-seeded cultivars adapted to low-latitude environments.

Discussion

Genomic variations in the form of SNPs, InDels and reseq-SVs underpin phenotypic diversity and crop improvement46,47. While SNPs typically alter codons, SVs often disrupt regulatory elements, alter chromatin structure, and modulate DNA methylation to fine-tune gene expression48,49. By leveraging 42.7 × deep resequencing data from 984 globally diverse accessions, this study establishes the most comprehensive soybean SV map to date. We employed a consensus reseq-SV-calling strategy integrating four established tools. While this multi-tool approach aligns with the methodological standards set by previous studies in other species17,20, it has not yet been reported in soybean. Consequently, this SV map provides a critical resource for gene discovery and breeding. We independently validated these reseq-SVs against 24 assembled genomes using MUMmer and Syri, alongside manual IGV inspection of 200 random reseq-SVs. Our high-quality reseq-SV set achieved an accuracy of 87% (Supplementary Data 15), and successfully recapitulated previously characterized reseq-SVs, e.g., Mao125 and PH1326. While breakpoint resolution and complex repeat regions remain challenges for short read sequencing, the high-depth, multi-algorithm strategy used in this study effectively suppresses false positives and negatives and overcomes the inherent constraints of short-read SV detection (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 3–6). Collectively, our analysis of 984 soybean resequencing datasets generated a high-resolution, genome-wide variation map integrating SNPs, InDels, reseq-SVs and PAVs (Supplementary Fig. 10).

PCA analysis demonstrated that reseq-SVs more sharply distinguish the genetic divergence between cultivated and wild soybeans than SNPs (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 4). Unlike SNPs, which primarily affect single codons, reseq-SVs frequently alter gene regulation through dosage effects (e.g., GmMATE1 deletion increasing seed weight) or chromatin rewiring (e.g., CHS-linked methylation changes). Although SNPs remain essential for genetic mapping, a subset of reseq-SVs is associated with epigenetics and thus provides complementary insights for functional gene discovery35. Extending this principle of complementarity, our analyses demonstrate that reseq-SVs and assembly-based PAVs capture distinct structural landscapes. Although these two variant classes are conceptually complementary, we deliberately maintained their analytical separation in subsequent GWAS. By keeping these frameworks distinct, we ensured the maximal discovery power of pangenome-based PAVs for identifying trait-associated loci that are inaccessible to short-read approaches. Then, we performed GWAS using the PAV dataset, which revealed significant loci for 100-seed weight and leaf shape that were missed by SNP- and reseq-SV-based GWAS (Fig. 3H, I and Supplementary Fig. 11).

Domestication and improvement altered soybean traits to suit agricultural needs. Our dataset, comprising wild, landrace, and elite cultivated soybean accessions, offers a powerful resource for detecting selective sweeps associated with domestication and improvement. This study showed that SNP- and reseq-SV-based sweeps exhibit only partial overlap, highlighting their complementary roles in capturing distinct signals of artificial selection. For instance, many selection intervals harboring reseq-SVs encompass functionally annotated genes associated with nitrogen efficiency and stress responses (Supplementary Data 12). Wild soybeans are recognized for their superior adaptability and enhanced stress tolerance and nitrogen utilization efficiency compared to cultivated soybeans50. The question of whether artificial selection eroded these beneficial alleles can be addressed by reseq-SV-based analysis (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 12–16).

Parallel GWAS analyses of SNPs, InDels, reseq-SVs, and PAVs (Supplementary Figs. 18–44) revealed that reseq-SVs indentified 676 trait-associated loci, of which 417 were linked to quality traits and 259 to quantitative traits. By leveraging reseq-SV data, we elucidated the genetic mechanisms underlying the essential domestication traits, seed size enlargement and seed coat color transition. This demonstrates the potential of reseq-SVs to unveil cryptic genetic variation.

Beyond validating the framework, our reseq-SV GWAS approach demonstrates significant advantages in uncovering QTLs. Unlike SNPs, reseq-SVs often exhibit distinct LD patterns and can act as causal variants by directly altering gene dosage or regulatory structure (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 5). For instance, reseq-SVs in GmMATE1 and other candidate genes provided enhanced resolution in pinpointing causal variants compared to flanking SNPs, thereby offering a direct molecular mechanism explaining continuous variation in seed size and weight (Fig. 7). This highlights the potential of reseq-SV GWAS to uncover hidden genetic architecture underlying complex quantitative traits.

TE insertions in the CHS gene cluster emerged as a pivotal SV driving the shift from black to yellow seed coats (Fig. 5). While INV variants were nearly universal in black-seeded accessions, their retention in 18 yellow-seeded accessions indicates the existence of additional regulatory layers (Supplementary Fig. 47). Reseq-SV GWAS revealed that three genomic deletions at site I corresponded to transposon excisions. In our analysis, all accessions carrying both INV events and TE insertions showed yellow seed coat colors (Supplementary Fig. 47). Around 77.8% (14/18) of the accessions containing TE1/TE2 insertions but lacking INV events also showed a yellow seed coat, suggesting that TE insertions can affect seed coat color independently of INV events. Furthermore, around half (4/7) of the accessions containing a single TE insertion but no INV events were yellow, suggesting that the number of TEs may affect methylation levels, thereby influencing CHS gene expression and subsequent seed coat color transition. Accessions lacking both INV and TE insertions displayed a black seed coat. It is worth noting that, in addition to the TE-methylation mechanism described here, the well-characterized INV (I locus) pathway regulates seed coat color through a distinct siRNA-mediated route, in which CHS inverted-repeat transcripts are processed into siRNAs that repress CHS expression post-transcriptionally37,51. Considering that TE–methylation acts at the transcriptional level through DNA methylation, whereas the INV–siRNA axis acts at the post-transcriptional level through mRNA degradation, our analysis suggests that TE insertions and INVs within the CHS cluster region could synergistically contribute to the seed coat color transition from black to yellow.

Reseq-SV GWAS pinpointed the loss of GmMATE1, a negative regulator of seed weight, as a key event in cultivated soybeans (Fig. 6). Phenotypic validation confirmed its role, mirroring findings in rice and maize (OsGFD1, OsBIRG1 and ZmBige1) where loss of family members increased grain size52–54 (Fig.7 and Supplementary Figs. 59, 60). In rice, the OsBIRG1 mutation promoted cell expansion by increasing cell turgor pressure through the accumulation of chloride ions52. The seed size expansion observed due to GmMATE1 deletion may mirror the function of its rice homologs. OsBIRG1 exports Cl⁻ to maintain ion homeostasis; its loss leads to excessive vacuolar Cl⁻ accumulation, increasing osmotic potential and driving cell expansion and ultimately increasing seed size52. Unlike OsBIRG1 mutants, deletion of GmMATE1 did not cause biomass decrease. Thus, the seed enlargement in the GmMATE1-deleted cultivated soybeans was not caused by a source-sink trade-off proposed in rice52. Moreover, GFD1, a MATE transporter, was reported to interact with OsSWEET4 and OsSUT2 to synergistically regulate carbohydrate partitioning between the stem and grain53. Consistent with the increased grain size phenotype, these results suggest a conserved sugar transport mechanism underlying the yield trait, which is further supported by the significant increase in leaf biomass (Supplementary Fig. 62). GmMATE1 exhibits minimal basal expression under normal conditions but is induced by flooding stress (Supplementary Fig. 63), implying a mechanism different from its rice homolog52,53. Wild soybeans might retain GmMATE1 for flood adaptation, whereas domestication favored its loss to maximize seed size. Genetic evidence for causality can be obtained by reintroducing GmMATE1 into a deletion accession or disrupting the gene in native GmMATE1-containing lines.

This study establishes reseq-SVs as indispensable tools for dissecting domestication genetics and agronomic trait architecture. By revealing epistatic interactions, pleiotropic effects, and hidden selection signatures, reseq-SVs complement SNP-based analyses, offering a fuller picture of soybean evolution. The candidate genes and mechanistic insights reported here lay a foundation for future research and molecular breeding, empowering efforts to balance yield with quality in modern soybean varieties.

Methods

Plant materials and deep-resequencing

Soybean germplasm, consisting of 940 representative soybean accessions, was selected according to different geographical locations. The accessions included 95 wild samples (Glycine soja Siebold & Zucc.), 448 landrace lines and 397 cultivars of Glycine max [(L.) Merr.] (Supplementary Data 1). All Soybean germplasm were grown at the Agricultural Experiment Station of Zhejiang University (Changxing, Zhejiang, China). The young leaves were collected about 4 weeks after planting, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Genomic DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method55. These data can be retrieved via the SoyOD portal56. At least 1 μg genomic DNA was used to construct sequencing libraries according to the manufacturer's instructions (BGI Inc., Shenzhen, China). Paired-end 150-bp sequencing libraries with an insert size of approximately 300 bp were sequenced on a BGISEQ-T7 platform. The datasets were expanded by including 44 publicly available deep re-sequencing genomes distinct from the current accessions8,32,33. Detailed information of the 984 accessions, including geographical distribution and sequencing coverage and depth, is provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Phenotypic identification of 27 traits

The 940 accessions were collected from different regions. For consistent phenotyping, all accessions were planted at the Zhejiang University experimental station in Sanya, Hainan (18°18′N and 109°6′E) during the winter season in 2021, and numerous phenotypes were documented. All 940 accessions were subsequently planted at another Zhejiang University experimental station located at Changxing, Zhejiang province (30°53′N and 119°39′E) during the summer seasons in 2022 and 2023. Twenty-seven agronomic traits were targeted in this study, including 8 qualitative and 19 quantitative traits. The eight qualitative traits, including cotyledon color, seed crack, flower color, hypocotyl color, leaf shape, seed coat color, hilum color and pod shattering, were measured in two successive years from 2021 to 2022. Eight of the quantitative traits, including seed length, seed width, seed thickness, seed length/width, seed length/thickness, seed width/thickness, seed volume and seed area, were measured using a vernier caliper. Environmentally sensitive traits, like branch numbers, plant height, and node numbers, were recorded as the mean of five measurements across all environments. The contents of moisture, oil and protein were determined by a FOSS spectral analyzer (FOSS, Infratec™ NOVA). Flowering period (vegetative stage), period from flowering to maturity (reproductive stage), and growth period (whole growth stage) were recorded in 2021–2023. The 100-seed weight and soil and plant analyzer development (SPAD) value were measured as the mean of three measurements. Detailed information regarding the phenotyping procedures and scoring standards is provided in SoyOD (https://bis.zju.edu.cn/soyod/home/)56.

Read mapping and small variant calling

Raw sequencing data from 984 soybean accessions were obtained. Paired-end reads were processed using SOAPnuke (v2.2.3) to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. We mapped the paired-end reads for the 984 accessions to the Zhonghuang 13 (ZH13_v2) reference genome57 using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17-r1188) with default parameters. The unmapped, non-unique and duplicated reads were filtered using SAMtools (v1.14)58 and by the Picard package (v2.27.5) for duplicated reads. We then essentially followed procedures described in the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, v4.2.2.0) for data sorting, base recalibration, joint variant calling and variant filtration55. Variant detection was performed using the GATK HaplotypeCaller, genotyping was done with GenotypeGVCFs, and the separation of SNPs and InDels was performed using the GATK SelectVariants option. To remove potentially false positive SNPs, a quality filtering of raw SNP calls was performed using VariantFiltration in GATK according to the following criteria: SNPs with genotype call quality divided by depth (QD) < 2.0, mapping quality (MQ) > 40.0, phred-scaled P-value of Fisher exact test for strand (FS) < 60.0, strand odds ratio (SOR) > 3.0, mapping quality rank sum test (MQRankSum) < − 12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < − 8.0 were retained. The InDels set was filtered with parameters QD < 2.0, MQ > 40.0, FS < 200.0, SOR > 10.0, MQRankSum < − 12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < − 8.0.

Structural variation detection and genotyping

The original reads were processed to remove adapters and low-quality sequences using SOAPnuke. Paired-end reads were aligned to the ZH13 v2 reference genome using the BWA-MEM algorithm with default parameters. Four tools, namely GRIDSS (v2.13.2)59, Manta (v1.6.0)60, Lumpy (v0.2.13)61, and Delly (v1.1.6)62, were used for reseq-SV detection (Supplementary Fig. 2). For Lumpy, SAMtools was used to extract split reads, and lumpyexpress was used to detect breakpoints with the following parameters: lumpyexpress -P -B -S –D. To further improve accuracy, an in-house script was used to filter variants, applying a quality threshold ( > 20) and requiring split-read support. Reseq-SVs were then genotyped using SVTyper (v0.7.1). For GRIDSS, Manta and Delly were run with default parameters to detect and genotype reseq-SVs. To enhance variant-calling reliability, we merged results from all four reseq-SV-detection tools using SURVIVOR (v1.0.7) with stringent criteria: retaining consensus calls detected by at least two tools to minimize false positives, allowing a maximum breakpoint distance of 1000 bp to account for mapping ambiguities, and setting a minimum reseq-SV size of 50 bp to exclude small artifacts (command: “SURVIVOR merge 1000 2 1 1 0 50”). Finally, all results were merged using SURVIVOR based on the distance between breakpoints63, reseq-SV type, and strand orientation. To further improve accuracy, we filtered out deletions smaller than 50 bp or larger than 10 Mb. As a result, variants with minor allele frequencies of < 0.01 were discarded from the genotype data, leaving high-quality reseq-SVs for the imputation of soybean haplotype data and GWAS. Transposon sites were identified based on the ZH13 v2 reference using the EDTA (v2.0.1) pipeline with the default parameters64.

Comparative genomic analysis and quality evaluation of soybean reseq-SVs

To assess the accuracy of the reseq-SVs detected, genome assemblies from 24 accessions were collected33. The genome sequences were compared between the reference genome and the 24 genome assemblies using the NUCmer program (v4.0.0rc1) in MUMmer465. Following the alignment, the blocks were filtered in one-to-one alignment mode. SVs were detected using the delta-filter program from MUMmer with a minimum length of 100 bp and a similarity > 90% (parameters: -m -i 90 -l 100). The delta file was converted into readable matching coordinates using the show-coords program. SyRI (v1.6.3) was used to extract the features and coordinates of SVs66. Finally, a VCF file containing 48 samples (24 assemblies and 24 resequenced accessions) was generated by merging the SV information from resequencing and assembled genomes using SURVIVOR software. We used the ‘shuf -n’ command to randomly select 200 variants to assess accuracy.

Graph-based genome construction and PAV identification

We collected 56 publicly accessible soybean genome assemblies30,32,33,37,51,67–75 to construct a complete graph-based pan-genome, which includes identified PAV information. The completeness of the genomes was assessed using BUSCO (v5.8.0)76 (embryophyta_odb10 dataset), while assembly continuity metrics (N50, N90 and auN) were calculated using QUAST (v5.3.0). To achieve this, we initially aligned the 55 published soybean genomes to the Zhonghuang 13 T2T reference genome using the NUCmer tool from the MUMmer package (v4.0.0rc1), with the settings ‘-c 1000 -l 40’. Subsequently, we detected the alignment blocks using the delta-filter program (parameters: -1 -i 90 -l 100), selecting only one-to-one alignments. PAVs were detected using the delta-filter program from MUMmer with minimum length of 100 bp and a similarity > 90% (parameters: -m -i 90 -l 100), the delta file was converted into readable matching coordinates using the show-coords program (parameters: -THrd). In the final steps, we utilized the SyRI (v1.6.3) pipeline to detect PAVs. PAVs were extracted using a custom Perl script, merged across accessions with svimmer, and used to construct a graph-based pan-genome via vg (v1.46.0). To genotype the non-redundant PAV set in 984 accessions with short-read sequencing data, we mapped reads to the previously constructed graph-based genome using vg map with default parameters, followed by vg pack and vg call for variant calling. This enabled PAV detection in accessions without assembled genomes.

Based on the rearranged proteins from 56 genomes, we identified gene families using Orthofinder (v2.5.5)77, with the following parameters: -S diamond -M msa -T fasttree. To further dissect the pan-genome, we classified the gene families into four categories: core (present in all 56 samples), softcore (present in > 90% of samples but not all; 51–55), dispensable (present in more than one but < 90% of samples; 2–50) and private (present in only 1 sample). Genes that could not be assigned to any orthogroup (unassigned genes) were not incorporated into the pan-genome classification. The pan-genome plateau was determined using R packages. The plateau was defined as the point where the growth rate at each sampling step fell below 0.1%. Gene family classification was used to compare structural characteristics across different gene categories. Gene length, coding sequence (CDS) length, and exon number were extracted from the genome annotation files and compared among gene family classes using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R (v4.x) using the agricolae package for the Student-Newman-Keuls test and the ggplot2 package for plotting. Homologous protein pairs were identified using MCScanX78. The selection pressure of each pair was calculated with Ka/Ks Calculator Toolbox (v2.0)79. Functional annotation with GO and KEGG was performed using eggnog (v2.1.13)80, and the Pfam domains were annotated by InterProScan (v5.77)81. Enrichment analysis was carried out with TBtools82, and GO terms with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Annotation of genetic variants

Annotation of detected SNPs, InDels, reseq-SVs and PAVs were performed based on a gene model of the ZH13 genome using ANNOVAR83. Based on the genome annotation GFF file, SNPs, InDels and reseq-SVs were categorized according to default precedence in exonic regions (variant overlaps a predicted coding region), splice sites, ncRNA (variant overlaps a transcript without coding annotation), 5′ UTRs and 3′ UTRs, intronic regions (variant overlaps an intron), upstream and downstream regions (variant overlaps the 1 kb region upstream or downstream of a transcription start site), and intergenic regions. Furthermore, coding exons with SNPs were further grouped into synonymous or nonsynonymous substitutions. Similarly, InDels and reseq-SVs within exonic regions were classified into categories including frameshift deletions, non-frameshift deletions, stopgain variants (prematurely terminates translation) and stoploss variants (loss of the stop codon leading to continued translation).

Population genetics analysis

To analyze the population structure and construct an evolutionary tree, we screened a subset of bi-allelic and high-quality SNPs with a missing rate < 50% and a MAF > 1% using VCFtools (v0.1.17)84. LD was then calculated using PLINK (v1.90b6.21)85 with the option –indep -pairwise 50 10 0.2. A neighbor-joining tree was constructed based on the Reynolds’ genetic distances with 1000 bootstrap replicates using the Phylip (v.3.695)86 from a distance matrix. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using the iTOL website87 (https://itol.embl.de/). PCA of SNPs and SVs across the whole genome was performed using PLINK software PCA program. Population structure was analyzed using the Admixture (v1.3.0) program with k = 2–10, following the order suggested by the phylogenetic analysis. Graphs were drawn using the R package pophelper.

The parameter r2 value for the LD was calculated for pairwise SNPs, InDels, PAVs and reseq-SVs within each chromosome using PopLDdecay (v3.41)88 with the parameters -SubPop (designated subpopulation), -MaxDist 1000. The LD decay plot was generated against the distance using a custom R script. LDBlockShow was used to generate LD block heatmaps for specified genomic regions using the default parameters89.

Selective sweep analyses and diversity pattern inference

Nucleotide diversity (π)90 was calculated with the SNP and reseq-SV data for wild, landrace and cultivar groups with VCFtools using a window size of 100 kb and a step size of 10 kb. Evidence for selection across the genome during domestication and improvement was evaluated in two comparisons: wild soybeans vs landraces for domestication and landraces vs cultivars for improvement. Accordingly, the πwild/πlandrace and πlandrace/πcultivar values were calculated. The population divergence statistic FST was estimated using 100-kb sliding windows with a step size of 10 kb. Tajima’s D values90 were calculated using SNP and reseq-SV data with the parameter --TajimaD 100000. The top 5% of windows with the highest FST and π ratio values were identified as selective sweeps during domestication and improvement, respectively. The cross-population composite likelihood ratio (XP-CLR, updated by Hufford et al. and based on Python)36 was used to detects selective sweeps by analyzing allele frequency differentiation at linked loci between two soybean populations. We used the top 5% of XP-CLR value as the threshold for identifying potentially selected reseq-SVs. To mitigate problems with running speed and software operation, we first ran analyses on single chromosomes and then merged the result. XPCLR was performed with the following parameters: --rrate 1 × 10−8, --ld 0.95, --maxsnps 200, --size 100 kb, --step 10 kb. The highest XP-CLR values, representing the top 5%, were defined as selected regions. We retrieved relevant QTL information from Soybase (www.soybase.org). All QTL information was based on positions in the W82 genome, and was converted to the ZH13 genome positions by genome coordinate transformation using UCSC liftOver software91 via the following detailed pipeline. The first chain file was generated by minimap2 (v2.26, parameter: -cx asm5 -cs)92 and then converted by transanno (v0.4.4) software. Finally, the genome position coordinate transformation was generated by CrossMap (v0.6.6, based on Python)93. The densities of SNPs, InDels, reseq-SVs and PAVs for the bi-allelic variants were calculated using VCFtools --SNPdensity 100 kb.

Identification of reseq-SVs under selection during soybean evolution

To evaluate the significance of reseq-SVs in soybean evolution, the occurrence frequencies of each reseq-SV were calculated in wild, landrace, and cultivar groups. Reseq-SVs with higher or lower occurrence frequencies in landraces compared to wild accessions, and in cultivar compared to landraces, were designated as showing positive or negative selection, respectively, during domestication or improvement. The significance of the differences in frequencies for each reseq-SV between the two compared groups (wild versus landrace for domestication, and landrace versus improved for improvement) was determined using Fisher’s exact tests in R. The resulting raw P-values were then subjected to the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction for false discovery rate (FDR). Reseq-SVs with significantly different frequencies (FDR < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1) were identified as those under selection. Functional classes with FDR < 0.05 were regarded as significantly enriched.

GWAS was performed with a mixed linear model (MLM) implemented in the efficient mixed-model association expedited (EMMAX) algorithm94. SNPs, InDels, reseq-SVs, and PAVs were used as test markers. Stringent quality control filters were applied prior to association analysis: for SNPs, markers with MAF < 0.05 or a missing rate > 0.1 were excluded; for InDels, markers with MAF < 0.01 and a missing rate >< 0.5 were excluded; for reseq-SVs, markers with MAF < 0.01 were excluded; and all detected variant sites were retained for PAV-GWAS. Phenotypic data processing for GWAS varied depending on the trait type. For qualitative traits, extreme values were excluded prior to GWAS due to their relatively high consistency, and they were subsequently coded as numbers and analyzed as quantitative traits. For continuous quantitative traits, outliers compromising normality (identified using the Shapiro-Wilk test) were removed. Subsequently, combined data from both years in Changxing were integrated to calculate the mean values for GWAS. For specific traits, including oil, protein, and moisture contents, best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs) were calculated using an R package and visualized. For these traits, the GWAS results derived from the Hainan, Changxing, and BLUP datasets were comparatively displayed. The whole-genome significance threshold and the extremely significance threshold were set at −log 10(0.05/n) and −log 10(0.01/n), respectively, where n represents the total number of tested variants for each specific GWAS. Finally, Manhattan and quantile-quantile (QQ) plots were generated using the CMplot R package.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR analysis

All samples were collected from different varieties, and total RNA was isolated using RNA-easy isolation reagent (R701-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Takara Bio). qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 480 machine (Roche, Germany) using TB Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara Bio) under the following cycle conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s and 72 °C for 20 s. All amplification reactions were performed with three biological and two technical replicates. The housekeeping gene GmCYP2 was used as an internal control for sample normalization95. Transcript levels were calculated relative to GmCYP2 using the 2-ΔCt method. The primers used for qRT-PCR are shown in Supplementary Data 16.

Vector construction and soybean transformation

For overexpression and subcellular localization, the coding sequence of GmW1 from the purple-flowered variety (Zhonghuang No.13) was cloned into the modified pTF101.1 (Addgene, plasmid ID: 134770) destination vector containing a FLAG tag (pTF101-W1-FLAG). The primers used for amplifying this fragment are listed in Supplementary Data 16. After verification by DNA sequencing, the construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 for soybean transformation. Then, homozygous seeds were used for phenotypic analysis.

Methylation analysis and detection assays

Methylation data were downloaded from the NGDC (PRJCA000740)41. The dataset included raw methylation data from wild accessions with black seed coats (PI 548402, PI 597459 C, and PI 378692) and cultivated accessions with yellow seed coats (ZH35, DN50, and JD23). Zhonghuang13 v2 was used as the reference genome. Raw whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data were filtered using fastp (v0.12.4) with default parameters. The resulting high-quality clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using Bismark (v0.24.2)97. The methylation level at each cytosine site was calculated using BatMeth298 with the following command: BatMeth2 calmeth -Q 20 --remove_dup --coverage 4 -nC 1. Finally, methylation levels were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.5.2).

To verify whether TE insertions correlated with methylation changes at the CHS locus, we leveraged McrBC digestion, a methylation-sensitive restriction assay. McrBC cleaves DNA at methylated CpG/CHG sites (where H = A/C/T) but leaves unmethylated regions intact99. McrBC-based methylation analysis was performed as described previously100. Briefly, genomic DNA (0.5 – 1 μg) was digested for 1 h at 37 °C with 30 U of McrBC enzyme (Takara Bio). A mock digestion was performed in parallel without enzyme. Then, PCR amplification was performed to identify the degree of methylation. qRT-PCR was directly performed on the McrBC-digested and mock samples, using the TB Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara Bio).

Luciferase report experiment

The promoter sequences of SoyZH13_15G048502 and SoyZH13_12G099200 were cloned from the soybean cultivar ZhongHuang 13. Approximately 2000 bp and 3000 bp upstream of the coding sequences of SoyZH13_15G048502 and SoyZH13_12G099200, respectively, were cloned to generate the full-length promoter constructs. In addition, internal deletions of 551 bp and 2298 bp were generated within these respective promoters using fusion PCR to create the deletion constructs. These promoter fragments were inserted into the pGreenII-0800-LUC vector (Addgene, plasmid IDs: 108228) via homologous recombination (using KpnI and BamHI sites), with the wild-type promoter constructs serving as controls. The recombinant constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (pSoup) (Weidi, China), and positive transformants were selected on YEP medium containing 50 mg/L kanamycin and rifampicin. The verified Agrobacterium cultures were grown, harvested by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min, and resuspended in infiltration buffer (2.35 mM MES, 100 μM acetosyringone, and 1/2 MS medium) to an OD600 of 0.8. The bacterial suspensions (100 μL per infiltration site) were infiltrated into the leaves of four-week-old tobacco plants using a needleless syringe. After infiltration, the plants were kept in darkness for 48 h. Subsequently, the leaves were sprayed with 100 mM potassium D-luciferin, and luminescence signals were captured using the Tanon-5200 in vivo imaging system. Relative luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (GenScript, China) and calculated as the LUC/REN ratio. The primers used for vector construction are shown in Supplementary Data 16.
