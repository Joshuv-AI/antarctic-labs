Self-management for youth and young adults with childhood-onset chronic conditions: A scoping review of parent and caregiver perspectives
Authors: Monique M. Ridosh, Lin Li, Kristin Cleverley, Lexi Ewing, Linda Tirabassi Mathis, Cara C. Young, Brooke Allemang, Catherine A. Harwood, Lynn Kysh, Jennifer Emilie Mannino, Jihye Lee, Sharon Hudson, Manu Raam, Benjamin C. Schwartzman, Cecily L. Betz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe purpose of this scoping review was to explore self-management research conducted in the health care transition (HCT) literature, focusing on youth and young adults (YYA) with childhood-onset chronic conditions (CCC) and involving data collected from parents/caregivers regarding their perceptions and involvement in their child's self-management.MethodsArticles from a larger scoping review (N = 640) that provided an overview of all articles published exploring self-management research in HCT literature were assessed for the extent to which parent/caregiver perspectives of self-management among their YYA aged 9–35 years with CCC were included in the HCT literature. The initial review criteria (protocol published in 2021) were modified to focus on primary research publications. The review process was guided by the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis. Study samples and methods were synthesized using descriptive analyses. Purpose statements were analyzed based on the domains of the Health Care Transition Research Consortium (HCTRC) HCT model.ResultsStudies (n = 218) including parent/caregiver perspectives were published from 1992 to 2025. Demographic data reported in these publications included parent/caregiver type (i.e., mother/father; 52%), age (26.6%), and race/ethnicity (21.5%). Research methods included quantitative (53%), qualitative (38.5%) and mixed (8.2%) with the majority (83%) employing a cross-sectional design. The Domains of HCTRC HCT model addressed in the literature in descending order were Individual (90%), Family/Social Support (62%) Health Care System (35%) and Environment (4%).ConclusionsResearch conducted with parental/caregiver input has numerous shortcomings, including inconsistent reporting of demographic data, and overreliance on cross-sectional designs. Research and practice recommendations are suggested to advance the development and implementation of inclusive models of care.

Introduction

The health care transition (HCT) service model has gained widespread acknowledgment regarding its importance in facilitating continuity of care from pediatric to adult-focused healthcare providers for youth and young adults (YYA) with childhood-onset chronic conditions (CCC). Since the 1990s, this field of practice and research has continued to evolve and expand despite the lack of public and private insurance funding associated with health care transition services in health care.1, 2 Despite these challenges, the health care community, families, and self-advocates have promoted and created HCT services and supports to enhance optimal outcomes for YYA with CCC and their families.1, 3 Over the years of programmatic development, implementation, and testing, prototypes of service delivery models have evolved to address the comprehensive transition needs of YYA with CCC and their families.4, 5, 6, 7

HCT service components include those directed to YYA with CCC, their families, and healthcare service providers. In an ideal HCT process, YYA with CCC receive ongoing service coordination and referrals to facilitate the move to adult-focused health care providers and transition to adulthood. Facilitating transition to adulthood involves assisting YYA with CCC and their families with accessing post-secondary education, employment services, and community living options. As YYA with CCC advance through this transition period, they are encouraged and supported to learn and achieve the developmental tasks associated with late adolescence and emerging adulthood. These tasks include learning to assume more individual responsibility for self-care, engagement in future life planning, and enlarging social and community networks.8, 9 Compared with YYA in general, YYA with CCC are faced with additional daily self-management responsibilities associated with their chronic condition.10, 11

Parents/caregivers (hereafter referred to as caregivers) have an integral role in supporting, advocating, and assisting their child while navigating through the HCT period. During this time, caregivers, based upon YYA with CCC needs and capabilities, should initiate and relinquish their primary responsibilities and oversight of condition management.12 The process of shifting responsibility is based upon family-centered and YYA-centered interests, needs, and preferences, and has been referred to as “shared management.”13, 14 As the shared management model depicts, caregivers assume primary responsibilities for condition management until the time the child can assume more responsibilities for their self-management. This phased reduction of caregiver responsibilities conceptualizes the caregiver serving as a consultant and back-up for assistance. Subsequently, caregiver oversight of condition self-management recedes as YYA competencies with condition self-management are acquired. Throughout this process, more involved caregiver responsibilities may be needed, for example during times of health complications, service/program changes, and secondary conditions.

However, caregiver expertise and experience managing their child’s chronic care needs does not necessarily translate to effective transition and adult-focused service referrals.15, 16 There are new challenges that arise for YYA and their caregivers as they learn to navigate transitions to adult-focused systems of care that involve postsecondary education, employment, and community living agencies. These adult-oriented services operate under distinct missions and organizational structures, each centered on promoting self-sufficiency among the individuals they serve.

While the importance of evidenced-based practices for the transfer of self-management responsibilities from caregivers to YYA with CCC has been widely reported, evidence is lacking as to the efficacy of HCT intervention models to foster the transfer (or shift) of condition self-management from caregiver to YYA with CCC.17, 18, 19 There is a need to better understand the scope and depth of the published literature examining this component of the HCT process. Such understanding can provide direction for future research and the development of evidence-based interventions addressing the important transfer and delegation of self-management responsibilities from caregiver to YYA with CCC.

The primary objective of this review was to examine the body of empirical evidence that explored caregivers’ perspectives pertaining to HCT and their child’s self-management. For the purpose of this review, the self-management concept was operationalized in the scoping review protocol that was previously published.20 Secondary objectives of this review were to provide quantitative data on the types of research designs and methodology used, sample characteristics, and evaluation of self-management (e.g., transition readiness) in HCT planning. Mapping the research conducted during this review period provides insight into the evolution of the literature and identifying priorities for future research.

Methods

Protocol registration

Our review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Reviewer’s Manual guidelines to ensure a rigorous approach, enhanced methodological transparency, and quality reporting.21 A description of our study’s methodology can be found in the published protocol20 and the primary scoping review.

Eligibility criteria and information sources

This scoping review adhered to the JBI Reviewer’s Manual guidelines.20 A clinical and research librarian with extensive consultation experience with scoping and systematic reviews assisted the review team with the development and implementation of the data search strategy that involved three phases. Initially, the search was based upon the original protocol published in 202121 and involved these processes: a) accessing the following databases: MEDLINE, Embase & Embase Classic, CINAHL Complete, PsycINFO, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Web of Science. The original scoping review screened a total of 17,999 references from 1992 through 2024.

Given the extensive literature collected through these means, the review team revised the search criteria in January 2022 to yield more research-focused citations. The inclusion criteria were: (a) YYA with CCC 9–35 years of age; (b) focused on the HCT process; (c) self management was reported as a study predictor, outcome, covariate, intervention/mixed methods focus, qualitative exploration. The exclusion criteria removed secondary sources and specifically limited the following: (a) review articles, (b) scoping reviews, c) book chapters, (d) clinical trial protocols, e) clinical commentaries/editorials, (f) dissertations, (g) conference abstracts, (h) non-English publications, and (i) government/professional documents. A final review was conducted to update the articles reviewed through April 2025 (Supplementary Table 1).

Inclusion criteria for the years from 9 years to 35 years were selected to represent the emerging years of puberty and into adulthood where documentation of individuals with special health care needs often are delayed in assuming a full range of adult responsibilities. The upper age of 35 years was selected to fully capture those who achieved adulthood activities.20

Articles selected for this review were limited to those that included caregivers as study participants. The PRISMA flow chart22 (Fig. 1) depicts the search strategy process used for this scoping review. A total of 218 articles were selected for this scoping review (Supplementary Table 2).Fig. 1

Data charting and items

A data extraction template was created on the Covidence online platform. This template included diagnosis, purpose statement, self-management outcome, community services accessed, sample demographics, country of origin in publication, and research methods/design. For each citation, two independent reviewers within the study team applied the revised inclusion/exclusion criteria to screening titles and abstracts for full-text citation retrieval. All retained full-text citations underwent a further evaluation by two independent reviewers to determine full texts retrieval. Full-text articles underwent a further evaluation by two independent reviewers to determine eligibility for the final phase. All discrepancies were reviewed by a third team member at both title/abstract screening and full text review. Some discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by the same study team member who had been an initial reviewer. Citations and data were extracted and compiled in an Excel database.

Data cleaning and synthesis

Data cleaning and analysis were conducted in Excel. The modified extraction template also included an item labeled “sources of information,” which enabled more efficient database management. Sources of information were identified as a data extraction item after the compilation of articles were amassed. This was not used as a screening feature with the selection of articles for this scoping review. The ‘sources of information’ were obtained from the full text review that aimed at identifying the study’s source of study data. Although the purpose of the scoping review was to provide an overview of the health care transition literature that captured self-management, some studies also included parent/caregivers or providers or a combination of these as study participants (“information sources”) adding value to the studies. Once the initial study overview was analyzed and completed, it became evident that a further deep dive would offer more depth to the initial phenomena of interest, thus a deeper analysis that had participants as parents and or caregivers was conducted.

Citations were categorized as a source of information from caregiver, YYA, provider, or a combination of these groups. This sub setting approach facilitated the analysis of caregivers as a distinct subpopulation within the broader scoping review. This paper reports on the sub-population of caregivers as participants in the study of YYA self-management.

Caregiver sample characteristics

Caregiver sample characteristics were reported in 56% (n = 123) of the studies in this review. The most frequently reported characteristic was caregiver type (i.e., mother/father; 52%, n = 114), followed by caregiver age (26.6%, n = 58), and race or ethnicity (21.5%, n = 47). In addition to caregiver participants, most samples included YYA (n = 194, 89%), whereas providers were included to a far lesser extent (22%; n = 48).

Research designs and methodology

Most studies conducted were quantitative (53%, n = 116), followed by qualitative (38.5%, n = 84), and mixed or multi-method designs (8.2%, n = 18). Studies predominantly used cross-sectional designs (83%, n = 181); considerably fewer studies used longitudinal designs (17%, n = 37). A total of 24 (11%) studies involved the evaluation of transition interventions.

Data sources

Several methods were used to collect data from these studies. Interviews (38%, n = 84) were the most frequently used for data collection purposes; focus groups were hosted in 12% (n = 26) of studies. Sixty-three (29%) of the reviewed studies utilized questionnaires; 41 (19%) of the studies conducted surveys. Relatively few studies (5%, n = 10) accessed medical records as data sources.

Purpose statements

Analysis of purpose statements was conducted using the Health Care Transition Resource Consortium (HCTRC) HCT model.27 This model is composed of four Domains: Individual (adolescents and emerging adults with special health care needs), Family/Social Support, Environment, and Health Care System domains. Initially, two reviewers independently coded purpose statements as to their alignments with HCTRC HCT Domains. Two reviewers met to compare responses and resolve discrepancies. Subsequently, another cycle of review was conducted with a third reviewer using the same process .

Purpose statements were examined as to their theoretical relevance to the Health Care Transition Research Consortium (HCTRC) health care transition (HCT) model.27 The Individual Domain was represented in 90% (n = 196) of studies. Aims in this domain focused largely on parent/caregivers’ perceptions of the YYAs’ health care transition process, factors to improve self-management skills, identifying predictors of health status, treatment adherence, increasing understanding of the progressive development of self-management, and exploring educational materials to improve YYAs’ knowledge and self-efficacy regarding illness management.

The Family/Social Support Domain aligned with 62% (n = 135) of studies. Variables of interest included allocation of treatment responsibility, how condition management skills are transferred from parent to YYAs, shifts in parent roles related to the HCT process, and exploration of parent perceptions of diverse factors, such as HCT readiness, and division of labor between youth and conflicts with self-management. Considerably fewer purpose statements aligned with the Health Care System Domain (35%, n = 77). Examples of studies in this domain identified predictors of health care use, HCT program efficacy, HCT program evaluation with input from parents/caregivers, and identifying content for validity of various HCT instruments. A minuscule number of the purpose statements addressed the Environment Domain (4%, n = 8). One study described the association of vocational issues related to HCT programs, more specifically this included environmental concerns related to YYAs’ a spectrum of resources that impact care on family roles in the community, school and the providers, another study focused on how parents work with the school, providers, and community in planning for health care transition. Studies most often had purpose statements that aligned with two domains. The breakdown of Domains in the purpose statements included: one Domain (n = 61, 28%), two Domains (n = 116, 53%), three Domains (n = 39, 18%), and four Domains (n = 2, 0.9%).

Themes were analyzed from the purpose statements. Revisions were made after reviews by two separate reviewers to achieve consensus and interrater reliability. The identified themes were parental perceptions/experiences, parental roles/ transfer of responsibility, self-management, self-efficacy and program evaluation/outcomes/psychometrics of instruments. Not surprisingly, parental perceptions/experiences demonstrated the greatest overlap frequency (22.2%), followed by self-management (17.7%), program evaluation/outcomes/psychometrics (11.85%), self-efficacy (4.4%) and role/transfer responsibility (4.4%).

Discussion

This scoping review provides insights and broadens understanding of the status of research that has been conducted with caregiver samples exploring the extent of self-management examined in health care transition of YYA.

Publication trends

The span of this review’s search parameters encompasses a time frame of over 30 years, yet studies examining caregivers’ perceptions and involvement in the self-management component of HCT were scarce prior to 2005, with the majority of studies published in the last eight years of this review. This publishing trend suggests that researchers were less inclined to explore caregiver perspectives related to the HCT process until more recently. It may be that researchers are now better appreciating caregiver perspectives about HCT to enhance understanding of the process not only for children but also for caregivers themselves. Incorporating multiple perspectives about the HCT process into the research design provides more descriptive contexts of YYA’s self-management knowledge and skills and can provide additional insights about the trustworthiness of YYA’s self-report about self-management.26, 28 Additionally, the inclusion of caregivers has the potential to improve HCT service development and implementation by extending consideration of needed adjustments with service provision as has been demonstrated in other settings and with different populations.29

Geographic profile of studies reviewed

Our findings indicate a geographic predominance of research from a majority of English-speaking countries, with nearly half of the papers originating in the United States. This trend parallels other scoping reviews examining caregiver HCT experiences,15 YYA with complex care needs and their caregivers,30, 31 and the context of HCT interventions for YYA and caregivers.32 In contrast, other reviews reflected a more multinational distribution with limited U.S. representation. For instance, a scoping review exploring HCT experiences among YYA with solid organ transplants, as reported by patients, caregivers, and providers in qualitative studies, found only one U.S. study among the nine reviewed.33 Similarly, Kanthasamy et al. 34 identified no U.S. representation among the twelve studies in their review of caregiver adaptation of individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities during HCT. It is relevant that geographic profiles of studies be noted, particularly in terms of systems of care and age of transfer. For example, in the United States, there is no nationalized health system, but rather a mixed model of publicly and privatized funding of health care compared to other countries, such as Canada and the United Kingdom. The age of transfer to adult-focused providers differs with respect to countries as well. In the United States, the age of transfer of care generally occurs at 21, whereas in Canada it more commonly occurs at18, 35. These environmental factors warrant consideration with the interpretation of findings and generalizability to other nations.

Conditions of YYA

YYA with physical conditions were the focus of previous scoping reviews examining the role of parents in health care transition as was evident in this scoping review.15, 29, 31, 32 One previous published scoping review focused on IDD.33 No previous scoping reviews were located examining parent involvement of YYA with mental health conditions during HCT. The previous and current scoping reviews adopted a non-categorical approach with the examination of parental/caregiver involvement during the HCT thereby obscuring relevant diagnostic considerations of specific conditions such as those with solid organ transplants and YYA with complex and time-intensive chronic management needs. As these scoping reviews demonstrate, limited data exist to guide practice and research advances as it pertains to diagnostic needs of YYA with not only physical conditions but also IDD and mental health conditions.

Data sources

As this scoping review demonstrated, the vast majority of tools used in this scoping review were self-report instruments. Self-report instruments are limited in terms of the biases evident in their sensitivity and specification as well as respondent bias.36, 37, 38 Few studies incorporated data sourced from medical records or large data sets enabling examination with larger sample sizes with more advanced statistical models.

Self-management outcomes

All studies of this review examined self-management as it pertained to condition management; whereas approximately one-third explored broader transition-related outcomes relegated to education, employment and community living. As this and other reviews reported narrowly defined outcomes focused on condition-management.15, 29, 31, 32

Caregiver demographics

As findings reveal, there are substantial gaps in the reporting of caregiver demographic data within HCT-related research. A small proportion of the studies provided any type of caregiver demographic information, and among those, the type and depth of data collected varied considerably. Important demographic characteristics, such as race, ethnicity, marital status, educational background, and employment status, were rarely reported, despite their relevance to understanding caregiver factors influencing HCT outcomes. These limitations result in an incomplete understanding of the caregiver factors that may influence the HCT process. Comprehensive demographic data are essential not only for characterizing caregiver participants, but also for identifying how caregiver characteristics may shape both caregivers’ engagement and YYA outcomes during and after transition.

The reporting limitations of caregiver demographic data are consistent with those noted in other HCT scoping reviews involving parents/caregivers. Several reviews documented caregiver sample size without providing corresponding demographic details.15, 33, 34 In another review examining caregivers of YYA with complex care needs, neither caregiver sample size nor demographics were reported.32 Similarly, Kanthasamy et al.34 reported only limited caregiver demographic data across the 12 studies included in their review, noting that ethnicity was reported in one study, nationality in three, age range in seven, and caregiver type in nine.

Collectively, the scarce data highlights a significant limitation in the evidence base. The lack of robust caregiver demographic data restricts our understanding of caregivers’ role in the HCT process. Improved and standardized demographic reporting would clarify how caregiver characteristics influence transition readiness and outcomes. and would better inform the development of family-centered and YYA HCT interventions.

Research designs

Quantitative designs were the most frequently used research design, followed by qualitative and mixed method designs. Other designs, such as secondary analyses and intervention studies, were far less often selected. This pattern aligns with findings from prior scoping reviews. For example, Ellison et al.15 and Kinch et al.32 similarly reported a majority of quantitative studies, with mixed methods and qualitative designs used less frequently. In contrast, the reviews by Li33 and Kanthasamy34 were composed of only qualitative studies. The Loura review30 included a broader range of designs, including qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods. Just 17% of studies in this review used a longitudinal approach, a finding partially reflected in the review by Kanthasamy et al.34 where about one-third of studies were longitudinal designs. The majority of descriptive cross-sectional designs may pose limitations for future development of caregiver interventional research in HCT models. Cross-sectional data provide only a snapshot of caregiver experiences and perceptions, limiting the ability to examine HCT outcome trajectories or causal pathways.39 Longitudinal designs are needed to illuminate how caregiver needs and engagement evolve throughout the transition process and to evaluate the effectiveness of HCT interventions over time.40, 41 Expanding the use of longitudinal designs will be critical for advancing caregiver-centered HCT models.

Purpose statements

The analysis of the purpose statements revealed that approximately one third of the studies included in this review omitted reference to the family/social support domain in purpose statements, although caregivers were included as respondents. The primary focus of most purpose statements was on YYA and less on the other HCT service model domains of the Health Care System and Environment. The purpose statements reflected a narrow focus when exploring self-management in the context of health care transition. As evident in this review, which was centered on exploring caregiver involvement and perceptions, the omission of caregivers from the purpose statements may be construed as likely an oversight, as these studies explored caregiver perceptions and participation in self-management. This same issue may also be relevant to the other Domains.

Self-management outcomes

As revealed in this scoping review, all studies examined condition management; whereas to a lesser extent approximately one-third of the studies examined outcomes pertaining to postsecondary education, employment and community living outcomes. This trend is evident in other scoping reviews exploring parental involvement and perspective during health care transition.15, 30, 32, 33 Beginning more than thirty years ago, health care transition planning is a recommended model of services that incorporates a comprehensive approach to health care transition outcomes that include the benchmarks associated with adult development.42

Transition readiness assessments

As revealed by the findings of this review, transition readiness assessments were used most often to measure concepts pertaining to self-management knowledge and skills needed to successfully transfer care to adult-focused health care providers. The concepts measured in these transition readiness assessments align with concepts associated with condition self-management knowledge and skills (e.g., knowledge, medication adherence, responsibility, skills, self-efficacy, problem solving).

Clinical and research implications

As this review indicates, evidence is lacking to guide the development and implementation of HCT service models that are specifically targeted to address caregiver concerns and issues during the transition period. Scant evidence exists to guide service providers in the provision of services for caregivers in addressing their support needs and fostering the process of “letting go.”43 Given the exposed gaps and limitations of caregiver research in HCT, the mapping of studies presented in this scoping review guides researchers of future studies. To promote optimal HCT outcomes and foster productive shifting of condition self-management responsibilities from caregivers to YYA, knowledge of caregiver needs and perspectives is needed.

Limitations

The limitations associated with scoping reviews are well known, as they are designed to provide a mapping of the body of literature rather than an in-depth analysis of the quality of research reported in systematic reviews. Limitations included poor categorization of data, which limited more in-depth mapping. This review did not explore differences reported for the caregiver (i.e. biological parent/nonbiological caregiver; parent gender). An in-depth review of the caregiver roles was not undertaken. This review was limited in its ability to provide more precise data on how self-management was measured. This review is hampered by the limited caregiver demographic data available in the studies reviewed that would provide relevant insights. Additionally, this review was limited to English-language articles; relevant research published in other languages was not integrated in this report.

Conclusion

A scoping review of the HCT literature spanning more than 30 years (1992–2024), exploring caregivers’ perceptions and involvement in the self-management component of the health care transition for YYA with CCC, revealed new insights and understanding. With a total of 218 studies included in this review, the findings revealed the shortcomings of the research conducted with caregiver input. Studies including caregivers were not evident in significant numbers until 2007, nearly fifteen years after the earliest results of HCT studies were reported. The limitations presented in this review include the conspicuous lack of demographic data on the caregiver subsamples themselves, thereby hindering substantive analysis of caregiver influences and roles in the HCT of YYA related to self-management. Other factors limiting such analysis included the prominent use of cross-sectional designs. In the analysis of purpose statements exploring the scope of HCT phenomenon, based upon the HCTRC HCT theoretical framework, the Domain of Individual was found most often. Fewer purpose statements were associated with Family/Support Domains, as in about one-third of reviewed studies, and far fewer included in the Health Care System and Environment Domains. The implications for research and practice are suggested to advance the development and implementation of family- and person-centered models of care.
