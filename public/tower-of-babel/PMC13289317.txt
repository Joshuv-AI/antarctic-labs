Activation of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells prior to radiopharmaceutical therapy improves antitumor response
Authors: Daeun Shim, Colette Mouton, Jena E Moseman, Hansel Comas Rojas, Donghwan Jeon, Malick B Idrissou, Reinier Hernandez, Jamey P Weichert, Douglas G McNeel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) delivers radiation systemically, enabling the treatment of metastatic cancers. Beyond killing tumor cells, RPT can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. With RPTs and immunotherapies already approved or in development for prostate cancer, many preclinical and clinical studies are evaluating their use in combination. However, due to the radiosensitivity of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, further studies are needed to determine the effects of RPT on these cells to better inform the sequence of immunotherapies that activate T cells when given with RPT.MethodsE.G7-OVA tumor-bearing mice received naïve or activated OT-I CD8+T cells prior to or following the administration of RPT using 90Y-NM600. Changes in tumor growth were monitored, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were evaluated for phenotypic and functional markers. The murine prostate tumor model TRAMP-C1 was used to evaluate this approach using tumor antigen-specific vaccination with 90Y-NM600.ResultsAntitumor efficacy was improved if OT-I CD8+T cells were present and activated prior to 90Y-NM600 administration than if the cells were delivered after RPT. Similarly, in vivo activation of adoptively transferred OT-I CD8+T cells, using ovalbumin (OVA)-specific vaccination, prior to RPT slowed tumor growth and increased the frequency of tumor-infiltrating OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells with effector memory phenotype and effector molecule production. Blockade of type I interferon, but not the upstream inhibition of stimulator of interferon genes, abrogated tumor growth delay resulting from the combination treatment. Tumor antigen-specific vaccination prior to 90Y-NM600 administration similarly improved antitumor outcomes in the TRAMP-C1 tumor model.ConclusionsOur study suggests that tumor-specific CD8+T cells need to be present and activated prior to RPT to enhance antitumor outcomes. This study highlights the importance of considering the effects of RPT on tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells when combining other T-cell activating therapies with RPT, as they may similarly display sequence-dependent antitumor outcomes.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Radiotherapy/radioimmunotherapy, Vaccine, T cell

Background

Radiation remains a central component of cancer therapy, used in the treatment of approximately half of patients with cancer.1 External beam radiotherapy is considered a first-line treatment for many localized early-stage cancers. While it can also be used to treat individual metastatic lesions, it often is not feasible to treat all sites of widespread metastatic disease. However, for metastatic cancers, radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) may be used. Consisting of a radioisotope, often linked to a tumor-targeting moiety, RPT allows for a systemic, tumor-selective delivery of radiation.2 As a result, RPT offers several advantages, such as simultaneous targeting of metastatic lesions and diagnostic capability, depending on the radioisotope used.3 There are currently 57 radiopharmaceuticals used for disease diagnosis and 13 for therapy worldwide.2 Therapeutic radioisotopes include α-emitting radium-223 and β-emitting phosphorus-32, strontium-89, lutetium-177, yttrium-90, or iodine-131. Current Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved RPTs are used to treat prostate cancer, neuroendocrine tumors, hepatocellular carcinoma, thyroid carcinomas, and non-Hodgkin’s follicular lymphoma.2

As the radioisotope decays, continuous low-dose rate irradiation is delivered to tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment. Immunogenic cell death may result, releasing danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). In surviving cells, DAMPs may engage toll-like receptors (TLRs), or nucleic acid-sensing pathways such as cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) or retinoic acid-inducible gene 1 (RIG-I) may be activated. Downstream type I interferons (IFNs) are then induced, activating dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages to promote antigen cross-presentation and chemokine release, ultimately recruiting and activating tumor-specific CD8+T cells.4 However, RPTs can also induce immunosuppressive effects, such as the recruitment of CD4+regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), along with depletion of radiosensitive CD8+T cells.4 5 These effects were observed in murine prostate cancer models using 90Y-NM600, a radiopharmaceutical alkylphosphocholine analog that localizes to tumor lipid rafts. 90Y-NM600 initially decreased CD8+T cell infiltration, while increasing the frequency of Tregs and MDSCs, thereby hindering the efficacy of RPT.5 6

Given its ability to modulate the tumor immune microenvironment, RPT is being evaluated in combination with immunotherapies.7 8 This approach is particularly relevant in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), given there are already FDA-approved RPTs (177Lu-PSMA-617 (Pluvicto) and 223RaCl2 (Xofigo)) as well as immunotherapies (a therapeutic cancer vaccine sipuleucel-T and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade for microsatellite instability-high tumors). Additional immunotherapies aimed at activating CD8+T cells to target prostate tumor antigens such as prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA), epithelial cell adhesion molecule, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), and kallikrein-related peptidase using chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) or bispecific T cell engagers (BiTEs) are currently in clinical trials.9,11 Preclinical studies combining RPT with immunotherapy in prostate cancer have shown mostly favorable responses. Combining 223Ra or 225Ac-PSMA-617 with anti-PD-1, or 177Lu-PSMA-617 with PSCA CAR-Ts increased tumor infiltration of CD8+T cells and improved survival.12,14 However, clinical trials evaluating RPT in combination with immunotherapy in patients with mCRPC have shown mixed results. Combining 223RaCl2 with anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-(L)1) blockade showed no clinical benefits or insignificant effects on CD8+T cell infiltration, regardless of the administration schedule.15 16 Administration of the therapeutic cancer vaccine sipuleucel-T with 223RaCl2 improved PSA decline and survival outcomes, but paradoxically lowered immune responses to the vaccine target antigen.17 Combining 177Lu-PSMA-617 with anti-PD-1 yielded favorable objective responses, 70% when administered concurrently or 56% when a single priming dose of RPT was followed by anti-PD-1.18 19 More trials are currently ongoing to evaluate RPT with immunotherapies.20 21 These preclinical and early-phase clinical studies demonstrate that combining RPT with immunotherapies holds promise for immunologically cold cancers like prostate cancer. However, further studies are needed to determine the effects of RPT on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, in order to guide the timing and choice of RPT and immunotherapy agents to further improve their antitumor efficacy.

In this study, we aimed to determine the effects of 90Y-NM600 on tumor-specific CD8+T cells to guide the approach of combining T cell-activating therapies with RPT. Due to the intrinsic radiosensitivity of CD8+lymphocytes, we expected the presence and activation status of tumor-specific CD8+T cells to be greatly affected by radiation. We hypothesized that the sequence of T cell-activating therapy with respect to RPT would affect antitumor efficacy. To test this, we used a well-studied ovalbumin (OVA)-expressing tumor model. OT-I CD8+T cells were adoptively transferred, either naïve or activated ex vivo or in vivo using antitumor vaccination, to determine when tumor-specific CD8+T cells needed to be present and activated relative to a single dose of 90Y-NM600. The TRAMP-C1 murine prostate cancer model was then used to assess whether this approach of T cell-activation delivered with RPT was potentially translatable to prostate cancer.

Methods

Radiosynthesis and labeling of 90Y-NM600

90YCl3 was purchased from PerkinElmer (Shelton, Connecticut, USA). Radiolabeling of NM600 and purification were performed as previously described.22 23

Dosimetry estimations

Using a Monte Carlo-based dosimetry assessment platform, in vivo dosimetry estimations were derived from previous reports of EL4 (parent cell line of E.G7-OVA) and TRAMP-C1, and in vitro dosimetry was calculated using a cell monolayer model as previously described.523,25 Based on previous studies, 1.85 MBq and 4.63 MBq delivered approximately 5 Gy and 12.44 Gy to the E.G7-OVA tumors, respectively.23 9.25 MBq delivered 5 Gy to the TRAMP-C1 tumors.5

Cell line

E.G7-OVA (derivative of EL4, Cat # CRL-2113) and TRAMP-C1 (Cat # CRL-2730) cell lines were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). E.G7-OVA cells were transduced via lentivirus to express high levels of PD-L1 as previously described (referred to as “E.G7-OVA”).25 Cell lines were maintained using the recommended cell culture media by ATCC. Primary murine splenocytes and tumor lysates were cultured in mouse media consisting of Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium with L-glutamine (Corning, Corning, New York, USA, Cat # 10–040 CV), 10% fetal bovine serum (GeminiBio, West Sacramento, California, USA, Cat # 100–106), 50 µM β-MeOH (Gibco, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA, Cat # 21–985-023), 1% HEPES (Cytiva, Wilmington, Delaware, USA, Cat # SH30237.01), 1% sodium pyruvate (Corning, Cat # MT25000CI), and 200 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Corning, Cat # 15–140-122).

Mice

C57BL/6J (Cat # 000664), C57BL/6-Tg(TcraTcrb)1100Mjb/J (referred to as “OT-I”, Cat # 003831), and B6(Cg)-Sting1tm1.2Camb/J (referred to as “STING KO”, Cat # 025805) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA). All mice were housed in microisolator cages under aseptic conditions. Mice were kept on a 12-hour light-dark cycle. All experiments were performed during the light cycle.

Tumor studies

Treatment studies using E.G7-OVA tumor model

5–7 weeks old female C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously implanted with 1×106 E.G7-OVA cells into the right flank on day −10. Animals were randomized to treatment groups, and a single dose of 90Y-NM600 was administered intravenously via the tail vein on day 0. CD8+T cells were isolated from OT-I spleens using a CD8+T cell negative selection kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, British Columbia, USA, Cat # 19853), and 0.5×106 cells were intraperitoneally injected into each mouse 3 days before, 3 days after, or 10 days after 1.85 MBq 90Y-NM600 administration. In some studies, OT-I splenocytes were activated in the presence of 0.1 µg/mL SIINFEKL peptide in mouse media for 48 hours at 37°C. CD8+T cells were then isolated, and 0.5×106 ex vivo activated cells were intraperitoneally injected into the tumor-bearing mice on day −3 or day 3.

For vaccination studies, 0.5×106 naïve OT-I CD8+T cells were adoptively transferred on day −3. On day −2, 100 µg of a plasmid DNA vaccine encoding ovalbumin (pTVG-sOVA) or empty vector control (pTVG4) was administered intradermally in the ear pinna. As an alternative, 100 µg of H-2b-restricted peptide epitope from chicken ovalbumin, OVA257-264 (SIINFEKL), or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) control, was administered subcutaneously in the flank as a vaccine. TLR3 (100 µg/mouse Poly (I:C), HMW, InvivoGen, Cat # vac-pic) and TLR9 agonists (50 µg/mouse ODN1826, Integrated DNA Technologies, San Diego, California, USA) were co-delivered with peptide or PBS control as adjuvants.26 Where indicated, some mice received an intraperitoneal administration of 0.5 mg monoclonal anti-mouse IFN alpha/beta receptor subunit 1 (IFNAR1) antibody (clone MAR1-5A3, Bio X Cell, Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA, Cat # BE0241) on day −1 and 0.25 mg on days 2 and 6 following RPT, or 750 nM STING antagonist H-151 (InvivoGen, San Diego, California, USA, Cat # inh-h151) in 10% Tween-80 for seven consecutive days starting on day 0. In separate studies, 6–20 weeks old STING−/− mice were treated as described above.

Tumors were measured every other day using calipers, and the tumor volume was calculated according to the formula: (12) × (long axis) × (short axis)2. Group tumor growth curves were stopped when a mouse in each treatment group reached its endpoint. For immunophenotyping, tumors were collected and digested in medium containing 0.2 mg/mL DNAse I (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA, Cat # DN25-1G), 2 mg/mL collagenase (Sigma, Cat # C0130-5G), and one tablet of protease inhibitor (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA, Cat # 11836145001) per 50 mL of digest medium. The obtained single-cell suspension was used for flow cytometry.

Treatment studies using TRAMP-C1 tumor model

6–7 weeks old male C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously implanted with 1×106 TRAMP-C1 cells in a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel (Corning, Cat # 356234) and PBS on day −26. 100 µg of pTVG4 or DNA vaccine encoding the androgen receptor ligand binding domain (pTVG-AR) was injected intradermally, with or without co-administration of TLR3 and TLR9 agonists as adjuvants, on days −17 and −3. 9.25 MBq 90Y-NM600 was intravenously administered on day 0. Tumors were measured by calipers two times per week.

In vitro activation of OT-I CD8+ T cells with 90Y

DCs were isolated from wildtype C57Bl/6 mice bearing B16 melanoma tumors expressing FLT3L using a pan-DC negative selection kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat # 19863). Isolated DCs were pre-loaded with SIINFEKL (2 µg/mL) at 37°C for 2 hours, washed, and incubated 1:1 with naïve OT-I CD8+T cells in a 6-well plate in mouse media. After 48 hours, 90Y was then added to each well to deliver either 2 Gy or 5 Gy absorbed dose in 48 hours. Approximately 0.27 MBq/mL 90Y was added to deliver 2 Gy, and 0.67 MBq/mL 90Y was added to deliver 5 Gy. Each condition was tested in triplicate. Cells were collected at 24 and 48 hours following the incubation with 90Y-containing media for flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry

1−2×106 cells from digested tumor lysates were plated into each well of a 96-well microtiter plate, and the Fc receptor was blocked using αCD16/32 (BD, Cat #553142) for 20 min at 4°C. Cells were then stained for surface markers for 30 min and fixed using the eBioscience FoxP3 Fixation/Permeabilization kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA, Cat # 00–5523-00). For intracellular cytokine staining, 1×106 cells were plated and activated with αCD3/CD28 beads for 4 hours at 37°C in the presence of GolgiStop protein transport inhibitor (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA, Cat # 554724). Cells were permeabilized and stained for an additional 30 min and assessed by flow cytometry using an Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The flow cytometry results were analyzed using FlowJo V.10.10.0 software (BD, Ashland, Oregon, USA). Representative gating strategies are shown in online supplemental figures 1–3. All antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table 1.

RNA sequencing

Tumors were collected and stored at −80°C until radioactivity reached background. Then the samples were thawed, finely minced, and homogenized in TRIzol using a bead beater. Homogenates were centrifuged, and supernatants were collected for RNA isolation using Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, California, USA, Cat # R2050). Gene expression analysis was performed using the nCounter Mouse Immune Exhaustion Panel (Brucker Spatial Biology, Bothell, Washington, USA, Cat # XT-M-EXHAUST-12) on the nCounter MAX system (Brucker Spatial Biology). Data were analyzed using the Rosalind Bio platform,27 and graphed using GraphPad Prism V.10.6.1.

Statistics

All comparative analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.6.1. Tumor growth studies were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, and survival studies were analyzed using a log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Flow cytometry results were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA as indicated. P value<0.05 was considered statistically significant for all studies.

Results

90Y-NM600 (RPT) increased infiltration of immune cells into E.G7-OVA tumors, but not OVA257-264-specific CD8+ T cells

Previous studies have shown that RPT, using the pan-cancer-targeting treatment agent 90Y-NM600, can dynamically modulate the tumor immune microenvironment, as evidenced by an initial depletion and subsequent enrichment of CD8+T cells.5 23 Here, we investigated whether 90Y-NM600 could specifically increase the number of tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells, using the E.G7-OVA tumor model. Tumors were implanted on day −10, and 1.85 MBq or 4.63 MBq RPT was administered intravenously on day 0 (figure 1A). Based on the previously reported dosimetry of the parental cell line EL4, we estimated that low dose RPT delivered approximately 5 Gy and the high dose delivered 12.44 Gy to the E.G7-OVA tumors.23 As expected, tumor growth was delayed in a dose-dependent manner (figure 1B, online supplemental figure 4). Tumors were collected on days 3 and 14 following RPT and analyzed for immune infiltrates. Although the total number of tumor-infiltrating immune cells was similar across treatment groups, high dose initially depleted CD8+T cells, followed by a significant increase in the numbers of CD8+T cells 14 days post-RPT (figure 1C,D), as expected based on a previous study using a murine prostate cancer model.5 However, neither low nor high dose increased the number of OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells (figure 1E). Additionally, the number of DCs was not affected by RPT, whereas immunosuppressive MDSCs and CD4+Tregs significantly increased 14 days after high-dose treatment (figure 1F–H). Further characterization of the tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells showed that RPT initially decreased the percentage of effector memory population while increasing memory precursor effector cells and had no significant effects on the central memory or short-lived effector populations (figure 1I–L). Taken together, these findings indicated that although RPT led to a time-dependent increase in CD8+T cell infiltration, the frequency of tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells did not specifically increase. This finding led us to hypothesize that combining RPT with a therapy that could increase the frequency of tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells may potentiate antitumor responses. Because the low dose 90Y-NM600 was by itself insufficient to delay tumor growth, we chose to use this subtherapeutic dose in subsequent studies to specifically assess whether combination with CD8+T cell targeted therapies improved therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, we reasoned that, as a translational direction, future combination therapies using RPT might be best explored using lower doses that elicit fewer adverse effects.

Delivery of naïve OT-I CD8+ T cells before or after RPT did not enhance antitumor response

Since RPT did not increase the frequency of tumor-infiltrating antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, at least CD8+T cells specific for the dominant H-2Kb epitope of ovalbumin, we next investigated whether RPT could increase the number of intratumoral OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells if they were present in greater numbers before or after RPT. Similar to the previous study, tumors were implanted on day −10, and 1.85 MBq 90Y-NM600 was administered on day 0. OT-I CD8+T cells were adoptively transferred into tumor-bearing mice on days −3, 3, or 10 (figure 2A). To account for the radiosensitivity of lymphocytes, OT-I CD8+T cells were purposefully delivered after 1 and 3 half-lives had elapsed (90Y T1/22.67 days). Delivery of naïve OT-I CD8+ T cells did not significantly affect the tumor growth, and no difference was observed if these cells were delivered before or after RPT (figure 2B, online supplemental figure 5). When tumors were collected 14 days post-RPT, no significant changes in the numbers of CD8+ or OVA257-264-specific CD8+ T cells were observed (figure 2C,D, online supplemental figure 5D). These results indicated that low-dose RPT was insufficient to recruit naïve tumor-specific CD8+ T cells to tumors.

Delivery of activated OT-I CD8+ T cells prior to RPT significantly delayed tumor growth

Since the presence of naïve OT-I CD8+T cells did not affect tumor growth, we aimed to determine whether delivering activated OT-I CD8+T cells before or after RPT could improve antitumor efficacy. OT-I splenocytes were activated in vitro by incubating with cognate SIINFEKL peptide for 48 hours. 0.5×106 naïve or activated OT-I CD8+T cells were then adoptively transferred into tumor-bearing mice either on day −3 or day 3 relative to RPT administration (figure 3A). As shown in figure 3B, delivery of activated OT-I CD8+T cells alone delayed tumor growth, with one to two mice achieving a complete response (no palpable tumor by the end of the study). When activated OT-I CD8+T cells were delivered after RPT, a similar outcome was observed. However, delivering activated OT-I CD8+T cells before RPT improved antitumor outcomes (five of seven mice achieved a complete response), and this group demonstrated the best survival (figure 3B–C, online supplemental figure 6). This result was unexpected, given the radiosensitivity of CD8+T cells. To further elucidate the effects of RPT on activated CD8+T cells, OT-I CD8+T cells were activated in vitro by SIINFEKL-loaded DCs for 48 hours, 90Y was then added to the media, and cells were collected after 24 and 48 hours for immunophenotyping (online supplemental figure 7A). 90Y induced significant cell death within 24 hours (online supplemental figure 7B,C). However, the addition of 90Y significantly increased OT-I CD8+T cells with an effector memory phenotype, expression of activation marker CD137, while not significantly affecting the expression of inhibitory checkpoint molecules PD-1, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein-4, lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG3), T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), or T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3) (online supplemental figure 7D–J). These findings suggested that while radioactivity may kill activated tumor-specific CD8+T cells, it might also favorably enhance T-cell activation and differentiation. Collectively, these data suggested that CD8+T cell targeted therapy can be effectively combined with RPT, but tumor-specific CD8+T cells need to be ideally present and activated prior to RPT to improve antitumor efficacy.

In vivo activation of OT-I CD8+ T cells using a vaccine prior to RPT significantly improved antitumor response

Given that delivery of ex vivo activated tumor-specific CD8+T cells prior to RPT improved response, we next evaluated whether in vivo activation of OT-I CD8+T cells using a vaccine prior to RPT would similarly improve antitumor outcomes. E.G7-OVA tumors were implanted on day −9, and 0.5×106 naïve OT-I CD8+T cells were adoptively transferred on day −3. OVA-encoding DNA plasmid (pTVG-sOVA) or empty vector control (pTVG4) was intradermally injected on day −2, followed by low-dose RPT on day 0 (figure 4A). Vaccination before RPT significantly delayed tumor growth and improved survival compared with RPT alone, pTVG-sOVA alone, or pTVG4 with RPT (figure 4B,C, online supplemental figure 8). To determine whether vaccine-activated OT-I CD8+T cells were recruited following RPT, tumors were collected on days 3, 7, and 14 for flow cytometry. Despite no significant differences in the number of total tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells after each treatment, there was a significant increase in OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells, with predominantly effector memory phenotype, on day 7 post-vaccine and RPT (figure 4D–F, online supplemental figure 8B). Compared with vaccine or RPT monotherapy, administering pTVG-sOVA prior to RPT also significantly increased the number of tumor-infiltrating OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells producing effector molecules, including granzyme B, perforin, IFN-γ, and interleukin-10 (figure 4G, online supplemental figure 9). Similar tumor growth and survival trends were observed when SIINFEKL peptide vaccine was administered prior to RPT (online supplemental figure 10). Furthermore, we evaluated whether RPT affected the proliferation of infiltrating tumor-specific CD8+T cells, as well as the TCF1+stem like subsets, given reports that local radiation enhanced the proliferation capacity of intratumoral CD8+T cells, including stem-like populations.28 29 Following the delivery of OT-I CD8+T cells and pTVG-sOVA, tumors were collected post-vaccination on day 0 (prior to RPT) and on day 7 (with or without RPT) (online supplemental figure 11A). The number of infiltrating OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells significantly increased on day 7 when RPT was administered, as well as the frequency of cells with a proliferating and stem-like (TCF1+PD-1+) phenotype (online supplemental figure 11B–D). Overall, these findings suggested that using an antitumor vaccine in combination with RPT increased the frequency of tumor-specific CD8+T cells with effector memory phenotype and improved their functional phenotype.

Vaccination prior to RPT upregulated genes associated with the type I interferon pathway

To explore potential mechanisms underlying the enhanced antitumor efficacy observed when vaccination was used prior to RPT, we assessed gene expression changes within the tumor. Similar to figure 4A, tumor-bearing mice received a DNA vaccine encoding ovalbumin alone or followed by RPT, or a vaccine control vector followed by RPT. Tumors were collected on day 7 and evaluated for gene expression changes by RNA sequencing. Heatmap of Gene Set Variation Analysis enrichment scores indicated increased enrichment of pathways such as type I IFN, chemokine signaling, myeloid immune evasion, and cytotoxicity following the combination treatment of pTVG-sOVA with RPT (figure 5A). Furthermore, differential expression analysis demonstrated that combining pTVG-sOVA with RPT significantly upregulated 275 genes compared with pTVG-sOVA alone (fold change ≥2, Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)-adjusted p value<0.05) and 117 genes compared with pTVG4 with RPT (fold change ≥2, BH-adjusted p value<0.1) (online supplemental table 2). Notably, canonical interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) downstream of type I IFN signaling, such as Oas1a/2/3, Ifit1/3/3b, Mx1/2, and Rsad2, were significantly upregulated when comparing vaccination with or without subsequent RPT (figure 5B) or when comparing RPT with or without antitumor vaccination (figure 5C). Other genes associated with type I IFN signaling were upregulated as well, including the feedback regulator Socs3, IFN receptor-associated kinase Tyk2, and effector ISGs Xaf1 and Gbp2 (figure 5B,C).30 31 Cell type profiling also indicated greater abundance of gene signatures associated with cytotoxic cells, DCs, and macrophages when pTVG-sOVA was administered prior to RPT (figure 5D). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that combining pTVG-sOVA with RPT significantly increased the infiltration of macrophages compared with untreated or monotherapies (online supplemental figure 12C). Although the frequency of DCs remained unchanged, expression of activation markers CD83 and CD86 was significantly increased on DCs and macrophages when RPT was administered (online supplemental figure 12D–F). These findings suggested that combining an antitumor vaccine with RPT likely induced the activation of the type I IFN pathway, which was not observed with either treatment alone. Additionally, activation of the type I IFN pathway could have contributed to the increased abundance and activation of DCs and macrophages, leading to downstream recruitment and enhanced functional phenotype of vaccine-activated tumor-specific CD8+T cells.

Blockade of type I IFN receptor abrogated tumor growth delay and worsened survival

To confirm whether combining an antitumor vaccine with RPT enhanced antitumor efficacy in a type I IFN-dependent manner, we treated tumor-bearing mice in the presence or absence of type I IFNAR1 neutralizing antibody (figure 6A). The addition of the IFNAR1 blockade abrogated the delay in tumor growth and improvement in survival observed with combined vaccine and RPT treatment (figure 6B–C, online supplemental figure 13). Because radiation can induce type I IFN by activating the nucleic acid-sensing cGAS-STING pathway, we similarly evaluated the role of cGAS-STING in mediating antitumor efficacy.32 33 However, STING blockade, either by using STING−/− mice or by administering STING antagonist H-151, did not abrogate the delay in tumor growth when pTVG-sOVA was delivered prior to RPT (online supplemental figure 14 and 15). Therefore, the combination approach of antitumor vaccine followed by RPT improved antitumor response through a cGAS/STING-independent type I IFN pathway.

Administration of AR-encoding DNA vaccine followed by low-dose RPT significantly delayed tumor growth in the murine TRAMP-C1 prostate cancer model

Because ovalbumin is a foreign antigen, and not a true tumor antigen, we next examined whether using a vaccine targeting a relevant tumor antigen prior to RPT could similarly lead to improved antitumor efficacy in a murine model of prostate cancer. TRAMP-C1 tumor cells were implanted into male C57Bl/6 mice on day −26, and a low dose 90Y-NM600 delivering 5 Gy to the tumor was administered on day 0. Two biweekly immunizations with a DNA plasmid encoding the androgen receptor ligand-binding domain (pTVG-AR), or vector control pTVG4, were given with TLR3 and TLR9 agonists as adjuvants either 3 days before or after RPT (figure 7A).32 34 Similar to the findings in the OVA model, administration of pTVG-AR prior to RPT significantly delayed tumor growth and improved survival compared with the monotherapies or vaccination post-RPT (figure 7B–C, online supplemental figures 16 and 17).

Discussion/conclusion

Preclinical and clinical studies have combined RPT with immunotherapies for many different cancer types, but optimal means of combining these therapies has not been well studied. Although largely dependent on factors like dose and physical properties of the radioisotope, one of the ways that RPT can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment is through depletion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, including CD8+T cells.29 Therefore, understanding how RPT affects tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes is important for guiding the timing of RPT with immunotherapy. Here, we used an OVA-expressing tumor model to determine when antigen-specific CD8+T cells need to be present and activated relative to RPT, in this case using 90Y-NM600. Our findings included: (1) a non-ablative dose of radiation is feasible when combined with T cell-activating therapy; (2) T cells need to be present and activated prior to RPT, which can be accomplished by using vaccination prior to RPT, and (3) the type I IFN pathway is important for the therapeutic efficacy of cancer vaccines when combined with RPT.

Radiation therapy aims to maximize tumor control by inducing tumor cell killing while minimizing damage to the nearby normal tissues. However, beyond inducing cell death, radiation can affect the tumor immune microenvironment in a dose-dependent manner. Low to moderate doses can induce type I IFN and promote CD8+T cell infiltration, whereas high doses elicit major histocompatibility complex-I upregulation and immunogenic cell death, releasing DAMPs.30 Especially at high doses, protumoral effects may also arise, including decreased type I IFN due to exonuclease TREX1 activation and increased infiltration of Tregs and MDSCs.5 31 35 Similarly, we observed dose-dependent effects of 90Y-NM600 in the E.G7-OVA tumor model. High-dose initially decreased the number of CD8+T cells, followed by a significant increase in the frequency of CD8+T cells 14 days later, along with MDSCs and Tregs (figure 1G,H). A prior study in a murine prostate cancer model similarly observed an increase in infiltrating CD8+T cells, along with increased PD-1 expression, following 90Y-NM600. Yet, the combination with PD-1 blockade worsened antitumor outcomes, as the PD-1 blockade increased the frequency and suppressive activity of Tregs.6 Similarly, 90Y-NM600 also increased infiltration of MDSCs, thereby suppressing CD8+T cells and worsening the therapeutic efficacy of androgen deprivation therapy.7 Hence, the dose-dependent (and the possible radionuclide-dependent) effects of RPT on the immunosuppressive populations should be carefully considered, as it may result in negative outcomes when combined with immunotherapies. The effects of RPT on these immunosuppressive cells have been observed in patients as well, though the current findings are limited and conflicting. Reduction in the frequency of Tregs in the peripheral blood of patients with differentiated thyroid cancer was observed following 131I treatment.36 However, in patients with mCRPC, there was an increase in the fractions of Tregs and MDSCs following 223RaCl2.37 Similarly, expansion of Tregs was observed following a single dose of 177Lu-PSMA-617 in patients with mCRPC.38 These contrasting findings in patients, while reflecting differences in cancer types and radioisotopes, collectively underscore that RPT can exert both antitumoral and protumoral effects across distinct lymphocyte populations. Ultimately, these immunosuppressive cells contribute to tumor-associated mechanisms of immunoresistance in both murine models and patients with prostate cancer.5 6 39 Therefore, using a non-ablative dose of RPT to increase tumor-specific CD8+ T-cell infiltration while limiting immunosuppressive populations may improve response to immunotherapy and reduce treatment-related toxicity.

We adoptively transferred OT-I CD8+T cells into tumor-bearing mice to determine the timing of tumor-specific CD8+T cell presence and activation with respect to RPT. We found that tumor-specific CD8+T cells needed to be both present and activated prior to RPT to enhance antitumor response. Similarly, therapeutic cancer vaccines administered prior to RPT in both E.G7-OVA and TRAMP-C1 tumor models significantly delayed tumor growth and improved survival (figures 4 and 7). This was surprising, as we expected tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells to be depleted by RPT, and hence that it might be advantageous to resupply these cells after RPT. Lymphocytes, particularly T cells, are highly radiosensitive.34 However, intrinsic radiosensitivity differs among T-cell subsets, with naïve cells being the most radiosensitive, followed by central and effector memory T cells, and tissue-resident memory populations.40 41 We similarly observed that activated OT-I CD8+T cells exposed to 90Y in vitro, despite reduced cell viability, showed a significant increase in an effector memory phenotype and elevated CD137 expression, suggesting these may be more radioresistant or induced with radiation (online supplemental figure 7). Indeed, recent evidence suggests that radiation does not substantially impair tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells as much as previously thought. Köksal et al demonstrated that radiation efficacy was driven by pre-existing intratumoral CD8+T cells, specifically TCF-1+stem like cells that proliferated and differentiated following irradiation.42 Arina et al showed that pre-existing T cells persisted after local radiotherapy, with enhanced motility and IFN-γ production, and reprogrammed towards a radioresistant tissue-resident memory phenotype.41 Although these studies used EBRT, they highlight the effects of radiation on the proliferation and functionality of pre-existing T cells. Although we did not directly assess changes in the functionality of infiltrating tumor-specific CD8+T cells, we similarly observed a significant increase in the quantity of OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells with effector memory phenotype when therapeutic cancer vaccines were administered prior to RPT (figure 4, online supplemental figure 9). While tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes had effector function as characterized by effector cytokine/enzyme secretion, these were not significantly greater in mice receiving vaccination prior to RPT, suggesting that the primary effect of this treatment sequence was quantitative, to increase the number of antigen-specific infiltrating cells, rather than a qualitative change in their function. Additionally, we observed a significant increase in the frequency of proliferating and TCF1+PD-1+ population over time when the vaccine was administered followed by RPT (online supplemental figure 11). Future studies to characterize the phenotypes and function of human tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells prior to and after RPT may be important to determine if there might be a similar sequence preference in combining immunotherapies with RPT to improve the efficacy of RPT for patients with cancer.

We found that the therapeutic efficacy of combining a cancer vaccine with 90Y-NM600 depended on type I IFN, a key mediator by which radiation modulates the tumor immune microenvironment.43 Although radiation-induced type I IFN is commonly attributed to activation of the cGAS-STING axis, we found STING was dispensable for the therapeutic efficacy of pTVG-sOVA with RPT (online supplemental figures 8 and 9).32 44 Others have similarly reported that radiation can induce type I IFN through alternative pathways. Previous studies suggest radiation can activate RIG-I as well as TLRs to induce downstream type I IFN.36,38 In our study, we did not assess the expression of genes directly associated with the RIG-I pathway, such as Ddx58, Mavs, or Ifih1. However, combining pTVG-sOVA with RPT significantly upregulated Tlr7, along with NF-κB-responsive genes (eg, Cxcl1, Il1a/b, Vcam1, Icam1, Ccl2, Cd40, Tnfaip3, Cd80, Cd86), potentially suggesting engagement of TLR/MyD88 pathways and NF-kB-driven inflammatory signaling.45 Upregulation of these genes associated with TLR signaling, in addition to the canonical ISGs, antigen-presenting cell activation, and inflammatory cytokines/chemokines, likely suggests the involvement of multiple innate immune pathways to promote type I IFN-dependent antitumor immunity (figure 5, supplemental table 2). In addition, a previous study showed that co-culturing 90Y-NM600-treated B16 STING−/− tumor cells with CD8+T cells increased IFN-γ expression, highlighting that RPT can affect CD8+T cell function via an alternative, cGAS-STING-independent mechanism.46 It is also interesting to note that combining pTVG-sOVA with RPT upregulated genes associated with type I IFN signaling even when compared with pTVG4 with RPT, suggesting that tumor-specific vaccination also contributed to type I IFN as well (figure 5, supplemental table 2). We suspect type I IFN was acting on tumor-infiltrating macrophages and DC, given our findings that there was an increase in the number of myeloid cells and markers associated with DC activation (online supplemental figure 12). However, many tumor and stromal cell types can respond to type I IFN signaling, and future studies will be needed to identify the specific cell types and signaling pathways affected by type I IFN release following these treatments.

Despite the central role of type I IFN in enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of antitumor vaccines with RPT in our study, at least as determined by IFNAR1 blockade, the opposing effects of type I IFN in antitumor immunity should be noted. Type I IFN is critical for antitumor immunity, but chronic signaling may contribute to immunoresistance. In fact, upregulation of type I IFN resulting in chronic inflammation has been shown to contribute to immunotherapy resistance.47 In addition, prolonged type I IFN signaling can promote a tumor microenvironment favoring T-cell exhaustion.48 Indeed, we observed that combining vaccines with RPT initially delayed tumor growth in our study, yet the tumors eventually grew, potentially suggesting a loss of T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. While further studies are needed to determine the mechanism underlying the loss of tumor control in our models, chronic exposure to type I IFN signaling may have contributed to T-cell exhaustion or tolerance. Therefore, although type I IFN-inducing therapies like such as vaccines or STING agonists may initially improve radiotherapy efficacy, their use with RPT, potentially resulting in prolonged type I IFN signaling, warrants further investigation.49 50 51

Although this study provided insights into combining T cell-activating therapies with RPT to enhance therapeutic efficacy, there are several limitations that should be noted. The EG7-OVA tumor model allowed us to evaluate the effects of vaccines and RPT on infiltrating OVA257-264-specific CD8+T cells. However, because OVA is a highly immunogenic non-“self” antigen, this model system may not fully reflect the challenges of activating tumor-specific CD8+T cells in immunologically cold environments. To address this limitation, we extended our studies to the TRAMP-C1 tumor model, using pTVG-AR to activate tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells against a native “self” antigen. Nevertheless, given the heterogeneity of tumors and the distinct mechanisms of immunoresistance observed across tumor types, this combination treatment should be evaluated in other cancer models as well, including mouse models with humanized immune systems. In addition, to determine the optimal timing of T-cell activation relative to RPT, antitumor vaccines were administered relative to a single dose of RPT delivering 5 Gy to the tumor. Yet, many FDA-approved RPTs are currently given on a fixed-activity, multicycle dosing schedule.52 For example, 177Lu-PSMA-617 is administered at 7.4 GBq every 6 weeks for up to six cycles.53 Estimating mean absorbed dose in patients remains challenging, as personalized dosimetry is not yet routinely implemented in clinical practice.52 While the injected activity we used was not based on a direct translational equivalent, it likely reflected the clinical reality that RPT is typically administered using fixed-activity regimens that may result in non-uniform, subablative tumor absorbed doses in some patients. We also understand that since RPT is typically administered in multiple cycles, timing with T cell-activating therapies may be clinically challenging to implement. Yet, our study provides proof of concept that such combinations can improve antitumor outcomes, and that the sequence of treatments is important to consider. Future studies should evaluate a combination approach using T-cell targeted therapies and multicycle RPT that may better reflect clinical practice.

While combining cancer vaccines with 90Y-NM600 significantly delayed tumor growth in E.G7-OVA and TRAMP-C1 tumor models, tumor control was eventually lost, suggesting that the tumors acquired resistance. We used a β-emitting RPT 90Y-NM600 throughout the study. Immunomodulatory effects of RPT largely depend on the physical properties of the radioisotope, such as its half-life and linear energy transfer (LET).54 α-emitters with high LET can induce localized irreparable DNA damage, inducing greater cytotoxicity compared with β-emitters with lower LET. A previous study showed that 225Ac-NM600 significantly reduced immunosuppressive Tregs and MDSCs, while β-emitters, such as 177Lu-NM600 or 90Y-NM600, increased these populations in murine prostate cancer models.5 29 Since these immunosuppressive cells can limit the efficacy of both RPT and therapeutic cancer vaccines, α-emitting RPT will be explored as a future direction to determine if this can be used to deplete these cells and potentially circumvent this mechanism of treatment resistance.5 6 In addition, exhaustion of intratumoral CD8+T cells over time represents another barrier to antitumor immunity.55 Reactivation of exhausted intratumoral CD8+T cells or enhancement of de novo CD8+T cell recruitment via booster vaccination, or in combination with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), could be explored to prolong tumor control.56 55 57

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that the timing of therapies should be considered when combining T cell-activating therapies with RPT. Specifically, activation of tumor-specific CD8+T cells prior to RPT was important for improved antitumor response. Although we have focused on therapeutic cancer vaccines to activate tumor-specific CD8+T cells, other T cell-activating therapies for cancers, including BiTEs and ICB, should be considered for combination with RPT and may similarly display sequence-dependent antitumor outcomes. Given the growing number of BiTEs, CAR-T cell therapies, and ICBs under evaluation in combination with RPTs for prostate cancer treatment, further study of the timing and approaches to combination will be necessary to improve therapeutic efficacy and patient outcomes.
