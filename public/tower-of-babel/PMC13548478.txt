Psychophysiological changes during breast cancer treatment: a longitudinal analysis of subjective and objective indicators
Authors: Iva Burešová, Petr Květon, Martin Jelínek, Jana Halámková, Igor Kiss, Iva Hrnčiříková, Kateřina Kapounková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in early detection and treatment of breast cancer have led to improved survival rates, shifting attention toward the physical and psychological consequences of treatment. Adjuvant therapies are frequently associated with fatigue, reduced quality of life, sleep disturbances, and psychological distress, yet longitudinal data capturing changes in both subjective and objective health indicators during treatment remain limited. The present study aimed to examine changes in selected psychophysiological characteristics in breast cancer patients from diagnosis through completion of adjuvant treatment.MethodsA longitudinal design with two assessment points was employed: before treatment initiation and after completion of adjuvant therapy. The sample consisted of 75 women with breast cancer (mean age = 52.04; sd = 11.63). Self-report measures assessed fatigue (MFI), quality of life (EORTC QLQ-C30), anxiety and depression (HADS), sleep quality (PSQI), and motivation for physical activity (MPAM-R). Objective indicators included body mass index (BMI) and functional exercise capacity measured by the six-minute walk test (6MWT). Changes over time were analyzed using General Linear Models for repeated measures, with age and treatment duration included as covariates. Holm–Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple testing.ResultsFollowing treatment, patients exhibited significant increases in physical and general fatigue and a marked deterioration in the physical component of quality of life. BMI increased modestly but significantly over time. In contrast, no significant change was observed in objectively measured physical fitness (6MWT). Depressive symptoms significantly decreased after treatment, suggesting psychological adaptation despite physical deterioration. Age-related effects indicated greater post-treatment worsening of sleep quality in younger women, whereas older women showed a more pronounced decline in physical quality of life.ConclusionsThe findings reveal a notable discrepancy between subjective health perceptions and objective physical capacity during breast cancer treatment. They underscore the importance of routine monitoring of fatigue, quality of life, and body weight, and support the implementation of individualized, age-sensitive supportive care strategies. Addressing psychophysiological burden during treatment may improve long-term well-being and clinical outcomes in breast cancer survivors.

Background

The global incidence of breast cancer continues to rise, and it remains the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women worldwide. Although the highest incidence has long been observed in women over the age of 50, recent years have also seen an increase in the number of newly diagnosed cases among younger women [1]. Despite significant advances in contemporary medicine, breast cancer remains the second leading cause of death worldwide after cardiovascular diseases and continues to be the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women [2].

Treatment of breast cancer, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, biological therapy, and endocrine treatment, is relatively often associated with both acute and long-term adverse effects [3–5]. Longitudinal research demonstrates that although some treatment-related adverse effects gradually subside over time, a substantial subgroup of patients experiences chronic and late sequelae. These are particularly reflected in persistent fatigue, pain, hormonal and cardiovascular complications, as well as a wide range of other long-term somatic symptoms.

Among the most persistent and clinically significant adverse effects is fatigue, which remains at a high level or may even worsen over time in approximately 20–40% of women, including 4–6 years after completion of treatment [6]. Many physical symptoms associated with breast cancer treatment, such as pain or skin- and breast-related symptoms, typically peak during or shortly after treatment and subsequently partially decline over a period of 1–5 years. Nevertheless, even in this case, symptoms persist long term in a specific subgroup of patients [6, 7].

Most breast cancer survivors report not only long-term physical consequences but also social and, in particular, psychological sequelae (up to 90%), including the aforementioned fatigue, pain, neuropathy, lymphedema, depression, and anxiety [8, 9], as well as a wide range of other difficulties. These symptoms substantially reduce not only quality of life but also patients’ functional status [10]. When treatment-related side effects negatively affect adherence, they may also adversely influence the course of treatment and overall survival [3, 11].

Most of the aforementioned physical difficulties associated with breast cancer treatment are simultaneously reflected in the overall burden of psychological distress. A higher number and greater intensity of physical symptoms during treatment (e.g., pain, fatigue, nausea, neuropathy) predict higher levels of subsequent cancer-specific distress as well as general psychological distress after treatment completion [12]. Long-term physical complications (chronic pain, lymphedema, reduced mobility, fatigue, cognitive impairments) not only impair patients’ functional capacity but also adversely affect their quality of life, and are most commonly associated with depression, anxiety, negative body image, and sexual dysfunction [8, 13]. Among women with greater physical impairment following treatment, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, and fatigue occur more frequently; at the same time, these symptoms may mutually reinforce one another, contributing to a self-perpetuating cycle of psychological and physical burden [14, 15].

Current research indicates that up to 52% of women with breast cancer experience clinically significant psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic symptoms [16]. This burden may persist for 5–10 years after diagnosis, particularly in the form of depressive and anxiety symptoms [17], which are frequently accompanied by fear of cancer recurrence [18]. These conditions are typically associated with sleep disturbances, as various breast cancer treatments increase the risk of impaired sleep quality, thereby contributing to the overall disability associated with cancer treatment [19]. Insufficient sleep duration and poor sleep quality following diagnosis are, moreover, associated with an increased risk of breast cancer progression, especially among premenopausal women [20].

Although sleep disturbances have not been shown to consistently predict overall mortality or breast cancer recurrence, poor sleep quality is strongly associated with impaired quality of life, depressive symptoms, and reduced energy levels, representing a substantial burden for breast cancer survivors [21]. Despite the fact that cancer-related fatigue—one of the most prominent consequences of treatment and a complex, multidimensional symptom—is associated with severe emotional dysfunction and a reduced sense of meaning in life [22], it is substantially underestimated by patients and often remains an unaddressed clinical problem from the physicians’ perspective as well [13]. Breast cancer survivors (BCS) are also more vulnerable to reduced quality of life as a result of the direct effects of cancer or its related treatments, particularly in the physical, emotional, social, and cognitive domains [23]. As diagnostic and treatment approaches have improved substantially over time, quality of life has become a key outcome measure in breast cancer clinical trials as well as in survivorship research [24]. The growing number of women living longer with potential treatment-related side effects further underscores the need for comprehensive assessment of survivorship burden [19] and for the development of effective strategies to manage the adverse consequences of breast cancer treatment.

In addition to pharmacotherapy, one of the most effective non-pharmacological approaches to managing the above-mentioned difficulties is regular physical activity of appropriate intensity. Its primary mechanism of action lies in its positive effects on cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular strength, and overall physical performance [25]. Consequently, physical activity facilitates patients’ ability to maintain engagement in activities of daily living and enhances functional independence [26, 27], significantly reduces fatigue [25], and improves quality of life and emotional functioning, while also reducing anxiety and, in some cases, depression. Although the effects on anxiety and depression are generally small to moderate, they are considered clinically meaningful [26, 27]. Physical activity also has the potential to favorably influence adipose and lean body mass, as well as selected metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers [27]. For these reasons, the present study examined changes in motivation for physical activity, baseline levels of physical fitness, body mass index (BMI), and other related psychological constructs, such as anxiety, depressive symptoms, sleep quality, and overall quality of life, in patients with breast cancer from the time of diagnosis through the completion of treatment.

In recent years, research in the field of breast cancer treatment has increasingly shifted toward a more comprehensive evaluation of the treatment course, integrating both objective and subjective indicators. In addition to traditional clinical parameters, such as survival, treatment toxicity, and performance status, growing importance is being attributed to patient-reported outcomes (PROs), i.e., outcomes reported directly by patients. These measures primarily encompass health-related quality of life [28], physical and emotional functioning, the presence of symptoms and treatment-related adverse effects, as well as pain and fatigue [29]. The integration of subjective and objective health indicators throughout the course of breast cancer treatment is essential, as only their combination provides a comprehensive and clinically relevant understanding of treatment effectiveness and its impact on patients [30].

A growing body of evidence further indicates that PRO values assessed prior to treatment initiation have substantial prognostic significance. Scores in domains such as physical functioning, pain, and other patient-reported measures are capable of predicting not only overall survival but also the likelihood of severe adverse events across different stages of breast cancer, including advanced disease [31, 32]. Large-scale analyses based on individual patient data (IPD) confirm that baseline PROs—particularly physical functioning—represent independent predictors of both survival and treatment-related toxicity. These indicators may therefore serve as valuable complements to traditional tools such as the ECOG performance status, contributing to more informed clinical decision-making [32]and supporting the personalization of treatment strategies [31].

In this context, the assessment of psychophysiological changes—specifically the integration of subjective data on quality of life and functional status with objective indicators of physical functioning—may provide particularly valuable insights. Psychophysiological changes refer to objectively measurable alterations in physiological functions that are elicited or modulated by psychological states, processes, or stress. As these changes arise from the dynamic interaction between psychological and physiological processes, they are typically investigated in response to stress, emotional experiences, or therapeutic interventions [33]. Given that such changes can, in turn, influence patients’ experiences and behavior, their monitoring throughout breast cancer treatment is of considerable importance. It may contribute both to the early identification of patients at risk and to the implementation of targeted supportive interventions aimed at improving quality of life as well as treatment outcomes.

The present study aims to capture the pattern of changes in outcomes derived from subjective psychological scales designed to assess the level of psychological distress, alongside selected objective physical indicators (cardiorespiratory fitness assessed by the 6-minute walk test [6MWT] and body mass index [BMI]). These measures provide insight into whether patients’ psychological and physical status improves, remains stable, or deteriorates over time, while simultaneously enabling timely adjustments to therapeutic or rehabilitation plans [34]. Whereas elevated BMI in patients and breast cancer survivors is associated with poorer treatment response, a higher risk of recurrence, and an overall worse prognosis [35], the 6MWT reflects global health status—particularly cardiorespiratory fitness—which is related not only to quality of life but also to pain, anxiety, depression, body composition, and overall physical fitness [36].

In contrast, subjective measures, encompassing quality of life, pain, fatigue, and psychological status, reflect the impact of treatment on daily functioning, yet without the context of clinical data, they do not provide a comprehensive picture of health status. The systematic integration of these approaches may therefore allow for a more precise evaluation of the benefits and risks of treatment and may also serve as an important basis for decision-making regarding personalized, high-quality, and value-based care [14].

Methods

Research aim and design

The aim of the study is to examine associations between the mental and physical side effects of breast cancer treatment and subsequently assess changes in selected psychophysiological characteristics in patients with breast cancer across the course of treatment by assessing these variables at two time points: first, shortly after disclosure of the diagnosis at the beginning of the treatment process, and second, following the completion of adjuvant cancer-related therapy. We hypothesize that, over the course of treatment, there will be a deterioration in psychophysiological characteristics. Specifically, both subjective and objective indicators of physical activity are expected to decline, along with sleep quality, while fatigue levels are anticipated to increase and quality of life to decrease. In addition, the study will examine the extent to which age and the individual duration of treatment moderate these potential changes.

Procedure

At both the first and second assessment points, participants independently completed a battery of self-report questionnaires, with approximately 60 min allocated for this task. Subsequently, they underwent gait assessment on a walking simulator under the supervision of professionally trained staff. All measurements were conducted under standardized laboratory conditions, using the same equipment across both time points. In addition, body height and body weight were measured using calibrated instruments, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on these measurements.

Study sample

A total of 132 patients diagnosed with breast cancer were initially enrolled in the study. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed diagnosis of malignant tumor with indicated adjuvant chemotherapy based on platinum derivatives, taxanes or vinca alkaloids, (2) ECOG performance status of probands 0, 1 or 2, (3) ability to walk 400 m without sitting or assistance from another person, (4) estimated survival time of at least nine months, (5) willingness to be included in the study and to be in contact with the investigators for at least 9 months, (6) the patient understands the informed consent and signs it. Exclusion criteria were (1) inability to perform physical activity, (2) terminal stage of cancer or other disease, (3) untreated or uncontrolled lung, joint or cardiovascular disease, (4) previous cardiovascular event (e.g. stroke, myocardial infarction) in the last six months, (5) acute or chronic disease of the immune system or other disease that directly affects this system (e.g. lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, immunosuppressive treatment), (6) pregnancy or breastfeeding, (7) current treatment with beta-blockers, (8) VO2max as an athlete, (9) untreated hypertension, (10) patient who refuses to sign informed consent, 11) thoracic surgery 12 months before inclusion in the project, 12) patient who is unable to perform physical activity due to the presence of one or more contraindications to exercise, 13) inability to exercise due to several obstacles (arthritis, psychiatric disease, dementia, etc.).

Participants were recruited from a hospital that serves as a regional center for the treatment of various types of cancer for residents of four regions within the Czech Republic. Consequently, the resulting sample is not restricted by any specific demographic characteristic or breast cancer subtype. Data collection took place between 2022 and 2023. Only patients who provided valid data for at least two thirds of the 21 assessed psychophysiological characteristics at a given assessment point were included in the analyses. The final sample size was 112 in the first measurement and 75 in the second measurement.

Instruments

The 6-minute walk test

Is a widely used standardized field test for the indirect assessment of cardiorespiratory fitness and is applied across various oncological populations [37]. It enables the evaluation of functional exercise capacity, the assessment of responses to medical interventions, and the prediction of cardiorespiratory fitness [38]. Therefore, it can be considered a valid indicator of important components of overall health in women with breast cancer [39]. The test was conducted in accordance with a standardized protocol [40]. Patients were instructed to walk as fast as possible along a 30 m corridor for a maximum duration of six minutes. The total distance in meters walked (6MWD) was recorded as the primary outcome measure.

Body mass index

(BMI) was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²).

Self-report scales

A total of five methods were employed, as presented in Table 1 together with all relevant characteristics. For all questionnaire measures, scale scores were calculated as the mean of the corresponding items, provided that at least two thirds of valid responses were available for the given scale. In the case of the QLQ-C30, scale scores were transformed to a percentage scale in accordance with the scoring manual, such that higher scores indicate better functioning/health.

Statistical procedures

Prior to all analyses, potential multivariate outliers were identified using Mahalanobis distances, following the recommendations of Tabachnick and Fidell [47]. Reliability in terms of internal consistency of the scales was estimated using Cronbach’s α based on data from the first measurement. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine the associations among the variables of interest. Differences in the assessed characteristics before the initiation and after the completion of treatment were analyzed using a general linear model for repeated measures, with age and treatment duration subsequently included as covariates. Treatment duration was dichotomized such that a value of 0 corresponded to the typical treatment length of three months (87.5% of participants in this category), and a value of 1 corresponded to a typical duration of six months (92.5% of participants). The remaining participants were assigned to the nearest category (see the Sample section for further details). Given the large number of statistical tests performed, the Holm–Bonferroni method [48] was applied in the analysis of differences in the examined characteristics in order to control the family-wise error rate. Effect sizes were interpreted using the benchmarks proposed by Cohen [49], defining small (η² = 0.01), medium (η² = 0.06), and large (η² = 0.14) effects.

The needed sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1. Assuming a medium effect size, a correlation of 0.5 between repeated measurements, and a statistical power of 0.80, a minimum of 63 participants was required to detect changes using repeated measures GLM in the examined characteristic. This calculation was based on a significance level of 0.003, as determined by the Holm–Bonferroni correction.

Results

At the first assessment, the study sample consisted of 112 women aged 30 to 76 years (mean age = 51.64, SD = 11.28). In case of 32 women the treatment duration ranged from 2.5 to 3.5 months (typical duration of 3 months in 28 cases) and from 4.5 to 6 months in case of 80 women (typical duration of 6 months for n = 74). Among these patients, breast cancer was diagnosed at stage I in 41 patients, stage II in 49 patients, and stage III in 22 patients. In terms of performance status according to the ECOG index, 95 patients were classified as category 0 and 17 patients as category 1.

All patients underwent surgical treatment in combination with chemotherapy (Paclitaxel in 97 patients, Docetaxel in 10 patients, AC plus Paclitaxel in 4 patients, and Epirubicin in 1 patient), with additional treatments administered as appropriate (radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and biological therapy).

The second assessment was conducted after completion of treatment and included 75 patients who met the same inclusion criterion regarding the minimum number of valid scores. The mean age of participants at the second assessment was 52.04 years (SD = 11.63).

To assess potential systematic differences between participants who completed both measurement waves and those who did not complete the study, we compared these groups with respect to age and baseline levels of the assessed characteristics. A statistically significant difference emerged only for the anxiety dimension (t = 2.21, df = 109, p < 0.05), with participants who completed both measurements exhibiting modestly lower anxiety levels (d = 0.45). Prior to conducting the analyses, tests were performed to identify multivariate outliers using Mahalanobis distances. As no cases exhibited extreme distances from the study sample (p < 0.001), all 112 response vectors were retained for the main analyses.

In the first step, relationships among the examined variables at the first measurement were explored (see Table 2). For the sake of clarity, globally oriented scales were selected from the individual instruments (MFI–General Fatigue, QLQ–Global Health Status/Quality of Life), and for the MPAM-R instrument, a total score reflecting the overall level of motivation was computed.

Several expected patterns emerged from the bivariate analyses of the examined variables. General fatigue showed moderate associations with sleep quality and overall quality of life. Anxiety and depression, reflecting their strong mutual interrelation, displayed a comparable pattern of associations with the remaining variables, being negatively related to global quality of life and positively related to general fatigue and sleep disturbances.

The demographic variable age was, as anticipated, negatively associated with BMI and motivation for physical activity, as well as with the objective indicator of physical fitness (6MWD). With respect to the walking test, it is noteworthy that performance was positively associated with subjectively reported quality of life and, in particular, showed a negative association with anxiety.

As shown in Table 3, treatment-related effects were most evident for variables reflecting subjectively perceived physical characteristics. A significant change observed in physical fatigue, which was also mirrored in an increase in overall fatigue. A comparable decline was found in the physical domain of quality of life. In addition, a small but statistically significant increase in body mass index was detected (the relatively high value of the partial eta squared index can be attributed not to the absolute magnitude of the change, but rather to the high consistency of the changes, as reflected by the strong correlation between the two measurements). Notably, a relatively pronounced decrease in depressive symptoms was observed, with its statistical significance approaching the corrected threshold.

In addition to examining pre–post differences in the levels of the assessed variables, we explored whether these changes were moderated by age or treatment duration. Treatment duration did not emerge as significant moderation in case of any measured characteristic, while age moderated the change in sleep quality (F(1, 70) = 4.03, p = 0.049, partial η² = 0.054) and physical quality of life (F(1, 71) = 5.79, p = 0.019, partial η² = 0.075). Specifically, the change in sleep quality between the second and first assessments was negatively correlated with age (r = − 0.235, p < 0.05), indicating a greater deterioration in sleep quality among younger participants compared with older individuals. Specifically, the difference in PSQI scores between the second and first measurements was 0.20 in the youngest tertile of the sample (30–45 years), 0.10 in middle-aged patients, and − 0.09 in the oldest tertile (58 years and older). In contrast, for the physical domain of quality of life, the adverse treatment effect was more pronounced in older participants (r = 0.246, p < 0.05). The change in QLQ physical scores between the second and first assessments was − 8.80 in the youngest tertile of the cohort (30–45 years), -10.87 among middle-aged participants, and − 18.18 in the oldest tertile (≥ 58 years).

Discussion

The implementation of a longitudinal study with assessment points conducted after diagnosis/before treatment initiation and after the completion of adjuvant treatment remains relatively uncommon in psychosocial oncology research. Consequently, the present findings hold considerable potential for application in both research and clinical practice, including secondary prevention. The applied combination of methods, aimed at mapping selected psychophysiological manifestations associated with the adverse effects of adjuvant treatment, enabled the identification of symptoms that are frequently underestimated in routine medical documentation, in line with the conclusions of Bhattacharya et al. [50]. At the same time, several meaningful trends were identified across the assessed characteristics, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of their trajectories over the course of treatment. Analyses of the first measurement revealed several expected associations among the studied variables, such as moderate relationships between overall fatigue and both sleep quality and quality of life—associations that have been repeatedly documented in women with breast cancer [13, 22]. Similarly, anxiety and depression exhibited anticipated patterns of associations, showing positive relationships with overall fatigue and sleep problems [19] and negative relationships with global quality of life. These associations have been described in the literature as part of both the short-term and long-term consequences of cancer treatment [6, 9]. Moreover, the negative correlation between 6MWT performance and both anxiety and quality of life is noteworthy. While most findings aligned with expected patterns, this result suggests a link between functional capacity and psychological well-being as previously reported by [51].

The study results further empirically confirm that adjuvant treatment has a relatively pronounced negative impact on patients’ physical functioning, thereby documenting a deterioration in physical condition over the course of treatment. Statistically and clinically meaningful changes were observed particularly in physical fatigue, overall fatigue, and the physical domain of quality of life.

A more detailed examination of effect sizes further supports the clinical relevance of the observed changes. The increase in physical fatigue (partial η² = 0.153) and the decline in physical quality of life (partial η² = 0.419) correspond to large effects, indicating substantial deterioration in domains directly related to patients’ physical functioning. The increase in general fatigue (partial η² = 0.122) and body mass index (partial η² = 0.148) falls within the medium-to-large range, suggesting clinically meaningful changes over the course of treatment. In all the mentioned cases, the observed changes can be considered at least clinically important, whether referring to physical and general fatigue [52], BMI [53], or physical quality of life [54]. These findings are consistent with previous research in this area, supporting the notion that cancer-related fatigue is multifactorial in nature [55] and represents a highly prevalent adverse effect of treatment [6]. As such, it may have substantial negative consequences for quality of life and the ability to perform everyday activities [56].

In this context, the identification of a discrepancy between objective and subjective functioning can be considered particularly informative. While patients’ performance in the 6-minute walk test, reflecting objective physical fitness, did not change significantly across the two measurement points, patients’ subjectively perceived physical quality of life deteriorated markedly. This mismatch suggests that the perception of physical limitations in women with breast cancer undergoing treatment is strongly influenced by fatigue, depressive symptoms, and overall treatment burden, rather than by an actual decline in physical capacity. The discrepancy between objective and subjective indicators may be explained by several interrelated factors. Subjective perception of physical fitness is a complex construct that encompasses not only physical capacity but also psychological, symptomatic, and contextual influences. Oncological treatment frequently leads to chronic fatigue that is not directly proportional to actual physical performance [57]. As a result, patients may experience pronounced exhaustion despite preserved physical capacity, which can negatively affect their subjective evaluation. Negative self-perception may also be influenced by anxiety and depressive symptomatology, contributing to cognitive bias. In such cases, patients may rate their physical health more poorly than is reflected by objective measures [58]. Furthermore, treatment burden and its side effects may reduce overall perceived quality of life and physical well-being, even when objective physical performance remains relatively stable [59]. At the same time, the perception of treatment-related symptoms is not merely a reflection of physiological status but is also shaped by attentional focus, expectations, and the interpretation of bodily signals [60]. Oncology patients may be more sensitive to bodily changes and may perceive them as more severe, further contributing to the observed discrepancy. As such, this finding represents a valuable contribution to the discussion on discrepancies between subjective and objective health indicators in oncology. Although the performance in the walking test remained stable over time, its negative association with anxiety and simultaneous positive association with quality of life further support the assumptions of several authors regarding the utility of this test as a clinical screening tool for global health status in cancer patients [37, 39].

The study further provides evidence of psychological adaptation to oncological illness, manifested by a statistically significant (and at the lower range of clinical significance according to [61]) decrease in depressive symptoms at the post-treatment assessment. This finding, although substantial, is somewhat unexpected in light of conclusions reported by other authors, e.g. [14, 16]. It suggests that the post-treatment phase may represent a period of psychological relief for patients, during which adaptation to the diagnosis may lead to a reduction in depressive symptoms despite a concomitant deterioration in physical condition. At the same time, this result can be viewed as a contribution to the necessary differentiation between emotional and somatic trajectories of adaptation.

Several age-specific effects also emerged as particularly informative, especially in the context of personalized care. Interaction analyses indicated that younger women experienced a greater deterioration in sleep quality following treatment completion, whereas older women exhibited overall lower motivation for physical activity and a more pronounced decline in physical quality of life after treatment. With regard to the interaction effect of age on sleep disruption, it is possible to consider the influence of treatment on hormone levels. In premenopausal women, oncological treatment—particularly chemotherapy or endocrine therapy—may induce abrupt ovarian failure (i.e., iatrogenic or treatment-induced menopause), resulting in a rapid decline in estrogen levels [62]. Such a sudden hormonal shift, to which the organism is not adapted, is associated with more pronounced symptoms, including sleep disturbances, hot flashes, and night sweats, all of which directly disrupt the sleep cycle [63]. In contrast, in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, estrogen levels are already physiologically reduced due to aging [64]. Although oncological treatment may further influence hormonal levels (e.g., through anti-estrogen therapies), the relative change is less pronounced than in younger women. As a result, the organism is partially adapted to low estrogen levels, and sleep disturbances may therefore be less severe. Overall, these findings underscore that treatment does not affect patients uniformly but rather interacts with their developmental stage. This conclusion is further supported by the study by Berger et al. [65], which identified younger age, lower levels of physical activity, and higher fatigue as the strongest predictors of poor sleep quality in women with breast cancer.

The study findings thus support the desirability of a personalized approach to supportive oncological care, demonstrating that during treatment it is particularly important to target cancer-related fatigue, subjectively perceived physical fitness, and body weight control. Although the increase in BMI between the two measurement points was modest, it was statistically significant. While an average increase of approximately 1.5 kg/m² was observed, this change corresponds to a moderate weight gain that may not necessarily result in a shift between BMI categories for all patients. However, BMI have been consistently associated in the literature with less favorable outcomes in breast cancer survivors [66], particularly in terms of recurrence risk and metabolic health. Given that BMI in women with breast cancer is strongly associated with prognosis, treatment response, and comorbid conditions, its regular monitoring has clear clinical relevance in treatment planning and is recommended as part of the overall risk profile [67]. This is further underscored by evidence from analyses of adjuvant trials showing that higher BMI at the time of diagnosis represents an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality [68]. However, the clinical impact of this increase should be interpreted with caution, as the study was not powered to assess prognostic endpoints. These conclusions further highlight the need to implement psychoeducational interventions focused on the interpretation of bodily signals and personalized care, including early screening for fatigue, which remains underdiagnosed according to the literature [50]. Moreover, it can be considered important to take into account the individual clinical implications of the identified discrepancy between objective and subjective assessments of patients’ physical performance, which should serve as a basis for a personalized approach when selecting appropriate supportive interventions. Another key objective of systematic clinical care should be age-differentiated interventions: for younger patients, strategies targeting sleep and anxiety should be prioritized, whereas for older patients, greater emphasis should be placed on maintaining functional independence and promoting physical rehabilitation.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the issue of attrition may have affected the generalizability of the findings. Although we examined potential systematic differences between participants who completed both measurement waves and those who dropped out, statistically significant difference was observed in baseline anxiety levels, with completers reporting slightly lower anxiety. This suggests that the final sample may be somewhat biased toward individuals with better initial psychological functioning, which should be considered when interpreting the results.

Second, the reliability of two assessed constructs—sleep quality and cognitive functioning—was relatively low. Reduced internal consistency may have attenuated the observed effects. Consequently, findings related to these variables should be interpreted with caution, and future research should consider employing more robust or alternative measurement instruments.

Another potential limitation is that physical assessment were conducted after the completion of self-report questionnaires, which may have introduced some degree of mental fatigue. However, we assume that this fatigue was not substantial enough to systematically influence the outcomes of the subsequent performance-based test.

Conclusion

The present study provides longitudinal evidence highlighting meaningful discrepancies between subjective and objective indicators of health while identifying age-specific trajectories of change during adjuvant treatment. By offering empirical justification for the integration of age-tailored interventions into oncological care, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of treatment burden. Given that effective prevention and management of treatment-related adverse effects are closely linked to long-term outcomes, such an approach may ultimately contribute to improved prognosis, reduced risk of recurrence, and increased survival.
