Nurse’s perspectives on continuing professional development at Zomba Central Hospital, Malawi: a qualitative inquiry
Authors: Charles Ntalika, Eric Umar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuing Professional Development (CPD) is a fundamental component of nursing practice, facilitating the ongoing acquisition of knowledge and skills beyond initial training. It serves as a mechanism for nurses to remain abreast of current advancements, fostering their professional development and ultimately enhancing the delivery of quality patient care. However, in low-resource settings like Malawi, limited evidence exists regarding nurses’ perspectives on CPD, particularly, alignment of CPD programmes with nurses’ clinical needs and practice realities. The study aimed at exploring nurses’ perspectives on CPD at Zomba Central Hospital (ZCH), Malawi. The specific objectives of the study were to; explore nurses’ perceived CPD needs, examine the congruency of nurses’ needs and their participation in CPD programmes, explore nurses’ experiences of CPD programme delivery, and asses the factors that influence participation in CPD programmes.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative interpretive design. Using purposive sampling, 15 nurses were interviewed. Data were collected through interview guide and analyzed using thematic analysis. Coding and theme development were conducted to enhance consistency and credibility.ResultsFive themes were identified. Nurses actively identify their learning needs through self-assessment and clinical practice often with inadequate institutional coordination. CPD was perceived as important for professional development contributing to improved knowledge, clinical skills, confidence and patient care. There is a perceived misalignment between CPD programmes and nurses’ needs, particularly in practical skill development. Current CPD set up lack post-training mentorship and support thereby constraining the application of learning in practice. Some CPD sessions are relevant to departmental needs, but many lack practical applicability and fail to address the knowledge gaps. Diverse interactive learning resources are highly valued. Most CPD facilitators lack expertise and proficiency in teaching skills. Barriers to CPD participation include scheduling conflicts, workload pressures, and financial obstacles such as limited access to data bundles and transportation costs.ConclusionExploring nurses’ perspectives on CPD at Zomba Central Hospital assisted in determining the relationship between nurses’ actual needs and the CPD programmes in which they engage. CPD is valued by nurses but is not often aligned with their clinical needs. Strengthening needs-based and practice-oriented CPD, institutional coordination and mentorship, and addressing structural barriers may enhance participation and translate learning into practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04955-8.Keywords: Continuous professional development, Nurses’ needs, Nursing practice, Institutional requirements, Career development, Patient care

Introduction

Continuing Professional Development (CPD) is widely recognized as a means by which members of a profession maintain, improve and broaden their knowledge, expertise and competence, and develop personal and professional qualities required in their professional lives [1]. Mlambo et al. described CPD in nursing as a lifelong learning process that enables nurses to respond to ongoing changes in healthcare, maintain competence, and enhance professional practice [2]. Its primary aim is to ensure safe, high-quality, evidence-based care while fostering professional growth [3]. Evidence further suggests that effective CPD is linked to improved patient outcomes, increased confidence and enhanced workforce retention [4].

The strategic importance of CPD is also evident in its contribution to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 on ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being, and its role in advancing Universal Health Coverage (UHC) through workforce strengthening [5]. Nurses as the largest and widely distributed group within the health workforce, play a central role in the healthcare delivery, and provision of essential services [6, 7]. In this context, CPD supports the health system to respond to the changing patient needs by ensuring that nurses have up-to-date knowledge and skills, thereby reducing health disparities [8].

Globally, there has been a shift from traditional, didactic CPD models towards competency-based, learner-centered, and blended approaches that combine face-to-face learning with e-learning and micro-learning [9]. Literature highlights that such models reflect increasing emphasis on self-directed, flexible and technology enabled learning pathways [2]. For example, findings from a multi-country study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa found that online CPD was valued for its flexibility and accessibility, particularly in overcoming geographical barriers [10]. However, Jackson and Manley cautioned that CPD programmes still risk being provider-led rather than co-designed with practitioners. In context, limiting their relevance to everyday clinical practice and influence nurses’ engagement and perceived value of such programmes [11].

In sub-Saharan Africa, CPD systems are implemented differently across countries, with variations in regulatory frameworks, delivery approaches, and enforcement mechanisms. While some countries have formalized CPD requirements linked to professional licensure renewal, others rely on institutional-based training programmes [2, 12, 13]. However, despite such frameworks being in place, CPD implementation is often constrained by limited resources and donor-driven priorities, with limited emphasis on systematic, needs-based planning [14]. This reliance on externally driven training priorities may reduce the alignment of CPD programmes with contextual clinical needs.

Across the region, barriers are consistently reported. These barriers include, inadequate funding, weak infrastructure, high workloads, and poor enforcement of policies [15]. In Kenya, Masai observed that nurses mostly took part in CPD to go up in their careers and renew their licenses, but they did not have sufficient time, employment support, or money resources to participate in CPD activities [16]. Similarly, in Rwanda, access to CPD was impeded by the distance from training facilities, insufficient financing, and a lack of trainers [15]. These challenges are consistent with those reported in other low and middle income countries (LMICs), where nurses face logistical barriers such as travel costs and relief staff [17].

CPD participation is influenced by an interplay of individual, situational, and institutional factors. This interplay is well captured in Cross’s Chain of Response (CoR) model, which conceptualizes participation as a dynamic process shaped by learners’ motivation, life circumstances and external support systems [18]. This model provides a useful framework for understanding how these factors shape nurses’ engagement with CPD within clinical settings.

In Malawi, the regulation of CPD for nurses and midwives is enshrined in the Nurses and Midwives Act, which mandates the accumulation of CPD points annually for license renewal [19]. CPD activities typically include, short courses, workshops, in-service training, self-directed learning and participation in professional networks, as demonstrated in a multi-country evaluation of CPD systems [20]. Bvumbwe & Mtshali note that such initiatives aim to transform nursing education and strengthen the health system [6]. Evidence from Malawi demonstrates that well-designed and context-specific CPD interventions can lead to meaningful improvement in clinical practice and competence among healthcare workers. For example, programmes, such as the Practical Approach to Lung and HIV/AIDS in Malawi (PALM-PLUS), and critical care training initiatives have demonstrated improvements in clinical decision-making and quality care through structured on-site educational approach [21, 22]. However, despite these positive outcomes, such interventions are often programme specific and do not reflect the CPD experiences of nurses.

Despite the existence of a national CPD framework in Malawi, participation appears to be largely compliance-driven. Nurses participate in CPD primarily to accumulate points for license renewal rather than to acquire knowledge and skills to improve clinical practice and patient out-comes [14]. This raises concerns about whether CPD programmes are adequately aligned with nurses’ perceived learning needs to enhance competence and patient care. Studies conducted across sub-Saharan African have reported similar patterns of misalignment [14, 15], suggesting broader systemic challenges in CPD design and implementation, including inadequate mechanism for needs assessment and weak integration with clinical practice requirements. Despite these regional insights, there is limited empirical evidence on how nurses’ experience CPD, particularly regarding its relevance to clinical practice and factors influencing participation. Therefore, this study aimed to explore nurses’ perspectives on CPD focusing on their perceived learning needs and experiences with CPD programmes.

Methods

Aim of the study

The study aimed at exploring nurses’ perspectives on CPD at Zomba Central Hospital, Malawi, including alignment of CPD programmes and nurses’ needs, their adequacy and factors influencing participation.

Study design and setting

This study adopted a qualitative interpretive design. This approach is used to explore and understand how individuals make sense of their experiences within their social and professional contexts. It enables the exploration of meaning that individuals or groups ascribe to a social or human problem [23]. This design was used to explore nurses’ perspectives on CPD, particularly how they interpret its relevance to clinical practice, their experiences of programme delivery, and the factors influencing their participation.

The study was conducted at the Zomba Central Hospital (ZCH), a tertiary referral hospital situated in Zomba District, in the eastern region of Malawi. The hospital provides specialized healthcare services across multiple departments including, medical, surgical, peadiatric, obstetrics, and critical care units. The hospital employs nurses and midwives across different cadres, including nurse-midwife technicians, registered nurse/midwives and nursing and midwifery officers, all of whom are required to participate in CPD as part of professional regulatory requirements. CPD is coordinated at both hospital and departmental levels, with sessions organized by departmental focal persons or designated CPD coordinators. CPD participation contributes to accumulation of CPD points required for annual license renewal as mandated by the Nurses and Midwives Council of Malawi (NMCM).

ZCH was selected as the study site due to its role as a tertiary referral facility providing specialized care across diverse clinical areas. The hospital manages a wide range of conditions requiring nurses to maintain broad and up-to-date clinical competencies, making it an appropriate setting for exploring nurses’ perspectives on CPD.

Study population

The study population consisted of nurses who had been permanently working at ZCH for a period of not less than one (1) year. This criterion was applied to ensure that participants had adequate exposure to the hospital’s clinical environment and existing CPD activities. This ensured that participants could provide informed and contextual perspectives on CPD access, relevance and implementation.

Sample size

Fifteen eligible nurses and midwives were enrolled in this study. This was aimed at collecting a substantial amount of rich and diverse data to address the research objectives as more time was devoted to each participant. Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants for the study as it allows selection of information-rich subjects to enhance the credibility of the study [15]. Participants were drawn from various departments including medical, surgical, obstetric, and peadiatric to capture diverse perspectives across clinical areas. The sample included nurse-midwife technician, registered nurses-midwives, and nursing and midwifery officers, ensuring representation across different cadres with relevant experience and exposure to CPD.

Data collection and analysis

The data were collected using an interview guide containing open-ended questions that allowed control over the line of questioning and invited participants to respond in their own words about the various elements of their CPD program experiences and nursing learning needs. This approach enabled participants to express their perspectives in their own words and facilitated in-depth exploration of their experiences related to CPD [23, 24]. The interview guide was developed specifically for this study based on the study objectives and relevant literature on CPD.

This interview guide was piloted with two nurses from a similar setting to assess clarity and relevance, and minor revisions were made accordingly. The guide is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data collection was conducted by the lead author from December 2023 to January 2024 following ethical approval from the College of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (COMREC) and the Zomba Central Hospital Research Committee.

The interviews were conducted face-to-face in English, as all participants were proficient in the language. Interviews were held in private rooms within each department to ensure confidentiality and minimize interruptions. Each interview lasted between 45 and 72 min. A total of 20 participants were approached to participate in the study, of whom 15 consented and completed the interviews, while 5 declined.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participants were assigned unique identity number for confidentiality. With permission, all interviews were recorded to ensure accurate capture of responses. In addition, field notes were recorded during and immediately after each interview to document non-verbal cues, contextual factors and preliminary analytical insights, thereby enhancing the depth and contextual richness of the data. Transferring of audio recordings of each interview from the audio recorder to a password secured laptop was done after each day of the interview to ensure data privacy.

Data saturation

Data collection proceeded concurrently with preliminary analysis and continued until meaning saturation was achieved. Saturation is defined as the point at which no new insights, themes or dimensions of existing themes emerged from the successive interviews [25, 26]. Saturation was assessed through on-going comparison between in-coming data with previously coded materials. Interviewing was stopped when no new information was being generated from the last three interviews and when it was deemed saturation had been achieved.

Data analysis

Audio recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim within 24 h of data collection to preserve contextual detail and support early familiarization with the data. Timely transcription in qualitative research facilitates immersion and supports iterative, reflexive analysis [27, 28]. Each transcript was cross-checked against the original audio recording to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Data were analyzed using thematic content analysis as described by Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke [27]. This approach emphasizes the researcher’s active role in identifying patterns of meaning across the dataset. Analysis followed six recurring phases: familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing of themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report.

The process started with familiarization of the data. The researcher began by reading and re-reading the transcripts to achieve immersion and develop understanding of the data [27]. After familiarization, the first round of coding was done. Open coding was used to identify meaningful information. Preliminary open coding was used to identify and categorize themes and patterns in the data during initial coding [24]. This stage made it possible to create a collection of codes that address every important aspect of the data. Identified codes were written on a plain paper where emerging ideas were noted separately.

After generating the initial codes, the researcher proceeded with searching for themes. In this process, the codes were then organized into potential themes, seeking connections, and patterns. To identify patterns and connections the codes were compared across the transcripts.

A review of the themes was then carried out. This method was used to ensure that the themes were clear, logical, and meaningfully connected to the facts. As a result of this process, a number of themes were combined and redefined to ensure coherence and clarity. After going over the subjects, each was clearly defined and given a name. Evidence from the transcripts supported each theme.

The final themes were then organized to provide a coherent and meaningful representation of nurses’ perspectives on CPD. This provided a structured framework for presenting study findings. The researcher prioritized accuracy in transcription and systematic coding to promote trustworthiness.

The researcher did not involve an independent co-coder as this is consistent with the interpretive nature of thematic analysis, which emphasizes the researcher’s active and reflexive role in meaning making rather than seeking inter-coder agreement [27]. Analytical rigor was then enhanced through repeated review of transcripts and coding decisions to ensure consistency and depth of interpretation.

Reflexivity

The lead researcher is a nurse working in the Malawi health system, which provided familiarity with the study context and facilitated rapport with participants. However, this positionality also had the potential to introduce bias. To minimize this, the researcher maintained reflexive awareness throughout the research process by reflecting on personal assumptions and their potential influence on data collection and interpretation [28].

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness in qualitative research ensures quality by assessing credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability [29]. This study focused on capturing nurses’ needs regarding their engagement in CPD.

Credibility, which reflects data truthfulness, was ensured through purposive selection of participants meeting inclusion criteria and the use of structured interview guide. During the interview, the researcher employed rephrasing and summarizing to confirm understanding and accurate representation of participants’ experience [28].

Dependability which refers to consistency of the research process, was ensured through detailed documentation of study procedures and the use of uniform interview guide. The research process and data were reviewed by the study supervisor, who acted as an auditor to verify accuracy. All raw data were securely stored to allow for external review, and data analysis followed established qualitative procedures to ensure consistency [24, 29, 30].

Confirmability was ensured through verbatim transcription of audio recorded interviews and careful verification of transcripts for accuracy. Findings were grounded in participants’ accounts through the use of direct quotations, ensuring that interpretations reflected participants’ perspectives rather than researcher’s bias [31].

Transferability, which indicates the applicability of the findings to other contexts, was enhanced through detailed documentation and purposive sampling and detailed description of the study setting and study of participants. This enables readers to apply the findings in another similar setting [30].

Results

Fifteen participants were interviewed, comprising of ten females and five males, from different clinical departments and nursing cadres. The socio-demographic characteristics of participants are presented in Table 1. The analysis of the participants’ responses generated five interrelated themes. The themes illustrate how nurses experience CPD as both a valued mechanism for professional growth and practice shaped by contextual and structural constraints. These were; (1) CPD as a reactive mechanism for professional growth, (2) systemic misalignment between CPD provision and contextual clinical realities, (3) negotiating learning needs within institutional constraints, (4) translating CPD into sustained clinical practice, and (5) structural constraints and enablers shaping CPD engagement.

Together, these themes reflect an interplay between individual motivation, perceived relevance, environmental conditions in shaping CPD engagement and outcomes. Participants’ quotations were used to demonstrate their experience with CPD and to support each theme.

CPD as a reactive mechanism for professional growth

This theme revealed areas where nurses had strong demand for CPD. The assessment of CPD needs was important to understanding nurses’ professional development objectives and expectations. Three sub-themes were identified: perceived importance of CPD, skills enhancement for patient care and, specialized training needs.

Participants consistently emphasized CPD as essential for maintaining competence within a rapidly evolving clinical environment. CPD was practiced not only as a professional requirement but as a critical mechanism for bridging the gap between prior training and the realities of everyday practice. These accounts highlights how nurses’ self-assessment of competence and perceived value of learning, shaped their engagement with CPD.

Participants described CPD as enabling them to remain current in a changing healthcare environment. They stated that CPD aided their professional development by helping them stay current on new practices and knowledge that are critical for patient care.

To me it has been good because I have learnt a lot since we are leaving in a global world where there are so many changes, so CPD keeps us updated on what is knew on the ground (Participant 3).

Beyond knowledge acquisition, CPD was closely associated with increased confidence and autonomy in practice. Several participants praised CPD programmes for helping them gain confidence and concentration at work. They stated that CPD allowed them to create goals and develop expertise, thereby improving patient care.

I can say it has improved my confidence in taking care of my patients. Because what I learnt, I do it confidently. (Participant 4).

This growing confidence appeared to shift practice from reliance on others towards greater independence. This is evidenced by the following narratives:

Due to this CPD, I have changed my attitude…. Knowledge has also changed because sometimes when a patient comes you would wait for a clinician to come and review the patient just because you do not know about the condition. Even the technical know-how has changed. (Participant 6).

However, along these perceived benefits participants highlighted persistent gaps in practical and context specific skills, particularly in emergency and hands-on care. Nursing was described as a highly practical profession that requires continuous skills development. The participants acknowledged the critical need for practical skill development to improve patient care standards. Participants pointed gaps in skill development;

The skills part because most of the things we do are hands on……. Skills like NGT insertion, oxygen cylinder, most nurses don’t know how to put patient on oxygen cylinder as they rely on oxygen concentrator. And some small skills like colostomy care. (Participant 2).

Emergency care was also emphasized as priority area:

Enough knowledge and skills on patient care, […] Emergency care. Most of the time patients come while already resuscitated and I miss a lot because I see patients that are already seen by others. (Participant 10).

Transition across clinical settings further exposed limitations in preparedness:

There are conditions such as severe malnutrition, marasmus and kwashiorkor, chronic conditions such as rheumatic heart disease, diabetes in children that I meet at under-five which I am not trained on because previously I was working in a male surgical department and was moved to the under-five without proper training. (Participant 5).

Taken together, these findings position CPD as both a driver of professional growth and the response to ongoing gaps between expected and actual competence.

Systemic misalignment between CPD provision and contextual clinical realities

While CPD was widely valued, participants described a recurring disconnect between CPD provision and the realities of clinical practice. This misalignment shaped perceptions of programme relevance and effectiveness. Some CPD activities were experienced as highly relevant when aligned with immediate clinical priorities.

The CPD sessions on infection prevention during the COVID-19 era were very useful and directly aligned with our needs (Participant 1).

However, across narratives, participants consistently reported instances where CPD content did not reflect their day-to-day clinical demands. One participant expressed frustration, saying:

Sometimes, the topics covered do not align with what we need, and it’s hard to find presenters with the right expertise (Participant 2).

Participants further emphasized that the effectiveness of some CPD programmes depended on delivery approaches, particularly in the inclusion of practical and interactive components.

Another emphasized the importance of practical demonstrations, stating,

Online sessions are good, but for topics that require hands-on practice, practical demonstrations are crucial (Participant 2).

Conversely, limitations in facilitator expertise were perceived to reduce the quality of learning.

Finding presenters with the right expertise for some topics is challenging, and it affects the quality of the training (Participant 2).

A recurring pattern across accounts was the absence of on-going support following training

After completing CPD programmes, ongoing support and mentorship are essential to reinforce new skills, but these are often lacking (Participant 4).

And another participant added,

We need more consistent post-CPD support to ensure the application of new skills in our daily practice (Participant 7).

These finding indicate that CPD effectiveness is shaped by not only availability, but by alignment with clinical realities, quality of facilitation and continuity of learning support. Within this context of variable alignment of CPD provisions and practice needs, participants described actively negotiating their own learning priorities.

Negotiating learning needs within structural constraints

Participants described their CPD needs as actively constructed through interaction of personal experiences, clinical exposure and institutional expectations. Rather than passively receiving training, nurses engaged in an on-going process of identifying and prioritizing learning needs.

At individual level, learning needs emerged through self-assessment and direct engagement with clinical challenges.

My target was to gain some skills on most of conditions in postnatal. I identified the needs according to my performance. I was not performing well, for example, when a patient with Post-Partum Hemorrhage (PPH) comes, it was a problem for me to manage. Patients with conditions that need Cardio-Pulmonary Resuscitation (CPR), I wasn’t conversant, so I was sure I need more knowledge to beef up the little knowledge I had (Participant 3).

Exposure to unfamiliar or complex cases frequently prompted knowledge of gaps

Management of various conditions at my department because each and every day we meet conditions of which some we only learnt in books. (Participant 12).

Participants also articulated broader development goals including, interpersonal and professional competencies.

Actually what I would want much is to improve on customer care as I handle clients and also I need to improve on conduct, improve on skills especially cannula insertion (Participant 15).

At institutional level, CPD priorities were often shaped by organizational goals such as infection prevention and quality improvement. Beyond personal and departmental priorities, nurses highlighted institutional requirements as another driver for CPD participation. Programmes targeted institutional wide goals such as, infection prevention, quality improvement and emergency care:

The CPD programmes that I participated in include infection prevention update, stepwise quality improvement, and resuscitation training (Participant 9).

However, participants highlighted inconsistencies in institutional coordination, which shifted responsibility for learning on to individuals.

The CPD programme at ZCH is not doing ok because we had one head of department coordinating the programme but she went to school and relocated to UK. So it is a personal effort that you attend a CPD session or go online (Participant 5).

The negotiation reflects a form of constrained agency, where nurses balance personal learning priorities with institutional expectations within a context of limited structural support. While CPD was perceived valuable and actively pursued, participants described how its benefits were realized or constrained in practice.

Translating CPD into sustained clinical practice

Participants described clear pathways through which CPD translated into improvements in clinical practice. Those pathways involved acquisition of practical skills, increased confidence and enhanced ability to manage patient care. Participants reported gaining directly applicable clinical skills. One participant working in a surgical ward stated the following.

In the surgical ward I learnt chest drain removal, wound dressing, care of bowel obstruction patients (Participant 1).

Another participant credited CPD for gaining skills in operating medical equipment,

Since I graduated from school I didn’t know how to operate oxygen cylinder but through CPD I can operate the gauge without problems (Participant 5).

These skills contributed to increased confidence in clinical care.

I am more confident when discharging duties and feel satisfied. (Participant 8).

Concurring with this sentiment another participant said,

When you see struggling patient get better after managing him you feel motivated and satisfied (Participant 12).

This progression from skill acquisition to confidence highlights how CPD strengthens self-efficacy in practice.

Participants also described improvements in communication and patient interaction.

In agreement, one participant said,

I attended a session on counselling and this helps me to provide proper counselling to patients and guardians (Participant 13).

Highlighting the positive influence of communication, participants reported having learned that excellent communication was part of the treatment, as stated;

My improvement in communication has helped me learn that good way of communication heals. (Participant 5).

In addition, CPD enhanced management of complex or previously challenging conditions. For instance;

At under-five we do receive severely sick children with malnutrition, diabetes type 1, sickle cell and others. At first it was a challenge managing them but now it’s easy to manage them. (Participant 4).

Another participant presented a specific example of enhanced management, while demonstrating the real benefits of CPD in increasing patient care.

Managing urology patient; am able to solve a problem of blocked catheter as I learnt how to flash it (Participant 14).

These accounts illustrate how CPD contributes to practice through integrated pathways linking knowledge, skills and confidence to improved patient care.

Despite these benefits, participants’ ability to engage with and apply CPD was shaped by broader structural conditions.

Structural constraints and enablers shaping CPD engagement

Participants identified a range of structural factors that influence their ability to access and engage with CPD. These factors operate as both constraints and enablers within the learning environment.

Workload and competing clinical demands were consistently reported as barriers.

Accordingly, the following narratives were recorded;

When on duty I could not leave the ward to attend a session. (Participant 1)

Sometimes due to inadequate staff you have a busy schedule on the ward such that you fail to attend CPD sessions. (Participant 9).

Scheduling practices further limits participation, particularly for nurses working on shifts

One of the challenges is time as sometimes we do online. People do suggest time and mostly it is 7:30 pm of which everyone is home. This time most people are busy while the session has started. Others join late after other session has gone a long way. On face to face it is done after the morning hand over and if someone is on a night shift going to participate won’t be effective. It would be better if there is a specific day spared for CPD. (Participant 2).

Financial constraints also restricted access, especially for online and off-duty participation. The following narratives highlight the findings:

Participants mentioned,

Distance from area of residence… Transport cost.” (Participant 14)

Sometimes due to lack of data bundle I am unable to attend online CPD during my free time at home” (Participant 15).

This emphasizes the need for institutional support to provide the financial resources required for CPD initiatives. These results imply that CPD participation is hampered by scheduling, time, workload, and financial constraints, underlining the importance of flexible and accessible CPD options that suit the diverse demands of healthcare professionals’ work and personal circumstances.

At the same time, several enabling factors supported engagement. Professional requirements and regulatory expectations acted as motivators. For example;

The sticker that says CPD requirement fulfilled from the nurses’ council. And the knowledge gained also acts as a motivation to participate (Participant 5).

Peer influence and observed improvement in practice also encouraged participation.

I saw other nurses managing patients well and upon asking how they manage they told me it’s through the CPD sessions they attend. (Participant 14).

Communication platforms facilitated awareness of CPD opportunities. Participants stressed the significance of effective communication and access to information in facilitating their participation in CPD programmes. This is evidenced by the following narratives;

Share it on nursing WhatsApp forum a week before. (Participant 1)

Use WhatsApp and sometimes use memos in the wards…. When there is CPD they communicate on WhatsApp that tomorrow there is CPD on this topic. And because of that it has helped me to achieve my objectives. (Participant 3).

However, organizational culture and staffing levels played a critical moderating role. Participants stressed the need for a culture that embraces continuous learning and innovation:

People get used to do the same things because of over staying in one department. There is resistance to change. This affects my motivation because I feel there will be no change because of other people’s attitude (Participant 10).

Participants suggested that fixing personnel shortages could increase participation rates, saying,

Being appointed as the vice CPD coordinator for my department has motivated me to actively participate in CPD activities. However, addressing staffing shortages to alleviate workload pressures could further enhance participation rates. (Participant 13).

These findings indicate that a variety of factors influence CPD participation, including motivation and rewards, communication and information access, and the workplace culture and environment. Healthcare organizations can encourage CPD participation by addressing these concerns and providing a supportive environment that fosters continual learning and professional progress.

Discussion of results

The study reveals a fundamental tension between compliance-driven CPD systems and the need for meaningful, practice-oriented learning within a resource constrained health system. Rather than functioning as structured pathway for continuous professional development, CPD emerges as a relational and system-dependent process shaped by the interaction of individual motivation, workplace realities, and institutional conditions. Interpreted through Cross’s Chain of Response (CoR) Model, participation is not a linear outcome of programme availability but a dynamic process in which each stage of self-evaluation, attitudinal disposition, goal alignment, access to opportunities, and institutional support, is actively mediated by structural constraints. These findings extend the CoR Model by demonstrating that, in low-resource settings such as Malawi, structural limitations are not distal influences but central forces that reshape how learning is initiated, accessed, and sustained [32].

CPD as a reactive mechanism for professional growth

CPD in this context functions less as a proactive developmental pathway and more as a reactive response to clinical uncertainties. As much as CPD is appreciated for improving confidence, competence and the application of theoretical knowledge into practice, the main reason for people get involved is to fill immediate clinical gaps. This shows that motivation is mostly based on deficits, not development, coming from dealing with challenging cases, emergencies, and a sense of one’s inadequacy in clinical skills. This finding is consistent with evidence from low-resource contexts, where CPD has demonstrated efficacy in enhancing professional competence and confidence [31]. Similarly, studies demonstrate that CPD improves clinical performance and supports lifelong learning among health professionals in resource constrained environments [33].

However, this study enhances existing literature by illustrating that motivation is not rooted in long-term professional development but in the necessity to address immediate practice demands. This disputes prevailing concepts within CPD frameworks that define learning as an active and career focused endeavor.

In the CoR Model, this indicates that self-evaluation is not merely an internal cognitive process but is also stimulated by external systemic pressures. In this context, motivation becomes episodic and reactive, which makes it harder to keep up with professional development and encourages short-term skill acquisition over long-term competence building [34]. This reinterprets CPD engagement as a means of professional survival in limited-resource clinical settings rather than a formal development pathway.

Systemic misalignment between CPD provision and practice realities

A significant conclusion of this study is the ongoing misalignment between CPD programmes and the learning requirements of nurses, especially concerning practical and department specific competencies. Similar concerns have been documented in South Africa and other sub-Saharan African contexts [35, 36]. However, this study transcends the characterization of misalignment as a design issue, framing it instead as a systemic disjunction between knowledge production and frontline clinical demands. This misalignment shows that the needs assessment processes are not working well because structured or participatory mechanisms do not properly inform the CPD content. Evidence from sub-Saharan Africa shows that CPD programmes that do not perform systematic needs assessments often do not fit well with the clinical needs of practitioners, which makes them less effective [37]. The current findings indicate that the issue transcends mere technical deficiencies in programme design, encompassing institutional and power dynamics, whereby frontline nurses possess limited influence over CPD content and priorities.

In CoR framework, this shows a disconnect between setting goals and finding learning opportunities that are useful. Nurses can figure out what they need to learn based on their clinical experience, but these needs are not always met by the right CPD activities. As a result, participation becomes more about following the rules than actually learning, which hurts both motivation and results.

Negotiating learning needs within structural constraints

Participation in CPD is a negotiated process that is shaped by the interplay of individual agency and structural constraints. Nurses proactively assess their learning needs and pursue opportunities to fulfill them, illustrating professional dedication and adaptability. This is in line with what has been found in South Africa, where nurses have been shown to take charge of their own learning by deciding what to focus on based on their clinical experience and career goals [38]. Nonetheless, this agency is exercised in environments marked by workload pressures, staffing deficiencies, and limited institutional support [32].

Consequently, access to CPD is inconsistent and frequently reliant on individual initiative rather than organized distribution. This contradicts the CoR Model’s premise of stable opportunity structures, illustrating that in low-resource contexts, opportunities are disjointed and variable. This results indicate that nurses are not merely passive recipients of CPD but rather active participants operating within constrained systems. Their participation is self-directed and informal learning, such as online CPD, demonstrates adaptive strategies to address institutional deficiencies. But this personalized way of learning may make differences in competence between departments worse and make it harder to create a unified professional learning environment.

Translating CPD into sustained clinical practice

The research shows that CPD helps people improve their clinical skills, communication, confidence, and ability to handle complicated cases, especially when learning is based on practice. These results align with findings from Ethiopia and Kenya, where CPD has demonstrated improvements in clinical competence and patient care outcomes [2, 33], alongside research from South Africa highlighting the significance of experiential learning methodologies [39].

However, the translation of CPD into ongoing clinical practice is limited by the lack of organized mentorship and support after training. Without reinforcement mechanisms, the advantages of CPD are short-lived, constraining their influence on enduring practice. This is in line with what other health systems research has found: that CPD effects are often not long-lasting without support structures at work [40]. The finding is further corroborated by findings in Uganda, where lack of follow-up support reduced the long-term impact of CPD [41]. This underscores a significant deficiency in the reinforcement stage of the CoR Model, where continuous support is essential to maintain behavioral change. The results show that mentorship, supervision, and peer support are not optional parts of the process, but necessary parts of making learning part pf everyday life. Without these, CPD is still sporadic and does not lead to real, long-lasting improvements in clinical performance [6, 20].

Structural constraints and enablers shaping CPD engagement

Structural factors such as workload, staffing shortages, scheduling conflicts, and financial challenges have a big effect on how many people participate in CPD. These factors serve as substantial impediments to participation, aligning with findings from Malawi and other resource-limited contexts [2].

However, the current study enhances this understanding by illustrating that these constraints do not function independently but interact cumulatively to limit engagement. For instance, the combined effects of workload stress and scheduling conflicts make it almost impossible to participate, no matter how motivated a person is. This changes the way we think about participation from personal choice to a result of structural conditions, where institutional capacity controls who can learn. The study also shows how adaptive mechanisms, like digital communication platforms and professional incentives, can help ease some of these problems. Evidence from Rwanda corroborates the function of digital communication in enhancing CPD accessibility in resource-constrained environments [42].

The results also show how important it is for facilitators to be knowledgeable in order to make CPD work. The quality and credibility of facilitators affect not only how well they teach but also how much people enjoy and value CPD. This is in line with research that stresses the importance of skilled facilitators in improving educational outcomes [3, 11], suggesting that CPD effectiveness depends not only on access but also on the quality of delivery.

Overall, the findings position CPD not as a neutral educational intervention but as a system-embedded process shaped by structural realities, institutional priorities and professional agency. CPD programmes are perceived as most effective when they are practice-oriented, contextually relevant, and supported by experienced facilitators and post-training guidance. However, the persistent misalignment, limited access, and weak reinforcement mechanisms indicate that current CPD systems are insufficiently responsive to frontline clinical demands. Addressing these challenges requires a shift from compliance-oriented models towards context-responsive and learner-centered approaches that incorporate structured needs assessment, workplace-based learning and sustained support mechanisms. Future research across various healthcare environments is essential to investigate the restructuring of CPD systems to more effectively meet nurses’ educational requirements and facilitate ongoing enhancements in clinical practice.

Recommendations

Policy implication

Given the misalignment between CPD provision and clinical needs, the NMCM and Ministry of Health and Sanitation to introduce routine facility CPD needs assessments, annual CPD plans, and audits to ensure contextual CPD delivery. Facilities should have dedicated CPD funding to support local training. Deliberate monitoring system to be put in place to track participation and competency outcomes.

Practice implication

To address the lack of post-training support and mentorship, and practical training sessions, facilities should prioritize hands-on, needs-based training and supported mentorship and supervision to enhance knowledge and skill application.

To address workload and scheduling barriers, facilities should improve staffing levels on CPD days for flexible participation and ensure attendance is need-based.

Facilities to promote the use of low-cost digital platforms such as WhatsApp to improve CPD access and participation.

Education implication

Regarding challenges faced when transitioning between departments, facilities should put in place orientation or bridging programmes that are competency-based and practical.

Promote CPD that use interactive and blended learning approaches to enhance knowledge acquisition and ease access. Ensure that evaluation mechanisms include competency-based assessments.

To address facilitator expertise, training institutions to introduce special training to equip facilitators with skills on how to facilitate the CPD programmes.

Limitations of the study

The study has some limitations which should be taken into account in the interpretation of the results. Firstly, the sample size of the study was limited to one tertiary hospital in the eastern region of Malawi, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other healthcare setting with different structure, resources and staff dynamics.

Secondly, the number of nurses who fulfilled the inclusion criteria and could participate in this study was also restricted. While purposive sampling allowed in-depth rich data to be gathered, the views of nurses in other departments may not have been fully represented. Those who were more engaged in CPD activities may have been more likely to take part in the study, which could have affected the results.

Third, the study was based on participants’ subjective experiences and perceptions which are prone to recall bias and social desirability. They may have made much emphasis on the positive aspects and under reported the negative experiences. This may create a skewed view of the real effects and issues related to CPD programmes. While attempts were made to include nurses from a variety of departments, the views of some clinical areas may not be included.

Fourth, consistent with a qualitative interpretive design, the findings represent contextual and interpretive accounts of participants’ experiences rather than objective or generalizable truths. The meanings generated are shaped by participants’ perspectives and the researcher’s interpretive engagement with the data.

Finally, the lead researcher’s nursing professional background may have influenced data interpretation. However, this was mitigated through reflexive awareness and systematic coding procedures to enhance analytical rigor.

Conclusion

The study highlights a critical tension between compliance-driven CPD participation and the need for meaningful, practice oriented learning among nurses at ZCH. While CPD was valued for enhancing confidence, knowledge and practical skills, its impact was limited by misalignment with nurses’ learning needs, insufficient practical components, and lack of post training support. Participation was further shaped by contextual constraints, including workload, scheduling, and financial barriers, as well as institutional factors such as coordination and facilitator expertise.

These findings underscore the need to reorient CPD towards needs-based and practice-focused approaches, including structured needs assessment, increased use of ward-based and hands-on training, and provision of protected time for participation. Strengthening facilitation capacity, and introducing mentorship and follow-up mechanisms are essential to enhance engagement and translation of learning into practice.
