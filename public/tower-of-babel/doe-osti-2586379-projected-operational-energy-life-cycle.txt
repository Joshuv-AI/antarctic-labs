EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Regionalized life cycle assessment (LCA) results for the production and utilization of electricity,
natural gas, fuel oil, and propane as commodities within residential and commercial buildings
were previously provided using models based on data from year 2020 and prior. This report
provides updated LCA results for residential and commercial utilities using models that are
representative of years 2020 for the natural gas supply chain and 2022 for electricity generation
and consumption in the U.S. In addition, methods for the regionalization of the models were
revised based on updated Balancing Authorities (BA) in 2022 for electricity, and new
downstream delivery regions from the most recent natural gas model from the National Energy
Technology Laboratory (NETL). The geographical results were further assigned to each U.S. ZIP
Code from the recently published 2020 Census.
Forecasted life cycle profiles for all utilities were also updated using the U.S. Energy Information
Administration Annual Energy Outlook 2023 (AEO 2023), based on two scenarios: the Reference
Case and a Side Case (Low Zero-Carbon Technology Cost). For electricity consumption results,
two additional forecasts were investigated based on two scenarios from the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) Cambium 2023 model: Mid Case and Low Renewable and
Battery Cost.
While only 100-year Global Warming Potential (GWP-100) with climate carbon feedback (CC-FB)
and Cumulative Energy Demand are discussed in this report, the complete results, including
Acidification Potential, Eutrophication Potential, Freshwater Ecotoxicity Potential, GWP-100
without inclusion of CC-FB, Human Health Impacts Potentials (Cancer, Non-Cancer), Ozone
Depletion Potential, Particulate Matter Formation Potential, and Photochemical Smog
Formation Potential, are tabulated for each ZIP Code in the Excel worksheets that accompany
this analysis.

1 INTRODUCTION
In 2023, the National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) published complete regionalized
inventories for the production and utilization of petroleum products, natural gas, and electricity
for residential and commercial scenarios in the United States for the National Institute of
Standards and Technology (NIST) Engineering Laboratory (EL) in the report, “Operational Energy
Life Cycle Data Development for the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)
Building Industry Reporting and Design for Sustainability BIRDS) Neutral Environmental
Software Tool (NEST).” [1] The goal of this analysis is to provide an update on the previous NETL
reports based on newly available models from NETL.
Additionally, this analysis utilized new inventories to develop updated projected future life cycle
profiles for each energy commodity (electricity, natural gas, fuel oil and propane). The
projections were based on trends provided by the U.S. Energy Information Administration
through the Annual Energy Outlook 2023 (AEO 2023) with projections to 2050. For all utilities,
two scenarios were investigated based on the AEO 2023 data: the Reference Case, which is the
baseline case, and the Low Zero-Carbon Technology Cost Case (Side Case). Moreover, two
additional scenarios were included for the projections for electricity. These additional scenarios
were derived from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) Cambium 2023 Mid Case
and Low Renewable Cost Case projections through 2050.
The findings of the analysis were submitted to the National Institute of Standards and
Technology (NIST) Engineering Laboratory (EL) to be used for the development of measurement
science and incorporation into decision-support tools for evaluating building and facility capital
investments. All the results of this analysis are intended to be made available to the public.

 8

2 SCOPE AND BOUNDARIES
Updated NETL baseline models were used to generate LCA results for four residential and
commercial building utilities: natural gas, fuel oil, propane (liquified petroleum gas), and
electricity.

2.1 GOAL
The goal of this analysis is to compile the environmental impacts and cumulative energy use of
the production and consumption of natural gas, fuel oil, propane, and electricity in residential
and commercial buildings for each ZIP Code in the continental United States, using the most
recently available databases and inventories from the NETL and Federal LCA Commons. The
results of this analysis are intended for use by the NIST-EL for the development of measurement
science and incorporation into decision-support tools for evaluating building and facility capital
investments.

2.2 FUNCTIONAL UNIT
For the fuel resources, the functional unit is 1 megajoule (MJ) of energy, based on the higher
heating value (HHV), delivered to and combusted in the residential or industrial boiler within
the respective ZIP Code, in the form of natural gas, fuel oil, and propane. For electricity, the
functional unit is 1 megawatt hour (MWh) of electricity (120 Volt, consumption mix)
transmitted to the user in the respective ZIP Code.

2.3 BOUNDARIES
The scope of the LCA for all utilities is cradle-to-grave, which starts at raw material acquisition
and ends at consumption at the residential or commercial site. The NETL Natural Gas LCA model
includes emissions from production to distribution, while the NETL Petroleum Products LCA
model includes all upstream emissions (i.e., Well-to-Tank). Similarly, the U.S. EPA Electricity Life
Cycle Inventory (eLCI) model includes all upstream emissions from facility-level generation of
electricity to transmission and production of consumption mixes. End-of-life (EOL) scenarios are
included as combustion of fuels in residential and commercial heating systems.

2.5 LIFE CYCLE IMPACT ASSESSMENTS
TRACI 2.1 and modified FEDEFL v1.0.0 will be used to generate LCA results from the NETL
inventories. Furthermore, the results will be categorized as either Scope 1, 2 or 3 emissions
based on the emitting process within the commodity production chain and end of life scenario.
The categorization is based on the US EPA’s Greenhouse Gas Inventory and Development
Guidance. [2] For natural gas, fuel oil and petroleum, upstream emissions (i.e., cradle-to-gate or
production) will be categorized as Scope 3, while downstream emissions (i.e., gate-to-grave or
direct emissions from combustion in a boiler) will be categorized as Scope 1. For electricity,
direct emissions from power plants will be categorized as Scope 2, while emissions from fuel
production, fuel transport and construction of facilities will be included under Scope 3.

2.5.1 TRACI 2.1
The environmental impacts were assessed using the Tools for Reduction and Assessment of
Chemicals and Other Environmental Impacts version 2.1 (TRACI 2.1). [3] TRACI 2.1 includes the
following impact categories:
 • Acidification
 • Eutrophication
 • Freshwater Ecotoxicity
 • Global Warming, with and without inclusion of climate-carbon feedback
 • Human Health Impacts (Cancer, Non-Cancer, Particulate Matter)
 • Ozone Depletion

 • Smog Formation
Greenhouse gas emissions are reported on a common mass basis of carbon dioxide equivalents
(CO2e) using the global warming potential of each gas from the Intergovernmental Panel on
Climate Change Fifth Assessment Report (IPCC AR5) for 100 years. [4] The impact factors for
Global Warming Potential (GWP) with and without inclusion of climate-carbon feedback and
including carbon dioxide (CO2) from methane (CH4) oxidation were derived from the IPCC
“Climate Change 2013: The Physical Science Basis” report and are summarized in Exhibit 2-2. [5]
Climate-carbon feedback considers the changes in the rate of land and ocean carbon uptake due
to climate change. As the global mean surface temperature increases, the rate of carbon uptake
by land and the oceans decreases, which increases the concentration of carbon dioxide in the
atmosphere compared to a scenario with a constant global mean surface temperature. This
phenomenon causes a positive feedback loop where climate change can increase non-linearly
due to ongoing climate change. [5]

2.5.2 FEDEFL v1.0.0
Cumulative Energy Demand was quantified using the Federal Elementary Flow List (FEDEFL)
Inventory Methods v1.0.0, which provides the primary energy content, in terms of high heating
value or HHV, of different energy resources (e.g., crude oil, natural gas, bituminous coal, sub-
bituminous coal, etc.) at their extraction point. The energy demands were categorized based on:
[6]
 • Renewable energy extracted from earth in MJ
 • Non-renewable (i.e., fossil and nuclear fuel) extracted from earth in MJ
 • Total energy extracted in MJ
The FEDEFL Inventory Method was further modified by using the energy density for uranium
(i.e., 560,000 MJ/kilogram of natural uranium), as reported by Frischkneckt for the Cumulative
Energy Demand – Standard approach. [7]

3 METHODOLOGY
To generate regionalized LCA results for the energy flows, a list of ZIP Codes within the
continental United States was retrieved. Each ZIP Code was assigned, based on its location, to a
natural gas delivery region (natural gas), PADD (Petroleum Administration for Defense Districts)
region (petroleum products), and Balancing Authority (electricity). The most recent NETL
natural gas, petroleum, and electricity baseline models were used to generate environmental
impacts and determine cumulative energy consumption for each energy flow at the delivery
region, PADD, and Balancing Authority level. Afterwards, the LCIA results were designated to
each ZIP Code based on its corresponding basin, PADD, and Balancing Authority.
Further, the environmental impacts for each fuel commodity were provided for three stages or
boundaries: upstream, downstream and total. Upstream impacts (i.e., Scope 3) of the natural
gas, propane and fuel oil supply chain corresponded to emissions and impacts for cradle-to-gate
(i.e., extraction, processing and delivery of the fuels to the end user), while downstream
impacts (i.e., Scope 1) were gate-to-grave results (i.e., combustion of fuel in a boiler at the end
user). Total environmental impacts were calculated from the summation of upstream and
downstream LCA results.
For the LCA of electricity consumption, Scope 2 impacts pertained to direct emissions of the
power plants during the generation of electricity, while Scope 3 impacts included upstream fuel
production, construction impacts for renewable sources and power plant construction.
In the second part of the analysis, previously updated life cycle models for the consumption of
electricity, natural gas, propane and fuel oil in residential and commercial buildings were
adapted to include projections based on the AEO 2023 Reference and Low Zero-Carbon
Technology Cost Case. A summary of the methodology is provided:
 • Generation mixes for each year for each Balancing Authority from 2023 to 2050 were
 calculated based on the AEO 2023 annually projected generation mixes for each
 Electricity Market Module (EMM) region. Additionally, for electricity, two forecast
 scenarios were analyzed based on NREL’s Cambium 2023 model for the Mid Case and
 Low Renewables Cost Case. These were used similarly to estimate regionally projected
 generation mixes based on Generation and Emissions Assessment (GEA) Regions.
 • Natural gas shares per type of natural gas and per technobasin within each U.S. delivery
 region were calculated from 2021 to 2050 based on the AEO 2023 annually projected
 trends in the generation of shale, tight, conventional natural gas and coal-bed methane
 in the United States and trends in dry natural gas production for each Oil and Gas Supply
 Module (OGSM) region.
 • The petroleum baseline model was adapted based on projected domestic crude oil
 production, imports and exports every 5 years from 2021 to 2050 based on the AEO
 2023 data.

3.1.2 Natural Gas
NETL’s baseline models provided regionalized production and delivery of natural gas, fuel oil,
propane and electricity. The natural gas baseline model was updated with data representative
of the U.S. 2020 natural gas supply chain. The model and results were published in the report,
“Life Cycle Analysis of Natural Gas Extraction and Power Generation: U.S. 2020 Emissions
Profile.” [11] In comparison with the previous natural gas baseline model used in preceding
report, the current model provides LCI results per delivery region in the U.S. (i.e., Midwest,
Northeast, Pacific, Rocky Mountain, Southeast and Southwest), instead of per technobasin and
well type.

 13

3.1.3 Electricity
The most recent version of the U.S. EPA Electricity Life Cycle Inventory version 2.0.0 (eLCI) was
used to model the environmental impacts of electricity generation and consumption for each
Balancing Authority. [12] More information about the eLCI model is available through NETL,
“Development of an Open-Source Life Cycle Baseline for Electricity Consumption in the United
States”. [13] The base year for the model was set to 2022, which would represent historical net
power generation and fuel consumption of power generators as reported in the year 2022. The
model calculated emissions using the following inventories and years:
 • Toxics Release Inventories (TRI): 2021
 • National Emissions Inventory (NEI): 2020
 • Resource Conservation and Recovery Act Information (RCRA Info): 2021

3.1.4 Fuel Oil and Propane
NETL’s petroleum baseline reports the upstream production of fuel oil and propane per PADD
region. This model was first released in 2017, through the report “Updating the U.S. Life Cycle
GHG Petroleum Baseline to 2014 with Projections to 2040 Using Open-Source Engineering-Based
Models” [14], but was updated with data available for 2021 from the U.S. Energy Information
Administration. [15] [16] For this analysis, the previous baseline model was modified by
updating crude oil domestic production, PADD interchanges of crude oil, import and export
countries, and by updating the refinery model to include the production of propane. Refining
processes were updated with PRELIM model (Petroleum Refinery Life Cycle Inventory Model
v1.5) available from the Energy Technology Assessment Research Group from the University of
Calgary. [17]

3.1.5 Combustion Emissions
Emissions from the combustion of natural gas in commercial or industrial boilers were retrieved
from NETL’s unit process, “Combustion of Natural Gas”, for uncontrolled external combustion
boilers, which was assumed as the most utilized boiler in the U.S. [18] However, as more boilers
with more effective control technologies (e.g., wet scrubber, flue gas circulating, low NOx, etc.)
become available in the market, the environmental impacts of this unit process may have to be
updated.
Emissions from the combustion of propane [19] and fuel oil [20] were imported from the
Federal LCA Commons and were derived from the combustion of these fuels in an average
industrial boiler.

3.3 LIFE CYCLE ASSESSMENT
The LCIA was performed using openLCA version 2.3.1 [22] using the impact assessment
methods: TRACI 2.1 and modified FEDEFL v1.0.0 (Uranium). LCA results were retrieved for each
natural gas delivery region, Balancing Authority (electricity), and PADD Region (fuel oil and
propane).

3.4 LIFE CYCLE PROJECTIONS
Data from the AEO 2023 Reference and Low Zero-Carbon Technology Cost scenarios were used
to develop the projected life cycle models for all utilities. The projections were developed by the
EIA using the National Energy Modeling System, which provides projections of energy
production, demand, imports and prices. The Reference Case (REF) is the benchmark scenario
which was developed using the current expectations on energy production, delivery and
consumption. The Low Zero-Carbon Technology Cost scenario (LZTC) assumes an accelerated
rate of technology cost declines for renewable energy, nuclear energy and diurnal storage
through 2050. Compared to the Reference Case, the Low Renewables Cost scenario presumes a
40% cost reduction in cost by 2050, as well as reduction in capital, operating and manufacturing
costs for these technologies. [23]
In addition to this analysis, projections for electricity models were prepared using two scenarios
from NREL’s Cambium 2023: the Mid Case (MID) and Low Renewable Energy Cost (LRC) Case.
Cambium utilizes two models, the Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS) model and
PLEXOS, to simulate hourly and annual emissions, cost and operational data for different
parameterized scenarios of the U.S. electricity sector. According to NREL’s report, the Mid Case

 17

utilized central or median estimates for fuel and technology costs, average demand growth of
1.8% per year and with consideration of electricity sector policies as of September 2023, while
excluding nascent technologies (e.g., generation from hydrogen, biopower with carbon capture
and storage, etc.). In the Low Renewable Energy Cost Case, the assumptions were derived from
NREL’s Annual Technology Baseline report from 2023 [24], and assumed lower costs for
batteries, geothermal, hydropower, solar and wind sources and greater performance
improvements. [25] The implementation of the projections is discussed below.

AEO 2023 projections were integrated by using simulated “Generation by Fuel Type” in billion
kilowatt-hours (BkWh) per year, from 2023 to 2050, for each technology in each EMM region
(AEO 2023 Tables 54 and 56): coal, petroleum, gas, nuclear, conventional hydroelectric power,
geothermal, biogenic municipal waste, wood and other biomass, solar thermal, solar
photovoltaic, wind and offshore wind. Similarly, the projections included Cambium 2023 data
for generation for every five years (2025, 2030, 2035, 2040, 2045 and 2050) for the following
technologies: coal, natural gas CC, natural gas CT, nuclear, oil-gas-steam, hydropower,
geothermal, biopower, land-based wind, offshore wind, utility-scale photovoltaic, rooftop
photovoltaic, and concentrating solar power.
For each future year, all plants that are using one type of fuel were modified to have an increase
or decrease in generation based on the AEO or Cambium projection for the region where each
plant is located. For example, AEO 2023 forecasted that generation from coal will decrease from
58.699 BkWh to 39.836 BkWh between 2025 and 2026 in the EMMR region TRE (Texas
Reliability Entity). The projections would calculate new net generation for each plant that is
using coal in the TRE region, regardless of Balancing Authority, such that the total generation
from coal in the TRE region in 2026 would be equal to 39.836 BkWh. The projection would
calculate the decrease based on the proportion of generation of each plant using coal to the
total generation of all plants using coal in the same EMM region. This step was iterated for each
fuel source for each EMM region from 2023 to 2050 (AEO) or for each GEA region every 5 years
from 2025 to 2050 for Cambium within the same scenarios. In the final step, for each projection
year, the generation mix for each Balancing Authority was calculated by aggregating the
projected generation of each plant of the same fuel source across the different EMM or GEA
regions within the same Balancing Authority. Exhibit 3-6 shows a sample of calculated
generation mixes up to 2050 under the AEO 2023 Reference Case for the Balancing Authority
Electric Reliability Council of Texas, Inc. (ERCO), which had plants located across three EMM
regions: TRE, MISS and SPPS. On the other hand, Exhibit 3-7 shows the calculated generation
mixes from Cambium 2023 Mid Case for ERCO, which had plants located across three GEA
regions: ERCOT, MISO South and SPP South. Both sets of projections showed an increasing share
for solar and hydro sources up to 2050, but Cambium estimated a faster rate of reduction in
shares of coal and natural gas. Comparison of the two exhibits also showed differences in the
shares of nuclear sources by 2050, with Cambium projecting no nuclear power in ERCO by 2050.
On the other hand, Cambium projections for ERCO showed greater solar and wind power
contributions by 2050 than the AEO projections. The differences observed between these
scenarios for ERCO, as well as other Balancing Authorities, reflect differences in reported
forecasts from AEO and Cambium. Identification of the causes of the differences between these
two data sources is beyond the scope of this analysis.

4.1 REGIONALIZED OPERATIONAL ENERGY LIFE CYCLE IMPACTS
While the analysis included all metrics available from the TRACI 2.1 Impact Assessment Method,
the summary and discussion of the results in this section will be focusing on the findings for
global warming potential (100-year) with climate-carbon feedback (GWP-100 with CC-FB) and
cumulative energy demand (CED) only. While the results are provided for both GWP with and
without climate-carbon feedback in the associated Excel spreadsheet, all the results pertaining
to GWP in this section will refer exclusively to GWP with climate-carbon feedback. Similar trends
were observed for results for GWP with and without climate-carbon impacts. Complete LCIA
results are available in the accompanying worksheets.

4.1.1 Electricity
Exhibit 4-1 shows a choropleth on a U.S. map for the GWP-100 results for the year 2022 (base
year), while Exhibit 4-2 shows the results per Balancing Authority. The U.S. GWP-100 for year
2022 based on this analysis was 486 kg CO2e per MWh of electricity consumed, with a range of
1 to 1,062 kg CO2e per MWh based on Balancing Authority. The lowest GWP-100s were
estimated for 124 ZIP Code areas in PUD No. 1 of Douglas County (DOPD), Public Utility District
No. 1 of Chelan County (CHPD), City of Tacoma, Department of Public Utilities, Light Division
(TPWR) and Public Utility District No. 2 of Grant County, Washington (GCPD). All the ZIP Code
areas serviced by these Balancing Authorities were mapped in the northwestern U.S. in the
state of Washington. These four areas consumed entirely electricity from hydroelectric power
plants in 2022.
On the other hand, the highest GWP-100 results were estimated for 475 ZIP Code areas serviced
by the LG&E and KU Services Company as agent for Louisville Gas and Electric Company and
Kentucky Utilities Company (LGEE), which consumed primarily coal and natural gas as fuel in
2022. Almost up to 99% of LGEE’s generation mix is from non-renewable fuel. The ZIP Code
areas serviced by this BA were primarily in Kentucky and some parts of Virginia.
LGEE was followed by Gainesville Regional Utilities (GVL), Western Area Power Administration –
Rocky Mountain Region (WACM), Seminole Electric Cooperative (SEC) and South Carolina Public
Service Authority (SC), which all had majority consumption of non-renewable fuels (e.g., coal
and natural gas). GVL and SEC serviced 15 and 251 ZIP Code areas in Florida, respectively, while
SC serviced 251 areas in South Carolina. The WACM service areas encompassed 370 total ZIP
Codes across Colorado, Montana, Nebraska, New Mexico, South Dakota, Utah and Wyoming.

4.1.2 Natural Gas
A full discussion of the emissions of the natural gas supply chain (i.e., production through
distribution) was provided in a separate NETL report, “Life Cycle Analysis of Natural Gas
Extraction and Power Generation: U.S. 2020 Emissions Profile.” [11] Exhibit 4-8 provides the
GWP-100 and CED results derived for the combustion of 1 MJ of natural gas in a residential or
commercial boiler for the six delivery regions and the United States.
The U.S. GWP-100 for natural gas was 59.9 g CO2e per MJ, with a range of 57.8 to 63.6 g CO2e
per MJ across the different regions. Natural gas delivered to the Rocky Mountain and Pacific
regions demonstrated the highest GWP-100, which was approximately 5-6% higher than the
U.S.-wide calculation. These regions were found to have a majority composition of tight gas
from the Green River, Piceance and Uinta basins. On the other hand, the lowest GWP-100 was
found for the Northeast region (approximately 4% lower than the U.S.-wide calculation), which
had the highest contribution from shale gas from the Appalachian basin. According to the
accompanying NETL report, the primary source of emissions across all upstream stages was
combustion exhaust and venting from compressor systems. [11]
The U.S. CED for 1 MJ of natural gas was 1.091 MJ, with a range of 1.079 to 1.106 MJ. The
highest CED was computed for the Southwest region, while the lowest was computed for the
Northeast. Natural gas extraction was the main contributor to the CED of the natural gas supply
chain, with minor contributions from electricity and fuel consumption. NETL reported that for
the U.S. natural gas supply chain, 1.085 MJ of natural gas was required to be extracted from the
wells to deliver 1 MJ of natural gas through distribution.

4.2 PROJECTIONS OF OPERATIONAL PROFILES
4.2.1 Electricity
Four scenarios in total were analyzed for forecasting future emissions for electricity generation
and consumptions. Two scenarios were retrieved from AEO 2023, and two were retrieved from
NREL’s Cambium 2023. The four scenarios are listed below:
 • EIA AEO 2023 Reference Case (“REF”)
 • EIA AEO 2023 Low Zero-Carbon Technology Cost Side Case (“LZTC”)
 • NREL Cambium 2023 Mid Case (“MID”)
 • NREL Cambium 2023 Low Battery and Renewable Cost Case (“LRC”)
Exhibit 4-10 shows the GWP-100 and CED results for the United States from the base year 2022
and forecasted from 2023 to 2050. The top graph is based on the AEO 2023 REF, while the
bottom graph is based on AEO 2023 LZTC. The REF and LZTC scenarios forecasted a GWP-100 (kg
CO2e per MWh) of 239 and 145 for the U.S. by 2050, respectively. These are a potential
reduction from the base year GWP-100 of 486 kg CO2e per MWh: 51% under REF and 70%
under LZTC, which assumed more favorable parameters for renewable generation and costs. As
expected, the trend in the GWP-100 followed the fraction of renewable generation in the
United States. The REF scenario calculated for a 2050 U.S. grid mix of approximately 74% from
nuclear and renewable sources (e.g., biomass, conventional hydro, geothermal, solar and wind).
On the other hand, the LZTC forecasted approximately 89% integration of nuclear and
renewable sources by 2050. Reductions in Scope 2 emissions outpaced reductions in Scope 3
emissions, as possible emissions from directly emitting power plants (e.g., coal, natural gas, oil)
decreased. Both scenarios reported an accelerated integration of nuclear and renewable source
in the U.S. from 2026 to 2030, with a faster rate of acceleration in the LZTC. The slight increase
in GWP-100 in the REF case between 2022 to 2025 was due to differences in modeling scope
between NETL’s electricity model and EIA’s NEMS electricity module. NETL’s 2022 model
assumed lower contributions from coal in the MISO Balancing Authority than was reported in
the AEO 2023 for the same year, which led to an initial increase in emissions when the AEO
2023 forecasts were implemented within the NETL model.

5 RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE WORK
This report and the associated worksheets provide LCA results for each of the four utilities (i.e.,
electricity, natural gas, fuel oil and propane) widely consumed in residential and commercial
settings. The analyses utilized NETL models that were parameterized using data from a base
year (e.g., 2020 for natural gas, 2022 for electricity). Afterwards, the inventories derived from
these base year models were used to estimate future emissions by varying regional (e.g., EMM
region generation mixes for electricity) and technological shares (e.g., technobasin and source
of natural gas). By performing this analysis in this manner, life cycle inventories from the base
year were used to calculate for future emissions, which may lead to inaccuracy of the results.
For example, natural gas life cycle inventories from base year 2020 were used for natural gas
projections to 2050. However, this method does not account for potential changes in methane
emission rates that may be possible through implementation of methane mitigation programs
throughout the natural gas supply chain in the future. Possible scenarios, which include
industry-wide goals, should be investigated for inclusion in future forecasts. For example, NREL
considered in the calculation of emissions for Cambium 2023 the possibility of a linear decrease
of 30% of fugitive methane emissions in natural gas precombustion emissions. [25]
Another recommendation is to investigate long-run and short-run marginal generation for
electricity projections, such as those reported by NREL in Cambium 2023. [28] Because
projected emissions utilized historical averaged emissions from existing generators, these may
not accurately represent the marginal impacts associated with added loads to the grid as a
consequence of increased demands from residential or commercial sources in the future.
Additionally, the current electricity projections utilized fixed grid efficiencies (i.e., fraction of
electricity generated that is delivered to end user). Future work may investigate the changes in
grid efficiencies from increased integration of energy storage in the future, as well as adaptation
of energy efficiency and demand response programs by utilities.
