Evaluating documentation of follow-up laboratory testing in TB treatment monitoring in the Eastern Region of Ghana: a consistency analysis of three facility registers
Authors: Mavis Pearl Kwabla, William Dormechele, Lawrence Sena Tuglo, Ofosuhene O. Apenteng, Gideon Kye-Duodu, Eric Osei, Joyce Der, Yaw Adusi-Poku, Frank Baiden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe World Health Organization (WHO) recommends systematic follow-up testing for all bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB (PTB) cases under treatment to track progress towards successful treatment completion. However, this topic has received little attention from researchers. We assessed the extent of documentation of follow-up laboratory testing for TB treatment monitoring.MethodsMatched and unmatched records of bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases recorded between January 2016 and December 2017 across three TB registers from five districts of the Eastern Region were analyzed to assess documentation of follow-up laboratory testing at recommended treatment time points (months 2/3, 5, and 6/8). A cross-register consistency analysis assessed agreement between follow-up testing records documented in the laboratory register and those recorded in the district TB register, with the laboratory register treated as the reference source. A logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with documentation of follow-up laboratory testing.ResultsA total of 773 bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases were successfully matched across the three registers after record linkage. The mean age was 42.6 years (SD = ± 15.0). More than half of the PTB cases in the district register did not have follow-up test results documented at 2 or 3 months (62.9%, 95% CI: 59.4–66.2, p < 0.001), 5 months (72.5%, 95% CI: 69.2–75.5, p < 0.001), and 6 or 8 months (66.4%, 95% CI: 63.0–69.6, p < 0.001). Similarly, in the laboratory register, 45.8% of cases had missing follow-up laboratory test results at months 2 or 3, 65.9% at month 5, and 99.9% at month 6/8. In the treatment centre registers, 67.1%, 75.9%, and 75.4% of PTB cases did not have follow-up test results documented at months 2 or 3, 5, and 6 or 8, respectively. Cross-register consistency analysis showed that documentation status matched in more than half of the records across the assessed follow-up intervals, with the highest consistency observed at month 6/8 (66.3%; 95% CI: 62.8–69.5; p < 0.001). Adult age groups (15–64 years) had lower odds of having documented follow-up test results than children aged 0–14 years. PTB cases from the Kwahu West Municipality were more likely to have documented follow-up testing results (aOR: 3.9, 95% CI: 2.1–7.1, p < 0.001) compared to cases residing in Akyemansa District.ConclusionLogistic regression analysis showed that documented follow-up testing results were less common among several adult age groups than among children aged 0–14 years. Because this study assessed routine register documentation, this finding should be interpreted as a documentation gap rather than a confirmed absence of testing. Strengthening documentation practices and integrated reporting systems for follow-up testing should therefore be prioritized within the TB control programme.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health challenge, with untreated cases posing a considerable risk of transmission within communities [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), untreated TB patients in the general population have the potential to infect 10–15 other individuals in a year [2]. Consequently, effective TB control programmes are essential to ensure that all detected cases receive appropriate treatment and monitoring until they are declared cured, thereby reducing the infectiousness of affected individuals in the community [3]. Ensuring that patients receive the right treatment and monitoring is crucial for achieving a full cure. However, in resource-limited settings, monitoring of treatment response to anti-TB therapy remains unsatisfactory [4].

One fundamental aspect of TB treatment monitoring is the documentation of follow-up laboratory testing in line with recommended guidelines, particularly among bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB (PTB) patients [5]. These guidelines assess patient response to treatment and, if necessary, prompt adjustments to medications [6]. However, documentation of compliance with these guidelines varies across settings and across TB management systems, with some patients reportedly completing treatment without undergoing the necessary laboratory tests to confirm the treatment outcomes [7]. Furthermore, data on crucial indicators, such as sputum conversion and treatment outcomes, recorded in laboratory registers often exhibit incompleteness and inconsistency with health facility data.

Recognizing the importance of laboratory testing in TB control, the WHO recommends systematic follow-up testing for all bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases undergoing treatment, aiming to track progress towards successful treatment completion [2]. Currently, follow-up laboratory testing is performed at the end of months 2 or 3, 5, and 6 or 8 [5]. During the study period (2016–2017), TB treatment monitoring in Ghana followed the WHO TB treatment guidelines that recommended sputum smear microscopy follow-up at months 2/3, 5, and 6/8 for bacteriologically confirmed PTB patients. While WHO guidelines have since evolved with the introduction of shorter treatment regimens and updated consolidated TB guidelines, these monitoring intervals were the standard national practice during the data collection period. Therefore, the evaluation of documentation of follow-up laboratory testing in this study was interpreted with respect to the monitoring recommendations applicable at that time. Health facilities are expected to maintain accurate records, including laboratory registers, to track TB diagnosis and treatment outcomes. However, discrepancies between laboratory follow-up data and health facility records have been observed, highlighting potential gaps in treatment monitoring and TB case reporting. The extent of documentation of follow-up laboratory testing by healthcare providers remains unclear.

This study assessed the extent of documentation of follow-up laboratory testing in Ghana. It evaluated the consistency between follow-up data recorded in health facility records and laboratory registers through a cross-register consistency analysis.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted the study in five of the 26 districts in Ghana’s Eastern Region (Fig. 1), covering a population of approximately 3 million people [8]. At the time of this study, the Eastern Region was the 6th-largest region in Ghana, with a mix of urban and rural dwellers. A household in the region consists of four to eight people, and 54.4% of households have only one sleeping room, which increases the likelihood of TB spread [9]. The Eastern Region was chosen for this study because it has been one of the regions where electronic data-capturing tools, such as the District Health Information Management System I & II (DHIMS 1 & 2) software, have been implemented. This software is currently used for TB data capture in Ghana [10]. The use of this software is expected to improve data quality and TB detection; hence, evaluating the documentation of follow-up laboratory testing within the surveillance system in relation to TB follow-up laboratory testing guidelines applied in the region is a good proxy for evaluating the country’s situation [11].

Study design

This study was a retrospective audit of TB surveillance records, combined with record linkage analysis, across three routine TB data-collection sources: laboratory registers, district TB registers, and treatment centre registers [12]. Records of bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB patients diagnosed between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2017 were linked across the three registers to evaluate the completeness and consistency of documentation of follow-up laboratory testing. Capture-recapture methods were used to assess reporting completeness across the registers. Study population

The study population comprised bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases reported between January 2016 and December 2017 in five districts within the Eastern Region of Ghana. Confirmed PTB patients who met the inclusion criteria were patients with at least one of two sputum smear tests positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, or culture-confirmed cases, or those diagnosed as TB-positive by the GeneXpert® MTB/RIF assay.

Information on drug-resistance status was not consistently available in the routine registers and could therefore not be used to stratify the analysis. The follow-up intervals assessed in this study (months 2/3, 5, and 6/8) reflect the standard monitoring schedule for drug-susceptible pulmonary TB used in Ghana during the study period. Possible inclusion of drug-resistant TB cases, which may follow different monitoring schedules, is acknowledged as a limitation.

Sampling and sample size estimation

After assessing an 8-year trend in TB cases with known incidence, we determined that investigating two years of records (2016 and 2017) from the TB registers of the selected districts would be sufficient. Hence, all patients registered within two years (2016 and 2017) were included in the study. We obtained records from five districts within the Eastern Region: one district with a referral hospital (purposively selected for its central location and three laboratories), two rural districts, and two urban districts (randomly selected), all of which were equipped with their own TB diagnostic facilities. Districts lacking laboratory facilities or with shared laboratory capacity were excluded from the study to ensure clarity in case allocation. Each selected district had at least one laboratory for TB diagnosis, except the district in the regional capital, which had three, making it a hub for TB diagnosis and treatment across all 26 districts in the region. TB records from laboratories and health facilities registered in the selected districts were included in the matching process.

Data collection procedure

A retrospective audit of TB cases from 2016 to 2017 was conducted using records from three registries: laboratory, district, and treatment centre registers to evaluate the quality of reporting for laboratory follow-up testing results. TB follow-up laboratory testing records were extracted from the registers and manually entered into an Excel template, after which the records were matched at distinct intervals (months 2/3, 5, and 6/8).

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome of interest was documentation of follow-up laboratory testing records at recommended treatment milestones (months 2/3, 5, and 6/8). A case was classified as having documented follow-up testing if a laboratory result was recorded in the register at the specified interval. Both matched and unmatched records of bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases across the three registers were analysed to assess documentation of guideline-recommended follow-up testing at months 2 or 3, 5, and 6 or 8. A cross-register consistency analysis was conducted to determine the extent to which follow-up testing documentation in the district register agreed with documentation in the laboratory register. Cross-register consistency was defined as matching documentation status, either documented or not documented, for the same patient and follow-up interval across registers. The laboratory register was treated as the reference source because it is the primary point of test performance and result entry. In contrast, district registers may be affected by delays or omissions in downstream transcription. TB case records from the laboratory registry were compared with those from the district and treatment centre registry to assess record-matching accuracy and reporting consistency. The treatment centre register served as a third data source to verify the completeness of follow-up testing documentation and to support cross-validation of records identified in the laboratory and district TB registers. Finally, logistic regression was conducted to identify factors associated with the documentation of follow-up laboratory testing. Age group 0–14 years was used as the reference category in the logistic regression model. All analyses were performed using Stata/IC v14.2 (Stata, College Station, TX, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Model specification was evaluated using the link test, and multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (reference number GHS-ERC005/04/18) and the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Hannover Medical School in Germany (Nr. 7902_BO_K_2018). The study utilized secondary data; therefore, consent to participate was deemed unnecessary, as the TB Control Programme routinely collects TB registry data from the various data sources used in the study as part of national surveillance of infectious diseases in Ghana. Permission to implement the study was obtained from the National TB Control Programme. Record linkage across the three registers was performed before anonymisation using available personal identifiers and case characteristics. After linkage was completed, unique study identification numbers were generated, and personal identifiers were removed before analysis.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB patients

The survey included 773 patients with confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB). The average age of the participants was 42.6 ± 15.0 years, with a predominant male representation at 518 (67.0%). New Juaben accounted for most of the cases, 322 (41.7%), while Kwahu North contributed the fewest, 55 (7.1%). More than half (n = 391; 50.6%) of the cases were recorded in the year 2017 (Table 1).

Documentation of follow-up laboratory testing among pulmonary TB patients

Table 2 shows the proportion of patients with documented follow-up laboratory testing at the assessed intervals across the district, treatment centre, and laboratory registers. More than half of the confirmed TB cases follow-up tests were not documented at months 2/3 (62.9%, 95% CI: 59.4–66.2, p < 0.001), 5 (72.5%, 95% CI: 69.2–75.5, p < 0.001), and 6/8, 66.4% (95% CI: 63.0-69.6, p < 0.001) in the district register. Similarly, in the laboratory register, 45.8% (95% CI: 42.3–49.3, p < 0.001) of cases had follow-up laboratory test results missing at month 2 or 3, 65.9% (95% CI: 62.4–69.1, p < 0.001) at month 5 and 99.9% (95% CI: 99.1–100, p < 0.001) at month 6/8. In the treatment centre registers, 67.1% (95% CI: 63.7–70.4, p < 0.001), 75.9% (95% CI: 72.8–78.8, p < 0.001), and 75.4% (95% CI: 72.3–78.3, p < 0.001) of PTB cases did not have follow-up test results documented at months 2 or 3, 5, and 6 or 8, respectively (Table 2).

Consistency of follow-up laboratory testing documentation across registers

A cross-register consistency analysis was conducted to assess whether documentation status at the same follow-up interval was the same in the laboratory and district registers. Across the three follow-up intervals, more than half of the records were not documented in both registers: 261 (53.7%) at months 2/3, 360 (64.3%) at month 5, and 512 (99.8%) at months 6/8 (Table 3). Overall consistency between the laboratory and district registers was 58.9% (95% CI: 55.35–62.29, p < 0.001) at months 2/3, 54.9% (95% CI: 51.3–58.3, p < 0.001) at month 5, and 66.2% (95% CI: 62.8–69.5, p < 0.001) at months 6/8 (Table 4).

Factors associated with documentation of follow-up laboratory testing among pulmonary positive TB patients

Logistic regression was conducted to determine factors associated with documentation of follow-up laboratory testing (Table 5). Age and district were associated with documentation of follow-up testing in the multivariable model. Compared with children aged 0–14 years, several adult age categories had lower odds of having documented follow-up test results. In the adjusted model, the odds were significantly lower for patients aged 15–24 years (aOR 0.2, 95% CI 0.03–0.88), 25–34 years (aOR 0.1, 95% CI 0.02–0.70), 35–44 years (aOR 0.1, 95% CI 0.02–0.79), 45–54 years (aOR 0.2, 95% CI 0.03–0.95), and 55–64 years (aOR 0.1, 95% CI 0.02–0.67). PTB cases in Kwahu West Municipality were more likely to have documented follow-up test results than those in Akyemansa District (aOR: 3.9, 95% CI: 2.1–7.1, p < 0.001) (Table 5).

The multivariable logistic regression model showed a lack of fit based on the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.0008). However, the link test indicated no evidence of model specification error (p-value for _hatsq = 0.239). The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination ability (area under the ROC curve = 0.73). Some predictors, particularly age categories, exhibited high variance inflation factors (VIF > 10), indicating potential multicollinearity.

Discussion

This study assessed the documentation of follow-up laboratory testing among bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) patients across selected districts in the Eastern Region of Ghana using linked TB registry data. The findings reveal substantial gaps in the recording of follow-up laboratory results across district, treatment centre, and laboratory registers. These gaps highlight weaknesses in routine TB surveillance documentation and raise concerns about the reliability of treatment-monitoring data used for programme evaluation.

Across all three registers, a large proportion of patients had no documented follow-up laboratory test results at key treatment milestones (months 2/3, 5, and 6/8). The documentation gap was particularly pronounced in the laboratory register at months 6 and 8, where nearly all cases lacked recorded follow-up results. These findings suggest substantial gaps in routine documentation practices and highlight potential weaknesses in the reporting systems used to monitor TB treatment. Because this analysis relied on routine registry data, missing entries may reflect incomplete documentation rather than the absence of follow-up testing itself.

The cross-register consistency analysis further illustrates inconsistencies between the laboratory and district TB registers. Although more than half of the follow-up testing records were consistent across registers at each time point, substantial discrepancies remained, particularly at month five. These discrepancies likely reflect weaknesses in data recording, reporting pathways, or communication between laboratories and treatment facilities, all of which can compromise the reliability of TB surveillance data used for monitoring programme performance.

The high proportion of missing follow-up testing records likely reflects weaknesses in routine data flow and incomplete documentation. In settings that rely on paper-based or partially integrated reporting systems, laboratory results may be generated but not consistently transferred into district or treatment registers. Therefore, missing entries in the present study should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that follow-up testing was not performed, but rather as an indication of gaps in data recording and reporting systems.

Multivariable logistic regression identified age group and district of residence as significant factors associated with documentation of follow-up testing. Several adult age categories (15–64 years) had significantly lower odds of having documented follow-up test results compared with children aged 0–14 years. Because this analysis relied on routine registry data, the reasons for this pattern cannot be determined directly; however, it may reflect differences in treatment follow-up patterns, health-seeking behaviour, or documentation practices across age groups. District-level variation was also observed, with patients in Kwahu West Municipality significantly more likely to have documented follow-up test results than those in Akyemansa District, suggesting potential differences in programme implementation, data management practices, or health-system capacity across districts. One possible explanation for the lower odds of documented follow-up testing among adult age groups is the higher case volume typically managed in adult TB services, which may increase workload and reduce completeness of record-keeping. In contrast, paediatric TB cases were few and may have received closer individual attention. However, this interpretation should be approached with caution, particularly because children aged 0–14 years constituted a very small proportion of the sample.

Interestingly, sex and year of diagnosis were not significantly associated with documentation of follow-up testing, indicating that the observed gaps are likely systemic rather than temporal or sex-specific.

These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted globally and across sub-Saharan Africa. For instance, a WHO-supported study in Africa identified poor documentation and loss to follow-up as major barriers to effective TB treatment. Another study in Ghana also found that 92.5% of TB case referral records from pharmacies were not documented [13]. Similarly, a systematic review by Alipanah et al. (2018) [14] emphasized that non-adherence to TB treatment and monitoring protocols (as reported in previous studies) is a widespread issue, often resulting in delayed sputum conversion, treatment failure, and increased risk of drug resistance. The review also highlighted that interventions such as directly observed therapy (DOT), digital reminders, and community-based support significantly improved adherence, interventions that may be inconsistently implemented in the study districts.

Geographic disparities in TB adherence, particularly between urban and rural areas, have been reported across Africa. Urban districts with better health infrastructure tend to report higher adherence rates than rural or underserved regions [15]. In this study, sex was not significantly associated with documentation of follow-up testing. Although sex-related differences in TB incidence have been widely reported in the literature, our findings suggest that the gaps observed in follow-up testing documentation are more likely related to systemic programmatic factors rather than differences between male and female patients [16]. Mamo et al. (2023) [17] studied household contact screening adherence among PTB patients in Ethiopia and found that only 44.4% adhered to recommended screening protocols. Factors influencing documentation of follow-up testing included patient knowledge, health system support, and socio-demographic variables [17], paralleling the district and age-related disparities observed in our study.

Overall, these findings highlight substantial gaps in the documentation of follow-up laboratory testing within routine TB surveillance systems. Strengthening integrated TB data management systems, including digitalisation of laboratory-to-clinic reporting and routine reconciliation between laboratory, district, and treatment registers, may improve the completeness and reliability of follow-up testing records. Regular data quality audits and staff training in register completion should complement these system-level improvements. Improved documentation is essential for accurately evaluating treatment response and for ensuring the effectiveness of TB control programmes.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

The use of record linkage and capture-recapture methods enhances the study’s robustness and reliability in assessing documentation of follow-up laboratory testing. Conducting cross-register consistency analysis and logistic regression enables a comprehensive examination of the factors influencing the documentation of follow-up testing, providing valuable insights for targeted interventions. The findings of this study can inform TB management strategies, not only locally but also in similar settings globally.

Limitations

Incomplete or inconsistent data in health facility records and laboratory registers may have limited the accuracy of documentation assessments and introduced bias. The study’s focus on selected districts within a single region may also limit the generalizability of the findings to other geographic areas or populations. The retrospective design restricted access to comprehensive data, particularly on long-term treatment outcomes, actual test performance, treatment adherence, and health-system factors that may influence documentation. Because the study relied on routine surveillance records, it could only assess documentation of follow-up testing rather than confirm whether testing was performed. Missing entries in the registers may therefore reflect incomplete recording rather than the true absence of follow-up testing.

The regression findings should also be interpreted with caution. The multivariable logistic regression model showed poor fit according to the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, suggesting it may not have fully captured the structure of the observed data. Although the link test did not indicate major model specification error and the model showed acceptable discrimination, the adjusted odds ratios should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. Small cell counts, the small number of children aged 0–14 years in the reference category, zero documented follow-up testing events in Akyemansa District, and evidence of multicollinearity may have contributed to unstable estimates, particularly for age- and district-level effects. In addition, drug-resistance status was not consistently recorded in the registers, and the possible inclusion of drug-resistant TB cases may have affected classification against the standard follow-up schedule assessed.

Conclusion

This study identified substantial gaps in the documentation of follow-up laboratory testing for TB treatment monitoring across district, treatment centre, and laboratory registers in Ghana’s Eastern Region. A large proportion of patients had no recorded follow-up test results at recommended treatment milestones, indicating weaknesses in routine TB surveillance systems. The regression analysis suggested possible age- and district-level differences in documentation of follow-up testing; however, because of the poor model fit, small cell counts, complete separation in one district category, and evidence of multicollinearity, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. The main implication of the study is therefore the need to strengthen data-recording practices, improve coordination between laboratories and treatment facilities, and reinforce routine monitoring procedures within TB programmes to enhance the completeness and reliability of treatment-monitoring data.
