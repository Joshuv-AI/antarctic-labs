Combining blast resistance and high yield in F1 aromatic rice through classical hybrid breeding
Authors: Md Mamunur Rashid, Md Mominur Rahman, Md Mamunur Rashid, Md Arifuzzaman, Dilruba Ashrafun Nahar Majumder, Md Mahfuzul Hasan, Nayan Chandra Howlader, Walid Soufan, Ayman El Sabagh, Mohammad Sohidul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice blast caused by the Magnaporthe oryzae outbreak rapidly in Bangladesh brings about an imminent threat to the production of rice, causing an average 40% yield loss in boro rice. Aromatic rice varieties exhibit low yield due to limited genetic potential, leading to insufficient supply and high market prices. To address these challenges, a study was conducted from July 2021 to December 2022 at the Bangladesh Jute Research Institute, Nashipur Farm, Dinajpur, aiming to develop F₁ hybrid aromatic rice by combining high yield potential and blast resistance, using seven traditional aromatic cultivars for subsequent breeding programs. The study shows that the F1 generation often exhibits hybrid vigor, characterized by greater resilience and performance compared to parent lines. The F1 genotype AR08 exhibited the maximum 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline (2-AP) intensity score, while the Munni genotype showed the minimum. Grain yield hill⁻¹ varied significantly, with AR04 producing the highest yield, highlighting its yield potential, while BRRI dhan34 recorded the lowest yield. Leaf blast severity ranged from 0 to 38.09%, with AR03, AR04 and AR09 exhibiting maximum resistance. Overall, nine F1 aromatic rice lines with moderate to high blast resistance were developed through targeted parental crosses. Among them, AR03, AR04, and AR09 showed the best resistance to blast and the highest yield potential, while other F1 genotypes, such as AR01, AR06, AR08, AR10, and AR11, outperformed their parents in yield traits, with moderate resistance. AR01, AR03, AR04, AR05, and AR09 represent promising candidate parental materials for further breeding and multi-location, multi-season evaluation toward the development of blast-resistant, high-yielding aromatic rice varieties.Keywords: Classical breeding, F₁ hybrid rice, Blast resistance, Molecular markers, Resistance evaluation, Rice blastSubject terms: Biotechnology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L., 2n = 2x = 24) is the leading staple food in Asia, leading in both production and productivity. It is essential to the livelihoods of 160 million people in Bangladesh, which ranks as the third-largest producer and consumer of rice, with an annual output of 37 million metric tons1,2. As of 2024–2025, rice production has reached 535.8 million metric tons globally3. China leads global production with 144.62 million metric tons (28% of the total), followed by India with 137 million metric tons (26%). Bangladesh contributes approximately 7% of the world’s rice production4.

Rice production in Bangladesh is significantly affected by various biotic stresses, including fungal pathogens such as Magnaporthe oryzae (blast), Rhizoctonia solani (sheath blight), Sarocladium oryzae (sheath rot), bacterial pathogens such as Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (bacterial blight), and Bipolaris oryzae (brown spot). Both biotic and abiotic factors contribute to low yields, with pathogen-induced diseases being the most common biotic stress5,6. Currently, 31 rice diseases have been identified in Bangladesh, with ten considered major7,8. Among these, rice blast, caused by the fungus Pyricularia oryzae (Magnaporthe oryzae), is the most destructive, reducing grain yield and quality significantly9. Documented first in China in 1637 and later in Japan, Italy, the USA, and India, the rice blast causes a 30% yield loss in Bangladesh, particularly severe in the northern and central regions considered as the hotspot for blast disease, with up to 40% loss in Dinajpur10,11. According to relevant research during the Boro season, the incidences of blast disease were as high as 58.2% in the high-yielding rice cultivar BRRI dhan47, 39.8% in BRRI dhan29, and 20.3% in BRRI dhan28, and 59.8% in BRRI dhan3412,13.

Currently, 267 races of rice blast have been identified, and the disease severity is increasing, posing a significant threat worldwide. Rice blast can infect plants at all growth stages, from seedling to pre-maturity, often causing substantial yield reductions14. The disease manifests in forms such as leaf, node, neck, and panicle blast, each with specific symptoms causing severe damage.

The application of Simple Sequence Repeat (SSR) markers in the selection process for blast resistance has significantly advanced rice breeding efforts. Their high specificity, reliability, and efficiency in Marker-Assisted Selection (MAS) make them indispensable tools for accelerating the development of blast-resistant aromatic rice genotypes15–17. With the continuous advancement of molecular marker technologies, rice breeding has become increasingly precise and efficient in addressing the challenges posed by rice blast disease18,19.

Developing blast-resistant aromatic rice is critical for enhancing Bangladesh’s agricultural productivity and ensuring long-term food security20,21. Aromatic varieties such as BRRI dhan34 and Sadabadsha are highly valued for their fragrance and superior grain quality22,23; however, their high susceptibility to blast disease leads to substantial yield losses across major rice-growing regions. Improving disease resistance in these cultivars will promote production stability, support farmers in blast-affected areas, and enhance the economic importance of aromatic rice19,24. Moreover, resilient aromatic varieties hold strong potential for boosting export opportunities and strengthening Bangladesh’s position in the global rice market. Therefore, integrating blast resistance into aromatic rice is essential for maintaining crop resilience, sustaining food supply, and promoting national economic growth.

Despite notable advancements in blast epidemiology and the development of resistant rice varieties, a substantial research gap persists in the improvement of aromatic rice in Bangladesh. Most of the previous studies have focused on non-aromatic cultivars with very little effort to combine blast resistance with high yield potential in this premium rice group. For example25, investigated eco-friendly blast management in aromatic rice rather than breeding for simultaneous blast resistance and yield improvement. Although molecular markers such as SSRs have aided in more accurate resistance breeding, their integration with traditional hybridization methods of developing new aromatic genotypes adapted to blast-prone environments remains limited. Consequently, comprehensive evaluations of parental genotypes and their F1 hybrids for blast resistance, yield performance, and agronomic traits under local field conditions are still lacking. Addressing this gap, the objectives of the present study were to develop and assess F₁ aromatic rice hybrids combining blast resistance with improved yield through classical hybrid breeding. It was hypothesized that suitable parental combinations would produce hybrids with enhanced blast resistance and superior yield performance under field conditions in Bangladesh.

Materials and methods

Experimental location and duration

The study was carried out at the Bangladesh Jute Research Institute (BJRI) Research Field, Nashipur, Dinajpur-5200, Bangladesh and consisted of two phases: a preparatory phase and an experimental phase. The preparatory phase (July 2021-December 2021) involved the development of F1 hybrid seeds. The experimental phase (July 2022-December 2022) included a field evaluation of these F1 hybrids to determine agronomic traits, yield performance, and blast resistance. The location is in the Old Himalayan Piedmont Plain (AEZ-1) at 24.000° N latitude and 90.250° E longitude, 34 m above sea level26.

Soil conditions

Soil samples were collected from a depth of 0 to 15 cm at the experimental field. After being dried, ground, and passed through a 20-mesh sieve, the samples were analyzed at the Soil Resources Development Institute (SRDI), Nashipur, Dinajpur. The experimental pot soil was sandy loam, with 65% sand, 30% silt, 5% clay, had an acidic pH of 4.70 and low water retention capacity. Initial soil analysis showed 0.514% organic carbon, 0.04% total nitrogen, 55.85 ppm available phosphorus, 0.26 meq/100 g soil exchangeable potassium, and 9.93 ppm available sulfur (Table 1).

The preparatory phase involved developing F₁ hybrids of aromatic rice with blast resistance. Controlled hybridization was performed through emasculation of flowers one day before anthesis (4–6 p.m.) using vacuum suction, followed by bagging to prevent accidental pollination. The next morning, pollen from selected male parents was applied to emasculated flowers, which were re-bagged after pollination. Mature F₁ seeds were harvested 28–34 days later. A total of eleven F₁ hybrids were produced from crosses among all parental genotypes (Table 3) and were carefully extracted and stored under controlled dry conditions (relative humidity < 25%) for preservation35.

The 2nd phase of the research was from June to December 2022 and featured 18 genotypes in total, including seven parental lines and 11 F1 hybrids, which were selected for field trial, selection of suitable genotypes, and to make an overall comparison (Fig. 2).

Parental genotypes were planted in adjacent plots for direct comparison, while F₁ hybrid plots were netted for a short period (5–7 days) prior to flowering after artificial inoculation to prevent accidental cross-pollination. The net was removed before grain formation, ensuring that any potential effects on microclimate or disease development were minimal during the main evaluation period. The experiment followed a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications of genotypes, and each plot consisted of three raised planting rows to ensure thorough evaluation (Fig. 3).

Experimentation

In late June 2022, the experimental field was prepared by ploughing and laddering prior to transplanting 28-30-day-old aromatic rice seedlings. The parental genotypes and the corresponding F₁ hybrids were carefully established following pre-germination treatments. The experiment was conducted in 2.5 × 2.5 m plots with a 30 × 30 cm plant spacing. Fertilization was applied according to BARC recommendations, including cow dung, TSP, MOP, gypsum, and zinc sulfate during land preparation, with urea top-dressed in three splits at 15, 30, and 50 days after transplanting. Standard intercultural practices, including gap filling, weeding, irrigation, and pest management, were implemented throughout the cropping season. For artificial pathogen inoculum, Magnaporthe oryzae isolates were initially isolated from naturally infected plant parts and purified on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25 °C for 7 days to promote sporulation. Conidial spores were extracted by pouring each PDA plate with sterile distilled water containing 0.02% Tween 20 and adjusting the suspension to 1 × 105 conidia mL⁻¹. During the rice tillering stage, plants were uniformly sprayed once with the spore suspension until runoff. Harvesting targeted mature seeds for future study and yield assessment. Meteorological data for the research period were collected from the BJRI at Nashipur, Dinajpur, Bangladesh.

Collection of data

Morphology, phenology, growth, yield characteristics and yield

Plant height was measured from the plant base to the tip of the tallest leaf (cm), and flag leaf length was recorded in centimeters. Effective tillers were defined as panicle-bearing tillers and counted hill− 1 using five randomly selected hills plot− 1 across three replications. Days to 50% flowering were determined from transplanting to the stage when 50% of panicles had emerged. Panicle length was measured from the basal rachis node to the apex using ten randomly selected panicles. Total grains panicle− 1, including filled and unfilled grains, were counted from five randomly selected plants plot− 1. Thousand-grain weight was determined from oven-dried, clean grains using a digital balance. Grain yield and stover weight hill− 1 were recorded after proper drying. Quantification of 2-Acetyl-1-pyrroline (2-AP)

The intensity of 2-Acetyl-1-pyrroline was determined with 1.7% potassium hydroxide (KOH) using a modified Zaldal Aroma Test Protocol, which is an efficient and rapid method for detecting aroma in rice. This method is particularly suitable for identifying 2-AP intensity, the compound responsible for the characteristic aroma of fragrant rice cultivars. In this protocol, rice grains were soaked in a 1.7% KOH solution to facilitate the release of volatile aromatic compounds. The alkaline treatment helped break down the cell walls, enhancing the volatilization of 2-AP. After a few minutes, the samples were evaluated by a sensory panel consisting of seven expert trained members, and the experiment was conducted in three replications to ensure reliability. Panelists assessed the aroma by smelling the samples and comparing them with BRRI dhan28 (non-aromatic rice) as a control. The intensity of the fragrance was recorded on a scale ranging from low to high (e.g., 1 to 10), based on the collective perception of the panelists36,37.

Leaf Blast Severity Assessment

Blast assessment was conducted at 60 days after planting (DAT) by measuring the largest lesion’s dimensions (sum of length and width) and estimating total lesion length. Leaf blast severity (%) was quantified as the proportion of leaf area affected, with disease progression monitored until physiological maturity. Scoring followed the IRRI 0–9 visual scale, where 0 represents symptom-free plants and 9 denotes complete leaf damage or plant death (Table 4). Only the central plants plot− 1 were evaluated, excluding border plants. To identify blast resistant genes using SSR markers across seven parental genotypes and 11 F1 populations, genomic DNA was extracted from both parents and F₁ progeny for marker analysis. A total of seven SSR markers, each targeting specific known blast resistance genes, were used across 19 aromatic rice genotypes (with the AR08 genotype analyzed using two separate samples) (Table 5). Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

The Veriti Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA) was employed to do PCR amplifications in a 10 µL volume. 10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 200 µM dNTPs, 250 nM primers, 20–40 ng genomic DNA, and 0.25 U Taq DNA polymerase were all included in the reaction solution. One cycle at 94 °C for five minutes, thirty-five cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 30 s at 50–67 °C (depending on the primers), 30 s at 72 °C, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min comprised the PCR amplification technique. 100 mM EDTA, one-fifth volume of loading buffer, was added with the final products. 10 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0 8 µL were loaded vertically for electrophoresis on 2% (w/v) Meta agarose gel for 80 min with the following conditions: pH 7.5, 5% Ficoll 400, 0.05% bromophenol blue, and 0.05% xylene cyanol. To prepare and run the gel, 1 × TBE running buffer was utilized. Immediately following ethidium bromide staining, the gel was examined under ultraviolet (UV) light.

Scoring of R genes and confirmation of heterozygosity

The presence or absence of R genes in F₁ plants was determined using PCR amplification with the seven SSR markers described above. PCR products were separated on 2% agarose gels, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV light. Banding patterns were scored as “present” when a clear, expected-size band appeared, and “absent” when no band was observed. Each plant was amplified in triplicate to ensure consistency; any ambiguous bands were re-amplified for confirmation.

Heterozygosity in F₁s was confirmed by comparing banding patterns with both parental genotypes. Plants showing the resistant parent allele but lacking the unique susceptible parent allele were considered heterozygous. This approach ensured accurate identification of R gene inheritance in the F₁ population.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Statistix 10. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess differences among genotypes, and mean comparisons were conducted using LSD at p ≤ 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) and cluster analysis were performed using SPSS and OriginPro, respectively. Graphical illustrations were prepared with BioRender. For molecular analysis, SSR marker data were scored as binary (1 = presence, 0 = absence). Allele frequency, gene diversity, and polymorphism information content (PIC) were estimated using PowerMarker/GenAlEx software. Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) was conducted to partition genetic variation among and within genotypes. Genetic similarity coefficients were calculated, and a dendrogram was constructed using the UPGMA method.

A notable level of variation was observed in panicle length among the rice genotypes. Detection of resistant genes employing molecular study

Major blast-resistance (R) genes in rice genotypes were detected using seven SSR markers: RM224, RM208, RM247, RM527, RM72, RM541 and RM206. The PCR amplification products were observed on a 2% agarose gel (Fig. 12), revealing different gene-specific bands across several genotypes. The Pi-1 gene (157 bp) was found in genotypes corresponding to lanes 2, 4–6, 9, and 11–19, and the Pi-ta gene (131 bp) was found in lanes 1, 3, 4, 6–8, 10–13, and 15–19, demonstrating the presence of these genes in a large number of cultivars. The Pi-33 gene (166 bp) was amplified in lanes 1, 3, and 8; however, the Pi-b gene (173 bp) was not present in any of the genotypes examined. The Piz-5 gene (233 bp) was found in lanes 3, 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, and 17; no amplification of the Pi-9 gene (158 bp) was discovered, indicating that it was not present in the examined genotypes. The Pi-kh gene (147 bp) was successfully amplified in lanes 1, 2, 5, 7, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, 17, and 19. These findings confirm the presence of multiple blast-resistance genes in the tested rice genotypes and demonstrate the efficacy of SSR markers for precise molecular characterization of resistance traits, making them an important tool for marker-assisted selection in rice breeding programs.

Discussion

The success of any crop improvement initiative hinges on the magnitude of genetic variability and the heritability of target traits. A comprehensive assessment of genetic diversity is fundamental for understanding relationships among cultivars and facilitating effective selection in breeding programs39,40. In this study, both phenotypic and genotypic associations were analyzed for key quality parameters, such as grain aroma, 1000-grain weight, and yield, suggesting the possible presence of linkage or pleiotropy within specific genomic regions governing these traits. Notably, certain genomic loci associated with grain quality traits appear to have co-segregated in regionally adapted, farmer-preferred aromatic rice varieties through the course of domestication and selection41,42. Among the agronomic traits, panicle length, number of tillers hill− 1, grain weight, and harvest index were found as important factors of yield potential in the F1 hybrids43. These traits demonstrated strong and consistent associations with grain yield, indicating that indirect selection based on these parameters may significantly enhance breeding efficiency44. Furthermore, plant height showed a positive correlation with panicle length, while reductions in filled grain number and grain yield were linked to lower panicle numbers. The harvest index, reflecting reproductive efficiency, and 1000-grain weight, indicative of seed size, were found to have a direct and substantial impact on grain yield45. These findings collectively underscore the potential of classical breeding approaches in combining desirable quality and yield traits in aromatic rice genotypes.

Flag leaf length is a critical morpho-physiological trait in rice, as it serves as a major photosynthetic organ contributing assimilates during the grain-filling period. It has been widely recognized for its positive association with yield-contributing traits, particularly panicle development and grain filling46,47. In the present investigation, substantial genotypic variation in flag leaf length was recorded among the aromatic rice genotypes. Such variability is of considerable breeding interest, as prior studies have established a significant positive correlation between flag leaf length and panicle length, thereby underscoring its relevance as a potential selection criterion for yield improvement11.

The total number of tillers hill− 1 varied significantly among the genotypes, with AR04 producing the highest and Hazardana the lowest. A similar trend was observed for effective tiller numbers, where AR04 again ranked highest, while Hazardana and Kalovat Sugondhi showed the lowest and statistically comparable values. Regarding grain production, AR07 demonstrated superior performance in total grains panicle− 1, closely followed by AR03, whereas Kalovat Sugondhi produced the least. For filled grains panicle− 1, AR07 maintained the highest count, with AR03 slightly lower, and Kalovat Sugondhi again exhibited the lowest. The large or heavy panicle type hybrid exhibited a poorer rate of grain filling (low filled grain number and weight), even under the favorable environment, resulting in a lower extent of grain filling48. Panicle length showed considerable variation, with AR02 having the longest panicle and Sadabadsha the shortest. In our findings, maximum grain yield hill− 1 and harvest index were observed in AR04, whereas the maximum stover and biological yield were recorded in AR03. In our findings, maximum grain yield hill⁻¹ and harvest index were observed in AR04, whereas the maximum stover and biological yield were recorded in AR03.

Previous studies have reported that increases in grain yield are often associated with changes in biomass production and harvest index; however, the relationship may vary depending on genetic background and environmental conditions49–51.

A highly heritable trait, rice aroma is principally impacted by the essential component 2-AP and is regulated by certain genes. However, the genetic make-up of different aromatic rice types differs52–54. According to the findings of the present investigation, the AR08 genotype had the highest 2-AP score, whereas the Munni genotype showed the lowest level of 2-AP (0.69). The observed variation in aroma score among genotypes reflects differences in genetic regulation of 2-AP biosynthesis. Previous studies have indicated that no one chemical or combination of compounds distinctly distinguished a particular aromatic rice variety in the volatile analysis of mature grains from both aromatic and non-aromatic rice. According to findings55,56, the concentration of these compounds was what characterized the cultivars instead.

Additionally, PCA and hierarchical cluster analysis revealed that the high-yielding genotypes AR01, AR03, AR04, and AR05 clustered together due to their superior yield components, moderate to high levels of blast resistance as indicated by lower NSL and NBS values. In contrast, AR07 and B-34 showed great blast resistance but poor yield performance, indicating differential genotypic responses between yield potential and disease reaction under field conditions. The combination of heatmap clustering, PCA, and hierarchical cluster analysis was beneficial for illustrating genetic diversity and determining behavioral interactions across rice genotypes57. These multivariate approaches supported the identification of resistance patterns and genotype grouping based on blast disease response58,59, facilitating the selection of genetically diverse and resistant lines in rice breeding programs60,61.

Molecular screening utilizing seven SSR markers revealed the presence of major blast-resistance genes, Pi-1, Pi-kh, Piz-5 and Pi-ta across multiple genotypes, with AR05 and AR09 containing the most resistance genes. The complete absence of Pi-9 and Pi-b in all genotypes suggests scope for broadening the resistance base through future marker-assisted introgression of additional R genes to enhance durability of blast resistance59,61. Notably, AR03 and AR04 emerged as agronomically superior genotypes with strong blast resistance, demonstrating the value of combining molecular screening and phenotypic selection. These findings support the use of molecular techniques to accelerate the development of high-yielding, blast-resistant aromatic rice varieties62–64.

Based on comprehensive evaluations of morphological characteristics, yield components, grain quality, and molecular screening, F1 hybrids AR03 and AR04 emerged as the most promising candidates for the development of high-yielding aromatic rice. Hybrids AR01, AR05, and AR09 also demonstrated significant potential as sources of blast-resistant varieties, containing critical resistance genes. The integration of classical breeding with MAS is crucial for advancing these F1 hybrids toward the dual goals of high yield and durable blast resistance, in line with modern strategies for aromatic rice improvement65–68.

Furthermore, molecular characterization of the aromatic rice genotypes using SSR markers linked to major blast resistance genes revealed variable levels of genetic diversity (Table 11). RM206 (Pi-kh), RM527 (Piz-5), and RM247 (Pi-ta) exhibited the highest gene diversity (He = 0.475), followed by RM224 (Pi-1) (He = 0.44), while RM72 (Pi-33) showed moderate diversity (He = 0.401). In contrast, RM541 (Pi-9) and RM208 (Pi-b) were monomorphic, indicating limited allelic variation at these loci. The PIC values (0.00-0.475) suggest low to moderate marker informativeness, consistent with earlier reports on SSR-based diversity analysis in rice69–71. AMOVA further indicated that 100% of the total genetic variation was attributable to differences among genotypes, confirming substantial genetic differentiation among the evaluated lines. These results emphasize the importance of integrating molecular marker analysis with phenotypic evaluation to enhance the efficiency of selecting high-yielding, blast-resistant aromatic rice genotypes.

Conclusion

The findings of this study show the expression of hybrid vigor in the F1 aromatic rice generation, which outperforms and is more resilient than its parental lines. Nine F1 lines with moderate blast resistance were developed through classical breeding. Among them, AR01, AR03, AR04, AR05, and AR09 exhibited the highest blast resistance and yield performance. In addition, lines including AR01, AR02, AR06, AR08, AR10, and AR11 exceeded their parents in yield attributes while preserving moderate resistance. Molecular marker analysis confirmed the presence of key blast resistance genes, including Pi-1, Pi-kh, Piz-5, Pi-33 and Pi-ta, in selected parental and F₁ genotypes, demonstrating successful transmission. Overall, the recommended genotypes AR01, AR03, AR04, AR05, and AR09 present valuable genetic resources for breeding programs aimed at enhancing yield and blast resistance in aromatic rice in Bangladesh.

Limitations

The study was conducted at a single location during one season. Resistance gene detection was limited to the selected SSR markers, and no standard check variety was included for comparative evaluation of yield and blast resistance. The seven parental genotypes were primarily chosen based on blast resistance, while other important traits such as aromaticity, yield potential, and harvest index were not considered. Aroma evaluation was performed using the KOH sensory test without instrumental validation, and blast assessment was confined to leaf blast under controlled conditions. For a more comprehensive assessment, multi-location and multi-season trials, broader marker coverage, and instrumental aroma analysis are recommended in future studies.
