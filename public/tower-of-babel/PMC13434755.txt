Structural basis and immunogenic efficacy of porcine circovirus type 3 virus-like particle
Authors: Jinfu Su, Xiaodan Tong, Yanan Jiang, Yu Li, He Yan, Yan Liu, Yanwei Wang, Xiaorui Su, Zhe Sun, Mengyue Wang, Ningshao Xia, Mihnea Bostina, Shaowei Li, Wenqiang Pang, Qingbing Zheng, Kegong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porcine circovirus type 3 (PCV3) is an emerging swine pathogen associated with reproductive failure and systemic inflammation, but vaccines for related PCV2 provide limited cross-protection. Here, we determine high-resolution cryo-EM structures of PCV3 capsid protein assembled into virus-like particles (VLPs) and of PCV3 VLPs bound to the PCV3-specific antibody 2B5. The structures reveal differences from PCV2 in surface loops and define a conserved 2B5 epitope mainly involving the BC, EF and HI loops, providing a structural basis for PCV3-specific recognition. PCV3 VLPs induce sustained antibody responses in mice and pigs. In a pig challenge model, VLP immunization prevents viremia and viral replication in lungs and lymph nodes, as measured by quantitative PCR and in situ hybridization, with protection maintained for at least five months after boosting. These findings support PCV3 VLPs as a vaccine candidate against PCV3 infection.

Introduction

Porcine circovirus type 3 (PCV3) and porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2) are small, single-stranded DNA viruses that belong to the Circoviridae family, primarily infecting pigs and being associated with a variety of clinical diseases. The two viruses have significant differences in their pathogenic mechanisms and clinical manifestations. The pathogenicity of PCV2 is more clearly defined, as it can cause conditions such as post-weaning multisystemic wasting syndrome and porcine dermatitis and nephropathy syndrome1. In contrast, while PCV3 is also associated with certain diseases, including myocarditis and reproductive disorders, its specific pathogenic mechanisms and scope of effects remain largely unclear2. Since PCV3 was reported in the United States in 20163, PCV3-associated diseases have been documented in numerous countries, including Poland4, China5, South Korea6, the United Kingdom7, Italy8, Thailand9, Brazil10, Sweden11, Japan12, Russia13, Spain14, Denmark14, Germany15, Colombia16, Hungary17, and Serbia18. The geographical distribution of PCV3 is continually expanding. Clinically, infected pigs may exhibit symptoms such as dermatitis, nephropathy syndrome, reproductive disorders, myocarditis, multi-organ inflammation, stillbirths, and mummified fetuses19. Moreover, PCV3 frequently co-infects pigs with other significant reproductive viruses, such as PCV2 and porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), leading to mixed infections that often result in abortions, stillbirths, and mummified fetuses in sows. PCV3 has also been detected in pigs that show no apparent clinical symptoms. Increasing evidence suggests that PCV3 has emerged as a significant threat to the swine industry. This situation mirrors the initial emergence of PCV2, where its impact was not immediately evident in well-managed farms, reflecting the current epidemiological status of PCV320. As the prevalence of PCV3 infections rises, significant impacts on pig health are anticipated, necessitating heightened vigilance.

Efforts to isolate PCV3 virus strains have been undertaken by many researchers. Studies by Diaz et al. 21. and Wang et al. 22. both involved inoculating PCV3-positive tissues into PK-15 cells, confirming viral replication through quantitative PCR, indirect immunofluorescence, and in situ hybridization. However, sequential cell passages showed a declining viral load, indicating ineffective proliferation during passage. To elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms of PCV3, researchers at Iowa State University inoculated 5-week-old colostrum-deprived piglets intranasally and intramuscularly with tissue-derived PCV323. Although no overt clinical symptoms were observed post-infection, quantitative PCR (qPCR) detected viral shedding in serum, nasal, and intestinal samples. PCV3 genomic DNA was also identified in lung, lymph node, liver, and brain tissues, with in situ hybridization confirming viral replication, thus establishing a viable disease model for PCV3.

Currently, no commercial vaccines for PCV3 are available on the market. In contrast, the development of the PCV2 vaccine is well-advanced, with various types widely used globally to control PCV2-related diseases. Both PCV3 and PCV2 genomes encode three open reading frames (ORFs), coding replicase (Rep), capsid (Cap), and ORF3 proteins, respectively. Importantly, the Cap protein is the sole structural protein and contains the primary antigenic epitopes necessary for eliciting an immune response, making it an ideal candidate for subunit vaccine development24. A detailed study of the structure of PCV2 Cap has been conducted using advanced structural analysis techniques, including X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM)25–27. This structural information has been instrumental in designing PCV2 vaccines that elicit enhanced immune responses. As a result, a range of PCV2 vaccines, including inactivated, subunit, and genetically engineered types, have been developed and have effectively reduced the incidence of PCV2-related diseases and economic losses. There is currently no structural information available for PCV3, although PCV2 and PCV3 share genomic and protein structural similarities, significant differences exist. Structural studies have revealed a variety of PCV2-specific binding sites, which may account for the limitation of PCV2 vaccines for their cross-protective efficacy against PCV328–30. While the pathological characteristics of PCV3 have been preliminarily clarified, our understanding of the virus remains limited due to the complexity of its pathogenesis and immune mechanisms31. The limited virological and immunological research on PCV3 presents significant challenges to vaccine development32.

Here, we show that the PCV3 capsid protein expressed in a recombinant baculovirus system assembles into VLPs. Cryo-EM structures of PCV3 VLPs and the PCV3 VLP-2B5 Fab complex define capsid surface features and a conserved antibody epitope that distinguishes PCV3 from PCV2. We further evaluate PCV3 VLP immunogenicity in mice and pigs and test protective efficacy in pig challenge models.

Results

Characterization and structural analysis of PCV3 VLPs

In this study, a recombinant baculovirus system was employed to express the PCV3 capsid protein (Supplementary Fig. 1). The capsid proteins were expressed and purified using ion exchange chromatography. Negative-staining transmission electron microscopy (NSTEM) (Fig. 1A) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1B) analyses showed that the capsid proteins efficiently self-assembled into uniform and intact VLPs with a diameter of approximately 20 nm. To enhance our understanding of PCV3 VLP structure and enable a structural comparison with PCV2, cryo-EM was employed to elucidate the overall architecture and key loop regions of PCV3 VLPs (Supplementary Fig. 2). We resolved the cryo-EM structure of the PCV3 VLP at a resolution of 2.48 Å, exhibiting T = 1 icosahedral symmetry with an inner and outer diameter of approximately 14 nm and 20 nm, respectively (Fig. 1D–F and Supplementary Table 1), closely resembling the structure of PCV225. BLAST alignment shows a sequence identity of 43.90% between the capsid proteins of PCV2 and PCV3 studied (Supplementary Fig. 3A). An atomic model of one asymmetric unit (monomer) was built based on the density map, with the interpretable and visible range extending from amino acid P33 to L214. This model was then structurally and sequentially compared to the previously reported PCV2 capsid protein. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the backbone atoms (Cα) between the PCV3 and PCV2 monomers was 2.088 Å, indicating a structural difference (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, the RMSD values for the BC, CD, EF, GH, and HI loops were 4.941 Å, 1.522 Å, 2.000 Å, 3.415 Å, and 2.313 Å, respectively. Notable differences were identified in the major surface loop regions (BC, CD, EF, FG, HI loops) of PCV3 and PCV2 capsid proteins, providing structural insights into their differing immunogenicities (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Cryo-EM structure of PCV3 VLPs in complex with a PCV3-specific antibody

Considering the structural diversity of the capsid proteins between PCV3 and PCV2, we next performed a blocking enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to assess cross-reactivity of PCV3-positive sera to PCV2. Blocking ELISA has emerged as a preferred method for serological detection of PCV or other porcine viruses, attributed to its high specificity and sensitivity33–35. PCV3-positive serum samples were compared with positive sera from other common swine viruses, including PCV2, PRRSV, pseudorabies virus (PRV), classical swine fever virus (CSFV) and porcine parvovirus infection (PPV), in the blocking ELISA. The results demonstrated that PCV3-positive sera exhibited significant blocking potency of PCV3-specific immunoreaction, with blocking rates approaching 90%, whereas anti-sera against other common swine viruses showed no blocking potencies against PCV3-specific immunoreaction (Fig. 2A). This indicates that PCV3-positive sera have a strong ability to block PCV3-specific immune reactions, while sera from other common swine viruses do not exhibit cross-reactivity with PCV3. A PCV3-specific monoclonal antibody, 2B5, was also selected to assess the cross-reactivity between PCV3 and PCV2. We performed enzyme digestion on 2B5 in preparation for the antigen-binding fragment (Fab) for subsequent immune complex formation used in cryo-EM structural analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4A). The dot blot experiment and ELISA both showed that 2B5 reacts specifically with PCV3 but not with PCV2, demonstrating the immunogenic differences between PCV3 and PCV2 (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C). To identify the primary epitope of 2B5 on PCV3 and elucidate the structural basis of PCV3’s immunogenic attributes, we prepared an immune complex of PCV3 VLPs in complex with an excess of 2B5 Fab (PCV3-VLP:2B5). Cryo-EM 2D-classification result of PCV3-VLP:2B5 clearly shows particles with a halo of extra density compared to purified PCV3 VLPs (Fig. 2C). The cryo-EM structure of this complex was then resolved at an overall resolution of 1.73 Å (Fig. 2D, E; Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 1). The density map shows that 2B5 Fab binds to an epitope near the 2-fold axis, and a total of 60 Fab molecules can bind to each VLP (Fig. 2D). However, only the variable domains (directly contacting the capsid epitope) of the bound Fab molecules exhibit smeared densities, indicating the structural flexibility of the 2B5 epitope or bound Fabs.

To achieve detailed interface characterization, local reconstruction was performed, yielding a local density map with a resolution of 2.02 Å (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Table 1). This enhanced local density map shows a clear Fab molecule, with density approximately as strong as that of the capsid shell (Fig. 2F). This facilitated precise model building and enabled the accurate identification of the Fab epitope on the PCV3 capsid, primarily localized in the BC, EF, and HI loops (Fig. 2F).

Structural basis of serotype specificity in PCV3

The atomic model of the PCV3-VLP:2B5 provides detailed views of several important features, including the 2B5 paratope, the 2B5 epitope and the Capsid-Fab interactions. Footprint analysis of 2B5 binding reveals the simultaneous interaction of one 2B5 Fab with three adjacent capsid monomers (Fig. 3A). Notably, only monomer 1 engages with the CDR region of the Fab, whereas monomers 2 and 3 interact with the FR region along the Fab’s side. The interaction surface areas for monomers 1, 2, and 3 with the 2B5 Fab are 640.6 Å2, 220.0 Å2, and 90.8 Å2, respectively. A total of 13 residues on the PCV3 capsid are involved in a single Fab binding (Monomer 1: P54, D56, N57, K58, Q122, V191, P192, E193, K194, T195; Monomer 2: R68, T116; Monomer 3: S156) (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, Fab binding to PCV3 induces a slight conformational change in the BC loop. In-depth interaction analysis demonstrates that the 2B5 Fab interacts mainly with the BC and HI loops, as well as some residues in the EF loop, involving 15 hydrogen bonds and 2 salt bridges (Fig. 3C–E). Notably, these interactions are primarily mediated by the heavy chain complementarity-determining regions 2 and 3 (CDRH2 and CDRH3), as well as selected residues from the light chain CDR1 (CDRL1) (Fig. 3C–E). Specifically, CDRH2 residues R50 and H59 form two hydrogen bonds and a salt bridge with PCV3’s D56 in the BC loop, while CDRH2 residues Y54, D52, D55, and E57 form four hydrogen bonds and a salt bridge with PCV3 K194 and T195 in the HI loop (Fig. 3C). Within CDRH3, residues E99, S100, G102, and N103 establish four hydrogen bonds with PCV3’s N57 in the BC loop, Y101 forms three hydrogen bonds with PCV3’s E193 and V191 in the HI loop, and R106 interacts with PCV3’s Q122 in the EF loop through a hydrogen bond (Fig. 3D). As for the light chain of 2B5, CDRL1 residue N32 forms another hydrogen bond with PCV3’s Q122 in the EF loop (Fig. 3E).

To further assess the evolutionary conservation of the 2B5-binding site, sequence alignment was conducted on a total of 2283 full-length PCV3 capsid protein genes available in GenBank. The analysis revealed that most of the amino acids within the 2B5 epitope are highly conserved across the analyzed sequences, except for residue 56 (Fig. 3F). This residue appears as aspartic acid in 8% strains and asparagine in the remaining 92% (Fig. 3F). Although the structure evaluation in this study uses a PCV3 strain sequence with aspartic acid at residue 56, structural modeling and analysis suggest that the substitution of aspartic acid to asparagine at residue 56 (D56N) will result in the loss of only one salt bridge interaction (Supplementary Fig. 6). Considering the substantial hydrogen bond and salt bridge interacting network established between PCV3 and 2B5, the loss of one salt bridge will minimally impact the overall interaction between them. Collectively, the epitope conservation analysis suggests that 2B5 may broadly recognize diverse PCV3 strains.

Immunogenic evaluation of PCV3 VLPs in mice and pigs

The structural information of the PCV3 VLP indicates distinct immunogenicity compared to PCV2 and other swine viruses. To assess the potential of PCV3 VLP as a vaccine candidate, we first evaluated its immunogenicity in mice. Twenty female BALB/c mice, aged 5 to 6 weeks, were randomly assigned to two groups: a test group and a control group, each containing 10 mice. Each mouse in the test group received a subcutaneous injection of 20 µg of PCV3 VLPs formulated with a commercialized veterinary vaccine adjuvant, Freemix, which is primarily used as an aqueous composite immunoadjuvant for inactivated porcine circovirus vaccines, followed by a boost immunization three weeks later. The control group did not receive any immunization. Blood samples were collected prior to immunization, three weeks after the initial immunization, and one and two weeks after the booster to assess antibody levels using the established blocking ELISA method (Fig. 4A). Three weeks after the initial immunization, 9 of 10 immunized mice were positive for serum anti-PCV3 antibodies, with a percentage inhibition (PI) exceeding 50%. After two immunization doses, all mice tested positive, with PI > 69.58% at week 5 (Fig. 4B). A similar approach was subsequently employed to evaluate the immunogenicity of PCV3 VLP in pigs. Fifteen pigs, aged 14 to 21 days, were randomly assigned to three groups of five. Groups 1 and 2 were designated as immunization groups, receiving intramuscular injections in the neck with a low (100 µg) and a high (200 µg) dose of VLPs per pig, respectively. A second immunization was administered three weeks following the initial dose, while Group 3 served as the control group. Blood samples were collected to assess anti-PCV3 antibody levels using the same blocking ELISA method (Fig. 4C). Three weeks after the initial immunization, partial serum antibody positivity was detected in both dosage groups. In the low dose group, all pigs showed serum PI over 24.68%, with an average of 43.69%. In the high dose group, sera showed a higher average PI of 50.65%. Upon receiving the booster immunization, all serum antibodies became positive and sustained high levels. The two immunized groups showed average serum PI of 78.89% (low dose) and 83.51% (high dose), respectively (Fig. 4D), indicating that the PCV3 VLPs effectively induced a robust antibody response in pigs, confirming its significant immunogenic potential.

Simultaneously, the immunization of PCV3 VLPs was tested in pigs with pre-existing antibodies. Five pigs with a high level of pre-existing antibody (blocking rate above 80%, average of 83.87%) and five pigs with a low level (blocking rate below 80%, average of 60.58%) received intramuscular injections of 100 µg of PCV3 VLP per pig, followed by a boost immunization three weeks later. Blood samples were collected one, two, and three weeks after the initial immunization, and one and two weeks after the booster, to measure antibody levels. In the high-level group, antibody levels remained at a high level with a blocking rate above 80% during the whole period of the study. In the low-level group, antibody levels showed an upward trend around the third week post-initial immunization, reaching optimal levels (PI > 80%) after the booster (Fig. 4E). It appears that pre-existing antibodies affected the trend of serum antibody level increase following the first immunization in the low-level group, however, antibody levels consistently rose after the booster, indicating that pre-existing immunity has a minimal impact on the immunogenicity of PCV3 VLPs.

Sera from pigs immunized with PCV3 VLPs were further assessed for cross-reactivity against PCV2. While strong positive reactions were detected against the PCV3 antigens (PI average: 89.8%), no cross-reactivity was observed with the PCV2 antigens (PI average: 10.6%) (Fig. 4F), demonstrating no antigenic cross-reactivity between PCV2 and PCV3.

Immunoprotection of PCV3 VLPs against PCV3 infection in pigs

To further assess the immunoprotective efficacy of PCV3 VLPs, a similar pig immunization protocol, as depicted in Fig. 4C, was conducted. Two weeks after the booster, pigs were challenged with the PCV3 HN strain at an overall dose of 7.2 × 108.0 copies per pig to evaluate efficacy. This challenge involved a 3 mL nasal drip and a 3 mL intramuscular injection, each at a concentration of 1.2 × 108.0 copies/mL, followed by isolated feeding. Blood samples were collected prior to the challenge and weekly for four weeks post-challenge, with viremia assessed using quantitative fluorescent PCR for detecting PCV3 viral DNA. In the fourth week, post-mortem examinations were performed to obtain lung, hilar lymph node, and mediastinal lymph node tissue samples for quantitative fluorescent PCR and in situ hybridization (ISH) testing21,28,36–38 (Fig. 5A). In the control group, viremia was detectable in 2 out of 5 pigs in the first week post-challenge, with an average CT value of 38.9 ( ± 0.3). In the second week post-challenge, 4 out of 5 pigs exhibited viremia, with an average CT value of 33.0 ( ± 2.3). By the third week, the same 4 pigs still showed viremia, with an average CT value of 32.6 ( ± 1.3). In the fourth week, these 4 pigs continued to exhibit viremia, with an average CT value of 30.4 ( ± 1.3) (Fig. 5B). The gradual decrease in CT values from the first to fourth week, combined with the increasing number of pigs with detectable viremia, indicates heightened viral replication within the host (Fig. 5B). In contrast, no viremia was detected in both groups of immunized pigs post-challenge (Fig. 5B). Quantitative fluorescent PCR assessment of viral DNA revealed that in the first week post-challenge, four of five pigs in the control group exhibited viremia, with an average CT value of 38.1 ( ± 0.5). By the fourth week post-challenge, all control group pigs exhibited viremia, with a decreased average CT value of 30.4 ( ± 2.3) (Fig. 6C). Overall, these results verify the immunoprotective efficacy of PCV3 VLPs in preventing PCV3 infection. Quantitative PCR and in situ hybridization results demonstrated that in the non-immunized control group, PCV3 effectively infected and proliferated in the blood and key tissues, including the lungs, hilar lymph nodes, and mediastinal lymph nodes. However, in pigs receiving PCV3 VLPs immunization, whether in low-dose or high-dose groups, or even in the long-term protection procedure, PCV3 infection and replication were effectively inhibited, with no viremia or viral presence in tissues. These findings confirm that PCV3 VLPs serve as a potent vaccine candidate, provide robust immune protection against PCV3 infection, and effectively prevent the spread and replication of the virus in both systemic and respiratory/immune-related tissues.

Discussion

Since its initial detection in the United States in 2016, PCV3 has quickly emerged as a significant threat to the global swine industry3. PCV3 infection is associated with numerous severe diseases, including dermatitis, nephropathy syndrome, reproductive disorders, myocarditis, multi-organ inflammation, and fetal abnormalities such as stillbirth and mummified fetuses28. Although PCV3 and PCV2 both belong to the circovirus family and mature vaccines for PCV2 are globally available, significant differences exist in their genomic and protein structures3,24–27. Consequently, the cross-protection provided by PCV2 vaccines against PCV3 is limited. This limitation arises from structural differences in their major loop regions, as identified through cryo-EM analysis of PCV3 VLP as well as its immune complex with a PCV3-specific antibody 2B5 in this study, revealing the distinct immunogenic characterization of PCV3.

Our research successfully utilized a recombinant baculovirus system to express and purify the PCV3 capsid protein, facilitating its self-assembly into uniform VLPs. Our cryo-EM structure (2.48 Å) of PCV3 virus-like particles (VLPs) reveals critical differences in surface loop topology (BC/EF/HI loops) compared to PCV225–27, with a backbone RMSD of 2.088 Å. In addition, cross-comparison of the four available PCV2 structural models reveals that three high-resolution structures (PDB IDs 3R0R, 3JCI, and 5ZJU) are nearly identical, exhibiting only minor deviations from the 4.1 Å cryo-EM structure of PCV2 (PDB ID: 5ZBO)25–27. These structural variations explain the lack of cross-reactivity between PCV3 and PCV2 antibodies. Specifically, the epitope recognized by PCV3-specific antibody 2B5 (localized to BC/EF/HI loops) exhibits minimal overlap with PCV2 immunodominant regions, rationalizing the failure of PCV2 vaccines to confer cross-protection. This aligns with studies showing PCV2 subunit vaccines induce non-neutralizing antibodies due to decoy epitopes—a risk potentially mitigated by VLP-based designs that preserve native conformational epitopes39–42.

Crucially, our study demonstrated the strong immunogenicity of VLPs in vivo; PCV3 VLPs elicited robust, sustained antibody responses in both mice and pigs, with complete seroconversion43. Notably, antibody titers (PI > 80%) persisted for ≥5 months post-booster, preventing viremia and tissue replication, demonstrating the long-term immunogenicity of PCV3 VLPs44,45. PCV3 VLPs also effectively boosted antibody levels even in pigs with pre-existing immunity, overcoming potential interference from maternal antibodies46. Furthermore, pigs vaccinated with these VLPs showed effective immune protection against PCV3 challenges, preventing viral infection and spread within the body. These findings underscore the potential of PCV3 VLPs as a vaccine candidate, providing a viable approach to address the current absence of a specific PCV3 vaccine47.

Nonetheless, this study has certain limitations. Firstly, the study primarily concentrated on structural and immunological aspects without fully elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of PCV3 and its interactions with other circoviruses. Furthermore, although prophylactic research was conducted, further investigation is required to evaluate the vaccine’s efficacy under real-world conditions and its effectiveness against variant strains48. Our research provides a molecular basis for the PCV3 immune response, establishing a structural and functional foundation for future vaccine development. However, extensive epidemiological studies and validation of other immune system components are necessary to bolster these initial findings45,49,50.

Overall, this study enhances the understanding of PCV3’s immunological characteristics and proposes alternative strategies for developing effective PCV3 control measures to protect the global swine industry from emerging viral threats. PCV3 VLPs represent a structurally defined, highly immunogenic vaccine candidate with proven efficacy in preventing infection. By elucidating key epitopes, as well as the immunogenicity and protective potency of PCV3 VLPs, this study offers a roadmap for combatting PCV3, an emerging threat to global swine health.

Methods

Ethical statement

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with all relevant ethical regulations and the Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals. The study was performed in accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China. The animal experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the National Research Center for Veterinary Medicine under approval numbers 202401002, 202403001, 202404001 and 202406001. No human participants or human-derived samples were used in this study.

Cells

Sf9 cells were obtained from the National Veterinary Medicine Engineering Technology Research Center. SP2/0 mouse myeloma cells used for hybridoma generation were obtained from laboratory cell stocks maintained at the National Research Center for Veterinary Medicine. The original commercial source and catalog number for the SP2/0 cells were not available. All cell lines used in this study were routinely tested for mycoplasma and found to be mycoplasma-free.

Animals

Female BALB/c mice aged 5–6 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at 20–26 °C and 30–70% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Fourteen- to twenty-one-day-old piglets (Sus scrofa domesticus; mixed sex) were obtained from farms around Luoyang. Before enrollment, piglets were confirmed to be negative for PCV3 antibodies and negative for nucleic acids of African Swine Fever Virus, PCV2 and Porcine Reproductive and Respiratory Syndrome Virus. Piglets were housed in isolation or biosafety isolation facilities at 22–28 °C and 30–70% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to feed and water. Animals were monitored daily throughout the study.

Reagents

BamHI (Cat# FD0054), HindIII (Cat# FD0504) and T4 DNA ligase (Cat# EL0011) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. KOD One polymerase (Cat# KMM-201) was obtained from TOYOBO Biotechnology. Trans1-T1 competent cells (Cat# CD501) were purchased from Beijing TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A4416) and goat anti-mouse fluorescent secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# F9137) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Cellfectin II transfection reagent (Cat# 10362100) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit II (Cat# VR100) was purchased from Geneaid Biotech. Freemix adjuvant was obtained from Beijing Zhongke Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Lot# 20231124) and did not have a commercial catalog number. The Porcine Circovirus Type 3 fluorescent quantitative PCR detection kit was prepared in-house by Luoyang Putai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and did not have a commercial catalog number. The Pinpo RNA 2.0 universal single-color chromogenic RNA in situ hybridization detection kit and PCV3-specific probe were purchased from Guangzhou Pinpo Easyview Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Cat# PIT0001).

Antibodies

The PCV3-Cap monoclonal antibody 2B5 was prepared in-house and used for immunofluorescence, ELISA, dot blot, Fab preparation and cryo-EM complex formation. For immunofluorescence, purified 2B5 was used at 1:500, followed by goat anti-mouse fluorescent secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# F9137) at 1:1000. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A4416) was used at 1:5000 as the secondary antibody for indirect ELISA and screening of PCV3-specific hybridoma clones. HRP-conjugated 2B5 was prepared in-house from purified 2B5 and used at 1:20,000 in the blocking ELISA. The 2B5 and HRP-conjugated 2B5 antibodies were not commercially catalogued, and commercial catalog numbers were not available. The specificity of 2B5 was validated in this study by dot blot and ELISA, showing binding to PCV3 VLPs but not PCV2 VLPs.

Vectors

The pFastBacI vector was maintained at the National Veterinary Medicine Engineering Technology Research Center. Competent DH10Bac cells were prepared and stored by the same institution.

Synthesis of the target gene

The PCV3 capsid protein gene sequence is derived from the PCV3-US/MN2016 strain, with the GenBank accession number KX898030.1. The PCV3 Cap gene sequence was synthesized by Suzhou Genewiz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and was optimized based on the codon preference of the baculovirus system, with the amino acid residues from positions 8 to 16 at the N-terminus deleted.

Construction of PCV3-Cap donor plasmid

The optimized Cap gene was synthesized and digested along with the pFastBacI vector using BamHI and HindIII restriction enzymes. The digested Cap gene was then ligated into the pFastBacI vector using T4 DNA ligase to construct the recombinant donor plasmid. The plasmid was verified by double digestion with BamHI and HindIII and subsequently sent to Genscript Biotech Corporation for sequencing.

Acquisition of recombinant bacmid

The correctly sequenced recombinant donor plasmid was transformed into DH10Bac competent cells. These were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 4 h. A 100 μL aliquot of the culture was then spread onto solid media containing kanamycin, gentamicin, tetracycline, IPTG, and X-gal, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. White colonies were picked and streaked onto plates with the same antibiotic selection and IPTG and X-gal for 24 h at 37 °C. Selected white colonies were grown in liquid media with the same antibiotics for 12-16 h to extract the recombinant Bacmid, using blue colonies as negative controls. PCR identification was performed using Bac-M13 universal primers (Bac-M13F: 5’-CCCAGTCACGACGTTGTAAAACGACG-3’; Bac-M13R: 5’-AGCGGATAACAATTTCACACAGGAAAC-3’).

Rescue of recombinant baculovirus

Log-phase Sf9 cells were seeded into T25 flasks at a density of 3 × 106 cells per flask and incubated at 27 °C for 0.5-1 h to allow full adhesion. Transfection was performed following the protocol of Cellfectin II reagent, with untransfected cells as controls. The transfected cells were incubated at 27 °C, and the cytopathic effect (CPE) was monitored daily until 80–90% CPE was observed. The culture supernatant was harvested as the P1 viral stock, designated rAC-PCV3 Cap.

Identification of recombinant protein expression

Cells were infected with rescued P1 virus stock at an MOI of 0.1 to amplify P2 virus, and subsequently passaged to generate the P3 virus stock. The P3 virus stock was then used at a 1:200 dilution to infect cells for protein expression over a period of 4 days, achieving a cell viability of approximately 60%. The culture supernatant was harvested and analyzed by SDS-PAGE to assess the expression of the Cap protein.

For immunofluorescence analysis, cells in a 24-well plate were infected with the P3 virus stock at a 1:200 dilution. Upon observation of significant cytopathic effects, the cells were fixed with 80% pre-cooled acetone, followed by blocking with 1% BSA solution for 30 minutes. Subsequently, each well was incubated with 200 μL of 1:500 diluted PCV3-Cap monoclonal antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing three times with PBST, 200 μL of 1:1000 diluted goat anti-mouse fluorescent secondary antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The wells were washed three times with PBST and observed under a fluorescence microscope to detect fluorescence.

Purification of PCV3 VLPs

Proteins expressed from the P3 recombinant baculovirus stock were purified by ion-exchange chromatography using a HiPrep SP HP 16/10 cation-exchange column (Cytiva). Before sample loading, the column was equilibrated with 20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 6.5. After loading, the column was re-equilibrated with 20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 6.5, and bound proteins were eluted with 20 mM phosphate buffer containing 300 mM NaCl, pH 6.5. Fractions containing the target protein were collected and concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Millipore, 30 kDa molecular-weight cutoff).

Characterization of PCV3 VLPs

For negative-staining transmission electron microscopy (NSTEM), purified PCV3 VLPs were concentrated and applied onto 400-mesh copper grids. The grids were stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid, air-dried and examined using a transmission electron microscope.

For dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis, the Zetasizer Nano ZSE instrument (Malvern Panalytical) was equilibrated for 30 min before measurement. The detection temperature was set to 25 °C. Purified PCV3 VLP samples (1 mL) were transferred into a disposable cuvette for particle-size analysis.

For high-performance size-exclusion chromatography (HPSEC), purified PCV3 VLPs were analyzed using an LC-2050C high-performance liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu). The mobile phase was PBS, pH 7.2–7.4, filtered through a 0.1 μm membrane before use. An SRT SEC-500 column (7.8 mm × 300 mm; Sepax Technologies) was equilibrated with the filtered mobile phase before sample injection. Protein samples were filtered into sample vials, and 50 μL of each sample was injected. The flow rate was 0.5 mL/min, and elution was monitored using a UV detector at 280 nm.

Evaluation of recombinant VLP immunogenicity in mice

Twenty female BALB/c mice, aged 5–6 weeks, were randomized into two groups (n = 10 each). Group 1 was subcutaneously immunized with 20 μg PCV3 VLP protein emulsified with Freemix adjuvant (0.2 mL/mouse) with a booster on day 21. Group 2 served as a non-immunized control. Blood samples were collected for serum separation before immunization, on day 21, day 7 post-boost, and day 14 post-boost. The blocking ELISA was used to detect antibodies in sera collected at specified intervals before immunization, on day 21, day 7 post-boost, and day 14 post-boost.

Evaluation of recombinant VLP immunogenicity in pigs

Fifteen pigs, aged 14–21 days, were randomized into three groups (n = 5 each). Groups 1 and 2 received intramuscular immunization with 100 μg and 200 μg emulsified with Freemix adjuvant per pig, respectively, with a booster on day 21. Group 3 served as a challenge control. Blood samples were taken periodically, and antibody levels in serum were measured by blocking ELISA.

Establishment of a PCV3 blocking ELISA for antibody detection

Purified PCV3 VLPs were diluted to 2 μg/mL using carbonate buffer, and 100 μL per well was added to a 96-well microplate. The plate was incubated at 2–8 °C for 20–24 h. Following incubation, the liquid was discarded, and 150 μL of blocking solution was added to each well, incubating again at 2–8 °C for 20–24 h. The blocking solution was then removed, and the plate was dried in an environment with humidity below 20% before sealing for storage. For assay setup, serum diluted 1:10, along with undiluted positive and negative controls, was added at 100 μL per well and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After washing, HRP-conjugated PCV3 monoclonal antibody 2B5 was diluted 1:20,000 in antibody diluent, added at 100 μL per well, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The wells were washed again, and substrate solutions A and B were sequentially added at 50 μL per well, incubating in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min. Finally, 50 μL of stop solution was added per well, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

The blocking rate for each sample was calculated as follows: Blocking rate = [1 - (Sample A value/Mean negative control A value)] × 100%. Results were interpreted based on the following criteria: a blocking rate of ≥50% was considered positive, while <50% was considered negative.

The established ELISA method was employed to assess antibody detection sensitivity using two positive serum samples. Additionally, positive serum samples for PCV2, PRRSV, PRV, CSFV, and PPV were tested to assess the method’s specificity. A blocking ELISA method was also utilized to detect antibodies in the serum.

PCV3 challenge inoculum

The PCV3 HN strain used for challenge was obtained from PCV3-positive tissues collected and identified by Pulike Biological Engineering, Inc. The challenge inoculum was prepared from PCV3-positive tissue homogenates, quantified by qPCR and diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (0.1 M, pH 7.2–7.4) to the indicated genome copy numbers before challenge. Before use, the inoculum was confirmed to be negative for African Swine Fever Virus, PCV2, Porcine Reproductive and Respiratory Syndrome Virus, porcine parvovirus, pseudorabies virus, classical swine fever virus, foot-and-mouth disease virus and Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae.

Challenge protection effect measurement

On day 14 post-boost, immunized and control pigs were challenged intranasally and intramuscularly with PCV3 HN strain (1.2 × 108.0 copies/mL, 3 mL each). Prior to and shortly after challenge, injections of KLH emulsified with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant were administered at specified sites for monitoring. Observations spanned 28 days, with blood and tissue samples taken at specified intervals for PCR and in situ hybridization analyses.

Quantitative PCR detection of PCV3 DNA

PCV3 DNA in serum and tissue samples was detected using the Porcine Circovirus Type 3 fluorescent quantitative PCR detection kit supplied by Luoyang Putai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This kit was prepared in-house and did not have a commercial catalog number. Viral nucleic acids were extracted from 200 μL serum or approximately 25 mg tissue using the Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit II (Geneaid Biotech, Cat# VR100). Tissue samples were homogenized in 1000 μL phosphate-buffered saline (0.1 M, pH 7.2–7.4) before extraction. Nucleic acids were eluted in 50 μL elution buffer. qPCR reactions were performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch real-time PCR detection system. Samples with CT values below 40 were considered positive, whereas samples with no amplification or CT values at or above 40 were considered negative according to the kit instructions. Samples were initially tested once; samples with ambiguous amplification curves or CT values near the positivity threshold were retested.

In situ hybridization

Lung, hilar lymph node and mediastinal lymph node tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 1.5 μm. PCV3 nucleic acids were detected using the Pinpo RNA 2.0 universal single-color chromogenic RNA in situ hybridization detection kit (Guangzhou Pinpo Easyview Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Cat# PIT0001) and a PCV3-specific probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Positive signals were defined as red staining in tissue sections and were scored as negative, weak positive, moderate positive or strong positive based on signal intensity and distribution.

Evaluation of the duration of PCV3 VLP immunization

Ten pigs aged 14–21 days were randomly divided into two groups, each consisting of five pigs. Group 1 was designated as the protein immunization group and received an intramuscular injection of 100 μg of protein in a volume of 2 mL per pig. A booster immunization was administered 21 days after the initial immunization. Group 2 served as the challenge control group. Blood samples were collected at 7, 14, and 21 days post-primary immunization, and at 7, 14 days, 1 month, 2 months, 3 months, 4 months, and 5 months post-booster immunization to measure the levels of PCV3 antibodies.

Additionally, at 5 months post-booster, both the immunized group and the challenge control group were subjected to a challenge with the Porcine Circovirus Type 3 HN strain (1.5 × 108.0 copies/mL). The challenge was performed via intranasal administration of 3 mL and intramuscular injection of 3 mL per pig, under conditions of isolated housing. Three days before and after the challenge, each pig received injections of keyhole limpet hemocyanin (1 mg/mL) emulsified with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant at four sites (bilateral axillary and inguinal regions), with 0.5 mL administered per site. Following the challenge, observations were conducted over a period of 28 days. Blood samples were collected on days 7 and 28 post-challenge. On day 28, a necropsy was performed to collect lung tissue, hilar lymph nodes, and mediastinal lymph nodes. Detection of viral viremia and nucleic acids in tissues was conducted using PCV3 quantitative fluorescent PCR, while in situ hybridization was employed to detect PCV3 nucleic acids in tissues.

Immunogenicity assessment of PCV3 VLP in antibody-positive pigs

Five pigs with PCV3 ELISA blocking rates >80% and five with <80% were intramuscularly vaccinated with 100 μg per pig and boosted on day 21. Antibody detection followed specified collection intervals and methods.

Preparation of antibodies 2B5 and Fab fragments

BALB/c mice were immunized with PCV3 VLPs. Splenocytes were isolated from immunized mice with high antibody titers, and fused with SP2/0 cells induced by PEG, followed by screening in HAT medium. PCV3-specific hybridoma cells were initially screened by indirect ELISA using HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A4416; 1:5000) as the secondary antibody. The 2B5 monoclonal cell line was obtained by limiting dilution cloning. The cells were expanded and then intraperitoneally injected into mice, and ascites fluid was collected. The antibody was purified by protein G affinity chromatography, followed by purity verification and concentration determination. To obtain Fab fragments, the antibodies were digested with papain (Merck Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P4762) at a mass ratio of 1000:1 in a phosphate buffer containing 30 mM cysteine and 50 mM EDTA at 37 °C. The digestion reaction was quenched by the addition of 30 mM iodoacetamide. The resulting Fab fragments were further purified using MabSelect SuRe (Cytiva, Cat# 17543802) to remove Fc fragments and any residual full-length antibodies.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

The immune complexes were prepared by mixing viral particles with Fab at a molar ratio of 1:1.2 at 4 °C for 60 min. Aliquots (3 μL) of the immune complex or viral particles were applied to glow-discharged (20 mA for 60 s) holey carbon Quantifoil Cu grids (R2/1300 mesh, Quantifoil Micro Tools). The grids were blotted for 5 s at 100% relative humidity and 4 °C, then plunge-frozen in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher). Imaging was conducted on two transmission electron microscopes (TEM): a 300 kV Tecnai G2 F30 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), using SerialEM and EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the Tecnai G2 F30 TEM, images were captured using a Gatan K3 direct electron detector in 40-frame movie mode at a nominal magnification of 31,000×, achieving a physical pixel size of 1.0 Å (0.5 Å in super-resolution mode). Additional datasets were collected on the Titan Krios equipped with a Gatan K3 and a Gatan BioContinuum high-definition imaging filter, operating in zero-loss mode with a slit width of 20 eV, at a nominal magnification of 105,000x, achieving a physical pixel size of 0.85 Å (0.425 Å in super-resolution mode).

Image processing and 3D reconstruction

Motion correction and dose-weighting for all dose-fractionated movies were performed using Patch Motion Correction in cryoSPARC Live v4.7.1, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using Patch CTF Estimation in cryoSPARC Live v4.7.1. Low-quality micrographs were discarded prior to reconstruction. Subsequent reconstruction procedures (summarized in Supplementary Figs.) were carried out using cryoSPARC v4.7.151. In brief, particles were automatically picked using the “Blob picker” or “Template picker” and selected through several rounds of reference-free 2D classification. Selected high-quality particles underwent ab initio reconstruction, heterogeneous refinement, and non-uniform refinement. To enhance the quality of the cryo-EM density map in the flexible antibody interface region, we performed icosahedral symmetry expansion for each immune-complex particle in cryoSPARC v4.7.151, generating a total of 60 symmetry-expanded particles per immune-complex particle. A local mask encompassing only the capsid protein epitope region and the Fab variable region was then constructed. C1 local refinement (i.e., without imposing symmetry) was performed using this mask, with the fulcrum coordinates manually overridden to 163.707, 250.171, and 278.713 Å, defined at the centroid of the Fab variable-domain density (one binding site) to guide focused refinement. This refinement yielded a locally refined density map at an overall resolution of 2.02 Å, covering the capsid epitope region and the Fab variable region. Atomic model building and subsequent structural analyses were completed based on this locally refined density map. The final resolution was assessed using the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation method (threshold = 0.143 criterion52). Local resolution estimation was performed using the Local Resolution job in cryoSPARC v4.7.1. Density maps were enhanced using the DeepEMhancer job implemented in cryoSPARC v4.7.1 for visualization and model building, where indicated.

Model building, optimization, and 3D visualization

Initial models of PCV3 and 2B5 Fab were generated using the Alphafold353 software. Initial templates were fitted into the corresponding cryo-EM maps using ChimeraX54,55, followed by manual corrections and adjustments in Coot56 using real-space refinement. Further optimization of the generated models was performed in PHENIX57 using phenix.real_space_refine. An iterative cycle of manual model adjustment in Coot and real-space refinement in Phenix was conducted. The final atomic model was validated using MolProbity58,59. Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the cryo-EM data processing and optimization statistics. The buried surface area and interactions were analyzed using the PISA server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/) and the CCP460 program. Structural representations were generated using Chimera or ChimeraX. The electron density maps of the interaction regions in the PCV3-VLP:2B5 complex and the corresponding models are illustrated.

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software (Chicago, IL, USA) was used to perform statistical analysis. Repeated-measures one-way ANOVA with Geisser-Greenhouse correction where applicable, followed by two-sided Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, was used to compare post-immunization time points with baseline. Two-sided unpaired t tests were used to compare groups in the immunization experiments with pre-existing antibodies, with no correction for multiple comparisons. A two-sided paired Student’s t test was used to compare paired PCV2 and PCV3 kit responses for sera from PCV3-immunized pigs. P values, test statistics, degrees of freedom, effect size statistics and 95% confidence intervals are provided in the Source Data file where applicable.
