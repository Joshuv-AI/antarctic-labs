Anxiety, depression and psychological distress among small and medium-scale entrepreneurs in Lagos state, Southwest Nigeria
Authors: Mary Balogun, Anne Clayson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEntrepreneurs play a vital role in economic development but are exposed to significant psychosocial stressors, including financial uncertainty, long working hours, and job insecurity, which may adversely affect mental health. Evidence on anxiety, depression, and psychological distress among entrepreneurs in Nigeria remains limited. This study assessed the prevalence and predictors of anxiety, depression, and psychological distress among small and medium-scale entrepreneurs in Lagos State, Southwest Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 156 entrepreneurs registered with the National Association of Small and Medium Enterprises (NASME). Data were collected electronically using a structured questionnaire incorporating the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12), and a work-related stress scale based on the Job Demand–Control model. Descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to examine associations between socio-demographic and occupational factors and mental health outcomes.ResultsOverall,12.9% of respondents had anxiety,12.3% had depression, and 17.9% had psychological distress. High work-related stress was reported in 25.6% of participants, while 60.6% reported feeling stressed at work. Male entrepreneurs were less likely to have depression. (AOR 0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.54). Entrepreneurs who had dependents were more likely to have psychological distress (AOR = 4.50; 95% CI: 1.08–18.78). Good job control was protective against anxiety (AOR 0.17, 95% CI: 0.05–0.57), depression (AOR 0.10, 95% CI: 0.02–0.40), and psychological distress (AOR = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.04–0.40). High work-related stress significantly increased the odds of anxiety (AOR 3.43, 95% CI: 1.10–10.69) and psychological distress (AOR 5.33, 95% CI: 1.70–16.72). Working more than 40 h per week was protective against depression and psychological distress. ( AOR = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.08–0.73)ConclusionA substantial proportion of entrepreneurs in Southwest Nigeria experienced anxiety, depression, and psychological distress, largely associated with having dependents, high work-related stress and low job control while male entrepreneurs were less likely to have depression. These findings highlight the need for targeted mental health interventions, stress management support, and policies that promote healthier and more supportive work environments for entrepreneurs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28271-7.

Background

Entrepreneurs play a critical role in national economic development by stimulating innovation, creating employment opportunities, and driving industrial growth [1, 2]. Small and medium-scale enterprises form the backbone of economies, contributing substantially to gross domestic product and job creation [3. 4]. In Nigeria, SMEs account for about 90% of employment and over 70% of GDP, making them pivotal to economic resilience and poverty reduction [5, 6]. However, entrepreneurs often face unpredictable business environments, financial instability, long working hours, and high workloads that can lead to significant work-related stress [7, 8]. Persistent exposure to stress without adequate recovery can precipitate adverse physical and psychological outcomes including anxiety, depression, and burnout [9, 10]. The cumulative impact of these stressors extends beyond individual health to productivity losses, reduced innovation, and diminished business sustainability—posing a major public health and economic concern.

Several studies have examined mental health outcomes in different occupational groups; however, research focusing on entrepreneurs remains limited. Evidence suggests that entrepreneurs are prone to psychological strain due to the dual pressures of business survival and personal financial responsibility [11, 12]. Freeman et al. [13] found that 30% of entrepreneurs reported depression, 29% attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, 12% substance use disorder, and 11% bipolar disorder, with many experiencing co-occurring conditions. Similarly, Yu and Zhang [14] reported that almost one-third of young entrepreneurs in China suffered from anxiety and depression. Financial uncertainty, long working hours, and lack of work–life balance have been linked to increased mental distress among entrepreneurs [15, 16]. Studies in Europe and Asia indicate that entrepreneurs’ mental well-being influences firm innovation, decision-making, and overall performance [17]. Despite growing global evidence, African research remains sparse and predominantly limited to employees in formal sectors, with entrepreneurs—especially small and medium-scale business owners—often excluded [7].

The Nigerian business environment is characterized by “pervasive” stressors that hinder entrepreneurial development and well-being [18]. Entrepreneurs in Nigeria grapple with inadequate infrastructure, limited access to credit, high operational costs, and high-interest rate, inconsistent economic policies, multiple taxation, and “institutional bottlenecks” in loan applications [19, 20].

Lagos State is Nigeria’s economic hub and the largest city in Africa, with an estimated population exceeding 21 million [21, 22]. It contributes approximately 27% to Nigeria’s GDP and hosts over half of the country’s commercial activities [23–25]. Lagos also has the highest concentration of SMEs in Nigeria, employing over 5.5 million people [24, 26]. These characteristics make it an important setting for examining entrepreneurial experiences and outcomes.

Despite its economic opportunities, Lagos presents unique structural and environmental stressors. Entrepreneurs contend with unreliable electricity supply, severe traffic congestion, high population density, and elevated living and operational costs [19, 27–29]. These conditions increase the cost of doing business, reduce efficiency, and intensify work-related stress. The high-pressure and competitive environment further exposes entrepreneurs to significant psychological strain, potentially affecting their mental health outcomes.

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced additional severe economic and psychological strain for entrepreneurs globally and specifically within the Nigerian context [30]. The pandemic disrupted the interconnected world economy through global value chains, resulting in abrupt falls in commodity prices, travel restrictions, and frozen labor markets [31]. The negative impacts of the pandemic on Nigerian business activities included severe economic shocks, low production and sales, reduced investment, and critical cash flow difficulties [20]. The dwindling Nigerian economy caused widespread economic hardship for business owners, many of whom faced “cash traps” and a lack of essential supplies due to disrupted import chains [20, 32]. Consequently, entrepreneurs were forced to make difficult decisions regarding whether to continue or pause their operations, a process that contributed directly to heightened fear and stress [33]. The financial pressure and uncertainty caused by the health crisis were identified as primary drivers of mental health issues among entrepreneurs [34].

Little is known about the mental health status of small and medium-scale entrepreneurs in Nigeria, particularly in Lagos State, South-West Nigeria, which hosts a large concentration of micro and small enterprises. The few available local studies have focused on structural business challenges such as finance and infrastructure, with limited attention to psychological well-being; [35]. Given that entrepreneurship is integral to economic growth, understanding their mental health is vital for sustainable productivity. This study therefore assesses the mental health status of small and medium-scale entrepreneurs in Lagos State South-West Nigeria, examining the prevalence and predictors of anxiety, depression, and psychological distress. By addressing this knowledge gap, the study contributes novel data to guide evidence-based interventions and policies aimed at promoting workplace mental health within the informal and self-employed sectors of Nigeria’s economy.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted among small and medium-scale entrepreneurs in Lagos State, South-West Nigeria from October to December 2020. The research was part of a larger project aimed at investigating work-related stress and mental health among business owners. The target population comprised registered members of the National Association of Small and Medium Enterprises (NASME), which serves as the foremost umbrella organization for micro, small, and medium enterprises in Nigeria, providing a systematic and accessible sampling frame for studying the entrepreneurial population in Lagos State. The association facilitates networking, advocacy, and support for entrepreneurs, making it a suitable population for the study.

Inclusion criteria

Individuals must be registered members of NASME.

Entrepreneurs who had relocated to Lagos state within the previous six months. They must have owned their businesses for a minimum of three years to ensure a stable business environment. This time frame was selected to capture the long-term effects of entrepreneurship on mental health, as newer entrepreneurs may not have experienced the same pressures.

Exclusion criteria

Entrepreneurs who had relocated to Lagos state within the previous six months preceding the study were excluded to eliminate confounding variables related to recent changes in their business environment or personal circumstances.

Sampling method

A census approach of eligible NASME members with available contact details was attempted.

This technique allowed for the inclusion of all eligible participants, thereby increasing the representative of the sample. An electronic questionnaire was developed and distributed via email to participants using NASME’s database. The questionnaire was designed to be user-friendly and straightforward to encourage participation.

Data collection method

Permission was obtained from NASME to conduct the study among their members. An advert was sent through NASME to its members via email to inform them of the study and invite them to participate. Entrepreneurs willing to participate clicked on the link on the advert which directed them to the electronic questionnaire (Select Survey). The Participant Information Sheet (Supplementary File 1) and consent form (Supplementary File 1) were included in the electronic questionnaire. Entrepreneurs who have been Business owners for at least 3 years were included.

Entrepreneurs who have recently relocated to Lagos in the last 6months preceding the study were excluded. The study criteria was on the advert so that those who fulfil the criteria and willing to participate could do so.

Variables and measures

The primary dependent variables in this study were anxiety, depression, and psychological distress.

Anxiety and depression levels were measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale.

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is a 14-item self-report screening scale, developed by Zigmond and Snaith(1983) [36] in order to measure the presence and the severity of depression and anxiety among patients at general medical outpatient clinics. It contains two sub scales with 7 items each providing separate measures of anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D). This scale consists of 14 items, with seven items dedicated to each domain (anxiety and depression) [18, 19] Each item is rated on a four-point scale ranging from 0 to 3. Higher scores indicate higher levels of anxiety and depression, with possible scores for anxiety and depression each ranging from 0 to 21. The cutoff of 7/8 was recommended for each subscale to identify probable cases of clinically significant anxiety or depression (Zigmond and Snaith 1983) [17]. Specifically, three cutoff levels were identified: a score between 8 and 10 indicates a mild case, 11 and 14 a moderate case, and 15 or above a severe case [37]. The HADS was specifically developed to measure depression and anxiety states focusing on affective symptoms and minimizing the somatic symptoms such as dizziness, insomnia, and fatigue that are common among anxiety and depression patients but can also be related to physical disorders and thus are not specific to anxiety and depression. In this regard, the HADS is a relatively pure measure for reflecting psychological disturbances and is superior to other instruments that may inflate the rates of anxiety and depression. Hence, it is suitable to use in a non psychiatric setting [38].

The validity and reliability of the HADS have been demonstrated in various translated versions across culturally diverse groups [39]. A review of the 747 identified papers that used HADS in general adult population and various patient populations was performed to evaluate the psychometric properties and diagnostic efficacy of the HADS [40]. The study reported that most factor analyses demonstrated a two-factor model in good accordance for HADS-A and HADS-D.

Cronbach’s alpha for HADS-A varied from 0.68 to 0.93 (mean = 0.83) and for HADS-D, from 0.67 to 0.90 (mean = 0.82). The sensitivity and specificity for both HADS-A and HADS-D with the cutoff of 7/8 were frequently found to be in the range of 0.70 to 0.90 [41] During analysis, scores of 0–7 were categorised as No Anxiety or Depression while scores of 8 and above were categorized as Yes Anxiety or Depression.

While originally designed for hospital settings, this scale was developed to assess emotional disorder without confounding somatic symptoms [42, 43] and it has been extensively validated for use in various populations, including general populations and occupational groups such as employees [44–46]In Nigeria, HADS has demonstrated high sensitivity (85.0–98.5%) and specificity (86.5–91.1%) for community screening [47].

Psychological distress was assessed using the General Health Questionnaire developed by Goldberg and Blackwell in 1970 [48]. This instrument comprises 12 items that evaluate the participant’s self-reported mental health status over the past few weeks. A cut-off score of 3 or higher indicates the presence of psychological distress. The GHQ-12 is widely used in mental health research and has proven to be a reliable and valid tool for screening psychological distress across diverse populations [49–51] It is valued for its brevity and adaptability in various clinical and research settings [50].

Work-related stress was assessed using a validated set of items that evaluate perceived workload and control over work processes. These items were designed to capture the subjective experience of stress related to job demands and the degree of control entrepreneurs feel they have over their work environment. This measure of work-related stress is critical, as it directly relates to the mental health outcomes being studied. Work-related stress was measured using 10 items, which incorporated the Job-Demand-Control model, a highly influential framework in occupational stress research proposed by Karasek and further developed by Karasek and Theorell and Theorell and Karasek [52–55] The Job Demand–Control model, proposed by Karasek, is a foundational framework for understanding occupational stress by analyzing the interaction between job demands (workload, pace, and complexity) and job control (decision latitude and autonomy) [54, 56].The JDC model posits that job strain arises from the combination of high psychological job demands and low job decision latitude [33–35, 57–59]. In entrepreneurial and self-employed contexts, this model is frequently used to explain why business owners often maintain high levels of well-being despite exceptionally high work demands [17, 52]. While often used for employees, its components of “job demand” and “job control” are highly relevant to entrepreneurs who face high demands but often have high autonomy, creating a unique stress profile [54, 55, 60]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the 10-item work-related stress scale is 0.81.

A five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), was used to measure entrepreneur stress from job demand, with scores directly proportionate to the level of stress. The maximum stress score is 50. A score of < 35 is classified as a low stress level, while a score of ≥ 35 indicates a high stress level. Three questions assessed job control, giving a maximum score of 15. A score of ≥ 12 indicates good job control, and a score of < 12 indicates poor job control.

Additionally, one question was asked: “Do you believe you are stressed from your work?” Respondents answered Yes/No to determine reported work-related stress. Single-item measures for perceived stress have been shown to have content, criterion, and construct validity for group-level analysis and can be useful for expedient data collection in organizational contexts [61–65] While full scales provide detailed diagnostic information, single-item questions can offer a useful snapshot of perceived stress [64].

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25, a robust statistical software package widely used in social science research. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the socio-demographic and occupational characteristics of the participants. Continuous variables, such as age and years of engagement as an entrepreneur, were expressed as means ± standard deviation, while categorical variables, including gender, marital status, and educational attainment, were presented as frequencies and percentages. This descriptive approach provided a clear overview of the sample characteristics and facilitated the understanding of the population under study.

Univariate analyses were conducted to describe the distribution of key variables, allowing for an initial examination of the data. Following this, bivariate analyses were performed using Chi-square tests to explore associations between independent variables and mental health outcomes.

This step was essential for identifying potential relationships and patterns within the data, particularly concerning how various socio-demographic and occupational factors may correlate with anxiety, depression, and psychological distress.

To further elucidate the relationships between variables, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify factors independently associated with depression, anxiety, and psychological distress. We initially included depression, anxiety, and psychological distress in the same multivariable model; however, this approach resulted in over adjustment, evidenced by inflated odds ratios and wide confidence intervals, reflecting conceptual and statistical overlap among these mental health outcomes. Therefore, we fitted separate multivariable logistic regression models for each outcome variable and excluded the other mental health outcomes as covariates in the respective models.

In the depression model, we initially included both having children and having dependents as covariates. Because these variables exhibited high collinearity, we excluded having children from the model and retained having dependents to improve model stability and interpretability.

We applied the same set of covariates consistently across the models for anxiety and psychological distress.

We assessed multicollinearity among the independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The mean VIF was 4.51, indicating acceptable levels of multicollinearity. We reported adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, and we considered p values less than 0.05 statistically significant. The AOR, derived from the multivariable logistic regression model with all relevant covariates simultaneously adjusted, provides a more accurate and scientifically defensible estimate of the independent contribution of each predictor.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from both the University of Manchester Research Ethics Committee (2020/9277/13786) and the Health and Research Ethics Committee of the Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LREC/06/10/1524). Additionally, permission to conduct the study was secured from the executives of NASME Lagos chapter. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (for human subjects’ research) and the National Code for Health Research Ethics in Nigeria.

Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants prior to their participation in the study. This process included a detailed explanation of the study's purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of responses. Participants were assured that their data would be used solely for research purposes and that their anonymity would be maintained throughout the study. All participants were also informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without penalty.

Discussion

This study addresses a significant gap in the literature regarding the work conditions and mental health status of entrepreneurs, particularly in Lagos State, Nigeria. While entrepreneurship is a cornerstone of economic growth, the unique pressures faced by business owners often differentiate their experiences from those of their employees. This cross-sectional study provides Lagos-specific empirical evidence on entrepreneurial mental health within an occupational stress framework, addressing a notable paucity of such data in sub-Saharan Africa.

Prevalence of anxiety, depression and psychological distress

The study found a notable prevalence of mental health challenges among entrepreneurs in Lagos State, with one-tenth experiencing symptoms of anxiety and depression, and one-fifth reporting psychological distress. These findings align with broader research indicating that entrepreneurs often face higher risks of depression and anxiety [35, 21, 13, 17]. Historically, the Nigerian Survey of Mental Health and Well-being reported a much lower 12-month prevalence of 5.8% for any DSM-IV mental disorder [66]. While more recent estimates suggest a rising trend in the general population—with point prevalence rates cited at 10.65% [67] and general prevalence at 11.1% [68]—the rates identified in our cohort remain at the upper threshold or significantly exceed these national averages. This suggests that entrepreneurs are experiencing psychological strain at nearly double the baseline of the average Nigerian citizen. The mental health burden identified in this study is also substantial when compared to other high-stress sectors in the country. For example, while the Nigerian construction industry is recognized for significant mental health risks, the consistent 10–20% range for entrepreneurs suggests that self-employment in this region carries a risk profile that is as critical, if not more so, than intensive labor sectors [69].Such mental health issues can stem from financial constraints, work overload, and the constant fear of failure inherent in entrepreneurial ventures [70]. The observed work conditions, including long working hours and infrequent leave, further underscore the demanding nature of entrepreneurship, where full-time commitment is often essential for business success [60]. Our findings suggest that in the Southwest Nigerian context, entrepreneurship functions primarily as a risk factor. The “protective” benefits of autonomy are nullified by the necessity of navigating a volatile regulatory environment and the absence of a social safety net [71]. This study confirms the existence of a “dark side” of entrepreneurship in Lagos, where the psychological rewards of independence are overshadowed by extreme work overload and governance-induced stress, making it a more psychologically hazardous path than salaried employment in the same region [21, 71].

From a public health perspective, these results highlight the critical need for targeted interventions and support systems for entrepreneurs, as their mental well-being directly influences their decision-making, motivation, action [17], business performance, and, by extension, national economic stability [7, 72].

Factors associated with anxiety, depression and psychological distress

Sociodemographic factors

We observed that male respondents were less likely to have depression. This is consistent with global trends where females often report higher rates of depression [73–75]. While global data generally reports that depression is more prevalent in women than men [75], some entrepreneurial-specific studies during the pandemic (e.g., in Pakistan) found no significant difference between genders [76]. However, in Nigeria, the “burden of care” and the context of scarcity often fall disproportionately on women, potentially aggravating their mental health concerns [77]. In Nigeria, higher levels of depression was reported among older females [78] and among female entrepreneurs facing high household demands [22]. The prevalence of depression among female entrepreneurs may be exacerbated by the societal expectation that women often manage the “double burden” of household responsibilities and entrepreneurial endeavors [79].

Entrepeneurs who had dependants were more likely to experience psychological distress, aligning with previous findings that an increased number of dependents positively correlates with psychological distress among women household heads [22]. This finding highlights the compounded pressures faced by entrepreneurs who also bear significant familial responsibilities, particularly within socio-economic contexts where support systems may be limited [21]. In many African countries, a high dependency ratio means women entrepreneurs must rely on daily sales to fund the basic needs of multiple family members, which increases the experience of work-family conflict and poor mental health [80]. In studies across East Africa, having more than four children was significantly associated with psychological distress.

Occupational factors

An interesting finding from this study was the association between good job control and a reduced likelihood of anxiety, depression, and psychological distress. This suggests that the ability to manage one’s work autonomously can act as a crucial buffer against the negative mental health impacts typically associated with demanding work schedules [81, 82, 53, 54, 60].

Research has consistently shown that control over one’s work can mitigate stress, anxiety, and depression in demanding roles [83–86]. While traditional employment often focuses on the “high-strain” quadrant (high demands and low control), entrepreneurship is typically characterized as an “active job” proposition involving both high demands and high control [52, 56]. This high-demand/high-control configuration is expected to generate “eustress”—a positive form of stress that fosters a feeling of mastery and allows individuals to improve their work performance [87]. Research indicates that the high autonomy inherent in self-employment (the ability to decide what, when, and with whom to work) is positively related to mental well-being and acts as a buffer against the negative effects of intense effort and concentration [15, 17], the negative effect of emotional demands on burnout [45].

Conversely, high levels of work-related stress, were found to increase the likelihood of anxiety and psychological distress. Nigerian entrepreneurs experience high level of stress from high levels of risk they face, intense work loads, long work hours, being responsible for their company and employees, and operating under severe financial constraints. Entrepreneurs particularly those in Lagos State experience significant additional work-related stressors, from infrastructure deficit; providing their own electricity and water [26, 88], high cost of living, traffic congestion jams) [25, 28, 29], inflation and multiple taxation [22].

Entrepreneurs who worked more than 40 h per week were less likely to experience psychological distress, contradicting the general assumption that longer work hours universally contribute to poor mental health. However, this counterintuitive finding may be attributable to the perceived autonomy and job control inherent in entrepreneurship, which can mitigate the adverse effects of extended work periods by fostering a sense of accomplishment and purpose [13, 89, 52]. Additionally, this finding may be susceptible to reverse causality — whereby entrepreneurs experiencing psychological distress may be less able to sustain long working hours, such that those working extended hours represent a self-selected, psychologically healthier sub-group. Furthermore, survivorship bias may operate in this context: entrepreneurs who have remained in business long enough to be registered with NASME and to sustain long working hours may represent those who have successfully adapted to the demands of entrepreneurship and may therefore exhibit systematically lower distress compared with those who exited the sector prior to enrolment. Entrepreneurs often appraisal long hours as a “challenge stressor” rather than a threat [17]. Working long hours can be interpreted as a positive signal that the business is doing well, which can increase work satisfaction [17]. The Job Demand–Control model explains that high demands (like 40 + hour weeks) lead to “eustress”—a positive form of stress—when combined with high job control (autonomy) [52, 87]. For entrepreneurs, having the decision authority to choose how and when to work those hours mitigates the potential for burnout and mental exhaustion [15, 56]. Even when entrepreneurs have fewer “recovery experiences” (like detachment or relaxation) than employees, their high levels of mastery and control over their long hours can increase their overall well-being [15, 90].

Strengths and limitations

This study provides rare empirical data on the mental health of entrepreneurs in Nigeria, an understudied occupational group with growing economic importance. Validated instruments (HADS and GHQ-12) were used to assess anxiety, depression, and psychological distress, enhancing measurement reliability. The study incorporated work-related stress and job control measures, offering a more comprehensive understanding of occupational determinants of mental health. The use of multivariable logistic regression allowed the identification of independent predictors of mental health outcomes.

This study has some limitations inherent in its cross-sectional design, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships or the temporal sequence of associations between factors like work conditions and mental health outcomes [91–93] The reliance on self-reported data for mental health assessments also introduces a potential for reporting biases, as individuals may underreport symptoms due to social desirability or stigma [91, 93–95] While the response rate was comparable to previous Nigerian studies, the generalizability of findings may be affected by the specific sampling frame and potential underrepresentation of certain entrepreneurial segments [95]. Our sample was drawn from registered NASME members which likely represents entrepreneurs with higher social capital and business stability compared to the broader informal sector in Nigeria. In addition, the use of electronic questionnaire could have introduced digital literacy and socioeconomic bias inherent in electronic recruitment. Future research should consider longitudinal designs, objective measures where possible, and account for potential confounding variables like age, period, and cohort effects to provide a more comprehensive understanding of entrepreneurial mental health in Nigeria.

Conclusion

The findings of this study establish that entrepreneurs in Southwest Nigeria bear a disproportionate psychological burden, with morbidity rates for anxiety and depression that effectively double the national baseline of 5.8% to 11.1% [66, 68]. The mental health profile of these SME owners is defined by a complex interplay of protective and risk factors. In alignment with global occupational health models, high levels of autonomy and decision-making latitude serve as a primary protective factor, potentially mitigating the severity of symptoms for those with resilient business structures [13, 52]. Conversely, the “dark side” of entrepreneurship—characterized by extreme work overload, financial volatility, and governance-induced stress—functions as a potent risk factor that often overrides the benefits of autonomy [21, 71]. Gender and the number of dependents emerge as critical social determinants, with female entrepreneurs and those with high domestic responsibilities showing increased vulnerability to psychological distress due to the “double burden” of professional and household management.

Therefore, it is imperative to develop and implement targeted public health interventions and supportive policies that enhance mental health resources and foster resilience among entrepreneurs to ensure their continued contribution to economic development. We recommend that the government should prioritize the stabilization of the regulatory environment and infrastructure (power and transport) to reduce the “environmental tax” on mental health. Policy consistency can directly lower the allostatic load on entrepreneurs. NASME should facilitate the creation of “Entrepreneurial Resilience Circles”structured, peer-led support groups that provide a low-cost, stigma-reducing environment for owners to share coping strategies for occupational stress. Public health actors should utilize existing trade association meetings as touchpoints for routine, non-invasive mental health screenings, moving care closer to where entrepreneurs operate. A larger, multi-site studies to capture the diversity of Nigerian entrepreneurship is also recommended.
