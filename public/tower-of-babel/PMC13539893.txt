Diagnostic yield and feasibility of EBUS-guided cautery-assisted transbronchial forceps biopsy for suspected benign mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy: a prospective self-controlled pilot study
Authors: Changjian Lin, Hong Xue, Fan Zhou, Daxuan Wang, Lanfeng Li, Xiaohong Lin, Liping Huang, Baosong Xie, Xiaoqin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) has limited diagnostic yield in benign mediastinal lymphadenopathy. This study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of EBUS-guided cautery-assisted transbronchial forceps biopsy (EBUS-ca-TBFB), a technique that utilizes an electrocautery knife to create a tunneling tract at the prior TBNA site, for the diagnosis of benign mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy.MethodsIn this prospective, self-controlled cohort study, 14 adult patients with unexplained mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy clinically suspected to be benign after routine clinical, radiological, and available laboratory evaluation were consecutively enrolled between February 2023 and December 2024. Each patient underwent EBUS-TBNA followed by EBUS-ca-TBFB at the same target nodal station whenever feasible. The diagnostic yield, sensitivity, accuracy, and safety profiles of both techniques were compared.ResultsAt the patient level, the diagnostic yield of EBUS-ca-TBFB was significantly higher than that of EBUS-TBNA (85.7% [12/14] vs. 21.4% [3/14], P < 0.001). At the lesion level, yields were also higher with EBUS-ca-TBFB (81.3% [13/16] vs. 15.0% [3/20], P < 0.001). Sensitivity and overall accuracy were notably improved with EBUS-ca-TBFB (92.3% vs. 23.1% and 92.9% vs. 28.6%, respectively). No severe complications, such as significant bleeding, pneumothorax, or infection, were observed in any patient.ConclusionsIn this small prospective self-controlled pilot cohort, EBUS-ca-TBFB appeared to provide additional histological diagnostic value after conventional EBUS-TBNA in selected patients with suspected benign mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy. No severe procedure-related complications were observed. However, given the small sample size and single-center design, the diagnostic benefit and safety profile of this technique require confirmation in larger multicenter studies.

Background

Mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy is a common clinical finding. However, establishing a definitive etiology remains challenging, especially when tumor biomarkers are negative and a benign process is suspected. Procuring adequate tissue for histopathological assessment is crucial for ruling out malignancy, guiding therapy, and providing prognostic information, as pathology remains the gold standard for mediastinal disease diagnosis [1, 2].

Available sampling approaches include endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA), computed tomography–guided percutaneous biopsy, and surgical mediastinoscopy. Although mediastinoscopy yields ample, architecturally intact specimens, its invasiveness and complication risks preclude its routine first-line use [1, 3]. Percutaneous biopsy is constrained by anatomical factors; many mediastinal and hilar nodes lie deep and near vital structures such as the heart and great vessels, increasing the technical difficulty and procedural risk. EBUS-TBNA is widely adopted for lung cancer staging and the diagnosis of malignant disease, yet provides primarily cytologic specimens of limited volume, which restricts its diagnostic performance for benign entities, including sarcoidosis, infectious granulomatous disease, pneumoconiosis, IgG4-related disease, and Castleman disease [4–7]. To improve diagnostic yield, consensus guidelines recommend adjunctive transesophageal sampling as well as multistation approaches in conjunction with standard EBUS-TBNA [8–11].

Cautery-assisted transbronchial forceps biopsy (EBUS-ca-TBFB) has emerged as a promising technique for obtaining larger, structurally preserved specimens, thereby enhancing tissue diagnosis particularly in granulomatous disease, while maintaining an acceptable safety profile [12, 13]. Building on the techniques described by Bramley and Ray, we refined the cautery-assisted ‘window’ approach to further improve sampling success. In this prospective self-controlled pilot study, we enrolled fourteen adult patients with unexplained mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy that was clinically suspected to be benign after routine clinical, radiological, and available laboratory assessment. Patients with confirmed malignant disease, including lymphoma, were excluded from the present benign-disease analysis. Both EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-ca-TBFB were sequentially performed to evaluate the feasibility, diagnostic yield, and safety profile of EBUS-ca-TBFB as an adjunctive tissue-acquisition technique after conventional EBUS-TBNA.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This prospective, self-controlled pilot cohort study was conducted in the Department of Pulmonology, Fujian Provincial Hospital, between February 2023 and December 2024. Consecutive adult patients presenting with unexplained mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy were identified through radiology reports and specialist referrals and were subsequently screened for eligibility according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria before enrollment.

In this study, “suspected benign mediastinal or hilar disease” referred to unexplained mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy that was clinically considered more likely to be non-malignant after routine clinical, radiological, and available laboratory evaluation. Radiological assessment was mainly based on mediastinal-window chest CT findings, including lymph node size, morphology, density, distribution, presence of calcification, and preservation or absence of a fatty hilum when identifiable. Patients with radiological evidence of a primary lung tumor, peripheral pulmonary lesion suitable for biopsy, or systemic findings strongly suggestive of malignancy were not included in the present benign-disease analysis.

Available serum tumor biomarkers were reviewed when clinically indicated, but they were not required as a uniform mandatory panel for all patients. The assessed markers included one or more of the following: carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cytokeratin 19 fragment (CYFRA21-1), squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), pro-gastrin-releasing peptide (ProGRP), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), and carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9). No patient had available serum tumor biomarker results that strongly suggested malignant disease at the time of enrollment. Specifically, available serum tumor biomarker levels were within the normal range or below two times the upper limit of normal according to the institutional laboratory reference ranges.

The standard pre-bronchoscopy evaluation included a detailed medical history and physical examination, review of chest CT imaging, routine blood tests, coagulation profile, liver and renal function tests, electrocardiography, and assessment of cardiopulmonary and anesthesia-related procedural risks. Additional examinations, such as echocardiography, coronary CT angiography, vascular imaging, PET-CT, or infection-related tests, were performed when clinically indicated.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) at least one mediastinal or hilar lymph node with a short-axis diameter ≥ 10 mm on thin-slice chest CT; (3) absence of a definite peripheral pulmonary lesion suitable for percutaneous or bronchoscopic biopsy; (4) clinical suspicion of benign or non-malignant mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy requiring tissue diagnosis, such as sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, pneumoconiosis, or other granulomatous or inflammatory disease; and (5) completion of the standard pre-bronchoscopy evaluation.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) contraindications to bronchoscopy, including severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction, coagulation disorder, massive hemoptysis, or intolerance to anesthesia; (2) inability to localize the target lesion by EBUS after more than 20 min of exploration; (3) marked vascularity or close proximity to major vessels identified by EBUS or color Doppler, indicating a high procedural risk; and (4) confirmed malignant disease, including metastatic malignancy or lymphoma. Although lymphoma was considered in the initial differential diagnosis during screening, patients with confirmed lymphoma were excluded from the present benign-disease cohort.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Fuzhou University, Fuzhou, China (approval number: K2025-11–001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All participants were adults aged 18 years or older, and no minors were included in this study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures

For EBUS-TBNA, a 22-gauge EBUS-TBNA needle (NA-201SX-4022; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted through the working channel of the convex-probe EBUS bronchoscope (BF-UC260FW; Olympus) under real-time ultrasound guidance. After the needle was advanced into the lesion, approximately 20–30 to-and-fro movements were performed under continuous negative pressure, with the goal of obtaining three passes per target whenever technically feasible and clinically safe.

For EBUS-ca-TBFB, a high-frequency electrocautery needle knife (KD-31C-1; Olympus) was introduced through the EBUS bronchoscope working channel. The electrosurgical generator (Erbe VIO 300 D; Erbe Elektromedizin, Germany) was set to cutting mode at 40 W. At the prior EBUS-TBNA puncture site, a small longitudinal mucosal incision of approximately 2–3 mm was created under EBUS guidance. After the incision was created, the needle knife was advanced approximately 10–20 mm into the lesion under EBUS guidance to confirm entry into the target area, and was then withdrawn. Disposable tissue biopsy forceps (MTN-BF-18/12-A-C-1; Nanjing Micro-Tech, Nanjing, China; outer diameter, 1.8 mm) were then advanced through the cautery-assisted tract under combined direct endoscopic and ultrasound guidance to obtain tissue cores (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Lymph node biopsy using the electrocautery knife via EBUS-guided fenestration. The EBUS-ca-TBFB sampling protocol was standardized before the study. At least three forceps biopsy passes were performed for each target lesion whenever technically feasible and clinically safe, with the procedural goal of obtaining at least three visible tissue fragments, each with a long-axis diameter of approximately ≥ 3 mm, for histological processing. Additional forceps biopsies could be performed if the visible specimen volume was considered insufficient and the procedural risk remained acceptable.

Technical success of EBUS-ca-TBFB was defined as successful creation of the cautery-assisted tract, advancement of the biopsy forceps into the target lesion under endoscopic and/or EBUS guidance, and retrieval of visible macroscopic tissue cores suitable for histological processing.

After sampling, the biopsy site was inspected bronchoscopically for active bleeding. Minor bleeding was managed by observation, suction, and topical hemostatic measures such as cold saline or diluted epinephrine when needed. For persistent or moderate bleeding, bronchoscopic wedging or occlusion of the bleeding airway and repeated topical hemostatic measures were planned. For severe bleeding, escalation measures included balloon blockade, systemic hemostatic treatment, anesthesiology support, airway protection, interventional radiology or surgical consultation, and intensive care if necessary.

Definitions of sampling success, diagnostic yield, and final diagnosis

Sampling success and diagnostic yield were assessed separately. Sampling success was defined as the acquisition of visible specimen material suitable for pathological processing, irrespective of whether a specific diagnosis was ultimately established. For EBUS-TBNA, sampling success was defined as the retrieval of visible tissue fragments or cytological/cell-block material after needle aspiration; punctures yielding only blood, air, or acellular material were considered sampling failures. For EBUS-ca-TBFB, sampling success was defined as the retrieval of macroscopic tissue cores using biopsy forceps through the cautery-assisted tract. Diagnostic yield was defined as the proportion of patients or nodal stations in which the obtained specimen provided a specific pathological or etiological diagnosis consistent with the final clinical diagnosis. Rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) was not available and was not used during either EBUS-TBNA or EBUS-ca-TBFB. Therefore, intra-procedural specimen adequacy was assessed macroscopically by the operator and subsequently evaluated by routine pathological processing.

The final clinical diagnosis was established using a composite reference standard based on all available clinical information, including histopathological findings from EBUS-TBNA and/or EBUS-ca-TBFB, microbiological results such as tuberculosis culture when available, clinical and radiological features, treatment response, and at least 6 months of clinical-radiological follow-up. A diagnosis of sarcoidosis was based on compatible clinical-radiological findings, histological evidence of non-necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, and exclusion of tuberculosis, fungal infection, and malignancy. Tuberculosis was diagnosed based on necrotizing granulomatous inflammation and/or positive microbiological evidence, including tuberculosis culture when available, or compatible clinical response to anti-tuberculosis treatment. Cases with granulomatous inflammation that did not meet the criteria for sarcoidosis or tuberculosis during follow-up were classified as chronic granulomatous disease of unknown etiology. No additional tissue sampling by CT-guided biopsy, mediastinoscopy, or surgical biopsy was performed after the study procedures. One patient remained unclassified because a definitive diagnosis was not established and the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 20.0 (IBM Corp.). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Patient-level and nodal-station-level diagnostic yields were compared using McNemar’s test for paired data where appropriate, and Fisher’s exact test was used for unpaired comparisons. Sampling success rates were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy, were calculated using the final clinical diagnosis based on the composite reference standard. Because of the very small sample size and the limited number of non-diagnostic or unclassified cases, specificity and NPV were interpreted descriptively. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 21 patients with unexplained mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy were screened. Seven patients were excluded from the present benign-disease analysis because a malignant diagnosis was established, including metastatic malignancy and lymphoma. Among these excluded patients, 2 were diagnosed with lymphoma. The final analysis therefore included 14 adult patients with clinically suspected benign mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy.

The cohort included 7 men and 7 women, with a mean age of 47.64 ± 13.29 years (range, 24–74 years). Seven patients were symptomatic, including cough in five, dyspnea in one, and back pain in one, whereas the remaining seven patients were asymptomatic. All patients had unexplained mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy clinically suspected to be benign before bronchoscopy. Diagnostic performance at the patient level

Among the 14 enrolled patients, a final diagnosis was established in 13 patients using the composite reference standard described above, including histopathology, microbiological results when available, clinical-radiological features, treatment response, and follow-up. The final diagnoses included sarcoidosis in 4 patients, tuberculosis in 3, chronic granulomatous disease of unknown etiology in 4, inflammatory pseudotumor in 1, and pneumoconiosis in 1. One patient remained unclassified because the study procedures did not establish a definitive diagnosis and the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up; no additional tissue sampling was performed.

The diagnostic yield of EBUS-ca-TBFB was significantly higher than that of EBUS-TBNA at the patient level (85.7% [12/14] vs. 21.4% [3/14], P < 0.001). Among the 11 patients with nondiagnostic EBUS-TBNA results, EBUS-ca-TBFB established a specific diagnosis in 9 patients. Therefore, the absolute incremental diagnostic yield of EBUS-ca-TBFB over EBUS-TBNA was 64.3% (9/14) in the overall cohort and 81.8% (9/11) among patients with nondiagnostic EBUS-TBNA results. The combined yield of EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-ca-TBFB was 85.7% (12/14) and did not further improve upon EBUS-ca-TBFB alone.

Lesion-level diagnostic yield by nodal station

A total of 20 nodal stations were sampled by EBUS-TBNA, and 16 nodal stations were sampled by EBUS-ca-TBFB. Target lymph nodes were selected based on pre-procedural imaging, nodal size, radiological representativeness, accessibility, and procedural safety. When EBUS-ca-TBFB was performed, it was performed at the same target nodal station and through the prior EBUS-TBNA puncture tract whenever feasible. Most sampled lesions were station 7 lymph nodes. At station 7, the diagnostic yield was 14.3% (2/14) for EBUS-TBNA and 85.7% (12/14) for EBUS-ca-TBFB. For station 11Rs, the diagnostic yield was 33.3% (1/3) for EBUS-TBNA and 50.0% (1/2) for EBUS-ca-TBFB. EBUS-ca-TBFB was not performed in stations 4R or 11Ri. Because of the small number of nodal stations outside station 7, station-specific statistical comparisons were not performed. Overall lesion-level diagnostic yield was higher with EBUS-ca-TBFB than with EBUS-TBNA (81.3% [13/16] vs. 15.0% [3/20], P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Sampling success rate by nodal station

Sampling success was evaluated at the nodal-station level. EBUS-TBNA achieved a sampling success rate of 90.0% (18/20 stations), whereas EBUS-ca-TBFB achieved a sampling success rate of 100.0% (16/16 stations). This difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.19). The two EBUS-TBNA sampling failures yielded only blood or air despite repeated needle passes. In contrast, EBUS-ca-TBFB retrieved visible macroscopic tissue cores from all sampled stations. Sampling success was defined as acquisition of processable specimen material and was analyzed separately from diagnostic yield, which required a specific pathological or etiological diagnosis (Table 3).

Diagnostic performance metrics

Diagnostic performance metrics are summarized in Table 4. Using the final clinical diagnosis as the reference standard, sensitivity was markedly higher for EBUS-ca-TBFB than for EBUS-TBNA (92.3% vs 23.1%; P < 0.001). Both techniques demonstrated perfect specificity (100%). The negative predictive value (NPV) was higher with EBUS-ca-TBFB (50.0% vs 9.1%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.14). Consequently, the overall diagnostic accuracy of EBUS-ca-TBFB was significantly greater than that of EBUS-TBNA (92.9% vs. 28.6%; P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Procedure-related complications

No severe procedure-related complications, including life-threatening bleeding, pneumothorax requiring intervention, mediastinal infection, or mediastinal emphysema, were observed. Intraprocedural bleeding was the most common adverse event; all bleeding episodes were mild and resolved spontaneously or were controlled with local topical hemostatic measures. Post-procedural mild hemoptysis occurred in three patients (21.4%) and resolved within 48 h without additional intervention. One patient (7.1%) developed transient high-grade fever, which resolved within 48 h. One patient experienced transient vomiting during recovery from anesthesia, which subsided within 30 min.

Discussion

Mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy can result from both benign and malignant conditions [14]. In many regions, benign causes are mostly infectious (like tuberculosis), inflammatory (such as sarcoidosis), or occupational [15]. However, telling these apart is clinically challenging because their imaging and clinical features often overlap. This is particularly true in areas with a high burden of granulomatous diseases.

Getting a definitive pathological diagnosis for benign mediastinal nodes is notoriously difficult. The clinical diagnostic rate historically sits at only about 33% [12]. This is partly because benign conditions are often diagnoses of exclusion, requiring long-term follow-up to rule out cancer. Furthermore, diagnosing benign lesions requires more tissue. Pathologists need enough sample volume to evaluate granuloma morphology, necrosis, and inflammatory cell distribution, and to perform special stains. Unfortunately, standard biopsy tools often fail to retrieve enough tissue from these fibrotic or necrotic nodes. As a result, clinicians are frequently left with non-specific inflammatory findings.

EBUS-TBNA is currently the standard of care for sampling these nodes [16–19]. It performs exceptionally well for malignancies, with yields near 100% [12, 13]. However, for benign diseases, the yield drops drastically to 33%–62.7% [12, 13, 20]. The problem lies in the specimen type. Standard 21G or 22G needles provide cytological aspirates. These aspirates simply do not retain the structural architecture needed to diagnose benign, fibrotic lymph nodes. In fact, two cases in our own study yielded absolutely no viable tissue via standard needle aspiration.

The 21.4% diagnostic yield for EBUS-TBNA in our cohort might seem surprisingly low. However, this reflects our specific patient selection. We specifically enrolled patients with suspected benign, granulomatous, or fibrotic diseases. These lesions require intact histological architecture for diagnosis, not just cytology. We also did not have Rapid On-Site Evaluation (ROSE) available to guide our needle passes. Finally, we used strict diagnostic criteria: we classified non-specific lymphocytic inflammation as non-diagnostic to ensure our results reflected true clinical utility.

To get larger histological samples, cautery-assisted transbronchial forceps biopsy (EBUS-ca-TBFB) was developed. Early attempts at forceps biopsy relied on repeatedly puncturing the airway with a needle to create a tract, which often yielded small, fragmented samples. Later, Ray et al. showed that cautery-assisted methods better preserved tissue architecture in diseases like sarcoidosis [13]. Still, much of the previous literature included heavily mixed malignant cohorts, concluding that the "windowing" technique added little value for oncology patients. Our study is different because we focused strictly on benign scenarios. By using a high-frequency electrocautery knife for just 5 to 10 s, we widened the existing TBNA puncture site. This allowed the forceps to enter smoothly and provided a local coagulation effect to reduce bleeding. As a result, we achieved a 100% macroscopic sampling success rate.

EBUS-ca-TBFB replaces the cytological limitations of needle aspiration with core tissue biopsies. This preserves granuloma morphology and fibrosis patterns, which explains the high sensitivity (92.3%) and accuracy (92.9%) in our cohort. While other techniques like transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy are also emerging for benign lymphadenopathy [21, 22], EBUS-ca-TBFB is an easy, highly accessible extension of routine EBUS. The main technical challenge is simply tracking the initial needle tract through the limited endoscopic view so the forceps can be guided in accurately.

This study has several limitations. It is a small, single-center, self-controlled pilot study. This limits subgroup analysis and broad generalizability. Also, a single experienced operator performed all procedures. Thus, the safety and success rates might differ for clinicians still in their learning curve. We also excluded lymphoma patients—a group that heavily relies on core tissue biopsies—so we cannot extrapolate our findings to lymphoma workups. Finally, there is an inherent sequential bias. We performed the forceps biopsy through the tract just created by EBUS-TBNA. The needle aspiration likely loosened the target tissue, making the subsequent forceps biopsy easier. Therefore, we consider EBUS-ca-TBFB a complementary add-on to standard EBUS-TBNA, rather than a standalone alternative.

Conclusions

In conclusion, performing EBUS-ca-TBFB immediately after conventional EBUS-TBNA provides significant histological value for patients with suspected benign mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy. We observed no severe complications in this small pilot study. However, larger, (multicenter )trials are necessary to fully confirm its diagnostic benefits, evaluate the sequential order effect, and establish its safety profile.
