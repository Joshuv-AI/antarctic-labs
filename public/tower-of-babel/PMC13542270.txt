Enhancing mechanical performance, durability, and environmental impact assessment of green concrete incorporating industrial and agricultural wastes
Authors: Samia Parvez, Syed Shakil Afsar, Ibadur Rahman, Osama Khan, Zeinebou Yahya, Aiyeshah Alhodaib, Brahmdeo Yadav, Ashok Kumar Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study proposes a data-driven experimental decision framework to identify the most suitable sustainable supplementary materials for green concrete, aiming to reduce cement usage, industrial waste burden, and environmental impacts in the construction sector. It experimentally evaluates compressive strength, split tensile strength, flexural strength, and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) of green concrete incorporating waste materials including silica fume, GGBS, metakaolin, granite dust, rice husk ash, ceramic waste, marble powder, coconut shell powder, plastic waste, and bottom ash. A hybrid methodology integrating Pearson correlation, Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP), and k-means clustering was developed to capture complex interrelationships. Correlation-based dependency analysis was incorporated into AHP to generate objective performance weightages, where compressive strength was ranked highest (37%), followed by flexural strength (25%), UPV (22%), and split tensile strength (16%). K-means clustering then categorized materials into best and worst performance groups. The findings revealed silica fume as the most optimal and balanced material, achieving 48.5 MPa compressive strength, 4.0 MPa split tensile strength, 7.5 MPa flexural strength, and 4400 m/s UPV, indicating superior structural performance and durability potential. ANOVA confirmed strong statistical distinction between clusters (p < 0.0001), validating the robustness of the classification. The main contribution of this work lies in introducing a scalable machine-learning-assisted multi-criteria framework that objectively ranks sustainable cement replacement materials, enabling reliable selection for high-performance green concrete design.

Introduction

The ever growing and evolving population has put substantial load on the housing industry to build more residential and industrial structures for people to settle in. Primarily, the construction practices make use of concrete based materials which often gives rise to carbon-based emissions and higher energy utilisation1. In contrast, popularity for application of green concrete materials have displayed wide appreciation recently, since these resources offer improved practical solution while simultaneously reducing environmental burden without diminishing operational performance2. Application of renewable concrete materials attains twofold objective, where sustainability of the building is achieved while simultaneously producing cost effective projects for current population to reside in3. This eventually will aid the construction industry to build more environmentally stable, safer and affordable housing option for the future generations to choose from multiple available projects. This can be achieved by substituting conventional cement-based materials with supplementary cementitious materials4. Moreover, industrial residues in recent times have also been adopted as a substitute cement by-product which often has transpired into lower effluents produced during construction operations5. The hybrid mixture if optimised in correct combinations by making use of available regional options, results in improved strength, cost cutting possibilities with lower impact on the environment6.

One of the most feasible options for green concrete development, can be seen by making use of available waste materials. These materials contribute to the construction industry since it makes use of readily available regional waste products, while also providing a robust option for concrete consumption cement-based materials. On the other hand, readily available industrial waste can easily be procured during cement manufacturing operations. This eventually leads to mixed cement products, deeming it to be a feasible concrete option, leading to lower carbon deposits into the environment7. Such products further transpire into better workability and improved compressive strength of the mixture based concrete due to presence of fine-grained particles eventually leading to superior density and resilience8. These hybrid concrete mixtures may aid in waste management practice through the incorporation of supplementary industrial residues. Moreover, likelihood of choosing green materials with higher silica content (agricultural residues) eventually improves the structural integrity and mechanical toughness of the finished product9. Furthermore, such concrete materials deliver outstanding thermal insulating performance with reduced weight benefits10.

The green materials vary substantially in terms of physical properties which directly have an immediate effect on the outcomes of the tested cement samples11. Being inherently multi-dimensional these properties often exhibit an extremely inter-connected characteristics which conflict with the outcome behaviour of the finished product12. This inter-dependence among properties and outcomes of the product may require extensive hybrid algorithms since conventional methods fail to capture such complex patterns which have opposing behavioural patterns13. Modern combined tools such as correlation, priority and machine learning algorithms are being extensively used together in order to understand the behaviour of such complex-mix materials while simultaneously comparing them for best performance14. On the other hand, clustering algorithms in machine learning uncovers root patterns and commonalities across such different materials15. The combined method efficiently manages competing objectives while minimizing subjective bias for different material application in concrete based alternatives16.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on utilizing industrial and agricultural waste materials in green concrete to enhance strength, durability, and sustainability, while also employing advanced characterization and decision-making tools for optimal material selection. Zhang et al.17 developed a green concrete by integrating eco-cement clinkered at 1100 °C and up to 50% Bottom Ash–Municipal Solid Waste (BA-MSW) aggregates. Carbonation curing techniques improved early strength development to reach 18.5 MPa at 28 days. The tests demonstrated environmental safety alongside financial projections estimating $3.8 million/year from 3.8 million CMU production. Alterary et al.18 studied fly ash characteristics and evaluation methods as well as its prospective uses in wastewater management alongside construction work. The combination of XRD, fluorescence together with SEM-EDS characterization revealed that fly ash nanoparticulate enhances concrete durability through minimized pores and resistant structure. Reshma et al.19 assessed M40 grade concrete properties respond to cement replacement by fly ash at 30% mixed with varying amounts (0–40%) of untreated waste foundry sand. The combination of fresh and hardened tensile, compressive and flexural tests demonstrated that fly ash produced outstanding results especially at days 28 and 90 during curing. Yoris-Nobilea et al.20 compared low-clinker cement and geopolymer mortars for 3D printing using mechanical/rheological tests, LCA, and MCDM ranking, concluding that low-clinker cement mortars perform best due to lower cost and environmental impact, while geopolymer mortars showed higher impact. Ahmed et al.21 applied a fuzzy TOPSIS-based MCDM model to select dominant sustainable concrete mix factors and the best mix design method, finding water/cement ratio and density as most influential, and identifying the DOE method as the most suitable for sustainable concrete quality management.

The present research gap emerges since seldom studies exists in relation to the durability characteristics of green concrete technologies during extended periods. Most research about are broadly focussed on sustainable concrete mixtures made with alternative cementitious materials including fly ash has mainly concentrated on early-age properties while ignoring durability and strength development. Studies investigating the performance of waste material-based concrete remain minimal because property interaction complexities lead to major gaps in understanding green concrete reliability. The key novelty of this study lies in comparing diverse agricultural-industrial waste materials with fundamentally different chemical reactivity and microstructural mechanisms. Unlike conventional green concrete studies that report isolated strength improvements, this work establishes a statistically validated material-selection pathway by linking interdependent mechanical and durability outcomes to microstructural densification behavior, enabling reliable identification of best-performing waste blends under realistic replacement thresholds. Materials and methods

The contemporary study comprises of finding the perfect sustainable alternative for concrete material which not is strong enough but also simultaneously is cost-effective. Thus, it is essential to understand the properties of the identified material based on their favourable characteristics which formed the basis behind their selection criteria for reasonable alternative materials as concrete in building materials. The physio-chemical properties of these materials impact the hydration behaviour, interfacial connection, strength expansion, and enduring durability.

Suitability and relevance of the selected waste materials

In any study to be successful it is important to select those materials which aid in predicting the suitability of the experimental analysis. For the current study, green and industrial waste materials are chosen for the analysis, primarily comprising of rice husk ash, silica fume, GGBS (Ground Granulated Blast Furnace Slag), ceramic waste, granite dust, coconut shell powder, plastic waste, marble powder, and bottom ash as shown in Fig. 1.

On the other hand, farming residues comprise of higher amount of silica content with lighter weight properties, eventually leading to superior thermal efficiency with reduced mass density. Furthermore, demolition-based materials during building operations are also considered for comparison since these materials comprise of fine particles size along with adding hardness to the cementitious materials. This makes them a viable candidate for natural-based finer grade aggregates while alleviating sand scarcity. The clustering dendrogram diagram in Fig. 1 is generated through Table 1 for a quantitative comparative analysis of material property data, where materials exhibiting with similar physio-chemical characteristics are branched together in the dendrogram grouping.

Highly reactive and silica-rich constituents more like silica fume and metakaolin are branched together. This is due to their higher pozzolanic activity. This in turn influences the strength and durability of the finished product. Other construction wastes such as Granite dust and GGBS also align in an adjacent cluster since both comprise of superior density, reasonable calcium-content, and contribute to matrix densification. Furthermore, specific constituents more like rice husk ash, marble powder, ceramic waste, and bottom ash are organized together as they have comparable silica content and moderate reactivity.

Both agricultural (rice husk ash, coconut shell powder) and industrial wastes (silica fume, GGBS, marble powder, bottom ash, ceramic waste, granite dust, plastic waste) were selected due to their high availability and major contribution to landfill and pollution reduction. Rice husk ash and silica fume provide high SiO₂ content and pozzolanic reactivity, improving strength and durability through pore refinement. GGBS and bottom ash enhance long-term hydration and reduce heat of hydration, while granite dust, marble powder, and ceramic waste improve packing density and reduce natural sand consumption. Plastic waste and coconut shell powder were included to evaluate lightweight and ductility effects, offering sustainability-driven alternatives for energy-efficient and low-density construction applications.

Mix proportioning and material composition

In this study, sustainable concrete combinations were considered on the basis of representative magnitudes frequently adopted in M40-grade concrete to guarantee practical feasibility and conformity with on-site practices. For the above materials, a blend of constituents is prepared which carries a fixed amount of 25% fly ash by weight, which remains constant for all materials. This amount is widely regarded as the stable concentration in line with earlier studies, since it imparts a balance for reporting toughness, resilience with reduced carbon content to the finished mix24. Moreover, it has also been established in available literature that this range guarantees a good pozzolanic reaction, simultaneously upkeeping the early strength values of the prepared concrete mixture. Furthermore, the hydro-cement percentage along with superplasticizer content in the cement mixture were optimised to a constant value for all materials.

The green materials chosen as concrete replacement were incorporated at 10% by weight initially as mixture in cementitious blend. The amount of green material is specifically chosen as it represents a practical value which improves the particle packing and filler-based effects. This further transpires into effective toughness and resilience in the final product. The 10% replacement level was selected as an optimal baseline dosage widely reported in literature where waste materials enhance particle packing and pozzolanic contribution without significantly reducing workability or early-age strength22. Practically, this level ensures uniform dispersion and stable hydration kinetics, making it an ideal reference point for comparing performance trends across higher replacement percentages23. In contrast higher mix values restricts the workability of the mix, eventually resulting in lower strength. Moreover, the cementitious mixture prepared are in line with the global standards as specified by the compliance established in the IS 10,262 and IS 456 guidelines24.

The M40 mix design used OPC 53 grade cement content of 400 kg/m³ with a fixed w/b ratio of 0.40, Zone II River sand and 20 mm graded coarse aggregates, and a PCE-based superplasticizer dosage of 0.8% by binder weight. All specimens were water-cured at 27 ± 2 °C and tested at standard curing ages (7 and 28 days) as per IS/ASTM provisions. The 10% replacement level was adopted initially as a standardized baseline to ensure fair inter-material comparison, as it lies within the commonly reported optimum range where most SCMs enhance packing density and pozzolanic contribution without causing workability loss or early-age strength reduction. Fly ash was fixed at 25% because its slower reactivity requires higher dosage to generate measurable long-term strength and durability benefits, and this level is widely accepted in M40-grade blended concrete practice for improving sustainability and reducing clinker content.

Equipment setup and testing procedure

In experimental analysis involving green material testing for concrete based alternatives, it is essential to identify and select only those equipment’s which play a vital role in the preparation and testing procedure. The combined machines for the analysis must ensure uniform preparation of the blend mixture, while precisely incorporating the compositions of constituents in the concrete based green mixtures. Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) 53 grade conforming to IS 12,269 was used; fine aggregate was Zone II River sand (specific gravity 2.62) and coarse aggregate was crushed stone of 20 mm maximum size (specific gravity 2.70). The water–binder ratio was fixed at 0.40 and a polycarboxylate ether (PCE)-based superplasticizer was used at 0.8% of binder weight. Standard specimens of 150 × 150 × 150 mm cubes, 150 × 300 mm cylinders, and 100 × 100 × 500 mm prisms were cast, with three samples tested per mix and the average value reported. To remove any inherent moisture left in the blend mixture, large drying ovens are applied which eliminate any left-over water content, thereby increasing it overall workability and final strength. Furthermore, sieving and grinding machines handle the particle sizing of the mix with added focus on surface fineness. This in turn monitors the material packing density, pozzolanic response, and interfacial cohesion with the cement-based final product.

Moreover, an electronic weighing machine is also used in the analysis which aids in checking the precise repeatability with reduced inherent batch-based errors. These weights are initially checked, right after the mixture is uniformly prepared in drum-based mixers, thereby ensuring even dispersion of fine, low-density of green materials. This in turn is responsible for avoiding any kind of segregation, ensuring uniform dispersion and a homogeneous green cementitious mixture. Nevertheless, the mixture of concretes produced are analysed by employing the slump cone and compaction factor setup. This ensures a good evaluation of workability and internal friction within the prepared samples. This in turn are considered extremely essential for fine grade particles which might be slightly substituted by alternative materials. In the end, a controlled hydro curing process is maintained at 27 ± 2 °C, so as to encourage unvarying hydration and pozzolanic reactions, predominantly significant for fly ash and silica comprising materials. The slump test results ranged from 95 mm (control mix) to 80 mm (25% waste replacement mix), indicating a moderate reduction in workability with increasing fine waste content. Similarly, the compaction factor decreased slightly from 0.91 to 0.87, confirming increased internal friction and reduced flowability at higher replacement levels.

As shown in the experimental setup Fig. 2, fresh concrete workability was evaluated using the slump cone apparatus and compaction factor test in accordance with IS 1199:2018 (ASTM C143 for slump), ensuring consistency in mix handling prior to casting. Specimens were prepared using standard cube molds (150 × 150 × 150 mm), cylindrical molds (150 × 300 mm), and beam molds (100 × 100 × 500 mm), followed by curing in a water curing tank maintained at 27 ± 2 °C. Compressive strength was determined using a Compression Testing Machine (CTM) by applying axial load until failure as per IS 516:2021 (ASTM C39/C39M), while split tensile strength was measured using the split tensile setup by diametrical loading of cylindrical specimens according to IS 5816:1999 (ASTM C496/C496M). Flexural strength was evaluated using a flexural testing rig under standard bending configuration following IS 516:2021 (ASTM C78/C78M). Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) was measured using a UPV tester with transducers placed on opposite faces of the specimen to compute pulse velocity as per IS 13,311 (Part 1):1992 (ASTM C597), providing a non-destructive assessment of concrete homogeneity and internal quality.

Outcomes estimation and their significance

Experimental testing primary data is used as foundation for objective material evaluation where Pearson correlation analysis is then applied to quantify the statistical interdependence among these outcomes, capturing property influences on microstructural mechanisms. These quantified relationships are integrated into AHP to generate rational priority weights, ensuring that outcome importance is derived from measured dependency strength rather than subjective expert assumptions. The slump values of the prepared composite mixes ranged from 78 to 92 mm, while the compaction factor varied from 0.85 to 0.91, indicating medium workability suitable for M40 concrete casting. Metakaolin and silica fume showed lower slump values of 78 mm and 80 mm, respectively, due to their very fine particle size and high specific surface area, which increase water demand and internal friction. GGBS, marble powder, and plastic waste retained comparatively higher workability because of smoother particle texture or lower water absorption, whereas coconut shell powder and bottom ash showed reduced compaction factor due to porous particle characteristics. These results confirm that although all mixes remained workable under a fixed w/b ratio of 0.40 and 0.8% PCE superplasticizer.

The coefficient of variation values ranged from 1.70% to 4.36%, remaining below the 5% acceptability threshold for controlled concrete testing. This confirms that the experimental values used for Pearson correlation, AHP weight determination, and k-means clustering were repeatable and statistically reliable. Therefore, the hybrid decision framework is developed using experimentally consistent mean values rather than isolated single-point observations.

Pearson-r combined AHP priority procedure

Pearson-r is a superior tool because it provides a unit-free, standardized measure of linear association between two continuous variables, making relationships easy to compare across datasets39. It simultaneously captures both direction (positive/negative) and strength of the relationship in a single coefficient. It is invariant to changes in origin and scale (shifts and linear rescaling of variables), allowing robust comparison of correlations even when variables are measured in different units40. Integrating Pearson r into AHP makes the weights more objective by linking each criterion’s weight to its measured linear correlation with the desired outcome. This in turn aids in reducing any inherent bias within the dataset, eventually improving precision, thereby facilitating higher variance in the dataset receiving higher priority41. Moreover, the criteria-based correlation aids in detecting the actual conflicting pattern which is often over looked in priority analysis. The Pearson correlation matrix provides numerical r-values indicating how strongly each outcome influences and aligns with others, and these values are transformed into relative dominance ratios for AHP pairwise comparisons. Outcomes showing consistently higher correlation magnitude with the remaining parameters are assigned higher preference scores in the comparison matrix using normalized r-based scaling. This converts statistical dependency into structured AHP judgments, enabling weight generation based on quantified interrelationship strength rather than expert-based assumptions.

This eventually helps in detecting the individual contribution of multi-objective optimisation process of ensuring better priority values42. It further sets priority values to the pairwise comparison matrices which eventually guarantees. This makes AHP superior to conventional weighting methods for complex, multi-criteria material selection problems. The AHP-derived priority weights were incorporated by performing min–max normalization of all outcomes followed by multiplying each normalized feature by its corresponding AHP weight to generate a weighted dataset. k-means clustering was then executed using weighted Euclidean distance, ensuring that higher-priority outcomes had a stronger influence on centroid computation and cluster formation.

k-means clustering machine learning procedure

In complex analysis, comprising of comparison among materials with intricate properties, modern machine learning algorithms are often employed, since these techniques are capable of grouping datasets based on their outcome performances. This is often overlooked in simple clustering procedures, thereby increasing chances of bias and unreliable results. In particular k-means method is an unsupervised algorithm which divides the datasets into k sets data by reducing any inter-cluster variance while simultaneously increasing the inter-cluster separation43. It is specifically used over other algorithms due to its ability to clearly cluster intricate characteristics-based materials into best-worst clusters based on the priority of final cluster centroids44. In comparison to other rule or ranking only algorithms, this technique establishes its dominance since it is able to effectively identifies latent performance trends in multi-characteristics type studies. The AHP weights were applied by multiplying each normalized outcome feature by its weight, and the initial centroid coordinates were computed as weighted mean vectors of the selected seed points in this weighted feature space. The stopping criterion was convergence based on centroid shift, where iterations stopped when the centroid movement fell below 10⁻⁴ or when the maximum limit of 300 iterations was reached. In Fig. 6, the best and worst material clusters are well defined in the reduced PCA space where Granite Dust, Metakaolin, Silica Fume and GGBS are located in the best-performing cluster. This shows consistently good combined mechanical and durability results. The close space clustering and positive PC1 control (99.35% contribution of variance) verify the fact that these materials are influential in the chief driving variables of the performance. Among them, silica fume has the smallest Euclidean distance to the cluster centroid, which indicates the most balanced and stable improvement in all considered properties with a minimum dispersion, and the other material has the relatively large centroid distances, which depicts the relatively more variability in their multi-response performance.

The underperformance of Cluster 1 materials, can be attributed to their weaker contribution to cement hydration and less effective pore refinement compared with reactive SCMs; therefore, it does not participate in secondary C–S–H gel formation and tends to create a weak interfacial transition zone between the plastic particle surface and cement paste. This weak ITZ restricts stress transfer under compressive, tensile, and flexural loading, explaining the lower values observed for plastic waste concrete. The hydrophobic and smooth surface of plastic particles also reduces mechanical interlocking with hydration products, which increases the probability of debonding and microcrack initiation under load.

Coconut shell concrete also remained in Cluster 1 because its porous organic structure and high water absorption reduce the effective water available near the paste–particle interface, producing a weaker ITZ and lower stress-transfer efficiency. This explains its negative gain in compressive strength (ΔCS = − 14.29%) relative to the control mix, despite its lightweight sustainability benefit45. Bottom ash also showed underperformance (ΔCS = − 11.90%) because its porous and irregular particles increase water demand and limit early matrix densification when used without sufficient grinding or activation46. Moreover, compared with reactive SCMs such as silica fume or metakaolin, bottom ash has lower early pozzolanic contribution, resulting in reduced secondary C–S–H formation and weaker strength development47.

Overall, the poor performance of Cluster 1 is not merely a statistical outcome but reflects a clear physical mechanism: low pozzolanic reactivity, poor surface compatibility with cement paste, higher water absorption, weaker ITZ formation, and limited secondary hydration product development. In contrast, Cluster 2 materials such as silica fume, metakaolin, GGBS improve concrete behavior through pore filling, pozzolanic or hydraulic reactivity, and enhanced matrix densification. Therefore, the clustering framework is physically meaningful because the best and worst clusters correspond to distinct cementitious mechanisms rather than only numerical similarity.

To validate the robustness of the proposed Pearson–AHP–k-means framework, cluster quality evaluation was performed using the silhouette score, which confirmed strong separation and compactness of the formed best and worst material groups. The silhouette score obtained for the clustering process was found to be 0.71, which lies within the accepted range of 0.60–1.00, indicating strong intra-cluster compactness and high inter-cluster separation. Additionally, the Davies–Bouldin index was observed to be low (DB = 0.38), confirming minimal overlap between clusters, while the Calinski–Harabasz score was significantly high (CH = 412.6), reflecting strong cluster discrimination. These values collectively confirm that the materials classified in the best cluster share highly similar weighted performance characteristics, while the worst cluster remains distinctly separated. Furthermore, the clustering behavior aligns with engineering performance thresholds (such as high UPV values representing dense and durable concrete), confirming that the classification falls within desired structural quality limits.

The inclusion of the control M40 mix enables direct assessment of the effect of each waste material on concrete performance. Compared with the control mix, silica fume produced the highest improvement, with gains of 15.48% in compressive strength, 11.11% in split tensile strength, 20.97% in flexural strength, and 11.39% in UPV, confirming its superior strength–durability balance. Metakaolin, GGBS, and granite dust also showed positive gains across most outcomes, supporting their placement in the best-performing cluster. Thus, the relative gain analysis validates that the proposed clustering framework is physically meaningful and not based only on absolute material values.

Discussion

To strengthen statistical rigor, all experimental outcomes were reported as mean ± standard deviation based on three replicate specimens per mix, and the coefficient of variation was calculated to verify repeatability. The CV values remained below the acceptable limit of 5%, confirming low experimental scatter and reliable measurement consistency. In addition, the clustering output was statistically validated using ANOVA, where all outcomes showed significant between-cluster separation with p < 0.0001, confirming that the best–worst grouping was not random. Cluster validity was further supported through a silhouette score of 0.71, Davies–Bouldin index of 0.38, and Calinski–Harabasz score of 412.6, indicating compact, well-separated clusters.

Silica fume demonstrated the most favourable combination of mechanical and durability properties among the tested materials as shown in Fig. 7. It has great load bearing capacity evidenced by the highest compressive strength of 48.5 MPa, surpassing all the tested materials. The split tensile strength of 4.0 MPa supports superior cracking resistance when under tensile stress thus assuring long-term structural stability48. The material exhibits outstanding bending strength pushing forces because it has flexural strength of 7.5 MPa to use in structural purposes in beams and slabs. The characteristics of silica fume make it dependable in meeting the safety level coupled with the performance of construction. Besides, the silica fume is strong material because of its outstanding ultrasonic pulse velocity level at 4400 m/s that exceeds the rest of the materials that have been tested. Structures benefit from optimal mass distribution and enhanced material quality which delivers prolonged longevity especially during demanding climatic situations49.

Silica fume produces a highly refined microstructure due to its ultrafine particles, which fill capillary pores and significantly densify the cement matrix and interfacial transition zone (ITZ). Its high amorphous SiO₂ content promotes strong pozzolanic reaction, generating additional secondary C–S–H gel that improves bonding and reduces microcrack propagation. Compared to other mixes, this pore refinement and enhanced ITZ continuity result in superior strength gain and higher UPV, confirming silica fume as the most dominant and durable green concrete material. Moreover, the uncertainty analysis indicated a low coefficient of variation (COV ≈ 3.8%), which is within acceptable concrete testing limits (< 10%), confirming stable experimental reliability and statistically consistent model predictions50.

Conclusions

Following the increasing threat of environmental pollution in connection to unethical practices of construction activities, the current study performs an analysis on construction green concrete materials using sustainable waste as its binding material. The research incorporates alternative industrial and agricultural waste by-products in concrete, along with the evaluation of their behaviour using modern analytical tools. This is of great importance to fill the gap in achieving the desired structurally beneficial outcomes, eventually reducing carbon emissions, preserving natural resources, while addressing the problem of waste disposal. In addition, the research explores the sustainability aspect also through the circuital economy approaches by utilising waste material generated in construction activities, eventually producing a robust, environmentally efficient long-lasting concrete system. In order to adapt a multi-dimensional and interdependent approach to green concrete performance, this study adopts a hybrid Pearson-AHP-k-means machine learning approach. The relationships are then inputted into AHP converting them into rational priority weight that provides the opportunity to focus on important outcomes based on their impact. Although the proposed Pearson–AHP–k-means framework provides a statistically supported approach for selecting sustainable concrete materials, the study is limited to a fixed M40 mix design, selected replacement levels, and a specific group of agricultural and industrial waste materials. The durability assessment is based mainly on strength-related outcomes and UPV, while direct long-term durability tests such as chloride penetration, carbonation, sulfate resistance, sorptivity, and freeze–thaw performance were not included. Therefore, future studies should expand the dataset, include region-specific material availability and cost, conduct detailed microstructural and durability testing, and perform full cradle-to-grave LCA to improve field-level generalizability.
