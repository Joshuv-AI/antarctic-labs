Clonal evolution and mutational trajectories of metastatic colorectal cancer shaped by anticancer therapies
Authors: Won Hee Lee, Bun Kim, Chang Hyun Nam, Hyun Yang Yeo, Dong Woon Lee, Sung Chan Park, Jae Hwan Oh, Sung-Sik Han, Myong Cheol Lim, Hail Kim, Young Seok Ju, Hee Jin Chang, Yongjun Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evolutionary dynamics of cancer genomes at single-cell resolution under therapeutic pressure remain elusive. Hence, we perform whole-genome sequencing of 58 single-cell-derived tumoroids and 18 matched bulk tumors from six patients with metastatic colorectal cancer. High-resolution phylogenies reveal the clonal evolution of cancer with quantitative and qualitative profiling of therapy-associated mutations at the single-cell level. We uncover pronounced inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity in burden and spectrum of chemotherapy-induced mutations, with preferential enrichment in more proliferative lineages, highlighting differential mutagenic effects across co-existing cancer cell populations. Late-stage mutational trajectories uncover strikingly dynamic genomic alterations, including complex structural variants, extrachromosomal DNA amplifications, and LINE-1 retrotranspositions, often involving alterations in cancer driver genes. Together, we show the clonal architecture and mutational landscapes of advanced cancers under therapeutic pressure, providing an important foundation for future studies of late-stage cancer evolution under therapies.Subject terms: Cancer genomics, Colorectal cancer, Phylogenetics

Introduction

Metastatic colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality1. Although various studies have mapped the mutational landscape2 and intra-tumoral genetic heterogeneity3–6, the late-stage clonal evolution, particularly in the relapsed and metastatic regions shaped by therapeutic pressure, remains poorly understood. Selective pressures, such as anticancer therapies and specific organotypic microenvironments, may foster subclones harboring specific late-stage mutations7–9, which ultimately drive cancer-related mortality. Many previous studies have relied on bulk-tumor tissue sequencing10–12, which provides only tissue-level resolution and thus fails to capture the majority of late-stage subclonal mutations restricted to a small number of cancer cells and clonal trajectories during cancer progression. Single-cell whole-genome DNA sequencing has the potential to elucidate the late-stage mutations, but these technologies are still suboptimal, with incomplete genome coverage and substantial error rates across most mutation types, including point mutations and genomic rearrangements13,14.

Recent advances in organoid culture techniques have enabled single-cell resolution analysis of intra-tumoral genomic heterogeneity3. By clonally expanding multiple individual cancer cells followed by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of the clones, it is now possible to sensitively and precisely detect late-stage mutations and trace the dynamics of subclonal lineages during anticancer therapies.

Here, using shared somatic mutations as endogenous barcodes, we reconstruct subclonal evolutionary trees, allowing for the temporal decomposition of mutations throughout the trajectory of subclonal evolution. Applying the approach, we reveal late-stage mutations and the trajectories of advanced cancer clones in six metastatic CRC patients, with WGS of 58 clonal tumor organoids (tumoroids), 18 bulk-tumor tissues (8 primary and 10 metastatic), and 6 matched normal (blood) at a mean depth of 51x (Fig. 1a). Each tumor specimen is divided into two parts, to conduct both bulk-tissue sequencing and tumoroid establishment (Methods15). Metastatic tissues are collected from the liver (all patients) and, if available, from other organs, including the peritoneum, ovary, and lymph node (Supplementary Data 1). Of the 76 tumor samples, 10 tissues and 46 tumoroids are post-treatment through FOLFOX (5-fluorouracil, folinic acid, and oxaliplatin; 6 tissues and 27 tumoroids), FOLFIRI (5-fluorouracil, folinic acid, and irinotecan; 6 tissues and 34 tumoroids), and/or radiation therapy (1 tissue and 7 tumoroids).

Results

Somatic mutations and reconstruction of clonal phylogenies

Per tumor sample, we detected a mean of 13,418 single-nucleotide variants (SNVs; range, 4917–28,938), 1771 short insertions and deletions (indels; range, 686–3324), and 98 structural variations (SVs; range 7–246) (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b; Supplementary Data 2). Using SNVs as lineage barcodes, we reconstructed clonal phylogenies to understand the evolutionary trajectories of metastatic CRC progression, as previously described3,16 (Fig. 1b–g; Supplementary Fig. 2). Despite the smaller number of SVs, clonal phylogenies reconstructed from SVs showed topologies consistent with SNV-based trees, supporting the robustness of the inferred structures (Supplementary Fig. 3). On average, 56.0% of somatic base substitutions detected in a specimen were shared across all tumor samples in a patient (located in the trunk of the phylogenies), likely representing early-stage acquisitions in the mutational history. The remaining 44.0% of base substitutions were classified as late-stage mutations, restricted to downstream branches of the phylogenetic trees (Supplementary Fig. 1b; Supplementary Data 3). Overall, the canonical driver mutations of CRC, such as mutations in TP53, KRAS, and APC, were mostly truncal, or were present in the first node of the phylogenies (Fig. 1b–g; Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating their role in tumor initiation, in agreement with previous reports17. Some genetic mutations were late-stage drivers, for example, the homozygous SMAD4 exon1 deletion restricted to liver metastatic clones of patient MC05 (female, in her 60 s) (Fig. 1f; further explored below).

Clonal trajectories of CRC metastasis

The clonal phylogenies revealed the origin and foundation timing of cancer lineages during tumor development and metastasis. For example, three synchronous primary tumors in patient MC02 (male, in his 80 s; Supplementary Fig. 1c) shared only 16 base substitutions to each other, suggesting that the three lineages branched at the fetal developmental stage18, indicating three independent tumorigenesis in the patient. Of note, the corresponding liver metastatic tissue and clones (Liver-FT and Liver-O1 to O7) were clustered with one of the three primary tumors (Primary3-FT, located in the hepatic flexure of the colon), pinpointing it as the exclusive metastatic origin (Fig. 1c).

Across the clonal phylogenies from all six patients (Fig. 1b–g), metastatic clones had a late-stage common ancestral node, suggesting that a single subclonal lineage is likely a dominant source of distant metastasis.

To assess the representation of tumoroid-defined lineages in bulk tumors of their origin, we quantified the combined VAFs of tumoroid-specific mutations in matched bulk tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 5a–h; Methods). Overall, these VAFs were low but clearly above sequencing artifact levels, supporting the presence of tumoroid lineages in bulk tumors. For example, in MC03, tumoroid-specific mutations were detectable in the primary tumor (combined VAF, 0.003). The low frequencies of these lineages can be explained by two non-exclusive scenarios: (1) regional sampling bias between the bulk tissue used for sequencing and the material used to establish tumoroids, or (2) selective expansion of specific lineages during in vitro tumoroid establishment. Interestingly, clonal phylogenies of MC06-Peritoneum, MC04-Ovary, and MC03-LymphNode suggested that clonal divergence at these sites occurred earlier than in liver metastases, consistent with previous reports17,19. Indeed, the combined VAFs of the later-branching clone-specific mutations in the early-diverging bulk tissues were indistinguishable from background sequencing noise, supporting truly early divergence of peritoneal, ovarian, and lymph node metastases, at least in these cases (Supplementary Fig. 5i).

Although the distribution of tumoroid clones generally mirrored the clonal composition of the corresponding tumor tissue, a notable exception was observed in patient MC03 (male, in his 70 s). In this case, eight tumoroid clones derived from the primary tumor tissue (Primary-O1 to O10) shared a lineage with liver metastatic tissues, rather than with their collection site (the primary tumor tissue, Primary-FT). We speculate that this mutant lineage was present at the primary tumor site as a minority, suppressed by niche saturation, and therefore undetectable by bulk-tissue WGS. The lineage could have been selectively expanded during metastasis and tumoroid culture. The metastatic lineage acquired thousands of additional mutations, including a BRCA1 loss-of-function mutation, MYC amplification, and a homozygous truncation of SMAD4 (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 1d). Of note, SMAD4 loss-of-function is known to enhance Wnt/β-catenin signaling20, a pathway essential for liver metastasis21 and, importantly, conferring a strong selective growth advantage under in vitro organoid culture conditions22.

Mutational footprints of anticancer therapies during clonal evolution

To assess the mutational footprints of anticancer therapies in cancer cells, we decomposed the spectrum of mutations in the trunk and each branch using known mutational signatures (reported in COSMIC database v3.2 and previous literatures)12,23,24 (Fig. 1b–g; Supplementary Data 4). In total, base substitutions and indel mutations were attributed to 13 SBS and 8 indel signatures (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 6). For example, ~30,000 somatic base substitutions detected in the MC06-Liver-O5 sample were decomposed into 9 SBS signatures (Fig. 2b). As expected, the ‘clock-like’ signatures, including SBS1, SBS5/40, ID1, and ID2, were universal across all tumor samples sequenced (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 6a; Supplementary Data 5). In addition, SBS37 (etiology unknown) and SBS41 (etiology unknown) were found in both treatment-naive and chemotherapy-treated tumor samples (Fig. 2a). Given their presence across both groups and the lack of prior evidence linking these signatures to anticancer therapies3, they seem unlikely to represent therapy-associated mutational processes.

In contrast, mutations attributable to a few signatures were clearly enriched in post-treatment samples. For example, SBS17b and SBS17a mutations were elevated in tumors with 5-fluorouracil exposure (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 6b, c; SBS17b; mean 398 vs. 149, P = 9.885e-05, for SBS17a; mean 233 vs. 85, P = 0.0001), independent of tumor site. Our observation extends previous reports identifying the association between 5-fluorouracil and SBS17a and SBS17b12,25,26. Likewise, tumoroids exposed to oxaliplatin often exhibited specific signatures, SBSoxaliplatin and/or SBScisplatin/oxaliplatin, consistent with prior report12, although these signatures are not yet archived in the COSMIC reference signatures (Fig. 2a). Similarly, IDradiation, an indel signature previously established from the genome sequences of irradiated human cells23, was observed in the tumoroids of patient MC05 (Supplementary Fig. 6d), who underwent radiotherapy in combination with both FOLFOX and FOLFIRI regimens.

Unexpectedly, SBS25, a mutational signature previously observed in Hodgkin’s lymphoma samples treated with alkylating agents, such as procarbazine- or dacarbazine-containing regimens27 was detected in post-treatment tumoroids from patient MC06, who received both the FOLFOX and FOLFIRI regimens.

Collectively, we classified SBS17a/SBS17b (5-fluorouracil), SBSoxaliplatin, SBScisplatin/oxaliplatin (both for oxaliplatin), and SBS25 (not specified) as chemotherapy-associated base substitution signatures (SBSchemo), as these were base-substitution signatures significantly enriched in chemotherapy-exposed tumor specimens. Of the genotoxic agents applied to patients, we did not detect any mutational signature specific to irinotecan treatment (topoisomerase I inhibitor). Overall, we identified an average of 1400 SBSchemo base substitutions per clone, accounting for approximately 8.0% of total mutation burden (range, 0.1–17.3%). These mutations were mostly detected in the downstream branches of the phylogenies (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f), and were rarely observed in bulk tissues (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, phylogenetic tree reconstruction revealed additional therapy-associated mutations by resolving late-stage subclonal branches (Supplementary Figs. 1a, 7), consistent with their acquisition following therapeutic exposure. In turn, these findings underscore the utility of clonal tumoroid approaches for the sensitive evaluation of therapy-associated mutational signatures in cancer.

Uneven chemotherapy-associated mutational burden across clones

Despite the overall positive correlation between therapy-associated mutational burden and duration of chemotherapy exposure (mean 142 [95% CI, 116–167] SBSchemo mutations/chemotherapy-month) (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Fig. 8a), we observed substantial inter-patient and intra-tumoral variation. For instance, SBScisplatin/oxaliplatin signature was detected in only five, out of 27 tumoroids exposed to oxaliplatin (Fig. 2a). Of the 12 tumoroids derived from patient MC03, mutations attributable to SBSoxaliplatin were totally absent in two clones. Similarly, the SBS17b mutational burden varied considerably among post-treatment tumoroids derived from the same individual, highlighting the uneven mutagenic consequences of chemotherapy across cancer cells. In contrast, individual clones from MC04 accumulated comparable numbers of SBSchemo mutations despite differences in duration (Fig. 1e). Interestingly, the uneven SBSchemo mutational burden was associated with the number of endogenous clock-like mutations in the clones (SBS1, SBS5/40), well-known for their association with cell proliferation28 and age24. For example, in patients MC04 and MC06, clones with a higher burden of SBSchemo mutations also exhibited a strong positive correlation with the number of mutations attributable to SBS1 and SBS5/40 (Fig. 3b, c; Supplementary Fig. 8b–e). Such associations were not observed between SBSchemo and other non-clock-like signatures (SBS18, SBS37, SBS41) with the exception of SBS13, an APOBEC-mediated signature whose mechanism can be deeply related to cell replications29.

Our observation extends to patient MC03, where SBS3 was included as a clock-like mutational signature in the case (Fig. 3d). Notably, SBS3 is rare in CRC, but commonly observed in homologous recombination–deficient (HRD) breast and ovarian cancers30–32. In MC03, a post-treatment cancer lineage acquired a BRCA1 loss-of-function mutation via an intra-exonic LINE-1 retrotransposition, which likely led to HRD within the lineage (Supplementary Fig. 1d). We speculate that, in tumors with HRD, SBS3 may have replaced the typical endogenous mutational signatures such as SBS1 and SBS5/40.

We then validated the association between SBSchemo and clock-like mutations using external datasets, including normal blood26 and colon epithelial cells33. In these datasets, we observed consistent trends from all informative cases (275 clones from 4 normal blood cell donors; 12 clones from 2 normal colon epithelium donors) (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. 9a, b).

Collectively, our findings indicate that cellular lineages undergoing more cell divisions, and thereby accumulating greater numbers of clock-like mutations, are more susceptible to SBSchemo mutations following treatment. Conversely, this suggests that lineages in a quiescent or cell cycle–arrested state34, are less likely to acquire SBSchemo mutations during chemotherapy. Of note, cancer dormancy, a state in which cancer cells remain quiescent with arrested cell cycles, has been proposed as one of the mechanisms underlying resistance to chemotherapy35–40 (Fig. 3f).

This association was also evident from the phylogenetic structure: sister lineages that produced more tumoroids tended to accumulate more mutations (Fig. 3g). For example, in clonal evolutions of patients MC03, MC04 and MC06, one daughter lineage consistently produced more tumoroids than the other, with the repeated emergence of ‘dominant subclones’, resulting in an asymmetric tree topology (Supplementary Fig. 9c–g). In line with our speculation, dominant lineages showing a higher mutational burden than their counterpart minor or dormant lineages (concordant) were more frequently observed compared to dominant lineages showing a lower mutational burden (discordant) in patients MC03, MC04, and MC06 (P = 9.413e-11; Fig. 3h; Supplementary Fig. 9f), suggesting that lineages with higher cell proliferation rates had accumulated more mutations during chemotherapy. Interestingly, mutations attributable to SBSchemo signatures were absent in all cancer clones derived from patient MC05, despite a documented history of prolonged exposure to FOLFOX and FOLFIRI (Figs. 1f and 3i). In addition, clonal asymmetry could not be determined due to the extremely low number of non-shared mutations between tumoroids, suggesting that the tumoroids originated from a single clone. Notably, this patient received combined radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Consistent with this, tumoroids from patient MC05 exhibited indel mutations attributable to the IDradiation signature, at levels consistent with a prior report in irradiated normal cells (~12 indels per Gy)23. This could be explained by therapy-induced dormancy selection. If chemotherapy imposed strong negative selection against proliferative or damage-prone cancer cells41–43, post-therapy cancer tissues will predominantly consist of dormant cell lineages. In contrast, ionizing radiation is a physical mutagen which can penetrate and damage DNA in both proliferative and dormant cells. Consequently, post-therapy dormant lineages would exhibit radiation-associated but not chemotherapy-associated mutations, and lack asymmetry in the phylogeny (Fig. 3j).

Late-stage driver mutation in the course of clonal evolution

In the clonal phylogenies, we observed numerous acquisitions and losses of driver mutations within individual cancer lineages. Most interestingly, we found mutations in genes with clear evidence of convergent evolution, characterized by recurrent and independent changes across distinct lineages. For instance, in patient MC02, three synchronous primary colorectal cancers independently acquired loss-of-function mutations in tumor suppressor genes, such as TP53 and APC (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, independent allele-specific MYC oncogene amplifications were detected in MC02-Primary and MC02-Liver clones (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 10a), indicating parallel acquisition and selection for similar oncogenic drivers during early tumorigenesis.

The dynamic process was also observed during tumor evolution (Fig. 4c–e; Supplementary Fig. 10b–e). In the subclonal tumor evolution of patient MC04, cancer acquired at least three independent mutations in the oncogene ERBB2, including a hotspot mutation in the serine/threonine kinase domain (T862A)44 and two amplification events (Supplementary Fig. 10b): (i) breakage–fusion–bridge (BFB) cycles, which focally amplified the T862A-mutant allele into 6–17 copies (Supplementary Fig. 10c–e; Supplementary Data 6), and (ii) broad, whole-chromosomal amplification of T862-wildtype allele (Fig. 4c, d; Supplementary Fig. 10b). The clonal phylogeny suggests that both amplification events were initiated from the trunk, and may have continued in downstream lineages. Interestingly, amplicons by BFB cycles were often lost within liver metastasis and Primary-O1 and Primary-O3 clones (Fig. 4d, e; Supplementary Fig. 10c). The phylogeny suggests that the loss of the amplicon occurred at least three times independently, presumably due to functional redundancy between two amplicon events.

Additionally, stepwise loss-of-function of a tumor suppressor gene, SMAD3 was acquired. Following copy number–neutral LOH in the 15q arm, a frameshift SMAD3 mutation was acquired in one of the amplified alleles. The mutation was restricted to the primary tumoroids, suggesting it was acquired after divergence between the primary and metastatic lineages. The wild-type SMAD3 allele was further lost, resulting in homozygous SMAD3 loss-of-function in a subset of tumoroids from the primary tumor (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Fig. 10f). A similar stepwise loss-of-function was observed with APC truncating mutations in the patient MC02 liver metastasis, and with BRCA1 in patient MC04 (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 1d).

Unexpectedly, loss of oncogenic mutations was also observed. In patient MC06, NRAS G12D mutation was observed in MC06-Primary-FT. However, liver metastasis clones dropped the mutation through a subsequent event of large deletion of the chromosome arm (Fig. 4g; Supplementary Fig. 10g). This suggests that the functional role of NRAS G12D is not essential for tumor metastasis.

Evolution of complex genomic rearrangements

The WGS of clonal tumoroids enabled us to trace complex genomic rearrangements (CGRs), such as chromothripsis, in clonal evolution45–48. In the phylogenies, we found nine subclonal chromothripsis events in addition to two early-stage events (Supplementary Fig. 11; Supplementary Data 6).

Some CGR events acquired more complexity as clones advanced, although CGRs are typically considered as single catastrophic events45,46. For example, in patient MC05, clonal phylogenies indicate two independent CGRs (chromosome 1 and 13; chromosome 6 and 19) in early-stage cancer evolution, resulting in two derivative chromosomes (Fig. 5a–c; Supplementary Fig. 12a, b; Supplementary Data 6). After duplication of each derivative chromosome, the two derivatives subsequently rearranged into a single dicentric chromosome in the later branches. Careful reconstruction of the breakpoints and copy number status revealed a subsequent secondary CGR event causing newly acquired clustered breakpoints and copy-number alterations affecting multiple chromosomal segments. Such evolution of rearrangements involving multiple chromosomes was also observed in patients MC03 and MC06 (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d; Supplementary Data 6), suggesting that post-CGR regions are prone to additional rearrangements during clonal evolution.

In patient MC03, we traced the dynamic change of extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) underlying focal amplification of the MDM2 oncogene. The event likely originated from multichromosomal chromothripsis (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b; Supplementary Data 7). Although the ecDNA copy number was overall consistent between the primary tumor and liver metastasis (P = 0.8375; Supplementary Fig. 13c), a subsequent sequence change was observed across the clones, which revealed the evolution and segregation of ecDNA in clonal progression (Fig. 5d, e; Supplementary Fig. 14). In Liver2-O4 and Liver2-O5, we observed a replacement of the ecDNA pool with a new mutant ecDNA, suggesting dynamic changes in ecDNA composition during clonal evolution.

Some genes showed higher rates of genomic rearrangement. For example, we observed recurrent large-deletions in the RBFOX1 gene locus in tumoroids (Fig. 5f). Overall, two patients (MC03 and MC06; 33.3%), 7 tumor lesions (38.9%), and 17 (22.4%) individual CRC samples had RBFOX1 deletions (Fig. 5g). The deletion rate (proportion of samples with RBFOX1 deletions among total samples) was significantly higher than that observed in healthy clones of the colon of tumor-unmatched individuals49 (22.4 vs. 1.5%; P = 5.662e-26; Fig. 5g; Supplementary Data 6), suggesting the loss as a characteristic of neoplastic cells. Notably, a similar incidence of RBFOX1 deletion (per-lesion level) was found in the public cohort of bulk-tissue CRC50 (Supplementary Fig. 15b). The size of deletions ranged from 2 kbp to 470 kbp, frequently spanning exon3 of the RBFOX1−010 (ENST00000547372) transcript (Fig. 5f; Supplementary Fig. 15a). Interestingly, 15 samples (19.7%) showed three or four concurrent RBFOX1 deletions, which was more than expected from the two-hit hypothesis for tumor suppressor genes, suggesting the inherent fragility of this genomic region in CRC. Similarly, we observed repeated large deletions in the MACROD2 locus in patient MC03 (Supplementary Fig. 15c; Supplementary Data 6).

L1 retrotranspositions throughout cancer evolution

Finally, we examined the retrotranspositions of long interspersed element-1 (LINE-1 or L1) during clonal evolution (Supplementary Fig. 16). L1 is a mobile element in the human genome51,52 (Fig. 6a), and its mobilization has been frequently observed in CRC53,54 and in normal colorectal epithelium49. Despite its potential role in tumorigenesis and clonal evolution through insertional mutagenesis54–57, L1 retrotransposition activity in cancer subclones remains poorly understood58.

In our clonal phylogenies, we detected a total of 1,138 somatic L1 retrotransposition events (soL1Rs) (Supplementary Fig. 17a; Supplementary Data 8). On average, each clone harbored 104 soL1Rs (range, 8–291), which is substantially higher than the number observed in normal colorectal organoids (three per clone, Fig. 6b)49. In addition, metastatic tumoroids exhibited a higher soL1R burden than primary tumoroids (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Of all detected events, 224 (20%) were truncal events in the phylogenies, acquired before the diversification of the tumoroid clones.

The burden of branch soL1Rs showed a broad positive correlation with the extent of chemotherapy exposure (Fig. 6b), although with substantial inter-patient and intra-tumoral heterogeneity. For instance, clones in MC04 showed no clear increase in soL1R burden with increasing chemotherapy duration. Interestingly, similarly to SBSchemo base substitutions mentioned above, soL1R burden was also positively correlated with the number of clock-like endogenous mutations at the clone level (Supplementary Fig. 17c, d). As a result, despite the increased absolute number of soL1Rs observed in the phylogenetic branches, the soL1R rate, defined as the number of soL1Rs per single SBSclock-like (SBS1 and SBS5/40; SBS3 for MC03), was comparable between branches and trunks (11.5 vs. 10.9; P = 0.9409), despite slight differences in qualitative features or retrotranspositions, including soL1R insertion length (P = 0.0338), and the proportion of transductions (P = 5.701e-05; Supplementary Fig. 18a–k). These findings suggest that the soL1R accumulation rate is largely proportional to the number of cell divisions, and that the apparent increase in soL1Rs following chemotherapy likely reflects the additional cell divisions during chemotherapy (Fig. 6c). In line with our speculation here, clones of MC05, where combined chemo- and radiotherapy likely eliminated most actively proliferating cells, showed a much lower proportion of branch soL1Rs than other patients with chemotherapy (43.6% vs. 84.4%; P = 2.017e-07; Supplementary Fig. 18l).

Of the 1138 retrotransposition events detected, 76 (6.7%) were transductions58, which co-inserted 3′ unique sequences of the L1 source element, enabling fingerprinting of the source origin of the retrotranspositions (Fig. 6d, e; Supplementary Figs. 17a, 19; Supplementary Data 8, 9). We observed substantial heterogeneity in L1 source activity, both between patients and among clones within an individual (Supplementary Fig. 19). For example, the L1 source at 22q12.1-2 was activated in downstream branches after chemotherapy exposure in patient MC04. In contrast, the same source was active in the trunk but not in downstream branches in patient MC06. Several other sources also showed retrotransposition activity in downstream branches (i.e., 14q24.2, 1p13.1, 6p22.3–3 in patient MC04; 6p24.1 in patient MC06), suggesting that each L1 source may change its activity over time in the course of clonal evolution, although our observations were limited predominantly to two individuals (Fig. 6d, e).

Discussion

Previous studies of late-stage clonal evolution in metastatic CRC under anticancer therapies have mostly focused on specific driver genes (e.g., KRAS, EGFR)59–61, or lacked the resolution to capture late-stage clonal evolution due to the limitations of bulk-tissue sequencing4,10,11. Using clonal tumoroids from primary and metastatic tumors, we reconstructed the late-stage subclonal mutational history in metastatic CRC under anticancer therapies at single-cell resolution. With extensive filtering and mutation rescue, most detected mutations were concordant with the inferred clonal phylogenies (Methods, Supplementary Data 10).

This study highlights some important features of clonal evolution in metastatic CRC during anticancer therapies. We quantified the precise burden of therapy-associated mutations in single clones. Previous studies estimated that several hundred mutations accumulate per month of chemotherapy in bulk tumors12, but the additional mutational burden across single-cells has remained unclear, as bulk-tissue sequencing cannot efficiently capture subclonal mutations within post-therapy cancer tissues.

Our approach reveals a positive association between SBSchemo and SBSclock-like mutations. These findings suggest that chemotherapy-associated mutations preferentially accumulate in more proliferative cancer cells, a pattern further supported by the phylogenetic structure and external datasets26,33. We speculate that this may reflect asymmetric cell division giving rise to dormant cells in CRC62–64, whereby dormant cells with limited proliferative activity accumulate fewer SBSchemo41–43 and SBSclock-like mutations65–67 during anticancer therapies, as observed in patient MC05. However, functional studies to clarify the causal role of dormancy in metastasis and treatment resistance35–40, as well as approaches that mitigate potential selection bias in tumoroid culture, are required in the future.

The evolutionary trajectories revealed features of both linear progression and parallel evolution68. Recurrent acquisition of identical driver mutations in synchronous primary tumors, late-stage ERBB2 copy-number loss, and activation of the same L1 source elements in independent clones exemplify parallel (convergent) evolution. In contrast, linear evolution was observed in the stepwise development of CGRs and sequential loss-of-function events in tumor suppressor genes. Whether these evolutionary patterns are linked to anticancer therapies or differentially contribute to therapeutic resistance warrants further investigation.

Notably, some driver mutations were lost during clonal evolution, possibly reflecting their functional redundancy at the late stage. Previous reports have documented loss of driver mutations under targeted therapies69, but such events in the absence of targeted agent usage have not been described. These dynamics underscore the importance of tailoring treatment strategies to cancer clonal evolution. For instance, patient MC06 developed metastatic clones lacking NRAS mutations that might have benefited from anti-EGFR therapy, a standard treatment withheld because an NRAS mutation was detected in the primary tumor. Future studies that functionally validate whether the loss of oncogenic driver mutations renders clones susceptible or resistant to specific targeted therapies will be essential for developing individualized treatment strategies in metastatic CRC.

Although CGRs are generally considered as single catastrophic events45,46, careful dissection of genomic rearrangements revealed that some continue to evolve during late-stage clonal evolution and occasionally affect cancer genes. Radiation may act as a trigger by promoting the integration of two distinct CGRs harboring vulnerable rearrangement breakpoints. Clonal drift of ecDNA70,71 generated substantial heterogeneity in ecDNA composition across clones, as observed in MC03, which may influence their selective advantage. We also observed repeated deletions in potential fragile sites, such as RBFOX1, which encodes an RNA-binding protein involved in alternative splicing and shows haploinsufficiency72,73, supporting a potential role for RBFOX1 as a CRC-specific driver74.

The soL1R counts in metastatic CRC were significantly higher than in post-developmental normal colon epithelium49 and bulk-tissue primary CRC50, highlighting enhanced L1 retrotransposition activity in late-stage CRCs under anticancer therapies. The enhanced activity is in line with the evidence of clones with increased proliferative capacity, as increased soL1R counts were most evident in those with increased therapy-associated mutational burdens. Indeed, we identified some sources gaining activity in branches with SBSchemo, although their activity in trunks may have been overlooked. Future studies should explore how factors like promoter methylation58,75, defense mechanisms against L1 retrotransposition76, and double-strand break rates77 interact with anticancer therapies.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small number of patients limits the generalizability of our findings, given the inter-patient heterogeneity in CRC78. However, our primary objective was to resolve and describe the late-stage subclonal mutations at single-cell resolution, which are largely obscured in bulk-tissue sequencing. Using whole-genome sequencing data from 58 single-cell-derived tumoroids, our dataset provides a valuable resource for dissecting late-stage evolutionary dynamics under therapeutic pressure at the single-cell level.

Second, tumoroid culture may introduce selection biases during in vitro growth. The mutational burden, lineage variability, and mutational signatures observed here may be skewed toward more proliferative lineages that preferentially form organoids and may therefore not fully recapitulate the native mutational architecture. However, these lineages are not purely artefactual, as they are detectable in bulk tissues, albeit at low frequencies. Future integration of single-cell DNA amplification-based WGS13 will enable more rigorous evaluation of selection bias and clonal representativeness.

Further, we did not explore the transcriptomic and epigenetic changes79,80, and lacked functional validation. Most metastatic tumoroids were derived from liver metastases, in part due to limited tumoroid formation efficiency from other metastatic sites. The numbers of tumoroids across sites was heterogeneous, and multi-region sampling would be required to draw definitive conclusions on a common ancestral node of a metastatic tumor. Lastly, clones completely eradicated by anticancer therapy would not have been captured.

Clonal tumoroids provided detailed insights into the clonal evolution of subclonal lineages in metastatic CRC during anticancer therapies. We identified the burden and timing of therapy-associated mutations, the heterogeneity of these burdens, and the diverse dynamics of driver mutations, CGRs, and retrotransposons during late-stage clonal evolution. These findings highlight the importance of resolving subclonal mutations at single-cell resolution to accurately understand the dynamic evolution of cancer cells under anticancer therapies. Future studies exploring how heterogeneous clonal evolution during anticancer therapies affects cancer metastasis and treatment resistance would be essential to overcome the therapeutic challenges in metastatic CRC.

Methods

Human tissue collection

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the National Cancer Center (NCC2019-0058) and Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST; KH2021-225) and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Metastatic CRC tumor tissues were obtained from surgical specimens of 18 tumors (eight primary and ten metastatic) from six patients (4 males and 2 females). Sex of the participants was determined from clinical records, and confirmed by sequencing depth of sex chromosomes in whole-genome sequencing. The study was not designed to evaluate sex- or gender-specific differences; therefore, sex- and gender-based analyses were not performed, as the cohort size was not sufficient for meaningful stratified analyses. Both treatment-naïve and therapy-exposed patients were selected based on the availability of surgically resected primary and metastatic tumors, in line with the study’s aim of dissecting late-stage mutations in metastatic disease. Consistent with institutional practice, most resected metastases were from the liver. Cases were included if fresh-frozen primary tumor tissues were available in the institutional tissue bank. The detailed patient characteristics are described in Supplementary Data 1. For fresh-frozen bulk-tissue WGS, tumor pieces approximately 1 × 1 × 1 cm³ were collected. Identically sized tissues from the same tumors were used for clonal tumoroid establishment. Matched peripheral blood samples were also collected for germline comparison.

Bulk tumoroid culture from cancer tissues

Tissues were sent for tumoroid culture within 30 min of surgical resection. Tumoroid establishment procedures and media compositions were based on previously described protocols15 (Supplementary Table 1). Surgically resected CRC tissues were cut into ~1 mm³ sections, washed with PBS, and dissociated using digestion buffer (collagenase 6.25 mg/mL, Dispase I 1.6 ml/vial in DMEM with 2.5% FBS) at 37 °C for 45 min with gentle shaking. Isolated cells were embedded in growth-factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning) and plated on a 24-well cell culture plate (Corning). Pellets were resuspended in Matrigel, and 1 × 105 cells were plated in 20 µL droplets to ensure sufficient dilution for single-cell–derived clonal tumoroid culture, modified from a previous protocol81. After Matrigel polymerization at 37 °C for 15 min, 1 mL of tumoroid culture medium was added per well. The full medium composition is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Single-cell sorting and clonal expansion of single-cell-derived tumoroids

After 1–2 passages, tumoroids were harvested and dissociated using Accutase (Stemcell Technology), washed, resuspended in Pre-Sort Buffer (BD FACS), and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Falcon). Single-cell sorting was performed using a FACSMelody (BD Biosciences) with gating based on forward- and side-scatter. Sorted single cells were seeded at low density (500 cells per well) in growth-factor-reduced Matrigel in 12-well plates. When tumoroids reached 150-200 um in diameter, individual tumoroids were manually picked using a 200 μL pipette under an inverted microscope. The picked tumoroid was placed in an Eppendorf tube and dissociated by Accutase (Stemcell Technology). After dissociation, it was washed with Pre-Sort Buffer (BD FACS) and replated in growth-factor-reduced Matrigel (20 uL) in 24-well plates. Tumoroids were cultured at a humidified 37 °C/5% CO2 incubator with media changes every 2–3 days. Clonal tumoroids were harvested when they grew confluent enough for nucleic acid extraction and tumoroid stock, typically 2–3 passages after single-cell sorting (Supplementary Data 11). Only patients with successful establishment of clonal tumoroids (≥5 clones per tumor site) from at least one lesion were included in the analyses. Exceptions were made in limited cases where tumoroid establishment from liver metastases was insufficient, and other metastatic sites were unavailable (MC03-Liver1 and MC03-Liver2).

Library preparation and whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from clonal tumoroids, bulk tumor tissue, and matched peripheral blood using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA libraries were generated using TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Prep Kit (Illumina) and sequenced on either the Illumina HiSeq X Ten or NovaSeq 6000 platforms. Tumoroid and blood samples were sequenced to a target depth of 30×; bulk tumor tissues to 100×.

Read alignment, variant calling and filtering of SNVs and short indels

Sequenced reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37) by the BWA-MEM algorithm82. Duplicate reads were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates, followed by indel realignment (RealignerTargetCreator and IndelRealigner) and base recalibration (BaseRecalibrator and Apply BQSR), with GATK. Somatic SNVs and short indels were identified using Mutect283 and Strelka84 using blood as matched normal samples, and germline variants were called using HaplotypeCaller85. Somatic mutation calls from Mutect2 and Strelka were merged into a unified set per sample. Variants were filtered to exclude those with VAF ≥ 0.01 in the panel of normals (in-house made), VAF ≥ 0.01 in any population dataset in dbSNP (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/), VAF ≥ 0.01 in the matched normal sample, mean mismatched base per variant read ≥2.5, fraction of clipped bases per variant read ≥0.1, or low mapping quality and/or base quality, to remove contamination from germline mutations and low-quality calls.

Copy number status and cancer cell fraction analysis

Copy number status, tumor cell purity, and ploidy were estimated using Sequenza86. For low-quality libraries, Sequenza results required further smoothing to decrease the number of fragments. To address this issue, we manually adjusted the delta parameter in Sequenza, a value that decides break points to perform efficient segmentation during copy number analysis. Then, we calculated cancer cell fraction (CCF) in each variant as previously described87 using a formula that estimates mutation copy number88. Briefly, the formula incorporates VAF, tumor cell purity, absolute copy numbers in both tumor and normal cells, and average ploidy from Sequenza to estimate the fraction of cancer cells carrying each mutation.

Phylogenetic tree construction from somatic SNVs

Somatic SNVs were used to reconstruct patient-specific phylogenetic trees following a previously described method3. In brief, after somatic variant calling and filtering, CCF was annotated to each somatic mutation to exclude subclonal variants. Mutations were considered clonal if CCF > 0.75 (or >0.6 in samples with estimated tumor cell fraction <0.5 by Sequenza tool). All clonal SNVs from a patient were merged and rescued using read-level data. To rescue potentially missed calls, each mutation in the merged mutation set was searched for variant supporting reads across all patient samples, excluding those with ≤5 total read counts. A VAF cutoff of 2.5% was applied to call the mutation present in a sample.

This process generated a binary matrix indicating the presence or absence of each mutation across patient samples. Mutations shared by all samples were excluded. Phylogenetic trees were then reconstructed from this binary matrix using Phylip89 (Mix method, Wagner parsimony, jumble = 100). The most parsimonious tree per patient was obtained in a Newick format.

Allocation of short variants into the phylogenetic tree and rescue of unfit mutations

We used the Python package ETE3 (v3.1.2, http://etetoolkit.org/) for downstream modification and analysis of the phylogenetic trees in Newick format. Each patient-specific Newick file was used to allocate all merged mutations to the corresponding branches for both SNVs and short indels. A mutation was assigned to a branch if it was present in all descendant clones (i.e., leaves of the tree) originating from that branch. For SNVs, we used the binary matrix generated during phylogenetic reconstruction, indicating the presence or absence of each mutation in each sample. For indels, we applied the same approach, using the SNV-based tree as the structural framework. Indels were merged across all samples for each patient, and a binary matrix was constructed using a VAF threshold of 2.5% to define presence or absence per sample. Indels were then allocated to the tree using this matrix.

To evaluate tree quality, we calculated a “tree fit rate” for SNVs and short indels, defined as the proportion of mutations confidently allocated to a branch relative to the total merged mutations per patient. Most unfit mutations arose from systematic sequencing biases or low read support, resulting in missed calls. To improve the tree fit rate, we attempted to “rescue” unfit SNVs and short indels by identifying their likely positions along the phylogenetic tree. Using a merged mutation set from all 82 tumor and normal samples across the six patients, we generated an “unmatched panel of normals” by annotating each mutation with variant read support across all BAM files. Mutations supported by ≥2 reads in any sample from another patient were removed as likely sequencing artifacts. A mutation was considered present in a sample if supported by ≥1 high-quality read (or ≥2 reads for low-purity bulk-tumor samples), allowing recovery of variants previously missed due to low coverage.

Following rescue, final mutation sets were obtained for each branch and trunk, leading to substantial increases in tree fit rates, with nearly all trees exceeding 90% (Supplementary Data 10). Manual review of the remaining unfit mutations revealed several causes, including (1) mutation loss due to chromosomal copy number loss, (2) absence of supporting reads in low-purity samples, and (3) inclusion of subclonal variants during initial clonal mutation calling. A small subset of unresolved cases may reflect residual sequencing artifacts.

Assessing lineage representation in bulk tissue using combined VAF

To estimate the representation of each tumoroid-defined lineage within the corresponding bulk tumor tissue, we calculated a branch-level combined VAF. For a given tumoroid-specific branch in the phylogenetic tree, all somatic mutations assigned to that branch were queried in the matched bulk tumor BAM file to determine the presence and number of variant-supporting reads.

For each branch, the combined VAF was calculated by aggregating read counts across all mutations in that branch. Specifically, the total number of variant-supporting reads across all branch-specific loci (A) was divided by the total number of reads covering those loci (N, defined as variant-supporting plus reference-supporting reads):

where i indexes mutations assigned to the branch.

Because the majority of tumoroid-specific mutations were obviously not clonally present in bulk tissue and therefore mostly showed zero variant-supporting reads individually, this aggregated approach was used to increase sensitivity for detecting low-frequency lineages. Combined VAFs were calculated for each branch independently. To estimate confidence intervals for combined VAF, we modeled the total number of variant-supporting reads A as a Poisson-distributed variable with mean λ = Np, where p represents the underlying allele fraction. Under the rare-variant assumption (small p), exact two-sided 95% confidence intervals for the rate parameter were calculated based on the Poisson distribution, and converted to confidence intervals for the combined VAF by normalizing to the total read depth N. As a reference for background signal, combined VAFs were also calculated using sets of mutations derived from unrelated patients.

To validate early metastatic divergence of certain samples, we specifically examined whether mutations assigned to phylogenetic branches arising after divergence were detectable in the corresponding early-diverging samples. To minimize potential misassignment due to limited detection sensitivity in bulk tissues, branches immediately following divergence were excluded from this analysis, as mutations in these branches may be missed in early-diverging samples with low tumor purity. For the mutations from the post-divergence branches, combined VAFs were calculated in the early-diverging bulk-tissue BAM files using the approach described above. Combined VAFs from post-divergence branches were then compared with these background estimates to assess whether the observed signal exceeded sequencing noise.

Mutational signature analysis

We used SigProfiler90 to extract mutational signatures for each sample, as well as for each trunk and branch of the phylogenetic trees. We conducted an initial decomposition against a COSMIC v3.2 reference set (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/signatures/) and subsequently retained only those signatures that demonstrated high cosine similarity and were stably detected across multiple phylogenetic branches. Cisplatin/oxaliplatin-associated signature (SBScisplatin/oxaliplatin), oxaliplatin-associated signature (SBSoxaliplatin)12, and radiation-associated signature (IDradiation)23 were included in the reference set to find therapy-associated mutational signatures. The final unified signature set included SBS1, SBS3, SBS5/40, SBS13, SBS17a, SBS17b, SBS18, SBS25, SBS37, SBS41, SBSoxaliplatin, SBScisplatin/oxaliplatin, ID1, ID2, ID5, ID6, ID7, ID8, ID12, ID14, and IDradiation. This curated set was then uniformly applied to all trunks and branches for final signature fitting and quantification. The cosine similarity and reconstruction error metrics from signature decomposition are shown in Supplementary Data 4.

Validation of the association between SBSchemo and SBSclock-like

To validate the association between SBSchemo and SBSclock-like mutations observed in our metastatic CRC tumoroids, we analyzed publicly available whole-genome sequencing datasets of clonally expanded normal tissues from patients exposed to chemotherapy, including normal blood cells26 and normal colon epithelium33. For the normal blood cell dataset, SBS1, SBS5, and SBSBlood91 were collectively defined as SBSclock-like. SBS-A, SBS-B, SBS-C, SBS-D, SBS-E, SBS-F, SBS-G, and SBS-H were defined as SBSchemo, as previously annotated in the original study. For the normal colon epithelium dataset, SBS1 and SBS5 were defined as SBSclock-like, while SBS17 and SBS35 were collectively defined as SBSchemo, based on their reported association with fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-based chemotherapy exposure.

To assess within-patient associations, only individuals with five or more independent clones were included in the analysis to ensure sufficient statistical power for intra-individual correlation testing. Patients with clones having fewer than 100 SBSchemo mutations were excluded. In cases where a single lineage exhibited apparent clonal expansion (i.e., multiple highly related clones derived from the same ancestral lineage with their trunk length longer than one-third of the average total mutational burden in the patient), the average mutational burden across those related clones was calculated and treated as a single representative clone. This approach was applied to minimize potential overrepresentation bias from expanded sublineages.

Finding cancer driver mutations

To identify canonical cancer driver events, we searched for oncogenic missense mutations, copy number amplifications of oncogenes, truncating mutations in tumor-suppressor genes, and copy number deletions of tumor-suppressor genes using the COSMIC Cancer Gene Census (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/census). For structural rearrangements, an event was considered cancer-driving if at least one breakpoint overlapped a known cancer gene in the case of inversions or translocations. For deletions and tandem duplications, a rearrangement was classified as cancer-driving if it encompassed coding regions of a known cancer gene. Translocations were also screened for known oncogenic fusions. In the case of tumor-suppressor genes, homozygous loss-of-function mutations were classified as canonical cancer-driving mutations.

For non-canonical driver events, particularly those acquired during late-stage clonal evolution, we manually curated all coding-region amino acid–altering mutations. Mutations were considered potentially oncogenic if they occurred at known hotspot residues annotated in the COSMIC database. For potential tumor suppressors, we assessed whether homozygous loss-of-function mutations were present. To evaluate the potential effect of heterozygotic loss, we used data from gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) to assess haploinsufficiency. Genes with a probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) ≥ 0.9 and an observed/expected (o/e) ratio for loss-of-function variants ≤ 0.4 were considered potentially intolerant to loss-of-function variants and suggestive of haploinsufficiency. To further contextualize candidate genes, all coding-region amino acid–altering mutations were queried in PubMed to assess any prior association of the gene or specific variant with cancer.

Analysis of clonal asymmetry

To explore asymmetric clonal evolution, we quantified the clonal asymmetricity in the phylogenetic tree. Because all internal nodes in the trees were bifurcating, we performed a pairwise comparison between terminal nodes from the two lineages emerging from each bifurcation. Specifically, we compared (1) the number of mutations accumulated from the bifurcation point to each terminal node and (2) the number of sister descendant clones per lineage. To evaluate whether mutation burden and clonal expansion were positively correlated, each bifurcation node was classified as concordant or discordant. A node was considered concordant if the lineage with the higher average mutation counts also gave rise to more descendant clones; conversely, it was classified as discordant if the lineage with the higher mutation burden produced fewer descendants. Under the null hypothesis of no association between mutation burden and clonal expansion, the proportion of concordant and discordant nodes was expected to approximate 50:50. A chi-square test was applied to assess whether the observed distribution significantly deviated from this expectation.

Finding, filtering, and rescuing genomic rearrangements

We identified genomic rearrangements using DELLY92. To remove false-positive and germline variants, we applied GREMLIN, a machine learning-based tool trained on tumor–normal pairs from the PCAWG project50 and additional data used in Lee et al.87. GREMLIN refines structural variant (SV) calls and annotates each event with a tier and a “gremlin score” reflecting its likelihood of being a true somatic event. SVs were filtered by excluding events with a gremlin score <0.95 for tier 1 or <0.5 for tier 2. We also removed SVs with insufficient variant-supporting reads or lacking adequate split-read support (SA-tagged reads) across the breakpoint. Inversions, deletions, and duplications with >10% of mate-pair reads mapping to a different chromosome near the breakpoint were excluded, as were calls with low mapping quality (Phred score <60) or those found in a panel of normal samples (generated from PCAWG50 and Lee et al.87). Additionally, tier 2 inversions smaller than 10 kbp were excluded, as these rearrangements were often flagged as false-positives during visual inspection using the Integrative Genomics Viewer93.

To rescue potentially missed events, we merged all somatic rearrangements detected across clones from each patient and re-analyzed the combined set using GREMLIN. The resulting GREMLIN-refined rearrangement calls were manually reviewed in IGV to confirm breakpoint support in each sample. The curated rearrangement set was then used to reconstruct phylogenetic trees using the same approach as for SNVs. As expected, the overall tree topology was identical to that reconstructed from SNVs in all patients. The only difference was that some terminal branches lacked rearrangements, preventing resolution of the exact order of clonal branching. Rearrangements were assigned to branches in the reconstructed phylogenetic tree if they were present in all descendant clones of that branch. Rearrangements that could not be confidently mapped to the tree were manually re-examined in IGV across all samples to identify potential false-positive or false-negative calls that could explain the inconsistency.

Clustering and analyzing genomic rearrangements

Somatic genomic rearrangements were clustered into complex genomic rearrangement (CGR) events as described in Lee et al.87. Briefly, breakpoints were first clustered based on their proximity, using a distance threshold of <5 Mbp. Simple rearrangements occasionally merged into nearby complex clusters; therefore, short deletions and duplications (<1 kbp) that were more than 10 kbp away from other breakpoints were excluded from clustering. Balanced inversions and translocations were also excluded.

After preprocessing, clusters containing >10 rearrangements were classified into one of five CGR categories based on copy number patterns, the number of involved chromosomes, and the proportion of balanced breakpoints. These categories included classical chromothripsis, balanced chromothripsis, multichromosomal chromothripsis, chromoplexy, and focal amplification. An ecDNA-generating event was identified based on clustered rearrangements and focal amplifications. The VAFs and CCFs of ecDNA-specific SNVs and breakpoints were used to estimate subclonal ecDNA copy number and dynamics.

Where feasible, we manually reconstructed derivative chromosomes based on breakpoint configurations and copy number changes. The phylogenetic tree was used to determine whether each CGR originated as a single event or accumulated stepwise over time. In cases where stepwise accumulation was suspected, all rearrangement breakpoints were manually inspected using IGV to rule out missed events that could lead to misinterpretation of CGR timing.

L1 retrotransposition calling and filtering

L1 retrotransposition calling and filtering were performed following the method described in Nam et al.49. Briefly, soL1Rs were identified using a combination of MELT94, TraFiC-mem58, DELLY92 and xTea95. The resulting calls were merged, and insertions detected in matched peripheral blood samples were excluded as likely germline variants. To remove residual false positives, all candidate soL1Rs were manually reviewed using IGV. Key features supporting true soL1Rs included the presence of poly-A tails, target site duplications (TSDs), and a sufficient number of variant-supporting reads (≥10% of total reads at the site). For each insertion, the 5′ and 3′ ends of the inserted segment were annotated to estimate the size of the L1 insertion and to determine whether the event involved L1-mediated 3′ TD. A retrotransposition was considered a 3′ TD if the inserted segment mapped to a unique, non-repetitive genomic sequence and a full-length L1 source element was located within 15 kb upstream.

To calculate the VAF of each soL1R, we divided the number of L1-supporting read pairs by the total number of informative read pairs spanning the insertion breakpoints. Reference-supporting read pairs were counted twice, as insertions are supported by discordant read pairs at both ends of the insertion.

After manual filtering, all validated soL1Rs from each patient were merged. To address potential sensitivity limitations in L1 detection, we performed rescue in which each soL1R was checked for evidence in its sister clones. If the insertion was confidently detected in a phylogenetically related clone, it was inferred to be present in the shared branch. Following this rescue, soL1Rs were allocated to their respective branches in the phylogenetic tree. Using soL1Rs that included 3′ transduced unique sequences, we mapped the activity of L1 source elements along the phylogenetic tree using the L1 source list from Nam et al.49. To compare soL1R counts between conditions, we included samples from publicly available treatment-naïve CRCs from the PCAWG cohort50 and normal colorectal epithelial organoids from Nam et al.49.

Statistics and reproducibility

This study was designed as an observational genomic analysis of patient-derived metastatic colorectal cancer samples and matched clonal tumoroids. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses, and the experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Statistical analyses were performed using Python (v.3.8.3). Unless otherwise specified, comparisons between two groups were performed using two-sided independent t-tests. Correlations were assessed using linear regression analyses. Sex- and gender-based analyses were not performed because the study was not designed to evaluate sex-specific effects, and the small sample size precludes meaningful stratified analysis.
