Recurrent Left Gastric Artery Pseudoaneurysm Treated by a Combined Interventional Endoscopic Ultrasound and Radiology Approach
Authors: Rutvij Tope, Sanjana Bhagwat, Abhishek Patil, Mukul Mutatkar, Rajendra Pujari, Amol Bapaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVisceral artery pseudoaneurysms (VAPAs) are a rare complication of pancreatitis, with the left gastric artery (LGA) among the least commonly involved vessels. Endoscopic ultrasound–guided coiling and glue embolization have shown great success in their management; however, the literature on the disease course of LGA VAPAs after successful embolization is limited. We report a case of LGA VAPA, as a complication of chronic pancreatitis, which recurred 3 times and required a total of 4 embolization procedures (3 endoscopic ultrasound-guided). This case highlights the need for regular postembolization monitoring for recurrence of the LGA VAPA, despite a successful embolization procedure.

INTRODUCTION

Visceral artery pseudoaneurysms (VAPAs) are a known complication of pancreatitis with a reported prevalence of 0.01% to 0.2%.1 Rupture of a VAPA can lead to dangerous intraluminal or intra-abdominal hemorrhage and is associated with high mortality, making early diagnosis and management crucial.2The splenic artery is most commonly involved (60%), followed by the hepatic artery (20%).3 However, a pseudoaneurysm (PA) of the left gastric artery (LGA) is uncommon.2,3 We report a case of a recurrent LGA-PA as a complication of chronic pancreatitis, in a patient with left sided portal hypertension, requiring multiple embolization procedures (both endoscopic ultrasound [EUS]–guided and conventional angiographic).

CASE REPORT

A 24-year-old man, with chronic pancreatitis and suspected left-sided portal hypertension presented with gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed, which showed no evidence of bleed from the ampulla or elsewhere in the upper GI tract. Hence, computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed which showed a peripancreatic collection communicating with the main pancreatic duct. A PA arising from the LGA was seen in the gastrohepatic space (measuring 17.6 × 15.1 mm) (Figure 1) with hyperdensity tracking from the PA to the collection, suggestive of acute hemorrhage. Using EUS-guided technique, the PA was embolized using coil and cyanoacrylate glue. Embolization was confirmed with absent flow on Doppler imaging (Figure 2). Follow-up CTA and a repeat EUS 21 days later showed previous coils in situ and no flow on Doppler. He then underwent endoscopic retrograde pancreatography, pancreatic duct (PD) sphincterotomy, and PD stenting.

First episode. (A) LGA PA measuring 17.6 × 15.1 mm on linear EUS. (B) Patent PA showing high flow on Doppler. (C) Coil being placed in the PA. (D) Doppler showing absent flow after coiling and glue embolization. EUS, endoscopic ultrasound; LGA, left gastric artery; PA, pseudoaneurysm.

Seventy-two days postcoiling, the patient presented with GI bleed and a significant drop in hemoglobin by 4 g/dL requiring 3 packed cell transfusions. EGD showed an ulcer with a suspicious bleeding spot along the lesser curvature, which was the site of puncture during previous coiling. However, there was no active bleeding. CTA did not demonstrate recurrent or residual PA hence conventional angiography was performed. This showed a recurrent PA adjacent to the previously placed coils which seemed to be the most likely cause of bleed (Figure 3). Positioning of the catheter was difficult; however, angioembolization of the branch of LGA was successfully performed using coils. The patient had no further bleed and was discharged.

Second episode. (A) Esophagogastroduodenoscopy demonstrated a suspicious forming ulcer on the lesser curvature - at the site of previous puncture. (B) Computed tomography angiography showing previous coils in place. The presence of a PA could not be commented upon due to significant coil-related artefacts in the area. (C) Conventional angiography showing a new PA adjacent to the previously placed coils. LGA, left gastric artery; PA, pseudoaneurysm.

Ninty-two days after the second episode, he presented with recurrent bleeding and a hemoglobin of 2.6 mg/dL. The CTA showed a possibility of a new PA, but the vessel of origin could not be commented on due to coil-related artefacts. EGD showed a deep ulcer at the site of previous transgastric puncture and coiling, but no active bleeding. EUS confirmed a new PA (≈5.9 mm) of the LGA, adjacent to the previous one, which was managed by EUS guided coiling and cyanoacrylate glue. Complete embolization was confirmed with absent flow on Doppler (Figure 4). The patient was discharged after stabilization.

Third episode. (A) EGD showing possibly the same ulcer, now appearing to be a deep ulcer, on the lesser curvature. No active bleeding was seen. (B) Computed tomography angiography showing a possible PA superior to the previously placed coils. (C) Doppler on EUS showing flow through the PA measuring about 5.9 mm in diameter. (D) EUS image showing the coils in place. EGD, esophagogastroduodenoscopy; EUS, endoscopic ultrasound; LGA, left gastric artery; PA, pseudoaneurysm.

Twenty-four days after this episode, once again the patient presented with upper GI bleed. EGD showed a healing deep ulcer on the lesser curvature of the stomach without active bleeding. On EUS, an ≈ 8.6-mm feeder vessel was noted in close vicinity to this ulcer and the previously placed coils. This vessel was coiled and embolized, and the patient was discharged. Forty-five days later, the patient's hemoglobin had stabilized and a repeat EUS showed the previous coils and glue in situ with no recurrence of the PA.

At the 92 days of follow-up after the last bleeding episode, EUS showed coils in situ, with no flow on Doppler and no new PA (Figure 5). Endoscopic retrograde pancreatography, and PD stent exchange was performed. A total of 113 days passed since the last PA was coiled, and 92 days since the feeder vessel was coiled. Thus, in nearly 4 months of follow-up, the PA has not recurred.

DISCUSSION

Although the incidence of LGA VAPA is low, and recurrence of VAPA even lower, rupture can lead to life-threatening GI bleed, associated with mortality rates of up to 70% if left untreated.2 Since there is no reliable marker to predict rupture, all VAPAs irrespective of their size must be treated with embolization.4 Owing to the high mortality rate following rupture, maintaining close vigilance postembolization is vital.5 EUS-guided embolization has shown similar success rates as compared with conventional angiography for treatment of VAPAs. EUS-guided embolization has also shown promise as a salvage modality after failure of radiologic embolization. Hence, EUS-guided embolization may be preferred in view of difficult location of VAPAs.6

Our patient presented with GI bleed. EGD showed no bleeding source; CTA, however, showed an LGA-PA. EUS-guided coiling and glue embolization was performed. Although obliteration of the PA was confirmed, the PA recurred 72 days later. Thus, we pursued a different technique (conventional angioembolization), with successfully embolization of the PA. Although successful, this procedure proved to be technically challenging due to the tortuous arterial anatomy and preexisting coil. Thus, the in the third instance, we again opted for an EUS-guided approach.

It is important to differentiate treatment failure (residual PA) from PA recurrence. After each embolization, absence of flow was confirmed by either Doppler or angiography. This was followed by at least a month-long period of recovery before abrupt presentations to the emergency room. Imaging at each presentation showed new PAs and feeder vessels at locations adjacent to the previous coils. This pattern is more consistent with recurrence than treatment failure.

A thorough literature review revealed only one prior instance of recurrent LGA PA.7 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported instance of multiple recurrences of an LGA PA following both EUS-guided coiling and conventional angioembolization—2 techniques which have demonstrated very high success rates in embolizing VAPAs.5,8 The appearance of new PAs at adjacent locations points toward the dynamic nature of vascular remodeling seen after acute or chronic pancreatitis.

Therefore, despite their rarity, all cases of LGA VAPA warrant embolization. Recurrence remains a possibility regardless of the treatment modality, underscoring the need for vigilant postembolization monitoring. Further studies are required to quantify the risk of LGA VAPA recurrence after successful embolization using either of the above techniques.
