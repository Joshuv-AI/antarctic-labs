Recombinant adeno-associated virus-mediated HSV-TK/GCV gene therapy for ovarian cancer: an experimental study
Authors: Yanqiang Xiong, Wu Zhou, Jianlin Yang, Yajun Li, Ye Shen, Yafeng Lv, Chan Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer is one of the most aggressive malignancies of the female reproductive system, and the 5-year survival rate is lower than 45%. This study aimed to evaluate a selectively targeted adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector for ovarian cancer, and to investigate its specificity and antitumor efficacy in order to provide experimental evidence for gene therapy of ovarian cancer.MethodsThe epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM)-specific binder EC1 was fused into the AAV2 capsid protein VP2 to construct an EpCAM-targeted recombinant vector (AAV2MEC1). The targeting and transduction capacity of AAV2MEC1 were assessed in vitro and in vivo. The antitumor effect of AAV2MEC1 was evaluated using the herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase/ganciclovir (HSV-TK/GCV) suicide gene system.ResultsAAV2MEC1 specifically infected ovarian cancer SKOV3 cells and efficiently delivered the HSV-TK gene to tumor tissues. Compared with wild-type AAV2, AAV2MEC1 markedly increased local reporter gene expression in tumors, while reporter gene expression was barely detectable in mouse tissues including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney. In the presence of GCV, AAV2MEC1-TK significantly inhibited tumor growth. At the experimental endpoint, tumor volume and tumor weight in the AAV2MEC1-TK group were reduced by 1.8-fold and 2.3-fold, respectively, compared with those in the AAV2-TK group. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in the AAV2MEC1-TK group were 13.33 U/L and 14.52 U/L, respectively, which were significantly lower than those in the AAV2-TK group (132.92 U/L and 79.09 U/L, respectively)ConclusionsAAV2MEC1 is a gene delivery vector with favorable tumor targeting that enables selective in vivo targeting of ovarian cancer, providing experimental support for precision gene therapy in ovarian cancer.

Highlight box

Key findings

• AAV2MEC1 showed marked tumor accumulation in an SKOV3 ovarian cancer model.

• Using the herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase/ganciclovir (HSV-TK/GCV) suicide system, AAV2MEC1-TK combined with GCV significantly inhibited tumor growth compared with AAV2-TK, with significantly reduced endpoint tumor volume and weight.

• Compared with wild-type AAV2, AAV2MEC1 markedly reduced hepatic biodistribution and liver injury (histology and aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase), indicating improved systemic safety.

What is known and what is new?

• Wild-type adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) exhibit inherent tissue tropism (e.g., AAV2 preferentially accumulates in the liver). Epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) is highly expressed in many epithelial-derived malignancies (including ovarian cancer) but is limited in normal tissues. Modifying AAV with targeting ligands or binding domains can alter its tissue distribution.

• Incorporation of the EpCAM-specific DARPin (EC1) into AAV2 VP2 to generate AAV2MEC1 reprograms AAV2 tropism, enabling selective in vivo targeting of ovarian tumors while retaining or enhancing the antitumor efficacy of the therapeutic payload (HSV-TK) and substantially reducing hepatic off-targeting and toxicity.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Ligand-decorated AAV vectors can improve tumor selectivity and reduce organ toxicity after systemic administration, demonstrating potential for precision gene therapy in ovarian cancer.

• Advance rigorous preclinical evaluation—validate efficacy in intraperitoneal/orthotopic and metastatic models and across cell lines/PDX with varied EpCAM expression; assess long-term transgene expression, immunogenicity, and dose-response relationships and safety studies to support clinical translation.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is one of the most aggressive malignancies of the female reproductive system. Although its incidence ranks third among gynecologic malignancies, it has the highest mortality rate, the 5-year survival rate is lower than 45% (1,2). Surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy has improved short-term survival in recent years, however, drug resistance, recurrence, and distant metastasis remain major causes of poor prognosis (3). Therefore, developing novel therapies with high tumor specificity and low systemic toxicity is crucial for improving long-term survival.

Gene therapy, which uses exogenous genetic material to modulate gene expression, restore tumor suppressor function, activate antitumor immunity, or directly kill tumor cells, has become an important exploratory strategy for ovarian cancer treatment (4). Several gene therapy approaches have been investigated in ovarian cancer, including suicide gene therapy, tumor suppressor gene replacement, oncolytic virus therapy, RNA interference or microRNA-based therapy, immune gene therapy, and genome editing. Suicide gene therapy, represented by the herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase/ganciclovir (HSV-TK/GCV) system, can convert a relatively non-toxic prodrug into cytotoxic metabolites within transduced tumor cells and can also induce a bystander effect in neighboring malignant cells (5). This strategy has a clear mechanism and is suitable for evaluating tumor-targeted gene delivery systems. Tumor suppressor gene replacement, such as restoration of p53 or other cancer-related regulatory genes, may inhibit tumor proliferation and promote apoptosis; however, its efficacy is often limited by tumor heterogeneity and insufficient delivery efficiency (6). RNA interference and microRNA-based therapies can selectively silence oncogenic pathways or reverse drug resistance, but their clinical application requires stable and efficient delivery platforms (7). Oncolytic viruses can replicate selectively in tumor cells and induce direct lysis and antitumor immune responses, but safety, immune clearance, and controllability remain important concerns (8). Genome editing technologies, such as CRISPR/Cas systems, provide the possibility of precise gene modification, but off-target effects and delivery barriers still restrict their translation (9,10). Therefore, safe, efficient, and tumor-specific gene delivery remains a central bottleneck in ovarian cancer gene therapy.

Viral vectors are central to gene delivery research because of their high transduction efficiency. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) has low immunogenicity, can mediate long-term stable expression, and can infect both dividing and nondividing cells (11,12). AAV has been widely applied in gene therapies for neurological disorders, inherited metabolic diseases, and ophthalmic diseases, and several AAV-based products have been clinically approved (13-15). These successful clinical applications demonstrate the translational potential of AAV vectors. Nevertheless, most approved AAV-based therapies are designed for monogenic diseases rather than solid tumors. Compared with genetic disorders, treatment of solid tumors places higher demands on delivery vectors, particularly with respect to tumor specificity, safety, and control of in vivo biodistribution (16).

Different AAV serotypes exhibit intrinsic tissue tropism: for example, AAV2 and AAV8 tend to accumulate in the liver after systemic administration, whereas AAV5 and AAV6 are more likely to infect lung tissue (17-19). Such natural tropism limits AAV application in tumor gene therapy: high transduction of non-target tissues (especially liver) may cause toxicity, while insufficient local transduction of tumors reduces antitumor efficacy. Thus, preserving the advantages of AAV while achieving efficient, selective targeting of tumor tissue is a core challenge in AAV-based cancer applications. Capsid engineering to alter receptor recognition and achieve targeted delivery has recently become a focus. Introducing specific ligands, peptides, or antibody fragments into the AAV capsid can confer new targeting capabilities, reduce dependence on native receptors, and reshape in vivo biodistribution (17,20). Targeted AAVs have demonstrated improved tumor selectivity and safety compared with wild-type AAV in various tumor models, providing theoretical and experimental rationale for their application in cancer gene therapy (21).

Epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein widely expressed in epithelial-derived malignancies including ovarian, breast, and colorectal cancers (22,23). EpCAM plays important roles in tumor initiation, progression and metastasis; its overexpression often correlates with poor differentiation, increased invasiveness, and worse prognosis (24). Critically, EpCAM expression in most normal tissues is relatively low and is mainly restricted to the basolateral membrane of epithelial cells, making it an attractive tumor targeting antigen.

Designed ankyrin repeat proteins (DARPins) are engineered small proteins characterized by structural stability, high affinity and low immunogenicity. The EpCAM-specific DARPin EC1 was selected for AAV2 capsid modification for several reasons. First, EC1 specifically recognizes EpCAM, which is highly expressed in many ovarian cancer cells, including SKOV3 cells, thereby providing a molecular basis for tumor-selective binding (25). Second, EC1 has a relatively small molecular size and stable structure, which makes it more suitable for incorporation into viral capsid proteins than large antibody fragments. Third, fusion into VP2 is expected to display EC1 on the virion surface while preserving the essential structural functions of VP1 and VP3, thereby maintaining vector packaging and infectivity. Theoretically, this EC1-VP2 fusion strategy may reduce dependence on native AAV2 receptor-mediated entry, redirect AAV2 tropism toward EpCAM-positive tumor cells, increase local tumor transduction, reduce nonspecific hepatic distribution, and improve therapeutic safety.

In prior work, we integrated the EpCAM-specific DARPin EC1 into the AAV2 VP2 capsid protein to construct the EpCAM-targeted recombinant vector AAV2MEC1 and demonstrated its favorable targeting in breast cancer models (26). In this study, we evaluated AAV2MEC1 in ovarian cancer models. We systematically assessed its in vitro and in vivo targeting and delivery capabilities and combined it with the HSV-TK/GCV suicide gene system to investigate antitumor efficacy. We further explored the potential clinical relevance of AAV2MEC1-mediated HSV-TK/GCV therapy and expanded the discussion of safety issues, including non-hepatic toxicity and the need for future assessment of vector genome persistence or integration. This study aimed to provide experimental evidence for precision gene therapy of EpCAM-positive ovarian cancer. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0408/rc).

Methods

AAV packaging and purification

HEK-293T cells provided by Professor Zhang Ye from Peking Union Medical College. Recombinant AAVs were packaged in HEK-293T cells using a three-plasmid system. For AAV2 production, pAAV-Rep/Cap, the transgene plasmid, and pHelper were co-transfected at a mass ratio of 8:6:10. For AAV2MEC1 production, pRVP1/3M, pEC1-VP2M, the transgene plasmid, and pHelper were co-transfected at a mass ratio of 6:6:6:10. HEK-293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide (CO2). Cells were transfected when they reached approximately 70–80% confluence. Transfection was performed using (insert transfection reagent or method) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 60–72 h post-transfection, culture supernatants and cell pellets were collected. Five-fold PEG8000 (final concentration 1×) was added to the supernatant and incubated at 4 ℃ overnight, followed by centrifugation at 5,000 r/min for 60 min at 4 ℃ to collect the precipitate. The precipitate was combined with freeze-thaw lysates from cell pellets, resuspended, and treated with a universal nuclease at 37 ℃ for 1 h to remove residual host DNA and RNA. Crude virus preparations were purified by iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation and concentrated/buffer-exchanged using 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff ultrafiltration devices (Millipore, MA, USA) to obtain high-titer AAV.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) titration of virus

AAV genome titers were determined by quantitative real-time PCR targeting the AAV inverted terminal repeat (ITR). Primer sequences: forward 5'-CTCAATGGGTGGAGTATTT-3'; reverse 5'-GAGTGAAGCAGAACGTGGG-3'. PCR conditions: 95 ℃ for 3 min; 39 cycles of 95 ℃ for 10 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. Standard curves were generated from serial dilutions of ITR plasmid standards of known copy number. Viral genome copies per mL (vg/mL) were calculated from sample Ct values. Each sample was tested in technical triplicate. A no-template control and plasmid standard control were included in each run. Only standard curves with acceptable amplification efficiency and linearity were used for viral genome titer calculation.

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

All animal experiments were conducted according to the “Three Gorges University Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of China Three Gorges University (No. 2024010K1). Female BALB/c-nu mice (4–5 weeks old) were obtained from Wuhan Luobin Life Technology Co., Ltd. [License SCXK(E)2024-0036]. SKOV3 cells provided by Professor Yuan Cheng from China Three Gorges University, they were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum at 37 ℃ in an incubator with 5% CO2. For subcutaneous xenografts, 2×106 SKOV3 cells in 100 µL were injected into the dorsal flank. When tumors reached approximately 3 mm in diameter (~1 week), mice were injected via tail vein with 2×1011 vg of AAV2-luciferase or AAV2MEC1-luciferase. One week after viral injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and given intraperitoneal D-luciferin (20 mg/kg; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Images were acquired 15–20 min after luciferin injection using an IVIS Lumina XRMS system (PerkinElmer, USA). Imaging data were analyzed with the manufacturer’s software. For ex vivo imaging, tumors and major organs including liver, heart, lung, kidney, and spleen were harvested after euthanasia and imaged under the same exposure conditions.

In vivo antitumor experiment

When subcutaneous tumors reached ~3 mm diameter, mice were randomized into three groups (n=6 per group) and treated with saline [normal saline (NS)], AAV2-TK, or AAV2MEC1-TK. Viral vectors were administered by tail-vein injection. The viral dose was 2×1011 vg per mouse. Forty-eight hours after viral delivery, mice received intraperitoneal GCV (100 mg/kg) every other day. Body weight and tumor dimensions were recorded every 2 days. At study end, mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia; tumors and organs were harvested and fixed. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (length × width^2)/2. Mice were monitored for general condition, activity, food intake, body weight change, and signs of distress throughout the experiment.

Liver and kidney function assays

Under anesthesia, blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus and allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h, then centrifuged at 3,000 r/min for 10 min to obtain serum. Serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (GOT) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (GPT) activities were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Endpoint absorbance at 510 nm was measured with a microplate reader. Sample net absorbance values were calculated against controls and converted to enzyme activities using standard curves.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Five-micrometer sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 70%) to water. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer under pressure for 5–8 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2. Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibody against luciferase (1:400; CSB-MA315104, Cusabio). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:200; Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei) at room temperature for 1 h and developed with DAB. Slides were air-dried and examined by light microscopy for histological analysis.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Paraffin sections (5 µm) of heart, liver, kidney and lung were stained with H&E using an automated stained and coverslipped with neutral resin. Slides were observed under light microscopy for pathological assessment. Pathological evaluation focused on tissue architecture, inflammatory infiltration, cellular degeneration, necrosis, and other treatment-related abnormalities. Representative images were selected from each group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and figure generation were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were performed using two-way ANOVA or unpaired t-tests as appropriate. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Targeting specificity of AAV2MEC1 for ovarian cancer cells

To evaluate the in vivo targeting and transduction efficiency of the AAV2MEC1 vector in SKOV3 tumors, 2×106 SKOV3 cells were implanted subcutaneously into the dorsal flank of each female BALB/c-nu mice. Mice then received a single tail-vein injection of 2×1011 vg of luciferase-expressing AAV2 or AAV2MEC1. Seven days post-injection, in vivo bioluminescence imaging was performed. Mice administered AAV2 exhibited broadly distributed signals, with strong signal predominating in the thoracic region, whereas mice in the AAV2MEC1 group displayed intense bioluminescent signals exclusively localized to the tumors, with no detectable signal in other tissues (Figure 1A). Ex vivo imaging of harvested organs further indicated that the signal in the AAV2 group originated primarily from the liver, while the signal in the AAV2MEC1 group originated from the tumor tissue (Figure 1B). Luciferase expression in tumor and liver sections was subsequently assessed by IHC; staining patterns were consistent with the imaging results, and tumor sections from the AAV2MEC1 group showed strong DAB staining (dark brown) (Figure 2). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the engineered AAV2MEC1 vector selectively targets SKOV3 tumors in vivo.

In vivo inhibitory effect of AAV2MEC1 on ovarian cancer

HSV/TK induces cytotoxicity by phosphorylating GCV into the toxic metabolite GCV triphosphate. In a subcutaneous SKOV3 xenograft model in BALB/c-nu mice, AAV vectors encoding HSV/TK were administered via a single tail-vein injection on day 7. Two days later, GCV was delivered intraperitoneally at 100 mg/kg every other day for a total of six doses (Figure 3A). Over the 13-day treatment period, tumor growth was comparable between the AAV2 and NS groups, whereas mice receiving targeted suicide-gene delivery (AAV2MEC1 group) exhibited a marked reduction in tumor growth beginning on day 4 (Figure 3B). At the end of the study, mice were euthanized and tumors were harvested (Figure 3C); tumor weights in the AAV2MEC1 group were significantly lower than those in the AAV2 and NS groups (Figure 3D). Together, these data indicate that AAV2MEC1-mediated delivery of HSV/TK effectively suppresses tumor progression in vivo.

To assess potential off-target toxicity, five major organs from each group were collected for H&E staining. In the AAV2 group, liver sections showed disruption of hepatic cord architecture, sinusoidal dilation and congestion, focal stromal fibrosis, and prominent lymphocytic infiltration. Splenic hemorrhage was also observed, and kidney sections exhibited interstitial vascular dilation and congestion with perivascular lymphocytic infiltration. In contrast, these pathological changes were not detected in the AAV2MEC1 group (Figure 4). Consistent with the histopathological findings, serum AST and ALT levels were significantly higher in the AAV2 group than in the NS and AAV2MEC1 groups (Figure 5). Collectively, these results suggest that the engineered AAV2MEC1 vector enables targeted delivery of HSV/TK to SKOV3 tumors, achieving antitumor efficacy without overt hepatic or renal toxicity in vivo.

Discussion

Ovarian cancer’s insidious onset, high recurrence rate and chemotherapy resistance limit the effectiveness of conventional surgery plus platinum chemotherapy, highlighting the need for therapies with greater tumor selectivity and lower systemic toxicity (3,27). This study addressed the challenge of improving AAV targeting to ovarian tumors by developing the capsid-engineered AAV2MEC1 vector and evaluating its therapeutic potential with the HSV-TK/GCV suicide gene system.

Gene therapy for ovarian cancer has been explored through multiple strategies, including suicide gene therapy, immune-modulatory gene delivery, tumor suppressor gene restoration, RNA interference, oncolytic virotherapy, and genome editing. Each approach has specific advantages and limitations (5,6,28). Suicide gene therapy has a clear mechanism and can generate a bystander killing effect, but its therapeutic efficacy depends strongly on efficient tumor-specific gene delivery. Immune gene therapy can reshape the tumor microenvironment, but its activity may be influenced by immune suppression within ovarian cancer ascites and peritoneal metastases. RNA-based therapies are highly flexible but require repeated delivery or stable vectors. Oncolytic viruses may achieve direct tumor lysis and immune activation, but safety and controllability remain concerns. Therefore, vector targeting and delivery specificity are central determinants of therapeutic success.

AAV2’s natural hepatic tropism following systemic administration can be advantageous for liver-directed therapies but problematic for cancer treatment, as non-target transduction increases toxicity and reduces intratumoral delivery (29). Our biodistribution data show that AAV2MEC1 reduces hepatic accumulation and concentrates transgene expression in tumors, consistent with reports that capsid engineering can redirect AAV tropism and improve tumor selectivity.

EpCAM was chosen as the targeting antigen due to its high expression in ovarian cancer and limited distribution in normal tissues (30). IHC and in vivo imaging demonstrate colocalization of transgene expression with EpCAM-positive tumor regions, supporting an EpCAM-dependent targeting mechanism and validating the vector design.

The use of EC1 as the targeting ligand and VP2 as the fusion site was based on both biological and structural considerations. EC1 provides EpCAM-specific recognition (25), while VP2 modification allows ligand display on the AAV capsid surface without completely replacing the essential VP1/VP3 capsid framework. This configuration is expected to maintain vector packaging and infectivity while conferring a new receptor-binding property. Therefore, the EC1-VP2 fusion strategy may enhance selective binding to EpCAM-positive ovarian cancer cells, increase local transgene expression in tumor tissue, and reduce off-target distribution mediated by native AAV2 tropism.

The HSV-TK/GCV system was used as a proof-of-concept therapeutic modality. HSV-TK phosphorylates the prodrug GCV into cytotoxic metabolites, inducing tumor cell death and yielding a bystander effect that amplifies killing (31,32). Although newer gene modalities exist, HSV-TK/GCV remains a robust, well-characterized system for evaluating delivery platforms. Our results indicate that AAV2MEC1-TK plus GCV produces stronger tumor suppression than non-targeted AAV2-TK, implying more efficient local delivery and expression of the therapeutic gene.

From a clinical perspective, AAV2MEC1-mediated HSV-TK/GCV therapy may have potential value for ovarian cancer in several settings. First, ovarian cancer frequently presents with peritoneal dissemination and residual microscopic disease after cytoreductive surgery (33). A tumor-targeted vector capable of selectively delivering suicide genes to EpCAM-positive cells may be useful as an adjuvant strategy to eliminate residual tumor cells. Second, because EpCAM is expressed in many epithelial ovarian cancers, EpCAM-targeted AAV delivery may be suitable for molecularly selected patients with EpCAM-positive tumors (25). Third, the HSV-TK/GCV system allows pharmacological control because cytotoxicity depends on GCV administration, providing a degree of controllability compared with constitutively toxic gene expression. However, clinical translation would require optimization of delivery route, vector dose, patient selection according to EpCAM expression, repeated-administration feasibility, immune response monitoring, and long-term safety evaluation.

Safety is critical for viral vector translation. Histology and serum biochemistry indicate that AAV2MEC1 markedly reduces liver injury observed with wild-type AAV2, suggesting that EpCAM-targeted capsid modification lowers off-target hepatic transduction and improves overall safety—an important consideration for systemic administration.

Nevertheless, liver toxicity is only one component of vector safety. Potential adverse effects in other organs, inflammatory responses, immune responses against the capsid or transgene product, hematological toxicity, renal toxicity, reproductive toxicity, and long-term vector persistence should also be considered (34). In the present study, H&E staining of heart, lung, and kidney tissues provided preliminary evidence that obvious acute pathological injury was not observed in these organs. However, more comprehensive safety assays, including hematology, renal function, inflammatory cytokines, anti-AAV neutralizing antibodies, anti-transgene immune responses, and long-term tissue biodistribution, should be performed in future studies. Although AAV vectors generally show low integration frequency and predominantly persist as episomal genomes, the presence of vector DNA in the host genome cannot be ignored. Detection of vector genome integration or persistence should be included in further safety assessment. Limitations remain. The study employed subcutaneous xenografts that do not fully replicate intraperitoneal growth and dissemination of ovarian cancer. Assessments focused on short-term efficacy and acute safety; long-term transgene expression, immune responses, and vector immunogenicity were not evaluated. Furthermore, experiments were conducted in a single EpCAM-high cell line (SKOV3) validation across models with varied EpCAM expression is necessary to define the vector’s applicability.

In summary, AAV2MEC1 improves tumor selectivity and safety of AAV-mediated gene delivery in ovarian cancer and shows promising antitumor effects in the HSV-TK/GCV system. These findings provide experimental evidence for targeted AAV applications in ovarian cancer gene therapy and support further optimization and translational studies, including evaluation in orthotopic or disseminated models and extended immunological and long-term safety assessments.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated that the capsid-engineered AAV2MEC1 vector, displaying an EpCAM-specific DARPin on the VP2 subunit, achieves enhanced tumor-selective transgene expression while significantly reducing off-target hepatic toxicity in ovarian cancer models. Coupled with the HSV-TK/GCV suicide gene system, AAV2MEC1 mediated robust antitumor effects in vivo, outperforming non-targeted AAV2. These findings validate the EC1-VP2 strategy as a promising approach for precision gene therapy of EpCAM-positive malignancies.
