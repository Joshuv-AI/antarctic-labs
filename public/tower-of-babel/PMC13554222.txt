Optimizing the mechanical performance of sustainable industrial waste modified concrete using supervised machine learning modeling and feature importance analysis
Authors: Mohamed AbdelMongy, Md Alhaz Uddin, Md Habibur Rahman Sobuz, Samir Mazumder, Mohammad Azad, Md Kawsarul Islam Kabbo, Mohammed Jameel, Sani Aliyu Abubakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe goal of the current study is to close a significant gap in the literature about the representation of concrete produced from industrial waste. In example, prior research experiences low levels of model explainability, limited generalizability, and limited data, especially because of their inability to capture nonlinear interactions among features. In order to overcome these limitations, authors apply seven supervised machine learning models to a large dataset of 711 observations. The two main new features of the suggested strategy include a thorough, systematic assessment of ensemble learners and SHAP-based model interpretability. With a maximum test accuracy of R2 = 0.881, RMSE = 5.65 MPa, MAE = 4.17 MPa, and MAPE = 25.15%, the Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR) outperformed other cutting-edge models such as CatBoost (R2 = 0.879) and Histogram Gradient Boosting (R2 = 0.878). Based on the findings from the SHAP analysis, it is clear that the machine learning model shows more sensitivity to the fine aggregate content than to cement and other materials, making it the most influential factor in the global model. Water content and curing age follow. In actual use, the suggested model and its intuitive Python-based graphical user interface (GUI) can quickly and affordably estimate compressive strength without requiring expensive and time-consuming laboratory testing, guaranteeing the successful incorporation of industrial waste products into the concrete mixture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57625-9.Keywords: Compressive strength, industrial waste, Histogram gradient boosting, Extra trees regressor, Categorical boostingSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete is the most widely used construction material in the world, forming the literal foundation of our civilization1,2. The rapid pace of progress in the fields of economics and urbanization has led to an explosion of infrastructure development and subsequent decommissioning of tired infrastructure3–5. A key player is Portland Cement (PC), a key constituent of concrete. Cement production is a parasitic process that consumes high quantities of energy, leading to the emission of large amounts of carbon dioxide (CO2), causing rapid degradation of our planet6–9. Hence, it requires a move away from traditional manufacturing and towards alternative, eco-friendly sources of energy, which can ensure a sustainable path for the future10–13. Failure to do so will result in irreversible damage to posterity, which necessitates an improvement in the current recycling strategies that can help to provide alternatives to certain materials used in manufacturing, resulting in the least footprint on the environment14,15. Research has shown that the most feasible solution to solve the environmental concerns associated with the construction industry is the utilization of industrial by-products, which can act as supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs)16–21. The most commonly used materials for concrete, namely silica fume (SF), fly ash (F Ash), and GGBFS, have been successfully used to partially replace the clinker in cement, which can reduce greenhouse gas emissions from traditional concrete by up to 80%22–26. This solution addresses not just the natural resources used for concrete construction, but it also addresses the waste disposal problems of vast amounts of industrial waste sent to landfills27–31.

The intricacy and amount of industrial refuse also pose serious logistical problems related to disposal activities, further exacerbated by the increase in environmental mandates and the reduced landfill availability32–34. The construction industry may be able to improve sustainable resource activities by utilizing these materials as the aggregate within the concrete product29,31,35–46. Even though industrial waste-based concrete offers significant advantages over alternative concrete products, the use of such products is limited by the intricacy of this product47–50. This product is a heterogeneous composite material that exhibits mechanical properties as a result of an intricate relationship with cement, sand, gravel, and industrial by-products51,52. The compressive strength of concrete, which is the most vital property to ensure the safety and durability of structures, depends on a variety of nonlinear factors such as the water-binder ratio, particle size distribution of SCMs, percentages of aggregates, and curing conditions26,28,29,43,53–58.

The compressive strength of high-performance concrete materials has always been obtained by conducting experiments. This involves taking concrete materials and casting them in cubical or cylindrical forms, where they are allowed to cure for a specific period, weeks being the standard unit of time, which can usually be 7, 14, or 28 weeks. Afterwards, a compression test is conducted on these materials. Though this methodology is effective, labor-intensive experiments are expensive, time-consuming, and cumbersome to conduct59. The logistics of conducting experiments are adversely affected by insufficient space to allow these materials to cure60. Though empirical formulas have been put forward to effectively gauge the strength of these materials, these are ineffective when describing non-linear aspects involving concrete materials infused with waste materials. Thus, a high degree of urgency exists for developing advanced models to establish a nexus between input parameters and concrete materials infused with industrial waste.

The paradigm of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) has enabled a revolutionary approach in solving problems in civil engineering involving regression and classification61–65. ML is different from natural approaches as it can learn from data and establish patterns and relationships between variables with a high level of accuracy25,38,66–68. In addition, other techniques such as numerical simulations and modeling also garnered significant attention because of the low cost and rapid prediction of materials properties69–72. Various ML approaches, such as ANN, SVM, and DT, have already been employed for the evaluation of the mechanical properties of different types of concrete materials. Gradient Boosting (GB), Categorical Boosting, Bagging Regressor, and Random Forest (RF) can also be employed for predicting the strength of concrete with recycled aggregate concrete, where the main influencing factor is identified as cement content73,74. Other approaches, such as Multi-Expression Programming (MEP) and Gene Expression Programming (GEP), have demonstrated the merit of these approaches over conventional regression methods for alkali-activated concrete. It is worth mentioning that though these ML approaches contribute to cost-effective solutions, the latest trend of research has focused on ensemble learning to ensure better predictive efficiency of the model. Ensemble learning approaches combine several weak learners to develop a strong learner, which is able to perform better during generalization. For instance, the application of Gradient Boosting (GBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and CatBoost has demonstrated their merit against conventional approaches to the prediction of concrete strength. In fact, the development of stacked ML approaches, which combine the efficiency of GBoost, RF, and Extra Trees Regressor (ETR), has demonstrated their merit to the extent of achieving R2 values as high as 0.96 for concrete mixes involving concrete. In addition, the combination of DNNs with gradient boosting methods represents a comprehensive tool for the efficient prediction of tabular as well as complex non-linear data38,39,56,75,76. From simple to complex, this process has recently been even more streamlined with the development of Automated Machine Learning frameworks like AutoGluon through the automation of an expert-driven task, hyperparameter tuning. These hybrid approaches improved the R2 score from 5 to 10% and considerably reduced MAE, hence giving a strong basis for construction material design56,77–79. A major bottleneck towards the broad acceptance of AI in the construction sector is related to the “black box” nature of complex models like DNNs. Engineers have to be absolutely clear as to how a model arrives at a particular prediction to ensure structural safety. Recently, researchers started using interpretability tools like SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)80.

SHAP values provide a unified measure of feature importance, revealing the global and local contributions of features such as SF, F Ash content, water binder, and curing ages, among others. SHAP, for instance, might give insights into the contributions made towards the models’ ability to make the predicted outcomes, e.g., 20%-25% is contributed by the fly content in the decision-making involved in the modeling of the sustainable mixes of concrete materials42,81–83. Although progress has been made in addressing the problem using ML, there are still critical gaps in the existing literature regarding the modeling of concrete materials, regardless of the material source, due to small, limited data sets, which lead to overfitting. In addition, there is limited parametric analysis using robust tools such as SHAP and PDP in the analysis of the nonlinear sensitivity associated with industrial waste materials, modifying the concrete mixes. The majority of existing works consider network-based approaches, i.e., MLPNN and SVM, or tree-based approaches, i.e., RF and DT, with few works attempting to offer a comprehensive comparison or a hybrid combination of these quite different paradigms.

Using interpretability models like SHAP, which have advanced beyond the “black-box” approach to prediction, this research has made significant progress in predicting tangible qualities. For instance, Zhang, Pei-Fu, et al.84 were able to clarify the non-linear impacts of SCMs in high-performance and ultra-high performance concrete using hybrid ML-SHAP models. Similarly, Wang, Hongwei, et al.38 employed SHAP to examine how the water-to-cement ratio and FA content affected the compressive strength of the concrete. However, there is a significant research gap because many of the studies that are now available employ small data sets and do not take into account the complex relationships between variables in concrete mixed with various types of industrial wastes.

This research aims to fill these gaps by implementing a robust set of traditional and hybrid ML techniques, such as GBoost, Categorical Boosting, Bagging Regressor, Random Forest, Hist Gradient Boosting, Extra Trees Regressor, K-Nearest Neighbor models, on a comprehensive dataset of 711 data points collected from previous published Scopus indexed journals. The highest standards were used when creating this database to ensure that the testing protocols and the laboratory conditions in which the tests were performed were consistent. The study considers nine significant input variables, which represent a wide range of mix components and environmental factors. This study will ensure reliability in the results by performing cross-validation and considering multiple statistical metrics during both the training and testing phases.

While the SHAP approach and ensemble modeling techniques have been used in recent publications to predict concrete strength, this study focuses on analyzing the non-linear mechanical properties of sustainable concrete affected by various types of industrial by-products during its early-age curing period. Specifically, the study uses a unique set of 711 samples to demonstrate that, according to sensitivity tests conducted using the SHAP approach, the primary strength determinant for such waste-based concrete mixes is the fine aggregate content rather than the cement itself.

Research significance

This study is of immense significance in the context of the burning need to achieve sustainable construction through the optimization of concrete modified by industrial waste via a supervised machine learning algorithm. Though the existing body of knowledge had hitherto been restricted to the use of conventional mix or small data sets to make predictions, the work presented in this study utilized a large data of 711 samples, well above the benchmark required to make a quality model, to make accurate high-precision predictions of the compressive strength. Unlike the existing body of knowledge, where the predictive models were considered “black boxes,” this work included SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to understand the effects of variables such as the amount of fine aggregate and the amount of water, along with other factors, in determining the strength. The study shows that when multiple ensemble learners, such as Gradient Boosting and Random Forest, are compared, they offer higher statistical reliability and accuracy than does existing literature. Moreover, such findings expose critical interactions of materials, such as early curing and the influence of different amounts of industrial waste, not well covered in previous works. This research eventually provides a robust, data-driven framework that enables engineers to optimize the use of industrial by-products without compromising structural integrity, hence moving toward an environmentally friendly and economical approach in modern concrete technology. Methodology

The construction flowchart for the predictive model involves several key steps, shown in Fig. 1. Initially, a dataset of sustainable industrial waste-modified concrete is created. This is followed by the selection of essential features while filtering out unnecessary data. The dataset is then divided into training and testing sets to assess its performance. The fourth stage involves training the different models using the training set, with performance evaluated through a five-fold cross-validation process aimed at optimizing hyperparameters. Once the model is tuned, it is tested on new, unseen data to ensure reliability, and if the accuracy is satisfactory, the model is finalized. The final step utilizes SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to analyze the influence of each feature on model predictions, identifying key variables and their trends in predicting concrete compressive strength (CS).

Data collection and description

The quantity of data points in a dataset has a major impact on a machine learning model’s dependability. The Compressive Strength (CS) of sustainable industrial waste modified concrete was predicted in this work using a dataset with nine input factors. The input parameter for this study, industrial waste, is regarded as a composite variable that refers to materials with almost equivalent impacts on the mechanical and structural development of the concrete matrix and extremely similar chemical properties. In this regard, industrial waste types such as waste glass powder, waste marble powder, ceramic waste powder, limestone powder, ground granulated blast furnace slag and metakaolin are among the 711 samples acquired for the tests. The materials are organized together to enhance the effectiveness of machine learning algorithms due to their shared additional binding agents or micro-fillers. A rigorous gathering information process was carefully adhered to in data extraction in order to ensure high reproducibility and minimize variations resulting from various testing conditions. Specifically, only those data points that met the following criteria were considered: (i) the concrete specimens were tested under very similar curing and testing conditions; (ii) the compressive strength was evaluated by means of standard tests (e.g., ASTM tests); and (iii) all information regarding mix designs, including the values of all nine input parameters, was available. The meta-data thus created can be employed as a reliable data set for supervised machine learning due to the above-mentioned rigorous data cleaning and filtering procedure, which minimizes the potential for systematic errors. The train-test split method (90% training data and 10% testing data) was used to test the model’s performance metrics for this particular study. It should be mentioned that although the GridSearchCV methodology used a 5-fold cross-validation strategy to optimize the model hyperparameters, the mean standard deviation values were not calculated using this method. Statistical uncertainty analysis is therefore outside the current scope of the study. The Standard Scaler approach, which centers the data around the mean and scales features to unit variance, was used to prepare the data. This procedure improves model performance by guaranteeing constant feature scaling, which is essential for techniques that are sensitive to feature scaling, such as gradient boosting regression. Model development

As illustrated in Fig. 12, the establishment of the GBR, model consists of three key steps:

Optimization process for tuning model hyperparameters and model evaluation100.

Step 1: Defining the Initial Hyperparameters.

Learning_rate, n_estimators, subsample, criteria, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf, max_depth, min_weight_fraction_leaf, min_impurity_decrease, max_features, loss, and alpha were the hyperparameters employed in this study. The sample domain for the optimization process is defined by the starting ranges and categories of these hyperparameters. For example, n_estimators are sampled between 50 and 500, but learning_rate is sampled from a logarithmic scale between 1 × 10− 4 and 1.0. This enables a large configuration space, improving the framework’s quick convergence to ideal parameters and enabling effective sampling.

Step 2: Training.

Instead of employing the initial 80:20 ratio, a repeated 5-fold cross-validation (CV) technique was used to increase the statistical significance of the model assessment. It is an iterative process that splits the data into five sets, trains the model on four partitions, then assesses it on the fifth partition. The cycle is repeated several times by mixing the data at random. By allowing each point in the database to be tested and trained several times on the same sample, this procedure greatly lowers the degree of bias in the outcomes. All rounds of the cross-validation procedure are averaged to determine the values of the metrics used in model assessment.

Step 3: Hyperparameter Optimization.

The tuning process for all machine learning models in this research project was performed using grid search cross-validation method known as GridSearchCV. Grid search cross-validation method exhaustively searched for the optimal parameter combination within a certain portion of the parameter space by performing five folds cross validation process. The ideal configuration is determined by combining the hyperparameters that produce the best average cross-validation performance.

The final model is trained on the whole training set using the optimal hyperparameters when the optimization procedure is finished. Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2) are then used to assess the model’s prediction performance on the test set. The outcomes validate the advantages of employing hyperparameter optimization by showcasing the model’s efficacy and capacity for generalization.

Feature importance (Shapley method)

Many machine learning algorithms are highly predictive, yet they frequently fail to provide an explanation for their predictions. In order to improve the explainability of compressive strength (CS) predictions for sustainable industrial waste modified concrete, this work presents SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). SHAP calculates how each input variable—including mix and material design characteristics—contributes to the model’s forecasts. It calculates SHAP values, which show how a feature’s knowledge affects the model’s output for particular predictions. SHAP uses Shapley values derived from game theory to combine local interpretability with efficient credit allocation. Both global and local interpretability are produced by this method; the former shows how feature distributions impact overall model performance, while the latter makes clear how particular characteristics contribute to particular predictions. SHAP’s primary objective is to connect model outputs to specific attributes by evaluating their influence100. Missingness guarantees that features that are absent from a model contribute zero, resulting in Shapley values of zero for such features. According to consistency, a feature’s Shapley value will either rise or stay the same if a model is modified in a way that causes its marginal contribution to either increase or keep the same. Furthermore, tree-based SHAP has advantages like lower computing costs without sacrificing prediction accuracy. In order to improve the interpretability of the model’s predictions, this work calculates Shapley values using tree-based SHAP.

Performance evaluation metrics

To ensure that the performance of our predictive tool is assessed in a fair manner relative to other models that might be used on this scenario, a number of different metrics can be applied: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and the Coefficient of Determination (R2). While previous models tend to have assessed their output in terms of perhaps MAE and MAPE at best, we will examine a number of different factors in this case in order to provide a full assessment. MAE provides a mean level of error and does not take outliers into account as heavily due to the varying properties of concretes. While MAE is used for mean purposes in this case due to practicality. RMSE calculates this result to a standardized level in regards to its original units for better understanding and interpretation, while R2 provides a representation of how much of the variances in the actual result within the data can be accounted for by this.

Model explainability and interpretability

Although the initial statistical analysis using distribution characteristics is a crucial beginning point that enables the investigator to gain a fundamental understanding of linear correlations and density distribution, it is insufficient for modeling the complex nonlinear relations typical of sustainable concrete compositions. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) approach, which helps go beyond straightforward correlations between variables toward a comprehensive high-dimensional viewpoint of machine learning algorithms, is used to overcome this issue. SHAP values represent a consistent measure of relevance for each characteristic and its marginal contribution to all potential sets of features, such as fine aggregate or curing age. This is the main difference between SHAP values and linear correlations.

Figure 16 shows that the average impact of the different parameters in the model can be observed in the form of a bar graph in the SHAP feature importance bar chart. The most significant impact was observed in the fine aggregate with an enormous positive impact value of 10.68. The amounts of fine aggregates determine the skeletal structure and ITZ qualities since the water-to-cement ratio is linked to the porosity and chemical bond strength of the cement paste matrix. In order to achieve the ideal packing density, which ensures a reduction in the volume of cement paste required to fill the voids within the aggregate framework and avoids stress concentration, fine aggregates are crucial to the micro-filling process. Furthermore, the model’s enhanced sensitivity to the fine aggregates’ concentration may be explained by their effect on the overall surface area involved in the interaction with the cement matrix, which in turn affects the material’s resistance to cracking. Followed by the water with 2.74, and curing age with 2.72, which have approximately the same significance. After that come the medium variables that have significant effects, such as the 1.37 impact by the coarse aggregate, 0.97 by the superplasticizer, and 0.91 by the industrial waste. The cement with an impact value of 0.72, fly ash with 0.52, and silica fume with 0.18, which indicates that the less significant importance differences between supplementary cementitious materials and aggregate variables exist. The model’s reported global sensitivity to the percentage of industrial waste components and other SCM components is a direct result of their distinct physicochemical characteristics. Finely ground industrial waste materials, such as waste glass powder, waste marble powder, ceramic waste powder, limestone powder, ground granulated blast furnace slag and metakaolin, have two effects when added to the sustainable concrete matrix: physical particle packing and late-stage pozzolanic reactions. On the one hand, the small particles of glass and marble powder serve as tiny fillers, ensuring the best possible particle packing and minimizing the cementitious matrix’s porosity from the beginning. However, industrial wastes that include reactive aluminosilicates like metakaolin and glass fines contribute to pozzolanic reactions whereby the unstable Ca(OH)2 crystals that have been produced through the hydration of Portland cement are consumed. Therefore, the latter are transformed to very stable gel forms of calcium silicate hydrates (C-S-H) and calcium aluminosilicate hydrates (C-A-S-H).

Bar plot of SHAP analysis of several different variables.

In addition, in the SHAP values graph in Fig. 17, the fine aggregate was observed as having the most significant impact, with its positive relation with SHAP Values (+ 5, + 15), but its small quantities reduce the output to -15. On the contrary, the large quantities in the dataset reduce the output with its negative SHAP values (-2, -4). However, in the curing age variable, it shows an entirely different pattern where its small quantities reduce the output significantly with − 7, but the longer age improves the output with its positive impact. Nevertheless, variables such as the coarse aggregate, superplasticizer, and industrial waste merely result in a moderate impact with an approximate value nears-zero, but the cement, fly ash and silica fume were shown with minimal impact in between SHAP values (-1, + 1). The importance of the packing density and the hydration of the concrete altered by the industrial waste is shown by the SHAP analysis results. Important factors connected to the internal skeleton structure that affect its load-bearing capacity are the volume fraction and the distribution of the fine aggregate particles. C-S-H gel forms as a result of improved hydration mechanisms brought on by aging. As a result, the transition zone between the cement paste and the industrial waste particles becomes denser as individuals age. The low importance of cement’s contribution as compared to fine aggregate suggests that, in these environmentally friendly combinations, optimizing the ratio of aggregate to binder is crucial for compressive strength.

Graphical user interface

This section outlines a significant advancement meant to meet the practical demands of engineers and designers for the effective implementation of hybrid boosting models. A Python web application has been created that features a model with optimized hyperparameters, accessible through a Graphical User Interface (GUI) tool. This GUI is designed to predict and classify the compressive strength (CS) of concrete using the best available model for both classification and prediction, as shown in Fig. 21. Users can input values for example Cement (30–604 kg/m3), fine aggregate (0–1032.8 kg/m3), coarse aggregate (305–2649 kg/m3), silica fume (0–150 kg/m3), fly ash (0–247 kg/m3), water (78–262 kg/m3), industrial waste (0–509 kg/m3), superplasticizer (0–24 kg/m3), and curing age (3-365 days), into a streamlined layout, with the CS value and its classification displayed dynamically after input. The GBR, the GUI engine, has outstanding generalization capabilities (Test R² = 0.881). A distinct validation approach that used an external dataset not used in the initial training process was used to assess the generated GUI-based web application’s reliability. The absence of fresh laboratory test results allowed for this. A range of peer-reviewed mixtures were employed in the external validation procedure to confirm the model’s generality. The compressive strength of the exterior mixtures was predicted using the GBR, and the results showed low variation. The database contains the web application’s code. By contrasting the GUI’s output with the held-out 10% test set, its validity is confirmed.

Conclusion

Improving the mechanical properties of sustainable concrete with the use of industrial waste materials is an important aspect in promoting sustainable construction. This is the first research to play a crucial role in using supervised machine learning to enable the estimation of the compressive strength of sustainable concrete. With the identification of key factors affecting sustainability, this research will be important in allowing reductions in costs in experiments and promoting sustainable materials for use in the construction sector.

Sustainable Waste Integration: The study was able to identify a proper way to integrate industrial waste, silica fume, and fly ash into concrete to ensure it remains strong while being environmentally sustainable. In this case, a simple explanation would be that this research involved the use of intelligent computer algorithms that can determine how strong green concrete will be without the need to construct and then destroy the concrete in a laboratory setting each time. Even though machine learning algorithms’ prediction skills have shown great success, there are a few limitations that must be acknowledged in order to appropriately evaluate the results. First off, despite the sizeable dataset (711 samples), there could be issues extrapolating the results to any mixes outside of the input parameter range under consideration, particularly those with odd water-to-cement ratios or curing conditions. The accuracy of the predictions provided in different geographic contexts may also be affected by the inherent variability of the chemical and physical properties of the waste utilized, which frequently depends on the region of collection and manufacturing operations. Lastly, the algorithms may have trouble predicting durations longer than the actual values since the majority of the data samples are concentrated at the normal ages for curing (e.g., 28 days).

Limitations and future works

The created ensemble learning models for the compressive strength of industrial waste-modified concrete have a high prediction reliability, it is important to highlight some limitations.

The initial limitation is that a different train-test split structure is used to assess the predictions of model performance. Fold-by-fold reporting of standard deviations and probabilistic bounding of variance were not carried out due to the higher computational expense of performing uncertainty quantification (UQ) processes for different ensemble learning algorithms. In our upcoming research, we will address the problem of conducting comprehensive UQ, which will demand particular algorithmic design and expertise.
