CLT-Forge: A Scalable Library for Cross-Layer Transcoders and Attribution Graphs
Florent Draye, Abir Harrasse, Vedant Palit, Tung-Yu Wu, Jiarui Liu, Punya Syon Pandey, Roderick Wu, Terry Jingchen Zhang, Zhijing Jin, Bernhard Schölkopf, [0.3em] Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems, Tübingen, Germany

Abstract
Mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand how Large Language Models (LLMs) represent and process information. Recent approaches based on dictionary learning and transcoders enable representing model computation in terms of sparse, interpretable features and their interactions, giving rise to feature attribution graphs. However, these graphs are often large and redundant, limiting their interpretability in practice. Cross-Layer Transcoders (CLTs) address this issue by sharing features across layers while preserving layer-specific decoding, yielding more compact representations, but remain difficult to train and analyze at scale. We introduce an open-source library for end-to-end training and interpretability of CLTs. Our framework integrates scalable distributed training with model sharding and compressed activation caching, a unified automated interpretability pipeline for feature analysis and explanation, attribution graph computation using Circuit-Tracer, and a flexible visualization interface. This provides a practical and unified solution for scaling CLT-based mechanistic interpretability. Our code is available at https://github.com/LLM-Interp/CLT-Forge .



1
Introduction

Figure 1:
Overview of the CLT-Forge framework.

Understanding how Large Language Models (LLMs) represent and process information is a central goal of mechanistic interpretability. A key assumption in this area is the
linear representation hypothesis
, which posits that semantic features are encoded as directions in the activation space of neural networks
(
Park et al. 2023
)
. Building on this idea,
transcoders
approximate the computation of MLP layers by replacing their dense nonlinear transformations with sparse, linear combinations of interpretable features computed from the same inputs
(
Dunefsky et al. 2024
)
. Since MLPs constitute a primary source of nonlinearity in transformer architectures, this substitution enables a structured view of model computation in terms of feature interactions. In particular, computation can be represented as a feature
attribution graph
, where nodes correspond to features and edges capture causal influence through linear pathways
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
;
Lindsey et al. 2025b
)
. Such graphs can be efficiently computed, pruned, analyzed, and intervened upon to yield mechanistic explanations to the internal working mechanism of LLMs
(
Hanna et al. 2025
)
.

However, a major limitation of this approach is that the size of attribution graphs can quickly grow to hundreds of nodes, making them difficult to analyze in practice.
This redundancy stems in part from the fact that similar features often emerge across multiple layers, leading to duplicated nodes that represent the same underlying concept in different forms.

Cross-Layer Transcoders (CLTs)
address this issue by sharing features across layers while allowing layer-specific decoding
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
)
. This reduces redundancy and produces much more compact and interpretable attribution graphs
(
Lindsey et al. 2025a
)
. However, CLTs introduce significant computational overhead in training due to their large parameter count.

Despite recent progress, there are limited open-source frameworks that support end-to-end training of CLTs at scale, where industry labs such as Anthropic do not release their proprietary framework.
Most open-source codebases largely lack essential components such as efficient distributed training with optimal model sharding, or activation caching with quantization and compression for memory efficiency. Furthermore, post-hoc analysis remains a major bottleneck: interpreting millions of features requires retrieving activating data, computing statistics, and generating explanations (e.g., via automated interpretability), yet existing tools are fragmented and poorly integrated. Visualization is similarly limited; while tools such as Neuronpedia
(
Lin and Bloom
)
provide very useful interfaces, they remain difficult to extend and integrate into research workflows.

In this work, we introduce the first unified library for CLTs that enables scalable training and post-hoc interpretability analysis. Our contributions are:

•

Scalable training infrastructure
: Support for large-scale CLT training with efficient GPU sharding and optimized activation caching with compression and quantization.

•

Efficient attribution computation
: Native integration with Circuit Tracer
(
Hanna et al. 2025
)
to efficiently compute and prune feature-level attribution graphs.

•

Built-in workflow for automated interpretability (autointerp) with flexible visual interface
: A unified and scalable autointerp pipeline with a visual interface for exploring features, visualizing attribution graphs, and performing interventions.

The code is released under the MIT License and a demonstration video is available at
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GRzU9g7qsIU
.

2
Preliminary

2.1
Cross-Layer Transcoders

Let
h_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}
denote the input to the MLP at layer
\ell
for a single token position. In both standard and cross-layer transcoders, this representation is first projected into a sparse feature space via an encoder:

z_{\ell}=\sigma\left(W^{\ell}_{\text{enc}}h_{\ell}+b^{\ell}_{\text{enc}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{features}}},

(1)

where
W^{\ell}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{features}}\times d_{\text{model}}}
and
b^{\ell}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{features}}}
are layer-specific parameters, and
\sigma
denotes a sparsifying nonlinearity. In practice, we use JumpReLU as
\sigma
. In a standard transcoder, the MLP output at layer
\ell
is reconstructed by decoding features from the same layer:

\hat{m}_{\ell}=W^{\ell}_{\text{dec}}z_{\ell}+b^{\ell}_{\text{dec}},

(2)

where
W^{\ell}_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{features}}}
and
b^{\ell}_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}
.

Cross-Layer Transcoders (CLTs) extend this formulation by allowing features extracted at earlier layers to contribute to the reconstruction at later layers. The MLP output at a target layer
\ell^{\prime}
is reconstructed as:

\hat{m}_{\ell^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell\leq\ell^{\prime}}W^{\ell\rightarrow\ell^{\prime}}_{\text{dec}}\,z_{\ell}+b^{\ell^{\prime}}_{\text{dec}},

(3)

where
W^{\ell\rightarrow\ell^{\prime}}_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{features}}}
maps features from layer
\ell
to layer
\ell^{\prime}
.

This formulation reflects the intuition that semantic features can persist across layers while undergoing transformations. For a detailed comparison between cross-layer transcoders and
standard transcoders, we refer the reader to
(
Lindsey et al. 2025a
)
.

A CLT can therefore be viewed as associating each transformer layer with an encoder
\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{ed}
and every ordered layer pair
(\ell_{s}<\ell_{t})
with a decoder
\mathbb{R}^{ed}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
ed=d_{\text{features}}
. Ignoring biases, the total parameter count scales as

\displaystyle N_{\text{CLT}}(L,d,e)=\left(\frac{L(L-1)}{2}+L\right)ed^{2},

(4)

highlighting quadratic growth in both the number of layers and model dimension. As an example, for a LLaMA 3.2 1B model with
L=16
,
d=2048
, and
e=48
, this corresponds to approximately
27.4
B parameters.

The main developments on CLTs originate from two works by Anthropic
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
;
Lindsey et al. 2025b
)
, which introduced the training procedures, attribution methods, graph pruning techniques, visualization, and intervention frameworks. More recently, there has been a broader effort to open-source CLTs and systematically evaluate them against standard transcoders
(
Lindsey et al. 2025a
)
.
Despite these advances, applications of CLTs in the literature remain limited. Existing studies include a replication of the “greater-than” mechanism
(
Merullo et al. 2025
)
and a multilingual analysis
(
Harrasse et al. 2025
)
, highlighting both the promise of CLTs and the need for more accessible and scalable infrastructure.

One existing open-source library for large-scale CLT training is Eleuther AI’s
CLT-Training
fork of Sparsify
(
EleutherAI 2024
)
, which relies on on-the-fly activation computation. Additionally, their work focuses on
TopK CLTs
, where only a small subset of features is active during decoding. In contrast, we do not impose activation sparsity, precluding the use of specialized kernels. Another library
(
Tigges 2025
)
also supports feature sharding along the feature dimension with flexible activation functions. However, it does not provide a unified framework integrating scalable training with automated interpretability, attribution analysis, and interactive visualization. Other libraries support CLT training for smaller-scale models (e.g., GPT-2) using Distributed Data Parallel (DDP)
(
Tredal 2025
;
Lange 2025
)
.

2.2
Attribution Graph

Following
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
)
, the attribution score between feature
n
at layer
\ell
and position
k
, and feature
n^{\prime}
at layer
\ell^{\prime}
and position
k^{\prime}
is:

a_{\ell,k,n}^{\ell^{\prime},k^{\prime},n^{\prime}}=f_{k,n}^{\ell}\;J_{\ell,k}^{\ell^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\;g_{k^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}.

(5)

Here,
f_{k,n}^{\ell}
denotes the decoder vector associated with feature
n
at layer
\ell
and position
k
, while
g_{k^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}
denotes the corresponding encoder vector for feature
n^{\prime}
at layer
\ell^{\prime}
and position
k^{\prime}
. The term
J_{\ell,k}^{\ell^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
is the Jacobian mapping the MLP output at
(\ell,k)
to the MLP input at
(\ell^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. This Jacobian is computed during a forward pass of the model, where nonlinearities have been frozen with a stop-gradient operation. See
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
)
for more details. This workflow has been open-sourced in the Circuit-Tracer library
(
Hanna et al. 2025
)
.

2.3
Autointerp Workflow and Visual Interface

Once a dictionary learning model, such as an SAE or a CLT, has been trained, interpreting its features typically involves revisiting the input data to identify the most activating sequences for each feature, as well as computing summary statistics (e.g., most active tokens). These signals are then used to generate natural language explanations, often via prompting an LLM
(
Bricken et al. 2023
)
.

Several libraries support parts of this automated interpretability workflow. For example, Delphi
(
Paulo et al. 2024
)
focuses on extracting and caching latent activations, constructing prompts, and generating explanations. For visualization,
(
McDougall 2024
)
provides an interface for exploring individual features, which is an open-source replica of Anthropic’s foundational work
(
Bricken et al. 2023
)
. Finally, Neuronpedia provides a comprehensive interface for exploring learned features across models, including an attribution graph interface supporting clustering, interventions, and feature analysis
(
Lin and Bloom
)
. However, its complexity can make it less straightforward to adapt or extend for specific research needs.

These tools address different parts of the workflow and are not tightly integrated. As a result, practitioners often rely on multiple libraries, which can introduce friction and inefficiencies. This highlights the need for a unified CLT and attribution graph framework that combines efficient autointerp pipelines with a flexible and accessible visualization interface, enabling streamlined, end-to-end feature analysis.

Training a CLT requires reconstructing MLP outputs from their corresponding inputs, which in turn necessitates access to activations at all layers over large-scale datasets (e.g., hundreds of millions of tokens). We support two modes for handling activations. First, activations can be computed
on the fly
, which avoids storing large activation datasets and is useful in memory-constrained settings or during rapid experimentation (e.g., when exploring architectures or hyperparameters)
(
Bloom et al. 2024
)
. However, this approach is significantly slower during training. We primarily use this mode for lightweight workflows such as AutoInterp. The main approach is to precompute and store activations on disk prior to CLT training, which is more efficient in practice
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
)
.

However, this setup typically requires terabytes of storage. To address this, we apply symmetric per-layer quantization, which involves computing a scale factor from the maximum absolute activation per layer, followed by zstd entropy coding. We support int8, int4, and int2 quantization modes. While int8 offers a good balance, reducing storage 4–7
\times
over a float16 baseline, int4 and int2 achieve 7–12
\times
at the cost of higher quantization error. Activations are stored and loaded in fixed-size chunks, with the quantized data and per-layer scale factors for each chunk compressed independently. For a LLaMA 1B model trained on 300M tokens, this reduces the storage footprint from approximately 20TB to around 4TB. We observe only a 2–3% drop in activation reconstruction quality and a similar impact on CLT reconstruction performance, a trade-off that is essential to enable scalable training in practice.

During training, activation chunks are loaded into memory as needed. In a Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) setting, each worker processes different chunks. In contrast, when the CLT is sharded along the feature dimension (see Section
3.3
), all workers operate on the same data but on different subsets of features. Finally, similar to
(
Bloom et al. 2024
)
, we normalize activations to have approximately unit norm by dividing each activation by a normalization factor estimated from the first
N
batches.

Following prior work
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
)
, we train CLTs to reconstruct MLP outputs while enforcing sparse and well-behaved feature representations. The overall training objective is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}\left\|\hat{m}_{\ell^{\prime}}-m_{\ell^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{reconstruction}}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{0}\underbrace{\sum_{\ell}\tanh\!\big(C\,(z_{\ell}\odot\|\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}^{\ell}\|)\big)}_{\text{sparsity}}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\underbrace{\sum_{\ell}\mathrm{ReLU}\!\Big(\exp(\tau)-h_{\ell}^{\text{pre}}\Big)\|\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}^{\ell}\|}_{\text{dead features}}\,,

(6)

where
\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}^{\ell}
denotes the concatenated decoder weights at layer
\ell
,
h_{\ell}^{\text{pre}}
are the pre-activation values,
\tau
is a threshold parameter, and
C
is a scaling constant. The coefficients
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}
control the strength of the sparsity and dead-feature regularization terms.

In our implementation, we support multiple sparsity schedulers while using JumpReLU as the activation function. We follow the initialization and training recommendations from
(
Anthropic 2025
)
, including encoder bias initialization to control initial sparsity levels, as well as practical guidelines for hyperparameter selection.

3.3
GPU Sharding

As model size scales, the corresponding CLTs also quickly become too large for a single GPU.
While Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) could suffice for training CLTs on smaller models such as GPT-2, scaling to larger models would inevitably face memory limitations.

Although Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) could help in this front, it remains suboptimal for CLT architectures and encounters memory constraints at larger scales.
Following
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
)
, we instead adopt a feature-wise sharding approach, where features are split across multiple GPUs. Each worker is responsible for a subset of features, and outputs are aggregated across devices at the end of the forward pass. Our framework also supports DDP and FSDP to enable direct comparison.

For automated interpretability, we parallelize computation over the feature dimension. Each independent worker maintains a subset of encoder vectors on the GPU and computes the corresponding latent activations over large token streams (e.g., 10M tokens). During activation loading, we incrementally track and update the top-
K
activating sequences directly on the GPU, ensuring that memory usage remains minimal beyond storing these top activations. Within the same pass, we construct feature-level summaries, including top activating tokens, sequences, and optional metadata (e.g., language). If required by the user, these summaries are then used to automatically generate prompts for LLM-based explanation, providing representative examples and instructions to produce coherent feature descriptions. Results are finally stored in a per-worker database keyed by layer and feature index, which are merged into a single unified store upon completion, reducing the cost of writing millions of individual files. This design enables a fully integrated and parallelized, single-pass autointerp pipeline.

We developed a Python-based Dash interface (Figure
2
) that enables interactive visualization and exploration of attribution graphs.
The interface provides feature-level information, including top activating tokens, representative sequences, automatically generated explanations, and interventions.

In addition, similar to
(
Lin and Bloom
)
, the interface includes tools for clustering features and constructing higher-level structures. Users can define clusters, connect them via edges, and perform interventions at the cluster level. These operations trigger background jobs that leverage Circuit Tracer to compute the corresponding effects efficiently
(
Hanna et al. 2025
)
. The main objective of this interface is to provide a simplified and more easily extensible alternative to
(
Lin and Bloom
)
, enabling rapid experimentation and iteration in academic settings.

4
Evaluation

On GPT-2, our implementation matches prior work
(
Hanna et al. 2025
;
Merullo et al. 2025
;
Lindsey et al. 2025a
)
, achieving
\sim
0.8 explained variance at total
L_{0}=100
, a replacement score of
\sim
0.8, and graph completeness of
\sim
0.95 on standard prompts (e.g., antonym, category completion)
(
Harrasse et al. 2025
)
. Training dynamics are provided in Appendix
B
. Feature-sharded was tested against DDP while significantly improving memory efficiency (Sections
3.1
and
3.3
). We qualitatively validate the full pipeline (autointerp, attribution graphs, interface). Figure
2
shows an attribution graph for
“The opposite of ‘large’ is”
. Detailed analysis of this graph, demonstrating interpretable feature interactions consistent with known circuits, is provided in
Draye et al. 2025
. An example of multilingual analysis using our codebase can also be seen in our study
Harrasse et al. 2025
. Finally, our work also supports an example of interaction with the visual interface in the Demonstration Video.

5
Limitations and Future Work

In this section, we outline several key directions for future work, many of which reflect open problems in mechanistic interpretability at large.
First, attention maps are currently frozen when computing attribution graphs; incorporating attention attribution is an important next step
(
Kamath et al. 2025
)
. We plan to use the approach of
(
Draye et al. 2025
)
.
Second, CLTs remain expensive to train and analyze due to their parameter count and memory footprint, motivating research on more compute- and memory-efficient architectures.
Third, reconstruction error is optimized as a proxy for replacement score
(
Lindsey et al. 2025a
)
. We also found that directly optimizing replacement score led to unstable and noisy results, suggesting the need for more robust objectives or optimization schemes.

6
Conclusion

We introduced CLT-Forge, the first unified open-source framework for scalable training and interpretability of Cross-Layer Transcoders. Our library integrates efficient feature-sharded training, compressed activation caching, automated interpretability, attribution graph computation, and an extensible visualization interface into a single workflow. By combining these components, CLT-Forge enables end-to-end analysis of CLTs at scales that were previously difficult to access in open-source settings. We hope that this framework lowers the barrier to studying mechanistic interpretability at scale, facilitates reproducible research, and supports rapid experimentation in academic environments. Future work will focus on improving efficiency, extending attribution methods (e.g., attention attribution), and open-sourcing CLTs for larger models.
