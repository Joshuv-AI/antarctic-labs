Cardiovascular outcomes and mortality risk at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes in older adults: a retrospective population-based study using health records from Spain
Authors: Berta Fernandez-Camins, Elena Navas, Bogdan Vlacho, Josep Franch-Nadal, Giuseppe Maltese, Dídac Mauricio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundOlder-onset diabetes is often accompanied by multimorbidity, frailty, and variable metabolic profiles that complicate treatment decisions and alter risk-benefit considerations. Emerging evidence suggests that age at diagnosis is a major determinant of prognosis, with younger-onset cases showing higher lifetime risks of vascular complications. However, the prognostic implications of diabetes first diagnosed at older ages remain insufficiently characterised, particularly within Mediterranean populations. We investigated cardiovascular and mortality risks associated with type 2 diabetes diagnosed at older ages in Catalonia, Spain.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, population-based study using electronic health records from primary care centres in Catalonia, Northeast Spain. Data were collected from 1 January 2010 to 30 June 2023. We included adult participants with newly recorded type 2 diabetes between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2021 and excluded individuals with other types of diabetes or sustained insulin monotherapy during the first year after diagnosis. Individuals with type 2 diabetes were stratified by age at diagnosis (<65 vs ≥65 years), with follow-up for up to 10 years. Sex- and birth year-matched individuals without diabetes were included at a 1:3 ratio. Outcomes were cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios and relative hazard ratios (RHRs), with sex-stratified analyses.FindingsAmong 481,709 individuals with type 2 diabetes, 241,472 were diagnosed between ages 18 and 64 years and 240,237 at age ≥65 years. These individuals were matched with 1,398,836 individuals without diabetes. At 10 years, individuals diagnosed before age 65 had higher diabetes-associated excess risk of heart failure than those diagnosed at age ≥65 years (RHR 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.26). Within the older-onset population, individuals diagnosed at age 65–74 years had higher 10-year diabetes-associated excess risks than those diagnosed at age ≥75 years for ischaemic heart disease (RHR 1.14, 95% CI: 1.07–1.20), heart failure (RHR 1.20, 95% CI: 1.14–1.26), and all-cause mortality (RHR 1.16, 95% CI: 1.11–1.20).InterpretationDiabetes-associated relative excess risks for cardiovascular outcomes and mortality differed by age at first recorded type 2 diabetes diagnosis, with lower relative excess risks among those diagnosed at older ages. However, absolute cardiovascular and mortality burdens remained substantial in older adults. These findings should be interpreted as prognostic associations based on routine clinical recognition of diabetes rather than biological onset, and they support age-informed, but individualised, cardiovascular prevention rather than treatment decisions based on chronological age alone. Future research should clarify the mechanisms underlying age-specific differences in diabetes-associated cardiovascular and mortality risk, and determine how these findings can inform individualised prevention strategies for older adults.FundingThis research received financial support from the Instituto de Salud Carlos III, Ministry of Health, and by the Catalan Agency for Management of University and Research Grants (AGAUR, Generalitat de Catalunya), under the consolidated research group DAP.cat.group. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science from their earliest available records to March 1, 2025, without language restrictions, using combinations of the terms “type 2 diabetes”, “age at diagnosis”, “cardiovascular disease”, “mortality”, and “sex differences”. Reference lists of key articles were also screened. Eligible studies were population-based cohorts and meta-analyses examining age at type 2 diabetes diagnosis and cardiovascular or mortality outcomes. Overall evidence quality was high, with large, representative cohorts and validated outcome definitions. Across studies, the main limitations included heterogeneous age groupings, varying follow-up, and the potential for residual confounding.

Large national registry data from Sweden showed that individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes before age 40 had more than a fourfold higher risk of coronary heart disease and over a threefold higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with matched controls, with risks declining progressively after age 80. A pooled analysis of five cohort studies in older adults by Aponte Ribero and colleagues further confirmed that individuals aged 75–84 with diabetes had lower relative cardiovascular risk than those diagnosed at younger ages. Similar associations were observed in population-based cohorts from Australia, China, and the United States of America (USA), which all reported higher mortality risk among individuals with younger-onset type 2 diabetes.

When examining sex differences, a meta-analysis by Kalyani and colleagues reports that diabetes disproportionately erodes the usual cardiovascular advantage observed in women; however, the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) analysis by Bancks et al. did not find statistically significant differences between men and women, reflecting some heterogeneity across datasets.

Added value of this study

This large, population-based cohort study is, to our knowledge, the first to examine how age at type 2 diabetes and sex jointly influence cardiovascular and mortality outcomes in a Mediterranean population. By disaggregating older age groups, we demonstrate that individuals diagnosed after age 75 have markedly lower relative excess risks of ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, and all-cause mortality than those diagnosed earlier, despite greater baseline comorbidity and frailty. The study also identifies sex-specific risk trajectories: excess ischaemic heart disease risk declines more sharply with age at diagnosis in women, whereas reductions in men are most pronounced for peripheral artery disease. Our data highlight important heterogeneity in long-term outcomes by both age and sex at diabetes onset.

Implications of all the available evidence

The combined evidence shows that earlier onset of type 2 diabetes is associated with markedly higher cardiovascular and mortality excess risks, while excess risks are lower in those diagnosed later in life. Sex further modifies these associations, with particularly elevated relative risks in younger women. These findings support prioritising intensive preventive strategies in younger adults with diabetes, while advocating for individualised management in older adults that considers frailty, life expectancy, and patient preferences. Future research should clarify the mechanisms underlying age- and sex-specific differences to inform tailored clinical guidelines.

Introduction

The prevalence of type 2 diabetes has increased in recent decades, becoming one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1,2 More than 500 million people worldwide are currently living with diabetes, a figure projected to rise to over 640 million by 2030 and 780 million by 2045. This trend is largely driven by ageing populations, increasing obesity rates, and sedentary behaviours.1,3 In Spain, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes among adults is estimated at 14%,4 mirroring a pattern seen across other high income countries.3 The associated complications not only reduce quality of life and life expectancy but also impose a significant economic burden on healthcare systems.2 As the global population continues to age, the growing burden of type 2 diabetes among older adults represents a major public health concern, particularly in countries like Spain, where individuals aged 65 years and older already comprise nearly 20% of the population, a figure expected to rise to over 30% by 2050.5

Type 2 diabetes in older adults presents unique clinical features and management challenges.6 This population tends to exhibit variable combinations of insulin resistance, beta-cell failure, sarcopenia, frailty, and multimorbidity, which require individualised care that balances glycaemic control with quality-of-life considerations.6,7 Recent studies have highlighted the need to move beyond a one-size-fits-all model for diabetes classification and management, and advances in precision medicine have prompted a re-evaluation of type 2 diabetes classification,8 incorporating age at diagnosis as a meaningful stratification factor.9,10 Recent evidence further highlights the potential role of age at type 2 diabetes diagnosis as an important determinant of prognosis.11 A large-scale meta-analysis showed that younger-onset type 2 diabetes is associated with higher risks of vascular complications and all-cause mortality, while older-onset diabetes tends to carry a comparatively lower risk.12 More specifically, regarding different risk patterns in our Mediterranean population, a study by Ling et al. comparing differences in the excess mortality risk associated with type 2 diabetes between Ontario, England and Catalonia, showed lower mortality rates in older individuals in Catalonia.13 In contrast, a large, retrospective cohort in Belgium found that, after adjusting for diabetes duration, age at diagnosis did not significantly affect the rate of cardiovascular events.14 Hence, there is a need to further understand how older-onset type 2 diabetes specifically affects prognosis and complication risks across different age strata and clarify the role of frailty in these associations.

Considering the demographic shift toward an ageing population and the evolving understanding of age-related heterogeneity in type 2 diabetes, we hypothesised that individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes at older ages would exhibit a lower excess risk profile than those diagnosed at younger ages. For this purpose, we assessed cardiovascular and overall mortality risks associated with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes in older adults in our Mediterranean region, while also evaluating the burden of comorbidity and frailty across age at onset groups. Unlike studies that primarily rely on analyses adjusted by diabetes duration or prevalent diabetes cohorts, the present study evaluated cardiovascular and mortality risk from the time of diagnosis. It also provided a more precise stratification of older-onset diabetes, including individuals aged ≥75 years. Further, this study was conducted on a large Mediterranean population that was not represented in prior research. The use of matched control individuals without diabetes further allowed the assessment of excess risk across age groups.

Methods

For this retrospective population-based cohort study, we analysed data from the Information System for Research in Primary Care (SIDIAP), which is a database that routinely collects pseudo-anonymised data from the electronic clinical records of patients attending primary care centres managed by the Catalan Health Institute (Institut Català de la Salut). Covering data for more than 75% of the Catalan population (almost six million individuals), SIDIAP is a widely validated and representative source that reflects geographic, sex and age distributions of the overall population.15,16 This database includes sociodemographic characteristics, clinical variables, laboratory data, prescriptions, and pharmacy invoice data, morbidity (ICD-10 diagnostic codes) and specialist referrals.15 For this study, data were collected between the 1st of January 2010 and the 30th of June 2023. We included individuals up to December 31st 2021, ensuring a minimum follow-up period of 18 months (inclusion period). Individuals were followed up until any of the following events occurred: (a) death, (b) transfer to a region other than Catalonia, or (c) end of the study period. Stratification was performed according to the follow-up period. The REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data (RECORD) statement checklist for observational studies using routinely collected health data was followed.17

Study population selection

No formal sample size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective population-based study using routinely collected electronic health record data. Therefore, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible individuals identified during the study period.

We selected individuals with a new diagnosis of type 2 diabetes during the inclusion period. For the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, we used the International Classification of Diseases—Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code E11 and sub-codes, or individuals with reported glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) > 6.5% (48 mmol/mol), or prescription of glucose lowering agents (except metformin monotherapy). We excluded individuals with other types of diabetes (ICD-10 codes E10.9, E13.9, E23.2, and O24.419). We also excluded individuals on sustained insulin monotherapy during the first year after the onset of diabetes, as they may have been misdiagnosed. Because routinely collected electronic health record (EHR) data capture clinical recognition rather than the biological onset of diabetes, the terms “newly recorded” or “newly diagnosed” are used throughout the manuscript. We therefore do not assume that the recorded diagnosis date necessarily corresponds to the true onset of diabetes.

Control participants were selected from individuals without any recorded type of diabetes before or at the index date and without recorded diabetes during the follow-up period used for the matched comparison. These individuals were matched by sex and year of birth, using the nearest neighbour matching method with a ratio of 1:3. All individuals were classified according to age at type 2 diabetes onset as younger-onset (18–64 years) and older-onset (over 65 years). To explore differences in the oldest-onset population, the older-onset group was further sub-divided in two categories: onset at age 65–74 years, and at ≥75 years.

As a sensitivity analysis, we defined a restricted incident cohort including individuals with a first recorded diagnosis of type 2 diabetes from 2013 onwards, thereby introducing a 3-year washout period from 2010 to 2012 during which participants were required to be free of diabetes-related diagnostic codes, glucose-lowering prescriptions, and laboratory criteria compatible with diabetes.

Variables

The following data were collected from each individual at the time of type 2 diabetes diagnosis: sex, age, smoking status (non-smoker, former or active smoker), alcohol intake (at-risk intake [defined by the Spanish Ministry of Health guidelines as >40 g/day in men and >20 g/day in women], non-risk intake), body mass index, blood pressure (systolic blood pressure or SBP, and diastolic blood pressure or DBP), and socioeconomic status (measured by the MEDEA deprivation index).18 Sex was defined according to the biological/administrative classification recorded in the electronic health record. Information on gender identity was not available; therefore, all sex-stratified analyses refer to recorded sex rather than gender. Data on medication were obtained from the CatSalut drug pharmacy invoices database though ATC (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system) codes.19 The following laboratory parameters were retrieved: HbA1c, lipid profile (including total cholesterol, low density lipoprotein cholesterol or cLDL, high density lipoprotein cholesterol or cHDL, and triglycerides), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration Equation or CKD-EPI), and urine albumin to creatinine ratio (uACR).

Baseline comorbidities were assessed at the time of type 2 diabetes diagnosis. Hypertension was defined by ICD-10 codes I10–I13, I15 and/or treatment with antihypertensive drugs (ATC codes C02, C03, C07, C08, C09). Dyslipidaemia was defined by ICD-10 code E78 and/or treatment with lipid-lowering drugs (ATC code C10). Atherogenic dyslipidaemia was defined as low cHDL (<40 mg/dL in men, <50 mg/dL in women) and triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL. Chronic kidney disease was defined by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or urine albumin creatinine rate ≥30 mg/g persisting for 3 months or more. Other comorbidities were collected through ICD-10 codes (heart failure [ICD-10 codes I50.2, I50.2–4, I50.8, I50.9], stroke [ICD-10 codes I60–I69], ischaemic heart disease [ICD-10 codes I20–25], hepatic steatosis [ICD-19 code K76.0], retinopathy [ICD-10 codes E11.3, H35], peripheral artery disease [ICD-10 codes I70.2, I73.9]).

Peripheral artery disease and retinopathy were included if diagnosed within 1 year after the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes to ensure representativeness. History of cardiovascular disease was defined as the presence of any of the following: heart failure, peripheral artery disease, stroke or ischaemic heart disease. Cause-specific mortality data were not available in the current study dataset; therefore, cardiovascular outcome definitions were restricted to recorded non-fatal cardiovascular events. The eFRAGICAP validated score (based on the accumulation of 36 predefined deficits including active prescriptions, 50 clinical variables and 1656 ICD-10 diagnostic codes) was calculated for each individual as an estimate of frailty status.20

All-cause mortality and cardiovascular outcomes (heart failure, peripheral artery disease, stroke and ischaemic heart disease as previously defined) were recorded during the follow-up period. Given the broad prognostic scope of the study, cardiovascular outcomes were treated as co-primary outcomes and all-cause mortality as a secondary outcome.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, or as medians and interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were reported as absolute frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics of individuals with type 2 diabetes were compared by age at diagnosis using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Student's t test or Wilcoxon test for continuous variables, depending on their distribution. Normality was assessed using graphical inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots in conjunction with sample size considerations. Annual incidence rates per 1000 person-years were calculated by dividing the total number of events by total person-years of follow-up and multiplying by 1000.

To estimate the risk associated with type 2 diabetes across age groups, unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to calculate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for cardiovascular outcomes and all-cause mortality. Time to event was defined as the interval from the index date, corresponding to the date of type 2 diabetes diagnosis for individuals with diabetes or the matched inclusion date for controls, to the occurrence of the event, censoring, end of follow-up, or death, whichever occurred first. Primary multivariable models included sex, smoking status, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, body mass index, history of cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and retinopathy. Age was not included as a covariate in the primary age-stratified models because matching was performed by year of birth and analyses were conducted within predefined age strata, thereby controlling for age by design.

Hazard ratios were estimated within each age stratum by comparing individuals with type 2 diabetes with matched controls of the same age and sex. Relative hazard ratios were calculated as the ratio of age-stratified hazard ratios to allow direct comparison of diabetes-associated excess risk across age groups. Relative hazard ratios were calculated by dividing the adjusted hazard ratio for the younger-onset group by the corresponding adjusted hazard ratio for the older-onset group for each outcome. Estimates above 1 indicate greater diabetes-associated excess risk in the younger comparison group, whereas estimates below 1 indicate greater excess risk in the older comparison group. This approach was also used to compare individuals diagnosed at age 65–74 years with those diagnosed at age ≥75 years.

Analyses were stratified by follow-up period, with 5-year and 10-year estimates reported. Because participants diagnosed later in the inclusion period could not contribute a full 10 years of follow-up, the number at risk for each analysis horizon was reported. Sex-stratified analyses were performed to assess whether the association between type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular outcomes differed between men and women, as recorded in the electronic health record.

Because this study used routinely collected electronic health records, some covariates had missing values. The primary multivariable analyses were conducted using complete-case data for the covariates included in each model. We did not assume that missing data were missing completely at random, and the missing-at-random assumption cannot be verified in observational electronic health record data.

Several sensitivity and post-hoc analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings and to address issues raised during peer review. Analyses added during peer review are explicitly identified below as post-hoc analyses.

First, to assess the potential impact of misclassifying prevalent diabetes as newly recorded diabetes, a post-hoc restricted incident cohort analysis was performed. This cohort included individuals with a first recorded diagnosis of type 2 diabetes from 2013 onwards, thereby introducing a 3-year washout period from 2010 to 2012 during which participants were required to be free of diabetes-related diagnostic codes, glucose-lowering prescriptions, and laboratory criteria compatible with diabetes.

Second, to assess whether the primary estimates were affected by potential over-adjustment, post-hoc sensitivity analyses were performed excluding chronic kidney disease and retinopathy from the adjustment set. This was done because these conditions may represent early or previously established diabetes-related complications rather than independent baseline confounders.

Third, frailty, measured using the eFRAGICAP index, was included in post-hoc extended multivariable models. In these analyses, percentage change was calculated as the difference between the primary hazard ratio and the frailty-adjusted hazard ratio; negative values indicate attenuation of the hazard ratio after adjustment for frailty.

Fourth, for each cardiovascular outcome, post-hoc analyses were restricted to individuals without a prior history of the corresponding cardiovascular condition at baseline. This was done to reduce the possibility that incident cardiovascular outcome analyses included recurrent events or progression of pre-existing disease.

Fifth, because all-cause mortality was substantially higher in older age groups and could preclude observation of non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes, post-hoc competing-risk analyses were performed for all prespecified non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes: heart failure, peripheral artery disease, stroke, and ischaemic heart disease. Fine–Grey sub-distribution hazard models were fitted with all-cause mortality treated as the competing event. These analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the age-related patterns observed in cause-specific Cox models were robust to competing mortality. Because of the computational intensity of Fine–Grey models in the full cohort, these analyses were performed in large random age-stratified sub-cohorts, with n = 100,000 per age stratum. Cause-specific mortality was unavailable; therefore, fatal cardiovascular events could not be distinguished from non-cardiovascular deaths. Cumulative incidence function curves derived from these competing-risk analyses were also used for graphical presentation, with at-risk tables reported where applicable.

Sixth, to account for the matched design, post-hoc Cox proportional hazards models stratified by matched set were fitted. In these models, age and sex were controlled through the matching and stratification process, while smoking status, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, body mass index, and previous cardiovascular disease were included as covariates.

Seventh, to account for multiple comparisons across cardiovascular outcomes, post-hoc false discovery rate correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to the relative hazard ratio analyses.

Eighth, to complement the relative hazard ratio approach and formally assess whether the association between type 2 diabetes and each outcome differed by age group, post-hoc pooled Cox proportional hazards models including an interaction term between type 2 diabetes status and age group were fitted. For this comparison, age group was defined as <65 versus ≥65 years at diagnosis or index date. Models included the main effects for type 2 diabetes status and age group, as well as the type 2 diabetes × age–group interaction term. The same adjustment set as in the primary models was used. For each cardiovascular outcome, individuals with a prior history of the corresponding condition at baseline were excluded. Interaction p-values were obtained from Wald tests for the interaction term, and false discovery rate correction was applied across outcomes.

Ninth, E-values were calculated post hoc for the primary adjusted hazard ratios to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding.

Finally, to assess the potential impact of complete-case selection, a post-hoc missingness sensitivity analysis compared individuals included in the complete-case multivariable Cox models with those excluded because of missing covariate data. This comparison focused on the variables included in the adjustment set of the primary models: age, sex, smoking status, body mass index, estimated glomerular filtration rate, albumin-to-creatinine ratio, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and previous macrovascular disease. The aim of this analysis was to evaluate whether missingness was associated with measured demographic and clinical characteristics and to assess the plausibility of a missing-at-random mechanism conditional on observed covariates.

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2024.12.1+563, Kousa Dogwood for Windows).

Results

A total of 481,709 individuals with type 2 diabetes were included in the study. Of these, 241,472 were diagnosed between ages 18 and 64 years (younger-onset group), and 240,237 were diagnosed at age ≥65 years (older-onset group). These individuals were matched by sex and birth year with 1,398,836 individuals without diabetes. Baseline characteristics of the whole study population can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

There were differences in baseline characteristics between the younger- and older-onset type 2 diabetes groups (Table 1, Fig. 1). The younger-onset group had a higher proportion of men (59.8% vs. 40.5%), smokers (55.4% vs. 34.9%), at-risk alcohol use (3.5% vs. 1.3%), and social deprivation (48.2% vs. 37.0%). Most comorbidities were more frequent in the older-onset group, particularly hypertension (50.0% vs. 79.5%) and chronic kidney disease (18.5% vs. 52.1%). However, atherogenic dyslipidaemia (62.1% vs. 50.0%) and hepatic steatosis (4.6% vs. 2.7%) were more common in the younger-onset group. Poor (HbA1c 8–10%) and very poor (HbA1c >10%) glycaemic control were more frequent in the younger-onset group than in the older-onset group (HbA1c 8–10%: 12.6% vs. 9.5% and HbA1c >10%: 5.3% vs. 2.1%). A smaller proportion of the younger-onset group met cLDL targets compared to the older-onset group: among those without cardiovascular disease, only 20.4% achieved cLDL <100 mg/dL (vs. 25.9% in the older group), and among those with history of cardiovascular disease, just 1.1% achieved cLDL <70 mg/dL (vs. 2.2%). As expected, frailty was more prevalent in the older-onset group (15.3% vs. 4.2%). Analyses of age-specific risk factors are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Baseline prevalence of major comorbidities and frailty across age strata is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Heart failure and all-cause mortality risks were consistently higher in the 65–74 group across both sexes at follow-up. However, there were no significant differences in stroke risk between the late-onset subgroups.

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort study, we found that older age at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes was associated with differences in long-term cardiovascular and mortality outcomes. Particularly, individuals diagnosed after 75 years had significantly lower relative risks for ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, and all-cause mortality compared to those diagnosed at younger ages.

Our results are consistent with findings from the Swedish population reported in previous studies.21,22 In the Swedish registry, Rawshani et al. found that individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes before the age of 40 had more than a fourfold increased risk of coronary heart disease and over a threefold higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to matched individuals without diabetes. Similar to our findings, these risks decreased progressively with increasing age at diagnosis, with individuals diagnosed after the age of 80 exhibiting minimal to no excess cardiovascular risk. Our findings are also in agreement with those from a pooled analysis of five cohort studies by Aponte Ribero et al.,23 which showed that individuals aged 75–84 with diabetes had lower relative cardiovascular risk than those diagnosed at younger ages. Other population-based studies conducted in Australia,24 China,25 and the United States26 have likewise reported a higher mortality risk among individuals with younger-onset type 2 diabetes when compared to those diagnosed later in life. Our results corroborate these findings and extend this evidence to a Mediterranean region, demonstrating that individuals diagnosed later in life (particularly after age 75) had substantially lower relative cardiovascular and overall mortality risk, despite presenting with greater comorbidities and frailty at baseline. Although the findings were robust after adjustment for baseline frailty, residual confounding remains possible, particularly from functional status, longitudinal risk-factor control, treatment intensity, physical activity, diet, and time-varying use of cardioprotective therapies during follow-up. Because chronic kidney disease and retinopathy may represent early or previously established diabetes-related complications, their inclusion in multivariable models may attenuate diabetes-associated risk estimates. However, sensitivity analyses excluding these variables produced findings consistent in direction with the primary models, supporting the robustness of the overall interpretation while acknowledging the potential for over-adjustment. The formal interaction analyses were consistent with the RHR approach and provided statistical evidence that the relative association between type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular outcomes differed by age at diagnosis. These results support the interpretation that age at diabetes onset is associated with heterogeneity in diabetes-related excess risk. However, interaction p-values should be interpreted alongside the magnitude and precision of the RHR estimates, particularly in a very large cohort where small differences may achieve statistical significance. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as proof of distinct biological mechanisms by age group, but rather as evidence that the relative excess risk associated with type 2 diabetes varies across age strata.

Several factors may explain this risk attenuation of older age at diagnosis. Shorter exposure to chronic hyperglycaemia likely contributes to a reduced burden of microvascular and macrovascular complications. Older individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes may also face competing risks from other causes of death that limit the time available for diabetes-related complications to develop. In addition, the underlying pathophysiology of diabetes in older adults may differ from that seen in younger patients, with some studies suggesting that later-onset diabetes may involve slower beta-cell decline and a less aggressive metabolic profile.7 Additionally, our study expands upon the mentioned observations in previous cohorts by focussing specifically on older-onset diabetes, a group that remains underrepresented in diabetes research despite its rising prevalence. Our findings highlight the comparatively lower (though still clinically meaningful) excess risks faced by individuals diagnosed at older ages. Moreover, by disaggregating risk across distinct older age strata (e.g., 65–74 vs. 75 and older), our study contributes new insights into the ongoing discussion about how best to stratify and manage care for older adults with newly diagnosed diabetes. The apparent discrepancy between the overall <65 versus ≥65 mortality comparison and the 65–74 versus ≥75 subgroup comparison likely reflects the heterogeneity of the ≥65 group, together with healthy survivor selection and competing mortality in the oldest stratum. Importantly, the attenuation of the relative excess risk with increasing age at diagnosis should not be interpreted as an absence of clinically relevant risk. Absolute cardiovascular and mortality burdens remained high in older adults, indicating that type 2 diabetes continues to confer a relevant risk even at advanced ages. These findings therefore do not support therapeutic de-intensification based on age alone, but rather age-informed, risk-based and clinically individualised prevention. Similarly, attenuation of relative excess risk with older age at diagnosis should not be interpreted as evidence of a uniformly more benign phenotype, as competing mortality, healthy survivor selection, and shorter remaining life expectancy may also contribute to this pattern. Competing-risk analyses were broadly consistent with the main findings for heart failure; however, because cause-specific mortality was unavailable, these analyses could not distinguish cardiovascular from non-cardiovascular death. These findings should be interpreted in the context of existing risk-based management strategies and the limitations inherent to observational findings. Age at diagnosis may help contextualise the heterogeneity in cardiovascular risk, although it should not be interpreted in isolation from the overall risk profile, comorbidity burden, frailty, and life expectancy.

We identified sex-specific patterns, revealing important sex differences in how the excess risks of type 2 diabetes complications evolve with age at diagnosis. The sex-stratified analyses suggest that the excess cardiovascular risk associated with type 2 diabetes may differ between men and women, with some outcomes showing more pronounced relative differences in women. These findings should be interpreted in the context of known sex-related differences in cardiovascular risk profiles, disease presentation, and background event rates. Among women, the excess risk of ischaemic heart disease declined more sharply with older age at diagnosis, whereas in men, the most substantial reduction in risk was observed for peripheral artery disease. These findings build upon earlier evidence that sex influences the cardiovascular consequences of diabetes. In the CARDIA study, Bancks and colleagues found that women with type 2 diabetes experienced more adverse cardiovascular risk factor trajectories over time compared with men, although differences in incident coronary heart disease risk were not statistically significant.27 In contrast, a meta-analysis by Kalyani and colleagues concluded that diabetes largely eliminates the usual cardiovascular protection observed in women, particularly among those who are premenopausal, a phenomenon that may help explain the disproportionately higher relative cardiovascular risks observed in younger women with diabetes.28

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to account for these sex differences, including variations in insulin sensitivity, hormonal profiles, and patterns of fat distribution.29,30 Fluctuations in reproductive stages across the women lifespan (particularly the loss of oestrogen during menopause) exacerbate insulin resistance and may accelerate cardiovascular risk in women with diabetes, especially as they age. Notably, women without diabetes experience their greatest cardiovascular protection in younger adulthood. When diabetes develops during this period, this protection is lost or reversed, leading to a comparatively higher relative risk. In contrast, older women already face a higher baseline cardiovascular risk due to ageing and hormonal changes, meaning the relative impact of diabetes may become less pronounced.30 This could provide a plausible explanation for our observation that the relative cardiovascular risk associated with diabetes decreases more markedly with older age at diagnosis in women. Structural inequities in clinical care may worsen outcomes and should also be taken into consideration when discussing these results, as it has been described that women with diabetes are less likely than men to receive evidence-based cardiovascular treatments.28 Together, these results underscore that the timing of diabetes onset plays a critical role in shaping sex-specific cardiovascular risk trajectories, with the relative burden of disease diminishing more substantially with age in women than in men. The sex-specific estimates for peripheral artery disease should be interpreted with caution. In the older-onset subgroup comparison, the peripheral artery disease (PAD) RHR was above unity in men but close to unity and not statistically significant in women. This pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of a true opposite biological effect in women, but rather as absence of clear evidence for an age-related difference in this subgroup. We therefore prioritised the pooled RHR estimates for inference, as these provide greater statistical power and more stable estimates. Because the sex-specific PAD analyses were exploratory and involved smaller sex- and age-specific strata, the estimates may be vulnerable to imprecision, residual confounding, competing mortality, and differential PAD ascertainment. In addition, PAD is commonly underdiagnosed in routine clinical practice, particularly in women and older adults. For these reasons, we avoided drawing causal or mechanistic conclusions from this subgroup finding.

Our study has some limitations. As this was a retrospective analysis using routinely collected electronic health records, residual confounding remains possible, particularly from glycaemic severity, longitudinal risk-factor control, alcohol intake, liver disease, physical activity, diet, and treatment intensity. Available ethnicity data were too incomplete to allow robust reporting or adjustment. Diagnostic misclassification is also a possibility, despite the use of strict inclusion criteria to minimise errors. Another key limitation is that the date of diabetes diagnosis in routinely collected EHR data represents the first recorded clinical recognition of diabetes rather than the true biological onset of diabetes. This distinction is particularly important in older adults, in whom delayed detection, competing morbidity, and differential healthcare contact may influence the timing of diagnosis. Although a 3-year washout period cannot completely eliminate the possibility of delayed or previously undocumented diabetes, the restricted incident cohort analysis reduced the likelihood that the observed age-related pattern was driven by inclusion of prevalent, selected survivor cases. The EHR-based definition of type 2 diabetes was designed to reflect routine clinical diagnosis, but some diagnostic misclassification cannot be excluded. Previous work in Catalan primary care EHR data31 has shown that, although diabetes coding and classification errors exist in this setting, record-based algorithms combining diagnostic codes, glucose-lowering treatment, age at diagnosis, and glycaemic criteria can improve diabetes case identification and classification. Our case-definition strategy was informed by this approach and therefore combined diagnostic codes, laboratory criteria, and treatment information, rather than relying solely on diagnostic coding. We did not perform an HbA1c-restricted sensitivity analysis because requiring laboratory confirmation at the time of diagnosis would have excluded individuals with incomplete laboratory data and may have introduced differential selection by age, comorbidity, or healthcare use. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Control participants who remained free of diabetes into older age may represent a selected healthier subgroup; this introduces a healthy survivor bias and potentially influences the relative risk estimates in the oldest stratum. The prevalence of obesity found in this cohort should be interpreted in the context of Southern European epidemiological data, where obesity and related cardiometabolic risk factors are highly prevalent in the general adult population. Importantly, the control group was defined by the absence of diabetes rather than by absence of obesity or cardiovascular risk factors; therefore, the relatively high prevalence of obesity among the group without diabetes does not indicate that these are “healthy” individuals but rather reflects the underlying risk-factor profile of the source population. Treatment exposure was not modelled longitudinally. Therefore, the potential influence of changes over time in glucose-lowering, lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, and other cardioprotective therapies, including sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, cannot be fully addressed in the present study. Changes in diabetes treatment over the study period may have influenced observed risks. Calendar-period comparisons are difficult to interpret in the present design because later diagnosis periods have shorter potential follow-up and treatment uptake changed gradually over time. E-value analyses suggested that some observed associations would require a relatively strong unmeasured confounder to be moved to the null. However, E-values do not exclude residual confounding and should be interpreted alongside the limitations of routinely collected observational data. Additionally, frailty estimation using the eFRAGICAP index, while valuable, has not been validated in individuals under age 65, limiting the interpretation of our findings in this subgroup. This underscores the pressing need for the development and validation of frailty assessment tools tailored to younger adults, as early detection of frailty in this population may allow timely interventions that could modify long term outcomes. Importantly, the attenuation of relative cardiovascular excess risk with increasing age at diabetes diagnosis should be interpreted in the context of competing mortality. Older adults, particularly those diagnosed after age 75 years, have a higher background risk of death, which can reduce the observed incidence of non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes. Fine–Grey analyses treating all-cause mortality as a competing event were therefore performed for all prespecified non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes as sensitivity analyses. These analyses yielded results broadly consistent with the primary Cox models, suggesting that the observed age-related pattern was not explained solely by competing mortality. However, these analyses do not fully resolve the absence of cause-specific mortality data. Because fatal cardiovascular events could not be included in the cardiovascular outcome definitions, the total cardiovascular burden in older adults may have been underestimated. Moreover, missing data are an inherent limitation of routinely collected EHR studies. Complete-case analyses may introduce selection bias if the probability of covariate recording is related to prognosis. In our missingness sensitivity analysis, individuals included in and excluded from complete-case models had broadly similar sex distribution, smoking status, and renal function, although included individuals were slightly older and had higher recorded prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and previous macrovascular disease. This pattern is consistent with routine primary care practice, in which patients with established cardiovascular risk factors undergo more frequent monitoring and more complete structured recording. Because these differences were related to observed clinical variables included in the multivariable adjustment set, the findings are compatible with a missing-at-random mechanism conditional on measured covariates. However, this assumption cannot be proven, and residual selection bias due to unmeasured determinants of missingness cannot be excluded.

Nonetheless, the study has important strengths. It is based on a large, representative primary care population with extensive longitudinal follow-up and high quality real-world data, allowing robust estimates of excess cardiovascular and mortality risk. Unlike studies that primarily adjust for diabetes duration or include prevalent diabetes cohorts, the present study evaluated cardiovascular and mortality risk from the time of diagnosis, allowing a more direct assessment of early prognostic heterogeneity by age at onset. The inclusion of matched control groups further strengthens internal validity and provides valuable insights into the heterogeneity of diabetes expression across the lifespan. Significantly, the attenuation of relative excess risk with older age at diagnosis should not be interpreted as evidence that older-onset diabetes is biologically benign. This pattern may reflect shorter exposure to hyperglycaemia, delayed clinical detection, competing mortality, healthy survivor selection, and differences in background cardiovascular risk. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as prognostic associations based on routinely recorded clinical diagnoses rather than as evidence of distinct causal mechanisms.

In this large population-based study, the relative excess cardiovascular and mortality risks associated with newly recorded type 2 diabetes differed by age at diagnosis and were lowest among individuals diagnosed after age 75 years. However, this pattern may partly reflect shorter diabetes exposure, competing mortality, healthy survivor selection, and delayed clinical detection in older adults. Absolute risk remained high in older individuals, and the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that older-onset diabetes is uniformly benign. Instead, age at diagnosis may help contextualise prognosis alongside frailty, comorbidity, life expectancy, treatment burden, and patient preferences. Future research should aim to further delineate the mechanisms underlying age- and sex-specific differences in risk and inform evidence-based guidelines that optimise care across diverse patient populations.
