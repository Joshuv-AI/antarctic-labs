On the sequential topological complexity and the LS-category of the cofiber of higher diagonals for symmetric products of non-orientable surfaces
Jesús González, Ekansh Jauhari

Abstract
. For positive integers k k , n n , and g g with k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , we give a closed-form expression for the k k -th ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -zero-divisor cup length 𝗓𝖼𝗅 k ⁡ ( SP n ​ ( N g ) ) \zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})) of the n n -th symmetric product S ​ P n ​ ( N g ) SP^{n}(N_{g}) of the closed non-orientable surface N g N_{g} of genus g g . This allows us to estimate, and in some cases, completely determine, the k k -th sequential topological complexity 𝖳𝖢 k ⁡ ( SP n ​ ( N g ) ) \TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})) , as well as the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of the homotopy cofiber of the k k -th diagonal map S ​ P n ​ ( N g ) → ( S ​ P n ​ ( N g ) ) k SP^{n}(N_{g})\to(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k} . Our results recover previously known facts for even-dimensional real projective spaces ( g = 1 g=1 ) and closed non-orientable surfaces ( n = 1 n=1 ). In addition, we show that, as g g grows, 𝖳𝖢 2 ⁡ ( SP n ​ ( N g ) ) \TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g})) behaves in a different way as all other invariants 𝖳𝖢 k ⁡ ( SP n ​ ( N g ) ) \TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})) do. Likewise, as k k grows, we describe an eventual maximal-possible linear growth of 𝗓𝖼𝗅 k ⁡ ( SP n ​ ( N g ) ) \zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})) , which allows us to prove the rationality conjecture of Farber and Oprea for the TC-generating function of S ​ P n ​ ( N g ) SP^{n}(N_{g}) .

1.
Introduction and main results

The
k
-th sequential topological complexity
\TC_{k}(X)
of a topological space
X
is a numerical homotopy invariant, central to the field of topological robotics, that measures the difficulty in moving points continuously within the space
X
,
[
Far03
,
Rud10
]
. More precisely,
\TC_{k}(X)
is one less than the minimal number of
motion planning rules
required to tell an autonomous system (such as a robot) how to move between any
k
points of
X
. As is evident from the vast literature on topological complexity, computing
\TC_{k}(X)
for a general space
X
is a difficult task; so typically, one studies lower bounds and upper bounds to
\TC_{k}(X)
that often come from the cohomology of
X^{k}
and the homotopy dimension of
X
, respectively. One of the most useful lower bounds to
\TC_{k}(X)
is the
k
-th zero-divisor cup length of
X
, denoted
\zcl_{k}(X)
, which is the cup length of the kernel of the homomorphism induced in cohomology by the
k
-th diagonal map
X\to X^{k}
. Indeed, this invariant determines
\TC_{k}(X)
for
k\geq 2
when
X
is a finite product of spheres
[
Far03
,
BGRT14
]
, a simply connected symplectic manifold
[
FTY03
,
BGRT14
]
, an orientable surface
[
Far03
,
Rud10
]
or its finite symmetric product
[
DCDJ
,
Jau25
]
, or a non-orientable surface for
k\geq 3

[
GGGL16
]
, just to name a few classes of closed manifolds.

The classical
motion planning problem
of continuously moving between pairs of points on a real projective space
P^{n}
dates back to the work of
[
FTY03
]
, where it was proved that
\TC_{2}(P^{n})
coincides with the immersion dimension of
P^{n}
when
n\neq 1,3,7
, and with
n
otherwise. More recently, the
sequential
motion planning problem of moving between the coordinates of
k
-tuples of points on
P^{n}
was studied in
[
CAG+18
,
Dav18
]
, where sharp lower bounds to
\TC_{k}(P^{n})
were obtained in terms of the
k
-th
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-zero-divisor cup length of
P^{n}
. In this paper, we extend the work on even-dimensional real projective spaces
P^{2n}
from
[
Dav18
]
to the
n
-th symmetric product
SP^{n}(N_{g})
of a closed non-orientable surface
N_{g}
of genus
g\geq 1
for
n\geq 1
. Namely, we estimate (and in some cases, precisely determine) the value
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
for each
k\geq 2
by giving closed-form expressions for the
k
-th
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-zero-divisor cup length of any
SP^{n}(N_{g})
. This work is an extension because, as noted by Arnold, Maxwell’s theorem on multipole representations of spherical harmonics yields a homeomorphism
SP^{n}(P^{2})\cong P^{2n}
, which can also be interpreted as a “quaternionic” analogue of the well-known homeomorphism
SP^{n}({\mathbb{C}}P^{1})\cong{\mathbb{C}}P^{n}
implied by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, see
[
BGZ02
,
KS06
]
.

Another useful lower bound to
\TC_{k}(X)
is given in terms of the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of the homotopy cofiber of the
k
-th diagonal map
X\to X^{k}
. This invariant, which we denote by
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})
, was studied in detail for
k=2
in
[
GCV13
,
Dra14
,
Dra16
,
GGV18
]
, for example, where it played a central role in the study of the classical topological complexity of closed non-orientable surfaces. For general
k
, a close relationship between
\TC_{k}(X)
and
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})
has recently been settled in
[
CAC+26
, Proposition 2.1 (2)]
. In this paper, we establish general comparisons between
\zcl_{k}(X)
,
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})
, and
\TC_{k}(X)
, which extend known properties for
k=2
and will play a central role in our assessment of
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})})
and
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. In particular,
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})
is bounded from below by
\zcl_{k}(X)
, so our computations of the
k
-th
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-zero-divisor cup length of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
in this paper give estimates (and in some cases, the exact value) of the quantity
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})})
. This enables us to compare the values
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
and
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})})
, and to show that they agree in a number of cases.

We will review some standard and useful facts about the symmetric products of non-orientable surfaces, topological complexity, Lusternik–Schnirelmann category, and zero-divisor cup length in Section
2
. For now, we describe the exact formulae for the
k
-th
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-zero-divisor cup length of the
n
-th symmetric product of the closed non-orientable surface of genus
g\geq 1
for each
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
, and their application towards the computation of
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})
and
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})})
. This cup length will be denoted by
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
and studied in detail in Section
6
. For simplicity, we divide the description of
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
into a few distinct cases.

The simplest case is when
k=2
. To describe
\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, it turns out that one only needs to know the consecutive
2
-powers that optimally bound
n
from above and below. Namely, for any given integer
n\geq 1
, there exists
e\geq 0
such that
2^{e}\leq n<2^{e+1}
, and then
\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
can be described entirely in terms of
g
,
n
, and
e
, depending only on how
g
interacts with the gap between
n
and
2^{e}
. The precise statement, which will be proven in Section
7
, is as follows.

1.1 Theorem
.

Let
2^{e}\leq n<2^{e+1}
for some
e\geq 0
. Then for
g\geq 1
, we have that

\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\begin{cases}2^{e+2}+g-2&\text{ if }g\leq 2n-2^{e+1}+1;\\
2^{e+1}+2n-1&\text{ if }g\geq 2n-2^{e+1}+1.\end{cases}

Cases with
k\geq 3
require more machinery. In particular, unlike the case
k=2
, one needs to better understand the binary expansion of
n
(actually that of
2n
) to describe
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. To that end, we follow the notations of
[
Dav18
]
and define the following.

1.2 Definition
.

Suppose the binary expansion of
m\geq 1
(where
2^{e}\leq m<2^{e+1}
for some
e\geq 0
) is given by
m=\delta_{e}2^{e}+\delta_{e-1}2^{e-1}+\cdots+\delta_{1}2^{1}+\delta_{0}2^{0}
, where
\delta_{j}\in\{0,1\}
. Define the set

S(m):=\left\{i\in\{0,\ldots,e\}\ \middle|\ \delta_{i}=\delta_{i-1}=1\text{ and }\delta_{i+1}=0\right\}.

For
0\leq i\leq e
, define also the number

Z_{i}(m):=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\left(1-\delta_{j}\right)2^{j}.

In other words,
Z_{i}(m)
is the sum of all
2^{j}
with
j\leq i
such that the
j
-th position in the binary expansion of
m
corresponds to
0
, and
i\in S(m)
if a sequence of at least two consecutive
1
’s begins at the
i
-th position in the binary expansion of
m
.

To describe
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
for
k\geq 3
, we will need to take
m=2n
above. Our description will utilize that of the
k
-th zero-divisor cup length of even-dimensional real projective spaces from the work of Davis, see
[
Dav18
]
. Before recalling that, we define the following.

1.3 Definition
.

For a finite-dimensional CW complex
X
and
k\geq 2
, define the
k
-th zero-divisors gap of
X
, denoted
\gap_{k}(X)
, as follows:

\gap_{k}(X):=k\cdot\dim(X)-\zcl_{k}(X).

In particular,

(1.I)

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))+\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk.

The quantities
\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})
and
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
are completely understood. Indeed, it was shown in
[
Dav18
]
that

(1.II)

\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=\max_{i\in S(2n)}\left\{0,2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)\right\}.

The description of
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, and hence that of
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, for
k\geq 3
will be divided in various parts. The case of odd
k\geq 3
is simpler. In the case when
k\geq 4
is even, we give three formulae for
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
depending on how
g
compares with the ratios
\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}
and
\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}
. The precise statement is as follows.

1.4 Theorem
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
n,g\geq 1
be integers.

(A)

If
k\geq 3
is odd, or if
k\geq 4
is even and
g\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+1
, then we have that

\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk-\max_{i\in S(2n)}\left\{0,2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}.

(B)

If
k\geq 4
is even and
\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+2\leq g\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+1
, then we have that

\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk+\left(\frac{k}{2}-1\right)\left(g-1\right)-\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})+1}{2}.

(C)

If
k\geq 4
is even and
g\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2
, then we have that

\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk.

In view of (
1.I
), Theorem
1.4
is equivalent to the following theorem that will be proven in Subsection
8.B
and Section
9
.

1.5 Theorem
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
n,g\geq 1
be integers.

(a)

If
k\geq 3
is odd, or if
k\geq 4
is even and
g\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+1
, then we have that

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\max_{i\in S(2n)}\left\{0,2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}.

(b)

If
k\geq 4
is even and
\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+2\leq g\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+1
, then we have that

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})+1}{2}-\left(\frac{k}{2}-1\right)\left(g-1\right).

(c)

If
k\geq 4
is even and
g\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2
, then we have that

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0.

Note that item (
b
) is empty precisely when
\bigl\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\bigr\rfloor=\bigl\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\bigr\rfloor
, in which case items (
a
) and (
c
) are complementary.

Since
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is a lower bound to both the
k
-th sequential topological complexity of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
and the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of the homotopy cofiber of the
k
-th diagonal map
SP^{n}(N_{g})\to(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k}
, our results on the former invariant have the following implications for the latter two invariants (see Section
4
for a proof).

1.6 Corollary
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
n,g\geq 1
be integers.

(i)

If
k\ \geq\ 3
is odd and
g\ \geq\ \left\lceil\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rceil+1
, or if
k\ \geq\ 4
is even and
g\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2
, or if
n=2^{e}
for some
e\geq 0
and
k\geq 3
, then we have that

\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk=\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})}).

(ii)

If
2^{e}\leq n<2^{e+1}
for some
e\geq 1
, then we have that

2^{e+2}-1\leq\cat(C_{\Delta_{2}SP^{n}(N_{g})})\leq\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq 4n-1.

In particular, the last three inequalities are equalities if
n=2^{e}
.

We note that in the case
g=1
(i.e., when
SP^{n}(N_{1})\cong P^{2n})
, our results recover those on the zero-divisor cup lengths of
P^{2n}
, see
[
Dav18
]
(and also
[
CAG+18
]
for
k=2
). Similarly, in the case
n=1
(i.e., when
SP^{1}(N_{g})=N_{g}
), our results recover the computation for
k\geq 3
in
[
GGGL16
, Section 5]
, as well as the folklore fact that
\zcl_{2}(N_{g})=3
(see, for instance,
[
Dra16
, proof of Theorem 3.4.4]
).

It is known from
[
CAC+26
, Proposition 2.1 (2)]
that, for a space
X
with abelian fundamental group,
\TC_{k}(X)
attains its maximal possible value
k\cdot\dim(X)
if and only if
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})
does so. Since
\pi_{1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is abelian for
n\geq 2
, Corollary
1.6
provides an explicit, new infinite family of examples for which the equalities
\TC_{k}(X)=\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})=k\cdot\dim(X)
hold for each
k\geq 2
(in fact, the previously-conjectured equality
\TC_{2}(X)=\cat(C_{\Delta_{2}X})=\TC^{M}(X)
holds in these cases for the
monoidal topological complexity
of
[
IS10
]
, see Remark
4.2
). That the equality
\TC_{2}(X)=\cat(C_{\Delta_{2}X})
holds for a few classes of spaces (such as spheres, path-connected
H
-spaces, simply connected closed symplectic manifolds, and real projective spaces) was shown in
[
GCV13
]
, while it follows from
[
Dra16
,
Dra17
,
CV17
]
that
\cat(C_{\Delta_{2}N_{g}})=3<4=\TC_{2}(N_{g})
for
g\geq 2
. In light of the latter inequality, the condition
e\geq 1
in Corollary
1.6
(
ii
) cannot be waived for
g\geq 2
.

Notations and conventions

In this paper, we consider (co)homology with
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-coefficients, unless specified otherwise. Similarly, zero-divisor cup lengths are considered only with
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-coefficients. The tensor product is always taken over the ring of integers and denoted
\otimes
. The notation
\smile
is omitted in writing cup products, i.e., we write
\alpha\beta
for a cup product
\alpha\smile\beta
. We use the term
map
to refer to both continuous functions and algebra homomorphisms. Unless otherwise noted, all spaces are assumed to be connected.

2.
Preliminaries and related work

2.A.
Symmetric products of closed non-orientable surfaces

Let
N_{g}
denote the closed non-orientable surface of genus
g\geq 1
, and
\Sigma_{n}
the symmetric group on
n\geq 1
symbols. A permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
permutes the factors in the Cartesian product
(N_{g})^{n}
. The orbit space of this action is the
n
-th symmetric product of
N_{g}
, denoted
SP^{n}(N_{g})
. It is well-known that
SP^{n}(N_{g})
is a closed smooth non-orientable
2n
-manifold. For example,
SP^{1}(N_{g})=N_{g}
and
SP^{n}(N_{1})\cong P^{2n}
, see
[
KS06
]
.

For
n>1\leq g
, it is a standard fact that
\pi_{1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\cong H_{1}(N_{g};{\mathbb{Z}})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{g-1}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, see, for instance,
[
KT13
]
. In particular,
SP^{n}(N_{g})
is not aspherical. However, as shown in
[
Jau25
]
,
SP^{n}(N_{g})
is always essential in the sense of Gromov (since its Lusternik–Schnirlemann category is
2n
), and its universal cover is
(n-1)
-connected. Because of fundamental group reasons,
SP^{n}(N_{g})
does not support a Riemannian metric of non-positive sectional curvature due to the Cartan–Hadamard theorem, and for
g\geq 2n+1
, it also does not support a Riemannian metric of non-negative Ricci curvature due to Bochner’s theorem on the first Betti number. The cohomology ring of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
is known due to
[
KS06
]
; we recall the description in Subsection
5.A
. For a survey on these manifolds, we refer the reader to
[
BGZ02
]
.

2.B.
Zero-divisor cup length and sequential topological complexity

For a CW complex
X
and integer
k\geq 2
, let
\Delta_{k,X}\colon X\to X^{k}
denote the diagonal map. In cohomology with coefficients in a ring
R
, this induces an algebra homomorphism
\Delta_{k,X}^{*}\colon H^{*}(X^{k};R)\to H^{*}(X;R)
. A cohomology class
\alpha\in\Ker(\Delta_{k,X}^{*})
is called a
k
-th
R
-zero-divisor
of
X
, and the length of the longest non-zero cup product of such zero-divisors is called the
k
-th
R
-zero-divisor cup length
of
X
, denoted
\zcl_{k,R}(X)
. We reserve the notation
\zcl_{k}(X):=\zcl_{k,{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}(X)
. For this and the following terminology and results, we refer the reader to
[
Far03
,
Far06
,
Rud10
,
BGRT14
]
.

The
k
-th sequential topological complexity
of
X
, denoted
\TC_{k}(X)
, is defined to be the smallest non-negative integer
n
such that
X^{k}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1}U_{i}
, where each
U_{i}\subset X^{k}
is open and admits a map
s_{i}\colon U_{i}\to X^{[0,1]}
satisfying

s_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\left(\frac{j-1}{k-1}\right)=x_{j}

for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in U_{i}
. For future reference, we shall call
s_{i}
a
motion planner
on
U_{i}
.

A homotopy invariant closely related to, and much older than,
\TC_{k}(X)
is the
Lusternik–Schnirelmann category
(or LS-category for short) of
X
, which is denoted
\cat(X)
and defined to be the smallest non-negative integer
m
such that
X=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m+1}V_{j}
, where each
V_{j}
is open and null-homotopic in
X
.

The following well-known result connects these invariants.

2.1 Theorem
.

For a CW complex
X
, ring
R
, and integer
k\geq 2
, we have that
\max\{\zcl_{k,R}(X),\cat(X^{k-1})\}\leq\TC_{k}(X)\leq\cat(X^{k})
.

It was shown in
[
Jau25
]
that
\cat((SP^{n}(N_{g})^{m})=2mn
for
m,n,g\geq 1
. Therefore, it follows that
2n(k-1)\leq\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq 2nk
. Because
SP^{n}(N_{1})\cong P^{2n}
, the integer
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{1}))
coincides with one less than the minimal number of motion planning rules required to rotate a line in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
that is fixed along a revolving joint at the origin via
k
positions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
. For
k=2
, this integer is the immersion dimension of
P^{2n}
(see
[
FTY03
]
); for
k\geq 3
, this integer is
2nk
in some cases (depending on the combinatorics of
k
and
n
) and undetermined in others, see
[
Dav18
]
.

2.C.
LS-category of the homotopy cofiber of the diagonal map

For a CW complex
X
and integer
k\geq 2
, let
C_{\Delta_{k}X}
denote the cofiber of the diagonal map
\Delta_{k,X}\colon X\to X^{k}
. Indeed,
C_{\Delta_{k}X}=X^{k}\cup_{\Delta_{k,X}}CX
is the mapping cone of
\Delta_{k,X}
. Since the image
\Delta_{k}X
of
X
under the cofibration
\Delta_{k,X}
is closed in
X^{k}
, we have that
C_{\Delta_{k}X}\simeq X^{k}/\Delta_{k}X
, see
[
Hat02
]
. Consequently, because
(X^{k},\Delta_{k}X)
is a
good CW-pair
(in the sense of
[
Hat02
]
), we see that

H^{s}(C_{\Delta_{k}X};R)\cong H^{s}(X^{k},\Delta_{k}X;R)

for any
s\geq 1
and ring
R
.

The following fact regarding the LS-category of
C_{\Delta_{k}X}
is a direct generalization of the well-known case with
k=2
. The proof is short, and we record it for completeness.

2.2 Theorem
.

For a finite-dimensional CW complex
X
, ring
R
, and integer
k\geq 2
, we have that
\zcl_{k,R}(X)\leq\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})\leq\min\{\TC_{k}(X)+1,k\cdot\dim(X)\}
.

Proof.

First, we prove
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})\leq\TC_{k}(X)+1
. If
\TC_{k}(X)=m
, then we can write
X^{k}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m+1}U_{i}
, where each
U_{i}\subset X^{k}
is open and admits a motion planner
s_{i}\colon U_{i}\to X^{[0,1]}
. It then turns out that for each
i
, the inclusion
U_{i}\hookrightarrow X^{k}
is homotopic to a map with values in
\Delta_{k}X
. Indeed, for any fixed
i
, using maps
\phi_{r}\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
defined by

\phi_{r}(t):=\frac{t(k-r)+r-1}{k-1}

for
1\leq r\leq k
, we can define the required deformation
H_{i}\colon U_{i}\times I\to X^{k}
of
U_{i}
as

H_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},t):=\left(s_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})(\phi_{1}(t)),\ldots,s_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})(\phi_{k}(t))\right).

A straightforward generalization of the proof of
[
Far06
, Lemma 18.3]
then yields
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})\leq\TC_{k}(X)+1
. The other upper bound
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})\leq k\cdot\dim(X)
holds because
C_{\Delta_{k}X}\simeq X^{k}/\Delta_{k}X
and
\dim(X^{k}/\Delta_{k}X)\leq k\cdot\dim(X)
. For the lower bound, suppose
\zcl_{k,R}(X)=n
, so that for
1\leq i\leq n
, there exist classes
\alpha_{i}\in H^{s_{i}}(X^{k};R)
satisfying
\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}\neq 0
and
\Delta_{k,X}^{*}(\alpha_{i})=0
. For each
i
, the long exact sequence for the pair
(X^{k},\Delta_{k}X)
gives

\cdots\to H^{s_{i}}(X^{k},\Delta_{k}X;R)\xlongrightarrow{j}H^{s_{i}}(X^{k};R)\xlongrightarrow{\Delta_{k,X}^{*}}H^{s_{i}}(X;R)\to\cdots,

from which we deduce that there exists
\beta_{i}\in H^{s_{i}}(X^{k},\Delta_{k}X;R)\cong H^{s_{i}}(C_{\Delta_{k}X};R)
such that
j(\beta_{i})=\alpha_{i}
. It follows from the naturality of the cup product that
\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{n}\neq 0
in
H^{*}(C_{\Delta_{k}X};R)
. Since the
R
-cup length of any CW complex is a lower bound to its LS-category (see, for instance,
[
CLOT03
, Proposition 1.5]
), we have that
\zcl_{k,R}(X)=n\leq\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})
.
∎

2.D.
Comparison from computations for
SP^{n}(M_{g})
and
P^{2n}

Similar to the symmetric products of closed non-orientable surfaces, one has the
n
-th symmetric product of the closed orientable surface
M_{g}
of genus
g\geq 0
, denoted
SP^{n}(M_{g})
. This is a closed smooth orientable
2n
-manifold which has been well-studied in topology and geometry—see, for instance,
[
Mac62
,
BGZ02
,
KS06
,
DCDJ
]
. There are several fundamental differences between
SP^{n}(M_{g})
and
SP^{n}(N_{g})
; we highlight some of these with a
\TC
-flavor coming from Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
and the results of
[
Jau25
,
DCDJ
]
.

(1)

The quantity
\zcl_{k,{\mathbb{Q}}}(SP^{n}(M_{g}))
is much simpler to compute than
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. The assessment of the former quantity is based on Macdonald’s description (see
[
Mac62
]
) of the ring
H^{*}(SP^{n}(M_{g});{\mathbb{Q}})
, and the answer depends only on how
g
compares with
n
. On the other hand, the computation of
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
uses Kallel–Salvatore’s description (see Section
5
) of the ring
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g});{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
and Davis’s analysis of the case
g=1
in
[
Dav18
]
; the answer depends heavily on the binary expansion of
n
, on both the magnitude and parity of
k
, as well as on the comparison of
g
with two ratios—indicated in the paragraph containing (
1.II
)—involving the invariant
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
.

(2)

The value
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(M_{g}))
is known for each
k
,
n
and
g
, while
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is unknown in many cases. Indeed, the former value is always equal to the quantity
\zcl_{k,{\mathbb{Q}}}(SP^{n}(M_{g}))
, while we (the authors) are aware of the equality
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
only when this number is maximal possible (see Corollary
1.6
). These comments also apply to the invariant
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}X})
for
X\in\{SP^{n}(M_{g}),SP^{n}(N_{g})\}
. As a result, the computation of the
\TC
-generating function (see
[
FO19
,
FKS20
]
) of
SP^{n}(M_{g})
is simpler than that of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
. In the former case, the
\TC
-generating rational function is explicitly described, with the integral polynomial in its numerator being a quadratic. In contrast, the numerator of the
\TC
-generating rational function of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
is mostly a cubic polynomial, and in general, we only have bounds on its degree, see Theorem
4.3
below.

As previously noted, the symmetric products of closed non-orientable surfaces generalize even-dimensional real projective spaces. The description of
\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})
from
[
Dav18
]
is simpler than that of
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. Indeed, our results show that for non-trivial genera (i.e.,
g\neq 1
), the value
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
depends on both the magnitude of
g
and the parity of
k
— see Example
3.1
for an explicit example, and also the comment following the statement of Lemma
9.1
.

All in all, the study of some homotopy invariants of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
, such as the zero-divisor cup lengths and sequential topological complexities, is more involved than those of
SP^{n}(M_{g})
and
P^{2n}
.

3.
Consequences and observations

In this section, we study the consequences of our main results for the zero-divisor cup lengths. We begin by looking at a simple example.

3.1 Example
.

Suppose
n=51
, so that
2n=102=2^{6}+2^{5}+2^{2}+2^{1}
. We have from
[
Dav18
]
that
\gap_{k}(P^{102})
is equal to
127-25k
if
2\leq k\leq 5
,
7-k
if
5\leq k\leq 7
, and
0
otherwise. Since
2,6\in S(102)
,
Z_{2}(102)=1
, and
Z_{6}(102)=25
, we get from Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
the following computations for
SP^{51}(N_{g})
:

\gap_{k}(SP^{51}(N_{g}))=\begin{cases}78-g&\text{ if }k=2,g\leq 39;\\
39&\text{ if }k=2,g\geq 39;\\
54-2g&\text{ if }k=3,g\leq 26;\\
30-3g&\text{ if }k=4,g\leq 7;\\
15-g&\text{ if }k=4,8\leq g\leq 14;\\
2&\text{ if }k=5,g=1;\\
1&\text{ if }k=6,g=1;\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is clear from this example that the computation of
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is strictly more complicated than that of
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
in most cases.

The following statement is a straightforward consequence of Theorem
1.1
.

3.2 Corollary
.

Let
2^{e}\leq n<2^{e+1}
for some
e\geq 0
. Then

\lim_{g\to\infty}\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2^{e+1}+2n-1.

By Corollary
1.6
, this limit attains its maximal possible value
\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
when
n=2^{e}\geq 2
, but is less than
4n-1
otherwise. In stark contrast to this case, we have the following when
k\geq 3
.

3.3 Corollary
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Then for
k\geq 3
, we have that

\lim_{g\to\infty}\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{g\to\infty}\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk.

Proof.

The assertion for the limit on the left follows for each
k\geq 3
directly by parts (
A
) and (
C
) of Theorem
1.4
. To justify the limit on the right, it suffices to note that
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq 2nk
for each
k
and
g
.
∎

We show in Corollary
6.3
that
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))
for fixed
n
and
k
. Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
help us obtain an upper bound on
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))
in terms of
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
in all cases. This is achieved by precisely controlling the linear growth of the
k
-th zero-divisor cup length (or equivalently, the linear decay of the
k
-th zero-divisors gap) when the genus
g
increases by
1
.

3.4 Proposition
.

Let
g\geq 1
and
2^{e}\leq n<2^{e+1}
for some
e\geq 0
.

(1)

If
k=2
, then we have that
\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))-\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq 1
, where equality holds precisely when
g\leq 2n-2^{e+1}
.

(2)

If
k\geq 3
is odd, then
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))-\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq k-1
, which is an equality if
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))\neq 2nk
, and is equal to
0
if
g\geq\left\lceil\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rceil+1
.

(3)

If
\ k\ \geq\ 4\
is even and
\ g\ \leq\ \left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor
, then we have the equality
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))-\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=k-1
.

(4)

If
k\geq 4
is even and
g=\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+1
, then we have the equality

\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))-\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\begin{cases}\frac{k+r-3}{2}&\ \text{ if }k\leq 2g+r;\\
g+r-1&\ \text{ if }k\geq 2g+r+1,\end{cases}

where
r
is the residue obtained after dividing
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
by
k
.

(5)

If
k\geq 4
is even and
\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+2\leq g\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor
, then we have the equality
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))-\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\tfrac{k}{2}-1
.

(6)

If
k\geq 4
is even and
g\ \geq\ \left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+1
, then we have the equality
\ \zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))\ -\ \zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, which is positive precisely when
g=\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+1
and
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})>0
.

Proof.

As
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))-\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))-\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))
, we will prove this proposition for the latter quantity for our convenience. Clearly, the assertions in (
1
) and (
5
) follow immediately from Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
(
b
), respectively. In the other cases, we proceed as follows.

(2)

We are done if
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0
, because then
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))=0
due to Corollary
6.3
. Note that
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\max\{0,\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)(g-1)\}
by (
1.II
) and Theorem
1.5
(
a
). So,

g\geq\left\lceil\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rceil+1\implies(g-1)(k-1)\geq\gap_{k}(P^{2n})\implies\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0.

Thus, as argued above, the difference is
0
in this case. Next, let us assume
0<\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
. Then we have from Theorem
1.5
(
a
) that

\displaystyle 0<\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}

\displaystyle=\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)(g-1).

If
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))\neq 2nk
, then
0<\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))
and so, in analogy with the above, we get
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))=\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)g
. Therefore, the equality with
k-1
follows in this case. Otherwise, if
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))=0
, then
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})\leq(k-1)g
, which gives
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq k-1
. So,
k-1
is indeed the general upper bound.

(3)

The hypothesis on
g
gives
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})>(k-1)g
. So, in analogy with the proof of (
2
), we get that
0<\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)g=\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))
, and then the asserted equality follows similarly.

(4)

We can write
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=(g-1)k+r
, where
0\leq r<k
. By Theorem
1.5
(
a
),
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is equal to

\max\left\{0,\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}=\max\left\{0,g+r-1\right\}=g+r-1

since
g+r-1\geq 0
. If is not difficult to check that
g\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor
if and only if
k\leq 2g+r
. So, if
k\leq 2g+r
, then Theorem
1.5
(
b
) gives

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))=\frac{(g-1)k+r+1}{2}-\left(\frac{k}{2}-1\right)g=\frac{2g+r+1-k}{2},

which means the difference is

g+r-1-\frac{2g+r+1-k}{2}=\frac{k+r-3}{2}\leq k-2.

Otherwise, if
k\geq 2g+r-1
, then
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))=0
by Theorem
1.5
(
c
), and so the difference is just
g+r-1
, which does not exceed
g+k-2
.

(6)

Here, we have
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))=0
because of Theorem
1.5
(
c
). Therefore, the asserted equality holds by definition. Note that
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0
when
g\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2
, and so the difference is
0
in this range. However, if
g=\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+1
, which implies
g\leq\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}+1
, then we claim that

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0\iff\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=0.

The reverse implication is obvious. To prove the forward implication, assume that
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0
. If
\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+2\leq g
, then we get the contradiction

1+\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})+1}{k-2}=g>\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}+1,

where the first equality comes from Theorem
1.5
(
b
). Hence, we must have
g\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+1
. In this range, Theorem
1.5
(
a
) gives

0=\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\max_{i\in S(2n)}\left\{0,2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}.

If
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})>0
, then
g>1
(in view of the equality above) and so the upper bound on
g
implies
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})>(k-1)(g-1)
. As argued in the proof of (
2
), we then get
0<\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)(g-1)=\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, which is a contradiction. So,
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=0
must be the case. Thus, in this case, the difference, namely
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, is positive if and only if
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})>0
.

∎

4.
Topological complexity and the rationality conjecture

We begin by proving Corollary
1.6
, whose implications we will subsequently discuss in this section.

Proof of Corollary
1.6
.

We first focus on the case of (
i
). The assertion for
k\geq 4
even is obvious from Theorem
1.4
(
C
). If
k\geq 3
is odd and
g\geq\left\lceil\tfrac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rceil+1
, then
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0
holds because Theorem
1.5
(
a
) implies the equality

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\max\left\{0,\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}.

In view of this,
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})})=\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk
follows from Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. If
n=2^{e}
and
k\geq 3
is arbitrary, then
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=0
due to
[
Dav18
]
. So, we get from parts (
A
) and (
C
) of Theorem
1.4
that

\displaystyle 2nk\geq\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))

\displaystyle\geq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk\ \text{ and}

\displaystyle 2nk\geq\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})})

\displaystyle\geq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk\ \text{ for all }\ k\geq 3.

Next, we look at the case of (
ii
). Recall that
\pi_{1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\cong H_{1}(N_{g};{\mathbb{Z}})
is abelian since
n\geq 2
, and that
SP^{n}(N_{g})
is a path-connected closed non-orientable
2n
-manifold. So,
[
CV21
, Theorem 1.2]
implies that
\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is not maximal, i.e., it does not exceed
4n-1
. From Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
, we have that

(4.I)

\dim(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2n<2^{e+2}-1\leq\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g})).

Thus,
\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\cat(C_{\Delta_{2}SP^{n}(N_{g})})\leq\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
holds by Theorem
2.2
and
[
GCV13
, Theorem 10 (2)]
, respectively. Finally, if
n=2^{e}
, then we have
\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq 4n-1\leq\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, so all inequalities are equalities.
∎

4.1 Remark
.

The result from
[
CV21
]
used above says that
\TC_{2}(M)<2\dim(M)
if
M
is a connected closed non-orientable manifold of dimension greater than 1 and with abelian
\pi_{1}(M)
. This result does not extend to
\TC_{k}(M)
for any
k\geq 3
in view of Corollary
1.6
.

4.2 Remark
.

As observed in (
4.I
), the inequality
\dim(SP^{n}(N_{g}))<\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
holds for
n,g\geq 1
, so
[
Dra14
, Theorem 2.5]
gives
\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\TC^{M}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. Here,
\TC^{M}(X)
stands for the
monoidal topological complexity
of
X
introduced in
[
IS10
]
, which is a quantity equal to either
\TC_{2}(X)
or
\TC_{2}(X)+1
for a CW complex
X
, and conjectured to be always equal to
\TC_{2}(X)
, see
[
IS10
]
. To the best of our knowledge, another conjecture on
\TC^{M}
(see
[
Dra14
, Section 4]
), that
\TC^{M}(X)=\cat(C_{\Delta_{2}X})
, is also still open. Our results give the equalities

\cat(C_{\Delta_{2}SP^{2^{e}}(N_{g})})=\TC_{2}(SP^{2^{e}}(N_{g}))=\TC^{M}(SP^{2^{e}}(N_{g}))=2^{e+2}-1

for
e,g\geq 1
. Therefore, the conjectures of both Iwase–Sakai and Dranishnikov are verified for infinitely many (non-trivial) symmetric products of closed non-orientable surfaces. The same assertion holds true for all symmetric products of closed orientable surfaces of genus
\geq 1
due to
[
DCDJ
, Theorem 6.8]
(see also
[
Jau25
]
).

We now verify the
rationality conjecture
of Farber and Oprea
[
FO19
]
for the
\TC
-generating function of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
for
n,g\geq 1
.

4.3 Theorem
.

Let
n,g\geq 1
be integers. Then the
\TC
-generating function of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
, namely the formal power series

f_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\ \TC_{k+1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\ t^{k},

represents a rational function of the form

\frac{P_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)}{(1-t)^{2}},

where
P_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)
is an integral polynomial whose value at
t=1
is
\cat(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. Moreover, the degree of
P_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)
does not exceed

\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{g-1}\right\rfloor+3

when
g\geq 2
. In fact, the degree of
P_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)
does not exceed
3
if either of the following is true:

(1)

g\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{2}\right\rfloor+2
and
n\geq 1
is arbitrary;

(2)

n=2^{e}
for some
e\geq 0
and
g\geq 1
is arbitrary.

Before supplying a proof, we make some remarks. Recall from
[
CAG+18
]
that
\gap_{2}(P^{2n})=4n-2^{e+2}+1>0
if
2^{e}\leq n<2^{e+1}
for some
e\geq 0
. Therefore, the condition in part (1) is
g\geq 2n-2^{e+1}+2
. Note that the rationality conjecture for
g=1
can be directly verified using
[
Dav18
, Proposition 1.4]
. Indeed, one has
\TC_{k}(P^{2n})=\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})=2nk
if
k\geq 2^{\ell+1}-1
, where
\ell
is the length of the longest string of consecutive
1
’s in the binary expansion of
n
. Since
\mathsf{cl}(P^{2n})=\cat(P^{2n})
is well-known (see, for instance,
[
CLOT03
]
), one can apply
[
FKS20
, Theorem 1]
to complete the verification. Such arguments work for
g\geq 2
as well (see Remark
6.6
); however, we will explicitly verify the rationality conjecture for
g\geq 2
by giving a relatively better upper bound on the degree of
P_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Fix
n\geq 1
and
g\geq 2
. Note that

k\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{g-1}\right\rfloor+3\iff k>\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{g-1}+2\iff g\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2.

The formula in
[
Dav18
, Theorem 1.6]
implies
\gap_{2}(P^{2n})\geq\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
for
k\geq 3
. In view of this and the above implications, we see that

k\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{g-1}\right\rfloor+3\implies g\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2\geq\left\lceil\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rceil+1.

Therefore, we apply Corollary
1.6
(
i
) to conclude that
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk
for
k\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{g-1}\right\rfloor+3
. For brevity, let
\mathscr{D}:=\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{g-1}\right\rfloor+3
. Then we can write

f_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathscr{D}-2}\ \TC_{k+1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\ t^{k}\ +\ 2n\sum_{k=\mathscr{D}-1}^{\infty}(k+1)t^{k}.

Of course,
\sum_{k=\mathscr{D}-1}^{\infty}(k+1)t^{k}=\tfrac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{k=\mathscr{D}-1}^{\infty}t^{k+1}\right)=\tfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac{t^{\mathscr{D}}}{1-t}\right)
. Hence,

f_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathscr{D}-2}\ \TC_{k+1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\ t^{k}\ +\ 2n\ \frac{\mathscr{D}t^{\mathscr{D}-1}-(\mathscr{D}-1)t^{\mathscr{D}}}{(1-t)^{2}}\\
=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}\left(2n\left(\mathscr{D}t^{\mathscr{D}-1}-(\mathscr{D}-1)t^{\mathscr{D}}\right)\ +\ (1-t)^{2}\ \sum_{k=1}^{\mathscr{D}-2}\ \TC_{k+1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\ t^{k}\right).

Because
\cat(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2n
due to
[
Jau25
, Theorem B]
, we see that the integral polynomial inside the above bracket indeed satisfies the asserted properties. This verifies the rationality conjecture in the general case with the upper bound
\mathscr{D}
on the degree of
P_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)
.

In the case of (1), if
k\geq 3
is odd, then

g\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{2}\right\rfloor+2\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rfloor+2\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rfloor+2\geq\left\lceil\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-1}\right\rceil+1.

Similarly, if
k\geq 4
is even, then the condition on
g
implies

g\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{2}\right\rfloor+2\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2.

Thus, Corollary
1.6
(
i
) gives
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk
for all
k\geq 3
. In the case of (2), we again get
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk
for all
k\geq 3
by Corollary
1.6
(
i
). So, in any case, we have the expression

\displaystyle f_{SP^{n}(N_{g})}(t)

\displaystyle=\TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\ t\ +\ 2n\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}t^{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}\left(\cat\left(SP^{n}(N_{g})\right)\left(3t^{2}-2t^{3}\right)\ +\ \TC_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\ t\left(1-t\right)^{2}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

The rationality conjecture, which is open for closed manifolds, has been verified for only a few classes of spaces (and even fewer classes of non-orientable manifolds), the most recent being that of the symmetric products of closed orientable surfaces, see
[
Jau25
]
(and also
[
FO19
, Section 8]
and
[
HL22
]
for some other known classes). Theorem
4.3
extends the class of such (non-orientable) manifolds, for many of which the
\TC
-generating function can be explicitly computed.

4.4 Remark
.

Fix some
n\geq 1
. Then it follows from the proof of Theorem
4.3
that for
g\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{\gap_{2}(P^{2n})}{2}\right\rfloor+2
and
r\geq k\geq 3
,

\TC_{r}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))-\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=(r-k)\cat(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2n(r-k).

In particular, the sequence
\{\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\}_{k\geq 3}
is an arithmetic progression with common difference
2n=\cat(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
for
g
moderately large. Of course, the same conclusions hold for
\cat(C_{\Delta_{k}SP^{n}(N_{g})})
as well in this range due to Corollary
1.6
(
i
).

We conclude this section by making an observation regarding the analog of the Ganea conjecture on LS-category
[
CLOT03
]
for sequential topological complexity.

4.5 Corollary
.

For a triplet
(n,g,k)
satisfying the hypotheses of Corollary
1.6
(
i
), we have that

\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})\times S^{m})=\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))+\TC_{k}(S^{m})

holds for all odd
m\geq 1
. In particular, the
\TC_{k}
-Ganea conjecture holds for
SP^{n}(N_{g})
with respect to odd spheres.

Proof.

For such triplets, we have
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. We also have from
[
Rud10
]
that
\zcl_{k}(S^{m})=\TC_{k}(S^{m})=k-1
. The results of
[
BGRT14
]
imply that
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))+\zcl_{k}(S^{m})\leq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})\times S^{m})

\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g})\times S^{m})\leq\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))+\TC_{k}(S^{m})
. This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Cohomological aspects

5.A.
The cohomology of
SP^{n}(N_{g})

The description of the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-cohomology ring of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
is due to Kallel and Salvatore
[
KS06
]
. We recall the details below.

The closed non-orientable surface
N_{g}
(for
g\geq 1
) has a CW structure consisting of one
0
-cell,
g

1
-cells, and one
2
-cell attached by the word
a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}\cdots a_{g}^{2}
, where
a_{i}
is the generator in
H_{1}(P_{i}^{2})
for
1\leq i\leq g
. Here,
P_{i}^{2}
is a copy of the projective plane, and we use the connected sum expression
N_{g}=\#_{i=1}^{g}P_{i}^{2}
. The elements
a_{i}
form a basis for
H_{1}(N_{g})
. We use the same notations for their images in
H_{1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
under the map induced by a basepoint inclusion
N_{g}\hookrightarrow SP^{n}(N_{g})
. Let
b\in H_{2}(N_{g})
denote the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-fundamental class and its image in
H_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
. We denote the Hom duals of
a_{i}
and
b
by
x_{i}
and
y
, respectively. In
H^{*}(N_{g})
, we have
x_{i}^{2}=y
and
x_{i}x_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
.

5.1 Theorem

(
[
KS06
, Section 4]
)
.

For
n,g\geq 1
, the cohomology ring
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is generated by the degree
1
classes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{g}
and the degree
2
class
y
, subject only to the following relations:

(1)

x_{i}^{2}=y
for each
1\leq i\leq g
, and

(2)

x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}y^{s}=0
whenever
r+s>n
for distinct indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
.

It is convenient to use
I_{n,g}
to denote the ideal generated by the above two sets of relations. Then the theorem says that
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\left[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{g},y\right]/I_{n,g}
.

5.2 Remark
.

It follows from
[
KS06
, Lemma 19]
that a monomial
x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}y^{s}
in
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
with distinct indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
vanishes if and only if
r+s>n
.

In fact, the following description can be derived from
[
KS06
, Theorem 6]
.

5.3 Theorem
.

For
n,g\geq 1
, an additive basis (the standard one for our purposes) for the vector space
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is formed by the monomials

y^{t}\ \prod_{i=1}^{g}x_{i}^{\varepsilon_{i}}=x_{1}^{2t+\varepsilon_{1}}\ \prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{i}^{\varepsilon_{i}}

satisfying
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
t\geq 0
, and
t+\sum_{i=1}^{g}\varepsilon_{i}\leq n
, where we write
y=x_{1}^{2}
without loss of generality (cf. Theorem
5.1
).

Taking
g=1
(resp.
n=1
), Theorem
5.3
recovers the cohomology ring of
H^{*}(P^{2n})
for
n\geq 1
(resp.
H^{*}(N_{g})
for
g\geq 1
).

5.4 Remark
.

For
g^{\prime}\geq g
, consider the ring homomorphism

\iota\colon H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\to H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g^{\prime}}))

given by
\iota(y)=y
and
\iota(x_{i})=x_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq g
. Since
\iota
maps the additive basis of its domain to a subset of the additive basis of its codomain, it is a monomorphism. We will need the ring monomorphism
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}\to H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g^{\prime}}))^{\otimes k}
given by the
k
-fold tensor product of
\iota
.

5.B.
The capacity function and the cohomology of products of
SP^{n}(N_{g})

For a given integer
k\geq 2
, we take the tensor product additive basis in the ring
H^{*}((SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k})\cong H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}
coming from the basis in Theorem
5.3
. For
1\leq r\leq k
, let
\proj_{r}\colon(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k}\to SP^{n}(N_{g})
be the projection onto the
r
-th Cartesian factor. For
1\leq i\leq g
, let

(5.I)

x_{r,i}:=\proj_{r}^{*}(x_{i})=1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1\otimes x_{i}\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k},

where
x_{i}
appears only at the
r
-th position.

5.5 Definition
.

The
capacity
of a basis element
x_{1}^{a}x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{g}^{\varepsilon_{g}}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
in Theorem
5.3
is defined as

c\left(x_{1}^{a}\prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{i}^{\varepsilon_{i}}\right)=n-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a+1}{2}\right\rfloor+\sum_{i=2}^{g}\varepsilon_{i}\right)\geq 0.

We also set
c(0)=-\infty
. Similarly, the
capacity
of a tensor product basis element
b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}
is defined as

c\left(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\right)=\sum_{r=1}^{k}c(b_{r}),

where
b_{r}:=\proj_{r}^{*}(x_{1}^{a_{r,1}}x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{r,2}}\cdots x_{g}^{\varepsilon_{r,g}})=x_{r,1}^{a_{r,1}}x_{r,2}^{\varepsilon_{r,2}}\cdots x_{r,g}^{\varepsilon_{r,g}}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}
for
1\leq r\leq k
.

5.6 Remark
.

For
a\geq 0
and
\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{g}\in\{0,1\}
, Remark
5.2
and Theorem
5.3
imply that the monomial
\mu:=x_{1}^{a}x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{g}^{\varepsilon_{g}}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is either a basis element or trivial, depending on whether
c(\mu)\geq 0
or
c(\mu)=-\infty
. In particular, for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
,

x_{1}^{a}x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{j}^{\varepsilon_{j}}\cdot x_{j+1}^{\varepsilon_{j+1}}\cdots x_{g}^{\varepsilon_{g}}=0\iff c(x_{1}^{a}x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{j}^{\varepsilon_{j}})<\varepsilon_{j+1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{g}.

In other words,
c(x_{1}^{a}x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{j}^{\varepsilon_{j}})
is the maximal number of distinct elements in
\{x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{g}\}
whose product with
x_{1}^{a}x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{j}^{\varepsilon_{j}}
is non-zero.

The following result, a straightforward Cartesian-wise extension of the previous fact, will be crucial for the computations in the final section of the paper.

5.7 Lemma
.

Let
g^{\prime}>g
and pick a basis element
b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}
as in Definition
5.5
. In terms of the ring map
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}\hookrightarrow H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g^{\prime}}))^{\otimes k}
in Remark
5.4
, we have

c\left(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{j}x_{e_{t},i_{t}}\right)\leq c\left(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\right)-j

for any integers
e_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
i_{t}\in\{g+1,\ldots,g^{\prime}\}
satisfying
i_{t}\neq i_{s}
whenever
t\neq s
and
e_{t}=e_{s}
. In particular,
b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{j}x_{e_{t},i_{t}}
vanishes whenever
c(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k})<j
.

We close the section with a couple of additional properties of the capacity function that will be useful later in the paper.

5.8 Proposition
.

Let
p_{r}\in\{0,\ldots,2n\}
for
1\leq r\leq k
. If
\sum_{r=1}^{k}p_{r}\leq A
, then

c\left(x_{1,1}^{2n-p_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n-p_{k}}\right)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{A}{2}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

By definition,
c(x_{r,1}^{2n-p_{r}})=n-\left\lfloor\tfrac{2n-p_{r}+1}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\tfrac{p_{r}}{2}\right\rfloor
. Therefore,

c\left(x_{1,1}^{2n-p_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n-p_{k}}\right)=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r}}{2}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{k}p_{r}}{2}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{A}{2}\right\rfloor.

∎

5.9 Proposition
.

Let
b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}\hookrightarrow H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))^{\otimes k}
be a basis element. Then for any basis element
\alpha
in the expansion of a product

p=b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\cdot\prod_{r=2}^{k}\left(x_{1,g+1}+x_{r,g+1}\right)^{\varepsilon_{r}},

we have the inequality

c(\alpha)\leq c\left(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\right)-\left\lfloor\frac{1+\sum_{r=2}^{k}\varepsilon_{r}}{2}\right\rfloor.

In particular, the product
p
vanishes if
c(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k})<\left\lfloor\tfrac{1+\sum_{r=2}^{k}\varepsilon_{r}}{2}\right\rfloor
.

Proof.

For convenience, write
\sum_{r=2}^{k}\varepsilon_{r}=2q+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
. By Lemma
5.7
, the summand in the expansion of
p
with the largest capacity is
b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\cdot x_{1,g+1}^{2q+\varepsilon}
, which is the same as
(b_{1}\ x_{1,1}^{2q}\ x_{1,g+1}^{\varepsilon})\otimes b_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}
. We have for its capacity that

\displaystyle c\left((b_{1}\ x_{1,1}^{2q}\ x_{1,g+1}^{\varepsilon})\otimes b_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\left(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\right)-(q+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=c\left(b_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{k}\right)-\left\lfloor\frac{1+2q+\varepsilon}{2}\right\rfloor.

∎

6.
k
-th zero-divisors

Let
n,g\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
be integers. Let
\Delta_{k,n,g}\colon SP^{n}(N_{g})\to(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k}
be the diagonal map. We now study the kernel of the
k
-fold iterated cup product multiplication, i.e., the kernel of the homomorphism

\Delta^{*}_{k,n,g}\colon H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}\cong H^{*}((SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k})\to H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))

induced by
\Delta_{k,n,g}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-cohomology. The subring
\Ker(\Delta_{k,n,g}^{*})
is the ideal of the
k
-th zero-divisors of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
. For instance, in terms of the notation in (
5.I
), elements of the form
x_{r,i}+x_{s,i}
are
k
-th zero divisors.

The case
g=1
of the following result was shown in
[
CAG+18
]
.

6.1 Proposition
.

For
k\geq 2
and
n,g\geq 1
, the
k
-th zero-divisors
x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}
, where
2\leq r\leq k
and
1\leq i\leq g
, generate the ideal
\Ker(\Delta^{*}_{k,n,g})
of
k
-th zero-divisors.

In preparation for the proof, we first define the ideal

J_{k,n,g}:=\left\langle x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\ \middle|\ 2\leq r\leq k\ \text{and}\ 1\leq i\leq g\right\rangle

generated by the specific zero-divisors of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
mentioned in Proposition
6.1
. It is clear that
J_{k,n,g}
is a sub-ideal of
\Ker(\Delta_{k,n,g}^{*})
. Additionally, for
t\geq 0
and
\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{g}\in\{0,1\}
, we set

m\left(t,\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{g}\right):=x_{1}^{t}\ x_{2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\ \cdots\ x_{g}^{\varepsilon_{g}}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g})),

and for
1\leq r\leq k
,

m_{r}(t,\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{g}):=\proj_{r}^{*}(m\left(t,\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{g}\right))=x_{r,1}^{t}\ x_{r,2}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\ \cdots\ x_{r,g}^{\varepsilon_{g}}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k},

where
\proj_{r}
is as in (
5.I
).

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Consider the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(SP^{k}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(SP^{k}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}/J_{k,n,g},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\proj_{1}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta^{*}_{k,n,g}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}

where
q
is the canonical projection and
j
is determined by
\Delta_{k,n,g}^{*}
on the quotient since
J_{k,n,g}\subset\Ker(\Delta_{k,n,g}^{*})
. The composition on the top is an isomorphism since
\text{proj}_{1}\circ\Delta_{k,n,g}
is the identity on
SP^{n}(N_{g})
. We prove that
i=q\circ\proj_{1}^{*}
is an isomorphism, which forces the corresponding property for
j
and, thus, the desired equality
J_{k,n,g}=\Ker(\Delta_{k,n,g}^{*})
.

Note that
i
is a monomorphism since
j\circ i
is so. On the other hand, the congruence
x_{r,i}\equiv x_{1,i}\text{ mod }(J_{k,n,g})
, valid for
1\leq r\leq k
and
1\leq i\leq g
, shows

\prod_{r=1}^{k}m_{r}\left(t_{r},\varepsilon_{r,2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r,g}\right)\equiv m_{1}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{k}t_{r},\sum_{r=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{r,2},\ldots,\sum_{r=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{r,g}\right)\text{ mod}\left(J_{k,n,g}\right).

The element on the left-hand side is a typical generator of the domain of
q
, while the image of the element under
q
on the right-hand side belongs to
\operatorname{Im}(i)
. It follows that
i
is an epimorphism.
∎

Because
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
is the length of the longest non-trivial cup product in
\Ker(\Delta^{*}_{k,n,g})
, Proposition
6.1
yields the following characterization of
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
.

6.2 Corollary
.

For
k\geq 2
and
n,g\geq 1
, we have that

\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\max\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a_{r,i}\ \middle|\ 0\neq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{a_{r,i}}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}\right\}.

For fixed
n
, we now compare the
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
values as
k
and
g
vary.

6.3 Corollary
.

For
k\geq 2
and
n,g\geq 1
, we have that

\cdots\geq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g+1}))\geq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\geq\cdots\geq\zcl_{k}(P^{2n}).

Proof.

In view of Proposition
6.1
, this follows from the fact that the ring inclusion

H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}\hookrightarrow H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g^{\prime}}))^{\otimes k}

constructed for

in Remark

maps
x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\in H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}
into the corresponding
k
-th zero divisor in
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g^{\prime}}))^{\otimes k}
.
∎

6.4 Corollary
.

For
k\geq 2
and
n,g\geq 1
, we have that

\cdots\geq\zcl_{k+1}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\geq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\geq\cdots\geq\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g})).

Proof.

For
k_{1}\leq k_{2}
, consider the projection
\pi\colon(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k_{2}}\to(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k_{1}}
that forgets the last

Cartesian factors of
(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k_{2}}
. This induces a monomorphism
\pi^{*}\colon H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k_{1}}\to H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k_{2}}
satisfying

for

and

. Thus, the inequality
\zcl_{k_{2}}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\geq\zcl_{k_{1}}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
holds in view of Proposition

.
∎

6.5 Example
.

Corollary
6.3
gives
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk
for any
g\geq 1
, provided
\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})=2nk
. In turn, as proved in
[
Dav18
, Proposition 1.4]
, the latter equality holds for
k\geq 2^{\ell+1}-1
, where
\ell
is the length of the longest string of consecutive
1
’s in the binary expansion of
n
. Note that
\TC_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
can attain its maximal possible value
2nk
without
\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})
doing so, see Corollary
1.6
.

6.6 Remark
.

The non-decreasing sequence in Corollary
6.3
is bounded from above by
2nk
, so it is eventually constant. On the other hand, the sequence in Corollary
6.4
is unbounded with an eventually strict linear behavior. Indeed, Example
6.5
shows that all terms with
k
large enough in the sequence of Corollary
6.4
are maximal possible (i.e., equal to
2nk
).

The following result reduces the search for tuples
(a_{r,i})
realizing
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
in Corollary
6.2
.

6.7 Proposition
.

For
k\geq 2
and
n,g\geq 1
, the maximal sum in the set of Corollary
6.2
is attained with a tuple of exponents
(a_{r,i})
satisfying the following three properties:

(1)

a_{2,1}\geq a_{3,1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{k,1}
;

(2)

1\geq a_{2,2}\geq a_{2,3}\geq\cdots\geq a_{2,g}\geq 0
;

(3)

a_{r,i}\in\{0,1\}
for all
2\leq r\leq k
and
2\leq i\leq g
.

Proof.

Assume
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\sum a_{r,i}
, as in Corollary
6.2
. Say
a_{r,i}=2\alpha_{r,i}+\varepsilon_{r,i}
with
\varepsilon_{r,i}\in\{0,1\}
and
\alpha_{r,i}\geq 0
for
2\leq r\leq k
and
2\leq i\leq g
. The relation
x_{r,i}^{2}=x_{r,1}^{2}
gives

\displaystyle\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{a_{r,i}}

\displaystyle=\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{2\alpha_{r,i}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{\varepsilon_{r,i}}

\displaystyle=\left(x_{1,1}+x_{r,1}\right)^{2\alpha_{r,i}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{\varepsilon_{r,i}}.

The non-zero product corresponding to the tuple
(a_{r,i})
then becomes

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{a_{r,i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{r=2}^{k}\left(x_{1,1}+x_{r,1}\right)^{a_{r,1}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,1}+x_{r,1}\right)^{2\alpha_{r,i}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{\varepsilon_{r,i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{r=2}^{k}\left(x_{1,1}+x_{r,1}\right)^{a_{r,1}+2\sum_{i=2}^{g}\alpha_{r,i}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{\varepsilon_{r,i}}

\displaystyle=:\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{b_{r,i}}.

The new set of exponents
(b_{r,i})
satisfies Property (
3
) and still realizes
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
, since
\sum a_{r,i}=\sum b_{r,i}
by construction. Next, a permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
produces an automorphism on
(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{k}
that permutes the Cartesian factors. If
\sigma
fixes
1
, then the corresponding automorphism
\sigma^{*}
on
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes k}
satisfies

0\neq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{b_{r,i}}\xmapsto{\sigma^{*}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{\sigma(r),i}\right)^{b_{r,i}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{b_{\sigma^{-1}(r),i}}.

Thus, the tuple of exponents
(c_{r,i}:=b_{\sigma^{-1}(r),i})
realizes
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
and satisfies both Properties (
1
) and (
3
) for suitably chosen
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
.

Lastly, any permutation
\rho\in\Sigma_{g}
induces an automorphism
\rho^{*}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{g},y]
by permuting the classes
x_{i}
. This descends to an automorphism, still denoted by
\rho^{*}
, on
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{g},y]/I_{n,g}
, where
I_{n,g}
is the ideal of relations described in Theorem
5.1
. Choosing
\rho
so that
c_{2,\rho^{-1}(2)}\geq\cdots\geq c_{2,\rho^{-1}(g)}
and
\rho(1)=1
, we get

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{c_{r,i}}\xmapsto{(\rho^{*})^{\otimes k}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,\rho(i)}+x_{r,\rho(i)}\right)^{c_{r,i}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{c_{r,\rho^{-1}(i)}}.

So, the tuple of exponents
(d_{r,i}:=c_{r,\rho^{-1}(i)})
attains
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
and satisfies Properties (
1
), (
2
), and (
3
).
∎

7.
The case of
k=2

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
.
Recall that
2^{e}\leq n<2^{e+1}
for some
e\geq 0
. For convenience, let us write
n=2^{e}+d
, where
0\leq d<2^{e}
. We divide the proof into two cases.

Case (1):
g\leq 2d+1
.

By Proposition
6.7
, the largest possible product of zero-divisors that is potentially non-zero is

(7.I)

\left(x_{1,1}+x_{2,1}\right)^{2^{e+2}-1}\ \prod_{i=2}^{g}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{2,i}\right).

We show (
7.I
) is indeed non-zero, which yields the assertion. Note that, although all binomial coefficients
2^{e+2}-1\choose j
with
0\leq j\leq 2^{e+2}-1
are odd (by Lucas’s theorem), Theorems
5.1
and
5.3
imply that the expansion of (
7.I
) as a sum of basis elements contains the monomial

\left(x_{1,1}^{2^{e+2}-1-2n}\otimes x_{2,1}^{2n}\,\right)\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}=\left(x_{1,1}^{2^{e+1}-2d-1}\ \prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}\right)\otimes x_{2,1}^{2n}\ ,

which is indeed a basis element in view of Theorem
5.3
and the current hypothesis
g\leq 2d+1
.
∎

Case (2):
g>2d+1
.

We have established the non-vanishing of the cup product

\left(x_{1,1}+x_{2,1}\right)^{2^{e+2}-1}\ \prod_{i=2}^{2d+1}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{2,i}\right),

so that
\zcl_{2}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\geq 2^{e+2}+2d-1
. Equality holds here in view of Proposition
6.7
(
2
) and the fact that

\left(x_{1,1}+x_{2,1}\right)^{2^{e+2}-1-\ell}\ \prod_{i=2}^{2d+\ell+2}\left(x_{1,i}+x_{2,i}\right)

vanishes for all
0\leq\ell\leq\min\{2^{e+2}-1,g-2d-2\}
. Indeed, a potential basis summand in the expansion of such a product would have the form

x_{1,1}^{a}\otimes x_{2,1}^{b}\ \cdot\ \prod_{i\in C}x_{1,i}\otimes\prod_{j\in D}x_{2,j}=\left(x_{1,1}^{a}\prod_{i\in C}x_{1,i}\right)\otimes\left(x_{2,1}^{b}\prod_{j\in D}x_{2,j}\right),

where
a+b=2^{e+2}-1-\ell
and
C\cup D=\{2,\ldots,2d+\ell+2\}
with
C\cap D=\varnothing
. But then

\max\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{a+1}{2}\right\rfloor+|C|,\left\lfloor\frac{b+1}{2}\right\rfloor+|D|\right\}\leq n,

so that

(7.II)

\left\lfloor\frac{a+1}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b+1}{2}\right\rfloor+2d+\ell+1\leq 2^{e+1}+2d,

which is impossible. This is because if
\ell
is even, then
a
and
b
would have opposite parities and so

\left\lfloor\frac{a+1}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b+1}{2}\right\rfloor+\ell+1=\left\lfloor\frac{a+b+2}{2}\right\rfloor+\ell+1=2^{e+1}+\frac{\ell}{2}+1,

which contradicts (
7.II
). Likewise, if
\ell
is odd, then
a
and
b
would have the same parity, so that

\left\lfloor\frac{a+1}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b+1}{2}\right\rfloor+\ell+1\geq\left\lfloor\frac{a+b+2}{2}\right\rfloor-1+\ell+1=2^{e+1}+\frac{\ell+1}{2},

which again contradicts (
7.II
).
∎

7.1 Remark
.

The choice of zero-divisors of
SP^{n}(N_{g})
matters while forming long lengths of non-trivial cup products in
H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))^{\otimes 2}
. Indeed, it was explained in
[
Jau25
, Section 6.C]
that
\prod_{i=1}^{j}(x_{1,i}+x_{2,i})^{2}=0
for
j\leq\min\{g,n\}
.

8.
The case of odd
k\geq 3

In this section, we first develop a method to approach Theorem
1.5
and then obtain a proof of its part (
a
) for
k\geq 3
odd.

8.A.
General preparation

The discussion in this subsection applies to all
k\geq 3
(and not just to odd
k
), and will be used in Section
9
as well.

Working on
H^{*}(P^{2n})^{\otimes k}\cong H^{*}(SP^{n}(N_{1}))^{\otimes k}
, let
\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
denote the set consisting of all those non-negative integers
\ell\leq 2n
for which there is a non-zero cup product as in (
8.I
) below with the indicated basis element in its expansion.

(8.I)

\prod_{r=2}^{k}\left(x_{1,1}+x_{r,1}\right)^{a_{r,1}}=x_{1,1}^{\ell}\otimes x_{2,1}^{2n}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n}+\cdots.

Note that other basis elements (if any) in the expansion of (
8.I
) have the form

(8.II)

x_{1,1}^{t_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{t_{k}}\text{ with }t_{r}<2n\text{ for some }r\in\{2,\ldots,k\}.

Basis elements as the one indicated in (
8.I
) play a critical role in
[
Dav18
]
, where it is shown that
\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})=a_{2,1}+a_{3,1}+\cdots+a_{k,1}=2n(k-1)+\ell
and

(8.III)

2n-\ell=\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=\max_{i\in S(2n)}\{0,2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)\},

provided
\ell=\max\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
; see (
1.II
) and Definition
1.2
.

Building on Davis’s work, we now consider
extended
products

(8.IV)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\!\!\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{a_{r,i}}=\left(x_{1,1}^{\ell}\otimes x_{2,1}^{2n}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n}+\cdots\right)\!\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}\!\!\left(x_{1,i}+x_{r,i}\right)^{a_{r,i}}

for each
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
with corresponding product (
8.I
), which fixes the exponents
a_{r,1}
, and for each set of additional exponents
a_{r,i}\in\{0,1\}
with
2\leq r\leq k
and
2\leq i\leq g
. This time, we focus on the monomial

(8.V)

\left(x_{1,1}^{\ell}\prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}^{\sum_{r=2}^{k}a_{r,i}}\right)\otimes x_{2,1}^{2n}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n},

which, because of (
8.II
), arises in the expansion of the left-hand side of (
8.IV
) only from the multiplication of the leading summands of factors in the product on the right-hand side of (
8.IV
). In particular, assuming (
8.V
) is non-trivial, we get

(8.VI)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a_{r,i}\leq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\quad\text{and}\quad\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq 2nk-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a_{r,i}.

Furthermore, as discussed in (
8.III
), the last two inequalities specialize to

(8.VII)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a_{r,i}\leq\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}));\\
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a_{r,i},\end{split}

provided
\ell=\max\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
. The benefit of the last two estimates is that the quantities
\zcl_{k}(P^{2n})
and
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
appearing therein are completely understood (see (
8.III
)). Our target quantities
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
and
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
can then be determined in a number of cases by proving that some suitable choice of the additional exponents
a_{r,i}
(
i\geq 2
) renders equalities in (
8.VII
). The simplest such scenario is given in Proposition
8.1
below. In the few cases where this strategy falls short, the precise values of
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
and
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
will be obtained using the more general setting in (
8.VI
).

8.1 Proposition
.

Take
n\geq 1
,
k\geq 3
, and
\ell=\max\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
. In the setting of (
8.VII
), assume that the element in (
8.V
) is non-zero and that

a_{r,i}=1
for all
2\leq r\leq k
and
2\leq i\leq g
. Then both estimates in (
8.VII
) are sharp. In particular,

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=\max_{i\in S(2n)}\left\{0,2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}.

Proof.

The proof is parallel to that for the case
k=2
and
g\leq 2d+1
in Section
7
. Namely, under the present hypotheses, Proposition
6.7
shows that (
8.IV
) is the largest possible non-zero product of
k
-th zero divisors. The fact that this product is non-zero comes from the assumed non-triviality of (
8.V
).
∎

8.B.
Proof of Theorem
1.5
(
a
) for
k\geq 3
odd.

In the expression (
1.II
) for
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
,
Z_{i}(2n)
is odd for each
i\in S(2n)
. Since
k
is odd,
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
is even, say equal to
2m
. If
m=0
, then we are done since
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=0
in view of Corollary
6.3
. So, let us assume that
m>0
. Take
\ell:=\max\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
, so that
\ell=2(n-m)
in (
8.III
). Say
k=2p+1
for some
p\geq 1
, and write
m=qp+s
for some
s\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}
and
q\geq 0
.

We divide the proof into two cases.

Case (1):
1\leq g\leq q+1
.

In this case, Theorem
5.1
and Remark
5.2
imply

x_{1,1}^{\ell}\ \prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}^{k-1}=x_{1,1}^{2(n-m)+2p(g-1)}\neq 0,

because
x_{1,1}^{2}=\proj_{1}^{*}(y)
and
n-m+p(g-1)\leq n-m+pq=n-s\leq n
. This yields the non-triviality of (
8.V
) when
a_{r,i}=1
for all
2\leq r\leq k
and
2\leq i\leq g
. Proposition
8.1
then yields the asserted description of
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
.
∎

Case (2):
g\geq q+2
.

Note that
2s<2p=k-1
by definition. First, for
g=q+2
, we have

x_{1,1}^{\ell}\ \prod_{i=2}^{q+1}x_{1,i}^{k-1}\ x_{1,q+2}^{2s}=x_{1,1}^{2(n-m)+2(pq+s)}=x_{1,1}^{2n}\neq 0,

which yields the non-triviality of (
8.V
) when
a_{r,i}=1
for all relevant pairs
(r,i)
, except for
a_{r,g}=0
with
2s+2\leq r\leq k
. Using (
8.VII
), we then get the optimal estimate

\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\gap_{k}(P^{2n})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a_{r,i}=2m-(k-1)(g-2)-2s=0.

For
g\geq q+3
, Corollary
6.3
then gives
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))\leq\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{q+2}))=0
. We now note that for each
g\geq q+2
, this is indeed the desired conclusion. This is because the inequalities
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=2m<2pq+2p\leq(k-1)(g-1)
imply

2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)-(k-1)(g-1)\leq 2m-(k-1)(g-1)<0

for all
i\in S(2n)
, so
\max_{i\in S(2n)}\left\{0,2^{i+1}-1-kZ_{i}(2n)-(k-1)(g-1)\right\}=0
.
∎

8.2 Remark
.

In the above proof, the second hypothesis of Proposition
8.1
is not satisfied in the case
g\geq q+2
, yet the estimates in (
8.VII
) are sharp. Thus, the conditions in Proposition
8.1
for the sharpness of the estimates in (
8.VII
) are sufficient but not necessary.

9.
The case of even
k\geq 4

In this section, we complete the proof of the remaining parts of Theorem
1.5
. We thus assume
k=2\lambda
for some
\lambda\geq 2
. We can safely assume
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})>0
too, because otherwise we are done in view of Corollary
6.3
. Under these conditions, (
1.II
) implies that
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})
is odd, say
\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=2h-1
with
h\geq 1
. We will analyze three different cases depending on how
h
compares to the products
(g-1)(\lambda-1)
and
(g-1)\lambda
. Indeed, in terms of the current notations, the conditions in Theorem
1.5
translate as

\displaystyle g\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k}\right\rfloor+1

\displaystyle\iff(g-1)\lambda<h;

\displaystyle g\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\gap_{k}(P^{2n})}{k-2}\right\rfloor+2

\displaystyle\iff(g-1)(\lambda-1)\geq h.

First, we prove Theorem
1.5
(
a
) for
k\geq 4
even.

Case (1):
(g-1)\lambda<h
.

Taking
\ell:=\max\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
and
a_{r,i}:=1
for
2\leq r\leq k
and
2\leq i\leq g
, the monomial

x_{1,1}^{\ell}\ \prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}^{\sum_{r=2}^{k}a_{r,i}}=x_{1,1}^{2n-2h+1}\prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}^{k-1}=x_{1,1}^{2n-2h+2(g-1)(\lambda-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}x_{1,i}

is non-zero because
n-h+(g-1)(\lambda-1)+g\leq n
. This yields the non-triviality of (
8.V
) for the indicated values of
a_{r,i}
, and then Proposition
8.1
gives the desired expression for
\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))
.
∎

In preparation for the discussion of the other two cases, we fix

(9.I)

\prod_{r=2}^{k}\left(x_{1,1}+x_{r,1}\right)^{a_{r,1}}=x_{1,1}^{\ell}\otimes x_{2,1}^{2n}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n}+\cdots,

which is a given non-zero product (
8.I
) associated with
\ell:=\max\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
. Note that
\ell=2n-\gap_{k}(P^{2n})=2n-2h+1
is odd, so that one of the exponents
a_{r_{0},1}
in (
9.I
) must be odd. Setting
a^{\prime}_{r_{0},1}:=a_{r_{0},1}-1
and
a^{\prime}_{r,1}:=a_{r,1}
for
r\neq r_{0}
, Lucas’s Theorem gives

(9.II)

\prod_{r=2}^{k}\left(x_{1,1}+x_{r,1}\right)^{a^{\prime}_{r,1}}=x_{1,1}^{\ell-1}\otimes x_{2,1}^{2n}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n}+\cdots\neq 0,

so that
\ell-1\in\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
as well.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.5
(
c
).

Case (2):
h\leq(g-1)(\lambda-1)
.

Note that
g\geq 2
. Write
h=\alpha(\lambda-1)+\beta
, where
\alpha\in\{0,\ldots,g-2\}
and
\beta\in\{0,\ldots,\lambda-1\}
. Working in the context of Subsection
8.A
for
\ell-1=\max\mathcal{L}_{n,k}-1\in\mathcal{L}_{n,k}
, take the product in (
9.II
) as the corresponding non-zero expression (
8.I
). Also, for
(r,i)\in\{2,\ldots,k\}\times\{2,\ldots,g\}
, take

a^{\prime}_{r,i}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $i\leq\alpha+1$ and $r\leq k-1$, or $i=\alpha+2$ and $r\leq 2\beta+1$;}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then

x_{1,1}^{\ell-1}\ \prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}^{\sum_{r=2}^{k}a^{\prime}_{r,i}}=x_{1,1}^{2n-2h}\left(\prod_{i=2}^{\alpha+1}x_{1,i}^{k-2}\right)x_{1,\alpha+2}^{2\beta}=x_{1,1}^{2n-2h+(k-2)\alpha+2\beta}=x_{1,1}^{2n}\neq 0,

which yields the non-triviality of the analogue of (
8.V
) in the context of (
9.II
). Therefore, the second inequality in (
8.VI
) gives the best possible estimate

\displaystyle\gap_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))

\displaystyle\leq 2nk-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
1\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a^{\prime}_{r,i}=2nk-\sum_{r=2}^{k}a^{\prime}_{r,1}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq r\leq k\\
2\leq i\leq g\end{subarray}}a^{\prime}_{r,i}

\displaystyle=2nk-(2n(k-1)+\ell-1)-(\alpha(k-2)+2\beta)

\displaystyle=2n-(2n-2h)-(\alpha(k-2)+2\beta)=0.

∎

Lastly, we prove Theorem
1.5
(
b
) by establishing the equivalent Theorem
1.4
(
B
).

Case (3):
(g-1)(\lambda-1)<h\leq(g-1)\lambda
.

Our task is to establish the equality
\zcl_{k}(SP^{n}(N_{g}))=2nk-t
, where
t:=h-(g-1)(\lambda-1)
. Clearly

\displaystyle x_{1,1}^{\ell}\ \prod_{i=2}^{g}x_{1,i}^{\sum_{r=2}^{k}a_{r,i}}

Proof of Lemma
9.1
.

We can safely assume
m=x_{1,1}^{2n-p_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k,1}^{2n-p_{k}}
, with
p_{r}\geq 0
for
1\leq r\leq k
, as well as
\deg(e)=\gap_{k}(P^{2n})+d-t+1
, which, by hypothesis, agrees with
d+h+(g-1)(\lambda-1)
. Then
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}=2h-1+d
, so that Proposition
5.8
gives

c(m)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2h-1+d}{2}\right\rfloor=h+\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor.

The vanishing of
m\cdot e
then follows from an iterated application of Proposition
5.9
and the fact that

\sum_{i=2}^{g}\left\lfloor\frac{\delta_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor>h+\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor,

where
\delta_{i}:=\sum_{r=2}^{k}\delta_{r,i}
. In turn, the previous inequality is a special case of the purely algebraic relation

(9.IV)

\min\left\{\,\sum_{i=2}^{g}\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}0\leq\varepsilon_{i}\leq k-1,&\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{g}\varepsilon_{i}=d+h+(g-1)(\lambda-1)&\end{matrix}\hskip-8.53581pt\right\}>h+\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor,

whose validity is argued next.

First, note that
\delta_{i}\leq k-1=2\lambda-1
for
2\leq i\leq g
, so

(g-1)(2\lambda-1)\geq\sum_{i=2}^{g}\delta_{i}=\deg(e)=d+h+(g-1)(\lambda-1),

which yields the third inequality in

(g-1)(\lambda-1)<h\leq d+h\leq(g-1)\lambda

(the other two hold by hypothesis). We can then write
d+h=(g-1)\lambda-j
for some
j\in\{0,\ldots,g-2\}
, so that every summation
\sum\varepsilon_{i}
in (
9.IV
) equals
(g-1)(k-1)-j
. It follows that any tuple
\varepsilon:=(\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{g})
having
j
coordinates equal to
k-2
and the rest equal to
k-1
, which is unique up to permutation, gives an instance of the set in (
9.IV
) that also satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{g}\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{j+1}\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor+\sum_{i=j+2}^{g}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=j(\lambda-1)+(g-j-1)\lambda=\lambda(g-1)-j>h+\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor,

where the last inequality holds because
h=(g-1)\lambda-j-d
and
d>\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor
. The inequality in (
9.IV
) will then follow once we argue that

(9.V)

\sum_{i=2}^{g}\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\sum_{i=2}^{g}\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor

holds for any other instance
\varepsilon^{\prime}:=(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime}_{g})
of the set in (
9.IV
). For this, note that, just as the tuple
\varepsilon
has
exactly

g-j-1
coordinates (say the first ones) with the maximal possible value
k-1
, the tuple
\varepsilon^{\prime}
must have
at least

g-j-1
coordinates (say again the first ones) with the maximal possible value
k-1
. We can ignore these first
g-j-1
coordinates, as they represent no difference for both sides in (
9.V
). From the remaining coordinates, we can likewise ignore those in
\varepsilon^{\prime}
together with the corresponding ones in
\varepsilon
with value
k-2
. Let
R\subset\{2,\ldots,g\}
denote the set of the remaining coordinates. All of these then have value
k-2
in the case of
\varepsilon
, while value different from
k-2
in the case of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Let
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
(resp.
R^{\prime\prime}\subseteq R
) consist of the coordinates where
\varepsilon^{\prime}
has value strictly smaller than
k-2
(resp. value equal to
k-1
). Since
\varepsilon_{i}+1=k-1
is odd for
i\in R
, we see that

(9.VI)

\displaystyle i\in R^{\prime}

\displaystyle\implies 0\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}}{2}\right\rfloor;

(9.VII)

\displaystyle i\in R^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\implies 1=\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor,\text{ as well as }\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}=1.

By definition of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
we have
\sum_{i=2}^{g}\varepsilon_{i}=\sum_{i=2}^{g}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}
, which by construction yields

\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\varepsilon_{i}+\sum_{i\in R^{\prime\prime}}\varepsilon_{i}=\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{i\in R^{\prime\prime}}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime},

so that

\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\left(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{i\in R^{\prime\prime}}\left(\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{i}\right)=|R^{\prime\prime}|,

where the last equality uses (
9.VII
). From this equality, (
9.VI
), and (
9.VII
), we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{g}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}+1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}+1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+\sum_{i\in R^{\prime\prime}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}+1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\left(-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+|R^{\prime\prime}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\left(-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\left(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in R^{\prime}}\left(\left(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\right)-\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\geq 0.

This verifies (
9.V
), thereby completing the proof.
