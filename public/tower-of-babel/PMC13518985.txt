Degree-based Inverse Prodeg indices linking graph theory and cheminformatics with sharp results and QSPR modeling
Authors: Mohammed Alsharafi, Yusuf Zeren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDegree-based topological indices are widely used in mathematical chemistry because they provide simple numerical descriptions of molecular graphs and can support structure–property analysis. In this work, we introduce the Inverse Prodeg index IP(G) and its coindex as new degree-derived graph invariants. Unlike product-, sum-, and mixed-degree descriptors such as the Randić, sum-connectivity, harmonic, atom-bond connectivity, geometric-arithmetic, Sombor, Nirmala, inverse Nirmala, and misbalance prodeg indices, IP(G) reduces to the vertex-wise concave sum , whereas transfers the same inverse square-root degree weighting to nonedges using the original graph degrees. We establish their main mathematical properties, including bounds involving graph order, size, and degree extrema, equality cases, Nordhaus–Gaddum-type inequalities, exact expressions for standard graph families, and estimates under several graph operations. These results show that the proposed descriptors are analytically tractable and computationally efficient, with linear-time computability in the number of edges. To examine their chemical relevance, we evaluate a small Prodeg-based descriptor family on a dataset of 90 aromatic-carboxylate compounds using training and external test sets generated by the Kennard-Stone algorithm. The models indicate that these descriptors capture useful structure-property information, especially for size- and thermodynamics-related endpoints. Linear regression and partial least-squares regression achieved the strongest average external-test performance among the considered Prodeg-only models, with mean external-test across the nine core endpoints, while Ridge regression was close with mean external-test . Nonlinear methods did not improve the average prediction accuracy. Additional validation through bootstrap analysis, Y-randomization, residual diagnostics, and applicability-domain assessment supports a non-spurious but dataset-dependent predictive signal. Overall, the Inverse Prodeg index and its coindex provide mathematically well-founded and practically useful graph descriptors, although broader validation and combination with chemically richer descriptors are needed before claiming general predictive superiority.Keywords: Inverse Prodeg index, Coindex, Degree-based topological descriptors, Graph operations, QSPR, External validationSubject terms: Chemistry, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Degree-based topological indices are foundational in chemical graph theory and network science, serving as powerful quantitative descriptors for molecular structures and complex networks1–4. The classical Zagreb indices, introduced by Gutman and Trinajstić1 and further developed in subsequent work2, remain central to this field owing to their simplicity and strong structure-property performance. These and many subsequent variants have been employed extensively in the prediction of physicochemical properties (QSPR/QSAR)4,5, in the analysis of biological and social networks6, and in the design of technological systems. A recurring motivation for new indices is increased flexibility, interpretability, or sensitivity to salient structural features (e.g., branching or the prevalence of low-degree vertices). The general Zagreb frameworks provide one such direction, introducing tunable parameters to better capture fine structural nuances4,7.

Despite their success, many classical edge-weighted descriptors may respond similarly on graphs with distinct degree distributions, or they may overweight a small set of high-degree vertices when negative exponents are absent. In QSPR settings, such behavior can reduce interpretability when one seeks a descriptor that (i) preserves analytic tractability, (ii) remains sensitive to branching via the degree sequence, and (iii) yields clean extremal characterizations. These considerations motivate indices built from concave transforms of degrees, where majorization tools (e.g. Karamata-type arguments) become directly applicable.

Recent advances have focused on sharp inequalities, extremal characterizations, and Nordhaus–Gaddum-type relations linking a graph to its complement5,7,8. These results deepen the connection between a graph’s degree sequence and global topology, with implications for both theory and applications. In particular, complement-based descriptors and coindices have received sustained attention in both theoretical developments and nanostructure applications9–11.

Unlike edge-coupled weights that depend symmetrically on both endpoints (e.g., ABC, GA, Randić, N, , ), IP(G) ultimately aggregates a vertex-wise concave transform. Consequently, IP(G) is highly sensitive to degree-sequence majorization: by Karamata’s inequality, for fixed , the sum is maximized by more balanced degree distributions and minimized by more uneven ones. This concavity yields clean extremal theory via majorization and Cauchy–Schwarz arguments.

The novelty of the present descriptors (Table 1) is therefore not merely the introduction of another degree formula, but the combination of four features: (i) an inverse square-root edge contribution that collapses exactly to a vertex-wise concave degree sum, (ii) direct sensitivity to degree-sequence balance and branching, (iii) a complementary nonedge version that keeps the degree information of the original molecular graph, and (iv) closed-form extremal behavior under standard graph families and graph operations. These features distinguish the proposed descriptors from both product-degree and sum-degree families and also make their chemical interpretation transparent.

Graph operations-such as disjoint union, join, complement, Cartesian and lexicographic (composition) products, strong and tensor products, corona, rooted product, and hierarchical/generalized hierarchical products-are a standard device in chemical graph theory for building complex networks from simpler components and for transferring structural information between families of graphs. A substantial literature develops closed forms, recurrences, and bounds for classical indices under these operations. Foundational contributions by Khalifeh, Yousefi-Azari, and Ashrafi derived exact expressions for the hyper-Wiener index and for the first and second Zagreb indices across several operations24,25, while Ashrafi, Došlić, and Hamzeh introduced and analyzed Zagreb coindices in the same setting26. Multiplicative Zagreb indices under graph operations were investigated by Das and coauthors27. Azari and Iranmanesh studied distance-based descriptors-most notably the eccentric-distance sum (EDS)-for a variety of operations28, obtained Zagreb index formulas for rooted products29, established sharp lower bounds for the Narumi-Katayama index under operations30, and proved inequalities for the multiplicative sum Zagreb index31. Measures of irregularity and eccentric connectivity on composite graphs and operation-generated families have been analyzed in32–35, and higher-order Zagreb (co)indices, including the third and hyper-Zagreb coindices, have been derived for standard operations36. Product constructions tailored to hierarchical modeling are covered by the generalized hierarchical product37 and its Hosoya polynomial38. More recently, complement–based operations and coindex-type constructions have been explored for hyper-Zagreb and related descriptors10,39,40, while disjunction and symmetric difference operations have also been investigated from the viewpoint of topological indices and coindices41.

In line with this body of work, we analyze IP(G) under the join, Cartesian product, strong product, tensor product, disjunction, and symmetric difference, providing exact formulas in cases where they are available (e.g. tensor products) and sharp bounds in the remaining cases.

Quantitative structure-property relationship (QSPR) modelling that leverages graph-theoretic descriptors has become a staple for predicting physicochemical, pharmacological, and ADMET properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) of drug molecules. A substantial body of recent work has focused on degree-based and neighborhood degree-based indices to encode branching and local environments in molecular graphs. For antituberculosis agents, Adnan et al. demonstrated that degree-based indices yield strong predictive performance in QSPR analyses42. Balasubramaniyan and Chidambaram employed neighborhood degree-based descriptors for asthma drugs, highlighting the added value of local degree information43. Gnanaraj et al. extended these approaches to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), uncovering significant structure-property relationships44. For emergent indications, Kalaimathi and Balamurugan modeled physicochemical and ADMET parameters of candidate monkeypox therapeutics via molecular graph representations45, while Kansal et al. introduced temperature-based indices to model COVID-19 drugs, illustrating methodological adaptability to rapidly evolving therapeutic contexts46. Separately, Hayat et al. developed novel temperature-based indices to model properties of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and silicon carbide nanotubes47,48. Most recently, Manonmani and Selvarani combined multilinear regression with topological indices to analyze antiviral medications, improving the precision of property predictions49. Recent work has also tested structure sensitivity and chemical applicability of novel degree-based indices and applied degree-based regression models to anticancer or blood-cancer drug datasets67,68. Earlier, Syed et al. reported successful QSPR modelling for breast-cancer drugs using degree-centric descriptors50. In addition, degree-sensitive indices such as the hyper-Zagreb and Sombor families have been shown to correlate well with experimental properties in QSPR settings, including applications to trees, monocarboxylic acids, and related molecular classes51,52.

Recent studies further illustrate the current movement toward descriptor–QSPR integration: Arockiaraj et al. used integrated face-degree topological indices for QSPR modeling of aluminum and magnesium hydroxide networks53; Kara et al. combined Sombor topological indices with entropy measures for QSPR modeling of anticancer drugs54; Henry et al. integrated QSAR/QSPR, clustering, and docking for oxadiazole anticancer candidates55; and topological characterization has also been applied to kekulene tessellations and related nanostructures56.

Recent QSPR/QSAR studies increasingly combine molecular descriptors with nonlinear and interpretable machine-learning models, including XGBoost, random forests, support vector machines, and neural-network architectures. For example, recent work has used FNN, XGBoost, and RF models with SHAP interpretation for octanol–water partition prediction57, graph-theoretic descriptors with regression and feedforward ANN models for drug-property prediction58, and XGBoost/ensemble models with SHAP analysis for bioactivity or toxicity-related QSAR tasks59,60. These developments show that modern QSPR research is moving toward nonlinear prediction together with explainable feature-attribution tools. In the present study, the Prodeg descriptors are therefore evaluated mainly as compact, interpretable graph-theoretic variables; their integration with larger descriptor pools, XGBoost-type models, neural networks, and SHAP-based interpretation is a natural direction for future work.

From a chemical-informatics viewpoint, degree-based descriptors quantify local connectivity and branching in molecular graphs, where the vertex degree reflects the valency/neighbor environment of an atom. The proposed Inverse Prodeg (IP) index incorporates inverse square-root degree weights, thereby assigning relatively larger contributions to low-degree (terminal) atoms and less-substituted environments. Such structural features are closely tied to functional group exposure, molecular branching, and local heterogeneity, which are frequently implicated in variations of physicochemical and ADMET-related properties. This evidence base motivates our introduction of the Inverse Prodeg index as a simple, interpretable, and theoretically tractable degree-based descriptor; in the sequel we place it within the broader index landscape and quantify its predictive value.

By (1), IP(G) aggregates a concave transform of the degree sequence, which makes it particularly sensitive to branching (degree-sequence majorization) while remaining analytically tractable (via Karamata or Cauchy-Schwarz arguments). The coindex transfers the same local weighting to the complement, enabling Nordhaus-Gaddum-type analyses. Next, we develop sharp bounds, extremal results, and NG-type inequalities for IP and , derive closed forms for classical graph families, and present externally validated QSPR experiments on an aromatic-carboxylate compound dataset.

QSPR benchmarking and external validation via degree-based prodeg descriptors

Dataset and physicochemical endpoints

Compound set. The dataset comprises n=90 unique organic compounds represented by molecular name, InChI, InChIKey, molecular formula, and canonical SMILES. The series is dominated by substituted benzoic-acid and salicylic-acid (o-hydroxybenzoic acid) scaffolds, including two reference drug molecules (aspirin and salicylic acid) together with a broader panel of systematically modified analogs. Structural modifications include (i) O-acylation and esterification (e.g., acetoxy-, propionyloxy-, and related carbonate/ester motifs), (ii) alkoxy substitution (e.g., methoxy/ethoxy/propoxy), and (iii) extensive halogenation (chloro/bromo/fluoro and fluoroacyl/perfluoroalkyl motifs), as well as a small subset of nitrogen-containing derivatives (e.g., amide/nitro/cyano substituted aromatics). Across the dataset, the heavy-atom count ranges from 10 to 31 (median 17), with 64 distinct molecular formulas, providing moderate but meaningful diversity within a coherent aromatic-carboxylate family.

This compound set was selected because aromatic carboxylates provide a chemically interpretable and structurally coherent series with systematic substitution patterns, publicly traceable Cheméo records, and sufficient variation in size, branching, halogenation, and functional-group substitution to test whether degree-derived descriptors track physicochemical trends. The restricted scope also reduces scaffold heterogeneity, making it easier to interpret the graph-theoretic signal.

Physicochemical properties and data source. Physicochemical property values were compiled from Cheméo (https://www.chemeo.com/) (units as reported). A total of 12 endpoints were collected: McVol (McGowan’s characteristic volume, mL/mol), (critical volume, m/kmol), (standard Gibbs free energy of formation, kJ/mol), (standard gas-phase enthalpy of formation, kJ/mol), (standard enthalpy of fusion, kJ/mol), (standard enthalpy of vaporization, kJ/mol), (Log water solubility, mol/L), (octanol/water partition coefficient), (critical pressure, kPa), (normal boiling point, K), (critical temperature, K), and (normal melting point, K). For transparency and reproducibility, the curated dataset used in modeling (including identifiers) is provided in the Supplementary Information (retrieved on 07 Jan 2026; record URLs/IDs and any selection rules are given in SI).

Predictors (Prodeg indices). The predictor space comprised four degree-based Prodeg indices, , computed from the hydrogen-suppressed molecular graph of each compound (definitions in Section Introduction). These indices were used as the sole descriptors to assess whether degree-derived topological information can explain and predict the selected physicochemical endpoints.

Chemically, the four Prodeg descriptors encode complementary aspects of the hydrogen-suppressed molecular graph. The descriptor IP gives comparatively larger relative contribution to terminal and low-degree atoms because the increment from degree one to degree two is larger under the concave square-root scale than under convex degree powers. The coindex measures how the same degree information is distributed across nonadjacent atom pairs, and therefore reflects global separation of low-degree environments within a molecule. In contrast, MPI is convex in the degree and emphasizes more substituted or branched environments. This complementarity explains why the descriptor set is useful for size-related and thermodynamic endpoints, while properties strongly influenced by three-dimensional packing, hydrogen bonding, or specific electronic effects require chemically richer descriptors.

Scope of the QSPR benchmark. The empirical study is intentionally restricted to a small, chemically coherent aromatic-carboxylate series and to four degree-based Prodeg descriptors. It is therefore a controlled test of whether the proposed descriptors contain useful signal, not a claim that they outperform all established descriptor families. Comparisons with broad cheminformatics descriptor sets (e.g., constitutional, electronic, 3D, and fingerprint descriptors) and with standard topological indices such as Zagreb and Randić descriptors are identified as necessary follow-up work.

Endpoints used for the core benchmark and validation split. Although 12 properties were compiled, three endpoints (, , and ) were excluded from the core benchmark because degree-descriptor-only models produced weak and/or unstable generalization. During revision, the tabulated values were rechecked against the curated Cheméo worksheet. The value 33.85 for aspirin belongs to the fusion enthalpy field , whereas the gas-phase formation enthalpy used in the core benchmark is kJ/mol for aspirin. Accordingly, the core benchmark focused on nine endpoints: McVol, , , , , , , , and .

To ensure rigorous external validation, the full dataset (n=90) was partitioned into a training set (n=72) and an independent test set (n=18) using the Kennard-Stone algorithm applied in standardized four-dimensional Prodeg space (80:20 split). The split was constructed using predictors only (no endpoint information); standardization here is used solely for distance computation in descriptor space. All subsequent preprocessing (including scaling), cross-validation, and hyperparameter tuning were performed exclusively on the training set. Models were then refit on the full training set and evaluated once on the fixed external test set. To enable consistent comparisons across endpoints and accommodate intrinsically single-output learners, we fit one independent model per endpoint (nine separate fits) under the same split.

Benchmarking and model selection

To establish rigorous baselines and test whether nonlinear learning provides added value beyond a linear Prodeg-to-property mapping, we benchmarked a suite of regression models for predicting physicochemical properties from four degree-based Prodeg descriptors (, IP, , MPI). The core endpoint set comprised nine properties: . External validation split and leakage control. The full dataset (n=90) was partitioned into a training set (n=72) and an independent test set (n=18) using the Kennard-Stone algorithm in the four-dimensional Prodeg descriptor space (80:20 split). Kennard-Stone selection used predictors only (no endpoint information). Standardization for distance computation was applied only in descriptor space. All subsequent preprocessing (including scaling), cross-validation, and hyperparameter tuning were performed exclusively within the training set. After training-only model selection, each final configuration was refit on the full training set and evaluated once on the fixed external test set. This workflow follows established QSAR/QSPR validation guidance and is designed to prevent test leakage.64–66

Candidate models and endpoint-wise fitting strategy. We benchmarked a naive mean predictor (DummyMean), linear/latent-linear baselines (OLS, PLS regression, ElasticNet, and Ridge_perTargetAlpha), and nonlinear learners spanning instance-based, kernel, tree-ensemble, boosted-tree, and neural-network families (Table 6). Because several algorithms are intrinsically single-output and because optimal regularization/complexity may differ across endpoints, we adopted a one-model-per-endpoint protocol: nine independent fits under the same fixed split, enabling fair property-wise comparisons.

To avoid overstating correlation-based evidence, we interpret model quality through a combined validation profile rather than through training correlations alone. This profile includes external-test , RMSE, and MAE; training-only cross-validation; bootstrap confidence intervals on the fixed test set; Y-randomization; residual diagnostics; and leverage-based applicability-domain analysis. Because degree-based graph descriptors often share size and branching information, individual standardized coefficients are interpreted as associative indicators rather than causal effects, and endpoint-level accuracy is emphasized over isolated descriptor-response correlations.

Benchmark outcome and model ranking. Across the nine-endpoint task, linear/latent-linear models provided the strongest overall generalization. OLS and PLS2 achieved the top mean external-test performance (Table 8), with Ridge/α close behind; see Fig. 1. In contrast, nonlinear learners did not improve average external predictivity. Overall, with only four degree-based Prodeg descriptors, the dominant structure-property signal for the selected endpoints is captured by a near-linear mapping; additional model flexibility does not translate into reproducible gains on the external test set. These results should be read as supportive evidence for descriptor relevance within this dataset, not as definitive validation of universal predictive superiority.

Uncertainty quantification via bootstrap confidence intervals. Because the external test set is limited (), we quantified uncertainty in external performance using a nonparametric bootstrap over the test compounds (B=2000 resamples with replacement), recomputing test metrics for each resample (model fixed; no refitting). Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for external-test , RMSE, and MAE are reported in Supplementary Table S3, defined in the Supplementary Information as “Bootstrap confidence intervals for external-test metrics under the fixed Kennard–Stone split”.

Y-randomization as a chance-correlation control. To rule out spurious correlations, we performed Y-randomization by permuting the training responses and rerunning the same training-only workflow under the identical fixed Kennard-Stone split (N=200 permutations; predictors unchanged). The null distribution of mean external-test (averaged across the nine endpoints) is centered near and below zero, and none of the randomized runs matched the observed performance for the true labels (Fig. 4). This supports that the reported predictivity is not attributable to chance alignment between descriptors and responses.

Conclusion

Mathematical contribution. We introduced the Inverse Prodeg index IP(G) and its coindex as degree-based invariants with applications in extremal graph theory and cheminformatics. We established sharp bounds in terms of order, size, and degree extrema, including equality characterizations for regular and extremal graph classes. We also obtained Nordhaus–Gaddum-type sum and product inequalities for IP(G) and , exact values for several classical families (including paths, cycles, stars, complete and multipartite graphs, wheels, ladders, hypercubes, and friendship graphs), and estimates under common graph operations such as Cartesian, strong, disjunction, symmetric difference, join, and tensor product. The corrected tree result shows that, among trees of fixed order, the star minimizes IP and the path maximizes it. The index is computable in linear time in the number of edges, which facilitates large-scale applications.

Empirical QSPR findings. To assess empirical utility in QSPR, we constructed a curated dataset of n=90 aromatic-carboxylate compounds and compiled physicochemical endpoints from Cheméo. Using four degree-based Prodeg descriptors computed from hydrogen-suppressed molecular graphs, we performed an external-validation benchmark under a fixed Kennard–Stone 80:20 split with training-only preprocessing, cross-validation, and hyperparameter tuning. The dataset audit corrected the endpoint label for the aspirin thermodynamic value; the relevant column is treated as standard enthalpy of fusion rather than gas-phase enthalpy of formation.

Across the nine-endpoint benchmark, linear/latent-linear mappings provided the most reproducible external predictivity among the candidate models: OLS and PLS achieved identical mean external-test performance (mean ), with Ridge regression close behind (mean ). Nonlinear learners did not improve average external-test performance in this restricted Prodeg-only setting. Endpoint-wise, predictability was strongest for size- and thermodynamics-related targets and weakest for , consistent with the sensitivity of melting behavior to effects not fully encoded by degree-only topology. Robustness checks, including bootstrap confidence intervals, Y-randomization, residual diagnostics, and applicability-domain analysis, support that the observed predictivity is not merely a chance correlation, but it remains most reliable within the descriptor-space domain covered by the training set.

Limitations and future work. The empirical results should be interpreted as supportive evidence that IP(G) and related Prodeg descriptors encode useful degree-based information, not as a universal superiority claim over established topological, constitutional, electronic, 3D, or fingerprint-based descriptors. The reported values are also conditional on the curated Cheméo records, the fixed Kennard–Stone split, and the restricted aromatic-carboxylate chemical domain. The dataset is small and chemically coherent, which improves interpretability but limits generalizability. Future research should benchmark IP(G) and against classical descriptors such as Zagreb, Randić, and Sombor families across larger and chemically diverse datasets, extend the analysis to additional acyclic/tree families and weighted or directed graphs, and integrate these descriptors with chemically enriched representations and interpretable machine-learning tools.

The IP(G) index therefore provides a mathematically tractable and computationally efficient descriptor for research at the interface of discrete mathematics, network science, and cheminformatics.
