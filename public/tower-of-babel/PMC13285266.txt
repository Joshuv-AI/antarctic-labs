Interpol review of forensic fire debris analysis and fire investigation, 2023-2025
Authors: Michelle Evans
Published: 2026

Introduction

This review covers research in the areas of fire debris analysis and fire investigations since the 20th International Forensic Science Managers Symposium in 2022. The literature includes forensic and fire-related articles, standards, and books published since 2022 to complete the previous review by this author in 2023 [1].

In the prior paper by this author, the introduction highlighted civil unrest in 2020 and the use of commonly available materials to start fires and destroy property. Even as technology advances, the primary method for causing destruction generally remains the same, likely because of the ease of obtaining supplies, like bottles, fabric, liquids, combustible materials, and ignition sources (e.g., lighters and matches). Methods for recovering and identifying ignitable liquids in devices and debris are also relatively unchanged over time; however, investigators are taking advantage of the Internet of Things and social media to determine the timing of events, the origin of the fire, and the perpetrators.

While analysis of ignitable liquids continues to follow the traditional path, new techniques are emerging and attempting to gain traction. Research into using artificial intelligence (AI), like machine learning, to classify ignitable liquids dominates laboratory publications, as authors try to eliminate bias through data modeling and statistics. The effect of fire on the human body and the identification of burned human remains has long been a subject of interest and is discussed in a variety of articles. Many of the fire investigation publications discuss bias, its impact and how to avoid it. Fire research highlighted materials and scenarios commonly encountered in fire investigations.

There are entire journals and conference proceedings focused on fire, including its impact on materials and safety, as well as those that discuss wildland fires, engineering, and fire science. Articles relevant to forensic fire investigations were selected for this paper to cover ignitable liquid analysis, other related forensic laboratory analyses, investigations, and fire research.

Fire debris analysis

Standards

The following standards related to fire debris analysis have been published by ASTM International:

ASTM E3197-23: Terminology relating to examination of fire debris [2].

ASTM E1386-23: Practice for separation of ignitable liquid residues from fire debris samples by solvent extraction [3].

ASTM E1388-24: Practice for static headspace sampling of vapors from fire debris samples [4].

ASTM E2997-25: Test method for analysis of biodiesel products by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. [5].

The International Association of Arson Investigators (IAAI) Fire Investigation Standards Committee (FISC) published an article summarizing the current ASTM standards affecting fire debris analysis [6]. All applicable standards from evidence collection to ignitable liquid extraction and data interpretation are discussed in brief.

General

Low et al. [7] provided a review of fire debris analysis from 2014 to 2021. The topics covered included current trends with canines, evidence collection, extraction, analysis, and data interpretation.

NicDaeid [8,9] wrote two chapters for the Encyclopedia of Forensic Sciences on the analysis of fire debris. Brief overviews of evidence collection, extraction, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis, and data interpretation are provided; however, all the figures in both chapters are from a previously published book not related to the encyclopedia.

Macturk et al. [10] presented a review of two-dimensional gas chromatography (GC x GC) in forensics, which included a discussion on research involving ignitable liquid analysis. In general, while numerous articles have been published on GC x GC for ignitable liquids, the technology still needs more development before being implemented for routine use in forensic laboratories.

Capistran and Sisco [11] developed a rapid (approximately 1 min) GC-MS method for screening fire debris samples for ignitable liquids. In a follow-up article, Capistran [12] discussed the use of solid phase microextraction (SPME) with rapid GC-MS. Major compounds in gasoline and diesel fuel were able to be identified, showing proof of concept that could be applied to field portable instruments.

Berry et al. [13,14] investigated the use of dynamic vapor microextraction (DVME) for ignitable liquid analysis. The research evaluated the influence of fire debris containers, instrument settings, and simulated fire debris on the effectiveness of DVME.

The above-referenced articles by Berry et al. were part of over-arching research conducted by Suiter and Jeerage [15]. Ultimately, the goal was to find an alternative to typical headspace concentration methods using DVME and evaluate its effectiveness for ignitable liquid extractions.

Gregg et al. [16] followed up previous work by Berry et al. [14] and reassessed the DVME factor effects for each chemical class. This research highlighted specific parameters that were critical for optimizing recovery of ignitable liquid vapors from simulated debris using DVME.

Valdes and Almirall [17] used capillary microextraction of volatiles (CMV) to sample the headspace of simulated and real fire debris samples for ignitable liquids. By combining CMV with a face-down heated can, the technique provided a portable, dynamic extraction that could be used at fire scenes to rapidly collect and analyze ignitable liquid vapors.

Perna et al. [18] compared GC-MS to direct analysis in real time mass spectrometry (DART-MS) for the analysis of weathered paint thinner. DART-MS was able to identify less volatile and polymeric compounds not seen in typical GC-MS ignitable liquid analysis, indicating that DART-MS could be a complementary technique to enhance current practices.

Perna et al. [19] detected gasoline and paint thinner on various substrates using SPME with DART-MS. The technique worked best for volatile components of the ignitable liquids, with higher extraction temperatures needed for the less volatile compounds.

The above-referenced articles by Perna et al. were part of over-arching research conducted by Zhang and Chong [20]. The primary goals of the project were to evaluate DART-MS for ignitable liquid analysis and to apply chemometrics to the data to identify ignitable liquids and residues.

Fernando and Bandara [21] examined the effect of the presence of automobile fluids on the identification of gasoline in fire debris samples. Various vehicle oils were mixed with gasoline and burned; however, gasoline could still be identified, as there were very few interfering compounds.

Tran et al. [22] analyzed activated charcoal strip samples preserved for 14 years from fire debris evidence extraction, as well as reference ignitable liquids preserved for 21 years on granular activated charcoal. No or minimal changes were observed in the preserved extracts, demonstrating the effectiveness of charcoal for sample preservation over extended periods of time.

Schwenk [23] discussed the ignitable liquid classification of small engine fuels and the ability of canines to detect their presence at fire scenes. Twenty-one different brands and types of fuels were purchased over a 6-year period to document formulation changes and explore the options for reporting the various classifications identified.

Hodálik et al. [24] weathered gasoline on burned and unburned carpet to observe changes at various time intervals. As expected, the most volatile compounds were the most affected, emphasizing the need for prompt sampling of debris to avoid deleterious loss.

Jarrett's thesis [25] examined six different types of tape as potential collection media for a simulated ignitable liquid on porous (concrete) and non-porous (tile) surfaces. While the tape did have interferences, collection efficiency was generally greater than 10%.

Kilic et al. [26] compared two different extinguishing methods, smothering and water suppression, on the recovery and identification of gasoline from common household fabric materials. SPME with GC-MS was able to detect gasoline on most items, regardless of extinguishment, for up to 60 days post burning.

Bascou [27] analyzed the effect water would have on the recovery of gasoline using SPME with DART-MS. The author concluded that water decreased the signal intensities produced by gasoline and that with more water present, the intensities were more greatly affected.

Avissar et al. [28] spiked samples of bitumen (asphalt) with gasoline and diesel fuel to determine effects on evaporation and data interpretation. The bitumen interacted differently with the two ignitable liquids, likely due in large part to the compositions of each and retained the ignitable liquids longer than expected.

Suvar et al. [29] evaluated portable GC-MS for the analysis of air samples taken from laboratory-simulated fires. Smoke and surrounding air from actively burning and extinguished material were collected, and volatile organic compounds, including those from an ignitable liquid, were able to be identified.

Paál et al. [30] reviewed the various analytical techniques used to identify wood, char, and ignitable liquids found in fire scenes. The authors noted that a comprehensive chemical examination of debris in the origin could help investigators more easily determine the cause of the fire.

Armstrong's [31] thesis research used polarized light microscopy (PLM) to examine the optical properties of unburned and burned common household materials that could be found in a fire. The intent of the study was to collect data to populate a reference database for public use to assist with identifying the starting fuel or fuels.

Yankova et al. [[32], [33], [34]] explored the use of high field nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR) to identify and distinguish different brands of gasoline. Through the position of proton resonances, NMR gave unique spectral patterns, allowing for individualization and source associations for the gasoline samples analyzed.

Chemometrics

Horrocks et al. [35] wrote a chapter in a book on chemometrics in forensic science that focused specifically on common methods used in fire debris analysis. The authors provided a review of the numerous publications covering the topic, as well as background information on the various models.

Calle et al. [36] applied machine learning techniques to headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) for the identification of ignitable liquids on burned substrates. Only one of the models used was able to correctly classify 100% of the ignitable liquids, although all models were able to detect ignitable liquids in complex matrices.

Miller et al. [37] created a GC-MS data set of ignitable liquid samples that could be publicly used for training and statistical modeling. Gasoline and distillates from the UK were analyzed neat and evaporated multiple times to populate the database with various file formats.

Huang et al. [38] explored the use of Raman and AI to identify gasoline. The authors determined the optimal Raman position for the liquids and were able to detect up to 70% weathered gasoline without needing an analyst to interpret the data.

In two publications and a thesis, Whitehead et al. [39,40] and Whitehead [41] discussed machine learning and potential workflows for identifying ignitable liquids in fire debris. By using linear sequential unmasking, along with machine learning and existing ignitable liquid databases, the authors assert that error and bias can be minimized.

Ghazi et al. [42,43] reviewed chemometric techniques that can be applied to GC-MS data to prepare it for analysis and interpretation. Various methods of preprocessing were discussed, including normalization, peak alignment, and background subtraction.

Ghazi et al. [44] compared the performance of two different algorithms for predicting the presence of gasoline based on GC-MS total ion chromatogram (TIC) data. The authors determined that the naïve Bayes algorithm was the better of the two, but further work is needed on a larger sample size.

Pasternak et al. [45] tested the viability of off-the-shelf chemometric software for the identification of ignitable liquids in 374 previously examined casework samples. The software agreed with the original expert opinions and did not produce any false positive or false negative results, proving that it may be useful in the laboratory after further testing with more complex ground truth samples.

Akmeemana et al. [46] used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to classify ground truth samples of ignitable liquids. When tested on 50,000 computer generated data sets, results indicated that the CNN was best suited for neat liquids and simple substrates rather than complex mixtures.

Denn's thesis [47] demonstrated that in the presence of heat, porous materials do not protect an ignitable liquid from evaporation but instead provide resistance to mass transfer, causing the liquid to retain much of its original appearance. To account for this mass transfer effect, the author created and verified a correction factor for a thermodynamic model used to predict ignitable liquid weathering.

The publication by Smith et al. [48] presented research efforts from a multi-year grant, which resulted in multiple publications previously discussed [1]. The overarching goal of the project was to refine kinetic and thermodynamic models to assist with predicting the level of gasoline evaporation in fire debris samples.

Park et al. [49] developed three machine learning classification models using 4000 samples from suspected arson cases. The CNN model proved to be the most accurate in classifying the data, because its algorithm was based on pattern recognition and not material-based learning.

Misolas et al. [50] determined optimum conditions for thermal desorption of ignitable liquids from activated charcoal strips and then analyzed samples using headspace-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-GC-MS). Once optimized, classification of ignitable liquids recovered from simulated fire debris samples was achieved with principal component analysis.

In two related publications, Boegelsack et al. [51,52] discussed the potential of cross-contamination of ignitable liquids on wildfire debris, evaluating the effects of packaging, storage, and substrate interferences with multidimensional GC (GC x GC) analysis. Chemometrics were used to help characterize cross-contamination in the presence of complex matrices and different packaging and storage conditions.

Huang and Chung [53] investigated AI classification systems for identifying gasoline to determine the strengths and weaknesses of the transfer learning networks. Using data collected from headspace-solid phase microextraction-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS), a pre-existing CNN model was retrained and showed promising results for all but spiked samples and those with low levels of gasoline present.

Purdum's thesis [54] focused on machine learning techniques to identify fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) in fire debris samples. The models were trained on data from 310 samples of various substrates and vegetable oils and proved to be able to classify correctly the fatty acids identified in the debris.

Liqui et al. [55] utilized Pearson product correlation coefficients to determine the extent of interference of polystyrene-butadiene (SBr) on gasoline identification. Due to the presence of alkylbenzenes in both the polymer and in gasoline, the relative abundances of the compounds of interest in gasoline changed significantly when exposed to burned SBr residues.

Fire-related forensic analyses

General

Ellingham et al. [56] edited a book that focused on the analysis of burned human remains. Chapters covered search and recovery efforts for scene investigators, traditional and novel approaches to examining, identifying, and analyzing burnt remains, and numerous relevant case studies.

Madea and Doberentz [57] and Bohnert [58] each wrote chapters for different publications specifically dealing with injuries resulting from heat exposure. The different classifications of burns and their effect on the body both externally and internally are discussed with a focus on fire-related deaths.

Tomsia et al. [59] discovered an alternative source for DNA in charred remains. Complete genetic profiles were obtained after isolating DNA from fibrous cartilage of two intervertebral discs, allowing for identification of the victims.

A chapter written by Gin [60] discussed the use of Rapid DNA analysis in mass fatality incidents, specifically those involving victims of significant fires. This new technology was used successfully to quickly identify victims of several notable fires, including the Camp Fire and Conception Dive Boat Fire in California. Rapid DNA is significantly faster than traditional laboratory analysis, although reference samples are required regardless of which method is used.

Sarancha et al. [61] studied isotope ratios of pig and human bones before and after burning using two different types of heating methods, one with a muffle furnace (human) and one with an open flame (pig). Due to variability seen in the oxygen isotope, the authors cautioned against using carbonate and phosphate to identify burned remains in casework.

Mensch and Falkenstein-Smith [62] used a camera flash to measure soot photoacoustic response, which was plotted with gravimetric measurements to determine soot deposition thickness on different substrates. This method provided a proof of concept that can be explored for further applications in fire research and in evaluating burn patterns at fire scenes.

Di et al. [63] used infrared photography and four different enhancing reagents to visualize bloodstains after exposure to a fire. The infrared photos provided better contrast than standard photography for thermally exposed bloodstains. When used in conjunction with an enhancing reagent, visualization improved; however, the conclusions limited the usefulness of the chemicals to very specific temperatures and timeframes, which is impractical to determine in a real fire scene.

Dongbai et al. [64] proposed using the oxidation characteristics of metal to determine if an “accelerant” was present in a fire scene. The authors chose copper and ethanol as common examples of a metal and ignitable liquid, respectively, and used a variety of instrumental techniques to examine the oxide layer formed on the metal in the presence of an ethanol-fueled fire.

Kozbor et al. [65] examined the effect of an ignitable liquid-fueled fire on the presumptive detection of blood. Common household flooring materials were burned with ignitable liquids and showed a decrease in blood detection ability; however, if soot was removed, detection improved.

Nuzzolese et al. [66] exposed human teeth to high temperatures to identify color changes that could be used to associate with maximum fire temperatures reached. Results were used to create a standardized colorimetric scale to allow for more objective color interpretations.

Grignani et al. [67] used blood samples from living individuals of a known age to develop a model that could predict the age of burned human remains. Through DNA methylation analysis, the authors were able to accurately determine the age of approximately 80% of the remains examined and concluded that the reliability of prediction was primarily affected by the integrity of the DNA collected.

Pathology

Rosa et al. [68] examined the use of X-ray fluorescence (XRF) to detect elements from sharp tools (e.g., knives and axes) that may remain in bones post-burning. Pig rib bones were cut with various tools and subjected to burning at four different temperatures. While the elements from the blades were detectable in the bone cuts pre-burning, not all were identifiable after heat exposure.

Barreiro et al. [69] utilized various forms of imaging and fluorescence to attempt to distinguish bones from burned debris. The authors tried to determine the bones’ natural spectral reflectance and luminescence properties; however, the results indicated that spectral reflectance was not the best method for differentiating bone from debris, but luminescence showed some promise to suggest further research in this area.

Rodrigues et al. [70] compared two established age-at-death estimation methods on their applicability to burned skeletons. The results showed that changes to bones due to heat can interfere with age determination. This study was limited to bones from individuals over 60; therefore, the effect of heat on the age estimation of younger skeletons has not been determined.

Wolska et al. [71] used three different methods to estimate height from burned remains. Of the three methods, the chemosteometric approach, which uses infrared information obtained on the bones, provided the best estimate for skeletal height.

Harding et al. [72] examined the effects of burning on 42 unembalmed human cadavers to dispel common misconceptions, such as tongue protrusion and skull fracturing. The authors determined that these phenomena do not indicate what has originally been proposed and are instead dependent on the fire scene and pre-existing trauma to the victim.

Harding et al. [73] completed another study that investigated how a body moves in real time fire scenes. After examining 39 compartment and vehicle fires, the authors concluded that the pugilistic pose was not the most common reaction of a body when exposed to heat. Both qualitative observation and quantitative data recording led to a better understanding of thermally induced body movements based on fire scenario and original body position.

Nishio et al. [74] analyzed postmortem blood samples by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to identify a cyanide (CN) metabolite, 2-aminothiazoline-4-carboxylic acid (ATCA). This metabolite showed increased concentrations in the blood of fire victims and was positively correlated to the findings of both carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) and CN, indicating a potential new marker to identify CN poisoning.

Sasao et al. [75] used GC-MS to analyze the heart blood of 121 victims of fire-related deaths to determine the relationships between concentration of COHb and volatile hydrocarbons and between the volatile hydrocarbons, benzene, toluene, xylene, styrene, and ethylbenzene. By quantifying the results, the concentrations of benzene, toluene, and xylene may be useful for cause of death determination in fires.

Simonit et al. [76] reviewed autopsies from fire-related deaths from a span of 28 years to determine what, if any, patterns were seen in the burn features. Of the 39 cases that had full autopsies, suicide victims showed greater fire damage than accidental deaths, which was attributed to both the victims’ intentions and more intense postmortem burning.

Galloway et al. [77] examined historical data on bone color as it relates to flame temperature and compared it to their own observations obtained from experimental work. The authors argue that relying solely on bone color to determine environmental temperatures ignores other important factors that need to be considered. Since much of the previous research has focused on non-fleshed bones, the results cannot be extrapolated to normal fire incidents involving fleshed bones, as the addition of flesh will affect bone color.

Macias et al. [78] developed a quantitative screening method on the color of burned bones to determine which bones would yield the best DNA results. The color scale showed that light and dark brown bones were the only types to produce samples that could be successfully genotyped. This tool provides laboratories with an efficient method for screening evidence so that the best samples can be selected for analysis.

Logan and McColl [79] analyzed burned porcine (pig) bones using XRF and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to evaluate changes in the mineral and crystalline structure. Data collected demonstrated that compositional changes were dependent on the heat source (muffle furnace vs. open flame) and other materials present during the burning process.

George et al. [80] discussed the benefits of a partnership between several agencies that resulted in multiple research projects and the development of a continuing education program on studying burned remains. By using donated bodies and program resources, research has been conducted on bullet detection, genetic material and sharp trauma survivability, and ignitable liquid effects on human tissue and bones.

Oliva et al. [81] used post-mortem computed tomography (PMCT) to differentiate between traumatic and heat injuries in charred bodies. Even though only a limited number of cases were studied, results indicated that PMCT was a valuable screening technique that would help a medical examiner determine the course of action when evaluating burned bodies for autopsies.

Sane et al. [82] performed a retrospective analysis on autopsies conducted between 2009 and 2016 in India to evaluate the effect of postmortem burns on the determination of cause and manner of death. While there were very few postmortem burn cases, those that were evaluated demonstrated that the burns did not obscure the cause of death.

De Coster et al. [83] provided a review on the use of strontium, oxygen, and carbon isotope analysis for the forensic examination of burnt human remains. The authors concluded that more research needs to be conducted to determine the effect of fire on isotope ratios within various human bones and body tissues.

Case studies

Oshima et al. [84] presented a case where a vehicle caught fire after a traffic accident and postmortem blood analysis of the victim showed an atypical correlation between COHb and benzene, a volatile compound in smoke. The high concentration of COHb and low concentration of benzene indicated that the individual perished as a result of carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning prior to the accidence, and not the subsequent vehicle fire.

Raniero et al. [85] discussed three cases where the original presumed cause of death of charred bodies was changed after thorough multi-discipline investigations. The authors stressed the importance of sharing knowledge and information, including crime scene and laboratory work, to accurately identify the cause and manner of death for burned remains.

Tarzia et al. [86], Callegari et al. [87], and d’Abate et al. [88] emphasized a multi-discipline approach when examining charred remains, since the effects of fire can often obscure cause and manner or death. Several cases of burned bodies were presented to show how the autopsy, crime scene investigation, and laboratory analysis were intertwined.

Gianfreda et al. [89] explored the identification of 11 severely burned victims from four different types of scenes. The authors recommended sampling from soft tissue, as well as more than one type of tissue, to increase the likelihood of obtaining a useable DNA profile.

Fire investigation

Standards

The following standards related to fire investigation have been published by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA):

NFPA 921-24: Guide for fire and explosion investigations [90].

NFPA 1321: Standard for fire investigation units [91].

General

In 2022, the International Association of Fire Chiefs, the IAAI, and the NFPA published the 6th edition of a textbook that covers the principles and practices of NFPA 921 and NFPA 1033 that fire investigators need to follow [92]. The topics covered were updated to reflect the current versions (at the time) for NFPA 921 and 1033 and can be used to meet requirements for specific fire investigation courses.

A textbook devoted to crime scene investigations includes a chapter on arson and explosives by Fenoff et al. [93]. This is written for a crime scene investigator (CSI); however, much of it applies directly to a fire investigator with topics on area of origin, burn patterns, fire suppression, and fire debris evidence collection and preservation.

Kaur et al. [94] wrote a chapter on fire investigation for a book concerning the management of crime scenes within forensics. The chapter highlights various aspects of the investigation from the scene to sample collection to ignitable liquid analysis; however, references are sparse and the list does not include NFPA 921.

Lentini and Daeid and Lentini [95,96] have two entries in the Encyclopedia of Forensic Sciences concerning fire scenes. One discussed interpretation of fire patterns to correctly identify a fire's origin, and the other provided an overview of proper fire scene examination and evidence collection and preservation.

Gorbett and Kozhumal [97] developed an AI model to evaluate lines of demarcation in a fire scene and compared it to standard image processing techniques used to identify edges in patterns. The AI model was shown to be more accurate in detecting edges in simple fire damaged walls; however, more research is needed on complex fire patterns.

Avato and Hottle [98] provided general tips to new fire investigators and potential mentors that should not only be applied during training but throughout their careers. Some of the advice listed includes thinking critically, being open to learning, and sharing the knowledge gained so that others can be well-informed.

Avato [99] discussed the impact of bias on a fire investigation and how investigators can be more aware of the influence of bias on their decisions. Acknowledging bias and understanding more about its origins will help fire investigators minimize its effect on investigations.

Harding et al. [100] explored how similar archaeological excavation techniques are to fire scene investigations. The authors provide a historical perspective on how these disciplines are related and how the archaeologists’ removal and documentation of each layer is also applied by fire investigators during scene processing.

Friedlander et al. [101] reviewed the intersection of fire science and forensic anthropology when investigating fatal fire scenes. The authors emphasized the importance of fire investigator and anthropologist partnerships and the role of each when remains are recovered in a fire scene.

Freitas and Rodrigues [102] presented a protocol for investigating building fires. The intent was to have a methodology that not only addressed the scene processing but also the investigation, to include examining trends that may exist. The 31-step process is discussed in detail along with a simplified flow chart for easy reference.

Colwell and Knox [103] described factors that can affect eyewitness observations in fire investigations. The authors noted that eyewitness statements are frequently inaccurate and as a baseline, should be considered incorrect until proven otherwise during the investigation.

Bradshaw's doctoral dissertation [104] focused on understanding certain behaviors that can influence fire investigators when conducting a scene investigation. The goal of the research was to identify behavioral interventions that can be implemented as a policy with the ultimate hope of eliminating wrongful prosecutions based on faulty investigations.

Schumacher et al. [105] discussed investigating food truck fires and explosions related to propane systems. The authors described the general design of food trucks and provided a detailed review of a case study involving a propane tank that exploded, causing the deaths of two individuals. Suggestions were offered on how to best process and document a scene like this.

Ellington [106] addressed the impact of fires on individuals and their potential ability to safely exit a building. The article described elements of fire that make it untenable and methods that can be used to assess the available safe egress time (ASET) and the required safe egress time (RSET) for building occupants.

Dodsworth [107] and Servida et al. [108] both examined using the internet of things (IoT) with building management systems, such as the fire alarm, environmental control, and burglar alarm systems, to better determine the area of origin. While Dodsworth based results off interviews with subject matter experts, Servida et al. tested two controlled burn scenarios. If used correctly, IoT data can reduce subjectivity in fire investigations and identify not only a more precise origin but also how the fire progressed.

Babrauskas [109] wrote a brief article detailing the history of fire investigation publications and current issues with those choosing not to follow NFPA 921. The author provided an example of a scene that was improperly investigated and hopes to raise awareness of the issue to prevent unethical behavior.

In a 2023 editorial, Babrauskas explained the difference between negative proof and positive proof as it relates to fire investigations [110]. He emphasized proper use, and not abuse, of the scientific method and basic scientific principles by fire investigators.

Morgan [111] studied the factors resulting in wrongful convictions covering numerous disciplines, including fire investigations, both scene and laboratory analysis. Errors ranged from incorrect determination of origin and cause to improper reliance on a canine to laboratory analysis issues. Of the 74 fire cases identified as having wrongful convictions, only six did not have a forensic error associated with them.

Ellington [112] discussed the value of a fire investigator's report and provided a summary of its format and content. The author emphasized the need to be clear, accurate, and thorough when writing the report so that it correctly portrays all aspects of the investigation.

Health and safety

In 2022, the IAAI published the 3rd edition of a best practices document on health and safety for the fire investigator [113]. The guide provides detailed recommendations for employers and fire investigators on scene considerations, personal protective equipment (PPE), and hazard awareness.

Galaj and Saleta [114] wrote a chapter on how ventilation conditions during a fire can affect the toxicity of the environment. Based on several experiments using different polymers, results confirmed that toxic gas concentrations were much higher in a sealed room than in a ventilated one.

Pauley [115] discussed potential health concerns associated with live burns used in training and demonstrations. The author points out the hazards that remain long after the fire is extinguished and reminds those conducting the burns that appropriate PPE is necessary to protect participants and attendees.

Krzemińska and Szewzyńska [116] examined polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) contamination in firefighter gear used during actual fire suppression efforts and effective cleaning mechanisms. The concentration of PAHs was highest in the outer and middle layers of the protective clothing and lowest on the inner thermal barrier, which indicates the clothing traps much of the carcinogens. Most, but not all, of this contamination could be removed using appropriate cleaning in a washing machine.

Horn et al. [117] reviewed options available in the fire service to reduce exposure to cancer-causing contaminants. Topics discussed included PPE worn during fire suppression; decontamination procedures used on clothing, individuals, and other fire service gear; design of fire stations; and training situations.

Goldfarb et al. [118] developed a job-exposure matrix (JEM) for the Fire Department of the City of New York (FDNY) to assist with determining the amount of smoke particles emitted during fires. The aim of the study was to create a tool to aid in evaluating firefighting exposures and health-related effects of the job.

Rinaldi et al. [119,120] wrote two related articles on the use of virtual reality (VR) in fire scene investigations. The authors discuss the potential value and implementation of VR in training scenarios and in initial briefings for indoor fire scenes.

Islam's thesis [121] aimed to identify fabric properties in firefighter clothing that can affect smoke particle deposition and transmission. Using simulated wood fires, results demonstrated that ventilation and air flow impacted the level of particle deposition on both the inner and outer fabric layers, suggesting that specific types of fabric would be better for PPE.

Bralewska [122] provided a review of 32 articles published between 1987 and 2023 on air pollution inside fire stations. The author summarized the findings in the studies, which included the locations of the air sampling, and the types and concentrations of pollutants most commonly identified.

Bakali et al. [123] used silicone wristbands to monitor fire investigator exposure to PAHs. Greater concentrations of PAHs were recovered at “hot” scenes than from “warm” or “cold” scenes, although concentrations were also affected by amount of time spent on scene and material involved in the fire.

Case studies

Plumer [124] presented a case study involving tealight candles covered with flowerpots that were used as an alternative heating method in a house. Since multiple points of origin were discovered, security camera footage inside the structure was valuable to eliminate an incendiary fire cause. Testing was performed to replicate the fire scenario and determine the cause, which was narrowed down to a potential pool fire ignition.

Simonit et al. [125] emphasized the value of an interdisciplinary team approach when processing complex scenes. The case study discussed concerned a charred body found inside a burning vehicle. With assistance from the fire investigator to determine origin and possible ignition scenarios, the medical examiner was able to narrow down a probable cause of death.

Canines

Schwenk et al. [126] conducted a study to determine if F-500 Encapsulator Agent could affect canine alerts and laboratory identification of ignitable liquids. Results showed that this suppression technique might interfere with both the canine detection at a scene and with the laboratory analysis.

Fire research

General

McKinnon and Bellamy [127] described the creation of a publicly available database that includes material properties and fire test results to be used in support of fire models. The authors detailed the experiments used to generate data for over 100 materials and discussed sources of uncertainty, as well as a case study to validate the use of the database.

NicDaeid [128], Grimwood [129], and Martin and Pepler [130] have chapters in the Encyclopedia of Forensic Sciences on fire chemistry, thermal degradation, and thermodynamics, respectively. Each touch on topics relevant to fire research and would be a resource for fire investigators.

Rantuch [131] wrote an extensive book on the ignition of polymers, which includes information on their composition, degradation mechanisms, and ignition parameters. While all polymers are briefly discussed, the focus of the book primarily concerns synthetic polymers.

Ramamurthi's [132] book on ignition sources had comprehensive information on types of initiation for fires, explosions, and detonations of solids, liquids, and gases. The author focused on the effect of confinement on ignition, covering thermal, chemical, and electrical sources.

Babrauskas [133] provided a review of the conditions needed for the electrical ignition of gases and solids. The author noted that while much research has been done, there remains little information on minimum requirements to create an arc, and more research should be conducted to establish values for voltage, current, and power where ignition cannot occur.

Babrauskas [134] described the history of the cone calorimeter, which was invented in 1982. The author detailed the research needs and development that led to its creation.

Abraham et al. [135] reviewed the scientific basis for identifying arc melting and provided a history of its inclusion in NFPA 921. The goal of the publication was to lend support to the current methodologies and emphasize the need for proper training in the technique.

Li et al. [136] investigated arc bead formation on residential electrical cables as a result of current overload. Arcing and subsequent beading under various overload conditions were captured using a high-speed camera and showed that arcing events occur repeatedly and randomly over short periods of time.

Babrauskas [137,138] wrote a two-part article concerning the existing research on the ability to ignite walls from small or medium fuel packages. Studies have shown that the likelihood of ignition will depend on the type of material, heat release rates, and proximity to the wall.

Gorbett et al. [139] tested thresholds of heat damage that would destroy visual indicators of enhanced soot deposits on smoke alarms. Deformation and discoloration sufficient to mask soot deposits occurred at temperatures above 3350C, depending on the type of detector and location of the deposits.

Casaroli et al. [140] explored surface coloring and scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) on AISI 316 stainless steel sheets used for flue pipes to determine changes seen when exposed to heat. Based on the results, temperatures of a fire could be determined by looking at the color, morphology, chemical composition, and microstructure of the steel.

Babrauskas [141] discussed a variety of factors concerning smoldering fires, including materials that can smolder and burn rates. The article is a brief overview of the author's latest book on the topic.

Korinek [142] examined overheating poor electrical connections (OPCs), also known as glowing or high-resistance connections in the fire investigation field. The author used a variety of techniques to study liquid oxide conductors and provided investigators with features that can be utilized in the field to determine if an OPC occurred.

Kropotova et al. [143] analyzed the gases emitted during combustion of different materials, such as wood, linoleum, and plastic, to determine if the gas concentration could be used to locate the source of the fire. The primary gases released and their concentrations relative to the burning materials were identified experimentally, and results showed that the gas composition in air will take on the profile of the source material.

A second paper by Kropotova et al. [144] followed up the previous publication by analyzing the growth and movement of pyrolysis products for wood materials. While this research focused primarily on the fire safety aspects of gas flow, the model the authors developed could be used to identify the type of material in the fire by examining the concentrations of pyrolysis and combustion products emitted.

Like the work by Kropotova et al. [144], Wichmann [145] used GC-MS to identify the pyrolysis and combustion products produced by various types of insulation materials, primarily those from renewable resources. Data showed that the compounds released from burning natural material were similar to those released by synthetic material (polystyrene) but less abundant and therefore considered to be less hazardous.

Suzanne et al. [146] examined the relationship between char depth of wood and heat exposure to determine if conclusions could be drawn regarding fire duration. The authors demonstrated that cumulative exposure could be estimated based on measuring the char layer thickness, which would be useful in identifying flame spread or duration.

Li et al. [147] and Mozaffari et al. [148] discussed the feasibility of using vision color (RGB) image data from smoke and flames to predict when flashover will occur. AI models were created and tested in full-size compartments using visual video images and showed promising results for flashover prediction.

Jones [149] presented a case study concerning the death of a child where the ignition point was difficult to determine due to the substrate involved. Tests were conducted on the specific type of carpet used to demonstrate the possibility that it could self-propagate the fire, causing the pattern of fire development witnessed in this investigation.

Preimesberger et al. [150] how the size and temperature on two types of wood commonly used in construction and furniture can affect auto-ignition characteristics. The authors heated beech and spruce in various sizes at multiple temperatures to collect auto-ignition data and compare the differences seen between the two wood species.

Tang et al. [151] studied the effects of aging on PVC cables and how that can affect fire risks. Numerous tests were conducted to collect data on parameters such as ignition time, heat release rate, and critical heat flux. Results indicated that thermally aged cables have a lower risk of ignition, heat release, and fire growth, but the cables will drip more readily, increasing the risk of igniting surrounding materials.

Michelin and Perkins [152] reported on the use of laser pointers as a new portable source of ignition. Various laser pointers were tested with readily available combustibles and showed that an output as low as 1644 mW can potentially ignite some common household items.

Zhang et al. [153] used flame characteristics to differentiate between ignitable liquids used to start fires. By utilizing video footage and calculating the probability density function of the flame apex angle, researchers were able to distinguish gasoline from diesel fuel, regardless of amount and substrate.

Gorbett et al. [154] examined the depth of drywall calcination when exposed to heat. Several controlled tests were conducted with the most relevant result being that a depth of 7 mm indicated wall exposure to heat greater than 30 kW/m2 for at least 10 min.

Silveira et al. [155,156] presented a two-part article on thermocouple insulation failure and the impact on temperature measurements in research involving fire-related deaths. The articles discussed the cause and effect of the deterioration and the associated measurement error.

Liu et al. [157] developed an automated image-based system to assist with interpreting fire patterns in indoor scenes. The goal was to provide an objective measure that would limit human bias; however, while the proof of concept was successful, further work must be completed on more scenes with an emphasis on the complexity of origin determinations.

Lithium-Ion batteries (LIBs)

Braxton et al. [158] created a translational forensic roadmap to be used to share research on LIB fires with the public and private fire community, including investigators, fire departments, insurance agencies, and battery manufacturers. Since the use of LIBs and fires related to them are becoming more common, the roadmap is intended to promote the connection of research, battery technology development, and regulatory review to allow everyone involved in LIBs to work together for investigations and safety considerations.

Liang and Zou [159] used inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to examine blood from individuals who perished in a fire as a result of a lithium-ion battery (LIB). Quantitative analysis of the blood was able to identify high levels of lithium in each victim, with the greatest concentration being in the individual located nearest the point of origin.

Zhang et al. [160] provided a comprehensive review of fire-extinguishing materials that can be used in LIB fires. The authors discussed the behavior of LIB fires and how, based on its properties, there is currently no singular best fire suppression strategy or fire extinguishing agent. Different methods were considered, with the recommendation that a fire extinguishing agent for LIBs needs to be developed that would be highly thermally conductive and electrically insulating, as well as inexpensive and non-toxic.
