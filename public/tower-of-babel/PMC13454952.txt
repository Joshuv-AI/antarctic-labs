Tumour‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles Containing dsRNA Induce Degradation of Ribosomal Protein mRNA in Platelets
Authors: Gaoge Sun, Yuhuan Tao, Zihan Liu, Kaixiang Zhang, Shuai Zuo, Shanwen Chen, Shufang Ning, Tianyi Zhang, Pengyuan Wang, Ying Zhang, Zhi John Lu, Hang Hubert Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracellular vesicles (EVs) mediate intercellular communication within the tumour microenvironment by carrying cargoes from paracrine parent cells. EVs have attracted great research interest for their ability to carry nucleic acids into recipient cells and modulate cellular functions. However, previous studies have largely focused on RNA sequence information rather than RNA structure features. Here, we observed that EVs derived from colorectal cancer cells are enriched with endogenous double‑stranded RNA (dsRNA), a danger‑associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that leads to the activation of dsRNA‑sensing pathways in recipient cells. Crucially, we investigated the specific crosstalk between tumour‐derived EVs and circulating platelets. As anucleate cells, platelets are uniquely suited models for isolating the effects of exogenous nucleic acids. Our analysis reveals that endogenous dsRNA from tumour EVs activates the platelet OAS–RNASEL innate immune ribonuclease cascade and the RNASEL/ABCE1/PELO axis, resulting in the decay of ribosomal protein mRNAs. This study, spanning from clinical observation to mechanistic validation, uncovers a novel pathway of tumour–platelet communication. We identify EV‐enriched endogenous dsRNA as a functional mediator that enables tumour cells to directly reprogram platelet transcriptomes, revealing a new dimension of tumour–immune modulation.Keywords: double‐stranded RNA, extracellular vesicles, platelets, ribosomal protein mrna

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanosized membrane‐bound structures that transport proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids between cells. The nucleic acid cargo of EVs is not a mere reflection of the parent cell's content but is selectively sorted during biogenesis, resulting in an enrichment of small RNAs and nucleic acid fragments (Das et al. 2019; Dellar et al. 2022). EVs were considered as cellular ‘garbage bins’ for a long time. Following the discovery by Lötvall et al. that EVs can transfer functional mRNAs and miRNAs to recipient cells and mediate intercellular communication, an expanding range of important EV functions was reported (Valadi et al. 2007). The membrane of EV protects RNA from extracellular RNase (Zhang et al. 2021) and mediates substance exchange and signalling transduction (Wang et al. 2020). The RNA cargoes of EVs can serve as mRNA supplements and mediate RNA interference by miRNA in recipient cells based on a sequence‐dependent mechanism. However, in physiological processes, one in every 10 EVs contains a mRNA on average, while each EV encapsulates one mRNA fragment (Wei et al. 2017). Studying these RNA fragments and downstream signalling controlling proliferation, apoptosis and stress responses is meaningful.

Beyond their sequence‐based roles, EV‑transported RNAs can also function as danger‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). In a sequence‑independent manner, these RNAs can activate innate immune pathways via pattern recognition receptors including Toll‐like receptors (TLRs), retinoic acid‐inducible gene I‐like receptors (RLRs), nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain‐like receptors (NLRs), and cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS) (D'Ambrosi et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2019; Vanaja et al. 2016). While EVs carry diverse RNA species, the presence and function of cellular double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) in EVs remain unexplored. A key question is whether tumour cells sort endogenous dsRNA into EVs that prevent intracellular danger signal accumulation and enable intercellular manipulation in microenvironments (Chen and Hur 2022).

To investigate the structure‑dependent functions of EV‑associated nucleic acids, we employed platelets as a model system. As anucleate cells, platelets possess a transcriptome that is subject to continuous decay and is susceptible to modulation by external stimuli in the absence of confounding transcriptional regulation (Best et al. 2015). This makes platelets suitable for studying extracellular RNA‑mediated signalling. Furthermore, the mechanisms by which the tumour microenvironment influences platelet biology are not fully understood (D'Ambrosi et al. 2021). Herein, we used dsRNA‐immunoprecipitation and sequencing (dsRIP‐seq) to find that enriched dsRNA in tumour EVs. Tumour EV dsRNA activated 2′–5′‐oligoadenylate synthetases (OAS)‐Ribonuclease L (RNASEL) pathway and ATP Binding Cassette Subfamily E member 1 (ABCE1)‐pelota (PELO) ribosome rescue pathway, resulting in decreased ribosomal protein (RP) mRNA expression in platelets. Moreover, we observed the decrease of RP mRNA levels in gastrointestinal cancer patient platelets and plasma cell‐free RNA (cfRNA) through the analysis of bulk RNA‐seq data, and validated using samples and cell lines of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. This research sheds light on a novel mechanism by which tumour EVs deliver dsRNA to platelets, activating the RNASEL‐ABCE1‐PELO axis, leading to selective degradation of RP mRNAs, a potential biomarker for cancer diagnostics. We first demonstrate that EVs carrying cellular dsRNA may act as the ‘garbage bin’ mechanism to avoid innate immune response accumulation in tumour cells.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Human embryonic kidney 293T/17 (HEK293T/17, GNHu44) cells were obtained from the Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Human colon carcinoma (HCT‐116) cells and human normal colon mucosal epithelial cell (NCM‐460) cells were kindly gifted by Dr Feng Qian, Tsinghua University. Cells were supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 10099), 100 U/mL penicillin and 0.1 µg/mL streptomycin (Transgen, FG101‐01) in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, 11965092). Cells were treated for 2–5 min with 0.25% pre‐warmed Trypsin‐EDTA (Gibco, 25200072) at 37°C until dissociation. The culture medium was added to stop digestion. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 800 rpm for 3 min for the following experiment or passaging. Human T cell line (Jurkat, TCHu96), human natural killer cell line (NK‐92, GNHu20), and human leukaemia monocytic cell line (THP‐1, TCHu57) cells were obtained from the Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Human megakaryoblastic leukaemia cell line (MEG‐01, CL0498) and Gastric Epithelial Cell Line‐1 (GES‐1, CL0563) cells were obtained from Procell, Wuhan. Blood cells were cultured with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI 1640, Gibco, 11875093). Cells were subcultured at a density of 3 × 105 cells per millilitre in medium for every 2–3 days. All cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified cell incubator with 5% CO2.

Platelet‐like particles (PLPs) secreted by MEG‐01 cells were isolated from the media (Takeuchi et al. 1991). The media were centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min, purified by a 5 µm cellulose acetate filter (SARTORIUS, S7594) and pelleted at 3500 × g for 30 min.

EV Isolation

EVs were isolated by differential ultracentrifugation from the cell media cultured for 2 days and characterized following MISEV2023 guidelines (Welsh et al. 2024). EV‐depleted FBS was centrifuged from 50 mL FBS at 130,000 × g for 18 h at 4°C (Type 45 Ti rotor, Beckman Coulter). The supernatant was carefully collected, avoiding the loose pellet, sterile‐filtered (0.22 µm), aliquoted, and stored at –80°C until use. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% EV‐depleted FBS. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 20 min in duplicate, 12,000 × g for 20 min in duplicate and purified by a 0.22 µm PVDF filter (Millipore, SLGVR33RB). EVs pellet was precipitated by 120,000 × g ultracentrifugation for 60 min at 4°C and refilled with pre‐cooled phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). EVs were resuspended in 100–200 µL PBS by ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 60 min at 4°C. Density gradient centrifugation (DGC) was used to further separate into EVs and NVEPs. EVs were added on top of a continuous sucrose gradient (10%–50% w/v) created from Biocomp GRADIENT MASTER 108 and centrifuged at 180,000 × g for 18 h at 4°C. The gradient products were collected in 12 fractions. Size exclusion chromatography was performed using Exosupur exosome purification kit (Echo Biotech) equilibrated with PBS. The concentrated EVs sample was loaded onto the column, and 0.5 mL fractions were sequentially collected. Fraction 7 from SEC, containing the peak EV elution as determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis and immunoblot, was collected and designated as ev‑SEC.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis

NanoSight LM10 system (Malvern Panalytical, UK) and NTA3.4 software were used to quantify the particle number and diameter of EVs. Vesicles were diluted 100 times in PBS for analysis and recorded for 60 s with three technique repeats. The results are presented as the average ± SD at a detection threshold of 10.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

The EV morphology was observed using a Hitachi H‐7650B transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan) at 80 kV, 150,000× magnification. Fresh EV samples were incubated with formvar/carbon film on copper grids (Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co. Ltd) for 5 min. After being washed with ultrapure water five times, the samples were negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate (UA) for 2 min.

Immunoblot Analysis

1 × 106 HCT‐116 cells and 3 × 1010 HCT‐116 EVs were treated with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.2% Triton X‐100, pH 7.5) at 4°C. Supernatants of lysate were mixed with 6× protein loading buffer (Transgen, DL101‐02) and solubilized at 95°C for 10 min. Proteins in different samples were separated on 10% SDS polyacrylamide gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010) (100 V, 300 mA, 60 min). 5% non‐fat dry milk diluted in TBST (0.1% Tween‐20 in Tris‐buffered saline) was used for blocking non‐specific binding sites. Subsequently, membranes were probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and washed with TBST for 5 min three times. Membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and washed with TBST for 5 min three times. The immunoblot was developed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific, 34578) and iBright CL1500 Imaging System‐Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc. (United States).

Native Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis Immunoblot Analysis

For detection of RNASEL dimerization, fresh cells were lysed by pre‐cooled lysis and supernatants of lysate were mixed with 5× Native Gel Sample Loading Buffer (Beyotime, P0016N). Proteins in different samples were separated on BeyoGel Plus Precast PAGE Gel (Beyotime, P0466S) in Native PAGE Electrophoresis Buffer with Tris‐Gly (Beyotime, P0014G) at 4°C and proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes at 4°C for 120 min (80 V, 250 mA). The following immunoblot protocols were the same as the protocols in 2.5.

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR Analysis

Total cellular RNA and EV RNA were isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The purified RNA concentration was measured by Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific, USA).

For relative mRNA expression analysis, 1 µg of total RNA for each sample was reverse transcribed with the NovoScript Plus All‐in‐one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Novoprotein, E047) and then quantified on a CFX96 Real‐Time System (Bio‐rad, USA), using NovoStart SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein, E096). The expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH mRNA expression levels.

Confocal Fluorescence Analysis

MEG‐01 cells were facilitated to adhere to the plate using 0.1 mg/mL Poly‐D‐lysine (Beyotime, ST508). In live cell imaging, the cell membrane of MEG‐01 was marked by CellMask Green Plasma Membrane Stains (Thermo Scientific, C37608). HCT‐116 EVs were labelled by PKH26 Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Mini Kit (Sigma–Aldrich, MINI26) or transfected with pCMV‐CD9‐mCherry. Fluorescence from cell and EV samples was observed on a Nikon AXR NSPARC system (Nikon, Japan).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

The PLPs surface marker analysis and EV endocytosis rate of MEG‐01 cells treated with PKH26 (Sigma–Aldrich, MINI26) or 5 µM DiO (3,3′‐dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate, Beyotime) dyed HCT‐116 EVs were analyzed by LSRFortessa SORP (BD Bioscience, USA). Cells were resuspended and filtered by Falcon 100 µm Cell Strainer (Corning, 352360) before flow cytometry analysis. PLPs were isolated and probed with fluorescent antibodies. CD41/CD61‐FITC (1:200, Biolegend, 362804), CD41a‐PE (1:200, Thermo Scientific, 12‐0419‐41), CD62p‐PerCP eFLuor 710 (1:200, Thermo Scientific, 46‐0626‐80), CD63‐APC (1:200, Thermo Scientific, 17‐0631‐82) and CD61‐APC eFluor780 (1:100, Thermo Scientific, 47‐0619‐41) were used for labelling PLPs. The PLPs were clustered by CD41 and CD62P through t‐distributed Stochastic Neighbour Embedding (tSNE) for dimensionality reduction in FlowJo software.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation

Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) was performed to detect the protein‐protein interaction. Cells were transfected with a particular protein‐expression plasmid tagged with Flag or Myc, including p‐CMV‐3×Flag‐PELO, pCMV‐Myc‐ABCE1, pCMV‐Myc‐HBS1L, pCMV‐Myc‐RNASEL. Cells were treated with lysis buffer, and supernatants of lysate were mixed with 5 µL Anti‐FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads (Sigma–Aldrich, M8823) or 20 µL Anti‐Myc Immunomagnetic Beads (Selleck, B26301). Tubes were kept flipping overnight at 4°C. After three times wash with pre‐cooled lysis buffer with 300 mM NaCl, samples were mixed with 5× protein loading buffer, followed by solubilization at 95°C for 10 min. For detecting interacting proteins, immunoblots were performed according to the protocols above.

MRNA Pull‐Down

Cells were treated with PLB lysis (1 mM DTT, 200 units/mL RNase OUT, 1% EDTA‐free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES‐NaOH pH 7, 0.5% NP‐40). Supernatants of lysate were mixed with NET‐2 buffer (20 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 1 mM DTT, 200 units/mL RNase OUT, 50 mM Tris‐HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5% NP‐40) at a 1:9 ratio. 20 µL BeyoMag Oligo (dT)25 Magnetic Beads (Beyotime, R0075) for each sample were added, and tubes were kept flipping overnight at 4°C. After three washes with pre‐cooled NT‐2 buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5% NP‐40). Proteins binding with mRNA were separated from magnetic beads with or without RNase A (Thermo Scientific, R1253) and RNase H (Beyotime, D7089) treatment. Samples were mixed with 5× protein loading buffer, followed by solubilization at 95°C for 10 min. For detecting mRNA‐binding proteins, immunoblots were performed according to the protocols above. For RNA transfection into PLPs, total RNA was complexed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 15 min to form lipid nanoparticles.

Dot Blot

For dsRNA dot blot analysis, 20 µL Cell lysate and EV samples were dotted on 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, HATF00010) and air‐dried. Membranes were blocked following the protocols for immunoblot and probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Appropriate secondary antibodies were then added, and blots were then developed as described above for immunoblotting.

High‐Resolution Mass Spectrum Detection of 2–5A

1010 EVs, 1.2 × 107 MEG‐01 cells, 1.2 × 107 MEG‐01 cells treated with 1:3000 or 1:10000 ratio of HCT‐116 EVs were collected for 2–5A detection. ROC Curve

Samples were obtained from GSE142987, GSE174302, GSE186607, including CRC (N = 90), ESCA (N = 23), LUAD (N = 34), STAD (N = 71), HCC (N = 57) and healthy controls (N = 98). For each cancer type, a separate binary classification task was constructed by comparing that cancer group with healthy controls. The feature matrix was restricted to ribosomal protein mRNAs. For each binary task, samples were randomly divided into a training set and a testing set at a ratio of 8:2 using a stratified sampling strategy to ensure that both classes were represented in each subset. Stratified data partitioning was performed using the createDataPartition function in the caret package (version 7.0‐1).

Random forest classifiers were then trained on the training set using the selected RP‐mRNA features. Random forest modelling was implemented with the randomForest package (version 4.7‐1.2), and class probabilities for the testing set were generated using the prediction function with probability output. Model performance was evaluated in the independent testing set by receiver operating characteristic analysis, and the area under the ROC curve was calculated. ROC curves and AUROC values were generated using the pROC package (version 1.19.0.1), which provides the roc function for ROC/AUC calculation and related visualization utilities.

DsRNA Pull‐Down and Analysis

The dsRNA pull‐down methods were adapted as follows (Gao et al. 2020). 1/10 of the cells (3 × 107) or EVs (1.5 × 1011) suspension was set as input RNA control in 1 mL of QIAzol. For the dsRNA pull down, EVs or cells were added with 600 µL of dsRIP lysis buffer (100 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris‐HCl pH 7.4, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% IGEPAL CA‐630, 100× Protease Inhibitor, 1.2 µL Recombinant RNase Inhibitor) and incubated on ice for 5 min for lysis, then centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred equally into two new tubes on ice, followed by the addition of 5 µL of SCICONS anti‐dsRNA antibody (J2) (1 µg/µL) to one tube and 5 µL of IgG (1 µg/µL) as a negative control at 4°C with rotation overnight. A total of 500 µL of dsRIP lysis buffer with RNA/protease inhibitors and 25 µL of Protein G Dynabeads were added and incubated for 1 h. The supernatant was discarded and washed on a magnetic rack four times. One milliltre of QIAzol was added to retrieve dsRNA from beads. The input RNA and dsRNA from beads were added with 200 µL of chloroform and 5 min incubation on ice, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The upper aqueous phase was collected and added with an equal volume of isopropanol. RNA pellet was centrifuged at 13,000 g for 30 min at 4°C and washed with 800 µL of 75% ethanol (RNase‐free) with centrifuge at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. RNA pellet was resuspended in 22 µL of RNase‐free water. RNA fragment size was checked on Agilent 4200 TapeStation. The purified dsRNA was further utilized for PLPs treatment and characterized by RNA sequencing as dsRIP‐seq. The number of HCT‐116 EVs for dsRIP was used at a 3000:1 ratio relative to PLPs. To distinguish dsRNA type from cells and EVs, scan for 50‐nt windows containing ≥3 editing sites where ≥1% of the reads at that site were edited. Overlapping editing‐enriched windows within 2.5 kb were combined. For dsRNA transfection into PLPs, dsRNA was complexed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 15 min to form lipid nanoparticles.

RNA‐Seq

A mixture of 22 µL total RNA, 20 µL nuclease‐free water, 5 µL 10× DNase I buffer, 2 µL DNase I (10 units) (Takara, 2270B), 1 µL Ribonuclease Inhibitor (Takara, 2313B) was incubated for 20 min at 37°C. RNA was then purified RNA by Zymo RNA clean & concentration (Zymo Research, R1016). For 6 µL purified RNA, 4 µL 5× first‐strand buffer was added at 95°C to fragmentize RNA. Input RNA was incubated for 2.5 min, while immunoprecipitation dsRNA was incubated within 10 s. RNA was end‐repaired and polyadenylated in a reaction mixture of 10 µL RNA fragments, 4.6 µL nuclease‐free water, 2 µL 10× T4 polynucleotide kinase buffer (without ATP), 2 µL ATP (10 mM) (NEB, P0756S), 0.5 µL T4 polynucleotide kinase (10 U/µL) (NEB, M0201S), 0.4 µL Poly(A) polymerase (5 U/µL) (NEB, M0276S), 0.5 µL RNase Inhibitor (40 U/µL). The mixture was incubated at 37°C in a preheated, hot‐lid thermal cycler for 30 min, then inactivated at 65°C for 20 min. Six microlitres RNA was purified by Zymo RNA clean & concentration, annealed with oligo(dT) with 1 µL oligo(dT) primer (Takara, 3806) at 72°C for 3 min, then on ice for 2 min, then reverse transcripted with 4 µL 5× First‐Strand Buffer, 2 µL dNTP Mix (10 mM each) (Takara, 4019), 2 µL DTT (20 mM), 2 µL PEG 8000 (50% w/v, RNase‐free) (Beyotime, R0056‐2 mL), 2 µL DMSO (Sigma–Aldrich, 276855), 0.5 µL template switch oligo (2 µM), 0.5 µL RNase inhibitor (40 U/µL), 2 µL SMARTScribe reverse transcriptase (Takara, 639538) at 42°C for 90 min, then heated to 70°C for 10 min. To remove redundant template switch primer. One microlitre UDG (5 U/µL) (NEB, M0280S) was added to the RT product, then incubated at 37°C for 60 min. The product was pre‐amplified by SeqAmp DNA Polymerase (Takara, 638509) for six cycles of PCR reaction and purified by 1.8× DNA selection beads (Yeasen, 12601ES). The rDNA was depleted by sgRNA‐guided Cas9 (NEB, M0386M). The final amplification was done by SeqAmp DNA Polymerase for 16 cycles of PCR reaction. The amplified library was purified twice with 1× DNA selection beads. The final library was sequenced on DNBSEQ‐T7 for 10 Gbase per library.

Results

Downregulation of RP mRNA in Cancerous Patients' Plasma cfRNA and Platelets

First, we identified a notable clinical observation from RNA‐seq analysis of patient plasma and platelet samples from public database and our previous sequencing data. RP mRNA is rich in total transcripts, which constitute approximately 5% of total transcriptome mRNA, despite representing only 0.4% of total human protein‐coding genes (Ban et al. 2014). The 80S ribosome in human cells is composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and 80 types of ribosomal proteins (Dermit et al. 2020). We first found that the ribosome pathway was downregulated in plasma cfRNA from gastrointestinal cancer by gene ontology analysis (Figure 1A) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) from our sequencing results (Figure 1B). RP mRNA expression was below the average of housekeeping genes in gastrointestinal cancer, including stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) and CRC, compared to the healthy donors (HD) (Figure 1C). The specificity of RP mRNA downregulation was not influenced by tumour stage, gender or age difference (Figure 1D‐F).

Through a comprehensive screening of 184 RNAs in the ribosomal pathway (Figure 1G), RPS8, RPL7A and RPL13 were found to exhibit downregulation of RP mRNA (Figure 1H). Further, testing the plasma cfRNA from patients with STAD, CRC and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) confirmed the decreased expression of RPS8, RPL7A and RPL13 (Figure 1I). We further used a RP‑mRNA‑based random forest model to evaluate the area under the ROC curve to from CRC (N = 90), ESCA (N = 23), LUAD (N = 34), STAD (N = 71), HCC (N = 57) and healthy controls (N = 98) (Figure 1J). The area under the ROC curve was 0.871 for CRC, 0.876 for STAD, 0.868 for ESCA, 0.868 for HCC and 0.921 for LUAD.

In addition to plasma cfRNA, we have also observed a decrease in RP mRNA in platelets or blood EVs of cancer patients. RP mRNA was downregulated in blood EVs from CRC, hepatocellular cancer (HCC) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), plasma cfRNA from oesophageal cancer (ESCA), LUAD, STAD and platelets from breast cancer (BRCA), CRC, glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) (Figure 2A, upper panel). However, it was upregulated in solid tumour tissues (Figure 2A, lower panel). RP mRNA expression represents the state of ribosome biogenesis. Ribosome pathway was the only one that overlapped in platelets, plasma cfRNA and plasma EVs across various tumour sequencing datasets by KEGG analysis (Figure 2B). Each of the RP mRNA genes was shown in different types of cancer in platelets, plasma EVs, plasma cfRNA and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) (Figure 2C).

Platelets are the key factor in explaining the decrease of RP mRNA in blood components. The origin of plasma EVs was mostly contributed by platelets (Puhm et al. 2021). Considering that the plasma cfRNA was also primarily derived from platelets (Figure 2D), the RNA variation in tumour patients' blood may result from platelets instead of solid tumours. Platelets are the dominant source of cfRNA (Figure 2D). As a result, the reduction in platelet RP mRNA was reflected by a corresponding decrease in RP mRNA detected in cfRNA. Notably, while RP mRNA levels in platelets remained stable in non‑cancer conditions, they were significantly reduced across multiple cancer types, which was analyzed from RNA‐seq data from different types of tumour‐educated platelets from Best et al. (Figure 2E) (Best et al. 2015).

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the decrease of RP mRNA, we analyzed the RNA integrity in platelet and plasma cfRNA. As platelets are anucleate and cannot transcribe new mRNA once released from megakaryocytes, RNA degradation becomes a major determinant of their transcriptome. Compared to the overall total mRNA integrity number (RIN), which showed no significant difference between cancerous plasma cfRNA and platelets versus the control group, the RP mRNA was incomplete in the cancer group (Figure S1A, left panel). However, the integrity of RP mRNA in the primary tumours was higher than in normal tissue (Figure S1A, right panel). The RP mRNA is more susceptible and incomplete than other mRNAs in plasma cfRNA and platelets. Consistent with this, Z‑score analysis revealed significantly lower RP mRNA expression in cancer‑derived plasma and platelets compared to controls (Figure S1B), the Y‐axis value is the probability per unit on base from X‐axis. The coverage of housekeeping gene descended linearly from 5′‐to‐3′ end, however, the coverage of RP mRNA across gene bodies was higher preference in 3′ end indicating a 5′‐to‐3′ degradation may occur in plasma (Figure S1C). Compared to the housekeeping gene, the RP mRNA stability showed lower coverage in plasma cfRNA, and plasma EVs than in tumour tissue (Figure S1D). These findings suggested that the expression or integrity of RP mRNA is decreased in platelets, which resulted in tumour plasma RP mRNA decay.

EVs in Tumour Microenvironment Caused RP mRNA Decrease in Platelets

To identify the factors inducing the reduction of RP mRNA in platelets, we collected supernatants from five different types of cancer cells. These supernatants containing EVs, metabolites and secreted proteins were centrifuged to remove dead cells and debris. Platelet‐like particles (PLPs) derived from MEG‐01 megakaryocytes were then treated with the supernatants for 24 h to model platelet responses in vitro (Takeuchi et al. 1991) (Figure S2A). Exposure to tumour supernatants—particularly from the human colorectal carcinoma cell line HCT‐116—resulted in decreased expression of RPS6, RPL7A and RPS8 mRNA in PLPs (Figure 3A).

Moreover, to find whether EVs could decrease the RP mRNA in PLPs, the HCT‐116 supernatant was further divided into extracellular particles (EPs) containing EVs and EV‐depleted supernatant by ultracentrifugation (UC) to treat PLPs. Both EPs and supernatant isolated from HCT‐116 cell culture were effective in reducing RPS8, RPL7A, RPL13 mRNA levels in PLPs (Figure 3B). According to the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles 2023 (MISEV2023) (Welsh et al. 2024), the EPs we collected could be further categorized by the vesicular or non‐vesicular feature. Subsequently, EPs were purified by density gradient centrifugation (DGC) into 12 different fractions based on their density difference (Figure S2B) and by size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Figure S2D) based on their size to exclude other contaminants. The Top 3 of the EV recovery rate after DGC were Fractions 8–10. By immunoblotting, non‐vesicular extracellular particles (NVEPs) were concentrated in Fractions 1–3 characterized by HSP70, while the EVs were shown in subsequent groups (Figure S2C). Fraction 8, which exhibited the highest EV recovery rate, effectively decreased RPL7A, RPS8 and RPL13 mRNA but not 18s rRNA expression in PLPs (Figure 3C). However, Fraction 3, with NVEP, did not induce RP mRNA downregulation. The membrane structure of HCT‐116 EVs was characterized by transmission electronic microscopy (TEM) (Figure 3E). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed the diameter of HCT‐116 EVs was around 135.9 ±5.2 nm (Figure 3F). These EVs were rich in protein markers such as CD9, CD63 and ALIX, but they lacked GM130, a Golgi apparatus marker (Figure 3G).

Tumour EV induction caused RP mRNA downregulation in PLPs, which was more significant than non‐tumour EVs. We found that tumour cell (HCT‐116) secreted more EVs than non‐tumour cells (NCM‐460, normal human colorectal epithelial cells) under the same thawing number and collection time, as determined by immunoblot and NTA characterization (Figure 3H,I). When treated with EVs secreted from the same number of parent cells (1 × 107) and the same culture time (48 h), RPS6, RPL7A, RPS8 and RPL13 mRNA showed a significant decrease in HCT‐116 EV‐treated PLPs, while remaining unchanged in NCM‐460 EV‐treated PLPs (Figure 3D). Immunoblot showed that the RPS3, RPS6 and RPS8 expression decreased in HCT‐116 EV‐treated or HCT‐116 supernatant‐treated PLPs (Figure S3A). Moreover, HGC‐27 gastric cancer cell EVs could decrease the RP mRNA levels of PLPs (Figure S3F), but GES‐1 gastric normal cell EVs could not (Figure S3G).

Furthermore, validation through qRT‐PCR and RNA‐seq confirmed that HCT‐116 EVs caused the reduction of RP mRNA in PLPs. Especially, mRNA levels of RPS6, RPL7A, RPS8, RPL13 and RPL23 were significantly downregulated in HCT‐116 EV‐treated PLPs (Figure 4A). Notably, the transcriptome is similar in EV‐induced PLPs (Figure 4B) and plasma cfRNA from gastrointestinal cancer patients (Figure 1C). The RP mRNA downregulation was accompanied by the elevation of mitochondrial RNA levels, suggesting that cytosolic RNA degradation may have occurred in EV‐treated PLPs. The total RP mRNA levels showed significant downregulation in EV‐treated PLPs compared to all transcripts or total mRNA (Figure 4C). Of the 77 RP mRNAs quantified by RNA‐seq, 27 of them showed significant reduction after HCT‐116 EV treatment (p < 0.05, fold change > 2) (Figure 4D). Gene enrichment analysis highlighted pathways such as nuclear‐transcribed mRNA catabolic process and nonsense‐mediated decay (Figure 4E), indicating that the RP mRNA downregulation may be driven by the mRNA catabolic enzymes. The ribonucleoside metabolic process was negatively regulated in PLPs. Each RP mRNA showed downregulation preference in HCT‐116 EV‐treated PLPs (Figure 4F).

Meanwhile, HCT‐116 EVs did not significantly affect RP mRNA levels such as RPS3, RPS6, RPL7A, RPS8 and RPL13 in other blood immune cells. We tested T cells (Jurkat), natural killer cells (NK92), monocytes or macrophages (THP‐1 without or with 24‐h PMA treatment) (Figure S3B‐E). The uptake of HCT‐116 EVs did not induce a decrease in RP‐mRNA in these immune cells, possibly due to the transcriptional replenishment of mRNA from their nucleus (Figure S4B).

DsRNA Enriched in EVs Stimulates Platelet Function and Transcriptome Alterations

EV induction also influenced PLP function. Flow cytometry results showed that the positive ratio of platelet glycoprotein CD41 and CD62p (P‐selectin) was upregulated in PLPs by platelet activator 10 µM ADP or 0.1 U/mL Thrombin induction but downregulated by HCT‐116 EV treatment (Figure 5A). Owing to the enlarged surface from the open canalicular system (OCS), the platelets are susceptible to the uptake EVs (Xu et al. 2018). By flow cytometry assays, MEG‐01 cells were evaluated for EV uptake by endocytosis of DiO‐stained (Figure 5B) or PKH‐26‐stained HCT‐116 EVs (Figure S4A). Treatment with 10 µM Dynasore, an endocytosis inhibitor, EV uptake ratio was significantly reduced. Immunofluorescence imaging showed that MEG‐01 cells internalized the fluorescently labelled HCT‐116 EVs, which were generated by endogenous transfection of HCT‐116 cells with a CD9‐mCherry plasmid (Figure S4C). After 2 h of incubation, 14.9% of MEG‐01 cell internalized dyed‐EVs, as detected by a fluorescent signal. Real‐time immunofluorescence observations found that the PKH‐26 labelled EV moved as a part of the MEG‐01 cell (Figure S4D). In 0.2 s interval, red fluorescent EV was internalized in the recipient cell, while the external EV quickly showed up at 0.8 s scene with Brownian motion. Total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF)—structured illumination microscopy (SIM) observation indicated that EVs were internalized in the cell or absorbed on the plasma membrane (Figure S4E).

The cargoes from EVs were responsible for RP mRNA reduction in platelets. We further investigate the content that affects RP mRNA degradation in PLPs. The total RNA collected from HCT‐116 EVs transfected into PLPs significantly induced RPS3, RPS6, RPL7A, RPS8 and RPL13 mRNA downregulation in PLPs (Figure 5C). Furthermore, we purified dsRNA from HCT‐116 EVs by immunoprecipitation with dsRNA antibody J2 from EVs. Interestingly, dsRNA from EVs transfected into PLPs could also trigger RP mRNA decay in PLPs (Figure 5D). Besides RPS6 (p = 0.0512), the mRNA levels of RPS3, RPL7A, RPS8 and RPL13 were significantly decreased in EV dsRNA‐treated PLPs.

The structure of EVs serves as a protective barrier, safeguarding the internal dsRNA from degradation by external RNases. Both HCT‐116 EVs and double‐stranded poly I:C led to the downregulation of RP mRNA in PLPs. The downregulation of RPS8, RPL7A and RPL13 mRNA in HCT‐116 EV‐treated PLPs was not affected by RNase III, a dsRNA‐specific enzyme. This suggested that the dsRNA was encapsulated within EVs rather than forming EV corona or attached to the EV membrane surface. In contrast, the treatment with synthesized dsRNA poly I:C and RNase III did not induce RP mRNA degradation in platelets. The membrane of EVs also facilitates the transfer of polar nucleic acids across the non‐polar cytoplasmic membrane into recipient cells. While normal cells typically do not contain excessive endogenous RNA, tumour cells may produce abnormal autologous dsRNA, particularly when targeted by immune cells (Chen and Hur 2022, Cottrell et al. 2024). We explored the EV‐mediated cellular communication process between tumours and platelets, especially focusing on the dsRNA carried by EVs.

The content of dsRNA was validated through dsRIP‐seq of both HCT‐116 cells and HCT‐116 EVs (Figure 5E). Two criteria were selected for further comparison. Criterion 1 showed that HCT‐116 EVs enriched 674 types of dsRNAs compared to HCT‐116 cells. Criterion 2 showed that MEG‐01 cells treated with HCT‐116 EVs enriched 33 types of dsRNA (Figure S5A), with five of them shown in both groups. The expression of β‐actin showed no significant change from input or dsRIP; however, DNAJA3, WWP2, RP11‐426L16.10 as protein‐coding RNA, RP11‐196G4.2 as lncRNA, and RP11‐887P2.3 as pseudogene showed significant increases after dsRNA antibody enrichment (Figure 5F). Thirteen types of mRNA, 8 types of lncRNA, 6 types of miRNA, 19 types of snoRNA, 39 types of snRNA, 3 types of pseudogene, 1 ribozyme, 1 scaRNA, 1 sRNA and 1 miscRNA were concentrated in HCT‐116 cells than MEG‐01 cells (Figure S5B).

Based on the dsRIP‐seq results, we analyzed the Minimum Free Energy (MFE) of dsRNA sequences in cells or EVs. MFE ranking identifies the most thermodynamically favoured duplex, giving RNA‐folding and dsRNA structure prediction (Sasaki et al. 2025). The analysis of randomly selected one‐thousandth dsRNA fragments revealed that RNA in EVs with lower predicted MFE was marked (Figure 5G). Although EVs have a smaller diameter, making it more difficult to accommodate long‐chain RNA, the lower MFE also suggests a preference for dsRNA enrichment in tumour EVs compared to parent cells. The MFE of the five enriched dsRNAs in both EVs and EV‐treated cells were also calculated (Figure S6A). Furthermore, we analyzed the 5467 dsRNA regions from cells and EVs based on RNA editing sites. Most of the dsRNA originated from introns (27.25%) or the 3'UTR region (22.46%) (Figure 5H), which were higher than the normal cellular RNA distribution ratio. The HCT‐116 EV with less encoding‐exon enriched more promoter and 3'UTR regions than the HCT‐116 cell (Figure 5I). After 10 µM exosome secretion inhibitor GW4869 was added to HCT‐116 cells, more dsRNA puncta were concentrated in the cells (Figure S4F).

DsRNA Activates RNASEL/ABCE1/PELO Axis Led to RP mRNA Degradation in Platelets

We found that dsRNA in EVs activated the OAS‐RNASEL dsRNA‐sensing pathway, which would lead to global RNA degradation. The dsRNA activated the OAS enzyme, which would produce dimerized or multimerized 2′–5′‐linked oligoadenylate (2–5A) molecules from adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (Chen and Hur 2022). 2–5A presence in MEG‐01 cells with HCT‐116 EVs treatment was detected by high‐resolution liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS). MEG‐01 cells significantly increased 2–5A content upon uptake of EVs secreted by HCT‐116 cells (Figure 6A). The main formation of oligomeric 2–5A existed as a dimer or trimer (Figure S6B). The absence of 2–5A in HCT‐116 EVs (HEV group) indicates that 2–5A production originates in PLPs.

2–5A stimulates RNASEL dimerization and activation, facilitating intracellular RNA degradation (Hornung et al. 2014). Native PAGE assays confirmed RNASEL dimerization in PLPs stimulated by HCT‐116 EVs (Figure 6B,C). Knockdown of RNASEL inhibitor ABCE1 increased both RNASEL dimer and monomer. HCT‐116 EV treatment increased the dimerized form of RNASEL in a time‐dependent way within 1 h. The knockout of RNASEL would lead to no significant change in RP mRNA expression after poly I:C induction (Figure 6D), suggesting that RNASEL plays an important role in RP mRNA degradation after platelet activation. The significant upregulation of 2–5A and the activation of RNASEL promote RP mRNA degradation in PLPs. The dsRNA sensors, such as RIG‐I, MDA5, TLR3, activated downstream transcription factors and the PKR activation‐mediated translational, but they do not directly regulate the transcriptome of platelets (Cottrell et al. 2024).

To construct the interaction between RNASEL and RP mRNA, we found the possible downstream factors. Mills et al. demonstrated that the overexpression of ribosome rescue protein Pelota (PELO) in MEG‐01 cells led to the degradation of accumulated mRNA on stalled ribosomes in platelets, culminating in RP mRNA degradation (Mills et al. 2017). Furthermore, the findings suggest that PELO may also contribute to the reduced production of platelets and plasma RP mRNA in cancer patients. Given that PELO is a typical ribosome rescuer and novel mediator in immune response (Wu et al. 2023), it is necessary to investigate the PELO‐related mechanism of RNA degradation in platelets. The knockdown of PELO mRNA by siRNA led to the elevation of RP mRNAs in PLPs (Figure S6C). The interaction between ABCE1 and HBS1L with PELO was reported in the no‐go decay and non‐stop decay RNA quality control pathway (Shao et al. 2016). Notably, ABCE1 regulates the ribosome rescue pathway, as well as being known as RLI (RNASEL inhibitor), making a bridge between the ribosome and the RNase degradation pathway (Ramnani et al. 2021). Also, RNASEL has been reported to interact with PELO, a notion supported by previous reports that link RNASEL and PELO in the innate immune response (Nogimori et al. 2020). Using AlphaFold‐Multimer (Evans et al. 2021), we predicted the potential interactions between PELO and candidate domains. Domains with prediction scores (average predicted aligned error) below 25 were considered to enable affinity for interactions. Our prediction suggested an interaction between PELO and RNASEL (Figure 6E), which was confirmed by co‐immunoprecipitation (co‐IP) experiments. These experiments constructed an interaction network between PELO, HBS1L, ABCE1 and RNASEL (Figure 6F).

The relationship between PELO expression and RP mRNA levels in CRC was investigated. A large‐scale sequencing study identified increased levels of PELO in both platelets and plasma cfRNA from tumour patients (Figure S6D). We examined the expression levels of RPS6 and PELO in plasma samples from CRC patients and healthy donors by immunoblot analysis. PELO was detected at high levels in tumour plasma, while RPS6 expression was found to be low (Figure S6E). Plasma samples from 10 CRC patients and 10 healthy individuals showed a significant increase in PELO levels in the CRC patient group (Figure S6F,G), while the RPS6 expression in cancerous plasma was downregulated. The relative mRNA content of RPS6, RPS8 and RPL7A in plasma cfRNA was decreased (Figure S6H).

PELO was found to interact with HBS1L, ABCE1 and RNASEL. Both PELO and ABCE1 were found to bind the poly (A)tail of mRNA by co‐IP assay with poly‐dT magnetic beads (Figure 6G), while the binding was reversed by RNase A/H treatment to exclude non‐specific interaction. In conclusion, the interaction between PELO and RNASEL was related to mRNA binding and degradation, and ABCE1 was the mediator that linked the existing pathways from inhibition of RNASEL to stalled mRNA with PELO/HBS1L in platelets by EV stimulation (Figure 6H). The process illustrates how EV stimulated the OAS‐RNASEL pathway, leading to intracellular RNA degradation and a decrease in RP mRNA levels within platelets. Our findings indicated that EVs were enriched with tumour cell‐derived dsRNAs, which activated the OAS‐RNASEL pathway and led to RP mRNA downregulation in platelets.

Discussions

In this study, we identified the presence of cellular dsRNA enrichment in EVs, although the intracellular origin mechanisms of cellular dsRNA were unidentified. Various self‐derived dsRNAs were enriched in EVs with lower MFE than parent cells based on the unknown sorting mechanism or the specialty of EVs, which may be revealed by artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted RNA structure prediction, such as ARES or RhoFold+ (Zhang et al. 2023; Townshend et al. 2021) based on deep learning frameworks. The genetic information of nucleic acids depends on their integrity. Even a single‑base substitution, insertion, or deletion can significantly alter the final expression product or sequence recognition. The molecular mechanisms governing RNA sorting into EVs remain poorly understood (O'Brien et al. 2020). Due to their small size, EVs that predominantly carry short RNA fragments can still form complementary double‑stranded structures. Incomplete nucleic acids cannot convey the full genetic information to recipient cells, yet they can serve as signals to activate downstream RNA‐sensing pathways by their structure feature. Recent work showed that EVs package defective and aberrant noncoding RNAs (Williams et al. 2025). Blocking EV formation leads to accumulation of such RNAs and activation of antiviral defences, supporting a model in which one normal function of EVs is to remove RNAs that would otherwise trigger unwanted immune responses. Tumour cells may also accumulate cellular dsRNA (Sasaki et al. 2025). Tumour‐derived EVs export these danger signals to regulate platelets and other recipient cells. This may also operate in autoimmune or neurodegenerative diseases driven by cellular dsRNA, broadening the known roles of EVs and their dsRNA cargo in cell‐cell communication.

Unlike previously reported EV functions, tumour‐derived dsRNA activates the OAS‐RNASEL pathway in platelets, an RNA degradation mechanism distinct from canonical TLR3 or RIG‐I dsRNA sensing signalling that activates transcriptional regulation. ABCE1 no longer inhibited RNASEL, but together with PELO facilitated the cytosolic RNA decay by activated RNASEL. PELO and ABCE1 extract the mRNA from the ribosome and participate in the mRNA degradation process. The RNASEL/ABCE1/PELO axis provides a pathway that the tumour microenvironment regulates RP mRNA therefore affecting the function of platelets. This highlights a unique RNA‐sensing pathway in anucleate cells, which may have evolved to detect external RNA but is co‐opted by tumour EVs to manipulate platelet function.

The transcriptome of platelets reflects the impact of external stimuli on platelets without transcriptional regulation. Prior studies demonstrated that tumours and their secreted EVs are rich in RP mRNA (Wei et al. 2017). However, RP mRNA was degraded in platelets treated with EVs, proving that internal changes were greater than the influence of ingesting external EVs. Ribosome biogenesis is closely linked to cell proliferation, which causes increased expression of RP mRNA in tumours (Elhamamsy et al. 2022). However, the downregulation of RP mRNAs holds significant implications for the inhibition of ribosomal protein synthesis that matches the state of platelets. RP mRNA is abundant in cell content and sequencing (Pelletier et al. 2018), which means the detection of RP mRNA was less affected by technique bias, making this decrease more precise to be verified by sequencing. Therefore, the decrease in RP mRNA in the plasma and platelets of cancer patients is expected to become an unconventional biomarker. Notably, tumour EVs failed to induce RP mRNA decay in nucleated immune cells, likely due to active transcription replenishing mRNA pools.

In our research, we observed a decrease in RP mRNA levels in platelets and plasma. However, the reduction in RP mRNA has not been utilized to predict tumours. The predictive value of the degree of RP mRNA reduction or degradation for tumours requires accuracy, sensitivity and sufficient sample size for screening and early detection (Crosby et al. 2022). Additionally, after RP mRNA was degraded, its RNA integrity was lower. With the sequencing technology development in the future, the RP mRNA integrity may serve as a better predictor of tumours than the decreased expression fold change. The RNASEL pathway was activated in platelets and led to overall mRNA degradation, especially the downregulation of RP mRNA by RNA‐seq and qRT‐PCR results. When RNA degradation by activated RNASEL, the long‐term stable RP mRNA showed a decrease in proportion in platelets. We suggested that the RP mRNAs contain 5′ terminal oligopyrimidine motif (TOP) (Philippe et al. 2020) that is abundant, stable and sensitive to growth inhibition. However, this suggestion lacks experimental evidence. We only used HCT‐116 EVs from the colon cancer cell line in cellular mechanism verification and supplemented with HGC‐27 EVs from the gastric cancer cell line. EV‐induced RP mRNA decay in platelets is a chronic process, yet our experiments focused on short‐term (48 h) PLP analysis. This phenomenon needs to be verified in more types of cancers and long‐term experimental models, especially in organoid and animal experiments where EVs stimulate the degradation of RP mRNA in platelets. This study mainly used cell models and a limited set of clinical samples, with validation mainly performed in colorectal cancer cells. Therefore, the relatively small clinical sample size may not fully reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of tumours. Analysis of RNA‐seq databases revealed that RP mRNA is downregulated in platelets from multiple cancer types, suggesting that decreased platelet RP mRNA could serve as a potential tumour biomarker. We have built diagnostic models using RP mRNAs with AUC values ranging from 0.86 to 0.92 for five different tumour types. However, in clinical practice, RP mRNA detection would require higher sensitivity and specificity, and monitoring RP mRNA degradation might work better than simply measuring reduced levels. This model still needs better accuracy, sensitivity, and larger sample sizes before it can be used for cancer screening and early detection.

In summary, we found that tumour EVs carried dsRNA like the initially reported ‘garbage bin’ mechanism, which was internalized by platelets to trigger innate immune responses. Whether from viruses or abnormal tumour cells, dsRNA would be sensed and cleared by the innate immune systems by OAS‐RNASEL pathway. Beyond platelets, tumour EV dsRNA may also modulate other immune and stromal cells in the tumour microenvironment, affecting tumour angiogenesis, immune suppression and metastasis. Furthermore, we hope cellular dsRNA in EVs will be discovered in more biological processes, providing new insights and methods for synthetic biology and disease treatment. We suggested that in other nucleic acid‐related diseases, such as the accumulation of dsRNA in autoimmune diseases, EVs may also carry dsRNA and affect recipient cells. Tumour EV enriches endogenous dsRNA activated the OAS‐RNASEL dsRNA‐sensing pathway leading to the RP mRNA degradation in platelets. The sensing of dsRNA from EVs provides a new understanding of intercellular communication between tumours and platelets.
