Imaging-driven procedural strategies for coronary subclavian steal syndrome: Two cases of severe calcification and dual branch protection
Authors: Masayuki Endo, Shinsaku Yata, Shohei Takasugi, Shuichi Yamamoto, Misato Kishimoto, Kentaro Shinano, Shinya Fujii, Kensuke Matsumoto, Tomomi Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronary subclavian steal syndrome (CSSS) is an uncommon cause of myocardial ischemia in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting using an internal thoracic artery conduit. Endovascular treatment is the preferred treatment, although procedural strategies should be tailored to lesion characteristics and anatomical complexity. We report 2 cases of CSSS successfully treated with endovascular treatment. In the first case, a 75-year-old man had severe nodular calcified stenosis of the proximal left subclavian artery, requiring stepwise balloon dilatation followed by covered stent deployment. In the second case, an 83-year-old man had severe proximal left subclavian artery stenosis with a limited landing zone between the origins of the left vertebral artery and internal thoracic artery, accompanied by bilateral vertebral artery disease. Balloon protection of both branches was performed during stent deployment to preserve coronary and vertebrobasilar perfusion. These cases highlight the importance of individualized endovascular treatment strategies based on lesion morphology and branch vessel anatomy, including careful lesion preparation and branch protection in complex CSSS.Keywords: Coronary subclavian steal syndrome, Subclavian artery stenosis, Endovascular treatment

Introduction

Coronary subclavian steal syndrome (CSSS) is an uncommon but clinically important cause of myocardial ischemia in patients who have undergone coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) using an internal thoracic artery (ITA) conduit. CSSS is defined as myocardial ischemia resulting from retrograde blood flow from the coronary circulation through the ITA graft toward the subclavian artery (SA) due to a hemodynamically significant proximal SA stenosis [1]. Although the reported incidence of CSSS ranges from approximately 0.2%-6.8% in patients undergoing CABG with an ITA graft, the condition is likely underdiagnosed because of its heterogeneous and often nonspecific clinical presentation [2]. SA stenosis itself is relatively common, with a reported prevalence of approximately 11%-18% among patients with concomitant peripheral and coronary artery disease, including those evaluated for CABG [3].

Endovascular treatment (EVT) has emerged as the first-line therapy for CSSS due to its minimally invasive nature and favorable clinical outcomes [4]. Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty with stent implantation is widely regarded as an effective and safe approach for restoring antegrade flow in the ITA graft and relieving myocardial ischemia. However, EVT for CSSS can be technically challenging and often requires individualized procedural planning, particularly in cases with severe calcification or involvement of branch vessels such as the vertebral artery (VA) and ITA graft.

We present 2 cases of CSSS treated with EVT. The first case involved severe calcification of the left SA, requiring lesion preparation and covered stent implantation. In the second case, the origins of both the left VA and left ITA were located adjacent to the stenotic lesion, necessitating balloon protection of both branches during stent deployment to prevent plaque shift and preserve branch patency.

Case report

Case 1

A 75-year-old man with a history of CABG in 2005 was referred to the cardiology department because of progressively worsening exertional dyspnea since April 2025. His previous CABG consisted of a left ITA graft to the left anterior descending artery (LAD), a right ITA graft to segment No. 9, and a radial artery graft to the right coronary artery. He had also undergone transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve implantation in 2018. Physical examination revealed a systolic blood pressure difference of 25 mmHg between the upper extremities. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated patent coronary bypass grafts and severe calcified stenosis of the proximal left SA. The lesion was characterized by prominent nodular calcification, with the most heavily calcified segment located approximately 10 mm distal to the ostium of the left SA. Diagnostic angiography confirmed severe (approximately 90%) calcified stenosis of the proximal left SA with a pressure gradient of 40 mmHg across the lesion (Fig. 1). Echocardiography revealed diffuse left ventricular wall motion abnormalities with thinning of the ventricular wall, and the left ventricular ejection fraction was markedly reduced to 22%.

Preprocedural imaging of Case 1. (A) Computed tomography angiography demonstrates severe nodular calcified stenosis of the proximal left subclavian artery (SA) (arrow), with the most heavily calcified segment located approximately 10 mm distal to the ostium. (B) Diagnostic angiography confirms approximately 90% stenosis of the proximal left SA (arrow), corresponding to the heavily calcified lesion identified on CTA. The left internal thoracic artery (arrowhead) is patent.

EVT was performed via a left brachial artery approach with insertion of a 6-Fr sheath (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The lesion was crossed using a 4-Fr diagnostic catheter and a Vassallo NS1 guidewire (Cordis, Miami Lakes, FL). However, intravascular ultrasound (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) could not be advanced across the lesion. With additional backup support using a GoGo catheter (Medikit Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan), a 1.5 × 40 mm JADE balloon (OrbusNeich, Hong Kong, China) was successfully advanced but ruptured during dilation. Subsequently, a 3.0 × 40 mm JADE balloon (OrbusNeich, Hong Kong, China) was advanced and balloon angioplasty was performed. Further predilatation was performed using a 5.0 × 40 mm SHIDEN HP balloon (Kaneka Medix, Osaka, Japan). A 7.0 × 39 mm VBX covered stent (W.L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ) was subsequently deployed, followed by postdilatation with a 7.0 × 40 mm MUSTANG balloon (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA). Final angiography demonstrated successful revascularization with complete elimination of the pressure gradient across the lesion (Fig. 2). The patient’s symptoms improved significantly after the procedure.

Endovascular treatment of Case 2. (A and B) Because of the severely calcified and poorly compliant lesion, the stenotic segment of the left subclavian artery (SA) is gradually predilated using 3 × 40 mm and 5 × 40 mm balloons (arrows) before covered stent deployment. (C) Final angiography after covered stent implantation demonstrates satisfactory expansion of the left SA with complete elimination of the translesional pressure gradient (arrowhead).

Case 2

An 83-year-old man was referred to the cardiology department for evaluation of exertional chest discomfort. He had undergone CABG in 2013 with LIMA graft to the LAD. On physical examination, a marked systolic blood pressure difference of 60 mmHg was noted between the upper extremities. Myocardial perfusion scintigraphy using technetium-99m methoxyisobutylisonitrile demonstrated mild-to-moderate perfusion defects in the anterior wall, anteroseptal wall, and apex during stress, with incomplete redistribution at rest, suggesting a mixture of myocardial infarction and ischemia in the LAD territory. Echocardiography showed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 53%. Based on the suspected ischemia in the LAD territory, coronary angiography was planned. Diagnostic angiography revealed severe (approximately 99%) stenosis of the proximal left SA. The stenotic segment measured 26 mm in length and was situated in close proximity to the origins of both the left VA and left ITA. Specifically, the left VA originated 2 mm proximal to the proximal edge of the lesion, whereas the left ITA arose 6 mm distal to the distal edge of the lesion, leaving only a limited landing zone for angioplasty and stent deployment. Severe stenosis was also identified at the origin of the left VA, and additional stenosis was observed in the right VA (Fig. 3). After consultation with neurosurgeons and cardiologists, preservation of the left VA was deemed necessary. To prevent plaque shift and embolic complications during EVT, balloon protection of both the left VA and left ITA was planned.

Preprocedural imaging of Case 2. (A) Computed tomography angiography and (B) diagnostic angiography demonstrate severe (approximately 99%) stenosis of the proximal left subclavian artery (arrow). The stenotic segment measures 26 mm in length and is located immediately adjacent to the origins of both the left vertebral artery (VA; star) and the left internal thoracic artery (ITA; arrowhead). The left VA originate 2 mm proximal to the proximal edge of the lesion and exhibit severe ostial stenosis, whereas the left ITA arise 6 mm distal to the distal edge of the lesion, leaving only a limited landing zone for intervention. Additional stenosis is also present in the contralateral VA.

The procedure was performed using multiple vascular access routes. A 5-Fr Destination guiding sheath (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted via the right femoral artery, a 4-Fr sheath via the left femoral artery, and a 4-Fr sheath via the left brachial artery. From the right femoral approach, a 5-Fr catheter was advanced to selectively engage the LSA, and the lesion was crossed, establishing a pull-through configuration. From the left femoral approach, a 4-Fr diagnostic catheter was used to select the left SA, and an occlusion balloon catheter (LOGOS; PIOLAX Medical Devices, Inc., Kanagawa, Japan) positioned in the left VA. From the left brachial approach, a LOGOS balloon catheter was positioned in the left ITA. After intravascular ultrasound evaluation, 2 balloon-expandable stents (EXPRESS SD, 6.0 × 18 mm; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) were deployed in the left SA under balloon protection of the left VA and left ITA. Final angiography demonstrated good antegrade flow with preserved perfusion of the left VA and left ITA, and intravascular ultrasound confirmed adequate stent expansion (Fig. 4). The procedure was completed successfully without complications. The patient’s chest symptoms improved after the intervention.

Endovascular treatment of Case 2. (A) Two balloon-expandable stents are deployed across the left subclavian artery stenosis (SA) under simultaneous balloon protection of the left vertebral artery (VA) (arrow) and left internal thoracic artery (ITA) (arrowhead) to minimize the risks of plaque shift and branch compromise. (B) Final angiography demonstrates satisfactory expansion of the left SA with preserved patency of both the left VA and left ITA, maintaining vertebrobasilar and coronary perfusion.

Discussion

CSSS is a rare and often underrecognized cause of myocardial ischemia in patients with prior CABG using an ITA conduit. The diagnosis is frequently delayed because of nonspecific symptoms and the common assumption of progression of native coronary artery disease. However, careful physical examination can provide important diagnostic clues. In particular, a significant interarm systolic blood pressure difference (≥15-20 mmHg) is strongly suggestive of subclavian artery stenosis and should prompt further investigation [4]. Both of our cases demonstrated marked interarm blood pressure differences, highlighting the importance of routine bilateral blood pressure measurement in patients with suspected myocardial ischemia after CABG. Accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment planning of CSSS rely heavily on multimodality imaging. CTA provides detailed anatomical assessment of SA stenosis and its relationship to the ITA graft and enables evaluation of lesion characteristics, whereas magnetic resonance imaging and Doppler ultrasonography provide functional assessment of blood flow direction and cerebral circulation. Conventional angiography remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and enables simultaneous hemodynamic assessment. In particular, a significant pressure gradient across the lesion is an important diagnostic indicator of hemodynamically relevant stenosis and supports the indication for revascularization. In our cases, the combination of CTA, functional imaging, and invasive angiography allowed comprehensive evaluation and precise procedural planning.

EVT is currently considered the first-line therapy for CSSS because of its minimally invasive nature and favorable outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated high technical success rates exceeding 90% and significant clinical improvement following revascularization of the SA [5,6]. Compared with surgical revascularization, EVT is associated with lower peri-procedural morbidity and shorter recovery time. Reported complications are relatively infrequent but include distal embolization, stroke, vessel dissection, and restenosis [4,5]. These risks necessitate careful procedural planning, particularly in complex anatomical settings. Our cases illustrate the importance of tailored EVT strategies according to lesion characteristics. In the first case, severe calcification of the subclavian artery made device crossing and lesion expansion difficult, requiring stepwise balloon dilatation and adjunctive techniques for adequate lesion preparation prior to stent deployment. Preprocedural CTA demonstrated prominent nodular calcification, with the most severely calcified segment located approximately 10 mm distal to the ostium of the left subclavian artery. These imaging findings suggested markedly reduced vessel compliance and a potential risk of vessel injury during aggressive dilatation. This concern was further supported intraoperatively by rupture of the initial 1.5-mm balloon during predilatation, necessitating gradual lesion expansion with progressively larger balloons. Severe calcification is a well-recognized factor contributing to procedural difficulty and suboptimal stent expansion in peripheral interventions. In heavily calcified lesions, vessel rupture during aggressive balloon dilatation is a potential concern. Given the anticipated need for sufficient luminal expansion despite these hostile lesion characteristics, a covered stent was selected because it provides a protective barrier against vessel injury and may reduce the consequences of arterial rupture in heavily calcified lesions. Previous studies have suggested that covered stents may be advantageous in complex or heavily calcified lesions because they provide protection against vessel wall injury and reduce the risk of catastrophic complications associated with arterial rupture [7,8]. Therefore, careful lesion preparation and appropriate stent selection are essential to achieve optimal procedural outcomes in heavily calcified subclavian artery lesions. In addition to the degree of stenosis, detailed assessment of calcification morphology and distribution on preprocedural imaging may aid in selecting the optimal endovascular strategy for subclavian artery interventions.

In the second case, the anatomical relationship between the lesion and the adjacent branch vessels was the key determinant of the endovascular strategy. The 26-mm stenotic segment was located immediately between the origins of the left VA and left ITA, with only 2 mm and 6 mm separating the lesion from these branches, respectively. This limited landing zone increased the risk of plaque shift, branch compromise, and inaccurate stent positioning during intervention. Preservation of both branches was considered mandatory rather than optional. Occlusion of the left ITA could have resulted in recurrent myocardial ischemia because it served as the conduit for the left ITA–LAD bypass graft. In addition, severe stenosis was present at the left VA origin, and the contralateral VA was also diseased, making preservation of vertebrobasilar circulation essential. Therefore, simultaneous balloon protection of both the left VA and left ITA was performed during stent deployment. Protection of critical branch vessels, including the VA and ITA graft, is important during interventions involving the SA and complex supra-aortic anatomy to prevent embolic complications that may lead to serious neurological or cardiac events [9,10]. These findings highlight the importance of careful preprocedural assessment of branch vessel anatomy when planning EVT for complex SA lesions.
