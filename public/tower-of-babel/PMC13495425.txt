Genetic diversity and population structure of citrus rootstocks revealed by morphological traits and Start Codon Targeted (SCoT) polymorphism
Authors: Aadarsh Pandey, Akhilesh Kumar Srivastava, Subhash Chandra Singh, Chandra Mohan Singh, Vijay Sharma, Vishvajeet Singh, Ajay Kumar, Yamini Yadav, Dhirendra Rajpoot, Lalu Prasad Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe identification and utilization of genetically diverse citrus rootstocks are essential for sustainable citrus production. However, integrated studies combining phenotypic descriptors and functional molecular markers for comprehensive characterization of diverse citrus rootstocks remain largely unexplored under Indian agro-climatic conditions.ResultsIn the present study, seventeen commercially important, indigenous, and hybrid citrus rootstock genotypes were evaluated using 27 fruit and seed morphological traits and 19 Start Codon Targeted (SCoT) primers to assess phenotypic diversity, genetic relationships, and population structure. Considerable phenotypic variability was observed, with coefficients of variation exceeding 20% for 21 traits, while fruit weight (68.24%) and fruit shape (70.32%) exhibited the highest variability. Principal Component Analysis identified seven components with eigenvalues > 1, cumulatively explaining 86.9% of the total variation. Molecular analysis generated 63 bands, of which 83.41% were polymorphic, with an average polymorphism information content (PIC) of 0.324, demonstrating the effectiveness of SCoT markers in discriminating citrus rootstocks. Principal Coordinate Analysis and UPGMA clustering resolved the germplasm into four major groups largely corresponding to pedigree relationships. Population structure analysis identified two major genetic pools (K = 2), while Analysis of Molecular Variance revealed moderate but significant genetic differentiation among clusters (PhiST = 0.2159). Mantel analysis revealed a weak and non-significant association between morphological and molecular diversity (r = 0.071, P = 0.259), whereas Procrustes analysis indicated moderate congruence between the two datasets (r = 0.435, P = 0.086), suggesting that the two approaches capture complementary patterns of diversity.ConclusionsThe integrated use of morphological descriptors and SCoT markers provided a comprehensive assessment of genetic diversity and relationships among citrus rootstocks. The genetically distinct rootstocks identified in this study constitute valuable genetic resources for germplasm conservation and may serve as potential parental materials for future citrus rootstock breeding and improvement programmes under Indian conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09477-8.

Introduction

Citrus is a popular fruit crop in tropical and subtropical regions, making a significant contribution to global fruit production and nutritional security [1]. The success of citrus cultivation is heavily dependent on the availability of genetically diverse germplasm resources and appropriate rootstocks [2, 3]. Several citrus rootstocks are widely utilised worldwide because of their profound influence on tree vigour, productivity, fruit quality, and stress tolerance. Globally, commercial rootstocks such as Swingle citrumelo and trifoliate orange [Poncirus trifoliata (L.) Raf.] hybrids are extensively used owing to their disease resistance and suitability for high-density orchards; however, their performance is often constrained under saline, drought-prone, and alkaline soil conditions [4, 5]. In India, Rough lemon (Citrus jambhiri Lush.) and Rangpur lime (Citrus limonia Osbeck) remain among the most widely adopted rootstocks because of their excellent graft compatibility and vigorous seedling growth [6, 7]. Nevertheless, Rough lemon is highly susceptible to Phytophthora-induced diseases and may adversely affect fruit quality, whereas Rangpur lime may exhibit graft incompatibility and reduced performance under adverse environmental conditions [8]. These limitations underscore the need for continuous characterisation of citrus rootstock germplasm to identify genetically diverse and superior genotypes for future breeding and rootstock improvement programmes.

Selection of suitable citrus rootstocks depends on a combination of morphological, physiological, and agronomic attributes, including compatibility with scion cultivars, tolerance to abiotic and biotic stresses, tree vigour, and resistance to major diseases [9, 10]. Although stress tolerance and adaptability remain the primary objectives of citrus rootstock breeding [11, 12], the effective exploitation and conservation of rootstock genetic resources require comprehensive phenotypic characterisation [3]. In citrus, morphological descriptors, particularly fruit, seed, and floral traits, are widely employed for germplasm characterisation because they are relatively stable, easily measurable, and highly informative for discriminating closely related genotypes [3, 13, 14]. Fruit and seed characteristics also provide valuable insights into reproductive behaviour, propagation potential, and taxonomic relationships, thereby facilitating germplasm identification and supporting the efficient utilisation of rootstock diversity in breeding programmes [15, 16]. Furthermore, seed traits, germination behaviour, and seedling vigour have been recognised as important criteria for the early identification and selection of promising rootstocks, enabling efficient germplasm screening and reducing the duration of rootstock evaluation programmes [10]. Since rootstocks profoundly influence scion growth, yield, fruit quality, nutrient uptake, and stress responses, the characterisation of rootstock germplasm also provides a valuable phenotypic baseline for future investigations of rootstock-induced effects on scion performance and for the identification of superior rootstock–scion combinations for commercial cultivation [17].

Assessment of genetic diversity among citrus rootstocks is essential for the effective conservation, management, and utilisation of citrus genetic resources [18, 19]. Since morphological traits are often influenced by environmental conditions [20], molecular marker technologies have become indispensable tools for evaluating genetic variation and accurately discriminating closely related genotypes. A wide range of molecular marker systems, including ISSR, SSR, IRAP, and, more recently, SNP-based genomic approaches, have been extensively employed for assessing genetic diversity, cultivar identification, and germplasm characterisation in citrus [21–24]. Recent studies integrating morphological and molecular markers have successfully elucidated genetic relationships and diversity in citrus germplasm and rootstock populations [25, 26]. However, many of these marker systems require prior genomic information, involve relatively high costs, or are laborious to implement. In contrast, Start Codon Targeted (SCoT) markers are gene-targeted, highly reproducible, and cost-effective markers that amplify genomic regions flanking the ATG translation initiation codon without requiring prior sequence information [27]. Their ability to detect functional genetic variation has led to their widespread use in genetic diversity analysis, genetic relationship studies, and germplasm characterisation across several horticultural and perennial fruit crops [28–31]. Recent investigations have demonstrated the high polymorphism and discriminatory power of SCoT markers in citrus germplasm characterisation and breeding applications [32–34].

Despite these advances, most previous studies on citrus rootstocks have employed either morphological descriptors or molecular markers independently, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of rootstock diversity. Furthermore, integrated phenotypic and SCoT-based characterisation of diverse citrus rootstocks, encompassing both commercially important rootstocks and recently developed hybrid selections, remains largely unexplored under Indian agro-climatic conditions. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively integrated fruit and seed morphological descriptors with SCoT markers to characterise genetic diversity and relationships among Indian citrus rootstocks, underscoring the novelty and significance of the present investigation.

Accordingly, the present study evaluated diverse citrus rootstocks using fruit and seed morphological traits together with SCoT markers to: (i) assess the extent of phenotypic variability among citrus rootstocks, (ii) characterise their genetic diversity and genetic relationships using SCoT polymorphism, and (iii) identify genetically distinct and potentially valuable rootstock genotypes for future breeding and rootstock improvement programmes under Indian conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The present investigation was conducted on seventeen citrus rootstock genotypes comprising commercially important rootstocks, indigenous rootstocks, and advanced hybrid selections developed for citrus improvement programmes (Table S1). The experimental materials included Carrizo citrange, Sour orange, Cleopatra mandarin, Karna Khatta, Volkameriana, Rough lemon, Rangpur lime, Troyer citrange, Calamondin, Smooth Flat Seville (SFS), CRH-47, and six NRCC hybrid rootstocks (NRCC-1 to NRCC-6). Mature fruits were collected from healthy, true-to-type trees maintained in established citrus germplasm collections at ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI), New Delhi, ICAR-Central Citrus Research Institute (CCRI), Nagpur, and Agricultural Research Station (ARS), Sri Ganganagar, Rajasthan. The trees were approximately 8–15 years old, in the regular bearing stage, and maintained under recommended orchard management practices, including irrigation, fertilisation, pruning, and plant protection measures, at their respective locations. Detailed information regarding the parentage, botanical identity, and source of each rootstock genotype is provided in Table S1.

Morphological characterization

The phenotypic diversity of seventeen citrus rootstock genotypes was evaluated using twenty-seven fruit and seed morphological descriptors comprising thirteen quantitative and fourteen qualitative traits (Table 1). Characterisation was performed in accordance with the descriptor guidelines proposed by the International Union for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants (UPOV) and Bioversity International (formerly IPGRI) [35–37].

Since the experimental materials were maintained as germplasm accessions, each genotype was represented by a single healthy and true-to-type tree. For morphological characterisation, thirty mature fruits at the commercial ripening stage were randomly harvested from different canopy orientations (north, south, east, and west) of each tree to minimise positional bias and adequately capture within-tree phenotypic variation. All observations were recorded on healthy and normally developed fruits during the 2023-24 fruiting season, and the mean values obtained from the sampled fruits were used for subsequent analyses.

Quantitative trait analysis

Thirteen quantitative traits (Variables 1–13) were recorded following the descriptor guidelines (Table 1). Fruit length, fruit width, fruit axis diameter, rind thickness, seed length, and seed width were measured using a digital vernier calliper (Mitutoyo, Japan) with an accuracy of ± 0.01 mm. Fruit and seed weights were determined using a high-precision electronic balance. Fruit volume was estimated by the water displacement method, whereas specific gravity was calculated as the ratio of fruit weight to fruit volume. Numerical counts were recorded for the number of segments per fruit, the number of seeds per segment, and the number of seeds per fruit.

Qualitative trait analysis

Fourteen qualitative traits (Variables 14–27), including fruit shape, fruit base, shape of fruit apex, rind colour, pulp colour, fruit surface texture, adherence of albedo to pulp, albedo colour, cross-sectional shape of the axis, fruit axis type, seed shape, seed surface, seed colour, and cotyledon colour, were recorded through visual assessment following standard citrus descriptor guidelines (Table 1). To facilitate multivariate analyses, qualitative traits were transformed into standardised numerical scores (1–9) according to the descriptor states proposed by UPOV and Bioversity International [36–38].

Molecular analysis

Genomic DNA extraction

Total genomic DNA was isolated from healthy, young leaf tissues of the seventeen citrus genotypes. DNA extraction followed the conventional cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol as described by [39], with specific optimisations to account for the high secondary metabolite content in Citrus species. A key modification included the incorporation of 3% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) into the extraction buffer to facilitate the removal of polyphenols. The integrity of the isolated DNA was assessed by electrophoresis on 1% (w/v) agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide, whereas DNA purity and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically. Samples exhibiting an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered suitable for downstream analyses. Subsequently, all DNA samples were diluted to a working concentration of 50 ng µL⁻¹ using sterile nuclease-free water.

SCoT marker analysis and PCR amplification

Genetic profiling was conducted using Start Codon Targeted (SCoT) markers, with primers synthesised by Eurofins Genomics (USA). PCR amplifications were performed in a 20 µl reaction volume containing 1X PCR buffer (1.5 mM MgCl2), 250 µM of each dNTP, 0.8 µM of SCoT primer, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase (Sigma Aldrich, India), and 50 ng of DNA template. The thermal cycling was carried out in a Biometra Professional Thermocycler (Germany) using a program consisting of 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 50 °C for 1 min, and a 2-minute extension at 72 °C. A final extension step was maintained at 72 °C for 5 min to guarantee the complete synthesis of all PCR amplicons [40].

The amplified products were resolved by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels prepared in 1× TBE buffer and stained with ethidium bromide. A 100 bp DNA ladder (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used as a molecular size standard for estimating fragment sizes. Gel images were captured using a gel documentation system (Syngene, UK). To ensure the reliability of marker profiles, PCR amplifications were independently repeated at least twice, and only clear, distinct, and reproducible bands consistently amplified across replicate reactions were considered for scoring. Faint, smeared, ambiguous, or non-reproducible bands were excluded from the analysis. A binary data matrix was generated by scoring the presence and absence of reproducible bands as “1” and “0”, respectively, and subsequently used for molecular diversity analyses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV%), were computed for all quantitative traits. The frequency and percentage distribution of qualitative traits were also determined. Pearson correlation analysis, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and hierarchical clustering were performed using R software version 4.4.0 [41] to evaluate relationships among traits and summarise phenotypic variability among the citrus rootstock genotypes. Since qualitative traits were scored according to standardised descriptor states proposed by UPOV and Bioversity International, the assigned numerical scores were treated as ordinal variables and subsequently used for multivariate analyses, as commonly employed in citrus germplasm characterisation studies. Hierarchical clustering was performed using standardised data to assess phenotypic relationships among the genotypes.

Marker efficiency was assessed using polymorphic information content (PIC), calculated as PIC = 2fi(1 - fi), alongside marker index (MI) and resolving power (Rp). Genetic diversity parameters, including Nei’s gene diversity (h), effective allele number (ne), and Shannon’s information index (I), were estimated using GenAlEx v.6.5 [42]. Phylogenetic relationships among the genotypes were inferred using the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) based on Jaccard’s similarity coefficients.

Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) were performed using GenAlEx version 6.5 [42]. The significance of molecular variance components and PhiPT values was assessed using 999 permutations at a significance level of *P* < 0.05. Population structure was inferred using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) implemented in Python version 3.10 [43]. GMM was employed as a model-based clustering approach that does not assume Hardy-Weinberg or linkage equilibrium, making it suitable for the limited number of citrus rootstock genotypes evaluated in the present study. Models with different numbers of clusters (K = 1–10) were evaluated, and the optimal number of clusters was determined using Silhouette and Elbow methods.

Results

Phenotypic diversity and descriptive statistics

The descriptive statistics for 27 morphological and pomological characters in the evaluated citrus rootstock genotypes revealed substantial phenotypic variability (Table 1). Quantitative fruit traits showed significant ranges, with fruit weight displaying the most significant variation, ranging from 9.95 to 469.46 g (mean = 159.02 g). Similarly, fruit volume varied widely from 10.84 to 480.83 cc. Dimensions such as fruit length and fruit width showed relatively consistent mean values (6.62 cm and 6.65 cm, respectively), though their ranges indicated the presence of both highly small-fruited and large-fruited genotypes within the germplasm.

To quantify the extent of phenotypic variation, the coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated for all traits. According to the criteria adopted in previous diversity studies, a CV value exceeding 20% was considered indicative of high phenotypic variability. In the present study, 21 out of the 27 evaluated traits exhibited CV values greater than 20%. Among the quantitative traits, fruit weight (CV = 68.24%) exhibited the highest coefficient of variation, followed by fruit volume (CV = 62.82%) and seed weight (CV = 34.83%). In contrast, specific gravity (CV = 7.25%) exhibited the lowest variability, suggesting greater uniformity among the evaluated genotypes for this trait.

Among seed-related traits, the number of seeds per fruit ranged from 7.43 to 38.13 (mean = 22.82) with a CV of 30.10%, whereas seed weight varied from 9.42 to 32.00 g and exhibited a CV of 34.83%, indicating considerable phenotypic variation for seed characteristics.

Among the qualitative traits (coded variables), fruit shape (CV = 70.32%), fruit surface texture (CV = 61.74%), and shape of fruit apex (CV = 56.01%) exhibited the highest levels of variation. Moderate variability was observed for rind colour, pulp colour, and adherence of albedo to pulp, whereas albedo colour displayed comparatively low variation (CV = 17.70%). Overall, the evaluated fruit and seed descriptors revealed substantial phenotypic diversity among the studied citrus rootstock genotypes.

Frequency distribution of qualitative fruit and seed traits

The distribution frequencies for 14 qualitative traits revealed a wide spectrum of morphological diversity among the studied citrus rootstocks (Table 2). The fruit shape exhibited significant variations, with the spheroid type being the most frequent (7 genotypes; 41.18%), followed by ellipsoid and obolid shapes. Regarding the fruit base, the “concave collared” type was predominant (6 genotypes; 35.29%), while the shape of the fruit apex was most commonly mammiform (6 genotypes; 35.29%) rather than depressed (5 genotypes; 29.41%). External and internal colouration also showed high variability. Rind colour was distributed across seven categories, with “dark yellow” and “light orange” being the most prevalent (5 genotypes each; 29.41%). For internal traits, the pulp colour was primarily yellow (6 genotypes; 35.29%) or light yellow (5 genotypes; 29.41%), while albedo colour showed less diversity, with a vast majority of genotypes (13; 76.47%) displaying a white albedo. Textural and structural traits of the fruit were equally diverse. Both rough and smooth fruit surface textures were equally represented (5 genotypes each; 29.41%). Notably, the adherence of albedo to pulp was recorded as “weak” in 11 genotypes (64.71%), which is a significant trait for fruit processing and handling. The internal structure of the fruit axis was predominantly hollow (9 genotypes; 52.94%), and the cross-section shape of the axis was mostly irregular (11 genotypes). Seed morphology, critical for rootstock identification, showed that semi-deltoid and ovoid shapes were the most common (6 genotypes each; 35.29%). The seed surface was nearly evenly split between wrinkled (9; 52.94%) and smooth (8; 47.06%) types. Furthermore, seed colour was dominated by cream tones (10 genotypes; 58.82%), while cotyledon colour, a key marker for embryonic traits in citrus, was more frequently light green (6; 35.29%) than cream (4; 23.53%).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to simplify the complexity of the morphological dataset and identify the traits contributing most significantly to the total observed variation. PCA revealed substantial phenotypic variability among the citrus rootstocks, with the first seven principal components (PCs) having eigenvalues > 1 and cumulatively explaining 86.9% of the total variance (Table 3). The first two components, PC1 (28.42%) and PC2 (18.88%), together accounted for 47.29% of the total variation and were therefore used for biplot interpretation. PC1 was predominantly associated with major fruit size and yield-related traits, showing strong positive loadings for fruit length (0.87), fruit width (0.88), fruit weight (0.81), fruit volume (0.83), fruit axis diameter (0.86), rind thickness (0.73), segments per fruit (0.68), seeds per fruit (0.59), fruit base (0.77), and seed surface (0.80). This component primarily represents fruit size and structural attributes, indicating that these traits collectively contribute to overall fruit development. PC2 was mainly influenced by seed and internal fruit characteristics, with high positive loadings for seed length (0.84), seed width (0.77), adherence of albedo to pulp (0.80), seeds per segment (0.54), and albedo colour (0.51), while pulp colour (-0.62) exhibited a strong negative association. Thus, PC2 reflects seed morphology and internal fruit quality traits.

Pearson correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships among quantitative traits of citrus rootstocks, and the results are presented in Fig. 2. Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong and significant positive associations among major fruit size traits. Fruit length showed a high positive correlation with fruit width (0.93**), fruit weight (0.83**), and fruit volume (0.86**), while fruit width was also strongly correlated with fruit weight (0.93**) and fruit volume (0.95**), indicating a close interdependence among size-related traits. Fruit weight and fruit volume exhibited a very strong positive correlation (r = 0.996), reflecting the close association between these two fruit size attributes. Moderate positive correlations were observed between fruit size traits and rind thickness, axis diameter, and seeds per fruit, suggesting coordinated fruit development. Seeds per fruit was strongly associated with seeds per segment (0.81**) and rind thickness (0.71**). In contrast, seed traits showed relatively weak or negative associations with fruit size traits, although seed length and seed width (0.75**) and seed length and seed weight (0.50*) were positively correlated. The results indicate that fruit size traits are tightly linked, while seed traits are relatively independent, which is important for simultaneous improvement of fruit quality and seed characteristics in breeding programs.

Discussion

Phenotypic diversity and descriptive statistics

Comprehensive morphological characterisation is essential for understanding the extent of phenotypic variation available within crop germplasm collections [43–46]. In citrus rootstocks, such characterisation facilitates germplasm identification, assessment of reproductive attributes, and the preliminary selection of genetically diverse materials for breeding and conservation programmes [3]. Morphology-driven selection therefore remains an important component of citrus improvement programmes.

The significant phenotypic variation identified among the morphological and pomological traits, particularly the wide ranges observed for fruit weight and volume, highlights the broad phenotypic diversity present within the evaluated citrus rootstocks. High coefficients of variation in quantitative fruit attributes provide useful phenotypic variation that can be exploited during rootstock germplasm characterisation and preliminary selection programmes [47–51]. The high variability observed for fruit shape and fruit weight is consistent with previous citrus studies, where fruit-related traits were identified as major contributors to phenotypic differentiation among citrus genotypes [52]. The predominance of fruit-related traits among highly variable descriptors may reflect differences in reproductive biology and selection history among citrus species and hybrids, thereby facilitating the discrimination of closely related rootstocks.

Traits with low variability, such as specific gravity and albedo colour, exhibited greater stability across genotypes. Similar findings have been reported in recent morphological characterisation studies, in which particular structural properties are preserved despite total diversity, implying greater genetic control and less environmental effect [53]. These consistent features are useful for identifying varieties and standardising descriptors. Recent assessments in citrus have shown significant differences in seed number and related traits across populations, which are critical for both propagation efficiency and fruit quality improvement [54]. The significant variation observed in seed-related traits further highlights the diversity in reproductive characteristics among the genotypes and may influence propagation efficiency and nursery performance.

The observed variation in fruit and seed traits is particularly relevant for citrus rootstock improvement because seed characteristics have been recognized as important criteria for assessing propagation potential, germination behaviour, and early seedling vigour in rootstock breeding programmes [10]. Moreover, comprehensive characterisation of rootstock germplasm provides a valuable baseline for future investigations on rootstock-induced effects on scion growth, yield, fruit quality, and nutrient uptake, thereby facilitating the identification of superior rootstock-scion combinations for commercial cultivation [17].

Furthermore, the marked variation observed for qualitative descriptors such as fruit shape, surface texture, and apex morphology further emphasizes the morphological heterogeneity of the evaluated germplasm [55]. These qualitative descriptors are valuable for citrus taxonomy, classification, and genetic resource conservation.The substantial variation observed among fruit and seed traits highlights the broad phenotypic diversity present within the evaluated citrus rootstocks. However, because morphological traits are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, phenotypic variation alone may not fully reflect underlying genetic relationships. Nevertheless, the identification of highly variable as well as stable descriptors provides valuable information for germplasm characterization, conservation, and the preliminary selection of promising rootstocks for future breeding programmes.

Frequency distribution of qualitative fruit and seed traits

The qualitative trait investigation revealed significant morphological variability among citrus rootstocks, indicating a complex genetic makeup and adaptive development. Fruit form, base, and apex features were highly variable, which is consistent with recent research [56] indicating that fruit morphology, rind texture, and seed properties are reliable descriptors for cultivar distinction and germplasm characterisation. Such variance is frequently attributed to hybrid origin and long-term selection under a variety of environmental settings. The recent biochemical and genomic studies provide significant support for the extensive diversity observed in the colour of the rind and pulp. The primary factors that influence the colour variation of citrus fruits are pigments such as carotenoids, chlorophylls, and anthocyanins, which contribute to the spectrum of yellow, orange, red, and green tints [57]. Furthermore, comparative studies conducted on citrus cultivars demonstrate that tissue-specific carotenoid biosynthesis results in unique colouration patterns in the peel and pith [58]. This elucidates the observed diversity in the colour of the rind and pulp in the current investigation. In contrast, the relatively uniform expression of albedo colour (predominantly white) suggests a conserved trait with limited genetic variation. Similar findings have been reported in recent citrus studies [59], where certain internal structural traits remain stable due to tighter genetic regulation and lower environmental influence. Textural traits such as fruit surface and albedo adherence showed considerable variation, which is important for fruit handling, peelability, and processing quality [56]. The predominance of weak albedo adherence in the present study may have practical implications for improving peelability and processing efficiency.

The observed diversity in internal fruit axis structure and seed morphology further supports the presence of significant genetic variability. Seed traits, including shape, surface texture, and colour, are critical markers for rootstock identification and are often associated with reproductive biology and nucellar embryony. Recent studies also emphasise that morphological and biochemical diversity in citrus germplasm is closely linked with underlying genetic variation and can be effectively utilised for breeding and conservation strategies [60].

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) effectively summarised the morphological variability, with the first seven components explaining 86.90% of the total variance, indicating adequate representation of phenotypic diversity (Table). PC1 (28.42%) was primarily associated with fruit size and structural traits, including fruit length, width, weight, volume, axis diameter, rind thickness, and segment number, suggesting that these traits collectively drive major phenotypic divergence in citrus [61]. PC2 (18.88%) was dominated by seed morphology and internal fruit characteristics, such as seed length, seed width, adherence of albedo to pulp, seeds per segment, and albedo colour, with a negative association of pulp colour, highlighting the role of reproductive and internal quality traits in differentiating rootstocks [54]. The PCA indicates that fruit size attributes and seed-related traits are the principal contributors to variability, providing a reliable basis for selection and improvement of citrus rootstocks. The PCA biplot revealed distinct genotype separation, with Karna Khatta and Sour Orange related to larger fruits, and Carrizo and Troyer Citrange with seed attributes. The large distribution of genotypes across quadrants shows substantial phenotypic variety and lack of redundancy, similar to recent citrus studies [61, 62].

Pearson correlation analysis

The Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations among fruit size traits, indicating coordinated development. Significant correlations among fruit length, width, weight, and volume confirm that these traits are tightly linked and collectively determine fruit size, as also reported in recent citrus findings [61]. Moderate associations of fruit size with rind thickness, axis diameter, and seed number suggest integrated fruit structural development [60]. In contrast, seed traits showed weak associations with fruit size but strong correlations among themselves, indicating independent genetic control. This independence is beneficial for breeding, allowing simultaneous improvement of fruit and seed traits.

Molecular analysis using SCoT markers

The high level of polymorphism detected in the present study indicates substantial genetic variability among the evaluated citrus rootstocks and exceeds the polymorphism previously reported in citrus rootstocks using SCoT markers [63]. Although the total number of amplified bands per primer was relatively low, this may be attributed to the limited number of genotypes evaluated and the gene-targeted nature of SCoT markers, which amplify conserved genomic regions flanking the ATG translation initiation codon [28]. Therefore, the number of amplified loci generated by SCoT markers may vary depending on the genetic background of the germplasm and primer-template complementarity.

The discriminatory ability of marker systems is commonly evaluated using parameters such as resolving power (Rp), polymorphism information content (PIC), and marker index (MI) [64]. In the present study, Rp values ranged from 0.235 to 2.823, exceeding those reported in earlier study [32], thereby indicating the superior discriminatory capacity of the selected primers. Furthermore, the mean PIC values obtained in this study were comparable with those reported in previous studies [31, 32], confirming the moderate informativeness of SCoT markers for diversity assessment in citrus. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the gene-targeted SCoT marker system is effective for detecting genetic heterogeneity and characterising citrus germplasm.

Genetic diversity indices of SCoT markers

The effective number of alleles (Ne) is the reciprocal of the sum of squared allele frequencies, representing the number of equally frequent alleles required to produce the same level of genetic homozygosity as observed in the population [65]. Our results for the Ne value are consistent with the previous report [31]. The genetic diversity index (h) shows overall genetic variety and genotype differentiation by quantifying genetic variance within a population. Shannon’s index (I), in contrast, evaluates the uniformity and diversity of genetic traits within and between populations [66]. Greater genetic variety both within and between populations is indicated by higher values of Shannon’s index (I) and the genetic diversity index (h). The h and I indices of the current study showed a significant degree of locus diversity, and the results were consistent with several findings [67, 68].

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) and cluster analysis

The close agreement between PCoA and UPGMA clustering indicates the robustness of the genetic relationships inferred from SCoT markers. Similar concordance between multivariate and hierarchical clustering approaches has been reported in previous citrus diversity studies, confirming the utility of SCoT markers for elucidating genetic relationships among citrus germplasm [28, 32]. The resolution of the germplasm into four distinct groups suggests substantial genetic differentiation among the evaluated rootstocks.The clustering pattern largely reflected the known pedigree and taxonomic relationships among the evaluated genotypes (Table S1). For instance, Carrizo and Troyer Citrange clustered together, which is expected because both are hybrids derived from Poncirus trifoliata × Citrus sinensis and therefore share a common genetic background.

Similarly, the grouping of several NRCC rootstocks indicates close genetic relatedness among these selections. In contrast, the clustering of Rough Lemon, Rangpur Lime, Volkameriana, and other mandarins and limes within the same group may reflect their broader genetic affinity and shared ancestry within the Citrus gene pool. The distinct placement of certain rootstocks across different clusters further highlights the existence of unique genetic backgrounds that could be exploited for broadening the genetic base of citrus rootstock breeding programmes [43, 69]. Although bootstrap support values were not estimated in the present study, the strong concordance between PCoA and UPGMA analyses supports the reliability of the inferred genetic relationships among the evaluated citrus rootstocks.

Population structure analysis and molecular variance

The population structure analysis identified K = 2 as the optimal number of genetic clusters using the Elbow method and Silhouette Coefficient, partitioning the 17 citrus rootstocks into two distinct pools with minimal admixture and high membership probabilities (~ 1.0). The inferred K = 2 population structure is comparable to previous SCoT-based diversity studies, in which K values ranging from 2 to 9 have been reported depending on marker resolution and germplasm composition [31, 32]. Cluster 1 (SFS, CM, Troyer Citrange, CRH-47, Carrizo) forms a cohesive group of citrange-derived rootstocks, indicating shared hybrid ancestry from Poncirus trifoliata × Citrus sinensis, often linked to enhanced vigour and phytophthora tolerance [10]. The larger Cluster 2 (NRCC series, Volkameriana, Rough lemon, etc.) encompasses trifoliate-free mandarins and limes, suggesting greater allelic richness and adaptability, consistent with broader diversity in commercial rootstocks like Rangpur lime [10, 70].

Although PCoA and UPGMA resolved the germplasm into four groups, these distance-based methods are capable of detecting finer levels of genetic differentiation among closely related genotypes [43]. In contrast, model-based clustering approaches identify the most parsimonious underlying genetic structure within a dataset [71, 72]. Therefore, the four clusters identified by PCoA and UPGMA likely represent subgroups nested within the two broader genetic pools inferred by GMM, indicating the presence of both broad and fine-scale genetic structuring within the studied citrus germplasm.

The predominance of genetic variation within populations revealed by AMOVA is consistent with the outcrossing nature and high heterozygosity characteristic of Citrus species, which generally maintain substantial genetic variability within populations [73]. The moderate but significant genetic differentiation observed among clusters indicates the existence of distinct genetic groups despite the shared ancestry among several rootstocks. Furthermore, the concordance between AMOVA and clustering analyses supports the reliability of SCoT markers for detecting genetic structure within citrus germplasm. The presence of appreciable variation both within and among groups highlights the importance of conserving diverse citrus rootstock resources to broaden the genetic base available for future breeding programmes.

Nevertheless, the relatively small number of genotypes (n = 17) evaluated in the present study should be considered when interpreting the inferred population structure. Population structure analyses based on limited sample sizes may not fully capture the genetic complexity present within broader citrus germplasm collections. Therefore, the observed clustering patterns should be regarded as preliminary and require validation using larger and more diverse germplasm panels.

Association between morphological and molecular diversity

The weak Mantel association together with the moderate congruence revealed by Procrustes analysis suggests only partial correspondence between morphological and molecular diversity among the citrus rootstocks. Such discrepancies may arise because morphological traits are influenced by environmental conditions and developmental factors, whereas molecular markers capture variation directly at the DNA level [20]. Consequently, phenotypic similarity among genotypes may not necessarily reflect their underlying genetic relationships. Therefore, the integration of morphological and molecular approaches provides a more comprehensive and reliable assessment of genetic diversity in citrus germplasm.

Study limitations and future perspectives

While the present study provides valuable information on the diversity and genetic relationships among citrus rootstocks, future investigations should correlate genetic diversity with agronomic performance traits such as drought and salinity tolerance, disease resistance, graft compatibility, and rootstock-induced effects on scion performance to identify functionally superior rootstocks.

Despite their effectiveness in detecting polymorphism and resolving genetic relationships, SCoT markers are dominant in nature and therefore do not distinguish between homozygous and heterozygous loci [27]. Consequently, estimates of allelic diversity and population structure derived from SCoT markers should be interpreted with caution, particularly in genetically complex and highly heterozygous species such as Citrus [69]. Therefore, future studies integrating co-dominant marker systems, such as SSRs or SNPs, would provide higher resolution and enable a more precise assessment of genetic relationships and population structure among citrus rootstocks.

Conclusions

The present study revealed substantial phenotypic and molecular diversity among the seventeen citrus rootstock genotypes evaluated. Morphological analyses identified considerable variation in fruit and seed traits, while SCoT markers exhibited high polymorphism and effectively discriminated the genotypes. Multivariate, clustering, population structure, and AMOVA analyses collectively demonstrated the existence of distinct genetic groups within the studied germplasm. Furthermore, Mantel and Procrustes analyses indicated only partial correspondence between morphological and molecular diversity, highlighting the complementary nature of these approaches. Overall, the integration of morphological descriptors and SCoT markers provided a comprehensive and reliable framework for characterising citrus rootstock genotypes. The genetically distinct genotypes identified in this study may serve as valuable parental resources for future citrus rootstock breeding and germplasm conservation programmes. Further studies integrating agronomic performance traits and high-resolution co-dominant markers are required to validate the functional significance of the observed diversity patterns.
