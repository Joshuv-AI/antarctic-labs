Support of Continuous Smeary measures on Spheres


Abstract
. We investigate the support of smeary, directionally smeary, and finite sample smeary probability measures μ \mu with density ρ \rho on spheres 𝕊 m \mathbb{S}^{m} . First, in the rotationally symmetric case, we show that a distribution is not smeary, or equivalently, not directionally smeary whenever its support lies in a geodesic ball centered at the Fréchet mean of radius R m > π / 2 R_{m}>\pi/2 , where R m = π / 2 + O ⁡ ( 1 / m ) R_{m}=\pi/2+O(1/m) . In the general case, we show that neither directional nor full smeariness holds whenever the support is contained in a closed ball of radius π / 2 \pi/2 , however, past the support radius π / 2 , \pi/2, full smeariness may break down, but directional smeariness breaks down only past the support radius R m . R_{m}. Second, we prove sharpness of this threshold. For every ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , we show there exists m 0 ​ ( ε ) m_{0}(\varepsilon) such that for all m ≥ m 0 ​ ( ε ) m\geq m_{0}(\varepsilon) there exists a rotationally symmetric continuous smeary probability measure on 𝕊 m \mathbb{S}^{m} whose support lies in a ball of radius π / 2 + ε \pi/2+\varepsilon around the Fréchet mean. Third, in every dimension we construct directionally smeary continuous distributions supported in a ball of radius π / 2 + ε \pi/2+\varepsilon whose Fréchet function has Hessian of rank one. Finally, we study finite sample smeariness. We show that any continuous non-smeary distribution supported in a geodesic ball of radius π / 2 \pi/2 is necessarily Type I finite sample smeary, i.e. its variance modulation m n m_{n} satisfies lim n → ∞ m n > 1 \lim_{n\to\infty}m_{n}>1 . In the rotationally symmetric case, we further prove a curse-of-dimensionality phenomenon: the variance modulation increases with the dimension and can become arbitrarily large depending on the support.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Fréchet function, smeariness and directional smeariness.

The Fréchet mean provides a fundamental notion of expectation for random variables taking values in nonlinear spaces such as Riemannian manifolds.
For a random variable
X
on a manifold
M
, the Fréchet function is

F(p)=\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(p,X)],

and any minimizer of
F
is called a (population) Fréchet mean. Given i.i.d. random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim X
on
M
, the empirical Fréchet function is

F_{n}(p):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d^{2}(p,X_{j}).

Any minimizer of
F_{n}
is called a
sample Fréchet mean
. A measurable selection of sample Fréchet means is denoted by
\hat{\mu}_{n}
, that is,

\hat{\mu}_{n}\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{p\in M}F_{n}(p)\qquad

The asymptotic behavior of sample Fréchet means has been studied extensively in statistics on manifolds; see for instance
9
;
3
;
2
.

In Euclidean space the sample mean satisfies a classical central limit theorem with rate
n^{-1/2}
.
On curved spaces, however, the geometry of the manifold can interact with the distribution of the data in subtle ways.
In particular, degeneracy of the Hessian of the Fréchet function at the population mean may occur, leading to slower convergence rates of the sample mean.
This phenomenon is known as
smeariness
; see, for example,
10
;
11
;
7
.

More precisely, let
\bar{\mu}
be a Fréchet mean of a random variable
X
on a Riemannian manifold
M
, and let
F
denote the
lifted
Fréchet function in exponential coordinates at
\bar{\mu}
, viewed on
T_{\bar{\mu}}M
.
Adapting the subspace formulation in
7
, we say that

Definition 1.1

(
Smeariness and directional smeariness
)
.

Let
\bar{\mu}
be a Fréchet mean of a random variable
X
on a Riemannian manifold
M
, and let
F
denote the lifted Fréchet function
\widetilde{F}:=F\circ exp_{\bar{\mu}}
in exponential coordinates at
\bar{\mu}
, viewed on the tangent space
T_{\mu}M
. Let
V\subset T_{\bar{\mu}}M
be a linear subspace.

We say that
\mu
(or equivalently and interchangeably,
X
) is
smeary along
V
if
\widetilde{F}(x)>F(0)
for all sufficiently small
x\in V\setminus\{0\}
and if there exist a constant
C_{X}>0
and an exponent
\kappa>2
such that

\sup_{x\in V,\ \|x\|<\delta}|\widetilde{F}(x)-\widetilde{F}(0)|\geq C_{X}\delta^{\kappa}

for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
.

The infimum of all such exponents
\kappa>2
will be called the
smeariness exponent of
F
(or equivalently
\widetilde{F}
) along
V
. If this infimum is attained, we call the smallest admissible exponent the smeariness exponent.
If
V=T_{\bar{\mu}}M
, we simply say that
\mu
is
smeary
. If
V
is a proper nonzero subspace of
T_{\bar{\mu}}M
, we say that
\mu
is
directionally smeary along
V
. Stressing on
\kappa
if necessary, we also use the term
(\kappa-2)
-smeary. Note that for
non-smeary
measures,
\kappa=2,
which is not part of the definition, but the term
0
-smeary, often used in the literature, implies non-smeary and that
\kappa=2.

In the settings considered in this paper, we assume that the density
\rho
vanishes in a neighborhood of the cut locus of the supposed unique Fréchet mean (cf. Assumption
3.1
). Under this assumption, the lifted Fréchet function
\widetilde{F}
is
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of
0\in T_{\mu}M
. In particular, its Hessian and all higher derivatives in Definition
1.1
above are well defined at
0
. Thus, in our smooth setting, directional smeariness means that the quadratic term of
F
is nondegenerate in some directions, while along other directions the first nontrivial growth occurs only at order strictly higher than two.

A further distinction, introduced in
7
, concerns the origin of this degeneracy.
If the negative contribution causing the Hessian to vanish comes from geodesics interacting with the cut locus, one speaks of
cut-locus smeariness
; otherwise one speaks of
geometrical smeariness
.
For spheres of dimension at least two, the relevant phenomenon is geometrical rather than cut-locus smeariness
(
7
)
.

1.1.1.
Contribution of the paper to smeariness and directional smearineess:

Existing constructions of smeary distributions typically rely on delicate interactions between the geometry of the manifold and the probability measure generating the data.
As a consequence, the geometric and probabilistic mechanisms responsible for smeariness are often intertwined.
The present paper shows that, in the rotationally symmetric setting on
\mathbb{S}^{m}
, these two effects can be separated cleanly: the geometric input is encoded in an explicit kernel arising from the squared Riemannian distance, while the probabilistic input enters only through the radial weighting.
Equivalently, the Hessian and fourth derivative of the Fréchet function reduce to one-dimensional weighted integral conditions involving explicit geometry-only functions.
This representation is established in Proposition
3.2
.
This should be contrasted with earlier sphere constructions, such as
7
, where the fourth-order analysis is carried out within specific rotationally symmetric model families after reducing the Fréchet function to a one-variable problem, noting also that the distributions there were not absolutely continuous, unlike ours.
Here, by contrast, we first derive an explicit fourth-order expansion of the squared Riemannian distance itself (Equation (
4
)) and then use it systematically both for arbitrary rotationally symmetric densities in Section 3, and for the non-rotationally symmetric directional-smeariness constructions of Section 4.

This decoupling reduces the study of smeariness to the analysis of two geometric functions
b_{m}
and
h_{m}
that can be interpreted as eigenvalues of the second and fourth derivative tensors of the Fréchet function.
Their behavior is analyzed in Section
3.3
.
In particular, we show that
b_{m}
vanishes only at a specific radius
R_{m}
determined by the geometry of the sphere (Proposition
3.5
), while the behavior of
h_{m}
depends strongly on the dimension (Proposition
3.7
).

Using this structural decomposition we obtain a
sharp
geometric obstruction to smeariness.
Our first main theorem shows that rotationally symmetric distributions whose support is contained in a geodesic ball of radius
R_{m}
, the first zero of
b_{m}
, around the Fréchet mean cannot be smeary; see Theorem
3.10
.
We also show that

\frac{\pi}{2}<R_{m}=\frac{\pi}{2}+O(1/m).

Thus the ball of radius
R_{m}
appears as a natural geometric threshold beyond which degeneracy of the Hessian may occur.

We further show that this obstruction is semi-sharp, se Theorem
3.15
. More precisely, besides the Hessian threshold
R_{m}
, there is a second critical radius
S_{m}>R_{m}
, namely the first zero of the quartic coefficient
h_{m}
. For rotationally symmetric continuous measures, support contained in the ball of radius
R_{m}
rules out smeariness because the Hessian is strictly positive definite, while support contained in the ball of radius
S_{m}

still
rules out smeariness: even if the Hessian is forced to vanish, the quartic term remains negative, so the Fréchet mean becomes a local maximum rather than a smeary minimum. Thus smeariness can only become possible once the radial support extends beyond
S_{m}
. In dimensions
m=2,3
, this quartic obstruction is global, so rotationally symmetric smeariness cannot occur
at all
. On the other hand, when the support extends beyond
S_{m}
, one can engineer rotationally symmetric densities so that the Hessian vanishes while the fourth derivative remains positive. This is possible for high enough dimensions, cf. Theorem
3.12
.

The support picture becomes subtler once one leaves the rotationally symmetric setting.
In the general case, a hemispherical support still rules out full
2
-smeariness: Theorem
4.1
shows by constructing an example that if the support is contained in the closed ball of radius
\pi/2
around the Fréchet mean, then the Hessian of the lifted Fréchet function is positive definite, and more generally that if the support is contained in the larger ball of radius
R_{m}
, then the Hessian cannot vanish identically.
Thus the radius
\pi/2
remains a sharp obstruction for full smeariness in the general setting, whereas the radius
R_{m}
, with
\pi/2<R_{m}=\pi/2+O(1/m)
, remains a sharp obstruction for full smeariness in the rotationally symmetric setting.
At the same time, a support radius of
R_{m}
does not rule out directional smeariness.
Indeed, Section 4 shows that once the support is allowed to extend slightly beyond the hemisphere, the Hessian may become singular without vanishing completely, so that higher-order flatness survives only on a
one-dimensional
proper subspace of the tangent space.
This is realized explicitly in Proposition
4.4
, where for every
m\geq 2
and every
\varepsilon>0
an absolutely continuous measure supported in
B(N;\pi/2+\varepsilon)
is constructed whose Hessian is positive semidefinite of rank one.
Proposition
4.5
then shows that the third derivative vanishes and the fourth derivative is strictly positive on the degenerate directions, yielding a genuine directionally
2
-smeary example.

1.2.
Finite sample smeariness.

The last part of the paper turns to
finite sample smeariness
; see
13
;
12
;
6
. Below are the relevant definitions.

Definition 1.2

(
Variance modulation and finite sample smeariness
)
.

Let
\bar{\mu}
be a unique Fréchet mean of a random variable
X
on a Riemannian manifold
M
, and let
\hat{\mu}_{n}
be a sample Fréchet mean based on an i.i.d. sample of size
n
. Define

V:=F(\bar{\mu})=\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\bar{\mu},X)],\qquad V_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\bar{\mu},\hat{\mu}_{n})],

and the associated
variance modulation

m_{n}:=\frac{nV_{n}}{V}.

We say that the Fréchet mean is
finite sample smeary
if

1<\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}m_{n}<\infty.

More specifically, it is called

(i)
Type I finite sample smeary
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{n}>1,

(ii)
Type II finite sample smeary
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{n}=1.

Thus finite sample smeariness captures the phenomenon that, even when the asymptotic rate is classical, sample Fréchet means may display inflated dispersion over a substantial finite-sample regime. The remark below shows that for smeary distributions,
\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{n}=\infty
always.

In particular, once a smeary or directionally smeary regime is identified and the corresponding CLT is established (e.g. Theorem 2.11 in
11
), the associated variance modulation cannot remain bounded
in that regime
. See Remark
1.3
below. Thus finite sample smeariness corresponds to
bounded excess modulation over the Euclidean case
.

Remark 1.3

(
Smeariness, directional smeariness and divergence of the variance modulation
)
.

The relation between smeariness and finite sample smeariness is sometimes left implicit, so let us state the heuristic clearly. In the terminology of variance modulation, finite sample smeariness corresponds to

1<\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}m_{n}<\infty,

whereas smeariness is the regime in which the limiting modulation is unbounded. This point is made explicitly in earlier work where they
defined
smeariness as the limiting modulation being infinity; see
12
and
13
. Thus smeariness may be viewed as the worst possible asymptotic behavior from the perspective of variance modulation. Here, however, we use smeariness in the sense of the local Fréchet-function expansion (Definition
1.1
), and the point of the discussion below is that, under certain moment assumptions, this notion indeed forces divergence of the variance modulation.

More concretely, suppose the lifted Fréchet function
\widetilde{F}
at the population mean is smooth near
0
(a consequence of Assumption
3.1
used throughout the paper). Assume that the manifold smeary setup of
16
holds: there is an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
of
T_{\bar{\mu}}M
, integers
2\leq r_{1}\leq\cdots\leq r_{d}
, and positive constants
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}
as in Assumption 4.2.10 so that the lifted Fréchet function expands:
\widetilde{F}(x)=\widetilde{F}(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}{x_{i}}^{r_{i}}+o(\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{r_{d}})
. Note that, if one of the inequalities among
\lambda_{j}
becomes strict, this becomes directional smeariness, otherwise it is full smeariness. Let

U_{n}:=\exp_{\bar{\mu}}^{-1}(\hat{\mu}_{n}).

By
16
, the highest-rate asymptotics are governed by the highest-rate subspace

C_{\bar{\mu}}:=\operatorname{span}\{e_{i}:\ r_{i}=r_{d}\},

identified through the correction map
H_{\mu}

H_{\mu}(X):=h_{\mu}(\exp_{\bar{\mu}}X),\qquad h_{\mu}(x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log_{\bar{\mu}}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon,x}}{\varepsilon^{1/(r_{d}-1)}},

with
\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon,x}
the Fréchet mean of
(1-\varepsilon)\mu+\varepsilon\delta_{x}
.

Assume that there exists an index
i
with
r_{i}=r_{d}>2
such that

Y_{n,i}:=n^{1/(2(r_{i}-1))}\langle U_{n},e_{i}\rangle

converges in distribution to a random variable
Y_{i}
with
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]>0
, and that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\big[|Y_{n,i}|^{2+\delta}\big]<\infty

for some
\delta>0
. Then
\{Y_{n,i}^{2}\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable (cf.
4
), hence

\mathbb{E}[Y_{n,i}^{2}]\to\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]>0.

Therefore

n\,\mathbb{E}\|U_{n}\|^{2}\geq n\,\mathbb{E}\langle U_{n},e_{i}\rangle^{2}=n^{\,1-\frac{1}{r_{i}-1}}\mathbb{E}[Y_{n,i}^{2}]=n^{\frac{r_{i}-2}{r_{i}-1}}\mathbb{E}[Y_{n,i}^{2}]\to\infty,\because r_{i}>2.

Since locally
d^{2}(\bar{\mu},\hat{\mu}_{n})=\|U_{n}\|^{2},
it follows that

m_{n}=\frac{n\,\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\bar{\mu},\hat{\mu}_{n})]}{V}\to\infty.

This proves modulation blow up in (directional) smaeary cases.

1.2.1.
Contribution of the paper to finite sample smeariness:

Building on the above framework, we show that hemispherical support has a strong finite-sample consequence even in the absence of smeariness.
More precisely, Theorem
4.10
proves that every absolutely continuous distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{m}
with unique Fréchet mean
N
and support contained in the closed hemisphere about
N
, prohibiting both smeariness and directional smeariness, is necessarily Type I finite sample smeary, under the stated moment assumption
4.11
. This result is a generalization of Theorem 6 in
6
for continuous distributions with a density.
Thus hemispherical support prevents full smeariness, and in the general case also rules out directional smeariness inside the hemisphere, yet still forces the variance modulation to stay asymptotically above its Euclidean value.
Furthermore, in the rotationally symmetric case this effect becomes quantitatively stronger in higher dimension: Theorem
4.17
proves that the asymptotic modulation is monotone in the dimension and admits an explicit limit, yielding a curse-of-dimensionality phenomenon for non-smeary distributions supported strictly inside hemispheres.

Together the above results demonstrate that smeariness on spheres arises from a subtle interaction between curvature and the radial distribution of mass, and they provide a systematic framework for analyzing such degeneracies on the sphere.
In particular, the paper exhibits on spheres a
support-dependent transition
between full smeariness, directional smeariness, and finite sample smeariness, proving alongside two high dimensional implications: support of smeary distributions being contained in a super-hemispherical cap with difference to the hemisphere arbitrarily small, and non-smeary distributions with support in the hemisphere having increasing modulation with an arbitrary high limit.

3.
Fréchet function for probability measures on
\mathbb{S}^{m}

3.1.
Setup

Let

\mathbb{S}^{m}
–valued continuous random variable whose density is rotationally symmetric about its Fréchet mean, that we assume to be the North Pole
N
with no loss of generality. Denote by
-N

V:=\exp_{N}^{-1}\!\big(X\big|_{\mathbb{S}^{m}\setminus\{-N\}}\big)\in B(0,\pi)\subset T_{N}\mathbb{S}^{m}\cong\mathbb{R}^{m},

and write
V=R\Theta
, where
R=|V|\in(0,\pi)
and
\Theta\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}

the Jacobian of the exponential map
\exp_{N}
in normal coordinates. Accordingly, the volume form on
\mathbb{S}^{m}

d\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{S}^{m}}=R^{m-1}J(R)\,dR\,d\Theta=(\sin R)^{m-1}dR\,d\Theta.

Assume

\mathbb{S}^{m}

-N

\mu

\mathbb{S}^{m}\setminus\{-N\}

\tilde{\mu}

\exp_{N}^{-1}
to the ball

in
T_{N}

\tilde{\mu}
is a probability measure with a radially symmetric density
\rho(\left\lVert.\right\rVert)
on

of the form

(4)

d\tilde{\mu}(v)=\rho(\|v\|)\,J(\|v\|)\,dv,\qquad J(R):=\left(\frac{\sin R}{R}\right)^{m-1},\qquad dv=R^{m-1}dR\,d\Theta,

d\tilde{\mu}=\rho(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}dR\,d\Theta.

Denote by

the lift of the Fréchet function

.
Define the Fréchet function of

(or equivalently, of
\tilde{\mu}
) in normal coordinates about

. So we can write:

(5)

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{F}(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[d^{2}(\exp_{N}u,X)\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,\pi)}d^{2}(\exp_{N}u,\exp_{N}v)\,d\tilde{\mu}(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,\pi)}g(u;v)\,d\tilde{\mu}(v),\qquad x:=\exp_{N}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{R=0}^{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}g(u;R\Theta)\,\rho(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}d\Theta\,dR,\,v:=R\Theta,\Theta\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1},0\leq R\leq\pi.

where

is defined explicitly in Equation (
4
) with the functions
A_{i}
,
B_{i}
’s in Equation (
3
). Let us also remark that for
\mathbb{S}^{m}
, we have

.

(
Assumption on the density

)
.

Assume that

or equivalently

Proposition 3.2

(

)

Assume

Proof.

We first need the following technical lemma:

Lemma 3.4

(
Second and fourth spherical moments of the squared distance (including cross-moments)
)
.

Let
m\geq 2
. Let
d\Theta
denote the Riemannian volume measure on

\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m})\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
,
write
\theta_{i}
for the

(i) Odd monomials vanish.

For any indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{2r+1}\in\{1,\dots,m\}

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\theta_{i_{1}}\theta_{i_{2}}\cdots\theta_{i_{2r+1}}\,d\Theta\;=\;0.

(ii) Second moments.

For all
1\leq i,j\leq m
,

(10)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\,d\Theta\;=\;\frac{vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})}{m}\,\delta_{ij}.

Equivalently,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta\Theta^{\mathsf{T}}\,d\Theta\;=\;\frac{vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})}{m}\,I_{m}.

(iii) Fourth moments.

For all
1\leq i,j,k,\ell\leq m
,

(11)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\theta_{k}\theta_{\ell}\,d\Theta\;=\;\frac{vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})}{m(m+2)}\Big(\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}\Big).

(iv) Contractions.

For every
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

(12)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\theta\rangle^{2}\,d\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})}{m}\,\|w\|^{2},

(13)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\theta\rangle^{4}\,d\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{3\,vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})}{m(m+2)}\,\|w\|^{4}.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

The identities follow from the classical Funk–Hecke formula, which reduces
integrals over
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
of functions depending only on the inner product

\Theta\cdot y
to one–dimensional integrals in spherical polar coordinates.
More precisely, for any bounded measurable
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and any

,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}f(\Theta\cdot y)\,d\Theta=\operatorname{vol}(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{m}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\tfrac{m-1}{2})}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)\,(1-t^{2})^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\,dt,

see
5
.
Taking

and
f(t)=t^{4}
yields the scalar identities
(

) and (

). The tensor identities
(

) and (

) then follow by
rotational invariance of the surface measure. For a systematic treatment of
integration on the sphere and the Funk–Hecke formula in the context of spherical
harmonics, see also
(

, Chapter 3)
.
∎

(ii) Fourth derivative tensor of

at

.

Let

be as in Equation (

As observed in the previous section, the functions

are linked to the eigenvalues of the Hessian and the fourth order derivative of the Fréchet function

Proposition 3.5

(
Location and refined asymptotics of the unique zero of
b_{m}(R):=1+(m-1)R\cot R
)
.

Fix an integer
m\geq 2
and define

b_{m}:(0,\pi)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad b_{m}(R):=1+(m-1)R\cot R.

Then
b_{m}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\pi)
, has a unique zero
R_{m}\in(0,\pi)
, and this zero satisfies

R_{m}\in\Big(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\Big),\qquad R_{m}\searrow\frac{\pi}{2}\ \text{as }m\to\infty.

Moreover,
R_{m}
has the refined expansion

R_{m}=\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{2}{\pi(m-1)}-\frac{8}{\pi^{3}(m-1)^{2}}+O\!\Big(\frac{1}{(m-1)^{3}}\Big).

Proof.

Set
q(R):=R\cot R
for
R\in(0,\pi)
. Then
b_{m}(R)=1+(m-1)q(R)
, and
b_{m}(R)=0
is equivalent to

q(R)=-\frac{1}{m-1}.

We first show that
q
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\pi)
. Differentiating,

q^{\prime}(R)=\cot R-R\csc^{2}R=\frac{\sin R\cos R-R}{\sin^{2}R}.

Let
h(R):=\sin R\cos R-R=\tfrac{1}{2}\sin(2R)-R
. Then
h(0)=0
and

h^{\prime}(R)=\cos(2R)-1=-2\sin^{2}R<0\qquad\text{for }R\in(0,\pi),

hence

for all
R\in(0,\pi)
. Therefore
q^{\prime}(R)<0
on
(0,\pi)
, so
q
is strictly decreasing. Since by definition of
q(R),b_{m}(R)=1+(m-1)q(R)
, therefore it is immediate that
b_{m}(R)
is also strictly decreasing for all
R\in(0,\pi)
, proving the first part of this proposition.

Next,
\lim_{R\downarrow 0}q(R)=1
and
\lim_{R\uparrow\pi}q(R)=-\infty
(since
\cot R\to-\infty
as
R\uparrow\pi
).
Thus for each
m\geq 2
the equation
q(R)=-\frac{1}{m-1}
has a unique solution
R_{m}\in(0,\pi)
.
Since
q(\pi/2)=0
and
-\frac{1}{m-1}<0
, strict monotonicity implies
R_{m}>\pi/2
.
Moreover,
-\frac{1}{m-1}\uparrow 0
as
m\to\infty
and

,
so
R_{m}=q^{-1}(-\frac{1}{m-1})\searrow q^{-1}(0)=\pi/2
.

For the refined asymptotics, write
R_{m}=\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta_{m}

and set

.
Using

q(R_{m})=\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta_{m}\Big)\cot\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta_{m}\Big)=-\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta_{m}\Big)\tan\delta_{m}.

As

so the identity

becomes

-\sin R(3+\sin^{2}R)<0
,
so the numerator is negative. For
R\in(0,\pi/2)
, set

F(R):=(3+\sin^{2}R)\tan R-R(3+2\sin^{2}R).

Then the numerator equals
-\cos R\,F(R)
, and
\cos R>0
on
(0,\pi/2)
, so it suffices to show
F(R)>0
there.
A direct differentiation and simplification give

F^{\prime}(R)=4\tan^{2}R-2R\sin(2R)=4\tan R\left(\tan R-\frac{R}{1+\tan^{2}R}\right).

On
(0,\pi/2)
we have
\tan R>0
and
\tan R>R
(since
(\tan R-R)^{\prime}=\tan^{2}R>0
and
\tan 0-0=0
), hence

\tan R>R>\frac{R}{1+\tan^{2}R}
,
so
F^{\prime}(R)>0
on
(0,\pi/2)
. Moreover
F(0)=0
(by continuity), hence
F(R)>0
for all
R\in(0,\pi/2)
.
This proves
h_{2}(R)<0
on
(0,\pi)
.

(ii)
The identity for
h_{3}
is exact. On
(0,\pi/2)
,
\tan R>R
implies
\cot R<1/R
, hence
R\cot R-1<0
.
On
(\pi/2,\pi)
one has
\cot R<0
, hence
R\cot R-1<-1<0
. Thus
h_{3}(R)<0
on
(0,\pi)
.

(iii)
Let us first show that
h_{m}(R)\uparrow\infty
as
R\uparrow\pi.

Set
t:=\pi-R\downarrow 0
. Using

\sin(\pi-t)=t+O(t^{3}),\qquad\cos(\pi-t)=-1+O(t^{2}),\qquad\tan(\pi-t)=-t+O(t^{3}),

a direct expansion of the explicit formulas in (
3
) yields

A_{0}(\pi-t)=\frac{\pi}{4t^{3}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{t}\right),\qquad A_{2}(\pi-t)(\pi-t)^{2}=-\frac{3\pi}{2t^{3}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{t}\right),

A_{4}(\pi-t)(\pi-t)^{4}=\frac{5\pi}{4t^{3}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{t}\right).

Substituting into the definition

h_{m}(R)=A_{0}(R)+\frac{1}{m}A_{2}(R)R^{2}+\frac{3}{m(m+2)}A_{4}(R)R^{4}

gives

h_{m}(\pi-t)=\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{(m-3)(m-1)}{m(m+2)}\,\frac{1}{t^{3}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{t}\right).

Hence, for every
m\geq 4
, the leading coefficient is positive and

\lim_{R\uparrow\pi}h_{m}(R)=+\infty.

Note that the function
h_{m}
is continuous on
(0,\pi)
(in particular on
[\pi/2,\pi-\varepsilon]
for every
\varepsilon>0
).
Under the stated assumptions, choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
h_{m}(\pi-\varepsilon)>0
.
Since
h_{m}(\pi/2)<0
, the intermediate value theorem yields an
R_{0}\in(\pi/2,\pi-\varepsilon)
with
h_{m}(R_{0})=0
.
∎

By Proposition

above, there is a first zero of
h_{m}
for each

which we denote by
S_{m}
. Below, we analyze its asymptotics as

Proposition 3.8

(
Asymptotics of the first zero of
h_{m}
as
m\to\infty
)
.

Let
m\geq 4
and
A_{0},A_{2},A_{4}:(0,\pi)\to\mathbb{R}
be as in Equation
3
and, for each
m\geq 2
,
define

h_{m}(R):=A_{0}(R)+\frac{1}{m}A_{2}(R)R^{2}+\frac{3}{m(m+2)}A_{4}(R)R^{4},\qquad R\in(0,\pi).

Let

S_{m}:=\inf\{R\in(\pi/2,\pi):\,h_{m}(R)=0\}

denote the first such zero of
h_{m}
. Then

S_{m}\downarrow\frac{\pi}{2}\qquad\text{as }m\to\infty,

and more precisely,

S_{m}=\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{8}{\pi}\frac{1}{m}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right),\qquad m\to\infty.

Proof.

Set
S_{0}:=\pi/2
and write
S=S_{0}+\delta
with
\delta>0
small. From Equation (
3
) or (
16
), one has the identities

(18)

A_{0}(S_{0})=0,\qquad A_{2}(S_{0})=-\frac{4}{3\pi^{2}},\qquad A_{4}(S_{0})=\frac{16}{3\pi^{4}},

Using the above, one computes

h_{m}\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{m}A_{2}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{2}+\frac{3}{m(m+2)}A_{4}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{4}=\frac{1-m}{3m(m+2)}<0.

Moreover, by the Taylor expansion in Equation (
16
),

A_{0}\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+t\Big)=\frac{\pi}{24}\,t+O(t^{2}),

and hence

A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})=\frac{\pi}{24}>0.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
. Since
A_{0}
is
C^{2}
in a neighborhood of
S_{0}
, Taylor’s
theorem with remainder yields

A_{0}(S_{0}+\delta)=A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\,\delta+O(\delta^{2})\qquad\text{as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

Therefore, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
depending on
\eta
such that for all

\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
,

|O(\delta^{2})|\leq\eta\,A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\,\delta,

which implies

A_{0}(S_{0}+\delta)\geq(1-\eta)\,A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\,\delta.

Since
A_{2}
and
A_{4}
are continuous at
S_{0}
, there exists
C<\infty
such that for all

\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
,

\bigl|A_{2}(S_{0}+\delta)\bigr|\,(S_{0}+\delta)^{2}+\bigl|A_{4}(S_{0}+\delta)\bigr|\,(S_{0}+\delta)^{4}\leq C.

Therefore, for
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
and all
m\geq 2
,

h_{m}(S_{0}+\delta)\geq A_{0}(S_{0}+\delta)-\frac{C}{m}\geq(1-\eta)A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\,\delta-\frac{C}{m}.

Choose
\Delta>0
such that
(1-\eta)A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\Delta>C
, and set
\delta=\Delta/m
. For all sufficiently large
m
,
one has
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
and thus

h_{m}\!\left(S_{0}+\frac{\Delta}{m}\right)>0.

By continuity of
h_{m}
and since
h_{m}(S_{0})<0\forall m
, there exists a zero, denoted by
S_{m}
in
(S_{0},S_{0}+\Delta/m)
, hence

0<S_{m}-S_{0}\leq\frac{\Delta}{m}\qquad\text{for all sufficiently large }m,

which implies
S_{m}\to S_{0}=\pi/2
as
m\to\infty
. This proves the first part of the proposition.

To obtain the first-order asymptotic of
S_{m}
, we expand
A_{0}
at

S_{0}=\tfrac{\pi}{2}
:

A_{0}(S_{0}+\delta)=A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\,\delta+O(\delta^{2})\qquad(\delta\to 0).

Since
S_{m}-S_{0}=O(1/m)
, we may substitute
\delta=S_{m}-S_{0}
, giving

A_{0}(S_{m})=A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})(S_{m}-S_{0})+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right).

Moreover, by continuity of
A_{2}
and
A_{4}
at
S_{0}
,

A_{2}(S_{m})=A_{2}(S_{0})+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\right),\qquad A_{4}(S_{m})=A_{4}(S_{0})+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\right).

Write
\delta_{m}:=S_{m}-S_{0}
. By assumption
\delta_{m}=O(1/m)
.
Taylor’s theorem at
S_{0}
yields

A_{0}(S_{m})=A_{0}(S_{0})+A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\delta_{m}+O(\delta_{m}^{2})=A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})\delta_{m}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right),

since
A_{0}(S_{0})=0
and
\delta_{m}^{2}=O(1/m^{2})
.
Moreover, since
A_{2}
is
C^{1}
near
S_{0}
, the mean value theorem gives

A_{2}(S_{m})=A_{2}(S_{0})+O(|\delta_{m}|)=A_{2}(S_{0})+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\right),

and likewise
A_{4}(S_{m})=A_{4}(S_{0})+O(1/m)
.
Also,

S_{m}^{2}=S_{0}^{2}+2S_{0}\delta_{m}+O(\delta_{m}^{2})=S_{0}^{2}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\right),\qquad S_{m}^{4}=S_{0}^{4}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\right).

Hence

\frac{1}{m}A_{2}(S_{m})S_{m}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\Big(A_{2}(S_{0})+O(\tfrac{1}{m})\Big)\Big(S_{0}^{2}+O(\tfrac{1}{m})\Big)=\frac{1}{m}A_{2}(S_{0})S_{0}^{2}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right),

and similarly,

\frac{3}{m(m+2)}A_{4}(S_{m})S_{m}^{4}=\frac{3}{m(m+2)}A_{4}(S_{0})S_{0}^{4}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right).

Substituting these expansions into
0=h_{m}(S_{m})
yields

0=A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})(S_{m}-S_{0})+\frac{1}{m}A_{2}(S_{0})S_{0}^{2}+\frac{3}{m(m+2)}A_{4}(S_{0})S_{0}^{4}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right).

From Equation (
18
), using
A_{0}^{\prime}(S_{0})=\pi/24
,
A_{2}(S_{0})S_{0}^{2}=-1/3
, and
A_{4}(S_{0})S_{0}^{4}=1/3
, we obtain

0=\frac{\pi}{24}(S_{m}-S_{0})-\frac{1}{3m}+\frac{1}{m(m+2)}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right)=\frac{\pi}{24}(S_{m}-S_{0})-\frac{1}{3m}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right),

hence

S_{m}-S_{0}=\frac{8}{\pi}\frac{1}{m}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right),

as claimed.
∎

3.4.
Super-hemispherical obstruction for smeariness for rotationally symmetric densities

Theorem 3.10

(
Super-hemispherical sharp ball obstruction for smeariness under rotational symmetry
)
.

Let
\mu

\mathbb{S}^{m},\,m\geq 2
, rotationally symmetric about its unique local Fréchet mean

Let

Then

\mu\big(\{x\in\mathbb{S}^{m}:d(\bar{\mu},x)>R_{m}\}\big)>0.

if
\mu
is supported in the closed ball

\{x:d(\bar{\mu},x)\leq R_{m}\}
.

Proof.

Denote by
\rho(\left\lVert.\right\rVert)
the radially symmetric density of
\mu
, cf. Equation (
). As before, assume
\bar{\mu}=N
without losing generality.
By Proposition
3.2

-b_{m}(R)=-(1+(m-1)R\cot R)=(m-1)\bigl(-R\cot R\bigr)-1\leq(m-1)C_{J}-1\leq(m-1)C_{J}+1.

Hence

-I_{2}=-\int_{J}\phi_{2}(R)\,b_{m}(R)\,dR=\int_{J}\phi_{2}(R)\,(-b_{m}(R))\,dR\leq\bigl((m-1)C_{J}+1\bigr)\int_{J}\phi_{2}(R)\,dR.

Consequently, for all
m\geq m_{1}
,

(24)

\frac{J_{2}}{-I_{2}}\geq\frac{c_{0}}{\bigl((m-1)C_{J}+1\bigr)\int_{J}\phi_{2}(R)\,dR}.

By choosing
\phi_{1,m}
so that

\operatorname{supp}\phi_{1,m}\subset\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{2},\,\frac{\pi/2+R_{m}}{2}\Bigr),

one has

\int_{\pi/2}^{R_{m}}\phi_{1,m}(R)\,dR=\int_{\pi/2}^{(\pi/2+R_{m})/2}\phi_{1,m}(R)\,dR,

and hence the ratio on the right-hand of Equation (
23
) equals
\frac{2C}{m}
for all
m
.
Therefore the condition (
22
) reduces to

(25)

\boxed{\frac{c_{0}}{c_{J}\int_{J}\phi_{2}(R)\,dR}>2C},

which can be enforced by a suitable fixed choice of
\phi_{2}
, independently of
m
, e.g. taking
\phi_{2}
to be a small enough positive constant or having its integral on
J
small enough. Under this (purely quantitative) choice of
\phi_{1,m}
and
\phi_{2}
the strict inequality

\int_{J}\psi_{m}(R)\,h_{m}(R)\,dR>-\int_{\pi/2}^{R_{m}}\psi_{m}(R)\,h_{m}(R)\,dR

follows. In particular,

\int_{0}^{\pi/2+\epsilon}\rho(R)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}h_{m}(R)\,dR>0,

while by construction

\int_{0}^{\pi/2+\epsilon}\rho(R)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}b_{m}(R)\,dR=0,

so
\mu
is
2
-smeary. After normalizing
\rho
using
\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin R)^{m-1}\rho(R)\,dR=1,
This finishes the construction of the rotationally symmetric density
\rho(\left\lVert.\right\rVert)
above, and hence the proof of Theorem
3.12
.

∎

Proposition 3.13

(
The local Fréchet mean in Theorem

)

Fix

and set

Let

and let
\mu
be a rotationally symmetric probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{m}
around the North pole

, with radial density
\rho(R)\geq 0

supported in

Define, as in the beginning of Theorem
3.12
above

so that
\psi_{m}
is supported in

\int_{0}^{\pi}\psi_{m}(R)\,dR=1

Let

denote the population Fréchet function along the meridian through

\Phi_{m}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m}}d^{2}(x,y_{r})\,d\mu(x)

In particular, for any such

the point

is
not
a global minimizer of
\Phi_{m}

is not a global population Fréchet mean for the probability measure with the density constructed in Theeorem
3.12
above.

Proof.

Step 1: Integral formula for
\Phi_{m}(r)
.

Let

with
r\in[0,\pi]
and

\Theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}

\exp_{N}(w)=\cos R\,N+\sin R\,\Theta,\qquad\exp_{N}(u)=\cos r\,N+\sin r\,\Theta_{0}.

By the spherical law of cosines,

\cos\!\big(d(\exp_{N}(u),\exp_{N}(w))\big)=\cos r\cos R+\sin r\sin R\,\langle\Theta,\Theta_{0}\rangle.

Hence, using (
),

\Phi_{m}(r)=F_{\mu}(\exp_{N}(r\Theta_{0}))=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\arccos^{2}\!\Big(\cos r\cos R+\sin r\sin R\,\langle\Theta,\Theta_{0}\rangle\Big)\,\rho(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

Now set

By the standard reduction formula for spherical integrals,
the surface measure

on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
decomposes as

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}f(\langle\Theta,\Theta_{0}\rangle)\,d\Theta=vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-2})\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)\,(1-t^{2})^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\,dt.

Therefore

\Phi_{m}(r)=vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-2})\int_{0}^{\pi}\rho(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\arccos^{2}\!\big(\cos r\cos R+\sin r\sin R\,t\big)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\,dt\right)dR.

If we introduce

\psi_{m}(R):=vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-2})\,\rho(R)(\sin R)^{m-1},\qquad w_{m}(t):=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{m}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac{m-1}{2})}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{m-3}{2}},

then the inner integral may equivalently be written with the normalized weight

,
yielding the one–dimensional reduction

\Phi_{m}(r)=vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-2})\int_{0}^{\pi}\psi_{m}(R)\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\arccos^{2}\!\big(\cos r\cos R+\sin r\sin R\,t\big)\,w_{m}(t)\,dt\right)dR.

Step 2: specialize the above
\Phi_{m}(r)
to

and

.

At

,

(26)

\Phi_{m}(0)=vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-2})\int_{0}^{\pi}R^{2}\,\psi_{m}(R)\,dR.

At

,

(27)

\Phi_{m}\!\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)=vol(\mathbb{S}^{m-2})\int_{0}^{\pi}\psi_{m}(R)\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\arccos^{2}\!\big(t\sin R\big)\,w_{m}(t)\,dt\right)dR.

Then

Thus it suffices to show

on
I_{\delta,\epsilon}
for

large enough.

Step 3: A uniform Taylor bound of

on

Set

Hence for all

and

we have
|t\sin R|\leq s_{\delta,\epsilon}<1
.
Let

and define

A fourth–order Taylor estimate at

gives, for all

and since

and

yields

\int_{-1}^{1}f(t\sin R)\,w_{m}(t)\,dt\leq\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{2}+\frac{(\sin R)^{2}}{m}+\frac{M_{\delta,\epsilon}}{8}\,\frac{(\sin R)^{4}}{m(m+2)}.

Indeed, for

f(t\sin R)=f(0)+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}(t\sin R)^{2}+\frac{f^{(4)}(\xi_{t,R})}{24}(t\sin R)^{4},

for some
\xi_{t,R}
between

and

.
Since
f(0)=\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)^{2}
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=2
, and

|f^{(4)}(\xi_{t,R})|\leq M_{\delta,\epsilon}
for

we obtain

Integrating against

and using

gives (

).

In particular, since

on

,

\int_{-1}^{1}f(t\sin R)\,w_{m}(t)\,dt\leq\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{2}+\frac{s_{\delta,\epsilon}^{2}}{m}+\frac{M_{\delta,\epsilon}}{8}\,\frac{s_{\delta,\epsilon}^{4}}{m(m+2)}.

Step 4: a uniform lower bound for

on
I_{\delta,\epsilon}
.

For

we have

Step 5: choose

and conclude.

Combining (

) and (

\Delta_{m}(R)\geq(\pi\delta+\delta^{2})-\frac{s_{\delta,\epsilon}^{2}}{m}-\frac{M_{\delta,\epsilon}}{8}\,\frac{s_{\delta,\epsilon}^{4}}{m(m+2)}=(\pi\delta+\delta^{2})-\frac{\cos^{2}\delta}{m}-\frac{M_{\delta,\epsilon}}{8}\,\frac{\cos^{4}\delta}{m(m+2)}.

The right-hand side is positive for all

large enough; define
m_{\delta,\epsilon}
so that for all

it is
>0
. Then

on

, and since

) implies

\Phi_{m}(0)-\Phi_{m}\!\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)>0.

∎

3.6.
Semi-tightness of the support threshold under rotational symmetry

In this subsection we refine Theorem
3.10
.
That theorem shows that if a rotationally symmetric density is supported in the closed ball of radius
R_{m}
, then the Hessian at the Fréchet mean is strictly positive definite, hence smeariness is impossible. We now show that this obstruction is semi-tight: the first zero
S_{m}
of the quartic coefficient
h_{m}
always lies strictly beyond
R_{m}
for every
m\geq 4
, and therefore support up to
S_{m}
still precludes smeariness. As seen before, in dimensions
m=2,3
, the quartic coefficient is everywhere negative, so rotationally symmetric smeariness cannot occur at all.

Lemma 3.14

(
Ordering of the first zeros of
b_{m},h_{m}
)
.

For every integer
m\geq 4
,

R_{m}<S_{m},

where
R_{m}
is the unique zero of
b_{m}
from Proposition
3.5
, and
S_{m}
is the first zero of
h_{m}
from Proposition
3.8
.

Proof.

Set
c:=\cot R
. From Equation (
3
), a direct algebraic simplification (substituting the explicit expressions of
A_{0},A_{2},A_{4}
into (
8
)) yields the identity

h_{m}(R)=-\frac{m-1}{12m(m+2)}\Bigl(3Rmc^{3}+Rmc-9Rc^{3}-7Rc-3mc^{2}+9c^{2}+4\Bigr).

Let
R=R_{m}
. By Proposition
3.5
,
R_{m}
is the unique solution of

1+(m-1)R\cot R=0,

hence

R_{m}\cot R_{m}=-\frac{1}{m-1},\qquad\cot R_{m}=-\frac{1}{(m-1)R_{m}}.

Substituting
c=-\frac{1}{(m-1)R_{m}}
into the above formula for
h_{m}(R)
and simplifying gives

h_{m}(R_{m})=-\frac{R_{m}^{2}(m-1)^{2}(m+1)-m(m-3)}{4R_{m}^{2}m(m-1)^{2}(m+2)}.

We now show that the numerator is strictly positive. Since
R_{m}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
by Proposition
3.5
, we have

R_{m}^{2}>\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}.

Therefore,

R_{m}^{2}(m-1)^{2}(m+1)-m(m-3)>\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}(m-1)^{2}(m+1)-m(m-3).

Using
\pi^{2}>9
, we obtain

\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}(m-1)^{2}(m+1)-m(m-3)>\frac{9}{4}(m-1)^{2}(m+1)-m(m-3).

The right-hand side is a cubic polynomial in
m
with positive leading coefficient, and a direct check shows it is strictly positive for all
m\geq 4
. Hence

h_{m}(R_{m})<0.

By Proposition
3.7
,
h_{m}
has a first zero
S_{m}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
, and by definition one has

h_{m}(R)<0\quad\forall R\in\left(\frac{\pi}{2},S_{m}\right).

Since
R_{m}>\pi/2
and
h_{m}(R_{m})<0
, it follows that
R_{m}<S_{m}
.
∎

Theorem 3.15

(
Semi-tight support characterization under rotational symmetry
)
.

Let

is not smeary.

More precisely:

is strictly positive definite;

(b)

(iii)

For every

so that

constructs a rotationally symmetric

, smeariness is possible in the high-dimensional regime covered by Theorem
3.12

Proof.

Parts (i)(a) and (ii)(a) follow from Theorem
3.10
.

For parts (i)(b) and (ii)(b), write

and let

be the radial density. By Proposition

If

If

∎

4.
The case of densities not necessarily rotationally symmetric

4.1.
Setup for a general density

We now drop rotational symmetry for the measure
\mu
and keep the normal–coordinate framework of

\mu
.
Let
X
be an
\mathbb{S}^{m}

(not necessarily rotationally symmetric), and let

denote its law. Without loss of generality, assume that it has a local unique Fréchet mean at

As before in Section

to

Assume that

This reduces to (
5
) in Section

Define the

in normal coordinates by

\widetilde{F}(u):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[d^{2}(\exp_{N}u,X)\right]=\int_{B(0,\pi)}d^{2}(\exp_{N}u,\exp_{N}v)\,d\tilde{\mu}(v)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}g(u;R\Theta)\,\rho(R,\Theta)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR,

as in (

) in Section

Assume that the density

like in Section

Hessian formula (general density).

The Hessian
H:=D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)
of the lifted Fréchet function at the Fréchet mean is given by

(33)

H=2\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Big(\frac{R}{\tan R}\,I_{m}+\Big(1-\frac{R}{\tan R}\Big)\,\Theta\Theta^{\top}\Big)\,\rho(R,\Theta)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

Proof.

Let
H:=D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)
. For

\psi(t):=\widetilde{F}(tw).

By definition of the Hessian,

w^{\top}Hw=\psi^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\widetilde{F}(tw)\Big|_{t=0}.

By (

\widetilde{F}(tw)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}g(tw;R\Theta)\,\rho(R,\Theta)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

By Assumption
3.1
, differentiation at
u=0

under the integral sign is justified up to order

w^{\top}Hw=\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}g(tw;R\Theta)\Big|_{t=0}\,\rho(R,\Theta)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

By the second–order expansion of

(equivalently, the

Substituting this into the previous display yields

(34)

w^{\top}Hw=2\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Big(\frac{R}{\tan R}\,\|w\|^{2}+\Big(1-\frac{R}{\tan R}\Big)\,w^{\top}(\Theta\Theta^{\top})w\Big)\rho(R,\Theta)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR,

which proves (

∎

Next we prove a generalization of the Theorem

, but in our general setup. We note that in this case, if the support of the probability measure have a hemispherical support, then the Hessian still remains positive definite, preventing smeariness, but if it leaks past the hemisphere by a small amount, the Hessian cannot be identically zero, thereby preventing smeariness
again
, but this time, the Hessian can be singular, thereby directional smeariness can still occur.

Theorem 4.1

(
Hemispherical obstruction for smeariness and directional smeariness at a unique local Fréchet mean
)
.

In the setup of Section
4.1
, assume that the support of
\mu
is contained in the closed ball

\overline{B}(0;\pi/2)\subset T_{N}\mathbb{S}^{m}
, i.e.

\rho(R,\Theta)=0\quad\text{for a.e.\ }(R,\Theta)\text{ with }R>\pi/2.

Then
D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)
is positive definite. In particular,
\widetilde{F}
cannot be smeary or directionally smeary at
u=0

(equivalently,
N
cannot be a
2
-smeary local Fréchet mean). Equivalently, if
\det(D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0))=0
,
then necessarily

\mu\bigl(\{v\in B(0,\pi):\|v\|>\pi/2\}\bigr)>0,

i.e. the support of
\mu
is not contained in
\overline{B}(0,\pi/2)
.

Moreover.

Let
b_{m}(R):=1+(m-1)R\cot\,R
and let
R_{m}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
denote its unique zero (cf. Proposition
3.5
).
If
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\subset\overline{B}(0,R_{m})
, then
D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)\neq 0
.
In particular, if
D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)=0
, then necessarily

\mu\bigl(\{v\in B(0,\pi):\|v\|>R_{m}\}\bigr)>0.

Proof.

Let
H:=D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)
and fix
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
If
\rho(R,\Theta)
is supported in
R\leq\pi/2
, then
\tan R>0
on the support and hence

\frac{R}{\tan R}\geq 0
there. Moreover,

w^{\top}(\Theta\Theta^{\top})w=\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{2}\geq 0
.
Therefore the integrand in Equation (34) satisfies

\frac{R}{\tan R}\|w\|^{2}+\Bigl(1-\frac{R}{\tan R}\Bigr)\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{2}\geq 0\quad\text{for a.e.\ }(R,\Theta)\text{ in the support.}

Since
\rho\geq 0
and is positive on a set of positive
(\sin R)^{m-1}d\Theta dR
-measure,
it follows that
w^{\top}Hw>0
for all
w\neq 0
, i.e.
H
is positive definite.
The final implication follows by contraposition.

Moreover.

Taking traces in the Hessian identity (
33
) yields

\operatorname{tr}(H)=2\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Bigl(m\frac{R}{\tan R}+\Bigl(1-\frac{R}{\tan R}\Bigr)\Bigr)\,\rho(R,\Theta)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

Since

m\frac{R}{\tan R}+\Bigl(1-\frac{R}{\tan R}\Bigr)=1+(m-1)\frac{R}{\tan R}=:b_{m}(R)\text{ (See Proposition }\ref{prop:zero_bm})

we obtain

\operatorname{tr}(H)=2\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}b_{m}(R)\,\rho(R,\Theta)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

The function
b_{m}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\pi)
, satisfies
b_{m}(\pi/2)=1>0
,
and has a unique zero
R_{m}>\pi/2
. Hence
b_{m}(R)>0
for all
R<R_{m}
.
If
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\subset\overline{B}(0,R_{m})
, then
b_{m}(R)\geq 0

on the support and is strictly positive on a set of positive measure.
Therefore
\operatorname{tr}(H)>0
, so
H\neq 0
.
The final implication again follows by contraposition.
∎

Remark 4.2

(
Uniform lower bound on the Hessian eigenvalues under hemispherical support away from the equator
)
.

Let
H:=D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)
denote the Hessian of the pulled-back Fréchet function.
Using the general formula (cf. Equation (
33
)), if
\operatorname{supp}\rho\subset\{R\leq\pi/2\},\tfrac{R}{\tan R}\geq 0
and

1-\tfrac{R}{\tan R}\geq 0
, and therefore

w^{\top}Hw\;\geq\;2\|w\|^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\tfrac{R}{\tan R}\,\rho(R,\Theta)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

In particular, the smallest eigenvalue of
H:=D^{2}\tilde{F}(0)
satisfies

\lambda_{\min}(H)\;\geq\;2\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\tfrac{R}{\tan R}\,\rho(R,\Theta)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR.

Note, however, that
\tfrac{R}{\tan R}\downarrow 0
as
R\uparrow\pi/2
, so this bound
need not be uniform w.r.t. the measure if the support of
\rho
includes the set
\{R=\pi/2\}
.
If
instead
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\operatorname{supp}\rho\subset\{R\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon\}
, then

\tfrac{R}{\tan R}\geq c_{\varepsilon}:=\inf_{0\leq R\leq\pi/2-\varepsilon}\tfrac{R}{\tan R}>0,

and hence

w^{\top}Hw\geq 2c_{\varepsilon}\|w\|^{2},\qquad\lambda_{\min}(H)\geq 2c_{\varepsilon}>0.

4.2.
Super-hemispherical support and directional smeariness

Theorem
4.1
showed us that while the support lying in the closed ball of radius
R_{m}
prevents smeariness, it does not prevent
directional smeariness
. The goal of this section is to construct examples of directionally
2
-smeary random variables on
\mathbb{S}^{m}
for any
m\geq 2
whose support are bigger than
B(N;\pi/2)
but the difference with is
B(N;\pi/2)
arbitrary smaller. We start with a lemma and two propositions.

Lemma 4.3

(
Existence of an even continuous density with diagonal second-moment matrix
)
.

Fix
m\geq 2
and
e_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
\sigma
denote the surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
and let
\eta>0
.
Then there exists a continuous density
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\to(0,\infty)
with
\varphi(\Theta)=\varphi(-\Theta)
and

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\varphi\,d\sigma=1
such that the second-moment matrix

\Sigma:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta\Theta^{\top}\,\varphi(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta)

is diagonal in the orthonormal basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
:

\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{\parallel},\lambda_{\perp},\ldots,\lambda_{\perp}),\qquad 0<\lambda_{\parallel}<1,\quad 0<\lambda_{\perp}<\eta,\quad\lambda_{\parallel}+(m-1)\lambda_{\perp}=1.

In particular,

\Sigma e_{1}=\lambda_{\parallel}e_{1},\qquad\Sigma x=\lambda_{\perp}x\quad\text{for all }x\perp e_{1}.

Furthermore, one can choose
\phi
so that
\lambda_{\parallel},\lambda_{\perp}
are arbitrarily close to
1,0
respectively.

Proof.

For
\kappa>0
, define

\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta):=\frac{e^{\kappa\Theta_{1}^{2}}}{Z_{\kappa}},\qquad Z_{\kappa}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}e^{\kappa\Theta_{1}^{2}}\,d\sigma(\Theta),\qquad\Theta=(\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{m}).

Then
\varphi_{\kappa}
is continuous, strictly positive, even, and normalized. Set

\Sigma_{\kappa}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta\Theta^{\top}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta).

We show that all off-diagonal entries vanish. Fix
i\neq j
. Let
S_{j}:\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\to\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
be the reflection

S_{j}(\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{j},\ldots,\Theta_{m})=(\Theta_{1},\ldots,-\Theta_{j},\ldots,\Theta_{m})
. Then
S_{j}
preserves
\sigma
, and

\varphi_{\kappa}\circ S_{j}=\varphi_{\kappa}
since
\varphi_{\kappa}
depends only on
\Theta_{1}^{2}
. Moreover,

(\Theta_{i}\Theta_{j})\circ S_{j}=-\Theta_{i}\Theta_{j}
. Therefore

(\Sigma_{\kappa})_{ij}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta_{i}\Theta_{j}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}(\Theta_{i}\Theta_{j}\circ S_{j})(\varphi_{\kappa}\circ S_{j})\,d\sigma=-(\Sigma_{\kappa})_{ij},

hence
(\Sigma_{\kappa})_{ij}=0
. Thus
\Sigma_{\kappa}
is diagonal.

Next, since
\varphi_{\kappa}
is invariant under permutations of
\Theta_{2},\ldots,\Theta_{m}
, the diagonal entries

(\Sigma_{\kappa})_{22},\ldots,(\Sigma_{\kappa})_{mm}
are equal. Set

\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}:=(\Sigma_{\kappa})_{11}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta_{1}^{2}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta),\qquad\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}:=(\Sigma_{\kappa})_{22}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta_{2}^{2}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta),

so that

\Sigma_{\kappa}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa},\lambda_{\perp,\kappa},\ldots,\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}).

Since
\Theta_{1}^{2},\Theta_{2}^{2}\in[0,1]
and
\varphi_{\kappa}>0
with
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\varphi_{\kappa}\,d\sigma=1
, we have

0<\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}<1
and
0<\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}<1
. Also, because
\operatorname{tr}(\Theta\Theta^{\top})=\|\Theta\|^{2}=1
on

\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
,

\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}+(m-1)\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{\kappa})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\operatorname{tr}(\Theta\Theta^{\top})\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\varphi_{\kappa}\,d\sigma=1.

Proof of the ’
furthermore
’ part:
Here we show that as
\kappa\to\infty,\lambda_{\parallel},\lambda_{\perp}
concentrates near
1,0
respectively.
Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Let
A_{\delta}:=\{\Theta\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}:\Theta_{1}^{2}\geq 1-\delta\}
, then

\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}\geq(1-\delta)\int_{A_{\delta}}\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta)=(1-\delta)\Bigl(1-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\setminus A_{\delta}}\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta)\Bigr).

Now on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\setminus A_{\delta}
one has
\Theta_{1}^{2}\leq 1-\delta
, so

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\setminus A_{\delta}}e^{\kappa\Theta_{1}^{2}}\,d\sigma(\Theta)\leq e^{\kappa(1-\delta)}\,\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{m-1}).

Also, if
A_{\delta/2}:=\{\Theta\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}:\Theta_{1}^{2}\geq 1-\delta/2\}
, then
\sigma(A_{\delta/2})>0
and

Z_{\kappa}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}e^{\kappa\Theta_{1}^{2}}\,d\sigma(\Theta)\geq\int_{A_{\delta/2}}e^{\kappa\Theta_{1}^{2}}\,d\sigma(\Theta)\geq e^{\kappa(1-\delta/2)}\,\sigma(A_{\delta/2}).

Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\setminus A_{\delta}}\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\sigma(\Theta)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\setminus A_{\delta}}e^{\kappa\Theta_{1}^{2}}\,d\sigma(\Theta)}{Z_{\kappa}}\leq\frac{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})}{\sigma(A_{\delta/2})}\,e^{-\kappa\delta/2}\longrightarrow 0\,\,\text{as }\kappa\to\infty.

Hence
\liminf_{\kappa\to\infty}\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}\geq 1-\delta
. Since
\delta\in(0,1)
was arbitrary and
\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}\leq 1
, it follows that
\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}\to 1
. The trace identity

\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}+(m-1)\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}=1
then gives
\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}\to 0
. Thus we may choose

\kappa
so large that
0<\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}<\eta
. Setting
\varphi:=\varphi_{\kappa}
,

\lambda_{\parallel}:=\lambda_{\parallel,\kappa}
and
\lambda_{\perp}:=\lambda_{\perp,\kappa}
gives the claim.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(
Existence of an absolutely continuous directional smeary measure inside
B(N;\frac{\pi}{2}+\varepsilon)
with rank one Hessian
)
.

Fix
m\geq 2
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
N\in\mathbb{S}^{m}
be the north pole.
Then there exists an absolutely continuous probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{S}^{m}
that is directionally smeary at
N

whose support satisfies

\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\subset B(N;\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\varepsilon),

such that the lifted Fréchet function

\widetilde{F}(u)=\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\exp_{N}u,X)]

has vanishing gradient at
u=0
and its Hessian

H:=D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)

is positive semidefinite with

\operatorname{rank}(H)=1.

Proof.

Write normal coordinates
v=R\Theta\in B(0,\pi)\subset T_{N}\mathbb{S}^{m}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}

with
R\in(0,\pi)
and
\Theta\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, and set

a(R):=\frac{R}{\tan R},\qquad w_{m}(R):=(\sin R)^{m-1}.

Let
\varphi
be the density on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
as in Lemma
4.3
, so that its second moment matrix

\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{\parallel},\lambda_{\perp},\dots,\lambda_{\perp}),\qquad 0<\lambda_{\perp}<\lambda_{\parallel}<1.

Choose radii

R_{+}\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}),\qquad R_{-}\in(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\varepsilon).

Choose
g_{+}
supported in a small neighborhood of
R_{+}
and

g_{-}
supported in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
R_{-}
,
with

\int_{0}^{\pi}g_{\pm}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR=1.

Since
a(R_{+})>0
and
a(R_{-})<0
, we may arrange

\int_{0}^{\pi/2+\epsilon}a(R)g_{+}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR>0,\qquad\int_{0}^{\pi/2+\epsilon}a(R)g_{-}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR<0.

For
p\in[0,1]
set
g_{p}:=pg_{+}+(1-p)g_{-}
and define

\rho_{p}(R,\Theta)=g_{p}(R)\varphi(\Theta).

Let
X=\exp_{N}(R\Theta)
have law
\mu_{p}
.

Because
\varphi
is even,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta\varphi(\Theta)\,d\Theta=0,

so
\nabla\widetilde{F}(0)=0
, proving that
N
is a critical point of the Fréchet function. Next we analyze its Hessian.

The Hessian satisfies

H=2\big(s(p)I_{m}+(1-s(p))\Sigma\big),

where

s(p)=\int_{0}^{\pi}a(R)g_{p}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR.

Note that
s(p)<1
because
\frac{R}{\tan\,R}<1\forall R\in(0,\pi).
Moreover
s(1)>0
since
\frac{R}{\tan\,R}>0
on
(0,\pi/2)
, and
g_{1}=g_{+}
, supported on
(0,\pi/2)
. The eigenvalues of the Hessian
H
are
2\mu_{\parallel}(p),2\mu_{\perp}(p)
where

\mu_{\parallel}(p)=s(p)+(1-s(p))\lambda_{\parallel},\qquad\mu_{\perp}(p)=s(p)+(1-s(p))\lambda_{\perp}=\lambda_{\perp}+s(p)(1-\lambda_{\perp}).

Since
a(R_{-})=R_{-}\cot R_{-}<0
and
a
is continuous, there is an open interval

I_{-}\subset\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}+\varepsilon\right)
containing
R_{-}
such that

a(R)<\frac{1}{2}a(R_{-})<0
for all
R\in I_{-}
. Choose
g_{-}
supported in
I_{-}
and normalized by

\int_{0}^{\pi}g_{-}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR=1
. Then

s(0)=\int_{0}^{\pi}a(R)g_{-}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR\leq\sup_{R\in I_{-}}a(R)<\frac{1}{2}a(R_{-})<0.

Next choose the (angular) density
\varphi
in Lemma
4.3
so that
\lambda_{\perp}>0
is so small that

0<\lambda_{\perp}<-\frac{s(0)}{1-s(0)}.

This is possible because, for fixed
m
, Lemma
4.3
allows
\lambda_{\perp}
to be arbitrarily small and positive by taking
\kappa
sufficiently large. Since

\mu_{\perp}(0)=\lambda_{\perp}+s(0)(1-\lambda_{\perp}),

the above inequality is equivalent to
\mu_{\perp}(0)<0
.

On the other hand,

\mu_{\perp}(1)=\lambda_{\perp}+s(1)(1-\lambda_{\perp})>0

because
s(1)>0
and
0<\lambda_{\perp}<1
. Hence, by continuity, there exists
p^{*}\in(0,1)
such that

\mu_{\perp}(p^{*})=0
.

For this
p^{*}
we have

s(p^{*})=-\frac{\lambda_{\perp}}{1-\lambda_{\perp}}<0,

so in particular
1-s(p^{*})>0
. Therefore

\mu_{\parallel}(p^{*})=s(p^{*})+(1-s(p^{*}))\lambda_{\parallel}=\frac{\lambda_{\parallel}-\lambda_{\perp}}{1-\lambda_{\perp}}>0\because\lambda_{\parallel}>\lambda_{\perp}.

Thus
H
is positive-semidefinite and
\operatorname{rank}(H)=1
.

Finally note that by construction of the density
\rho_{p^{*}},

\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{p^{\ast}})\subset B(N;\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\varepsilon).

∎

Proposition 4.5

(
Vanishing of the third and strict positivity of the fourth derivative tensor
)
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\mu
be the probability measure constructed in Proposition
4.4
, whose angular density satisfies Lemma
4.3
. Let

\widetilde{F}(u)=\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\exp_{N}u,X)]

denote the lifted Fréchet function at
N
in normal coordinates.

Then the third derivative tensor
D^{3}\widetilde{F}(0)
vanishes, and for suitable choices of
g_{\pm}
in the density used in Proposition
4.4
, the fourth derivative tensor

D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)

is strictly positive definite as a symmetric
(4,0)
-tensor on
(e_{1})^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
, i.e.

D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)[v,v,v,v]>0,v\in(e_{1})^{\perp}\setminus\{0\},e_{1}:=(1,0,\dots 0)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Proof.

Let
V:=e_{1}^{\perp}
. By Proposition
4.4
, the Hessian

H=D^{2}\widetilde{F}(0)
is positive semidefinite of rank
1
and satisfies
\ker(H)=V
.

Write
v=R\Theta
with
R\in(0,\pi)
and
\Theta\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
. Since differentiation under the integral sign is justified, (cf. Assumption
3.1
), for every
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
k=3,4
,

D^{k}\widetilde{F}(0)[w,\ldots,w]=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}D_{u}^{k}g(0;R\Theta)[w,\ldots,w]\,g_{p^{\ast}}(R)\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,w_{m}(R)\,d\Theta\,dR.

Step 1: vanishing of the third derivative tensor.

From the cubic part of the Taylor expansion of
g(u;v)
(cf. Equation (
4
)) at
u=0
,

D_{u}^{3}g(0;R\Theta)[w,w,w]=6\Bigl(A_{3}(R)\,\langle w,R\Theta\rangle\,\|w\|^{2}+B_{3}(R)\,\langle w,R\Theta\rangle^{3}\Bigr).

Since
\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)
is even, i.e.
\varphi_{\kappa}(-\Theta)=\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)
, while

\langle w,\Theta\rangle
and
\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{3}
are odd in
\Theta
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\Theta\rangle\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta=0,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{3}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta=0.

Hence
D^{3}\widetilde{F}(0)[w,w,w]=0
for every
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Since
D^{3}\widetilde{F}(0)
is
symmetric, the polarization identity implies that
D^{3}\widetilde{F}(0)\equiv 0
.

Step 2: reduction of the fourth derivative to a radial integral.

Fix now
w\in V
. By the quartic part of the Taylor expansion of
g(u;v)
(cf. Equation (
4
)) at
u=0
,

D_{u}^{4}g(0;R\Theta)[w,w,w,w]=24\Bigl(A_{0}(R)\|w\|^{4}+A_{2}(R)R^{2}\|w\|^{2}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{2}+A_{4}(R)R^{4}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{4}\Bigr).

Therefore

D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)[w,w,w,w]=24\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Bigl(A_{0}(R)\|w\|^{4}+A_{2}(R)R^{2}\|w\|^{2}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{2}+A_{4}(R)R^{4}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{4}\Bigr)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,w_{m}(R)\,d\Theta\,dR.

Since
\Sigma=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta\Theta^{\mathsf{T}}\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta
has
eigenvalue
\lambda_{\perp}
on
V=e_{1}^{\perp}
, we obtain for every
w\in V

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{2}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta=\langle\Sigma w,w\rangle=\lambda_{\perp}\|w\|^{2}.

Note that, in the present situation the density
\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)
depends only on

\Theta_{1}^{2}
, and hence it is invariant under every orthogonal transformation
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
fixing
e_{1}
. In particular the map

w\longmapsto\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{4}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta

is invariant under the orthogonal group of
V=e_{1}^{\perp}
. Consequently there
exists a constant
c_{4}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{4}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta=c_{4}\|w\|^{4}\qquad\text{for every }w\in V.

Evaluating this identity at
w=e_{2}
gives

c_{4}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta_{2}^{4}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta=\beta_{\perp}.

Hence

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{4}\,\varphi_{\kappa}(\Theta)\,d\Theta=\beta_{\perp}\|w\|^{4}\qquad\text{for every }w\in V.

Substituting the last two identities into the expression for

D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)[w,w,w,w]
, we obtain

D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)[w,w,w,w]=24\|w\|^{4}\int_{0}^{\pi}\Bigl(A_{0}(R)+\lambda_{\perp}A_{2}(R)R^{2}+\beta_{\perp}A_{4}(R)R^{4}\Bigr)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)\,w_{m}(R)\,dR.

Thus the question of strict positivity of the fourth derivative above reduces to the sign of the scalar
integral

\int_{0}^{\pi}\Bigl(A_{0}(R)+\lambda_{\perp}A_{2}(R)R^{2}+\beta_{\perp}A_{4}(R)R^{4}\Bigr)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)\,w_{m}(R)\,dR.

If this integral is strictly positive, then

D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)[w,w,w,w]>0\qquad\text{for every }w\in V\setminus\{0\}.

Step 3: Positivity of the radial integral for suitable choices of
g_{+}
and
g_{-}
in Proposition
4.4
.

Set

I:=\int_{0}^{\pi}\Bigl(A_{0}(R)+\lambda_{\perp}A_{2}(R)R^{2}+\beta_{\perp}A_{4}(R)R^{4}\Bigr)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)\,w_{m}(R)\,dR.

By Step 2, it remains to show that
I>0
.

Choose
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
so small that

I_{\delta}:=\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta\right]\subset(0,\pi).

Choose
g_{+},g_{-}
as in Proposition
4.4
so that

\operatorname{supp}(g_{+})\subset\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}\right],\qquad\operatorname{supp}(g_{-})\subset\left[\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta\right],

and

\int_{0}^{\pi}g_{\pm}(R)\,w_{m}(R)\,dR=1.

Then

g_{p^{\ast}}=p^{\ast}g_{+}+(1-p^{\ast})g_{-},\qquad\operatorname{supp}(g_{p^{\ast}})\subset I_{\delta},\qquad\int_{0}^{\pi}g_{p^{\ast}}(R)\,w_{m}(R)\,dR=1,

and moreover

\left|R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right|\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\qquad\text{for every }R\in\operatorname{supp}(g_{p^{\ast}}).

Write
t:=R-\frac{\pi}{2}
. By the expansion of
A_{0}
near
R=\frac{\pi}{2}
proved earlier (cf. Lemma
3.6
), one has

A_{0}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+t\right)=\frac{\pi}{24}t+\frac{1}{3}t^{2}+O(t^{3})\qquad\text{as }t\to 0.

Also, since
a(R)=R\cot R
and
\tan t=t+O(t^{3})
near
t=0
,

a\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+t\right)=\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+t\right)\cot\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+t\right)=-\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+t\right)\tan t=-\frac{\pi}{2}t-t^{2}+O(t^{3}).

Hence

A_{0}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+t\right)+\frac{1}{12}a\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+t\right)=\frac{1}{4}t^{2}+O(t^{3}).

Therefore there exist
\delta_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon)
and
C_{0}>0
such that

\left|A_{0}(R)+\frac{1}{12}a(R)-\frac{1}{4}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}\right|\leq C_{0}\left|R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right|^{3}

for every
R\in\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta_{0},\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta_{0}\right]
.
Shrinking
\delta
further if necessary, we may assume
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
and

C_{0}\delta\leq\frac{1}{8}.

Then for every
R\in I_{\delta}
,

\left|R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right|\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{8C_{0}}\implies C_{0}\left|R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right|^{3}\leq\frac{1}{8}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2},

and so

A_{0}(R)+\frac{1}{12}a(R)\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{8}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{8}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}.

Equivalently,

(35)

A_{0}(R)\geq-\frac{1}{12}a(R)+\frac{1}{8}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}\qquad\text{for every }R\in I_{\delta}.

Next, the functions
R\mapsto A_{2}(R)R^{2}
and
R\mapsto A_{4}(R)R^{4}
are continuous at

R=\frac{\pi}{2}
, hence bounded on
I_{\delta}
. Thus there exists
M>0
such that

(36)

|A_{2}(R)R^{2}|+|A_{4}(R)R^{4}|\leq M\qquad\text{for every }R\in I_{\delta}.

Using (
35
), (
36
), and

\operatorname{supp}(g_{p^{\ast}})\subset I_{\delta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\pi}A_{0}(R)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR-\lambda_{\perp}M-\beta_{\perp}M

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{12}\int_{0}^{\pi}a(R)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR+\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}g_{p^{\ast}}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR-\lambda_{\perp}M-\beta_{\perp}M.

Since
p^{\ast}
is chosen in Proposition
4.4
so that

, one has

\lambda_{\perp}+s(p^{\ast})(1-\lambda_{\perp})=0,\qquad s(p^{\ast}):=\int_{0}^{\pi}a(R)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR,

and therefore

s(p^{\ast})=-\frac{\lambda_{\perp}}{1-\lambda_{\perp}}.

Hence

-\frac{1}{12}\int_{0}^{\pi}a(R)g_{p^{\ast}}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR=\frac{1}{12}\frac{\lambda_{\perp}}{1-\lambda_{\perp}}\geq 0.

Also, since
\left|R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right|\geq\frac{\delta}{2}
on
\operatorname{supp}(g_{p^{\ast}})
and

\int_{0}^{\pi}g_{p^{\ast}}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR=1
, we have

\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(R-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}g_{p^{\ast}}(R)w_{m}(R)\,dR\geq\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}.

Therefore

I\geq\frac{\delta^{2}}{32}-\lambda_{\perp}M-\beta_{\perp}M.

Finally, by Lemma
4.3
, we may choose
\kappa
so large that

\lambda_{\perp}>0
is arbitrarily small. Moreover, since
0\leq\Theta_{2}^{4}\leq\Theta_{2}^{2}
on

\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, one has

0\leq\beta_{\perp}\leq\lambda_{\perp}.

Hence, after increasing
\kappa
further if necessary, we may assume

2M\lambda_{\perp}<\frac{\delta^{2}}{64}.

Then

I>\frac{\delta^{2}}{32}-2M\lambda_{\perp}>\frac{\delta^{2}}{64}>0.

Substituting this into the identity obtained in Step 2 yields

D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)[w,w,w,w]=24\|w\|^{4}I>0\qquad\text{for every }w\in V\setminus\{0\}.

Thus the quartic form associated with
D^{4}\widetilde{F}(0)\big|_{V}
is strictly positive, i.e. the fourth derivative tensor is strictly positive definite on
V=e_{1}^{\perp}
.
∎

4.3.
Hemispherical support and finite sample smeariness

We start with two technical lemmas.

Lemma 4.6

(
Convergence of second moments under a uniform
(2+\delta)

Let

(2)

The second condition ensures uniform integrability condition for

. For the definition of uniform intgrability and for a proof of the lemma, see

.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The above lemma is the basis for the Assumption
4.11
.

Lemma 4.8

(
Strict spectral upper bound of Hessian
)
.

Let
\mu
be absolutely continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{m}
,
m\geq 2
, with unique Fréchet mean assumed to be
N
without loss of generality. Assume
\mu

. Let the Hessian

Let

\|\Theta\|=1

\langle w,\Theta\rangle^{2}\leq\|w\|^{2}\|\Theta\|^{2}=1

(37)

\displaystyle w^{\top}Mw

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Big(a(R)+(1-a(R))\cdot 1\Big)\,\rho(R,\Theta)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,d\Theta\,dR

\lambda_{\max}(M)<1

∎

Remark 4.13

(
On the necessity of the moment condition
)
.

The
(2+\delta)
–moment assumption

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\|\sqrt{n}\,U_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\bigr]<\infty,\qquad U_{n}:=\exp_{N}^{-1}(\widehat{\mu}_{n})

is not merely technical. Thanks to the Lemma
4.6
, it ensures
uniform integrability
of

\{\|\sqrt{n}\,U_{n}\|^{2}\}_{n\geq 1}
and is therefore required in order to
upgrade convergence in distribution from the non–smeary CLT

\sqrt{n}\,U_{n}\;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\;\mathcal{N}(0,4H^{-1}\Sigma H^{-1})

to convergence of their second moments:

n\,\mathbb{E}\|U_{n}\|^{2}\longrightarrow\operatorname{trace}\!\bigl(4H^{-1}\Sigma H^{-1}\bigr).

Convergence in distribution alone does
not
imply convergence of second moments.
The additional moment bound guarantees uniform integrability, which is the
standard sufficient condition for passing expectations to the limit. In particular, this condition is also implicitly required in the final step of the proof of Theorem 8 in
12
,
where convergence of scaled second moments is deduced from a CLT. Without such a moment bound, that passage is not justified in general. Moreover, in
12
, in Theorem 6, the exact same condition is required to pass to the convergence of the second moments, right after Equation (4) in that paper. Note that it is proven in
11
that
\sqrt{n}U_{n}
is tight, which implies
n\,\mathbb{E}\|U_{n}\|^{2}
is also tight. But in the proof, we need something stronger: namely convergence of the first moments of
n\,\mathbb{E}\|U_{n}\|^{2}
.

Proof.

Note that by hemispherical obstruction theorem (cf. Theorem
4.1
), no smeariness or directional smeariness occur and
H
is invertible, so we have cf.
11
, Theorem 2.11, P.22,

\sqrt{n}U_{n}\to_{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{N}(0,4H^{-1}\Sigma H^{-1})

Next, the moment assumption
4.11
and Lemma
4.6
together imply

n\,\mathbb{E}\|U_{n}\|^{2}\longrightarrow\operatorname{trace}\!\bigl(4H^{-1}\Sigma H^{-1}\bigr)=4\,\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma H^{-2}).

Since

\lambda_{\max}(H)I_{m}\succeq H\implies H^{-2}\succeq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(H)^{2}}I_{m},

and
\Sigma\succeq 0
, we obtain

\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma H^{-2})\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(H)^{2}}\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma).

By Lemma
4.8
,

\lambda_{\max}(H)<2
. Hence, the limiting modulation
m_{\infty}
satisfies:

m_{\infty}=\frac{\operatorname{trace}\!\bigl(4H^{-1}\Sigma H^{-1}\bigr)}{\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma)}\geq\frac{4}{\lambda_{\max}(H)^{2}}>1.

implying Type I finite sample smeariness.

∎

Remark 4.14

(

)
.

Fix

Let

on

4.4.
Curse of dimensionality on modulation of non-smeary random variables

This section shows that for non-smeary densities supported within the
closed
geodesic balls of radius
\pi/2
around the local unique Fréchet means, the modulation
may
increase monotonically with dimensions, and the limit can be very large. We start with the following definition.

Definition 4.15

(
Dimensionally comparable rotationally symmetric family
)
.

Let
N
denote the north pole. Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and set
R_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon
. For each
m\geq 2
, let
\mu^{(m)}
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{m}
, rotationally symmetric about
N
, with

\operatorname{supp}\mu^{(m)}\subset B(N;R_{\varepsilon}).

We say that
\{\mu^{(m)}\}_{m\geq 2}
is a
dimensionally comparable rotationally symmetric family
if there exists a measurable function

w:[0,R_{\varepsilon}]\to[0,\infty)

such that, for every
m\geq 2
,

0<Z_{m}:=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,dR<\infty,

and in normal polar coordinates
v=R\Theta\in T_{N}\mathbb{S}^{m}
the density of
\mu^{(m)}
with respect to polar volume measure has the form

\rho^{(m)}(R,\Theta)=\rho_{m}(R)\,\varphi_{m}(\Theta),\qquad\rho_{m}(R):=\frac{w(R)}{Z_{m}},

where
\varphi_{m}
is the uniform probability density on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
. Equivalently, the induced radial probability measure on
[0,R_{\varepsilon}]
is

\nu_{m}(dR)=\frac{w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}}{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(U)(\sin U)^{m-1}\,dU}\,dR.

Thus the family is generated by a
single dimension-independent unnormalized radial weight

w
, while the dependence on
m
enters
only
through the Jacobian factor
(\sin R)^{m-1}
and the normalizing constant
Z_{m}
.

Example 4.16
.

The following are dimensionally comparable rotationally symmetric families.

(1)

Uniform law on the truncated geodesic ball.

Take

w(R)\equiv 1.

Then

\nu_{m}(dR)=\frac{(\sin R)^{m-1}}{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(\sin U)^{m-1}\,dU}\,dR.

(2)

Truncated von Mises–Fisher family with fixed concentration parameter
\kappa
.

Since the von Mises–Fisher density is proportional to
e^{\kappa N^{\top}x}
and
N^{\top}x=\cos R,R:=d(N,x)
, take

w(R)=e^{\kappa\cos R}.

Then

\nu_{m}(dR)=\frac{e^{\kappa\cos R}(\sin R)^{m-1}}{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}e^{\kappa\cos U}(\sin U)^{m-1}\,dU}\,dR.

See
14
. That
N
is the Fréchet mean follows from rotational symmetry, implying
N
is a critical point and th support condition.

(3)

Truncated Watson family with fixed concentration parameter
\kappa
.

Since the Watson density is proportional to
e^{\kappa(N^{\top}x)^{2}}
and
(N^{\top}x)^{2}=\cos^{2}R
, take

w(R)=e^{\kappa\cos^{2}R}.

Then

\nu_{m}(dR)=\frac{e^{\kappa\cos^{2}R}(\sin R)^{m-1}}{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}e^{\kappa\cos^{2}U}(\sin U)^{m-1}\,dU}\,dR.

See
17
;
15
.

(4)

General truncated zonal family with fixed profile.

More generally, if
g:[-1,1]\to[0,\infty)
is measurable, then

w(R)=g(\cos R)

defines a dimensionally comparable rotationally symmetric family. See
14
.

The following theorem proves that for the above families in Definition
4.15
.

Theorem 4.17

(
Dimension monotonicity of asymptotic modulation for dimensionally comparable rotationally symmetric family
)
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and set
R_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon
.
For each integer
m\geq 2
, let
\mu^{(m)}
be an absolutely continuous probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{m}

with locally unique intrinsic Fréchet mean
N
, and assume that
\{\mu^{(m)}\}_{m\geq 2}
is a dimensionally comparable rotationally symmetric family about
N
, supported in
B(N;R_{\varepsilon})
, in the sense of the preceding definition
4.15
.
Assume further that the moment condition
4.11
holds for each
m
.
Let
\mathbb{S}^{m}\ni X^{(m)}\sim\mu^{(m)}
, set
\Sigma_{m}:=\operatorname{Cov}[\exp_{N}^{-1}(X^{(m)})]
, and let
H_{m}:=D^{2}\widetilde{F}_{m}(0)
.
Define the dimension-indexed asymptotic modulations

m_{\infty}^{(m)}:=\frac{\operatorname{trace}\!\bigl(4H_{m}^{-1}\Sigma_{m}H_{m}^{-1}\bigr)}{\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma_{m})}.

Then:

(1)

The sequence
\bigl(m_{\infty}^{(m)}\bigr)_{m\geq 2}
is strictly increasing with dimension
m
:

m_{\infty}^{(m+1)}>m_{\infty}^{(m)}\quad(m\geq 2).

(2)

The limit
\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}m_{\infty}^{(m)}
exists and is finite.

(3)

If the common radial weight
w
from the definition satisfies, for every
\delta>0
,

\int_{R_{\varepsilon}-\delta}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)\,dR>0,

then

\lim_{m\to\infty}m_{\infty}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{\bigl(R_{\varepsilon}\cot R_{\varepsilon}\bigr)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\Bigl(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\cot\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\Bigr)^{2}}.

Proof.

Proof of (1):

By the definition of a dimensionally comparable rotationally symmetric family, there exists a measurable function
w:[0,R_{\varepsilon}]\to[0,\infty)
such that

Z_{m}:=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,dR\in(0,\infty),\qquad\rho_{m}(R)=\frac{w(R)}{Z_{m}},

and in normal coordinates
v=R\Theta\in T_{N}\mathbb{S}^{m}
the density has the form

\rho^{(m)}(R,\Theta)=\rho_{m}(R)\,\varphi_{m}(\Theta),

where
\varphi_{m}
is the constant uniform density on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
.

Rotational symmetry implies

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\Theta\Theta^{\top}\,\varphi_{m}(\Theta)\,d\Theta=\frac{1}{m}I_{m}.

By the Hessian identity for the lifted Fréchet function in the
rotationally symmetric case
(cf. Equation (
7
)),

H_{m}=\frac{2}{m}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\rho_{m}(R)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}\,b_{m}(R)\,dR\right)I_{m}=2\Bigl(s_{m}+(1-s_{m})\frac{1}{m}\Bigr)I_{m}=\lambda_{m}I_{m},

where

s_{m}:=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,\rho_{m}(R)\,(\sin R)^{m-1}\,dR=\frac{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,dR}{Z_{m}},\qquad\lambda_{m}:=2\Bigl(s_{m}+\frac{1-s_{m}}{m}\Bigr).

Since
R_{\varepsilon}<\frac{\pi}{2}
, Theorem
4.1
yields
H_{m}\succ 0
; hence the non-smeary CLT holds, and together with Assumption
4.11
this justifies passage to second moments. Therefore the asymptotic covariance equals
4H_{m}^{-1}\Sigma_{m}H_{m}^{-1}
, and since
H_{m}=\lambda_{m}I_{m}
, the asymptotic modulation becomes

m_{\infty}^{(m)}=\frac{\operatorname{trace}\!\bigl((4/\lambda_{m}^{2})\Sigma_{m}\bigr)}{\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma_{m})}=\frac{4}{\lambda_{m}^{2}}.

So it is clear that
m_{\infty}^{(m+1)}>m_{\infty}^{(m)}\iff\lambda_{m+1}<\lambda_{m}.

Next we prove that
s_{m+1}<s_{m}.
Define the probability measure
\nu_{m}
on
[0,R_{\varepsilon}]
by

\nu_{m}(dR):=\frac{w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}}{Z_{m}}\,dR,\qquad\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\nu_{m}(dR)=1.

Then

c_{m}:=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\sin R\,\nu_{m}(dR)=\frac{Z_{m+1}}{Z_{m}}>0,

and hence

\frac{\sin R}{c_{m}}\,\nu_{m}(dR)=\frac{w(R)(\sin R)^{m}}{Z_{m+1}}\,dR=\nu_{m+1}(dR).

Therefore

s_{m+1}=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,\nu_{m+1}(dR)=\frac{1}{c_{m}}\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\sin R\,\nu_{m}(dR).

Subtracting

s_{m}=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,\nu_{m}(dR)

gives

s_{m+1}-s_{m}=\frac{1}{c_{m}}\left(\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\sin R\,\nu_{m}(dR)-\Bigl(\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,\nu_{m}(dR)\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\sin R\,\nu_{m}(dR)\Bigr)\right).

Using the standard symmetrization identity,

\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\sin R\,\nu_{m}(dR)-\Bigl(\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,\nu_{m}(dR)\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\sin R\,\nu_{m}(dR)\Bigr)

=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\bigl(R\cot R-U\cot U\bigr)(\sin R-\sin U)\,\nu_{m}(dR)\,\nu_{m}(dU).

Now
R\mapsto R\cot R
is decreasing on
(0,\pi/2)
, while
R\mapsto\sin R
is increasing on
[0,R_{\varepsilon}]\subset[0,\pi/2)
. Therefore

\bigl(R\cot R-U\cot U\bigr)(\sin R-\sin U)\leq 0\qquad\text{for all }R,U\in[0,R_{\varepsilon}].

Hence

s_{m+1}-s_{m}\leq 0,

that is,

s_{m+1}\leq s_{m}.

Since
\nu_{m}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
[0,R_{\varepsilon}]
, it cannot be supported on a single point; hence the inequality is strict, so
s_{m+1}<s_{m}.

Next we show that
\lambda_{m+1}<\lambda_{m}.
Since
\lambda_{m}=\frac{2}{m}\bigl(1+(m-1)s_{m}\bigr)
, we have

\lambda_{m+1}<\lambda_{m}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\frac{1+ms_{m+1}}{m+1}<\frac{1+(m-1)s_{m}}{m}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;m\bigl(1+ms_{m+1}\bigr)<(m+1)\bigl(1+(m-1)s_{m}\bigr).

Expanding and canceling gives

m+m^{2}s_{m+1}<m+1+(m^{2}-1)s_{m}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;m^{2}(s_{m+1}-s_{m})<1-s_{m}.

Thus
s_{m+1}<s_{m}
implies
\lambda_{m+1}<\lambda_{m}
whenever
s_{m}<1
. In our setting
0\leq R\cot R<1
on
(0,R_{\varepsilon}]
and
[0,R_{\varepsilon}]\subset[0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, hence

0\leq s_{m}<1,

so indeed

s_{m+1}<s_{m}\Longrightarrow\lambda_{m+1}<\lambda_{m}.

Proof of (2):

Let

R_{*}:=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}\{R\in[0,R_{\varepsilon}]:w(R)>0\}\in(0,R_{\varepsilon}].

Then for every
\delta\in(0,R_{*})
one has

\int_{R_{*}-\delta}^{R_{*}}w(R)\,dR>0.

Repeating the argument of part (3) below with
R_{*}
in place of
R_{\varepsilon}
shows that

\nu_{m}([R_{*}-\delta,R_{*}])\to 1\qquad(m\to\infty),

and therefore

s_{m}=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,\nu_{m}(dR)\to R_{*}\cot R_{*}>0.

Consequently

\lambda_{m}=2\Bigl(s_{m}+\frac{1-s_{m}}{m}\Bigr)\to 2R_{*}\cot R_{*}>0,

and hence

m_{\infty}^{(m)}=\frac{4}{\lambda_{m}^{2}}\to\frac{1}{(R_{*}\cot R_{*})^{2}}<\infty.

Proof of (3):

Assume now that for every
\delta>0
,

\int_{R_{\varepsilon}-\delta}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)\,dR>0.

Fix
\delta\in(0,R_{\varepsilon})
and write

q_{\delta}:=\sin(R_{\varepsilon}-\delta),\qquad\beta_{\delta}:=\int_{R_{\varepsilon}-\delta/2}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)\,dR>0.

Then

\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}-\delta}w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,dR\leq q_{\delta}^{\,m-2}\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}-\delta}w(R)\sin R\,dR.

Also,

Z_{m}=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,dR\geq\beta_{\delta}\,\sin(R_{\varepsilon}-\delta/2)^{\,m-1}.

Hence

\nu_{m}([0,R_{\varepsilon}-\delta])=\frac{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}-\delta}w(R)(\sin R)^{m-1}\,dR}{Z_{m}}\longrightarrow 0\qquad(m\to\infty),

because
q_{\delta}<\sin(R_{\varepsilon}-\delta/2)
. Therefore

\nu_{m}([R_{\varepsilon}-\delta,R_{\varepsilon}])\to 1\qquad(m\to\infty).

Since
R\cot R
is continuous on
[0,R_{\varepsilon}]
, we obtain

s_{m}=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,\nu_{m}(dR)\longrightarrow R_{\varepsilon}\cot R_{\varepsilon}\qquad(m\to\infty).

Therefore,

\lambda_{m}=2\Bigl(s_{m}+\frac{1-s_{m}}{m}\Bigr)\longrightarrow 2R_{\varepsilon}\cot R_{\varepsilon}\qquad(m\to\infty),

and consequently

\lim_{m\to\infty}m_{\infty}^{(m)}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{4}{\lambda_{m}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\bigl(R_{\varepsilon}\cot R_{\varepsilon}\bigr)^{2}}.

∎

Remark 4.18

(

s_{m}
)
.

An alternative proof of the monotonicity of
(s_{m})
is to view
m
as a real variable. Define, for

,

s(t):=\frac{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,w(R)(\sin R)^{t-1}\,dR}{\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)(\sin R)^{t-1}\,dR}.

Then
s_{m}=s(m)
for integers

. Set

A(t):=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,w(R)(\sin R)^{t-1}\,dR,\qquad B(t):=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)(\sin R)^{t-1}\,dR.

Since
0<R_{\varepsilon}<\frac{\pi}{2}
, one has

, and near

,

which is integrable for every
t\geq 2
. Hence differentiation under the integral sign is justified, and

A^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\,w(R)(\sin R)^{t-1}\log(\sin R)\,dR,

B^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}w(R)(\sin R)^{t-1}\log(\sin R)\,dR.

Therefore

s^{\prime}(t)=\frac{A^{\prime}(t)B(t)-A(t)B^{\prime}(t)}{B(t)^{2}}.

Expanding the numerator and combining the two products into a double integral gives

A^{\prime}(t)B(t)-A(t)B^{\prime}(t)

=\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}(R\cot R)\bigl(\log(\sin R)-\log(\sin U)\bigr)w(R)w(U)(\sin R)^{t-1}(\sin U)^{t-1}\,dR\,dU.

Symmetrizing, we obtain

A^{\prime}(t)B(t)-A(t)B^{\prime}(t)

=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\bigl(R\cot R-U\cot U\bigr)\bigl(\log(\sin R)-\log(\sin U)\bigr)w(R)w(U)(\sin R)^{t-1}(\sin U)^{t-1}\,dR\,dU.

Now
R\mapsto R\cot R
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, while
R\mapsto\log(\sin R)
is strictly increasing on
(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Hence

\bigl(R\cot R-U\cot U\bigr)\bigl(\log(\sin R)-\log(\sin U)\bigr)\leq 0

for all
R,U\in[0,R_{\varepsilon}]
, and therefore
A^{\prime}(t)B(t)-A(t)B^{\prime}(t)\leq 0
. Since
B(t)>0
, it follows that
s^{\prime}(t)\leq 0
for all
t\geq 2
. If
w
is not supported on a single point, then the inequality is strict on a set of positive
(R,U)
-measure, so
s^{\prime}(t)<0
. Consequently
s(t)
is strictly decreasing on
[2,\infty)
, and in particular

s_{m+1}<s_{m}\qquad\text{for all }m\geq 2.

We carried out a numerical experiment for the uniform distribution supported on a sub-hemispherical cap in

Appendix A
Symbolic Computation Code

The following Python (SymPy) code was used to compute the Taylor coefficients:

import sympy as sp

# Symbols
t, a, R, alpha = sp.symbols(’t a R alpha’, real=True)
r = t*a

X = (sp.cos(r)*sp.cos(R)
+ (sp.sin(r)/r)*(sp.sin(R)/R)*(t*alpha))

f = sp.acos(X)**2

series_f = sp.series(f, t, 0, 5)
series_noO = sp.expand(series_f.removeO())

# Extract coefficients
coeffs = {}
for k in range(5):
ck = sp.simplify(series_noO.coeff(t, k))
coeffs[k] = ck
print(f"C{k} =", ck)
