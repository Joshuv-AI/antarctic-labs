Assembly and comparative analysis of the mitochondrial genome of Pleione yunnanensis: genome structure and evolutionary insights
Authors: Jiamei Zheng, Zemei Zhu, Yue Zhang, Feiya Zhao, Liying Yang, Mingju Hu, Aien Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Pleione yunnanensis a terrestrial or semi-epiphytic herbaceous plant belonging to the Orchidaceae family, is valued for both its medicinal uses and ornamental appeal. Although its chloroplast genomes have been sequenced, its complete mt genome had not previously been resolved, limiting genetic and evolutionary studies of the species.ResultsIn this work, we assembled and characterized the first complete mt genome of P. yunnanensis, revealing a structurally complex, multibranched system composed of 14 circular-mapping molecules totaling 468,176 bp with a GC content of 44.32%. The genome encodes 44 annotated genes, including 28 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 15 tRNAs, and one rRNA. The multibranched architecture provides new evidence supporting the dynamic and recombinational nature of plant mt genomes. Repeat analysis uncovered 29 simple sequence repeats (SSRs), 19 tandem repeats, and 118 dispersed repeats, indicating a comparatively lower repeat abundance than that found in closely related orchids with similar mt genome sizes. Codon-usage profiling of PCGs showed a marked bias toward A/T-ending codons. Prediction of RNA editing sites identified 4,708 putative edits across mitochondrial PCGs. Most mitochondrial genes displayed Ka/Ks ratios close to 1.0, suggesting relaxed selective constraints or lineage-specific evolutionary patterns rather than strong positive selection. Moreover, we detected 69 chloroplast-derived homologous fragments, including 15 intact genes, suggesting ongoing plastid–mitochondrial DNA transfer. Phylogenetic reconstruction and collinearity comparisons demonstrated that P. yunnanensis clustered closely with Dendrobium species, including D. amplum and D. hancockii, within the Orchidaceae clade.Conclusions This study provides the first complete mt genome of P. yunnanensis, providing a foundational genomic resource for the genus Pleione. The results not only improve our understanding of mt genome structure and evolution in Orchidaceae, but also offer valuable molecular evidence for phylogenetic inference, germplasm identification, and conservation of this endangered medicinal species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09318-8.Keywords: Pleione yunnanensis, Mitochondrial genome, Repeat sequence, Homologous fragments, Phylogenetic

Background

Pleione, a genus of highly ornamental orchids characterized by a single pseudobulb per plant, predominantly grows in humus-rich soil, on tree trunks, or on moss-covered rocks at altitudes ranging from 900 to 3600 m [1]. The genus Pleione encompasses approximately 37 species, predominantly distributed in China and the western Himalayas, extending to subtropical regions such as India, Nepal, Myanmar, Vietnam, Laos, and Thailand [2]. Pleione is commonly used as a traditional Chinese herbal medicine to treat various diseases in Asian countries [3]. For instance, in China, P. bulbocodioides, P. yunnanensis, and Cremastra appendiculata are also referred to as “shan-ci-gu”. These plants have been demonstrated to possess efficacy in clearing heat and detoxifying, resolving phlegm and dispersing nodules, as well as treating snake bites [4]. The genus Pleione exhibits a distinctive floral morphology and a diverse spectrum of colors, including white, yellow, pink, purple, lavender, rose-red, and purplish-red [5]. Pleione was initially introduced to the Western world from the Himalayas during the 19th century. By the early 20th century, a greater number of Pleione species had been introduced into Western botanical gardens, and numerous novel varieties were bred. Pleione has become a popular horticultural plant in Europe and the United States. Currently, due to its high ornamental value, Pleione enjoys widespread popularity in the global flower market and holds substantial international market potential [6].

Within the genus Pleione, P. yunnanensis is particularly valued for its medicinal applications and edible pseudobulbs. Recent clinical and pharmacological studies have shown that its major bioactive compounds—notably dihydrophenanthrenes and bibenzyl derivatives, exhibit significant anti-tumor and anti-cancer activities [7, 8]. In China, more than ten commercial medicines and health products currently rely on the dried pseudobulb of P. yunnanensis as a primary ingredient. Unfortunately, the species faces increasing conservation pressure. Due to its specific habitat requirements and extremely low natural reproductive capacity, wild populations have become scarce, leading to its classification as an endangered species in the China Species Red List [9]. With the rapid expansion of artificial cultivation and a continually growing market, preserving genetic diversity and improving germplasm quality have become central challenges [1]. These developments underscore the urgent need for sustainable cultivation strategies and targeted conservation measures to prevent further decline of natural populations.

Despite their economic and ecological importance, Pleione species exhibit substantial morphological variation, often presenting intermediate forms and overlapping diagnostic traits. Their broadly overlapping distributions and weak reproductive barriers have further promoted intraspecific variation and ongoing speciation, yet these same factors complicate accurate taxonomic classification. As a result, species delimitation remains challenging, and several phylogenetic relationships within the genus are still unresolved [10]. Addressing these issues requires a deeper understanding of the genetic architecture and molecular processes that drive morphological diversification in Pleione, which will ultimately support more reliable taxonomic and evolutionary interpretations.

Genomic data serves as the foundation for germplasm enhancement and molecular breeding [11]. Within plant genetic systems, mt genomes assume crucial functions, not merely in energy metabolism but also in cellular development, stress responses, and evolutionary divergence [12]. In recent years, advances in molecular biology and bioinformatics have provided new tools to reveal the genetic architectures underlying morphological diversity in Pleione species [10]. Technologies like next-generation sequencing facilitate comprehensive genomic analyses, providing unparalleled perspectives on genetic variation, evolutionary trajectories, and adaptive mechanisms [13, 14]. Nonetheless, plant mt genomes typically exhibit large sizes, complex structures, a propensity for frequent gene rearrangements, and susceptibility to foreign DNA insertions, which pose challenges to sequencing and assembly [15]. In contrast to chloroplast and nuclear genomes, research on mt genomes within the Pleione genus is relatively limited, and there are no species with fully sequenced mt genomes. Regarding Pleione yunnanensis, there is currently a lack of systematic mt genome data, which constrains our comprehension of its genetic background, evolutionary patterns, and potential applications in molecular breeding.

To bridge this knowledge gap, the present study assembled and annotated the complete mt genome of P. yunnanensis using high-throughput sequencing. As the first reported mt genome within the genus Pleione, this dataset provides critical insights into mt genome organization, structural variation, and intracellular gene transfer in orchids. Moreover, it establishes an essential reference for resolving phylogenetic relationships within Pleione and related taxa, and offers valuable molecular resources for the conservation, identification, and genetic improvement of this endangered medicinal plant.

Materials and methods

Plant material sampling, DNA extraction, and sequencing

The young and healthy leaves of P. yunnanensis were harvested from private farming fields in Lijiang, Yunnan Province, China (coordinates: 99°27′41″E, 27°11′21″N). The collections of P. yunnanensis were conducted with the necessary permissions. Our experimental research, including the collection of plant materials, adheres to institutional, national, and international guidelines. Species identification was verified by Professor Aien Tao, and the specimens were deposited in the herbarium of Lijiang Culture and Tourism College (voucher specimen: PY20241120). To extract complete genomic DNA, the leaves were combined and crushed. High-quality genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves using a modified CTAB technique [16]. DNA quality and concentration were assessed using a Thermo NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and qualified samples were subjected to sequencing at Novogene Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Libraries were prepared using the SQK-LSK109 kit and long-read sequenced on the Nanopore PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Basecalling was executed via the built-in pipeline of the sequencing platform with default parameters. Initial reads were quality-filtered using NanoFilt and NanoPlot from the NanoPack toolkit. In addition, paired-end short-read sequencing were conducted on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to generate high-accuracy reads for genome polishing. The raw Illumina reads were processed using fastp v0.23.1 with the default parameters.

Mitochondrial genome assembly and annotation

For the chloroplast genome assembly, Illumina short reads were assembled using GetOrganelle with the parameters “-R 15 -k 21,45,65,85,105 -F embplant_pt” [17]. In contrast, the mt genome of P. yunnanensis was de novo assembled using PMAT v2.1.0 with the autoMito parameters [18]. PMAT splits polished long reads into fragments and assembles them with Newbler, identifies seed contigs via BLASTn against 24 mitochondrial PCGs, and extends them using connection information to build an initial assembly graph. Non-mitochondrial and unconnected contigs are removed to generate a simplified circular graph, which is visualized using Bandage v0.8.1 [19]. The entire mt genome of P. yunnanensis was annotated using the Online tool geseq (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) and OGDRAW (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html). The reference genomes for the mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were Liriodendron tulipifera (NC_021152.1 and NC_008326.1). The fully assembled mt genome sequence of P. yunnanensis is publicly accessible through the NCBI GenBank with accession numbers PX668362–PX668375.

Analysis of codon usage bias, repeat fragments prediction of RNA editing sites

The simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified via the MISA software (v2.1) with the subsequent thresholds: 10 for mononucleotide repeats, 6 for dinucleotide repeats, and 5 for trinucleotide, tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeat sequences, respectively [20]. To detect tandem repeats, the Tandem Repeats Finder v4.09 software was employed with the parameters: 2 7 7 80 10 50 500 -f -d -m [21]. The length and location of dispersed repeats were ascertained using vmatch (v2.3.1) with a Hamming distance of 3 and a minimal repeat size of 30 base pairs. The protein-coding sequences of the mt genome were extracted by means of PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [22]. Codon usage and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values for unique protein-coding genes (PCGs) were calculated using CodonW software and visualized through custom R scripts with ggplot [23]. The RNA editing sites in the mt genome were predicted using Deepred-Mt software (v0.1.0) with a probability threshold set at a minimum of 0.9 [24].

Identification of homologous fragments and collinear analysis

Mitochondrial plastid DNA (MTPT) can provide codons for unrelated protein-coding sequences within the plant mt genome and may also exert an influence on post-transcriptional RNA processing. To identify the MTPTs, homologous fragments between the plastome and mt genome of P. yunnanensis were analyzed by means of BLASTn software. The analysis was carried out with the following parameters: -evalue 1e-5, -word_size 9, -gapopen 5, -gapextend 2, -reward 2, and -penalty-3. The results were visualized using Circos v0.69-5 [25]. Moreover, the identified MTPTs were annotated using GeSEq. Regarding the two MTPTs situated in the inverted repeat regions of the plastome, they were counted only once. To map the Multiple Synteny Plot of P. yunnanensis with closely related species, MCscanX software was employed [26].

Analysis of non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) mutation rates

To assess the selective pressures exerted on mitochondrial genes, we employed Ka/Ks_Calculator v2.0 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/kakscalculator2) to calculate the ratio of non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka) to synonymous substitution rate (Ks) for PCGs common to the species depicted in the 21 species (Dendrobium amplum, Vitex trifolia, Apostasia fujianica, Squilla empusa, Danxiaorchis yangii, Squilla mantis, Leonurus japonicus, Salvia rosmarinus, Apostasia shenzhenica, Physochlaina orientalis, Capsicum pubescens, Elsholtzia blanda, Eupatorium lindleyanum, Mikania micrantha, Prunella vulgaris, Scutellaria tsinyunensis, Dendrobium hancockii, Lactuca sativa, Nicotiana attenuata, Salvia miltiorrhiza, and Liriodendron tulipifera). Subsequently, the results were presented as box plots using Origin 2021.

Phylogenetic analyses

We obtained 23 complete mt genome sequences from species representing six plant families: Orchidaceae, Magnoliaceae, Asteraceae, Lamiaceae, Verbenaceae, and Solanaceae, sourced from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. In addition, the mt genomes of Squilla empusa (NC 007444) and Squilla mantis (NC 006081) from Squillidae were used as outgroups. We used the MAFFT program to do a multiple sequence alignment in order to examine these common gene sequences [27]. After trimming the sequences with trimAl (v1.4.rev15) with the setting -gt 0.7, we compared the sequence junctions. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using IQ-TREE (v2.2.0, –m MFP, bootstrap = 1000) to infer evolutionary relationships based on the optimized alignments [28].

Results

Features of the P. yunnanensis mt genome

In our study, the reliability of the assembly was supported by the high concordance between long-read and short-read datasets, as well as by graph-based validation using Bandage, which confirmed the connectivity and structural integrity of the assembled contigs. The assembly results of the P. yunnanensis mt genome are shown in Fig. 1. Each cyclic graph represents one subgenomic circular molecule of the mt genome. The mt genome of P. yunnanensis presents a complex multi-branched conformation, with an average coverage depth of 22.8×. The fully sequenced mt genome of P. yunnanensis spans 468,176 bp, with a GC content of 44.32%, and is composed of 14 circular-mapping molecules (Fig. 2). The longest molecule (mtCh1) measures 59,468 bp, while the shortest molecule (mtCh12) is 19,061 bp (Supplementary Table S1).

The P. yunnanensis mt genome contains 28 protein-coding genes (PCGs), categorized into 17 conserved core genes and 11 variable genes. The core gene set includes four ATP synthase genes, four cytochrome c biogenesis genes, five NADH dehydrogenase genes, two cytochrome c oxidase genes, one cytochrome b gene, and one gene encoding a membrane transport protein. The 11 variable genes comprise two large-subunit ribosomal protein genes, eight small-subunit ribosomal protein genes, and one succinate dehydrogenase gene. In addition, one rRNA gene and 15 tRNA genes were identified. A complete list of annotated genes is provided in Table 1.

Analysis of codon usage bias

To conduct codon usage analysis, the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values of PCGs in the mt genome of P. yunnanensis were computed (Table 2). The 28 PCGs encoded a total of 7303 codons, including termination codons. Leucine (Leu) was the most abundant amino acid, represented by 902 codons (12.35%), followed by serine (Ser) with 608 codons (8.33%). In contrast, aspartic acid (Asp) was the least common, occurring 151 times (2.07%).

Analysis of repeat fragments

Repetitive sequences play important roles in the evolution of plant mt genomes [29]. In the mt genome of P. yunnanensis, we identified 29 simple sequence repeats (SSRs), comprising 22 monomers (75.86%) and 7 dimers (24.14%), while no tri-, tetra-, penta-, or hexanucleotide repeats were detected (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table S2). Among them, 21 of the monomeric repeat sequences were categorized as the repeat type of A or T, which was identified as the most prevalent SSR type, accounting for 72.41% of the total. Additionally, 4 of the dimeric repeat sequences were AT/TA, recognized as the second most abundant type of SSRs, constituting 13.80% of the total. Tandem repeats consist of two or more contiguous, nearly identical copies of a nucleotide pattern, resulting from tandem duplication events in the genome. A total of 19 tandem repeat regions were detected in the P. yunnanensis mt genome (Supplementary Table S3).

A Distributions of SSRs in different isoforms of the P. yunnanensis mt genome. Two types of SSRs are marked with different colors. B Number of dispersed repeats divided by length

In addition to SSRs and tandem repeats, the P. yunnanensis mt genome also contains 118 pairs of long dispersed repeats measuring ≥ 30 bp. Most of these repeats fall within the 30–39 bp range, representing 90 pairs (76.27%). By comparison, only 6 pairs measure 40–49 bp, 8 pairs measure 50–59 bp, 4 pairs fall within 90–99 bp, and another 4 pairs range from 100 to 199 bp (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table S4).

Analysis of RNA editing sites

RNA editing refers to post-transcriptional modifications in which RNA molecules undergo nucleotide insertions, deletions, or substitutions [30]. Such alterations can occur in mRNAs, tRNAs, rRNAs, and various non-coding RNAs, thereby increasing transcriptome complexity. In this study, we identified 4,708 putative RNA editing sites across 28 PCGs in the mt genome of P. yunnanensis (Supplementary Table S5 and Fig. 5). Among these PCGs, the ccmFN gene contained the highest number of editing events (423 sites), whereas nad4l exhibited only 53 sites.

Ka/Ks analysis

The nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution ratio (Ka/Ks) is a key indicator for assessing selective pressures acting on genes during evolution [31]. The Ka/Ks ratio facilitates the determination of whether a PCG is subject to selective pressure during the evolutionary process. Under neutral selection, the equality of Ka and Ks leads to a Ka/Ks ratio of 1. When Ka > exceeds Ks (Ka/Ks > 1), it implies positive selection, while if Ks > is greater than Ka (Ka/Ks < 1), it suggests negative (purifying) selection [32]. In this study, with P. yunnanensis serving as the reference species, a total of 7 PCGs shared among all the investigated species were identified (atp1, ccmFN, cox3, nad4, nad4L, nad6, and nad9), and their Ka/Ks ratios were calculated.

As shown in Fig. 6, all seven shared mitochondrial genes exhibited median Ka/Ks values below 1.0, indicating that purifying selection predominates during their evolution. Among them, nad4L displayed the lowest and most stable Ka/Ks values, indicative of strong purifying selection and high functional conservation. In contrast, cox3 showed the greatest variability, including several extreme outliers with Ka/Ks values exceeding 3.0. Similarly, nad9 exhibited a relatively broad distribution with multiple elevated Ka/Ks values above 1.5. The genes nad4, ccmFN, and nad6 exhibit relatively high median Ka/Ks ratios close to 1.0, suggesting relatively relaxed selective constraints compared with more conserved mitochondrial genes.

Ka/Ks ratio calculated for the 7 shared genes from the mt genome. Ka/Ks = 1 means neutral selection. Ka/Ks > 1 indicates positive selection. Ka/Ks < 1 suggests negative/purifying selection. Note: Different shared genes were depicted using different colors

Overall, this gene-specific heterogeneity underscores the diverse evolutionary pressures acting on mitochondrial protein-coding genes, reflecting their distinct contributions to respiration, oxidative phosphorylation, and related metabolic pathways [33]. These findings provide a clearer understanding of the evolutionary forces shaping the mt genome and contribute to explaining the adaptive characteristics of P. yunnanensis.

Analysis of homologous fragments between mitochondria and chloroplasts

Mitochondrial plastid DNA sequences (MTPTs) refer to chloroplast-derived DNA fragments that have been incorporated into the mt genome through inter-organelle DNA transfer [34]. In the P. yunnanensis mt genome, we detected 69 such homologous fragments, with a combined length of 65,879 bp, representing 14.08% of the total mitochondrial sequence (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table S6). These homologous fragments incorporated 15 annotated genes, among which 11 were tRNA genes (trnM-CAU, trnH-GUG, trnL-CAA, trnI-CAU, trnN-GUU, trnR-UCU, trnS-CGA, trnS-GCU, trnF-GAA, trnP-UGG, trnW-CCA) and 1 was rRNA genes (rrn26).

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic trees were constructed to examine the evolutionary relationships between the mt genome of P. yunnanensis and the published mt genomes of 24 species. Detailed information on these species is provided in Supplementary Table S7. The analysis showed that D. amplum, D. yangii, and D. hancockii were closely clustered with P. yunnanensis (Fig. 8). Moreover, based on the phylogenetic tree, the 25 species were classified into seven major clusters: Squillidae, Solanaceae, Verbenaceae, Lamiaceae, Asteraceae, Magnoliaceae, and Orchidaceae. P. yunnanensis belongs to the genus Pleione within the Orchidaceae family. In Fig. 8, it is clustered with the genera Apostasia (e.g., A. fujianica and A. shenzhenica), Dendrobium (e.g., D. amplum and D. hancockii), and D. yangii, which emphasizes the phylogenetic clustering of orchid plants. In the phylogenetic tree, P. yunnanensis clustered most closely with Dendrobium species, including D. amplum, D. hancockii, and D. yangii, whereas Apostasia species were located within the same broader Orchidaceae clade. Additionally, P. yunnanensis are located in the same major clade as species of the Dendrobium genus, which reflects the evolutionary associations within the Orchidaceae family. Nevertheless, they are relatively distant from other clades in the tree, such as the genera Squilla, Salvia, Scutellaria, and Liriodendron.

The phylogenetic relationships of P. yunnanensis with other 24 species

Furthermore, the node values in the phylogenetic tree represent branch support. The high support value for the clade containing P. yunnanensis indicates strong statistical support for its evolutionary relationship with surrounding clades. The clustering pattern in the phylogenetic tree aligns with the traditional taxonomic relationships at the family and genus levels, demonstrating the reliability of mt genome-based phylogenetic analysis.

Analysis of collinearity among P. yunnanensis and other seven species

To evaluate the relationship between the mt genome of P. yunnanensis and those of seven other Orchidaceae species, BLAST was employed to acquire the outcomes of pairwise comparisons of individual mt genomes, and a Multiple Synteny Plot was constructed using homologous sequences as conserved collinear blocks (Fig. 9). The findings indicate that P. yunnanensis and other species possess numerous orthologous syntenic genome blocks. The mt genomes of P. yunnanensis exhibited low collinearity with those of the other seven species, and the order of the collinear blocks varied among individual mt genomes. This implies that during the evolutionary process, substantial genome rearrangements may have occurred between P. yunnanensis and other species (including D. henanense, D. hancockii, D. amplum, A. shenzhenica, N. aragoana, A. fujianica, and D. yangii), resulting in extreme structural non-conservation.

Discussion

Mitochondria function as the principal loci of respiration in plants, producing the energy required for diverse biological processes. In the plant kingdom, the mt genome is highly variable and structurally flexible, exhibiting remarkable diversity across species [35]. Within Orchidaceae, studies on mt genome organization remain limited, including those concerning the genus Pleione. Recent advances in sequencing technologies, however, have rapidly increased the number of available plant mt genomes, enabling more comprehensive comparisons [36]. In this study, although experimental validation such as PCR amplification across junction regions was not performed, the consistency between sequencing platforms and graph-based assembly strongly supports the reliability of the reconstructed genome. We present the first fully assembled mt genome of P. yunnanensis, which consists of 14 circular-mapping molecules with a total length of 468,176 bp. This finding supports the hypothesis that plant mt genomes exist as a population of interconverting subgenomic molecules rather than a single master circle [37]. Compared with previously reported Orchidaceae species, the unusually high number of circular-mapping molecules in P. yunnanensis highlights lineage-specific genome fragmentation and recombination patterns [38]. Future experimental validation, such as long-read sequencing depth analysis, recombination assays, and molecular confirmation of alternative conformations, will be necessary to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Despite its multipartite and branched configuration, the genome is smaller than those of several closely related Dendrobium species, such as D. flexicaule (596,506 bp), D. nobile (772,523 bp), D. officinale (625,267 bp), and D. huoshanense (650,957 bp) [39]. These size differences likely reflect lineage-specific expansion or contraction of intergenic regions, which are strongly influenced by repeat accumulation and the acquisition of exogenous DNA [40].

Codon usage bias influences amino acid composition, protein folding, and overall protein function, thereby shaping an organism’s adaptability and survival [41]. This bias also reflects broader genomic evolutionary trends. In the mt genome of P. yunnanensis, 37 codons show RSCU values greater than 1, indicating a clear preference for certain synonymous codons. For example, TCA for serine (Ser) (RSCU = 1.48) and GCA for alanine (Ala) (RSCU = 1.09) exemplify these favored codons. Besides, the observed codon bias toward A/T-ending codons likely reflects underlying mutational pressure and may be associated with tRNA abundance, thereby influencing translational optimization, efficiency, and protein expression in plant mitochondria [42].

In addition, the P. yunnanensis mt genome contains 44 annotated functional genes, including 28 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 15 tRNA genes, and one rRNA gene. GC content, a key factor influencing amino acid composition and genome evolution in higher plants [43], is 44.32% in this species, a value comparable to that of other Orchidaceae mt genomes [44]. Together, these features indicate that while the gene content and nucleotide composition of the P. yunnanensis mt genome are broadly consistent with those of related orchids, its multichromosomal organization reflects a notably distinct structural pattern.

Repetitive sequences in plant mitochondria play a crucial role in determining genome size, structure, and recombination [45]. Repetitive elements, particularly dispersed repeats, are closely associated with homologous recombination and may directly contribute to the formation of the observed multibranched mitochondrial architecture [46]. In the mt genome of P. yunnanensis, we identified a large proportion of short repeats, predominantly 30–60 bp in length, which together constitute 88.14% of the dispersed repeats. In addition, 29 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected, comprising 22 mononucleotide repeats (75.86%) and 7 dinucleotide repeats (24.14%), while no tri-, tetra-, penta-, or hexanucleotide motifs were present. Although higher-order SSR types occur in some Orchidaceae species, their distribution varies considerably across lineages. When compared with A. fujianica and A. shenzhenica, P. yunnanensis exhibits the lowest number of repetitive elements, a pattern that likely contributes to its relatively compact mt genome within the family.

RNA editing is a crucial post-transcriptional process in plant mt genomes and is widespread across higher plants [47]. In the mt genome of P. yunnanensis, we identified 4,708 RNA editing sites across all protein-coding genes (PCGs), a number far exceeding that reported for other Orchidaceae species such as D. wilsonii (571) and D. henanense (567) [44]. All the RNA editing sites were the C-T (U) types, and the finding was similar to the identification results from other plant mt genomes [48]. Notably, the ccmFN gene contained the highest number of editing events (423 sites), indicating extensive post-transcriptional modification, whereas nad4l exhibited only 53 sites, suggesting a more conserved sequence. Such variation in editing frequency likely reflects differences in gene function and selective constraints during mitochondrial evolution. In addition, RNA editing events frequently result in amino acid changes that increase protein hydrophobicity or restore evolutionarily conserved residues, suggesting a compensatory mechanism to maintain protein function despite genomic mutations [49]. Overall, these results highlight the complexity of RNA editing in P. yunnanensis and offer new insights into post-transcriptional regulation within the Orchidaceae mt genome.

The Ka/Ks ratio is a key indicator for assessing phylogenetic relationships and the evolutionary pressures acting on protein-coding genes [50]. In plant mt genomes, most genes typically experience purifying selection, reflected by Ka/Ks values below 1. In our analysis, 7 shared PCGs exhibited Ka/Ks < 1 when comparing P. yunnanensis with related species, indicating that these genes have largely evolved under negative selection. This pattern aligns with findings in other Orchidaceae members, such as D. hancockii [51]. Notably, several PCGs exhibit Ka/Ks ratios close to 1.0, which likely reflect relaxed selective constraints rather than genuine positive selection [52]. Alternatively, these patterns may also arise from methodological limitations, such as low sequence divergence, alignment uncertainty, or rate heterogeneity among lineages [53].

Homologous DNA transfer from chloroplasts to mitochondria illustrates the genomic interactions and material exchange that occur between the two organelles. In P. yunnanensis, we identified 69 mitochondrial plastid-derived sequences (MTPTs) ranging from 108 to 35,084 bp, with a combined length of 65,879 bp, representing 14.08% of the entire mt genome. This proportion is substantially higher than that reported for Panax notoginseng (3.11%) [54], Sorghum arundinaceum (3.79%) [55], Calycanthus henryi (2.79%) [56], while being similar to that of Rehmannia chingii (16.34%) [57]. The identification of extensive MTPTs highlights the importance of intracellular gene transfer in shaping mt genome evolution. These transferred fragments not only contribute to genome expansion and structural complexity but may also play roles in functional innovation through gene retention or modification [58]. The relatively high proportion of MTPTs in P. yunnanensis suggests active historical DNA exchange between organelles, providing important insights into the mechanisms of organelle genome evolution and integration in orchids. Annotation of these transferred regions revealed 15 intact chloroplast-derived genes, including 14 tRNA genes (trnM-CAU, trnH-GUG, trnL-CAA, trnI-CAU, trnN-GUU, trnI-CAU, trnF-GAA, trnR-UCU, trnS-CGA, trnS-GCU, trnN-GUU, trnF-GAA, trnP-UGG, and trnW-CCA) and one rRNA gene (rrn26). These MTPTs contribute to the structural complexity of the mt genome and offer important clues for studying organelle evolution and functional integration.

To clarify the phylogenetic position of P. yunnanensis, we conducted a maximum likelihood (ML) analysis using mt genomes of 25 species, which included Squillidae, Solanaceae, Verbenaceae, Lamiaceae, Asteraceae, Magnoliaceae, and Orchidaceae, with Squilla empusa (NC 007444) and Squilla mantis (NC 006081) serving as the outgroup. In the resulting ML tree, P. yunnanensis is clustered with the genera Apostasia (e.g., A. fujianica and A. shenzhenica), Dendrobium (e.g., D. amplum and D. hancockii), and D. yangii, reflecting the conserved phylogenetic clustering typical of Orchidaceae. This pattern is broadly consistent with chloroplast-based phylogenetic results reported by Li et al. [10]. From the perspective of the degree of relatedness, P. yunnanensis clustered closely with several Dendrobium species in the ML tree, while Apostasia species were recovered within the same Orchidaceae lineage, consistent with their shared evolutionary origin within the family. Moreover, its placement within the same major clade as multiple Dendrobium taxa underscores the shared ancestry and diversification patterns within Orchidaceae. Although the current phylogenetic analysis includes representative species from multiple lineages, incorporating a broader sampling of Orchidaceae species in future studies would further improve phylogenetic resolution and provide a more comprehensive view of evolutionary relationships within the family.

Overall, this study identified a suite of candidate molecular markers, including simple sequence repeat (SSR) loci and highly polymorphic regions in mitochondrial genes, which could facilitate germplasm identification and population genetic analyses. Moreover, several gene families, such as nad, atp, and ccm, are highlighted as promising targets for exploring stress response mechanisms and metabolic regulation, thereby providing useful genomic resources for future breeding and conservation efforts. Furthermore, phylogenetic reconstruction based on mt genome data provided robust resolution of evolutionary relationships, supporting the taxonomic placement of P. yunnanensis and clarifying its affinity with closely related orchid genera. These findings offer valuable molecular evidence for addressing taxonomic ambiguities and contribute to the conservation and management of genetic resources in the genus Pleione.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully sequenced the mt genome of P. yunnanensis, revealing a unique multibranch conformation in its mtDNA structure. The discovery of a multipartite mt genome composed of 14 circular-mapping molecules with a multibranched architecture provides new evidence for the structural diversity and dynamic organization of plant mt genome. Furthermore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of codon preference, repeat sequences, homologous sequences, and RNA editing in P. yunnanensis, comparing these findings with closely related species. This research offers valuable insights for the breeding, conservation, and classification of P. yunnanensis, providing a foundation for understanding its genetic diversity and adaptive evolution. Future analyses may explore key mitochondrial genes and regulatory mechanisms, thereby facilitating the selection of superior germplasm and accelerating the development of improved cultivars.
