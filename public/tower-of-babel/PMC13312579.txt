Multi-omics unravels synergistic mechanism of mixed-strain fermentation by Pediococcus acidilactici and Staphylococcus xylosus for quality and flavor improvement in low-salt yellowfin tuna dark meat fish sauce
Authors: Lilan You, Hui Yu, Jiaqi Wu, Changwen Huang, Zhiqiang Guo, Juan Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the flavor regulatory mechanism of mixed-strain fermentation in low-salt fish sauce from yellowfin tuna dark meat, based on physicochemical properties, microbial communities, and metabolite profiles. Pediococcus acidilactici and Staphylococcus xylosus at the optimal ratio of 1:2 synergistically increased the amino acid nitrogen content to above 0.7 g/100 mL. Amplicon sequencing revealed distinct microbial communities among the unfermented, single-strain, and co-fermentation groups. Eight core differential genera were identified between co-fermentation and unfermented groups, with Lactiplantibacillus and Pediococcus dominant in the co-fermentation group. Compared with the unfermented group, SPME-GC–MS and LC-MS identified 16 key differential volatile metabolites and 20 downregulated non-volatile metabolites in the co-fermentation group. These metabolites enhanced fruity, fatty, cheesy, and other flavors of fish sauce, and were enriched in ABC transporter, purine/nucleotide metabolism, and other pathways. Overall, co-fermentation provides theoretical and technical support for the process development and flavor regulation of low-salt fish sauce.Keywords: Low-salt fish sauce, Co-fermentation, Microbial community, Volatile metabolites, Non-volatile metabolites

Introduction

Fish sauce is a fermented condiment derived from aquatic products, characterized by a transparent amber hue and a unique savory flavor, and is widely consumed, particularly in Southeast Asian countries (Han et al., 2023). Traditional fish sauce is produced via natural fermentation under high-salt conditions for more than six months, where the high salt concentration inhibits the growth of spoilage microorganisms (Li et al., 2023). However, this traditional process suffers from long fermentation cycles and high salt content, which not only fails to meet the demand for large-scale industrial production but also poses potential health risks to consumers. With advancements in processing technology, the co-fermentation process of enzymatic hydrolysis and microbial inoculation has emerged as the preferred method for rapid production of low-salt fish sauce (Gao et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2016). Zhou et al. (2021) prepared low-salt mackerel fish sauce by first hydrolyzing mackerel with acid protease and flavor protease for 2 h, followed by inoculation with Latilactobacillus sakei, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, and Weissella cibaria for 5 days of fermentation. The integrated enzymatic hydrolysis and microbial inoculation approach shortens the fermentation period, improves the utilization of raw materials and the stability of flavor, while also addressing the safety challenges related to low salt content, thereby enabling rapid production and quality enhancement of low-salt fish sauce.

In the low-salt fish sauce co-fermentation system, the selection of starter cultures is crucial, directly determining the fermentation efficiency and flavor quality of the fish sauce. Currently, research on fish sauce fermentation primarily focuses on single microbial strains, but this approach has limitations, such as narrow metabolic pathways and low bioconversion efficiency. Particularly under low-salt conditions, it is difficult to replicate the rich flavors formed by the synergistic metabolism of complex microbial communities in traditional natural fermentation (Zhu et al., 2021). In contrast, co-fermentation with multiple bacterial strains can promote more thorough hydrolysis of proteins through complementarity and cooperation among strains, constructing a rich and harmonious flavor system. Additionally, it enhances the stability of the fermentation system and meets the fermentation requirements of low-salt environments (Gao et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). Li et al. employed three fermentation strains (T. muriaticus, S. edaphicus, and B. subtilis) to co-ferment low-salt fish sauce with excellent flavor and significant inhibitory effects against spoilage bacteria. This demonstrates that co-fermentation with composite microbial starter cultures contributes to enhancing the quality and safety of low-salt fish sauce (Li et al., 2025).

The composition and metabolic activity of the microbial community directly determine the flavor profile of fermented foods (Han et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). The unique flavor of fish sauce is collectively contributed by various microbial metabolites, including volatile metabolites (esters, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones) and non-volatile metabolites (umami amino acids, taste-active peptides) (Gao et al., 2025; Giri et al., 2010; Han et al., 2023). The sensory quality of the final product is defined by the specific types, concentrations, and ratios of these compounds(Tomar et al., 2025). As flavor is a primary criterion for consumer evaluation of fermented foods, understanding its formation and regulation is a central focus of fish sauce research (Ding et al., 2020). However, existing studies have largely examined flavor development in single-strain fermentations or traditional spontaneous processes. A systematic exploration of the flavor differences and the intrinsic correlation mechanisms between fish sauce produced by mixed-strain co-fermentation and that produced by single-strain fermentation is still lacking.

Yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) is a high-quality deep-sea economic fish with tender flesh and excellent nutritional value. A large amount of byproducts are generated during its processing, among which dark meat accounts for a relatively high proportion (Lu et al., 2024). Dark meat is rich in chromoproteins, lipids, glycogen, and various vitamins. However, compared with ordinary muscle tissue, it has a coarse texture, a stronger fishy odor, and poor processability, resulting in a low resource utilization rate (Maeda et al., 2017). The accumulation of such byproducts not only pollutes the environment but also wastes resources. Therefore, the high-value utilization of yellowfin tuna dark meat has important economic and ecological significance.

In this study, yellowfin tuna dark meat was used as the raw material to prepare low-salt fish sauce via enzymatic hydrolysis combined with microbial inoculation fermentation. To establish an efficient co-fermentation system, two strains were screened from five microbial candidates using amino acid nitrogen (AAN) content and sensory evaluation as indicators, and their inoculation ratio was further optimized. High-throughput amplicon sequencing, HS-SPME-GC–MS, and LC-MS were employed to systematically compare the physicochemical properties, sensory quality, microbial community structures, and flavor compound profiles of low-salt fish sauce under uninoculated, single-strain, and mixed-strain fermentation conditions. The core objective of this study was to elucidate the intrinsic mechanism of the synergistic interaction between the two strains in regulating flavor formation during low-salt fish sauce fermentation. This research provides theoretical foundations and novel technological strategies for the high-value utilization of tuna processing byproducts and the industrial production of high-quality low-salt fish sauce.

Materials and methods

Material

Yellowfin tuna dark meat was provided by Hainan Orca Sea Fishing Service Co., Ltd., following their commercial fishing operations in the South China Sea (17°24′N, 110°36′E). Lactobacillus plantarum (L. plantarum, accession no. ACCC 11095), Pediococcus pentosaceus (P. pentosaceus, accession no. ATCC8081), Lactobacillus sakei subsp. sakei (L. sakei subsp. sakei, accession no. ATCC15521), Pediococcus acidilactici (P. acidilactici, accession no. DSM 20284), and Staphylococcus xylosus (S. xylosus, accession no. ATCC 29971) were purchased from Beijing Biobank Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Trypsin was purchased from Yangshao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Alkaline protease was obtained from Lonct Enzymes Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China).

Preparation of fish sauce

The preparation of fish sauce refers to the method described by Yu et al. (2025). Yellowfin tuna dark meat was homogenized into a paste using a tissue homogenizer, followed by steaming in an autoclave at 121 °C for 15 min. The homogenized dark meat was then mixed with water at a 1:4 (m/v) ratio, and the pH was adjusted to 8.3. Trypsin and alkaline protease (2:1, m/m) were added, and the mixture was incubated in a water bath at 53.3 °C for 4 h to obtain the tuna dark meat enzymatic hydrolysate. The hydrolysate was heated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 10 min to inactivate the enzymes. Finally, 0.2% glucose was added and mixed thoroughly, after which a defined amount of starter culture was inoculated and fermented for a specified period to produce the fish sauce.

Selection of microbial strains

The method for activating the microbial strain was adapted from Ma et al. (2022). L. plantarum, P. pentosaceus, L. sakei subsp. sakei and P. acidilactici were inoculated into MRS broth, while S. xylosus was inoculated into NB medium, with all strains being incubated at a constant temperature of 37 °C. The cultures were then centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. After removal of the supernatant, the cell pellets were resuspended in physiological saline to the original volume of the culture medium, yielding a bacterial suspension with a concentration of 6–7 lg CFU/mL. Five bacterial strains were inoculated into fermented hydrolysate (FH group) from tuna dark meat: (1) 6% L. plantarum (LP group). (2) 6% P. pentosaceus (PP group). (3) 6% L. sakei subsp. sakei (LS group). (4) 6% P. acidilactici (PA group). (5) 6% S. xylosus (SX group). Samples were collected from day 3 to day 9, with three replicates per group, for the determination of amino acid nitrogen content. Sensory evaluation was conducted on fish sauce from each group on the optimal fermentation day.

Determination of amino acid nitrogen (AAN)

The measurement was performed with reference to the Chinese National Standard GB 5009.235–2016. Specifically, 5 mL of the fish sauce sample was first diluted to 100 mL with water and mixed thoroughly. A 20 mL aliquot of the diluted solution was transferred to a beaker, followed by the addition of 60 mL of water. The mixture was titrated with a standard sodium hydroxide titration solution (0.050 mol/L) until the pH reached 8.2 as indicated by the acidimeter. Then, 10 mL of formaldehyde solution was added, and the titration was continued to a final pH of 9.2. A blank test was carried out simultaneously using 80 mL of water. Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation was performed according to the method of Liu et al. (2023). Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) was conducted on fish sauce samples from different groups. The evaluation was carried out by a panel of ten trained panelists (five male and five female, aged 20–30 years) who evaluated the fish sauce for color, aroma, bitterness, fishy odor, and umami intensity. All panelists had received specialized training in fish sauce sensory evaluation, possessed relevant evaluation experience, and had no history of smoking. Fish sauce samples were placed on white porcelain plates. Under natural light, panelists first observed the color, then smelled the aroma, and finally tasted an appropriate volume of liquid drawn with a pipette. Mouth rinsing was required between evaluations of different samples. Subsequently, the fish sauce samples from each group were scored using a 0–10 scale, where 0 indicated no perceived intensity, and 10 represented the maximum intensity of the attribute. Ethical approval was not required for this study by the affiliated institution (Hainan University, Haikou, China). This experiment was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to the sensory evaluation, all panelists were fully informed of the purpose, procedures, and potential risks of the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Appropriate protocols were followed to protect the rights and privacy of all panelists, and no personal information of participants was collected or disclosed. All panelists reserved the right to withdraw from the experiment at any time without any penalty.

Optimization of co-fermentation ratios

Integrating AAN content and sensory evaluation results, two strains were selected from five candidates for combined fermentation. Subsequently, a 6% inoculum of each of the two screened single strains and a 6% inoculum of their composite strain were separately inoculated into the same batch of enzymatic hydrolysate and fermented for 6 days. With the two single-strain fermentation groups set as controls, the AAN contents of fish sauce samples in the control groups and under various mixing ratios (3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, v/v) were determined. The optimal mixing ratio was ultimately identified and designated as the co-fermentation group (CO) for subsequent trials.

Microbial amplicon sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using the CretMag Power Soil DNA Kit (CretBiotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) following the protocol provided by Metware Technology Biological Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The concentration and purity of DNA were determined by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA was diluted to 1 ng/μL based on the concentration. The V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR using the specific primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The PCR products were checked by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, quantified, and purified. A sequencing library was constructed using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for MGI V2 (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Subsequently, the library was sequenced on an DNBSEQ sequencing platform. The resulting sequencing data were processed and denoised using QIIME 2 (version 2024.5) to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASV).

Determination of volatile metabolites

Hs-SPME-GC–MS

The samples were equilibrated at 60 °C with constant shaking for 5 min and then subjected to headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) for 15 min using a 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS fiber (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA). After desorption, the volatiles were separated and identified by GC–MS (Agilent, USA). Chromatographic conditions: a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA) was used with helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The injector temperature was set at 250 °C with a solvent delay of 3.5 min. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial hold at 40 °C for 3.5 min, increased to 100 °C at 10 °C/min, then to 180 °C at 7 °C/min, finally raised to 280 °C at 25 °C/min and held for 5 min. LC-MS

A 50 μL aliquot of the sample was mixed with 150 μL of an acetonitrile-methanol internal standard extraction solution (acetonitrile: methanol = 1:4, v/v). The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and centrifuged (10,000 ×g, 4 °C, 10 min). Then, 150 μL of the supernatant was transferred and allowed to stand at −20 °C for 30 min. After a second centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 4 °C, 10 min), 120 μL of the supernatant was aspirated and placed into the corresponding vial insert for instrumental analysis. Chromatographic conditions: Separation was performed on an ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm × 1.8 μm; Waters). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, the flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 4 μL. The gradient program was as follows: mobile phase B increased from 5% to 20% over the first 2 min, then to 60% within the next 3 min, followed by a rise to 99% in 1 min and held for 1.5 min. Finally, mobile phase B was brought back to 5% in 0.1 min and held for 2.4 min. Mass spectrometry data were collected in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode with Analyst TF 1.7.1 software (Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada). The ion source parameters were set as follows: ion source gas 1, 50 psi; ion source gas 2, 60 psi; curtain gas, 35 psi; ion source temperature, 550 °C (positive mode) and 450 °C (negative mode), respectively; ion spray voltage floating (ISVF), 5000 V (positive) and − 4000 V (negative).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times (n = 3), with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0, employing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple range test to assess significant differences between groups (p < 0.05). Experimental data were graphed using GraphPad Prism 8.

Results and discussion

Selection of microbial strains

During fermentation, the AAN content of fish sauce fermented with different starter cultures was monitored. The LP group achieved maximum AAN content on day 7 of fermentation, while the PP, PA, LS, and SX groups all peaked on day 6 (Fig. 1A). On day 6 of fermentation, the AAN levels in the PA and SX groups were as high as 0.628 g/100 mL and 0.651 g/100 mL, respectively, significantly exceeding those of the other tested starter groups (Supplementary Table S1). These findings are consistent with previously reported metabolic traits of the two microorganisms. P. acidilactici can effectively enhance the total amino acid content in dry-cured mutton sausages (Jiang et al., 2023), while S. xylosus can promote protein degradation and amino acid synthesis in fermented sausages (Zheng et al., 2024). This aligns with the fermentation characteristics of the two strains studied in this research.

(A) AAN content in fish sauce fermented by different bacterial strains; (B) Appearance characteristics of fish sauce fermented to peak AAN levels by different strains (from left to right: L. plantarum, P. pentosaceus, P. acidilactici, L. sakei subsp. sakei, S. xylosus); (C) Sensory radar chart of fish sauce fermented to peak AAN concentration by different strains; (D) AAN contents in the single-strain (PA, SX) and different PA: SX co-fermentation groups on day 6 of fermentation.

AAN content is not the only critical metric for evaluating fish sauce quality. Equally important are the sensory characteristics, which play pivotal roles in shaping consumer acceptance and market selection (Ozcan et al., 2024). The appearance characteristics of fish sauce samples from the five single-strain groups at their respective maximum AAN time points were compared (Fig. 1B). The SX group exhibited a relatively lighter, typical reddish-brown color, whereas the PA group showed a darker, lustrous deep reddish-brown hue. Sensory evaluation (Fig. 1C) indicated that the PA group's fish sauce offered a bright color, rich aroma, and a well-balanced, pleasant taste, leading to excellent overall sensory quality. Although the SX group imparted a strong umami sensation, it was accompanied by a faint aroma and a noticeable fishy odor.

Comprehensive analysis of AAN content and sensory characteristics showed that although the SX group had the highest AAN content, it had obvious flavor defects. To counter these shortcomings, P. acidilactici was introduced for its capacity to generate a range of flavor compounds via metabolic activity, effectively compensating for the limitations of single-strain fermentation. Lactic acid bacteria are widely used in fermented food production due to their flavor-enhancing and bacteriostatic properties. Li et al. (2024) demonstrated that inoculating tilapia paste with P. acidilactici significantly enhanced desirable flavor components, confirming the strain's capacity to improve sensory qualities. Accordingly, this study implemented co-fermentation with P. acidilactici and S. xylosus for co-fermentation to synergistically enhance both AAN content and sensory quality in fish sauce.

Establishing the co-fermentation ratio

The ratio of co-fermentation strains is crucial to the effectiveness of fish sauce fermentation. In this study, PA and SX were mixed at five ratios: 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3, and all groups were fermented for 6 days to investigate their effects on fish sauce fermentation. The highest AAN content in fish sauce was achieved at a PA: SX ratio of 1:2 (Fig. 1D), exceeding 0.7 g/100 mL and significantly higher than other composite ratios and single-strain fermentation groups (p < 0.05). Han et al. (2024) found that co-fermentation using IMAUJBP3 and IMAUJBR3 lactic acid bacteria significantly improved the flavor of mutton fermented sausages. Similarly, Li et al. (2025) indicated that beef sausages fermented through co-fermentation of Lactobacillus sakei and Staphylococcus carnosus exhibited superior flavor and color compared to those fermented solely with Lactobacillus sakei. Accordingly, samples fermented for 6 days with a PA: SX ratio of 1:2 were used for subsequent microbial and metabolomic analyses.

Microbial composition analysis of fish sauce fermented under uninoculated, single-strain, and co-culture conditions

Microbial β-diversity

A PCoA plot (Fig. 2A) was generated using the Bray-Curtis distance algorithm to perform β-diversity analysis on the bacterial community structures of the four groups. PCoA1 and PCoA2 explained 55.54% and 44.19% of the variance, respectively, accounting for 99.73% of the total explainable variance. Samples closer together on the PCoA coordinate plot exhibit greater similarity (Li et al., 2023). This high proportion indicates strong explanatory power. The samples exhibited distinct clustering trends in the plot, distributed across different regions. Microorganisms in the FH group were primarily concentrated in the upper right corner, while those in the PA, SX, and CO groups occupied the lower left corner, indicating that fermentation significantly altered the microbial composition of fish sauce. The microbial community structure showed a clear divergence between the co-fermentation (CO) group and the single-strain fermentation (PA and SX) groups, indicating that the use of mixed bacterial cultures contributed to distinct ecological profiles in the fish sauce samples.

Analysis of Microbial Composition. (A) β-diversity analysis; (B) α-diversity analysis; (C) Stacked bar plot showing relative abundance at the phylum level; (D) Stacked bar plot showing relative abundance at the genus level; (E) t-test analysis of core microbial genera between the CO and FH groups.

Microbial α-diversity

Alpha diversity analysis further evaluates changes in microbial community richness and evenness. The variations in α-diversity indices during fish sauce fermentation were examined (Fig. 2B). The Ace index and Chao1 index of the FH group were higher than those of the CO group, and significantly higher than those of the PA and SX single-strain fermentation groups (p < 0.05). Additionally, the Shannon index and Simpson index were significantly higher than those of other inoculated fermentation groups (p < 0.05). The Ace index and Chao1 index represent species richness, with higher values indicating greater species abundance. The Shannon and Simpson's Diversity Indices are measures that directly reflect species diversity and evenness within a community. Higher values signify greater species diversity and evenness. These results indicate that both species richness and evenness decreased after inoculation fermentation. This may be attributed to the rapid proliferation and dominance of the inoculated fermentation strains, which significantly suppressed the growth of most hybrid bacterium during low-salt fish sauce fermentation, limiting them to extremely low abundances. Similarly, reduced microbial richness and diversity were also observed in low-salt fish sauce from silver pomfret (Pampus Argenteus) fermented using the Tetragenococcus halophilus 2MH-3 as a starter culture (Han et al., 2024).

Microbial composition analysis

Fig. 2C shows the distribution of bacterial communities at the phylum level for the FH, PA, SX, and CO groups. Gene sequences obtained from the four sample groups, 11 bacterial phyla were identified: Cyanobacteri, Fusobacteriota, Planctomycetes, Campylobacterota, Actinobacteriota, Crenarchaeota, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and other phyla. Among these, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were the predominant phyla in all four fish sauce groups, with similar results observed in low-salt fish sauce (Li et al., 2022) and traditional fish sauce (Wang et al., 2018). The strong acid-producing and bacteriostatic capabilities of the inoculated P. acidilactici can inhibit other spoilage bacteria (Li et al., 2024). Thus, the Firmicutes dominated in the PA group, while the Proteobacteria was most prevalent in the SX group. This is speculated to be due to the fact that although the inoculated S. xylosus possesses strong protein and fat hydrolysis capabilities, its acid production and secretion of specific antibacterial substances were weaker than those of P. acidilactici, making it unable to quickly establish an absolute advantage to suppress the undesirable odor-producing bacteria belonging to the Proteobacteria (Gao et al., 2024). This finding further corroborates previous research indicating that while the SX group achieved higher AAN content in fish sauce, its flavor profile remained underdeveloped. Notably, the CO group exhibited a more balanced ratio of Firmicutes to Proteobacteria compared to the SX group. The presence of S. xylosus ensured efficient degradation of proteins, maintaining elevated AAN levels, while P. acidilactici contributed to flavor optimization by producing acids and suppressing undesirable metabolic activities of spoilage-related strains within the Proteobacteria. This synergistic interaction effectively reduced off-flavor generation, resulting in dual improvement in both ANN content and flavor profile in the fish sauce.

The microbial composition at the genus level in low-salt fish sauces from different groups is shown in Fig. 2D. Bacillus exhibited the highest abundance in the FH group, largely attributable to the indigenous microbiota carried by the fish raw materials. The unfermented system maintained a near-neutral pH, lacking the acidic or high osmotic pressure extremes that typically develop during fermentation. These conditions aligned well with the growth preferences of the Bacillus, enabling it to thrive and secure a dominant ecological niche in the natural system, which lacks the regulatory influence of fermentative microbial strains (Settachaimongkon et al., 2025). In the SX group, the abundances of Bacillus and Staphylococcus were relatively high. In the PA group, Lactiplantibacillus exhibited the highest abundance, followed by Pediococcus. After inoculation with P. acidilactici, this strain rapidly utilized the nutrient substrates in the system, metabolizing them to produce organic acids such as lactic acid and acetic acid, while simultaneously secreting small amounts of antibacterial substances. This process established an acidic environment that inhibited the growth and reproduction of acid-intolerant spoilage bacteria (Li et al., 2024). Lactiplantibacillus, as a common indigenous beneficial bacterium in fermented fish products, initially presents in higher numbers. Leveraging its broad substrate utilization capacity and tolerance to extreme conditions, it eventually replaced Pediococcus during the mid to late stages of fermentation (Li et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2021). In a study on tilapia surimi co-fermented with Pediococcus acidilactici and Latilactobacillus sakei, L. sakei demonstrated a stronger ability to adapt to the fermentation environment compared to P. acidilactici, rapidly establishing dominance within the microbial community (Li et al., 2024). Furthermore, in the CO group, a similar microbial structure was observed, with Lactiplantibacillus showing the highest abundance, followed by Pediococcus. In summary, different fermentation treatments led to distinct microbial community structures in the fish sauce of each group, and these microbial differences may further influence the metabolic profiles during fermentation, ultimately contributing to variations in the flavor of the fish sauce.

Selection of core differential genera

Eight genera with significantly different abundances (p < 0.05) were identified between the CO and FH groups through differential analysis of microorganisms at the genus level based on the t-test (Fig. 2E). These genera included Bacillus, Carnobacterium, Brevundimonas, Leuconostoc, Pseudomonas, Pseudarthrobacter, Pediococcus, and Lactiplantibacillus. Compared to the FH group, the abundance of Bacillus decreased significantly in the CO group, while the relative abundances of Pediococcus and Lactiplantibacillus increased substantially, establishing them as the dominant genera in the fermentation system. Under the co-fermentation conditions of the CO group, Pediococcus and Lactiplantibacillus rapidly became the predominant microorganisms. Their metabolic processes continuously produced organic acids such as lactic acid, leading to a gradual and stable decline in the pH of the fermentation system (Zhang et al., 2023). The resulting antimicrobial metabolites could inhibit the growth of other undesirable microorganisms (Zapasnik et al., 2022), thereby effectively reshaping the microbial community structure of the fish sauce.

Volatile metabolite analysis of uninoculated versus single/mixed strain fermentation of low-salt fish sauce

Compositional analysis of volatile metabolites

Among the volatile metabolites identified in the four experimental groups, hydrocarbons were the predominant chemical class in the FH group (26.73%) and PA group (26.62%), whereas alcohols accounted for the largest proportion in the CO group (20.91%) and SX group (28.00%) (Fig. 3A). In terms of overall chemical class distribution, hydrocarbons, heterocyclic compounds, alcohols, esters, ketones, amines, and aldehydes constituted the major fractions in all fermented groups. These compound classes are well-recognized as characteristic metabolic products of microbial fermentation (Liao et al., 2023). To further examine the effect of fermentation on flavor composition, PCA was conducted on the samples from each group (Fig. 3B). The results revealed differences in flavor composition between single-strain and mixed-strain fermentation systems.

(A) Pie chart showing the relative proportions of volatile metabolites by chemical class; (B) PCA plot (Ellipses represent 95% confidence intervals around group centroids); (C) Heatmap of volatile metabolites in the four experimental groups grouped by chemical class; (D) Heatmap of volatile metabolites with ROAV ≥0.1.

Esters typically exhibit low odor thresholds, meaning even trace amounts can significantly influence a food's aroma profile (Zhao et al., 2024). In this study, the total ester content in fish sauce samples increased after fermentation (Fig. 3C), demonstrating that microbial metabolic activities during the process effectively promoted ester biosynthesis. Comparative analysis between groups further revealed that the CO group had higher ester levels than the PA and SX groups. This difference may result from the synergistic interaction of the two bacterial strains in the composite system, which accelerated the accumulation of ester precursors and the esterification reaction process, thereby enhancing ester synthesis efficiency (Han et al., 2024). Esters contributed pleasant floral and fruity notes to the CO group's fish sauce (Chen et al., 2022), resulting in a more appealing flavor and a marked improvement in sensory quality.

The content of heterocyclic compounds in the SX group was significantly higher than that in the FH, CO, and PA groups, with the PA group showing the lowest content. A study on lactic acid bacteria-fermented douchi demonstrated that lactic acid bacteria can inhibit the biosynthesis of heterocyclic compounds (Feng et al., 2024). In the present study, the low heterocyclic compound content in the single-strain lactic acid bacteria fermentation system (PA group) aligns with this finding. In contrast, the SX group, which was not inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, exhibited substantial heterocyclic compound production, likely due to interactions between S. xylosus and other metabolites in the system. Heterocyclic compounds contribute characteristic roasted and nutty flavors to food (Liu & Quan, 2024). The content of these compounds in the CO group lies between the levels found in the PA and SX groups. This intermediate concentration helped avoid the overly simple flavor profile resulting from insufficient heterocyclics in the PA group, while also preventing the heavy, excessive taste that could arise from their over-accumulation in the SX group. As a result, the CO group developed a well-balanced and harmonious flavor suitable for fish sauce.

After fermentation treatment, alcohol content increased significantly in the CO, PA, and SX groups. Notably, alcohol levels were significantly higher in the CO and SX groups than in the PA group (p < 0.05), a difference primarily driven by greater enrichment of phenylethyl alcohol in these two groups. Phenylethyl alcohol is a natural flavor compound widely present in various fruits, imparting a characteristic fruity and rosy aroma. It is also an approved food flavoring in China (Huang et al., 2024). The lower level of phenylethyl alcohol in the PA group may be attributed to the preferential utilization of glucose by lactic acid bacteria for glycolysis (Martinussen et al., 2013), which generates lactic acid and energy to maintain their growth advantage, thereby reducing the carbon flux toward phenylethyl alcohol biosynthesis. Previous research has confirmed that Li et al. (2025) successfully enhanced both the flavor and quality of Baijiu by screening Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains with high phenylethyl alcohol production for fermentation. The significant increase in phenylethyl alcohol content in the CO and SX groups is the key reason for their fish sauce samples exhibiting a sweet aroma.

Amines are a class of compounds widely present in aquatic products and were detected in all groups in this study. Their formation primarily relies on the catalysis of amino acid decarboxylation by microbial-secreted amino acid decarboxylases, and most amines tend to impart undesirable flavors to products. The PA group exhibited lower amine levels relative to the FH group, indicating that lactic acid bacteria fermentation can effectively reduce the accumulation of amines in aquatic products (Ma et al., 2024). In contrast, the amine content in the CO and SX groups increased to varying degrees. This may be attributed to the specific metabolic activities of non-lactic acid bacterial strains within the system, whose metabolic pathways likely promoted the forward progression of amino acid decarboxylation.

Ketones are key contributors to food flavor systems, imparting sweet, fruity, and other aromatic notes (Liang et al., 2024). In this study, ketone content was significantly higher in the SX group compared to the FH, CO, and PA groups (p < 0.05), a difference primarily attributed to the higher accumulation of 2-methyltetrahydrofuran-3-one. This compound exhibits bread-like, sweet, and nutty aromas (Yang et al., 2025) and is commonly present in various food matrices.

Hydrocarbons are primarily generated through lipid oxidation and degradation. Most have relatively high odor thresholds and, overall, contribute less to food aroma compared to other compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, and esters (Li et al., 2022). The hydrocarbon content in the fermented CO, PA, and SX groups showed an increase compared to the FH group, indicating that the fermentation process promoted the oxidative degradation of lipids in the fish sauce substrate to some extent.

ROAV analysis

ROAV is a key indicator for assessing the contribution of individual volatile metabolites to the overall aroma of a sample (Fang et al., 2024). Volatile metabolites with ROAV ≥1 were defined as key odor-active metabolites contributing significantly to the characteristic aroma of fish sauce, whereas those with ROAV values ranging from 0.1 to 1 also exerted a modulatory effect on the overall aroma profile(Wang et al., 2025). Accordingly, a total of 37 volatile metabolites that contributed to the overall aroma of fish sauce were identified based on the threshold of ROAV ≥0.1 (Table 1). These metabolites were grouped into 10 chemical classes comprising 11 aldehydes, 6 ketones, 6 esters, and other minor chemical classes. Among them, 2,3-diethyl-5-methylpyrazine, 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, and (3E,5Z)-1,3,5-undecatriene exhibited high contribution levels (ROAV >1) across all four groups, serving as the key odor-active metabolites shared by all samples. These three metabolites contributed baked, roasted (Nguyen & Munafo, 2025), and pineapple (Kelley & Cadwallader, 2018) aromas, respectively. Notably elevated concentrations of ethyl isovalerate were detected in the CO and PA groups, which contributed prominent fruity notes to the overall aroma profile of the corresponding fish sauce samples (Lu et al., 2021; Niu et al., 2020).

Pyrazine compounds (2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine and 2,3-diethyl-5-methylpyrazine) contribute roasted and nutty aromas to fish sauce. Their formation relies on the Strecker degradation of amino acids and subsequent condensation during the Maillard reaction (Wang et al., 2025). As shown in the ROAV heatmap (Fig. 3D), the relative levels of these pyrazines were significantly elevated in the SX group following fermentation. Although the excess accumulation intensified the characteristic nutty note, it masked the delicate fruity and floral aromas contributed by esters and aldehydes, leading to a coarser and less layered flavor profile (Dong et al., 2025). In contrast, the CO and PA groups contained moderate levels of pyrazines, which contributed to better flavor harmony and superior sensory quality. 2-aminoacetophenone, a ketone with grape-like aroma, is closely associated with microbial metabolism of tryptophan (Alvarez-Fernandez et al., 2019). It balances the salty and savory base notes of fish sauce while enhancing flavor complexity. Its content was higher in all fermented groups (CO, PA, SX) than in the unfermented FH group. (3E,5Z)-1,3,5-undecatriene exhibits an extremely low odor threshold and consistently high ROAV values across all four groups, contributing a pronounced pineapple aroma. Its concentration was higher in all fermentation groups compared to the FH group, indicating that fermentation promotes lipid oxidation to generate this compound. This establishes a fresh foundational flavor profile for fish sauce. Additionally, (E,E)-3,5-octadien-2-one and methyl 2-methylbutyrate exhibited significantly higher ROAV values in the CO group than in the single-strain fermentation groups (PA and SX), and these two compounds contributed a rich fruity note to the overall aroma profile of the fish sauce (Xiao et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2018). Simultaneously, the waxy scent of decanal, the cheesy note of isovaleric acid, and the fatty aroma of (2E,4Z)-2,4-decadienal synergistically interacted in the CO group (Chen et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2020), enriching the aromatic complexity and creating a more intricate and harmonious overall flavor profile.

Identification of key differential volatile metabolites

To investigate the metabolic mechanisms and flavor formation principles of co-fermentation, this study conducted a systematic analysis of the differential volatile metabolites between the CO and FH groups. Principal component 1 explained 62.9% of the variance in the OPLS-DA model (Fig. 4A), and clear separation was achieved between the two groups, indicating that co-fermentation significantly altered the volatile flavor profile of the fish sauce. As shown in the permutation test (Fig. 4B), the model exhibited a high explanatory power (R2Y = 0.999) and predictive ability (Q2 = 0.979), with both indices being close to 1. Both values approached 1, and the intercept of the Q2 regression line on the vertical axis was negative, indicating that the model possessed good explanatory and predictive capabilities without overfitting, and could thus be reliably used for the subsequent screening and identification of differential flavor markers.

Analysis of differential volatile metabolites between the CO and FH groups. (A) OPLS-DA score plot; (B) OPLS-DA permutation test plot; (C) Volcano plot of CO vs FH groups; (D) Venn diagram; (E) Heatmap of 16 key differential volatile metabolites.

A volcano plot was generated using the screening criteria |log2FC| > 1, VIP > 1, and p < 0.05 (Fig. 4C). Compared with the FH group, the CO group contained 343 upregulated and 48 downregulated differential metabolites. The much higher number of upregulated substances suggests that more diverse metabolites were generated during fermentation due to microbial conversion of substrates.

Through Venn diagram analysis, volatile metabolites with ROAV ≥0.1 in both the FH and CO groups were intersected with differential compounds identified in the CO vs FH volcano plot that met the criteria of |log2FC| > 1, VIP > 1, and p < 0.05. This intersection analysis ultimately identified 16 key volatile metabolites that decisively contributed to the flavor differences between the two groups (Fig. 4D). These metabolites are classified into 7 chemical classes, comprising 4 aldehydes, 3 esters, 3 alcohols, 2 ketones, 2 organic acids and their derivatives, 1 phenol, and 1 ether (Fig. 4E). The majority of them showed a significant increase in abundance after fermentation. Aldehydes constituted the highest proportion among the key differential volatile metabolites. Compared with the FH group, three aldehydes ((E)-2-nonenal, (E)-2-undecenal, and 2-nonenal) showed markedly higher relative abundances in the CO group. (E)-2-nonenal is characterized by intense green and cucumber-like notes (Wang et al., 2025), (E)-2-undecenal imparts fruity notes (Shi et al., 2025), whereas 2-nonenal exhibits fatty flavor and serves as a vital aromatic constituent in meat products (Li et al., 2023). The accumulation of these aldehydes effectively diversified the aroma profile of the CO group and alleviated the monotony of a single flavor. Both isovaleric acid and pentanoic acid are typical cheesy flavor compounds in fish sauce. The higher content of pentanoic acid in the CO group is likely attributed to the conversion and generation by lactic acid bacteria through metabolizing abundant substrates (Liu et al., 2024). Ethyl isovalerate, a characteristically pleasant, fruity-scented compound, was present at significantly higher levels in the CO group compared to the FH group (p < 0.05), imparting a fresh, fruity aroma to the CO group (Jia et al., 2024). O-Cresol imparts phenolic and smoky notes, aromas commonly associated with traditionally smoked foods (Huang et al., 2022). Following fermentation, its concentration showed a slight increase in the CO group. This modest rise helped mitigate potential flavor deterioration while preserving a subtle smoky character.

Overall, these 16 key differential volatile metabolites are the pivotal components driving the flavor differences between the CO and FH groups during fish sauce fermentation. By enriching specific volatile metabolites and achieving a balanced distribution across various volatile metabolite classes, the CO group realized a significant enhancement in flavor quality.

Non-volatile metabolites analysis of uninoculated versus single/mixed strain fermentation of low-salt fish sauce

Compositional analysis of non-volatile metabolites

Non-volatile metabolites are key chemical components that determine the taste attributes and core nutritional functions of food (Kim et al., 2023). They serve as both direct contributors to the umami taste of fish sauce and crucial precursors for volatile metabolites. Their compositional characteristics directly define the flavor complexity and quality attributes of fish sauce. In this study, untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed on fish sauce samples from the FH, CO, PA, and SX groups. A total of 8203 metabolites were identified in both positive and negative ion modes, classified into 24 categories (Fig. 5A). Amino acids and their metabolites constituted the largest category (28.29%), providing precursors for the umami and sweet tastes of fish sauce. This result provides direct metabolomic evidence for the metabolic pathway in fish sauce fermentation that centers on protein degradation to produce delicious amino acids. The second largest category, benzene and its derivatives (14.86%), most likely originates from the microbial transformation of aromatic amino acids. These compounds serve as precursors for flavor substances such as phenols and aromatic acids. The third largest category, heterocyclic compounds (11.33%), is closely associated with the Maillard reaction and Strecker degradation, serving as the primary source of roasted and nutty flavors such as those from pyrazines and furans. Additionally, aldehydes, ketones, and esters (8.24%) together with organic acids (6.69%) constitute a crucial flavor foundation. The former contributes fruity and creamy notes, while the latter directly influences the system's acidity and helps balance the overall flavor. Although accounting for a relatively small proportion, lipid-related metabolites such as fatty acids and glycerophospholipids also exert a potential influence on the flavor's lingering quality, richness, and overall harmony.

Compositional analysis of non-volatile metabolites. (A) relative abundance heatmap analysis; (B) PCA analysis (Ellipses represent 95% confidence intervals around group centroids).

PCA analysis

PCA analysis was conducted on the non-volatile metabolites to assess compositional differences among the fish sauce samples from the FH, CO, PA, and SX groups. In the resulting score plot (Fig. 5B), the two principal components, PC1 (31.1%) and PC2 (26.72%), together explained 57.82% of the total variance, representing the major metabolic features across the different groups. The QC samples clustered tightly together and were distinctly separated from all experimental groups, indicating stable and reliable metabolomic detection. Clear separation was observed between different groups, indicating highly distinct metabolite profiles and confirming that both the FH group and the fish sauce produced under different inoculation treatments possess unique metabolic characteristics.

Screening and analysis of differential non-volatile metabolites

(1) OPLS-DA analysis.

To identify differential metabolites among different fish sauce samples, pairwise OPLS-DA analyses were conducted for the CO vs FH, CO vs PA, and CO vs SX groups. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1(A1-E1), sample points across groups were clearly separated into left and right clusters, demonstrating excellent discrimination performance. Supplementary Fig. S1(A2-E2) indicates that the three models achieved an R2Y value of 1 for the Y matrix, with prediction accuracies (Q2) of 0.977, 0.938, and 0.958, respectively. These values, all close to 1, demonstrate high model fitting capability and predictive accuracy across the three groups.

(2) Screening of differential metabolites.

Volcano plots were generated using the screening criteria |log2FC| > 1, VIP > 1, and p < 0.05 to identify differential metabolites (Fig. 6A-C). A total of 3411, 1991, and 2776 differential metabolites were identified in the CO vs FH, CO vs PA, and CO vs SX comparisons, respectively. Among these, 1531, 960, and 1085 were upregulated, while 1880, 1031, and 1691 were downregulated.

Analysis of Differential Non-Volatile Metabolites. (A-C) Volcano plots for the CO vs FH, CO vs PA, and CO vs SX comparisons, respectively; (D—F) Heatmaps of the top 20 key differential non-volatile metabolites with VIP > 1 for the CO vs FH, CO vs PA, and CO vs SX comparisons, respectively.

(3) Analysis of key differentiating non-volatile metabolites.

Based on VIP values, the top 20 key differential non-volatile metabolites from the CO vs FH, CO vs PA, and CO vs SX comparisons were identified and visualized in heatmaps (Fig. 6D-F). Among the 20 differential non-volatile metabolites identified in the CO vs FH comparison, all showed a downregulated trend in abundance in the CO group, with amino acids and their metabolites representing the largest proportion. This suggests that during fermentation, microorganisms degrade proteins and metabolize amino acids to generate various flavor compounds (Li et al., 2023). Small peptides, as important flavor precursors, can be further degraded or participate in reactions to contribute to flavor formation (Saud et al., 2024; Shang et al., 2025), thereby enriching the flavor complexity of the fish sauce. The downregulation of nucleotides and its metabolites may result from the depletion of carbon sources and nutrients during the late fermentation stage, prompting microorganisms to absorb and degrade nucleotides such as guanosine and inosine as alternative carbon and nitrogen sources (Petridis et al., 2015). In the CO vs PA group, the vast majority of differential compounds exhibited higher abundance in the CO group, including flavor compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, esters, and alcohols, indicating a significant advantage of co-fermentation in flavor compound accumulation. In the CO vs SX group, the abundance of certain heterocyclic compounds decreased in the CO group, consistent with volatile metabolite analysis results. This is presumed to be related to the inhibition of heterocyclic compound biosynthesis by lactic acid bacteria (Feng et al., 2024). Simultaneously, the abundance of secondary metabolites such as alkaloids and benzene derivatives also decreased in the CO group, most of which exhibit undesirable flavors (Heravi et al., 2018). These results indicate that co-fermentation significantly alters the composition of non-volatile metabolites in fish sauce, promoting the accumulation of flavor compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, and esters. Simultaneously, it reduces the abundance of undesirable flavor-related metabolites such as alkaloids, thereby comprehensively optimizing the flavor profile of fish sauce. This may be attributed to the fact that P. acidilactici produces acids during fermentation, which lowers the pH (Zhang et al., 2023), inhibiting the growth of spoilage bacteria and off-flavor-producing microorganisms and thus reducing the formation of bitter and putrid odor compounds. Meanwhile, these organic acids serve as abundant precursors for esterification reactions (Xiang et al., 2023), thereby increasing ester content. S. xylosus, as a coagulase-negative staphylococcus, secretes proteases that are relatively stable under weakly acidic conditions (Wang et al., 2022). These proteases accelerate protein degradation, generating various flavor compounds and their precursors (Sun et al., 2023).

Metabolic regulation and microbial association analysis of fish sauce under mixed-strain fermentation

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was used to perform a combined pathway enrichment analysis on the 16 volatile and 20 non-volatile differential metabolites screened from the CO vs FH comparison (Fig. 7A). Pathways such as ABC transporters, purine metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, protein digestion and absorption, and D-amino acid metabolism were highly enriched with significant differences. The activity of the ABC transporter pathway reflects an enhanced efficiency in microbial transmembrane substance transport during fermentation, ensuring a smoother supply of substrates for intracellular metabolism and more efficient efflux of metabolic products (Hao et al., 2023). Purine metabolism is a central pathway for nucleotide synthesis and degradation (Song et al., 2024), and nucleotides are essential components of RNA and DNA (Dong et al., 2019). The activation of this pathway reflects that microorganisms in the fermentation system are in a highly active state of growth and reproduction, requiring rapid synthesis of nucleic acids and ATP to support cell division (Li et al., 2021). Inosine monophosphate (IMP) and guanosine monophosphate (GMP) generated by the nucleotide metabolic network serve as potent umami enhancers, exhibiting umami intensity far surpassing that of monosodium glutamate (Mun et al., 2022). These compounds effectively enrich the umami complexity of fish sauce. The protein digestion and absorption pathway promotes amino acid generation, thereby regulating the flavor of fish sauce, which aligns with the findings reported by Zhang et al. (2025) in their study on flavor differences in Jinhua ham. D-Amino acids are key components of microbial peptidoglycan and are closely associated with cell wall synthesis (Lin et al., 2023). The enrichment of their metabolic pathways further corroborates the vigorous growth state of microorganisms during fermentation. Some D-amino acids (e.g., D-alanine) exhibit sweet taste properties (Ardalani et al., 2024), and lactic acid bacteria, as probiotics, are capable of synthesizing these compounds, which exert positive effects on the taste and flavor profiles of fermented foods (Sugahara et al., 2021). Microbial community analysis revealed a high abundance of Lactiplantibacillus in the CO group, which improves fish sauce flavor by secreting D-amino acids. The accumulation of these D-amino acids not only directly imparts a pleasant sweet note but also effectively neutralizes the bitter and astringent notes arising from excessive protein degradation. Furthermore, it exerts a marked synergistic effect with umami substances such as IMP and GMP, significantly enhancing the depth and harmony of the overall flavor profile of fish sauce.

Integrated analysis of differential metabolites and microbial taxa in co-fermentation. (A) KEGG enrichment analysis of the 40 differential metabolites; (B) Heatmap showing integrated analysis of differential metabolites and microbial taxa (* indicates statistical significance, p < 0.05).

The protein digestion and absorption pathway degrades proteins to generate free amino acids, which serve as the core substrates for other metabolic pathways. Purine metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and D-amino acid metabolism utilize these substrates to synthesize flavor compounds. The ABC transporter pathway exports intracellularly synthesized flavor compounds into the fermentation broth. Concurrently, ATP generated by nucleotide metabolism provides energy for the ABC transporter pathway, forming a positive feedback loop. Co-fermentation significantly enhances the flavor profile and taste complexity of low-salt fish sauce through multiple pathways, including material transport, umami compound production, amino acid supply, and sweetness formation, by leveraging the aforementioned core metabolic pathways.

These results indicate that, as the dominant genera in the CO group, increased abundances of Pediococcus and Lactiplantibacillus promote the formation of flavor compounds such as aldehydes and esters through their intrinsic esterase and lipase activities as well as via amino acid Strecker degradation. Concurrently, they suppress the growth of undesirable bacteria like Bacillus, thereby reducing the production of undesirable flavor compounds such as heterocyclic compounds. The negative correlations between Pediococcus/Lactiplantibacillus and non-volatile metabolites reflect that during their proliferation, these genera actively utilize nucleotides, small peptides, and certain amino acids as carbon and nitrogen sources to fulfill nutritional requirements for growth and reproduction, thereby leading to the downregulation of these non-volatile metabolites.

In summary, correlation analysis between key genera and metabolites reveals that the increased abundances of Pediococcus and Lactiplantibacillus play a central regulatory role in driving the accumulation of volatile metabolites like aldehydes and esters in the CO group, consuming non-volatile nutrient substrates to sustain their own metabolic activity, while simultaneously suppressing the proliferation of spoilage bacteria such as Bacillus and reducing the formation of off-flavor compounds, including heterocyclics. This provides key insights into the molecular mechanisms by which co-fermentation regulates the flavor quality of fish sauce.

Conclusion

In this study, co-fermentation of P. acidilactici and S. xylosus at a 1:2 ratio enhanced the flavor quality of low-salt fish sauce. The CO group exhibited distinct differences from the PA, SX, and FH groups in microbial community structure as well as in the composition of both volatile and non-volatile metabolites. Microbial amplicon sequencing results indicated that Lactiplantibacillus and Pediococcus were the core dominant genera in the CO group. Analysis of volatile and non-volatile metabolites revealed that co-fermentation induced microorganisms to utilize non-volatile substrates such as nucleotides and amino acids, while enriching characteristic flavor compounds including aldehydes, ketones, and esters. These key differential metabolites were significantly enriched in pathways such as ABC transporters and purine/nucleotide metabolism, and their levels were significantly correlated with changes in microbial community structure. In conclusion, co-fermentation of PA and SX can effectively improve the flavor quality of low-salt fish sauce by modulating the microbial community structure and mediating the activation of key metabolic pathways, thereby providing a theoretical basis and technical support for the process and flavor optimization of low-salt fermented fish sauce.
