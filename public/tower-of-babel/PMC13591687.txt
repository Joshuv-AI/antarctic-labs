Comparative efficacy of twice-daily 0.01% atropine combined with Orthokeratology versus DIMS lenses in myopia control: an elastic net penalized regression analysis
Authors: Guoli Zheng, Yi Xu, Qingqing Li, Fenghua Chen, Chang Liu, Xuefei Lou, Peimi Wu, Xuehui Wu, Jun Mao, Kunchao Wu, Zongyan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing prevalence of childhood myopia necessitates effective intervention strategies. This study compared the 1-year efficacy of twice-daily 0.01% atropine combined with either Orthokeratology (OKA) or Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (DIMSA) lenses in controlling axial length (AL) elongation, and utilized Elastic Net penalized regression to identify key predictive factors.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 220 myopic children (220 eyes), divided into the DIMSA group (n=120) and the OKA group (n=100). Axial length changes (ΔAL) over 12 months were evaluated. Multivariable linear regression and subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the independent associations. Additionally, propensity score methods and E-value sensitivity analyses were employed to address potential treatment-selection bias and unmeasured confounding. An Elastic Net regression model with 5-fold cross-validation was employed to handle multi-collinearity and perform feature selection for potential predictors of ΔAL.ResultsThe OKA group demonstrated significantly slower AL elongation than the DIMSA group (0.157 ± 0.154 mm vs. 0.313 ± 0.221 mm, P < 0.001). Multivariable regression indicated that the DIMSA regimen was associated with greater AL elongation compared with the OKA regimen (β= 0.144, P < 0.001). This association remained robust across all propensity score models, with high E-values (> 5.0) indicating strong resistance to unmeasured confounding. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests revealed a consistent association of OKA with less axial elongation across different age and myopia severity strata. Elastic Net regression identified shorter baseline AL and younger age as the primary predictors of faster progression. Notably, the Elastic Net models revealed distinct predictive architectures: the OKA group exhibited a parsimonious pattern primarily associated with age and baseline AL, whereas the DIMSA group displayed a "multi-driver" pattern associated with broader baseline covariates.ConclusionsThe combination of twice-daily 0.01% atropine with OKA was associated with less axial elongation in this retrospective cohort compared to the DIMSA regimen. Elastic Net regression suggested distinct predictive structures for each treatment, providing a basis for hypothesis generation.Keywords: Myopia, Atropine, Orthokeratology, Defocus incorporated multiple segments

Introduction

In recent decades, the widespread use of electronic devices has been accompanied by a sharp increase in the global prevalence of myopia among adolescents. Projections indicate that by 2050, nearly half of the world’s population will be affected by this condition. Insufficient management of myopia during childhood significantly elevates the risk of developing pathological myopia, which can lead to sight-threatening complications such as choroidal neovascularization, retinal detachment, and posterior staphyloma. By that time, the proportion of individuals globally with high myopia may reach as high as 10%. Therefore, it is crucial to develop and implement more effective and personalized strategies for the timely control of myopia progression [1–3]. Current approaches to myopia management primarily involve optical interventions, including spectacles and contact lenses, as well as pharmacological treatments.

In China, atropine eye drops are currently the most widely used pharmacological intervention for the prevention of myopia progression. Although the exact mechanism by which atropine slows axial elongation is not fully understood, it is well established that its effect is not mediated primarily through ciliary muscle relaxation (accommodation). Current evidence suggests that atropine may act on non-muscarinic pathways, potentially involving retinal dopamine release, scleral remodeling, and choroidal thickening [4–6]. Numerous studies have shown that the efficacy of atropine in slowing myopia progression is concentration-dependent. Specifically, atropine at concentrations of 0.02%, 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.1%, and 0.5% has been shown to be more effective than 0.01% atropine in delaying the progression of spherical equivalent refraction (SER) and axial length (AL) [7–10]. However, higher concentrations of atropine eye drops are more likely to negatively impact children’s quality of life by affecting accommodation and pupil diameter. Long-term follow-up studies indicate that, compared to other concentrations, 0.01% atropine represents a favorable balance between safety and efficacy, with the weakest rebound effect after discontinuation and minimal side effects [10]. Conventionally administered once daily at the standard dose, 0.01% atropine eye drops maintain a therapeutic concentration in the scleral tissue for approximately 12 to 16 h. However, the efficacy of 0.01% atropine for myopia control is often insufficient in some children. Adopting a twice-daily dosing frequency may enhance its efficacy in controlling myopia progression by prolonging its duration of action within the sclera.

Orthokeratology (OK) and Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (DIMS) are commonly employed optical methods for myopia control.

Orthokeratology, as the most widely utilized overnight contact lens modality, temporarily flattens the central corneal curvature through the hydrodynamic forces of the tear film beneath the lens during sleep, thereby correcting myopia and allowing patients to achieve good unaided daytime visual acuity. More importantly, the steepening of the mid-peripheral cornea induced by the reverse zone design focuses peripheral light rays anterior to the retina, creating a “peripheral retinal myopic defocus” that effectively suppresses excessive axial elongation [11–13]. Long-term studies have demonstrated that OK significantly delays AL elongation compared to single-vision spectacles (SVS) [14].

DIMS lenses ensure clear central vision while utilizing a densely distributed peripheral micro-lens array to create a sustained myopic defocus signal on the peripheral retina. This inhibits the signaling pathways that stimulate excessive ocular axial elongation [15]. DIMS lenses are increasingly adopted in clinical practice due to their non-invasive nature, ease of use, and absence of contact lens-related risks such as ocular infections [16, 17]. A randomized controlled trial in 2020 established the efficacy of DIMS lenses for myopia control, demonstrating a 52% reduction in myopia progression and a 62% reduction of AL elongation compared to SVS in children [15]. While previous studies have primarily compared the efficacy of SVS with OK or DIMS, direct comparisons between OK and DIMS remain limited, particularly concerning combination therapies involving 0.01% atropine—especially the twice-daily utilization adopted in this study. The present retrospective analysis aims to evaluate and compare the effects of combining twice-daily 0.01% atropine with either OK or DIMS on myopia progression in children. Furthermore, this study employs an Elastic Net Penalized Regression model to explore potential clinical parameters predictive of the AL elongation in each treatment group.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective study analyzed clinical data from 220 myopic children (220 eyes) treated at the Department of Ophthalmology, the First People’s Hospital of Guiyang, between June 2023 and July 2024. Participants were assigned to one of two combination therapy groups. This was a retrospective study without random assignment. The allocation to the Orthokeratology combined with atropine (OKA) group or the DIMS combined with atropine (DIMSA) group was determined through a shared decision-making process involving the prescribing physician and the children’s guardians. The choice was primarily guided by a combination of clinical suitability and family preferences.

DIMSA: DIMS + 0.01% atropine twice daily.

OKA: OK + 0.01% atropine twice daily.

All subjects completed a minimum follow-up period of 12 months. The primary outcome was the change in AL after one year of treatment. Data were subsequently obtained from outpatient refractive and orthokeratology electronic medical records. The retrospective study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Guiyang on July 30, 2025 (Approval No. 2025-S101), prior to data extraction. Because the research involved the retrospective analysis of anonymized, pre-existing clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

In this study, to avoid potential bias in statistical inference due to interocular correlation, we adopted a monocular inclusion criterion. Specifically, when both eyes of a participant met the inclusion criteria, we systematically selected data from the right eye for analysis.

Eye examinations

Pre-treatment assessments included intraocular pressure (IOP), spherical and cylindrical refraction, AL, corneal topography, and slit-lamp examination. IOP was measured three times using a non-contact tonometer (TX-20P, Canon, Japan) and averaged. Subjective cycloplegic refraction was performed using a phoropter (RT-5100, NIDEK, Japan) after pupillary dilation with compound tropicamide eye drops administered every 10 min for three doses. Cycloplegia was considered complete when pupil diameter reached ≥ 8 mm or the light reflex was absent. Spherical and cylindrical powers were measured three times and averaged. The spherical equivalent refraction (SER) was calculated as sphere + ½ cylinder. Axial length (AL) was measured three times using the IOLMaster 700 (ZEISS, Jena, Germany) and averaged. To minimize diurnal and mechanical confounders, all measurements were standardized to the morning (8:00 AM – 11:00 AM); for the OKA group, measurements were performed 2–4 h after overnight lens removal to allow corneal stabilization. Readings were validated only under high quality-control criteria (SNR > 2.0, intra-visit SD < 0.02 mm) by an experienced technician masked to the treatment groups. Corneal topography was performed using a corneal topographer (Keratograph 70900, OCULUS, Wetzlar, Germany) to obtain parameters relevant to orthokeratology lens fitting. Corneal topography was performed prior to non-contact tonometry to avoid any influence of the air puff on corneal curvature measurements. The mean K was calculated as ½ (Flat K + Steep K).

The primary efficacy endpoint was the difference in AL elongation between the two groups after one year of treatment. Furthermore, subjects were categorized based on ΔAL into three groups: axial shortening (ΔAL < 0 mm), stable (0 ≤ ΔAL < 0.2 mm), and progression (ΔAL ≥ 0.2 mm) [18]. Adverse events related to lens wear were monitored in both groups, including assessment of pupil diameter, accommodative function, and slit-lamp findings such as corneal punctate staining, graded according to the method described by Terry et al. [19]. All examinations were performed by experienced operators and professionally qualified optometrists.

Myopia control program

The Orthokeratology lenses employed in this study were Paragon CRT lenses (USA). The lens material featured an oxygen permeability (Dk) of 100, a light transmittance of 95%, a refractive index of 1.442, a wetting angle of 42°, and a specific gravity of 1.1. The base curve optical zone diameter (BOZD) is 5.00 mm. Participants were instructed to wear the lenses nightly for at least 8 h. Follow-up assessments, including visual acuity, axial length changes, corneal topography changes (e.g., treatment zone characteristics and centration), and potential adverse events, were conducted on day 1, week 1, week 2, month 1, month 3, and every 2–3 months thereafter.

The DIMS used in this study were the Xinlexue® lenses (HOYA®), developed by The Hong Kong Polytechnic University and based on Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (D.I.M.S.) technology. The lens design features a peripheral zone approximately 33 mm in diameter containing 396 microlenses, each introducing + 3.50 D of defocus to generate multifocal myopic defocus signals. The center of the lens comprised a hexagonal clear optical zone with a diameter of approximately 9 mm. All lenses were fitted according to standard clinical protocols. We explicitly instructed patients in the DIMSA group to maintain full-time spectacle wear (≥ 12 h/day), including during near work. Patients who removed their DIMS lenses for reading due to near-blur were excluded as non-compliant, ensuring that the evaluated DIMSA cohort received continuous peripheral defocus during both distance and near tasks. Follow-up examinations were conducted every three months.

Both groups received 0.01% atropine eye drops (Shenyang Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China) twice daily, with an average interval of 12 h between administrations.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using Python3.11 (scipy 1.14, statsmodels 0.14, scikit-learn 1.5, pandas 2.2, matplotlib 3.9). For baseline comparisons, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between the two groups using the independent-samples t-test (with Welch’s correction applied when Levene’s test indicated unequal variances). Skewed variables were presented as medians [interquartile ranges, IQRs] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated to evaluate baseline covariate balance, with an SMD ≥ 0.1 considered indicative of a meaningful imbalance [20]. Intra-group changes in axial length (AL) before and after treatment within the OKA and DIMSA groups were evaluated using paired-samples t-tests. For inter-group analysis, differences in AL change (ΔAL) were compared using the independent-samples t-test, with Cohen’s d calculated to estimate the effect size. The Mann-Whitney U test was also employed as a sensitivity analysis. The distribution and differences in ΔAL between the groups were visualized using box plots with superimposed scatter points. Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying subjects according to age (≤ 10 years vs. > 10 years, based on the median age of the cohort) and baseline myopia severity (low myopia: SER > -3.0 D; moderate myopia: SER ≤ -3.0 D). The inter-group mean differences in axial length change and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated within each stratum and presented using forest plots. Potential interaction effects were tested by incorporating interaction terms (Treatment Group × Age strata, and Treatment Group × Myopia strata) into the linear regression models. A multiple linear regression model was constructed with ΔAL as the dependent variable. Independent variables included the treatment group (DIMSA = 1, OKA = 0), age, sex, baseline AL, and baseline covariates demonstrating imbalance (SMD ≥ 0.1). To circumvent multicollinearity, flat and steep corneal curvatures were averaged and incorporated as Mean K. Unstandardized regression coefficients (β), 95% CIs, and P-values were reported. Predictor selection was performed utilizing an Elastic Net penalized regression model. A 5-fold cross-validation (CV) approach was used to determine the optimal hyperparameters: a grid search was conducted for the L1-ratio over the set (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9), and alpha was tuned across 50 logarithmically spaced candidate values from logspace (-4, 1). The model was fitted after standardizing the predictor variables, and features with non-zero coefficients were reported. Concurrently, Pearson correlation coefficients between each predictor and ΔAL were calculated as descriptive measures of association. To evaluate the stability of hyperparameter selection, the CV process was repeated 50 times using random seeds (0 to 49), reporting the mean ± SD of the model metrics. To minimize potential treatment-selection bias inherent in the non-randomized design, sensitivity analyses were conducted utilizing four propensity score (PS) methods. The PS was estimated via logistic regression encompassing baseline covariates: age, sex, height, Pre-AL, IOP, BCVA, sphere, cylinder, SER, flat K, and steep K. The four methods included: [1] Propensity Score Matching (PSM): 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching on the logit of the PS with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations, followed by covariate-adjusted weighted least squares (WLS) regression [21]; [2] Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW): utilizing stabilized weights trimmed at [0.05, 0.95] with HC1 robust standard errors; [3] Overlap Weighting (OW): weighting the treated group by 1 − PS and the control group by PS; and [4] PS Covariate Adjustment. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD) and visualized via Love plots. Furthermore, to quantify the minimum strength of an unmeasured confounder required to fully explain away the observed inter-group differences, E-values were calculated following the methodology by VanderWeele and Ding [22]. Cohen’s d was converted to an odds ratio (OR = exp(π·d/√3)) to derive the E-value. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics between groups

A total of 220 children (220 eyes) were included in this study. The DIMSA group comprised 120 subjects (120 eyes), and the OKA group consisted of 100 subjects (100 eyes). No statistically significant differences were observed between the DIMSA and OKA groups across baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including sex, age, height, BCVA, IOP, spherical power, cylindrical power, SER, baseline AL, flat corneal curvature, and steep corneal curvature (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). Standardized mean difference (SMD) analysis revealed that the SMDs for all baseline variables ranged between 0.052 and 0.225. Specifically, flat K (SMD = 0.225), steep K (SMD = 0.217), baseline sphere (SMD = 0.194), SER (SMD = 0.191), and sex (SMD = 0.157) exhibited SMDs > 0.1, indicating a mild baseline imbalance. Other variables, including age (SMD = 0.052), baseline AL (SMD = 0.094), and IOP (SMD = 0.084), demonstrated good inter-group balance. Although these differences did not reach conventional statistical significance (P > 0.05), according to the predefined threshold, they were identified as potential confounders and were consequently adjusted for in the subsequent multivariable regression models.

Comparison of clinical efficacy between DIMSA and OKA

At the 12-month follow-up, axial length (AL) in the DIMSA group increased from a baseline of 25.00 ± 0.81 mm to 25.31 ± 0.77 mm, with a mean axial elongation (ΔAL) of 0.313 ± 0.221 mm (P < 0.001). In the OKA group, AL changed from a baseline of 25.07 ± 0.72 mm to 25.23 ± 0.68 mm, with a mean ΔAL of 0.157 ± 0.154 mm (P < 0.001). This indicates that both combination regimens were associated with significant axial progression over one year.

The OKA group exhibited significantly less axial elongation than the DIMSA group (0.157 ± 0.154 mm vs. 0.313 ± 0.221 mm; t = 6.057, P < 0.001) (Table 2; Fig. 1). The mean inter-group difference was 0.156 mm (95% CI: 0.106 to 0.206 mm), indicating that the OKA group experienced 0.156 mm less axial growth on average than the DIMSA group. The calculated effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 0.808). Sensitivity analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test (conducted due to potential non-normal distribution of ΔAL in the OKA group) also confirmed a significant inter-group difference (P < 0.001), demonstrating that the findings were robust and independent of distribution assumptions.

The categorical distribution of AL changes differed significantly between the two groups (χ²= 15.292, P < 0.001). Patients were classified based on ΔAL into three categories: axial shortening (ΔAL < 0 mm), stable (0 ≤ ΔAL < 0.2 mm), and progression (ΔAL ≥ 0.2 mm). The DIMSA group was predominantly characterized by progression (78/120, 65.0%), followed by stable (37/120, 30.8%), and axial shortening (5/120, 4.2%). In contrast, the OKA group showed a significantly lower proportion of progression (40/100, 40.0%), a higher proportion of stable cases (47/100, 47.0%), and 13 cases of axial shortening (13.0%) (Fig. 2).

Categorical distribution of axial length changes after 1 year of treatment

A multivariable linear regression model was constructed with ΔAL as the dependent variable. The model yielded an R² of 0.293 (adjusted R² = 0.276), explaining 29.3% of the variance in ΔAL. After adjusting for age, sex, baseline AL, and Mean K, the treatment group allocation (DIMSA = 1, reference OKA = 0) remained a significant independent predictor of greater axial elongation (β = 0.144, 95% CI: 0.096 to 0.192, P < 0.001). This indicates that the DIMSA group experienced 0.144 mm more axial growth than the OKA group after adjustment, which is closely aligned with the unadjusted difference (0.156 mm), suggesting that the observed inter-group disparity was not primarily attributable to baseline covariate imbalances.

Subgroup analysis

In the age-stratified analysis, the inter-group difference in ΔAL was 0.150 mm (95% CI: 0.091 to 0.210) for the ≤ 10 years subgroup (DIMSA: n = 79, OKA: n = 69) and 0.181 mm (95% CI: 0.102 to 0.260) for the > 10 years subgroup (DIMSA: n = 41, OKA: n = 31). Regarding myopia severity, the inter-group difference was 0.157 mm (95% CI: 0.097 to 0.217) for the low myopia subgroup (DIMSA: n = 78, OKA: n = 69) and 0.159 mm (95% CI: 0.070 to 0.248) for the moderate myopia subgroup (DIMSA: n = 42, OKA: n = 31). The direction of effect was consistent across all subgroups, and no 95% CIs crossed zero, indicating that the OKA regimen was consistently associated with less axial elongation than DIMSA in all strata (Fig. 3). Interaction tests showed that the interaction between group and age strata (P = 0.630) and between group and myopia severity (P = 0.889) were not statistically significant. These findings indicate that the difference in axial elongation between the two combination therapies remained stable across different age and myopia levels, suggesting that the greater association of OKA with reduced elongation compared to DIMSA is not modified by patient age or baseline refractive status. These subgroup analyses are exploratory. The lack of statistically significant interaction does not prove equivalence across subgroups, but it supports the consistency of the treatment effect direction.

Predictive performance and feature selection via elastic net regression

Elastic Net regression was employed to explore the relative associations of various candidate predictors for ΔAL. Due to the high multicollinearity between height and age, age was prioritized for inclusion in the final analysis. By integrating L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) penalties, the Elastic Net model effectively manages collinearity while performing automated variable selection. In the DIMSA group, the optimal model achieved an R² of 0.182 at α = 0.0001. Since the regularization strength was minimal, the model approximated an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, retaining non-zero coefficients for all five candidate predictors (Fig. 4a). Baseline AL (Pre-AL) exhibited the largest standardized coefficient (-0.1351), identifying it as the strongest available predictor of ΔAL in the DIMSA group. It was followed by Mean K (-0.0651), SER (-0.0389), and age (-0.0317), while sex demonstrated the weakest predictive influence (0.0214) (Fig. 4b). Pearson correlation analysis corroborated these findings: in the DIMSA group, Pre-AL (r = -0.346, P < 0.001) and age (r = -0.262, P = 0.004) were significantly and negatively correlated with ΔAL. The positive correlation between Mean K and ΔAL did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.164, P = 0.073), and no significant linear associations were found for sex (r = -0.012, P = 0.898) or SER (r = 0.104, P = 0.261) (Table 3; Fig. 4c). The low regularization strength in this group suggests a complex variation pattern of ΔAL within the DIMSA cohort, where individual predictors exhibit limited discriminative power, requiring contributions from all candidate variables to maximize predictive utility. In the OKA group, the optimal model achieved an R² of 0.206 at α = 0.1456. The regularization strength was substantially higher than that of the DIMSA group, resulting in a significantly sparser model (Fig. 4d). Among the five predictors, only Pre-AL (-0.0225) and age (-0.0358) retained non-zero coefficients. The coefficients for sex and SER were shrunk exactly to zero, while the coefficient for Mean K was negligible (0.0063) (Fig. 4e). Pearson correlation analysis showed that in the OKA group, Pre-AL (r = -0.344, P < 0.001) and age (r = -0.404, P < 0.001) were also significantly and negatively correlated with ΔAL. Mean K showed a significant positive correlation (r = 0.241, P = 0.016), while the positive correlation for SER approached the threshold of significance (r = 0.193, P = 0.054). Sex showed no significant correlation (r = -0.024, P = 0.810) (Table 3; Fig. 4f). Under stronger regularization, the Elastic Net model for the OKA group filtered out Mean K, SER, and sex, suggesting that the predictive information provided by these variables was redundant or effectively represented by Pre-AL and age. This model parsimony suggests that the predictive structure of ΔAL in the OKA group is primarily associated with baseline axial length and age. These models are exploratory and lack external validation.

Sensitivity analysis and E-values

Sensitivity analyses evaluating the inter-group differences in ΔAL were performed using the unadjusted model alongside the four propensity score methods (Table 4). Across all adjustment strategies, the point estimates remained significantly positive (indicating greater elongation in the DIMSA group), with no 95% CIs crossing zero (all P < 0.001). This demonstrates that the OKA regimen was consistently associated with less axial elongation regardless of the confounding-adjustment method employed. The PSM model yielded the most conservative difference (0.11 mm). This attenuation primarily reflects the strict matching criteria, which excluded marginal cases with non-overlapping PS distributions (the OKA cohort retention rate was 53%, n = 53); nevertheless, the difference within this highly comparable matched subset remained clinically significant. The three full-cohort methods (IPTW, OW, and PS adjustment) produced remarkably consistent point estimates (~ 0.150 mm), indicating robustness against weighting methodologies. As illustrated in the Love plots (Fig. 5), the SMDs for the vast majority of covariates were substantially reduced to near or below 0.1 after matching/weighting, confirming effective baseline balancing. Furthermore, the E-value analysis revealed that all point estimate E-values exceeded 5.0 (Table 5). This indicates that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both the treatment allocation and ΔAL by an odds ratio (OR) greater than 5.0 to fully explain away the observed differences. This threshold is substantially higher than the typical association strengths (OR 1.5–2.5) of common unmeasured confounders such as outdoor time or parental myopia. The lower confidence limits of the E-values also consistently remained > 2.8. Even in the most conservative PSM model, the derived E-value (5.20) underscored the robustness of our conclusions against potential unmeasured confounding.

Complications

Regarding atropine tolerability, in our routine clinical observations, the twice-daily regimen was generally well-tolerated, and no serious adverse events affecting the children’s daily life or study—such as severe symptomatic photophobia, persistent mydriasis, or near-vision difficulty—were documented during the 1-year follow-up. However, quantitative data on dynamic pupil size, accommodative amplitude, and standardized near-vision symptom scores were not routinely recorded in these retrospective clinical archives. Furthermore, because our inclusion criteria strictly required a complete 12-month follow-up, our final cohort inherently represents a ‘completer population.’ Therefore, the safety profile reported here is subject to completion bias; children who experienced early intolerable adverse effects would have had their treatment modified and were not captured in this analysis. Among children wearing orthokeratology, corneal fluorescein staining was graded as follows: 75 individuals (75.0%) had grade 0, 22 (22.0%) had grade 1, 2 (2.0%) had grade 2, and 1 (1.0%) had grade 3 staining. No cases of grade 4 corneal staining or severe conjunctival infection were observed.

Discussion

This study conducted a systematic comparison of the combined effects of twice-daily 0.01% atropine combined with either DIMS or OK on myopia progression in children, specifically focusing on axial elongation. Using an Elastic Net regression model, we also further identified key predictors associated with changes in axial length.

We adopted a twice-daily administration of 0.01% atropine, a regimen infrequently reported in previous literature. A one-year investigation by Xiao Yang et al. indicated that twice-daily atropine significantly slowed both axial elongation and SER progression compared to once-daily administration [23]. Previous studies have shown that combining 0.01% atropine with OK can further mitigate axial lengthening, with annual increases averaging between 0.07 mm and 0.29 mm [24–26]. In our cohort, the combination of OK and twice-daily atropine resulted in a mean AL change of approximately 0.157 mm after one year. Previous studies have reported that combining 0.01% atropine with DIMS lenses delays axial elongation compared to DIMS alone, with an average increase of less than 0.1 mm after one year [27, 28]. In the present study, we observed that the mean axial elongation was approximately 0.313 mm, which may be attributed to the younger age of the participants in our cohort, as the adjunctive effect of atropine to DIMS has been primarily observed in children aged 10–14 years [29].

Both the continuous inter-group comparison and the categorical analysis demonstrated that the OKA group experienced less axial elongation than the DIMSA group. This contrasts with the findings of Cheng Peng et al. [30], who reported no significant difference between OK and DIMS when combined with once-daily 0.01% atropine. Because our study did not include monotherapy or once-daily atropine control arms, it remains unclear whether this discrepancy is directly attributable to the increased dosing frequency of atropine. Previous studies have demonstrated that the myopia-control efficacy of OK lenses is, to some extent, pupil-dependent; a larger pupil diameter allows a greater proportion of the mid-peripheral myopic defocus induced by corneal reshaping to reach the retina, thereby potentially enhancing the treatment effect [31, 32]. In contrast, the constant delivery of myopic defocus via the 33-mm peripheral microlens array in DIMS spectacles may be less reliant on minor pupillary fluctuations. Since we did not measure pupil sizes in this cohort, whether the twice-daily atropine regimen induced significantly greater pupillary dilation compared to a once-daily regimen remains to be verified by future prospective measurements. Additionally, the optical efficacy of OK-induced corneal reshaping experiences a gradual diurnal regression throughout the day [33]. Twice-daily atropine administration might provide a more sustained, complementary pharmacological inhibition of scleral remodeling during this period of optical decay. However, because dynamic pupil size and optical-defocus profiles were not directly measured, these proposed interactions represent theoretical combined effects rather than proven pharmacological-optical synergy. Future factorial-design prospective trials are essential to rigorously evaluate these potential mechanisms.

Furthermore, a total of 18 children across both the OKA and DIMSA groups exhibited axial shortening at the 1-year follow-up, a phenomenon that has been previously reported in children undergoing myopia control interventions, including orthokeratology and repeated low-level red-light therapy. Proposed mechanisms include choroidal thickening, scleral remodeling, and changes in ocular deformation [6, 34–37]. Follow-up observations in our clinical practice suggest that such axial shortening is often transient, and most children eventually resume axial elongation over longer follow-up periods.

Utilizing the Elastic Net regression model, age was identified as a significant negative predictor of ΔAL in both the DIMSA and OKA groups. Younger children exhibited more rapid axial progression, a finding highly consistent with numerous longitudinal studies on juvenile myopia [29, 38, 39]. Donald Everett et al. highlighted baseline age as the strongest independent factor influencing myopia progression, noting more rapid axial growth in younger children that decelerates with age. Early-onset myopia (e.g., at 6–7 years) may also reflect a stronger genetic predisposition. Furthermore, younger children are likely in an earlier, more active phase of myopia progression, while older children may have entered a more stable stage [40]. Concurrently, baseline axial length (Pre-AL) exerted a substantial influence on the model, particularly within the DIMSA group, serving as another key negative predictor of axial elongation. Children with longer baseline AL may experience stronger peripheral myopic defocus signals, particularly in the nasal and temporal retina. Low-concentration atropine (0.01%) might enhance myopia control by reducing hyperopic defocus in these regions [23, 41]. Interestingly, our Elastic Net and Pearson correlation analyses revealed a significant positive correlation between baseline Mean K and ΔAL exclusively in the OKA group. This implies that steeper corneas were associated with faster axial elongation. We hypothesize that this is fundamentally related to the AL/CR (axial length-to-corneal radius) ratio. The axial length-to-corneal radius ratio (AL/CR) may provide a more physiologically relevant metric, as corneal curvature can compensate for axial growth during ocular development [42, 43]. A steeper cornea (higher Mean K) results in a smaller CR, consequently elevating the AL/CR ratio. Recent evidence, such as the findings by Chong et al. [44], suggests that a higher AL/CR ratio signifies an aggressive structural mismatch that may resist the peripheral myopic defocus signals generated by Orthokeratology, leading to a diminished myopia control effect.

Notably, the low alpha value (α = 0.0001) observed in the DIMSA group suggests that axial elongation in this cohort may be associated with the collective effects of multiple baseline factors. In contrast, the progression in the OKA group was primarily associated with two main factors: baseline AL and age. It is important to interpret these Elastic Net findings cautiously. The R² values for our models were modest (0.182–0.206), and the number of candidate predictors was small. From a biological perspective, this is expected because myopia progression is a highly complex, multifactorial process. Crucial determinants such as genetic predisposition, outdoor exposure duration, and near-work intensity were unavailable in this retrospective dataset. Therefore, these models are purely exploratory, reflect only the available baseline optometric parameters, and lack external validation.

No atropine-related complications affecting quality of life were observed in this study, despite the increased dosing frequency. Regarding OK, only 3 children (3.3%) exhibited corneal staining of grade 2 or higher, indicating that severe corneal complications can be minimized through rigorous fitting protocols and standardized follow-up [8].

Although our findings offer valuable insights for personalized myopia management, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the non-randomized, retrospective design inherently introduces selection bias. Treatment allocation was influenced by clinical judgment and parental preference; thus, families with higher health literacy or greater financial resources may have preferentially chosen the OKA regimen. Furthermore, while our Elastic Net model adjusted for key measured baseline variables, unmeasured confounders—such as outdoor time, parental myopia, near-work intensity, and OK-specific treatment-quality factors (e.g., precise lens centration, quantitative treatment-zone characteristics, and exact overnight wearing time)—could not be systematically analyzed. Second, our ability to systematically track initial screening numbers or specific early discontinuation rates was limited. Because our dataset exclusively comprised 12-month completers, our findings are inherently subject to completion bias, which may lead to an overestimation of overall treatment adherence and tolerability. Third, objective quantitative data on dynamic pupil size, accommodative amplitude, and standardized photophobia symptoms were not systematically recorded. The absence of these individual biometric and functional safety data prevents a direct, quantitative evaluation of the mydriatic and cycloplegic effects induced by the twice-daily atropine regimen. Finally, the 1-year follow-up restricts the evaluation of long-term efficacy and potential rebound effects. Given that myopia control is a prolonged process, our conclusions should not be extrapolated beyond this study period. Future large-scale, prospective randomized controlled trials with extended follow-ups and rigorous CONSORT reporting are essential to fully validate these combined effects, evaluate adherence rates, and elucidate the underlying optical-pharmacological mechanisms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study suggests that the OKA regimen was associated with less axial elongation than the DIMSA regimen in this retrospective cohort after adjustment for measured baseline covariates. This association is robust and remains consistent across varying age groups and baseline myopia severities. Furthermore, by utilizing Elastic Net penalized regression, we addressed traditional multicollinearity limitations to identify distinct predictive patterns for the two combination therapies. The DIMSA cohort follows a complex pattern associated with an array of baseline characteristics, whereas the OKA cohort is characterized by a highly parsimonious pattern primarily associated with baseline axial length and age. These findings not only suggest a potentially greater optical-pharmacological combined effect of OKA but also provide a valuable reference for individualized myopia management. Future large-scale, prospective randomized controlled trials with extended follow-ups are warranted to further validate these long-term combined effects and test the underlying synergistic hypotheses.
