The failure mode and evolution process of geosynthetic reinforced embankments under rainfall
Authors: Changshu Pan, Xiangfu Li, Xiaofei Jing, Xiaoshun Zhang, Xiaoming Hu, Bangxing Gong, Zhiqiang Li, Dan Xie, Fanchuan Zhou, Yang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As global climate change continues, extreme rainfall events occur more frequently, exacerbating water-related damage to reinforced embankments. To solve this problem, 16 groups of reinforced embankment failure model tests were carried out using a self-developed model test device under multi-factor coupling, with rainfall intensity, gradient, and number of reinforcement layers used as variables. The distribution and evolution laws of pore water pressure and soil pressure were systematically analyzed, and the failure process of reinforced embankments was visualized. The failure mode, mechanism, and characteristics of the reinforced embankment were revealed, and the evolution of the failure process was determined. Finally, sensitivity analysis was conducted on rainfall intensity, slope, and number of reinforcement layers. The results show that both pore water pressure and earth pressure have obvious burial depth and near slope effects, but the burial depth effect on pore water pressure is opposite to that of earth pressure. Unlike traditional soil slopes, which fail through circular arc or broken-line slip, reinforced embankments exhibit progressive erosion failure under “hydro-mechanical coupling effect”. The failure evolution of the reinforced embankment follows the sequence of water infiltration → soil softening → gully expansion → local failure → “pore erosion” failure → overall instability. The primary factor driving failure is the deterioration in soil strength caused by the decrease in matrix suction, rather than a sudden increase in pore water pressure. The results of this study have important theoretical value for reinforced embankments.

Introduction

As economical, efficient, and environmentally adaptable geotechnical structures, reinforced embankments have been widely used in highways, railways, and water conservancy projects. Reinforced embankments significantly improve the overall stability and bearing capacity of the structure through the synergistic effect of geosynthetics (such as geogrids and geotextiles) and fill materials1,2. However, under rainfall conditions, the long-term performance of reinforced embankments is often greatly challenged. Water infiltration can lead to decrease in matric suction and the attenuation of shear strength in unsaturated soils3–11. It may also degrade the friction performance of the reinforcement-soil interface, ultimately inducing embankment instability or even overall collapse. As global climate change continues, extreme rainfall events are occurring with increasing frequency12, further exacerbating the problem of water-induced damage in reinforced embankments. For example, in 2013, a large-scale landslide occurred on a reinforced highway embankment in Zhejiang, China, due to continuous heavy rainfall, resulting in substantial economic losses13,14. In 2022, heavy rain damaged a reinforced embankment on a highway in Guangdong, China, resulting in damage to a 300-m-long section15. Similar cases have been reported both in China and internationally16–20, indicating that rainfall-induced reinforced embankment failure has become a key problem in geotechnical engineering. Because this critical issue warrants further attention, an in-depth study of the failure mode and evolutionary mechanism of reinforced embankments under rainfall conditions holds important theoretical and practical value for improving disaster prevention and mitigation capacities and optimizing engineering design.

The hydro-mechanical coupling effect in unsaturated soil is the core mechanism behind rainfall-induced slope failure. Recent studies have shown that the combination of rainfall intensity and duration has a nonlinear controlling effect on the attenuation rate of matric suction21–26. Additionally, soil permeability anisotropy can significantly affect the temporal and spatial expansion path of the wetting front27–29. Through centrifugal model experiments30–35, it has been found that under seismic conditions, when the rainfall intensity exceeds 70% of the soil’s saturated permeability coefficient, a transient saturated zone preferentially forms near the reinforcement interface, resulting in a sharp drop in the pull-out resistance of the reinforcement36,37. Discrete element simulation38 has further shown that fine particle migration can block drainage channels, accelerate the accumulation of excess pore water pressure, and ultimately disrupt the soil arching effect.

The failure mode of reinforced embankments involves the dynamic decoupling process of the mechanical response at the reinforcement-soil interface. Experimental studies have confirmed that the strain-hardening characteristics of reinforcement can delay the penetration of the sliding surface39, but its tensile stiffness decays exponentially once the water content exceeds a critical threshold40. Based on distributed optical fiber sensing technology41, researchers have captured a bimodal distribution in the strain field of the reinforcement during rainfall, indicating a competitive mechanism between local debonding and overall stretching. Large-scale model experiments have revealed that the reinforcement-soil interface acts as a preferential water channel in reinforcement soil structures42, indicating that geosynthetics enhance the anisotropy of soil hydraulic conductivity43,44. It should be noted that existing numerical models often simplify the Mohr–Coulomb criterion with the limit equilibrium method45–50. However, these approaches struggle to characterize the nonlinear migration of stress paths in progressive failure. Therefore, there is a urgent need to develop a multiphase coupled constitutive model based on critical state theory.

Although previous studies have yielded remarkable achievements, three core technical limitations still persist in existing research:Most studies adopt single-factor variable analysis and fail to reveal the hydro-mechanical response mechanism under the coupled interaction of rainfall intensity, gradient, and number of reinforcement layers.Current failure mode research only focuses on the circular arc or broken-line slip of conventional soil slopes, while neglecting the characterization of the progressive erosion process and localized failure characteristics unique to reinforced embankments.The dominant controlling factors for rainfall-induced instability of reinforced embankments remain undefined, and quantitative sensitivity analysis of multiple influencing factors is absent.

To address the above research gaps, this study employs a self-developed rainfall model test apparatus to systematically investigate the failure modes, evolutionary laws and underlying failure mechanisms of reinforced embankments under rainfall conditions. The research outcomes carry significant theoretical significance and practical engineering value for refining the stability analysis theory of reinforced embankments and improving the safety and durability of road engineering.

The innovations of this study are summarized as follows:An independently developed artificial rainfall model test apparatus was adopted to realize visualized monitoring of the entire failure process of reinforced embankments, as well as synchronous acquisition of pore water pressure and earth pressure data.A novel “progressive erosion failure” mode of reinforced embankments under rainfall is revealed for the first time, and its six-stage evolutionary path and unique “pore erosion” failure characteristics are clarified.It is clarified that the dominant failure mechanism lies in soil strength deterioration caused by matric suction attenuation, which revises the conventional cognition that instability is induced by a sudden sharp rise in pore water pressure.The primary and secondary influencing order of rainfall intensity, slope gradient and number of reinforcement layers on pore water pressure is quantitatively determined.

Model tests

Test device

In this study, a self-developed indoor model test device was used to carry out the experiments. The design of the device is shown in Fig. 1. It mainly consists of a model tank, artificial rainfall system, and monitoring system. The details of the test device are as follows.

Model tank. The model tank is rectangular in shape, constructed from tempered glass, and has dimensions of 200 cm × 60 cm × 50 cm (length × width × height). The outlet end of the model tank is connected to a drainage groove, ensuring that rainwater collected in the accumulation box can be discharged in a timely manner. This prevents water from pooling at the slope toe, which could otherwise soak the soil and affect the test results. The structure layout of the model tank is shown in Fig. 2.Artificial rainfall system. The artificial rainfall system consists of three main parts: the rainfall sprinkler, the rainfall support frame, and the water supply system. The rainfall sprinkler is a conical atomizing nozzle made of 304 stainless steel. Rainfall intensity is regulated using a flowmeter and control valve, allowing simulation of four levels: 14, 28, 42, and 70 mm/h. The corresponding uniformity values are 0.93, 0.84, 0.83, and 0.81, with an effective rainfall coverage area exceeding 3 m2. The rainfall support frame measures 2 m × 1 m × 4 m (length × width × height). The water supply system includes a QX6-25-0.75 three-phase submersible pump (power = 0.75 kW, interface diameter = 25 mm, head = 25 m, weight = 20 kg), along with a water pipe, flowmeter, valve, and water storage tank (dimensions = 1.5 m × 0.6 m × 0.5 m, length × width × height).Monitoring system. The monitoring system consists of two high-resolution cameras, five BX-2 resistance earth pressure sensors, five vibrating wire pore water pressure sensors, and a data acquisition system composed of a TST3828EW dynamic strain meter and a computer. The cameras have a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. The BX-2 resistance earth pressure sensors have a diameter of 2.5 cm, a thickness of 1 cm, a resistance wire length of 3 m, and a measurement range of 0–0.2 MPa. The vibrating wire pore water pressure sensors are 7.5 cm long and 2 cm in diameter, with a resistance wire length of 3 m, and the same measurement range of 0–0.2 MPa.

Testing materials

Undisturbed soil samples were collected from a slope near the east gate of Chongqing University of Science and Technology. The soil is classified as weathered residual-colluvial silty clay of the Jurassic Shaximiao Formation. Its basic physical and mechanical parameters were determined via laboratory geotechnical tests, and the test results are presented in Table 1. The natural moisture content of the undisturbed soil is 20.78%, placing it in a relatively moist state. This condition fails to reflect the dry or low-moisture working state of actual engineering embankments after long-term air drying and prior to rainfall. To realistically simulate the initial dry condition of embankments before rainfall, the initial moisture content of the test soil in this study was adjusted to 10% with reference to previous studies51–54. This moisture content is lower than the plastic limit of the soil. It not only guarantees the forming stability and compaction uniformity during model filling, but also magnifies the response characteristics of matric suction attenuation and soil strength deterioration in the process of rainfall infiltration by comparing the “initial dry state” with the “rainfall-saturated state”, which helps reveal the disaster mechanism induced by rainfall. After the test soil was prepared at an initial moisture content of 10%, its physical and mechanical parameters were retested (note: liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index, maximum dry density, and minimum dry density are inherent properties of the soil that do not vary with moisture content and therefore were not retested). The retest results are presented in Table 2.

Based on the following considerations, this study adopted reticular glass fiber as the reinforcement material (with its performance parameters presented in Table 3):Glass fiber exhibits high tensile strength, low elongation, and stable mechanical properties. It has been widely used in reinforced soil model tests and can clearly reflect the reinforcement-soil interaction and the confinement effect of the reinforcement.Compared with conventional geogrids or geotextiles, glass fiber mesh has a smaller thickness and moderate flexibility, facilitating layered placement in small-scale models. It can avoid layered interface disturbance and uneven compaction induced by excessive thickness of reinforcement materials.Previous studies55–58 have demonstrated that glass fiber reinforcement can effectively simulate the tensile, pullout, and interfacial friction behavior of geosynthetics in soil.

Test scheme

To study the influence of the three factors of rainfall intensity, number of reinforcement layers, and gradient on the earth pressure, pore water pressure, and failure process of the reinforced embankment, the test design included orthogonal tests with three factors and four levels. The standard orthogonal array L16(43) was selected, and a total of 16 test runs were conducted. Referring to the studies by Lin59 and Zuo60, and combined with historical rainfall intensity data of Chongqing City, four levels of rainfall intensity were set in this study: q = 14, 28, 42, and 70 mm/h (q represents the rainfall intensity). Meanwhile, four gradient levels were designated for the slope gradients: θ = 30°, 35°, 40°, and 45° (θ represents the gradient). With reference to the research of Chen61, Gao62 and Wu63, the reinforcement densities was assigned four values: N = 0, 1, 2, and 4 (N represents the number of reinforcement layers). The detailed experimental scheme of this study is presented in Table 4. Each factor was assigned four levels to facilitate a comprehensive analysis of how variations in each factor influence the performance indices of the reinforced embankment. To analyze the changes in the soil pressure and pore water pressure, five earth pressure sensors and five pore water pressure sensors were embedded in the embankment model (Fig. 3). The layout of the reticulated glass fiber, schematics of the reinforced embankment, and a physical diagram are shown in Figs. 4 and 5.

Test results and analysis

Variations in pore water pressure

The time-history changes of pore water pressure at different locations on the slope are shown in Fig. 6. As illustrated in the diagram, the pore water pressure followed the order of UK3 > UK5 > UK4 > UK2 > UK1, indicating that the increase in pore water pressure exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity across the slope. The greatest variations occurred at the toe of the slope, followed by the shoulder, while the remaining parts had significantly lower variations.

There are several reasons for the abovementioned relationship. First, when rainfall occurs, rainwater infiltrates the soil and transforms into groundwater primarily at the top and surface of the slope. Because the rainfall intensity is greater than the permeability coefficient of the soil, water tends to accumulate at the top of the slope, leading to ponding, while the surface facilitates the formation of runoff. At this stage, the rainwater flow can be divided into two parts: (1) seepage water infiltrating into the soil and (2) runoff, including surface runoff, flowing from the top of the slope to the toe. These two components are collectively referred to as seepage water and runoff water. Under identical conditions, the soil at position No. 3 is directly scoured by runoff flowing from the top to the toe of the slope, and it is also influenced by seepage water. As a result, this location experiences the maximum rainwater effect. The soil pores then quickly fill with water, leading to the highest increase in pore water pressure at this point. However, position No. 5 receives less runoff than position No. 3, though it is still affected by seepage water. In addition, being located at the junction of the slope crest and surface, position No. 5 is subject to greater stress than the other points due to the weight of the overlying soil, making it more prone to damage. Once damage occurs, the soil structure becomes more porous, allowing for additional seepage. Therefore, the increase in the pore water pressure at position No. 5 is greater than that at position No. 4, but remains lower than that at position No. 3.

The soil at position No. 4 receives only seepage water and is affected by both seepage force and gravity. However, since this location is flat and not on a slope, it is relatively stable and less prone to structural failure. In addition, the burial depth at position No. 4 is 10 cm, which is shallower than at position No. 2 (20 cm). Therefore, the increase in pore water pressure at position No. 4 is greater than that at position No. 2 but less than that at position No. 5. Although the burial depth of position No. 2 is relatively deep and does not receive seepage water immediately, the seepage water from position No. 5 continues to infiltrate vertically, while seepage from position No. 3 penetrates horizontally. These combined effects cause position No. 2 to gradually accept both vertical and horizontal seepage water. Therefore, the increase in pore water pressure at position No. 2 is less than that at position No. 4. Position No. 1, having the greatest burial depth and the farthest distance from the slope surface, is the least affected by rainfall. Consequently, it exhibits the smallest increase in pore water pressure.

The burial depth of sensor No. 2 is 20 cm, while the burial depths of sensors No. 3, No. 4, and No. 5 are all 10 cm. By comparing and analyzing the pore water pressure increases between sensors No. 2 and No. 3, it was found that pore water pressure increases more significantly closer to the toe of the slope. Similarly, a comparison between sensors No. 4 and No. 5 showed that the pore water pressure is greater closer to the slope shoulder. Furthermore, comparing sensors No. 2 and No. 5 showed that the deeper the burial depth, the smaller the increase in pore water pressure. This is because the shallower the burial depth and the closer the position is to the toe of the slope, the greater the effect of the rainwater on the soil and the greater the increase in pore water pressure.

In unsaturated soils, pore water pressure uw is negatively correlated with matric suction (ua−uw), where ua represents pore air pressure. Matric suction is the key parameter controlling the effective stress and shear strength of unsaturated soils. Positions No. 3 and No. 5 are located near the slope face and slope crest, where rainfall infiltration is fastest and the reduction in matric suction is most pronounced, making them critical zones for embankment failure. Therefore, this study focuses on analyzing the temporal evolution of pore water pressure (reflecting changes in matric suction) at these positions, as shown in Figs. 7 and 8. Under different testing conditions, the evolution of pore water pressure can be divided into four stages, which essentially correspond to the dissipation process of matric suction in unsaturated soils: (1) Initial stable stage: The soil remains unsaturated, matric suction stays high, and pore water pressure remains stable. (2) Rapid growth stage: Water infiltrates rapidly, matric suction is quickly lost, and pore water pressure increases sharply. (3) Slow growth stage: The soil transitions toward saturation, matric suction continues to decrease, and pore water pressure rises slowly. (4) Later stable stage: The soil approaches saturation, matric suction approaches zero, and pore water pressure stabilizes.

Based on the experimental data, a quantitative statistical analysis was performed on the pore water pressure increments at the slope toe (K3) and slope crest (K5) under different test conditions. The results show that the increment at K3 ranges from 1.16 to 5.44 kPa, with an average value of 2.71 kPa, while the increment at K5 varies from 0.83 to 4.25 kPa, with a average value of 2.31 kPa. Statistical analysis of test data reveals that test case T-14 (q = 70 mm/h, θ = 35°, N = 2) produces the maximum pore water pressure increment, with 5.44 kPa at K3 and 4.25 kPa at K5. This demonstrates that pore water pressure evolution is governed by the coupled action of multiple factors, rather than being controlled by a single factor alone.

Variations in earth pressure

Figure 9 shows the time-history changes of the earth pressure in different parts of the slope. As can be seen from Fig. 9, the earth pressure at the different positions follows the order of PT1 > PT2 > PT4 > PT3 > PT5, indicating that the deeper the burial depth and the shorter the distance from the slope surface, the greater the change in soil pressure.

This relationship can be explained as follows. The earth pressure at a measuring point is related to the bulk density of the soil above it. Generally, the greater the soil moisture content above a sensor, the greater the weight of the overlying soil, the greater the earth pressure at the measuring point. The burial depth of earth pressure sensor T4 is 10 cm, while the burial depths of sensors T1 and T2 are both 20 cm. Therefore, based on the burial depth along, it can be concluded that the vertical earth pressure measured by T4 is less than that measured by T1 and T2. Because T1 and T2 share the same burial depth, the vertical earth pressures at these two positions should theoretically be equal. However, the horizontal distance between earth pressure sensor T1 and the shoulder of the slope is greater than that of T2, implying the amount of soil loss above T1 is less than that of T2. Therefore, PT1 > PT2. According to the test results, the failure progresses from the surface of the slope to the shoulder, and then from the shoulder to the trailing edge at the top of the slope. The amount of soil loss follows the order of shoulder of the slope > surface of the slope > trailing edge of the top of the slope. Sensor T3 is located beneath the surface of the slope, T4 is beneath the trailing edge at the top, and T5 is beneath the shoulder. Therefore, the earth pressure follows the order of PT4 > PT3 > PT5. As can be seen from Fig. 9, the earth pressure curves for T1 and T4, as well as those of T2 and T5, are very similar. This similarity arises because the soil above T4 is also part of the soil above T1. Thus, the soil loss reduces the self-weight stress above both T1 and T4. The same relationship applies to T2 and T5, resulting in synchronized changes in earth pressure changes at these positions.

Failure evolution process of reinforced embankment

Figure 12 shows the failure process of the reinforced embankment. It can be seen from Fig. 12 that after rainfall infiltration, multiple gullies of different sizes are first generated on the surface of the slope. Then, the gullies gradually extend to the shoulder and the toe of the slope until they penetrate the entire slope surface. When a gully penetrates the slope, a large-scale collapse occurs at the shoulder, resulting in greater damage than in other areas. As the gullies deepen and widen, they eventually reach the reinforcement layer. Rainwater then scours the reinforcement and gradually infiltrates beneath it, resulting in a phenomenon known as “pore erosion”. A comprehensive analysis reveals that the failure evolution process of the reinforced embankment as follows: After rainwater infiltration, (1) gullies are formed on the surface of the slope; (2) the gullies gradually develop toward the shoulder and the toe of the slope; (3) the gullies continue to widen and deepen; (4) the gullies extend across the entire slope surface; (5) “pore erosion” occurs at the reinforcement; and (6) large-scale failure of the slope occurs.

The reasons for the above-mentioned phenomena are as follows: The rainfall intensity is greater than the soil infiltration capacity (q > Ks), indicating that not all rainwater infiltrates the soil. Instead, a portion infiltrates while the rest forms runoff on the slope surface. In addition, raindrops cause splash erosion during rainfall. As a result, the combined effects of raindrop impact and slope runoff increase the sediment transport capacity of the water flow, which further aggravates soil erosion and causes gullies to form on the slope surface. In addition, during rainwater infiltration, the soil bulk density at the shoulder of the slope increases, resulting in tensile stress and forming a tensile stress zone at the shoulder. This contributes to large-scale damage in that area.

Fig. 12Erosion failure process of reinforced embankment.

Erosion failure process of reinforced embankment.

The “pore erosion phenomenon” refers to a situation in which the reinforcement maintains the overall structure of the slope, but a cavity is formed beneath it. In this process, finer soil particles are washed out through the hole, while larger particles remain in place, helping to preserve the overall structure of the slope. The formation process of the “pore erosion phenomenon” is as follows. First, the runoff continuously scours the slope surface and forms gullies after the formation of slope runoff, and then the gullies gradually widen and deepen. When the bottoms of the gullies reach the position of the reinforcement, and the infiltrated water continues to erode the soil below the reinforcement, carrying it to the surface of the slope, where it then flows down to the toe of the slope. Finally, a hole develops under the reinforcement. Subsequently, the rainwater continues to infiltrate, and one or more holes are formed under the next layer of reinforcement. Similarly, in the reinforced slope, holes form under each layer of reinforcement under rainfall conditions (Fig. 13).

The reasons for this phenomenon are as follows: The reinforcement material strengthens the integrity of the overall structure of the slope, so the soil is not extensively carried away by rainwater but it is instead eroded locally. In addition, the impact force of the rainwater cannot damage the reinforcement, which remains intact within the slope and continue to maintain structural integrity of the slope. As a result, rainwater can only erode the slope by passing through the reinforcement. When the gullies deepen to the level of the reinforcement, the soil beneath it is subjected to two types of water flow: (1) water flow that directly penetrates through the reinforcement into the underlying soil and (2) water that flows along the reinforcement to the surface of the slope. The water flow that passes through the reinforcement directly softens the soil below, reduces its mechanical strength, and gradually leads to its failure. Meanwhile, the water that flows along the reinforcement continues to scour the surface of the lower soil. Under the combined action of these two types of water flow, the soil beneath the reinforcement is continuously eroded, and a hole is ultimately formed.

Discussion

Failure mode

According to the time-history diagram of slope failure, the failure mode of the reinforced embankment differs from the circular arc or broken-line sliding commonly observed in traditional soil slopes. Instead, the reinforced embankment undergoes a progressive erosion failure caused by the water-force coupling effect under the action of rainwater erosion. The hydro-mechanical coupling effect is reflected in the following three aspects: (1) the seepage force changes the stress field distribution within the slope, resulting in deflection of the principal stress direction; (2) the matrix suction gradually decreases until it is lost, resulting in a decrease in the effective stress; and (3) the increased soil moisture content decreases the friction coefficient at the reinforcement-soil interface20,27,36. The progressive erosion failure can be divided into the following stages: water infiltration, soil softening, gully expansion, local damage, “pore erosion” damage, and overall instability (Fig. 14). The evolutionary process of these six stages fully corresponds to the transition of unsaturated soil from a state of high matric suction and high effective stress to low matric suction and low effective stress.

Water infiltration stage: Initial attenuation of matric suction. In the early stage of rainfall, water gradually infiltrates into the interior of the embankment through the pore channels on its surface. During this stage, the water content within the embankment gradually increases, the matrix suction decreases, and the local saturation of the surface layer increases. Although the mechanical properties at the reinforcement-soil interface are affected by the presence of water, the reinforcement material can still effectively restrain soil deformation, and the overall stability of the slope is not significantly affected. Therefore, at this stage, only a small number of cracks and gullies form on the slope surface due to slope runoff.

Soil softening stage: Matric suction rapidly decreases, leading to a reduction in effective stress and softening of the soil. With the continued infiltration of water, the soil’s water content increases, resulting in the formation of a transient saturated zone in the upper part of the slope. Mechanical parameters such as shear strength and elastic modulus begin to deteriorate, and the soil gradually softens. The softened soil becomes more prone to deformation under the combined effects of its own weight and rainwater splash erosion. As the soil softens, the tensile force in the reinforcement changes, affecting the cooperative performance between the reinforcement material and the soil. However, the reinforcement material can still maintain the overall stability of the slope, so the soil undergoes only minor deformation. Consequently, the number of gullies increases during this stage, and they gradually develop toward the shoulder and the toe of the slope.

Gully expansion stage: Continuous loss of matric suction, coupled with insufficient shear strength, leads to gully development. As the rainfall continues, the reinforcement material remains in a humid environment for an extended period, and its mechanical properties gradually decrease. Under the combined action of soil softening and reinforcement performance degradation, the stress distribution inside the embankment changes. When the tensile stress in the soil exceeds its tensile strength, displacement occurs, and gullies begin to appear on or within the embankment. The formation of gullies further aggravates the rainwater infiltration, forming a vicious circle that accelerates gully expansion. In addition, the widening gullies compromise the integrity of the soil, weaken the anchoring effect of the reinforcement, and further reduce embankment stability. At this stage, the gullies caused by the soil stress redistribution are superimposed on those formed by the previous runoff erosion, causing them to gradually widen and deepen.

Local damage stage: Matric suction in the local soil mass approaches zero, and insufficient effective stress induces local collapse. With the continuous expansion of gullies and the continuous reduction in soil strength, localized damage occurs in the embankment, such as partial collapse at the shoulders and surface of the slope. These local damages change the stress distribution within the embankment, intensifying stress concentrations in undamaged areas and accelerating further damage.

“Pore erosion” damage stage: Complete loss of matric suction at the reinforcement-soil interface, coupled with the decay of interfacial effective stress, induces pitting erosion. Under ongoing rainfall, the mechanical properties at the reinforcement-soil interface are seriously weakened. As a result, the soil becomes displaced relative to the reinforcement and is transported to the toe of the slope by runoff. When gullies reach the reinforcement, large volumes of rainwater infiltrate directly through it. The infiltrated water continues to erode the soil beneath the reinforcement, carrying it to the slope surface, and eventually down to the toe of the slope, leading to the formation of a cavity beneath the reinforcement.

Overall instability stage: Extensive soil saturation and loss of matric suction lead to the effective stress insufficient to maintain stability. As local damage and pore erosion damage accumulates, the overall stability of the embankment fails, leading to a large-scale collapse near the main gully. At this stage, gullies at the slope top may cut entirely through the embankment, resulting in roadway interruption and seriously affecting traffic and surrounding environmental safety. Moreover, broader new gullies may form on the slope, further exacerbating roadbed damage.

According to our comprehensive analysis, the failure of the reinforced embankment has three characteristics. (1) the failure process exhibits pronounced spatial–temporal progressivity; (2) there is a lag between water migration and deformation development; (3) there is an obvious pore erosion phenomenon in the later stage of failure.

Failure mechanism

Based on monitoring data from the 16 sets of tests, the onset of embankment failure consistently lagged behind the stage of sharp pore water pressure increase (taking test T-10 as an example: the sharp increase stage occurred between 400 s and 780 s, lasting 380 s; initial embankment failure occurred at 840 s, with a lag of 60 s). Failure did not occur simultaneously with the sharp increase in pore water pressure. This indicates that the failure of the reinforced embankment was not caused directly by the pore water pressure surge. Although no matric suction sensors were installed for direct measurement in this study, drawing on classical unsaturated soil mechanics theory, the sharp increase in pore water pressure is merely a hydraulic response to water infiltration. The observed lag in failure suggests that the failure of the reinforced embankment was not directly triggered by the pore water pressure surge. Based on the intrinsic mechanism of water infiltration → matric suction reduction → degradation of soil shear strength, we infer that the reduction in matric suction leading to soil strength degradation was the core controlling factor in the rainfall-induced failure of the reinforced embankment. This inference is also reflected in the findings of Manish Dewrari64. The failure mechanism of the reinforced embankment can be systematically explained by combining the unsaturated soil shear strength equation with the experimental observations, as follows:

The shear strength formula for unsaturated soils is given as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau = c^{'} + (\sigma - u_{a} )\tan \phi ^{'} + (u_{a} - u_{w} )\tan \phi ^{b}$$\end{document}

In the equation, (ua−uw) denotes matric suction, the core parameter governing the shear strength of unsaturated soils. c’ and φ’ are the effective cohesion and effective internal friction angle of the soil mass, respectively; φb refers to the matric suction friction angle.

After rainfall impinges on the slope surface, it rapidly converts into seepage flow and surface runoff. Continuous infiltration of seepage flow gradually raises the soil moisture content, causing the soil mass to transition from an unsaturated state toward saturation. This process is accompanied by an increase in pore water pressure uw and a synchronous decline in matric suction (ua−uw). Previous studies20,27,36 have indicated that rising moisture content in unsaturated soil significantly deteriorates the frictional properties of the reinforcement-soil interface, reduces the interface friction coefficient, and further weakens the synergistic working capacity of reinforcement and soil.

Based on the 16 sets of model tests, it was clearly observed that the initial failure time of the embankment consistently lagged behind the stage of sharp increase in pore water pressure, directly verifying that failure was not triggered by a sudden change in pore water pressure uw, but rather resulted from the gradual dissipation of matric suction and the continuous reduction in effective stress, leading to degradation of the shear strength of the unsaturated soil. The progressive degradation and instability process can be described as follows: rainfall infiltration drives an increase in soil moisture content and degree of saturation → non-linear decrease in matric suction (ua−uw) → significant reduction in unsaturated effective stress σ’ → rapid decline in soil shear strength τ; simultaneously, matric suction is lost at the reinforcement-soil interface, causing a concurrent degradation of interfacial effective stress and frictional restraint.

Under the combined action of soil self-weight stress and seepage forces, the overturning force acting on the embankment soil gradually exceeds the sliding resistance, ultimately leading to progressive failure. Although the reinforcement placed in the embankment can restrain soil deformation and improve overall stability through reinforcement-soil interaction, it can only delay the onset of failure; it cannot prevent the intrinsic strength degradation of the unsaturated soil caused by the loss of matric suction. Under prolonged or high-intensity rainfall, the dual degradation of soil strength and interface restraint becomes irreversible, and the slope may still eventually fail. Furthermore, after failure, “pore erosion” occurs. This is the core reason, from the perspective of unsaturated soil mechanics, why reinforced embankments can still undergo failure under extreme rainfall conditions.

Limitations of this study

This physical model was constructed as a small-scale laboratory model, without systematic quantitative derivation or validation of scaling laws (similarity criteria) for physical model testing, which represents a core limitation of this study. In this test, the model dimensions were determined solely based on the geometric similarity principle (model embankment height of 40 cm, geometric similarity ratio CL = 1:20, corresponding to a prototype embankment height of 8 m), while also adhering to the basic requirements of mechanical similarity (mechanical parameters of the reinforcement and soil were taken as measured from laboratory geotechnical tests). However, complete similarity scaling relationships for the gravity field, seepage field, and reinforcement-soil interaction were not rigorously derived. As a result, direct quantitative extrapolation of the model results to practical engineering applications is subject to limitations.

Furthermore, the model box was made of tempered glass. To avoid glass breakage, the degree of compaction of the embankment model was lower than that used in actual engineering practice, which is the primary reason for the earlier occurrence of the sudden increase in pore water pressure. In addition, this study used only a clay with a single particle size, whereas actual engineering backfill typically consists of soils with a range of particle sizes. Despite these limitations, the patterns observed in this study are reliable. Taken together, these factors make the results more suitable for qualitatively revealing the failure mode and evolution characteristics of reinforced embankments under rainfall, whereas quantitative application to practical engineering would require calibration based on site-specific geological conditions, compaction standards, and actual embankment dimensions.

In future work, our research group will conduct large-scale model tests and in-situ field tests based on full similarity scaling laws (including geometric, hydraulic, and mechanical similitude), improve the model similarity design, and further enhance the engineering applicability of the research findings.

Analysis of experimental repeatability and uncertainty

To evaluate the reliability of the test results, three repeated tests were conducted under condition T-6 (q = 28 mm/h, θ = 35°, N = 0). The results showed that the coefficients of variation for the peak values of pore water pressure and earth pressure were both less than 8%, indicating good repeatability of the experiments. The main sources of uncertainty are as follows: (1) Difficulty in achieving completely consistent compaction density during manual filling; (2) Deviations in homogeneity and anisotropy between the manually prepared soil and natural field soil; (3) Although the rainfall uniformity was controlled within the range of 0.81–0.93, spatial variability still existed; (4) Slight deviations in the placement positions of the sensors may introduce local measurement errors.

Conclusions

In this study, the rainfall intensity, gradient, and the number of reinforcement layers were taken as variables. Using a self-developed model testing device, changes in pore water pressure and soil pressure at different positions were monitored under different test conditions via orthogonal testing. The failure process of the reinforced embankment was observed, and the failure mode, failure mechanism, and failure characteristics of the reinforced embankment were finally revealed.
