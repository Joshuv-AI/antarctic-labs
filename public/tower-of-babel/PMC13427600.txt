What could we know about avian-type aortic arch?
Authors: Hisato Takagi
Published: 2026

I read with great interest the excellent case report for avian-type aortic arch by Aras et al,1 published in April 2026. In general, three branches arise from the aortic arch in the following order: (1) the brachiocephalic trunk [the common trunk of the common carotid artery (CCA)/right CCA and subclavian artery (SCA)/right SCA (RSCA)], (2) left CCA (LCCA), and (3) left SCA (LSCA). Meanwhile, two branches may, albeit extremely rarely, originate from the aortic arch: (1) the common carotid trunk formed by the right CCA and LCCA and (2) the common subclavian trunk (CSCT) formed by the RSCA and LSCA. This configuration was classified as type 6 by Ergun et al.2 This pattern is known as the avian-type3 aortic arch, which was first described in a cadaver by Poultsides et al.4 During embryogenesis, paired aortic arches arising from the ventral aorta undergo highly regulated regression and persistence.5 The third arches give rise to the CCAs, whereas the fourth arches contribute to the definitive aortic arch and SCAs. An avian-type configuration may be plausibly interpreted as reflecting persistence of shared ventral aortic segments in conjunction with incomplete separation of derivatives of the third and fourth arches, thereby producing fusion of carotid and subclavian origins into two common trunks (common carotid trunk/CSCT). Our comprehensive literature search identified seven case reports of the avian-type aortic arch and nine contemporary anatomical studies (published since 2000 and including ≥100 individuals) for the arch (Table). The pooled prevalence of the avian-type aortic arch is 0.022%. The RSCA originating from the CSCT coursed behind the esophagus in two1,4 of the seven patients, which formed a complete vascular ring and incurred no symptom. Although traumatic aortic dissection (AD) was coexistent in one case,6 no patient was complicated with spontaneous AD.
