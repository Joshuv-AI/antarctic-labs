A Spatial Localizer for Electrons in Insulators Preprint: APS/123-QED
Haylen Gerhard, Yifan Wang, Alexander Cerjan, Wladimir A. Benalcazar

Abstract
The location of electrons governs phenomena ranging from chemical bonding and electric polarization to the topological classification of band insulators and the emergence of correlated states in quantum matter. While a prescription exists for finding local state representations of electrons in one-dimensional insulators, no comparably general theory exists in higher dimensions. Here, we introduce a general framework for finding the location of electrons in insulators in two and three dimensions based on the spectral properties of quantum-mechanical operators that we term Spatial Localizers . This framework naturally extends the notion of Wannier centers to insulators with boundaries, defects, and disorder, which we use to establish a position-space formulation of the bulk–defect correspondence for electronic charge. This framework also yields maximally localized electronic states. As two representative examples, we show that these states reduce to maximally localized Wannier functions in atomic insulators, whereas in Chern insulators they form coherent states that mirror the coherent-state structure of Landau levels in the quantum Hall effect.

In this work, we introduce a general framework for finding local state representations of electrons in insulators beyond one dimension. Our approach is based on the spectral properties of quantum-mechanical operators that we term
Spatial Localizers
, which address the geometric constraints described above by being compatible with periodic boundary conditions and simultaneously preserving the non-commutative structure of projected position operators across multiple directions. In contrast to variational approaches, our prescription consists of solving an eigenvalue problem; therefore, it is ansatz-free and non-iterative. It provides a gauge-invariant characterization of composite bands and applies to systems with crystalline symmetry or disorder, under periodic or open boundary conditions. A central result of this framework is the extension of the notion of Wannier centers to insulators with open boundaries, lattice defects, or disorder, which we use to establish a general bulk–defect correspondence for electronic charge. A second central result is that, beyond identifying the centers of electronic charge, Spatial Localizers also yield their associated wavefunctions, maximally localized in a rigorous sense. In atomic insulators, these states lead to MLWFs, whereas in Chern insulators they take the form of coherent states, directly mirroring the coherent-state structure of Landau levels in the quantum Hall effect (QHE)
Fradkin 2013
;
Thouless 1984
;
Girvin and Jach 1984
.

, is related to the gauge-invariant quantum metric via
\Omega_{I}=\int\Tr(g_\kv)d^{2}{\bf k}
, where
[g_{\bf k}]_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}([\eta_{\bf k}]_{ij}+[\eta_{\bf k}]_{ij}^{\dagger})
and
[\eta_{\bf k}]_{ij}=\sum_{n}\bra{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n,{\bf k}}}(I-P_{\bf k})\ket{\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n,{\bf k}}}
is the quantum geometric tensor over the unit-cell periodic components of occupied Bloch states
\ket{u_{n,{\bf k}}}

Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
. The second contribution,
\tilde{\Omega}
, is the variance of the state with respect to the position operators projected into
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{occ}}
[Appendix D]. Unlike the quantum geometric contribution,
\tilde{\Omega}
is gauge-dependent and commonly minimized via the projection method and optimization algorithms

;
Mostofi et al. 2008
;
Pizzi et al. 2020
. Thus, we expect states that minimize
\Delta{\tilde{R}}^{2}
to serve as optimal seed/trial states for constructing Wannier bases in OALs.

The Spatial Localizer framework we introduce in this work generates maximally localized states by minimizing
\Delta\tilde{R}^{2}
via exact diagonalization of the eigenproblem
L({\bf r})\ket{\psi^{L}({\bf r})}=\mu({\bf r})\ket{\psi^{L}({\bf r})}
. It does so regardless of the presence of strong topological invariants, lack of discrete translation invariance, and without the need for ansätze or gauge-fixing procedures. The maximally localized states are obtained by separating the spatial and embedding degrees of freedom in the localizer eigenstates
\ket{\psi^{L}({\bf r})}
via the Schmidt decomposition

\ket{\psi^{L}({\bf r})}=\sum_{i}s_{i}\left(\ket{p_{i}({\bf r})}\otimes\ket{\xi_{i}({\bf r})}\right),

(8)

where
s_{i}\geq 0
are the Schmidt values, obeying
\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}=1
, and
\ket{p_{i}({\bf r})}
and
\ket{\xi_{i}({\bf r})}
are, respectively, the “physical” and “embedding” Schmidt vectors. We order the Schmidt values
\{s_{i}\}
in descending order, and define the first physical Schmidt vector,
\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}
, as our “localized state”. As we will now see, the states
\{\ket{p_{1}({\bf r}^{*})}\}
extracted from the
N_{\textrm{occ}}
positions
\{{\bf r}^{*}\}
with minimal
\mu({\bf r})
are maximally localized. In particular, in OALs, the states
\{\ket{p_{1}({\bf r}^{W})}\}
extracted from the
N_{\textrm{occ}}
WCs
\{{\bf r}^{W}\}
yield MLWFs, while in insulators in strong topological phases, the Spatial Localizer generates a set of
N_{\textrm{occ}}
exponentially-localized coherent states, overcomplete in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{occ}}
by an amount given by the strong topological invariant.

For the OBC Spatial Localizer

\displaystyle\Delta\tilde{R}_{H}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tX}{\tY}}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\mu^{2}({\bf r})-\norm{\expval{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(\rv)}}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{s_{1}}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1(\rv)} \comm{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}} \ket{\psch_i(\rv)}}\commutator{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}}\ket{p_{i}({\bf r})},

and the arguments in

and

are omitted to simplify the presentation,
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}({\bf r})={\tilde{X}}(x)\mathbf{\hat{x}}+{\tilde{Y}}(y)\mathbf{\hat{y}}

\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}
. Finally, it is useful to note that
{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}({\bf r})={\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}({\bf 0})-V^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{occ}}{\bf r}V_{\mathrm{occ}}
[c.f. (

L({\bf r})
and its

Let us start by considering OAL insulators under OBC. Regardless of whether or not

In insulators with strong topological invariants, a system with OBC is necessarily gapless due to edge-localized topological states that cross the bulk band gaps, rendering it metallic. To consider fully insulating configurations, we study strong topological insulators only under PBC.

We study the Qi–Wu–Zhang (QWZ) model under PBC, a minimal lattice Hamiltonian for a Chern insulator

\sum_{\mathbf{R}}e^{{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{k_{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\ket{p_{1}({\bf r}^{*}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}=0,

Such a gauge has recently been proposed as an optimal gauge for constructing WFs in Chern insulators

;

;

. Our findings corroborate these proposals from the complete opposite perspective: the Spatial Localizer finds the most localized state within
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{occ}}
via a gauge-invariant eigenvalue problem in position space, and this state –the coherent state– is found to correspond to the vortex gauge in reciprocal space only a posteriori. To generate a Wannier basis from these coherent states, it suffices to restore support at the
\mathbf{k_{v}}
point of the BZ. In our model, the second physical Schmidt vector,
\ket{p_{2}({\bf r})}
, has support at the
\mathbf{k_{v}}
point and, when considered in superposition with
\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}
, furnishes a gauge for localized WFs.
Specifically, we construct a state as
\ket{\psi^{T}({\bf r})}=\alpha_{1}\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}+\alpha_{2}\ket{p_{2}({\bf r})}

\alpha_{1}\gg\alpha_{2}
and utilize a Löwdin procedure

;
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
to produce WFs. These WFs have amplitudes that decay from their centers as

, in accordance with the known lower bound

;
Brouder et al. 2007
;
Panati 2007
;
Monaco et al. 2018
[Fig.
4

We have shown that the Spatial Localizer framework solves the problem of electron localization in extended systems. In OALs, Spatial Localizers find MLWFs. Remarkably, these representations are unique; states pulled from positions
{\bf r}\neq{\bf r}^{W}
are still centered around their nearest WC
{\bf r}^{W}
, and possess the same spread. The collection of these unique MLWFs spans the space of occupied energy bands, forming “Wannier representations” of the bands. In Chern insulators, on the other hand, the Wannier representations are
not
unique. The coherent state
\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}
is always centered around the extraction position
{\bf r}
, even for
{\bf r}
not corresponding to minima of the LIF,
{\bf r}^{*}
. In other words, there is a continuum of coherent states centered anywhere in the unit cell, as one has in the QHE
Fradkin 2013
;
Girvin and Jach 1984
[Appendix F]. To any arbitrary extraction point
{\bf r}
corresponds a WF (constructed using
\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}
and adding the necessary support at
\mathbf{k_{v}}
) centered at
{\bf r}
and with a gauge vortex in reciprocal space centered at
\mathbf{k_{v}}
, such that the polarization
\frac{\mathbf{P}}{e}=-\frac{{\bf r}}{V_{c}}-\frac{C}{2\pi}\hat{z}\times\mathbf{k_{v}}
remains invariant [Appendix F], where
V_{c}
is the volume of the unit cell. This relation, recently derived in Ref.
Gunawardana et al. 2025
and independently verified with our Spatial Localizer, sheds light on the problem of polarization and weak topology in Chern insulators
Coh and Vanderbilt 2009
;
Gunawardana et al. 2025
;
Vaidya et al. 2024
;
Zhang and Barkeshli 2025
. We do find, however, that maximal localization of a coherent/Wannier state is obtained from the position
{\bf r}^{*}
that minimizes the LIF,
\mu({\bf r}^{*})
. This is in agreement with recent work using the vortex gauge approach, in which maximally localized WFs are associated with an optimal vortex location, determined from Fourier components of the Berry curvature
Gunawardana et al. 2024
.

Appendix A
Wannier Centers and Wilson Loops

In this appendix, we discuss the relation of Wannier centers (WCs) as eigenvalues of a Wilson loop and the connection to the projected Resta position operator
Alexandradinata et al. 2014
;
Benalcazar et al. 2017b
. This connection is crucial, as the eigenvalues of a Wilson loop, which correspond directly to WCs, are powers of the eigenvalues of the projected position operator. We can then use a Spatial Localizer, constructed from projected Resta position operators, to search for approximate eigenstates (approximate maximally localized Wannier functions) of those operators.

We begin with a discussion of Bloch states and the Resta position operator. We then project the Resta position operator onto a space of occupied bands and discuss how WCs manifest in the spectrum of a Wilson loop (a Wilson loop in this context is understood as the repeated application of the projected Resta position operator).
For a more detailed discussion, see Section III.B of
Benalcazar et al. 2017b
.

Our starting point is the set of Bloch states

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{n,{\bf k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}e^{{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\ket{u_{n,{\bf k}}}\otimes\ket{{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle=\ket{u_{n,{\bf k}}}\otimes\ket{{\bf k}},

(11)

which diagonalize the Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H\ket{\psi_{n,{\bf k}}}=E_{n,{\bf k}}\ket{\psi_{n,{\bf k}}},

(12)

where
n
labels the band index,
{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}
is a direct lattice vector,
{\bf k}
is the crystal momentum defined over the Brillouin zone (BZ) of the crystal, and
\ket{u_{n,{\bf k}}}
is defined over the unit cell degrees of freedom, and obey
\braket{u_{n,{\bf k}}|u_{m,{\bf k}}}=\delta_{m,n}
. The Hamiltonian eigenstates are orthonormal,
\braket{\psi_{n,{\bf k}}|\psi_{m,{\bf q}}}=\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf q}}\delta_{m,n}
. Under periodic boundary conditions (PBC), we use the Resta position operator
Resta 1998
so that the position operator is well-defined on the periodic Hilbert space that
H
acts on.

We now move to a 1D analysis for simplicity (i.e.,
{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}\rightarrow x
and
{\bf k}\rightarrow k
). In position space, the 1D Resta position operator is formulated as

X_{R}=\sum_{x}e^{{\mathrm{i}}\Delta kx}I\otimes\outerproduct{x}{x},

(13)

where I is the
N_{\textrm{b}}\times N_{\textrm{b}}
identity matrix with
N_{b}
as the number of bands.
Furthermore, we have
\Delta k=2\pi/N
and
N
as the number of unit cells in the system.

Using the Fourier transform
\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}kx}\ket{k}
, we have

\begin{split}X_{R}&=\sum_{x}e^{{\mathrm{i}}\Delta kx}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}kx}e^{{\mathrm{i}}k^{\prime}x}I\otimes\outerproduct{k}{k'}\\
&=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x}e^{{\mathrm{i}}(k^{\prime}+\Delta k-k)x}I\otimes\outerproduct{k}{k'}\\
&=\sum_{k}I\otimes\outerproduct{k + \Delta k}{k},\end{split}

(14)

where we used
\delta_{k,k^{\prime}+\Delta k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x}e^{{\mathrm{i}}(k^{\prime}+\Delta k-k)x}
in the last line.

To project
X_{R}
into occupied energy bands, we can define the projector as

P=\sum_{k}P_{k}\outerproduct{k}{k},\quad P_{k}=\sum_{n}\outerproduct{u_{n,k}}{u_{n,k}},

(15)

where
n
runs over occupied bands and
\ket{u_{n,k}}
is the periodic component of the Bloch state
\ket{\psi_{n,k}}
. Furthermore, we use the notation
P_{k}\outerproduct{k}{k}\equiv P_{k}\otimes\outerproduct{k}{k}
for brevity. Projecting
X_{R}
into the occupied subspace, we have

\displaystyle PX_{R}P=\sum_{k}P_{k+\Delta k}P_{k}\outerproduct{k+ \Delta k}{k}.

(16)

To simplify our analysis, we proceed on the basis of occupied states, labeled only by the occupied bands and
k
. We move to this basis by using the “half projector,”
V_{\mathrm{occ}}=\sum_{n,k}\outerproduct{\psi_{n,k}}{n,k}
, which is a matrix with the occupied states as column vectors; the usual “full projector” is
P=V_{\mathrm{occ}}V_{\mathrm{occ}}^{\dagger}
. Here, the sum over
n
runs over the occupied bands. Note that this is equivalent to the construction

V_{\mathrm{occ}}=\sum_{k}V_{k}\outerproduct{k}{k},\ \ V_{k}=\sum_{n}\outerproduct{u_{n,k}}{n}.

(17)

Furthermore, the definition of
V_{\mathrm{occ}}
above is equivalent to the definition in the main text,
V_{\mathrm{occ}}=\sum_{E_{i}<E_{F}}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{i}

E_{F}
(see Appendix
B

The use of

V_{\mathrm{occ}}^{\dagger}X_{R}V_{\mathrm{occ}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&G_{k_{N}}\\
G_{k_{1}}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&G_{k_{2}}&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix},

(18)

where
[G_{k}]_{mn}=\bra{u_{m,k+\Delta k}}u_{n,k}\rangle
and both
m
and
n
run over occupied bands, such that
[V_{\mathrm{occ}}^{\dagger}X_{R}V_{\mathrm{occ}}]_{k+\Delta k,k}=[G_{k}]_{mn}

We now look to solve for the eigenvalues of

C.1
Derivation of lower bound on a sum of variances

Our starting point is the Heisenberg-Robertson uncertainty relation (HRUR)
Robertson 1929
for two observables (Hermitian operators)
{\tilde{X}}
and
\tilde{Y}
,

\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tX}{\tY}}}\leq(\Delta{\tilde{X}})(\Delta\tilde{Y}),

(40)

which holds for
any
state in the Hilbert space that
{\tilde{X}}
and
\tilde{Y}
act on. While this bound can be informative, the localizer structure naturally provides bounds on a sum of variances, rather than a product. Therefore, we note the relation

2\Delta{\tilde{X}}\Delta\tilde{Y}\leq(\Delta{\tilde{X}})^{2}+(\Delta\tilde{Y})^{2},

which follows from
(\Delta{\tilde{X}}-\Delta\tilde{Y})^{2}\geq 0
. We then have

\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tX}{\tilde{Y}}}}\leq(\Delta{\tilde{X}})^{2}+(\Delta\tilde{Y})^{2}.

(41)

The bound (
41
) was also derived in
Maccone and Pati 2014
. While (
41
) is useful in the case of two operators, we wish to generalize it to
d\geq 2
operators since the structure of a localizer generalizes to arbitrarily many (Hermitian) operators. To do this, we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}=\sum_{j<k,k=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}+\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{k}^{2}}{d-1}\right),

(42)

\displaystyle\sum_{j<k,k=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}+\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{k}^{2}}{d-1}\right)\geq\sum_{j<k}\frac{\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tX_j}{\tX_k}}}}{d-1}.

(43)

We now have a lower bound on the sum of the variances of arbitrarily many operators that is in the spirit of the HRUR. Namely, we have

\sum_{j<k}\frac{\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tX_j}{\tX_k}}}}{d-1}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\expectationvalue{\tX^2_j}.

(44)

C.2
Derivation of upper bound on a sum of variances

We note that, in this derivation, we omit the explicit
{\bf r}
dependence on operators and states for brevity until the
{\bf r}
dependence is relevant in Eq. (
50
). To find an upper bound for the sum of variances, consider a localizer eigenstate
\ket{\psi^{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{occ}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\Gamma}
such that
L\ket{\psi^{L}}=\mu\ket{\psi^{L}}
and
L^{2}\ket{\psi^{L}}=\mu^{2}\ket{\psi^{L}}
.

\ket{\psi^{L}}
admits a Schmidt decomposition

\ket{\psi^{L}}=\sum_{i}s_{i}\ket{s_{i}}=\sum_{i}s_{i}\ket{p_{i}}\otimes\ket{\xi_{i}}.

The bound discussed in Lemma 1.2 of
Loring 2015
and reproduced in (
36
) informs us that
\ket{p_{1}}
may be optimally localized (i.e, with minimal variance) with respect to the operators used to construct a localizer. As such, we consider
\ket{\psi^{L}}
decomposed as

\ket{\psi^{L}}=s_{1}\ket{s_{1}}+\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}\ket{\phi},

(45)

where
\ket{\phi}
contains the rest of the Schmidt decomposition and is orthogonal to
\ket{s_{1}}
, i.e.,
\bra{s_{1}}{\phi}\rangle=0
.

In a similar spirit, we aim to provide an upper bound on the variance of
\ket{p_{1}}
that we can directly relate to the Heisenberg-Robertson uncertainty relation. We consider the expectation value of the scalar component of
L^{2}
[see eq. (
99
)] with respect to
\ket{s_{1}}

{\sum_{j}\expectationvalue{\tilde{X}^2_j \otimes I}}=\absolutevalue{\expval{L^2} - \sum_{j < k} \expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \otimes\Gamma_j\Gamma_k}}.

We first note that

\begin{split}\bra{s_{1}}{\tilde{X}^{2}_{j}}\otimes I\ket{s_{1}}&=\bra{p_{1}}{\tilde{X}^{2}_{j}}\ket{p_{1}}\bra{\xi_{1}}I\ket{\xi_{1}}\\
&=\bra{p_{1}}{\tilde{X}^{2}_{j}}\ket{p_{1}},\end{split}

yielding

{\sum_{j}\expectationvalue{\tilde{X}^2_j}}=\absolutevalue{\expval{L^2} - \sum_{j < k} \expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \otimes\Gamma_j\Gamma_k}}.

By the triangle inequality,
\absolutevalue{a+b}\leq\absolutevalue{a}+\absolutevalue{b}
, we have

\sum_{j}\expectationvalue{\tilde{X}^2_j}\leq\absolutevalue{\expval{L^2}}+\sum_{j<k}\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \otimes\Gamma_j\Gamma_k}}.

Now we estimate the commutator term. Since the expectation value is w.r.t. a product state
\ket{s_{1}}=\ket{p_{1}}\otimes\ket{\xi_{1}}
, we have

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \otimes\Gamma_j\Gamma_k} }=\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1} \comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \ket{\psch_1} \bra{\esch_1} \Gamma_j\Gamma_k \ket{\esch_1}}\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}\ket{p_{1}}\bra{\xi_{1}}\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}\ket{\xi_{1}}.\end{split}

We then use the fact that
\norm{\Gamma_j\Gamma_k}\leq\norm{\Gamma_j}\norm{\Gamma_k}
and
\norm{\Gamma_j}=1
to remove the Clifford element component, yielding

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{\bra{s_1} \comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \otimes\Gamma_j\Gamma_k \ket{s_1}}\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}\otimes\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}\ket{s_{1}}\leq\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1} \comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \ket{\psch_1} }\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}\ket{p_{1}}.\end{split}

We can now substitute back into the main bound, yielding

\sum_{j}\expectationvalue{\tilde{X}^2_j}\leq\absolutevalue{\expval{L^2}}+\sum_{j<k}\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}}}.

Note that
L^{2}
is a positive-semidefinite operator, i.e.,
\bra{s_{1}}L^{2}\ket{s_{1}}\geq 0
. This makes the absolute value around
\expectationvalue{L^2}
optional, although we retain it for this derivation so that terms will collect into a simpler expression. We now aim to make estimates of
\expectationvalue{L^2}
. We consider

\begin{split}&\bra{s_{1}}L^{2}\ket{\psi^{L}}=\bra{s_{1}}L^{2}\left(s_{1}\ket{s_{1}}+\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}\ket{\phi}\right),\\
\implies&\mu^{2}s_{1}=s_{1}\bra{s_{1}}L^{2}\ket{s_{1}}+\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}\bra{s_{1}}L^{2}\ket{\phi},\\
\implies&\bra{s_{1}}L^{2}\ket{s_{1}}=\mu^{2}-\frac{\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}}{s_{1}}\bra{s_{1}}L^{2}\ket{\phi}.\end{split}

We then have the updated bound,

\begin{split}\sum_{j}\expectationvalue{\tilde{X}^2_j}\leq\ &\mu^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}}{s_{1}}\absolutevalue{\bra{s_1} L^2 \ket{\phi}}L^{2}\ket{\phi}\\
+&\sum_{j<k}\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}}},\end{split}

(46)

where again we utilized the triangle inequality.
We now estimate the
\absolutevalue{\bra{s_1} L^2 \ket{\phi}}L^{2}\ket{\phi}
term by,

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{\bra{s_1} L^2 \ket{\phi}}L^{2}\ket{\phi}\leq&\sum_{j}\absolutevalue{\bra{s_1} \tX_j^2 \otimes I \ket{\phi}}{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}\otimes I\ket{\phi}\\
&+\sum_{j<k}\absolutevalue{\bra{s_1} \comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \otimes\Gamma_j \Gamma_k \ket{\phi}}\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}\otimes\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}\ket{\phi}.\end{split}

(47)

Looking at the first term, we have

\bra{s_{1}}{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}\otimes I\ket{\phi}=\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}}\bra{p_{1}}{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}\ket{p_{i}}\bra{\xi_{1}}I\ket{\xi_{i}},

which vanishes due to
\bra{\xi_{1}}I\ket{\xi_{i}}=\bra{\xi_{1}}{\xi_{i}}\rangle=0
for
i\neq 1
by the properties of the Schmidt decomposition. We now make estimates of the commutator terms in Eq. (
47
),

\begin{split}&\absolutevalue{\bra{s_1} \comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \otimes\Gamma_j \Gamma_k \ket{\phi}}\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}\otimes\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}\ket{\phi}\\
&=\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}}\absolutevalue{\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_i}{\sqrt{1-s_1^2}} \bra{\psch_1}\comm{\tX_j}{\tX_k}\ket{\psch_i}\bra{\esch_1}\Gamma_j\Gamma_k\ket{\esch_i}}\commutator{\tX_j}{\tX_k}\ket{p_{i}}\bra{\xi_{1}}\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}\ket{\xi_{i}},\\
&\leq\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{\sqrt{1-s_{1}^{2}}}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1}\comm{\tX_j}{\tX_k}\ket{\psch_i}}\commutator{\tX_j}{\tX_k}\ket{p_{i}},\end{split}

(48)

yielding the bound

\begin{split}\sum_{j}\expectationvalue{\tilde{X}^2_j}\leq\ &\mu^{2}+\sum_{j<k}\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{s_{1}}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1} \comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \ket{\psch_i}}\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}\ket{p_{i}}\\
+&\sum_{j<k}\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}}}.\end{split}

(49)

Lastly, using

\sum_{j}{\expectationvalue{\tX^2_j(r_j)}}=\sum_{j}\left(\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}+\expectationvalue{(\tX_j(0) - r_j)}^{2}\right),

(50)

we have the final upper bound

\begin{split}\sum_{j}\Delta{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}\leq\ &\mu^{2}({\bf r})-\sum_{j}\left(\expectationvalue{\tX_j(0)}-r_{j}\right)^{2}+\sum_{j<k}\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}}}\\
+&\sum_{j<k}\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{s_{1}}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1(\rv)} \comm{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k} \ket{\psch_i(\rv)}}\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}\ket{p_{i}({\bf r})}.\end{split}

(51)

Note that the step in (
50
) is only meaningfully valid for PBC localizers in the thermodynamic limit. See Appendices
C.4
and
D
for discussion on the bounds for PBC Spatial Localizers. From Eq. (
51
), we see that the LIF of
L(\mathbf{r})
, the Schmidt values of
\ket{\psi^{L}({\bf r})}
and the first off diagonal of the commutators
\commutator{\tilde{X}_j}{\tilde{X}_k}
projected into the basis of physical Schmidt vectors play a role in determining an upper bound on the variance of the physical Schmidt vector
\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}
. We note that the LIF can be replaced with any eigenvalue of
L({\bf r})
, so long as one considers physical Schmidt vectors of the corresponding localizer eigenvector.

C.3
Special case of 2 spatial dimensions: saturating the uncertainty relation

In 2 spatial dimensions, we have
{\tilde{X}}
and
\tilde{Y}
, with the upper bound

\begin{split}\Delta\tilde{X}^{2}+\Delta\tilde{Y}^{2}\leq&\mu^{2}+\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{s_{1}}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1} \comm{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}} \ket{\psch_i}}\commutator{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}}\ket{p_{i}}\\
+&\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}}}}-\norm{\expval{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(\rv)}}^{2},\end{split}

Such a bound is always valid in the case of OBC, and is valid in the case of PBC in the thermodynamic limit, provided
\ket{p_{1}}=\ket{p_{1}({\bf r})}
only has support near the extraction point,
{\bf r}
(Appendix
C.4
). Additionally, we have the uncertainty relation of Eq. (
41
), yielding

\begin{split}&\Delta\tilde{R}_{H}^{2}\leq\Delta\tilde{X}^{2}+\Delta\tilde{Y}^{2}\leq\Delta\tilde{R}_{H}^{2}+\mathcal{S},\\
&\Delta\tilde{R}_{H}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\expval{\comm{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}}}},\\
&\mathcal{S}=\mu^{2}-\norm{\expval{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(\rv)}}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{s_{i}}{s_{1}}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psch_1} \comm{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}} \ket{\psch_i}}\commutator{\tilde{X}}{\tilde{Y}}\ket{p_{i}},\\
\end{split}

(52)

where we see that the uncertainty bound is saturated if we have
\mu=0
and
s_{1}=1
(recall that
\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}=1
). Thus, for a 2D Spatial Localizer, the combination of
\mu
and
s_{1}
directly indicates how close we can get to saturating the HRUR with physical Schmidt vectors.

A natural further question is “when are we guaranteed to have
\mu=0
and/or
s_{1}=1
?”
In 2D OBC, we will always have
N_{occ}
unique zeros of the LIF (
\mu=0
points), where by “unique” we mean that the zeros correspond to distinct
\ket{p_{1}}
. To see this, consider the square of a 2D OBC localizer,

\begin{split}L^{2}({\bf r})&=({\tilde{X}}^{2}+{\tilde{Y}}^{2})\otimes I+{\mathrm{i}}\commutator{\tX}{\tY}\otimes\sigma_{z},\\
&={\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}{\tilde{Z}}\oplus{\tilde{Z}}{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger},\end{split}

(53)

where
{\tilde{Z}}={\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})={\tilde{X}}(x)+{\mathrm{i}}{\tilde{Y}}(y)
. Thus, the LIF of
L({\bf r})
is equivalent to the square root of the LIF of
{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r}){\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
. Thus, the LIF of
{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r}){\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
will have
N_{\mathrm{occ}}
LIF zeros, with the locations corresponding to the spectrum of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
. This can be seen by looking at an alternate representation of
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
,

\begin{split}{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})&={\tilde{X}}(x)+{\mathrm{i}}{\tilde{Y}}(y),\\
&={\tilde{X}}(0)+{\mathrm{i}}{\tilde{Y}}(0)-(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)V_{\mathrm{occ}}^{\dagger}V_{\mathrm{occ}},\\
&={\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})-\lambda({\bf r})V_{\mathrm{occ}}^{\dagger}V_{\mathrm{occ}},\end{split}

(54)

where
\lambda({\bf r})
is a number in the complex plane, determined by our choice of
{\bf r}
. From a spectral decomposition of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
, one can see that
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
will have a zero eigenvalue iff
\lambda({\bf r})
is equal to one of the
N_{\mathrm{occ}}
eigenvalues of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
.
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
then has
N_{\mathrm{occ}}
locations where
\mu({\bf r};{\tilde{Z}})=0
(counting degeneracies).
{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r}){\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
and
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r}){\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r})
then have zero eigenvalues at the same locations as
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
since
\sigma({\tilde{Z}}({\bf r}){\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r}))=\sigma({\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r}){\tilde{Z}}({\bf r}))=\{s_{i}({\tilde{Z}}({\bf r}))^{2}\}
where
s_{i}({\tilde{Z}}({\bf r}))
are the singular values of
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
(for non-normal operators, such as
{\tilde{Z}}
, the LIF should be defined using the singular value spectrum rather than the eigenvalue spectrum). We can then find the minima of the LIF for a 2D OBC system by simply diagonalizing
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
. Additionally, at the locations where
\lambda({{\bf r}^{*}})
is an eigenvalue of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
, the Spatial Localizer
L({{\bf r}^{*}})
will have at least a two-fold degenerate spectrum at zero by the chiral symmetry of
L({\bf r})
. Diagonalizing this subspace with respect to the chiral operator (
I\otimes\sigma_{z}
for 2D OBC) yields distinct localizer eigenstates corresponding to right/left eigenstates of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda({{\bf r}^{*}})
. To see this, note that the 2D OBC Spatial Localizer has the form

L({\bf r})=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\\
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})&0\end{pmatrix}.

(55)

In the case of a two-fold degenerate subspace corresponding to
\mu({{\bf r}^{*}})=0
, the basis of this subspace can be chosen as the eigenstates of the chiral operator, notated as
\ket{\psi_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
with the form

\begin{split}&\ket{\psi_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}=\begin{pmatrix}\ket{\psi_{\tilde{Z}}(\lambda({{\bf r}^{*}})}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\\
&\ket{\psi_{2}({{\bf r}^{*}})}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\ket{\psi_{{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}}(\lambda({{\bf r}^{*}})}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(56)

where
\ket{\psi_{\tilde{Z}}(\lambda({{\bf r}^{*}})}
(
\ket{\psi_{{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}}(\lambda({{\bf r}^{*}})}
) is the right (left) eigenvector of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda({\bf r}^{*})
and saturate the HRUR
Trifonov 1994
. We note that operators like
{\tilde{Z}}({\bf r})
have been referred to as a “vortex function” in the context of the vortexability of Chern bands
Ledwith et al. 2023
;
Okuma 2024
. However, our use case in this work is more general, as we are interested in both atomic and topological bands.

C.4
Equivalence between OBC and PBC in the thermodynamic limit

In this section, we discuss the equivalence in properties of OBC and PBC Spatial Localizers in the thermodynamic limit. Specifically, we (i) make the argument that the bounds derived for OBC Spatial Localizers can apply to PBC Spatial Localizers (in the thermodynamic limit) provided
\ket{p_{1}}
is localized and (ii) show that there exist at least
N_{\text{occ}}
points for a PBC Spatial Localizer where the LIF scales as
\mu(r^{*})\propto N^{-2}
, which correspond to the WCs.

C.4.1
Small angle approximation on localized states

Recall that the bounds for a PBC Spatial Localizer contain terms that look like
\bra{p_{1}({\bf r})}{\tilde{X}}_{j,J}({\bf r}){\tilde{X}}_{k,K}({\bf r})\ket{p_{i}({\bf r})}
where
j,k
denote spatial directions and
J,K\in\{S,C\}
denote the real/imaginary components of the projected Resta position operators used to construct a PBC Spatial Localizer.
Concretely, we have the components

\begin{split}{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}({\bf r})&=P\cos\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)P-P,\\
{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r})&=P\sin\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)P,\end{split}

(57)

which are equivalent to the definition given in Equation (
22
) up to whether we use the full projector,
P
, or the half-projector,
V_{\mathrm{occ}}
. We emphasize that the choice of
P
or
V_{\mathrm{occ}}
amounts to a choice of basis so long as one is working with states in the occupied subspace corresponding to the projector
P
. Furthermore, we note that the effect of shifting
{\bf r}
on the operators (
57
) amounts to a shift in the extraction point of the Spatial Localizer.

We now consider a localized wave packet,
\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
that lies purely in the occupied subspace and is localized around some point
{\bf r}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\begin{split}&P\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}=\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}},\\
&\bra{{\bf r}}{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}\rangle\approx 0\ \ \text{for}\ \ \absolutevalue{\rv-\rv'}>\epsilon,\end{split}

(58)

where
\epsilon
is some positive number that denotes the radius of the region in which
\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
has support. We represent the expansion of
\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
in the position basis as
\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}=\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\ket{{\bf r}}
where
\alpha_{\bf r}\equiv\bra{{\bf r}}{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}\rangle
. We now consider the action of
{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}({\bf r}^{\prime})
and
{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r}^{\prime})
on
\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
. For
{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
, we have

\begin{split}{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}&({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}=\left[P\cos\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)P-P\right]\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}\\
&=P\cos\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}-P\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}\\
&=P\cos\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\ket{{\bf r}}-P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\ket{{\bf r}}\\
&=P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\cos\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{{\bf r}}-P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\ket{{\bf r}}\\
&=P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\cos\left(\frac{({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{{\bf r}}-P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\ket{{\bf r}}\\
&=P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\left[\cos\left(\frac{({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)-1\right]\ket{{\bf r}}.\\
\end{split}

(59)

For
{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
, we have

\begin{split}{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}&=P\sin\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)P\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}\\
&=P\sin\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}\\
&=P\sin\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\ket{{\bf r}}\\
&=P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\sin\left(\frac{(\hat{{\bf R}}-{\bf r}^{\prime}I)\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{{\bf r}}\\
&=P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\sin\left(\frac{({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{{\bf r}}.\\
\end{split}

(60)

For clarity of discussion, we have the net results of the application of
{\tilde{X}}_{j,J}({\bf r}^{\prime})
on
\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
as

\begin{split}{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}&=P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\left[\cos\left(\frac{({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)-1\right]\ket{{\bf r}}\\
{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}&=P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\sin\left(\frac{({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{{\bf r}}.\end{split}

(61)

Recalling that
\alpha_{\bf r}\approx 0
for
\absolutevalue{\rv-\rv'}>\epsilon
, we can make a small angle approximation of the cosine and sine terms for small
\frac{\epsilon}{N_{j}}\ll 1
. This yields

\begin{split}{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}&\approx P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\left[1-1\right]\ket{{\bf r}}=0,\\
{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}&\approx P\sum_{\bf r}\alpha_{\bf r}\left(\frac{({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf b}_{j}}{N_{j}}\right)\ket{{\bf r}}\\
&=\frac{2\pi}{N_{j}}{\tilde{X}}_{j}(r^{\prime}_{j})\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}.\end{split}

(62)

We have now shown that, for a state
\ket{\psi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}}
localized around
{\bf r}^{\prime}
, the action of
{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}({\bf r}^{\prime})
and
{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r}^{\prime})
on
\ket{\psi_{\bf r}^{\prime}}
can be approximated respectively as 0 and
\frac{2\pi}{N_{j}}{\tilde{X}}_{j}(r^{\prime}_{j})
for
2\pi r^{\prime}_{j}={\bf r}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf b}_{j}
due to the reciprocal lattice relation
\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot{\bf b}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
. Thus, if the first physical Schmidt vector,
\ket{p_{1}({\bf r}^{\prime})}
, remains localized around the point
{\bf r}^{\prime}
as
N_{j}
increases (which we observe for all PBC systems tested), the OBC and PBC bounds on the sum of variances derived earlier in this section become equivalent in the thermodynamic limit.
We note that, while exponential localization of
\ket{p_{1}({\bf r}^{\prime})}
is optimal for the equivalence to hold at smaller
N_{j}
, the equivalence holds as
N\rightarrow\infty
so long as
\alpha_{\bf r}\approx 0
for
\absolutevalue{\rv-\rv'}>\epsilon
regardless of other qualities of the distribution of
\alpha_{\bf r}
.

C.4.2
\order{N^{-2}}
indicator scaling at
N_{\textrm{occ}}
points

In this section, we discuss the existence of
N_{\textrm{occ}}
points where the LIF scales as
\mu({\bf r})\propto N^{-2}
in the thermodynamic limit. We begin by stating some assumptions required for the scaling behavior.

Assumption 1
(Existence of localized states)

Fix a radius
\epsilon>0
that is independent of system size. We consider
\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime};\epsilon)}\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{occ}}
to be the subset of localized states whose position-space weight is concentrated within a distance
\epsilon
of
{\bf r}^{\prime}
. We assume
\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime};\epsilon)}\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{occ}}
is non-empty at points of interest.

We define the operator norm on the restricted sector as

\norm{A}_{\mathrm{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime})}=\max_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime};\epsilon)}}A\norm{A\ket{\psi}},

(63)

where we have
\norm{\ket{\psi}}=1
. Note that
\norm{A}_{\mathrm{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime})}
is not the full operator norm of
A
, but only the norm of
A
acting on localized states.

Assumption 2
(Small norms on localized states)

There is a constant
c_{\epsilon}
(independent of system size) such that, for all
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime};\epsilon)}
,

\frac{2\pi}{N_{x}}{\tilde{X}}(x^{\prime})\norm{\frac{2\pi}{N_x}\tX(x') \ket{\psi}}<\frac{{c_{\epsilon}}}{N_{X}},\quad\frac{2\pi}{N_{y}}{\tilde{Y}}(y^{\prime})\norm{\frac{2\pi}{N_y}\tY(y') \ket{\psi}}<\frac{{c_{\epsilon}}}{N_{y}},

(64)

where
{\bf r}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

We now note that the 2D PBC Spatial Localizer can be written as

L_{N}({\bf r})=L_{\text{eff}}({\bf r})+\Delta_{N}({\bf r}),

(65)

where

L_{\text{eff}}({\bf r})=\frac{2\pi}{N}{\tilde{X}}(x)\otimes\Gamma_{2}+\frac{2\pi}{N}{\tilde{Y}}(y)\otimes\Gamma_{4},

(66)

with
\Delta_{N}({\bf r})
holding the rest of the Spatial Localizer, and we assume
N_{x}=N_{y}=N
. We then have

\norm{\Delta_N(\rv')}_{\mathrm{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime})}=\order{N^{-2}}

(67)

since
\epsilon
is independent of system size.

L_{\text{eff}}({\bf r})
can be seen as a 2D OBC Spatial Localizer utilizing a reducible representation of
\text{Cl}_{2,0}
, and thus there exists
N_{\text{occ}}
choices of
{\bf r}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
such that
L_{\text{eff}}({\bf r}^{\prime})
has at least two zero modes where
L_{\text{eff}}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{\psi^{L}({\bf r}^{\prime})}=0
where the
{\bf r}^{\prime}
points are determined by the spectrum of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})={\tilde{X}}(0)+{\tilde{Y}}(0)
. These zero modes can be made to manifest as product states between
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{occ}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\Gamma}
where the physical Schmidt vectors are the right/left eigenvectors of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
and the embedding Schmidt vectors are eigenstates of the chiral operator (Appendix
C.3
).
Let the right (left) eigenstates of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
notated as
\ket{\psi_{\tilde{Z}}(\lambda({\bf r}^{*})}
(
\ket{\psi_{{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}}(\lambda({\bf r}^{*})}
) where
\lambda({\bf r}^{*})=x^{*}+{\mathrm{i}}y^{*}
is a corresponding eigenvalue of
{\tilde{Z}}(\mathbf{0})
.

Assumption 3
(Localization of
{\tilde{Z}}
eigenstates)

For each
{\bf r}^{*}
, we have
\ket{\psi_{\tilde{Z}}(\lambda({\bf r}^{*})}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}({\bf r}^{*};\epsilon)}
for some
\epsilon
that is independent of system size.

Given assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
, we have

\mu({\bf r}^{*};L_{N}({\bf r}^{*}))\leq\order{N^{-2}}\quad(N\rightarrow\infty).

(68)

To see this, consider a (normalized) state
\ket{\Psi({\bf r}^{*})}=\ket{\psi_{\tilde{Z}}(\lambda({\bf r}^{*})}\otimes\ket{\zeta}
where
\ket{\zeta}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\Gamma}
is a unit vector such that
L_{\text{eff}}({\bf r}^{*})\ket{\Psi({\bf r}^{*})}=0
. The choice of
\ket{\zeta}
can be the corresponding eigenvector of the embedding component of the chiral symmetry operator of the Spatial Localizer, as all PBC Spatial Localizers possess chiral symmetry. We then have

\begin{split}L_{N}({\bf r}^{*})\norm{L_N(\rv^*) \ket{\Psi(\rv^*)}}&=\left(L_{\text{eff}}({\bf r}^{*})+\Delta_{N}({\bf r}^{*})\right)\norm{\left(L_\text{eff}(\rv^*) + \Delta_N(\rv^*)\right)\ket{\Psi(\rv^*)}}\\
&=\Delta_{N}({\bf r}^{*})\norm{\Delta_N(\rv^*)\ket{\Psi(\rv^*)}}\\
&\leq\norm{\Delta_N(\rv^*)}_{\mathrm{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime})}\norm{\ket{\Psi(\rv^*)}}\\
L_{N}({\bf r}^{*})\norm{L_N(\rv^*) \ket{\Psi(\rv^*)}}&\leq\order{N^{-2}}.\\
\end{split}

(69)

We have now proven the existence (given our assumption of
\ket{\psi_{\tilde{Z}}(\lambda({\bf r}^{*})}
being localized) of a state such that
L_{N}({\bf r}^{*})\norm{L_N(\rv^*) \ket{\Psi(\rv^*)}}\leq\order{N^{-2}}
. By the variational principle, we then have that

\mu({\bf r}^{*};L_{N}({\bf r}^{*}))\leq\order{N^{-2}}\quad(N\rightarrow\infty)

(70)

since
\mu({\bf r}^{*};L_{N}({\bf r}^{*}))\leq L_{N}({\bf r}^{*})\norm{L_N(\rv^*)\ket{\Psi}}\ \forall\ \ket{\Psi}
such that
\norm{\ket{\Psi}}=1
. As an example of this scaling, we present
\mu({\bf r}^{*})
for both the topological regimes of the QWZ model in Figure

where we see
N^{-2}
scaling in the LIF,
\mu
, for both the trivial and topological regimes of the Qi-Wu-Zhang (QWZ) model
Qi et al. 2006
at the minimum of
\mu
. Furthermore, we see the same scaling behavior of
\mu
at the maximal point in the unit cell for the topological QWZ model[Fig.

{\tilde{Z}}^{\dagger}({\bf r})
) from the Spatial Localizer. Additionally, we observe the quantity
\absolutevalue{1-s_1}\propto N^{-2}
, suggesting that the localizer eigenstate corresponding to the LIF converges to the product state
\ket{\Psi({\bf r}^{*})}
(or its chiral counterpart) in the thermodynamic limit.

(71)

where

and
\mathcal{S}
are defined in Eq. (
38

Note that, for PBC localizers,

Appendix D
Periodic Spatial Localizers and the Marzari-Vanderbilt localization functional

Here, we discuss connections between the upper bound derived in Appendix
C
for PBC Spatial Localizers and the Marzari-Vanderbilt (MV) localization functional
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
. Specifically, we illustrate an alternative viewpoint on why our derived bound is relevant for periodic Spatial Localizers. That is, a viewpoint beyond the argument that
{\tilde{X}}_{j,C}({\bf r})\approx 0
and
{\tilde{X}}_{j,S}({\bf r})\approx\frac{2\pi}{N}{\tilde{X}}_{j}(r_{j})
in the thermodynamic limit, where we then obtain the same viewpoint as in a OBC Spatial Localizer. We restrict our analysis to 1D here, but extending to multiple spatial dimensions and non-cubic lattices is straightforward and amounts to including additional projected Resta position operators in the definition of a Spatial Localizer. Concretely, we use one Resta position operator for each pair of
\pm{\bf b}
vectors used in the method of MV
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
to define an isotropic gradient in
k
-space.

We note that this section considers localizers that use the full projector
P

Appendix E
Constructing localized state representations for occupied bands from the Spatial Localizer

In this appendix, we present how to use a Spatial Localizer to construct a localized state representation. We begin by describing how to use the LIF to find favorable locations to extract localized states, and discuss how the case of commuting projected position operators
{\tilde{X}}_{j}
results in WCs arising as zeroes of the LIF. Then, given a choice of extraction locations, we discuss how to process localizer eigenstates to extract the localized states and (if desired) orthogonalize the states to construct a localized and orthogonal basis.

Given a Spatial Localizer
L(\mathbf{r})\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{occ}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\Gamma})
, we outline a two–step procedure to obtain a localized orthonormal basis (WFs under translation symmetry, or Wannier-like orbitals when translation symmetry is broken).

Step 1: Locate centers via the LIF.

Scan the LIF,
\mu(\mathbf{r})
, over positions
\mathbf{r}
in the system. Its (global) minima identify WCs (or generalized centers) from which we will extract localized states. For systems with discrete translation invariance, it suffices to scan a single unit cell; spatial symmetries can further reduce the scan region by reflection/rotation of
\mu(\mathbf{r})
.

In multi-band settings (e.g., more than one electron per cell), the physically relevant localized content may live in the
low-lying localizer bands
and not exclusively in
\mu({\bf r})
. In such cases, examine a small set of the lowest localizer bands during the scan.

A particularly transparent situation occurs when the projected position operators commute,
[\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{X}_{k}]=0
for all
j,k
. Then the minima of
\mu(\mathbf{r})
are zeros that coincide with the simultaneous eigenvalues
\{{\bf r}^{W}\}
of the
\tilde{X}_{j}
. This can be seen from the square of the localizer,

\begin{split}L^{2}({\bf r})&=\sum_{j}{\tilde{X}}_{j}^{2}\otimes I+\sum_{k<l}{\commutator{\tX_k}{\tX_l}}\otimes\Gamma_{k}\Gamma_{l}\\
&=Q\otimes I+\sum_{k<l}{\commutator{\tX_k}{\tX_l}}\otimes\Gamma_{k}\Gamma_{l},\end{split}

(99)

where
Q
is the quadratic operator
Cerjan et al. 2023
. When the commutators vanish, there exists a common eigenbasis
\{\,|\phi_{W}\rangle\,\}
for all
\tilde{X}_{j}
, with
\tilde{X}_{j}(0)=\sum_{W}x^{W}_{j}\,|\phi_{W}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{W}|
. Evaluating

\begin{split}\sum_{j}{\tilde{X}}^{2}_{j}(r_{j})&=\sum_{j}({\tilde{X}}_{j}(\mathbf{0})-r_{j}I)^{2}\\
&=\sum_{j,W}(r^{W}_{j}-r_{j})^{2}\outerproduct{\phi_W}{\phi_W}.\end{split}

(100)

shows that the spectrum of
\sum_{j}\tilde{X}_{j}^{\,2}(\mathbf{r})
is the set
\{\,\sum_{j}(r^{W}_{j}-r_{j})^{2}\,\}
. Hence
L^{2}({\bf r})
(and by Hermiticity,
L({\bf r})
) exhibits a zero in the LIF exactly at
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}^{W}
, and at those points the localizer eigenvectors reproduce (up to the embedding multiplicity
g
) the simultaneous eigenvectors
|\phi_{w}\rangle
.

Step 2: Disentangle embedding DOFs via a Schmidt decomposition.

At each chosen center
{\bf r}^{W}
, take one (or more) eigenvector(s) of
L({\bf r}^{W})
and decompose across the physical and embedding Hilbert spaces,

|\psi^{L}\rangle=\sum_{i}s_{i}\,|s_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i}s_{i}\,|p_{i}\rangle\otimes|\xi_{i}\rangle,\qquad\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}=1,

(101)

where
\{|p_{i}\rangle\}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{occ}}
and
\{|\xi_{i}\rangle\}\in\mathcal{H}_{\Gamma}
are orthonormal Schmidt vectors and
s_{i}
are Schmidt values. As a practical choice of
trial WF
at
{\bf r}^{W}
, take the leading physical Schmidt vector
|p_{1}\rangle
. The resulting set of trial states is not guaranteed to be orthogonal; apply Löwdin orthogonalization to obtain an orthonormal localized basis.

In periodic crystals a convenient workflow is: (i) extract one localizer eigenstate per occupied band in a single unit cell, (ii) project the corresponding trial states onto the occupied Bloch subspace, and (iii) perform
k
-resolved Löwdin orthogonalization, recovering a Wannier-style construction compatible with the standard projection method. In essence, we are using the projection method
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
once we have extracted trial states from a Spatial Localizer. However, we emphasize that the physical Schmidt vectors are already restricted to live in the occupied subspace, so there is no loss of norm from projecting the physical Schmidt vectors onto the Bloch states (i.e., it is more of a basis transformation than projection, but the overall method is the same).
Without translation symmetry, one needs to extract each state they are interested in from the minima of
\mu
and perform Löwdin orthogonalization on the entire set.

Remarks on degeneracies and band selection.

Degenerate minima of
\mu(\mathbf{r})
can arise from symmetry or from multiple occupied bands. In such cases, one can harvest the physically distinct localized content by comparing the
physical
Schmidt vectors obtained from a small set of low-lying localizer bands. A systematic (localizer) band-selection criterion is an interesting direction for future work, but is not required for the examples considered here.

F.1.1
Comparison with Wannier90

Here we illustrate the use of our Spatial Localizer framework on the three-band model of monolayer
\mathrm{WSe_{2}}
discussed in the main text.
The periodic Spatial Localizer constructed for this model inherits
chiral symmetry
; as a result, its spectrum is symmetric about
0
, so we may confine attention to the non-negative part.
Additionally, we must consider a third Resta position operator
X_{3,R}=X_{2,R}^{\dagger}X_{1,R}^{\dagger}
due to the triangular lattice
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
, resulting in the Clifford elements
\Gamma_{j}
having dimension
g=8
. We use the Weyl-Brauer matrices
Brauer and Weyl 1935
as the
\Gamma_{j}
, which correspond to the generators of the Clifford algebra
\mathrm{Cl}_{6,0}
.

Scanning the (positive) localizer bands corresponding to the LIF reveals a two-fold degenerate minimum at
{\bf r}^{W}=\frac{1}{3}(\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{2})
as seen in Fig.
7
. We denote these degenerate localizer eigenvectors by
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
.
Let

|\psi_{j}\rangle\;=\;\sum_{i}s_{i}^{(j)}\,|p_{i}^{\,j}\rangle\otimes|\xi_{i}^{\,j}\rangle,\qquad j=1,2,

be their Schmidt decompositions into
physical
(
|p_{i}^{\,j}\rangle
) and
embedding
(
|\xi_{i}^{\,j}\rangle
) vectors.
We observe that the two Schmidt spectra are identical, and thus
\ket{\psi_{1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}}
are related by a product of unitary operators acting separately on physical and embedding sectors,
U_{\text{occ}}\otimes U_{\Gamma}
, i.e. they are equivalent up to local unitary operations.

For both eigenvectors, the leading Schmidt value
s_{1}^{(j)}
is non-degenerate.
In our numerics, we find

\left|\bra{p^{(1)}_{1}}{p^{(2)}_{1}}\rangle\right|=e^{{\mathrm{i}}\phi},\qquad\bra{\xi^{(1)}_{1}}{\xi^{(2)}_{1}}\rangle=0,

(112)

i.e., the two degenerate localizer eigenvectors share the same leading
physical
Schmidt vector (up to phase), and differ primarily in the embedding sector, despite the degenerate LIF. The role of symmetries and degeneracies of localizer spectra/eigenstates will be discussed in future work.

Although the Spatial Localizer produces localized states, it is not yet proven that it invariably yields
maximally
localized WFs.
For
\mathrm{WSe_{2}}
, we observe that the WFs constructed from
|p_{1}^{\,1}\rangle
are
real
up to a global phase, a common heuristic of having reached the global minimum of the spread functional
Marzari et al. 2012
in the absence of spin-orbit coupling.

To test optimality, we use Wannier90
Mostofi et al. 2008
;
Pizzi et al. 2020
;
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
, a standard software package for finding maximally localized WFs by optimizing the gauge of Bloch functions. In this method, one needs (i) the Bloch functions for the occupied bands and (ii) an ansatz state to serve as an initial gauge choice.
Besides the physical Schmidt vector
|p_{1}^{\,1}\rangle
, we supplied two intuitive ansatz states:
(i) a delta-function centered on a W atom, and
(ii) a delta-function centered on
{\bf r}^{W}=\tfrac{1}{3}(\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{2}
), which is the WC predicted by band representation theory.
Both trials are symmetrized to the trivial
C_{3}
irrep, yielding

\begin{split}|g_{\text{ion}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigl(C_{3}^{2}+C_{3}+I\bigr)\,|0,0\rangle,\\
|g_{\text{wc}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigl(C_{3}^{2}({\bf r}^{W})+C_{3}({\bf r}^{W})+I\bigr)\,|1,0\rangle,\end{split}

(113)

where
|n,m\rangle
is the basis state located at the unit cell
n\mathbf{a}_{1}+m\mathbf{a}_{2}
with equal phase and weight on internal degrees of freedom.
After 200 total iterations of spread minimization, we find that the
\ket{p_{1}}
WF spread did not change, while the
|g_{\text{wc}}\rangle
WF spread converges to the spread of the
\ket{p_{1}}
WF (see Figure
9
). Furthermore, the
|g_{\text{ion}}\rangle
WF spread became trapped in a higher-spread local minimum.

The
\mathrm{WSe_{2}}
test therefore supports, but does not yet prove, the conjecture that Spatial Localizers furnish a gauge of maximally localized WFs without external seeding or knowledge of the symmetry representations present in the occupied bands. Establishing general conditions for this property and clarifying the role of symmetries, such as space group and anti-unitary symmetries, remain directions for future work.

F.2
Space group F222 Model

As a three-dimensional example, we consider a space group F222 model
Cano et al. 2022
. This model realizes two obstructed atomic limits whose Wannier centers lie at the inequivalent Wyckoff positions
4a
and
4b
, located at
\mathbf{r}_{\text{4a}}=(0,0,0)
and
\mathbf{r}_{\text{4b}}=(0,0,\tfrac{1}{2})
in conventional coordinates. The lattice is face-centered orthorhombic, with primitive direct-lattice vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(0,b,c),

(114)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(a,0,c),

(115)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(a,b,0),

(116)

and corresponding reciprocal vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{1}

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(-\frac{1}{a},\frac{1}{b},\frac{1}{c}\right),

(117)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(\frac{1}{a},-\frac{1}{b},\frac{1}{c}\right),

(118)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{3}

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(\frac{1}{a},\frac{1}{b},-\frac{1}{c}\right).

(119)

For simplicity, we set
a=b=c=1
. In the minimal two-band description, the Bloch Hamiltonian takes the stacked Rice–Mele form

H_{0}(\mathbf{k})=-\frac{\epsilon}{2}(1+\sigma_{z})-\frac{t}{2}\left(1+\sigma_{z}\cos k_{z}+\sigma_{y}\sin k_{z}\right),

(120)

which transitions between the two obstructed atomic limits as the model parameters are varied.

To move away from this effectively stacked one-dimensional limit while preserving the symmetry of the model, we add the perturbation

\Delta H(\mathbf{k})=(\cos k_{x}-\sin k_{y})\sin k_{z}\,\sigma_{x}.

(121)

This term introduces explicit dependence on all three crystal-momentum components and mixes the
k_{z}
chain structure with the transverse directions. The full Hamiltonian is therefore

H(\mathbf{k})=H_{0}(\mathbf{k})+\Delta H(\mathbf{k}).

(122)

In the calculations shown in the main text, this perturbation is included so that the model is no longer reducible to decoupled Rice–Mele-like chains, while the two insulating phases remain associated with Wannier centers at
4a
and
4b
.

For visualization, we evaluate the LIF along the boundary of a conventional F222 unit cell with one octant removed. This choice makes the inequivalent candidate Wannier-center locations visible within a single three-dimensional rendering and allows the minima associated with the two phases to be read off directly from the boundary data. In the phase connected to the
4a
limit, the minima occur at the corner-type positions of the cell, whereas in the phase connected to the
4b
limit they occur at the corresponding half-translated positions. The octant cut therefore provides a convenient way to display, in a single figure, the distinct real-space center structure of the two obstructed atomic phases.

Regarding the construction of the corresponding Spatial Localizer, we note that one needs to include a 4th Resta position operator,
X_{4,R}=X_{3,R}^{\dagger}X_{2,R}^{\dagger}X_{1,R}^{\dagger}
to ensure isotropic localization.

F.4.1
Overcompleteness relations

Given a coherent state
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
with Berry connection vortex centered at
{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}
, an overcompleteness relation (an equation showing the linear dependence of a set of states) can be made for the set of states comprised of
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
translated to every unit cell in the system.
Let
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}
denote
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
centered at the unit cell located at
{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}=n_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}+n_{2}\mathbf{a}_{2}
for primitive lattice vectors
\mathbf{a}_{i}
and
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}
can then be described as

\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}=T_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}=\sum_{\bf k}e^{{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\alpha_{\bf k}\ket{\psi_{{\bf k}}},

(125)

where
T_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}
is the translation operator,
\ket{\psi_{\bf k}}
are Bloch states of the occupied Chern band, and
\alpha_{\bf k}
are complex coefficients. Furthermore, we have
\alpha_{{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}}=0
(the coherent state has no support at the center of the vortex) and we have made use of the relation
T_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}\ket{\psi_{\bf k}}=e^{{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\ket{\psi_{{\bf k}}}
for position-space translations on Bloch states. For a vortex center
{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}
, we then have the overcompleteness relation

\begin{split}\sum_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}&=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}},{\bf k}}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}e^{{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\alpha_{\bf k}\ket{\psi_{{\bf k}}}\\
&=\sum_{\bf k}\alpha_{\bf k}\sum_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}e^{{\mathrm{i}}({\bf k}-{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}})\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\ket{\psi_{{\bf k}}}\\
&\propto\sum_{\bf k}\alpha_{\bf k}\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}}\ket{\psi_{{\bf k}}}\\
&=0,\end{split}

(126)

where the last line is zero due to the combination of
\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}}
and
\alpha_{{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}}=0
. Such an overcompleteness relation has been discussed
Li et al. 2024
in the context of a generalized Perelomov identity
Perelomov 1971
for Chern bands. In our case for the QWZ model with
C=1
,
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
has a vortex located at the
M
point (
{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}=(\pi,\pi)
), yielding the overcompleteness relation

\begin{split}\sum_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}}\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}&=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\pi(n_{1}+n_{2})}\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}\\
&=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}}(-1)^{(n_{1}+n_{2})}\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}}+{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}})}\\
&=0,\end{split}

(127)

which has a striking similarity to the overcompleteness relation given by Thouless
Thouless 1984
for the coherent states of the lowest Landau level, where the only difference is a factor of
(-1)^{n_{1}n_{2}}
due to the use of magnetic translations in the symmetric gauge of the Quantum Hall Effect (QHE)
Fradkin 2013
;
Okuma 2024
as opposed to lattice translations in a Chern insulator. We note that the overcompleteness can be seen directly from the magnitude of the overlaps between
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
and the occupied Bloch states
\ket{\psi_{1,{\bf k}}}
, given by
\langle\psi_{1,{\bf k}}\absolutevalue{\langle\psi_{1,\kv} \ket{\psch_1(\rextract)}}
. We see that
\langle\psi_{1,{\bf k}^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{\langle\psi_{1,\kv'} \ket{\psch_1(\rextract)}}=0
. Furthermore, we note that the overlap
\langle\psi_{1,{\bf k}^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{\langle\psi_{1,\kv'} \ket{\psch_2(\rextract)}}\neq 0
, which motivated the use of
\ket{p_{1}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
and
\ket{p_{2}({{\bf r}^{*}})}
in the construction of WFs for the Chern phase. We present the overlaps
\langle\psi_{1,{\bf k}}\absolutevalue{\langle\psi_{1,\kv} \ket{\psch_i(\rextract)}}
for all physical Schmidt vectors in Figure
14
.

We note that we do not present data regarding the WFs in the Chern phase as a general method for constructing WFs from the coherent states is beyond the scope of this work and we do not generally expect that
\ket{p_{2}({\bf r})}
will be optimal for constructing a WF in superposition with

F.4.3
Vortices and Polarization

Figure 19:
Berry curvature
\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})
(a) and corresponding smooth potential
\phi_{s}({\bf k})
(b) of the QWZ model in the Chern phase (
C=1
,
m=1
).

Recently, Gunawardana et al.
Gunawardana et al. 2025
proposed a gauge-invariant method for calculating the polarization of a Chern insulator, provided one is in the vortex gauge
Gunawardana et al. 2024
, corresponding to a vortex (or vortices in the case of
\absolutevalue{C}>1
) in a divergence-free Berry connection. The polarization for a single band is calculated as

\frac{\mathbf{P}}{e}=-\frac{{\langle\hat{{\bf R}}\rangle}}{V_{c}}-\frac{C}{2\pi}\hat{z}\times\mathbf{k_{v}},

(128)

where
{\langle\hat{{\bf R}}\rangle}
is the WC,
V_{c}
is the volume (area in 2D) of the unit cell,
C
is the Chern number, and
\mathbf{k_{v}}=k_{vx}\hat{x}+k_{vy}\hat{y}
is the location of the vortex in the Brillouin zone. We note that the corresponding expression in
Gunawardana et al. 2025
differs by a minus sign on the second term. This is due to a difference of sign in the definition of the Berry connection, and thus a difference of sign in the calculation of the Chern number.

For the WF of the
C=1
QWZ model presented in the main text, we approximate the vortex location as the average momentum of the magnitude of the Berry connection in the Brillouin zone, e.g.,

\begin{split}k_{vx}&=\frac{\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}dk_{x}dk_{y}\ \ k_{x}|\mathcal{A}(k_{x},k_{y})|}{\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}dk_{x}dk_{y}\ \ |\mathcal{A}(k_{x},k_{y})|},\\
k_{vy}&=\frac{\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}dk_{x}dk_{y}\ \ k_{y}|\mathcal{A}(k_{x},k_{y})|}{\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}dk_{x}dk_{y}\ \ |\mathcal{A}(k_{x},k_{y})|}.\end{split}

(129)

With these (approximate) vortex locations, we calculate a polarization equal to the value predicted in
Gunawardana et al. 2025
up to
\order{10^{-2}}
. We note that the work in
Gunawardana et al. 2025
uses a spin-rotated QWZ model. However, such a spin rotation will have no effect on polarization as all intra-unit-cell degrees of freedom lie at the same point in position space (i.e., the spin rotation operator commutes with the position operator and has no effect on the Berry connection).

Furthermore,
Gunawardana et al. 2024
predicts the optimal vortex location, i.e., the
{\bf k}_{\mathbf{v}}
that minimizes the variance of the corresponding WF, to be located at the maximum of the potential,
\phi_{s}({\bf k})
, which can be calculated as

\phi_{s}({\bf k})=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\mathcal{F}({{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}})}{\norm{\transvec}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}},

(130)

where
\mathcal{F}({{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}})
are the Fourier components of the Berry curvature. We observe that our vortex center is located at the predicted optimal location, namely the
M
point for the Chern insulator in this work, with an error of
\order{10^{-2}}
. We present both the Berry curvature,
\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})
and the smooth potential
\phi_{s}({\bf k})
in Figure
19
.
