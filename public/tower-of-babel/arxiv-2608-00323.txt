On a conjecture of Han and Xiong for fractional Gaussian binomial coefficients
Ken Ono

Abstract
. Han and Xiong recently extended the Gaussian binomial coefficient [ r + k k ] q \genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r+k}{k}_{q} to positive rational r r and conjectured that its “integer trace”, the integer-exponent part of the resulting power series, is coefficientwise largest at r = 1 / 2 r=1/2 . We prove a support-dominance theorem comparing rational parameters under an explicit divisibility condition. It settles the conjecture for every r ≥ 1 / 2 r\geq 1/2 and reduces the full conjecture to the unit fractions r = 1 2 ​ m r=\frac{1}{2m} , only finitely many of which are nontrivial for each fixed k k . A computer computation then verifies the conjecture for every positive rational r r and every k ≤ 200 k\leq 200 . The theoretical results were autonomously produced and verified in Lean by AxiomProver.

1.
Introduction and Statement of Results

The binomial coefficient
\binom{N}{k}
counts the
k
-element subsets of an

N
-element set. Its classical
q
-analogue is defined using the

q
-shifted factorial

(z;q)_{n}:=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-zq^{j}),\qquad(z;q)_{0}:=1.

For integers
N\geq k\geq 0
, the
Gaussian binomial coefficient
(or

q
-binomial coefficient
) is

(1)

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{k}_{q}:=\frac{(q;q)_{N}}{(q;q)_{k}(q;q)_{N-k}}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{N-k+j}}{1-q^{j}}.

Despite its appearance as a rational function,
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{k}_{q}
is a polynomial
in
q
with nonnegative integer coefficients, and it relates to the classical
objects in several standard ways. First, it recovers

\binom{N}{k}
as a limiting case: each factor

(1-q^{N-k+j})/(1-q^{j})
in (
1
) tends to
(N-k+j)/j
as

q\to 1
, whence

\lim_{q\to 1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{k}_{q}=\binom{N}{k}.

Second, it satisfies the finite
q
-binomial theorem

(2)

(z;q)_{k}=\sum_{s=0}^{k}(-1)^{s}q^{\binom{s}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{s}_{q}z^{s},

which specializes at
q=1
to the classical binomial theorem

(1-z)^{k}=\sum_{s=0}^{k}(-1)^{s}\binom{k}{s}z^{s}

(for example, see

[
1
, Theorem 3.3]
or
[
2
, Chapter 1]
).
Combinatorially,
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{k}_{q}
is the rank-generating function for integer
partitions whose Ferrers diagrams fit inside a
k\times(N-k)
rectangle
(for example, see

[
1
, Chapter 3]
and
[
4
, Section 1.7]
).

Han and Xiong
[
3
]
recently asked what becomes of these objects
when the index is allowed to be
fractional
. For a positive rational
number
r
and an integer
k\geq 0
, they define

(3)

G_{r,k}(q):=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r+k}{k}_{q}:=\frac{(q^{r+1};q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}},

which agrees with (
1
) when
r
is a positive integer (take

N=r+k
). If
r=a/b
is nonintegral and in lowest terms, then
G_{r,k}(q)
is
naturally a formal power series in the fractional power
q^{1/b}
. Han and
Xiong extract from it an ordinary power series by discarding every term whose
exponent is not an integer. Writing
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{Z}}
for this
integer trace

(made precise in Section
2
), they set

(4)

\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q):=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(G_{r,k}(q)\bigr)\in\mathbb{Z}[[q]].

When
r
is a positive integer, every exponent occurring in
G_{r,k}
is
already an integer, nothing is discarded, and
\mathcal{T}_{r,k}=G_{r,k}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r+k}{k}_{q}

is the ordinary Gaussian binomial polynomial. Thus taking the trace is
nontrivial only when
r\notin\mathbb{Z}
. In that case,
Proposition
2.2
shows that the finite
q
-binomial
numerator retains only the summands whose indices are divisible by the
reduced denominator of
r
, often leaving a sparse subseries; the resulting
trace is no longer given by an evident product formula.
Example
1.2
carries out the extraction for three values of
r
.
To compare such series coefficientwise, for
F,G\in\mathbb{Q}[[q]]
we write

F\succeq_{q}G\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[q^{n}](F-G)\geq 0\quad\text{for every }n\geq 0.

This fractional-index framework is due to
[
3
]
. We have
changed only the notation, writing
\mathcal{T}_{r,k}
for their

\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}r+k\\
k\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]_{q}\big|_{\mathbb{Z}}
and

\succeq_{q}
for their
\geq_{q}
. The central conjecture of

[
3
, Conjecture 2.2]
asserts that, among
all positive rational parameters,
r=1/2
produces the largest integer trace.

Conjecture 1.1

(Han–Xiong)
.

For every positive rational number
r
and every integer
k\geq 1
, we have

(5)

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q).

The following example illustrates the conjecture in the smallest case that
our results identify as genuinely nontrivial.

Example 1.2
.

Take
k=4
, and let us extract the trace for
r=1/4
from the definition. Here

z=q^{r+1}=q^{5/4}
, and (
2
) gives

\bigl(q^{5/4};q\bigr)_{4}=1-q^{5/4}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{1}_{q}+q^{7/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{2}_{q}-q^{27/4}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{3}_{q}+q^{11}.

Each
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{s}_{q}
is a polynomial in
q
with integer exponents, so all the
exponents contributed by the
s
-th term lie in
E+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where
E
is
the exponent displayed in front of it. Consequently the
s
-th term
contributes to the integer trace precisely when that displayed exponent is an
integer. Of the five values
0,\ \tfrac{5}{4},\ \tfrac{7}{2},\ \tfrac{27}{4},\ 11
only
the first and last qualify, and since
1/(q;q)_{4}
has integer exponents we may
extract the trace of the numerator first and divide afterwards:

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{4},4}(q)=\frac{1+q^{11}}{(q;q)_{4}}=1+q+2q^{2}+3q^{3}+5q^{4}+6q^{5}+9q^{6}+11q^{7}+15q^{8}+\cdots.

Note that three of the five terms of the numerator, including the entire
contribution of
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{2}_{q}
, are discarded.

The same computation for
r=1/2
has
z=q^{3/2}
and exponents

E=\tfrac{s(s+2)}{2}
, that is
0,\ \tfrac{3}{2},\ 4,\ \tfrac{15}{2},\ 12
. Now the
terms
s=0,2,4
survive, and each carries the sign
(-1)^{s}=+1
, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},4}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+q^{4}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{2}_{q}+q^{12}}{(q;q)_{4}}=\frac{1+q^{12}}{(q;q)_{4}}+\frac{q^{4}}{(q;q)_{2}^{\,2}}

\displaystyle=1+q+2q^{2}+3q^{3}+6q^{4}+8q^{5}+14q^{6}+19q^{7}+29q^{8}+\cdots,

using
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{2}_{q}/(q;q)_{4}=1/(q;q)_{2}^{\,2}
. Subtracting,

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},4}(q)-\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{4},4}(q)=\frac{q^{4}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{2}_{q}+q^{12}-q^{11}}{(q;q)_{4}}=q^{4}+2q^{5}+5q^{6}+8q^{7}+14q^{8}+\cdots\succeq_{q}0,

in agreement with (
5
). The inequality is not termwise
obvious: the numerator contains
-q^{11}
, so positivity is
produced only after division by
(q;q)_{4}
. That numerator is the polynomial

D_{2,4}
of Section
2.4
, and this is the smallest case in
which the phenomenon occurs.

Finally,
r=1/3
shows what an odd denominator does. Here the exponents are

0,\ \tfrac{4}{3},\ \tfrac{11}{3},\ 7,\ \tfrac{34}{3}
, so only
s=0
and
s=3

survive. But
s=3
is odd, so its sign is
(-1)^{3}=-1
and

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{3},4}(q)=\frac{1-q^{7}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{4}{3}_{q}}{(q;q)_{4}}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{4}}-\frac{q^{7}}{(q;q)_{3}(q;q)_{1}}.

This series has
[q^{19}]\mathcal{T}_{1/3,4}=-8
. Thus an integer trace need not
have nonnegative coefficients; the conjecture compares two traces and makes
no separate positivity assertion about either one.

Han and Xiong established Conjecture
1.1
in several cases. They
proved it for every positive rational
r
when
k\leq 3

[
3
, Proposition 4.1]
, and for every
k\geq 1
in the two infinite
parameter families
r=m+1/2
(
m\geq 0
an integer)

[
3
, Proposition 5.1]
and
r=m
(
m\geq 1
an integer)

[
3
, Proposition 5.2]
. Beyond these families, they verified
the two parameters
r=1/3
and
r=1/4
for all
1\leq k\leq 150
by exact
symbolic computation
[
3
, Propositions 6.1 and 6.3]
.

In this paper we prove a comparison principle for pairs of rational
parameters whose positive summand-index sets satisfy an explicit divisibility
condition. The two infinite families above follow directly from this
principle, and the cases
k\leq 3
follow after the canonical reduction below.
The earlier finite computations for
r=1/3
and
r=1/4
are instead subsumed
by our exact verification through
k=200
. For a positive rational number

x
, let
\operatorname{den}(x)
denote the denominator of
x
in lowest terms. Our main
theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.3

(Support dominance)
.

Let
u=c/d
and
r=a/b
be positive rational numbers in lowest terms, and
suppose that

(6)

d\ \text{is even},\qquad d\mid\operatorname{lcm}(b,2),\qquad u\leq r.

Then, for every integer
k\geq 1
, we have

\mathcal{T}_{u,k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q).

Theorem
1.3
includes monotonicity for each fixed
even denominator and also compares parameters with different reduced
denominators whenever the divisibility condition in
(
6
) holds. Taking
u=1/2
, whose denominator

d=2
is even and divides
\operatorname{lcm}(b,2)
for every
b
, we obtain the conjecture
on the entire half-line
r\geq 1/2
.

Corollary 1.4
.

For every rational number
r\geq 1/2
and every integer
k\geq 1
, we have

\mathcal{T}_{1/2,k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q).

Corollary
1.4
recovers both infinite families of

[
3
]
(namely
r=m
and
r=m+1/2
) and extends them to every
reduced fraction
a/b\geq 1/2
, of arbitrary denominator.
Consequently, Conjecture
1.1
is open only for parameters

0<r<1/2
, and there, by Theorem
1.6
below, only
for
k>200
.

Theorem
1.3
does more: it collapses that remaining
interval onto a single canonical sequence of parameters. Define the

even core
of a positive rational
r
by

(7)

r^{\flat}:=\frac{1}{\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{den}(r),2)},

so that if
r=a/b
is reduced, then

r^{\flat}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{b},&b\text{ even},\\
\frac{1}{2b},&b\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Therefore,
r^{\flat}
depends only on the denominator of
r
, not on its numerator.
In these terms the reduction takes the following form.

Theorem 1.5

(Canonical reduction)
.

For every positive rational number
r
and every integer
k\geq 1
, we have

(8)

\mathcal{T}_{r^{\flat},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q).

Consequently, Conjecture
1.1
is equivalent to the family of
inequalities

(9)

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2m},k}(q)\qquad(m\geq 1,\ k\geq 1).

Moreover, for each fixed
k
it suffices to verify (
9
) in
the finite range

(10)

2\leq m\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor.

We call the unit fractions
\frac{1}{2m}
the
primitive parameters
of this
reduction. Theorem
1.5
shows that numerators
greater than one and odd reduced denominators never produce new cases: the
entire two-parameter conjecture, over all of
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
, is governed by the
single sequence
1/4,\,1/6,\,1/8,\ldots
(the case
m=1
being an equality).
For example, a proof of the primitive comparison for
r=1/6
would
automatically yield the comparison for
r=1/3
, whereas the reverse
implication does not follow from the dominance relation. The reduction is
purely structural and does not depend on computation.

For each fixed
k
, the reduction leaves only finitely many primitive
inequalities. Using the sufficient cumulative-coefficient criterion below,
an exact computation verifies all of them for
k\leq 200
, extending the
numerical evidence of
[
3
]
from two parameters through
k=150
to

all
parameters through
k=200
.

Theorem 1.6

(Finite verification)
.

For every positive rational number
r
and every integer
k
with

1\leq k\leq 200
,

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q).

The proof of Theorem
1.6
is computer-assisted.
The program checks a finite list of coefficient inequalities over the
integers, using exact arithmetic and complete coefficient vectors. It uses
no floating-point arithmetic, degree truncation, or probabilistic testing.
Theorems
1.3
and

1.5
are entirely independent of that calculation.
We stress that we do not prove (
9
) uniformly in
m
and

k
; in particular, the all
k
case
r=1/4
remains open. The precise
remaining problem is recorded at the end of
Section
3
.

This paper builds directly on
[
3
]
. The fractional-index
extension (

), the integer trace, the order

, the exponent

and trace formula, the denominator-parity

The proof of Theorem

2.
Nuts and bolts

This section collects the tools required for the proofs of the results
stated in the introduction: the integer-trace formalism, the exact trace
formula, a shift-dominance lemma, and the cumulative-coefficient criterion
behind the exact verification.

2.1.
The integer trace

We recall the formal setting of
[
3
, Section 1]
. Let

\mathcal{P}:=\bigcup_{M\geq 1}\mathbb{Q}[[q^{1/M}]]

be the directed union of fractional power series rings (i.e. whose exponents are
nonnegative rationals with a common denominator). Since the reduced
denominator of
r
is unbounded as
r

ranges over
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
, no single
\mathbb{Q}[[q^{1/M}]]
contains the whole family

\{G_{r,k}\}
, and
\mathcal{P}
provides a common ambient ring. If

F(q)=\sum_{\alpha\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}c(\alpha)q^{\alpha},

then its
integer trace
is

(11)

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{Z}}(F):=\sum_{n\geq 0}c(n)q^{n}.

As observed in
[
3
]
, this is the natural projection of
\mathcal{P}
onto

\mathbb{Q}[[q]]
retaining the integer-exponent part; it is
\mathbb{Q}
-linear. It is not a
ring homomorphism, but it commutes with multiplication by an ordinary power
series, which is the only property we need.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
F\in\mathcal{P}
and
H\in\mathbb{Q}[[q]]
, then

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{Z}}(FH)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{Z}}(F)H.

Proof.

Every exponent occurring in
H
is an integer. Hence, for an exponent

\alpha
occurring in
F
and an exponent
n
occurring in
H
, the sum

\alpha+n
is an integer if and only if
\alpha
is an integer. Explicitly,
if
F=\sum_{\alpha}c(\alpha)q^{\alpha}
and
H=\sum_{n}h(n)q^{n}
, then for
an integer exponent
n_{0}\geq 0
the coefficient of
q^{n_{0}}
in
FH
is

\sum_{\alpha+n=n_{0}}c(\alpha)h(n)
, a finite sum since
0\leq n\leq n_{0}
, and
every
\alpha
contributing to it is the integer
n_{0}-n
. Retaining
the integer-exponent terms before or after multiplication by
H
therefore
gives the same series.
∎

2.2.
The exact trace formula

Substituting
z=q^{r+1}
into the
q
-binomial theorem
(
2
) gives, as in
[
3
, eqs. (6)–(7)]
,

(12)

(q^{r+1};q)_{k}=\sum_{s=0}^{k}(-1)^{s}q^{E_{r}(s)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{s}_{q},\qquad E_{r}(s):=\binom{s+1}{2}+rs.

Since
\binom{s+1}{2}=\binom{s}{2}+s
, our
E_{r}(s)
equals the exponent

E(s,r)=\binom{s}{2}+(r+1)s
of
[
3
, eq. (7)]
.
Han and Xiong call
\{s:0\leq s\leq k,\ E_{r}(s)\in\mathbb{Z}\}
the
integer
support
of
\mathcal{T}_{r,k}
, since only these indices contribute integer powers of

q
to the numerator. The following proposition is a restatement of their
analysis in
[
3
, Section 2]
, recorded here in the form used below;
we include the short proof to keep the paper self-contained.

Proposition 2.2

(Trace formula)
.

Let
r=a/b
be a positive rational number in lowest terms. For
k\geq 1
,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{a/b,k}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s\leq k\\
b\mid s\end{subarray}}(-1)^{s}q^{E_{a/b}(s)}\frac{1}{(q;q)_{s}(q;q)_{k-s}}

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/b\rfloor}(-1)^{bj}\frac{q^{\binom{bj+1}{2}+aj}}{(q;q)_{bj}(q;q)_{k-bj}}.

Proof.

The integer
\binom{s+1}{2}
contributes no fractional part to
E_{a/b}(s)
, so

E_{a/b}(s)\in\mathbb{Z}
if and only if
as/b\in\mathbb{Z}
, that is, if and only if

b\mid as
; and since
\gcd(a,b)=1
this holds exactly when
b\mid s
.
Lemma
2.1
and
(
12
) therefore give

\mathcal{T}_{a/b,k}(q)=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s\leq k\\
b\mid s\end{subarray}}(-1)^{s}q^{E_{a/b}(s)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{s}_{q}.

Finally,

\frac{1}{(q;q)_{k}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{s}_{q}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{s}(q;q)_{k-s}},

which proves (
13
); setting
s=bj
gives
(
14
).
∎

For later use, set

(15)

\Phi_{k,s}(q):=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{s}(q;q)_{k-s}}\qquad(0\leq s\leq k).

Each
\Phi_{k,s}(q)
has nonnegative integer coefficients. Indeed, the
classical expansion
1/(q;q)_{m}=\sum_{n\geq 0}p_{m}(n)q^{n}
, in which
p_{m}(n)

counts the partitions of
n
into parts of size at most
m

[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
, exhibits
\Phi_{k,s}
as a product of two such
series. In the language
of Proposition
2.2
, the summand indexed by
s
occurs if
and only if
b\mid s
, and its sign is positive exactly when
s
is even.
Hence the
positive summand-index set
of
\mathcal{T}_{r,k}
is

(16)

\{s:0\leq s\leq k,\ \operatorname{lcm}(b,2)\mid s\},

and the
negative summand-index set
is

(17)

\{s:0\leq s\leq k,\ b\mid s,\ s\text{ odd}\}.

In particular, the negative summand-index set is empty when
b
is even. This
parity
dichotomy is observed in
[
3
, Section 2]
, where it is the
motivation for singling out
r=1/2
; the point of
Theorem
1.3
is that it also drives a comparison
between two different parameters.

2.3.
A shift-dominance lemma

The key point is that moving a monomial to a later integral exponent can only
decrease its product with
\Phi_{k,s}(q)
in coefficientwise order.

Lemma 2.3

(Shift dominance)
.

Let
k\geq 1
, let
0\leq s\leq k
, and let
A
and
B
be nonnegative
integers with
A\leq B
. Then

(18)

q^{A}\Phi_{k,s}(q)\succeq_{q}q^{B}\Phi_{k,s}(q).

Proof.

The claim is immediate when
A=B
. Suppose that
A<B
. Since
k\geq 1
, at
least one of
s
and
k-s
is positive. Thus the denominator in
(
15
) contains at least one factor
1-q
; cancelling one
such factor exhibits
(1-q)\Phi_{k,s}(q)
as a finite product of
geometric-series factors
(1-q^{e})^{-1}
, whence

(19)

(1-q)\Phi_{k,s}(q)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[[q]].

Using
1-q^{B-A}=(1-q)(1+q+\cdots+q^{B-A-1})
, we obtain

\bigl(q^{A}-q^{B}\bigr)\Phi_{k,s}(q)=q^{A}(1+q+\cdots+q^{B-A-1})(1-q)\Phi_{k,s}(q).

Every factor on the right has nonnegative coefficients, proving
(
18
).
∎

The condition
k\geq 1
is needed only to guarantee a factor
1-q
in the
denominator, and every comparison in Conjecture
1.1
has
k\geq 1
.
Lemma
2.3
combines two properties of the coefficientwise
order established in
[
3
, Lemma 3.1]
, namely that

(q^{A}-q^{B})/(1-q)\succeq_{q}0
for
A\leq B
and that division by factors

1-q^{j}
preserves the order; we have stated it in the single form needed
here.

2.4.
A cumulative-coefficient criterion

The exact verification in Section
3
rests on the following
sufficient criterion, which converts a coefficientwise inequality between two
traces into a
finite
list of integer inequalities. For
m\geq 1
,
Proposition
2.2
applied to
r=\frac{1}{2m}
(so
a=1
,

b=2m
, every sign is positive, and

E_{1/(2m)}(2m\ell)=\binom{2m\ell+1}{2}+\ell=2m^{2}\ell^{2}+(m+1)\ell
) gives

(20)

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2m},k}(q)=\frac{A_{m,k}(q)}{(q;q)_{k}},

where

(21)

A_{m,k}(q):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor k/(2m)\rfloor}q^{2m^{2}\ell^{2}+(m+1)\ell}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2m\ell}_{q}.

For
m=1
, this is exactly the numerator for
r=1/2
computed in

[
3
, eq. (19)]
. Put

D_{m,k}(q):=A_{1,k}(q)-A_{m,k}(q).

The family
D_{m,k}
directly generalizes the numerator polynomial

H_{k}
of
[
3
, eq. (24)]
: since
2m^{2}\ell^{2}+(m+1)\ell
specializes
at
m=2
to
\ell(8\ell+3)
and at
m=1
to
2\ell(\ell+1)
, and the terms

\ell=0
cancel in the difference, one has

(22)

D_{2,k}(q)=H_{k}(q)\qquad(k\geq 1).

The case
m=2
of the criterion below is therefore a statement about their
polynomial, and
[
3
, Conjecture 6.2]
, which predicts that

H_{k}(q)\succeq_{q}0
for
k\geq 19
, would imply it in that range.

Lemma 2.4

(Cumulative-coefficient criterion)
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
m\geq 1
. If

(23)

\frac{D_{m,k}(q)}{1-q}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[[q]],

then

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2m},k}(q).

Moreover, if
N=\deg D_{m,k}
and
D_{m,k}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}d_{n}q^{n}
, then
(
23
) is equivalent to the finite set of
inequalities

(24)

\sum_{n=0}^{j}d_{n}\geq 0\qquad(0\leq j\leq N).

Proof.

Since
(q;q)_{k}=(1-q)(q^{2};q)_{k-1}
, we have

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)-\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2m},k}(q)=\frac{D_{m,k}(q)/(1-q)}{(q^{2};q)_{k-1}}.

For
k=1
, the reciprocal of
(q^{2};q)_{k-1}
is
1
. For
k\geq 2
, it
has nonnegative integer coefficients and is the generating function for
partitions into parts from
\{2,3,\ldots,k\}
. Thus
(
23
) implies the desired inequality.

Since
1/(1-q)=1+q+q^{2}+\cdots
, the coefficient of
q^{j}
in

D_{m,k}(q)/(1-q)
is
\sum_{n=0}^{\min(j,N)}d_{n}
. For
j>N
, this value is
the constant total sum

\sum_{n=0}^{N}d_{n}
, which is already the case
j=N
in
(
24
). Hence the infinite coefficientwise condition is
equivalent to the displayed finite list.
∎

Criterion (
24
) is a systematic form of the pairing
strategy of
[
3
, Propositions 6.1 and 6.3]
. In the relevant
numerator polynomials—their
H_{k}
for
r=1/4
and the analogous polynomial
for
r=1/3
—each negative term
-c_{2}q^{d_{2}}
is matched with an earlier
positive term
c_{1}q^{d_{1}}
satisfying
c_{1}\geq c_{2}>0
, so that

(c_{1}q^{d_{1}}-c_{2}q^{d_{2}})/(1-q)\succeq_{q}0
. To see the equivalence with
partial sums, first collect like powers and split each nonzero coefficient
into signed unit monomials. A greedy scan from low to high degree can then
pair every negative unit with an unmatched positive unit at a smaller degree
exactly when every running sum is nonnegative. The criterion is thus
checked by one pass over the coefficients rather than by a search for a
pairing.

3.1.
Proof of the support-dominance theorem

The proof compares the two trace formulas supplied by
Proposition
2.2
summand by summand. Recall the setting:

u=c/d
and
r=a/b
are reduced,
d
is even,
d\mid\operatorname{lcm}(b,2)
, and
u\leq r
.
The first hypothesis makes every surviving summand of
\mathcal{T}_{u,k}
positive; the
second embeds the positive summand-index set of
\mathcal{T}_{r,k}
into the
summand-index set of
\mathcal{T}_{u,k}
; the third places each matched summand of

\mathcal{T}_{u,k}
at an exponent no larger than that of its partner.
Lemma
2.3
converts those exponent inequalities into
coefficientwise dominance, and the summands left unmatched contribute with a
favorable sign.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Write
u=c/d
and
r=a/b
in lowest terms, put
L=\operatorname{lcm}(b,2)
, and abbreviate
the three relevant index sets by

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle:=\{s:0\leq s\leq k,\ d\mid s\},

\displaystyle R_{+}

\displaystyle:=\{s:0\leq s\leq k,\ L\mid s\},

\displaystyle R_{-}

\displaystyle:=\{s:0\leq s\leq k,\ b\mid s,\ s\text{ odd}\},

so that
U
is the summand-index set of
\mathcal{T}_{u,k}
, while
R_{+}
and

R_{-}
are, respectively, the positive and negative summand-index sets
(
16
) and (
17
) for
\mathcal{T}_{r,k}
.
Since
d

is even, every element of
U
is even, so by
(
16
) and (
17
) every surviving
sign is positive. Proposition
2.2
therefore gives

(25)

\mathcal{T}_{u,k}(q)=\sum_{s\in U}q^{E_{u}(s)}\Phi_{k,s}(q).

On the other hand, separating the positive and negative summand-index sets for

\mathcal{T}_{r,k}
,

(26)

\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q)=\sum_{s\in R_{+}}q^{E_{r}(s)}\Phi_{k,s}(q)-\sum_{s\in R_{-}}q^{E_{r}(s)}\Phi_{k,s}(q).

Because
d\mid L
, any
s
divisible by
L
is divisible by
d
; that is,

R_{+}\subseteq U.

Splitting the sum (
25
) as

U=R_{+}\sqcup(U\setminus R_{+})
and subtracting
(
26
) gives the exact identity

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{u,k}(q)-\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in R_{+}}\bigl(q^{E_{u}(s)}-q^{E_{r}(s)}\bigr)\Phi_{k,s}(q)

\displaystyle+\sum_{s\in U\setminus R_{+}}q^{E_{u}(s)}\Phi_{k,s}(q)+\sum_{s\in R_{-}}q^{E_{r}(s)}\Phi_{k,s}(q).

The last two sums have nonnegative coefficients; note that the terms of

R_{-}
, which enter
\mathcal{T}_{r,k}
negatively, therefore enter the

difference
positively, and so help rather than hinder. For an index

s\in R_{+}
, both exponents
E_{u}(s)
and
E_{r}(s)

are nonnegative integers (since
d\mid L\mid s
and
b\mid L\mid s
), and the
hypothesis
u\leq r
gives

E_{u}(s)=\binom{s+1}{2}+us\leq\binom{s+1}{2}+rs=E_{r}(s).

Lemma
2.3
shows that every summand in the first sum of
(
27
) also has nonnegative coefficients. Hence

\mathcal{T}_{u,k}(q)-\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[[q]]
, as required.
∎

Each hypothesis in (
6
) was used at exactly one
point of the argument. The divisibility condition cannot
simply be dropped: the pair
u=1/4
,
r=1/2
satisfies the other two
hypotheses but not
d\mid\operatorname{lcm}(b,2)
, and Example
1.2
shows that

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{4},4}(q)\not\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},4}(q)
.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

Apply Theorem
1.3
with
u=1/2
. Its denominator is

d=2
, which is even and divides
\operatorname{lcm}(b,2)
for every positive integer
b
.
The remaining hypothesis is precisely
1/2\leq r
.
∎

Theorem
1.3
also propagates any established
even-denominator case to a larger set of parameters.

Corollary 3.1

(Propagation)
.

Let
d
be even. Suppose, for a fixed

, that

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{c/d,k}(q)

for some reduced positive rational

. Then

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{a/b,k}(q)

for every reduced
a/b\geq c/d
satisfying

.

Proof.

Theorem
1.3
gives

\mathcal{T}_{c/d,k}\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{a/b,k}
, and coefficientwise order is transitive.
∎

3.2.
Proof of the canonical reduction

The even core (
7
) was designed so that
Theorem
1.3
applies to the pair
(r^{\flat},r)
with
nothing left to check: its denominator is even by construction, and it is at
most
r
because its numerator is
1
and its denominator is a multiple of

r
. That gives
(
8
) at once, and the equivalence with the unit-family
(
9
) follows by composing it with the conjectured

. The finite range (
10
) is
then a matter of identifying the two traces being compared when
2m
exceeds

k
, at which point only the summand
s=0
survives on the right.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
r=a/b
be in lowest terms and put
L=\operatorname{lcm}(b,2)
. The rational number

r^{\flat}=1/L
is reduced and has even denominator
L
. Moreover,

r^{\flat}\leq r.

Indeed, if
b
is even, then
r^{\flat}=1/b\leq a/b
; if
b
is odd, then

. Theorem
1.3
, with

and

, now gives (
8
).

If Conjecture
1.1
holds, then (
9
) follows by

9
) holds. Since

is even, write

. Then

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2m},k}(q)=\mathcal{T}_{r^{\flat},k}(q)\succeq_{q}\mathcal{T}_{r,k}(q),

where the last inequality is (
8
). This proves the

It remains to justify the finite range (
10
). If

, then the only multiple of
2m
in
\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
is zero, so
Proposition
2.2
gives

(28)

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2m},k}(q)=\Phi_{k,0}(q)=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{k}}.

For

, the same proposition gives

(29)

\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}q^{2j(j+1)}\Phi_{k,2j}(q)\succeq_{q}\Phi_{k,0}(q),

where the equality is
[

, eq. (19)]
and the dominance holds
because every summand has nonnegative coefficients.
Thus, every case with

of
(

As a first consequence, the reduction gives a second proof of

, the proof of
