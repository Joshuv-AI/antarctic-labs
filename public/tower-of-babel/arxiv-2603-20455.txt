Time-Reversed BSDEs for Accurate Gradient Estimation in Diffusion Models
Yuhang Mei, Amirhossein Taghvaei

Abstract
There is a growing literature adopting a stochastic optimal control (SOC) perspective to fine-tune diffusion models and related generative policies. A prominent class of methods, known as iterative diffusion optimization , solves the SOC problem by simulating the diffusion process, evaluating a loss function, and applying stochastic optimization algorithms, with adjoint matching emerging as a state-of-the-art approach. However, the adjoint process used in these methods is not adapted to the forward diffusion filtration, which can lead to unstable or high-variance gradient estimates. In this paper, we revisit gradient estimation in diffusion models through the lens of backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs). We propose an alternative estimator based on a time-reversed BSDE formulation introduced in our prior work, which produces an adjoint process adapted to the underlying filtration. This adapted structure leads to more stable gradient estimates with potentially lower variance. We analyze the accuracy of the proposed estimator and compare it with adjoint matching. Numerical experiments on fine-tuning toy diffusion models demonstrate improved gradient stability and competitive performance.

Compared to training a new diffusion model from scratch, fine-tuning an existing model provides a more efficient and cost-effective approach.

A common strategy to fine-tuning diffusion models is to introduce a reward function that measures the quality of generated samples. This formulation naturally enables the use of both reinforcement learning (RL)

and stochastic optimal control (SOC) methods
[

The interplay between SOC and generative modeling has recently garnered significant attention
[

,

,

,
18
,

]
.
Motivated by these developments, we adopt the SOC perspective in this paper and focus on a class of SOC-based methods known as

[
20
]
. These methods fine-tune the model by repeatedly simulating the diffusion process, evaluating a loss function on the generated samples, and updating the parameters via stochastic optimization. Among them, adjoint matching has emerged as a state-of-the-art technique
[
12
]
. However, the adjoint process employed in these methods is not adapted to the forward diffusion filtration, which may lead to unstable or high-variance gradient estimates.

These limitations motivate the development of more principled adjoint methods in which the adjoint process is adapted to the forward filtration and characterized by a backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE), as introduced in the stochastic maximum principle

Here are the contributions of the paper: (i) We propose a novel and accurate gradient estimator for fine-tuning diffusion models; (ii) we present both adapted and non-adapted adjoint processes and establish a theoretical comparison; (iii) We conduct extensive numerical experiments comparing our method with baseline approaches on both linear and nonlinear stochastic models, and demonstrate its effectiveness, outperforming adjoint matching in a full fine-tuning task on a toy diffusion model.

II
Problem Formulation

Consider a
n
-dimensional diffusion process
X^{o}=\{X^{o}_{t};\,0\leq t\leq T\}
governed by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\,\mathrm{d}X^{o}_{t}=f(t,X^{o}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t,X^{o}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},~~X^{o}_{0}\sim p_{0},

(1)

where
W=\{W_{t};\,0\leq t\leq T\}
is the
n
-dimensional standard Wiener process,
p_{0}
is the initial distribution,
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the drift function, and
g:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the diffusion matrix. These functions are assumed to satisfy the usual regularity conditions that ensure the existence of a strong solution
[
24
, Thm. 5.2.1]
.
We interpret the SDE (
1
) as a pre-trained diffusion model
[
3
]
. Specifically, the initial distribution, the drift function, and the diffusion matrix are designed such that the distribution of
X^{o}_{T}
, at the terminal time
T
, matches a target distribution
p_{T}
representing data.

We are interested in the problem of fine-tuning the diffusion model so that it generates samples from the “tilted” target distribution

\displaystyle q^{\star}_{T}\propto p_{T}e^{-\ell_{f}},

where
\ell_{f}
is a user-specified loss function that evaluates the quality of generated samples.
The modified or the controlled diffusion is denoted by
X=\{X_{t};\,0\leq t\leq T\}
and modeled by

\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=f(t,X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t,X_{t})(U_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}),~~X_{0}\sim q_{0},

(2)

where
q_{0}
is the modified initial distribution, and
U=\{U_{t};\,0\leq t\leq T\}
is the control input process which is assumed to be adapted to the filtration generated by the initial condition and the Wiener process.

The problem of sampling from the tilted target distribution can now be formulated as a stochastic control problem in which the initial distribution
q_{0}
and the control input
U
are selected so that the distribution of
X_{T}
matches the tilted target distribution
q^{\star}_{T}
. Using a KL-regularized variational characterization of the tilted distribution, together with Girsanov’s theorem, this problem can be expressed as the following stochastic optimal control problem:

\displaystyle\min_{q_{0},U}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\ell_{f}(X_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|U_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}\|p_{0}).

(3)

Stochastic optimal control problems of this form have a long history in the control literature, particularly in path-integral control
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, where the control-affine structure of the model (
2
) and the quadratic control cost in (
3
) arises naturally through a change-of-measure argument related to Girsanov’s theorem.
In particular, the optimal
controlled diffusion process that solves the SOC problem (
3
) has the path probability law

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}P_{X}}{\,\mathrm{d}P_{X^{o}}}=\frac{\exp\left(-\ell_{f}(X^{o}_{T})\right)}{{\mathbb{E}}[\exp\left(-\ell_{f}(X^{o}_{T})\right)]},

where the expectation is taken with respect to the original diffusion process (
1
). Specifically, the terminal distribution of the optimal controlled process
X_{T}
coincides with the tilted target distribution
q^{\star}_{T}
. The path probability law allows us to derive expressions for the optimal initial distribution and the optimal control
[
11
, Thm. 1]
, however, accurately estimating the expectations is challenging due to the high variance of the exponential weight
\exp(-\ell_{f}(X^{o}_{T}))

[
28
,
29
]
.
In this paper, we investigate an alternative class of optimization-based approaches, motivated by recent advances in diffusion models.

III
Adjoint processes

In this section, we present two adjoint processes that arise in the gradient estimation of the stochastic optimal control problem (
3
).
The first is a
non-adapted adjoint process
that appears in adjoint matching methods and is obtained via pathwise differentiation of the objective.
The second is an
adapted adjoint process
obtained from a BSDE.

III-A

Notations

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space and assume the Wiener process
W_{t}
is adapted to the filteration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. We will use the following Hilbert spaces throughout the exposition: (i) The Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the inner-product
\langle x,y\rangle:=x^{\top}y
; (ii) The space of deterministic control trajectories
L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with the inner product
\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}^{\top}v_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
; (iii) The space of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variables
L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with the inner-product
\langle X,Y\rangle:={\mathbb{E}}[X^{\top}Y]
; (iv) The space of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted stochastic control processes
L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times[0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with the inner product
\langle U,V\rangle:={\mathbb{E}}[\int_{0}^{T}U_{t}^{\top}V_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t]
.

For a functional
F:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, the Gâteaux derivative of
F
at
u\in\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}(u)\in\mathcal{H}
and satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(F(u+tv))=\langle\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}(u),v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{H}.

For any initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and deterministic control
u\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we use
X^{x,u}_{t}
to denote the solution to the controlled SDE (
2
) with
X^{x,u}_{0}=x
and
U_{t}=u_{t}
. Similarly, for any
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\xi\in L^{2}_{{\mathcal{F}}_{0}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and admissible stochastic control process
U\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we denote by
X^{\xi,U}_{t}
the solution to the same SDE with the random initial condition
X^{\xi,U}_{0}=\xi
and the control input
U
. In particular, the controlled diffusion (
2
) can be written as
X_{t}=X^{\xi,U}_{t}
where the distribution of
\xi
is equal to
q_{0}
.

Using this notation, define the sample objective function

\displaystyle J(x,u)(\omega):=\beta\ell_{f}(X^{x,u}_{T}(\omega))+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|u_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\omega\in\Omega.

When the initial condition and control are random, the corresponding objective function is written as

\displaystyle J(\xi,U)=\beta\ell_{f}(X^{\xi,U}_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|U_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

With this notation, the SOC objective function in (
3
) can be expressed compactly as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[J(\xi,U)]+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}\|p_{0}),\quad\text{where}\quad\xi\sim q_{0}.

For the purposes of this section we temporarily ignore the KL-divergence term and focus on the derivatives of
\mathbb{E}[J(\xi,U)]
. The derivative of the KL term will be addressed separately in Sec.
V-C
.

III-B

Non-adapted adjoint process

Throughout this subsection we assume the diffusion matrix is independent of the state, i.e.

g(t,x)=g(t)
.
The non-adapted adjoint process arises when computing the derivatives of the sample objective
J(x,u)(\omega)
with respect to the initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and deterministic control trajectory
u\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for a fixed sample
\omega\in\Omega
. Under suitable regularity conditions
[
30
, Ch. V, Thm. 39]
, the mapping
x\to X^{x,u}_{t}(\omega)
is known to be differentiable and the sensitivity matrix
\eta^{x}_{t}(\omega):=\frac{\partial X^{x,u}_{t}(\omega)}{\partial x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
solves the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\eta^{x}_{t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,X^{x,u}_{t})\eta^{x}_{t},\quad\eta^{x}_{0}=I.

The (non-adapted) adjoint process
\mathsf{Y}^{x,u}_{t}
, is defined by the backward differential equation

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{Y}^{x,u}_{t}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=-[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,X^{x,u}_{t})]^{\top}\mathsf{Y}^{x,u}_{t},\quad\mathsf{Y}^{x,u}_{T}=\nabla\ell_{f}(X^{x,u}_{T}).

(4)

so that the duality relationship

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big((\mathsf{Y}^{x,y}_{t})^{\top}\eta^{x}_{t}\big)=0

holds true.
This identity allows the derivatives of the objective to be expressed in terms of the adjoint process:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}(x,u)(\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Y}^{x,u}_{0}(\omega),

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}(x,u)(\omega)\right)_{t}

\displaystyle=u_{t}+g(t)^{\top}\mathsf{Y}^{x,u}_{t}(\omega).

The derivation follows from a standard variational analysis of the objective function
J(x,u)(\omega)
, for a fixed
\omega\in\Omega
, combined with the above duality relation and is omitted for brevity.

For a random initial condition
\xi\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and a stochastic control
U\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times[0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the corresponding adjoint process is denoted by
\mathsf{Y}_{t}
and formally defiend as

\mathsf{Y}_{t}(\omega):=\mathsf{Y}^{\xi(\omega),U(\omega)}_{t}(\omega).

III-C

Adapted adjoint process

The adjoint process appearing in the previous subsection is not
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted.
In contrast, the stochastic maximum principle (SMP) introduces an adapted adjoint process obtained as the solution of a BSDE
[
21
]
.
Define the Hamiltonian

H(t,x,y,z,u):=y^{\top}(f(t,x)+g(t,x)u)+\text{Tr}(zg(t,x)),

(5)

for
(t,x,y,z,u)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The adapted adjoint process
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
is defined by the BSDE

-\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}(t,X_{t},Y_{t},Z_{t},U_{t})\,\,\mathrm{d}t-Z_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},~Y_{T}=\nabla\ell_{f}(X_{T}).

(6)

The adaptedness of
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
enables a variational analysis of
{\mathbb{E}}[J(\xi,U)]

[
21
, Sec. 4.3]
. In particular, for any perturbations
\zeta\in L^{2}_{{\mathcal{F}}_{0}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
V\in L^{2}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\Omega\times[0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\mathbb{E}[J(\xi+\epsilon\zeta,U)]\Big|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}^{\top}\zeta]

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\mathbb{E}[J(\xi,U+\epsilon V)]\Big|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(t,X_{t},Y_{t},Z_{t},U_{t})^{\top}V_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

Consequently, following the definition of Gâteaux derivative,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\mathbb{E}[J(\xi,U)]

\displaystyle=Y_{0},

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial U}\mathbb{E}[J(\xi,U)]\right)_{t}

\displaystyle=U_{t}+g(t,X_{t})^{\top}Y_{t}.

This variational analysis differs slightly, and is in fact simpler, than the one used in the SMP. In particular, it considers variations in the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times[0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
rather than the needle-type perturbations that are typically employed in the SMP framework.

III-D

Relationship between adapted and non-adapted adjoint processes

Assume again that
g(t,x)=g(t)
. A relationship between the two adjoint processes is provided using the linearity of the BSDE (
6
). Let

A_{t}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,X_{t}),

and define the matrix processes

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\Phi_{t}

\displaystyle=-A_{t}^{\top}\Phi_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\,\mathrm{d}\Psi_{t}=\Psi_{t}A_{t}^{\top}\,\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\Phi_{0}=\Psi_{0}=I.

By the application of the Itô rule,
one can verify that

\Psi_{t}\Phi_{t}=I
, or
\Phi_{t}=\Psi_{t}^{-1}
, for all
t\geq 0
. These matrix-valued processes are used to provide an explicit solution to the BSDE
[
21
, Sec. 7.2]
.
The BSDE dynamics yields

\,\mathrm{d}(\Psi_{t}Y_{t})=\Psi_{t}Z_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
.
Integrating from
t
to
T
, and multiplying by
\Phi_{t}
, gives

Y_{t}=\Phi_{t}\Psi_{T}\nabla\ell_{f}(X_{T})-\int_{t}^{T}\Phi_{t}\Psi_{s}Z_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},

where we used the terminal condition
Y_{t}=\nabla\ell_{f}(X_{T})
.
The solution
Y_{t}
is obtained by taking conditional expectation

Y_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{t}\Psi_{T}\nabla\ell_{f}(X_{T})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right].

On the other hand, the non-adapted adjoint satisfies

\mathsf{Y}_{t}=\Phi_{t}\Psi_{T}\nabla\ell_{f}(X_{T})
,
concluding the identity

Y_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{Y}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}].

Thus the adapted adjoint process can be interpreted as the conditional expectation of the pathwise adjoint process, implying variance reduction by projection onto the filtration.
Moreover, the difference between
Y_{t}
and
\mathsf{Y}_{t}
can be written explicitly as

\mathsf{Y}_{t}-Y_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}\Phi_{t}\Psi_{s}Z_{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

From a computational standpoint, the two adjoint processes behave differently.
The non-adapted adjoint
\mathsf{Y}_{t}
is obtained by solving a backward ordinary differential equation along each simulated trajectory, which makes it straightforward to compute once the forward trajectory
X_{t}
is known.
In contrast, the adapted adjoint
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
is numerically challenging to simulate because the BSDE (
6
) imposes a terminal condition at time
T
, while the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
is generated forward in time.
Our proposed methodology deals with this issue.

IV
Proposed methodology

Our numerical approach is based on a time-reversed formulation of the BSDE (
6
). This construction relies on two key ingredients: (i) the connection between BSDEs and certain partial differential equation (PDE), and (ii) the time-reversal of diffusion processes. To present this construction, we assume that the control input is given in feedback form,

\displaystyle U_{t}=k(t,X_{t}).

(7)

where
k:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a feedback policy. Under this assumption, the controlled diffusion (
2
) becomes a Markov process whose dynamics are fully determined by the state
X_{t}
. This Markov structure allows the solution of the BSDE to be characterized through a PDE representation and enables the derivation of a time-reversed formulation that forms the basis of our numerical algorithm.

IV-A

PDE solution of the BSDE

We begin by recalling the well-known connection between BSDE and PDE
[
31
,
32
]
. Let
\phi:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sufficiently smooth function satisfying the terminal value problem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(t,x)+\mathcal{L}\phi(t,x)+h(t,x)=0,\quad\phi(T,x)=\nabla\ell_{f}(x),

(8)

where the second-order differential operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\phi(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}(t,x)^{\top}\big(f(t,x)+g(t,x)k(t,x)\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\big(G(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)\big),

with
G(t,x):=g(t,x)g(t,x)^{\top}
, and

\displaystyle h(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\big(t,x,\phi(t,x),g(t,x)^{\top}\partial_{x}\phi(t,x),k(t,x)\big).

(9)

Applying Itô’s formula to
\phi(t,X_{t})
along the controlled diffusion (
2
) shows that the processes

(Y_{t},Z_{t})=\big(\phi(t,X_{t}),\,g(t,X_{t})^{\top}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}(t,X_{t})\big)

(10)

solves the BSDE (
6
). Throughout the paper we assume that the PDE (
8
) admits a sufficiently regular solution.
Such existence and regularity results hold under standard conditions on the coefficients of the diffusion and the Hamiltonian; see, e.g.,
[
21
, Ch. 7 Sec. 4]
. While solving the PDE directly is generally intractable in high dimensions, this representation provides the key insight needed to construct a time-reversed stochastic representation of the BSDE.

IV-B

Time-reversed diffusion

Let
\tilde{X}:=\{\tilde{X}_{t}:=X_{T-t};\,0\leq t\leq T\}
denote
the time-reversal of the diffusion process
X
. According to the time-reversal theory for diffusion processes
[
33
,
34
]
, the time-reversed process
\tilde{X}
satisfies the SDE

\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=\tilde{f}(t,\tilde{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{g}(t,\tilde{X}_{t})\big(\tilde{U}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}\big),

(11)

where
\tilde{W}_{t}
is a standard
n
-dimensional Wiener process (independent of
W_{t}
). The coefficients of the reversed diffusion are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=-f(T-t,x)+s(T-t,x),

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=-g(T-t,x),\qquad\tilde{U}_{t}:=k(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t}),

where
s:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the
score function
associated with the marginal distribution of
X_{t}
. The score function is defined component-wise as

s_{i}(t,x):=\frac{1}{p(t,x)}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\big(G_{i,j}(t,x)p(t,x)\big),

(12)

where
p(t,x)
denote the probability density function of
X_{t}
,
G_{i,j}(t,x)
is the
(i,j)
-entry of the matrix
G(t,x)
.

In practice, the score function is not known explicitly and must be approximated from samples of the forward diffusion process.
A common approach is to estimate the score by solving the
implicit score matching
problem

\min_{\psi}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\psi(t,X_{t})\|^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}G_{i,j}(t,X_{t})\frac{\partial\psi_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(t,X_{t})\right],

(13)

where the expectation is taken with respect to both
t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,T]
and the state
X_{t}
of the forward diffusion
[
1
,
35
]
.
The time-reversed diffusion will allow us to construct a time-reversed BSDE which is described next.

IV-C

Time-reversed BSDE

The time-reversed BSDE is obtained by applying Itô’s formula to the process

\phi(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t})
, where
\phi
is the solution of the PDE (
8
) and

\tilde{X}_{t}
evolves according to the time-reversed diffusion (
11
).
After simplification, this yields

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\phi(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t})=

\displaystyle\left(h(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t})+c(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}(T\!-\!t,\tilde{X}_{t})^{\top}\tilde{g}(t,\tilde{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}

where
h
is the partial derivative of the Hamiltonian as defined in (
9
) and

c(t,x):=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}(t,x)^{\top}s(t,x)+\text{Tr}\big(G(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)\big).

Define the processes

(\tilde{Y}_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t}):=\big(\phi(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t}),\tilde{g}(t,\tilde{X}_{t})^{\top}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t})\big).

(14)

Then
\tilde{Y}_{t}
satisfies the SDE

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\big(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{Y}_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t},\tilde{U}_{t}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+c(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{Z}_{t}^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t},

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{0}

\displaystyle=\nabla\ell_{f}(\tilde{X}_{0}),

(15)

which can be interpreted as the time-reversal of the BSDE (
6
).
By construction, the path
\{(\tilde{Y}_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t});0\leq t\leq T\}
has the same probability law as
\{(Y_{T-t},Z_{T-t});0\leq t\leq T\}
.

IV-D

Numerical procedure

The key advantage of the representation (
15
) is that, unlike the original BSDE (
6
), it does not impose a terminal constraint.
Instead, the process can be simulated forward in time starting from the initial condition
\tilde{Y}_{0}=\nabla\ell_{f}(\tilde{X}_{0})
.
This property allows the BSDE to be approximated using standard forward simulation techniques.

The simulation of (
15
), however, requires the knowledge of
\phi
. To overcome this challenge, we follow an iterative procedure, where we start with an initial guess for
\phi
, simulate (
15
), and update
\phi
through the optimization

\min_{\phi\in\Phi}\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\tilde{Y}_{t}^{i}-\phi(T-t,\tilde{X}_{t}^{i})\|^{2}

(16)

where
\{(\tilde{X}_{t}^{i},\tilde{Y}_{t}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
are simulated trajectories generated by the coupled system (
11
)–(
15
). The time-reversed BSDE (
15
) therefore provides the basis for a practical numerical algorithm for solving the original BSDE.

Remark 1

The proposed methodology remains applicable even when the score function
s

is only approximately known. In particular, the function

\phi
can still be learned by solving the regression problem (
16
). In practice, the accuracy of the learned function
\phi
is highest in regions of the state space where the reversed trajectories are sampled more frequently. The role of the score function is to ensure that these trajectories remain consistent with the forward diffusion, thereby concentrating samples in regions that are most relevant for the SOC problem and improving the accuracy of
\phi
where it matters most for the optimization.

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present numerical results for three experiments: a linear model, a stochastic pendulum, and the full fine-tuning procedure on a toy diffusion model. The first two experiments are designed to assess the ability of the proposed framework to compute the adapted adjoint process and to compare its accuracy, for gradient estimation, against the non-adapted adjoint process. The third experiment demonstrates the use of our BSDE solver for fine-tuning a diffusion model, and compares its performance with adjoint matching, which relies on the non-adapted adjoint process. V-A

Verification in linear quadratic setting

We consider the
2
-dimensional linear system

\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=AX_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\epsilon B\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},~~X_{0}=\xi\sim q_{0},

(17)

and the objective function with the quadratic terminal cost

{\mathbb{E}}[J(\xi)]:={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{2}X_{T}^{\top}Q_{f}X_{T}\right],

(18)

where
\epsilon>0
denotes the strength of the noise,

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&-0.5\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad Q_{f}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},

q_{0}
is standard Gaussian, and
T=2
. We simulate and compare two numerical methods to estimate the derivative
\frac{\partial{\mathbb{E}}[J(\xi)]}{\partial\xi}
. The ground-truth is
\phi(0,\xi)
where
\phi
solves the PDE (
8
). In the linear case, the exact solution is
\phi(t,x)=G(t)x
where
G(t)
solves the differential Riccati equation
[
23
, Eq. 15]
.

Time-reversed BSDE (TR-BSDE):
To estimate
\phi(0,x)
, we use the iterative procedure that alternates between simulating the time-reversed BSDE (
15
) and solving the optimization (
16
). Although the ground-truth function
\phi
is linear in this example, we parameterize it with a neural network in order to validate the proposed training procedure. We perform
10
outer iterations. In each iteration, we simulate (
15
), then train the neural network for
\phi
over
2000
optimization steps, and then use the updated
\phi
in the next round of BSDE simulation, as described in Algorithm
1
.

Projected non-adapted adjoint process (PNAA):
To estimate
\phi(0,x)
, we use the relation
\phi(0,\xi)=Y_{0}={\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}_{0}\mid\mathcal{F}_{0}]
, where
\mathsf{Y}
denotes the non-adapted adjoint process. Specifically, we simulate
N
sample trajectories
\{(X^{i},\mathsf{Y}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
with random initial conditions
X^{i}_{0}=\xi^{i}\sim q_{0}
, and then estimate
\phi
by solving the regression problem

\min_{\phi\in\Phi}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathsf{Y}^{i}_{t}-\phi(t,X^{i}_{t})\|^{2}.

(19)

The procedure is detailed in Algorithm
3
.
To ensure a fair comparison with TR-BSDE, we use the same neural network architecture and the same total number of training iterations.

To quantify the accuracy of gradient estimation, we use the following mean-squared error (MSE) criterion:

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|G(0)\xi^{i}-\phi(0,\xi^{i})\right\|^{2}_{2},\quad\xi^{i}\sim q_{0},

(20)

where
G_{0}\xi^{i}
is the ground truth value and
\phi(0,\xi)
is its estimate obtained from the two methods above.
Figure
1(a)
illustrates the effect of the noise strength
\epsilon
on the performance of the two estimators. As expected, the MSE increases with
\epsilon
for both methods. However, the proposed TR-BSDE method consistently achieves lower error than PNAA across all noise levels, reflecting the fact that the non-adapted adjoint process yields inherently noisier gradient estimates.

Optimal initial distribution for inverted pendulum

We consider the nonlinear stochastic pendulum

f(x)=\begin{bmatrix}x(2)\\
\sin(x(1))-0.01x(2)\end{bmatrix},~~g(x)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0.5\end{bmatrix},

and the objective

{\mathbb{E}}[J(\xi)]:={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{2}X_{T}(2)^{2}+1-\cos(X_{T}(1))\right],\quad\xi\sim q_{0}.

(21)

We study the problem of optimizing
{\mathbb{E}}[J(\xi)]
with respect to the initial distribution
q_{0}
, assumed to have the empirical form

q_{0}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\delta_{\theta^{i}},

where the support points
\theta^{i}
are treated as optimization variables. The gradient of the objective with respect to each variable
\theta^{i}
is given by
\phi(0,\theta^{i})
, where
\phi
solves the PDE (
8
).

We approximate
\phi
using both TR-BSDE (Algorithm
1
) and PNAA (Algorithm
3
) , and use the resulting gradients to optimize for
\theta^{i}
through a gradient descent procedure. For comparison, we also implement a standard automatic differentiation (AutoDiff) baseline, which computes gradients by backpropagating through a time-discretized version of the SDE. We run the optimization algorithm for
15000
iterations.
The resulting accumulation points of
\theta^{i}
are shown in Fig
1(b)
and Fig.
1(c)
, together with a heat map of the objective function
{\mathbb{E}}[J(\xi)\mid\xi=\theta]
, estimated by Monte Carlo simulation.
It is observed that the points produced by TR-BSDE concentrate more tightly in low-cost regions than those obtained by PNAA. Moreover, the PNAA result includes several points that drift far from the origin and are therefore omitted from the figure. Compared with the AutoDiff baseline, TR-BSDE also converges substantially faster. Overall, these results indicate that the proposed method yields more accurate and lower-variance gradient estimates for this nonlinear system.

V-C

Fine-tuning diffusion model

We consider a pretrained one-dimensional diffusion model
that generates samples from the bimodal distribution

p_{T}=0.5\mathcal{N}(-3,1)+0.5\mathcal{N}(3,1)

Our goal is to fine-tune this model so that it generates samples from the tilted distribution defined through the terminal cost

\ell_{f}(x)=\beta\frac{1}{2}(x-3)^{2}

where
\beta>0
is the tilting parameter. We assume the nominal initial distribution
p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and parameterize the modified initial distribution
q_{0}=\mathcal{N}(\mu,Q^{2})
, where
\mu
and
Q
are optimization variables. The objective is to solve the SOC problem (
3
) by jointly optimizing the initial distribution
q_{0}
and the control law
U_{t}=k(t,X_{t})
.

Initial distribution optimization:
To optimize the initial distribution, we use the closed-form expression for the KL divergence between one-dimensional Gaussian distributions

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}\|p_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(Q^{2}+\mu^{2}-\log Q^{2}-1),

then we can obtain the the gradient of cost with respect to the initial distribution mean and variance

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}[{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim q_{0}}[J(\xi,U)]+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}\|p_{0})]=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{0}]+\mu,

(22a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial Q}[{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim q_{0}}[J(\xi,U)]+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}\|p_{0})]=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{0}(X_{0}-\mu)/Q]

\displaystyle+Q-1/Q.

(22b)

TR-BSDE:
To compute the optimal control law, we initialize with the zero control
k(t,x)=0
, solve the BSDE, and learn
\phi
from Algorithm
1
. After each update of
\phi
, we set

k(t,x)=-g(t)^{\top}\phi(t,x)
. This choice is motivated by the stochastic maximum principle, which implies that at optimality
U_{t}=-g(t)^{\top}Y_{t}
and
Y_{t}=\phi(t,X_{t})
. The parameters of the initial Gaussian distribution are updated using (
22
) and
Y_{0}=\phi(0,x)
. The procedure appears in Algorithm
2
.

Adjoint matching
[
12
]
:
The control law
k(t,x)
is parameterized by a neural network and learned by regressing
k(t,X_{t})
onto
-g(t)^{\top}\mathsf{Y}_{t}
, where
\mathsf{Y}_{t}
is the non-adapted adjoint process. After a fixed number of regression iterations, the adjoint process is re-simulated under the updated control law. The procedure appears in Algorithm
4
. As in TR-BSDE, the parameters of the initial Gaussian distribution are updated using (
22
), with the difference that
Y_{0}
is replaced by the non-adapted process
\mathsf{Y}_{0}
.

We compare two methods for tilting parameter
\beta\in\{\frac{1}{50},\frac{1}{20},\frac{1}{8},\frac{1}{6},1\}
.
The experimental results are shown in Fig.
2
. It is observed that when
\beta
is small, the two methods exhibit similar performance. However, as
\beta
increases, the fine-tuning task becomes more challenging, and the proposed TR-BSDE outperforms the Adjoint matching approach.
