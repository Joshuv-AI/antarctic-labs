Paying in the dark: third-party administrator fees and the limits of employer cost control in self-insured health plans
Authors: Christine Buttorff, Petra W Rasmussen, Mallika Bhandarkar, Mekdes Shiferaw, Erin L Duffy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmployers are one of the largest purchasers of health care in the United States, covering more than 165 million people, yet they face persistent difficulty controlling costs. Most enrollees are in self-insured plans, which rely on third-party administrators (TPAs) to build provider networks and adjudicate claims. The fees employers pay these administrators are an under-documented source of health care spending. While recent litigation and news reporting allege that administrators overpay providers and charge large fees, the peer-reviewed literature offers limited detail on how these compensation mechanisms operate.MethodsWe conducted 22 semi-structured interviews with 34 participants during the fall of 2025, drawn from 4 groups nationwide: employers and employer coalitions; TPAs and partners; benefit consultants and brokers; and legal and policy experts. Transcripts were coded and analyzed for key themes.ResultsParticipants distinguished the base administrative fee, which employers compare across vendors, from additional fees that are less visible at contract signing. These include percentage-based shared-savings fees on out-of-network claims, overpayment-recovery fees, retained network discounts, a-la-carte service fees, and cross-plan offsetting. Interviewees described 2 pathways to address these fees—expanded transparency and clearer fiduciary accountability.ConclusionWhile potential solutions exist, market consolidation may limit employers’ ability to act on disclosed information.

Introduction

Employers are one of the largest purchasers of health insurance and health care services in the U.S., but they struggle to hold down costs. In 2025, average annual employer-sponsored health insurance premiums for family coverage reached nearly $27 000—a 26% increase over 2020.1 These rising costs affect a large proportion of nonelderly Americans, with more than 165 million enrolled in employer-sponsored coverage in 2025.2

Several recent publications explain barriers self-insured employers face in holding down costs. These barriers include the role of health care consolidation and vertical integration,3-6 the role of third-party administrators (TPAs) and other entities in managing health benefits for employers,7,8 and a lack of in-house expertise to negotiate lower prices or other contracting concessions such as quality reporting.9,10 Even well-resourced employers face barriers including employee resistance to network changes and insurance carrier consolidation that undermine their ability to shop effectively.11,12

TPAs play a central role in the management of health benefits for self-insured employers, which cover 67% of enrollees with employer-sponsored insurance in the U.S..2 Two common types of TPAs are carrier-led TPAs and independent TPAs. When an employer contracts with a large health insurer for core administrative services—provider network building and claims adjudication—this is known as a carrier-led TPA. Independent TPAs are those not affiliated with a major carrier. Independent TPAs perform similar functions to carrier-led TPAs although they may differ in how they design their provider network: some maintain their own networks while others lease carrier networks or combine the two approaches.

In addition to TPAs, there are other vendors that support the administration of self-insured benefits. “Re-pricing” companies, for example, work on the claims adjudication side with the TPA to determine the final negotiated amount with the provider, particularly for out-of-network services.13 Employers may also choose to add “point solutions” such as digital care vendors, centers of excellence, or condition-specific management programs (e.g., maternity, behavioral health, musculoskeletal) to the core TPA services. Pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) design formularies and process pharmacy claims. In some cases, TPAs may own these other vendors including re-pricers, point solutions, and PBMs. Finally, brokers and consultants serve as intermediaries across this ecosystem, helping employers evaluate TPAs and vendors, negotiate contracts, and assemble benefit packages.

Employers pay fees to each of these entities, which may be an unchecked source of additional health care costs. In fact, recent lawsuits and news stories allege that companies administering health benefits are not acting in plan participants’ best financial interests, are overpaying providers, and are paying themselves large fees for claims adjudication.14,15 Outside of these lawsuits and news stories, the peer-reviewed literature is missing detail on TPA compensation mechanisms, how informants who operate inside these arrangements describe them, and the barriers to addressing these fees. Against this backdrop, we conducted a set of key informant interviews to better understand TPA fees and potential policy solutions which are difficult to assess in quantitative data sources.

Methods

We conducted 22 semi-structured interviews during the fall of 2025 with participants from 4 key informant groups: (1) employers and employer coalitions; (2) TPAs and partners; (3) benefit consultants and brokers; and (4) legal and policy experts. We contacted individuals or organizations that fell into one of these categories and allowed participants to include their colleagues as they felt appropriate. Across the 22 interviews, 34 individuals participated (summarized in Table 1).

Interviews were conducted via a videoconferencing platform and lasted approximately 1 hour. The research team used semi-structured interview guides tailored to each respondent category to ensure some consistency across interviews while allowing flexibility for the discussion of new topics. Interviews were transcribed, coded, and analyzed for key themes. To code the data, we used MuseQDA, an AI-assisted qualitative research tool developed by RAND. Leveraging OpenAI language models, MuseQDA supports coding and thematic analysis. The software operates within RAND's secure computing environment, which restricts external data access and model retraining. The research team developed the codebook, following a combination of deductive reasoning for key themes in the interview guides and inductive reasoning for themes within the major topic areas. MuseQDA coded transcripts with researcher review of the applied codes. The research team summarized key themes. RAND's institutional review board certified the study as exempt.

Results

Interviewees consistently distinguished base administrative fees—almost always calculated at the per-member or per-employee-per-month level—from everything else (Table 2). Multiple participants described the administrative fee as the number employers fixate on, even though it is rarely the full cost: “Most HR and C-suite executives say, ‘What’s our admin fee?’ That’s what our trustees ask for. Even though we educate them from here to tomorrow … it does create this incentive to create a really low admin fee and find other ways to get money. Because that’s the spreadsheet you’re being compared against” (TPA/Partner 4). The administrative fee functions as an anchor that makes other fees less salient.

Beyond the administrative fee, the most pervasive category of fee type mentioned was the percentage-based shared savings fees, especially for out-of-network claims. One participant described the out-of-network claim repricing process this way:

The third-party administrator and the re-pricers that they hire [have] this broad range of ability to negotiate down a price or force the provider into taking a price, and then [the TPA] will collect up to 50% of the difference between the billed rate and the paid rate … You could have a claim for $100,000 that the provider makes, which is probably crazy. They’ll negotiate it down to $1,500, then they will bill the plan $31,500, $30,000 of which is a fee for supposedly negotiating it down to $1,500, and it will show up as that’s the cost of the claim … I don't think they really tell you that $30,000 of that is a fee. (Legal/Policy 3)

The shared savings fees were described as a regular part of the TPA business model: “Every TPA may do it a little bit differently, but that definitely is one of the pricing levers that TPAs do utilize” (TPA/Partner 2). One independent TPA described two voluntary steps they take to limit fees: “We typically put a cap on our fees at 25 grand to where none of our fees will exceed that. Also we cannot make more money than what was paid to the provider” (TPA/Partner 1).

A related fee category was percentage-based overpayment recovery or payment-integrity fees, incurred either in pre- or post-payment scenarios. Interviewees describe TPAs going back to recover “overpayment” errors—sometimes errors the TPA itself enabled—and then charging a percentage of the recovered amount as a fee (Legal/Policy 6). Several respondents explicitly criticized the logic of these fees: “On paper, they shouldn't be collecting fees on their own mistakes, but what I have heard is that they are … We don't know how widespread, but these are big players. It seems concerning that these fees are being applied” (Legal/Policy 1).

In addition to claims-based fees, interviewees mentioned several sources of indirect compensation. One independent TPA participant said that when interacting with a large carrier network rented for specific employers, the carrier would take the paid amount and take a 3%-5% fee for “network access,” reducing the paid amount to providers (TPA/Partner 3). In addition, several interviewees noted that the vertical integration in the industry allows fees to be charged across affiliated entities in ways that are difficult for employers to track: “Many TPAs also own provider groups or are part of a provider organization or own pharmacies or PBMs or all manner of other things that they are able to charge themselves either preferred prices or upcharge in certain ways and do intercompany transfers amongst the different entities within the corporation that allows them to hide a bunch of money and not have good transparency” (Employer/Coalition 2).

Finally, interviewees described a wide array of add-on fees. One interviewee stated that these fees exist because employers want customization: “[Employers] like ultimate customization... and being able to select the types of services that they buy and get it at a lower cost from an admin perspective” (TPA/Partner 2). For example, employers report being charged to obtain their own claims data or to audit it, often with limitations. One participant described this saying: “If you want to audit your data … the auditor is not of the employer's choosing. And then the other term that came along with that data availability was you could only get 20 claims per month” (Legal/Policy 4). Other fees included reporting fees (TPA/Partner 2) or add-on services such as more advanced patient navigation or additional clinical programs (Consultant/Broker 1). Fraud, waste and abuse fees were also mentioned, with one participant questioning their validity, saying, “I just feel that's part of what they do as a TPA, and it shouldn't be an extra fee in there” (Consultant/Broker 2). Litigation fees were a newer fee type related to claims processed through the federal Independent Dispute Resolution process that was created as part of the No Surprises Act (Legal/Policy 4).

One less mentioned source of TPA revenue was cross-plan offsetting, described as a practice where a TPA recoups an alleged overpayment to a provider from one plan by withholding or reducing payments owed under a different plan: “They come back and say, we overpaid you on a claim under one of our [fully] insured plans … We will only pay you X amount, and we will use the rest of the money that should have been paid to you from the self-funded plan to reimburse ourselves for our overpayment on our fully insured plan” (Legal/Policy 3). The offset is not occurring within the same plan or even the same funding arrangement, but across distinct lines of business. This process was discussed almost exclusively by legal and policy experts and described as persisting despite regulatory guidance.

Interviewees explained that the fees have been difficult for employers to control because they are generally not understood at contract signing. As one legal and policy interviewee put it: “[Employers] probably wouldn't catch it right away because, while they might get monthly claims reports, [the TPAs are] usually not, ‘Here's the billed charge. Here's the negotiated amount. Here's the difference. Here's the fee. And here's why we calculated it accurately.’ They wouldn't see that whole linear progression” (Legal/Policy 4). One interviewee said demanding audits was a critical step: “Especially in the large market, [we] go back and have data requests that say we need all out-of-network spend, including all fees charged by claim, and require that from them. And typically, they do eventually provide it. They kick and scream a little bit, but they will” (Consultant/Broker 2). Several interviewees noted that recent lawsuits or high-profile news stories have begun to increase employer awareness but more needs to be done: “Many employers, even if they’ve heard of the lawsuits, they’re still unsure that it's happening to them because they don't have full transparency in their data” (Employer/Coalition 2).

Approaches to increasing fee disclosures

Interviewees described two complementary pathways to improve employers’ ability to see and limit additional fees: increased transparency and fiduciary accountability. Interviewees also raised several strategies to opt-out of the TPA market altogether including fully insuring, direct contracting, and defined-contribution models, but since these are not this paper's focus we do not discuss these alternatives further.

Increasing transparency

The Consolidated Appropriations Act (CAA) of 2021 required certain disclosures and access to information (also known as the “gag clause prohibition”).16 Multiple interviewees stated that while administrative fees remain the most consistently disclosed element, shared-savings fees continue to sit in a gray zone: “[Even though] your contract doesn't include a gag clause, it is still incredibly hard for employers to get access to meaningful claims data and that is because the carriers and the PBMs and others have said, ‘we don't want to do it because it's going to expose us and make us look bad’” (Employer/Coalition 5).

Despite the CAA, interviewees emphasized that large employers have more leverage when demanding transparency with TPAs. As one interviewee explained: “If you are smaller … they will say, ‘Nope, sorry, go pound sand.’ So, anytime you’re making pretty significant interruptions into their standard process … there is a have and have not depending on your size, the influence you have on their business” (Employer/Coalition 6). One participant said the US Department of Labor's (DoL) 2012 rulemaking that required fee disclosures for 401(k)s may be informative17: “The two things that I would take away from the [retirement plan] experience though is that once there was sunlight, you didn't actually need those small employers to leverage the data that they had and negotiate. The industry all knew that they couldn't get away with what they had before, and fees started to drop” (Legal/Policy 5).

Fiduciary obligations

The Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) of 1974 regulates self-funded health plans at the federal level. Fiduciary obligations generally mean that the employer must manage plan assets in the interest of plan participants and must prudently manage the assets.18 With respect to health benefits, employers are supposed to make informed decisions about the selection of vendors to manage plan assets and to be reviewing the performance of their TPA under the statute. However, most find these functions difficult.19

One interviewee explained the logic of increased fiduciary responsibility: “[TPAs] basically control how the money is being spent by plans. That's the administration of a plan and management of a plan. That to me makes them a fiduciary, but they will deny that” (Legal/Policy 3). Many interviewees cautioned that whether fiduciary responsibility can be assigned to vendors operating plan assets, such as TPAs, is a legally complex question that is still working its way through courts, so more nuanced regulatory definitions may be needed.

Even though increased transparency and clearer fiduciary responsibility have the potential to harmonize incentives for employers and TPAs, these complementary solutions may not reliably lower fees in the short term in a consolidated TPA market. Consolidation has reduced employer options, as national carriers have absorbed regional TPAs and other vendors to offer all-in-one solutions, leaving employers with fewer choices which reduces their bargaining leverage (Legal/Policy 4). Many interviewees explained that few options can limit negotiating power. As one employer put it: “To negotiate tough, you have to be able to walk away” (Employer/Coalition 4). Interviewees noted that smaller regional employers may have alternative options that allow them to walk away if they are geographically concentrated, such as regional independent TPA or opting out by direct contracting.

Others thought that consolidation may also mute the impact of fee disclosure rules similar to the 401(k) rule noted above: “If you’re in a 401(k) plan, you’ve got service providers nationwide, lots of them competing with each other. You don't have that in health care. You’ve got maybe one TPA in your geographic area, maybe two. They’re both bad. They both won't give you information. So, really, what is your bargaining power?” (Legal/Policy 3). Another interviewee noted that there is no easy fix for the horizontal and vertical integration in the health care markets: “The [Federal Trade Commission] has [limited health care staff]. They simply have not prioritized this and in my opinion, the [Attorneys General] around the country were completely asleep at the wheel. They have allowed this wild consolidation and it's going to be really hard to undo” (Employer/Coalition 1). (1)

Discussion

Our interviews document the layered fee architecture operating within self-insured employers’ TPA arrangements. Some of these fees appear in recent litigation filings and news reporting but have received limited treatment in the peer-reviewed literature. The fees may have at one time been commonsense solutions for other problems, such as encouraging the TPA to negotiate downward high out-of-network bills, even if now their widespread use is contributing to high health care spending.20

Several interviewees identified the CAA 2021 as a starting point for fee disclosure, while noting that its requirements have not consistently produced visibility into claim-level fees. After our interviews concluded, the CAA of 2026 amended ERISA's service-provider compensation disclosure requirements to apply to TPAs and PBMs beginning in 2028. The CAA 2021's disclosure obligations were interpreted to apply to brokers and consultants only. Whether the amended disclosure requirements reach these claim-level and indirect forms of compensation, as opposed to direct administrative fees alone, will determine how much of the fee architecture documented here becomes visible to employers.

At nearly the same time, the DoL issued a proposed rule on PBM fee disclosure.21 The proposed rule strengthens employers’ auditing rights as a key enforcement mechanism, including barring restrictions on information sharing and limits on which entities can perform the audits. However, the proposed rule is subject to changes following the notice-and-comment stage, any potential legal challenges, and resolving any duplications or conflicts with the CAA of 2026. Regulations can be slow to implement due to court challenges: prior DoL rules defining fiduciary status for retirement plan service providers were vacated following litigation,22 and the Supreme Court's decision in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo effectively increased courts’ power to overturn regulatory decisions.23,24

Disclosure requirements identify fees but do not, on their own, determine whether employers can act on that information. Consolidation limits most employers’ ability to move business in response to a disclosed fee. Regional or independent TPAs may be a solution in some markets, but a large national employer likely needs a national carrier as a TPA.

Fiduciary status may provide more leverage; however, the status of TPAs and other entities under ERISA's fiduciary provisions remains unsettled. Recently enacted legislation in California and Indiana defines PBMs and/or TPAs as fiduciaries for plans they are able to regulate.25-27 As these state laws are implemented, they may provide evidence on the barriers and facilitators relevant to federal action.

This study has several limitations. We interviewed a purposive sample of informants selected for their knowledge of self-insured plan administration. Informants who declined to participate may differ from those who took part, including TPAs whose practices were a subject of the interviews. Our findings do not establish the prevalence or aggregate magnitude of these fees across the self-insured market, due to the lack of data sources that document fees. Some practices, such as cross-plan offsetting, were raised almost exclusively by legal and policy experts, indicating variation in awareness across groups rather than frequency. Quantifying the prevalence and cost of these fee mechanisms with quantitative data is a priority for future research.

Conclusion

The additional fees documented here are difficult for employers to identify without claim-level data and the means to audit it. Whether expanded disclosure requirements change the fees employers pay will depend on enforcement and remains the space to watch.
