Non-diffracting meronic spin defects of light Preprint: APS/123-QED
Nilo Mata-Cervera, Miguel A. Porras, Yijie Shen

Abstract
Optical vortices are singularity lines where the light field intensity vanishes and its phase is undefined. These threads of darkness are adorned by Gauss’s law as lines of pure longitudinal polarization where the polarization plane tilts and winds around. We unveil the resulting spin field as a unique structure which unifies both topological textures and defects, as it includes a point defect of undefined spin surrounded by a meronic texture which spans half the spin unit sphere. Moreover, this intricate topological structure of transverse spin does not spread in propagation, is localized arbitrarily below the wavelength of light and presents highly anisotropic features. Here we describe these hidden topologies of transverse spin embedded in simple scalar vortex beams, highlighting the diversity of topological structures that arise in two different spaces—the spin unit sphere and the transverse-axial Poincaré sphere—and discuss the underlying aspects behind their subwavelength localization.

II
Texture-like defects

Topological structures are usually classified into two categories
35
;
37
:

•

Topological defects
: These are special points, lines, and surfaces of discontinuous or undefined physical value, where the behavior of the singular properties around the discontinuity identifies topologically distinct field configurations (e.g. phase singularity at the center of a vortex beam, the spin defect at the center of a focused vector beam, and the L lines in a polarization vector field).

•

Topological textures
: Patterns that continuously fill a surface or volume (in the absence of any singularity), where different topological arrangements are classified by the map between the physical space and order-parameter space (e.g. 2D meron, skyrmion, 3D hopfion, 4D skyrmion).

Here, we propose a different class of topological structures possessing both features of defect and texture simultaneously. Such structure includes a defect at the center as a singularity of vanishing spin, while in the vicinity of the defect the surrounding spin texture resembles a meron structure (mapping to a half 2-sphere). This contrasts with transverse spin vortices, where the vector field is contained in a plane and circulates around a nodal point of the field (whose orientation is fully undefined); and meron skyrmionic textures, where the vector field spans a half 2-sphere being well defined everywhere even if some components of the field vanish at specific locations (but the field itself is never zero). We denote such field as a meronic defect, see Fig.
1
. Hereinafter, we show how such unique meronic defects appear naturally around optical vortex lines in the vicinity of the phase singularity.

III
Non-transverse polarization around vortex lines

Consider the most elementary type of vortex beam: uniform polarization and cylindrically symmetric intensity. The intensity vanishes at the center, the phase singularity. Such wave field itself satisfies Helmholtz equation, or its paraxial counterpart, and as any type of structured wave it is naturally accompanied by a faint longitudinal field, making the entire electric field consistent with Gauss’s law in free space
(\nabla\cdot\bm{E}=0)
. While the intensity of the axial field is generally insignificant in paraxial waves, it plays a predominant role at regions where the transverse field vanishes, near its phase singularities. As a result, the polarization state inevitably becomes structured around the zeros of a uniformly polarized transverse field. Coupling of field components through Gauss’s law is reminiscent of the vector nature of light, evoking that even in simple settings light cannot be treated as a scalar wave. The complex envelope
\bm{\psi}
of the electric field
\bm{E}=\bm{\psi}e^{ikz}
reads

\bm{\psi}=\psi_{\perp}\bm{u}_{\perp}+\psi_{z}\bm{u}_{z}=\psi_{\perp}\left(\bm{u}_{\perp}+\rho e^{i\gamma}\bm{u}_{z}\right),

(1)

where the longitudinal field
\psi_{z}=\psi_{\perp}\rho e^{i\gamma}
is expressed with respect to the transverse field through the field ratio
\rho e^{i\gamma}=\psi_{z}/\psi_{\perp}
,
k=2\pi/\lambda
is the wavenumber for a monochromatic field with wavelength
\lambda
. The uniform transverse polarization
\bm{u}_{\perp}
can be written as

\bm{u}_{\perp}=\cos\left(\alpha_{\rm ps}/2\right)e^{-i\varphi_{\rm ps}/2}\bm{u}_{R}+\sin\left(\alpha_{\rm ps}/2\right)e^{i\varphi_{\rm ps}/2}\bm{u}_{L}

(2)

where
\alpha_{\rm ps}
and
\varphi_{\rm ps}
are the polar and azimuthal angles in the Poincaré sphere (PS), the parametric space representing all transverse polarization states
3
,
\bm{u}_{L,R}=(\bm{u}_{x}\pm i\bm{u}_{y})/\sqrt{2}
are the Jones vectors of left- and right-handed circular polarization (LCP and RCP respectively). The orientation of the polarization ellipse in the transverse plane is given by
-\varphi_{\rm ps}/2
and its ellipticity angle
\chi=\pi/4-\alpha_{\rm ps}/2
. RCP and LCP are the north
\left(\alpha_{\rm ps}=0\right)
and south
\left(\alpha_{\rm ps}=\pi\right)
poles of the PS respectively, where the azimuth
\varphi_{\rm ps}
is singular and so does the orientation of the polarization ellipse. The relative weight between RCP and LCP determines the expected value of the
z-
component spin angular momentum
\sigma_{z}

6
, which for a uniformly polarized beam (
2
) is given by
\sigma_{z}=\cos\left(\alpha_{\rm ps}\right)
, yielding
\sigma_{z}=-1
for LCP, and
\sigma_{z}=+1
for RCP. Ignoring global constants and envelopes, the transverse complex amplitude around the phase singularity can be written as

\psi_{\perp}(r,\phi,z)\propto\left(\frac{r}{w(z)}\right)^{|\ell|}e^{i\ell\phi},

(3)

where
w(z)
is a
z-
dependent scaling parameter which accounts for the divergence of the beam,
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
and
\phi=\tan^{-1}\left(y/x\right)
are the radial and azimuthal coordinates in the transverse plane
18
. Once the transverse field is known, the longitudinal field is fixed by Gauss’s law, which for paraxial waves reads
\psi_{z}=(i/k)\nabla_{\perp}\cdot\bm{\psi}_{\perp}
, where
\nabla_{\perp}\cdot
is the transverse divergence
29
. The longitudinal field of a vortex beam (
3
) dominates around its phase singularity for
\ell>0
and RCP, and for
\ell<0
and LCP. On the other hand, it becomes negligible with respect to the transverse field around its phase singularity for
\ell<0
and RCP, and for
\ell>0
and LCP
30
. For elliptical polarization the longitudinal field can be reconstructed from these two cases yielding

\psi_{z}=\frac{i|\ell|}{kr}\left(\frac{r}{w(z)}\right)^{|\ell|}\sqrt{1+\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\right)\sigma_{z}}

\times\exp\left[i\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\right)\left(|\ell|-1\right)\left(\phi+\varphi_{\rm ps}/2\right)\right],

(4)

where we have expressed it in terms of the spin charge
\sigma_{z}
and the sign of the topological charge
\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\right)
. Around the phase singularity, a non-transverse polarization structure emerges if the field ratio between transverse and longitudinal components is non-trivial. From (
2
) and (
4
) we obtain

\rho e^{i\gamma}=\frac{\psi_{z}}{\psi_{\perp}}=\frac{i|\ell|}{kr}e^{-i\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\right)\left(\phi+\varphi_{\rm ps}/2\right)}\sqrt{1+\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\right)\sigma_{z}}.

(5)

If the longitudinal field is negligible the ratio becomes trivial
\psi_{z}/\psi_{\perp}=0
and the non-transverse polarization texture is lost. We remark that the polarization structure around the vortex core given by Eq. (
5
) is indeed independent of the shape of the vortex beam, as shown in the Appendix (
VII.5
). Yet, our analysis is limited to paraxial fields, therefore we neglect spin-orbit coupling and other depolarization effects arising in tight-focusing conditions
26
.

Eq. (
5
) is analogous to the quotient between
x-
and
y-
polarized fields introduced by Poincaré to describe the transverse polarization
36
, but here it involves the transverse and longitudinal complex amplitudes of the wave. The resulting transverse-axial (TA) polarization state can be represented parametrically by expressing (
5
) in the Riemann sphere
1
. In this parametric space north and south poles are points of longitudinal and transverse polarization. The degree of longitudinal polarization is given by the polar angle

\alpha_{z\perp}=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{|\psi_{z}|^{2}-|\psi_{\perp}|^{2}}{|\psi_{z}|^{2}+|\psi_{\perp}|^{2}}\right)=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}-1}{\rho^{2}+1}\right),

(6)

while the azimuthal angle represents the phase difference

\varphi_{z\perp}=\arg\left(\psi_{z}\psi_{\perp}^{\star}\right)=-\gamma.

(7)

We refer to this space as the transverse-axial Poincaré sphere (TA-PS) characterized by the spherical angles
\alpha_{z\perp}
and
\varphi_{z\perp}
or by the TA-Stokes vector
\left(\sin\alpha_{z\perp}\cos\varphi_{z\perp},\sin\alpha_{z\perp}\sin\varphi_{z\perp},\cos\alpha_{z\perp}\right)
.

The spatially-varying field ratio (
5
) in fact defines a skyrmionic polarization texture, a topological map from the TA-PS to the transverse plane
31
. The polarization texture covers all the surface of this parametric sphere, from the north pole
\alpha_{z\perp}\to 0
which is mapped to the vortex core
\left(\rho\to\infty\right)
, gradually descending to the south pole
\alpha_{z\perp}\to\pi
as the polarization becomes transverse
\left(\rho\to 0\right)
away from the phase singularity. All azimuths on the TA-PS
\varphi_{z\perp}=-\gamma
are present as we complete a full turn around the phase singularity. Thus, polar and azimuthal angles in the TA-PS are mapped into radial and azimuthal coordinates of the transverse plane,
\alpha_{z\perp}=\alpha_{z\perp}(r)
and
\varphi_{z\perp}=\varphi_{z\perp}(\phi)
respectively, a mapping with nonzero skyrmion number
31

Q_{z\perp}^{\star}=\iint\sin\alpha_{z\perp}\frac{\textrm{d}\alpha_{z\perp}}{\textrm{d}r}\frac{\textrm{d}\varphi_{z\perp}}{\textrm{d}\phi}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}\phi=\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\right).

(8)

This skyrmion number is only valid for the unstable configuration (
3
) and is bounded to
\pm 1
, we denote this case with the superscript (
⋆
). In real settings the skyrmion number
Q_{z\perp}
becomes unbounded when (
3
) unfolds its phase singularities, as we discuss later in
IV.3
.

Unlike in a paraxial skyrmionic beam where the transverse polarization ellipse features all ellipticities and in-plane orientations, the TA skyrmionic texture (
5
) is only parametric. The TA-PS captures information of the relative phase and amplitude between transverse and longitudinal fields, but does not provide a direct connection with the geometry of the 3D polarization ellipse. The later depends on the transverse polarization itself (
2
), which is absent in the construction of the TA-PS (
6
,
7
). Yet, the polarization texture (
5
) can be described using physical magnitudes such as the 3D orientation of the polarization plane. As we discuss in the next section, when we express the features of the polarization texture (
5
) in physical space, the emerging topologies are richer and differ with respect to the highly symmetric skyrmionic textures in the TA-PS (
6
,
7
,
8
).

IV
Meronic spin defects of light

Non-transverse polarization is connected to transverse spin angular momentum (SAM) density, emerging at points where the polarization ellipse is not orthogonal to the wave’s mean linear momentum
13
. In the paraxial approximation, both electric and magnetic polarization ellipses contribute equally to the SAM density, therefore ignoring constants the SAM can be evaluated from (
1
) as
\bm{S}=\textrm{Im}\left(\bm{\psi}^{\star}\times\bm{\psi}\right)

11
;
42
.
The three-component SAM density can be expressed in terms of the TA field ratio (
5
), the full expression can be found in Eq. (
26
) of
VII.1
. We only focus on the direction of the SAM (unit vector orthogonal to the polarization ellipse), expressed in therms of azimuthal
\varphi_{\rm spin}=\tan^{-1}\left(S_{y}/S_{x}\right)
and polar
\alpha_{\rm ps}=\cos^{-1}\left(S_{z}/|\bm{S}|\right)=\cos^{-1}\left(s_{z}\right)
angles

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm spin}=-\frac{\varphi_{\rm ps}}{2}+\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\tan\left(\varphi_{z\perp}\right)}{\tan\left(\chi\right)}\right),

(9)

\displaystyle s_{z}=\cos\left(\alpha_{\rm spin}\right)=\frac{\sigma_{z}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{z}^{2}+2\rho^{2}\left(1-\cos\left(2\gamma\right)\sqrt{1-\sigma_{z}^{2}}\right)}}.

(10)

Eq. (
9
) connects the azimuthal angle of the SAM density (measured relative to the transverse polarization orientation
-\varphi_{\rm ps}/2
) with the azimuth of the TA-PS
\varphi_{z\perp}=-\gamma
, the ellipticity angle of the transverse polarization
\chi
scaling their relationship. From (
9
) we identify the poles of the TA-PS (singular
\varphi_{z\perp}
) as singularities of the transverse SAM (singular
\varphi_{\rm spin}
), points of undefined azimuthal orientation of the polarization plane. These are also the phase singularities of the transverse and longitudinal fields (where
\gamma
is singular). Intuitively, the north pole of the TA-PS corresponds to longitudinal polarization where the polarization plane is not defined (the ellipse degenerates to an axial line) and at the south pole the polarization plane lays completely on the transverse plane thus its azimuth is also undefined. Eq. (
9
) and (
10
) provide a representation of the TA polarization structure (
5
) using the real space polarization plane orientation, in contrast to the coordinates in the TA-PS or the TA-Stokes vector which themselves only refer to an abstract parametric space. The resulting SAM texture constitutes a meronic defect, a spatially-varying SAM field with a singularity
\bm{S}=0
at the center.

IV.2
Anisotropic textures

When the transverse polarization state is not circular
\left(\sigma_{z}^{2}\neq 1\right)
, meronic spin defects become highly anisotropic by virtue of the term
\tan\left(\chi\right)
in (
9
) and the factors in
\sigma_{z}^{2}
in (
10
). The contours of the spin azimuth (
9
) squeeze around the major axis of the transverse polarization ellipse (at
\phi=-\varphi_{\rm ps}/2
), and the contours of the polar angle (
10
) lose their circular symmetry. The topological features of the spin defect are invariant upon these deformations; one of them is the topological charge of the transverse SAM vortex (
9
) calculated as the circulation of (
9
) in a path surrounding the phase singularity. From the properties of the inverse tangent function, it follows immediately that the singularities of
\varphi_{\rm spin}
are of the same order as the singularities of
\gamma
, and the ellipticity
\chi
in (
10
) only changes their relative sign. Since
\varphi_{\rm ps}
is constant and
\textrm{sgn}\left(\chi\right)=\textrm{sgn}\left(\sigma_{z}\right)
we can express the circulation as

\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\nabla\varphi_{\rm spin}\cdot\textrm{d}\bm{l}=-\frac{\textrm{sgn}\left(\sigma_{z}\right)}{2\pi}\oint\nabla\gamma\cdot\textrm{d}\bm{l}=\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\sigma_{z}\right),

(13)

where we have used the phase of the field ratio in (
5
). Eq. (
13
) determines the topology of the transverse SAM vortex, yielding circulations for
\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\sigma_{z}\right)=1
and saddles for
\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\sigma_{z}\right)=-1
. Since a circulation cannot be continuously deformed into a saddle
23
, there is a degenerate case (neither vortex nor saddle) which delimits them. This happens if the transverse polarization is linear
\sigma_{z}=0
, where the SAM density is entirely transverse and points orthogonally to the major axis of the transverse ellipse. The streamlines of the SAM density in a frame
x^{\prime}y^{\prime}
aligned with the transverse polarization ellipse’s major axis (a rotation of the

-\varphi_{\rm ps}/2
) satisfy
\textrm{d}y^{\prime}/\textrm{d}x^{\prime}=\tan\left(\varphi_{\rm spin}+\varphi_{\rm ps}/2\right)
, and yield the family of curves

x^{\prime 2}\frac{\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\right)}{\tan\left(\chi\right)}+y^{\prime 2}=C,

(14)

with
C
a constant. These curves are are hyperbolas (saddles) for
\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\sigma_{z}\right)<0
and ellipses (circulations) for
\textrm{sgn}\left(\ell\sigma_{z}\right)>0
. The minor axis of the transverse polarization ellipse coincides with the major axis of an elliptical streamline, with its eccentricity given by
\sqrt{1-\tan^{2}\left(\chi\right)}
. The streamlines degenerate into straight lines for linear polarization
\chi=\sigma_{z}=0
, where the topological charge of the SAM vortex is degenerate.

In addition to the anisotropy of the transverse SAM, the geometry of the out-of-plane component
s_{z}=\cos\left(\alpha_{\rm spin}\right)
changes with
\tan\left(\chi\right)

(15)

with

s_{z}

contour as the ratio between the maximum
r_{\rm max}
and minimum
r_{\rm min}
radii in (

\frac{r^{2}_{\rm max}}{r^{2}_{\rm min}}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-\sigma_{z}^{2}}}{1-\sqrt{1-\sigma_{z}^{2}}}=\frac{1+\sin{\left(\alpha_{\rm ps}\right)}}{1-\sin{\left(\alpha_{\rm ps}\right)}},

(16)

where the dependence of
s_{z}
factors out. As expected Eq. (

IV.3
Topological charge

Ideal propagation and confinement

These topological features have been discussed for a certain propagation plane

VI
Discussion

We have shown the topological structure of non-transverse polarization and transverse spin that arises naturally at scalar vortices around their phase singularity. The polarization texture presents skyrmionic topology in a parametric space describing the relative phase and amplitudes between transverse and longitudinal fields akin to the Poincaré sphere and associated Stokes skyrmionic beams. On the other hand, the resulting distribution of transverse spin angular momentum constitutes a meronic defect with fractional topology. The spin meronic defect presents highly anisotropic features which are result of the ellipticity of the transverse polarization, but such anisotropy is not present in the parametric representation of the polarization texture.

These topological structures are strongly localized below the wavelength of light, product of the weak amplitude of longitudinal fields in the paraxial regime. The same subwavelength localization is maintained regardless of the size of the vortex beam, its convergence or divergence and the propagation distance. Similar behavior is found when the vortex beam is perturbed and the phase singularities split, yet each of the split singularities will give rise to their own non-diffractive polarization texture. As propagating counterparts, these textures complement previously reported spin structures such as spin defects
46
, localized around a point in 3D space; three-dimensional spin quasi-particles
45
;
49
;
44
, distributed in a 3D volume; and evanescent topological spin structures
22
;
21
, localized around material interfaces.

This kind of non-transverse polarization textures presents unique properties that distinguish them significantly from Stokes skyrmionic textures. Breaking with previously known properties of polarization textures, which are intrinsically unbounded and diffractive
50
, the structures shown here are localized below the wavelength of light and propagate without diffraction. Moreover, their size is fixed by constituent relations—Gauss’s law which couples different field polarization components—in contrast with Stokes skyrmionic beams where the confinement of the texture is not guaranteed by any natural law. Second, boundary conditions are naturally provided by the paraxiality of the wave, which guarantees the polarization state to become transverse within few wavelengths away from the phase singularity, in other words, the polarization state is fixed away from the center of the texture. This behavior could be maintained for different kinds of perturbations applied onto the field profile as long as the paraxiality is preserved, and drastically differs with Stokes skyrmionic textures where the polarization state far away from the origin is not guaranteed to be fixed after perturbation.

As a result of the strong localization and boundary conditions provided by paraxiality, these textures approximately feature integer wrappings of their parametric space, and the wrapping number can only change by quantized amounts when a new singularity is introduced in the field. Therefore it would be interesting to study whether these textures present stronger robustness under perturbations than standard polarization textures, where integer wrappings are not protected
33
. It remains unexplored how these features extend to the non-paraxial regime, where the difference between electric and magnetic spins is prominent. Spin meronic defects with diffraction-free propagation around vortex lines have recently been demonstrated in acoustic waves both theoretically and experimentally
4
. These topological structures could in principle find counterparts in other systems such as surface waves
39
, quantum
32
, and condensed matter
14
, where strong localization together with non-diffraction might find different applications. In optical systems, super-resolution techniques based on vortex phase singularities have achieved picometric metrology and object localization well below the diffraction limit
51
;
27
. It is a direction to explore whether the performance of these metrology techniques could be affected by the non-transverse polarization texture arising around the singularity, where the spin structure can be confined to arbitrarily small scales.
