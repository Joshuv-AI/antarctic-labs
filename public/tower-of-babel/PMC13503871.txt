A dual-receptor checkpoint primes enterovirus D68 for respiratory cell entry
Authors: Shengjian Liang, Haoran Guo, Zhili Xu, Zan Liu, Wanying Yang, Xiaoke Liu, Zhenyu Wei, Jichao Hou, Wei Huang, Wei Wang, Jiachen Li, Wendong Ma, Zhikang Deng, Haocong Qian, Yaxin Wang, Jialin Liu, Zihe Rao, Wei Wei, Zhiyong Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MFSD6 is a newly identified receptor that mediates the invasion of respiratory cells by enterovirus D68 (EV-D68), a non-polio enterovirus that causes severe respiratory disease and poliomyelitis-like illness in children. Here, we report near-atomic-resolution cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of historical and contemporary AFM-associated EV-D68 strains, together with their complexes bound to the third extracellular loop of MFSD6 (MFSD6-L3). These structures uncover a previously unrecognized “binary gating switch” mechanism of virus-receptor engagement that differs from the reported model. In this mechanism, the N200-V208 segment of MFSD6, carrying a glycosylated Asn207, inserts into one capsomer, whereas the sialyl-Gal-terminated glycan of MFSD6 engages an adjacent capsomer. Neu5Ac binding induces conformational rearrangements that expel the pocket factor, destabilize the virion, and prime infection. Functional analyses further define the contributions of the receptor-contacting residues and glycans to viral attachment and entry. Together, our findings refine the molecular basis of EV-D68 recognition of MFSD6 and reveal a glycan-mediated dual-lock mechanism that may enhance receptor specificity, prevent premature uncoating, and ensure productive infection only upon engagement of the correct host receptor. These results provide broader insight into enterovirus tropism and establish a framework for structure-guided antiviral design.

Introduction

EV-D68, a member of human enterovirus species D, caused a major outbreak in the United States in 2014, with 1,153 reported cases and 14 deaths, and is now considered a major public health concern1,2. As a distinctive type of human enterovirus, EV-D68 spreads primarily through respiratory transmission, causing bronchiolitis and pneumonia3–5. Remarkably, EV-D68 infection is associated with severe neurological complications such as paralysis, acute flaccid myelitis (similar to polio), and cranial nerve dysfunction6–9. The understanding of how EV-D68 infects respiratory and neurological cells is important for the development of therapeutics to prevent and treat EV-D68-associated diseases.

Receptor engagement is a key determinant of virus entry, as it directly initiates the conformational changes required for membrane penetration and release of the viral genome into the host cell10. This step not only determines whether the virus can bind to a given cell but also contributes to cellular and tissue tropism, as productive entry is restricted to cell types that express the appropriate receptor. Sialic acid and heparan sulfate were first identified as the non-proteinaceous receptors of EV-D6811–13. Structural studies have shown that when sialic acid binds to the canyon region on the surface of EV-D68, it triggers a cascade of conformational changes in the virus, ejecting a fatty-acid-like molecule and facilitating virus uncoating11. Intercellular adhesion molecule 5 (ICAM-5) was subsequently identified as a protein receptor for both sialic-acid-dependent and -independent EV-D68 strains14. Because ICAM-5 is enriched in the telencephalon region of the brain15, the discovery of neuron-specific ICAM-5 as EV-D68 receptor provided a potential link to its neurotropic pathogenesis. Very recent works identified major facilitator superfamily-domain containing protein 6 (MFSD6) as a key receptor for EV-D68 infection in respiratory cells16,17. The expression of MFSD6 is necessary for virus attachment and infection, and the third loop (MFSD6-L3; residues K153-E285) of MFSD6 is involved in the recognition of EV-D6816,17. The recombinant protein comprising the MFSD6-L3 fused to Fc potently and effectively blocked EV-D68 infection in vitro and prevented lethality in newborn mice16.

Here, we report cryo-EM structures of the EV-D68 prototype strain Fermon and two contemporary 2014 outbreak isolates, US/MO/14-18947 and US/KY/14-18953, both in the apo state and in complex with human MFSD6-L3 at near-atomic resolution. These structures reveal a glycan-dependent binary gating mechanism of virus–receptor engagement and refine our understanding of MFSD6-mediated EV-D68 entry into respiratory cells.

Results

Cryo-EM structure determination

The virus particles of the EV-D68 strain Fermon were cultured in RD cells and purified by ultracentrifugation. The third loop of MFSD6 (MFSD6-L3; residues K153-E285) fused with a C-terminal human antibody constant region (Fc) tag (MFSD6-L3-Fc) was produced in HEK293F cells. The EV-D68 particles were incubated with MFSD6-L3-Fc to yield the complex. A total of 3308 and 2744 cryo-EM micrographs for EV-D68 alone or in complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc were collected, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Table 1). Symmetry expansion and local refinement were applied to the pentamer around the icosahedral 5-fold axis.

For EV-D68 alone, two kinds of EV-D68 particles were observed in cryo-EM images, i.e., the mature virion (EV-D68-mature) and the procapsid (EV-D68-procapsid). For EV-D68:MFSD6-L3-Fc complex, three types of particles were observed, including the mature virion in complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc (EV-D68-mature:MFSD6-L3), an empty particle in complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc, and the procapsid without receptor binding (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 1 and 2). Single-particle reconstructions with C5 symmetry yielded the nominal resolutions of 2.23 Å, 2.93 Å, and 3.17 Å for the mature virion, an empty particle in complex MFSD6-L3-Fc, and the procapsid alone, respectively (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 2). Although the procapsid was also observed in the sample, no particles representing the procapsid potentially in complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc were observed (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating the procapsid may have a distinct structural feature to prohibit receptor binding.

To assess whether MFSD6 recognition is conserved across strains, we additionally determined apo and MFSD6-bound structures of two 2014 outbreak isolates (US/MO/14-18947 and US/KY/14-18953), as described in the section “Strain-independent recognition of MFSD6 by EV-D68”.

Overall structures

The EV-D68 mature virion is assembled by 60 copies of each of four proteins, VP1 to VP4, in a typical pseudo T = 3 icosahedral symmetry (Fig. 1a). The capsid accommodates deep surface depressions (‘canyons’) around each icosahedral 5-fold axis. For the EV-D68 mature virion alone, the models of VP1 (residues I1-T296), VP2 (residues S10-T247), VP3 (residues G1-Q247), and VP4 (residues I30-L68) were built in the densities (Supplementary Fig. 3a,b). As with other enteroviruses, the EV-D68 mature virion possesses a hydrophobic pocket that is located in the interior of the VP1 β-barrel, underlying a canyon-like surface depression18 and harboring a natural lipid termed as “pocket factor”. The shape of density corresponding to the bound pocket factor in the EV-D68 mature virion suggested it is possibly a sphingosine (SPH) molecule, which differs from a fatty acid of about 10 carbon atoms found in a previously reported structure of EV-D68 mature virion (PDB: 4WM8)19, but was observed in poliovirus and EV-A7120,21 (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 4a and b). For the procapsid, the models of EV-D68 VP1 (residues V54-M290), VP2 (residues L14-T247), and VP3 (residues G1-Q247) were built in the densities (Supplementary Fig. 3a and b). The structure of the procapsid is similar to previously reported structures, and the SPH molecule was not observed in the VP1 pocket17 (Supplementary Fig. 4c and d).

For the EV-D68 mature virion in complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc, the models of EV-D68 VP1 (residues I1-T296), VP2 (residues S10-T247), VP3 (residues G1-Q247), and VP4 (residues I30-L68) were built in the densities (Supplementary Fig. 3a and 3b). The SPH molecule is absent (Supplementary Fig. 4e). For the empty particle in complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc, the models of EV-D68 VP1 (residues Q51-T296), VP2 (residues L14-T247), VP3 (residues G1-Q247), and VP4 (residues S36-Q44) were built in the densities (Supplementary Fig. 3a and b). It is noteworthy that although both the empty particle in the complex form and the procapsid do not have viral genome, the empty particle exists in its complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc, but the procapsid cannot. They also exhibited distinct structures (Supplementary Fig. 4f). According to our structure, the procapsid of EV-D68 lacks the VP4 protein entirely, which is consistent with previous structural reports22 (Supplementary Fig. 3b and 4c). However, in the empty particle in complex with MFSD6-L3-Fc, we observed density for VP4 and have built models of VP4 (residues S36-Q44) based on this density (Supplementary Fig. 3b and 4f). Although this MFSD6-bound empty particle lacks viral RNA, only weak and partial VP4 density is detectable, in contrast to the stronger VP4 density observed in the apo mature virion and the MFSD6-bound mature particle. This residual VP4 density may reflect partial occupancy, conformational heterogeneity, or averaging of flexible residual density, rather than retention of a fully ordered VP4 protein after RNA release. The pocket factor density is also absent in this empty particle (Supplementary Fig. 4g). We therefore describe this particle as an MFSD6-bound empty particle with B-particle-like features. Importantly, we did not observe a canonical RNA-containing A-particle intermediate, such as the altered particle reported for ICAM-5-treated EV-D6822. Therefore, although MFSD6 engagement is associated with pocket factor ejection and the appearance of an empty particle class, we do not infer the complete genome-release pathway from these structures alone.

Interactions of EV-D68 and MFSD6-L3

In the cryo-EM density of EV-D68-mature:MFSD6-L3-Fc, two distinct densities attributable to MFSD6-L3-Fc were observed (Fig. 1c, d). The first part binds at the canyon region on the right rim of one capsomer, while the second part binds at the canyon region on the left rim of another capsid protomer (Supplementary Fig. 4h).

In the first region, a continuous density is located in the canyon region and interacts with VP1 G-H loop, VP2 E-F loop, and the C-terminus of VP3 (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Fig. 5a). The feature of the densities guided the modeling of MFSD6N200-MFSD6V208 with a glycosylated N207 residue of MFSD6-L3, which is similar to the previously reported MFSD6:EV-D68 complex structure17. The virus-receptor interfaces at this region in the complexes of MFSD6-L3 with the mature or the B-particle are identical (Fig. 1d). The contacting area on VP1, VP2, and VP3 accounting for the interactions in this part with MFSD6-L3-Fc are calculated as 569 Å2, 149 Å2, and 180 Å2, respectively. We also observed a bulk of density adjacent to N207 (Supplementary Fig. 5b), which was suggested as the glycan linked with N20717. The interacting residues of MFSD6-L3 includes residues MFSD6N200, MFSD6L201, MFSD6L202, MFSD6E204, and MFSD6R205 that contact with VP1, MFSD6N200 that contact with VP2 (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Table 2). Among these interactions, the side chain of MFSD6N200 forms hydrogen bonds with the side chain of VP1T94. The side chain of MFSD6E203 establishes hydrogen bonds with multiple residues on VP1, including K92, G151, T216, and N219, and also forms a salt bridge with the side chain of VP1K92. The main-chain oxygen of MFSD6R205 forms a hydrogen bond with the main-chain nitrogen of VP1L208, and the side chain of MFSD6R205 forms another hydrogen bond with the main-chain nitrogen of VP1L208. Additionally, the main chain at position MFSD6L202 forms a hydrogen bond with the side chain of VP1T216. Furthermore, MFSD6L201 and MFSD6L202 contribute to a hydrophobic pocket that interacts with VP1I262 and VP1L107, and VP3A245. In addition, the N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) at MFSD6N207 forms hydrogen bonds with the side chains of VP2T137, VP2T138, and VP2T139, the main chain of VP2S140, and the side chain of VP3H240 (Supplementary Fig. 5c).

The most remarkable difference between our cryo-EM density with the reported structure17 is that a convincing density was identified in the canyon region on the left rim in an adjacent capsomer (Fig. 1e). The shape of this block of density fits well with Neu5Acα2-3Galβ1-4GlcNAc (3′SLN) in a previously reported structure of EV-D68 in complex with 3′SLN (PDB: 5BNP)11. We named this site as “Neu5Ac-binding site”. Neu5Ac is a commonly occurring sialic acid in mammalian cells and was reported to be capable of mediating EV-D68 cell entry11. This structural feature suggests that a Neu5Ac-containing glycan may bind in this second binding site and play a crucial role in virus entry.

Structural and glycomic dissection of MFSD6 reveals Neu5Ac-mediated recognition by EV-D68

Global identification statistics revealed comparable numbers of MS/MS spectra, intact glycopeptides, and mapped glycosylation sites between the two constructs, indicating similar overall analytical coverage (Fig. 2a). We next assessed sialylated glycan occupancy at individual glycosylation sites. For each site, sialylated intact glycosites were quantified based on MS/MS spectral counts, and site-specific sialylation occupancy was calculated as the proportion of sialylated glycan spectra relative to total glycopeptide spectra identified at that site (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs 6a and 6b). Notably, glycosylation sites located in proximity to N207 exhibited relatively higher sialylation occupancy, whereas more distal sites showed lower levels of sialylated glycan modification. This spatial trend suggests that sialylated glycans near the MFSD6 receptor-binding region may contribute more prominently to EV-D68 engagement.

To further characterize O-GalNAc glycosylation, we quantified the numbers of intact O-GalNAc glycosylation sites at which specific O-glycan types were identified in MFSD6-L3-Fc and MFSD6-L3-N207A-Fc (Fig. 2c). Among the detected O-glycan species, H2N2A1 was identified at the largest number of intact O-GalNAc glycosylation sites, indicating that it represents the most frequently observed sialylated O-glycan across sites.

To further resolve site-specific differences in O-glycan composition, we examined the relative distributions of individual sialylated O-glycan types at each O-GalNAc glycosylation site (Fig. 2d). This analysis revealed pronounced heterogeneity in sialylated O-glycan composition across sites. Notably, the N2H2A1 glycoform constituted a relatively large fraction of the sialylated glycopeptide spectra at multiple O-GalNAc sites, with the highest relative proportion observed at T204.

To assess whether the observed density corresponds to glycosylation at N207, we generated a mutant in which N207 was substituted with alanine (MFSD6-L3-N207A-Fc) and determined the cryo-EM structure of EV-D68 in complex with this mutant. In the resulting cryo-EM map, the density previously attributed to N207 glycosylation was absent, whereas the densities corresponding to the Neu5Ac-binding glycan and the MFSD6 N200-V208 polypeptide remained clearly visible (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Mass spectrometric analysis of MFSD6-L3-N207A confirmed the loss of glycosylation at N207, with other glycosylation sites remaining intact (Fig. 2b). These findings indicate that the density observed at the Neu5Ac-binding site is not derived from N207 glycosylation.

Next, we aimed to identify the exact structure of the polysaccharide on the opposite side. However, the MFSD6-L3 peptide has extensive glycosylation modifications (Fig. 2b), and the density of our MFSD6-L3:EV-D68 complex is not sufficient to define the exact amino acid residues with the glycans. Also, the MFSD6-L3 peptide does not form any higher-order structures in our previous complex structure analysis, which is consistent with the results from AlphaFold 3 (AF-Q6ZSS7-F1). Therefore, these results support truncating the MFSD6-L3 peptide to find the minimal peptide that can bind to EV-D68. This would narrow down the search for the polysaccharide structure. To this end, we generated a series of MFSD6-L3 truncations fused with a C-terminal human antibody constant region (Fc) tag and evaluated their binding to EV-D68 using Bio-Layer Interferometry. Owing to the stable binding of MFSD6 and the EV-D68 particles, dissociation did not occur during the BLI dissociation phase, making it impossible to calculate an exact Kd value. Nevertheless, the binding curve enabled the identification of peptides capable of binding to EV-D68. Results showed that the peptides of MFSD6-L3-K153-E285, MFSD6-L3-V160-P252, MFSD6-L3-T170-L242, MFSD6-L3-P180-E232, and MFSD6-L3-R196-L226 retained binding, whereas MFSD6-R196-T216 did not, identifying MFSD6-R196-L226 as the minimal binding peptide (Fig. 2e).

To further characterize the glycosylation of the MFSD6-R196-L226 peptide, we conducted a targeted mass spectrometry analysis. The analysis of MFSD6-L3-R196-L226-Fc revealed N-linked glycosylation at asparagine 207 and O-linked glycosylation (Neu5Ac-Gal-GalNAc) at serine 209, with no other glycosylated residues detected (Fig. 2f, g). To confirm whether these two glycans are the ones that interact with EV-D68, we determined the structure of the EV-D68 in complex with MFSD6-R196-L226. The cryo-EM analysis of the EV-D68:MFSD6-R196-L226 complex shows clear densities for the N200-T208 polypeptide, the N207 glycosylation, and the density in the Neu5Ac-binding site (Fig. 3a). Guided by this density, we modeled the terminal sugars Neu5Ac and Gal into the structure (Fig. 3a, b), suggesting that the O-linked Neu5Ac-Gal-GalNAc on S209 engages the Neu5Ac-binding site. Interestingly, the MFSD6-R196-T216 peptide, which includes S209, cannot directly bind EV-D68 in BLI binding experiments. We speculate this may result from the truncated N-terminus or altered glycosylation. Next, we treated the MFSD6-R196-L226 peptide with sialidase and incubated it with EV-D68 for structural analysis. However, given that full-length MFSD6-L3-Fc harbors multiple terminal Neu5Acα2-3Gal glycan epitopes (Fig. 2d), we tested whether glycans from other sites could also occupy the Neu5Ac-binding site. In the S209A mutant in complex with EV-D68 (EV-D68:MFSD6-L3-S209A), cryo-EM still exhibited Neu5Ac-binding site density, indicating alternative glycosylation sites can contribute. The binding of Neu5Ac in MFSD6 to EV-D68 causes the ejection of the pocket factor

Neu5Acα2-3Gal binds to the canyon region on the left rim of another capsid protomer (Fig. 3a). The contacting areas on VP1 and VP3 that interact with Neu5Acα2-3Gal are 145 Å² and 219 Å², respectively. Neu5Ac in Neu5Acα2-3Gal forms strong interactions with EV-D68 via hydrogen bonds with the side chains of VP1N275 and VP3R104, and the main chain atoms of VP3P231 (Fig. 3b). Upon binding with the Neu5Ac in MFSD6-R196-L226, VP1N212-L220 in the VP1 G-H loop and βH, as well as VP1W93-N96 around the C-terminus of βC, move towards the hydrophobic pocket in VP1, with a maximum distance of ~2 Å (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Specifically, the residue VP1I217 in the G-H loop moves into the pocket (Supplementary Fig. 7b). This conformational change narrows the hydrophobic pocket and expels the pocket factor SPH.

To further explore the function of Neu5Ac on MFSD6, we analyzed the structure of EV-D68 in complex with MFSD6-R196-L226 after sialidase treatment (EV-D68:MFSD6-R196-L226-NAtreat). We found that when the MFSD6 N200–V208 peptide harboring glycosylated N207 binds to one capsomer, the density corresponding to Neu5Acα2-3Gal at the Neu5Ac-binding site was absent. However, the density of the pocket factor SPH remained visible (Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Fig. 7c). This indicates that loss of Neu5Ac in MFSD6-R196–L226 impairs pocket factor egress. We subsequently assessed EV-D68 attachment and entry using sialidase-treated A549 cells. The results confirmed that sialidase treatment substantially reduced EV-D68 attachment and entry for both Fermon and MO strains (Fig. 3d, e). This highlights the critical role of Neu5Ac in MFSD6 during viral infection.

To further validate the generality of our findings, we examined an independent EV-D68 epidemic strain, Beijing-R0132 (GenBank accession no. KP240936), which was isolated from a patient with severe pneumonia in Beijing. We assessed the inhibitory activity of a panel of MFSD6-Fc mutant proteins against EV-D68 infection in biologically relevant primary human bronchial epithelial cells (HBECs). Consistent with our observations for the EV-D68 prototype strain Fermon and the North American epidemic strain MO, MFSD6-Fc variants carrying the N200A, L201A, L202A, E203A, N207A, or S209A mutations completely lost their neutralizing activity against the Beijing-R0132 strain. These results mirror the functional phenotypes observed in entry assays and are in strong agreement with the structural insights derived from the EV-D68-MFSD6 complex structures (Fig. 5d). Collectively, these data establish that residues N200, L201, L202, E203, and N207 within MFSD6 are indispensable for efficient EV-D68 infection, whereas R205 is largely dispensable.

In conclusion, our mutation experiments highlight the importance of the role of N207 glycosylation in viral attachment and entry and key interaction residues between MFSD6 and EV-D68.

Glycosylation-dependent infection across epidemic EV-D68 strains

To specifically assess the functional importance of glycosylation at positions N207 and S209 in MFSD6, we generated stable Vero cell lines, which are non-permissive for EV-D68 entry, expressing wild-type MFSD6 or MFSD6 variants carrying the R205A, N207A, S209A, or N207A/S209A mutations. We then evaluated the ability of these MFSD6 variants to mediate viral entry using the AFM-associated EV-D68 MO strain. Mutation of either N207 or S209 markedly impaired MFSD6-mediated viral entry, and the combined N207A/S209A mutation resulted in a similarly reduced entry efficiency. In contrast, the R205A mutant retained entry activity comparable to that of wild-type MFSD6, serving as a control mutation with minimal impact on viral entry (Fig. 5e).

Similarly, we examined the Asian EV-D68 epidemic strain Beijing-R0132 and evaluated the inhibitory activity of MFSD6-Fc variants carrying the R205A, N207A, or S209A mutations in physiologically relevant airway organoid models. In contrast to wild-type MFSD6-Fc and the R205A control mutant, MFSD6-Fc proteins harboring the N207A or S209A mutations exhibited a markedly reduced protective effect against EV-D68 infection (Fig. 5f). Notably, this phenotype is fully consistent with the impaired entry observed for the North American epidemic strain MO in cell-based entry assays.

These results are consistent with our structural and biochemical data. The S209A substitution not only eliminates the Neu5Ac moiety at this position, but also disrupts the canonical N-linked glycosylation sequon at N207 (N207-X208-S209). Although Neu5Ac residues on other sites could, in principle, engage the same pocket, loss of S209 is expected to abolish N207 glycosylation, resulting in a combined defect (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Consistent with this interpretation, S209A causes a more pronounced reduction in both MFSD6-mediated viral entry and the neutralizing activity of MFSD6-Fc (Figs. 5d-5f). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MFSD6 glycosylation is functionally required for efficient MFSD6-dependent EV-D68 entry and infection.

We next determined the structures of MO and KY in complex with human MFSD6-L3. In both cases, MFSD6-L3 engages the viral capsid through a conserved binding mode. On one capsomer, MFSD6-L3 interacts via a peptide-mediated interface spanning residues N200-V208, including contributions from the N207-linked glycan. Notably, on the adjacent capsomer, we observed a second, cooperative interaction mediated by the terminal sialic acid (Neu5Ac), consistent with the binding geometry and molecular contacts previously observed for the Fermon strain (Fig. 6b).

Importantly, MFSD6 engagement in both MO and KY induces displacement of the pocket factor from the VP1 pocket, mirroring the receptor-triggered conformational changes seen in the historical strain (Fig. 6b). Together, these data demonstrate that the MFSD6 recognition model and the associated pocket factor egress are highly conserved across EV-D68 strains spanning distinct temporal origins and pathogenic profiles. This conservation indicates that the MFSD6-sialic acid-dependent ‘binary gating’ mechanism represents a general feature of EV-D68 entry rather than a strain-specific adaptation.

Discussion

Our study provides a detailed report on the structure of mature EV-D68 virion apo and in complex with the respiratory receptor MFSD6. Our apo reconstructions of EV-D68 mature virions contain a well-defined pocket factor consistent with a native infectious state. We found that the pocket factor in mature EV-D68 virion differs from previously reported structures (PDB: 4WM8)19 but is similar to the SPH molecule described in poliovirus and EV-A7120,21. However, in a recent study17, the particle termed ‘mature’ lacks a discernible pocket factor and exhibits inward movement of the VP1 G-H loop with residue VP1I217 towards the pocket (Supplementary Fig. 8a). These features are consistent with a pocket-factor-depleted or receptor-primed capsid state, suggesting that the two datasets may capture distinct particle states or preparation histories. Importantly, beyond the peptide-mediated engagement reported previously, our maps resolve an additional Neu5Ac-terminated glycan density at a neighbouring protomer, providing a structural basis for a dual engagement checkpoint coupled to pocket-factor ejection (Supplementary Fig. 8b). This interpretation is further supported by our strain-independent structures and functional data, which collectively argue for a conserved mechanism rather than a strain-restricted phenomenon (Fig. 6).

Our study elucidates the structural basis for respiratory receptor MFSD6 usage by EV-D68. By integrating peptide- and glycan-mediated receptor interactions into a unified model, our study provides a mechanistic framework linking MFSD6 engagement to capsid priming and host-cell entry. This dual-checkpoint mechanism may help explain the tissue tropism of EV-D68 and suggests that subtle variations in receptor glycosylation could modulate viral infectivity and pathogenesis.

In the Picornaviridae family, receptor engagement occurs through diverse structural modes that reflect differences in capsid architecture, receptor type and viral entry mechanism23,24. Several enteroviruses and rhinoviruses use canyon or canyon-proximal regions for receptor binding, including interactions with carbohydrate receptors such as sialic acid in EV-D68, immunoglobulin-like receptors such as PVR/CD155 for poliovirus, ICAM-1 for major-group rhinoviruses and CAR for coxsackievirus B, as well as SCARB2 for EV-A7125–28 (Fig. 7a–d). Other picornaviruses use distinct receptor-binding topologies: low-density lipoprotein receptor family members bind minor-group rhinoviruses near the five-fold apex, whereas aphthoviruses such as foot-and-mouth disease virus lack a canonical canyon and engage integrin receptors through exposed surface loops, including the VP1 G-H loop.Fig. 7Comparison of EV-D68:MFSD6-L3-Fc binding mode with other Picornaviruses.Asymmetric units of virus-receptor for structural comparison. Against this background, the MFSD6-mediated recognition mode described here represents a distinct dual-contact mechanism. The N200-V208 segment of MFSD6 engages one capsomer, whereas a sialyl-Gal-terminated glycan contacts an adjacent capsomer. This coordinated engagement across neighbouring capsomers provides a structural basis for receptor-triggered pocket factor release and capsid priming.

The unique ‘binary gating switch’ binding mode and ‘glycan-mediated dual-lock’ mechanism for respiratory cell entry observed in EV-D68 may represent an evolutionarily advantageous strategy for enhancing infection specificity and efficiency. This ‘binary gating switch’ binding mode could provide a higher degree of specificity in receptor recognition, reducing the likelihood of premature uncoating and ensuring that the virus only initiates infection when bound to the correct host receptor. It would be of interest to investigate whether a similar binding mode exists in other picornaviruses, as this could represent a conserved or convergently evolved mechanism for optimizing host-cell interaction.

The resurgence of EV-D68 in recent years, associated with acute flaccid myelitis (AFM), underscores the need for effective preventive and therapeutic strategies1,2. Understanding the molecular details of EV-D68’s entry mechanism enhances our capacity to develop targeted antiviral agents and vaccines. The glycan at N207 and the sialyl-Gal-terminated glycan on MFSD6 are critical for EV-D68 binding and entry, which could be targeted by glycan analogs that mimic the natural receptors, potentially blocking viral attachment and entry. Our findings provide a blueprint for structure-guided antivirals.

Methods

Cell lines

Human lung carcinoma A549 cells (CRM-CCL-185, ATCC), human rhabdomyosarcoma RD cells (CCL-136, ATCC), human embryonic kidney 293 T cells (CRL-3216, ATCC), and African green monkey kidney Vero cells (CCL-81, ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; PM150210, Pricella) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin solution. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

For stable MFSD6-expressing lines, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with pCDH-MFSD6 (wild-type or mutant), pRSV-Rev, pMDLg/pRRE, and pCMV-VSV-G. The supernatants of transfected HEK293T cells were collected after 48 h, clarified by centrifugation (3000 × g, 10 min), and passed through a 0.45 μm filter. The harvested lentiviral particles were then pelleted through a 20% sucrose cushion in an SW41 Ti rotor at 134,000 × g for 2 h. The concentrated lentiviral particles were incubated with Vero cells for another 48 h before being selected with 3 μg/mL puromycin.

EV-D68 purification

EV-D68 prototype Fermon (VR-1826, ATCC), the EV-D68 circulating strain from the 2014 U.S. outbreak, US/MO/14-18947 (VR-1823, ATCC), and the EV-D68 epidemic strain isolated from a patient with severe pneumonia in Beijing, Beijing-R0132 (kindly provided by Dr. Zhenglun Liang) were propagated in RD cells. At 3 days post-infection (dpi), cells and supernatant were collected and clarified by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min. The clarified supernatant was treated with DNase I (10 μg/ml), RNase A (7.5 μg/ml), and 10 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), then layered onto a 20%(w/v) sucrose cushion and ultracentrifuged at 174,500 × g (SW32 Ti rotor) for 2 h to pellet crude virions. Resuspended crude virus was applied to a 10–35% (w/v) potassium tartrate gradient, which was ultracentrifuged at 221,000 × g (SW41 Ti rotor) for 2 h. The virus band was buffer-exchanged into PBS, concentrated with an Amicon Ultra-4 300-kDa cut-off centrifugal concentrator (Millipore, USA), and stored at 4 °C.

Preparation of the recombinant MFSD6-L3 protein

DNA fragments encoding the second extracellular loop (L3, residues 153-285) of human MFSD6 (GenBank NM_017694.4) and serial MFSD6-L3 truncations (MFSD6-L3-K153-E285, MFSD6-L3-V160-P252, MFSD6-L3-T170-L242, MFSD6-L3-P180-E232, MFSD6-L3-R196-L226, and MFSD6-L3-R196-T216) were codon-optimized and commercially synthesized (SupraGene, China). Each fragment was cloned in-frame downstream of the native signal peptide and upstream of the human IgG1-Fc tag in pcDNA3.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HEK293F cells (500 ml culture) were transfected with a mixture containing 1 mg of plasmids and 3 mg PEI at a cell density of 2.5 ×106 cells/ml. The supernatant was collected 72 h after transfection, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min, and sterile-filtered using a 0.45 μm filter to remove the cell debris and purified using protein A Sepharose 4B (GenScript, USA). After elution, the target protein was concentrated and purified by Superdex-200 size exclusion chromatography column (GE Healthcare, USA) in PBS. Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE analysis. The plasmids of MFSD6 mutants were constructed via site-directed mutagenesis, with each of the identified residues involved in binding with EV-D68 mutated separately.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

The EV-D68 Fermon was incubated with MFSD6 for 30 minutes on ice. Subsequently, the samples (4 µL) in the buffer (PBS, pH 7.4) were applied onto a H2/O2 glow-discharged, 300-mesh Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 holey gold grids (Quantifoil, Micro Tools GmbH, Germany). The grid was then blotted for 5.0 s with a blot force of 0 at 8 °C and 100% humidity and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cryo-EM data for EV-D68 mature virion, EV-D68 procapsid, EV-D68 in complex with MFSD6-L3, EV-D68-B-particle:MFSD6-L3, and procapsid in EV-D68:MFSD6-L3 were collected using a 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a Gatan K3. The images were recorded at 81,000× magnification with a super-resolution pixel size of 1.055 Å/pixel. The exposure time was set to 2.7 s, delivering a total accumulated dose of 50 electrons per Å². Movies were automatically captured using the EPU software, with a defocus range from -2.0 μm to -1.0 μm. For EV-D68 in complex with MFSD6-R196-L226, MFSD6-L3-S209A, MFSD6-R196-L226-NAtreat, and MFSD6-L3-N207A, data were collected on the 300 kV Titan Krios microscope, equipped with a TFS FALCON 4i. The images were recorded at 130,000× magnification with a super-resolution pixel size of 0.931 Å/pixel. The exposure time was set to 5.68 s with a total accumulated dose of 50 electrons per Å2. Statistics for data collection and refinement are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Cryo-EM data processing

All dose-fractioned images were motion-corrected and dose-weighted by patch motion correction, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) of each micrograph was estimated by patch CTF estimation in cryoSPARC29. Particles from high-quality 2D classes were used for training in Topaz. After Topaz extraction and 2D classification, good particles were selected for subsequent reconstruction. Ab Initio Reconstruction was performed on all particles to generate three volumes, which were then refined through Heterogeneous Refinement and non-uniform refinement with I1 symmetry. Block-based reconstruction was then applied around the five-fold axes to improve the local density of receptor-associated features, glycan densities and pocket-factor-related conformational changes30. After defocus correction, 3D classification (with C1 symmetry) without alignment was performed in relion3.1.231. Good classes were selected for local refinement with C5 symmetry in cryoSPARC, and local resolutions were estimated in cryoSPARC (Supplementary Fig. 1 and 2, Supplementary Table 1).

Model building and refinement

To build the structures of EV-D68 and its complex with MFSD6 peptides fused to Fc, the coordinates of PDB:4WM819 were individually placed and fitted by rigid-body refinement into the cryo-EM maps in UCSF Chimera32. The models were rebuilt using Coot33 with the guidance of the cryo-EM maps and were refined with real space refinement using Phenix34. The data validation statistics are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Structural figures were generated with Pymol35, UCSF Chimera, and ChimeraX36. Amino acid residues of EV-D68 capsid proteins are numbered according to the structural numbering convention used for the EV-D68 Fermon virion structure reported by Liu et al. (Science, 2015; PDB: 4WM8), to facilitate direct comparison with previous EV-D68 structural studies.

Sample preparation for proteomic analysis

For comprehensive digestion, 150 μg of target proteins were subjected to buffer exchange with either 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC, pH 8.0) or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.8) using an Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filter unit (3 kDa molecular weight cutoff). For samples resuspended in 300 μL of 50 mM ABC, proteins were first digested with 5 μg of sequencing-grade Trypsin/LysC (Promega) at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by the addition of 5 μg of Glu-C endopeptidase and continued incubation at 37 °C for another 12–16 h. In contrast, proteins resuspended in 300 μL of PBS were digested with 5 μg of Glu-C endopeptidase alone, with incubation at 37 °C for 16–20 h.

Peptides obtained in the PBS system were desalted using Pierce™ C18 Tips (100 μL bed volume, Thermo Scientific™) by centrifugation and subsequently dried using a SpeedVac concentrator. Peptides from the ABC system were directly dried without desalting.

LC-MS/MS analysis

All samples were analyzed using an Easy-nLC 1000 nanoflow LC system, which was connected with an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer with an EASY-Spray ionization source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Survey scans of precursors were collected in the Orbitrap from 350-2000 Th, with the resolution of 120,000 at 200 m/z. Monoisotopic precursor selection was enabled for peptide isotopic distributions, precursors of z = 2–8 were selected for data-dependent MS/MS scans for 3 seconds of cycle time, and dynamic exclusion was set to 15 seconds with a ± 10 ppm window set around the precursor monoisotope. Higher-energy collisional dissociation with stepped collision energy (sceHCD), followed by product-dependent electron-transfer/higher-energy collisional dissociation (sceHCD-pd-EThcD), were used for MS/MS fragmentation. The initial data-dependent MS/MS scan was acquired using HCD with the first mass of 120 Th, a NCE of 30 ± 10, and a resolution of 30000 at 200 m/z. ETD fragmentation was triggered by glycan oxonium fragments. Upon detection of any of the glycan oxonium ions (i.e, m/z of 168.0654, 186.076, 204.0865, 300.1302, 366.1395, 388.1469, et al.) was detected in the sce-HCD spectrum within 10 ppm mass accuracy, additional precursor isolation and EThcD acquisition were performed, with supplemental activation of 35. For the sceHCD method, the data-dependent MS/MS scan was only acquired using HCD with the first mass of 100 Th, a NCE of 30 ± 10, and a resolution of 30000 at 200 m/z.

MS Data analysis

The MS raw data were processed using pGlyco3 with the “HCD+EThcD” mode (https://github.com/pFindStudio/pGlyco3/releases/tag/pGlyco3.1). MS/MS spectra were searched against the FASTA files of the MFSD6-L3-Fc (WT), MFSD6-L3-N207A-Fc, and MFSD6-R196-L226-Fc. The N-glycans were searched against pGlyco-N-Human-multi, and O- glycans against Multi-Site-O-Glycan. Check glycan legality was enabled. The parameter precursor tolerance was set to ± 10 ppm and fragment tolerance ± 20 ppm. Other parameters were at default values37.

Bio-layer interferometry (BLI) analysis

BLI analyses were performed by the ForteBio Octet Red system (ForteBio Inc., USA). The purified MFSD6-L3 proteins (100 nM) were immobilized onto the AHC biosensors (Sartorius 18-5060) in 200 μl loading buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% BSA, pH 7.5) for 100 s. Coated sensor tips were dipped into the kinetic buffer for a baseline measurement of 100 s, then dipped into wells containing the various concentrations of EV-D68 for 480 s. Subsequently, a buffer wash of 300 s was applied to allow the dissociation of molecules from the sensor. Data analysis was performed with the software Octet (v.6.4, ForteBio). Two independent experiments were conducted for each sample.

Viral attachment and entry assay

A549 cells were pre-chilled on ice or pre-warmed to 37 °C, then incubated with EV-D68 (MOI = 2) for 2 h at the indicated temperature. After extensive washing with ice-cold or warm PBS, total RNA was extracted and viral RNA levels were quantified by RT-qPCR as described above.

Real-time quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and reverse-transcribed with Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen Biotech). PCR amplification was performed on a SLAN-96S real-time PCR system (Hongshi, China) with Hieff qPCR SYBR Green MasterMix (11201ES03, Yeasen Biotech) and the following primers:

GAPDH-F, 5′-CCCACTCCTCCACCTTTGACG-3′;

GAPDH-R, 5′-CACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCA-3′;

EV-D68-F, 5′-ACATGGTGTGAAGAGTCTATTGAGCT-3′;

EV-D68-R, 5′-CCAAAGTAGTCGGTTCCGC-3′.

Cycling conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s and 72 °C for 20 s. Melt-curve analysis confirmed single amplicons. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the reference gene.

Viral titer assay

Viral titers were determined by the appearance of CPEs in A549 cells using microtitration analysis according to the Reed-Muench method. Briefly, A549 cells were cultured under standard conditions in 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well. EV-D68 was serially diluted (10-fold) with DMEM containing 2% FBS and added to the cells. Viral titers are expressed as the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50).

Immunoblotting

Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5 mM EDTA, supplemented with protease inhibitors) for 30 min on ice. The samples were mixed with 1X loading buffer (0.08 M Tris, pH 6.8, with 2.0% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.1 M DTT, and 0.2% bromophenol blue) and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min. The cell lysates were separated via SDS‒PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes via a semidry apparatus (Bio‒Rad). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat powdered milk for 20 min. The nitrocellulose membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against EV-D68 VP1 (GeneTex, GTX132313, rabbit polyclonal antibody) and HSP90β (Starter Bio, S0B0073, recombinant rabbit monoclonal antibody, clone SDT-359-59). The secondary antibody used for immunoblotting was alkaline phosphatase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, catalog no. 111-035-045). Staining was conducted using 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate and nitrotetrazolium blue chloride solutions, which were prepared from chemicals obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Establishment and differentiation of airway organoids

Human airway organoids were generated with the PneumaCult Airway Organoid Kit (05060, STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, HBECs were seeded in a Matrigel dome and cultured for one week in PneumaCult Airway Organoid Seeding Medium. The medium was changed every 2 days by carefully aspirating the medium and adding 750 µl of Airway Organoid Seeding Medium. The organoids were subsequently cultured for four weeks in PneumaCult Airway Organoid Differentiation Medium.
