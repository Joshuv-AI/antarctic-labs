Abstract
. We investigate barycenters of Gaussian process laws in adapted Wasserstein space. The adapted Wasserstein distance refines classical optimal transport by enforcing compatibility of transport plans with the temporal flow of information, and is therefore well suited for stochastic systems with filtration constraints, as common in stochastic control, mathematical finance and sequential decision problems. Within this framework, we consider weighted Fréchet means of Gaussian process laws and prove that the associated barycenter problem admits Gaussian solutions. We derive a characterization of adapted Wasserstein barycenters in terms of the means and covariance operators of the underlying processes, and we analyze their existence, uniqueness, and regularity properties under natural assumptions. The Gaussian setting reveals a tractable and structurally rich subclass of adapted transport problems, bridging adapted optimal transport and Bures–Wasserstein geometry. Our results identify adapted Wasserstein barycenters as natural representatives of collections of Gaussian models and suggest new applications in stochastic optimization, robust finance, and sequential statistics.



1.
Introduction

In recent years, optimal transport has become a central tool for comparing probability measures and for extracting representative distributions from heterogeneous data. One of the most popular ways to construct such representative distributions are the so-called
Wasserstein barycenters
, which provide a notion of average measure that respects the underlying geometry of the Wasserstein space of distributions. Since the landmark work
[
4
]
of Agueh and Carlier, Wasserstein barycenters have found applications in statistics, machine learning, imaging, economics, and uncertainty quantification. An incomplete list is given by
[
37
,
21
,
32
,
33
,
7
,
19
,
14
,
16
,
15
]
; see also
[
34
,
31
,
24
,
20
]
and the references therein for an overview.

In many applications however, the objects of interest are not static distributions but stochastic processes or filtered probability laws, and in such settings the classical Wasserstein framework ignores a key structural feature: time and information. This limitation is particularly important when one compares laws on path space under adaptedness constraints. In stochastic control, mathematical finance, and sequential decision problems, couplings between random evolutions should respect the filtration, so that decisions at a given time can depend only on past and present information. The
adapted Wasserstein distance
was introduced in
[
5
,
27
,
26
,
35
,
8
,
29
]
precisely to capture this temporal structure. Unlike the classical Wasserstein distance, it penalizes discrepancies between stochastic processes while enforcing a dynamic consistency condition on transport plans. As a result, it is the natural metric for problems involving nested information, causal transport, and stability of stochastic systems
[
9
,
11
]
. In this vein,
adapted barycenters
may be viewed as dynamic analogues of Fréchet means, but in a geometry shaped not only by state discrepancy but also by the admissible flow of information.

Adapted barycenters space are also of practical interest. They provide a principled way to average stochastic models while preserving temporal information, and thus offer natural candidates for representative models in applications where the timing of information is essential. This is relevant, for example, in scenario reduction for multi-stage stochastic optimization, in model aggregation for time-series distributions, and in robust mathematical finance, where one seeks central objects among competing adapted models.

This paper offers the first results on adapted Wasserstein barycenters of Gaussian processes. We focus here on discrete time: given a family of filtered Gaussian probability measures, we ask whether there exists a measure that minimizes the weighted sum of squared adapted
2
-Wasserstein distances to the given marginal distributions, whether such minimizers are unique, and how their structural properties differ from those of classical Wasserstein barycenters.
While such properties have been extensively studied for the classical Wasserstein distance as mentioned above, the adapted setting is intrinsically non-symmetric in time, is sensitive to the filtration carried by the laws, and typically lacks a number of tools that are available in the standard optimal transport framework. In particular, the interaction between temporal constraints and convexity properties of the transport functional creates new analytical challenges.

Our analysis builds on the geometry of the adapted Bures–Wasserstein space recently established in
[
2
,
25
,
1
]
. Our main results can be summarized as follows: We show in Theorem
4.1
that adapted
2
-Wasserstein barycenters of Gaussian processes exist and that they agree with adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenters, via the multicausal reformulation of
[
3
]
and backward induction arguments similar to
[
25
]
. In Theorem
4.4
we establish uniqueness of adapted Wasserstein barycenters between laws of Gaussian processes. The proof of Theorem
4.4
builds on a decomposition of the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance into a sum of standard Bures–Wasserstein distances of modified covariance matrices; see Proposition
4.2
. This result is interesting in its own right, as it offers a natural isometry between the adapted Bures–Wasserstein space and a product of standard Bures–Wasserstein spaces; in particular, the adapted barycenter problem is equivalent to
T
independent Bures–Wasserstein barycenter problems (where
T
is the discrete time horizon). Once this isometry is established, uniqueness follows from classical results for Wasserstein barycenters between Gaussians. Next, we give a first-order characterization of the adapted Wasserstein barycenter via a fixed-point equation in Theorem
4.6
. We explain that this characterization recovers the classical case for
T=1
already established in
[
4
]
and how it can be used to determine the adapted Waserstein barycenter numerically, similarly to
[
7
,
36
]
. Our last result, Theorem
4.9
, establishes regularity of the adapted Wasserstein barycenter of regular processes, i.e. those processes with positive definite covariance matrix.

In Section

2.
Statement of the main results

Before stating our main results, we set up some notation, that is compatible with
[
1
]
. For the remainder of the paper we fix a stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
supporting
T
independent
d
-dimensional standard Gaussians
G_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
for
t=1,\ldots,T
. For
t=1,\ldots,T
denote by
\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\sigma(G_{s}:s\leq t)
the filtration generated by the driving noise. Let
\mathbb{L}
denote the set of block-lower triangular matrices

\mathbb{L}:=\left\{L=\begin{pmatrix}L_{1,1}&0&\cdots&0\\
L_{2,1}&L_{2,2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
L_{T,1}&L_{T,2}&\cdots&L_{T,T}\end{pmatrix}:L_{t,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\right\}.

We may identify the set of Gaussian processes
\mathrm{GP}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t=1}^{T})
with
\mathbb{L}
. Indeed, given
L\in\mathbb{L}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{dT}
, we define a Gaussian process
\mathbb{X}=(X_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
by setting
X_{t}:=a_{t}+(LG)_{t}=a_{t}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}L_{t,s}G_{s}
. Conversely, for every positive semi-definite
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{dT\times dT}
there exists
L\in\mathbb{L}
such that
LG
has covariance
\Sigma
. Two matrices
L,M\in\mathbb{L}
define the same Gaussian law if and only if
L=MO
for some block-diagonal orthogonal matrix
O\in\mathbb{O}:=\{\mathrm{diag}(O_{1},\ldots,O_{T}):O_{t}\in O(d)\}
. We endow
\mathbb{L}
with such equivalence relation and we write
[L]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
for the equivalence class. We say that
L\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
if
(L^{\top}L)_{t,t}
is positive definite for all
t=1,\dots,N
; equivalently, the process
\mathbb{X}=a+LG
has a non-degenerate conditional distribution at each time step.

The adapted Wasserstein distance between Gaussian processes admits an explicit representation in terms of the Cholesky factors, see Section
3
for more details. As shown in
[
1
,
25
]
, for
\mathbb{X}^{1}=a^{1}+L^{1}G
and
\mathbb{X}^{2}=a^{2}+L^{2}G
,

\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\mathbb{X}^{2})=\|a^{1}-a^{2}\|_{2}^{2}+d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L^{1}],[L^{2}]),

where

d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L^{1}],[L^{2}])=\min_{O\in\mathbb{O}}\|L^{1}-L^{2}O\|_{F}^{2}

is the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. Given
N
Gaussian processes
\mathbb{X}^{i}=a^{i}+L^{i}G
and convex weights
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}>0
, i. e.
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1
, the
adapted Wasserstein barycenter problem
asks for solution of

(2.1)

\inf_{\mathbb{X}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}^{i}),

where
\mathrm{FP}_{2}
denotes the set of filtered probability measures with finite second moment. Our main result establishes that this problem is well posed and characterizes its solution.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N}\in\mathrm{GP}
be Gaussian processes with
\mathbb{X}^{i}=a^{i}+L^{i}G
,
a^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{dT}
,
L^{i}\in\mathbb{L}
, and let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}>0
be convex weights. Then we have the following:

(i)

Existence and Gaussianity.

The barycenter problem (
2.1
) admits a minimizer
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}
, and every minimizer is Gaussian. Thus, the problem reduces to

\inf_{\mathbb{X}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}^{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\|a^{*}-a^{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\inf_{[L]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L],[L^{i}]),

where
a^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}a^{i}
.

(ii)

Uniqueness.

The minimizer
[\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
is unique. Moreover, the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance decomposes as

d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L],[M])=\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L},\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}),

where
d_{\mathrm{BW}}
is the classical Bures–Wasserstein distance and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}=\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}
is the column covariance associated with the
t
-th truncated column of
L
(see Section
3.3
for precise definitions). In particular, the barycenter problem decouples into
T
independent classical Bures–Wasserstein barycenter problems.

(iii)

Characterization.

[\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}]
is the minimizer of the adapted barycenter problem if and only if there exist
O_{i}\coloneq O_{i}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L})\in O^{*}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L},L^{i})
for each
i=1,\ldots,N
such that the fixed-point equation

\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}L^{i}O_{i}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L})

holds, where
O^{*}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L},L^{i})=\arg\min_{O\in\mathbb{O}}\|\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}-L^{i}O\|_{F}^{2}
is the set of Procrustes optimizers.

(iv)

Regularity.

If
L^{1},\ldots,L^{N}\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
, then
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is given in Section
4
. Part
(i)
is established in Theorem
4.1
via a multicausal reformulation and backward induction. Part
(ii)
follows from the column decomposition of Proposition
4.2
combined with the classical uniqueness result of
[
4
]
; see Theorem
4.4
. Part
(iii)
is proved in Theorem
4.6
by exploiting the alternating minimization structure of the objective. Finally, part
(iv)
is shown in Theorem
4.9
via a perturbation argument.

2.1.
Open questions

We conclude this section with open questions that arise in view of our
results:

(1)

Non-Gaussian barycenters.
Our results establish existence, uniqueness, and a characterization of adapted Wasserstein barycenters for Gaussian processes, relying crucially on the explicit reduction to the finite-dimensional adapted Bures–Wasserstein problem. It is natural to ask whether analogous results hold for broader classes of processes. The multicausal reformulation of Theorem
4.1
does not require Gaussianity, and the existence of adapted barycenters in
\mathrm{FP}_{2}
is guaranteed by
[
3
, Theorem 4.5]
under general assumptions. However, uniqueness and a structural characterization beyond the Gaussian case remain open. Natural candidates for further investigation would be Markov processes, where the conditional structure is sufficiently tractable to exploit the dynamic programming principle (
3.2
), yet rich enough to go beyond the Gaussian setting.

(2)

Continuous-time barycenters.
All results in this paper are formulated in discrete time. Extending the theory to continuous-time processes, such as diffusions driven by Brownian motion, is of significant interest for applications in mathematical finance and stochastic control. The continuous-time adapted Wasserstein distance has been studied in
[
10
]
, but the barycenter problem in that setting is open, to the best of our knowledge. Already in the Gaussian case, the question of whether the adapted barycenter of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes is itself a diffusion, and how the fixed-point equation (
4.11
) extends to a continuous-time analogue involving operator-valued equations, appears nontrivial to us.

(3)

Geodesic structure and the column decomposition.

Thanks to Proposition
4.2
we may establish an isometric decomposition of
(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O},d_{\mathrm{ABW}})
into a product of classical Bures–Wasserstein spaces
(\mathbb{S}_{+}^{(T-t+1)d},d_{\mathrm{BW}})
for
t=1,\ldots,T
, where
\mathbb{S}^{m}_{+}
denotes the set of symmetric positive definite matrices of size
m
. As shown in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, this isometry is genuine at the level of both the metric and the equivalence relation, since two matrices
L,M\in\mathbb{L}
represent the same class in
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
if and only if they define the same covariance matrix
\tilde{\Sigma}^{L}_{t}=\tilde{\Sigma}^{M}_{t}
for all
t
. A natural question is therefore how this product structure interacts with the fine geometric theory of
(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O},d_{\mathrm{ABW}})
developed in
[
1
]
. As observed in Remark
4.5
, several geometric consequences follow readily from the product structure; whether finer structures such as geodesics and the logarithmic map can be fully recovered from it remains open.

(4)

Entropic adapted barycenters.
The recent work
[
2
]
studies entropic regularization of the adapted Wasserstein distance between Gaussian processes. A natural extension of our results is to define and study
entropic adapted barycenters
, i.e. Fréchet means with respect to the entropic adapted Wasserstein distance. It would be interesting to investigate whether these regularized barycenters admit closed-form characterizations analogous to the fixed-point equation (
4.11
), whether uniqueness holds under weaker assumptions due to the strict convexity introduced by the entropic penalty, and how the entropic barycenter converges to the adapted Wasserstein barycenter as the regularization parameter vanishes.

(5)

Robust stress testing of pricing models.
In financial risk management, stress testing requires constructing plausible adverse scenarios that are consistent with a collection of reference pricing models. The adapted Wasserstein barycenter provides a natural candidate for a central scenario that respects the temporal flow of information inherent in financial models. More precisely, given a set of competing stochastic models for asset prices or risk factors, the adapted barycenter aggregates them into a single representative model while preserving the filtration structure, which is essential for hedging and dynamic trading strategies. It would be interesting to investigate how adapted barycenters can be used to define stress scenarios as perturbations of the central model within an adapted Wasserstein ball, extending the distributionally robust approach of
[
9
,
12
]
to the multi-model setting. The Gaussian framework developed in this paper offers a computationally tractable starting point for such applications, and the fixed-point iteration of Algorithm
1
provides a concrete tool for computing these central scenarios in practice.

3.
Notation and preliminary results

In this section we collect the notation and background material used in the proofs of Section
4
. The Gaussian process parametrization and the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance were introduced in Section
2
. Here, we recall the adapted Wasserstein distance for general filtered processes, introduce the column decomposition of the Cholesky factor, and review the multicausal reformulation of the barycenter problem.

3.1.
Adapted Wasserstein distance

We recall the adapted Wasserstein distance following the framework of
[
11
,
3
]
.

A (discrete-time)
filtered process
is a five-tuple
\mathbb{X}=(\Omega^{\mathbb{X}},\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{X}},X)
, where
(\Omega^{\mathbb{X}},\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{X}})
is a probability space,
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{X}}=(\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
is a filtration, and
X=(X_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
is an
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{X}}
-adapted process taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We denote by
\mathrm{FP}_{2}
the set of equivalence classes of filtered processes with finite second moment, where the equivalence relation identifies processes that have the same adapted distributions in the sense of Hoover and Keisler
[
27
]
; or equivalently that have zero adapted Wasserstein distance, see
[
11
, Theorem 1.5]
.

Given two filtered processes
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}
, we write
\mathrm{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
for the set of couplings on the product space
(\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}},\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}})
with marginals
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{X}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{Y}}
. A coupling
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
is called
bicausal
if, for each
t=1,\ldots,T
, the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{T}
is conditionally independent of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}
under
\pi
, and symmetrically
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{T}
is conditionally independent of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}
under
\pi
; see
[
11
, Definition 2.1]
. We write
\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
for the set of bicausal couplings.

The
adapted
2
-Wasserstein distance
is defined as

\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):=\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})}\int\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|X_{t}-Y_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\,d\pi.

By
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
,
\mathcal{AW}_{2}
is a metric on
\mathrm{FP}_{2}
and we call
(\mathrm{FP}_{2},\mathcal{AW}_{2})
the
Wasserstein space of (discrete-time) stochastic processes
.

When
\mathbb{X}^{1}
and
\mathbb{X}^{2}
are Gaussian processes with
\mathbb{X}^{j}=a^{j}+L^{j}G
for
L^{j}\in\mathbb{L}
,
j=1,2
, the adapted Wasserstein distance admits the closed-form representation

\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\mathbb{X}^{2})=\|a^{1}-a^{2}\|_{2}^{2}+d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L^{1}],[L^{2}]),

where

d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L^{1}],[L^{2}])=\min_{O\in\mathbb{O}}\|L^{1}-L^{2}O\|_{F}^{2}=\|L^{1}\|_{F}^{2}+\|L^{2}\|_{F}^{2}-2\,\mathrm{tr}(S)

is the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance,
\mathbb{O}=\{\mathrm{diag}(O_{1},\ldots,O_{T}):O_{t}\in O(d)\}
is the set of block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, and
S=\mathrm{diag}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{T})
with
S_{t}
the singular value matrix of
((L^{2})^{\top}L^{1})_{t,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, see
[
1
,
25
]
.

3.2.
Multicausal optimal transport

A key ingredient in the proof of existence (Theorem
4.1
) is the multicausal reformulation of the barycenter problem. We briefly recall the necessary notions; see
[
3
]
for a comprehensive treatment.

Given
N
filtered processes
\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}
, we write
\mathrm{Cpl}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N})
for the set of couplings on the product space
(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Omega^{i},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{F}^{i})
with marginals
\mathbb{P}^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, and we denote by
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t}
the product filtration at time
t
. A coupling
\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N})
is called
multicausal
if, for each
i=1,\ldots,N
and each
t=1,\ldots,T
, the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{T}
is conditionally independent of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t}
under
\pi
; see
[
3
, Definition 2.4]
. We denote the set of multicausal couplings by
\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{mc}}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N})
. For
N=2
, multicausality reduces to bicausality. We assume throughout that
\Omega^{i}=\Omega^{i}_{1:T}:=\Omega^{i}_{1}\times\cdots\times\Omega^{i}_{T}
for some measurable spaces
(\Omega^{i}_{s})_{s=1}^{T}
, so that elements
\omega^{i}\in\Omega^{i}
are identified with paths
\omega^{i}_{1:T}=(\omega^{i}_{1},\ldots,\omega^{i}_{T})
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{i}=\bigotimes_{s=1}^{t}\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{s}^{i})\otimes\bigotimes_{s=t+1}^{T}\{\emptyset,\Omega^{i}\}.

We write
\mathbb{P}^{i}_{t+1,\omega^{i}_{1:t}}
for the regular conditional distribution of
\omega^{i}_{t+1}
given
\omega^{i}_{1:t}:=(\omega^{i}_{1},\ldots,\omega^{i}_{t})
under
\mathbb{P}^{i}
, and
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{1:t}:=(\omega^{1}_{1:t},\ldots,\omega^{N}_{1:t})
for the collection of all paths up to time
t
.

By
[
3
, Example 4.23]
, the barycenter problem (
2.1
) with quadratic cost is equivalent to the multicausal optimal transport problem

(3.1)

\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{\mathrm{mc}}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\left\|X^{i}-\phi^{0}(X^{1},\ldots,X^{N})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

where
\phi^{0}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x^{i}
is the optimal aggregation map. Moreover, by
[
3
, Theorem 4.19]
, any optimizer
\pi^{*}
of (
3.1
) yields a barycenter
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}
whose process is given by
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{X}}}{X}=\phi^{0}(X^{1},\ldots,X^{N})
under
\pi^{*}
. By
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
, the multicausal problem satisfies a dynamic programming principle: defining the value functions backward in time by

(3.2)

V(t,\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{1:t})=\inf\left\{\int V(t+1,\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{1:t},\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{t+1})\,\pi(d\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{t+1})\;\middle|\;\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{t+1,\omega^{1}_{1:t}},\ldots,\mathbb{P}^{N}_{t+1,\omega^{N}_{1:t}})\right\},

with terminal condition
V(T,\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\|X^{i}(\omega^{i})-\phi^{0}(X^{1}(\omega^{1}),\ldots,X^{N}(\omega^{N}))\|_{2}^{2}
.
Solving at each time step (
3.2
) yields an optimal kernel
K^{*}_{t+1}(\omega_{1:t};\cdot)
. Finally, the optimal multicausal coupling is constructed by solving composing the resulting kernels at each time step.

3.3.
Column decomposition

In this subsection we introduce notation that exploits the block-lower triangular structure of
L\in\mathbb{L}
column by column. This decomposition will play a central role in the proof of the uniqueness Theorem
4.4
, where it allows us to reduce the adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter problem to
T
independent classical Bures–Wasserstein barycenter problems.

For
L\in\mathbb{L}
and
t=1,\ldots,T
, we denote by
L_{\bullet,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{dT\times d}
the
t
-th block column of
L
. Since
L
is block-lower triangular, the first
t-1
blocks of
L_{\bullet,t}
vanish, and
L_{\bullet,t}
takes the form

L_{\bullet,t}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
L_{t,t}\\
L_{t+1,t}\\
\vdots\\
L_{T,t}\end{pmatrix}.

Stripping off the zero blocks, we define the
truncated column

\tilde{L}_{t}:=\begin{pmatrix}L_{t,t}\\
L_{t+1,t}\\
\vdots\\
L_{T,t}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T-t+1)d\times d},

so that
L_{\bullet,t}=(0,\ldots,0,\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top})^{\top}
. Probabilistically, the truncated column
\tilde{L}_{t}
encodes how the noise
G_{t}
introduced at time
t
propagates to all subsequent times
t,t+1,\ldots,T
: if
X_{s}=\sum_{r=1}^{s}L_{s,r}G_{r}
, then the contribution of
G_{t}
to the future path
(X_{t},\ldots,X_{T})
is precisely
\tilde{L}_{t}G_{t}
. The associated
column covariance

(3.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}:=\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T-t+1)d\times(T-t+1)d}

is a positive semi-definite matrix of rank at most
d
that captures the covariance structure of this contribution. As we shall see in Proposition
4.2
, the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance between two processes decomposes into the sum of classical Bures–Wasserstein distances between their column covariances, comparing independently at each time step how each noise source propagates forward.

4.
Proofs of the main results

This section is devoted to the proof the main results as stated in Theorem
2.1
.

4.1.
Existence

We first show that the minimizer of the barycenter problem over
\mathrm{FP}_{2}
is Gaussian. The proof combines the multicausal reformulation of the barycenter problem, see
[
3
, Example 4.23]
, with the dynamic programming principle for multicausal transport and the Gaussian structure.

Theorem 4.1

(Existence of the adapted barycenter)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N}\in\mathrm{GP}\subset\mathrm{FP}_{2}
be Gaussian processes with
\mathbb{X}^{i}=a^{i}+L^{i}G
,
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R},L^{i}\in\mathbb{L}
, and let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}>0
be convex weights.
Then, the adapted Wasserstein barycenter problem

\inf_{\mathbb{X}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}^{i})

admits a minimizer
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}
. Moreover, the minimizer
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathrm{GP}
is Gaussian and the barycenter problem reduces to the adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter

(4.1)

\begin{split}\inf_{\mathbb{X}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}^{i})&=\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{dT},[L]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\|a-a^{i}\|^{2}_{2}+d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L],[L^{i}]))\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\|a^{*}-a^{i}\|^{2}_{2}+\inf_{[L]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L],[L^{i}]),\end{split}

where
a^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}a^{i}
is the Euclidean barycenter of the means.

Proof.

We observe that existence of a minimizer for the adapted barycenter problem has already been established in
[
11
, Theorem 6.7]
and
[
3
, Theorem 4.5 and Remark 4.6]
. Moreover, if the adapted barycenter
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}
is a Gaussian process, then we may restrict ourselves to
\mathrm{GP}
, so that the adapted Wasserstein distance is equivalent to the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance.
By
[
1
, Remark 1.2]
, the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance between Gaussian processes decouples so that the barycenter problem reduces to (
4.1
). Thus, it remains to show that the minimizer of the barycenter problem is Gaussian.

Throughout the proof we use results from
[
3
]
. In order to work in their setting, as recalled in Section
3.2
, we take
\Omega^{i}_{t}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{P}^{i}=\text{Law}(G_{1},\dots,G_{T})
as well as
X_{t}^{i}(\omega^{i})=a^{i}+L^{i}\omega^{i}_{t}
for all
i=1,\dots,N
and
t=1,\dots,T
; in other words we identify
\omega_{t}^{i}=G_{t}
through
\mathbb{P}^{i}=\text{Law}(G_{1},\dots,G_{T})
. We note that
X^{i}
is an affine transformation of
\omega^{i}
and sometimes write
(X_{t}^{i})_{\#}\mathbb{P}^{i}
to denote the law of
X_{t}^{i}
under
\mathbb{P}^{i}
. Furthermore, we note that
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{i}
as defined in Section
3.2
might be strictly finer than
\sigma(X_{1}^{i},\dots,X_{t}^{i})
as was remarked in
[
1
, Remark 1.5]
— however this does not affect the arguments below.

Step 1.

Multicausal reformulation
. By
[
3
, Example 4.23]
, the barycenter problem with quadratic cost
c^{i}(x,y)=\lambda_{i}\|x-y\|^{2}_{2}
is equivalent to the multicausal optimal transport problem (
3.1
)

\inf_{\mathbb{X}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X}^{i},\mathbb{X})=\inf_{\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{mc}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\left\|X^{i}-\phi^{0}(X^{1},\ldots,X^{N})\right\|^{2}_{2}\right],

where
\phi^{0}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x^{i}
is the unique optimal aggregation map. Moreover, by
[
3
, Theorem 4.19]
, any optimizer
\pi^{*}
of the right-hand side yields a barycenter
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}
whose process is given by
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{X}}}{X}=\phi^{0}(X^{1},\ldots,X^{N})
under
\pi^{*}
.

Step 2.

Dynamic programming principle
. By
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
, the multicausal problem on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) satisfies a dynamic programming principle. Let
V(T,\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\|X^{i}(\omega^{i})-\phi^{0}(X^{1}(\omega^{1}),\ldots,X^{N}(\omega^{N}))\|^{2}_{2}
, and for
t=T-1,\ldots,1,0
define the value functions backward in time as

V(t,\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{1:t})=\inf\left\{\int V(t+1,\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{t+1})\,\pi(d\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\omega}}}{\omega}_{t+1})\;\middle|\;\pi\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{mc}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{t+1,\omega^{1}_{1:t}},\ldots,\mathbb{P}^{N}_{t+1,\omega^{N}_{1:t}})\right\},

where
\mathbb{P}^{i}_{t+1,\omega^{i}_{1:t}}
denotes the conditional distribution of
\omega^{i}_{t+1}
given
\omega^{i}_{1:t}
under
\mathbb{P}^{i}
. Note that the optimal multicausal coupling
\pi^{*}\in\mathrm{Cpl}_{mc}(\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N})
is constructed by solving (
3.2
) at each time step, applying the pushforward by
X_{t+1}
and composing the resulting kernels
(X_{t+1})_{\#}K_{t+1}^{*}
.

Step 3.

The Gaussian structure propagates backwards in time
. We now make use of the specific structure of the Gaussian inputs. Each
\mathbb{X}^{i}
is driven by
X^{i}(\omega^{i})=a^{i}+L^{i}\omega^{i}
and
\mathbb{P}^{i}=\text{Law}(G)
, where
G=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{T})
are independent standard Gaussians. At each time step
t
, the conditional distributions of
X_{t+1}^{i}
under
\mathbb{P}^{i}_{t+1,\omega^{i}_{1:t}}
are Gaussian. Indeed, by the Cholesky structure,
X^{i}_{t+1}
conditioned on
(X^{i}_{1},\ldots,X^{i}_{t})
has a Gaussian distribution with conditional mean that is linear in
(X^{i}_{1},\ldots,X^{i}_{t})
and conditional variance that does not depend on the conditioning values (see
[
25
, Lemma 4.3]
). Therefore, at each step of (
3.2
), we are solving a multimarginal optimal transport problem between
N
Gaussian
distributions.

Step 4.

The optimal coupling at each step is Gaussian
. We observe that the cost function at each step is quadratic. Indeed,
\phi^{0}
is linear and the terminal cost
V(T,\cdot)
is quadratic in
(X_{1},\dots,X_{T})
, hence, by a simple backward induction argument (see for instance the analogous proof of
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
), the value function
V(t+1,\cdot)
is quadratic in
(X_{1},\dots,X_{t})
.
Since at each time step of (
3.2
) the conditional marginals are Gaussian and the cost is quadratic, the one-step multimarginal problem reduces to an optimization over the correlation structure of a jointly Gaussian vector. Therefore, the optimal coupling at each step is Gaussian.
More precisely, the optimal joint distribution of
(X^{1}_{t+1},\ldots,X^{N}_{t+1})
given
(X^{1}_{1:t}(\omega^{1}_{1:t}),\ldots,X^{N}_{1:t}(\omega^{N}_{1:t}))

is the unique optimizer of a multimarginal quadratic Gaussian transport problem, and is therefore itself Gaussian by the classical theory of optimal transport between Gaussian measures; see e.g.
[
25
, Lemma 4.3]
. In particular, the optimal law
(X_{t+1})_{\#}K^{*}_{t+1}(\omega_{1:t};\cdot)
is Gaussian with mean affine in
(X^{1}_{1:t}(\omega^{1}_{1:t}),\ldots,X^{N}_{1:t}(\omega^{N}_{1:t}))
and covariance independent of
\omega_{1:t}
.

Step 5.

The barycenter is Gaussian
. We show by induction on
t
that
(X^{1}_{1:t}(\omega^{1}_{1:t}),

\ldots,X^{N}_{1:t}(\omega^{N}_{1:t}))
is jointly Gaussian under the optimal multicausal coupling
\pi^{*}
constructed by composing the one-step kernels from Step 4. At time
t=1
, the law
(X_{1})_{\#}K_{1}^{*}\in\mathrm{Cpl}((X^{1}_{1})_{\#}\mathbb{P}^{1}_{1},\ldots,(X^{N}_{1})_{\#}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{1})

is the optimizer of a multimarginal quadratic transport between
N
Gaussian marginals, and is therefore a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
. Now suppose
(X^{1}_{1:t}(\omega^{1}_{1:t}),\ldots,X^{N}_{1:t}(\omega^{N}_{1:t}))
is jointly Gaussian under
\pi^{*}
. By Step 4, the optimal law
(X_{t+1})_{\#}K^{*}_{t+1}(\omega_{1:t};\cdot)
is Gaussian with mean affine in
(X^{1}_{1:t}(\omega^{1}_{1:t}),\ldots,X^{N}_{1:t}(\omega^{N}_{1:t}))
and covariance
\Sigma_{t+1}
independent of
\omega_{1:t}
. A random vector whose conditional distribution given a Gaussian vector has affine mean and fixed covariance is jointly Gaussian with the conditioning vector, hence
(X^{1}_{1:t+1}(\omega^{1}_{1:t+1}),\ldots,X^{N}_{1:t+1}(\omega^{N}_{1:t+1}))
is jointly Gaussian under
\pi^{*}
. By induction,
(X^{1}_{1:T}(\omega^{1}_{1:T}),\ldots,X^{N}_{1:T}(\omega^{1}_{1:T}))=(X^{1},\dots,X^{N})
is jointly Gaussian under
\pi^{*}
. Finally, since
\phi^{0}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x^{i}
is linear, the barycenter process

\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{X}}}{X}=\phi^{0}(X^{1},\ldots,X^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}X^{i}.

is Gaussian as a linear image of a jointly Gaussian vector. Therefore
X\in\mathrm{GP}
.

∎

4.2.
Uniqueness

In this section we show uniqueness of the barycenter problem. Our proof proceeds by observing that the adapted Wasserstein distance between Gaussian processes reduces to the sum of classical Bures-Wasserstein distances between their “time-truncated” covariances. From the classical theory we then infer uniqueness of the minimizer on the modulo space
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For every
L,M\in\mathbb{L}
,

(4.2)

d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}(L,M)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}\bigl(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L},\,\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}\bigr),

where
d_{\mathrm{BW}}
denotes the classical Bures–Wasserstein distance and we recall the definition of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}
given in (
3.3
).

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{O}=\{\mathrm{diag}(O_{1},\ldots,O_{T}):O_{t}\in O(d)\}
is block-diagonal, the Procrustes formula decouples to

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}(L,M)

\displaystyle=\min_{O\in\mathbb{O}}\|L-MO\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{(O_{1},\ldots,O_{T})}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|L_{\bullet,t}-M_{\bullet,t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\|L_{\bullet,t}-M_{\bullet,t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2},

where the last equality holds because
O_{t}
acts only on the
t
-th column, so the minimizations are independent. Since
L_{s,t}=M_{s,t}=0
for
s<t
(block-lower triangularity), the zero rows do not contribute to the Frobenius norm, hence

\|L_{\bullet,t}-M_{\bullet,t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\tilde{L}_{t}-\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

Therefore, recalling the notation introduced in Section
3.3
,

(4.4)

\min_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\|L_{\bullet,t}-M_{\bullet,t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2}=\min_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\|\tilde{L}_{t}-\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

We claim that, for each
t
,

(4.5)

\min_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\|\tilde{L}_{t}-\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2}=d_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L},\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}).

In fact, this is a well-known result, see e.g.
[
30
, Proposition 3]
. We give a self-contained proof here for completeness.

To prove (
4.5
), we expand the left-hand side. Recall that for any matrix
A
, the squared Frobenius norm satisfies
\|A\|_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(A^{\top}A)=\mathrm{tr}(AA^{\top})
, where the second equality follows from the cyclic property of the trace. Therefore:

\displaystyle\min_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\|\tilde{L}_{t}-\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{L}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\tilde{M}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}-2\max_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\langle\tilde{L}_{t},\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}\rangle_{F}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L})+\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})-2\max_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}\cdot O_{t}^{\top}),

where we used again the cyclical property of the trace in the form
\|\tilde{L}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t})=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top})=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L})
.
By the von Neumann trace inequality, the maximum in (
4.6
) is

\max_{O_{t}\in O(d)}\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}\cdot O_{t}^{\top})=\mathrm{tr}(S_{t}),

where
S_{t}
is the diagonal matrix of singular values of
\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

We now compute the Bures–Wasserstein distance. By definition,

(4.7)

d_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L},\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L})+\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})-2\mathrm{tr}\Bigl(\bigl((\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}\,(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr).

It remains to show that

(4.8)

\mathrm{tr}\Bigl(\bigl((\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}\,(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr)=\mathrm{tr}(S_{t}).

To verify (
4.8
), note that
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}=\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}=\tilde{M}_{t}\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}
. The matrix

(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}\,(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}\,(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}

is positive semi-definite of rank at most
d
(in a space of dimension
(T-t+1)d
). Its nonzero eigenvalues coincide with those of

\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}\,(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\tilde{L}_{t}=\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}\tilde{L}_{t}=\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{M}_{t}\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}=(\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t})^{\top}(\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

where we applied the general identity: for any matrices
P\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, the nonzero eigenvalues of
PQ
coincide with those of
QP
, with
P=(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{L}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T-t+1)d\times d}
and
Q=\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(T-t+1)d}
.

The eigenvalues of
(\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t})^{\top}(\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t})
are
\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{d}^{2}
, where
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}
are the singular values of
\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Therefore,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\Bigl(\bigl((\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}\,(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}^{2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{k}=\mathrm{tr}(S_{t}).

Finally, combining (
4.3
), (
4.6
), (
4.7
), and (
4.9
) yields (
4.2
).
∎

Remark 4.3

(Probabilistic Interpretation)
.

The column decomposition (
4.2
) has a natural probabilistic interpretation. The truncated column
\tilde{L}_{t}
encodes how the noise
G_{t}
introduced at time
t
propagates to all subsequent times
t,t+1,\ldots,T
, that is

\text{if }X_{s}=\sum_{r=1}^{s}L_{s,r}G_{r}\text{ then the contribution of }G_{t}\text{ to }(X_{t},\ldots,X_{T})\text{ is }\tilde{L}_{t}G_{t}.

The column covariance
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}=\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}
captures the covariance structure of this contribution. Then the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance compares two processes by comparing, independently at each time step, how each noise source propagates forward.

We now make use of the column decomposition of the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance to show uniqueness of the minimizer of the barycenter problem.

Theorem 4.4

(Uniqueness of the adapted barycenter)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}^{1},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{N}\in\mathrm{GP}\subset\mathrm{FP}_{2}
be Gaussian processes with
\mathbb{X}^{i}=a^{i}+L^{i}G
,
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R},L^{i}\in\mathbb{L}
, and let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}>0
be convex weights.
Then, the adapted Wasserstein barycenter
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}
of

\inf_{\mathbb{X}\in\mathrm{FP}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{AW}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}^{i})

is unique.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
we know that the barycenter problem admits a minimizer and that any minimizer is Gaussian. Since the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance factors, see
[
1
, Lemma 2.8]
, we may assume without loss of generality that
a^{i}=0
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Hence, by Proposition
4.2
, the adapted barycenter problem reduces to
T
independent classical Bures–Wasserstein barycenter problems

(4.10)

\inf_{L\in\mathbb{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L],[L^{i}])=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\inf_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}\in S_{+}^{(T-t+1)d}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,d_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t},\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L^{i}}).

By the uniqueness result of Agueh and Carlier
[
4
, Theorem 6.1]
, each classical Bures–Wasserstein barycenter
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{*}
is unique in
S_{+}^{(T-t+1)d}
.

It remains to show that the collection
(\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\tilde{\Sigma}_{T}^{*})
determines a unique equivalence class
[\bar{L}]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
. We claim that two matrices
L,M\in\mathbb{L}
satisfy
[L]=[M]
in
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
if and only if

\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}=\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}\quad\text{for all }t=1,\ldots,T.

Indeed,
[L]=[M]
means
L=MO
for some
O=\mathrm{diag}(O_{1},\ldots,O_{T})\in\mathbb{O}
. This is equivalent to
\tilde{L}_{t}=\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}
for each
t
. Then

\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}=\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}=\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}O_{t}^{\top}\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}=\tilde{M}_{t}\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}=\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}.

Conversely, suppose
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L}=\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M}
for all
t
, i.e.,
\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}=\tilde{M}_{t}\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}
. Then
\tilde{L}_{t}
and
\tilde{M}_{t}
have the same singular values, and there exists
O_{t}\in O(d)
such that
\tilde{L}_{t}=\tilde{M}_{t}O_{t}
. Setting
O=\mathrm{diag}(O_{1},\ldots,O_{T})\in\mathbb{O}
, we get
L=MO
, hence
[L]=[M]
.

Finally, since each
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{*}
is unique and the collection
(\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\tilde{\Sigma}_{T}^{*})
determines a unique equivalence class, the adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter
[\bar{L}]
is unique.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The column decomposition in Proposition 4.2 establishes

d^{2}_{\mathrm{ABW}}(L,M)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}d^{2}_{BW}(\tilde{\Sigma}^{L}_{t},\tilde{\Sigma}^{M}_{t}),

expressing the adapted Bures–Wasserstein distance as a sum of classical Bures–Wasserstein distances between the column covariances
\tilde{\Sigma}^{L}_{t}=\tilde{L}_{t}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\top}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}^{M}_{t}=\tilde{M}_{t}\tilde{M}_{t}^{\top}
. This decomposition amounts to an isometry

(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O},\,d_{\mathrm{ABW}})\;\cong\;\prod_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{(T-t+1)d}_{+},\,d_{BW}\bigr).

A natural question is whether the aforementioned isometry allows one to recover the geometric results of
[
1
]
from classical Bures–Wasserstein geometry.

For instance, the non-negative curvature of
(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O},d_{\mathrm{ABW}})
stated in
[
1
, Theorem 1.4]
follows directly. Indeed,
d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}
is a sum of terms
d_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{L},\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{M})
, and since as observed in
[
38
]
the classical Bures–Wasserstein space
(\mathbb{S}_{+}^{m},d_{\mathrm{BW}})
has non-negative curvature in the Alexandrov sense, (i.e.,
d_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}
is semi-concave along geodesics), the sum inherits this property, see
[
18
, §4.1]
. Similarly, the tangent cone description of
[
1
, Theorem 1.7]
at a point
[L]\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}/\mathbb{O}
splits as a direct product

T_{[L]}(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O})\;\cong\;\prod_{t=1}^{T}T_{\tilde{\Sigma}^{L}_{t}}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{(T-t+1)d}_{+,\leq d}\bigr),

by the product splitting of tangent cones in Alexandrov geometry, see
[
18
, §4.1, §4.6]
, and the exponential map of
[
1
, Theorem 1.9]
factors accordingly.

However, it is less clear whether the finer geometric structures of
[
1
]
, such as the characterization of geodesics and the structure of the logarithmic map, can be fully recovered from the product structure alone.

4.3.
Characterization

In view of Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.4
, we now characterize the unique adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter via a fixed-point equation.

Theorem 4.6

(Characterization of the adapted barycenter)
.

Let
L^{1},\ldots,L^{N}\in\mathbb{L}
and let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}>0
be convex weights.
Then
[\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
is the unique minimizer of the adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter problem if and only if there exist
O_{i}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L})\in O^{*}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L},L^{i})
for each
i=1,\ldots,N
such that

(4.11)

\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}L^{i}O_{i}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}).

We call (
4.11
) the fixed-point equation of the adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter problem.

Proof.

The proof is based on the alternating minimization structure of the barycenter problem. Indeed, writing the objective function as

F(L;O_{1},\ldots,O_{N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\|L-L^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2},

we may restate the barycenter problem as
\inf_{L\in\mathbb{L}}\inf_{O_{1},\ldots,O_{N}\in\mathbb{O}}F(L;O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})
.

Sub-problem 1.
Optimize over
L
for fixed
O_{i}
. For fixed
O_{i}\in\mathbb{O}
, the map
L\mapsto F(L;O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})
is strictly convex and quadratic. Since
L^{i}O_{i}\in\mathbb{L}
for all
O_{i}\in\mathbb{O}
, the unique minimizer over
L\in\mathbb{L}
is the weighted mean

(4.12)

L=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}L^{i}O_{i}.

Sub-problem 2.
Optimize over each
O_{i}
for fixed
L
. For fixed
L=\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
, the minimization over each
O_{i}
decouples into
N
independent Procrustes problems

O_{i}\in\arg\min_{O\in\mathbb{O}}\|\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}-L^{i}O\|_{F}^{2}=O^{*}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L},L^{i}).

By
[
1
, Lemma 3.3]
, if
((L^{i})^{\top}\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L})_{t,t}=U_{t}^{i}S_{t}^{i}(V_{t}^{i})^{\top}
is the SVD, then

(4.13)

(O_{i})_{t}=V_{t}^{i}Q_{t}^{i}(U_{t}^{i})^{\top}

for some
Q_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{rk}(S_{t}^{i}))
, where
\mathrm{rk}(S_{t}^{i})
is the rank of the diagonal matrix
S_{t}
containing the singular values of
((L^{i})^{\top}\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L})_{t,t}
and
\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{rk}(S_{t}^{i}))
is the subset of orthogonal matrices

\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{rk}(S_{t}^{i}))\coloneqq\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&0\\
0&Q^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\,\colon\,Q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{O}(d-\mathrm{rk}(S_{t}^{i}))\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{O}(d).

When
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
and
((L^{i})^{\top}\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L})_{t,t}
is positive definite, the optimizer is unique with
Q_{t}^{i}=I_{d}
.

The adapted barycenter is the point at which both sub-problems are solved simultaneously. We now verify this formally.

Let
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
be a solution of the adapted Wasserstein barycenter problem. For each
i
, choose
O_{i}\in O^{*}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L},L^{i})
. Fixing these
O_{i}
, Sub-problem 1 shows that
L\mapsto F(L;O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})
is uniquely minimized at
L=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}L^{i}O_{i}
. Since
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
minimizes the full objective and the
O_{i}
already solve Sub-problem 2 for
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
,
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
must also minimize Sub-problem 1, hence (
4.11
) holds.

Conversely, suppose
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
satisfies (
4.11
) for some
O_{i}\in O^{*}(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L},L^{i})
. Then
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
solves Sub-problem 1 for the given
(O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})
, and by assumption each
O_{i}
solves Sub-problem 2 for
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
. Hence the pair
(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L},(O_{1},\ldots,O_{N}))
is a critical point of the joint objective
F
. Since the barycenter is unique in
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
by Theorem
4.4
,
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
is the minimizer.
∎

4.4.
Regularity of the barycenter

In this section we show that the adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter of regular processes is itself regular. Recall that
L\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
if and only if
(L^{\top}L)_{t,t}
is positive definite for all
t=1,\ldots,T
.

Theorem 4.9

(Regularity of the barycenter)
.

Let
L^{1},\ldots,L^{N}\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
and let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}>0
be convex weights.
with
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1
.
Then the unique minimizer
[\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}]\in\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
of the barycenter problem

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,d_{\mathrm{ABW}}^{2}([L],[L^{i}])

satisfies
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
.

Proof.

We know by Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.4
that the barycenter problem admits a unique Gaussian minimizer. By Proposition
4.2
, the barycenter problem decomposes column by column. The
t
-th column of the minimizer is given by the truncated barycenter functional

(4.18)

F([L])=\inf_{\tilde{L}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T-t+1)d\times d}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\min_{O_{i}\in O(d)}\|\tilde{L}_{t}-\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2},

where
\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T-t+1)d\times d}
is the
t
-th truncated column of
L^{i}
. Since
L^{i}\in\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{reg}}
, each
\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}
has full column rank
d
. We need to show that the minimizer
\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}
of (
4.18
) has also full column rank
d
for every
t
. In order to do so, we show by a contradiction argument that if the minimizer
\tilde{L}^{*}_{t}
does not have full column rank then we can construct a competitor for (
4.18
) such that
F([\tilde{L}^{\varepsilon}_{t}])<F([\tilde{L}^{*}_{t}])

Suppose that
\mathrm{rk}(\tilde{L}_{t}^{*})<d
for some
t
. Then there exists a unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}v=0
. By the fixed-point equation
(
4.11
) the minimizer is characterized by
\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}^{*}
for
O_{i}^{*}\in O(d)
. Thus,

(4.19)

0=\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}v=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}^{*}v.

We now construct a perturbation that strictly decreases the objective (
4.18
). For each
i
, set
A_{i}:=(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Since
\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}v=0
, we have
A_{i}v=0
for all
i
. Write
v^{\perp}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:u^{\top}v=0\}
for the orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{Span}(v)
and
A_{i}|_{v^{\perp}}\colon v^{\perp}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the restriction of
A_{i}
to
v^{\perp}
. Since
\dim(v^{\perp})=d-1
, we have
\mathrm{rk}(A_{i}|_{v^{\perp}})\leq d-1
for every
i
.

Step 1.

Choice of the perturbation direction
. Fix
i=1
. Since
\mathrm{rk}(A_{1}|_{v^{\perp}})\leq d-1
, the orthogonal complement
\mathrm{Im}(A_{1}|_{v^{\perp}})^{\perp}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is nonempty. Let
w\in\mathrm{Im}(A_{1}|_{v^{\perp}})^{\perp}
be a unit vector. Since
\tilde{L}_{t}^{1}
has full column rank
d
, the matrix
(\tilde{L}_{t}^{1})^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is positive definite and thus invertible. Define

h:=\tilde{L}_{t}^{1}\,\bigl((\tilde{L}_{t}^{1})^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{1}\bigr)^{-1}w\;\in\mathbb{R}^{(T-t+1)d}.

Then
(\tilde{L}_{t}^{1})^{\top}h=w
, and by the choice of
w
:

(4.20)

c_{1,\perp}:=\text{projection of }(\tilde{L}_{t}^{1})^{\top}h\text{ onto }\mathrm{Im}(A_{1}|_{v^{\perp}})^{\perp}=w\neq 0.

For
i=2,\ldots,N
, write
c_{i,\perp}
for the projection of
(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h
onto
\mathrm{Im}(A_{i}|_{v^{\perp}})^{\perp}
.

Step 2

The perturbation
. Consider
\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}+\varepsilon\,hv^{\top}
for
\varepsilon>0
. The Procrustes distance for each
i
satisfies

\min_{O_{i}\in O(d)}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}\|_{F}^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{S}_{t}^{\varepsilon}),

where
\tilde{S}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
is the diagonal matrix of singular values of
(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. We now turn to

(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=A_{i}+\varepsilon\,\bigl((\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\bigr)v^{\top}.

Choose a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in which
v=e_{d}
. In this basis,
A_{i}=(\hat{A}_{i}\mid 0)
where
\hat{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-1)}
and the perturbation gives
A_{i}+\varepsilon\,(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\,e_{d}^{\top}=(\hat{A}_{i}\mid\varepsilon\,(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h)
. The Gram matrix is

\bigl(A_{i}+\varepsilon\,(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\,v^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\bigl(A_{i}+\varepsilon\,(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\,v^{\top}\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{A}_{i}^{\top}\hat{A}_{i}&\varepsilon\,\hat{A}_{i}^{\top}(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\\
\varepsilon\,h^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}\hat{A}_{i}&\varepsilon^{2}\,\|(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\|^{2}_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

By the Schur complement (see
[
28
, Section 0.8.5]
), the smallest eigenvalue contributed by the new direction is

\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(\|(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\|^{2}_{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\,h^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}\hat{A}_{i}(\hat{A}_{i}^{\top}\hat{A}_{i})^{-1}\hat{A}_{i}^{\top}(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h\bigr)=\varepsilon^{2}\,\|c_{i,\perp}\|^{2}_{2}+O(\varepsilon^{4}),

where we use that the Schur complement extracts the component of
(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}h
orthogonal to
\mathrm{Im}(\hat{A}_{i})=\mathrm{Im}(A_{i}|_{v^{\perp}})
. Therefore,

(4.21)

\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{S}_{t}^{\varepsilon})=\mathrm{tr}({\tilde{S}_{t}})+\varepsilon\,\|c_{i,\perp}\|_{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+},

where
\tilde{S}_{t}
is the diagonal matrix of singular values of
(\tilde{L}_{t}^{i})^{\top}\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}
.

Step 3.

Conclusion
. Using
\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\|h\|^{2}_{2}
and summing over
i
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\min_{O_{i}}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\min_{O_{i}}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}-\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon^{2}\|h\|^{2}-2\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\|c_{i,\perp}\|+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

By (
4.20
),
\|c_{1,\perp}\|=\|w\|=1>0
. Since
\lambda_{1}>0
, the term
-2\varepsilon\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\|c_{i,\perp}\|\leq-2\varepsilon\lambda_{1}
is strictly negative and of order
\varepsilon
, dominating
\varepsilon^{2}\|h\|^{2}_{2}
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. This gives

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\min_{O_{i}}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2}<\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\min_{O_{i}}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}-\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2},

contradicting the minimality of
\tilde{L}_{t}^{*}
.
∎

t
-th column problem reads

(5.5)

\inf_{\tilde{L}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{T-t+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\min_{O_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}}\|\tilde{L}_{t}-\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\|_{F}^{2}.

For any fixed choice of signs
(O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})\in\{+1,-1\}^{N}
, the minimizer over
\tilde{L}_{t}
is the weighted mean
\tilde{L}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}
, and the corresponding objective value equals

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}\|_{F}^{2}-\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Since the first term is independent of the signs, the global minimum of (
5.5
) is attained by the sign vector
(O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})
that maximizes

(5.6)

f(O_{1},\ldots,O_{N}):=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}O_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\,((L^{i})^{\top}L^{j})_{t,t}\,O_{i}O_{j}.

We now use the assumption
\alpha_{k}^{i}\geq 0
. By (
5.3
),
L^{i}_{r,t}=\sigma_{t}^{i}\prod_{k=t+1}^{r}\alpha_{k}^{i}\geq 0
for all
r\geq t
, with
L^{i}_{t,t}=\sigma_{t}^{i}>0
. Therefore, for every pair
i,j
,

(5.7)

((L^{i})^{\top}L^{j})_{t,t}=\sum_{r=t}^{T}L^{i}_{r,t}\,L^{j}_{r,t}\geq L^{i}_{t,t}\,L^{j}_{t,t}=\sigma_{t}^{i}\sigma_{t}^{j}>0,

since each summand is a product of two non-negative numbers and the
r=t
term is strictly positive. We claim that
f
is maximized by
O_{i}=+1
for all
i
. Indeed, for any sign vector
(O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})
,

(5.8)

f(+1,\ldots,+1)-f(O_{1},\ldots,O_{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\,(1-O_{i}O_{j})\,((L^{i})^{\top}L^{j})_{t,t}\geq 0,

because each term in the sum is non-negative: if
O_{i}O_{j}=+1
the factor
(1-O_{i}O_{j})
vanishes, and if
O_{i}O_{j}=-1
the factor equals
2
while
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\,((L^{i})^{\top}L^{j})_{t,t}>0
by (
5.7
). Moreover, equality in (
5.8
) holds if and only if there is no pair
(i,j)
with
O_{i}O_{j}=-1
, i.e., all signs are equal. Since
O_{i}=+1
for all
i
and
O_{i}=-1
for all
i
yield the same equivalence class in
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{O}
, the optimal signs are unique up to equivalence.

The corresponding minimizer of (
5.5
) is
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\tilde{L}}}}{\tilde{L}}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\tilde{L}_{t}^{i}
, i.e.,

\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{t,s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}L^{i}_{t,s},\quad 1\leq s\leq t\leq T.

By (
5.2
) and (
5.3
),
this gives
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\mathbb{X}}}}{\mathbb{X}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{X}^{i}
, which is the Knothe–Rosenblatt barycenter.
∎

Remark 5.2

(The barycenter of
AR(1)
processes is generally not
AR(1)
)
.

The Cholesky factor of an
AR(1)
process with parameters
(\alpha_{t}^{i},\sigma_{t}^{i})
has entries
L_{t,s}^{i}=\sigma_{s}^{i}\prod_{k=s+1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{i}
. Its defining structural property is the independency on
s
of the ratios
L_{t,s}^{i}/L_{t-1,s}^{i}=\alpha_{t}^{i}
for all
s<t
. When
\alpha_{t}^{i}\geq 0
and
\sigma_{t}^{i}>0
for all
i
and
t
, by the proof of Proposition
5.1
for each
i
we have
\varepsilon_{t}^{i}=1
for every
t
, and the barycenter is
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{t,s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{s}^{i}\prod_{k=s+1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{i}
. Hence, the barycenter is
AR(1)
if and only if the ratio

\frac{\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{t,s}}{\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{t-1,s}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{s}^{i}\alpha_{t}^{i}\prod_{k=s+1}^{t-1}\alpha_{k}^{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{s}^{i}\prod_{k=s+1}^{t-1}\alpha_{k}^{i}}

is independent of
s
for each
t
. This is a weighted average of
\alpha_{t}^{i}
with weights proportional to
\lambda_{i}\sigma_{s}^{i}\prod_{k=s+1}^{t-1}\alpha_{k}^{i}
, which depend on
s
through the product
\prod_{k=s+1}^{t-1}\alpha_{k}^{i}

whenever the
\alpha_{k}^{i}
vary across
i
. Thus, for
T\geq 3
and heterogeneous
\alpha_{t}^{i}
, the barycenter is not
AR(1)
.

The column decomposition (Proposition
4.2
) gives an intuitive explanation. The barycenter problem decouples into independent Bures–Wasserstein problems for each noise column, one per time step. The
AR(1)
property requires consistency across columns (
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{t,s}/\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{t-1,s}
independent of
s
), but since the columns are optimized independently, no mechanism enforces this structural constraint. In particular, for
T=2
the condition is trivial (only one ratio to check) and the barycenter is always
AR(1)
, while for
T\geq 3
it generally fails.

The adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter vs. the classical Bures-Wasserstein barycenter

We construct an explicit example where the adapted and classical barycenter do not coincide. Consider
N=2
processes with equal weights
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
, time horizon
T=2
, and parameters
\sigma^{1}_{1}=\sigma^{2}_{1}=1
,
\sigma^{1}_{2}=\sigma^{2}_{2}=1
,
\alpha^{1}_{2}=0.5
,
\alpha^{2}_{2}=-0.5
. By (
5.3
), the Cholesky factors are

L^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0.5&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad L^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-0.5&1\end{pmatrix}.

We compute the adapted barycenter using the column decomposition of Proposition
4.2
. For the first column (
t=1
), the truncated columns are
\tilde{L}_{1}^{1}=(1,\,0.5)^{\top}
and
\tilde{L}_{1}^{2}=(1,\,-0.5)^{\top}
. Since
O(1)=\{+1,-1\}
, we need to find the signs
\varepsilon_{1}^{1},\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\in\{+1,-1\}
that maximize
\|\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon_{1}^{1}\tilde{L}_{1}^{1}+\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\tilde{L}_{1}^{2})\|_{F}^{2}
, as in the proof of Proposition
5.1
. By direct computation:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}^{1}=+1,\;\varepsilon_{1}^{2}=+1:

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(1,0.5)^{\top}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1,-0.5)^{\top}=(1,\,0)^{\top},\quad\text{norm}^{2}=1,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}^{1}=+1,\;\varepsilon_{1}^{2}=-1:

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(1,0.5)^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}(1,-0.5)^{\top}=(0,\,0.5)^{\top},\quad\text{norm}^{2}=0.25,

and the remaining two cases give the negatives of these, with the same norms. Hence
\varepsilon_{1}^{1}=\varepsilon_{1}^{2}=+1
is optimal (up to a global sign flip), yielding
\tilde{\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}}_{1}=(1,0)^{\top}
. For the second column (
t=2
), the truncated columns are the scalars
\tilde{L}_{2}^{1}=\tilde{L}_{2}^{2}=1
, so trivially
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\tilde{L}}}}{\tilde{L}}_{2}=1
. Therefore, the adapted barycenter is

(5.9)

\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}=\frac{1}{2}(L^{1}+L^{2})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=I_{2},

and in particular,
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
has covariance matrix
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{ABW}}=I_{2}
. In conclusion, as the off-diagonal entries of
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
cancel due to the opposite signs of
\alpha^{1}
and
\alpha^{2}
, the adapted barycenter is a white noise process with no temporal correlation.

On the other hand, the covariance matrices of the two input processes are

\Sigma^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\
0.5&1.25\end{pmatrix},\qquad\Sigma^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.5\\
-0.5&1.25\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\Sigma^{1}
and
\Sigma^{2}
share the same diagonal entries: the squaring
\Sigma^{i}=L^{i}(L^{i})^{\top}

preserves the sign of
\alpha^{i}
in the off-diagonal entries, the diagonal entries depend only on
|\alpha^{i}|
, so the two input processes are indistinguishable at the level of marginal variances. As a result, the classical Wassestein barycenter averages two covariance matrices whose off-diagonal contributions cancel by symmetry, but whose diagonal contributions accumulate without cancellation.
The covariance
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}
of the classical Bures–Wasserstein barycenter solves

\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma^{1}\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}+(\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma^{2}\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr].

We may numerically compute

\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}\approx\begin{pmatrix}0.934&0\\
0&1.199\end{pmatrix}\neq I_{2}=\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{ABW}}.

The classical barycenter has zero off-diagonal entries, but its diagonal entries differ from those of the adapted barycenter.
This discrepancy arises because the classical distance operates on
\Sigma=LL^{\top}
,
where the diagonal entries are insensitive to the sign of
\alpha^{i}
and the
off-diagonal contributions of
\Sigma^{1}
and
\Sigma^{2}
cancel only upon averaging.
By contrast, the adapted distance operates on
L
directly, where the opposite signs
of the autoregressive coefficients are visible at the level of the Cholesky factor and
cancel immediately, yielding a white noise barycenter with identity covariance

\Sigma_{\mathrm{ABW}}=I_{2}
.

6.
Numerical illustrations

We illustrate the difference between the adapted and classical Bures–Wasserstein barycenters through numerical experiments. We consider
N=10
univariate
AR(1)
processes on a time horizon
T=30
, with parameters paired symmetrically

\alpha^{i}\in\{0.92,\,0.85,\,0.75,\,0.60,\,0.50,\,{-0.92},\,{-0.85},\,{-0.75},\,{-0.60},\,{-0.50}\},

\sigma^{i}\in\{1.0,\,1.3,\,0.8,\,1.5,\,1.1,\,1.0,\,1.3,\,0.8,\,1.5,\,1.1\},

and equal weights
\lambda_{i}=1/10
.
The symmetric pairing of the
\alpha
-parameters is the key design choice: each positive
\alpha^{i}
is matched with its negative counterpart with the same volatility
\sigma^{i}
. As shown in the
T=2
example of Section
5
, the sign of
\alpha^{i}
appears in the off-diagonal entries of
\Sigma^{i}=L^{i}(L^{i})^{\top}
but not in the diagonal entries. Consequently, both the adapted and the classical barycenter exhibit near-zero off-diagonal entries due to cancellation. Therefore, the two barycenters are distinguished not by their correlation structure but by their marginal variances.

The adapted barycenter
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}
is computed via the Procrustes fixed-point iteration (
4.11
), while the classical barycenter
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}
is computed via the Bures–Wasserstein iteration (
4.14
). Both barycenters are then used to generate sample paths from
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{X}}}{X}=\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}G
and
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{X}}}{X}_{\mathrm{BW}}=\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{\mathrm{BW}}G
, where
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{L}}}{L}_{\mathrm{BW}}
is the Cholesky factor of
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}

Discussion

The numerical experiments confirm and extend the theoretical predictions of Section
5
. The adapted Bures–Wasserstein barycenter operates at the level of the Cholesky factor
L
, where the sign of the autoregressive parameter
\alpha^{i}
is visible and opposite signs cancel. The classical Bures–Wasserstein barycenter operates at the level of the covariance matrix
\Sigma=LL^{\top}
, where the diagonal entries are insensitive to the sign of
\alpha^{i}
, so no such cancellation occurs. In this symmetric setup, both barycenters exhibit near-zero off-diagonal entries, as the off-diagonal contributions of
\Sigma^{i}
and
\Sigma^{j}
with opposite autoregressive coefficients cancel upon averaging in both cases. The distinction between the two barycenters therefore manifests primarily in the marginal variances (Figure
3(a)
) and in the diagonal structure of the covariance and Cholesky heatmaps (Figures
4
–
7
), consistent with the explicit
T=2
computation
\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{ABW}}=I_{2}\neq\accentset{\overline{\hphantom{\Sigma}}}{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{BW}}\approx\mathrm{diag}(0.934,1.199)
of Section
5
.
