Calcined oyster shell powder as a lime substitute in earthen building materials
Authors: Sheng Bi, Zeyu Chao, Mingyang Feng, Chunyu Yang, Yucheng Guo, Ding Wen Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined calcined oyster shell powder (COSP) as a lime substitute in earthen building materials using a controlled substitution design and a simplified cradle-to-gate carbon assessment. Five mixtures with increasing COSP replacement levels were prepared, and their fresh-state behavior, 28-day compressive strength, EDS-derived elemental ratio, SEM morphology, and calculated binder-related carbon emissions were evaluated together. The mini-slump results showed that COSP substitution caused only limited changes in spread diameter, while the post-vibration morphology varied across the substitution series. The 28-day compressive-strength results indicated that the COSP-containing mixtures remained within a comparable strength range for stabilized earthen materials, although they did not show a clear strength-enhancing effect compared with the lime-based reference mixture. The elemental ratio R = Ca/(Si + Al) varied non-monotonically, and SEM observations suggested differences in local matrix compactness and heterogeneity among mixtures. Under both baseline and low-carbon electricity scenarios, the calculated mass-based binder-related carbon emissions decreased with increasing COSP replacement. When normalized by compressive strength, the carbon intensity results became more sensitive to the measured mechanical response, indicating that COSP substitution should be evaluated as a strength–carbon trade-off rather than as a purely mechanical optimization problem. Overall, the results suggest that COSP can serve as a potential low-carbon lime substitute in earthen building materials under the tested conditions. The main contribution of this study is to provide a coupled comparison of mechanical response, elemental composition, microstructural observation, and carbon-accounting results for shell-derived calcium substitution in earthen materials. The findings should be interpreted within the selected experimental design and carbon-accounting assumptions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58082-0.

Introduction

The construction sector consumes large amounts of mineral resources and contributes substantially to greenhouse gas emissions1. Within mineral-based binder systems, lime is discussed less frequently than cement, but its production still involves considerable carbon emissions because the calcination of calcium carbonate releases CO₂ directly from the raw material2. In addition to fuel and electricity demand, this process-related emission is inherent to the conversion of CaCO₃ to CaO. Reducing the environmental burden of lime-based materials therefore requires not only improvements in process efficiency, but also exploration of alternative calcium sources3.

Shell waste, particularly oyster shell waste, has attracted increasing attention as a potential secondary calcium resource4. Large quantities of bivalve shells are generated annually through aquaculture and shellfish consumption, and disposal by stockpiling or landfilling may create environmental and land-use pressures. Because oyster shells are mainly composed of CaCO₃, they can be converted by calcination into CaO-rich powders with chemical similarity to conventional lime5. Previous studies have explored shell-derived materials in concrete, cementitious systems, geopolymers, and related applications, mainly focusing on mechanical performance, hydration-related mechanisms, pore refinement, or shrinkage control6,7. These studies indicate that shell-derived calcium sources can be technically feasible in binder systems, but their role in earthen building materials remains less clearly understood8,9.

Existing studies also differ in how they discuss the environmental implications of shell-derived materials. Reported carbon results are often sensitive to system boundaries, functional units, and assumptions related to shell treatment and energy supply10,11. Recent LCA-oriented studies also show that the environmental benefit of shell-derived materials depends strongly on allocation rules, processing energy, replacement function, and the selected functional unit12. At the same time, most studies emphasize either strength development or carbon-related comparison, while fewer consider how mechanical response, elemental composition, and simplified carbon results change together13. This is particularly true for earthen building materials, where the balance between material performance and low-carbon substitution remains insufficiently discussed14,15. Recent reviews on earth-based construction and stabilized rammed earth have shown that binder type, stabilizer dosage, curing condition, fiber reinforcement, and testing method can strongly influence the mechanical response of earthen materials16.

Another limitation is that performance changes under shell substitution are often interpreted mainly through bulk strength results or qualitative microstructural observations, without sufficiently linking them to shifts in elemental composition. For calcium-rich binder systems, variations in the relative proportions of Ca, Si, and Al may influence reaction products, matrix continuity, and compressive strength17. However, this relationship has not been clearly examined together with comparative carbon results in lime-stabilized earthen building materials under a consistent substitution design18.

In this context, the present study investigates calcined oyster shell powder (COSP) as a partial or complete substitute for lime in earthen building materials. A controlled substitution design was adopted in which the proportion of lime and COSP was varied while other main mixture parameters were kept constant. Fresh-state behavior, 28-day compressive strength, elemental composition from EDS, and SEM observations were analyzed together with a simplified cradle-to-gate carbon comparison.

The aim of this study is to provide a screening-level experimental comparison of how COSP substitution influences fresh-state behavior, compressive response, elemental ratio variation, and comparative binder-related carbon results in earthen building materials. Particular attention is given to whether COSP replacement can reduce calculated binder-related carbon emissions while keeping compressive strength within a comparable earthen-material range, and whether the Ca/(Si + Al) ratio provides a useful descriptive indicator for discussing the coupled changes in composition, microstructure, and mechanical response. The results are intended to clarify both the potential and the limitations of using shell-derived calcium sources as lime substitutes under the present experimental conditions and accounting assumptions. The novelty of this study lies in coupling controlled COSP–lime substitution, replicate-level mechanical evaluation, EDS-derived elemental ratio, qualitative SEM observation, and simplified carbon accounting within a single earthen-material mixture series.

Methodology

Experimental design

To examine the effect of calcined oyster shell powder (COSP) as a lime substitute on the mechanical response and comparative carbon results of earthen building materials, a controlled substitution design was adopted. Five mix groups (A1–A5) were prepared. A1 was used as the reference group, and A2–A5 contained progressively higher COSP replacement levels while maintaining the same water-to-binder ratio and fiber dosage. The COSP used in this study was obtained by electric-furnace calcination of oyster-shell waste followed by grinding to approximately 1250 mesh. The detailed furnace operation and energy-consumption data were based on supplier-provided industrial processing records and are described in Sect. “System boundary”.

The water-to-binder ratio, aggregate grading, and fiber dosage were kept constant across all groups, and only the mass ratio between quicklime and COSP was varied19. This design allowed the effect of calcium-source substitution on fresh behavior, compressive strength, elemental composition, and comparative carbon indicators to be examined under consistent mixture conditions. Straw fiber and sodium alginate were kept constant because the present study focused on calcium-source substitution rather than on fiber or biopolymer optimization; previous studies have shown that bio-based additives and fibers can independently affect the mechanical and durability performance of earthen materials.

For each mix group, three specimens were prepared for 28-day compressive-strength testing. Three valid results were obtained for A1–A4. For A5, one specimen was damaged before a valid compressive-strength record could be obtained and was therefore excluded from the statistical calculation. Accordingly, the compressive-strength results are reported as mean ± standard deviation, with n = 3 for A1–A4 and n = 2 for A5. The coefficient of variation (CV) was also calculated to indicate within-group variability.

Simplified carbon assessment

System boundary

A simplified cradle-to-gate boundary was used to estimate binder-related carbon emissions associated with material production20. The boundary included raw material acquisition, calcination, and grinding, but excluded transport, construction-stage emissions, and use-stage processes.

For lime, the boundary included limestone extraction, calcination, and grinding. For COSP, it included oyster shell collection and pre-treatment, electric-furnace calcination, and subsequent grinding. The oyster shells were sourced from aquaculture waste in Shandong, China, and the calcined material was ground using industrial-scale milling equipment.

According to supplier-provided operational data, the calcination equipment processed 5 t of raw shells per batch over a 6 h cycle, with an average operating power of approximately 850 kW. This corresponds to an electricity consumption of about 5100 kWh per batch, or 1020 kWh per tonne of raw shell. In the present study, this value was approximated as 1.02 kWh per kg of finished COSP by applying a 1:1 mass assumption between raw shell input and calcined output. Because shell calcination may involve mass loss in practice, this assumption affects the absolute emission values and should be regarded as a simplification for comparative analysis. The carbon assessment in this study should therefore be interpreted as a screening-level comparison rather than as a full life-cycle inventory.

Oyster shells were treated as aquaculture by-products, and burdens from the cultivation stage were excluded21. Carbon emissions associated with COSP production were therefore limited to the post-discard processing stages within the defined system boundary. Loess and sand were excluded from the emission comparison because their contents were unchanged across all groups (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Simplified cradle-to-gate system boundary used for the binder-related carbon assessment.

Functional units

Two functional units were used for comparison. The first was per kg dry composite, representing mass-based carbon emissions. The second was per MPa compressive strength, in which emissions were normalized by 28-day compressive strength to allow comparison among substitution levels22.

Electricity scenarios and allocation assumptions

Two electricity scenarios were considered: a baseline grid scenario using the current average Chinese grid emission factor and a low-carbon scenario using a reduced grid emission factor. All other parameters were kept constant25.

Oyster shells were treated as waste by-products from aquaculture, with no upstream cultivation burden or avoided landfill credit included in the calculations. The resulting carbon values should therefore be interpreted within the assumptions and system boundary adopted in this study26.

Results

Fresh-state behavior

The fresh-state behavior of groups A1–A5 was evaluated using the mini-slump test, and the results are shown in Fig. 2. As the COSP substitution level increased, the slump and spread values changed within a relatively narrow range. The reference group A1 showed a slump of 5.7 cm and a spread diameter of 103.60 mm. For A2 and A3, the slump decreased slightly to 5.5 cm, with spread diameters of 102.04 mm and 101.41 mm, respectively. For A4 and A5, the slump increased to 5.8 cm and 6.0 cm, while the spread diameter remained close to 100 mm (100.30 mm and 100.53 mm, respectively).Fig. 2Fresh-state workability of mixtures with increasing COSP substitution: (a) retained slump height; (b) spread diameter. Values are shown as descriptive measurements from the present test series.

Fresh-state workability of mixtures with increasing COSP substitution: (a) retained slump height; (b) spread diameter. Values are shown as descriptive measurements from the present test series.

Overall, under the fixed water-to-binder ratio used in this study, increasing COSP replacement caused only limited changes in the macroscopic flow range. The spread diameter varied by less than 4% across all groups. This indicates that the replacement of lime by ultrafine COSP had only a limited influence on fresh-state workability as captured by the mini-slump test.

Because replicate-level fresh-state records were not available, these values are used descriptively and are not interpreted statistically.

In the present test configuration, a larger retained slump height after vibration reflects greater resistance to collapse rather than greater flowability in the conventional sense. Representative morphologies after vibration are shown in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Morphological evolution of fresh mixtures under vibration with increasing COSP substitution.

Morphological evolution of fresh mixtures under vibration with increasing COSP substitution.

For A1 and A2, the specimens generally retained a truncated-cone shape after vibration, with relatively smooth surfaces and limited visible cracking. In A3, the slump remained similar to that of A2, but the post-vibration morphology appeared more uniform, with fewer visible shear bands or localized cracks. In A4 and A5, the retained height increased further, but more obvious radial cracks and localized shear features were also observed.

These observations suggest that the substitution effect was not expressed simply as a monotonic change in fresh-state stability. Instead, the mixtures appeared to shift from relatively plastic deformation behavior at lower COSP contents toward greater shape retention at higher replacement levels, while also showing more localized cracking in A4 and A5. One possible explanation is that the ultrafine COSP particles influenced particle packing and stress redistribution during vibration. Under this interpretation, intermediate replacement levels may have promoted a more uniform fresh-state structure, whereas higher replacement levels may have increased rigidity together with local brittleness. This interpretation remains qualitative and is based on the observed morphology in Fig. 3.

A3 showed a relatively uniform appearance after vibration, whereas A4 and A5 showed more visible cracking and shear-related features. This suggests that the mini-slump diameter alone did not fully capture the changes induced by COSP substitution.

The reference mixture A1 showed the highest mean compressive strength, with a value of 1.811 ± 0.091 MPa. The COSP-containing mixtures showed lower mean values: 1.669 ± 0.106 MPa for A2, 1.679 ± 0.242 MPa for A3, 1.529 ± 0.097 MPa for A4, and 1.651 ± 0.161 MPa for A5. These results indicate that COSP replacement did not produce a strength-enhancing effect under the present mixture design. Instead, the COSP-containing mixtures remained within a similar low-strength range typical of stabilized earthen materials, while generally showing lower mean compressive strength than the lime-based reference mixture. Considering the standard deviations, especially for A3 and A5, the differences among COSP-containing mixtures should be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, the mechanical results are better interpreted together with the carbon and microstructural results rather than as a stand-alone performance ranking.

These visual differences are interpreted qualitatively. Although the failure patterns indicate that COSP substitution affected crack development and local fracture morphology, they should not be used alone to rank the mechanical performance of the mixtures. When considered together with the mean compressive-strength values and standard deviations in Fig. 4, the failure observations support the view that the COSP-containing mixtures exhibited variable mechanical responses rather than a clearly defined optimum replacement level.

Overall, under the present mixture design, COSP substitution maintained the compressive strength within a comparable range for stabilized earthen materials, although no clear strength-increasing trend was observed. Therefore, the mechanical results are better interpreted together with the carbon and microstructural results rather than as a stand-alone basis for defining an optimum replacement level.

These observations suggest that COSP substitution influenced both elemental composition and local matrix morphology. However, the SEM evidence remains qualitative and should not be used alone to explain or rank the compressive-strength performance of the mixtures. Although A3 and A4 showed intermediate R values and locally compact microstructural features, the compressive-strength results do not support a direct one-to-one relationship between R, SEM appearance, and mechanical performance.

When the elemental ratio, SEM observations, and compressive-strength results are considered together, the data suggest that the effect of COSP substitution was governed by a combined change in calcium-related composition, particle packing, and local matrix morphology, rather than by the calcium-related ratio alone. Neither very low nor very high R values can be directly linked to superior compressive performance in the present dataset. Instead, R = Ca/(Si + Al) is more appropriately interpreted as a comparative indicator for discussing how elemental composition changed across the substitution series.

These observations do not establish a strict compositional threshold or a predictive relationship between R and compressive strength. Nevertheless, they support the use of R as a useful descriptive parameter for comparing elemental balance, microstructural appearance, and mechanical response under COSP substitution.

Comparative carbon results and combined response

To compare binder-related carbon results under different electricity-carbon assumptions, cradle-to-gate emissions were calculated for all groups under both the baseline grid scenario and the low-carbon grid scenario. The carbon reduction ratio (CR) and carbon intensity of strength (CIS) were calculated using the equations defined in Sect. “Comparison indicators”.

COSP was produced by electric-furnace calcination, with an estimated electricity consumption of approximately 1.02 kWh per kg of raw shell and 0.02 kWh/kg for grinding27. Because a substantial part of the COSP-related emission term depends on electricity use, the calculated carbon results were sensitive to the adopted grid emission factor. In the present calculations, the baseline grid factor was 0.5856 kg CO₂/kWh, and the low-carbon scenario used 0.10 kg CO₂/kWh. These results should therefore be interpreted as comparative results within the system boundary and assumptions defined in this study.

Under the baseline grid scenario, the calculated mass-based binder-related carbon emissions decreased with increasing COSP replacement (Fig. 8). This decrease occurred because the emission factor assigned to COSP under the adopted calculation assumptions was lower than that of quicklime. Under the low-carbon electricity scenario, the absolute carbon values of COSP-containing mixtures decreased further, and the reduction became more pronounced at higher COSP replacement levels. Therefore, the mass-based carbon trend was comparatively regular across the substitution series.Fig. 8Mass-based binder-related carbon emissions of mixtures under baseline and low-carbon electricity scenarios.

Mass-based binder-related carbon emissions of mixtures under baseline and low-carbon electricity scenarios.

When normalized by 28-day compressive strength, the CIS values showed a more complex pattern (Fig. 9). This is because CIS depends not only on the calculated carbon emissions, but also on the measured mechanical performance. Under the baseline grid scenario, the reduction in mass-based emissions did not translate into a strictly monotonic reduction in CIS for all substitution levels, because the COSP-containing mixtures also showed changes in mean compressive strength. These results indicate that the main benefit of COSP substitution in the present study lies in calculated carbon reduction rather than compressive-strength enhancement. Therefore, the replacement series is better interpreted as a strength–carbon trade-off rather than as a single-point mechanical optimization.

When considered together with the elemental ratio and SEM observations, the results suggest that COSP substitution affected elemental balance, local matrix morphology, and calculated carbon intensity simultaneously. However, neither the elemental ratio nor the SEM observations establish a direct predictive relationship with compressive strength. Figure 10 is therefore interpreted as a comparative visualization of carbon–strength–composition relationships rather than as evidence for a predictive optimization model.

Taken together, increasing COSP replacement reduced calculated binder-related carbon emissions on a mass basis under the assumptions adopted here. However, the strength-normalized carbon results were influenced by the variability in compressive strength. This confirms that COSP substitution should be evaluated by considering both environmental benefit and mechanical-performance constraints.

Sensitivity analysis

A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine how the calculated carbon results responded to selected parameter uncertainties. Unlike the mechanical comparison, this sensitivity analysis focused only on the carbon-accounting model. Three parameters were varied: the grid emission factor (± 30%), the calcination electricity consumption of COSP (± 20%), and the lime emission factor (± 10%).

The sensitivity results showed that the calculated carbon indicators were most strongly affected by the grid emission factor, followed by the calcination electricity consumption of COSP. The lime emission factor had a smaller influence on the comparative results. This pattern reflects the fact that COSP production in this study was modeled as an electricity-dependent process, and therefore its calculated carbon burden was closely linked to the assumed electricity-carbon intensity.

Although the absolute carbon values changed under the tested parameter variations, the mass-based carbon-emission trend remained generally consistent: increasing COSP replacement reduced the calculated binder-related carbon emissions under the assumptions adopted in this study. However, when emissions were normalized by compressive strength, the results became more sensitive to the measured mechanical performance. Therefore, the sensitivity analysis supports the robustness of the mass-based carbon-reduction trend, but it does not independently validate a specific replacement threshold or an optimum mixture.

Discussion

The present results show that replacing lime with calcined oyster shell powder (COSP) affected the carbon and mechanical responses of earthen building materials in different ways. The calculated binder-related carbon emissions decreased more regularly with increasing COSP replacement, whereas the compressive-strength response showed greater variability. This indicates that COSP substitution should be discussed primarily as a carbon-reduction strategy, with compressive strength treated as a key performance constraint28,29. Similar strength–environment trade-off interpretations have been used in low-carbon binder systems, where reduced embodied carbon does not necessarily coincide with improved compressive strength.

The mechanical results did not show a clear strength-enhancing effect from COSP substitution under the present mixture design. The COSP-containing mixtures remained within a comparable low-strength range typical of stabilized earthen materials, but their mean compressive strengths were generally lower than that of the lime-based reference mixture30. Therefore, the mechanical results should not be used as a stand-alone basis for identifying a single optimum replacement level.

The EDS and SEM results suggest that COSP substitution influenced the elemental balance and local matrix morphology. The elemental ratio R = Ca/(Si + Al) varied non-monotonically across the substitution series, indicating that the calcium-related composition did not simply increase or decrease in proportion to the nominal COSP content. However, the mechanical comparison does not support a direct predictive relationship between R and compressive strength. Therefore, R is more appropriately interpreted as a descriptive compositional indicator for comparing elemental changes among mixtures.

SEM observations provided qualitative support for differences in matrix morphology. Some mixtures with intermediate R values showed locally denser-looking regions and more blurred particle boundaries, whereas other mixtures showed greater heterogeneity, pores, and microcrack-like features. However, these observations were qualitative and were not combined with quantitative pore-structure or phase analysis. For this reason, the SEM evidence should be used to support interpretation of local morphology rather than to rank mechanical performance or define a compositional threshold.

The carbon results were more consistent than the strength results. Under the adopted cradle-to-gate boundary, increasing COSP replacement reduced the calculated mass-based binder-related carbon emissions. This trend was strengthened under the low-carbon electricity scenario because COSP production was modeled as an electricity-dependent process31. However, the strength-normalized carbon intensity was influenced by both calculated carbon emissions and measured compressive strength. This explains why the CIS values did not follow a strictly monotonic pattern under all scenarios.

Overall, the results suggest that COSP can be considered as a potential low-carbon lime substitute in earthen building materials, but its use should be evaluated as a strength–carbon trade-off rather than as a direct strength-improvement strategy. The present dataset does not establish a universal optimum replacement level. Instead, it provides screening-level comparative evidence showing that COSP replacement can reduce calculated binder-related carbon emissions while maintaining compressive strength within a comparable range for stabilized earthen materials.

Interpretation of the substitution series

When the compressive-strength results, elemental ratio, SEM observations, and comparative carbon calculations are considered together, the substitution series suggests a clear distinction between environmental and mechanical responses. Increasing COSP replacement reduced the calculated mass-based carbon emissions, whereas the compressive-strength response did not follow the same regular trend.

This difference indicates that COSP substitution should be interpreted as a coupled strength–carbon comparison rather than as a simple linear optimization process. The carbon reduction was mainly controlled by the replacement of quicklime with COSP under the adopted emission factors and electricity scenarios. In contrast, compressive strength was influenced by multiple factors, including calcium-source replacement, particle packing, local matrix morphology, and experimental variability.

Within the present experimental series, the appropriate COSP replacement level should therefore be selected according to the required balance between acceptable mechanical performance and calculated carbon reduction. This interpretation is more suitable for the present screening-level dataset than defining a fixed replacement threshold based on compressive strength alone.

Limitations and implications

The scenario comparison showed that the calculated carbon results were sensitive to the assumed grid emission factor. Because COSP production in this study relied on electric calcination, lowering the grid carbon intensity reduced the calculated carbon burden of COSP-containing mixtures. Therefore, the low-carbon scenario should be regarded as an exploratory scenario analysis rather than as a forecast of future grid conditions. The carbon results should also be interpreted within the selected cradle-to-gate boundary, allocation assumptions, and simplified treatment of calcination yield. In particular, the 1:1 raw-shell-to-COSP mass assumption may underestimate the emission intensity per kg of finished COSP if the actual calcination yield is lower than 100%; therefore, the calculated values are used for comparative screening rather than absolute inventory reporting.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the carbon assessment focused on binder-related cradle-to-gate emissions and did not include transport, construction-stage processes, or use-stage effects. Second, the SEM and EDS results were used for qualitative microstructural and compositional interpretation rather than full reaction-product or pore-structure characterization. Third, although three specimens were prepared for each mixture, one A5 specimen was damaged and excluded; therefore, the A5 compressive-strength result is based on two valid specimens and is interpreted only as a full-replacement boundary case.

Despite these limitations, the results provide screening-level evidence that COSP can reduce calculated binder-related carbon emissions while maintaining compressive strength within a comparable range for stabilized earthen materials. Future work should include larger replicate numbers, longer-term durability testing, including wetting–drying resistance, water absorption, and dimensional stability, as well as quantitative pore/phase characterization using methods such as MIP, XRD, TG, or image-based pore analysis, together with more detailed yield-based carbon accounting of COSP production.

Conclusions

This study examined calcined oyster shell powder (COSP) as a substitute for lime in earthen building materials using a controlled substitution design and a simplified cradle-to-gate carbon assessment. Fresh-state behavior, replicate-level 28-day compressive strength, EDS-derived elemental ratio, SEM observations, and calculated binder-related carbon emissions were considered together.

COSP substitution caused only limited changes in mini-slump spread diameter, but the fresh-state morphology after vibration changed across the substitution series. Higher replacement levels showed greater retained height together with more visible cracking and localized shear features, indicating that mini-slump diameter alone did not fully capture the fresh-state changes induced by COSP substitution.

The compressive-strength results showed that the COSP-containing mixtures remained within a comparable low-strength range for stabilized earthen materials, but they did not show a clear strength-enhancing effect compared with the lime-based reference mixture. Because the differences among several mixtures were accompanied by experimental variability, the results do not support defining a statistically validated optimum replacement level based on compressive strength alone. A5 was interpreted only as a full-replacement boundary case because one specimen was damaged and only two valid strength results were available.

The elemental ratio R = Ca/(Si + Al) varied non-monotonically across the substitution series. SEM observations suggested differences in local matrix compactness and heterogeneity among mixtures, but these observations were qualitative and did not establish a direct predictive relationship between R and compressive strength.

Under both the baseline and low-carbon electricity scenarios, the calculated mass-based binder-related carbon emissions decreased with increasing COSP replacement. This was the most consistent trend observed in the study. When normalized by compressive strength, the carbon intensity results became more sensitive to the measured mechanical response, indicating that COSP substitution should be evaluated as a strength–carbon trade-off.

Overall, COSP showed potential for reducing calculated binder-related carbon emissions in earthen building materials, but the mechanical response remained variable and did not identify a universal optimum replacement level. The findings provide screening-level comparative evidence for evaluating shell-derived calcium substitution in earthen materials, while also showing that future optimization should consider mechanical performance, elemental balance, and carbon-accounting assumptions together. Future research should incorporate larger replicate numbers, longer-term durability testing, quantitative pore/phase characterization, transportation-stage emissions, and improved yield-based accounting of COSP production.
