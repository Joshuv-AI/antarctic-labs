<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., September 3, 2025</strong> - Covert CIA surveillance of a Soviet agent under diplomatic cover in Mexico City in 1968 led to the acquisition of intelligence on the “entire clandestine leadership” of the Colombian Communist Party and the nascent FARC insurgent group, according to declassified records from the John F. Kennedy assassination papers released earlier this year. Colombian government operations based on that intelligence led to the “roll up” of the Communist Party’s “clandestine printing press,” which was used to forge passports and other documents, according to a selection of CIA cables from the JFK documents published here today by the National Security Archive. (Documents 5-13)</p><p>Another group of records tells the story of a high-level defector from Castro’s Cuba who for several years ran a covert CIA listening post in Bogotá targeting the office of Novosti, the Soviet news agency that was closely associated with the KGB. The eavesdropping operation was led by former Cuban military official Manuel Villafana Martínez, who years earlier had been among the Cuban exiles involved in the failed April 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion. Villafana worked with another Cuban expatriate, Manuel Machado Llosa, the former treasurer of Castro’s revolutionary 26th of July Movement who ran a print shop in Colombia that provided cover for Villafana’s intelligence operation against Novosti. (Documents 14-20)</p><div style="background-color:#F5BC9A;margin:0px;padding:15px;"><article class="media media--type-image media--view-mode-full-image">fana’s intelligence operation against Novosti. <p><em>Document 14: A 1970 cable from the CIA station in Bogotá sheds light on Agency targeting of Soviet communications in Colombia.</em></p></div><p>The thousands of JFK records released earlier this year, including almost all the documents that remained classified in whole or in part, have not produced any bombshell revelations about the assassination of President Kennedy or changed the general consensus that Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone in killing JFK. Instead, the most interesting documents by far are the thousands of CIA cables, memos, case files and other records describing heretofore undisclosed covert operations and undercover intelligence agents that were believed to have some possible connection—however tangential—to the Kennedy assassination. Among these are scores of fascinating new documents about CIA operations in Colombia and the rest of Latin America that were intended to both monitor and counter Soviet and Cuban intelligence operations and support for leftist groups.</p><p>While the Colombian news media has reported on some of the newly released assassination papers—and some <a href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/HITLER%2C%20ADOLF_0003.pdf">downright bizarre</a> documents found in the CIA’s CREST database—this Electronic Briefing Book highlights some intriguing new declassified details from the JFK collection on Cold War spy operations in Colombia during the 1960s and 70s that other news organizations may have missed.</p><div style="background-color:#F5BC9A;margin:0px;padding:15px;"><article class="media media--type-image media--view-mode-full-image">the 1960s and 70s that other news organizations may have missed.</p><div style="background-color:#F5BC9A;margin:0px;padding:15px;"><article class="media media--type-image media--view-mode-full-image">

<p><em>Document 9: In a memo to Ambassador Charles Freeman, the CIA’s Mexico City station chief provides some details on how its surveillance of Soviet official Nikolay Leonov in Mexico City led to a big intelligence haul against the clandestine leadership of the FARC and the Colombian Communist Party.</em></p></div><p>One group of records details U.S. efforts to track Cuban “subversion” in Colombia during 1963, less than a year after the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962. By early February 1963, for example, the CIA had a report from a “fairly reliable” German official source that a top Cuban military intelligence official named Maximo Gruber had “been appointed technical leader of the Communist guerrillas in Sumapaz, Colombia,” a key early stronghold of the FARC guerrillas. (Document 1) An October 1963 FBI memo found in the collection describes how CIA interception of mail and radio communications in Colombia led one CIA analyst to determine that legendary Cuban intelligence chief Manuel Pinerio was in contact with an undercover agent known as “Roberto” who was living in Colombia near the Venezuelan border. (Document 4)</p><p>The CIA continually monitored the travel of Colombian (and other Latin American) nationals to and from Cuba during this period, tracked Soviet and Cuban funding of Colombian leftist groups, collected what scant evidence there was linking Cuba to Colombian insurgent groups, and secretly worked with civilian anti-communist groups. A 1963 report prepared for the Joint Chiefs of Staff from the “Sub-Committee on Cuban Subversion” said that leads developed by one “returnee” from Cuba had led the Bogotá station to investigate “members of both the Colombian Congress and the Bogota municipal government.” In another operation, the CIA had “subsidized and directed” the activities of a “Colombian Civic Action Group, composed of influential local businessmen,” that had “established an anti-Castro propaganda unit” and that was “actively collecting evidence of Cuban subversion in Colombia to support its propaganda campaign.” (Document 3)</p><p>Other records show that the CIA also fought the Cold War in the Colombian news media through covert propaganda operations to secretly plant information in the press. One CIA “prop play” described in the JFK papers was aimed at exposing secret Soviet efforts to support communist parties and revolutionary groups in Latin America at precisely the moment that Colombia was reestablishing relations with the Soviets after 20 years with no formal diplomatic representation.</p><div style="background-color:#F5BC9A;margin:0px;padding:15px;"><article class="media media--type-image media--view-mode-full-image">Soviets after 20 years with no formal diplomatic representation.</p><div style="background-color:#F5BC9A;margin:0px;padding:15px;"><article class="media media--type-image media--view-mode-full-image">

<p><em>Document 13: In a 1969 cable, the CIA station in Colombia reports that it has "placed articles in 'La Republica' and 'El Siglo'" identifying the Soviet official behind the financing of communist and insurgent groups in Colombia.</em></p></div><p>The operation began to take shape in April 1968 after the CIA’s Mexico City station observed legendary KGB Latin America hand Nikolay Leonov passing money to Colombian revolutionary figure Feliciano Pachón and an associate.

The Agency surveillance of Leonov led to the immediate arrests of the two couriers upon their return to Bogotá, seizure of $100,000 cash, and a treasure trove of intelligence about the clandestine leadership of the FARC and the Colombian Communist Party.</p><p>The next month, a cable from the Bogotá station described the CIA effort to secretly influence Colombian news reports to link the arrests and the subsequent “roll up” of the Communist Party leadership to a larger Soviet effort to support communist revolutionaries in Latin America.

The initial series of “distorted, fuzzy conflicting” articles had included the “insertion” of information on “the uncovering of a vast subversive net” across the region supported by the Soviets.

A new article published that day in <em>El Tiempo</em> and excerpted in the cable included important new details, based on “sources worthy of all credit,” indicating that Pachón had received the money “in Mexico from the hands of a functionary of the Soviet Embassy in Cuba,” a fact that was probably not widely known outside of a relatively small group of officials at the CIA.

In Colombia, Villafana used the pseudonym “Errol Lythgoe,” and cover for GICITRON was provided by another well-connected Cuban defector, Manuel Machado Llosa, the former treasurer of Castro’s revolutionary 26th of July Movement. As “GICITRON-4,” Machado managed the Colombian affiliate of a Mexican printing company that was used as a front for CIA operations.&nbsp;</p><p>By the 1970s, with more and more countries having recognized Cuba and invited Cuban diplomats to their capital cities, the CIA had grown wary of relying on Cuban exiles for intelligence operations in Latin America. “[W]e would do well to begin a gradual cut-back in our use of Cubans given the targetting [sic] that we presume will be made on them as the Cuban CI [counterintelligence] effort begins in the various countries,” reported CIA headquarters in a draft memo found among the JFK papers. The scaling back of the use of Cuban agents should be “keyed initially to where the official Cuban presence is,” the cable reads, and should therefore start with Peru, although Cuban agents working in Colombia, including “GICITRON-4” and “LITHGOE” are also mentioned in the document. (Document 20)</p><p>The previous month, CIA headquarters had cabled the Bogotá station to inquire about the status of CIA-managed Cuban agents in Colombia and to “assess threat growing PBRUMEN diplomatic presence in hemisphere creates for BKTRUST employed PBRUMEN assets,” using established Agency cryptonyms for Cuba (“PBRUMEN”) and CIA (“BKTRUST”). The message specifically asked about the status of four known Cuban CIA assets in Colombia: “Errol A. Lythgoe, Reginald M. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In apparent reply to the CIA headquarters cable of August 7 on the recommended reduction in reliance on Cuban agents, this memo describes how the individual known as GICITRON-4, Manuel Machado Llosa, is used by the Bogota Station. Machado provides “cover, status and funds for an operation handled by Errol R. Lithgoe,” the pseudonym of Manuel Villafana, “also a Cuban, who is nominally Subject’s partner in the business enterprise.” Although “he was involved in several clandestine Cuban activities while treasurer of the 26th of July Movement during 1957-58,” the CIA says that Machado “has received no training in clandestine operations” from the CIA. In any case, since Machado “provides cover for LITHGOE, he is not required to utilize any tradecraft,” adding that, “Having his own company provides for adequate cover.”</p></div>

In response, the undisclosed author of the memo asserts that: “[W]e would do well to begin a gradual cut-back in our use of Cubans, given the targetting [sic] that we presume will be made on them as the Cuban CI [counter-intelligence] effort begins in the various countries,” but the reduction in the use of Cuban agents should be “keyed initially to where the official Cuban presence is” and should therefore start with Peru. A seemingly disconnected section indicates that, “GICITRON-4, who has been Treasurer of the 26th July movement in the period 1957-58 provides cover for LITHGOE.”</p></div>
