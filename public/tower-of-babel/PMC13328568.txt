Fibroblastic aspartoacylase suppresses TGFβ-mediated responses and cancer progression
Authors: Ianire Astobiza, Catalina Capó-Serra, Cristina Viera, Javier Rodríguez, Patricia Carnicero, Miguel Juliá, Ainara Martinez, Carmen Pérez-López, María Subijana, Carolina Ortiz-Sanz, Saioa Garcia-Longarte, Isabel Mendizabal, Emily J Kay, Carla Riera-Domingo, Silvia Rivis, Onintza Carlevaris, Leire Egia-Mendikute, Sara Cascais, Natalia Martín-Martín, Sonia Fernandez-Ruiz, Veronica Torrano, Jana R Crespo, Mikel Pujana-Vaquerizo, Elisa Espinet, Andreas Trumpp, Noemi Eiro, Francisco J Vizoso, Ana Vivancos, Joan Seoane, David Gonzalo, Sofia Rey, Aida Santos-Martin, Aitziber Ugalde-Olano, Claudia Manini, Jose I Lopez, Diana Cabrera, Sebastian M Van Liempd, Juan M Falcon-Perez, Roger R Gomis, Asís Palazon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, and the field has predominantly focused on investigating metabolic alterations in tumour cells. However, the relevance, mechanism and consequences of metabolic adaptations in stromal cells remain understudied. Here, we identify aspartoacylase (ASPA) as a metabolic enzyme consistently repressed in tumour stroma and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). Importantly, we report a reciprocal crosstalk between ASPA and Transforming Growth Factor Beta (TGFβ) signalling that influences fibroblast behaviour. TGFβ suppresses ASPA expression in fibroblasts, whereas ASPA restrains TGFβ-dependent myofibroblast conversion, extracelullar cell matrix (ECM) remodelling, angiogenesis and pro-tumoral macrophage phenotypes. Analyses of human specimens revealed a strong negative prognostic value for ASPA in different tumour types, associated with TGFβ signalling levels and the generation of aggressive pro-tumoral responses. Our findings unveil ASPA expression in fibroblasts as a gatekeeper of TGFβ responses and activation in cancer progression.Subject terms: Cancer metabolism, Cancer microenvironment, Mechanisms of disease

Introduction

Tumour cells exploit diverse metabolic strategies to support uncontrolled proliferation, resist cell death, and adapt to fluctuating nutrient and oxygen availability1. Traditionally, studies have focused on cancer cell-autonomous metabolic alterations that support tumour pathogenesis, progression and resistance to therapy1,2. However, tumours are not composed of cancer cells in isolation. Instead, they function as complex ecosystems in which malignant, stromal, and immune cells engage in dynamic and reciprocal interactions that influence tumour characteristics3. Within this heterogeneous microenvironment, nutrient availability, waste accumulation, and metabolic by-products shape not only the behaviour of malignant cells, but also impact tumour microenvironment (TME) components and the overall trajectory of the disease4,5. However, how metabolic alterations shape the behaviour of stromal cells is not well understood.

Among the various stromal components of the TME, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) represent a predominant and functionally versatile population that plays a central role in supporting tumour growth, remodelling the extracellular matrix (ECM), regulating angiogenesis, and modulating immune responses6. CAFs do not represent a uniform cell type, but rather encompass a spectrum of phenotypic states that arise in response to contextual cues such as TGFβ, interleukin-1 (IL-1), hypoxia, and mechanical stress7. These cues drive the activation and differentiation of CAFs into various subtypes, including myofibroblastic (myCAF), inflammatory (iCAF), and antigen-presenting (apCAF) populations, each contributing uniquely to tumour progression and therapy resistance. In addition to their structural and signalling roles, CAFs exert substantial influence over the metabolic landscape of the TME. Through reciprocal metabolic interactions with cancer cells and immune populations, CAFs can supply key nutrients, modulate oxidative stress, and recycle toxic metabolic wastes into energetic substances, allowing cancer cells to undergo biosynthesis8. Moreover, CAFs may compete with cancer and TME cells for specific nutrients to meet the bioenergetic demands of their pathologically activated status5. These metabolic exchanges are increasingly recognized as a hallmark of CAF function. However, the regulatory mechanisms and metabolic enzymes that govern this crosstalk remain incompletely characterized.

Aspartoacylase (ASPA) is a cytosolic enzyme primarily known for its role in the central nervous system (CNS), where it catalyses the hydrolysis of N-acetylaspartate (NAA) into aspartate and acetate—a process critical for myelin lipid synthesis and neuronal function9. Recent studies have begun to implicate ASPA in broader metabolic processes, and emerging evidence suggests that it may play an underappreciated role in non-neuronal tissues, including adipocytes10,11 and cancer cells12. In cancer, high NAA levels have been associated with disease progression13. Impeding NAA production in ovarian cancer cells can affect glucose and glutamine metabolism, reduce tumour growth and influence pro-tumoral macrophage polarization13,14.

Here, we demonstrate that tumours consistently exhibit downregulation of ASPA in stromal fibroblasts, and that loss of ASPA regulates fibroblast activation and cancer progression, which is reflected in the strong negative prognostic value of the expression of this enzyme in human disease.

Results

Human tumour stroma and CAFs display reduced expression of ASPA

In search of new factors associated with aberrant TMEs, we interrogated stromal gene expression datasets from human breast (BCa), colorectal (CRC), ovarian (OvCa), lung (LCa) and prostate cancer (PCa) stroma against normal stroma (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). This approach identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that were consistently altered in tumour stroma, including 13 upregulated and 19 downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). Transcripts upregulated in tumour stroma included genes encoding for proteins with ECM organization (COL3A1, COL11A1, COL12A1, MMP11, CTHRC1, ITGB5), underlying the well-characterized desmoplastic nature of most TMEs15. Downregulated transcripts were more heterogenous, including genes encoding for transcriptional repressors (SIN3A, HLF, EFCAB6, LRRFIP1), for proteins with potential transcriptional-translational activity that are not well characterized (EIF4EBP2, AFF4, EFCAB2, L3MBTL4), or for proteins with metabolic functions (SIN3A, PLD1, ASPA, LDLR). Beyond antitumoral immune responses, the tumour suppressive features of stromal cells are not well delineated, so we decided to focus on downregulated genes. Among them, we selected ASPA (encoding for the metabolic enzyme aspartoacylase) (Fig. 1A) since (i) it exhibited robust and consistent downregulation in the stroma of all tumours analysed; and (ii) it was recently shown to have a tumour suppressive role in cancer cells12, but less is known about its function in cancer stroma. ASPA catalyses the deacetylation of N-acetylaspartate (NAA), an amino acid derivative highly abundant in the central nervous system (CNS), to generate free acetate in the brain, showing a high level of activity localized primarily in oligodendrocytes9. Mutations in ASPA cause Canavan disease (OMIM ID#271900), a lethal autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder, which is associated with insufficient myelin synthesis resulting from a deficiency in the supply of NAA-derived acetate9. Employing public datasets, we confirmed that both mRNA and protein levels of ASPA are consistently downregulated in different tumour types (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F). Initial analyses also hinted at a preferential immunoreactivity of ASPA antibodies (validated in the corpus callosum of rat brain tissue, Supplementary Fig. 1G) in the stromal compartment in normal tissues that was reduced in tumour stroma (Supplementary Fig. 1H). Similar to gene expression levels, we observed a marked downregulation of ASPA protein expression in the stromal compartment of human PCa specimens and murine BCa models compared to normal stroma (Fig. 1B, C). Contrary to a previous report12, no staining was observed in normal or cancerous epithelia. NAA is produced from acetyl-CoA and aspartate by NAT8L9 (Supplementary Fig. 1I). Alternatively, NAA can be interconverted to or from N-acetyl-aspartate-glutamate (NAAG) by RIMKLB or FOLH1/NAALAD2, respectively. When monitoring the complete metabolic pathway regulating NAA levels, no consistent expression changes were observed in the stroma of different tumour types beyond ASPA (Supplementary Fig. 1J), suggesting a distinct role for this enzyme. Next, we explored the cellular compartment contributing to ASPA expression using single-cell RNA-Seq (scRNA-Seq). First, we performed scRNA-Seq in a cancer adjacent region of a PCa patient, showing that ASPA expression was exclusively detected in fibroblasts (Fig. 1D). This result was validated using a public human BCa scRNA-Seq dataset, where ASPA expression was also restricted to fibroblast populations (Fig. 1E). NAT8L, FOLH1, NAALAD2 and RIMKLB exhibited inconsistent expression patterns in both datasets, with immune, epithelial and endothelial compartments showing higher expression. Moreover, analyses of scRNA-Seq from normal bladder mucosa and bladder cancer (BLCa) specimens showed a higher expression of ASPA in normal fibroblasts (NFs) and a relevant reduction in CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Next, we used flow cytometry-based cell selection coupled with gene expression analyses as a complementary approach to study the expression of ASPA in a cell compartment-specific manner, employing human PDAC specimens and BCa murine models. These analyses confirmed that fibroblasts were the main cell type expressing ASPA, and that CAFs exhibited a remarkable downregulation (Fig. 1F). Co-immunostaining of ASPA with the fibroblast marker vimentin revealed a preferential expression in fibroblasts in normal human mammary glands, and a reduction in fibroblasts adjacent to or within BCa regions (Fig. 1G). Similar findings were obtained in murine models, which also showed no relevant expression in vimentin-negative cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Of note, we confirmed that ASPA expression in benign PCa was mainly stromal (i.e. undetected in CK18-positive epithelial cells) and significantly reduced after malignant progression (Fig. 1H). To validate these findings, we turned to tractable models of NFs/CAFs. Initial analyses showed a consistent downregulation of ASPA expression in CAFs from different tumour types when compared to NFs (Fig. 1I). Employing both human and murine BCa and PCa models, we confirmed that ASPA expression is indeed reduced at both protein and mRNA levels in established CAF populations (Fig. 1J, K and Supplementary Fig. 2C, D), in line with the predicted increase in the accumulation of its substrate, NAA (Fig. 1L). ASPA protein levels in mNF1 and mCAF1 were independent of serum levels in culture (Fig. 1M) and similar results were obtained in murine mammary NF/CAFs freshly isolated from tumours (Supplementary Fig. 2E), ruling out any potential in vitro artefact. ASPA expression was low in cells positive for established markers of fibroblast activation such as αSMA, both in isolated cells and in murine normal mammary glands and carcinomas (Fig. 1N and Supplementary Fig. 2F, G). These results demonstrate a loss of ASPA expression in fibroblasts associated with myofibroblast conversion, as opposed to the reported downregulation of ASPA in the epithelial compartment in cancer12. To further compare the relative contribution of epithelial vs fibroblast ASPA expression in tumours, we measured its protein levels in multiple PCa and fibroblast cell lines, confirming predominant expression in fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 2H). Collectively, our data suggest that the metabolic enzyme ASPA is primarily expressed by fibroblasts outside the CNS and that it is consistently downregulated in tumour stroma and CAFs, suggesting a potential role in the generation of aberrant TMEs. Notably, ASPA protein and mRNA levels display a very low level of expression in normal and cancerous epithelia, suggesting a specific role in modulating fibroblast behaviour.

ASPA repression is associated with myCAF phenotypes downstream of TGFβ signalling

Next, we set out to explore the mechanisms regulating ASPA expression in fibroblasts. Recent studies suggest that CAFs are a collection of cells with diverse molecular features7. The current consensus groups them in populations enriched in ECM production with contractile properties that primarily emerge from TGFβ signalling activation (myCAFs); populations that produce high levels of chemokines/cytokines in response to inflammatory signals (iCAFs); and emerging subtypes characterized by vascular phenotypes (vascular CAFs/vCAFs) or antigen-presentation features (antigen-presenting CAFs/apCAFs). Each subtype may emerge in response to specific signalling cues7 (Fig. 2A). Leveraging the BCa scRNA-Seq dataset, we observed that ASPA expression was heterogenous, with myCAF subtypes presenting lower levels of expression (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 3A). ASPA mRNA levels negatively correlated with myCAF markers and processes in this analysis (Fig. 2B), as well as in datasets of NF/CAFs from different tumour types (Supplementary Fig. 3B). In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed a negative association of ASPA mRNA levels with TGFβ signatures in human stromal and NF/CAF datasets, whereas it had a positive association with NF phenotype gene signatures (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Therefore, we hypothesised that TGFβ activation leads to the transcriptional repression of ASPA in NFs. To test this possibility, we first subjected murine mammary NFs to stimulation with a different set of modulating factors (Fig. 2A). We observed that only TGFβ1 stimulation downregulated ASPA mRNA and protein expression, which was concomitant with an activation of TGFβ/SMAD3 signalling and upregulation of αSMA (Fig. 2C, D and Supplementary Fig. 3D). Similar findings were obtained with human NFs from breast, colon and prostate tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3E–G). Interrogation of independent human datasets of human prostate NFs confirmed that ASPA expression was reduced upon TGFβ1 treatment and not by IL6 (Supplementary Fig. 3H). Consistently, incubation of murine and human NFs with SIS3, a SMAD3 inhibitor, blocked ASPA mRNA downregulation by TGFβ1 (Fig. 2E, F). These results demonstrate that ASPA is transcriptionally repressed by TGFβ. To assess the potential mechanism of TGFβ-induced ASPA repression in cancer, we analysed human BCa and PCa scRNA-Seq datasets. This showed that TGFβ ligands can be expressed by different cells, with TGFB1 primarily expressed by immune cells (including macrophages) and TGFB2 and TGFB3 by fibroblasts and epithelial/cancer cells (Fig. 2G). To independently validate this result, we employed: (i) stablished murine models of BCa cancer cells (TS1); (ii) macrophages (RAW 264.7) at basal state (M0) and subjected to stimulations to promote M2-like states (M2); and (iii) our mNF1/mCAF1 cells in normal culture conditions (See Methods for details). Analysis of Tgfb1, 2, 3 by RT-qPCR in these models confirmed the preferential expression of Tgfb1 by macrophages, Tgfb2 by cancer cells and Tgfb3 by CAFs (Fig. 2H). Incubation of murine NFs with conditioned media (CM) from these models showed that M2-polarised macrophages had the greatest impact on Aspa repression in NFs, similar to levels induced by TGFβ1 (Fig. 2I), suggesting that ASPA downregulation is associated with inflammatory processes at early stages of tumour development (Fig. 2J). TGFβ/SMAD3-dependent transcriptional repression may require the activity of histone deacetylases (HDACs)16, which participate in epigenetic rearrangements modulating gene expression. Epigenomic analysis of the 3’ regulatory regions at the Aspa locus in murine CAFs, as compared to NFs, revealed a selective loss of H3K27Ac (Fig. 2K), an epigenetic mark of active enhancers. As expected, bona fide CAF marker genes such as Acta2, Fap, Lrrc15 and others presented increased H3K27Ac signal at regulatory regions in mCAF1, whereas peaks at NF marker genes (Cxcl1 and Pi16) were decreased (Supplementary Fig. 3I). ReMap analysis17 of the H3K27Ac peaks in Aspa promoter and enhancers identified SMAD2/3 motifs overlapping HDAC1-3 cis regulatory modules (CRMs) that constitute potential TGFβ inhibitory elements. Moreover, GSEA using a signature of genes repressed by HDAC1 and 2 in MEFs demonstrated the inverse association of ASPA levels and HDAC1/2 activities in NF/CAFs from different tumour types (Fig S3J). These results suggest a model whereby a TGFβ-mediated repression mechanism promotes the loss of ASPA in fibroblasts (Fig. 2L). In line with this idea, incubation of murine NFs with pan-HDAC inhibitors such as trichostatin A (TSA), or class I-specific HDAC inhibitors (PD106 and Romidepsin), abolished ASPA protein repression by TGFβ1 (Supplementary Fig. 3K). This finding was validated at the level of mRNA expression with PD106 in murine NFs (Fig. 2M). Importantly, incubation of unstimulated murine CAFs with both TSA and PD106 induced ASPA protein and mRNA expression, respectively (Fig. 2N and Supplementary Fig. 3L). Altogether, these results demonstrate the critical role of HDAC-mediated repression in the modulation of ASPA during CAF emergence, both downstream of TGFβ/SMAD3 and through epigenetic rearrangements.

ASPA loss in stromal fibroblasts induces myofibroblast conversion

To explore the role of ASPA in the function and regulation of fibroblasts, we first characterised the murine CAF system after ASPA ectopic expression. Analyses of cell growth in vitro showed no differences between control and ASPA-expressing murine CAFs in normal culture conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Furthermore, expression of ASPA in different mCAF1 clones did not affect the expression of myofibroblast marker αSMA, suggesting no observable effect on CAF activation (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Functional analyses of ECM remodelling activities employing the collagen: matrigel contraction assay, which measures the ability of a cell to remodel collagen by analysing changes in collagen plug area (Supplementary Fig. 4C) showed no relevant differences after ASPA expression in CAFs, or observable consequences in any system after addition of NAA (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Similar findings were obtained when the ability of CAFs to support murine BCa cell growth in 2D co-culture assays (Supplementary Fig. 4E) was analysed (Supplementary Fig. 4F, G). Together, these results indicate that recovery of ASPA expression in pathologically activated CAFs has no relevant phenotypic or functional consequences.

Next, we assessed whether ASPA downregulation played any role in early stages of fibroblast activation by knocking down Aspa expression in murine normal mammary gland fibroblasts (mNF) with two independent shRNAs. This approach reduced ASPA levels to those of CAFs (Fig. 3A) and was associated with a significant increase in NAA levels, indicative of defects in NAA metabolism (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, depletion of ASPA in mNF1 was associated with an increase in αSMA expression, and NAA addition in control or ASPA-silenced mNF1 had no effect in αSMA levels (Fig. 3A). Moreover, gene expression analyses confirmed that mNF1 present a general upregulation in the expression of myofibroblastic/myCAF genes after Aspa knock-down (Fig. 3C). These data suggest that ASPA may be suppressing fibroblast activation towards myofibroblast phenotypes, possibly through NAA-independent mechanisms. To test this notion, we investigated the ECM remodelling and promotion of cancer cell growth abilities, which are generally attributed to myofibroblast/myCAF phenotypes6,18 (Supplementary Fig. 4C–E). These analyses demonstrated that silencing Aspa expression in murine NFs increased these activities (Fig. 3D–F). Again, NAA supplementation had no overall effect on the evaluated parameters (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, we noticed that Aspa depletion potentiated the effects of TGFβ1 stimulation in gel contraction assays. Knocking down Aspa in mNF1 elicited a tumour-promoting phenotype as informed by in vivo co-injection assays (Fig. 3G), reducing tumour latency and accelerating tumour growth (Fig. 3H–J). Mammary tumours generated by co-injection of Aspa-silenced mNF1 were more desmoplastic when compared to control-mNF1 counterparts, as shown by the significant increase in fibronectin content and collagen deposition (Fig. 3K, L), αSMA expression (Fig. 3K), angiogenesis (Fig. 3M), and the presence of CD206+ macrophages (Fig. 3N). Altogether, these results indicate that ASPA expression restrains fibroblast activation and the generation of pro-tumorigenic fibroinflammatory phenotypes.

ASPA represses TGFβ signalling in stromal fibroblasts

To shed light on the mechanisms whereby ASPA affected fibroblast behaviour, we performed transcriptomic analyses of mNF1 after knockingdown Aspa with two independent shRNAs. Principal Component analysis (PCA) of RNA-Seq data showed that Aspa-depleted NFs presented a distinct transcriptional landscape apart from control NFs (Supplementary Fig. 5A), in line with our functional observations. This was also evidenced by the number of up- or downregulated DEGs after Aspa silencing (Supplementary Fig. 5B and Supplementary Data 1). Amongst the genes consistently upregulated after Aspa depletion, we observed genes associated with ECM remodelling, TGFβ and Wnt targets, as well as genes upregulated in BCa stroma and myCAFs (Fig. 4A). These data prompted us to perform GSEA against public databases (MSigDB ‘Hallmarks’ and ‘CTD’) and gene sets associated with processes related to fibroblast activation/CAFs (‘Curated’). This analysis showed that genes downregulated after Aspa knockdown were significantly associated with metabolic processes, including fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation and synthesis of amino acids (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Conversely, genes upregulated in Aspa-depleted mNF1 were markedly enriched in signalling pathways and biological processes associated with fibroblast activation, which included signatures of collagen production and modification, EMT, myCAF phenotypes and BCa stromal signatures (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). Of note, we observed a relevant upregulation of TGFβ and Wnt signalling signatures, as well as gene sets associated with ECM regulation after Aspa depletion in mNF1 (Fig. 4B). Together, these results suggest that Aspa represses signalling cascades associated with fibroblast activation, such as TGFβ and Wnt/β-catenin19,20 to modulate fibroblast behaviour. Using orthogonal approaches, we confirmed that Aspa knockdown in mNF1 increased activated levels of β-catenin and the transcriptional activity of TGFβ/SMAD3 in basal conditions (Fig. 4C, D). In line with functional results, Aspa-depleted mNF1 also presented increased SMAD2/3 transcriptional activity in response to TGFβ stimulation (Fig. 4D). In addition, stratification of fibroblasts from BCa and PCa scRNA-Seq datasets based on ASPA expression showed a negative association between ASPA levels and activation of TGFβ and Wnt signalling (Fig. 4E), or EMT and desmoplastic CAF signatures (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Of note, signatures for NF phenotypes were significantly upregulated in ASPA-high CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 5E). There were no relevant associations with other signatures related to CAFs such as NOTCH and NFκB (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Hierarchical analyses indicated that Wnt/β-catenin activation was downstream TGFβ signalling in this system, as the TGFBR inhibitor SB431542 inhibited both β-catenin and SMAD2/3 activation after Aspa silencing (Fig. 4F, G). In agreement, we observed that blocking TGFβ signalling reversed the functional activation elicited by Aspa knockdown in mNF1 at the level of αSMA expression (Fig. 4F) and ECM remodeling/gel contraction (Fig. 4H). Wnt/β-catenin inhibition with XAV939 had no functional consequences (Fig. 4H).

These data indicate that ASPA may be tuning the responsiveness of stromal fibroblasts to TGFβ stimulation. Together with previous data showing how TGFβ1 reduced ASPA expression, our results suggest that ASPA may be functioning as a critical gatekeeper of TGFβ responses in fibroblasts, and that ASPA loss is a critical step to elicit full myofibroblast activation. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the effects of constitutive ASPA expression in TGFβ1-induced myofibroblast activation of mNF1 (Fig. 4I). Gene expression analyses revealed that constitutive ectopic expression of ASPA reduced the upregulation of myofibroblast gene expression induced by TGFβ1 (Fig. 4J). GSEA analyses showed that gene sets associated with Wnt and TGFβ activation, EMT and desmoplastic CAF function were also significantly affected by constitutive expression of ASPA in TGFβ1-stimulated mNF1 (Supplementary Fig. 5F, G). In agreement, orthogonal analyses showed that ectopic expression of ASPA negatively impacted the transcriptional activity of TGFβ/SMAD3 in TGFβ1-stimulated mNF1 (Fig. 4J, K). These effects were accompanied by a substantial reduction in myofibroblastic functions such as gel remodelling activity (Fig. 4L). Moreover, to examine whether these effects relied on the catabolic activity of ASPA, we generated a loss-of-function E178D mutation of ASPA (Glu178Asp, ASPAMUT), which has been associated with Canavan disease and that substantially diminishes its enzymatic activity towards NAA21 (Supplementary Fig. 5H). Notably, constitutive ectopic expression of ASPAMUT behaved similarly to wild-type ASPA (ASPAWT) in all evaluated features (Fig. 4K, L), underlying a role of ASPA independent of NAA metabolism in restraining fibroblast activation.

Association between stromal ASPA levels and prognosis and fibroinflammation in cancer patients

To investigate the relevance of our findings in human cancer, we first evaluated the prognostic value of ASPA bulk mRNA expression in BCa and PCa patients. In both cases, low ASPA expression was associated with poorer disease-free survival in independent public datasets and with different stratification strategies (Fig. 5A, B and Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Importantly, ASPA repression was significantly associated with disease progression in various PCa datasets that contained information on metastatic specimens22–24, thus reinforcing its relevance in cancer aggressiveness (Supplementary Fig. 6C). We validated the bioinformatics strategy in an independent cohort composed of 140 cases with benign hyperplasia (BPH) or PCa (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Using nCounter from Nanostring, we corroborated that ASPA expression was reduced in PCa specimens, and that its repression was associated with shorter time to biochemical recurrence (Supplementary Fig. 6E, F). At the protein level, we observed that non-cancerous tissue consistently exhibited the highest protein immunoreactivity (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 6G) and that ASPA expression stratified patients with better clinical outcomes after surgery (Fig. 5C). We previously showed that ASPA expression is restricted to stromal regions in normal tissues and repressed in cancer stroma (Fig. 1). Accordingly, stromal ASPA expression also presented prognostic value in both PCa and BCa specimens at the level of protein or mRNA expression, respectively (Fig. 5D, E). Together, our results reveal that the tumour suppressive activity of ASPA is translated into prognostic potential in bulk molecular or histological analyses.

Next, we investigated the relationship between ASPA expression and processes associated with its function in CAFs, including TGFβ signalling activation, CAF phenotypes and pro-tumoural macrophage content. These analyses showed that ASPA-high PCa and BCa tumours displayed significantly lower levels of TGFβ activity, expression of desmoplastic myCAF gene sets, and gene signatures associated with tumour aggressiveness (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 6H). Conversely, ASPA loss was associated with a decrease in signatures associated with NF phenotypes (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 6H) and a concomitant increase in M2 pro-tumoral macrophage content (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 6I). As we have shown, ASPA downregulation can be a consequence of increased TGFβ1 levels in the TME, which may also impact some of the processes under investigation. This raises the possibility that our observations are not directly linked to ASPA activity in CAFs but rather through TGFβ-dependent responses. To address this issue, we generated a gene signature containing the top downregulated genes after ASPA depletion in murine NFs as a surrogate for ASPA activity (Supplementary Fig. 7A), obtaining similar results to ASPA expression in PCa and BCa (Supplementary Fig. 7B–D). Finally, we leveraged a BCa dataset containing RNA and metabolite measurements in cancerous and pathologically normal adjacent tissues (Fig. 5H)25. These analyses showed that ASPA downregulation in cancer is associated with an accumulation of NAA (Fig. 5I, J), showing a negative correlation between both parameters (Fig. 5K). Importantly, stratification based on NAA levels showed a significant increase in M2 macrophage content in NAA-high cancerous tissues (in line with the role of the metabolite in macrophage polarization14, Fig. 5L), which were also associated with poorer patient prognosis (Fig. 5M).

Altogether, our observations stablish stromal ASPA as a multifaceted tumour suppressor gene affecting fibroblast behaviour. Conceptually, these findings inform a framework whereby ASPA operates as a critical TME regulator by repressing fibroblast activation downstream of TGFβ signals through a mechanism independent from its metabolic activity.

Discussion

Given the crucial role of the TME in tumour progression, understanding the mechanisms governing their tumour-regulatory properties is essential for the refinement of prognostic and therapeutic strategies, as exemplified by immunotherapies and associated biomarkers26. Reciprocal metabolic interactions between tumour and the TME are emerging as key mechanisms influencing tumour characteristics4,5, both fuelling cancer cell fitness as well as altering the behaviour of cancer and stromal cells27–30. In this study, we provide evidence that repression of ASPA in stromal fibroblasts unleashes cell-autonomous TGFβ-SMAD2/3 signalling leading to pro-tumoral myCAF phenotypes. These findings represent an unappreciated role for ASPA in the TME that underlie the complex and intertwined nature of tumour-stroma interactions in human cancer.

Through a comprehensive analysis of altered genes in stroma, we uncovered that ASPA is consistently repressed in cancer stroma and CAFs across tumour types. A recent study proposed that ASPA repression was a key process in PCa cells promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion12. No assessment of ASPA expression in stroma or cellular components of the TME was reported. Despite our efforts, we were unable to corroborate a dominant role for ASPA or NAA in PCa cells in normal or nutrient-restricted conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8), suggesting that its contribution within tumour cells is minor compared to its role in the stroma. To further expand our findings, we have employed data mining analysis (including scRNA-Seq datasets), tissues from human specimens and GEMMs, and tractable cellular systems from different cancer types. Our findings illustrate that, both at mRNA and protein levels, ASPA is primarily expressed by stromal fibroblasts outside the CNS, where it plays a relevant tumour suppressive role.

ASPA is an enzyme responsible for catalysing the conversion of NAA to aspartate and acetate. Mutations in this gene result in the accumulation of NAA, leading to Canavan disease9,13. Increased NAA levels in cancer have been reported as a result of NAT8L overexpression in cancer cells or alterations in NAA-associated enzymes e.g. FOLH1/NAALAD213,14,31–33. Interestingly, NAA can promote pro-tumoral macrophage polarization14, and our correlative analyses suggested an association between ASPA expression, NAA levels, pro-tumoral macrophage content and cancer patient prognosis (Fig. 5H–M). Further analyses confirmed that PCa cells produce and secrete elevated levels of NAA (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Moreover, our analyses showed that NAA supplementation in vivo augmented the number of tumour-infiltrating pro-tumoral macrophages in xenografts and in a genetic mouse model of PCa without overt systemic effects in the immune compartment (Supplementary Fig. 9B–K). However, the extracellular NAA-clearing capacity of ASPA-overexpressing CAFs (which did not show changes in activation status, Supplementary Fig. 4) from DU145 cell conditioned media was limited (Supplementary Fig. 9L–N), thus suggesting that other experimental conditions or additional factors may be considered.

Our study reveals that ASPA depletion in fibroblasts influences their activation status. Silencing ASPA in unstimulated NFs induced myofibroblast conversion, increasing αSMA expression, ECM remodelling, fibroblast-dependent cancer cell proliferation, angiogenesis and tumour associated macrophage modulation. Mechanistically, we provide evidence that ASPA deficiency induces TGFβ-SMAD2/3 signalling in NFs, whereas ectopic expression of ASPA in NFs prevents their transition to a myofibroblast phenotype. These observations were further supported with scRNA-Seq data from clinical specimens, where ASPA levels were significantly associated with TGFβ-SMAD2/3 activation and desmoplastic CAF features. Our data suggests that this phenotype does not require the catalytic activity of the enzyme, since (i) NAA supplementation has no observable effects on these parameters; and (ii) an enzymatic dead mutant of ASPA21 had similar effects as the wild-type counterpart. This NAA-independent regulation may rely on protein-protein interactions with specific factors that influence cell signalling cascades34, as previously described for the tyrosine kinase LYN12. Further research is required to identify the specific effector and mechanism of action. Interestingly, our analyses also show that ASPA downregulation is primarily associated with the emergence of myofibroblast CAF phenotypes (myCAFs) downstream TGFβ-SMAD2/3 signalling. The impact of TGFβ signalling in the activation of pro-tumour transcriptional programs in fibroblasts is well documented, but little is known about TGFβ-mediated gene repression35. Our results indicate that this mechanism depends on class I HDAC activity, which likely removes acetyl groups at ASPA promoters and enhancers, driving ASPA downregulation. Similar findings have been recently reported for TGFβ-mediated repression of PPARGC1A in lung fibrosis via HDAC736, or the identification of TGFβ inhibitory elements (containing HDACs) promoting Pkcz loss in stromal fibroblasts in colon cancer models16. Given that other inflammatory stimuli altering fibroblast behaviour, such as interleukins or TNFα, do not appear to influence ASPA repression, these results suggest an unappreciated role of gene repression programs in modulating phenotypic conversion in fibroblasts that requires further investigation. Together, these observations demonstrate the existence of a reciprocal regulation between TGFβ and ASPA critical for the establishment of fibrodesmoplastic responses. This axis may be particularly relevant during early tumour development, when inflammation increases TGFβ levels leading to ASPA repression in fibroblasts and, as a consequence, to the potentiation of the cytokine signalling resulting in the acquisition of myofibroblast phenotypes.

The biological and mechanistic analyses of stromal ASPA in cancer provide a feasible explanation for the negative prognostic potential of this enzyme in several tumour types. Tumours from different tissues with low ASPA expression (mRNA or protein) exhibit poorer prognosis in the form of shorter recurrence-free survival. In line with our molecular findings, BCa and PCa patient data analyses corroborated that ASPA expression (or ASPA-dependent transcriptional signatures in fibroblasts) inversely correlated with TGFβ-SMAD2/3 activity, NAA levels and pro-tumour macrophage content. Whether stromal ASPA influences the efficacy of current lines of treatment and has any predictive value remains to be explored. It is plausible that ASPA repression in fibroblasts can lead to myCAF phenotypes7 that elicit a tumour-promoting immune reprogramming14,37, which could influence the efficacy of immunotherapies.

In summary, our observations establish ASPA as a stromal tumour suppressive gene regulating fibroblast activation within the TME. In turn, developing selective strategies to regulate the activity and function of ASPA could support the establishment of innovative therapeutic strategies.

Animal studies

Xenograft experiments with PCa models were performed upon subcutaneous injection of 1 × 106 PC3 cells in both flanks of Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu mice (male, 6-week-old). Once tumours were established (100 mm3), animals were treated daily with a dosage of 25 mg/kg of NAA peritumorally or the equivalent volume of physiological saline as control. Mice reaching a maximum tumour volume of 1500 mm3 were euthanized. The conditional tissue-specific Pten knock-out (males) was used for NAA supplementation studies. NAA was resuspended in physiological saline and injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 25 mg/kg on a daily basis. Treatment started when animals were 8 weeks old and finished when mice were 14 weeks old.

TS1 murine BCa cells were used to generate tumours in wild-type FVB/n. Briefly, 5 × 105 TS1 cells and 1.5 × 106 mNF1 (shC or sh4) were suspended in 100 μL of PBS: Matrigel (1:1) and injected subcutaneously into 6-8 week old females. Doxycycline was administered in water (1 mg/mL), changed every two days to avoid degradation. Tumour size was measured every other day using callipers. All mice were culled before any tumour reached the maximum allowed size (1,500 mm3). To calculate tumour latency, mice with tumours reaching a minimum size of 0.5 mm3 were considered as developing an observable tumour, and the day post-injection was recorded and used for the disease-free survival calculation.

Cell proliferation assays

For analyses of cancer cell proliferation in mono-culture, crystal violet staining40 was performed. Alternatively, soft-agar colony formation was performed as previously described40. For mNF1/mCAF1 co-culture analyses of cancer cell proliferation, 2 × 103 mNF1/mCAF1 and 1 × 103 GFP-labelled cancer cells were seeded in a 24-well plate with complete DMEM. When cells were attached, if needed, the growth conditions were changed as indicated. Cancer cell proliferation was monitored during 7 days by acquiring the GFP signal in different areas of the well with a 4× objective with the fluorescent microscope Nikon Eclipse TS100 every 24 h. To calculate the cancer cell growth index, the area covered by cancer cells in each field of view was calculated.

Invasion assays

Relative invasive growth41 was assessed by plating 700 cells upside down in suspension with 20% methylcellulose DMEM drops. After 3 days, when the spheres were formed, they were embedded in a collagen-based medium (55% collagen I, 20% DMEM 5X, 21.3% H2O2 and 3% 0.1 N NaOH), and images of cell spheroids were taken after collagen polymerization (0 h time point). After 48 h, images were taken and the relative invasive growth was calculated with the relative area increase.

ECM-remodelling assay

To assess force-mediated matrix remodelling, 1 × 105 mNF1/mCAF1 were embedded in 100 µL of a mixture of collagen I (BD Biosciences) and ECM gel mixture (Sigma) yielding a final collagen concentration of 4.6 mg/mL and a final ECM gel mix concentration of 2.2 mg/mL. The NF/CAF-containing gel was seeded in a 48-well plate and incubated at 37 °C. Once the gel was set, cells were maintained in complete medium and supplemented as indicated. Gel contraction was monitored daily by scanning the plates. Unless stated otherwise, the gel contraction value refers to the contraction observed after 2 days. To obtain the gel contraction value, the relative diameter of the well and the gel were measured using ImageJ software, and the percentage of contraction was calculated using the formula [(well area–gel area) / well area].

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells. For IF experiments, cells were seeded in sterilized cover glass slides and 24 h later they were treated as indicated, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min. Cells were permeabilised with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma) for 7 min, followed by a blocking step of 3% BSA 0.1% Tween for 1 h. Cells were then incubated with the corresponding primary antibody overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. The following day, the primary antibody was washed with PBS (3 × 5 min washes) and cells were incubated with the fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI for 1 h at RT. After washing the secondary antibody with PBS (3 × 5 min washes), glass covers were mounted in slides and imaged using a confocal microscope. IF images were obtained using a TCS SP5 Confocal Microscope (Leica).

Tissues. For histological analyses by IF, tissues were dissected into 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin blocks and serial sections were taken. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffined by inclusion into xylene, rehydrated and subjected to antigen retrieval using pH 6 sodium citrate buffer. Sections were then incubated with 0.2% Triton X-100, 10% Goat serum (Sigma) blocking solution for 1 h at RT. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in PBS. After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in PBS for 1 h at RT. Sections were subjected to a treatment to reduce autofluorescence using CuSO4 and NH4Cl for 10 min and mounted on slides. Confocal images were captured using a Leica SP6 microscope. IF images were analysed using ImageJ, and data analysed in Prism software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). For each staining, the positive area was calculated and normalized to the total area for all slices processed. To analyse the stroma, fluorescence images were acquired using a CRI Nuance Multispectral camera mounted on a Nikon 90i automated fluorescence microscope (at magnification 200×) and controlled by MetaMorph software. Each specimen was analysed in a similar manner, irrespective of tissue type. Briefly, five representative areas were randomly selected from stroma intermingled with tumour cells (Str Tum) and from the stroma located outside the tumour lesion (Str adj). For the metastatic samples, only five areas in the tumoral area were acquired (Str Met) since these samples consist essentially of tumour tissue. Image analysis was conducted using Fiji software. During the analysis, 8-bit images were thresholded at the same intensity value for a specific staining and converted to binary format. Each area analysed corresponded to 0.347 mm2 of tissue. Analyses of macrophage infiltration were performed as previously described14, using a similar approach on serial sections of 5 µm. Sections were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant without DAPI (Invitrogen, P36930) and analyses performed with an Olympus BX41 microscope and CellSense imaging software. In addition, representative images of ASPA staining from normal and cancerous breast, colon and prostate tissues were retrieved from the Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Antibodies are described in Supplementary Data 6.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Analysis of FFPE human prostates. Tissues were deparaffined using standard protocols and unmasking/antigen retrieval was performed using 0.0005% Tween Tris-EDTA pH 9 solution. ASPA primary antibody was incubated overnight at RT. Hematoxylin/eosin was applied as counterstaining. Secondary antibody, 3,30-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining and imaging method were routinely used by the Department of Pathology of Cruces University Hospital. A qualitative scoring system was based on negative (0), low (1) or high (2) immunoreactivity.

Analysis of FFPE human and murine breast. For IHC analysis of murine and human breast tissue (normal and cancer) and rat brains, tissues were dissected into 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin blocks and serial sections were taken. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffined by inclusion into xylene and rehydrated. Samples were then processed for H&E and Masson’s Trichrome staining following standard procedures. Alternatively, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were rehydrated before blocking endogenous peroxidase (2% H2O2 in methanol for 10 min) and subjected to antigen retrieval using pH 6 sodium citrate buffer. Sections were incubated for 30 min at RT with blocking solution and 1 h at RT with primary antibodies. Sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 50 min at RT, treated with DAB, counterstained with haematoxylin and mounted. Glass slides were scanned using Axio Scan.Z1 Slide Scanner (ZEISS) and analysed using QuPath, ImageJ and Prism. For each staining, the positive area was calculated and normalized to the total area (or indicated regions of interest) for all images processed, using ImageJ. For DAB images, Color Deconvolution (H DAB) in ImageJ was employed, DAB channel isolated and converted to 8 bit, images were thresholded at the same value, converted to binary format and % of positive area calculated. For Masson’s trichrome staining images, pictures were processed in QuPath using the ‘Normalize OD colors’ command. Resulting images were quantified in ImageJ using the red channel to quantify collagen deposition, following a similar protocol employed for DAB analysis. Unless described otherwise, values represent the average of at least 4 representative images from independent tumours. Antibodies are described in Supplementary Data 6.

Nanostring® analysis from FFPE prostates

Western blotting (WB)

To study protein expression by WB, cells after the indicated perturbation were washed with PBS, and protein lysates were obtained, mixed with Laemmli Sample Buffer and boiled. Lysates were run in NUPAGE gradient precast gels (Life Technologies) in MOPS buffer. Alternatively, lysates were resolved in sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) in running buffer. Resolved proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes at 400 mA for 1 h in cold transfer buffer. Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with 5% of milk diluted in T-TBS for 1 h, shaking at RT. Finally, the membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies in a 5% milk T-TBS solution. Next day, membranes were washed with T-TBS (3 ×10 min) and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies prepared in a 5% milk T-TBS solution for 1 h shaking at RT. After washing (3 ×10 min), Clarity Western ECL substrate kit was used at 1:1 ratio for protein detection using film or an Amersham ImageQuant 800 (Cytiva) imaging system. Antibodies used in this work and working dilutions are described in Supplementary Data 6. Uncropped WB scans are provided in the Data Source File or at the end of the Supplementary Information File.

Metabolomic analyses

NAA measurement in patient-derived fibroblasts (cNF, cCAF, pNF and pCAF) and mNF1. Intracellular metabolites were extracted with an aqueous solution of 50% LC-MS grade methanol (Fisher Scientific, 10284580) and 30% acetonitrile (Fisher Scientific, 10001334) in ultrapure water. Media samples were diluted 1:50 in an aqueous solution of 50% methanol and 30% acetonitrile and incubated with shaking at 4 °C. Samples were analysed using a Q-Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) in combination with a Thermo Ultimate 3000 HPLC system. 5 μL of cell extract was injected and the metabolites were separated over a 15 min mobile phase gradient from an initial content of 80% acetonitrile with 20% ammonium bicarbonate (pH 9.2), decreasing to 20% acetonitrile with a flow rate of 200 μL min−1. Metabolites were detected over a period of 25 min using the Q-Exactive mass spectrometer across a mass range of 75–1000 m/z and at a resolution of 35,000 (at 200 m/z). Samples were randomized for data acquisition. Peak identification and area quantification of NAA were carried out using TraceFinder software (v4.1) by comparison of the retention time and exact ion mass to that of an authenticated standard. Peak area was normalized to the amount of protein (in μg) obtained from the cells used for each sample. To assess NAA consumption, mNF1 were cultured with exogenous NAA (100 µM) for 24 h and intracellular and extracellular NAA content was analysed using the same approach.

Intracellular and extracellular NAA in prostate cell lines. Intracellular metabolite extraction was carried out using a pre-chilled extraction solution containing 50% LC-MS grade methanol, 30% LC-MS grade acetonitrile, 20% of ultrapure water, supplemented with 5 μM valine-d8 (CK isotopes, DLM-488) as internal standard. All extraction steps were performed in a dry ice-methanol bath to ensure metabolite stability. Extracellular metabolites were extracted by mixing cell culture media samples with the extraction solution at a 1:3 (v/v) ratio. The mixtures underwent homogenization for 15 min in a Thermomixer at 4 °C at maximum speed. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Millipore SeQuant ZIC-pHILIC analytical column (5 µm, 2.1 × 150 mm) coupled to a guard column (5 µm, 2.1 × 20 mm) with a binary solvent system. Solvent A consisted of 20 mM ammonium carbonate with 0.05% ammonium hydroxide, and Solvent B was pure acetonitrile. The chromatographic gradient was run at a flow rate of 0.200 mL/min as follows: 0–2 min: 80% B; 2-17 min: linear gradient from 80% B to 20% B; 17-17.1 min: linear gradient from 20% B to 80% B; 17.1-22.5 min: hold at 80% B. Sample injections (5 µL each) were randomized to minimize variability and analysed with LC–MS in a blinded manner. Pooled samples were generated from an equal mixture of all individual samples and analysed interspersed at regular intervals within the sample sequence as a quality control. Metabolites were measured with a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass spectrometer (HRMS) coupled to a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC. The mass spectrometer was operated in full-scan, polarity-switching mode, with the spray voltage set to +4.5 kV/-3.5 kV, the heated capillary held at 320 °C, and the auxiliary gas heater held at 280 °C. The sheath gas flow was set to 55 units, the auxiliary gas flow was set to 15 units, and the sweep gas flow was set to 0 units. HRMS data acquisition was performed in a range of m/z = 70–900, with the resolution set at 70,000, the AGC target at 1 × 106, and the maximum injection time (Max IT) at 120 ms. Metabolite identities were confirmed using two parameters: (1) precursor ion m/z was matched within 5 ppm of the theoretical mass predicted by the chemical formula; (2) the retention time of metabolites was within 5% of the retention time of a purified standard run with the same chromatographic method. Peak integration and chromatogram review were performed using TraceFinder software (v5.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The half minimum method was used to fill in missing values. Peak areas were normalized to the total ion count (TIC) of each sample, and these adjusted values (adj value) served as input for subsequent statistical analyses.

DU145 and mCAF1 intracellular and extracellular NAA measurements. Metabolite extraction from cells was performed by adding 500 µL an ice-cold solvent extraction liquid (methanol/water 50/50 % v/v) to each well of 6-well culture plates. Culture plates with extraction liquid were left on dry ice for 15 minutes. Next, 400 µL the obtained solutions were transferred to new Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C and 20,000 × g. Supernatants (300 µL) were evaporated with SpeedVac for 2 h, and pellets were resuspended in 150 µL water/acetonitrile/formic acid (39.9/60/0.1 % v/v/v). Metabolite was measured with an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (Acquity, Waters Inc., Manchester, UK) coupled to a Time-of-Flight mass spectrometer (SYNAPT G2, Waters Inc.). A BEH-AMIDE column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters Inc. Manchester, UK) thermostated at 40 °C, was used to separate the analytes before entering the MS. Mobile phase solvent A (aqueous phase) consisted of 99.5% water, 0.5% formic acid (FA) and 20 mM ammonium formate, while solvent B (organic phase) consisted of 29.5% water, 70% ACN, 0.5% FA and 1 mM ammonium formate. Separation of the analytes was performed using the following gradient: from 5% A to 50% A in 2.4 min in curved gradient (#8, as defined by Waters); from 50% A to 99.9% A in 0.2 min, constant at 99.9% A for 1.2 min, back to 5% A in 0.2 min. The flow rate was 0.250 mL/min and the injection volume was 2 µL. All samples were injected randomly. After every 5 injections, a QC sample was injected. The MS was operated in positive electrospray ionization mode in full scan (50 Da to 1200 Da). Capillary voltage was 500 V, sampling cone voltage was 25, source offset was 80 V, desolvation gas flow was 800 L/h, cone gas flow was 5 L/h and nebulizer gas flow was 6 L/h. Source temperature was 120 °C and desolvation temperature was 450 °C. A 2 ng/mL leucine-enkephalin solution in water/acetonitrile/formic acid (49.9/50/0.1 v/v/v%) was infused at 10 µL/min and used for a lock mass, which was measured every 32 s for 0.5 s. Spectral peaks were automatically corrected for deviations in the lock mass. Extracted ion chromatograms for the analytes of interest were extracted in their expected mass channels. Peak areas were integrated with TargetLynx software (Waters Inc.) to obtain raw signals. To correct for differences in biological material, signal drift and variation between extractions and injection volumes, median-fold change normalization (also called quotient normalization)44,45 was performed as follows. Features, defined as retention time mass-to-charge pairs, were extracted from full scan data using automatic peak detection in MarkerLynx software (Waters Inc.). Each injected sample yielded about 2000 features. The sample with the most detected features was chosen as the reference. For each feature, the signal in a sample was divided by the signal in the reference sample, resulting in about 2000 fold-change values per sample (with all values in the reference sample equal to 1). The median of these fold-change values was then calculated per sample and used to normalize the raw signals of targeted analytes.

NAA analysis in frozen tissues. For frozen tissues metabolic analysis, 20 mg of prostate lobes were used. Samples were homogenized in 500 µL ice-cold extraction buffer (methanol: water, 50:50, v/v) with a tissue homogenizer for 40 s at 6000 rpm. A volume of 400 µL of the homogenate was transferred to a new clean Eppendorf and a new homogenization cycle of 30 min at 1400 rpm at 4 °C was performed. To eliminate debris, samples were centrifuged for 15 minutes at 20,000 × g at 4 °C and 300 µL of the supernatant was collected. Supernatants were evaporated with Speedvac for 2 h. Pellets were resuspended in 150 µL of water: MeCN in a volume proportion of 40:60. The metabolite spectrum analysis was performed as previously described in DU145 and mCAF1 intracellular and extracellular NAA measurements.

RNA-sequencing processing and analysis

Samples were first quality checked and transformed into a library of stranded mRNA molecules using the TrueSeq® Stranded mRNA library preparation kit (Illumina). The stranded mRNA library was then sequenced at a depth of 40 million reads per sample, with a total read length of 150 paired-ended base pairs (bp), using a NovaSeq 6000 S4 system (Illumina). RNA-Seq data was pre-processed to perform quality check, removal of contaminants, alignment (Genome Reference: GRCm38) and quantification. Subsequent analyses were performed using either ‘count’ or ‘tpm’ (Transcript Per Million) matrix datasets. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering were performed using pre-filtered datasets after rlog transformation. DESeq2 software was used to identify the Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs, cutoff: Padj<0.05) and fold changes between experimental conditions, following the program guidelines. This dataset was also employed to derive gene signatures (gene sets) associated with Aspa silencing in mNF1, which were employed in subsequent analyses. Unless stated otherwise, expression values for each gene represented in the figures or tables were z-score normalized from the ‘tpm’ matrix dataset. Genes downregulated in murine NF1 after Aspa silencing with both shRNA treatment (log2FC < –1.5, Padj <0.005 threshold) were identified, transformed to human orthologs and employed to generate an ASPA gene signature described in Supplementary Data 8.

ChIP-Seq and Remap analysis

mNF1 and mCAF1 were subjected to ChIP-Seq analyses as previously described46. Briefly, 7–10 × 106 cells at subconfluency were fixed in 1% formaldehyde, washed, lysed and sonicated using the Bioruptor Pico sonication device (Diagenode; B01060001) using 10 cycles (30 sec on; 30 sec off) at maximum intensity. Purified chromatin was then separated for: (i) immunoprecipitation using Dynabeads Protein G (Life Technologies: 10003D) coated with 10 µg of Rabbit anti-Histone H3 acetyl K27 antibody (Abcam, ab4729) per ChIP experiment; (ii) non-immunoprecipitated chromatin, used as an input control; and (iii) assessment of sonication efficiencies using a 1% agarose gel. ChIP DNA was washed, eluted and treated with 2.7 μL of 1 mg/mL Ribonuclease A (RNaseA) for 30 min at 37 °C and subsequently incubated with 1.3 μL of 20 mg/mL proteinase K (Invitrogen) for 1 h at 55 °C. Then, DNA extraction was performed using SPRI magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, B23318). After elution in TE buffer, DNA was quantified using Qubit (ThermoFisher Scientific; Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer; #Q33216) high-sensitivity assay (ThermoFisher Scientific; #33216). Prior to sequencing, ChIP samples were library prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, NEBNext Ultra II DNA library prep kit for Illumina, #E7770, NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina, #E7335L). Adaptor ligated DNA was size selected with SPRI magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, B23318) for the Illumina sequencer (#NextSeq500). After library preparation, we performed qPCR, high-sensitivity DNA quantification and size selection measurement (Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system + High-sensitivity DNA measurement assay; 5067–4626) before sending samples for sequencing. Raw sequencing files processed by the Illumina NextSeq500 sequencer were obtained in ‘FASTQ’ format. Raw data was pre-processed to perform a quality check and removal of contaminants. The raw sequencing files were then aligned using the mouse reference genome 38 (GRCm38) as a reference. MACS2 was used for peak calling, and Bedtools for overlap and subsetting analysis. Tracks were visualized using the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/), focusing on the indicated areas. To explore TF binding regions at the Aspa locus, we employed ReMap (https://remap.univ-amu.fr/). Using the ReMap UCSC genome browser link (https://remap.univ-amu.fr/hsap_tracks_page) for the mouse genome, we explored the presence of Smad and HDAC1,2,3 Cis Regulatory Modules (CRMs) in established promoter and enhancer regions near the Aspa locus.

Gene-set enrichment analyses (GSEA)

For transcriptomic analyses by GSEA, gene count RNA-Seq data were first normalized using DESeq2. Pre-processed data was then run through the GSEA software (Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, USA) following the program guidelines. The specific settings applied in all analyses were: Number of Permutations (1000); Permutation Type (Gene set); Enrichment statistic (Weighted); Metric for ranking genes: (t-test). Analysed genesets were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) and Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/). In addition, in-house manually curated gene sets or gene sets from specific publications were employed and described in Supplementary Data 8. Results were represented using an in-house adapted script from GSEA Multi-sample Running Enrichment plots. Values represent the False Discovery Rate (FDR) and the Nominal Enrichment Score (NES) of each gene set. To calculate the gene-signature score in each individual sample, we used single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) Projection Software from the GenePattern platform, following the programs guidelines. Each ssGSEA enrichment score represents the degree to which the genes in a particular gene set are co-ordinately up- or downregulated within a sample. ssGSEA scores were z-score normalized. Similar approaches (GSEA and ssGSEA) were employed to investigate the enrichment and/or expression of particular gene signatures in different datasets

Expression analyses of public datasets and survival analyses

All the bioinformatics analysis were performed with available information downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), CANCERTOOL47 or xCell48 (Supplementary Data 9). For microarray-based datasets, probe-to-gene mapping was performed using the highest variance probe. Unless otherwise stated, expression values were z-score normalized.

To identify consistent differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in cancer stroma vs normal stroma, we studied the up- and down-regulated DEGs in the indicated datasets (unpaired heteroscedastic t-test, cuttoff p value < 0.05) and identified common DEGs. This analysis was represented using the difference in mean z-score between cancer and normal stroma gene expression. GSE20100 was employed to generate gene signatures of genes repressed by HDAC1 and 2 in MEFs (Supplementary Data 8).

For mRNA and protein analyses of ASPA expression in human specimens (normal, tumour and stage), we retrieved data from ProteinAtlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000108381-ASPA) for the indicated datasets, employing provided units (mRNA, TPMs; protein, nRPX). This resource was also employed to investigate ASPA expression in IHC tissues.

For additional analyses based on clinical material, PCa-TCGA and BCa-METABRIC gene expression and clinical information were retrieved from cBioportal. For TCGA, mRNA data were normalized in RSEM values and employed in downstream bioinformatics analyses. For the PCa-TCGA, samples with no disease-free survival data were excluded from further analyses. In addition, duplicated samples from an individual patient were also excluded. For the BCa-METABRIC dataset, non-classified molecular subtypes, normal samples and samples with missing tumour size, grade, ER, HER2, or PR status were removed from the analysis. In all cases, survival was censored at 120 months. These two datasets were employed in downstream analyses of gene expression, digital pathology analysis using xCell or survival analyses. Curated mRNA matrices from PCa-TCGA and BCa-METABRIC were subjected to single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) to calculate the gene expression score of different gene sets in each individual sample, which were z-score normalized for representation. For the ssGSEA analysis, genes with no values (N/A) in any of the analysed patient samples were removed from the analysis.

Curated PCa-TCGA and BCa-METABRIC datasets were also employed for prognosis analyses, together with additional PCa and BCa datasets retrieved from CANCERTOOL or KMPLOTTER49 online applications, as well as information generated in this study from Basurto and Champalimaud cohorts. For mRNA-based analyses of the prognostic value of ASPA expression in BCa stroma, the Finak dataset was employed50.

Digital pathology analyses to assess cell type composition in tumours

To infer macrophage content from bulk RNA datasets of solid tumours, we employed the online tool xCell48 following standard guidelines on dataset pre-processing and analysis. Unless indicated otherwise, the M2 macrophage content was calculated as the ratio between raw scores of the M2 macrophage and the monocyte signatures. Samples were then stratified based on ASPA expression, ASPA signature expression or NAA content.

Integrated analyses of gene expression and metabolites in BCa patient samples

Normalized mRNA gene expression, metabolite level data and clinical information from cancerous and normal adjacent BCa tissues were retrieved from GSE37751 and from the Supplementary Data from Terunuma et al25. For independent analyses (mRNA or metabolites separately) all profiled samples (cancer or normal) were included, with the exception of metabolic samples with no available data/no detection of NAA levels, that were excluded. For paired normal-cancer analyses, samples with no associated pair were excluded. For correlative analyses of RNA or RNA-based (i.e. xCell) and metabolite levels, only samples with both types of data were analysed. One sample appeared as normal in the metabolic dataset and as cancer in the RNA dataset, so it was excluded. Spearman correlation coefficient and simple linear regression with 95% confidence bands are shown. RNA data were z-score normalized for each gene in all analyses. NAA data was log2-normalized and used in the analyses. RNA data was employed to calculate M2 macrophage content employing xCell. M2 macrophage score was obtained for cancer specimens and samples stratified based on the mean NAA expression of samples with NAA data available. For survival analyses, patients were stratified based on NAA content between the lowest quartile and the rest; for this analysis, samples with undetected NAA levels were considered within the lowest quartile.

Single-cell RNA-Seq data collection, processing and analysis

For analysis of prostate tissues, a tumour adjacent (contralateral lobe) benign prostate specimen from a PCa patient was collected at Basurto University Hospital (Spain) at the time of robotic prostatectomy. Sequencing data was processed using 10X Cell Ranger with default parameters (version 7.0.1), aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome. In total, we obtained 5,032 cells. The R package Seurat V4.3.0.0 was used to import the raw count data. Cells that were considered of low quality were removed from the raw data and the following exclusion criteria were applied: (i) cells with less than 200 genes expressed; (ii) cells with more than 20% of genes derived from mitochondrial genes; and (iii) cells with less than 80% of complexity index (calculated as the number of genes detected per UMI). The DoubletFinder R package was utilized to identify the doublets from the raw counts data. Data was normalized, highly variable genes were identified, and raw counts data were scaled using the NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures and ScaleData functions. Using the ElbowPlot function, the appropriate number of principal components was obtained. Afterward, the dimensionality reduction projection (UMAP) was performed using the RunUMAP function to visualize and explore the dataset. The FindClusters function was implemented to identify potential cell clusters using 0.8 resolution. Cell clusters were annotated by the average expression of the well-established genes for prostate cancer cells: T cells (CD3D and CD3E), B cells (CD79A and IGKC), macrophages (CD14, CD68, CSF1R and LYZ), mast cells (KIT and MS4A2), fibroblasts (FN1 and PDGFRA), pericytes (ACTA2 and RGS5), endothelial cells (ENG, PECAM1 and VWF), and epithelial cells (AR, EPCAM, KRT5 and KRT8).

In addition, sc-RNA-Seq analysis was performed from public datasets of breast and bladder human cancers. Breast sc-RNA-Seq data were obtained from the European Genome-Phenome Archive, reference E-MTAB-1060. This dataset consists of 14 fresh human breast tumour tissue samples. Bladder sc-RNA-Seq data were obtained from the China National Center for Bioinformation, reference GSA-Human HRA000212, and consists of tumour and normal mucosa samples processed immediately after being obtained from bladder cancer patients of the study. Our analyses included only the ones with publicly uploaded metadata, remaining 5 tumour samples and 2 mucosae: (‘BC1’, ‘BC2’, ‘BC4’, ‘BC5’, ‘BC7’, ‘Mucosa1’, ‘Mucosa3’).

Each sample was pre-processed individually following the methodology of their original publication, with some changes in software versions in R. Once all individual cells were assigned to specific clusters/cell types, we assigned the expression of normalized gene counts to each cell. Individual cell gene expression was calculated with Seurat’s AddModuleScore function. Either the target gene or the gene signature was projected on the UMAP graphs to obtain a visualization of gene expression per cluster. In addition, matrices of gene/gene signature expression per cell were further processed for additional analyses. Briefly, for correlation analyses, the Pearson coefficient between the expression of particular genes/gene signatures was calculated for each identified cell type. For analyses of expression in each cell cluster, the average expression per cell type was calculated and then scaled within a 1 to 0 range (1 high expression, 0 no expression). For genes, where the lowest value was always 0, average values were scaled by dividing by the highest average value. For gene signatures, where certain cell types might contain negative values, average values were scaled by subtracting the minimum average value and dividing by the total range (maximum average value minus minimum average value). For certain analyses, cell types were further stratified depending on the tissue of origin (mucosal or tumour in the BLCA dataset).

Statistical analysis

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. n values represent the number of independent experiments performed, the number of individual mice, or the number of patient specimens. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. When n permitted, values were tested for Gaussian distribution using the D’Agostino-Pearson normality test. For Gaussian distributions, paired or unpaired Student’s t test and one-way ANOVA with Tukey post-test (for multiple comparisons) were performed. For non-Gaussian distributions, Mann-Whitney’s test and Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-test (for multiple comparisons) were performed. Unless stated otherwise, mean values and standard errors (SEM) are shown. For in vivo experiments, outlier analysis was performed to evaluate their potential influence on statistical outcomes. Samples identified as statistical outliers were excluded from the final analysis to ensure robustness of the results. Survival curves were estimated based on the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using a Mantel-Cox test, unless stated otherwise. Contingency studies were performed by Chi-square analysis. The confidence level used for all statistical analysis was 95% (p-value = 0.05). In general, this level was distributed in two tails (for experiments without predicted results). Where indicated, statistical test was one-tailed (for hypothesis-driven studies, i.e. in vivo analyses). Where indicated, individual p-values are shown; alternatively, the following symbols were used to describe statistical significance: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001; n.s., non-significant (also when no p value is shown).
