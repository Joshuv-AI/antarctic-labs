A subclade-associated genomic deletion encompassing vraDEH confers increased susceptibility to nisin A and bacitracin in Staphylococcus aureus CC121
Authors: Yujin Suzuki, Airi Odawara, Miki Kawada-Matsuo, Junzo Hisatsune, Chika Arai, Saki Nishihama, Hideki Shiba, Souichi Yanamoto, Takemasa Sakaguchi, Motoyuki Sugai, Hitoshi Komatsuzawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) play important roles in suppressing bacterial colonization and infection, and several AMPs are used as antimicrobial agents. Conversely, bacteria possess mechanisms that confer resistance to AMPs. We previously identified clinical Staphylococcus aureus isolates lacking the vraDEH genes, which are involved in nisin and bacitracin resistance. All such isolates belonged to clonal complex (CC) 121 and exhibited increased susceptibility to nisin A and bacitracin. The absence of vraDEH was accompanied by the absence of a 35,005-bp genomic region encompassing the biofilm-associated icaRADBC genes and a histidine biosynthesis operon. In a vraDEH-positive CC121 strain, this region was flanked by two IS1181 elements, whereas in vraDEH-negative strains it was replaced by a single IS1181 element, suggesting deletion through recombination between IS elements. Analysis of publicly available genomes revealed that all strains carrying the 35-kb deletion belonged to a single phylogenetic subclade of CC121. The downstream IS1181 insertion was frequently found in CC121 strains, whereas the upstream insertion was only found in this subclade. Across the S. aureus population, IS1181 copy number and insertion sites correlated with phylogenetic relationships, suggesting that lineage-associated IS1181 insertion may contribute to the genomic deletion in S. aureus CC121.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57770-1.

Introduction

Bacteria are known to be exposed to a number of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) during the process of colonization and infection in humans. These AMPs include defensins such as the human beta-defensin hBD-1,2,3, the human neutrophil peptide HNP-1, and the cathelicidin peptide LL-371,2. In addition, bacteriocins, AMPs produced by bacteria, also play a role in inhibiting the colonization and growth of competing bacteria in the same niche3. Furthermore, AMPs such as daptomycin, bacitracin, colistin, and polymyxin B are clinically used as antimicrobial agents, and some AMPs are expected to be new antimicrobial agents4.

On the other hand, bacteria also possess resistance factors against AMPs. Staphylococcus aureus, an opportunistic pathogen that sometimes causes several infections5,6, is known to resist AMPs through multiple mechanisms. Several transporters are known to play key roles in AMP resistance, and among them, VraDEH, a three-component ABC transporter, is a major factor in the efflux of bacitracin, nisin A, gallidermin and nukacin ISK-17,8. The expression of vraDEH genes is induced by the BraRS–BraAB system, a two-component regulatory system (BraRS) with a sensor transporter (BraAB) that senses the presence of these AMPs9. In addition, overexpression of the PmtABCD transporter has been reported to confer resistance to nisin A and hBD-310.

Modulation of the cell surface charge is another important system involved in AMP resistance. Since most AMPs are cationic, weakening the net negative charge of the bacterial cell surface by the Dlt system and MprF is thought to reduce AMP activity. These factors are positively regulated by a two-component regulatory system called GraRS in concert with the sensor transporter VraFG11. The Dlt system modifies the phosphate base of lipoteichoic acid and wall teichoic acid using D-alanine, and MprF also modifies the phosphate base of phosphatidylglycerol using lysine, thereby weakening the net negative charge of the cell surface12,13. Furthermore, factors such as cell wall thickness and regulators controlling cell wall synthesis are known to influence AMP susceptibility8,14.

S. aureus genomes contain multiple insertion sequences (ISs). An IS is the simplest transposable element and includes only a gene encoding a transposase whose flanking region includes short inverted repeats15. This repeat sequence is recognized by a transposase, which enhances the excision or replication of the IS element and subsequent insertion into another genomic position (transposition). Several IS elements exist in S. aureus genomes, such as IS256, IS257, IS1181, IS1182 and IS127216. Among them, IS1181 is a 1,512 bp IS sequence first identified in the S. aureus BM3121 strain17. The IS1181 sequence consists of a 1,359 bp open reading frame, a 23 bp incomplete inverted repeat, and an 8 bp directed repeat. BM3121 was predicted to harbor at least 8 copies of IS1181, and a subsequent study revealed that the number of IS1181 copies varies among strains, suggesting that IS1181 detection is useful for the typing of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains18. Furthermore, the insertion of IS1181 into the gene-coding region and recombination between IS1181 elements have been reported to cause phenotypic changes, gene deletion or large-scale genomic inversions in each specific case 19–21. Thus, IS1181 is thought to be associated with several genomic events. However, the understanding of IS1181 transmission and genomic events within the entire S. aureus population remains insufficient.

In a previous study, we showed that several S. aureus clinical isolates are more susceptible to bacteriocins such as nisin A, nukacin ISK-1, epidermin and Pep522. Among them, several strains were found to have lost the vraDEH gene. In this study, we found that the subclade-associated IS1181 insertions at specific chromosomal sites in S. aureus clonal complex 121 (CC121) are linked to a genomic deletion encompassing vraDEH genes, and to increased susceptibility to nisin A and bacitracin.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) test

The MICs of the antimicrobial agents were determined using the microdilution method as described previously27. According to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth (BD) was used for the nisin A, vancomycin and bacitracin tests, whereas cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth supplemented to 50 µg/mL Ca2+ was used for the daptomycin test28. Two-fold serial dilutions of each antimicrobial agent were prepared in 96-well round-bottom plates, and dilutions of overnight culture containing 1 × 105 cells were inoculated into each well containing 100 µL of medium. MICs were determined after static incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Nisin A and bacitracin were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. Vancomycin was purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan. Daptomycin was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan. Three independent experiments were performed, and the median was used as the exact MIC value.

Test of histidine auxotrophy

We used chemically defined medium (CDM) with or without L-histidine as the growth medium to evaluate the histidine auxotrophy of S. aureus strains. The method for preparing the CDM was described previously29. Overnight cultures of the S. aureus strains were adjusted to an OD660 of 1.0, and the cells were collected by centrifugation. After the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 1 × 108 cells were added to 3 mL of CDM with or without 1 mM L-histidine (Katayama Kagaku Industries, Osaka, Japan) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with shaking.

Quantification of biofilm formation

We performed a crystal violet staining assay to quantify the biofilm formation of S. aureus strains using a previously described method with minor modifications23. Aliquots of 100 μl of TSB containing 10% glucose were added to a 96-well flat bottom plate, and 1 × 105 S. aureus cells were inoculated and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The biofilms were washed with PBS three times and subsequently stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for 5 min. After being rinsed with PBS, crystal violet was eluted with a 30% acetic acid solution, and the absorbance (A570) of the solution was determined using an iMark Microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA).

Isolation and analysis of the 35-kb-deleted mutants of KSA13

CDM agar plates with or without 1 mM L-histidine were prepared to isolate the mutants that require histidine for growth from KSA13 culture. After KSA13 was cultured through 10 passages in TSB with 1 mM L-histidine, the culture was appropriately diluted and plated on trypticase soy agar (TSA) and incubated for 16 h. The colonies that appeared were replated on CDM plates and incubated for 48 h. The colonies that grew in CDM with histidine but not in CDM without histidine were selected, and the deletions of icaA, hisD and vraD in the mutants were confirmed by PCR using specific primers (Table S2). To sequence the missing region, the primers that annealed upstream of icaR and downstream of cspB were used for PCR (Table S2), and the obtained amplicon was subsequently purified using a QIAquick PCR purification kit (QIAGEN, Tokyo, Japan). Sanger sequencing was performed at Macrogen Japan Co. using 8 primers (Table S2), and the resulting sequences were integrated.

Determination of complete genome sequences of S. aureus strains

For long-read sequencing, genomic DNA was extracted from KSA13, KSA152, HSA44 and 33009-19-S058 using lysostaphin and a NucleoBond HMW DNA extraction kit (Macherey–Nagel, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol to determine the complete genome sequence. Long-read DNA sequencing was performed using PacBio HiFi reads at Macrogen Japan Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). Short-read Illumina sequencing of these strains was performed previously using Hiseq X FIVE platform at Macrogen Japan Corp22,24,25. The resulting fastq files were assembled with Illumina reads using hybracter v0.11.230.

Analysis of genomic information

Based on the whole-genome sequences, we performed several genomic analyses. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and the identification of MLST-defined clonal complexes (CCs) were performed using PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/). The whole-genome sequences of S. aureus available in the NCBI database were obtained using the ncbi-genome-download tool from the NCBI GenBank FTP server in FASTA format. Information on the isolation sites and sources associated with each genome sequence in GenBank was obtained from the biosample data. To identify 35-kb-deleted strains in public database, we collected the nucleotide sequences of the genes encompassed in the deleted region, as well as the four flanking genes outside the deleted region, excluding the IS1181 element. These sequences were used to construct a custom database and searched for the presence of these genes in S. aureus genomes using ABRicate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) with a minimum nucleotide identity threshold of 80% and a minimum coverage threshold of 80%. Strains lacking all genes within the deleted region while possessing all four flanking genes were identified as a putative 35-kb-deleted strain. Quality control of whole-genome sequencing was performed using CheckM231, and only genomic data with completeness greater than 95, contamination less than 5 and a contig N50 greater than 10,000 bp were used for further analysis. To reduce the computational load, we performed quality checks only on genomes classified as CC121 by MLST analysis and on genomes predicted to be the 35-kb-deleted strain, and only those genomes that met the criteria was used for the subsequent analyses (Fig. S1). The annotation of the genomes was performed using Prokka v1.14.632. Comparisons of DNA sequences and the detection of specific genes among S. aureus strains were performed using nucleotide BLAST (BLASTn) v2.15.0 + 33. The antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence factor genes were detected via ABRicate using the Resfinder and VFDB databases, respectively34,35. Genetic comparisons were performed using Easyfig v2.2.536. The clustering of the IS1181 sequence with the flanking sequence was performed using cd-hit v4.8.137.

Construction of the phylogenetic tree

The phylogenetic tree of CC121 strains was constructed using snippy v4.6.0 to create a reference-based alignment of core-genome SNPs using snippy-multi script and snippy-clean_full_aln script (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). The complete genome sequence of KSA13 was used as the reference. A recombination-free nucleotide alignment of the core genome was generated, and maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed using Gubbins v3.4.338 and RAxML-NG v1.2.239 with a GTRGAMMA model and 100 bootstrap replicates. The visualization and annotation of the phylogenetic tree were performed using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) software40.

Results

The vraDEH-negative strains were classified as CC121 and susceptible to nisin A and bacitracin

In a previous study, we identified several S. aureus strains with increased susceptibility to nisin A, Pep5, epidermin and nukacin ISK-1 among 149 clinical isolates22. These clinical isolates were collected from the nasal cavity of healthy individuals or the blood samples of patients with a bloodstream infection in Japan 23–25. We analyzed the amino acid sequences of genes involved in AMP resistance and found that several highly susceptible strains harbored frameshifts or nonsense mutations in BraR, BraA, BraB, GraR and VraG. In addition, three other strains, which were significantly more susceptible to epidermin, lacked the vraDEH genes (KSA152, HSA44, and 33009-19-S058). MLST analysis revealed that all of the vraDEH-negative strains were classified as clonal complex 121 (CC121) (including ST121 and ST7432; Table S1). Eight CC121 strains were identified among the 149 S. aureus clinical isolates, and the rate of vraDEH-negative strain in CC121 was 37.5% (3/8 strains). One vraDEH-negative strain isolated from a bloodstream infection was MRSA, and its SCCmec type was V (Table S1). MIC tests were performed against all the CC121 strains using nisin A, bacitracin, vancomycin and daptomycin. The nisin A MIC was twofold lower in vraDEH-negative strains than in other CC121 strains, except for one strain (HSA172) that harbored frameshifts in graR and vraG genes (Table 2). These genes are known to confer nisin A resistance8. The absence of vraDEH was associated with the absence of the 35,005-bp genomic region

We assessed the length of the missing region containing vraDEH in these strains by determining the complete genome sequence of three vraDEH-negative strains and the control strain (KSA13), which is the same lineage (CC121) and possesses vraDEH. The genomic comparison revealed that the region from 393 bp upstream of the end of icaR to 78 bp downstream of cspB (hereafter referred as ‘icaR-cspB region’, 35,005-bp in total) was missing in three vraDEH-negative strains (Fig. 1). This region contained other genes, such as the biofilm formation-related genes icaRADBC, lipase-encoding lip gene and histidine biosynthesis gene cluster his operon. Consistent with the genomic data, the three strains showed histidine auxotrophy in chemically defined medium (CDM) (Fig. 2), but no difference in the strength of biofilm formation was observed among the CC121 strains (Fig. S2). Interestingly, the IS1181 copies were present immediately upstream and downstream of the icaR-cspB region in KSA13 (Fig. 1), and this region was replaced by single copy of the IS1181 sequence in three vraDEH-negative strains. These IS elements were not observed in standard MRSA strain MW226. Subculture of the CC121 strain generated mutants carrying a 35-kb-genomic deletion

In KSA13, two identical IS1181 copies sandwiched the icaR-cspB region, and we speculated that recombination could occur between these IS elements and that the icaR-cspB region could be deleted. We verified this hypothesis by culturing KSA13 for ten passages and obtained 396 colonies. Among these colonies, three mutants with histidine auxotrophy were obtained (Fig. 3A). These mutants exhibited increased susceptibility to nisin A and bacitracin (Table 3). PCR using vraD, icaA, and hisD-specific primers resulted in a lack of amplification of these genes in the three mutants, whereas parent KSA13 strain resulted in amplification of all the genes (data not shown). The 35-kb-deleted strains belong to a single subclade of CC121

To verify the prevalence of strains carrying the deletion of icaR-cspB region (35-kb-deleted strains) within S. aureus populations, we performed genomic analysis using public genome database. The workflow of this analysis is shown in Fig. S1. We obtained 118,829 assemblies of S. aureus strains from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank FTP server and analyzed the presence of the genes located in the icaR-cspB region and flanking genes. The strains lacking the genes within icaR-cspB region while carrying the flanking genes on both sides of this region were classified as putative 35-kb-deleted strains. Using this criterion, 15 strains were identified as the 35-kb-deleted strains in the GenBank database. These 15 strains included our strains (KSA152, HSA44, and 33009-19-S058), and the other 12 strains were also classified as CC121 (Table S3). Among the 15 strains, 12 strains were isolated in Japan, 2 strains were isolated in China, and the isolation site of 1 strain was not described (strain JP197). The isolation sources of these strains (including our strains) were skin samples (n = 5), including samples from patients with impetigo or atopic dermatitis, nasal swabs (n = 2), and blood culture (n = 5), and other sources were unknown 41–43. We constructed a phylogenetic tree using the public genome data of all available CC121 genomes (n = 836) and found that the 35-kb-deleted strains were concentrated in one subclade (Fig. 4). This subclade consisted of 97 strains, among which 15 strains (15.46%) exhibited a putative 35-kb deletion. The strains included in this subclade were mostly isolated in Japan or China (We refer this subclade as an ‘East Asian subclade’) (Fig. S3). Most of the CC121 isolates from Japan belonged to this subclade (82 of 84 strains), but among the strains isolated in China, the proportion of strains belonging to this subclade was relatively low (12 of 55 strains).Fig. 4Phylogenetic tree of all CC121 strains in the GenBank database. Insertion of IS1181 upstream of icaR was also associated with the CC121 East Asian subclade

IS1181 is known to be present in multiple copies on the chromosome of S. aureus17. Since the presence of IS1181 upstream of icaR (IS1181icaR) and downstream of cspB (IS1181cspB) was predicted to be necessary for genomic deletion, we verified the presence of two IS1181 copies in publicly available S. aureus genomes to investigate whether the presence of two IS1181 copies is common in S. aureus. Since accurately determining the positions of IS1181 copies in the contig files using BLASTn analysis was difficult because IS1181 frequently disrupts contig continuity, the genomes with the assembly level ‘complete’ in the NCBI GenBank database (n = 2,550) were used for this analysis in addition to our four strains with complete genomes. IS1181cspB was detected in 24 strains, all of which were classified as CC121. Since there were 30 complete genome assemblies of CC121 strains in total, 80% of the CC121 strains harbored IS1181cspB. However, IS1181icaR was found in only one CC121 strain (strain SA11-SX) except for KSA13. This strain was isolated in China44 and harbored both IS1181cspB and IS1181icaR. We also assessed the presence of two IS1181 copies in our four vraDEH-positive CC121 strains only with contig assemblies by PCR using primers specific to each insertion site; all strains harbored IS1181cspB, and three strains harbored IS1181icaR (Fig. S4). We constructed a phylogenetic tree using the complete genomes of CC121 strains, the 35-kb-deleted strains (which was thought to harbor two IS1181 copies), and our CC121 collection to clarify the relationship between the possession of two IS1181 copies and phylogenetic data. Strains harboring IS1181icaR and 35-kb-deleted strains clustered together in a single subclade which was corresponding to the East Asian subclade (Fig. S4).

The number and insertion positions of IS1181 copies were associated with phylogeny

Since the possession of IS1181icaR was associated with genetic lineage, we also analyzed the relationship between the number of IS1181 copies and CC types in the complete genomes of S. aureus. On average, CC121 harbored the greatest number of IS1181 copies, followed by CC5 and CC97, whose numbers were similar. Relatively low numbers were observed in CC8 and CC1, whereas few IS1181 copies were detected in CC15, CC20, CC30, and CC45 (Fig. S5).

We defined the insertion sites based on the nucleotide sequence flanking each IS1181 copy rather than on absolute genome coordinates to examine the relationship between IS1181 insertion sites and the phylogenetic relationships of S. aureus strains. This approach avoids misclassifying IS positions because of differences in genome starting positions or large structural variations among strains caused by the insertion of mobile genetic elements, such as prophages and SCCmec.

For each detected IS1181 copy, we extracted nucleotide sequences between 500 bp upstream and 500 bp downstream of each IS1181 element. Since copies of IS1181 inserted at the same genomic position are expected to have highly similar flanking sequences, we used the similarity of these sequences as a proxy for positional identity. We collected the flanking sequences for all the IS1181 copies across all the strains (7,145 sequences in total) and clustered them based on nucleotide sequence identity using cd-hit, resulting in 858 clusters. Each cluster was treated as a distinct IS1181 insertion site, representing a group of strains with the same insertion site.

Next, we quantified the distribution of CCs among strains assigned to each insertion site cluster. For most clusters, strains from a single CC constituted the majority of the members, whereas strains from other CCs were rare or absent (Fig. 5). In addition, within individual CCs, the insertion site prevalence differed substantially across clusters (Fig. S6). Some insertion sites were frequently observed among strains in the CC, whereas others were detected only at low frequencies. Finally, we examined the relationship between insertion sites and phylogenetic structure within a CC by mapping insertion site cluster membership onto the phylogenetic tree of complete CC121 genomes (Fig. 6). Discussion

In this study, we identified S. aureus CC121 strains lacking 35,005-bp genomic region including vraDEH genes. These strains were more susceptible to bacitracin and nisin A than vraDEH-positive CC121 strains were (Table 2). We had previously identified these strains as being highly susceptible to epidermin22. Since VraDEH has been shown to affect susceptibility to nisin A, nukacin ISK-1, gallidermin and bacitracin7,8, the increased susceptibility to nisin A and bacitracin in 35-kb-deleted strains is thought to be due to the deletion of the vraDEH genes. However, the deleted region also contains other genes, such as icaRADBC, his operon, and lip, and their possible effects on antimicrobial susceptibility cannot be excluded. Further study is needed to determine the exact contributions of vraDEH and other genes within this region.

ica operon is known to be involved in biofilm formation in S. aureus45, and his operon is known to be involved in histidine biosynthesis46. Consistent with genomic analysis, histidine auxotrophy was observed in the 35-kb-deleted strains in CDM (Fig. 2), but the strength of biofilm formation did not differ under the crystal violet staining assay (Fig. S2). Since the contribution of icaADBC to biofilm formation can depend on strain background and culture conditions47, this result does not exclude a limitation of the assay under the condition tested or compensation by other biofilm-associated factors. Further analyses will be required to clarify this point.

The 35,005-bp genomic region was flanked by two IS1181 elements, which were oriented in the same direction in the vraDEH-positive CC121 strain KSA13, and they were replaced by one copy of IS1181 in the vraDEH-negative strains (Fig. 1). In addition, serial passage of KSA13 also yielded mutants showing the same genomic deletion (Fig. 3). These findings are consistent with a model in which the 35-kb deletion was generated through recombination between two IS1181 copies. These two IS1181 insertions were observed specifically in CC121, and IS1181icaR was detected only in strains belonging to a single subclade of CC121 (Fig. S4). This subclade corresponded to the subclade containing all identified 35-kb-deleted strains, and most strains in this subclade were isolated from Japan or China (East Asian subclade). These findings suggest that the IS1181 insertion upstream of icaR may be a subclade-associated genomic feature that predisposes this region to the 35-kb deletion. Within this subclade, strains isolated in Japan and China were phylogenetically separated, whereas the 35-kb-deleted strains were not phylogenetically biased (Fig. S3). IS1181 insertion sites were correlated with phylogenetic relationships both between and within CCs (Figs. 5 and 6), suggesting that the observed IS1181 insertions are often inherited vertically. Together, these observations are consistent with the possibility that IS1181icaR was acquired in an ancestor of the East Asian subclade and inherited within this lineage.

In S. aureus, examples of gene deletion through recombination affecting phenotypes have been reported: recombination between ccrC genes within the SCCmec cassette resulted in the deletion of the mecA gene and the conversion of MRSA to MSSA in a specific ST398 strain48. Another study reported that the loss of the mutS and mutL genes due to recombination between IS1181 elements caused the accumulation of genetic mutations associated with linezolid resistance21. These strains were CC5 and harbored an unusually high number of IS1181 copies (Fig. S5, two points with particularly high numbers within CC5), although the reason remains unknown.

Since CC121 has the highest average IS1181 copy number among CCs (Fig. S5), we verified whether similar genomic deletion occurred between two IS1181 pairs in CC121 strains. Using the complete genome of CC121, we identified 13 genomic regions in which the distance between two IS1181 copies oriented in the same direction was 50,000 bp or less. We also searched for strains in which these regions were deleted in all of the CC121 genomes, including contig files, but no strains were identified (data not shown). Some of the regions contained genes essential for bacterial survival identified previously49, suggesting that even if a deletion occurs in these regions, it would be lethal and that these strains could not be isolated.

In Japan, CC121 has been reported to be frequently isolated from impetigo patients50,51. Consistent with this, the eta gene, which encodes exfoliative toxin A, a toxin responsible for bullous impetigo52, was frequently detected in both the CC121 East Asian subclade and the 35-kb-deleted strains (56.7% and 46.7%, respectively) (Fig. S3). In addition, three 35-kb-deleted strains were isolated from impetigo patients (Table S3). These findings suggest that this subclade includes impetigo-associated S. aureus strains. Further studies are needed to confirm the prevalence of the 35-kb-deleted strain among impetigo-associated strains and to examine the therapeutic implications of its increased susceptibility to bacitracin and nisin A in impetigo treatment.

There are several limitations in this study.

First, the precise molecular mechanism underlying the 35-kb deletion remains unknown. Although the deletion was thought to be caused by recombination between two IS1181 copies, this study did not directly determine the specific recombination mechanism, such as the involvement of RecA or other recombination-associated factors53.

Furthermore, our phylogenetic and epidemiological analyses relied on publicly available genome data, which may be affected by sampling bias in geographic origin, isolation period, and design of each study. In addition, the 12 strains with 35-kb deletion identified from public genomes were available only as contig assemblies. Although their gene presence/absence patterns were consistent with the 35-kb deletion, the exact deletion structure and junction sequences could not be confirmed in the absence of complete genome sequences.

The number of publicly available complete genomes informative for the insertion of IS1181icaR was also limited; only one public strain (strain SA11-SX) was found to harbor IS1181icaR, except for our strain. This limited dataset restricts the robustness of the analysis regarding the relationship between phylogeny and IS insertion. As the number of available S. aureus genomes continues to increase, future analyses involving a larger number of genome datasets are needed to further evaluate our findings.

In conclusion, we identified a genomic deletion encompassing vraDEH genes generated through recombination between two IS1181 copies in several S. aureus CC121 clinical isolates. The insertion sites of IS1181 were correlated with phylogenetic relationships in the S. aureus population, and the two IS1181 insertions and subsequent genomic deletion were observed in a single subclade of CC121 enriched for isolates from East Asia. These findings suggest that lineage-associated IS1181 arrangements may contribute to genomic structural variation and selected antimicrobial susceptibility phenotypes in S. aureus.
