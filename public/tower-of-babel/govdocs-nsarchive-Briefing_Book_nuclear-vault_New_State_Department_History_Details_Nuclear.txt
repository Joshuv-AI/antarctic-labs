<div class="node__content clearfix"><div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., April 18, 2024</strong> - After nine years of unwanted delays, the State Department’s Office of the Historian (OH) last month published a new <em>Foreign Relations of the United States</em> (<em>FRUS</em>) <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v04">compilation</a> on national security policy under President Jimmy Carter that will be of great value to historians, social scientists, students, and the interested public for years to come.

Edited by James Graham Wilson, the volume provides a wealth of information, much of it new, on a range of national security topics that absorbed the Carter administration from day one, including efforts to make nuclear targeting more flexible, continuity of government concerns (and related command and control issues), MX missile basing, military budgets, emergency action procedures, missile attack false alarms, the strategic balance, and strategic intelligence estimates.

Highlights from the new <em>FRUS</em> collection are featured in an Electronic Briefing Book published today by the National Security Archive.</p><p>Pentagon opposition to the inclusion of certain information in the new <em>FRUS</em> volume held up publication for almost a decade, and while many of those objections were eventually overcome, there are a number of examples, featured below, where the Defense Department successfully withheld information from the new <em>FRUS</em> that was already declassified years ago.</p><p>Even so, the new <em>FRUS</em> history is a vivid reminder of the terrifying scenarios contemplated by policymakers and war planners during the Cold War.

Although the first few years of the Carter administration was a period of U.S.-Soviet détente, and Carter a noted nuclear critic, the documents depict U.S.

policymakers dealing routinely with terrible hypotheticals, most notably the catastrophic consequences that would result from a failure of deterrence, where a U.S.-Soviet military crisis and confrontation could lead to nuclear exchanges with millions of casualties on both sides.

As a portent of the danger at hand, during 1979-1980, the North American Air Defense Command’s warning systems malfunctioned, producing false alerts of incoming missiles.

The system failures were corrected, but a memo included in the new <em>FRUS</em> shows that President Carter privately <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v04/d167">admonished</a> Secretary of Defense Harold Brown after the November 1979 incident: “This was a serious mistake and should have been reported to me immediately.”</p><p>Certainly, top officials were alarmed by what they perceived to be the terrible risks of the nuclear age.As a portent of the danger at hand, during 1979-1980, the North American Air Defense Command’s warning systems malfunctioned, producing false alerts of incoming missiles.

Finding ways to try to preserve the NCA was central to war plans that were designed to maximize presidential control over the process.</p><p>The perception of these vulnerabilities informed the Carter administration’s Continuity of Government (COG) program, another major issue covered in the new volume.

The RAC document numbers found at the top of each page of the Carter Library documents published in this compilation are coded to the Library’s collections indicating series number, box number, and folder number along with other data.

&nbsp;With this publication, the editor used the coding to provide the archival provenance for each document to the extent possible.</p><p>What made it possible for this<em> FRUS </em>volume to be published after the long delays caused by the Pentagon’s review were appeals by OH staffers and the impact of decisions by the Interagency Security Classification Appeals Panel (ISCAP), which successfully overcame many of the Pentagon’s objections to this latest volume.

The fact that ISCAP decisions are binding (although appealable to the President) and are based on majority rule made all the difference.

In the end, ISCAP allowed the Defense Department to withhold, in whole or in part, 39 of the documents selected by the <em>FRUS</em> editors.

As noted above, some of the denied information has been declassified for years, but OH evidently believed it was better to publish this excellent compilation as soon as possible rather than to continue to engage in interagency arguments over the text.</p><p>The new volume is one of many <em>FRUS</em> compilations to have been held up in the declassification process that OH coordinates with the Defense Department and the CIA, among others.<div class="node__content clearfix">

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Brown responded to Brzezinski’s question about the level of “Soviet fatalities if population were attacked directly in the SIOP rather than following current policy, which targets industry.” The so-called “philosophy” issue refers to the priority of the then-current SIOP which was to retard Soviet post-war recovery in part by targeting industrial assets, as opposed to population as such. The latter approach was a descendant, although on a horrifically larger scale, of the World War II “area bombing” campaigns that produced large-scale urban casualties.</p><p>According to Brown, the industrial targets option was “estimated to produce Soviet fatalities of about 33 percent (84.5M) of the total population (includes estimated effects from fallout).” By contrast, in a direct attack on the population using SIOP weapons and Poseidon weapons otherwise committed to military targets, the “estimated fatalities would increase to about 44 percent (115M).” Interestingly, while this figure was declassified, the Pentagon withheld similar numbers from <em>FRUS</em> <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v04/d124">Document 124</a>.</p><p>The excision in this memorandum demonstrates the deep-seated nature of secrecy surrounding the SIOP. One possibility is that it concerns the number of weapons required by the plan’s largest option.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>One of the innovations of Nixon’s NSDM 242 was that it directed the creation of a “Secure Reserve Force” of strategic weapons systems whose purpose would be “protection and coercion during and after nuclear conflict.” Survivable forces such as SLBMs or bombers and ICBMs in hardened shelters would constitute the core of the SRF that could be used for retaliation or retaliatory threats (coercion). Some, such as Deputy Secretary of State Warren Christopher wondered whether, if such a change leaked, it would harm U.S.-Soviet relations, to which Brzezinski observed “should this leak … upset the Soviets, too bad.”</p><p>Also discussed was the targeting of population centers and command-and-control assets. This continued discussion from a few weeks earlier (See D<a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v04/d118">ocument 118</a>), where there was some controversy with JCS Chair David Jones arguing that it was illegal, but where others believed that what really deterred the Soviets was a “threat to the Soviet population.” During the discussion of targeting options against the Soviet Union’s “C3-leadership,” David Aaron asked whether it was “wise to destroy Soviet ability to withhold and control strategic forces from the center.” Nevertheless, Brown asserted that a successful attack on the Soviet leadership could “require very early strikes in a conflict.”</p><p>The discussion of a targeting strategy that promoted “regionalization” of the Soviet Union prompted Brzezinski to call explicitly for an approach “that inflicts higher levels of damage on the great Russians and creates fears that the Soviet Union would dissolve.” The dialog turned to economic targets, including war-supporting industries as a specific target set, and whether that would reduce collateral fatalities, with Deputy Under Secretary of Defense (International Security Affairs) Walter Slocombe noting that “such targeting might involve 10-15 million fatalities if no care were taken to avoid them.” The meeting concluded with the topic of Hard Target Kill capabilities, which ICBM accuracy improvements could improve, and whether such capabilities were destabilizing, as some in Congress feared. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>This June 1980 letter from FEMA Director Macy to President Carter gave perspective on the Nine Lives exercise discussed above. Conducting Nine Lives in early May 1990 were FEMA and the Joint Chiefs with the participation of other agencies, including State, CIA, and Justice, along with White House offices. The exercise posited a “rapidly deteriorating worldwide situation leading to nuclear war,” but with enough warning time to “disperse selected Presidential successors to pre-identified locations prior to the attack.” Various emergency plans were reviewed, exercised, and evaluated, including the Treetop plan, to “support a dispersed successor” and dispersal of the Vice President under tactical warning, the SIOP and “communications connectivity to the dispersed successors.” Macy reported that, “To my knowledge, this was the first time a coordinated and concurrent exercise of emergency plans in support of the President and his potential successors has been conducted.”</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Odom reported on the response to a false warning systems incident the night before, a hitherto undisclosed event. At 9:32 PM NORAD and the NMCC convened a conference call after a report came in about the detection of missile launches from a Kurile Island site where the “‘fan’ of one of the missile tracks included Alaska, Canada, and the tip of Oregon.” The conference call ended once warning system operators determined that “the impact of both missiles were determined to be in Kamchatka.” Odom reported that procedures worked properly: he was alerted promptly, NEACP “rolled out” at 9:32 PM, SAC readiness changed at 9:30 PM, and “The duty officers at NORAD and the NMCC sorted out the ‘anomalies’ in the computer displays quickly.”</p><p>As noted, the volume provides good coverage of the other NORAD false alarms of November 1979 and June 1980, including more complete versions of several documents from 1980.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>At the end of the Carter administration, Odom prepared for his boss his perspective on the “bureaucratic play” during the previous four years concerning major developments in the national security system as conveyed through presidential directives and changes in government organization. It is a fuller version of the memorandum (<em>FRUS</em> <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v04/d211">Document 211</a>) that Brzezinski sent to President Carter months earlier. Beginning with PRM-10/PD-18, Odom’s report covered the creation of FEMA, PD-41 on civil defense, PD-53 on telecommunications, PD-57 on mobilization requirements, PD-58, and PD-59. Odom argued that the series of directives represented a radical break from the “1914 syndrome” by facilitating “mobilization, defense,” and “command and control for a long conflict.” Nevertheless, he also acknowledged that they “remain essentially a paper policy” until they “significantly affected the budget process, program designs in Defense, or operational procedures in the JCS and unified commands.”</p><p>Prepared by a White House insider who had full access to the records, but also a strong point of view, this is a highly informative memorandum, with interesting details on a number of issues. For example, Odom believed that the Presidential Directive on Civil Defense (PD-41) implicitly abandoned the view “that strategic defense is impossible or destabilizing.” He also cited a memorandum on “C<sup>3</sup>I” that Brzezinski sent to Charles Duncan on 30 September 1978 (See <em>FRUS</em> <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v04/d92">Document 92</a>), noting concern about “vulnerability to a relatively small Soviet attack, and our lack of C<sup>3</sup>I endurance in the event of a war;” both of those points are excised from the FRUS version.
