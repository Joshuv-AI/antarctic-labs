 Executive Summary
 Wyoming stands at a pivotal point in deﬁning its energy future, and the Wyoming Carbon
 Blueprint positions the state to lead the nation in carbon management through a coordinated
 buildout of carbon capture, transportation, utilization, and geologic storage. Building on
 Wyoming’s long history as a major producer of coal, oil, and natural gas, and supported by
 favorable policies, skilled labor, and robust subsurface resources, the Blueprint outlines a
 pathway to develop one of the world’s ﬁrst open-access carbon hubs capable of capturing
 between 10 and 25 million tonnes of CO₂ per year within ﬁve years, and up to 45 million tonnes
 within a decade. In doing so, it advances the concept of “No Carbon Left Behind”: a statewide
 strategy that prevents stranded CO₂ by aligning capture, transport, and storage systems into a
 single coherent infrastructure network.
 The state’s industrial landscape provides a compelling platform for this vision. Wyoming’s 73
 capturable emission sources collectively emit more than 46 million tonnes of CO₂ annually, with
 coal power plants alone producing over 70% of these emissions. Additional contributions from
 the oil and gas, mining, and reﬁning sectors underscore the central role that fossil energy
 continues to play in the state’s economy.
 The Blueprint details the technical readiness of capture pathways, examining pre-combustion,
 post-combustion, and oxy-fuel systems, as well as absorption, adsorption, membrane,
 cryogenic, and calcium looping technologies. Cost estimates underscore the importance of
 targeting high-volume, lower-cost capture opportunities—particularly from coal units—while
 acknowledging the diversity of industrial CO₂ streams across the state. With literature-based
 capture costs averaging $58 per tonne and modeled costs averaging $123 per tonne, Wyoming’s
 ability to combine diverse emitters into larger, optimized networks will be essential for
 maintaining cost-effectiveness.
 Transport modeling reveals that no single mode can meet the state’s needs alone. Wyoming’s
 existing CO₂ pipeline infrastructure, roughly 600 miles today, remains limited in geographic
 reach. To bridge this gap, the Blueprint presents an integrated approach using a combination of
 pipelines, rail, and trucking. Pipelines remain the lowest-cost option for high-volume ﬂows, yet
 rail offers surprisingly strong advantages for medium volumes and long distances, while trucks
 provide short-term ﬂexibility for remote or early-stage deployments. Intermodal modeling shows
 that rail-based options are signiﬁcantly more efficient than truck-based systems, reinforcing
 their role as Wyoming scales toward a statewide CO₂ network.

 Wyoming’s greatest strategic advantage lies beneath its surface. The Blueprint provides a basin-
 by-basin evaluation of geological storage potential and concludes that the Greater Green River
 Basin, anchored by the Rock Springs Uplift and Moxa Arch, offers some of the most promising
 deep saline storage formations in the United States, with total capacities sufficient to store
 Wyoming’s industrial emissions for decades. Additional opportunities exist in the Powder River
 Basin and Wind River Basin, though each basin exhibits its own blend of constraints related to
 depth, salinity, trap integrity, or basin geometry. Advanced modeling with NRAP quantiﬁes plume
 extent, pressure evolution, and leakage risk across a range of simulated storage conditions,
 demonstrating both the viability of Wyoming subsurface systems and the importance of careful
 site selection, wellbore management, and long-term monitoring.
 The Blueprint also emphasizes the importance of CO₂ utilization, particularly enhanced oil
 recovery (EOR), which has a decades-long track record in the state. Wyoming’s existing nine
 CO₂-EOR projects, coupled with its undeveloped potential of over 2 billion barrels of recoverable
 oil, offer a near-term commercial pathway for captured CO₂. Additional long-term opportunities
 include synthetic fuel production, materials manufacturing, chemical conversion, and food and
 agricultural applications. These utilization pathways can support diversiﬁcation of Wyoming’s
 industrial base while complementing permanent sequestration.
 Wyoming’s policy landscape is among the most supportive in the nation for CCUS deployment.
 The state holds primacy for Class VI wells, enabling signiﬁcantly faster permitting timelines than
 federal processes. Combined with federal 45Q tax credits and state incentives such as SF 17,
 developers can potentially access up to $95 per tonne of CO₂ stored or used for EOR. The
 Blueprint also highlights non-technical challenges, such as regulatory coordination, workforce
 gaps, supply chain risks, and community acceptance, and offers strategies to mitigate them
 through transparent siting, proactive engagement, and workforce development programs.
 Finally, the Blueprint introduces a statewide CCS Readiness Index that integrates storage
 potential, emissions, and transport opportunities to identify which counties are most prepared
 for investment. Sweetwater County emerges as the most CCS-ready region, with strong
 candidates also found in Natrona, Converse, Campbell, and Carbon counties. This readiness
 framework enables planners, policymakers, and industry to align investment with the state’s
 highest-potential corridors.
 Taken together, the Wyoming Carbon Blueprint presents an integrated, deeply informed
 roadmap that captures the full technical, economic, regulatory, and social dimensions of
 deploying CCS at scale. It identiﬁes the opportunity for Wyoming not only to decarbonize its
 existing energy system but to lead nationally in building the infrastructure, workforce, and
 industrial base of the carbon management economy.

 SECTION 1

 1 Introduction
 Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) offers both economic opportunities and
 industrial innovations in Wyoming, including the potential for enhanced oil recovery, providing
 CO2 as a feedstock to novel manufacturing production, and leveraging federal business
 incentives related to carbon. To encourage bold but thoughtful CCUS development, this project
 will create a statewide assessment of the opportunities, risks, and beneﬁts of implementing
 carbon utilization and storage at scale in Wyoming.
 This state-level plan for CCUS, the Wyoming Carbon Blueprint, will design an approach to an
 open-access carbon hub development focused on the concept of “No Carbon Left Behind” (i.e.,
 no stranded CO2), sharing information with commercial partners and business developers along
 the way. The business case underlying the Carbon Blueprint will aim to capture, transport, and
 store between 10 and 25 MtCO2/yr in its ﬁrst ﬁve years of operation, not including construction,
 from a diverse range of 30 facilities across the southern half of Wyoming. Within 10 years of
 starting operation, this would increase to as much as 45 MtCO2/yr from around 60 facilities
 across the entire state. We recognize the need to make a real-world business case, considering
 the current and future capture, storage, utilization, and transportation needs. At the same time,
 this project incorporates workforce readiness, the current regulatory and policy landscape, and
 other critical commercial considerations to make Wyoming home to the world’s largest open-
 access carbon hub.

 2.2 Methods of Carbon Capture
 Point source carbon capture is separated into three categories depending on when and how the
 CO2 is captured: pre-combustion, post-combustion, or oxy-fuel combustion capture. In pre-
 combustion capture, CO2 is removed before any fuel combustion occurs in a series of chemical
 reactions that generate hydrogen and CO2. In post-combustion capture, CO2 is removed from
 the ﬂue gas of a power or industrial plant. In oxy-fuel combustion, fuel is combusted in a nearly
 pure oxygen environment, increasing the concentration of CO2 after combustion, which
 decreases the energy intensity of separating and purifying the CO2.4

 2.2.1 Pre-Combustion Capture
 Pre-combustion carbon capture removes CO2 from fuels before combustion using a process
 called fuel gasiﬁcation. The fuel, which can be any carbonaceous material like coal, natural gas,
 or biomass, is reacted with steam and oxygen (or air) to generate fuel gas, alternatively known as
 synthesis gas or syngas.5 The fuel gas is composed of hydrogen gas, H2, and carbon monoxide,
 CO. While syngas has a variety of uses, it can be further reacted with steam and a catalyst to
 make a pure stream of hydrogen and CO2.5 The CO2 is then separated, leaving only hydrogen.
 This method applied to natural gas (methane) is known as steam methane reforming and is
 currently the primary production process to create hydrogen in the United States.6 Capturing
 CO2 before combustion can be an overall cheaper and less energy-intensive process than post-

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                 4

 and oxy-combustion, but it necessitates a customized plant layout.7 Often, this is too expensive
 to implement on an existing plant and is not suitable for all applications.

 2.2.2 Oxy-Combustion Capture
 Oxy-fuel combustion involves combusting all fuel in a primarily oxygen environment instead of
 air. Air is mostly nitrogen (78%), so isolating oxygen before combustion increases the
 concentration of CO2 in the exit stream (80-98% CO2). This reduces the size of equipment and
 energy required to purify the CO2; in some cases, only water needs to be condensed from the
 exit stream to have pure CO2.8 Additionally, removing nitrogen before combustion signiﬁcantly
 decreases the formation of toxic NOx compounds.4 This reduction lessens the need for
 additional emissions control equipment, reducing overall capital costs. Combustion in an
 oxygen environment produces more heat than in air, which also decreases the amount of fuel
 required. However, this requires careful monitoring of the system and recirculation of existing
 ﬂue gas to prevent the stream from overheating.9 This combustion method requires a dedicated
 air separation unit to produce oxygen from ambient air, which is relatively expensive and has a
 high energy requirement.7 Oxy-fuel combustion technology could be integrated into natural gas
 power plant infrastructure and would beneﬁt from further research on optimizing oxygen
 separation.8

 2.2.3 Post-Combustion Capture
 Post-combustion carbon capture refers to technology that captures and separates CO2 from the
 ﬂue gas of a plant after combustion has occurred. The ﬂue gas stream is typically diluted with
 CO2, requiring a more selective separation process. CO2 must be separated from typical post-
 combustion pollutants, such as sulfur oxides (SOx), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and particulate matter
 (PM), as these pollutants can adversely impact many common separation mechanisms.
 Removal of the pollutants requires additional equipment to perform preconditioning, which
 raises the capital cost and energy penalty for operation but may also result in a reduction in local
 air emissions.10 Because the CO2 is separated from a plant’s exit stream, this method is well-
 suited for retroﬁtting existing plants and is often cheaper than changing an entire plant’s design.
 Post-combustion carbon capture is currently the most mature and widely adopted method of
 CO2 capture.11

 2.3 CO2 Capture Technologies
 Whether pre-, post-, or oxy-fuel combustion capture is chosen, the stream of CO2 can be
 separated and puriﬁed through a variety of different mechanisms. The ﬁve most common are
 absorption, adsorption, membrane, cryogenic, and calcium looping.
 Absorption capture technology can be broken into chemical and physical absorption. Chemical
 absorption is typically paired with post-combustion carbon capture and is the most mature and
 widely used capture technology today.11 In chemical absorption, CO2 is reacted in a reversible
 reaction, typically with an amine-based solvent.12 The CO2 forms an intermediate product by
 bonding with the amine while other gases in the stream pass through, before the rich solvent is
 heated, releasing the CO2 as a pure stream and regenerating lean solvent. Physical absorption is
 similar to chemical absorption in that CO2 permeates the entirety of the absorbent, although
 through a non-reactive mechanism. In physical absorption, CO2 capture preferentially happens
 at lower temperatures and higher concentrations of CO2; therefore, physical adsorption capture

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                   5

 is commonly used in pre-combustion capture systems and is often outperformed by chemical
 absorption.13
 Adsorption mechanisms trap CO2 on an adsorbent’s surface, which is then released by changing
 the incoming temperature and pressure, or by adding an electric current to drive desorption.
 Adsorbents are chosen based on their surface area, pore structure, and chemical structure,
 which determine the amount of space that the CO2 can occupy and how it binds to the surface.4
 Some adsorbents require a large amount of energy to release the captured CO2, while others
 capture CO2 only for a short period before releasing it again.14 A signiﬁcant challenge facing this
 mechanism of capture is the cost to manufacture the adsorbent, coupled with the rate of
 adsorbent degradation.4 There is a wide range of adsorbents that are viable capture options,
 including activated carbon, alumina, silica, metal-organic frameworks, zeolites, and amine-
 based sorbents.
 Membrane technology uses porous and permeable material to selectively allow for CO2 transfer.
 Gas is blown over the membrane, CO2 adsorbs to one side, travels through the membrane due to
 a concentration gradient, and then desorbs on the other side as a high-purity CO2 stream. This
 process requires a signiﬁcantly lower amount of energy than absorption capture methods, has
 low operational complexity, is easily integrated into a variety of plant and ﬂue gas conditions,
 and has lower capital costs.15 To sustain a proper concentration gradient, CO2 needs to enter
 with a high partial pressure, which can be limiting for certain applications yet remains
 competitive due to its lower energy penalty and capital costs.
 Cryogenic carbon capture relies on the principle that chemical species have different freezing
 and condensation points, allowing for separation by cooling and compressing an incoming gas
 stream until the CO2 either becomes a liquid or solid.15 This design allows for higher purity and
 selectivity toward CO2 compared to other capture mechanisms; however, the cooling
 requirement adds a signiﬁcant energy load that can be cost-prohibitive.4 When coupled with
 oxy-fuel combustion, it becomes an attractive method of purifying CO2.16
 Calcium looping reacts calcium oxide with CO2 in a carbonator to form calcium carbonate
 (limestone), which is later heated in a calciner to regenerate calcium oxide and release a pure
 CO2 stream.4 Calcium oxide and carbonate reactions lose efficacy after several cycles and must
 be replaced. Calcium looping requires high temperatures for both carbonation and calcination,
 which makes it an attractive pairing with oxy-fuel combustion processes. It has been suggested
 that calcium looping is the cheapest method of carbon capture for the cement industry when
 compared to amine-based post-combustion absorption and oxy-fuel combustion.17 A summary
 of the ﬁve most common mechanisms of carbon capture is shown below in Figure 2.

 3 Transportation
 An essential element in developing a Carbon Blueprint for Wyoming is to understand the
 different pathways available for transporting CO₂ from capture sites to storage or utilization
 locations. While pipelines remain the most common option, with more than 5,000 miles already
 operating in the U.S., other modes can also play a role depending on distance, geography, and
 scale. In particular, rail and road offer ﬂexibility for facilities that capture or need to move smaller
 volumes of CO₂, or where pipeline construction may be less feasible.
 In this study, we evaluate CO₂ transport not only through pipelines but also by rail and truck. For
 both modes, we consider two conﬁgurations: tanker transport, in which CO₂ is moved in
 specialized pressurized containers, and intermodal transport, in which containers can be
 shifted between different modes along the supply chain. By including all these modes, the
 analysis provides a more complete picture of potential transport solutions across Wyoming.

 3.1 Pipeline transport
 CostMAPPRO is a geospatial modeling tool designed to estimate the cost of routing CO₂ pipelines
 within any area of interest. The tool creates two complementary surfaces: a cost surface and a
 routing surface. The cost surface captures the ﬁnancial impact of terrain, land cover, slope, and
 other physical variables on pipeline installation. The routing surface reﬂects environmental,
 social, and regulatory considerations that inﬂuence where pipelines may or may not be placed.
 Keeping these surfaces separate is important. While the cost surface ensures accurate
 estimates, the routing surface helps identify paths that avoid sensitive areas, such as critical
 habitats or densely populated zones, even if those routes are not the cheapest.
 To construct these surfaces, CostMAPPRO integrates various geospatial layers that fall into three
 main categories: general, corridor, and barrier. General layers include variables such as slope or
 population density. Corridor layers represent linear infrastructure like roads or railways that may
 facilitate routing. Barrier layers represent features such as rivers or highways that are costly or
 undesirable to cross. The speciﬁc geospatial layers used in this study are summarized in Table 7.
 By combining these weighted inputs, the tool generates a gridded representation of the
 landscape, assigning both cost and routing values to each cell.

 Truck transportation is highly ﬂexible and suitable for short distances and smaller project

 3.2.3 Truck and Rail Transport Efficiency
 Beyond the discussion of capacities and costs, an important consideration is the overall
 efficiency of CO₂ transport by truck and rail. Efficiency captures how well each mode performs
 as a function of transport distance and total CO₂ volume, and it highlights the trade-offs that
 emerge when comparing alternatives. While pipelines typically provide the most stable
 performance, both truck and rail options show declining efficiency with increasing distance or
 decreasing shipment size. Understanding these patterns is essential for evaluating when truck
 or rail transport may serve as viable complements to pipeline infrastructure.
 The concept of net CO₂ transport efficiency captures the proportion of CO₂ that successfully
 arrives at the storage site after accounting for the emissions associated with transport activities.
 It is deﬁned as the amount of CO₂ delivered minus the amount emitted, divided by the amount
 originally transported. This formulation highlights that efficiency is not only determined by
 technical capacity but also by the energy and emissions linked to the entire logistics chain.
 In practice, a number of additional factors inﬂuence this calculation. These include the energy
 required for liquefaction before transport, the role of upstream and downstream buffer storage,
 and the use of trailers and containers. Fuel consumption for both trucks and trains is a major

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                   11

 driver, while intermediate storage and boil-off gas losses can further reduce efficiency. Finally,
 reconditioning energy at the point of injection must also be considered. Together, these
 elements provide a more complete lifecycle perspective of transport performance.
 When comparing the average efficiency of the four main transport combinations, clear
 differences emerge (see Figure 4). Rail-intermodal shows the highest efficiency at 0.83, followed
 by rail-tanker at 0.75. Truck-based systems perform notably worse, with truck-tanker averaging
 0.64 and truck-intermodal 0.61. These results indicate that, in general, rail is a more efficient
 mode for long-haul transport, while trucks are more affected by energy and emissions losses
 over comparable distances.

 A central outcome of this study, regarding CO₂ transport, is the comparison of transport costs
 across pipelines, rail, and truck systems. The literature review and subsequent modeling make it
 clear that the relative cost-effectiveness of each mode depends strongly on both the
 transported volume and the travel distance. Table 13 provides a summary of costs associated

 The results indicate that truck transport is economically suitable only at small scales, generally
 under 1 MtCO₂ per year, and for distances between 10 and 600 km. In this range, trucks provide

 adapts to both near-term and long-term needs.

 3.3 Combination of Transportation Modes
 Intermodal transportation allows CO₂ to move across more than one type of transport network,
 switching between modes on its way from the source to a storage or utilization destination.
 Assigning a single mode of transport (pipeline, rail, or road) between source and destination is
 relatively straightforward, but intermodal transport introduces the possibility of combining
 multiple modes in a single trip, which presents technical challenges for modeling. Various
 combinations of transport modes that can be considered are:

 • Truck and Rail Combination: This combination would be used when the CO2 emission
 source is located far from the injection site, and direct rail access to the site is unavailable.
 • Truck and Pipeline Combination: Utilized when the initial capture site is not directly
 connected to the pipeline network. The CO2 is ﬁrst transported by truck to a pipeline
 terminal, where it is then injected into the pipeline for long-distance transport.
 • Rail and Pipeline Combination: Effective for large-scale operations with centralized CO2
 capture facilities and distributed injection sites. The CO2 is moved by rail to a main pipeline
 hub and then distributed through pipelines to various injection locations.
 • Intermodal and Tanker Combinations: Depending on the scale and distance, intermodal
 containers or tankers can be used in both truck and rail combinations to optimize cost and
 ﬂexibility. The CO2 can be transported by rail tankers for long distances and then transferred
 to truck intermodals for the ﬁnal leg to the injection site, or vice versa, depending on
 logistical and cost considerations. Figure 5 presents examples of intermodal transitions that
 can be generated within the SimCCSPRO.

 4 CO2 Storage
 Geologic storage of CO2 in the subsurface requires reservoir rocks, seal rocks or conﬁning
 zones, and structural or stratigraphic traps. Reservoir rocks must be sufficiently porous and
 permeable to host high volumes of CO2, at depths between 800 and ~4000m. Storage must be
 deeper than ~800m to ensure that the injected CO2 reaches supercritical conditions, which
 increases storage efficiency and security.31 Storage at depths greater than 4000m requires
 additional compression to inject CO2.32 In-situ reservoir ﬂuids must be non-potable, and
 therefore have total dissolved solid (TDS) concentrations above 10,000 mg/L.33 Seal rocks must
 have low porosities and permeabilities, capable of trapping buoyant columns of gas by capillary
 forces, have an extrinsic stress state that can withstand opening-mode fracture formation,34 and
 are preferably laterally continuous. Physical traps are usually structural (such as anticlines or
 domes) or stratigraphic (pinching out of speciﬁc sedimentary layers) and prevent the lateral or
 upward migration of CO2.
 The most promising targets for geologic CO2 storage in Wyoming are deep Paleozoic saline
 aquifers in the Greater Green River Basin (GGRB) in southwestern Wyoming.35 Most of the
 research into CO2 storage has focused on the structural traps of the Rock Springs Uplift (RSU)
 and Moxa Arch (MA) in the GGRB.36–39 Regional geologic evaluation of stacked saline storage in
 the limbs of the RSU has led to a preliminary estimated storage capacity between 6.7 and 17.2
 million metric tons per square mile, enough to store Wyoming’s industrial CO2 emissions.36,38
 Below is an ordered list of targets for further study for geologic CO2 storage, ranked from high to
 low priority:
 • Rock Springs Uplift, Greater Green River Basin
 • Moxa Arch, Greater Green River Basin
 • Powder River Basin
 • Wind River Basin
 • Denver Basin
 • Bighorn Basin
 As an alternative to deep saline storage in Wyoming, CO2 can be stored in depleted oil and gas
 reservoirs through enhanced oil recovery (EOR), or enhanced coalbed methane (i.e., natural gas)
 recovery (ECBM). Lynds40 assesses the CO2 storage potential of Laramide basins in Wyoming
 through these distinct methods. This report focuses mostly on summarizing the saline storage
 potential in Wyoming and compiles data contained in Lynds40, as well as information on storage
 assessment units (SAUs) outlined in the USGS Geologic Framework for the National Assessment

 basin, Wind River basin, Bighorn basin, Powder River basin, Hanna-Shirley-Laramie basins, and
 Denver Basin (Figure 6). These are all basins that formed in the Laramide orogeny, and most have
 a synclinal fold axis, adjacent to a monoclinal or anticlinal uplift (Figure 6).

 4.1 Basin-wide Potential
 4.1.1 Greater Green River Basin
 The GGRB covers southwest Wyoming and is a consolidation of sub-basins (Green River, Great
 Divide, Washakie, and Sand Wash Basins), which are separated by anticlinal or domal uplifts.
 The GGRB is bounded to the west by the Sevier overthrust belt, to the south by the Uinta
 Mountains, and to the north by the Wind River Mountains. The GGRB contains large
 accumulations of naturally occurring CO2, such as the La Barge platform.
 4.1.1.1 Potential for CO2 storage
 The structure of the GGRB with sub-basins and adjacent uplifts makes it a good target for CO2
 storage in Wyoming. The basin hosts the largest geologic traps in Wyoming, the Rock Springs

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                             18

 Uplift and the Moxa Arch. The natural CO2 accumulations demonstrate that the basin can host
 large volumes of gas on geologic timescales. Some of the Mesozoic reservoir units geologically
 capable of hosting CO2 (e.g., adequate depth, porosity, and permeability) may not be suitable for
 storage based on salinities less than 10,000 mg/L, such as the Nugget Sandstone. Lynds 40
 suggests storage may be limited by the depth of the reservoir units, as most are greater than
 ~4000m (13,000 feet), meaning additional compression of CO2 would be required at the surface.
 Other storage opportunities in the GGRB include storage in depleted oil and gas ﬁelds, through
 EOR or ECBM (the basin hosts several mineable coal ﬁelds). The younger Eocene Wasatch and
 Green River formations are lacustrine and ﬂuvial sediments that host coalbed natural gas.
 4.1.1.2 Hydrogeology
 Compiled water data for the Nugget Sandstone, Tensleep/Weber Sandstone, Madison
 Limestone, and Bighorn Dolomite from the GGRB suggests that in the Rock Springs Uplift, the
 Nugget Sandstone receives local, fracture-assisted recharge and therefore salinities are too low
 for CO2 storage 42. Buursink et al.43 show that Nugget Sandstone SAUs are conﬁned to the
 western side of the basin where salinities are suitable. Industrial-grade groundwaters (deﬁned at
 TDS > 5000 mg/L) are mostly encountered at depths greater than 600m, and salinity generally
 increases with depth.44

 4.1.2 Wind River Basin
 The Wind River Basin is bordered to the south by the Wind River Mountains. Gentle dip slopes
 ﬂank the mountains and continue to the basin axis. A large proportion of the basin is tribal land
       (Figure 6).
 4.1.2.1 Potential for CO2 storage
 Unlike GGRB, there are no major geologic structures capable of hosting large volumes of CO2 in
 WRB. The synclinal geometry of the basin promotes up-dip migration of CO2 in the absence of
 other geologic traps. However, extensive studies from Keefer 45 and Kirschbaum et al.46 ensure
 structural conﬁgurations and sediment thicknesses are well-deﬁned in this basin. Some
 stratigraphic traps, such as up-dip lateral changes in the Phosphoria Formation, are present.
 Several reservoir-seal pairs are present at the required depths for deep saline aquifer storage.
 Hydrocarbon ﬁelds are in smaller faulted anticlines or domal structures along the basin margins
 and could be considered for CO2 storage. Lynds40 suggests the Big Sand Draw, Riverton Dome,
 Steamboat Butte, and Poison Spider West hydrocarbon ﬁelds meet the depth, pressure, and
 seal criteria for CO2 storage. Other hydrocarbon ﬁelds were either too deep, too shallow, or
 contained complexly faulted traps. There may be potential for CO2-ECBM in the Mesaverde
 formation. CO2-EOR in the WRB is currently employed in the Beaver Creek, Big Sand, and Grieve
 oil ﬁelds in the Madison Limestone, Tensleep Sandstone, and Muddy Sandstone.47
 4.1.2.2 Hydrogeology
 Industrial-grade groundwaters (deﬁned at TDS > 5000 mg/L) are more prevalent in the basin
 interior or across the northern margin.48 The mean groundwater salinity exceeds 10,000 mg/L
 TDS at depths greater than ~3000m. However, the deeper Tensleep Sandstone and Madison
 Limestone storage reservoirs have lower salinities (~2500 mg/L), compared to shallower
 Mesozoic units. Both the Tensleep and Madison aquifers are also normally pressured and
 receive recharge from exposures on the ﬂanks of the Wind River and Granite Mountain uplifts, in
 contrast to the anomalously pressured Mesozoic aquifers at shallower stratigraphic depths.

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                19

 The TDS concentrations in the Tensleep Sandstone only range from 146 to 1060 mg/L. 49 The
 Madison Limestone is a major aquifer near the ﬂanks of the uplifts due to solution-enlarged
 fractures and has an average salinity of only 200 mg/L.49 The low salinities may disqualify both
 deep reservoirs for CO2 storage.

 4.1.3 Bighorn Basin
 The Bighorn Basin is located to the north of the state and is bounded by the Bighorn Mountains
 to the north, and the Owl Creek, Absaroka, and Beartooth mountains to the south and west.
 4.1.3.1 Potential for CO2 storage
 Much like the Wind River Basin, the Bighorn Basin has a bowl-shaped geometry and a distinct
 lack of geologic traps needed to store large volumes of CO2. The Tensleep Sandstone, Madison
 Limestone, and the Mesozoic Crow Mountain Sandstone are the most promising reservoirs for
 deep saline CO2 storage in the Bighorn Basin. However, the Tensleep and Madison units are at
 depths greater than 13,000 feet in the Bighorn Basin, so they are not currently suitable.
 Hydrocarbon ﬁelds are not depleted in the Bighorn Basin, and recovery is still economic;
 therefore, CO2 storage in depleted oil and gas reservoirs is not yet an option. Lynds40 suggests
 that CO2 storage outside of EOR is likely not possible. The Madison Limestone is unsuitable for
 CO2 storage through EOR due to its highly fractured nature, and storage through ECBM is also
 not feasible in the Bighorn Basin, as coal mining has only occurred on a very small scale. 40
 4.1.3.2 Hydrogeology
 In general, TDS concentrations in the Bighorn Basin increase with depth. Extremely high
 salinities are infrequent in the Bighorn Basin, but otherwise, patterns of salinity are like WRB,
 where the deepest Paleozoic units exhibit a decrease in salinity at depths greater than 11,000
 feet. The Tensleep and Madison aquifers receive recharge from exposures along the ﬂanks of the
 Bighorn Mountains and currently supply water for domestic, public, industrial, and irrigation
 uses.48 Taucher et al49 at the Wyoming State Geological Survey (WSGS) suggest these aquifers
 have high development potential for all water uses, which makes them unlikely targets for CO2
 storage.

 4.1.4 Powder River Basin
 The Powder River Basin (PRB) is an asymmetrical basin in northeast Wyoming with a gently
 dipping eastern limb and a steeply dipping western limb. PRB is bounded to the west by the
 Bighorn Mountains, to the south by the Laramie Mountains, and to the north by the Miles City
 arch in Montana. The basin has a long history of oil production and hydrocarbon extraction and
 is now Wyoming’s primary coal-producing basin, from which natural gas is also produced (Figure
 6). The basin has been extensively studied for CO2 storage, mostly through enhanced coalbed
 methane or smaller-scale reservoir units. Most of the basin has been covered by 2D seismic
 surveys, and the USGS made four seismic surveys publicly available in the ‘Madison Project’.50
 4.1.4.1 Potential for CO2 storage
 Structural traps in PRB are not present, and the basin has a bowl-shaped geometry much like
 the other Laramide Basins in Wyoming, so this would need to be considered for storage in saline
 aquifers. Lynds 40 calculated that theoretical storage resources in deep saline aquifers in the
 PRB are up to seven times larger than those in the other sedimentary basins in Wyoming. The

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                20

 most suitable storage reservoirs are the Minnelusa Sandstone and the Madison Limestone, if the
 salinity is sufficiently high.
 Cretaceous-aged sandstones – e.g., Muddy Sandstone, Frontier Sandstone – are all signiﬁcant
 hydrocarbon producers in the basin, so they likely have suitable porosity and permeability to
 store CO2. Deposition of the Cretaceous units was related to transgression or regression of the
 Western Interior Seaway, so they are laterally continuous over large distances and interbedded
 with localized mudstones and siltstones that can behave as seals. However, Lynds 40 mentions
 that these are more suitable for CO2 storage in depleted hydrocarbons, rather than as deep
 saline aquifer storage. Due to the high production of coalbed methane in the PRB, it is more
 likely that CO2 will be stored through ECBM, especially in unmineable coal seams.
 4.1.4.2 Hydrogeology
 The principal aquifer systems in the PRB are up to 2500m deep, and groundwater ﬂow is
 conﬁned and northerly.51 The Madison Limestone aquifer and Tensleep aquifer are the most
 frequently accessed units for public water, and water quality is good to excellent according to
 EPA standards.52 These aquifers have the most suitable characteristics for CO2 storage, but their
 low salinities and public water use may disqualify them for further consideration.

 4.1.5 Hanna-Shirley-Laramie Basins
 The Hanna, Shirley, and Laramie Basins are separated internally by smaller Laramide uplifts, but
 they are small and are not major hydrocarbon producers in comparison to the other basins in
 Wyoming.
 The Laramie Basin, the largest of the three, has a maximum depth of ~10,000 feet. In contrast,
 the Hanna Basin is small but is up to 40,000 feet deep with very thick sequences of Upper
 Cretaceous and lower Tertiary rocks.53 The Shirley Basin is a very shallow syncline with depths of
 only 7000 feet. Oil and gas exploration has been focused on the deeper sediments in the Hanna
 Basin.
 The Paleozoic strata that often form the most desirable CO2 storage reservoirs in other Wyoming
 basins are less than 2500 feet thick in the Hanna Basin,54 and instead the major sediment
 accumulations are Cretaceous to Paleocene ﬂuvial and lacustrine deposits and can be up to
 19,000 feet thick.55,56 Lynds suggests that the CO2 storage potential through saline aquifers, EOR,
 or ECBM in the Hanna Basin is low. The basin is too deep for saline storage, and the steeply
 dipping basin geometry encourages up-dip migration of CO2.40 Hydrocarbon production volumes
 in the Hanna-Shirley-Laramie Basins have been small, suggesting limited pore space available
 for economic CO2 storage, and none of the oil and gas ﬁelds are considered good candidates for
 storage through EOR.57 The lack of coal-bed natural gas ﬁelds also limits any potential for
 storage through ECBM.

 4.1.6 Denver Basin
 The Denver Basin is an asymmetrical foreland-type basin located in the southeastern corner of
 Wyoming. It is mostly located in Colorado, but encompasses areas of Wyoming, South Dakota,
 Kansas, and Nebraska. The basin is more than 13,000 feet deep, but the majority of the
 stratigraphic section is of Cretaceous age or younger, deposited during and after the Laramide
 orogeny. Strata on the western side of the basin dip steeply eastward, and eastern strata dip
 more gently to the west.

 4.1.6.1 Potential for CO2 storage in the Denver Basin
 The basin lacks adequate traps for the storage of CO2 in saline aquifers, and the dipping strata
 promote migration of CO2 to the surface. Lynds 40 did not explore storage in deep aquifers in the
 Denver Basin further because the ‘basin did not meet the criteria’; however, there are four Class

 potential storage volume available through CO2-EOR, on the other hand, is not high enough

 storage through ECBM is unlikely as there has been no development of coalbed natural gas in
 the Denver Basin. The USGS determined ﬁve SAUs in the Eastern Denver Basin; however, only
 one SAU (Plainview and Lytle Formations SAU) was identiﬁed in the north and west Denver

 equivalent of the Dakota Group.

 4.2.1 Fault Zones & Seismicity
 Wyoming hosts many active and inactive fault zones (Figure 7) that could pose risks to
 subsurface CO2 storage, from seismicity and damage to infrastructure, to the presence of non-
 sealing faults promoting upward migration of CO2. The GGRB shows the most risk of leakage
 along fault zones in the RSU area (Figure 7).
 We evaluated studies on induced seismicity by the Wyoming State Geological Survey (WSGS)
 and used data from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) Earthquake Catalog to deﬁne
 seismicity risk in Wyoming. Studies show that no instances of induced seismicity associated
 with oil and gas development in the Powder River Basin have been documented.59,60
 The Earthquake Catalog shows that the largest earthquake in Wyoming since 1990 was a
 magnitude 5.8, taking place in the western part of the state, away from most of the proposed
 CO2 storage sites. Most earthquakes in Wyoming have been less than magnitude 4, and future
 earthquakes of similar magnitude are unlikely to cause major damage (Figure 7). The chances of
 a damaging earthquake occurring in the next 100 years in an area chosen for CO2 storage are
 less than 25% in the Greater Green River Basin, or 25-50% in the Wind River Basin, southern
 Bighorn Basin, and western Powder River Basin.

 4.2.2 Abnormal Pressure and Temperature Zones
 Anomalously pressured zones (over- or under-pressured) exist in the subsurface in at least the
 Hanna, Denver, and Powder River Basins. In the Hanna Basin, prior studies have shown that
 basin-centered gas accumulations with over- and under-pressured compartments occur at
 depths greater than 10,000 feet – depths not usually considered for CO2 storage.61 In the Denver
 and Powder River Basin, studies show abnormally pressured areas exist in the Frontier, Muddy,
 and Dakota Formations. Our review suggests that it is important to be aware of abnormally
 pressured areas as they can pose risks to the safe storage of CO2. If a reservoir is already over-
 pressured, injecting CO2 could cause the pore pressure to exceed the breakthrough pressure, a
 seepage channel to be developed through the caprock, and capillary leakage of CO2. Localized
 areas of high pressure in a reservoir can also cause tensile failure of caprocks and compromise
 the integrity of the storage site.
 In contrast, we found that areas with abnormal temperature and geothermal gradients only exist
 close to Yellowstone National Park and are unlikely to be a major risk for safe CO2 storage in
 Wyoming.

 industrial uses and must be avoided for injection. In contrast, deeper Cretaceous and Paleozoic
 formations typically have moderately saline to briny waters, often with limited or no beneﬁcial
 use due to higher TDS or exceedances in constituents like radionuclides, sulfate, metals, and

 CO2 storage, as they combine adequate thickness and reservoir properties with minimal risk to
 USDWs.

 4.4 Summary of CO2 storage in Wyoming
 Overall, the GGRB has the greatest potential for deep saline CO2 storage due to the presence of
 large anticlinal traps within the basin, such as the Rock Springs Uplift and Moxa Arch, which
 have been the focus of most prior studies. The bowl-shaped geometry of many Laramide basins
 may promote up-dip migration of CO2, underscoring the importance of structural traps for
 containment. Existing Class VI injection wells - located along a synclinal axis in the western
 GGRB and in the Denver Basin -together with ongoing CarbonSAFE projects (Figure 9), highlight
 both the regulatory framework and technical feasibility for commercial-scale storage.
 From a hydrogeologic perspective, some of the deepest formations most favorable for large-
 volume storage, including the Tensleep and Madison Formations, often contain water with TDS
 <10,000 mg/L, raising potential conﬂicts with USDW protection. Lynds40 summarizes that the
 primary obstacles to CO2 storage in deep saline aquifers are aquifer salinity, fractured and
 faulted reservoirs, extreme formation depths, and inadequate porosity and permeability.
 SCO2TPRO results suggest the Wind River Basin may have the highest theoretical storage
 potential, followed by the Greater Green River Basin (Figure 9), though these estimates do not
 account for basin geometry. Furthermore, much of northeastern Wyoming falls within protected
 lands or critical habitats (Figure 10).

 5 CO2 Utilization
 Utilization for CCUS includes a variety of established and new technologies. Enhanced oil
 recovery (EOR) has long been a cornerstone of carbon utilization in Wyoming, where CO₂
 injection into depleted oil ﬁelds has proven highly effective at revitalizing mature reservoirs and
 unlocking signiﬁcant volumes of stranded oil. However, the state’s CCUS landscape is rapidly
 expanding beyond EOR into a broader suite of utilization opportunities that convert captured
 CO₂ into fuels, materials, chemicals, and high-value consumables. These emerging utilization
 technologies, including synthetic aviation fuel production enabled by low-carbon hydrogen,
 mineralized construction materials such as cement and aggregates, CO₂-derived chemicals like
 methanol and polymers, and even food- and beverage-grade CO₂ for algae cultivation or
 carbonation, allow Wyoming to diversify its economy while integrating its long-standing
 expertise in energy and carbon management. With supportive policies, an existing industrial and
 pipeline network, and substantial natural resource advantages, Wyoming is positioned not only
 to scale CO₂-EOR in the near term, while supporting an integrated carbon utilization economy
 that includes energy, manufacturing, agriculture, and advanced materials.

 5.1 Enhanced Oil Recovery
 EOR is a process by which ﬂuids are injected into a depleted oil ﬁeld to re-pressurize the
 reservoir and stimulate additional oil production. EOR is crucial for increasing oil production
 from existing depleted ﬁelds, with CO2 injection being the most promising method statewide,
 outperforming other EOR efforts in Wyoming. Further, Wyoming's oil ﬁelds are technically
 suitable for CO2 miscible ﬂooding. Wyoming began using EOR in 1986, and currently, nine
 commercial-scale ﬂoods that use CO2 are active in the state. According to the Enhanced Oil
 Recovery Institute in Wyoming, the injection volumes of CO2 into each of the 13 ﬁelds and
 reservoirs are shown in Figure 11.47 Nine (seven different ﬁelds) are large-scale ﬂoods that are
 now underway, and the remaining four were preliminary investigations. These are Wyoming’s top
 ﬁelds and are prime candidates for utilizing the captured CO2 emissions potential for future oil
 recovery. The Almond Sand at Patrick Draw is a particularly well-characterized reservoir that
 achieved 38% oil recovery through waterﬂooding. With CO2-EOR, recovery increased to 60% of
 the original oil in place (OOIP), indicating potential for similar or better results in Wyoming if
 future CO2-EOR projects are designed properly. More importantly, there remain high-potential
 ﬁelds, such as the Big Horn Basin, that the current infrastructure does not reach. CCUS buildout
 in Wyoming could tap into these untouched ﬁelds by providing captured CO2 emissions for CO2-
 EOR.47

 5.1.2 Barriers and Uncertainties
 Although Wyoming offers favorable conditions for the large-scale deployment of CO₂-enhanced
 oil recovery, no new project has been initiated in the past decade, which indicates that
 signiﬁcant challenges remain to be overcome. The uneven distribution of infrastructure
 compatibilities, particularly in the Bighorn Basin, impedes the full realization of its CO2-EOR
 potential. Although WCPI addressed some of the challenges connecting main CO₂ lines, it is still
 up to the individual operator’s ability to develop ﬁeld-level spur lines.
 CO₂-EOR projects require substantial upfront capital and exhibit extended payback periods;
 their economic viability and risk mitigation are generally contingent upon asset scale, reservoir
 continuity, and integration with established CO₂ transport infrastructure. Wyoming’s
 predominantly small, privately held operators compete with shorter-term, lower-risk drilling for
 limited capital. CO₂-EOR projects for small and intermediate-scale operators are poised to face
 ﬁnancial and legal challenges. For example, operators may seek alternative stimulation
 methods such as a horizontal well with multi-stage hydraulic fracturing, rather than CO2-EOR.
 CO2 injected into isolated assets may bring protests from neighboring parties.
 The economic viability of CO₂-EOR projects is highly sensitive to ﬂuctuations in global oil prices.
 Sustained pricing in the range of $50 to $60 per barrel is generally required for project
 proﬁtability. While tax incentives help offset operational costs, they are insufficient to fully
 mitigate revenue risks associated with declining oil prices.
 Despite Wyoming’s leadership in regulatory streamlining, certain permitting issues and
 additional regulatory requirements remain, creating uncertainties in project timelines. For
 instance, obtaining aquifer exemptions through the EPA is required even with state Class II
 primacy. On federally managed lands, National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) reviews and
 right-of-way approvals can be delayed by legal challenges or stakeholder opposition.

 5.2 Near-Term and Future Carbon Utilization
 Opportunities
 Effective utilization of CO2 can transform a waste product into valuable commodities, ranging
 from fuels to materials, chemicals, and even products for human consumption. These products
 can provide durable carbon storage or meet the demand for a circular carbon economy, where
 materials and energy are reused and recycled to prevent net emissions to the atmosphere.
 Wyoming, with its abundant natural resources and industrial infrastructure, stands to beneﬁt
 signiﬁcantly from advances in CO2 utilization technologies.

       5.2.1 Fuels

 fuels and provide a means of storing energy in a dense and transportable form. The
 thermochemical conversion of CO2 into carbon monoxide before reacting with hydrogen in the
 Fischer-Tropsch synthesis process has demonstrated higher technology readiness compared to
 the direct conversion of CO2, which is currently not viable at scale.68 When CO2 is hydrogenated

 5.2.2 Materials
 CO2 can be permanently sequestered by converting it into carbon-derived materials such as
 cementitious material and aggregates, reducing the life cycle emissions of construction
 materials 69 Mineralization locks carbon away in a stable solid form that will not re-enter the
 atmosphere. Incumbent concrete manufacturers typically do not use CO2 as a feedstock, and
 often emit large volumes of CO2 from process emissions and fuel combustion to meet high
 thermal demands. Generally, these materials are produced at a signiﬁcant scale with low-proﬁt
 margins but could beneﬁt from improved material properties and the Inﬂation Reduction Act’s
 transferable tax incentive of $60/tonne of qualiﬁed CO2 use.70 The production of inorganic
 construction materials is relatively well-developed, and in some cases has resulted in cost
 savings.71
 Elemental carbon materials such as carbon ﬁbers, graphite, and carbon-carbon composites can
 also be synthesized from captured CO2. These materials are highly durable and lightweight,
 making them valuable in the automotive and aerospace industries. Elemental carbon products
 have further applications in electronics, energy technology, pollutant removal, and more.71

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                 35

 5.2.3 Chemicals
 Captured CO2 can also be used as a feedstock for producing various chemicals, such as urea,
 methanol, and polymers.72 These conversions can be accomplished through either chemical or
 biological pathways. Chemical conversion of CO2 into organic products often requires
 signiﬁcant energy input to overcome reaction barriers, storing a portion of that energy in the
 organic bonds of the product. In addition to yielding a variety of important chemical precursors
 and solvents, the process can be used for important agrochemicals such as fertilizers, which
 can often be produced economically on a small scale.71 Other possible end-use applications of
 chemical conversion products include plastics and polymers, fabrics, insulation, cleaning
 products, paints and varnishes, and more.71 Wyoming’s established chemical industry,
 supported by its natural gas resources, could be signiﬁcantly expanded to include CO2-based
 chemical production while helping diversify the state’s economy by creating value-added
 products.

 5.2.4 Consumables
 CO2 utilization can also be leveraged to produce high-value consumables such as food, animal
 feed, and pharmaceuticals. For example, algal species and cyanobacteria such as spirulina are
 widely acknowledged as safe and healthy dietary supplements or whole foods due to their
 protein content.73 Beyond human consumption, algae cultivated using captured CO2 can also be
 processed into nutrient-rich animal feed, which has particular relevance for regions with strong
 agricultural industries such as Wyoming, where local livestock could beneﬁt from high-nutrient,
 algae-based feed options. Additionally, hybrid systems capable of producing yeast, mushroom-
 producing fungi, and algae have been able to increase the overall efficiency of solar energy to
 food conversion.74
 CO2 has also been extensively used for beverage carbonization, where it not only reduces
 reliance on extracted CO2 but also contributes to a circular carbon economy by repurposing
 existing emissions from industrial processes.75

 NRAP-Open-IAM (National Risk Assessment Partnership-Open Integrated Assessment Model)
 provides a computational framework for CO2 and brine leakage risk at a storage site by
 integrating stochastic reservoir realizations and wellbore data to generate site-speciﬁc leakage
 metrics.84 To produce reservoir simulations required by NRAP, a geological domain is
 constructed from layer surface elevations using LaGrit (Los Alamos Grid Toolbox), a toolkit for
 generating unstructured computational meshes used in subsurface ﬂow and transport
 modeling. Within the domain, storage reservoirs are heterogeneous, with porosity and
 permeability assigned as spatially-correlated ﬁelds derived from well-log data, while conﬁning
 layers are treated as low-porosity low-permeability barriers to ensure CO2 containment. An
 ensemble of 100 reservoir realizations is generated to capture variability in the geologic
 parameters and support uncertainty quantiﬁcation.
 FEHM (Finite Element Heat and Mass transfer code), a coupled ﬂow and transport simulator,
 models the injection and post-injection behavior of supercritical CO2 and brine for each of the
 reservoir realizations in the ensemble, producing the reservoir responses that serve as input for
 the NRAP simulations. Leakage is evaluated at hypothetical and existing well locations using the
 time-dependent pressure and saturation values at those points, providing site-speciﬁc
 predictions of CO2 and brine leakage risk. The total reservoir impact due to injection at a storage
 site is characterized through the Area of Review, deﬁned as the region where either CO2
 saturation is present or reservoir pressures exceed the critical threshold required to drive brine
 leakage. A high-level overview of the data required for LaGrit, FEHM, and NRAP is in Figure 16.
 Select areas of three Wyoming basins are modelled: the Wind River Basin (WRB), the Powder
 River Basin (PRB), and the Greater Green River Basin (GGRB). Each basin was selected to model
 a speciﬁc concern for each basin—in the GGRB, leakage was modeled through faults, through
 PRB leakage was modeled via uncemented wells to active oil and gas zones and USDW, and in

 injection. The ﬁnal state of the domain after a 10,000-day hydrostatic simulation, shown in

 6.4 NRAP Model
 Outputs from the FEHM reservoir simulations are integrated into NRAP Open-IAM through a
 lookup table approach, where reservoir pressures and CO2 saturations at selected nodes are
 interpolated over time to provide input for leakage wellbore components.84 Simulation results for
 Meeteetse and Mesaverde are modelled independently in NRAP, with no shared or combined
 simulations between the two reservoirs. Each reservoir includes 100 realizations, and every
 realization drives a separate NRAP simulation. Potential leaking well locations within the
 simulations are restricted to nodes at the top of the reservoir and within a 5km radius of the
 injection well. For both Meeteetse and Mesaverde units, 3,788 nodes across the three injection
 sites are used in each NRAP simulation. Every hypothetical well is treated as an Open Wellbore
 component,87 a reduced order model that simulates the leakage of CO2 or brine from the
 reservoir up an uncemented well to the overlying aquifer, the Wind River unit. The time-
 dependent pressures and saturations at the nodes drive the inputs for the Open Wellbore
 components.
 Within the NRAP framework, critical pressure calculations are used to assess whether reservoir
 pressures are sufficient to drive brine up an open conduit and into the overlying aquifer/USDW.

 6.7 High-Level Conclusions
 Three basin-wide analyses were performed to evaluate geologic CO2 storage performance
 and risk across different regional settings in Wyoming. Simulations and geographic models
 incorporated site-speciﬁc stratigraphy, geologic parameters such as reservoir porosity and
 permeability, and existing leakage pathways to quantify outcomes such as pressure response
 and leakage rates to overlying aquifers. Of the three basin-wide analyses performed using NRAP,
 the Powder River Basin exhibited the highest overall risk of leakage, due to the large presence of
 oil and gas wells within the region. Although the basins differ geologically, the Areas of Review
 remain largely consistent across case studies since the injection parameters used are the same,
 and porosity/permeability distributions built into the reservoir components are broadly similar.

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                        63

 Key limitations within the NRAP framework include the scarcity of well log and reservoir
 property data for Wyoming basins. The constructed geologic models rely on values typically
 found for sandstone reservoirs. Additionally, layer surface elevations were converted into a
 computational model, and geometric detail was reduced during this process to maintain
 computational feasibility. Parameter bounds within the NRAP components restrict the analysis
 to areas that are consistent with the model requirements. As a result, careful site
 characterization and scenario selection are necessary to ensure that NRAP can provide accurate
 leakage metrics for a given domain. The NRAP framework is best utilized following detailed
 reservoir characterization, with an ensemble of reservoir simulations to quantify any uncertainty
 within the geologic parameters and support risk assessment.

 7 Non-technical
 Challenges
 Wyoming is widely regarded as one of the most CCS-ready jurisdictions in the United States,
 owing to a mature statutory framework that addresses pore space ownership, long-term liability,
 and state primacy for Class VI wells administered by the Wyoming Department of Environmental
 Quality (WDEQ). These elements create a comparatively streamlined environment for permitting
 and regulatory oversight, as evidenced by the number of existing Class VI well projects that have
 been ﬁled with the state. Nonetheless, non-technical barriers, such as regulatory, legal, supply
 chain, social, and other commercial considerations, will impact project timelines, costs, and
 community acceptance across the state.

 7.1 Regulatory & Policy Environment
 While any one aspect of CCUS alone requires many different regulatory and legal considerations
 and timelines, the buildout of an integrated capture, transport, and storage system will require
 navigating many different federal, state, and local agencies and policies. Below is a list of some
 of the different agencies, along with their approval timelines, that are important for CCUS
 projects.

 7.1.1 National Regulations and Policies
 A range of federal regulations outlines the requirements and responsibilities necessary to ensure
 compliance and mitigate potential risks associated with industrial activities. These regulations
 are designed to protect natural resources, promote sustainable practices, and safeguard the
 well-being of local communities. Understanding these regulations is crucial for developers in
 project planning.
 National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)
 The NEPA requires federal agencies to evaluate the environmental impacts of their proposed
 actions before making decisions. This assessment is crucial for ensuring that environmental
 considerations are integrated into the planning and decision-making processes of federal
 projects. NEPA mandates the preparation of an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) for major
 federal actions that signiﬁcantly affect communities and the environment.89 An EIS analyzes the

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                 65

 potential environmental impacts, explores alternatives to the proposed action, and outlines
 mitigation strategies. Many of these elements align with those in the Wyoming Industrial Siting
 Council (WY-ISC) application.
 Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA)
 The RCRA governs the management of hazardous and non-hazardous solid waste.90 This
 regulation establishes a framework for the proper handling, treatment, and disposal of waste
 generated during industrial operations. Developers must be aware of RCRA's requirements to
 ensure the safe management of waste materials, including the generation, transportation,
 storage, and disposal of hazardous waste. Compliance with RCRA not only protects human
 health and the environment but also minimizes legal liabilities associated with improper waste
 management. This includes maintaining accurate records of waste generation and disposal
 practices, and implementing appropriate waste minimization strategies.
 Endangered Species Act (ESA)
 The ESA is designed to protect threatened and endangered species and their habitats.
 Developers must assess whether their projects could impact any listed species or their habitats.
 This evaluation involves consultation with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service or the National
 Marine Fisheries Service to determine if any species may be adversely affected by project
 activities.91 If potential impacts are identiﬁed, developers may not build their facility on federal
 land, but pipelines may be. Compliance with the ESA is crucial for avoiding legal challenges and
 ensuring that projects align with conservation goals.
 Federal Land Policy and Management Act (FLPMA)
 The FLPMA governs the management of public lands and provides guidelines for land use
 planning and resource management. Developers may not build their facility on federal land, but
 it is very likely that pipelines for CO₂ transportation may involve federal land. If so, compliance
 with FLPMA is essential. This includes obtaining the necessary permits and adhering to federal
 land management policies, which may require environmental assessments and public input.92
 Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) Regulations
 OSHA sets and enforces standards to ensure safe working conditions for employees.
 Developers must ensure compliance with OSHA regulations applicable to carbon capture
 facilities, including requirements for worker safety, health training, and hazard communication.93
 Compliance with OSHA standards is essential for protecting worker health and safety and
 minimizing the risk of accidents or injuries on the job site. Developers should implement
 comprehensive safety programs and training to ensure that employees are aware of workplace
 hazards and proper safety protocols.
 Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Regulations
 FERC regulates the interstate transmission of electricity, natural gas, and oil. If developers’
 projects involve partnerships with renewable energy developers for energy generation or
 transmission components, engagement with FERC may be necessary.94 This includes obtaining
 the appropriate permits and ensuring compliance with FERC regulations governing energy
 markets, pricing, and reliability. Understanding FERC's regulatory framework is important for
 ensuring that developers' energy-related activities align with federal requirements and market
 dynamics.

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                   66

 7.1.2 State Regulations and Policies
 Wyoming Department of Environmental Quality
 Wyoming holds primacy over the issuance of Class VI permits in the state.95 Given that the
 approval process for a Class VI permit can take between six and 12 months, it is important for
 developers to consider the broader implications of this timeline, particularly for the
 transportation of CO₂ from capture sites to designated storage locations. An excellent overview
 of timelines and considerations for Class VI well applications is available on the Wyoming DEQ
 site, and the University of Wyoming School of Energy Resources Publication “Wyoming’s Class
 VI Regulations: Managing the Risk of CO2 Storage.”96,97
 To be granted Class VI primacy, a state must meet or exceed the requirements set forth by the
 Environmental Protection Agency for Class VI wells. Obtaining a Class VI well permit in Wyoming
 requires navigating a structured review process managed by the Wyoming Department of
 Environmental Quality (DEQ), which has primacy for Class VI wells under the Safe Drinking
 Water Act. Applicants begin with informational and pre-application meetings, followed by
 submission of a two-part permit application that includes detailed geological, technical,
 operational, and administrative information, as outlined in Wyoming’s Water Quality Rules,
 Chapter 24.98 The DEQ then conducts a completeness review and an in-depth technical
 evaluation to ensure the project will protect underground sources of drinking water, followed by
 public notice and comment periods before a Permit to Construct is issued. Final authorization to
 inject occurs only after well construction, testing, and, often, the unitization of pore space.
 Wyoming’s ﬁrst Class VI permits took roughly 10 months to review, demonstrating the state’s
 streamlined but rigorous process.
 Industrial Siting Council
 The Wyoming Industrial Siting Council (ISC) oversees permitting for new industrial facilities, a
 role that is especially relevant for projects installing capture technologies or for infrastructure
 that may be co-located with storage operations, such as direct air capture (DAC) systems, wind
 or solar installations, or natural gas plants serving data centers. Sections 8 through 10 of the
 Wyoming Industrial Development Information and Siting Act (1975) outline detailed
 requirements for project developers, emphasizing impact analysis, responsible
 decommissioning, and water management, and are designed to safeguard long-term
 environmental quality and community interests.
 The regulatory framework for assessing the socioeconomic and environmental impacts of
 industrial projects is primarily governed by W.S. 35-12-109, Section 8, which outlines the
 permitting responsibilities of the ISC. This seven-member council, appointed by the governor
 with Senate approval and with no more than 75% from a single political party, is responsible for
 issuing permits for industrial facilities over 283 million dollars, with some language speciﬁcally
 for nuclear, solar, wind, and commercial and hazardous waste, even below this total project
 cost. Members serve staggered six-year terms to ensure continuity and meet at least four times
 a year to conduct business.
 To grant a permit, the ISC must determine that the proposed facility will not seriously threaten
 the environment, has considered the socioeconomic impacts of the project, and has adequately
 planned for the health, safety, and welfare of communities. Applicants must identify the area of
 interest (AOI), e.g., the geographic region likely to experience signiﬁcant environmental,
 economic, or social impacts. Construction schedules, milestones, and timelines must be

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                  67

 provided, along with detailed workforce estimates, job types, employment trends, expected
 immigration, wage levels, and beneﬁts. Applicants must also analyze the project’s consistency
 with regional land use plans and assess how it will alter economic trends, employment,
 population demographics, and public services. A ﬁscal analysis must estimate project-related
 costs and revenues for local governments and include potential ﬁnancial assistance payments.
 Additionally, the ISC requires a cumulative assessment to evaluate infrastructure demands and
 public service needs over time, accounting for both short- and long-term effects.
 The environmental impact evaluation must describe expected emissions, discharges, and other
 adverse effects on air, water, wildlife, and plant life, and propose mitigation measures. It should
 address physical, chemical, biological, and radiological discharges and include strategies to
 prevent public nuisances and protect health and safety. The application must also describe
 potential land use changes, assess impacts on local wildlife and sensitive species, and detail
 how local labor will be used in the project’s development. Moreover, evidence of community
 engagement is required, such as formal notiﬁcations to local governments and opportunities for
 feedback before submission. The applicant must quantify both mitigated and unmitigated
 impacts on local governments, with ﬁscal projections provided on a calendar-month basis.
 Engineering and research ﬁrms in Wyoming offer expertise in these assessments and can
 provide essential technical support to advancing projects.

 7.1.4 Open access pipelines
 Given the numerous basins in Wyoming capable of storing CO2, most business case models
 developed for this Roadmap did not require long pipelines for permanent CO2storage. For other
 business opportunities, such as EOR or other utilization industries, creating long pipelines could
 be critical for regional economic development. While thousands of miles of CO2 pipelines have
 already been constructed, most are for EOR. The scale at which these pipelines need to be
 developed to take advantage of 45Q tax credits and meet some industry-speciﬁc emission
 reduction goals could require between 30,000 and 96,000 additional miles of pipeline.99
 Minimizing barriers to construction will be a signiﬁcant factor in the future of the CCUS industry.
 One legal question is how and whether CO2 pipelines should be regulated in the same way as
 other common infrastructure, such as railroads, natural pipelines, or roads. A recent paper by
 Lewis and Righetti examines this in great detail, including the history of common carrier status
 across a range of infrastructure.100 This paper highlights the responsibilities and beneﬁts that
 come with common carrier status. Notably, in exchange for allowing non-discriminatory access
 to the linear infrastructure, other beneﬁts, such as eminent domain and public funds, are often
 conveyed. Their conclusion for CO2 pipelines is that, at present, existing legal and regulatory
 requirements will not support the buildout of integrated common carrier pipelines for carbon
 removal at scale. This is especially important when considering interstate pipeline development.
 Wyoming provides no state-level economic regulation or common carrier requirements for CO₂
 pipelines, and the Wyoming Public Service Commission (PSC) has not asserted jurisdiction over
 them because CO₂ is not treated as a regulated gas.100 CO₂ pipelines may exercise eminent
 domain under Wyo. Stat. § 1-26-814 even without being common carriers, although takings must
 meet the constitutional requirement of “public use” or qualify as a “way of necessity.”100,101 This
 means CO₂ pipelines can generally operate as closed, private systems. Common carrier
 obligations apply only when federal law is triggered, such as when CO₂ pipelines cross federal
 land under the Mineral Leasing Act (MLA) with naturally occurring CO₂, in which case federal law
 mandates open-access common carriage.100 Wyoming is therefore one of the least restrictive
 states for CO₂ pipeline developers, though legal challenges to eminent domain may pressure
 operators to provide broader access.

 7.2 Community Support & Social License to Operate in
 Wyoming
 2020 Social License to Operate Study
 The 2020 study investigates Wyoming residents’ values, beliefs, and preferences regarding the
 state’s energy future, motivated by rapid market-driven shifts affecting coal, oil, and gas.102 Using
 a statewide survey (522 responses) and a Q-study of 24 energy-sector stakeholders, the authors
 examined the “social license to operate, ” which is the level of public acceptance granted to
 different energy activities. Residents broadly supported all major energy types, particularly
       natural gas (83%), oil (71%), solar (69%), wind (66%), and coal (63%). However, support
 diminished or became uncertain for emerging technologies like nuclear energy, CCS/CCUS, rare
 earth mining, and energy storage, largely due to limited public familiarity.
 In this 2020 report, CCS/CCUS was one of the least understood energy technologies among
 Wyoming residents. Over 55% of residents described themselves as “neutral/not sure” when
 asked whether they supported CCS/CCUS, and 32.2% explicitly said they were not sure about

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                    70

 these technologies. Residents reported wanting more information before forming an opinion.
 This uncertainty positioned CCS as a poorly recognized technology outside specialized industry
 circles.
 Wyoming residents expressed a strong desire to simultaneously increase both fossil fuel
 production (58% wanting increases) and renewable development (70% wanting increases),
 rejecting a binary choice between the two. Values deeply tied to Wyoming’s identity, such as
 scenic beauty, wildlife, recreation, and community, were the highest-ranked considerations
 when evaluating energy options. Concerns about climate change were widespread; Q-study
 participants overwhelmingly rejected the notion that climate change is a hoax, and most survey
 respondents saw climate change as real, even if they differed on its causes.
 The Q-study identiﬁed three themes:
 • Renewable Emphasis, where climate concerns dominate, CCS skepticism is common,
 and a “just transition” for workers is essential.
 • Economic Emphasis, emphasizing diversiﬁcation, openness to CCS and nuclear, and
 using conventional fuels as a bridge while responding to external market demands.
 • Quality of Life Emphasis, prioritizing jobs, affordable energy, and community stability,
 with a strong interest in nuclear energy and pragmatic acceptance of CCS.
 Across themes, participants shared frustration with the lack of proactive state-level planning.
 The report concludes that Wyoming residents grant broad social license to most energy types,
 but conditional license to CCS and nuclear depending on better information. A consistent desire
 emerges: a comprehensive statewide energy strategy that protects wildlife and landscapes,
 maintains community well-being, strengthens economic resilience, and prepares Wyoming for
 inevitable market-driven changes reshaping the state’s energy portfolio.
 2023 Social License to Operate: Replication Study
 The 2023 replication study revisits Wyoming energy attitudes using a refreshed 2022 survey (357
 responses) and a new Q-study of 22 stakeholders. It documents both continuity and evolution in
 public opinion since 2019–2020. Support for fossil energy remains strong, with 76% favor
 natural-gas-based electricity, 75% favor oil-based electricity, and 69% support coal mining.
 Rooftop solar (70%) and nuclear (57%) also retain substantial favor. Meanwhile, support for wind
 has declined (from 65.5% in 2019 to 51% in 2022), and many emerging industries, such as
 CCUS, hydrogen, and rare earth elements, show high uncertainty (38–45% “neutral/not sure”),
 demonstrating a major information gap.
 By 2023, with 43% remaining “neutral/not sure” about CCS, the knowledge base had expanded.
 Residents were increasingly aware of speciﬁc CCS applications (e.g., point source capture,
 direct air capture, permanent geologic storage, CO₂ EOR). This expansion was driven by real
 projects: Wyoming’s Class VI primacy for storage permitting, EOR expansion, and federal
 investments in low-carbon energy industries. As a result, public familiarity improved, even if not
 uniformly.
 CCS/CCUS views have grown more detailed and nuanced. Residents tend to support
 point-source carbon capture, materials production using CO₂, and CO₂-EOR, while showing
 ambivalence toward direct-air capture and permanent geologic storage, especially storage of
 CO₂ imported from other states. Over 40% believe geological storage can enable reliable,
 low-carbon energy from fossil fuels, but concerns remain about safety, continued fossil reliance,

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                 71

 and economic feasibility. Nuclear energy attitudes also evolved: 67% say nuclear power
 provides reliable low-carbon energy, though 61% remain concerned about waste disposal.
 The replication’s Q-study identiﬁes three updated themes:
 • Climate Change as Existential Threat: a polarized theme reﬂecting both urgent
 pro-climate-action views (Theme 1A) and skepticism about climate urgency or human
 causation (Theme 1B).
 • All-of-the-Above for Resilience: supporting any combination of energy types, including
 CCS and nuclear, as long as they protect communities and align with economic realities.
 • Economics and Communities First: emphasizing job preservation, fossil industry
 stability, and practical development of nuclear and CCS to sustain coal-dependent
 regions like Kemmerer.
 Across themes, residents consistently prioritize communities, economic stability, responsible
 siting, and wildlife protection.
 A major new thread compared to 2020 is the growing urgency around community impacts.
 Respondents emphasize anxiety about job losses, uneven transition beneﬁts, and uncertainty in
 energy markets. The report highlights increasing support for nuclear, CCS, and critical minerals
 as Wyoming gains experience with Class VI primacy, TerraPower’s small modular reactor
 project, and rare earth development. The authors conclude that Wyoming’s social license is
 expanding for emerging energy types, but only when economic beneﬁts, transparency, and
 alignment with community values are guaranteed.
 Study Comparison
 Taken together, the 2020 and 2023 studies show a Wyoming public that remains broadly
 supportive of a diversiﬁed, inclusive energy mix, with growing sophistication in how residents
 evaluate emerging technologies, especially CCS, nuclear, and critical minerals. In 2020,
 CCS/CCUS and nuclear energy were marked primarily by uncertainty; residents needed more
 information and tended to view CCS as either beneﬁcial to fossil competitiveness or as an
 unclear technology that might perpetuate fossil dependence. By 2023, as Wyoming
 implemented Class VI primacy and new projects such as TerraPower’s reactor and the Bear
 Lodge REE pilot advanced, public support for nuclear, CCS, and REE mining increased
 measurably. Yet this greater support is paired with more detailed concerns about safety,
 economics, and community impacts. This suggests a maturing understanding rather than
 unquestioning acceptance.
 Both studies ﬁnd that community well-being, wildlife, landscapes, and economic resilience are
 central to social license in Wyoming. However, the 2023 study emphasizes community-level
 vulnerability far more than the 2020 study. Whereas the 2020 themes focused on values, climate
 concerns, and economic diversiﬁcation, the 2023 themes place communities at the core of
 every energy decision, which reﬂects heightened anxiety about job losses, rapid transition
 timelines, and uneven distribution of beneﬁts. Support for CCS and nuclear increasingly
 depends not only on climate or economic narratives, but on whether they directly safeguard
 local communities, provide long-term jobs, and reuse existing energy infrastructure. In sum,
 Wyoming’s social license for energy is broad, but increasingly conditional: emerging
 technologies gain acceptance only when they demonstrably protect communities, reﬂect
 Wyoming values, and offer credible economic pathways during turbulent energy transitions.

 7.3 Wyoming CCUS Workforce
 While states with a strong history of oil and gas investment, like Wyoming, offer deep talent in
 pipeline construction, compression, and subsurface operations, the national rollout requires
 specialized competencies: CO₂ pipeline engineering with fracture control, geologic modeling for
 saline storage, advanced compressor design and maintenance for dense phase service, and
 continuous MRV (monitoring, reporting, and veriﬁcation) expertise. These skills are
 simultaneously in demand across LNG, hydrogen, and petrochemical expansions, creating tight
 labor markets, higher mobilization costs, and longer onboarding for training and qualiﬁcation.
 Wyoming’s existing energy workforce provides a strong foundation for carbon capture and
 storage (CCS) deployment, although in recent years, there have been fewer jobs. The oil and gas
 sector directly employs on the order of 8,400 jobs in November 2025, 600 fewer than the
 previous year. This decrease is reﬂected in the number of oil and gas rigs operating in December
 of 2025, which at 9 oil and 3 gas rigs, are fewer than the 12 oil and 4 gas rigs in December of
 2024.103 The job losses of the last year mirror losses in the recent past, where reports suggest a
 decrease of 10,000 mining, oil and gas jobs from 2014 to 2024.104 Federal labor statistics
 indicate roughly 14,700 workers in mining, quarrying, and oil and gas extraction as of December
 2025, an employment pool with directly transferable skills in drilling, compressor operations,
 and subsurface management relevant to CCS.105

 At the state level, the labor-force participation is very high. The seasonally adjusted
 unemployment rate stood at 3.2% in August 2025 and 3.4% in December 2025, below the
 seasonally-adjusted unemployment rate of 4.4% for the whole of the United States.106107 Given
 low statewide unemployment ﬁgures despite decreasing job opportunities in the mining, oil, and

 employees from other states.
 For capture retroﬁts and new builds, labor demand will include construction trade workers,

 operations staff, sales representatives, commercial freight workers, and more. A U.S.

 7.4 National Supply Chain Constraints
 Carbon capture and storage (CCS) relies on an interconnected chain of activities that must all
 function reliably and on synchronized timelines. While the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has
 characterized the CCS supply chain as posing no signiﬁcant supply chain risks, experience from
 recent project cycles indicates that practical, near-term constraints still create signiﬁcant
 bottlenecks.113 In other words, the larger view of material availability can obscure regional
 scarcities, specialized engineering requirements, and state-and-local permitting dependencies
 that collectively slow delivery and raise costs. Because Wyoming is part of a national ecosystem
 for both materials (such as steel for pipelines or solvents for capturing CO2) and a skilled
 workforce, understanding national supply chains for the rapidly scaling industry is more relevant
 than looking only for Wyoming materials to meet project needs.
 Transport availability and the pipeline gap
 A central constraint for transportation is the current buildout of CO₂ pipelines. The United States
 operates roughly 5,300 miles of CO₂ pipeline today, where much of it is oriented toward
 enhanced oil recovery (EOR) in the Rockies and along the Gulf Coast. This network is much
 smaller than what would be required to connect diverse industrial point sources to suitable
 storage formations at the national, or even the state, scale. As a result, many developers have
 yet to move captured CO₂ at commercial volumes on reliable schedules.
 Recent events highlight uncertainty with the new buildout of CO2 pipelines. Multiple greenﬁeld
 projects intended to build large, multi-state CO₂ trunklines have been canceled or delayed after
 encountering public opposition, siting denials, or difficulties securing land rights. Notably,
 Navigator CO₂ Ventures and Wolf Carbon Solutions withdrew their proposals in the Midwest,
 while Summit Carbon Solutions continues to pursue permits across several states but has
 struggled to secure all necessary approvals and property rights.114–116 These project outcomes
 reduce near term transport capacity and heighten risk for emitters that require ﬁrm takeaway to
 justify capture investments.
 Beyond availability, safety and regulatory dynamics add complexity to pipeline delivery. The
 2020 CO₂ pipeline rupture near Satartia, Mississippi, which resulted in dozens of
 hospitalizations, catalyzed calls for stricter rules and, in some states, a moratorium on
 permitting new CO₂ pipelines pending federal rule updates.117–119 Developers now face longer

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                  74

 siting reviews and expanded emergency response expectations, which add time and engineering
 overhead to project plans.
 Finally, CO₂ is not another hydrocarbon. Its thermophysical behavior and sensitivity to
 impurities, including risks associated with corrosion, hydrate formation, and ductile or brittle
 running fractures, can require specialized materials. These needs increase the technical
 requirements for design and construction, lengthening schedules where specialized steels,
 welding procedures, and testing regimes must be employed.120
 Materials and equipment bottlenecks
 Although DOE’s assessment emphasizes that CCS draws from broadly available inputs, real-
 world deployments encounter regional and sectoral bottlenecks. That is especially true when
 multiple decarbonization programs (e.g., hydrogen, LNG export expansions, reﬁnery upgrades)
 compete for the same mills, fabricators, and OEMs at the same time.
 Pipeline considerations can be important for CCS projects, especially for those projects where
 emissions are located far from the storage site. CCS-grade line pipe faces procurement
 constraints because only a limited number of mills can produce the steel required for
 supercritical CO₂ transport. Technical literature shows these steels require signiﬁcantly higher
 fracture toughness and impurity-tolerant properties than conventional oil and gas pipe,
 narrowing the qualiﬁed supply base. At the same time, global line-pipe demand is rising due to
 hydrogen and CCUS infrastructure growth, which increases competition for the same
 high-performance pipe manufacturing capacity.121 CO₂ compression may be a supply-chain
 pinch point for CCS projects. Large CO₂ compressors require customized metallurgy,
 corrosion-resistant components, and specialized seals to handle dense-phase CO₂, which are
 design challenges documented in technical studies of CCS compressor systems.122
 On the capture side, many retroﬁts depend on large diameter stainless vessels (e.g.,
 absorber/stripper columns), high surface area heat exchangers, and specialized gas–liquid
 contactors, and availability of amine-based solvents for CO2 capture. The limited number of
 commercially viable products for capturing CO2 is signiﬁcant; at the present time,
 monoethanolamide (MEA) solvents show the greatest opportunity for early deployment in CCS
 projects. A recent review of the supply chain for amine solvents in the United States and Canada
 suggests a sharp, policy-driven spike in amine demand between 2028 and 2030, driven primarily
 by new CCS facilities seeking to qualify under U.S. IRA 45Q and Canada’s CCUS-ITC tax
 incentive schemes.123 Similar ﬁndings by the Department of Energy suggest a ten-fold increase
 in monoethanolamide (MEA) between 2025 and 2030.113 The volumes needed to meet proposed
 projects may create a temporary but severe supply chain risk, especially for MEA and proprietary
 solvent blends.
 Storage and transport interface issues
 Even with capture and transport solved, geologic storage remains another project constraint.
 There is not yet enough certiﬁed, bankable underground storage capacity to meet projected
 decarbonization volumes on the timelines required. Bringing new storage online involves multi-
 year site characterization, permitting (e.g., Class VI wells), monitoring plans, and often federal
 and state land coordination. As a result, pipelines and capture plants risk idling if storage
 readiness lags, increasing ﬁnancing risks.
 Competition for commercial CO₂ uses also illustrates system-level competition and potential
 instability. In recent years, the beverage, agriculture, and refrigeration markets have suffered

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                 75

 severe CO₂ shortages.124,125 As tax incentives and policy support permanent sequestration of
 captured CO₂, existing merchant markets face tighter supply and logistics competition,
 stressing trucking, rail terminals, and puriﬁcation capacity that previously served industrial
 users.
 Scheduling dynamics
 Project schedules are increasingly driven by permitting and land acquisition rather than purely
 by engineering or construction durations. The Congressional Research Service has documented
 how public opposition, eminent domain debates, and state siting denials contributed to
 cancellations and deferrals of major CO₂ pipelines across the Midwest, suggesting that legal and
 social license processes are now the critical path in many regions.126,127
 Network considerations compound this effect. DOE’s recent routing analysis shows that even
 when optimizing to minimize state border crossings, assembling efficient CO₂ corridors from
 distributed emitters to storage hubs still requires large, multi-jurisdictional systems.128 Each new
 crossing introduces its own environmental review timeline, consultation obligations, and risk of
 route adjustments, which can ripple through procurement and workforce mobilization plans.
 Depending on the size of the project, crossing county and municipal boundaries can add
 additional time to the project approval timeline.
 Moreover, CCS execution depends on many different project schedules, including Class VI
 permits (often multiyear), storage site characterization and test wells, pipeline right-of-way
 acquisition, acquisition of capture infrastructure and related materials, and MRV validation.
 Delays in any element can cascade into others, making CCS schedules sensitive to a multitude
 of supply chain, regulatory, and ﬁnancial timelines.
 Industrial scale-up limits
 Outside of oil and gas processing, where capture is comparatively mature, the track record for
 CCS at an industrial scale remains unproven when CO2 is not used for EOR. In the steel sector,
 for example, the number of commercial-scale projects under construction is small, and several
 efforts have underperformed or been canceled in adjacent sectors, reinforcing investor caution
 about rapid scale-up before 2030–2040. These outcomes do not undermine the technology so
 much as they expose the execution and market risks embedded in ﬁrst-of-kind or early mover
 deployments.

 strengthened when developers integrate environmental data with early social-site

 8 Business Case
 8.1 Introduction
 The deployment of Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) at scale presents a range of economic
 and development opportunities, such as supporting enhanced oil recovery (EOR), meeting
 regulatory requirements related to decreasing CO2 emissions, and promoting energy security by
 supporting infrastructure that can later support low-carbon technology such as hydrogen or CO2
 utilization. This report summarizes proposed business models, evaluates socioeconomic
 impacts of infrastructure deployment scenarios, and introduces a CCS Readiness Index for
 Wyoming. The analysis integrates techno-economic modeling, geospatial routing optimization,
 and workforce impact assessments to inform strategic decisions for CCS implementation. By
 examining phased buildout strategies, intermodal transportation options, and full-scale capture
 scenarios, the report provides actionable insights into cost drivers, infrastructure requirements,
 and regional readiness. These ﬁndings are meant to support a variety of interests, including
 project developers, policymakers, and investors, in aligning technical feasibility with regulatory
 frameworks, ﬁnancial incentives, and community priorities to accelerate CCS deployment.
 The CCS Readiness Index developed in this report provides a county-level assessment of
 Wyoming’s preparedness for CCS projects by integrating three critical dimensions: capture
 opportunities, transportation infrastructure, and storage potential. Each dimension is evaluated
 using weighted variables such as capturable emissions, routing cost surfaces, existing pipeline
 corridors, and geologic storage capacity. These indices are then combined into an overall
 readiness score, offering stakeholders a comparative view of where CCS investments are most
 viable. By highlighting areas with strong technical and logistical foundations, the Readiness
 Index serves as a strategic tool for guiding infrastructure planning, prioritizing policy support,
 and accelerating the development of carbon management hubs.

 8.2 CCS Business Cases
 Deploying Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) at scale depends on both the
 economics and reliability of moving CO₂ from emission sources to suitable geologic storage
 sites or utilization markets, the most developed of which is enhanced oil recovery (EOR). Routing
 optimization models combine operations research, geospatial analytics, and techno-economic
 modeling to create business case analyses by quantifying infrastructure layouts, costs, risks,
 and sequencing across multiple transport modes (pipelines, rail, trucking, marine) and/or hub
 and spoke conﬁgurations.

 This project utilized the SimCCSPRO toolchain to optimize regional network topology and

 • Strategic alignment with CCUS hubs: Evaluate hub-and-spoke pipelines and shared

 8.3.5 Job Estimates
 Employment impacts associated with CCS deployment were estimated using a combination of
 infrastructure-based and spend-based workforce methodologies, selected to align with the
 physical characteristics of each component of the CCS value chain and the level of public data
 available. Job estimates are intended to provide screening-level, order-of-magnitude indicators
 of workforce impacts rather than detailed staffing plans for individual facilities.

 Across all scenarios, employment is reported as average annual jobs, with construction
 employment representing temporary jobs during infrastructure build-out and operations
 employment representing ongoing, annual jobs during system operation.

 Methodology framework
 Workforce impacts were estimated separately for CO2 capture, transport, and storage, using
 approaches appropriate to each component:
 • Transport (pipelines) and storage (injection sites) were estimated using infrastructure-
 based employment multipliers, expressed as jobs per kilometer of pipeline or jobs per
 storage site, respectively. These multipliers are derived from publicly available CCS
 project ﬁlings and economic impact studies that report employment alongside physical
 infrastructure development.
 • Capture employment, which varies widely by facility type, retroﬁt design, and degree of
 integration with existing operations, was estimated using a spend-based economic
 multiplier approach rather than physical unit-based metrics.

 This hybrid framework ensures internal consistency with the optimization model outputs while
 avoiding false precision where standardized physical employment benchmarks do not exist.

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                              84

 Capture employment
 Capture employment was estimated using a capital and operating expenditure-based approach,
 applying Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) input-output employment multipliers to modeled
 capture costs. This approach follows standard federal economic impact methodologies and
 reﬂects how capture employment is treated in existing workforce analyses.
 •
 Construction (short-term) capture jobs are estimated by applying BEA jobs-per-million-
 dollar multipliers to capture capital expenditures (CAPEX) derived from SimCCSPRO and
 supporting cost models.
 •
 Operation (long-term) capture jobs are estimated by applying the same BEA framework
 to modeled expenditures (OPEX), representing persistent but modest employment
 during system operation.
 Capture employment is therefore reported at a screening level and interpreted as an aggregate
 workforce impact associated with deployment magnitude rather than as a facility-level staffing
 requirement.

 Transport Employment
 Pipeline transport employment is estimated using length-based multipliers expressed as jobs
 per kilometer of CO2pipeline. These multipliers capture both construction-phase employment
 during pipeline build-out and ongoing operational employment associated with pipeline
 operation and maintenance. Where available, total employment (direct, indirect, and induced) is
 reported to reﬂect broader economic activity supported by infrastructure deployment.

 Road-based transport employment is excluded from the ﬁrst-cut job estimates. Road segments
 in intermodal scenarios represent the use of existing transportation infrastructure rather than
 new infrastructure construction, and publicly available employment benchmarks do not scale
 cleanly with route length. This treatment maintains consistency with the physical outputs of the
 optimization model and avoids introducing poorly constrained employment estimates.

 Storage employment
 Storage employment is estimated on a per-site basis, reﬂecting the episodic and highly
 specialized nature of injection-site construction and operation. Employment multipliers are
 derived from published CCS hub studies that report construction and operational employment
 alongside the number of storage sites developed. To avoid overstating impacts, storage
 employment is treated conservatively and reported as average annual jobs during construction
 and operation phases.

 Interpretation
 Job estimates presented in this report should be interpreted as comparative indicators across
 scenarios, suitable for understanding relative workforce impacts of different deployment
 strategies. Differences in reported employment across scenarios primarily reﬂect changes in
 deployment scale, infrastructure extent, and sequencing rather than precise differences in labor
 requirements at individual facilities.

 Scenario-speciﬁc job results are presented in the Socioeconomics Impacts sections that follow,
 alongside brief interpretive text describing how employment varies across deployment cases.

 8.4 Two-Phase Buildout
 8.4.1 Background
 To better represent the buildout of infrastructure in the state of Wyoming, a scenario was
 developed using a two-phase implementation scheme. The ﬁrst phase of deployment involved
 capturing, transporting, and sequestering 15 MtCO₂. For this initial phase, referred to as Phase
 1, a 10-year ﬁnancing period at 10% interest (CRF = 0.1627) and a 30-year injection period were
 applied. CO₂ source facilities were ﬁltered to include only a single unit per facility, and the
 lowest-cost sequestration formations identiﬁed by SCO₂T modeling were utilized.

 Phase 2 introduced an additional 15 MtCO₂ for capture, transport, and storage. This phase was
 modeled to begin in year 5 with a 10-year ﬁnancing period and a 25-year injection period,
 resulting in a total project length of 30 years. Phase 2 leveraged the infrastructure established
 during Phase 1, allowing pipeline routes from Phase 1 to serve Phase 2 needs. In some cases,
 pipeline diameters were intentionally oversized during Phase 1 to accommodate the increased
 CO₂ volume anticipated in Phase 2.

 SimCCSPRO was employed to optimize results based on reaching user-speciﬁed capture targets
 for Phase 1. The model determined the optimal sources, pipeline routes, and storage sinks to
 minimize cumulative deployment costs for 15 MtCO₂. These selections— the sources, storage,
 and pipeline corridors—were then ﬁxed for Phase 2, which optimized deployment for a total of
 30 MtCO₂ by identifying additional sources, sinks, and pipelines to complement Phase 1
 infrastructure. Post-processing calculations were performed to retroactively increase pipeline
 diameters where necessary, and additional adjustments ensured accurate ﬁnancing for
 overlapping infrastructure components.

 8.5 Intermodal transportation options
 8.5.1 Background
 While SimCCSPRO typically models costs associated with the capture, transport, and geologic
 storage of CO2 via pipelines, identifying those facilities that may beneﬁt from rail or road can be
 important for a number of reasons. First, approval for pipeline construction necessarily takes
 large investments and long planning periods. Some facilities may have capturable CO2 earlier
 than a pipeline is available to transport it to a utilization or storage site. Utilizing truck or rail
 transportation could allow CO2 source facilities to reach the market and achieve revenue before
 ﬁnal pipeline construction is complete. Second, in some industries, the technology available to
 capture CO2 may not be at the volume required to build a pipeline but might still support
 commercial sales of CO2 as a product, allowing some revenue to be generated while additional
 capture technology is made available. Finally, some sectors or facilities may be interested in
 capturing CO2 as early adopters due to ﬁnancial incentives or company goals and priorities. In
 each of these cases, understanding how and when rail, road, and pipeline may be deployed, and
 at what cost, is important to planning how a carbon management hub may develop over time.
 Understanding when these alternate modes may be more economically advantageous than a
 pipeline is the second business case evaluated for this project.

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                     89

 Modeling when rail, road, or pipelines will be deployed as economically optimal routing
 solutions for transporting CO₂ required developing cost estimates for each transportation
 pathway, sub-setting existing road and rail infrastructure to include the most likely options
 connecting CO₂ facilities to storage and utilization locations, and adapting SimCCSPRO to create
 a network of transportation routes that only allow realistic transitions between trucks, rail, and
 pipelines. Although these mixed-mode cases represent higher costs compared to pipeline-only
 scenarios, they reﬂect transportation strategies that may be necessary for individual CO₂
 sources until pipelines are constructed or until CO₂ capture occurs at scale.

 To explore the ﬁnancial and routing consequences of expanding CO2 transportation modes to
 include pipelines, railroads, and roads, we utilize SimCCSPRO to select which combination of
 transportation options resulted in the lowest cost source-sink solution.

 Sources
 For the Intermodal transportation option business model, we ﬁltered all Wyoming CO2 to include
 only those within 1 km of existing highways or railways and under 1 MtCO2/yr in capturable
 emissions, resulting in forty-two facilities which were individually modeled using the intermodal
 transportation model. That is, for each source facility, SimCCSPRO was run to allow the model to
 select which transportation modes were most cost-effective. For large emissions, pipelines are
 far more efficient and cost-effective. For smaller amounts of emissions, however, intermodal
 options may be preferable, which is why we imposed the 1 MtCO2 threshold on source facilities.

 Sinks
 In these scenarios, storage sites were located in existing enhanced oil recovery (EOR) ﬁelds, an
 arrangement that would increase the local supply of CO2 for EOR while offsetting the costs of
 capturing CO2 with tax credits. To determine the location of the storage sites, EOR ﬁelds, existing
 railroads, and existing major roads were combined in GIS to determine point locations in EOR
 ﬁelds that were near rail and road. Each sink was assigned the same storage potential and cost,
 ensuring storage potential did not limit which sources were routed there. Because these
 facilities each had low volumes relative to coal plants, it is not unrealistic to assume all
 emissions could be routed to one storage ﬁeld. Each storage site was also given a uniform cost,
 ensuring all sources could route to any storage location based on transportation and capture
 costs, not storage costs.

 Routing
 To ensure realistic road and rail connections between sources and storage sites, additional data
 preparation steps were required. First, a subset of roads and railroads was selected to minimize
 run-time for the optimization model. It is assumed that major highways are the main
 thoroughfare for trucks, and existing railroads in the area are capable of transporting CO2. The
 intermediary transport between a source or a sink to a nearby highway or railroad is assumed to
 be done by trucking it along local streets.

 The local streets are extracted from the highly detailed TIGER/Line shapeﬁles provided by the
 U.S. Census Bureau to best represent the actual path a truck may take during transportation.
 These are selected by a reviewer identifying streets that form a path from a given source or sink
 to the nearest potential highway or railway transfer point. The street network is complex enough
 for this manual review to be more efficient than attempting to automate this process. There are

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                  90

 topology errors present in the U.S. Census Bureau street data that also make the task of
 automating local street selection ineffective.

 Once local streets that connect the sources and sinks to highways or railways have been
 determined, the input highway and railway datasets are further trimmed based on the
 connection points determined by the local streets. The endpoint of this process represents the
 potential critical path through streets, highways, and railroads that would be taken in an
 intermodal transport scenario. This has the beneﬁt of producing intermodal results that are
 realistic to the actual road network.

 Two different models were run for each CO2 facility: those with the default cost and routing
 surface with the trimmed rail and road network, and those with a routing surface meant to
 prioritize the located pipeline within the WPCI corridor, in addition to roads and rail.

 Note on the Localized Use of Pipelines in the Intermodal Solution
 Although the model solution clearly prioritizes road transportation, Figure 8 shows some short
 pipeline segments directly connected to certain emission sources. It is important to clarify that
 these segments should not necessarily be interpreted as dedicated infrastructure that would be
 built in practice, but rather as a consequence of the model’s spatial representation process.

 Speciﬁcally, these segments appear in situations where the modeled road network does not
 reach the exact point at which the emission source is located. In such cases, SimCCSPRO
 completes the connection between the source and the nearest road node through a pipeline
 segment, ensuring network connectivity and solution feasibility. From an operational
 standpoint, what would be expected in a real project is that the truck departs directly from the
 emission source, without the need to construct a pipeline to cover such a short distance.
 Therefore, these segments should be interpreted as artiﬁcial connector elements that close the
 network in the model, rather than as an explicit recommendation to invest in pipeline
 infrastructure for those cases.

 This clariﬁcation is relevant to avoid overinterpreting the role of pipelines in scenarios
 dominated by road transport and reinforces the interpretation that the observed pattern
 primarily reﬂects truck-based logistics, supported by the existing road infrastructure.

 Financial Considerations for Road-Based Truck Transportation
 The analysis of road-based CO₂ transportation incorporated in the intermodal model relies on an
 explicit decomposition of capital (CAPEX) and operational (OPEX) costs, consistent with
 approaches widely used in the specialized literature. For CAPEX, the main components
 considered include: trucks, tanker trailers or intermodal containers, liquefaction,
 reconditioning, and buffer storage. OPEX includes costs associated with fuel, operating
 personnel salaries, maintenance, permits, insurance, and other regulatory fees.

 The cost framework used is based on the Multimodal CO₂ Transportation Cost Model (MuMo-
 CoCo), developed by Myers (2024) and applied in Myers et al. (2024).134 MuMo-CoCo is an Excel-
 based model that estimates the capital, operational, and ﬁnancial costs of CO₂ transportation

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                97

 across different modes, primarily as a function of transported volume, distance, and selected
 transport mode. The model also allows for the incorporation of assumptions regarding operating
 and construction periods, providing a consistent basis for comparing intermodal alternatives
 under different scenarios.

 at 25% emissions to $125.15/tCO₂ at 100%. Capture costs per ton increase slightly with scale,
 while transport costs per ton rise more noticeably at full capture due to extensive pipeline
 requirements. Storage costs per ton remain in the $7.9–$9.6 range.
 The analysis shows that economies of scale are limited—total costs rise almost linearly with
 emissions, and unit costs increase modestly at higher capture levels. The largest cost driver is
 capture, followed by storage, with transport costs becoming signiﬁcant only at full-scale
 deployment. Because there is so much storage available in Wyoming, even when the WPCI
 corridor is weighted to be the preferred path for pipelines, few scenarios take advantage of this
 route. As a result, economies of scale for pipelines along the WPCI are unlikely to provide
 economic beneﬁts without other incentives for building a long pipeline.

 9 Conclusion
 Deciding to invest in CCS involves many competing factors. One of the most important
 components of a CCS project is suitable geology with target saline formations or depleted
 reservoirs capable of securely storing CO₂. Even with suitable geology, regulatory readiness
 plays a critical role in project investment—states with Class VI well primacy, well-deﬁned
 permitting pathways, and clear liability rules can signiﬁcantly reduce project timelines and
 uncertainty. Beyond geology and regulation, infrastructure availability is a major determinant.
 Proximity to industrial CO₂ sources and existing or planned pipeline networks can dramatically
 inﬂuence project feasibility and public acceptance. Financial incentives, such as federal 45Q tax
 credits and state-level programs, further shape the business case, while community
 engagement and workforce readiness ensure long-term project sustainability and social license
 to operate.
 Ultimately, CCS project siting requires balancing feasibility, cost efficiency, regulatory clarity,
 and stakeholder support. Companies that take a holistic approach—integrating these factors
 into their decision-making—are best positioned to deploy CCS successfully. To help investors
 new to Wyoming understand how the interrelated opportunities of carbon capture, transport,
 and storage are, this project has compiled county-level readiness indices.

 9.2 Transportation Opportunities
 Understanding transportation readiness is perhaps the most difficult aspect of the CCS planning
 process to model. Most often, CO2 is transported by pipeline—there are over 5,000 miles of CO2
 pipelines currently operating in eight states, with over 600 miles of these pipelines in
 Wyoming.135 Because CO2 can also be transported by rail and road, these are important

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                              105

 infrastructures to consider, with fewer interstates, major highways, and railways indicating a
 lack of readiness.
 An additional metric this report considers is the routing cost surface, that is, a digital map that
 integrates different geographic layers to capture how difficult it is to route a pipeline over land.
 For pipelines, the layers used in constructing the routing cost surface are:
 • Land cover: Land use characteristics from the National Land Cover Database.136
 • Government Land: Federal land designations from the USGS. 137
 • Population Density: Population density from Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s
 LANDSCAN program.138
 • Slope: Slope is calculated from the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s LANDFIRE
 dataset.139
 • Pipelines: Pipeline ROW data from state and federal sources.140
 • Transmission Lines: Electric Transmission line data from HSIP GOLD 141.
 • Railroads: Railway data from the U.S. Department of Transportation Rail Network
 data.142
 • All Roads: Roads from the U.S. Census Bureau TIGER/Line shapeﬁles.143
 • Major Roads: Roads from the National Transportation Atlas Database for large scales.144
 • Rivers: River data from the USGS.145

 Each layer is assigned a numerical value for a 720 x 720 m grid cell, and these values are then
 compiled for each cell. Lower values indicate that it is less difficult to move across terrain (such
 as ﬂat, low-population areas), and higher values indicate more difficult pipeline siting (such as
 areas with mountains or high population density). While each layer can be weighted differently
 for a given project, for example, to follow existing pipelines, avoid buffer areas around schools
 and hospitals, or make land designated as grasslands more desirable than ﬁelds.

 9.3 Storage Opportunities
 Determining the suitability of geologic storage for Class VI wells requires a detailed technical
 evaluation of the area’s geologic and hydrogeologic characteristics, models of the size and
 location of the CO2 plume, and assessing the existing area wells to determine how they will
 impact the project. Before these necessary in-depth studies, some preliminary work can
 identify, at a screening level, areas of Wyoming more suitable for storage than others. Good
 storage formations are not uniformly distributed across the county, and both private and public
 data and projects can provide data about those that offer the potential to permanently store
 CO2.
 To consider how ready a county is for storing CO2, data about existing projects, geologic
 characteristics, and modeled capture costs are presented in Table 9. Storage potential and
 average storage costs are derived from the SCO2TPRO tool. Based on the geologic characteristics
 of saline formations, including porosity, permeability, formation depth, formation thickness, and
 temperature, the amount of CO2 a formation can store (storage potential) and the costs of
 building and operating a Class VI well (storage costs) are calculated. In many locations, there
 are multiple formations at a well site where CO2 can be injected. While Class VI wells may inject
 in multiple formations at different depths at the same site, when calculating costs, total storage

 potential, and maximum high storage amounts for this readiness index, only the lowest cost
 formation in any 10km x 10km location was considered (Table 9). The total number of 10km grid
 cells with suitable geology within a count is listed as the variable “10 k count.” If a county has a
 CarbonSAFE project or Class VI well project with active injection or an active application with
 Wyoming’s Department of Environmental Quality, there is a “1” in the “CO2 Projects” column.
 Finally, while Class VI wells convey a larger tax credit for CO2 injection, Class II wells can also be

 9.5 Conclusions
 Wyoming is one of the country’s leaders in demonstrating the feasibility of CCUS projects. Few
 regions combine the elements that Wyoming brings together, including world-class geologic
 formations, a concentration of large industrial emitters, supportive policies, established CO₂
 utilization markets, and a population that, while still learning about CCS, shows a pragmatic
 openness to new energy technologies that protect jobs and communities.
 Wyoming’s subsurface formations, particularly in the Greater Green River and Powder River
 Basins, provide enough capacity to anchor one of the largest CO₂ storage hubs in North
 America. Combined with state primacy over Class VI permitting, the state has a regulatory
 advantage that can compress project timelines and attract private investment relative to many
 other states. In addition, the extensive presence of EOR ﬁelds and existing CO₂ pipelines offers
 an opportunity for early commercialization, enabling CCUS projects to advance before large-
 scale saline storage comes fully online.

Carbon Solutions | Wyoming Carbon Blueprint                                                                 113

 CCUS also offers Wyoming a pathway to stabilize and evolve its energy-dependent economy. As
 federal incentives accelerate national decarbonization, CCUS enables Wyoming’s legacy
 industries, including coal, natural gas, reﬁning, and extraction, to remain competitive while
 opening opportunities in hydrogen, synthetic fuels, CO₂-derived materials, and other advanced
 manufacturing sectors. The workforce, though impacted by declining fossil fuel jobs, is well-
 positioned for retraining into CCS-related roles, and state programs are already responding to
 this need.
 Challenges remain, as in any emerging industry, including supply chain constraints, siting
 considerations, and community opposition to projects. But Wyoming’s experience in siting large,
 energy-related projects, its established Industrial Siting Council, and the experienced scientists,
 research institutes, regulatory framework, existing projects, and commercial experience allow
 synergies not present in many other states. With coordinated investment and continued policy
 support, Wyoming can emerge as a national and global leader for large-scale carbon
 management.

 11.1 Powder River Basin
 The Powder River Basin model follows the same workﬂow as the Wind River Basin model
 featured in Section 6. Differences between the models are limited to basin-speciﬁc inputs and
 results; accordingly, workﬂow and ﬁgure explanations are not included. For the PRB, surface
 layer elevations are deﬁned using 35,543 elevation points for each layer, with 11 layers featured
 in the model. The layers span a domain of 78.2 km x 77.1 km, with a subset of the domain used
 to build a computational mesh of size 47.7 km x 77.1 km x 4.46 km. Further reﬁnement of the
 computational mesh takes place around the four injection wells placed in the model to better
 capture ﬂow and pressure gradients in the reservoir simulations, with the ﬁnest mesh resolution
 is 97m. The resulting mesh is comprised of 1,872,888 nodes.
 While the WRB model included 2 storage reservoirs due to its large storage capacity, the
 PRB uses a single storage reservoir, the Minnelusa/Madison unit. NRAP modeling is
 concentrated on the leakage receptor components, which are of particular concern given that
 the PRB is primarily an oil and gas-producing basin. Three Permian zones are identiﬁed as being
 most at risk (Permian Zone 1: Frontier formation, Permian Zone 2: Dakota-Lakota formation,
 Permian 3: Sundance formation), with existing oil and gas wells in the region assigned to the
 appropriate leakage receptor component based on the zone in which they are located. A total of
 1,064 known wells exist in the model, and all wells are also assigned to leak into the Wasatch
 formation to evaluate potential impacts on underground sources of drinking water. Reservoir
 simulations use the same injection parameters as the WRB model, with 100 realizations of
 lognormal Gaussian ﬁelds for porosity and permeability, and the NRAP well component is
 modeled as an open wellbore. Results are featured for Injection Site 4, where the leakage
 receptor component is set to be the USDW. Interpolation is performed at a well within the AoR,
 Wagensen Federal 12-17-4672, to assess local impacts.

 11.2 Greater Green River Basin
 The Greater Green River Basin model was constructed to analyze leakage risk through
 fault zones in the region rather than artiﬁcial penetrations. Initial testing showed that the model
 exceeds NRAP’s depth range for storage reservoirs- while a risk analysis was not performed, the
 model conﬁguration is provided for completeness. The GGRB model was conﬁgured using
 980,100 surface elevation points for six layers each. The layers span a domain of 30.14 km x
 30.14 km. The chosen reservoir in the model, the Weber formation, is thin and steeply dipping,
 leading to limitations for modeling the layer in LaGrit. To balance accurate representation of the
 model with computational efficiency, only a subset of the domain is modeled in the
 computational mesh, keeping the total number of nodes within FEHM’s capacity. Unlike the
 WRB and PRB models, subsetting in the GGRB model also took place in the z direction, with only
 the reservoir and conﬁning shales being characterized for FEHM simulations. The remaining
 layers are rebuilt into the NRAP model, as they are not relevant until later stages of the analysis,
 with the Wasatch formation is selected as the USDW for which to conduct a leakage risk
 analysis. The ﬁnal computational mesh consists of 670,321 nodes over a domain of 19.1 km x
 30.14 km x 5.7 km, with local reﬁnement applied around 36 faults in the region. Both the full
 mesh model and the reduced mesh suitable for FEHM are featured to highlight the model
 conﬁguration. As the reservoir lies near or outside the suitable depth range for NRAP, no results
 are produced for a leakage risk analysis. Portions of the domain that meet NRAP’s depth criteria
 are too distant from faults to pose a signiﬁcant leakage risk.

 12 Appendix:
 Transportation
 A comparison of the transportation options as a function of both distance and transported CO₂
 volume further illustrates these dynamics. Depending on the mode, net CO₂ transport efficiency
 changes in response to variations in shipment size and travel length. This variability was
 observed consistently across all intermodal transport options considered in this study,
 reinforcing that no single mode performs uniformly under all conditions.
 Detailed results for each mode are presented in Figure 20 to Figure 23. These ﬁgures illustrate
 how efficiency evolves for different combinations of CO₂ volume and transport distance, making
 it possible to identify thresholds where one option begins to lose competitiveness relative to the
 others. The four intermodal options evaluated include: Rail – Intermodal, Rail – Tanker, Truck –
 Intermodal, and Truck – Tanker.
 For rail-intermodal, efficiency remains highest as a function of both CO2 volumes and total
 distance. Even at very long distances (~4,600 km), efficiency remains at 0.82 if CO2 volumes are
 constant and high (0.96 MtCO2/yr). Rail transport can also maintain efficiency above 90% at
 distances just under 500 km. Over very short distances (< ~500km), the volume of CO2 in
 transport has little impact on efficiency. Similarly, very minimal efficiency is lost as the distance

 A comparison of the efficiency patterns for truck–tanker (Figure 22) and truck–intermodal
 transport (Figure 23) highlights important differences. Both options maintain relatively high
 efficiencies at short distances and high CO₂ volumes, but their performance diverges as
 distance increases. Truck–tanker sustains higher efficiencies up to around 2,600 km, after which
 efficiency drops sharply, reaching as low as 0.20. In contrast, truck–intermodal begins to show a
 decline much earlier, with noticeable reductions in efficiency already around 1,200–1,500 km,

 and it reaches an even lower minimum of 0.16 under conditions of low volume with long
 distance.
 Overall, truck–tanker demonstrates greater resilience at medium distances, whereas truck–
 intermodal degrades more gradually but across a wider range of distances. Despite these
 differences, both systems remain competitive at short distances with high CO₂ volumes.
 However, beyond intermediate distances, truck–tanker consistently outperforms truck–
 intermodal in terms of average efficiency.
