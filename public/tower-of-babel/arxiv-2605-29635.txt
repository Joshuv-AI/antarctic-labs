MoSSP: A Momentum-Based Single-Loop Stochastic Penalty Method for Nonconvex Constrained DC-Regularized Optimization
Luxuan Li, Chunfeng Cui, Xiao Wang

Abstract
In this paper, we study a structured class of nonconvex constrained stochastic problems with difference-of-convex (DC) regularization, where the feasible set is possibly nonconvex and the concave part of the DC regularizer is allowed to be nonsmooth. The fundamental challenge lies in maintaining feasibility for nonconvex constraints while achieving favorable oracle complexity. Although single-loop algorithms efficiently solve unconstrained DC optimization problems, their potential for constrained optimization with DC structure remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, we develop MoSSP , a Mo mentum-based S ingle-loop S tochastic P enalty method for such problems with provable complexity guarantees. The key idea is to apply a single stochastic proximal-gradient step to the Moreau envelope of the penalty plus the convex DC part, with the concave part’s proximal mapping computed in parallel. We derive two algorithm variants: a Polyak-momentum version with 𝒪 ⁡ ( ε − 4 ) \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4}) oracle complexity for finding stochastic ε \varepsilon -KKT points, and an improved 𝒪 ⁡ ( ε − 3 ) \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3}) version incorporating recursive momentum. Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms.

\displaystyle F(\bm{x})=\{f(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\mathbf{f}(\bm{x},\xi)]\}+h(\bm{x})-g(\bm{x}),

1.2
Related Work

For DC optimization problems, prior work has largely focused on unconstrained or convex-set constrained settings, where feasibility can be maintained by projection. The classical DC algorithm (DCA)
(
Tao & Souad 1986
)
linearizes the concave part and solves a sequence of convex subproblems. Following this idea of constructing tractable convex majorants, proximal, Bregman, and stochastic variants have been developed with global convergence or complexity guarantees
(
Wen et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2025a
;
Liu & Takeda 2022
;
Yang et al. 2025b
;
Nitanda & Suzuki 2017
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
. Handling general functional constraints, however, requires additional care. Recent efforts address convex inequality constraints in Problem (
1
) via convex approximations of the feasible region, obtaining favorable convergence guarantees under suitable constraint qualifications in the deterministic setting; see
(
Kanzow & Neder 2026
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Yu et al. 2021
)
. The difficulty becomes more pronounced when nonconvex constraints are present, since feasibility is generally difficult to maintain. While there are algorithms tailored to specific nonconvex constraints, such as manifolds
(
Bergmann et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2025
)
or conic sets
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
, such analyses are inherently geometry-dependent and do not extend easily to general nonconvex constraints. More recently,
Le Thi et al. 2024
studied DC composite optimization under nonconvex constraints via an exact penalty method, but non-asymptotic analysis in the stochastic setting remains unaddressed.

Note that most of the aforementioned methods are double-loop algorithms, in which a subproblem must be (approximately) solved in inner iterations. As a result, they can be relatively complex due to extensive hyperparameter tuning (e.g., the penalty parameter in
(
Le Thi et al. 2024
, Algorithm 3,)
) and the need for precise termination criteria for subproblem solvers
(
Yu et al. 2021
;
Liu & Takeda 2022
, e.g.,)
. The growing scale of data and models motivates the need for more efficient optimization methods. In this context, single-loop designs become attractive, especially in stochastic settings.
For Problem (
1
) with
g\equiv 0
or
h-g\equiv 0
, single-loop stochastic penalty algorithms, including augmented Lagrangian methods, already provide compelling oracle complexity guarantees for finding approximate KKT points
(
Alacaoglu & Wright 2024
;
Shi et al. 2026
;
Liu & Xu 2025
;
Lu et al. 2026
)
. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of such single-loop approaches normally depends on tractable subproblems, a condition typically met when
h
admits an efficiently computable proximal mapping. In Problem (
1
), however, the term
h-g
is generally non-prox-friendly. Consequently, single-loop algorithms capable of handling such nonsmooth DC regularizations under general nonconvex constraints remain scarce.

To address this inherent nonsmoothness in the DC structure, some recent work has explored alternative smoothing techniques. Specifically, several studies
(
Moudafi 2023
;
Sun & Sun 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Chayti & Jaggi 2025
)
apply the Moreau envelope to each convex component in the DC structure and then take their difference, yielding the Difference-of-Moreau-Envelopes (DME) for non-asymptotic convergence analysis. The DME approach preserves the global DC structure, and stationary points are recoverable via the inexpensive proximal operator of each original component. Among these works, the method of
Hu et al. 2024
is, to the best of our knowledge, the first single-loop method for stochastic difference-of-weakly-convex (DWC) optimization based on the DME idea, and achieves
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
oracle complexity. More recently,
Chayti & Jaggi 2025
introduced momentum into stochastic DC optimization by applying it to the concave component and obtained
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
oracle complexity. Despite these advances, most existing methods still rely on double-loop schemes to achieve state-of-the-art non-asymptotic rates
(
Nitanda & Suzuki 2017
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
, or are confined to unconstrained problems; see
(
Nitanda & Suzuki 2017
;
Xu et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Chayti & Jaggi 2025
)
. While
Sun & Sun 2023
developed an augmented Lagrangian algorithm for linearly constrained DC problems in a deterministic setting, such constructions can become unstable under stochastic noise due to sensitive multiplier updates, thus hindering their practical adoption.

In light of these limitations and to facilitate practical implementation, we develop an efficient single-loop, penalty-based method for solving Problem (

mo

tochastic

\blacktriangleright

Comparable complexity guarantee.
To the best of our knowledge, we establish the first complexity results for nonconvex DC-regularized optimization with nonlinear constraints. Under mild conditions,

\blacktriangleright

Novel theoretical analysis.
We develop a DC-aware complexity analysis that explicitly characterizes stochastic errors within the criticality measure. 2.1
Criticality in DC Optimization

Consider the unconstrained DC optimization problem:

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Psi(\bm{x}):=\phi(\bm{x})-g(\bm{x}),

(2.1)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is
m_{\phi}
-weakly convex (possibly nonsmooth), and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a proper, closed, and convex function.

As is well known, the
Moreau envelope
provides favorable smoothing properties for weakly convex functions. For the component function
\phi
and any
\mu\in(0,1/m_{\phi})
, its Moreau envelope
\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}
and the associated proximal mapping are well-defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle:=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\phi(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle:=\arg\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\phi(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}\right\},

(2.2)

respectively.
Note that
\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}
serves as a smooth surrogate of
\phi
with gradient
(
Moreau 1965
)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mu^{-1}(\bm{z}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z}))\in\partial\phi(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z})).

(2.3)

For the weakly convex optimization problem
\min\phi(\bm{x})
, given
\varepsilon>0
, one typically seeks an
\varepsilon
-stationary point of its Moreau envelope
\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}
, i.e.,
\bar{\bm{x}}
with
\|\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}(\bar{\bm{x}})\|\leq\varepsilon
, as a relaxed convergence criterion
(
Davis & Drusvyatskiy 2019
)
. However, this approach cannot be directly extended to Problem (
2.1
), as the objective
\Psi
may lack weak convexity. We thus introduce the notion of an
\varepsilon
-critical point
for DC optimization
(
Sun & Sun 2023
)
, i.e., a point
\bar{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
\varepsilon
-critical point
of
\Psi
if there exist

\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bar{\bm{u}}\in\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})-\partial g(\bar{\bm{y}})
such that
\max\{\|\bar{\bm{u}}\|,\|\bar{\bm{x}}-\bar{\bm{y}}\|\}\leq\varepsilon
. This two-point formulation serves as a natural stopping criterion for DCA-type methods in practice, since
\partial\phi
and
\partial g
are usually evaluated at different points at each iteration in standard DCA; for example, a subgradient
\xi^{k}\in\partial g(x^{k})
is used to compute
x^{k+1}
. Note that when
\varepsilon=0
, this definition recovers the classical DC criticality condition
(
Pang et al. 2017
)
.

Moreover, the overall Moreau envelope of
\Psi
in Problem (
2.1
) is computationally intractable. To this end, we apply the Moreau envelope to
\phi
and
g
individually and take their difference (DME) to define a smooth approximation of Problem (
2.1
) for any
\mu\in(0,1/m_{\phi})
:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Psi_{\mu}(\bm{z}):=\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z})-\mathcal{M}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}).

(2.4)

The smoothness of
\Psi_{\mu}(\bm{z})
was
established in
Hiriart-Urruty 1991
.

However, it is not immediately clear how the approximate solutions of
Problems (
2.4
) and (
2.1
) relate to each other. We establish that any
\varepsilon
-stationary point of
\Psi_{\mu}

can be converted into an
\varepsilon
-critical point of
\Psi
, with the detailed proof deferred to Appendix
B.5
. This correspondence plays a key role in our analysis. Complete properties of DME and the equivalence between solutions of these two problems are deferred to Appendices
B.3
and
B.4
, respectively.

Proposition 2.1
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, for any
0<\mu<1/m_{\phi}
, if
\bar{\bm{z}}
satisfies
\|\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})\|\leq\min\{1,\mu^{-1}\}\varepsilon
, then the point
\bar{\bm{x}}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bar{\bm{z}})
is an
\varepsilon
-critical point of
\Psi
with the auxiliary point
\bar{\bm{y}}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bar{\bm{z}})
.

Main Assumptions

Throughout the paper, we make the following assumptions for Problem (
1
). We assume
F^{*}:=\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(\bm{x})>-\infty
.

Assumption 2.1
.

Function
f(\cdot)
is

L_{f}
-smooth. Function
\bm{c}
is
L_{c}
-smooth. There exist
C,G>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\|\leq G,\,\sup_{\bm{v}_{h}\in\partial h(\bm{x})}\|\bm{v}_{h}\|\leq G,\,\sup_{\bm{v}_{g}\in\partial g(\bm{x})}\|\bm{v}_{g}\|\leq G,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|\leq G,\quad\|\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|\leq C,\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The boundedness condition is crucial for ensuring reliable convergence of the iterative sequence in stochastic constrained optimization. The inherent randomness of the process makes it difficult to guarantee that all iterates remain within a specific level set. The necessity of
2.1
is well established in
(
Berahas et al. 2021
;
Na et al. 2023b
;
Na et al. 2023a
;
Wang 2025
;
Sun & Sun 2023
)
.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists a constant
\sigma>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x},\xi)]=\nabla f(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x},\xi)-\nabla f(\bm{x})\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2},\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For nonconvex constraints, we impose the following nonsingularity condition on the constraints to control feasibility of the generated solutions. Constraint qualifications are often required in solving constrained optimization problems; see
(
Shi et al. 2026
;
Lu et al. 2026
;
Liu & Xu 2025
;
Curtis et al. 2024
)
.

Assumption 2.3
.

For the iterate sequence
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by the algorithms, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\|\geq\delta\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\|,\quad\forall\,k\geq 0.

3
Momentum-Based Single-Loop Stochastic Penalty Algorithms

3.1
Algorithmic Framework

We incorporate the nonconvex constraints in Problem (
1
) through a sequence of quadratically penalized DC objectives with nondecreasing penalty parameters
\{\rho_{k}\}\subset(0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big\{F_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}):=\underbrace{Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})}_{\psi_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x})}-g(\bm{x})\big\},

(3.1)

where
Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|^{2}
. To address the nonsmoothness of the penalty problems, we take the difference of the Moreau envelopes of each component to construct a smoothed surrogate function
F_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{z})
:

\displaystyle F_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{z})=\mathcal{M}_{\mu\psi_{\rho}}(\bm{z})-\mathcal{M}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}),\quad\text{with }\rho=\rho_{k}.

(3.2)

Crucially, by Proposition
2.1
, if
\bar{\bm{z}}
satisfies
\|\nabla F_{\rho,\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})\|\leq\min\{1,\mu^{-1}\}\varepsilon
, then any
\bar{\bm{x}}
with
\|\bar{\bm{x}}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bar{\bm{z}})\|\leq\varepsilon
is an
\varepsilon
-critical point of
F_{\rho}
.
This gives the approximate criticality criterion targeted in (
2.6
) or (
2.7
). Therefore, we aim to efficiently find a point
\bar{\bm{z}}
by iteratively minimizing
F_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{z})
.

A standard strategy is to apply gradient descent to
F_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{z})
, which inspires our framework. Following the properties of the Moreau envelope, the gradient of
F_{\rho,\mu}
at the point
\bm{z}^{k}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla F_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{z}^{k})

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}(\bm{z}^{k}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\psi_{\rho}}(\bm{z}^{k}))

(3.3)

\displaystyle-\mu^{-1}(\bm{z}^{k}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}^{k}))

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}\bigl(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}^{k})-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\psi_{\rho}}(\bm{z}^{k})\bigr).

This involves the proximal operators of
g
and
\psi_{\rho}
. However,
computing the exact proximal operator of the composite function
\psi_{\rho}
is typically intractable. To this end, we maintain a variable
\bm{x}
as an estimator of
\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\psi_{\rho}}(\bm{z})
and compute
\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z})
in parallel. Specifically, at each iteration, we update
\bm{x}^{k}
via a stochastic proximal gradient descent step

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}h}\bigl(\bm{z}^{k}-\mu_{k}\tilde{\nabla}Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})\bigr)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\{\langle\tilde{\nabla}Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k}),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{k}\rangle+h(\bm{x})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}^{k}\|^{2}\Bigr\},

where
\tilde{\nabla}Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})
is a stochastic estimator of
\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})
. Finally,
\bm{z}^{k}
is updated via a gradient-type step, where the gradient (
3.3
) is now partially estimated by replacing
\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}\psi_{\rho_{k}}}(\bm{z}^{k})
with
\bm{x}^{k+1}
from (
3.4
):

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{k+1}=\bm{z}^{k}-\beta\left(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})-\bm{x}^{k+1}\right),

(3.5)

where
\beta>0
is the stepsize. All parameter sequences are positive and will be specified later.

Our framework allows for flexible choices of
\tilde{\nabla}Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})
in the
\bm{x}
-update step (see (
3.4
)), enabling the integration of advanced momentum techniques to enhance both theoretical complexity and practical efficiency. We refer to this unified framework as
MoSSP
(
Mo
mentum-based
S
ingle-loop
S
tochastic
P
enalty).

:

, initial point

, positive parameters
\mu_{k}
,

, and

.

for

do

from (

).

Polyak momentum, also known as the heavy-ball method and originally proposed by
Polyak 1964
, has been extensively studied in large-scale nonconvex optimization
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Jelassi & Li 2022
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
. Motivated by this, at each iteration
k
, we draw an i.i.d. sample
\xi^{k}\sim\Xi
and construct a Polyak momentum-based stochastic estimator for
\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})
:

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{k}=\bm{s}^{k}+\rho_{k}\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k}),

(3.6)

where
\bm{s}^{k}
is updated by

\displaystyle\bm{s}^{k}=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha_{k-1})\bm{s}^{k-1}+\alpha_{k-1}\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k},\xi^{k}),&k\geq 1,\\
\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{0},\xi^{0}),&k=0.\end{cases}

(3.7)

The variable
\bm{x}^{k+1}
is then updated as:

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}h}\bigl(\bm{z}^{k}-\mu_{k}\bm{S}^{k}\bigr),

(3.8)

where
\mu_{k}>0
for
k\geq 0
. The complete algorithm, referred to as
MoSSP-P
, is summarized in
Algorithm

1
.

Oracle Complexity of MoSSP-P.
We now analyze the oracle complexity of
MoSSP-P for finding a stochastic
\varepsilon
-KKT point and a stochastic
\varepsilon
-stationary point, respectively. The maximum number of iterations is limited to a fixed integer
K
.

Note that
Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})
is smooth with Lipschitz constant
L_{\rho}
, where
L_{\rho}=\rho\tilde{L}
and
\tilde{L}={\rho_{0}^{-1}L_{f}}+G^{2}+CL_{c}
, which is crucial for the subsequent analysis (see
Lemma

C.1
).

We aim to bound the residuals in (
2.6
) and (
2.7
). Classical analysis of penalty methods in nonconvex constrained optimization often relies on the descent property of the penalty function. However, the concave component
-g
in our penalty function
F_{\rho_{k}}
breaks this descent property, rendering the standard potential function construction ineffective. To this end, we construct the following potential function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w})=Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}),

where
\bm{w}=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
. The function
\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w})
is bounded from below (see Appendix
C.2
).

To quantify the criticality of iterates
\bm{w}^{k}
generated by MoSSP-P, we define
\bm{u}^{k+1}
as:

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})-{\bm{S}^{k}}+\mu_{k}^{-1}(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})-\bm{x}^{k+1}).

(3.9)

It can be proved that
\bm{u}^{k+1}\in\partial\psi_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\partial g(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k}))
(see Appendix
C.3
).

Lemma

C.3
gives a one-step bound on
\bm{u}^{k+1}
relative to the iterate change (see Appendix
C.3
). The stochastic error in (
C.19
) implies that proper parameter settings are required to control variance and ensure favorable complexity. This differs from the deterministic framework for constrained DC optimization of
Sun & Sun 2023
. We next present an estimated convergence rate of MoSSP-P to guide parameter selection.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold. Let
\{\bm{w}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence generated by MoSSP-P
with the parameters satisfying

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{O}(K^{l}),\quad\mu_{k}\equiv\mathcal{O}(K^{{-\tau}}),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{O}(K^{{-\tau}}),\quad 0<\beta\leq 1,

where
0<l\leq\tau\leq 2l<1
is independent of
K
.
Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\max\{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{R+1}\|^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{R+1}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{R}g}(\bm{z}^{R})\|^{2}]\}

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\max\left\{\begin{gathered}K^{\tau-1}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w}^{0})+1\right),\\
K^{\tau-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{0}\|^{2}],\hskip 9.24994ptK^{-\tau}\end{gathered}\right\}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\max\left\{\begin{gathered}K^{-2l+\tau-1}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w}^{0})+1\right),\\
K^{-2l+\tau-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{0}\|^{2}],\hskip 9.24994ptK^{-2l}\end{gathered}\right\}\right).

Note that (
3.11
) indicates that the term
\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w}^{0})
may dominate the complexity due to
\rho=\rho_{0}K^{l}
. Motivated by
Xie & Wright 2021
;
Jin & Wang 2022
, we employ an approximately feasible initialization to mitigate this overhead. Let
\bm{x}^{0}
be an approximately feasible point satisfying
\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-l})
for some
0<l<1
. In practice, such an initialization can be produced by the two-phase procedure proposed by
Jin & Wang 2025
for weakly convex minimization with nonconvex constraints, where the first phase yields an approximately feasible point that warm-starts the second.
The main complexity result is presented below, with detailed analysis deferred to Appendix
C.4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
hold. Let
\{\bm{w}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence generated by MoSSP-P
with parameters satisfying

\displaystyle\rho_{k}\equiv\rho={\rho_{0}}K^{1/4},\quad\mu_{k}\equiv\mu=\frac{\mu_{0}}{K^{1/2}\max\{L_{f},\tilde{L}\}},

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha=\frac{\alpha_{0}\mu_{0}}{K^{1/2}},\quad 0<\beta\leq 1,

(3.12)

where
\rho_{0},\mu_{0},\alpha_{0}>0
are constants independent of
K
. If
\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/4})
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\max\bigl\{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{R+1}\|^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{R+1}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}^{R})\|^{2}]\bigr\}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\|^{2}\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2}).

Consequently, given
\varepsilon>0
, the oracle complexity of MoSSP-P to obtain a stochastic
\varepsilon
-KKT point
\bm{x}^{R+1}
satisfying (
2.6
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
. In the absence of
2.3
, we have
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\,\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\|^{2}\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2})
, and the oracle complexity of MoSSP-P to obtain a stochastic
\varepsilon
-stationary point
\bm{x}^{R+1}
satisfying (
2.7
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
.

The result in
Theorem

3.1
matches that of unconstrained stochastic first-order methods (SFOMs) for Problem (
1
) with
\bm{c}=\bm{0}
and
g=0

(
Ghadimi & Lan 2013
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
, as well as SFOMs for the unconstrained stochastic DC case
(
Chayti & Jaggi 2025
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
.

Remark 3.1
.

Existing analyses of penalty methods, including ALM analyses for nonconvex constrained problems, require penalty parameters to grow inversely with
\varepsilon
to attain the best-known complexity. This worsens the problem conditioning, as the smoothness constant grows as
L_{\rho}=\Theta(\rho)
. Without momentum, one must compensate with a smaller stepsize or a larger batch size, potentially yielding complexity no better than
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-5})

(
Jin & Wang 2022
)
; in particular, a naive extension of unconstrained stochastic DC methods (e.g.,
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
) to the penalty framework for Problem (
1
) risks such degradation. Under our penalty framework, momentum yields a genuine complexity
improvement by allowing the penalty, stepsize, and momentum parameters to be jointly calibrated under
\mathcal{O}(1)
batch size, thereby achieving the rate stated above at no additional cost from the nonconvex constraints.

The oracle complexity of MoSSP-P without an approximately feasible initialization is presented as follows.

Corollary 3.1
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.1
with parameters set as
\rho=\mathcal{O}(K^{1/5})
,
\mu=\mathcal{O}(K^{-2/5})
,
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(K^{-2/5})
, and
0<\beta\leq 1
, the oracle complexity of MoSSP-P
to obtain a stochastic
\varepsilon
-KKT point or a stochastic
\varepsilon
-stationary point (without
2.3
)
\bm{x}^{R+1}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-5})
.

Remark 3.2
.

Corollary

3.1
highlights the role of approximate feasible initialization in improving the complexity order. Since the schedule
\rho=\mathcal{O}(K^{l})
is required to control
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}\bigr]
, it inflates
\tfrac{\rho}{2}\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}
to
\mathcal{O}(K^{l})
when
\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, dominating the bound in (
3.11
). Such initialization confines this term to
\mathcal{O}(1)
, thereby improving the complexity order. In fact, our analysis can also be extended to an iteration-indexed penalty update
(
Alacaoglu & Wright 2024
)
without this requirement.

3.3
MoSSP-R: MoSSP with Recursive Momentum

Alternatively, we incorporate another variance reduction technique, recursive momentum (STORM)
(
Cutkosky & Orabona 2019
)
, into the
\bm{x}
-update. STORM introduces a correction term based on a single sample to generate a variance-reduced gradient estimate, making it suitable for large-scale applications.

At iteration
k
, we construct a recursive momentum-based stochastic estimator for
\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})
as:

\displaystyle\bm{D}^{k}=\bm{d}^{k}+\rho_{k}\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k}),

(3.14)

where
\bm{d}^{k}
is updated recursively by

\bm{d}^{k}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{0}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{0}}\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{0},\xi_{j}^{0}),&k=0,\\[3.0pt]
\begin{aligned} \nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k},\xi^{k})&+(1-\alpha_{k-1})\\
&\quad\left(\bm{d}^{k-1}-\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k-1},\xi^{k})\right),\end{aligned}&k\geq 1.\end{cases}

(3.15)

We begin at
k=0
by independently drawing a sample set
\{\xi_{j}^{0}\}_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{0}}
from the distribution
\Xi
, where the initial batch size is
|\mathcal{B}_{0}|=b_{0}
. For any
k\geq 1
, a single i.i.d. sample
\xi^{k}
is drawn from
\Xi
. We then update
\bm{x}^{k+1}
through

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{k+1}=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}h}\bigl(\bm{z}^{k}-\mu_{k}\bm{D}^{k}\bigr).

(3.16)

MoSSP-R

.

Oracle Complexity of MoSSP-R.
We now present complexity results for MoSSP-R for finding an approximate stationary point and an approximate KKT point, respectively, with detailed analysis deferred to Appendix
C.5
. In MoSSP-R, we construct
\bm{u}^{k+1}
as:

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{k+1}

\displaystyle={\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\bm{D}^{k}}+\mu_{k}^{-1}(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})-\bm{x}^{k+1}).

(3.17)

Before proceeding, we introduce an additional standard assumption, also known as the mean-squared smoothness assumption, widely used in variance reduction methods
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Fang et al. 2018
;
Cutkosky & Orabona 2019
;
Xu & Xu 2023
)
.

Assumption 3.1
.

For almost every
\xi\in\Xi
,
\mathbf{f}(\cdot,\xi)
is differentiable and
for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x};\xi)-\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{y};\xi)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq L_{f}^{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}.

By Jensen’s inequality, Assumption
3.1
implies that

f=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\mathbf{f}(\cdot;\xi)]
is
L_{f}
-smooth, whereas the converse does not hold in general.

As noted by
Xu & Xu 2023
, employing a moderately large initial batch size in variance reduction can improve the complexity order at a negligible cost. Consequently, we
establish the oracle complexity with an initial batch size
b_{0}=\mathcal{O}(K^{1/3})
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
and
3.1
hold. Let
\{\bm{w}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence generated by MoSSP-R with parameters satisfying

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\rho={\rho_{0}}K^{1/3},\quad\mu_{k}\equiv\mu=\frac{\mu_{0}}{K^{1/3}\max\{L_{f},\tilde{L}\}},

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\alpha=\frac{16\alpha_{0}\mu_{0}^{2}}{K^{2/3}},\quad 0<\beta\leq 1,

(3.18)

where
\rho_{0},\mu_{0},\alpha_{0}>0
are constants independent of
K
. If
\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/3})
with the initial batch size
b_{0}=\mathcal{O}(K^{1/3})
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\max\bigl\{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{R+1}\|^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{R+1}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}^{R})\|^{2}]\bigr\}

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(K^{-2/3}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\|^{2}\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(K^{-2/3}).

Consequently, given
\varepsilon>0
, the oracle complexity of MoSSP-R to obtain a stochastic
\varepsilon
-KKT point
\bm{x}^{R+1}
satisfying (
2.6
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
. In the absence of
2.3
, we have
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\,\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\|^{2}\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(K^{-2/3})
, and the oracle complexity of MoSSP-R to obtain a stochastic
\varepsilon
-stationary point
\bm{x}^{R+1}
satisfying (
2.7
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
.

The result in
Theorem

3.2
matches the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
lower bound for unconstrained SFOMs with variance reduction
(
Arjevani et al. 2023
)
and the best-known rate for nonconvex constrained stochastic optimization
(
Shi et al. 2026
)
under
3.1
, where the DC structure is absent. Crucially, it demonstrates that our single-loop penalty framework and analysis can effectively handle the DC structure without increasing the complexity order with respect to
\varepsilon
.

Under the assumptions of

-KKT point or a stochastic

-stationary point (without

)

is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})

5
Conclusion and Discussion

This paper proposes
MoSSP
, a simple single-loop stochastic penalty framework for general nonconvex constrained DC-regularized optimization. We develop two momentum-based variants, MoSSP-P and MoSSP-R, and establish their oracle complexity orders for finding two types of stochastic approximate solutions for Problem (
1
). Our analysis shows that exploiting the DC structure does not worsen the complexity order, with or without the constraint qualification. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic complexity study of single-loop stochastic methods for nonconvex, functionally constrained optimization involving DC structure.

Although we focus on equality constraints in Problem (
1
), the framework extends directly to inequality constraints:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle F(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})-g(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{x})=\bm{0},\ \ \bm{c}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{x})\leq\bm{0},

where
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{E}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
and
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{I}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
are smooth mappings, and
f
,
h
,
g
are as before. The quadratic penalty (
3.1
) becomes

Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})+\tfrac{\rho}{2}\bigl(\|\bm{c}_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{x})\|^{2}+\|[\bm{c}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{x})]_{+}\|^{2}\bigr),

where
[\,\cdot\,]_{+}\coloneqq\max\{\cdot,0\}
is applied componentwise. Correspondingly, the gradient estimators in (
3.6
) and (
3.14
) are updated by replacing
\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k})
with
\bm{J}(\bm{x}^{k})\coloneqq\nabla\bm{c}_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{x}^{k})\bm{c}_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{x}^{k})+\nabla\bm{c}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{x}^{k})[\bm{c}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{x}^{k})]_{+}
. This replacement preserves the smoothness of
Q_{\rho}
, and the error-bound condition in

2.3
can be replaced by

\left\|\bm{J}(\bm{x}^{k})\right\|\geq\delta\left(\|\bm{c}_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}+\|[\bm{c}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{x}^{k})]_{+}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2},\forall k\geq 0.

A natural direction is to extend our analysis framework to general DC optimization under nonlinear constraints, where the concave component admits the form
-\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\!\left[g(\bm{x};\xi)\right]
and
\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\cdot)
is no longer available in closed form, as in problems such as PU learning and partial AUC maximization.

A.1
Constrained DC and DC-Regularized Optimization

DC structures in learning problems commonly arise from two different sources. First, they may appear as an algebraic consequence of max-structured or minimax-type risk formulations, such as positive-unlabeled learning
(
Kiryo et al. 2017
)
, partial AUC optimization
(
Yao et al. 2022
)
, and related adversarial formulations, where the objective can often be rewritten or approximated as a DC function. Second, DC structure is deliberately introduced through sparsity-promoting nonconvex regularizers, such as capped-
\ell_{1}
, SCAD, MCP, or
\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}
, which approximate
\ell_{0}
-type sparsity while retaining a tractable convex-concave decomposition; see, e.g.,
(
Gong et al. 2013
, Table 1)
.

In the deterministic setting, several methods have been developed for constrained DC and DC-regularized optimization problems.
Zhou et al. 2024
proposed a proximal ADMM for structured DC programs whose objective is the difference of two possibly nonsmooth convex functions and whose constraints are given by a linear mapping into a closed convex set. Under the Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz property, they established convergence of the generated sequence to a critical point.
Lu et al. 2022
developed penalty and augmented-Lagrangian methods for structured nonsmooth constrained DC programs with DC inequality constraints. Under a pointwise Slater condition, they showed that any feasible accumulation point generated by the penalty method is B-stationary for the original problem; the augmented-Lagrangian variant further yields KKT-type optimality conditions together with accumulation points of auxiliary multiplier sequences.

Several related deterministic schemes, including augmented-Lagrangian, sequential-convexification, and moving-balls-approximation (MBA) type methods, have also been studied for inequality-constrained DC or DC-regularized problems
(
Kanzow & Neder 2026
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Yu et al. 2021
)
. Specifically,
Kanzow & Neder 2026
proposed a safeguarded augmented Lagrangian method for DC optimization with linear equality constraints and convex inequality constraints. Under a modified Slater constraint qualification, they established subsequential convergence to generalized KKT points. Nevertheless, their theoretical analysis relies on the convexity of the constraints, making its extension to nonconvex feasible sets such as Problem (
1
) challenging.
Yu et al. 2021
studied a line-search variant of MBA-type algorithms for DC-regularized problems with smooth inequality constraints. Under the MFCQ, they established convergence to stationary points when the constructed potential function satisfies the KL property. More recently,
Liu et al. 2025
developed an inexact MBA method for DC-regularized optimization coupled with differentiable inequality constraints whose gradients are locally Lipschitz continuous. Under the MSCQ, the partial bounded multiplier property, boundedness of the iterates, and the KL property of the constructed potential function, they achieved full sequence convergence to strong stationary points. They also established an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
for finding an
\varepsilon
-KKT point under the MFCQ and smoothness assumptions on the constraints. However, the MBA-type methods above are designed for inequality-constrained problems and rely on feasibility/interiority-type assumptions. Hence, they do not directly apply to equality-constrained feasible sets, such as Problem (
1
).

A.2
Stochastic Nonconvex Constrained Optimization

For stochastic optimization with nonconvex constraints, such as Problem (
1
) with
g=0
, stochastic penalty methods, including augmented Lagrangian (AL) approaches, have been extensively studied
(
Kushner & Sanvicente 1974
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Xu 2020
)
. Stochastic sequential quadratic programming (SQP) methods represent another prominent class of algorithms for addressing equality-constrained stochastic problems. Recent advances in stochastic SQP encompass convergence guarantees
(
Berahas et al. 2021
;
Na et al. 2023b
)
, analysis in expectation
(
Na et al. 2023a
)
, almost-sure convergence
(
Curtis et al. 2025
)
, and worst-case complexity bounds
(
Berahas et al. 2022
;
Na & Mahoney 2025
;
Curtis et al. 2024
)
. Notably, these methods differ in their computational structure: penalty methods require updating the penalty parameter in the outer loop and (approximately) minimizing the penalized subproblem in the inner loop, while SQP methods compute search directions by solving a sequence of quadratic programming subproblems.

Alternatively, single-loop penalty methods have gained traction for such problems, and many studies have focused on different settings of Problem (
1
). For Problem (
1
) with
h-g=0
,
Alacaoglu & Wright 2024
proposed a linearized quadratic penalty approach incorporating recursive momentum
(
Cutkosky & Orabona 2019
)
, achieving a sample complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-4})
via adaptive penalty parameters. To identify a stronger
\varepsilon
-stationary point with constraint violation at most
\varepsilon
,
Lu et al. 2026
introduced a linearized quadratic penalty method with truncated momentum. Under a regularity condition on constraint gradients, they achieved a nearly optimal complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-3})
using recursive momentum, whereas Polyak momentum yields
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-4})
with diminishing step sizes. Recently,
Zuo et al. 2025
studied an adaptive single-loop stochastic penalty method for solving nonconvex optimization with equality and inequality constraints, proposing an adaptive penalty parameter update scheme and establishing an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
oracle complexity bound under a local CQ condition. For Problem (
1
) with
g=0
,
Jin & Wang 2022
proposed a single-loop stochastic primal-dual method based on the linearized AL function for nonconvex problems with numerous functional constraints. To find an
\varepsilon
-KKT point, their approach achieves an oracle complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-6})
starting from an arbitrary point, and
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-5})
with a feasible initial point. Subsequently, under certain constraint qualifications,
Shi et al. 2026
developed a linearized AL method, establishing an oracle complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
(or a nearly optimal
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
with a nearly feasible initial point).

Within the exact penalty framework,
Yang et al. 2026
proposed a single-loop stochastic algorithm based on a hinge penalty method for Problem (
1
) with weakly convex objective and constraint functions, achieving an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-6})
oracle complexity for finding a near-
\varepsilon
-KKT point under a regularity condition. By combining the SPIDER-type variance reduction technique
(
Fang et al. 2018
)
,
Liu & Xu 2025
presented a single-loop stochastic subgradient method for nonconvex nonsmooth stochastic optimization with weak inequality constraints in expectation. Under a (uniform) Slater-type constraint qualification, the method achieves an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
sample complexity for evaluations of both the objective and constraint function subgradients, and
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-6})
for evaluations of the constraint function values to produce an
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
-KKT point. For problems with deterministic equality constraints,
Cui et al. 2025
proposed a method that achieves an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
sample complexity to find an
\varepsilon
-KKT point.

A.3
Unconstrained Stochastic DC Optimization

For the unconstrained case of Problem (
1
), while deterministic DC algorithms have been extensively studied
(
Le Thi & Pham Dinh 2018
)
, their stochastic counterparts (SDCA) with non-asymptotic convergence analysis remain relatively less explored, appearing only in a few works such as
(
Nitanda & Suzuki 2017
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
. However, these analyses require smoothness
(
Nitanda & Suzuki 2017
)
or Hölder continuity assumptions on the gradients of DC components
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
, which may be restrictive for many nonsmooth functions, such as the
\ell_{1-2}
norm. The Moreau envelope provides a powerful smoothing technique for handling nonsmoothness via the proximal operator and has been widely used in stochastic weakly convex optimization
(
Davis & Drusvyatskiy 2019
)
. Subsequently,
Sun & Sun 2023
applied Moreau envelope smoothing to both DC components, enabling non-asymptotic convergence analysis for unconstrained DC problems under a relaxed criticality criterion.
Hu et al. 2024
further proposed a single-loop stochastic method for nonsmooth difference-of-weakly-convex problems with an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
oracle complexity; however, their setting is unconstrained and their DWC analysis requires uniformly bounded second moments of stochastic subgradients for both components (see Assump. 4.6(iii) in
Hu et al. 2024
). More recently,
Chayti & Jaggi 2025
introduced momentum-based variance reduction for stochastic DC optimization, but their single-loop analysis assumes a smooth concave component and smooths only the convex component, which differs from our setting where the concave component in DC structure can be nonsmooth and the problem is further subject to nonconvex constraints.

Appendix B
Preliminaries

For the convergence analysis, we first introduce several definitions and preliminary results for nonsmooth DC optimization and Moreau-envelope smoothing. We use the following definitions of general subgradient and subdifferential
(
Davis & Drusvyatskiy 2019
;
Rockafellar & Wets 2009
)
.

Definition B.1
.

Consider a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and a point
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
f(\bm{x})
being finite. A vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a general subgradient of
f
at
\bm{x}
if

\displaystyle f(\bm{y})\geq f(\bm{x})+\langle\bm{v},\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle+o(\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\|)\text{ as }\bm{y}\to\bm{x}.

The subdifferential
\partial f(\bm{x})
is the set of subgradients of
f
at
\bm{x}
. For a continuously differentiable function
f
,
\partial f(\bm{x})=\{\nabla f(\bm{x})\}
; for convex functions, this coincides with the convex subdifferential. For notational simplicity, we abuse the notation
\partial f(\bm{x})
to denote an arbitrary subgradient from the corresponding subdifferential when the context is clear.

A mapping
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
is said to be
C
-Lipschitz continuous if
\|\mathcal{M}(\bm{x})-\mathcal{M}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\|\leq C\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|
for all
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
. A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
L_{f}
-smooth if it is continuously differentiable and has an
L_{f}
-Lipschitz continuous gradient, that is
\|\nabla f(\bm{x})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\|\leq L_{f}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|
for all
\bm{x},\,\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It then satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})\leq f(\bm{x}^{\prime})+\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|^{2},\quad\forall\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is
m_{\phi}
-weakly convex if
\phi(\bm{x})+\frac{m_{\phi}}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}
is convex.

Regarding the definition of the

of

Consider the unconstrained DC optimization problem:

(B.2)

\bar{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(

)

\displaystyle\bm{0}\in\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})-\partial g(\bar{\bm{x}}),\text{ or equivalently, }\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})\cap\partial g(\bar{\bm{x}})\neq\emptyset.

(

\bar{\bm{x}}
for a given

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\left(\partial g(\bar{\bm{x}}),\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})\right)\leq\varepsilon,

or equivalently, there exist
\bar{\bm{u}}_{1}\in\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})
and
\bar{\bm{u}}_{2}\in\partial g(\bar{\bm{x}})
such that
\|\bar{\bm{u}}_{1}-\bar{\bm{u}}_{2}\|\leq\varepsilon
. When
g
is non-differentiable, however, finding such a point remains challenging
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
. Instead,
Xu et al. 2019
focuses on seeking a
nearly
\varepsilon
-stationary point
\bar{\bm{x}}
; that is, there exists
\bm{x}^{*}
such that
\|\bar{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}^{*}\|\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathrm{dist}(\partial g(\bm{x}^{*}),\partial\phi(\bm{x}^{*}))\leq\varepsilon
.

\bar{\bm{y}}_{1}=\bar{\bm{x}}

B.3
Difference-of-Moreau-Envelopes
(DME) Smoothing

The DME smoothing approximation of
\Psi
in Problem (
B.2
) for any
\mu\in(0,1/m_{\phi})
is defined as

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mu}(\bm{z}):=\mathcal{M}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z})-\mathcal{M}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}).

(B.6)

The smoothness properties and approximation bounds of
\Psi_{\mu}
are summarized below
(
Hiriart-Urruty 1991
)
.

Proposition B.1
.

Consider Problem (
B.2
) and (
B.6
). For any
0<\mu<\frac{1}{m_{\phi}}
, the following hold.

(i)

\Psi_{\mu}
is continuously differentiable with gradient
\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bm{z})=\mu^{-1}(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z})-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z}))
.

(ii)

\nabla\Psi_{\mu}
is
(\frac{2-\mu m_{\phi}}{\mu-\mu^{2}m_{\phi}})
-Lipschitz continuous.

(iii)

For any
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that

\Psi(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bm{z}))\leq\Psi_{\mu}(\bm{z})\leq\Psi(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z})).

B.4
Solution Correspondence between Original Problem and its DME Surrogate

For completeness, we recall the theoretical relationship between the solutions of Problem (
B.2
) and its DME surrogate (
B.6
), as established in
Hiriart-Urruty 1991
and
Sun & Sun 2023
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{\Psi}:=\argmin_{\bm{x}}\Psi(\bm{x})
and
\hat{\Psi}:=\min_{\bm{x}}\Psi(\bm{x})
denote its solution set and optimal value, respectively. For the DME surrogate, we similarly define its optimal value as
\hat{\Psi}_{\mu}:=\inf_{\bm{z}}\Psi_{\mu}(\bm{z})
and its solution set as
\mathcal{X}_{\Psi_{\mu}}:=\argmin_{\bm{z}}\Psi_{\mu}(\bm{z})
.

Proposition B.2

(Stationary Point and Global Minimizer Correspondence
(
Hiriart-Urruty 1991
)
)
.

Consider Problem (
B.2
) and its DME surrogate (
B.6
). Suppose that
\mathcal{X}_{\Psi}\neq\emptyset
. For any
\mu\in(0,1/m_{\phi})
, the following statements hold:

(i) Stationary point correspondence.
If
\bar{\bm{z}}
is a stationary point of
\Psi_{\mu}
(i.e.,
\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})=\bm{0}
), then
\bar{\bm{x}}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bar{\bm{z}})
is a critical point of
\Psi
, satisfying

\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bar{\bm{z}})=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bar{\bm{z}}),\quad\Psi(\bar{\bm{x}})=\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}}).

(B.7)

Conversely, if
\bar{\bm{x}}
is a critical point of
\Psi
and
\bar{\bm{\xi}}\in\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})\cap\partial g(\bar{\bm{x}})
, then
\bar{\bm{z}}:=\bar{\bm{x}}+\mu\bar{\bm{\xi}}
is a stationary point of
\Psi_{\mu}
with
\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bar{\bm{z}})=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bar{\bm{z}})=\bar{\bm{x}}
and
\Psi(\bar{\bm{x}})=\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})
.

(ii) Global minimizer correspondence.
The set
\mathcal{X}_{\Psi_{\mu}}
is nonempty and
\hat{\Psi}=\hat{\Psi}_{\mu}
. Furthermore, if
\bar{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Psi_{\mu}}
, then
\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bar{\bm{z}})\in\mathcal{X}_{\Psi}
. Conversely, if
\bar{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Psi}
and
\bar{\bm{\xi}}\in\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})\cap\partial g(\bar{\bm{x}})
, then
\bar{\bm{z}}:=\bar{\bm{x}}+\mu\bar{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Psi_{\mu}}
.

B.5
Proof of
Proposition

2.1

Proof.

Let
\bar{\bm{z}}
be a point satisfying

\|\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})\|\leq\min\{1,\mu^{-1}\}\varepsilon
and set

\bar{\bm{x}}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu\phi}(\bar{\bm{z}}),\,\bar{\bm{y}}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bar{\bm{z}}).

Using (
2.3
), we have

\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{\phi}:=\mu^{-1}(\bar{\bm{z}}-\bar{\bm{x}})\in\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})
and
\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{g}:=\mu^{-1}(\bar{\bm{z}}-\bar{\bm{y}})\in\partial g(\bar{\bm{y}}).

Combining these with (
2.3
) yields

\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{\phi}-\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{g}=\mu^{-1}(\bar{\bm{y}}-\bar{\bm{x}})=\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}}).

Setting
\bar{\bm{u}}:=\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{\phi}-\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{g}\in\partial\phi(\bar{\bm{x}})-\partial g(\bar{\bm{y}})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\max\bigl\{\|\bar{\bm{u}}\|,\|\bar{\bm{x}}-\bar{\bm{y}}\|\bigr\}=\max\bigl\{\|\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})\|,\mu\|\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})\|\bigr\}=\max\{1,\mu\}\,\|\nabla\Psi_{\mu}(\bar{\bm{z}})\|\leq\varepsilon,

which implies that
\bar{\bm{x}}
is an
\varepsilon
-critical point of
\Psi
in the sense of Definition
B.2
.
∎

B.6
Discussion on
Definition

2.1

There are connections between
\varepsilon
-KKT points and
\varepsilon
-stationary points, but the two notions are not equivalent in general.
For a given point
\bm{x}
, if the constraint violation
\|\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|
is small, then the infeasible-stationarity measure

\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x})\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|
is also small. However, the reverse implication does not generally hold. In practical computations, an iterate may get trapped at a stationary point of the constraint-violation minimization problem
\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|^{2}
, satisfying
\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{*})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{*})=0
while
\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{*})\neq 0
, which is referred to as an
infeasible stationary point. Therefore, to guarantee that small infeasible stationarity implies small constraint violation and hence approximate feasibility,
a constraint qualification condition such as
2.3
is required. Under such a condition, an
\varepsilon
-stationary point further implies an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
-KKT point in expectation.

Appendix C
Proofs of Complexity Analysis

C.1
Proof Sketch

We provide a proof sketch of
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
. We outline how the DME-based residual generated by MoSSP-P and MoSSP-R yields the stochastic approximate solutions in
Definition

2.1
. The proof proceeds in four steps.

Step 1: From the algorithmic residual to the stochastic KKT inclusion.

Let us first set
\bm{y}^{k}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})
. The purpose of this step is to identify the random variables required in Definition
2.1
. For both MoSSP-P and MoSSP-R, the optimality condition of the
\bm{x}
-update implies, as shown in Appendix
C.3
, that

\bm{u}^{k+1}\in\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})+\partial h(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\partial g(\bm{y}^{k}).

Equivalently, by setting
\bm{\lambda}^{k+1}:=\rho_{k}\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})
, we obtain

\bm{u}^{k+1}\in\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})+\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{\lambda}^{k+1}+\partial h(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\partial g(\bm{y}^{k}).

Therefore, for the randomly chosen index
R
, the tuple

\bar{\bm{x}}:=\bm{x}^{R+1},\qquad\bar{\bm{y}}:=\bm{y}^{R},\qquad\bar{\bm{\lambda}}:=\rho_{R}\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1}),\qquad\bar{\bm{u}}:=\bm{u}^{R+1},

satisfies the KKT inclusion (
2.5
) almost surely. Hence, it remains to bound

\|\bm{u}^{R+1}\|,\qquad\|\bm{x}^{R+1}-\bm{y}^{R}\|,\qquad\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\|,

and, under
2.3
, the feasibility violation
\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{R+1})\|
.

Step 2: Establishing descent of the DC-structured potential function.

The concave term
-g
prevents the standard penalty objective from admitting a sufficient descent property. We therefore introduce the potential function

\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w})=Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}),\qquad\bm{w}=(\bm{x},\bm{z}),

which encodes the two proximal updates of the proposed algorithms. Under the mild parameter condition
\mu_{k}L_{\rho_{k}}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
,
Lemma

C.2
yields

\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k+1},\mu_{k+1}}(\bm{w}^{k+1})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})-\Omega(\mu_{k}^{-1})\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}+\Delta_{k+1}+\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mu_{k}\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}.

Here
\bm{e}^{k}
is the stochastic gradient estimation error, and
\Delta_{k+1}
accounts for the change of the smoothing parameter
\mu_{k}
. Thus, after summing over
k
, the iterate variation

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mu_{k}^{-1}\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}

is controlled by the initial potential difference, the parameter variation terms, and the cumulative stochastic error. In the constant-parameter setting used in the main theorems, the variation terms
\Delta_{k+1}
and
\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}
vanish, leaving only the initial potential difference and the stochastic error. This iterate variation bound is then used to control the criticality measure in the next steps.

Step 3: From iterate variation to residual bounds.

The one-step residual estimate in
Lemma

C.3
shows that there exists a constant
\kappa_{1}>0
independent of
K
such that

\max\{\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2},\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{y}^{k}\|^{2}\}\leq\kappa_{1}\left(\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}+\bigl(L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{-2}\bigr)\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}\right).

Thus the descent estimate from Step 2 reduces the averaged DME-induced criticality residual

\max\{\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2},\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{y}^{k}\|^{2}\}

to the iterate-variation term and the cumulative stochastic error.

The infeasible stationarity measure is then controlled via
Lemma

C.4
, which gives, for some constant
\kappa_{2}>0
independent of
K
, with
\underline{\rho}_{K}:=\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\rho_{k}
,

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\underline{\rho}_{K}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}+1\right).

This estimate makes explicit the role of the penalty parameter: a larger
\rho_{k}
improves feasibility control through the factor
\underline{\rho}_{K}^{-2}
, but it simultaneously increases the smoothness constant
L_{\rho_{k}}
in the criticality bound above. Thus, the penalty, smoothing, and momentum parameters must be carefully balanced. Under
2.3
, the infeasible stationarity bound further implies feasibility.

Step 4: Stochastic error control and parameter selection.

The two algorithmic variants differ mainly in how the cumulative stochastic error identified in Step 3 is controlled.

(i) For MoSSP-P, the Polyak error recursion contains an
\alpha_{k}^{-1}
-weighted variation term (
\alpha_{k}^{-1}L_{f}^{2}\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}
in (
C.32
)), which necessitates the parameter coupling
\alpha=\Theta(\mu)
. Consequently, the dominant criticality bound takes the form

\mathcal{R}_{P}:=\max\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{R+1}\|^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{R+1}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}^{R})\|^{2}]\Bigr\}\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu K}+\mu.

Balancing the two terms gives
\mu=\Theta(K^{-1/2}),\ \alpha=\Theta(K^{-1/2})
, and hence
\mathcal{R}_{P}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2})
. With
\rho=\Theta(K^{1/4}),\ \|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/4})
, the feasibility residual is also of order
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2})
under
2.3
. Thus
K=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
suffices, yielding the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
oracle complexity of MoSSP-P.

(ii) For MoSSP-R, the recursive momentum estimator does not introduce the
\alpha_{k}^{-1}
-weighted variation term (see (
C.44
)). This allows for the weaker coupling
\alpha=\Theta(\mu^{2})
, and the dominant criticality bound becomes

\mathcal{R}_{R}:=\max\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{R+1}\|^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}^{R+1}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}^{R})\|^{2}]\Bigr\}\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu K}+\mu^{2}.

Balancing the two terms gives
\mu=\Theta(K^{-1/3}),\ \alpha=\Theta(K^{-2/3})
. Together with the initial batch size
b_{0}=\Theta(K^{1/3})
, which controls the initial estimation error, we obtain
\mathcal{R}_{R}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-2/3})
. With
\rho=\Theta(K^{1/3})\mbox{ and }\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{0})\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/3})
, the feasibility residual is also of order
\mathcal{O}(K^{-2/3})
under
2.3
. Hence the setting
K=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
ensures an
\varepsilon
-approximate solution. Since
b_{0}=\mathcal{O}(K^{1/3})
is dominated by the total number of per-iteration oracle calls, MoSSP-R achieves
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
oracle complexity.

C.2
Constructed Potential Function

We define the potential function

\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w}):=Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{\mu g}(\bm{z}),

(C.1)

where
\bm{w}=(\bm{x},\bm{z})

is the finite infimum introduced in Problem (
1

\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})-g(\bm{x})\right\}\geq\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(\bm{x})=F^{*}>-\infty,\quad\forall\,\rho>0.

(C.2)

Unlike the convex case (where
g=0
), the concave component
-g
prevents the potential sequence
\{\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
from being trivially lower-bounded. However, since

is convex and
\sup_{\bm{v}_{g}\in\partial g(\bm{x})}\|\bm{v}_{g}\|\leq G
for all
\bm{x}
(
2.1
),

is

-Lipschitz continuous, and we can bound the potential function for any

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w})

\displaystyle\geq Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}-g(\bm{z})

\displaystyle\geq Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})-g(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|^{2}-G\|\bm{x}-\bm{z}\|

\displaystyle\geq Q_{\rho}(\bm{x})+h(\bm{x})-g(\bm{x})-\frac{G^{2}\mu}{2}

\displaystyle\geq F^{*}-\frac{G^{2}\mu}{2},

(C.3)

where the first inequality follows from

in (
B.1
), the second utilizes the Lipschitz continuity of

, and the third applies Young’s inequality. Under (

), the sequence

is non-increasing, hence there exists a constant

independent of

for all

\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})\geq F^{*}-\frac{G^{2}\bar{\mu}}{2}=:\mathcal{L}^{*},\quad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N},

(C.4)

which we use throughout the proofs.

\min\{\frac{(2\mu_{k})^{-1}-L_{\rho_{k}}}{2},\,\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}(\beta^{-1}-\tfrac{1}{2})\}=\frac{(2\mu_{k})^{-1}-L_{\rho_{k}}}{2}
. Then, substituting (
C.11
) and (
C.17
) into
(
C.10
) yields (
C.8
). Since the coefficient of
\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}
in (
C.8
) is positive, rearranging this inequality gives (
C.9
), completing the proof.
∎

We next derive the one-step bound on
\bm{u}
and the associated criticality measure during iteration.

Lemma C.3
.

Suppose that
2.1
holds.
Then, for any

k\geq 0
, with
\bm{u}^{k+1}
defined by (
3.9
) or (
3.17
), it holds
that

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq 3\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}+3\bigl(L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+(\mu_{k}\beta)^{-2}\bigr)\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2},

(C.18)

and

\displaystyle\max\{\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2},\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})\|^{2}\}\leq 3\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}+\bigl(3\left(L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+(\mu_{k}\beta)^{-2}\right)+\beta^{-2}\bigr)\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}.

(C.19)

Proof.

Recalling (
C.14
) and substituting it into (
3.9
) or (
3.17
), one obtains

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{k+1}=-\bm{e}^{k}+\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})+\frac{1}{\mu_{k}\beta}(\bm{z}^{k}-\bm{z}^{k+1}).

Consequently, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}+3\|\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}+3(\mu_{k}\beta)^{-2}\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}+3L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\bm{x}^{k}\|^{2}+3(\mu_{k}\beta)^{-2}\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}+3\left(L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+(\mu_{k}\beta)^{-2}\right)\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2},

(C.20)

where the first inequality follows from

\|\bm{a}+\bm{b}+\bm{c}\|^{2}\leq 3(\|\bm{a}\|^{2}+\|\bm{b}\|^{2}+\|\bm{c}\|^{2})
and the second inequality uses the
L_{\rho_{k}}
-smoothness of
Q_{\rho_{k}}
, establishing (
C.18
).

For the left-hand side of (
C.19
), one has, by (
C.14
),

\displaystyle\max\{\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2},\|\bm{x}^{k+1}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})\|^{2}\}\leq\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\beta^{-2}\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|^{2}.

Combining this with (
C.20
) gives (
C.19
).
∎

In general, finding a feasible solution for nonconvex constrained optimization problems is challenging. It is thus necessary to characterize infeasible stationarity measures
\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x})\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|^{2}
. Under a constraint qualification such as
2.3
, we can further bound constraint violations
\|\bm{c}(\bm{x})\|^{2}
. The following lemma establishes key bounds relating
\bm{u}
to these two measures.

Lemma C.4
.

Suppose
that the conditions of Lemma
C.2

hold. Let
\underline{\rho}_{K}:=\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\rho_{k}
. Then, for any
K\geq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\underline{\rho}_{K}^{2}K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{18G^{2}}{\underline{\rho}_{K}^{2}}.

(C.21)

Moreover, under
2.3
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\underline{\rho}_{K}^{2}\delta^{2}K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{18G^{2}}{\delta^{2}\underline{\rho}_{K}^{2}}.

(C.22)

Proof.

By (
C.6
), there exist
\bm{v}_{h}^{k+1}\in\partial h(\bm{x}^{k+1})
and
\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})\in\partial g(\mathrm{prox}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k}))
such that

\bm{u}^{k+1}=\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})+\bm{v}_{h}^{k+1}-\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k}).

This yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\|\rho_{k}\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})+\rho_{k}\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\|\nabla Q_{\rho_{k}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})+\bm{v}_{h}^{k+1}-\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})-\bm{v}_{h}^{k+1}+\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\bigl(\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|+\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|+\|\bm{v}_{h}^{k+1}\|+\|\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})\|\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|}{\rho_{k}}+\frac{3G}{\rho_{k}},

(C.23)

where the last inequality uses
\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|
,
\|\bm{v}_{h}^{k+1}\|
,
\|\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\mu_{k}g}(\bm{z}^{k})\|\leq G
from
2.1
.
Squaring both sides of (
C.23
)
gives

\displaystyle\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}}{\rho_{k}^{2}}+\frac{18G^{2}}{\rho_{k}^{2}}.

(C.24)

Summing (
C.24
) over
k=0,\dots,K-1
and
dividing by
K
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}}{\rho_{k}^{2}}+\frac{18G^{2}}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{1}{\rho_{k}^{2}},

where using
\rho_{k}\geq\underline{\rho}_{K}
for
0\leq k\leq K-1

establishes (
C.21
).

Under
2.3
, it is straightforward to obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{2}K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\bm{c}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2},

where substituting (
C.21
)
into the above inequality yields (
C.22
),
completing the proof.
∎

The following lemma establishes an expected averaged bound on
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}]
.

Lemma C.5
.

Suppose that
the conditions of Lemma
C.2

hold. Then, for
K\geq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}]\leq{}

\displaystyle\,\frac{24(\tfrac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2})}{\mu_{K}K}(\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{0},\mu_{0}}(\bm{w}^{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*})

\displaystyle+\frac{3+24(\tfrac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2})}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle+\frac{24(\tfrac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2})}{\mu_{K}K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]\right),

(C.25)

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is as defined in (
C.4
).

Proof.

Taking expectations in (
C.18
) and averaging over
k=0,\dots,K-1
, one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{u}^{k+1}\|^{2}]\leq\frac{3}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+(\mu_{k}\beta)^{-2})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]+\frac{3}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}].

(C.26)

Next, substituting (
C.9
) into the first term of (
C.26
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{3}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+(\mu_{k}\beta)^{-2})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\,\frac{12}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+\beta^{-2}}{\mu_{k}(1-2\mu_{k}L_{\rho_{k}})}\biggl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k+1},\mu_{k+1}}(\bm{w}^{k+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]+\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]\biggr).

(C.27)

Given
\mu_{k}\geq\mu_{K}
and
\mu_{k}L_{\rho_{k}}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
from (
C.7
), it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+\beta^{-2}}{\mu_{k}(1-2\mu_{k}L_{\rho_{k}})}\leq\frac{2}{\mu_{K}}(\frac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2}),

(C.28)

where we use the inequality
\frac{v^{2}+\beta^{-2}}{1-2v}\leq 2(\tfrac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2})
with
v=\mu_{k}L_{\rho_{k}}
.
Then, substituting this into (
C.27
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{3}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(L_{\rho_{k}}^{2}+(\beta\mu_{k})^{-2})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{24(\tfrac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2})}{\mu_{K}K}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{0},\mu_{0}}(\bm{w}^{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*}\bigr)+\frac{24(\tfrac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2})}{\mu_{K}K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\bigl(\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]\bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{24(\tfrac{1}{16}+\beta^{-2})}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}].

Combining this with the second term of (
C.26
), we obtain the desired inequality, completing the proof.
∎

(C.29)

\displaystyle+{}

(C.31)

C.4
Oracle Complexity Analysis of MoSSP-P

We begin by establishing a recursive relationship between
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k+1}\|^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]
.
The following lemma is adapted from
(
Gao et al. 2024
, Lemma 5.2,)
. For completeness, we provide the proof.

Lemma C.7
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
-
2.2
hold. Then, for any
k\geq 0
and
0<\alpha_{k}<1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k+1}\|^{2}]\leq(1-\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]+\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}L^{2}_{f}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]+\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(C.32)

Proof.

Let
\xi^{[k]}:=\{\xi^{0},\ldots,\xi^{k}\}
denote the collection of i.i.d. samples drawn up to iteration
k
in MoSSP-P, and let
\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\xi^{[k]}]
denote the corresponding conditional expectation.
Recalling (
C.5
) and (
3.6
), one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k+1}\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{S}^{k+1}-\nabla Q_{\rho_{k+1}}(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{s}^{k+1}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}[\|(1-\alpha_{k})(\bm{s}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1}))+\alpha_{k}(\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1}))\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(1-\alpha_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{s}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]+\alpha_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle\quad+2(1-\alpha_{k})\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{s}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1}),\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\rangle|\xi^{[k]}].

(C.33)

Notice that it follows from
2.2
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{s}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1}),\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\rangle|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\langle\bm{s}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1}),\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})|\xi^{[k]}]\rangle=0.

(C.34)

Then, substituting this into (
C.33
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k+1}\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(1-\alpha_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{s}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})+\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,(1-\alpha_{k})^{2}(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]+\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2})+\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(1-\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]+\frac{(1-\alpha_{k})^{2}}{\alpha_{k}}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,(1-\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]+\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}L_{f}^{2}\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2},

(C.35)

where the second inequality uses Young’s inequality
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq(1+\gamma)\|a\|^{2}+(1+\gamma^{-1})\|b\|^{2}
with
\gamma=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{1-\alpha_{k}}>0
and the bounded variance condition in
2.2
, and the third inequality uses the
L_{f}
-smoothness in
2.1
along with
(1-\alpha_{k})^{2}\leq 1
for
0<\alpha_{k}<1
. Taking the full expectation on both sides of this inequality yields (
C.32
), completing the proof.
∎

As can be seen from
Lemma

C.6
, the convergence rate is affected by the accumulated error
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]
. To establish an upper bound for this accumulated error, we impose the following parameter setting

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}},\,L_{\rho_{k}}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{k}}L^{2}_{f}\leq\frac{3}{4}\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}.

(C.36)

Lemma C.8
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
-
2.2
hold, and the
parameters satisfy (
C.7
)
and (
C.36
). Then,
it holds that for
K\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{3(\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{0},\mu_{0}}(\bm{w}^{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*})}{K}+\frac{2\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{0}\|^{2}]}{K}+\frac{3}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(\tfrac{C^{2}}{2}(\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k})+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}])+\frac{2}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(C.37)

Furthermore, if we set

\mu_{k}\equiv\mu,\,\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha,\,\rho_{k}\equiv\rho

for
k\geq 1
, it holds that for any
K\geq 1,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]\leq\frac{3(\mathcal{L}_{\rho,\mu}(\bm{w}^{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*})}{\mu K}+\frac{2\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{0}\|^{2}]}{\mu K}+\frac{2\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\mu}.

(C.38)

Proof.

Recalling (
C.8
), we take full expectation on both
sides of the inequality and substitute it into (
C.32
)
to yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k+1},\mu_{k+1}}(\bm{w}^{k+1})+\|\bm{e}^{k+1}\|^{2}]

(C.39)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})+\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]+\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}+L_{\rho_{k}}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{k}}L_{f}^{2})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle+(\mu_{k}-\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]+\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]+\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})+(1-\alpha_{k}+2\mu_{k})\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]-\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]+\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}+L_{\rho_{k}}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{k}}L_{f}^{2})\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]+\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})+\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]}{16\mu_{k}}-\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]+\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]+\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2},

where the last inequality follows from
1-\alpha_{k}+2\mu_{k}\leq 1
and
that
-\tfrac{1}{2}(2\mu_{k})^{-1}+\tfrac{1}{2}L_{\rho_{k}}+\alpha_{k}^{-1}L_{f}^{2}\leq-\tfrac{1}{16\mu_{k}}
under (
C.36
).

Taking expectation in (
C.9
) and using

\mu_{k}L_{\rho_{k}}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{w}^{k+1}-\bm{w}^{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k+1},\mu_{k+1}}(\bm{w}^{k+1})]+\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]+\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]).

Substituting this into (
C.39
)
and rearranging the inequality yields

\displaystyle\mu_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]\leq

\displaystyle\,3\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k},\mu_{k}}(\bm{w}^{k})]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{k+1},\mu_{k+1}}(\bm{w}^{k+1})]+\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{2}C^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}]\bigr)

\displaystyle+2(\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k+1}\|^{2}])+2\alpha_{k}^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(C.40)

Summing over
k=0,\ldots,K-1
and dividing by
K
yields (
C.8
).
When
\mu_{k}\equiv\mu,\,\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha,\,\rho_{k}\equiv\rho
, one has that
\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}=0
and
\Delta_{k+1}=0
, implying (
C.38
) and completing the proof.
∎

We now characterize the measurements in (
2.7
) for MoSSP-P with constant parameters
\rho_{k}\equiv\rho
,
\mu_{k}\equiv\mu
,
\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha
.

Lemma C.9
.

Suppose that the conditions of Lemma
C.8
hold. Then, it holds that

Lemma C.10

Suppose that Assumptions

Proof.

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{d}^{k+1}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})+(1-\alpha_{k})(\bm{d}^{k}-\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k},\xi^{k+1}))\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}[\|(1-\alpha_{k})\bm{e}^{k}+\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})+(1-\alpha_{k})(\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})-\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k},\xi^{k+1}))\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]

\displaystyle\,(1-\alpha_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}^{k}\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k+1})+(1-\alpha_{k})(\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})-\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k},\xi^{k+1}))\|^{2}|\xi^{[k]}\bigr],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}^{k},\xi^{k+1})-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k}),\bm{e}^{k}\rangle\mid\xi^{[k]}]

Let us focus on the second term (

\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2})
. Hence, to obtain a stochastic
\varepsilon
-stationary point,
K
should be of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
and the total oracle complexity to find an
\varepsilon
-stationary point is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
. Under
2.3
, the same complexity holds for finding a stochastic
\varepsilon
-KKT point, establishing the result in
Corollary

3.2
.

Appendix D
Experimental Results

D.1
Implementation Details

All experiments were conducted in MATLAB R2018b on a MacBook Pro (4-core processor, 16 GB RAM) under macOS 15.3.2.

Baselines
. We compare our algorithms with two double-loop baselines: SPDC
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Nitanda & Suzuki 2017
)
for simple convex-constrained DC(-regularized) optimization and SALM
(
Sun & Sun 2023
)
for linearly constrained DC-regularized optimization:

(i)

SPDC
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Nitanda & Suzuki 2017
)
:
This is a double-loop algorithm designed for solving DC problems by linearizing the concave component and adding a quadratic proximal term to construct a strongly convex subproblem at each outer iteration
k
. To handle the constraint
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}=1
, we apply the same quadratic penalty approach for nonconvex constraints in our comparison. To ensure strong convexity of the subproblem, we adopt an appropriate proximal parameter. The inner subproblem is solved iteratively using the stochastic subgradient descent method (SPG) described in
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
.

(ii)

SALM
(
Sun & Sun 2023
)
:
This double-loop method is designed for deterministic, linearly constrained composite DC optimization. The method handles constraints by constructing the Augmented Lagrangian Method (ALM), linearizing the AL function at each outer iteration, and solving the inner loop using a proximal gradient method. We adopt a similar strategy and adapt it to nonlinear constraints by applying the same linearization approach. Specifically, the concave part is linearized at
\bm{x}^{k}
, and the inner solver is used to update the solution. For the stochastic part, we use the same gradient estimators as in our algorithm.

D.2
Additional Experimental Results

As shown in
Figure

D.3
Experimental Results on Multiple Quadratic Equality Constraints

We test the proposed method on a quadratically constrained DC-regularized logistic regression problem

(
Jin & Wang 2022
;
Shi et al. 2026

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\bigl(1+\exp(-y_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\bm{x})\bigr)+\lambda\bigl(\|\bm{x}\|_{1}-\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle c_{j}(\bm{x}):=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}q_{j,\ell}\,x_{\ell}^{2}+\bm{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{x}-b_{j}=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,M.

(D.1)

Each constraint can be equivalently written as

c_{j}(\bm{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}Q_{j}\bm{x}+\bm{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{x}-b_{j}

\bm{x}_{\star}

is set to
