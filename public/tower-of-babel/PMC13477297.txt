Systematic Identification of Therapeutic Targets and Repurposed Drugs for Stroke: From Genome Causal Analysis to Multilevel Validation
Authors: Xia Zhang, Yi‐Ming Zhang, Ji‐Lai Li, Wei Wang, Wen‐Jun Tu, Hong‐Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a severe cerebrovascular disease characterized by narrow time windows and complications. This study aimed to identify novel drug targets and repurposed drugs for stroke.MethodsThis study used expression quantitative trait loci data from druggable genes in brain and blood as instrumental variables. Mendelian randomization, colocalization, and phenome‐wide Mendelian randomization were applied to evaluate causal relationships and potential side effects, with stroke and ischemic stroke as primary outcomes. Preclinical validation used oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion and middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion models. Pharmacological and behavioral assessments evaluated the therapeutic potential of candidate targets and drugs. Additionally, proteomic sequencing was performed following GGCX (γ‐glutamyl carboxylase) overexpression to explore its biological functions.ResultsElevated GGCX expression in brain and blood was potentially causally associated with reduced risk of stroke and ischemic stroke, supported by colocalization evidence, although potential cardiovascular risks could not be excluded. Drug repositioning identified ifenprodil as a candidate agent that reduced infarction volume, improved motor and cognitive functions, and reversed GGCX downregulation in mice. Ifenprodil treatment and GGCX overexpression alleviated oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion–induced injury and upregulated GGCX expression. Mechanistically, GGCX conferred neuroprotection by regulating protein homeostasis, suppressing inflammation, promoting metabolic recovery, and modulating nuclear transcriptional regulation.ConclusionsThis study established a potential causal link between GGCX and stroke risk, particularly ischemic stroke. GGCX represents a promising therapeutic target for ischemic stroke. Targeted GGCX expression upregulation and drug repurposing, particularly ifenprodil, may offer novel therapeutic avenues. Further validation is warranted to assess clinical efficacy and safety.Keywords: druggable gene, γ‐glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX), Mendelian randomization, phenome‐wide association analysis, repurposed drug, strokeSubject Categories: Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Ischemic Stroke

Research Perspective

What Is New?

Integrating Mendelian randomization with multimodel experimental validation, this study identified that the downregulation of GGCX (γ‐glutamyl carboxylase) expression in blood and brain is associated with stroke and ischemic stroke risk among the European population. Supported by such genetic and multilevel evidence in stroke, we further demonstrated that GGCX functions as a potential druggable neuroprotection target, with roles extending beyond coagulation regulation to the maintenance of protein and metabolic homeostasis and the mediation of immune inhibition.

What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

Comprehensive preclinical investigations are essential to delineate the specific molecular pathways by which GGCX exerts neuroprotection, expanding from its canonical role in hepatic coagulation, and systematically evaluate the potential cardiovascular risks associated with GGCX upregulation to establish a favorable safety profile for clinical intervention, as well as an ongoing observational trial (NCT07269288) that will evaluate the correlation and mechanism between GGCX and the prognosis of acute ischemic stroke.

Stroke, with ischemic stroke accounting for 80%, is one of the leading causes of disability and death worldwide, posing a significant public health burden. 1 , 2 , 3 Currently, the first‐line clinical treatment for ischemic stroke, such as recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, is severely limited by its narrow therapeutic time window and potential bleeding risks. 4 , 5 Meanwhile, the development of new drugs is a long and costly process. Therefore, the rapid and precise discovery of potential causal drug targets through innovative research methods and the exploration of the repurposing potential of existing drugs have become an urgent need in the field of stroke treatment. 6 , 7

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the treatment of ischemic stroke. 8 Various treatment strategies have shown great promise, with some focusing on improving blood circulation and others directly acting on brain tissue to exert neuroprotective effects. 9 , 10 A large body of clinical evidence indicates that strategies such as anticoagulation, antiplatelet therapy, anti‐inflammation, and neuroprotection can effectively reduce the risk of stroke and improve patient outcomes. 11 , 12 If common drug targets that play key roles in both brain tissue and blood can be identified, it will provide a direction for the development of new treatment strategies.

Mendelian randomization (MR), as a powerful genetic tool, is widely used to infer causal relationships between exposure factors and clinical outcomes. 13 , 14 , 15 MR analysis of drug targets uses genetic variations in the gene regions of drug targets, integrating transcriptomic and genetic data including data on expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) to simulate the regulatory mechanisms of gene expression. 16 , 17 , 18 This is similar to the way drugs produce therapeutic effects. In drug target research, MR can effectively simulate the effects of drug inhibition or activation of targets by mimicking the natural variation in gene expression levels. 19 This method has been successfully applied to the discovery of targets and prediction of drug efficacy in various diseases such as Parkinson disease and heart failure. 20 , 21 The complex pathophysiological mechanism of stroke is regulated by both brain tissue and peripheral blood. 22 Therefore, integrating multitissue eQTL data is crucial. 23 However, most existing studies rely on eQTL data from a single tissue, making it difficult to capture the tissue specificity and cross‐tissue regulatory networks of the target. 24 , 25 Most studies still remain at the genetic association level and lack multidimensional clinical and preclinical evidence validation.

This study proposes that there exist common druggable gene targets in the eQTL of brain and blood tissue in patients with stroke, and these crucial drug targets have potential causal associations with the risk of stroke. Targeted intervention on these crucial drug targets or drug repurposing based on these targets will offer novel strategies for the treatment of stroke.

This study will identify druggable genes associated with stroke and its subtypes via MR analysis of drug targets. The stability, safety, and side effects of these genes will be assessed through colocalization analysis and phenome‐wide MR analysis. Data support for the potential clinical translation of drug targets and drugs will be provided through drug reviews, preclinical stroke model experiments on drug targets, and drug interventions.

Druggable Genes Data and Instruments Selection

To systematically identify pharmacological targets for stroke, we curated 5859 druggable genes from the Drug–Gene Interaction Database version 4.2.0 (Data S1) and systematic reviews. 26 , 27 Genetic instrument variables acting as proxies for drug targets were constructed using cis‐eQTLs derived from 3 large‐scale consortia mainly composed of individuals of European ancestry: PsychENCODE (brain, n=1387), 28 eQTLGen (blood, n=31 684), 29 and GTEx version 8 (brain, n=670; blood, n=175) 30 (Table S1). We implemented a rigorous quality control protocol to ensure instrument validity. First, significant cis‐eQTLs (false discovery rate <0.05) located within ±100 kb of the transcription start site were extracted from the 3 consortia. Second, to ensure independence, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed using PLINK version 1.9 (https://www.cog‐genomics.org/plink/1.9/) with a strict threshold of r 2<0.01 within a 10 000 kb physical window, referencing the 1000 Genomes Project European panel. Third, to satisfy the exclusion restriction assumption of MR and minimize the risk of horizontal pleiotropy, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with direct associations with stroke phenotypes were identified and removed via LDtrait screening from the LDlink database (https://ldlink.nih.gov/ldtrait). 13 , 31 This step aimed to ensure that the retained instruments were more likely to influence stroke risk exclusively through the gene expression of the candidate target, rather than through alternative biological pathways. Finally, to mitigate weak instrument bias, we calculated the F‐statistic using the formula F=R 2 (n − k − 1)/[k (1 − R 2)] and retained only strong instruments with F>10 (Data S2–S5).

Study Population and Data Sources

All the GWAS summary data were sourced from the GWAS Catalog database (Table S1). Among them, the stroke‐related data (ebi‐a‐GCST90038613) included 6925 clinically diagnosed stroke cases and 477 673 control samples. 32 Additionally, we obtained another GWAS dataset for ischemic stroke (ebi‐a‐GCST90018864), which comprised 11 929 ischemic stroke samples and 472 192 controls. These 2 data sets mainly consisted of individuals of European ancestry and were used as outcome variables for analysis in this study. The dataset of hemorrhagic stroke is described in Table S2. The studies used in our analysis were approved by their respective institutional review boards, and informed consent was provided by all participants. Twelve blood samples, including 6 patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke through magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography and 6 healthy subjects (Table S3), were collected from Aerospace Center Hospital. This study was conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the Helsinki Declaration. The ethical declaration and ethical approval have been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Aerospace Center Hospital (2024082). All patients were provided with fully informed consent forms.

MR Analysis

Given the exploratory and hypothesis‐generating nature of this study aimed at identifying potential druggable targets, we used a multitiered validation strategy. Primary MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package version 0.5.8 in R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We used the Wald ratio method for single‐SNP instruments and the random‐effects inverse variance‐weighted (IVW) model for multi‐SNP instruments (≥2 SNPs) as the primary estimator. As an initial statistical filter, the FDR correction method by Benjamini–Hochberg was applied. 33 However, no candidate associations survived this correction. Consequently, to minimize false positives while avoiding the rejection of potentially meaningful biological signals, we relied on cross‐cohort reproducibility as our robust alternative criterion for advancing candidates. 34 , 35 This was defined as consistent nominal significance (P<0.05) and effect direction concordance in both the discovery and replication datasets. Only candidates satisfying these multitier criteria were advanced for downstream validation (eg, summary‐data–based MR [SMR], colocalization). Sensitivity profiling was assessed through a comprehensive sensitivity framework. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q statistic, and horizontal pleiotropy was detected via the MR–Egger intercept and the MR–Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier test (1000 simulations) to identify and correct for outliers. To distinguish true causal pleiotropy from LD artifacts, we integrated SMR analysis using the SMR software version 1.3.1 (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr/#Overview). For our analysis, we used default parameters in the SMR software. A robust causal mechanism was defined by a significant SMR association (P SMR <0.05) combined with a nonsignificant Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments (HEIDI) test (P HEIDI >0.05). The HEIDI test was applied to distinguish whether the observed association was consistent with a single shared causal variant model or with a linkage model in which the association is driven by distinct causal variants in linkage disequilibrium. A nonsignificant HEIDI test (P HEIDI >0.05) supports the single causal variant model, indicating that the SMR association is unlikely to be an artifact of LD between separate exposure‐ and outcome‐associated variants. Causal directionality was rigorously verified using the MR Steiger directionality test, ensuring that the variance explained by instrument variables in the exposure (gene expression) significantly exceeded that in the outcome (stroke or ischemic stroke). Furthermore, we conducted reverse MR analyses using stroke susceptibility SNPs as exposures and gene expression as outcomes to explicitly evaluate reverse causality.

Colocalization Analysis

To systematically evaluate the probability of a single shared causal variant driving both gene expression and stroke risk, we performed Bayesian colocalization using the COLOC R package version 5.2.3. For prioritized loci, we defined a testing region of ±1000 kb centered around the GWAS lead SNP. Standard priors (p1=p2=1×10−4; p12=1×10−5) were used to assess 5 mutually exclusive hypotheses (H0–H4). We applied a hierarchical selection strategy: Loci were first prioritized on the basis of strong evidence for association with both traits (PPH3+PPH4≥0.8) and then classified as colocalized only if they exhibited high support for a shared causal variant (PPH4>0.7). 16 Following colocalization, to evaluate whether the identified sentinel variants might influence stroke risk through biological pathways independent of the gene expression of the candidate target, thereby strengthening the plausibility of the exclusion restriction assumption underlying MR, the genetic architecture of these variants was characterized using LDproxy (https://ldlink.nih.gov/ldproxy). LD was evaluated by calculating D′ and R 2 for variant pairs within a 500‐kb window, retaining pairs with r 2≥0.5. Proxy SNPs in LD were identified using the 1000 Genomes European population data. The functional potential of these variants was subsequently annotated using RegulomeDB (prioritizing scores 1a–1f) and FORGEdb (scores >2 considered indicative of significant regulatory potential).

Phenome‐Wide MR Analysis

This study used the phenome‐wide MR approach to systematically evaluate the potential indications and safety issues of druggable target genes. The cis‐eQTL of blood were used as instrumental variables. Outcome data were obtained from the UK Biobank, predominantly of White British ancestry (n≤408 961), 36 which comprised summary statistics for 784 binary disease traits analyzed using the Scalable and Accurate Implementation of Generalized mixed‐model method to account for sample relatedness and case–control imbalance. Causal estimates were primarily calculated using the random‐effects IVW method. To ensure statistical rigor and control for the error rate in this high‐throughput analysis, statistical significance was defined by a stringent Bonferroni‐corrected threshold (P<0.05/783=6.386×10−5, adjusting for 783 traits excluding stroke itself). Meanwhile, associations meeting only the nominal threshold (P<0.05) were classified as suggestive. Robustness was further assessed through a comprehensive sensitivity framework, including Cochran's Q test for heterogeneity, and MR–Egger intercept and MR–Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier global tests to detect and correct for potential horizontal pleiotropy.

Drug Target Review and Molecular Docking

To supplement evidence and reveal potential opportunities for drug reuse, we consulted the PubChem and RCSB Protein Data Bank databases to investigate whether the drug target genes proposed by MR belong to any approved or investigational drug targets. In short, the structures of all drugs were downloaded from the PubChem database as compounds. The 3‐dimensional or 2‐dimensional protein structures encoded by druggable genes were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank database as ligands. All structures were preprocessed with Autodock Vina software version 1.2.0). 37 Finally, molecular docking was performed through compound–ligand interactions. The docking results were calculated and visualized using PyMol software version 2.5 (Schrödinger, Inc., New York).

Animal Studies

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Animal Welfare Law of Capital Medical University and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (AEE1‐2025‐590). This study adheres to the 3R principle (implementing alternative models, reducing the number of experimental animals, and optimizing experimental design). All animal experiments strictly follow the Guidelines for Reporting In Vivo Experiments in Animal Research. 38

Adult male C57BL/6J mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were used for in vivo experiments. Newborn Wistar rats within 24 hours of birth were used for a small number of in vitro experiments. All animals were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China) and had a standard healthy immune status. The experimental C57BL/6J mice are housed in a controlled environment (20 °C±2 °C with a 12‐hour light–dark cycle, humidity 55%–65%), with free access to drinking water and standard laboratory feed. During the experiment, all measures were taken to minimize animal suffering and strictly control the number of experimental animals. Adult male C57BL/6J mice (20–24 g) were selected for in vivo experiments. To avoid cage bias, the sham surgery group, vehicle group, and treatment group mice were housed in the same cage during randomization, and the order of administration between each group was randomly arranged. In addition, to avoid including only animals from the same litter, different batches of experimental animals were used in this study. Two authors (X.Z. and H.Q.W.) were responsible for experimental operations and data analysis but were not aware of the animal grouping. If an animal died within 24 hours after tMCAO surgery, it was not included in the end point analysis. The specific situation of experimental animals is as follows: data from mice excluded from the study (Tables S4 and S5).

Live Stroke Mouse MCAO Model

The development of the MCAO mouse model adhered to our previous protocol. 39 The experimental steps are briefly described as follows: First, the mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol. Second, the right common carotid artery, external carotid artery, and internal carotid artery were isolated. Third, the proximal common carotid artery and distal external carotid artery were ligated, and then a MCAO suture was inserted into the right internal carotid artery to induce cerebral ischemia. Fourth, the MCAO sutures were removed after 1 hour of occlusion, followed by reperfusion. Throughout the entire process, the body temperature of the mice was maintained at 37 °C until 2 hours after reperfusion. The surgery time for each animal should not exceed 15 minutes. All animal experimental procedures (including surgery, injection, weighing, neural motor behavior, etc) are carried out in the corresponding animal laboratory, and the temperature conditions are strictly controlled.

Measurement of Cerebral Infarction Volume and Cerebral Edema

After removing the animal, a mouse brain mold was immediately used to quickly slice the brain tissue into 5 coronal sections with a thickness of 1 mm. To evaluate the infarct and edema volume, the slices were stained with a 1.25% 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 minutes to visualize the infarct area. Red represents noninfarcted areas, while white (unstained) represents infarcted areas. The brain infarct volume was quantified using ImageJ software, with the calculation formula referring to our team's previous research. 39 The percentage of the cerebral infarct volume was calculated using the following equation: Ipsilateral infarct volume/Contralateral hemispheric volume × 100%. The percentage of cerebral edema volume was determined as an indicator of cerebral edema using the following equation: (Ipsilateral hemispheric volume − Contralateral hemispheric volume)/Contralateral hemispheric volume × 100%.

Neuromotor and Cognitive Functions

Twenty‐four hours after reperfusion, 2 neurological function tests were performed on the mice according to our previous research method. The first test was the Zea‐Longa score, with the following specific criteria: 0 points indicated no neurological deficit; 1 point indicated that the contralateral forelimb could not be fully extended, which was a mild deficit; 2 points indicated that the mouse turned to the contralateral side when walking, which was a moderate deficit; 3 points indicated that the mouse leaned to the contralateral side when standing, which was a severe deficit; 4 points indicated that the mouse could not walk independently and lost consciousness. The second test was the Y‐maze test, which was used to assess spatial working memory. Each arm of the maze was 30 cm long, 8 cm wide, and 15 cm high, with an angle of 120° between each arm. The mice were placed in the center of the maze and allowed to freely explore for 8 minutes. The sequence and total number of arm entries were recorded. Entering 3 different arms consecutively was counted as 1 alternation. The alternation percentage was calculated using the formula: (Number of alternations/(Total number of arm entries − 2)) × 100%. All evaluations were conducted by researchers who were unaware of the group information.

Study on Human and Rat Cells Under Hypoxic Conditions

Cell Culture

Human neuroblastoma SH‐SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, double antibiotics (penicillin–streptomycin), and mycoplasma removal agent. Cells were seeded at a density of 5×105 cells/mL in 6‐well plates (for Western blot) or 96‐well plates (for cell viability) and cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 until they reached 75% confluence.

Hippocampal neurons from neonatal Wistar rats were isolated and seeded at a density of 5×105 cells/mL in 6‐well plates (for Western blot) or 96‐well plates (for cell viability) that were coated with 0.01% poly‐D‐lysine for >12 hours. Four hours after seeding, the DMEM was replaced with Neurobasal‐A medium containing 0.3 g/L GlutaMAX, 2% B27, and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 7 days, with one third of the medium replaced every 3 days.

Establishment of OGD/R Model

The oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) model was established following a previous protocol. 39 Cells were transferred to serum‐free and glucose‐free DMEM (2 mL/well) and incubated in a 37 °C hypoxia incubator (2% O2, 5% CO2, 93% N2) for 1 hour. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with the corresponding complete medium (SH‐SY5Y: DMEM with serum; neurons: Neurobasal‐A medium with B27 and GlutaMAX) and reperfused at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours. The control group was cultured in complete medium under normoxic conditions throughout the experiment. All experiments were independently repeated at least 3 times.

Drug Intervention

During the establishment of the OGD/R model, ifenprodil at concentrations ranging from 1 to 100 μM was selectively added for 24‐hour intervention.

Lentivirus Transfection

GGCX overexpression (GGCX‐OE; HBLV‐h‐GGCX‐3xflag‐ZsGreen‐PURO) and negative control (HBLV‐ZsGreen‐PURO) lentiviruses were constructed by Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). When SH‐SY5Y cells reached 75% confluence, they were transfected with virus solution (multiplicity of infection: 20, GGCX‐OE: 3×108 TU/mL, negative control: 4.5×108 TU/mL) in combination with 5 μg/mL polybrene for 4 hours. Then, an equal volume of fresh medium was added. After changing the medium for 24 hours, the cells were further cultured for 24 hours to assess the transfection efficiency (Figure S1). During the reperfusion stage, 10 μM ifenprodil could be added for 24‐hour intervention.

Assessment of Cell Viability in Human and Rat Cells

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit‐8, following a previous study. 39 Briefly, human SH‐SY5Y cells and primary hippocampal neurons were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 0.325×105 cells per well and cultured for the designated time. After different experimental treatments, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit‐8 solution was added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C for 30 minutes to 2 hours. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a SynergyH1 microplate reader.

Statistical Analysis

This study used Prism software version 10.1.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) for t test statistical analysis. The results were presented as the mean±SEM. The detailed information for each analysis can be found in the figure legends.

Results

Analysis of Drug Target Genes in Stroke and Its Subtypes

To systematically identify pharmacological targets, we screened 5859 druggable genes using MR across independent discovery (eQTLGen, PsychENCODE) and replication (GTEx version 8) data sets identified from the Drug–Gene Interaction Database version 4.2.0) and research by Finan et al (Table S1; Data S1–S7). As no candidate associations passed the false discovery rate correction, this study thus adopted cross‐cohort reproducibility as a robust alternative screening criterion and conducted subsequent exploratory and hypothesis‐generating analyses on this basis. After excluding SNPs directly associated with stroke (Data S8), preliminary IVW or Wald ratio MR analyses identified 207 blood‐derived and 188 brain‐derived candidates associated with stroke susceptibility in discovery data sets (P<0.05; Figures S2 and S3; Data S9 and S10). Among these, 45 blood‐derived and 27 brain‐derived eQTLs were cross‐validated in the replication data sets (P<0.05; Figures S4–S7; Data S11 and S12). Cross‐validation across all 4 independent data sets prioritized 3 consensus targets—ALDH16A1, GGCX, and SLC33A1. Genetically predicted upregulation of these genes was associated with a reduced risk of stroke (odds ratio [OR] <1), exhibiting robust nominal significance (P<0.05) across discovery and replication cohorts. Therefore, the association between these 3 targets, which show consistency across multiple data sets, should be defined as an exploratory hypothesis‐generating association (Table 1; Figure 2A–2D; Figure S8). Comprehensive sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these associations in multi‐SNP models (eg, eQTLGen blood data), where no significant heterogeneity (Cochran's Q P>0.05) or horizontal pleiotropy (MR–Egger intercept P>0.05; MR–Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier global test P>0.05) was detected for any of the 3 genes (Data S13). Note that for brain‐derived instruments, sensitivity metrics were largely unavailable due to the use of single strong instruments (Wald ratio method for MR).

Specifically, only GGCX maintained protective associations with ischemic stroke populations in both the brain (OR, 0.916 [95% CI, 0.856–0.981], P Wald ratio =0.012) and blood (OR, 0.902 [95% CI, 0.824–0.988], P IVW =0.027), whereas evidence for ALDH16A1 and SLC33A1 was attenuated (Table 1; Figure 2E; Data S14). Sensitivity tests for ischemic stroke also showed no evidence of significant heterogeneity (Cochran's Q P>0.05) or horizontal pleiotropy (MR–Egger P>0.05; MR–Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier P>0.05) for the GGCX blood signal (Data S14). Similar MR analyses were also conducted in subtypes of hemorrhagic stroke (namely, intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage), but the results were not verified (P>0.05; Data S15 and S16).

Based on these findings, we focused subsequent analyses on GGCX as the lead candidate. To distinguish genuine vertical pleiotropy from LD artifacts, we first implemented a strict multilayered sensitivity framework. The SMR analysis validated that GGCX expression in both the brain and blood (from discovery and replication datasets) had a robust protective effect on ischemic stroke (P SMR <0.05). Furthermore, a nonsignificant HEIDI test result (P HEIDI >0.05) indicated that the observed association was driven by a single shared causal variant rather than by distinct variants in linkage disequilibrium (Data S17). However, the HEIDI test failed to pass the significance threshold in stroke (Data S17).

Causal directionality for GGCX expression in both the brain and blood (from discovery and replication datasets) was definitively established via Steiger filtering in stroke and ischemic stroke (P Steiger<0.05; Data S18 and S19). Reverse MR analyses supported a model where GGCX is the primary causal exposure for stroke and ischemic stroke, rather than a balanced feedback loop (stroke to GGCX, P reverse <0.05; ischemic stroke to GGCX, P reverse <0.05 in PsychENCODE and GTEx, while P reverse >0.05 in eQTLGen; Data S20 and S21), as the variance explained by the original exposure significantly outweighed that of the outcome (stroke/ischemic stroke to GGCX, r 2 exposure<r 2 outcome, while GGCX to stroke/ischemic stroke, r 2 exposure>r 2 outcome) (Data S22 and S23). We also evaluated the other 2 candidates (ALDH16A1, SLC33A1) via SMR and Steiger directionality testing (Table 1; Data S17–S23).

Colocalization Analysis

We conducted a colocalization analysis to further clarify the probability of genetic variants driving the potential causal relationship between drug target genes and stroke (Table 1; Data S24). The GGCX locus in the brain was primarily driven by distinct causal variants (PPH3=0.647, PPH4=0.199), despite a strong association with both traits (PPH3+PPH4=0.846; Table 1; Data S24). In blood, we observed compelling evidence that the GWAS signal for stroke and the eQTL signal for GGCX are driven by a shared causal variant (PPH4=0.745, PPH3+PPH4=0.808; Table 1; Data S24). In genetics, rs1972297 and rs6547623 were selected as genetic sentinel variants for the brain and blood signals, respectively (Figure 3A and 3B). Subsequently, we performed a fine mapping assessment. The results indicated that brain rs1972297 (r 2=1, D′=1, FORGEdb=7.0, RegulomeDB=3a; Figure S9A) was in complete LD with rs1009 (r 2=1.0, D′=1), which is a synonymous exon variant (A/G) with a high functional score (FORGEdb=7.0, RegulomeDB=1b; Table S6). Blood rs6547623 (r 2=1, D′=1, FORGEdb=4, RegulomeDB=7; Figure S9B) was in a shared LD block with rs1446669 (r 2=0.9385, D′=0.9908). rs1446669 is located in the distal enhancer region of GGCX (FORGEdb=8.0, RegulomeDB=4; Table S6). Overall, our MR and colocalization analyses suggest that GGCX has shared potential causal association evidence with stroke.

Phenome‐Wide MR Analysis

To strictly assess the potential beneficial or harmful effects of the prioritized druggable target genes on other traits, we performed a phenome‐wide MR screen across 783 traits in the UK Biobank, applying a stringent Bonferroni‐corrected threshold (P<6.39×10−5; Data S25). Interestingly, the predicted upregulation of GGCX was associated with a higher risk of coronary atherosclerosis (OR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.22–1.48]; P=2.03×10−9) and angina pectoris (OR, 1.26 [95% CI, 1.13–1.40], P=2.06×10−5) (Figure 4A; Data S26), suggesting that potential cardiovascular risks cannot be ruled out. In contrast, the associations of the other candidate genes (ALDH16A1 and SLC33A1) with the traits failed to survive the stringent Bonferroni correction (Figure 4B and 4C; Data S27 and S28).

The Validation in the Stroke Model

After confirming GGCX as a promising druggable target, we evaluated its expression levels in ischemic stroke through a multitiered strategy involving clinical, in vivo, and in vitro models (Figure 5A). In clinical samples from an Asian population, GGCX mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in the peripheral blood of stroke patients compared with that of healthy controls (Figure 5B; Table S7). In a mouse tMCAO/R model (1 hour of ischemia followed by 24 hours of reperfusion), cerebral infarction, edema, and neurological deficits were significantly aggravated relative to sham‐operated controls (Figure 5C–5F). Western blot analysis further revealed a marked reduction of GGCX protein in the ipsilateral cortex of tMCAO/R mice, which was not observed in animals of the sham group (Figure 5G and 5H). This phenotypic change was reproduced in primary rat hippocampal neurons subjected to OGD (1 hour of OGD), as evidenced by clear neuronal injury and a significant decrease in GGCX expression following OGD/R (1 hour of OGD followed by 24 hours of reperfusion) treatment (Figure 5I–5K). Collectively, these results demonstrate a consistent downregulation of GGCX expression under ischemic conditions across clinical, animal, and cellular models.

GGCX Overexpression Reverses Proteomic Alterations and Restores Functional Pathways in Stroke Model

Given the observed downregulation of GGCX expression in stroke models, we overexpressed GGCX in an OGD/R model of human SH‐SY5Y cells and performed proteomic analysis to explore its broader molecular mechanisms in ischemic stroke (Figure 6). Principal component analysis indicated high experimental reproducibility. Intergroup differences were primarily attributable to OGD/R treatment and varying levels of GGCX expression (Figure 6A). A total of 3419 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified across all samples, reflecting high proteome coverage (Figure 6B). Cluster analysis based on protein expression similarity further segregated the samples into 2 major clusters: the OGD/R treatment and the GGCX‐OE combined with OGD/R treatment (Figure 6C). GGCX‐OE treatment markedly reversed OGD/R‐induced alterations in protein expression by downregulating proteins such as LCORL, SCRIB, and FHL1 in cluster 6, and upregulating proteins including OPA1, COX series proteins, and CLU in clusters 4 and 7 (Figure 6D and 6E). These results demonstrate the bidirectional regulatory capacity of GGCX in cellular homeostasis. Volcano plot analysis identified 289 DEPs between the normoxia and OGD/R groups and 148 DEPs when comparing the GGCX‐OE plus OGD/R group to the OGD/R group alone. Notably, although most proteins dysregulated under OGD/R exhibited a downward trend, GGCX‐OE significantly reversed this pattern, indicating a restorative role (Figure 6F). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis showed that OGD/R‐downregulated proteins were enriched in the Notch signaling pathway, cardiac muscle contraction, and oxidative phosphorylation, whereas upregulated proteins were mainly involved in the interleukin‐17 signaling pathway. GGCX‐OE counteracted the overactivation of the interleukin‐17 pathway and restored the activity of key pathways, including cardiac muscle contraction, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and oxidative phosphorylation, as well as modulating processes related to mitophagy and ubiquinone biosynthesis. Notably, it also led to the downregulation of the lipid and atherosclerosis, transforming growth factor‐β signaling pathway. These results suggested that GGCX exerts neuroprotective effects through multipathway coordination (Figure 6G). Gene ontology biological process analysis revealed that GGCX‐OE modulated neural function–related processes, including endoplasmic reticulum stress response, metabolic adaptation, apoptosis, and learning or memory (Figure 6H). Fold‐change analysis of 41 DEPs further confirmed that GGCX‐OE normalized the expression of 15 OGD/R‐upregulated and 25 OGD/R‐downregulated DEPs, which participate in key processes such as mitochondrial function, gene regulation, and cell signaling (Figure 6I and 6J). These DEPs not only highlight the complexity and synergy of GGCX function but also provide a broader resource for investigating its novel roles. Subcellular localization analysis showed that DEPs were predominantly nuclear following OGD/R treatment. The proportion of nuclear DEPs increased further after GGCX‐OE treatment, suggesting an enhanced nuclear regulatory response to injury (Figure 6K). Domain enrichment analysis indicated that GGCX‐OE restored nuclear regulatory domains such as the transcription factors NFX1 family and promoted the expression of protein‐interaction domains like SKICH (Figure 6L). Subsequently, we constructed a weighted protein coexpression network analysis and identified 28 modules. Among these, the blue module, enriched in lysosomal metabolic pathways, showed significant phenotypic association and contained 10 DEPs including HSP90AA5P, GTF2F1, and OPA1. The turquoise module, associated with proteasome activity, contained the DEP ACIN1 (Figure S10). The key molecules within these modules, functioning as central hubs, may mediate the regulatory effects of GGCX on neural functional pathways.

Preclinical and Clinical Validation for Translation

Subsequently, we implemented a multilevel validation strategy encompassing in vivo and in vitro models (Figure 7A). In tMCAO/R mice, ifenprodil administration significantly reduced cerebral infarction volume, improved neurological motor function (Figure 7B–7E), and alleviated cognitive impairment (Figure 7F–7H) compared with saline‐treated (vehicle) controls, indicating neuroprotective efficacy during reperfusion. Based on genetic and drug target review evidence, we hypothesized that ifenprodil might restore GGCX expression after ischemia. Western blot analysis confirmed markedly decreased GGCX protein levels in the ipsilateral cortex of tMCAO/R mice relative to the contralateral side (Figure 7I and 7J). Ifenprodil treatment normalized GGCX expression to baseline levels, rescuing the ischemia‐induced pathological downregulation (Figure 7I and 7J). These results indicate that GGCX is a key target for the action of ifenprodil, providing a new direction for the treatment of ischemic stroke. To further validate the potential of ifenprodil in clinical application, we conducted validation studies using human SH‐SY5Y cells under OGD/R conditions. Ifenprodil intervention (10 μM) significantly enhanced cell viability following OGD and OGD/R treatments (Figure 7K). Ifenprodil treatment at different concentrations (5–20 μM) also reversed OGD/R‐induced GGCX downregulation (Figure 7L and 7M). Preliminary observations also suggested modulatory effects of ifenprodil on protein C and factor X levels in the ischemic cortex (Figure S12). To further assess target specificity, we established a GGCX‐OE system in SH‐SY5Y cells. GGCX‐OE alone produced cytoprotective effects similar to those of ifenprodil treatment (Figure 7N). GGCX‐OE significantly increased GGCX protein levels (Figure 7O and 7P). However, the combined treatment of GGCX‐OE and ifenprodil showed no synergistic effect (Figure 7O and 7P). Therefore, we proposed an effective therapy based on the drug target GGCX, providing a new approach for stroke treatment.

Discussion

This study identified potential drug targets common to both the brain and blood in stroke and its subtype, ischemic stroke, by integrating pharmacogenomic, eQTL, and GWAS data. Among the 3 potential drug targets, we found that a genetically predicted elevated GGCX level may have a potential causal association with a reduced risk of ischemic stroke in individuals of European ancestry. This conclusion was verified by including genetic colocalization analysis methods. No causal association was found with hemorrhagic stroke. Although the potential risk related to cardiovascular characteristics was not excluded, further research is needed. Additionally, the study found that targeting the upregulation of GGCX could reverse proteomic changes in ischemic stroke models and restore functional pathways. Moreover, the repurposed ifenprodil targeting GGCX could exert a neuroprotective effect in ischemic stroke.

Comparison With Related Research

Multiple GWASs have reported significant genetic associations between the GGCX locus and stroke and its subtypes. Several independent studies have found that GGCX‐related SNPs (such as Gln325Arg, rs9923231, and rs2592551) are associated with the risk of ischemic stroke. 40 , 41 A systematic review and meta‐analysis further confirmed that the intronic variant rs11676382 of GGCX is an important genetic determinant of warfarin dose requirements, with racial differences. 42 Recent studies have shown that rs11676382 cannot independently predict the 5‐year mortality rate of patients with acute ischemic stroke, suggesting that simply regulating coagulation function may not be sufficient to explain the impact of GGCX on the long‐term prognosis of stroke. Instead, other variants such as rs12714145 or specific microsatellite repeats may play a major role in the warfarin dose differences in Chinese and African populations. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 Additionally, the functional missense variant rs699664 (R325Q) has been confirmed to be involved in vascular calcification by affecting the γ‐carboxylation process of MGP (matrix Gla protein), rather than directly causing bleeding tendencies. 47 , 48 Although these studies have laid the foundation for exploring the role of GGCX in stroke, the causal relationship, specific mechanisms, and cross‐tissue genetic evidence between them remain unclear, and the effectiveness of GGCX as a target for stroke prevention and treatment remains to be verified. 45 , 49 , 50 , 51

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the treatment of ischemic stroke, with multiple novel therapies showing potential in reducing disease risk and improving prognosis. 8 , 52 , 53 Some of these strategies act on the peripheral circulatory system, while others directly target central nervous pathways. Against this background, the use of genetic methods to identify common targets that play crucial roles in both brain and blood and to clarify their association with the pathological process of stroke is of great significance for promoting precision treatment and mechanism research.

This study provides multidimensional evidence for the causal association between GGCX and ischemic stroke through the integration of multitissue eQTL data, MR, and Bayesian colocalization analysis. First, MR analysis indicated that a genetically predicted increase in GGCX expression level was significantly associated with a reduced risk of stroke and ischemic stroke in European populations. Second, cross‐tissue colocalization analysis showed that GGCX expression and stroke risk share the same causal genetic variants, especially in blood. Further fine mapping identified sentinel variant sites rs1972297 and rs6547623 from the brain and blood, respectively. These variants were not significantly linked to the warfarin‐related variant rs11676382 (r 2<0.1) but were highly linked to the MGP regulatory variant rs699664 involved in noncoagulation pathways (r 2=0.67). This suggests that GGCX may exert protective effects through mechanisms independent of its classical coagulation mechanism. These findings fill the gap in previous studies lacking causal association research and for the first time establish the potential protective role of GGCX in ischemic stroke from a genetic perspective. Subsequent functional experiments confirmed that intervention to upregulate GGCX expression could significantly reduce brain ischemic injury, thereby providing mechanism‐level support for its use as a therapeutic target.

Public Health and Clinical Significance

Existing evidence indicates that strategies such as lipid regulation, blood sugar control, anticoagulation, antiplatelet therapy, anti‐inflammation, and neuroprotection can effectively reduce the occurrence and recurrence of ischemic stroke and improve patient prognosis, especially in diagnosed populations. 4 This study, on the one hand, establishes GGCX as a potential target for reducing stroke risk, clarifies its neuroprotective role mediated by multiple mechanisms, and provides a new perspective for primary prevention. On the other hand, the potential repurposed drug ifenprodil targeting GGCX was screened out, providing a translational direction for clinical treatment. In conclusion, this study constructed a synergistic path from both prevention and treatment aspects, which has clear value for the prevention and treatment of stroke and lays a foundation for subsequent mechanism exploration and strategy optimization.

Mechanism Explanation and Significance

GGCX, a γ‐glutamyl carboxylase, is localized on the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum 54 and its classical function is to catalyze the carboxylation of glutamic acid residues in VKDPs (vitamin K‐dependent proteins), thereby maintaining the body's coagulation balance. 55 Studies have revealed that knockout of the GGCX gene leads to severe bleeding and death in mice. Clinically, patients with GGCX gene defects also exhibit an abnormally elevated international normalized ratio, and the mutant forms are also closely associated with bleeding phenotypes. 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 This study found that GGCX protein expression was pathologically downregulated in multiple ischemic stroke models. Given the core role of GGCX in the coagulation system, long‐term inhibition of its activity may induce bleeding risks. Therefore, future intervention strategies need to carefully assess its potential impact on coagulation function.

In recent years, significant breakthroughs have been made in the structure, function, and mechanism of GGCX. Studies have shown that GGCX can carboxylate cytoplasmic substrates (such as mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein) through structural rearrangement, thereby participating in the antiviral innate immune response. 54 High‐resolution structural analysis further revealed that cholesterol binding plays a key regulatory role in its conformational stability and catalytic activity. 58 , 60 The downregulation of the lipid and atherosclerosis pathway by GGCX overexpression, a key finding of our work, establishes a functional link to cholesterol metabolism and highlights its disease relevance. Moreover, the discovery of the nonclassical vitamin K cycle (mediated by ferroptosis suppressor protein 1) has expanded the function of GGCX to emerging biological processes such as antiferroptosis. 61 The continuous carboxylation mechanism is crucial for maintaining the functional integrity of VKDPs. 62 In addition, GGCX also participates in vascular and neuroprotective processes by carboxylating proteins such as MGP and Growth arrest–specific 6. These advancements have broadened the understanding of GGCX's noncoagulation functions and provided a basis for developing targeted strategies based on its structural characteristics and activity regulation mechanisms. 58 , 60 , 62 Based on the downregulation of GGCX expression in multiple ischemic stroke models and its potential neuroprotective role genetically, this study proposes a new neuroprotective strategy targeting the upregulation of GGCX. The effective inhibition of GGCX by the small molecule HF13141‐H5 validates its feasibility as a drug target. 63 Currently, there are no specific clinical intervention strategies to upregulate GGCX expression. We achieved a moderate upregulation of GGCX through GGCX overexpression, effectively reversing its decreased expression in the brain ischemia injury model and observing significant neuroprotective effects. Further proteomic analysis indicated that the upregulation of GGCX not only alleviates brain ischemia–reperfusion injury but also participates in regulating protein homeostasis, inhibiting inflammatory responses, promoting metabolic repair, and influencing nuclear transcriptional regulation processes.

In summary, this study reveals the multidimensional protective mechanisms of GGCX in ischemic stroke from a systems biology perspective, providing theoretical and experimental support for treatment strategies centered on GGCX upregulation. Although this strategy has good translational potential, its clinical application still requires a comprehensive assessment of potential interference with coagulation homeostasis to ensure a favorable risk–benefit ratio.

Clinical Translation Interpretation

Combining genetic and preclinical research evidence, this study emphasizes the clinical necessity of developing drugs that can safely upregulate GGCX expression. Among the existing drug‐screening methods, molecular docking technology has demonstrated significant advantages. We used this technology to screen out 7 marketed drugs that interact with GGCX as potential candidates, including ifenprodil, vitamin K, warfarin, anisindione, chamomile, primidone, and phenobarbital. Although these drugs have received clinical approval, their repurposing in stroke treatment still requires rigorous validation. We evaluated the candidate drugs on the basis of 3 principles: existing indications and repositioning basis, bleeding/thrombosis risk in stroke treatment, and consistency with the direction predicted by MR.

Among the drugs with stronger evidence, ifenprodil shows good translational potential. This drug was initially an α1 receptor antagonist, and as described in its Japanese prescribing information, it was approved as a cerebral vasodilator for poststroke sequelae, with reported mechanisms involving vasodilation, α receptor blockade, mitochondrial function improvement, and platelet aggregation inhibition, alongside a good safety profile and no reported bleeding risk. 64 In recent years, it has also shown significant clinical repurposing potential in diseases such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, posttraumatic stress disorder, 65 and COVID‐19, 66 along with beneficial effects observed in preclinical research of Alzheimer disease, 67 neuropathic pain, 68 and stroke. 64 , 69 We present novel evidence from integrated in vivo and in vitro models demonstrating ifenprodil's efficacy in reducing infarct volume and improving neurological function. Crucially, we also confirmed its action in restoring GGCX expression, a key finding that is directly concordant with the direction predicted by MR. The collective evidence strongly underscores the translational potential of both GGCX as a target and ifenprodil as a therapeutic agent. Notably, even without GGCX overexpression intervention, human cells can still benefit from drug intervention. Vitamin K, as an essential cofactor for GGCX, has seen significant progress in mechanism research. Structural biology studies have revealed a “substrate‐guided cofactor binding” mechanism, clarifying the crucial role of vitamin K in GGCX catalytic activity and conformational stability. 60 Functional studies have shown that vitamin K not only drives carboxylation reactions but also has functional compensation potential for some GGCX loss‐of‐function mutations. 70 Its action direction is consistent with the MR prediction, and it has shown clear efficacy and controllable risks in the context of vitamin K deficiency or anticoagulant therapy, demonstrating good clinical application prospects.

Among the drugs that require cautious evaluation, warfarin, as a VKORC1 inhibitor, indirectly inhibits GGCX activity by depleting reduced vitamin K. 59 This is inconsistent with the MR conclusion. Additionally, its extensive inhibition of VKDPs may increase the risk of hemorrhage and vascular calcification. The efficacy of warfarin is also restricted by VKORC1 polymorphism and a narrow therapeutic window. Similarly, synthetic anticoagulants like anisindione can inhibit GGCX function by forming dimers with it. 59 The direction of action is also inconsistent with the MR prediction, and the bleeding risk is unclear. Therefore, these 2 types of drugs need to be carefully considered in stroke treatment. Among the candidate drugs with weaker evidence, the interactions of chamomile, primidone, and phenobarbital with GGCX are only at the predictive level, lacking reliable and consistent directional support, and the clinical data on bleeding risk are limited. Their potential value still needs further verification.

Based on the above findings, we are conducting an observational study (NCT07269288) to evaluate the association between GGCX and the prognosis of ischemic stroke. Before obtaining clinical data, our preclinical studies have demonstrated that gene intervention strategies based on GGCX and drug‐repurposing strategies targeting GGCX are feasible for the treatment of ischemic stroke, offering an important foundation for subsequent translational research.

The present study has several strengths that contribute to its validity and significance. First, through genetic analysis, it reveals the potential causal relationship between the key drug target GGCX, which is common in both brain and blood, and the risk of ischemic stroke. Second, based on the drug‐repositioning strategy and preclinical experiments, it screens out ifenprodil, a potential candidate drug that can moderately upregulate GGCX expression to alleviate reperfusion injury and treat ischemic stroke. Finally, this study breaks through the traditional understanding of GGCX as a liver‐derived coagulation regulatory enzyme and systematically depicts its neuroprotective role in stroke, especially its biological functions in bidirectionally regulating protein homeostasis, synergistically inhibiting inflammation, repairing metabolic pathways, and participating in nuclear transcriptional regulation for the first time.

However, there are limitations to our study that should be acknowledged. First, our findings are subject to the inherent limitations of the MR approach. The MR approach relies on strong assumptions (eg, relevance, independence, exclusion restriction) that are difficult to fully satisfy in real‐world studies, which could introduce potential bias. Furthermore, genetic instruments typically reflect lifelong exposure effects, which may not directly translate to short‐term pharmacological interventions due to differences in mechanism and temporal scale. Consequently, MR is likely to miss causal associations that depend on transient dynamics or complex biological interactions. This study mainly focused on individuals of European ancestry, so the conclusions drawn may have certain limitations when generalized to other ancestral groups. Due to the limited individual ancestral data, it was not possible to conduct further stratified sensitivity analyses based on ancestral background. Given the small number of SNPs that could be identified for each drug target, it was challenging to perform robust MR association analyses. Some MR analysis results only reached the nominal significance level (P<0.05) and did not remain statistically significant after multiple testing correction. Therefore, this study adopted an exploratory research or hypothesis‐generating strategy to explore the potential associations between drug targets shared by the brain and blood and stroke. The occurrence of negative results may be due to insufficient statistical power, leading to limited sensitivity in detecting true associations. Although this study provided supportive evidence for the MR results through colocalization analysis, larger sample size MR studies are still needed to further validate the findings. There is a potential adverse association between the GGCX gene and some cardiovascular traits, and it is necessary to conduct systematic and in‐depth analyses for cardiogenic disease outcomes. Although genetic analysis has provided new insights, the potential of GGCX as a direct therapeutic target still needs to be verified through experimental studies. In addition, clinical trials are needed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of drug interventions targeting GGCX in stroke treatment, especially the balance between efficacy and risk. Future research should also include larger‐scale preclinical studies to reveal the molecular mechanisms by which GGCX exerts neuroprotective effects.

Conclusions

This study reveals a potential causal relationship between GGCX and stroke as well as ischemic stroke. GGCX holds significant value as a drug target and plays a protective role in ischemic stroke. Strategies for upregulating GGCX and drug‐repurposing schemes based on it are expected to offer new avenues for the treatment of ischemic stroke. To further systematically elucidate its molecular mechanism of action and verify the effectiveness and safety of its therapeutic strategies, more experimental studies and clinical trials are still needed.
