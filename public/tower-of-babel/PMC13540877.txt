From systemic immune dysregulation to oral disease: evidence linking food allergies and oral manifestations- a systematic review
Authors: Pratyasha Sharma, Srikala Bhandary, Sayan Das, Syed Taha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFood allergy is a common immune-mediated condition in children and is increasingly recognized to affect the oral cavity. Oral manifestations may represent early signs of allergic sensitization and can influence oral health, nutrition, and quality of life. This systematic review aimed to synthesize the available evidence on oral manifestations associated with food allergies in pediatric populations.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed Medline, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for studies published up to December 2025. Eligibility criteria included observational and clinical studies published in English that reported oral or dental manifestations in children and adolescents aged 0–18 years with diagnosed IgE-mediated or non-IgE-mediated food allergy. Study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment were performed independently by two reviewers in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsTwelve pediatric studies met the inclusion criteria. The most frequently reported oral manifestations were itching and tingling of the lips, tongue, palate, and oral mucosa following ingestion of specific foods, particularly fruits and nuts. Mild labial and oral mucosal swelling were also commonly described. These symptoms were consistent with oral allergy syndrome and were most frequently observed in school-aged children with pollen sensitization. Oral manifestations occurred rapidly after food exposure and were reproducible across different populations and geographic regions.ConclusionOral manifestations are common features of pediatric food allergy and frequently represent localized IgE-mediated reactions. Recognition of these symptoms is important for early identification of food allergy and appropriate referral. Increased awareness among pediatric dentists may contribute to improved detection and multidisciplinary management of affected children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08964-6.

Introduction

Food allergies are immune-mediated adverse reactions that occur when the body’s immune system mistakenly identifies certain proteins in food as harmful. [1] This results in the production of immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies, which trigger allergic symptoms upon subsequent exposure to the offending food. Food allergies can manifest in various forms, including immediate hypersensitivity reactions, which can lead to anaphylaxis, and delayed-type reactions. [2, 3] .

The pathophysiology of food allergy involves an interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors. During initial exposure, the immune system produces allergen-specific IgE antibodies. Upon re-exposure, these antibodies bind to mast cells and basophils, triggering the release of histamine and other inflammatory mediators. [4] This process results in various allergic symptoms, which can range from mild (such as hives and gastrointestinal discomfort) to severe (such as anaphylaxis). [1] .

In some cases, food allergies can involve mixed IgE and non-IgE mechanisms. Non-IgE- mediated reactions occur without the involvement of IgE antibodies, and they often cause delayed symptoms, such as gastrointestinal distress, which can be harder to diagnose. [3] Additionally, some food allergies may involve other immune system components, like T cells, that contribute to inflammation and allergic responses, making these reactions more complex. Kato et al. [5] described that pollen sensitization contributes to IgE-mediated cross-reactivity with structurally homologous food proteins, forming the immunological basis of oral allergy syndrome. Understanding these mechanisms helps in diagnosing and managing food allergies effectively.

The prevalence of food allergies has been increasing in recent years, particularly in developed countries. It is estimated that approximately 5–8% of children and 1–2% of adults in the United States have food allergies. [6] Common allergens include peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, fish, and shellfish. [7] Peters, et al. [8] reported that the prevalence of IgE-mediated food allergy in children varies depending on diagnostic criteria, with higher estimates observed in self-reported data compared to clinically confirmed cases.

Food allergies and sensitivities often manifest as oral symptoms, such as ulcers, burning sensation, or localized swelling, collectively described as oral allergic reactions. One of the most recognized presentations is Oral Allergy Syndrome (OAS), also called pollen-food allergy syndrome, in which sensitized individuals develop immediate itching, tingling, or edema of the lips, tongue, palate, or oropharynx after consuming cross-reactive fresh fruits, vegetables, or nuts. [9, 10] These symptoms occur due to the rapid release of histamine and other mediators in the oral mucosa following contact with the allergenic proteins. In more severe IgE-mediated reactions, angioedema may occur, particularly involving the lips and tongue. This can interfere with speech, mastication, and swallowing. Some chronic food-related hypersensitivities can also contribute to recurrent aphthous-like ulcers, erythema, or mucosal peeling due to sustained inflammation. [11] In children, allergic reactions may additionally present as gingival inflammation, perioral dermatitis, or exaggerated responses to minor trauma. [12] Rosa et al. [13] reported that hypovitaminosis is associated with recurrent oral mucosal lesions, indicating that not all oral manifestations in children are attributable to allergic mechanisms.

A systematic search of the literature on food allergies in children showed that the focus has been largely on systemic responses, with limited discourse on oral and dental manifestations. The studies describing oral symptoms are heterogeneous in design, population, and outcome reporting, and to date, no comprehensive synthesis has specifically evaluated these manifestations in pediatric populations. However, recognizing the oral manifestations is essential, as they may serve as early indicators of underlying food allergies and assist in timely referral and management.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to evaluate and synthesize the available evidence on oral manifestations associated with IgE-mediated and non-IgE-mediated food allergies in children and adolescents.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was registered prospectively on PROSPERO, registration number CRD420261277338.

Research question

This review aimed to answer following research question: “Which oral manifestations are associated with IgE- and non-IgE-mediated food allergies in children and adolescents?” This was further detailed using the PECOS [14] framework as.

Population (P): Children and adolescents aged 0–18 years.

Exposure (E): Diagnosed IgE-mediated or non-IgE-mediated food allergy.

Comparison (C): Populations without diagnosed food allergies.

Outcomes (O): Oral and dental manifestations, including Oral allergy syndrome (OAS); Labial, lingual, and oropharyngeal edema; Oral mucosal lesions; Gingivitis and periodontal inflammation; Dental caries; Enamel defects; Xerostomia; Salivary alterations.

Search strategy

An electronic systematic search of the literature was performed in the PubMed (Medline), Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases and the reference lists of articles published until December 2025. This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. [15] Search terms and keywords were used with Boolean operators, such as.

(“food allergy” OR “IgE mediated food allergy” OR “non-IgE mediated food allergy”.

OR “oral allergy syndrome” OR “pollen food allergy syndrome”) AND (“oral health” OR “oral manifestations” OR “dental caries” OR “gingivitis” OR “periodontal disease” OR “enamel defects” OR “xerostomia”) AND (“children” OR “pediatric” OR “adolescent”). The complete search strategy for each database is described in Supplementary Table 1.

The initial search retrieved 286 potential articles. After the duplicates were removed, 100 articles remained; the titles and abstracts of these 100 articles were read, resulting in the selection of 56 articles for reading the full text. The data of the 12 included articles were extracted.

Two reviewers (PS and SB) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were assessed independently. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (SD). The detailed characteristics of each of the included studies is explained in Table 1. Due to the heterogeneity of the studies and lack of quantitative outcomes in the included studies, a narrative synthesis was done. Possible sources of heterogeneity among included studies were explored qualitatively through structured subgroup comparisons. Studies were grouped and compared based on study design, geographic region, age group of participants, and type of food allergen involved. Differences in reported oral manifestations were examined across these subgroups to identify consistent patterns and potential sources of variability. Meta-regression was not performed due to the absence of pooled quantitative data.

Results

At the full-text screening stage, studies were excluded due to ineligible study design (reviews and case reports), adult-only populations, or absence of reported oral manifestations. The number of studies excluded under each category is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2).

PRISMA flow diagram

Qualitative subgroup comparisons identified several sources of heterogeneity among included studies. Studies conducted in clinical settings reported a higher frequency and wider range of oral manifestations than population-based school surveys. Age-related differences were observed, with oral manifestations reported more frequently in school-aged children and adolescents than in younger children. Geographic variation was minimal, as similar patterns of oral itching, tingling, and lip swelling were reported across studies from Europe, Asia, Australia, and North America.

Across the 12 included studies, oral itching and tingling were reported in all studies (12/12, 100%). Lip or perioral swelling was reported in the majority of studies (7/12, 58.3%). The most commonly implicated food triggers were fruits, particularly apple and peach, which were reported in 9 out of 12 studies (75%), followed by kiwi in 6 studies (50%) and hazelnut in 5 studies (41.7%). Peanut was specifically reported in 3 studies (25%). Overall, the pattern of findings was consistent with oral allergy syndrome across all included studies.

Only one study Rancé et al., [18] reported oral symptoms preceding systemic allergic reactions, while most studies primarily described localized oral manifestations without detailed documentation of systemic involvement.

The included studies predominantly reported oral manifestations in the context of IgE-mediated food allergies, particularly oral allergy syndrome. Data on non-IgE-mediated food allergies and their oral manifestations were minimal and inconsistently reported.

Discussion

Prevalence and pattern of oral manifestations

This systematic review demonstrates that oral manifestations are frequently reported in children with food allergy, with prevalence varying across populations and different study designs. Population-based studies, such as the school survey conducted by Fukao et al. [22] , reported oral symptoms in more than 10% of children following ingestion of specific foods. In contrast, clinic-based studies reported higher proportions of affected children, reflecting the increased likelihood of symptom recognition and reporting among patients referred for allergic evaluation. Despite these differences, the presence of oral symptoms was a consistent finding across all included studies, indicating that oral involvement is a common feature of pediatric food allergies.

The variation in prevalence across studies can be partly explained by differences in participant selection, age distribution, and diagnostic criteria. Studies focusing on children with established allergic diseases or pollen sensitization reported higher frequencies of oral symptoms compared with general population samples. However, even in undiagnosed school-based prevalence studies, oral manifestations were reported with sufficient frequency to support their relevance as a pediatric oral health concern.

Across all the included studies, itching of the lips, tongue, palate, and oral mucosa was the most frequently reported oral manifestation. Tingling sensations in the oral cavity were also commonly described, often occurring simultaneously with itching. Several studies reported mild swelling of the lips or perioral tissues, particularly following ingestion of nuts or certain fruits. These symptoms were consistently described as occurring rapidly after food exposure and resolving spontaneously without medical intervention.

Importantly, the descriptions of oral manifestations were similar across studies, regardless of geographic location or study design. Ivković-Jureković [19] and Bedolla-Barajas et al. [20] both reported oral pruritus as the dominant symptom, with secondary involvement of the throat and lips.

The results highlight that specific foods are repeatedly associated with oral manifestations in children. Fruits such as apple, peach, kiwi, melon, and mango were among the most frequently implicated triggers. Tree nuts and peanut were also commonly associated with oral symptoms, particularly swelling and oral discomfort. [27] These findings were consistent across studies conducted in Europe, Asia, Australia, and America. Rancé et al. [18] also reported that oral symptoms such as itching, tingling of the lips, and oral discomfort occurred in children with peanut allergy and, in some cases, preceded the onset of systemic allergic reactions. These findings indicate that oral manifestations may present as early features within the clinical course of food allergy. The study specifically describes oral symptoms occurring immediately after exposure and identifies their temporal association with subsequent systemic involvement, highlighting their clinical relevance in the recognition of allergic reactions.

Younger children were more likely to present with generalized food allergy, while oral manifestations became more apparent during school age, particularly in children with established pollen sensitization. The prospective cohort study by Kim et al. [26] demonstrated that food allergy in early childhood increased the likelihood of developing oral allergy syndrome later in life. As the children age and encounter repeated exposures to allergenic foods, localized oral reactions may emerge even when systemic reactions become less prominent.

Immunological mechanisms

The reviewed studies also showed a clear association between oral manifestations and other allergic conditions, particularly allergic rhinitis. Dondi et al. [23] reported oral itching in children with pollen-induced allergic rhinitis, indicating that respiratory and oral allergic responses often coexist. This association may be due to sensitization to airborne allergens which leads to cross-reactivity with structurally related food proteins.

The coexistence of food allergy, pollen sensitization, and oral manifestations highlights the interconnected nature of allergic diseases in children. Oral symptoms may therefore serve as an indicator of broader allergic conditions rather than an isolated oral reaction. [4] Together, these findings provide a coherent understanding of how oral manifestations fit within the spectrum of pediatric food allergy.

Limitations

Most included studies were observational in design, which limits the ability to establish a singular etiology behind food allergy and oral manifestations. Many studies relied on self-reported or parent-reported symptoms rather than standardized clinical assessments, which may have introduced reporting bias and variability in symptom recognition.

There was considerable heterogeneity in the diagnostic criteria used to define food allergy and oral manifestations. Some studies diagnosed a food allergy based on clinical history or questionnaires, while others included sensitization testing or specialist evaluation. Similarly, oral manifestations were often reported descriptively without standardized outcome measures, limiting comparability across studies.

In the data synthesis phase, the exclusion of gray literature and non-English publications may have contributed to publication bias and reduced the completeness of the evidence base.

Several studies did not include control groups of children without food allergy, which restricted the ability to estimate relative risk or quantify the strength of associations. Although this review included both IgE- and non-IgE-mediated food allergies within its scope, the available evidence was overwhelmingly derived from studies investigating IgE-mediated reactions, particularly oral allergy syndrome. As a result, the findings primarily reflect localized IgE-mediated mechanisms, while the role of non-IgE-mediated food allergies in oral manifestations remains insufficiently explored.

Finally, the absence of quantitative synthesis restricted the ability to statistically explore heterogeneity and assess small-study effects. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review.

Future research such as well-designed prospective studies with standardized diagnostic criteria for food allergy and validated assessment tools for oral manifestations would bolster the current evidence. Studies incorporating both oral and systemic outcomes, along with appropriate control groups, are necessary to better define the clinical significance and progression of these manifestations in pediatric populations.

Conclusion

This systematic review identifies the oral manifestations frequently associated with food allergy such as oral itching, tingling, and mild swelling of the lips and oral mucosa following ingestion of specific foods, particularly fruits and nuts. These manifestations represent localized IgE-mediated reactions and are often observed in children with pollen sensitization or a prior history of food allergy.

Clinical implications for dental practice

Pediatric dentists play a critical role in identifying and managing the oral health implications of food allergies and sensitivities. Their role extends beyond routine dental care to include early recognition of allergy-related oral changes, appropriate referral, and interdisciplinary coordination. During routine dental examinations, pediatric dentists may be the first healthcare professionals to observe signs suggestive of food allergy, such as oral mucosal irritation, localized swelling, or recurrent oral discomfort following food exposure. In some children, persistent oral mucosal inflammation may be associated with underlying allergic conditions, including eosinophilic esophagitis, further highlighting the importance of awareness of systemic allergic disease within dental practice. However, the role of non-IgE-mediated mechanisms in oral manifestations remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Further pediatric research using standardized oral assessment methods will strengthen understanding of these associations and help us form evidence-based dental care strategies.
