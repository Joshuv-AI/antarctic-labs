Digital psychological interventions in youth with neurological disorders: a systematic review
Authors: Yvette Keevers, Asmitha Sivaneswaran, Joanne Dudeney, Thomas Woldhuis, Milena Gandy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveYouth with neurological disorders (NDs) face increased risk of psychological difficulties and poor quality of life. This research aimed to systematically review the current state of evidence of digital psychological interventions (DPIs) for improving (1) psychological and/or (2) health-related outcomes in children and adolescents with NDs.MethodsSystematic searches were performed in four databases CENTRAL, EMBASE, PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and NeuroBITE between inception and 27 November 2024. Included studies were clinical trials (e.g., randomized controlled trials or single-group pre-post designs) investigating the efficacy of a DPI aimed at improving psychological and/or health-related outcomes in youth (<18 years) with NDs. Risk of bias was assessed via the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (RoB2) by two authors. Psychological outcomes included internalizing (e.g., depression), behavioral (e.g., externalizing symptoms), and/or cognitive symptoms (e.g., executive function). Health-related outcomes included physical symptoms (e.g., tics), adherence, and quality of life.ResultsTwenty studies (total participants = 1,508) met criteria and were included. Most studies (k = 10; 50%) focused on youth with traumatic brain injury. Findings revealed mixed trends of efficacy with 12 studies (60%) indicating that DPIs improved outcomes, mainly in behavioral outcomes. Limitations included multiple trials rated as having some concerns (k = 8; 47%) or high risk of bias (k = 5; 29%) due to poor methodological quality.ConclusionsDPIs may improve psychological and health-related outcomes; however, higher-quality research is needed to explore the potential of DPIs to address the psychological needs of youth with NDs.Keywords: digital psychological intervention; youth; neurological disorder, psychological outcomes; health-related outcomes

Neurological disorders (ND) comprise a range of conditions often resulting from disease or injury to the brain or nervous system (e.g., epilepsy, cerebral palsy [CP], headache disorders, traumatic brain injuries [TBI]; World Health Organization [WHO], 2020). NDs in youth account for a substantial share of the global disease burden, contributing both to early mortality and long-term disability (Newton, 2018). Due to a myriad of challenges associated with these conditions, it is unsurprising that psychological difficulties and comorbidities are common in youth with NDs compared to their peers without NDs (e.g. Baariu et al., 2022; Bjorgaas et al., 2013; Bennett et al., 2018; Whitney et al., 2019). For example, in the National Survey of Children’s Health 2016 in the United States, rates of anxiety problems for children aged 6–17 were reported as high as 33.2% and 30.1% in children diagnosed with epilepsy and CP, respectively. In comparison, the rate of anxiety in the control sample (i.e., children without NDs) was only 7.3%. Similar trends were observed for rates of depression, which were as high as 15.3% and 10.2% for children with intellectual disability and epilepsy, respectively, compared to only 3.4% for controls (Whitney et al., 2019).

The combination of psychological difficulties and managing an ND may deteriorate psychological and health-related outcomes. For instance, children with epilepsy face higher risks of anxiety, depression, and behavioral issues, which are associated with seizure unpredictability, stigma, emotional regulation difficulties, and medication side effects (Brodie et al., 2016; Dal Canto et al., 2018; Shanmuganathan et al., 2022). Youth with NDs and comorbid psychological disorders face greater negative outcomes compared to ND youth without these comorbidities, including poorer quality of life (QoL) (Healy et al., 2020), persistent pain (Tham et al., 2013), and reduced school functioning (O’Connor et al., 2012). These challenges may be further compounded by executive dysfunction and cognitive impairments, including intellectual disabilities and deficits in attention, memory, and visual-spatial functions, which are highly prevalent in youth with NDs (Fluss & Lidzba, 2020; Kim & Ko, 2016; Petranovich et al., 2020).

Despite the significant impact of psychological comorbidities in youth with NDs, many psychological difficulties are left untreated and unmanaged, highlighting gaps in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment (Barker et al., 2019; Fazel et al., 2021). These shortcomings in part stem from inadequate screening for psychological disorders, limited collaboration between neurologists and mental health professionals (Hesdorffer, 2016), and insufficient research to guide best practices (Gandy et al., 2021). Recognizing these gaps, the World Health Assembly has called for reforms to improve access to evidence-based psychosocial interventions, as outlined in the Intersectoral Global Action Plan (IGAP) on epilepsy and other neurological disorders (WHO, 2020, 2022). In response, various neurology organizations, including the National Institutes of Health (NIH; Whitney et al., 2019), have advocated for the integration of transdiagnostic psychological interventions into routine care within neurology and related medical settings to alleviate the burdens faced by youth with NDs.

Psychological interventions teach structured therapeutic strategies designed to address mental health, behavioral, and cognitive difficulties. These interventions are typically grounded in recognized therapies (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy [CBT]) and/or utilize psychotherapeutic support, psychoeducation, and skills development to support psychological outcomes. Evidence from individual trials suggests benefits such as improved attitudes toward illness, prosocial behavior, and reduced depression, anxiety, and behavioral difficulties in youth with epilepsy (Valizadeh et al., 2013; Carbone et al., 2014; Bennett et al., 2024), improved cognitive functioning and reduced anxiety and depression in youth with TBI (Treble-Barna et al., 2016; Soo et al., 2022), and reduced emotional and behavioral problems in youth with CP (Whittingham et al., 2014). However, these trials often have small sample sizes (e.g., Soo et al., 2022) and focus on single NDs (e.g., Whittingham et al., 2014) or a specific psychological outcome domain (Carbone et al., 2014).

Despite their potential efficacy, several barriers hinder delivery and access to psychological interventions for youth with NDs. These include a shortage of specialized mental health professionals, limited-service availability (especially in rural and low-income areas), and the high cost of traditional face-to-face therapy (Chan et al., 2022; Creutzfeldt et al., 2018; Gandy et al., 2018). Logistical challenges such as mobility issues, frequent hospitalizations, and competing school and family commitments further restrict access. Additionally, few neurology care settings globally have integrated psychological care services whereby youth are routinely offered access to, or referred on to, psychological interventions (Chan et al., 2022; Creutzfeldt et al., 2018). This gap is largely due to the financial and resource demands of embedding mental health specialists within neurological care settings (Gandy & Dudeney, 2024).

Digital psychological interventions (DPIs) offer a promising solution to accessibility barriers by delivering psychological skills through internet-based modules, either as standalone treatments, adjuncts, or in combination with face-to-face therapy (Andersson et al., 2018, 2019; Gandy et al., 2023). DPIs can be conceptualized as structured, theory-driven therapeutic programs utilizing structured online modules to provide therapeutic information and skills (Andersson et al., 2019). Typically delivered via web-based platforms or software, DPIs present content through text, video, or audio, often accompanied by homework tasks. They may be supported by clinician guidance or automated support functions if self-guided. DPIs provide several advantages, including cost-effectiveness, greater flexibility, reduced travel requirements, and improved access for underserved populations (Titov et al., 2019). Furthermore, there is growing consensus that youth may prefer to access psychological interventions through websites as they are highly accessible and familiar (Becker et al., 2016; Cunningham et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2021) with some evidence that they may be as efficacious as face-to-face interventions in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety in youth with NDs (Lehtimaki et al., 2021).

In the general population, considerable research now supports the equivalent efficacy of clinician-guided forms of internet-delivered psychological interventions compared with face-to-face care (Carlbring et al., 2018). In adults with a variety of NDs, a recent meta-analysis of 13 DPIs found that these interventions resulted in significant reductions in symptoms of depression and anxiety compared to controls (Gandy et al., 2024). However, this review focused solely on adult ND populations and measured only depression and anxiety. Emerging evidence from individual trials suggests DPIs may also benefit youth with NDs and improve both psychological and health-related outcomes in conditions such as epilepsy (Modi et al., 2019), TBI (Wade et al., 2017), tic disorders (Hollis et al., 2021), and CP (Whittingham et al., 2022). However, to the authors’ knowledge, no prior research has systematically reviewed the efficacy of DPIs for youth with a broad range of NDs and a broad range of outcomes. This information is important as it can inform the adaptation and optimization of DPIs for youth with NDs while laying the groundwork for further innovative, transdiagnostic strategies to better support youth with NDs.

This gap is particularly pertinent given the growing emphasis on transdiagnostic treatment approaches (Shah et al., 2020) and global public health efforts to improve broad psychosocial outcomes across all NDs and across the lifespan (WHO, 2022). Accordingly, this study aims to systematically review the literature on DPIs for youth with NDs, clarifying their role in improving both (1) psychological and/or (2) health-related outcomes.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was completed in compliance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Checklist (Page et al., 2021). The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023495419). The protocol was amended to include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing two or more active treatment arms.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they evaluated an online or DPI targeted for youth (<18) with an ND or for their caregiver(s) delivered via modules or learning systems in an online and/or digital format. Psychological interventions were defined as those based on a recognized therapy (e.g., CBT) or where the core focus of an intervention was to improve psychological outcomes involving some combination of psychotherapeutic support, psychoeducation, or skills development. Delivery formats included clinician- or self-guided, individual, or group-based. Participants were required to be <18 years old with an ND or the caregiver. NDs were defined by the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) classification system and consistent with previous research focused on those NDs typically managed within neurological care settings (Gandy et al., 2024). Eligible studies need to have measured at least one primary outcome in the domains of (1) psychological or (2) health-related outcomes measured using standardized youth or caregiver-reported measures. Psychological outcomes were broadly conceptualized as either internalizing symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety), behavioral symptoms (e.g., externalizing symptoms, compliance), or cognitive functioning (e.g., memory, executive functioning, attention). Health-related outcomes were conceptualized as either physical health symptoms (e.g., tics, migraine frequency), adherence (e.g., medication adherence), or QoL. These outcomes could be measured via either subjective (e.g., self-report) or objective (e.g., neuropsychological tests) symptom measures. Only peer-reviewed clinical trials (RCTs or single-group trials) were included, with both active (e.g., education) and inactive (e.g., waitlist) controls/comparators in RCTs accepted.

Exclusions applied to studies with participants aged ≥18 or presenting with purely symptom-based NDs (i.e., non-specific conditions such as headache or chronic pain) or where the only ND included was not primarily treated by neurology specialists (e.g., sleep disorders and neurodevelopmental behavioral disorders such as attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder and autism spectrum disorder). These criteria are in line with similar reviews in the adult ND literature (Gandy et al., 2024). Psychological interventions that could not be isolated from a multicomponent intervention were excluded. Case series and secondary analyses were also excluded. Additionally, interventions not delivered digitally, relying primarily on face-to-face components (>30%), or using only mobile communication (e.g., apps) were excluded.

Search strategy and study selection

The electronic databases EMBASE, PsycINFO, MEDLINE, NeuroBITE, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched from inception to November 27, 2024. The search strategy for the current review included keyword stems: (1) “Youth,” (2) “Neurological Disorders,” (3) “Psychological Treatments,” (4) “Digital,” and (5) “Clinical Trial.” Supplementary File S1 provides an example of the search strategy utilized. Reference lists were also reviewed to include any unidentified studies from the searches.

Selected studies were imported into EndNote 20.6 (2023), and duplicates were removed. Following this, Covidence (2024) was used to remove any remaining duplicates, and the title and abstract articles were independently screened by two authors (Y.K. and A.S.). Subsequently, full-text articles were double-screened by two independent authors (Y.K. and A.S.), with excellent inter-rater agreement (Kappa = .95). Discrepancies in evaluations were resolved via discussion by a third reviewer (M.G.).

Data extraction

Two authors (Y.K. and A.S.) independently extracted the relevant data from the eligible full-text articles into an Excel spreadsheet. Discrepancies were identified and resolved through consensus. Consistent with the PROSPERO protocol, data extracted included study details (author, year of publication, country), participant demographics (age, sex, ND diagnosis, total sample size), study design (RCT, single group), intervention characteristics (theoretical approach, setting, number of participants in condition [N], treatment type [e.g., CBT], type of technology used to deliver intervention, number of modules/weeks of intervention, level of human/therapist support), intervention target (child, caregiver, or both), control characteristics (N; type of control condition), primary outcome measures (type of validated pre-post measure used, caregiver measure or child measure), search findings (e.g., number of papers, information about interventions) and descriptive synthesis of the findings related to primary psychological and health-related outcomes (see Table 1).

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed by two independent reviewers (T.W. and A.G.). The Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool—Version 2 (RoB 2) was used to assess the risk of bias in the included RCT studies (Sterne et al., 2019). Studies were rated as having high risk, low risk, or some concerns by the two reviewers with excellent agreement (Kappa = .92). Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. Amendments were required in domain 2 and domain 4, specifically, we omitted items relating to concealment of intervention allocation to participants, as this is accepted practice in reviews of psychotherapy RCTs (Munder & Barth, 2018), given the inherent inability to blind participants to intervention. A quality assessment tool for pre-post studies with no control group under guidelines from the NIH (2021) was utilized for single-group studies. This tool evaluates the quality of studies based on 12 criteria (see Supplementary File S2 for a summary of the 12 criteria). Studies were rated as having good, fair, and bad risk based on reporting.

Results

Study selection

Following duplicate removal, 1,477 studies were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Following this, 158 full-text studies were reviewed, and 20 studies met eligibility criteria (see Supplementary File S3 for a list of references of studies that met eligibility criteria). The study selection process and reasons for exclusion for the systematic review are outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram in Figure 1.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection process.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are available in Table 1. The 20 eligible studies included 1508 youth with an ND or their caregivers. All studies focused on treating youth with a specific ND, with most (50%) studies focusing on youth with TBI or head trauma (k = 10), which were all conducted by the same research group in the United States. Four studies focused on treating youth with Tourette syndrome and other tic-related disorders (20%), three studies focused on migraine and other headache disorders (15%), and only one study each focused on treating youth with CP (5%) and epilepsy (5%). Several articles were excluded based on utilizing the same sample of a previous study of only family-reported measures. For example, three studies (Wade et al., 2015; Narad et al., 2015; Tlustos et al., 2016) analyzed outcomes from a previous included RCT (Wade et al., 2014).

Participant characteristics

Of the 18 studies that provided average age demographics, the average age was 12 years (range: 3–18 years). Most studies involved adolescent samples aged 10–18 (k = 11; 55%), six studies involved mixed adolescent and child samples (30%), and three studies involved only children under 10 years of age (15%). In the 17 studies that reported gender characteristics, the mean percentage of female participants was 39% (range: 4%–82%).

Study designs

Most studies were RCTs (k = 17; 85%), the remainder were single-arm pilot investigations (k = 3; 15%). Of the 17 RCTs, 20% included inactive control conditions involving treatment as usual (k = 2) or waitlist control (k = 2) with 50% using an active control comparison involving psychoeducation (k = 4), or an internet resource comparison (k = 6). Three studies (15%) compared active treatment arms.

Intervention characteristics

Table 2 provides a detailed summary of the intervention characteristics. Most interventions were based on principles of problem-solving therapy (k = 11; 42%) or traditional CBT (k = 6; 23%). Intervention duration ranged between 4 and 24 weeks.

Intervention targets

Of the 20 trials, 8 (40%) aimed to evaluate the benefits of a DPI in improving behavioral functioning in youth with TBI (Mast et al., 2014; Wade et al., 2005, 2006, 2008, 2014, 2017, 2018), or CP (Whittingham et al., 2022). Four studies aimed to improve executive functioning in youth with TBI (Wade et al., 2005, 2008, 2010, 2018) and one in epilepsy (Modi et al., 2019). The remaining 10 (50%) reported that the primary aim was to evaluate the benefits of DPIs to improve specific ND-related symptoms, including tic severity (Andrén et al., 2019, 2022; Hollis et al., 2021; Rachamim et al., 2022), headache symptoms (Law et al., 2015; Trautmann & Kröner-Herwig, 2008, 2010), and post-concussive symptoms (Kurowski et al., 2015; Wade et al., 2019, 2023). Two of these studies additionally aimed to improve the QoL in youth with TBI (Wade et al., 2019, 2023).

Intervention delivery

Eighteen (90%) studies included weekly or fortnightly clinician support (i.e., clinician-guided) to encourage engagement, understanding, review content, and/or review completion of homework exercises. The remaining two studies involved a self-guided format with no input from a clinician.

Overall, most RCTs were rated some concern for risk of bias (k = 8; 47%), five indicated high risk (29%), and four were low risk (24%). Intervention acceptability and efficacy

Due to significant heterogeneity between outcomes and a lack of appropriate data reporting of some studies, we could not perform a meta-analysis. Instead, the results are summarized in relation to those that reported significant and non-significant effects on primary outcome measures. Eighteen studies reported dropout rates, with an average rate of 14% (range: 0%–38%).

Psychological outcomes

Seven of 10 studies (70%) reported significant intervention effects on a psychological outcome.

Internalizing symptoms

Only one study focused on internalizing symptoms, finding improved internalizing symptoms and depression in youth with TBI following the DPI (Wade et al., 2008).

Behavioral symptoms

Positive results were described in five of eight (63%) studies measuring behavioral symptoms. These studies reported reduced intensity of behavior problems (Mast et al., 2014; Wade et al., 2017), antisocial behavior (Wade et al., 2005), caregiver–adolescent conflict and problem issues (Wade et al., 2008), and improved self-control and compliance in youth with TBI (Wade et al., 2006). The latter study also included a 6-month follow-up, which reported that treatment effects were sustained. Two RCTs failed to find significant changes in a measure of internalizing and externalizing behavior problems in the intervention group compared to the control group in youth with TBI (Wade et al., 2014, 2018).

Cognitive functioning

Of the five studies that measured executive function, significant results were only described in one study (20%) with a single-group design. It found improved subjective executive functioning in youth with epilepsy (Modi et al., 2019). The four studies that found no executive functioning improvements involved youth with TBI (Wade et al., 2005, 2008, 2010, 2018), one of which was a single-group study (Wade et al., 2005), while the other three were RCTs (Wade et al., 2005, 2010, 2018).

Health-related outcomes

Ten studies (50%) were primarily concerned with health-related outcomes. No studies assessed adherence-based outcomes.

Physical health outcomes

Positive results were described in five of ten studies measuring somatic symptoms, including reduced tic severity (Andrén et al., 2019; Hollis et al., 2021; Rachamim et al., 2022), cognitive and somatic symptoms of TBI (Wade et al., 2019), and post-concussive symptoms (Kurowski et al., 2015). One single-group study noted this effect was expected with the natural passage of time (Kurowski et al., 2015). Additionally, two studies included a control condition and reported that treatment effects were observed in the treatment but not the control group, with treatment effects being sustained at follow-up (Hollis et al., 2021; Rachamim et al., 2022). The remaining five studies (50%) failed to find significant differences in physical symptom improvement, that is, headache symptoms (Law et al., 2015; Trautmann & Kröner-Herwig, 2008, 2010), tic severity (Andrén et al., 2022), and brain injury symptoms (Wade et al., 2023), between treatment groups and control.

Quality of life

Of the two studies that measured QoL, one reported improved QoL in two online treatments compared to face-to-face treatment (Wade et al., 2019), while one failed to find significant differences in QoL between the DPI and treatment as usual (Wade et al., 2023).

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes the literature on DPIs for improving psychological and health-related outcomes in youth with NDs. As the first review to examine a broad range of NDs, we identified 20 clinical trials (17 RCTs, 3 single-group) exploring various DPIs across five ND groups. Most trials were focused on children managing TBI, with other underrepresented NDs. Most studies were concerned with either improving behavioral functioning or specific ND-related somatic symptoms. Many reported a high or uncertain risk of bias, namely due to problems with intention-to-treat analyses, missing outcome data, and selection and reporting of results. Moreover, sample sizes were small, with five trials including fewer than 20 participants each. While the review cannot make firm conclusions about the efficacy of interventions, some notable trends were observed.

DPI delivery

Most studies employed a modular approach via internet platforms, integrating multimedia content such as videos and interactive components. Within the extended literature, multimedia has been recognized as an effective avenue in fostering understanding in DPIs and may be particularly relevant for youth (Blake et al., 2023). Most studies provided weekly comprehensive clinician guidance or therapist support. Although no clear trend was observed between therapist involvement and intervention efficacy, prior meta-analyses suggest therapist support enhances adherence and engagement in DPIs (Beugen et al., 2014; Johansson & Andersson, 2012; Rickwood & Bradford, 2012). Given the limited understanding of engagement predictors in youth with NDs, future research should examine optimal forms of delivery including therapist involvement.

Psychological outcomes

Positive trends were observed in behavioral outcomes, with most studies measuring this domain reporting improvements. Notably, all three caregiver-targeted interventions were efficacious in enhancing behavioral functioning in youth with TBI and CP by fostering attachment and improving caregiver–child interactions (Wade et al., 2017; Mast et al., 2014; Whittingham et al., 2022). Although the limited number of caregiver-focused interventions restricts definitive conclusions, these findings align with a substantial body of literature identifying parent-focused psychological treatments as the “gold standard” for supporting children with challenging behaviors (Mingebach et al., 2018). For children and youth with NDs, previous systematic reviews have supported the efficacy of face-to-face parenting interventions in improving behavioral outcomes for youth with TBI (Brown et al., 2013), and CP (Whittingham et al., 2011), specifically.

In contrast, conjoint caregiver–youth problem-solving interventions for TBI yielded mixed results, diverging from support for these interventions in a previous review (Corti et al., 2019), likely due to our stricter eligibility criteria (e.g. exclusion of secondary analyses and long-term follow-up) and focus on primary outcomes. The lack of reporting transparency in studies identified and excluded in our search, of which many were included in Corti et al.’s (2019) review, led to significant ambiguity in determining secondary analyses, duplicates, and long-term follow-ups, underscoring the need for further transparency in reporting within this field.

Regarding cognitive functioning, all trials targeted subjective executive function, with only one DPI showing positive effects in youth with epilepsy (Modi et al., 2019), while four TBI trials found no benefit (Wade et al., 2005, 2008, 2010, 2018). This aligns with a previous review of digital and face-to-face psychological interventions in youth with TBI (k = 11) (Ross et al., 2011), suggesting DPIs may have limited efficacy for improving cognitive outcomes for this population. However, our observed trends may not generalize to other cognitive domains of functioning (e.g., memory) or other NDs.

Health-related outcomes

DPIs grounded in CBT showed promise in reducing tic severity in Tourette syndrome, with three studies reporting significant symptom reductions (Hollis et al., 2021; Rachamim et al., 2022). Although conclusions must be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of studies, this trend is consistent with a large-scale review (k = 70) of different treatment strategies for youth with Tourette syndrome, finding face-to-face CBIT to be effective in reducing tic severity (Hollis et al., 2016). In contrast, DPIs for migraine and headache-related disorders failed to improve somatic symptoms, mirroring meta-analytic findings in both youth and adults (Sharpe et al., 2019; Trautmann et al., 2006). Notably, active control conditions in these trials may have attenuated treatment effects due to the uncontrollability of non-specific effects of treatments (Edmond et al., 2019). It is also possible that physical symptoms require more time to improve, and this was not captured within the post-treatment time periods of the included studies. In addition, psychological interventions are primarily designed to target psychological processes rather than the physiological or behavioral mechanisms that are likely to drive physical symptom improvement. As such, the hypothesized mechanisms of change for these interventions may not directly influence physical outcomes. Taken together, such trends raise a crucial question about prioritizing physical symptom relief in psychological interventions for this population. Notably, as headache frequency increases, the associated impacts on anxiety and depression also intensify (Oh et al., 2014), yet most psychological interventions focused on pain management fail to address these comorbid psychological health issues effectively (Fisher et al., 2014). Future DPIs should incorporate broader psychological health support for this population.

Limitations of DPI research and future directions

Notably, only one of the included trials targeted internalizing symptoms (i.e., depression) as a primary outcome, despite extensive evidence that youth with NDs experience disproportionately high rates of these psychological difficulties, which have the potential to exacerbate ND challenges. For instance, depression can increase the development of epileptic activity in youth (Coppola et al., 2019). This is a striking omission given the well-established psychological burden in this population and may reflect a broader trend in the field to prioritize ND-specific or functional outcomes over mental health. While some studies assessed symptoms of depression and/or anxiety as secondary outcomes (i.e., Hollis et al., 2021; Law et al., 2015; Rachamim et al., 2022; Trautmann & Kröner-Herwig, 2010), our inclusion criteria focused on primary outcomes, and therefore these data were not captured, thus reflecting a limitation of the current review. Nonetheless, this raises a critical point; psychological health needs are not being foregrounded in the design and evaluation of DPIs for this population. Moreover, although face-to-face (e.g., Godara et al., 2024) and telephone assisted psychological interventions (e.g., Modi et al., 2021) have demonstrated efficacy in improving health-related factors such as medication adherence, we did not identify any DPI trials in this area.

Although excluded from the present review due to definitions of DPIs as web-based or platform-delivered interventions (e.g., Fisher et al., 2019), it is important to acknowledge related seminal work that incorporated elements of remote delivery for youth with NDs. Notable examples include the Mental Health Intervention for Youth with Epilepsy (MICE) trial, delivered via telephone or videoconferencing software (Bennett et al., 2024) and the Supporting Treatment Adherence Regimens (STAR) adherence intervention, which utilized telephone sessions (Modi et al., 2021). These trials showed promising effects in youth with epilepsy, MICE on comorbid mental health symptoms, and STAR on treatment adherence. Notably, the MICE trial adopted a modular, transdiagnostic protocol targeting shared psychological difficulties across conditions.

Of note, most studies in this review used disorder-specific protocols targeting a single ND (e.g., TBI) and one outcome domain. Currently, adult neurology research advocates for transdiagnostic digital interventions that address shared psychological and functional challenges across NDs (e.g., Gandy et al., 2020), aligning with the IGAP call for evidence-based solutions for psychosocial issues (WHO, 2020, 2022). By focusing on shared psychological needs, transdiagnostic approaches could enhance the scalability, scope and efficacy of interventions. However, this review found little effort to apply such models to youth, highlighting a key research gap in DPI development. Together, the current review in combination with those that utilize telephone and videoconferencing features highlight that multiple remote formats can be leveraged to improve access to care for youth with NDs. This may be particularly pertinent for older teens and adolescents with NDs who experience significant psychological challenges when transitioning into adult neurological care services (e.g., McGovern et al., 2021).

Some further limitations of the included studies relate to the varied acceptability of the interventions, with dropout as high as 38%. Higher dropout rates were linked to severe ND symptoms, non-white participants (Wade et al., 2018, 2019), and busy schedules (Modi et al., 2019). Many participants also failed to complete all modules, raising concerns about therapy dosage. The digital cumulative complexity model (DiCuCoM; Cross & Alvarez-Jimenez, 2024) explains engagement issues as an imbalance between workload (treatment/life demands) and capacity (illness burden/resources), where excess workload reduces adherence. As digital access alone does not eliminate barriers, future DPIs should aim for this balance. Co-design, that is, involving patients in the design, development, and implementation of DPIs, may be an important consideration for enhancing future intervention accessibility, engagement, acceptability, and effectiveness (Geirhos et al., 2021; Porche et al., 2022). In fact, no studies included in this review explicitly adopted a co-design approach, indicating an important avenue for future research. Promising examples exist in related telehealth literature, for instance, the MICE trials treatment protocol was co-developed with adolescent and caregiver input (Bennett et al., 2024).

Limitations of the current review

Despite its strengths (e.g., comprehensive scope, rigorous study selection), this review has several limitations. The small number of studies limits conclusions, and methodological heterogeneity prevented a meta-analysis. Findings are also skewed by the predominance of TBI-focused studies, reducing generalizability to the broader youth with NDs population. Methodological issues, including a high risk of bias and unclear treatment completion criteria, further affect reliability. Given the high risk of bias included in these studies, particularly regarding lack of pre-specified protocols, missing outcome data, and deviations from the intended intervention, future trials are recommended to consider these factors in their reporting and utilize tools such as the RoB 2 (Sterne et al., 2019) to reduce risk of bias and enhance the quality of their reporting. Lastly, as most studies (69%) were conducted in the U.S., results may not reflect diverse cultural and healthcare contexts.

Conclusion

In summary, this review synthesized research on DPIs for youth with NDs, revealing mixed findings. While some benefits were noted, particularly in reducing behavioral problems, evidence for cognitive and health-related outcomes garnered less support and attention. Significant methodological issues limit firm conclusions, highlighting the need for higher-quality research with regard to reducing missing outcome data and adhering to the intended intervention protocols. Given the high psychological comorbidity in youth with NDs, identifying effective, accessible treatments is crucial. Although DPIs are often considered a solution to care gaps, this review underscores the need for further rigorous evaluation and thoughtful development of DPIs that target broader outcomes. Furthermore, co-development of interventions and greater understanding about the acceptability and implementation factors around these DPIs unique to youth with NDs may serve to improve the design, efficacy, and uptake of these promising forms of treatments.
