Cullen and Woodall numbers in Padovan and Perrin sequences
Herbert Batte, Eric F. Bravo, Florian Luca

Abstract
Let { P n } n ≥ 0 \{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0} and { R n } n ≥ 0 \{R_{n}\}_{n\geq 0} denote the Padovan and Perrin sequences, both satisfying the recurrence U n + 3 = U n + 1 + U n U_{n+3}=U_{n+1}+U_{n} , but with initial values P 0 = P 1 = P 2 = 1 P_{0}=P_{1}=P_{2}=1 and R 0 = 3 R_{0}=3 , R 1 = 0 R_{1}=0 , R 2 = 2 R_{2}=2 , respectively. A Cullen number is a positive integer of the form m ⋅ 2 m + 1 m\cdot 2^{m}+1 for some integer m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 , while a Woodall number is a positive integer of the form m ⋅ 2 m − 1 m\cdot 2^{m}-1 for some integer m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 . In this paper, we determine all Woodall numbers in the Padovan sequence and all Cullen numbers in the Perrin sequence. Specifically, we prove that 1 1 and 7 7 are the only Woodall numbers in the Padovan sequence, and that 3 3 is the only Cullen number in the Perrin sequence.



Keywords and phrases
: Cullen number; Padovan number; Perrin number;
Woodall number;
2
-adic valuation; Linear forms in logarithms.

Introduction

In 1905, Reverend James Cullen
[
6
]
published the first study on the primality of numbers of the form
m\cdot 2^{m}+1
. He realized that, apart from
1\cdot 2^{1}+1=3
and with the possible exception of
53\cdot 2^{53}+1
, the first hundred of these numbers are composite. A year later, Cunningham
[
7
]
published the finding that
5591
divides
53\cdot 2^{53}+1
. The first non-trivial prime number of this form was discovered by Robinson
[
14
]
nearly fifty year later, who confirmed Cunningham’s suspicion that
141\cdot 2^{141}+1
was prime. These numbers are now called
Cullen numbers
. We denote them by
C_{m}
for every positive integer
m
. A Cullen number that is prime is called a Cullen prime.

In 1917, Cunningham and Woodall
[
8
]
, inspired by Cullen’s work, published the first paper on the primality of numbers of the form
m\cdot 2^{m}-1
. They proved that there were only three prime numbers of this form in the range
1\leq m\leq 29
, namely
2\cdot 2^{2}-1
,
3\cdot 2^{3}-1
, and
6\cdot 2^{6}-1
. These numbers are now called
Woodall numbers
, and primes of this form are called Woodall primes. We denote them by
W_{m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

In the mid-twentieth century, mathematicians wondered how often Cullen and Woodall primes appeared. In 1976, Hooley
[
9
]
showed that the natural density of positive integers
m\leq x
for which
C_{m}
is prime is of the order
o(x)
when
x\rightarrow\infty
. In other words, almost all of Cullen’s numbers are composite. Hooley’s proof was reworked by Hiromi Suyama to show that it works for any sequence of numbers of the form
m\cdot 2^{m+a}+b
, where
a
and
b
are integers, and in particular also for Woodall numbers. To date, sixteen Culllen primes and thirty-four Woodall primes are known. The largest are
C_{6679881}
and
W_{17016602}
. They were discovered through distributed computing and citizen collaboration via the international PrimeGrid project.

In 2003, Luca and Stănică
[
11
]
began the search for Cullen and Woodall numbers in linear recurrence sequences. They proved that there are only a finite number of them in any binary recurrence sequence that satisfies certain conditions. They applied their result to the Fibonacci and Pell sequences. They found that
1
and
3
are the only Cullen numbers that are also Fibonacci numbers, and
1
is the only Woodall number that is also a Fibonacci number; while the only Cullen number and the only Woodall number present in the Pell sequence is
1
. Recently, Alahmadi and Luca
[
1
]
proved that there is no Cullen number that is also a repunit number.

Regarding the presence of Cullen numbers and Woodall numbers in recurrent linear sequences of order greater than
2
, we have the recent results of Bérczes, Pink, and Young
[
2
]
, and Bravo and Irmak
[
4
]
. Bérczes et al.
[
2
]
considered the
k
-generalized Fibonacci sequence, which is a linear recurrent sequence for each fixed order
k\geq 2
. For
k=2
and
k=3
, this generalization gives the Fibonacci sequence and the Tribonacci sequence, respectively. They proved that
1
is the unique Cullen number that is also a
k
-generalized Fibonacci number for all
k>2
. Furthermore, they showed that
1
is a Woodall number that is present in any
k
-generalized Fibonacci sequence for all
k>2
, while
3
is a Woodall number that is only present in the Tribonacci sequence.

For their part, Bravo and Irmak
[
4
]
considered a pair of linear recurrent ternary sequences: the Padovan and Perrin sequences. The
Padovan numbers

\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are defined by the Fibonacci-like recurrence relation

P_{n+3}=P_{n+1}+P_{n}

with initial values
P_{0}=P_{1}=P_{2}=1
. The
Perrin numbers

\{R_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are defined by the same recurrence relation as the
Padovan numbers but with starting values
R_{0}=3
,
R_{1}=0
, and
R_{2}=2
.

Bravo and Irmak
[
4
]
determined that
3
,
9
, and
65
are all the Cullen numbers appearing in the Padovan sequence, and that
7
is the only Woodall number appearing in the Perrin sequence. Their approach combined a precise analysis of the
2
-adic valuations of

\{P_{n}-1\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{R_{n}+1\}_{n\geq 0}
to obtain absolute bounds, followed by a computational verification.

It is natural then to ask for the complementary results. What are all the Woodall numbers in the Padovan sequence, and what are all the Cullen numbers in the Perrin sequence? In this paper, we answer both questions completely. Our approach parallels that of

[
4
]
, but requires a new and careful analysis of the
2
-adic valuations of
\{P_{n}+1\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{R_{n}-1\}_{n\geq 0}
, which
are the objects relevant to Woodall numbers in Padovan and Cullen numbers in Perrin, respectively. We prove the following results.

1.1
Main Results

Theorem 1.1
.

The only Woodall numbers in the Padovan sequence are
1
and
7
.

Corollary 1.2
.

The only Woodall prime in the Padovan sequence is
7
.

Theorem 1.3
.

The only Cullen number in the Perrin sequence is
3
.

Corollary 1.4
.

The only Cullen prime in the Perrin sequence is
3
.

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
combine an application of Baker’s theory of linear forms in three logarithms of algebraic numbers and the
2
-adic valuation of
\{P_{n}+1\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{R_{n}-1\}_{n\geq 0}
, followed by a computational verification.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The Padovan and Perrin sequences

In this section, we collect some useful formulas and inequalities concerning Padovan and Perrin numbers. We begin with a closed formula that allows us to directly calculate the nth Perrin number.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

R_{n}=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n}+\gamma^{n},

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are the zeros of
\phi(X):=X^{3}-X-1
.

Proof.

Since
R_{n+3}-R_{n+1}-R_{n}=0
, the characteristic polynomial of the Perrin sequence is
\phi(X)
. This polynomial has one real root
\alpha
and two complex conjugate roots
\beta
and
\gamma
given by

\alpha=\sqrt[3]{\frac{9+\sqrt{69}}{18}}+\sqrt[3]{\frac{9-\sqrt{69}}{18}},\quad\beta=-\frac{\alpha}{2}+i\frac{\sqrt{3\alpha^{2}-4}}{2},\quad\gamma=\overline{\beta}.

Since the zeros of
\phi(X)
are distinct, there exist unique constants
c_{\alpha},c_{\beta},c_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha,\beta)
such that

R_{n}=c_{\alpha}\alpha^{n}+c_{\beta}\beta^{n}+c_{\gamma}\gamma^{n}\quad\text{for all }n\geq 0.

To determine
c_{\alpha}
,
c_{\beta}
, and
c_{\gamma}
, we solve the system of equations:

\displaystyle V\begin{pmatrix}c_{\alpha}\\
c_{\beta}\\
c_{\gamma}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\
0\\
2\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad V:=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\alpha^{2}&\beta^{2}&\gamma^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

It follows from Vieta’s formulas that the zeros
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
of
\phi(X)
must satisfy

\alpha+\beta+\gamma=-\frac{0}{1}=0,\quad\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha=\frac{-1}{1}=-1\quad{\text{\rm and}}\quad\alpha\beta\gamma=-\frac{-1}{1}=1.

Therefore,

\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}=(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)^{2}-2(\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha)=0^{2}-2(-1)=2.

Using Cramer’s rule, we get

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}3&1&1\\
0&\beta&\gamma\\
2&\beta^{2}&\gamma^{2}\end{vmatrix}}{|V|}=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}1+1+1&1&1\\
\alpha+\beta+\gamma&\beta&\gamma\\
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}&\beta^{2}&\gamma^{2}\end{vmatrix}}{|V|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}1&1&1\\
\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\alpha^{2}&\beta^{2}&\gamma^{2}\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}1&1&1\\
\beta&\beta&\gamma\\
\beta^{2}&\beta^{2}&\gamma^{2}\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}1&1&1\\
\gamma&\beta&\gamma\\
\gamma^{2}&\beta^{2}&\gamma^{2}\end{vmatrix}}{|V|}

\displaystyle=\frac{|V|+0+0}{|V|}=1,

where, in the penultimate equation, we use the fact that the determinant of a matrix is zero if it has two identical columns. Similarly, we find that
c_{\beta}=c_{\gamma}=1
. Now, we prove by strong induction that

R_{-n}=\alpha^{-n}+\beta^{-n}+\gamma^{-n}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 1.

Since
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are zeros of
\phi(X)
, it follows that
\alpha^{-1}
,
\beta^{-1}
,
\gamma^{-1}
are zeros of

X^{3}\phi(1/X)=1-X^{2}-X^{3}.

By applying Vieta’s formulas to
X^{3}\phi(1/X)
we get that

\alpha^{-1}+\beta^{-1}+\gamma^{-1}=-1\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad(\alpha\beta)^{-1}+(\beta\gamma)^{-1}+(\gamma\alpha)^{-1}=0.

Hence,

\alpha^{-2}+\beta^{-2}+\gamma^{-2}=(\alpha^{-1}+\beta^{-1}+\gamma^{-1})^{2}-2[(\alpha\beta)^{-1}+(\beta\gamma)^{-1}+(\gamma\alpha)^{-1}]=(-1)^{2}-2(0)=1.

Since
R_{-1}=R_{2}-R_{0}=-1
and
R_{-2}=R_{1}-R_{-1}=1
, we conclude that the statement holds for
n=1
and
n=2
. To finish the proof of the base case, note that

\displaystyle R_{-3}=R_{0}-R_{-2}=3-1

\displaystyle=3-(\alpha^{-2}+\beta^{-2}+\gamma^{-2})=(1-\alpha^{-2})+(1-\beta^{-2})+(1-\gamma^{-2})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-3}+\beta^{-3}+\gamma^{-3}.

Suppose that
R_{-k}=\alpha^{-k}+\beta^{-k}+\gamma^{-k}
for all
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Then

\displaystyle R_{-n}=R_{-n+3}-R_{-n+1}

\displaystyle=R_{-(n-3)}-R_{-(n-1)}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-(n-3)}+\beta^{-(n-3)}+\gamma^{-(n-3)}-(\alpha^{-(n-1)}+\beta^{-(n-1)}+\gamma^{-(n-1)})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-n}(\alpha^{3}-\alpha)+\beta^{-n}(\beta^{3}-\beta)+\gamma^{-n}(\gamma^{3}-\gamma)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-n}+\beta^{-n}+\gamma^{-n}.

∎

For the Padovan sequence, a Binet formula is given below.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

P_{n}=c_{\alpha}\alpha^{n}+c_{\beta}\beta^{n}+c_{\gamma}\gamma^{n},

where
c_{x}:=(x^{2}+x)/(3x^{2}-1)
for all
x\in\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}
.

Proof.

Let
P(X)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}X^{n}
be the generating function of the Padovan sequence; then

\displaystyle P(X)

\displaystyle=1+X+X^{2}+\displaystyle\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}(P_{n-2}+P_{n-3})X^{n}

\displaystyle=1+X+X^{2}+X^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{n}X^{n}+X^{3}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}X^{n}

\displaystyle=1+X+X^{2}+X^{2}(P(X)-P_{0})+X^{3}P(X),

so

P(X)=\frac{1+X}{1-X^{2}-X^{3}}.

Since the roots of
1-X^{2}-X^{3}=0
are distinct, by partial fractions

P(X)=\frac{1+X}{(1-\alpha X)(1-\beta X)(1-\gamma X)}=\frac{c_{\alpha}}{(1-\alpha X)}+\frac{c_{\beta}}{(1-\beta X)}+\frac{c_{\gamma}}{(1-\gamma X)}.

Here,

c_{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha^{2}+\alpha}{3\alpha^{2}-1},\quad c_{\beta}=\frac{\beta^{2}+\beta}{3\beta^{2}-1},\quad c_{\gamma}=\frac{\gamma^{2}+\gamma}{3\gamma^{2}-1}.

In the above we have used that
X^{3}-X-1=(X-\alpha)(X-\beta)(X-\gamma)
implies that

3X^{2}-1=(X-\beta)(X-\gamma)+(X-\alpha)(X-\gamma)+(X-\alpha)(X-\beta),

and so

3\alpha^{2}-1=(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha-\gamma),\quad 3\beta^{2}-1=(\beta-\alpha)(\beta-\gamma),\quad{\text{\rm and}}\quad 3\gamma^{2}-1=(\gamma-\alpha)(\gamma-\beta).

Consequently,

P(X)=c_{\alpha}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\alpha X)^{n}+c_{\beta}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\beta X)^{n}+c_{\gamma}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\gamma X)^{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(c_{\alpha}\alpha^{n}+c_{\beta}\beta^{n}+c_{\gamma}\gamma^{n})X^{n}.

Thus, Binet’s formula for the Padovan sequence is

P_{n}=c_{\alpha}\alpha^{n}+c_{\beta}\beta^{n}+c_{\gamma}\gamma^{n}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 0.

This formula can be extended to negative integers using, for example, strong mathematical induction. We omit the details.
∎

The following growth estimates hold for both sequences.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all non-negative integer
n
, we have

P_{n}\geq\alpha^{n-2}.

Proof.

We prove this result by strong induction on
n
. If
n=0,1,2
, then
P_{0}=1>0.57>\alpha^{-2}
,
P_{1}=1>0.76>\alpha^{-1}
, and
P_{2}=1\geq 1=\alpha^{0}
, respectively. Suppose that
P_{k}\geq\alpha^{k-2}
for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
. Thus, using the recurrence relation of the Padovan sequence and the fact that
\alpha^{-1}
is a zero of
X^{3}\phi(1/X)
, we obtain

P_{n}=P_{n-2}+P_{n-3}\geq\alpha^{n-4}+\alpha^{n-5}=\alpha^{n-2}(\alpha^{-2}+\alpha^{-3})=\alpha^{n-2}.

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

For all integer
n\geq 7
, we have

R_{n}-1>\alpha^{n-1}.

Proof.

We prove this result by strong induction on
n
. If
n=7,8,9
, then
R_{7}-1=6>5.41>\alpha^{6}
,
R_{8}-1=9>7.16>\alpha^{7}
, and
R_{9}-1=11>9.49>\alpha^{8}
, respectively. Suppose that
R_{k}-1>\alpha^{k-1}
for all
7\leq k\leq n-1
. Thus, using the recurrence relation of the Perrin sequence and the fact that
\alpha^{-1}
is a root of
X^{3}\phi(1/X)=0
, we obtain

R_{n}-1=R_{n-2}+R_{n-3}-1>(\alpha^{n-3}+1)+(\alpha^{n-4}+1)-1=\alpha^{n-1}(\alpha^{-2}+\alpha^{-3})+1>\alpha^{n-1}.

∎

Lemma 2.5
.

For all positive integers
r,s
we have the relations:

\displaystyle P_{r+s}

\displaystyle=P_{s-1}P_{r-1}+P_{s-2}P_{r}+P_{s-3}P_{r-2},

\displaystyle R_{r+s}

and

The following lemma provides doubling recurrences for the
Perrin numbers at indices
7\cdot 2^{k}
and
-7\cdot 2^{k}
,
which will be essential in the computational reduction step.

Lemma 2.10
.

The following hold.

(i)

R_{7}=7
,
R_{14}=51
,
R_{28}=2627
,

R_{-7}=-1
,
R_{-14}=-13
,
R_{-28}=67
.

(ii)

For all
k\geq 2
,

R_{7\cdot 2^{k+1}}=R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}^{2}-2R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}.

(iii)

For all
k\geq 2
,

R_{-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}=R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}^{2}-2R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}.

Proof.

Part (i) follows by direct computation from Lemma
2.1
. For part (ii),
we use Lemma
2.1
to expand

\displaystyle R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(\alpha^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\gamma^{7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+\beta^{7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+\gamma^{7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+2\big[(\alpha\beta)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\alpha\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\beta\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}\big].

Since
\alpha\beta\gamma=1
by Vieta’s formulas applied
to
\phi(X)
, we have

\alpha\beta=\gamma^{-1}
,
\alpha\gamma=\beta^{-1}
,
and
\beta\gamma=\alpha^{-1}
. Therefore,

(\alpha\beta)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\alpha\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\beta\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}=\alpha^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\gamma^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}=R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}.

Substituting back gives

R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}^{2}=R_{7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+2R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}},

from which part (ii) follows immediately.
For part (iii), we expand
R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}^{2}
using Lemma
2.1
as

\displaystyle R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(\alpha^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\gamma^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+\beta^{-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+\gamma^{-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+2\big[(\alpha\beta)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\alpha\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\beta\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big].

The first three terms give
R_{-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}
. For the
remaining terms, since
\alpha\beta\gamma=1
, we have

(\alpha\beta)^{-1}=\gamma
,
(\alpha\gamma)^{-1}=\beta
,
and
(\beta\gamma)^{-1}=\alpha
. Therefore,

(\alpha\beta)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\alpha\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\beta\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}=\gamma^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\alpha^{7\cdot 2^{k}}=R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}.

Substituting back gives

R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}^{2}=R_{-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+2R_{7\cdot 2^{k}},

from which part (iii) follows immediately.
∎

Further, the following lemma provides index-shifting recurrences for any sequence satisfying the Padovan recurrence, and will be applied to both
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{R_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
in the reduction steps of the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be any sequence satisfying
the recurrence
U_{n+3}=U_{n+1}+U_{n}
, and let
j
be
any integer. Then for all
k\geq 1
,

(i)

U_{j-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}=-U_{j}R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}+U_{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}+U_{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}},

(ii)

U_{j+7\cdot 2^{k+1}}=-U_{j}R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+U_{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}+U_{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}.

Proof.

Let the Binet formula of
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be

U_{n}=a\alpha^{n}+b\beta^{n}+c\gamma^{n},

for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha,\beta)
.
We prove part (i) by expanding
U_{j}R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}
using both Binet formulas as follows:

\displaystyle U_{j}R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(a\alpha^{j}+b\beta^{j}+c\gamma^{j}\right)\big(\alpha^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\gamma^{7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(a\alpha^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}+b\beta^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}+c\gamma^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)+a\alpha^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big[(\alpha\beta)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\alpha\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}\big]

\displaystyle\quad+b\beta^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big[(\beta\alpha)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\beta\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}\big]+c\gamma^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big[(\gamma\alpha)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\gamma\beta)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}\big].

Since
\alpha\beta\gamma=1
, we have
(\alpha\beta)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}=\gamma^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}
,
(\alpha\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}=\beta^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}
, and
(\beta\gamma)^{7\cdot 2^{k}}=\alpha^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}
. Substituting these relations and
regrouping, we obtain

\displaystyle U_{j}R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}

\displaystyle=U_{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}+\big(a\alpha^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}+b\beta^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}+c\gamma^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)\big(\alpha^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\gamma^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\big(a\alpha^{j-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+b\beta^{j-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+c\gamma^{j-7\cdot 2^{k+1}}\big)

\displaystyle=U_{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}+U_{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}-U_{j-7\cdot 2^{k+1}},

from which part (i) follows immediately. For part (ii), we expand
U_{j}R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}
using
both Binet formulas as follows:

\displaystyle U_{j}R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(a\alpha^{j}+b\beta^{j}+c\gamma^{j}\right)\big(\alpha^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\gamma^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(a\alpha^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}+b\beta^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}+c\gamma^{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)+a\alpha^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}\big[(\alpha\beta)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\alpha\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big]

\displaystyle\quad+b\beta^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}\big[(\beta\alpha)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\beta\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big]+c\gamma^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}\big[(\gamma\alpha)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}+(\gamma\beta)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}\big].

Since
\alpha\beta\gamma=1
, we have

(\alpha\beta)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}=\gamma^{7\cdot 2^{k}}
,

(\alpha\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}=\beta^{7\cdot 2^{k}}
,
and
(\beta\gamma)^{-7\cdot 2^{k}}=\alpha^{7\cdot 2^{k}}
.
Substituting these relations and regrouping, we obtain

\displaystyle U_{j}R_{-7\cdot 2^{k}}

\displaystyle=U_{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}+\big(a\alpha^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}+b\beta^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}+c\gamma^{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)\big(\alpha^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\beta^{7\cdot 2^{k}}+\gamma^{7\cdot 2^{k}}\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\big(a\alpha^{j+7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+b\beta^{j+7\cdot 2^{k+1}}+c\gamma^{j+7\cdot 2^{k+1}}\big)

\displaystyle=U_{j-7\cdot 2^{k}}+U_{j+7\cdot 2^{k}}R_{7\cdot 2^{k}}-U_{j+7\cdot 2^{k+1}},

from which part (ii) follows immediately.
∎

\nu_{2}(P_{50}+1)=\nu_{2}(922112)=\nu_{2}(2^{9}\cdot 1801)=9

\displaystyle\equiv R_{14k+13}\pmod{4},

completing the induction.

Case 3.

n\equiv 6\pmod{14}
.

We prove by induction that
R_{n}\equiv 5\pmod{8}
, which gives

for some integer

\nu_{2}(R_{n}-1)=\nu_{2}(4(2t+1))=2
. Let

for some integer

.
The base case is satisfied since

. Suppose that
R_{14k+6}\equiv 5\pmod{8}
for some fixed

. Applying

2.5
with

,
s=13
, and using the fact that
P_{11}=16
,

P_{12}=21
, and
P_{13}=28
, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{14(k+1)+6}=R_{(14k+7)+13}

\displaystyle=P_{12}R_{14k+6}+P_{13}R_{14k+5}+P_{11}R_{14k+4}

\displaystyle=21R_{14k+6}+28R_{14k+5}+16R_{14k+4}

\displaystyle\equiv 1+4R_{14k+5}\pmod{8}.

Now
14k+5\equiv 5\pmod{7}
, so

\nu_{2}(R_{14k+5})=0
by Lemma
2.7
, meaning

R_{14k+5}=2q+1
for some integer
q\geq 0
. Thus,

4R_{14k+5}=8q+4\equiv 4\pmod{8}
.
Therefore,

R_{14(k+1)+6}\equiv 5\pmod{8}.

Case 4.

n\equiv 12\pmod{14}
.

Let
n=14s+12
for some integer

. Then,
n+2=7\cdot 2(s+1)
. If

, then, by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, we have that

can be written uniquely as

for some integer

and some odd

. Therefore, every

of this kind

for some

and some odd

.

, we apply

2.9
with

to obtain

Since

by Lemma
2.1
, we have

R_{n}-1\equiv k\cdot 2^{t+1}\pmod{2^{t+2}}.

Since

is odd,
\nu_{2}(R_{n}-1)=t+1
. Since

with

odd,
\nu_{2}(n+2)=t
,
which leads to
\nu_{2}(R_{n}-1)=t+1=\nu_{2}(n+2)+1
.

Exceptional cases.

.

-adic valuation of

, now with

.

The formulas in Propositions

2.3
Linear forms in three logarithms

Our problems can be reduced to linear forms in three logarithms of algebraic numbers with very small absolute value (exponentially small in the coefficients of the linear form). Therefore, lower bounds for these linear forms exceeding upper bounds, and in which all the constants involved are explicit, reduce our problems to a finite amount of computation. We will use the special case of three logarithms in Matveev’s theorem. Later, we present a slight simplification of Theorem 2.1 from
[
12
]
applied with
n=3
, which can be found in
[
13
]
. First, we define the absolute logarithm Weil height of an algebraic number.

Definition 2.17
.

Let
\eta
be an algebraic number of degree
d
over
\mathbb{Q}
with minimal primitive polynomial

a_{d}X^{d}+a_{d-1}X^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{0}=a_{d}(X-\eta^{(1)})\cdots(X-\eta^{(d)})\in\mathbb{Z}\left[X\right].

The absolute logarithmic Weil height of
\eta
is given by

h(\eta)=\dfrac{1}{d}\Bigg(\log(|a_{d}|)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\max\big\{\log\big|\eta^{(j)}\big|,0\big\}\Bigg).

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{3}
be three distinct non-zero algebraic numbers, let
\log\alpha_{1}
,
\log\alpha_{2}
and
\log\alpha_{3}
be
\mathbb{Q}-
linearly independent logarithms of these algebraic numbers and let
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
and
b_{3}
be rational integers with
b_{1}\neq 0
. Put

\Lambda=b_{1}\log\alpha_{1}+b_{2}\log\alpha_{2}+b_{3}\log\alpha_{3}.

Let

D=[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}):\mathbb{Q}]\quad\text{and}\quad\chi=[\mathbb{R}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}):\mathbb{R}].

Let
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
be positive real numbers, which satisfy

A_{j}\geq\max\{D\,h(\alpha_{j}),|\log\alpha_{j}|\}\quad(1\leq j\leq 3),

where
h
is the absolute logarithmic Weil height.

Assume that

B\geq\max\{|b_{j}|A_{j}/A_{1}:1\leq j\leq 3\}.

Also define

\kappa_{1}:=\frac{5\cdot 16^{5}}{6\chi}e^{3}(7+2\chi)\left(\frac{3e}{2}\right)^{\chi}\left(26.25+\log(D^{2}\log(eD))\right).

If
\Lambda\neq 0
, then

\log|\Lambda|>-\kappa_{1}D^{2}A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}\log(1.5eDB\log(eD)).

All computational verifications in this work were performed using SageMath 10.6.

R_{n}\leq C_{51}
, which holds only if
n\leq 139
, since
C_{51}\in(R_{139},R_{140})
. A search in SageMath reveals that equation (
4.1
) has no solutions for
4\leq m\leq 51
and
15\leq n\leq 139
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

def padovan_mod_fast(n):
"""Compute P_n mod 2^100 for any n >= 0. Uses matrix exponentiation: O(log n) steps."""
if n <= 2:
return ZZ(1)
vn = M_pad^n * v0_pad
return ZZ(vn[0])
