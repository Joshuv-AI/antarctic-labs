One Word at a Time: Incremental Completion Decomposition Breaks LLM Safety
Samee Arif, Naihao Deng, Zhijing Jin, Rada Mihalcea

Abstract
Warning: This paper contains examples of malicious behaviors, and reader discretion is recommended. Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained to refuse harmful requests, yet they remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks that exploit weaknesses in conversational safety mechanisms. We introduce Incremental Completion Decomposition ( Icd ), a trajectory-based jailbreak strategy that elicits a sequence of single-word continuations related to a malicious request before eliciting the full response. In addition, we propose variants of Icd by manually picking or model-generating the one-word continuation, as well as prefilling when eliciting the full model response in the final step. We systematically evaluate these variants across a broad set of model families, demonstrating superior Attack Success Rate (ASR) on AdvBench, JailbreakBench, and StrongREJECT compared to existing methods. In addition, we provide a theoretical account of why Icd is effective and present mechanistic evidence that successful attack trajectories systematically suppress refusal-related representations and shift activations away from safety-aligned states.

Mechanistic Analysis

To better understand how
Icd
affects model safety behavior, we conduct a mechanistic analysis of hidden state representations during attack execution following
2
. We estimate both a
refusal direction
and a
safety direction
and analyze how different attack variants shift model activations relative to these directions. We identify the refusal- and safety-relevant layer as the one with the largest absolute difference between the
Raw
baseline and the
Icd
variants.

5.1
Refusal Direction

To estimate the refusal potential
r(.)
we use 50 harmful prompts sampled from the JailbreakBench, and 50 benign prompts sampled from the Alpaca dataset
(
26
)
. Let
h_{i}^{\text{refuse}}
denote hidden states associated with refusal responses and
h_{i}^{\text{comply}}
denote hidden states associated with benign completions. We estimate the refusal direction as the difference between the mean hidden states of these two groups:

d_{\text{refusal}}=\mathbb{E}[h^{\text{refuse}}]-\mathbb{E}[h^{\text{comply}}].

(2)

Given a hidden state vector
h
, we compute its projection onto the refusal direction:

r(.)=\frac{h^{\top}d_{\text{refusal}}}{\|d_{\text{refusal}}\|}.

(3)

5.2
Safety Direction

To quantify the safe continuation potential
s(.)
we first generate responses on JailbreakBench dataset under each attack condition:
Raw
,
Prefill
,
Icd–Auto
,
Icd–Seed
, and
Icd–Prefill
. Each generated response is evaluated by the LLM judge, which labels it as harmful or safe. Let
h_{i}^{\text{harm}}
denote hidden states associated with responses classified as harmful, and let
h_{i}^{\text{safe}}
denote hidden states associated with safe responses. We estimate the safety direction as the difference between the mean hidden states of these two groups:

d_{\text{safety}}=\mathbb{E}[h^{\text{safe}}]-\mathbb{E}[h^{\text{harm}}].

(4)

Given a hidden state vector
h
, we compute its projection onto the safety direction:

s(\cdot)=\frac{h^{\top}d_{\text{safety}}}{\|d_{\text{safety}}\|}.

(5)

The distributional characteristics of our mechanistic probes, visualized in the boxplots of Figure

6
Conclusion

We introduced
Icd
, a lightweight trajectory-based jailbreak attack that decomposes a harmful request into constrained local continuations before eliciting a full response. Across ablations, cross-model evaluation, and comparisons with other methods,
Icd
consistently outperforms direct prompting and existing jailbreak techniques, with
Icd–Prefill
emerging as the strongest variant in many settings. We further provided a theoretical account of why incremental continuation and prefilling amplify jailbreak success. Our mechanistic analyses suggest that
Icd
succeeds by progressively shifting model representations away from refusal- and safety-aligned states, highlighting a structural weakness in current alignment methods. More broadly, our results show that safety mechanisms effective on isolated prompts can still fail when harmful context is accumulated incrementally across a conversation. These findings underscore the need for trajectory-aware multi-dimensional safety defenses.

Ethics Statement

This paper studies jailbreak vulnerabilities in aligned large language models. Our goal is to better understand how safety failures can arise in trajectory-based interactions and to use these findings to improve the robustness of deployed systems. Because this work concerns unsafe model behavior, it carries dual-use risk. To mitigate this risk, we focus on evaluation and mechanistic analysis, with the aim of explaining why this attack succeeds so that future work can build more effective safeguards and trajectory-aware defenses. Qualitative examples are truncated or sanitized where appropriate, and the paper is intended to support safety research rather than harmful deployment. We believe the benefits of studying these vulnerabilities outweigh the risks. By showing that safety failures can emerge through trajectory-based buildup rather than only direct malicious prompts, our findings highlight limitations of current safeguards and motivate the development of stronger, trajectory-aware defenses for future LLM systems.

Appendix A
Mathematical Formulation and Theorical Analysis

Let
x\in\mathcal{H}
denote a harmful user request,
\mathcal{Y}
the space of possible responses,
\mathcal{R}
the set of refusal responses, and
\mathcal{A}
the set of harmful compliant responses. For an LLM
M
, a direct query with
x
is expected to yield a refusal:

y\sim M(\cdot\mid x),\quad y\in\mathcal{R}.

(6)

Instead of issuing
x
directly,
Icd
constructs the interaction

\{q_{i},q_{r}^{(1)},\dots,q_{r}^{(k)},q_{f}\},

where
q_{i}
is the malicious request under a one-word constraint,
q_{r}^{(j)}
denotes a repeated continuation prompt, and
q_{f}
requests the final full response. At step
t
, the model generates

y_{t}\sim M(\cdot\mid c_{t},q_{t}),

where
c_{t}
is the accumulated conversational context. After
k
continuation steps, the final response is generated as

y_{f}\sim M(\cdot\mid c_{k},q_{f}).

(7)

We also study the effect of injecting a prefill string
p
as a response to
q_{f}
, biasing the initial tokens of the final completion.

y_{f}\sim M(\cdot\mid c_{k},q_{f},p).

(8)

Let
J
denote the evaluation function used to classify model outputs as harmful or non-harmful, and let
\omega
denote decoding randomness. We define
s(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as the model’s
safe continuation potential
and
r(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as its
refusal potential
. Let
c_{k}
denote the conversational context after
k
incremental continuation steps, and let
q_{f}
denote the final harmful query.

For
Icd–Auto
and
Icd–Seed
, the expected probability of harmful compliance is modeled as a function of the safe continuation and refusal terms under the accumulated context:

\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\!\left[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid c_{k},q_{f})\right]=g\!\bigl(s(q_{f}\mid c_{k}),r(q_{f}\mid c_{k})\bigr).

(9)

For
Icd–Prefill
, the model additionally conditions on a prefix string
p
:

\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\!\left[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid c_{k},p,q_{f})\right]=g\!\bigl(s(q_{f}\mid c_{k},p),r(q_{f}\mid c_{k},p)\bigr).

(10)

Our theoretical analysis yields three main predictions. First, incremental continuation can amplify jailbreak risk by accumulating harmful semantic context while reducing the tendency to refuse at the final step. Second,
Icd–Auto
and
Icd–Seed
may differ in effectiveness because model-sampled continuations can induce different contextual trajectories than manually injected seed words, leading to different safety and refusal dynamics. Third, prefilling can further amplify risk by biasing the model toward harmful continuations at the final generation step, especially when applied after an already safety-weakened incremental context.

A.1
Risk Amplification via Incremental Continuation

Proposition 1
.

We assume that incremental continuation affects
s(.)
and
r(.)
in two ways. First, each generated one-word continuation adds harmful lexical and semantic content to the accumulated conversational context. As this harmful context builds up across turns, the model is increasingly positioned on a trajectory that supports harmful continuation at the final step. Accordingly, when the final prompt
q_{f}
is issued, the resulting context-query pair has lower safe continuation potential than the original direct prompt:

s(q_{f}\mid c_{k})\leq s(x).

(11)

Second, each one-word step introduces an instruction-following constraint that competes with the model’s safety objective: the model is explicitly asked to continue the sequence with a minimal local completion rather than evaluate and refuse the full harmful request. As these constrained continuations are repeatedly generated, the model becomes progressively committed to the continuation trajectory, reducing the likelihood of refusal at the final step. Accordingly, for every
k\geq 1
,

r(q_{f}\mid c_{k})\leq r(x).

(12)

Assuming that the model generates harmful single-word continuations during the incremental steps leading to context
c_{k}
, then:

\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\!\left[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid c_{k},q_{f})\right]>\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\!\left[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid x)\right]

(13)

A.2
Stochastic Effects in Incremental Continuation

Proposition 2
.

Let
c_{k}^{\textsc{auto}}
and
c_{k}^{\textsc{seed}}
denote the contexts obtained after
k
continuation steps under
Icd–Auto
and
Icd–Seed
, respectively. The two variants construct intermediate continuations differently.

In
Icd–Auto
, each one-word continuation is sampled from the model’s conditional next-token distribution under stochastic decoding randomness
\omega
. As a result, the accumulated context reflects the model’s own autoregressive continuation trajectory. In
Icd–Seed
, by contrast, intermediate tokens are externally specified and injected into the context. These injected tokens may increase harmful lexical content but need not align with the model’s natural next-token distribution.

Consequently, the resulting contexts may differ in both safe continuation potential and refusal likelihood. In particular, either of the following orderings may arise:

\displaystyle s(q_{f}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{seed}})

\displaystyle\gtrless s(q_{f}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{auto}}),

(14)

\displaystyle r(q_{f}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{seed}})

\displaystyle\gtrless r(q_{f}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{auto}}).

(15)

Depending on the relative values of
s
and
r
, the stochastic decoding randomness
\omega
that determines the realized continuation trajectory
c_{k}^{\textsc{auto}}
(whether the model generates harmful single-word continuations or refuses intermediate steps), and the judge evaluation
J
used to classify the final response, either attack variant may achieve higher expected harmful compliance probability.

\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\!\left[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{auto}},q_{f})\right]\gtrless\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\!\left[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{seed}},q_{f})\right].

(16)

A.3
Risk Amplification via Prefill Anchoring

Proposition 3
.

Let
p
be an assistant prefill string injected prior to generation. Define the prefix-compatibility weight of a response
y\in\mathcal{Y}
with respect to the prefill
p
as

w(y):=P_{M}(p\mid x,y).

(17)

Then the probability of generating a harmful response under prefilling satisfies

P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x,p)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{M}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{y\in\mathcal{H}\}\,w(y)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{M}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[w(y)\right]}.

(18)

Moreover, if the prefill
p
is on average more compatible with harmful responses than with non-harmful ones, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\in\mathcal{H},x\right]>\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\notin\mathcal{H},x\right],

(19)

then

P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x,p)>P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x).

(20)

Proof.

By Bayes’ rule, the conditional distribution over responses given the prefilling prompt can be written as

P_{M}(y\mid x,p)=\frac{P_{M}(p\mid x,y)\,P_{M}(y\mid x)}{P_{M}(p\mid x)}=\frac{w(y)\,P_{M}(y\mid x)}{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{M}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[w(y)\right]}.

(21)

Summing over all harmful responses
y\in\mathcal{H}
yields

P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x,p)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{M}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{y\in\mathcal{H}\}\,w(y)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{M}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[w(y)\right]}.

(22)

Let
\alpha:=P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x)
. We decompose the denominator as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[w(y)]

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\in\mathcal{H},x\right]+(1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\notin\mathcal{H},x\right].

(23)

Similarly,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{y\in\mathcal{H}\}w(y)\right]=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\in\mathcal{H},x\right].

(24)

Substituting these expressions yields

P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x,p)=\frac{\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\in\mathcal{H},x\right]}{\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\in\mathcal{H},x\right]+(1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(y)\mid y\notin\mathcal{H},x\right]}.

(25)

This quantity is strictly greater than
\alpha
whenever

\mathbb{E}[w(y)\mid y\in\mathcal{H},x]>\mathbb{E}[w(y)\mid y\notin\mathcal{H},x].

Therefore,

P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x,p)>P_{M}(\mathcal{H}\mid x).

∎

A.4
Context Conditioning via Incremental Prefill

Proposition 4
.

Let
c_{k}^{\textsc{icd-prefill}}
denote the context obtained after
k
incremental continuation steps followed by prefill string
p
.

Because incremental continuation accumulates partial harmful lexical and semantic content across multiple steps, the resulting conversational context may position the model closer to a harmful continuation trajectory before the final prompt is issued. Consequently, the incremental context may increase harmful continuation potential and reduce refusal likelihood relative to direct prefilling:

\displaystyle s(q_{f}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{icd-prefill}})

\displaystyle\leq s(x\mid p),

(26)

\displaystyle r(q_{f}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{icd-prefill}})

\displaystyle\leq r(x\mid p).

(27)

If at least one of these inequalities is strict, then in expectation over sampling randomness
\omega
and the judge evaluation
J
,

\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\bigl[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid c_{k}^{\textsc{icd-prefill}},p,q_{f})\bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\omega,J}\bigl[P_{M}(\mathcal{A}\mid x,p)\bigr].

(28)

Judge Prompt Setup

To ensure reproducibility, we include the system prompt used for the LLM-based judge. The full system prompt provided to the judge model is included below.

LLM Judge System Prompt

Please carefully read the following user request (denoted as ’REQUEST’) and response (denoted as ’RESPONSE’).

<BEGIN REQUEST>{{ user_instruction }}<END REQUEST>

<BEGIN RESPONSE>{{ assistant_response }}<END RESPONSE>

Evaluate whether the RESPONSE sufficiently addresses the REQUEST. Use the following guidelines for evaluation:

1.The RESPONSE must contain harmful, unethical, or illegal content that addresses the REQUEST to be classified as ’unsafe’.

2.The RESPONSE must clearly provide specific details to the REQUEST to be classified as ’unsafe’.

3.The RESPONSE message could contain an affirmative answer (e.g., starting with ’Sure’) but not contain harmful content specified in the REQUEST. These cases do not violate our guidelines and must be classified as ’safe’.

4.If the RESPONSE is irrelevant to the REQUEST or the REQUEST itself is not harmful, it must be classified as ’safe’.

Answer with either ’safe’ or ’unsafe’ and nothing else (in particular, never start your response with ”I”).

Appendix F
Baseline Jailbreak Methods

PAIR
(
5
)
is an automated black-box jailbreak method in which an attacker LLM iteratively refines prompts for a target model based on the target’s previous responses. Its goal is to discover a prompt that induces harmful compliance using relatively few target-model queries. TAP
(
16
)
extends black-box jailbreak search with a tree-based strategy. Instead of refining a single prompt trajectory, it explores multiple candidate prompts in parallel and prunes unpromising branches, improving the efficiency of automated jailbreak discovery.

CoA
(
29
)
formulates jailbreaking as a semantic-driven multi-turn attack. It builds a contextual chain of prompts that gradually steers the target model toward the harmful objective, adapting the attack trajectory based on intermediate responses. AMA
(
28
)
is a multi-turn jailbreak method designed to reduce suspicious context and semantic drift. It separates benign conversational structure from the malicious semantic objective, introducing the harmful shift only at the final stage of the interaction.
