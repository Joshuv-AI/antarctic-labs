Clinical Protocol for the Dietary Approaches to Longevity and Health (DiAL Health) Pilot Trial
Authors: Leanne M Redman, Courtney M Peterson, Gareth R Dutton, Kimberly L Drews, Stephanie T Broyles, Robert Dubin, Laura Q Rogers, Catherine M Champagne, Dennis T Villareal, Daniel S Hsia, Hannah E Cabre, Kaja Falkenhain, Katherine E Spring, Hanim E Diktas, Samantha F Kennedy, Corby K Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalorie restriction (CR) and time-restricted eating (TRE) consistently improve biomarkers of aging and health span, yet long-term adherence to these dietary strategies remains difficult. Technology-enabled approaches, including just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs), may facilitate greater engagement by delivering personalized support in response to an individual’s real-time behavior and circumstances.ObjectivesThe Dietary Approaches to Longevity and Health (DiAL Health) pilot trial is designed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of adaptive (JITAI-supported) compared with traditional implementations of CR and TRE and to identify outcome measures suitable for larger, long-term intervention studies.MethodsThis 24-wk, parallel-arm, randomized controlled pilot trial will enroll 90 healthy adults aged 22 to 45 y with a BMI of 22.0 to 29.9 kg/m2 into 1 of 5 groups: nonintervention control, traditional or adaptive TRE with an 8-h eating window, and traditional or adaptive CR targeting a 25% reduction in energy intake. Traditional interventions will emphasize in-person counseling, whereas adaptive interventions will be supported by mobile health (mHealth) technology and incorporate remote monitoring via digital tools and JITAI-based feedback to quantify eating behaviors in real time and deliver tailored adherence prompts.ResultsPrimary feasibility outcomes include adherence, retention, and participant satisfaction. Secondary outcomes to assess preliminary clinical efficacy include Klemera–Doubal as a composite measure of biological age and the metabolic syndrome score as a marker of health span. Additional measures including patient-reported outcomes and hallmarks of aging will be collected as exploratory outcomes. Effect sizes will be estimated to guide the selection of primary end points for future large-scale trials.ConclusionsThe DiAL Health pilot trial will provide essential data on the comparative feasibility and potential efficacy of adaptive compared with those of traditional dietary strategies, informing the development of scalable interventions aimed at improving health span and delaying biological aging.This trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05549362.Keywords: caloric restriction, aging, time-restricted eating, mHealth, JITAI, lifespan, feasibility, intermittent fasting, healthy aging, health span

Introduction

In 1900, only 4.1% of Americans were 65 y or older, whereas by 2050, the number of Americans aged 65 y or above will increase 5-fold [1]. Adults today are aging in an obesogenic environment providing plentiful access to highly caloric, nutrient-poor foods and reduced needs for physical activity. As a result, more people are living with chronic age-associated diseases, such as heart disease and diabetes, at earlier ages [2]. Advances to health care and pharmacotherapy also have led to people living longer, but with shorter health spans or more years affected by disease. This imposes significant health and economic burdens on people, societies, and nations [3]. Thus, to realize longer and healthier lives, there is a critical need to develop safe and sustainable nutritional interventions to slow the rate of biological aging and extend health span. Importantly, the scientific interest in these interventions extends beyond weight loss per se—which may now be achieved more effectively with pharmacologic approaches such as glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists—to their potential effect on the biological processes underlying aging.

Calorie restriction (CR), defined as sustained restriction of energy intake that is below energy requirements for weight maintenance and is nutritionally adequate, slows the rate of biological aging, reduces age-related comorbidities, and in preclinical models and possibly humans, extends lifespan [4]. For almost a century, studies of numerous animal species (e.g., worms, flies, mice) have shown that CR extends lifespan by 50% to 300% [5]. Recent clinical trials testing modest CR in young and middle-aged humans over relatively short durations (i.e., 6–24 mo) provide evidence that CR improves biomarkers of health span [[6], [7], [8], [9]] and biological aging [6,[10], [11], [12]]. However, CR trials extending beyond 2 y with longer-term follow-up are needed as adherence diminishes [6] and unintended consequences of CR (e.g., loss of bone mass) [[13], [14], [15], [16]] may accelerate as people age. Additionally, CR needs to be evaluated alongside and against alternative nutrition interventions that might be easier to follow and similarly effective at promoting longevity and health span.

An alternative to CR that has recently gained traction is time-restricted eating (TRE). TRE extends lifespan in rodents and extended daily fasting accounts for ≥40% of the life-extending effects previously attributed to CR [17]. Additionally, a recent article reported that circadian-aligned TRE extends lifespan in mice more than CR [18]. The effects were independent of changes in body weight, and studies in fruit flies suggest that increased autophagy during the biological night is the main mechanism for lifespan extension [19]. In humans, TRE spontaneously induces mild CR and 2% to 4% weight loss over 2 to 4 mo [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. To date, about three quarters of studies report that TRE reduces body weight, and meta-analyses conclude that TRE is indeed effective for weight loss [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. TRE also improves risk factors associated with a shorter health span (e.g., insulin resistance and blood pressure) [20,22,[24], [25], [26],[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]] and aging biomarkers (e.g., insulin-like growth factor 1 and autophagy markers) [39]. Notably, in pilot studies of TRE, self-reported adherence rates are high (≥80%) [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]], underscoring its potential as a prolonged nutritional strategy to slow aging. To date, there are very few direct comparisons between CR and TRE and especially outside the setting of obesity [42]. Specifically, to our knowledge, there are no data on the effect of TRE on biological aging and no studies examining the sustainability of TRE for longer than 1 y. To fill this gap, we propose a long-term (i.e., ≤5-y) RCT to compare the effectiveness of CR and TRE at slowing biological aging and improving health span. However, the successful implementation of such a long-term trial requires careful planning to establish intervention feasibility, optimize adherence strategies, and ensure high-fidelity intervention delivery.

Novel mobile health (mHealth) approaches are effective at promoting long-term adherence to nutritional interventions in a cost-effective framework and can boost adherence to CR. Mobile health (mHealth) technology (e.g., smartphones and wearables) has enhanced the effectiveness and personalization of interventions by synchronizing remote data collection with intervention delivery. We have shown that these just-in-time-adaptive interventions (JITAIs) [43,44] significantly enhance participant engagement and promote adherence (e.g., higher frequency of self-weighing) while being more cost effective [45]. Considering the urgent need to develop effective nutritional interventions to extend health span and slow biological aging, we are conducting a pilot project to determine the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of CR and TRE, which will be delivered both as a traditional intervention and in a JITAI format. Further, evaluation of CR and TRE JITAIs, in comparison with more traditional approaches, is critical to assess efficacy in relation to the burden of, and satisfaction with, these very different intervention approaches. Our project is designed to provide important preliminary data on adherence, participant satisfaction, and effect size estimates for a suite of primary and secondary aging outcomes to adequately power future trials.

The Dietary Approaches to Longevity and Health (DiAL Health) pilot trial aims to 1) measure the feasibility and 2) assess the preliminary efficacy of 2 CR and 2 TRE interventions to modulate health span and biomarkers of aging in healthy, young (25–49 y) individuals. The DiAL Health pilot trial includes a 24-wk intervention period, and the study will inform the design, interventions, and end points for an anticipated future trial that includes a 5-y intervention. The primary outcome is an intervention feasibility score comprised of intervention satisfaction ratings, retention, and percent adherence to the CR and TRE interventions. The preliminary efficacy of 1 composite measure of biological age (i.e., Klemera–Doubal) and health span [i.e., metabolic syndrome score (MSS)] are secondary outcomes. Patient-reported outcomes and measures of the 9 hallmarks of aging (i.e., genomic instability, telomere attrition, loss of proteostasis, impaired nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, epigenetic alterations, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, and altered intercellular communication) are exploratory outcomes.

Methods

Trial design

This 2-site pilot trial is being conducted at Pennington Biomedical Research Center (PBRC) in Baton Rouge, LA, and University of Alabama in Birmingham (UAB), AL. Ninety healthy, young (25–49 y) males and females without obesity [BMI (in kg/m2), 22–29.9] will be enrolled equally across the 2 sites and randomly assigned to a 5-group, parallel-arm, randomized controlled pilot trial for 24 wk (Figure 1). Following screening and baseline testing, participants will be randomly assigned (n = 18/group) to 1 of 5 groups for 24 wk: ad libitum Control, Traditional CR, Adaptive CR, Traditional TRE, and Adaptive TRE. Trial outcomes will be evaluated during a 10-d assessment period at baseline and week 24. A brief visit will occur during week 12 to help foster retention, measure weight, and assess safety-related outcomes. The trial is registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05549362).

Overview of the DiAL Health study design and assessments. Eligible participants (n = 90; BMI: 2229.9 kg/m2; 25–49 y) will be randomly assigned to 1 of 5 study arms: ad libitum Control, Traditional Time-Restricted Rating (TRE), Adaptive TRE, Traditional Calorie Restriction (CR), or Adaptive CR. CR interventions will target a 25% reduction in energy intake, whereas TRE interventions will prescribe an 8-h eating window. Adaptive arms will incorporate just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAI) to provide personalized adherence support. The primary outcome will be a feasibility intervention score, comprising intervention satisfaction ratings, retention, and adherence to the interventions. Other outcome assessments conducted at baseline (week 0) and follow-up (week 24) will include (top row, left to right) measurement of vitals (i.e., blood pressure, heart rate, and temperature), urine and stool samples (for biomarkers of aging and future analysis of the gut microbiome), body weight, skeletal muscle mass (via D3-creatine), physical activity (via accelerometry), questionnaires for participant-reported outcomes and assessment of dietary intake, blood draws (for markers of metabolic health and biological aging), energy expenditure (via doubly labeled water), body composition (via dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry), and continuous glucose monitoring (see Table 1 for more details on procedures).

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria (Figure 2) were carefully selected to yield a group of healthy, young, weight-stable individuals with normal weight or overweight BMI. The age range was selected to minimize the likelihood of females entering perimenopause during the trial. Inclusion criteria

The trial is open to all adult healthy male or nonpregnant premenopausal female, 25 to 49 years of age, with a BMI ≥22 and <30, clinical chemistry values within normal limits or deemed not clinically significant during screening, and a regular reported wakeup time between 05:00 and 08:00.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusionary factors were identified that could 1) interfere with the interpretation of the trial results (e.g., lifestyle factors that could confound outcomes), 2) negatively impact weight changes during the trial (e.g., recent weight changes, disordered eating, and bariatric surgery), 3) reflect having a chronic condition (e.g., current and recent medical and medication history), and 4) compromise successful completion of the trial (e.g., inability or unwillingness to be randomly assigned to the study groups or adhere to the trial protocol).

Recruitment and screening activities

We selected the 2 study sites (PBRC and UAB) because of their complementary expertise with CR and TRE interventions and clinical trials with collection of numerous metabolic and biological outcomes. We will use several recruitment methods, including posted flyers, advertisements directed to social media campaigns, e-mails to managed listservs, direct mailings, and recruitment service companies. Advertisement materials will direct participants to an online screening questionnaire that collects contact information and initial eligibility criteria (i.e., age, estimated BMI, some medical and medication criteria, and lifestyle factors). Individuals meeting the inclusion criteria (i.e., age, self-reported BMI, and select lifestyle factors) will be invited for the first of 2 in-person screening visits. Eligibility will be evaluated in a staged manner using information obtained during both screening and baseline assessments, with randomization occurring only after completion of all baseline procedures and confirmation of eligibility.

Before the initiation of any study procedures (Table 1), written informed consent will be obtained by trained staff. The first screening visit will occur in the morning following an 8-h overnight fast and include measurement of height and weight to confirm BMI, vital signs (i.e., blood pressure, pulse, and temperature), administration of questionnaires assessing medical history, medication/supplement use, dietary/activity patterns, depression (Beck Depression Inventory-II) [46], eating disorder symptoms [multifactorial assessment of eating disorders’ symptoms (MAEDS)], and a fasting blood draw [chemistry 26 panel and complete blood count (CBC)]. Participants will also receive instructions for completing a behavioral run-in task over the next 5 to 7 d; they will be required to record their dietary intake, including meal timing, and wear an activity monitor to record physical activity and sleep. Participants will be required to have ≥80% completion with the behavioral run-in task to satisfy this eligibility criterion.

Approximately 8 to 14 d later, participants will return for the second screening visit. This visit will include a physical examination with review of the clinical chemistry assays from the first visit, assessment of medication use, and review of the behavioral run-in task. Participants having only 50% to 79% completion of the behavioral run-in will be offered an additional opportunity for completion over the next 5 d. Participants who have failed the behavioral run-in will be excluded. A barrier interview will be conducted by an interventionist to assess the participant’s ability to participate in the study, willingness to be randomly assigned to any of the 5 groups, and ability to complete all assessment visits. Randomization and masking

Participants will be randomly assigned within site (2 sites, ∼45 per site) stratified by sex and BMI (2 levels; normal weight, i.e., 22.0–24.9; overweight, i.e., 25.0–29.9) to 1 of the 5 study groups. The randomization schedule will be prepared by the study biostatistician, and randomization will occur via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) [47,48] at the completion of baseline assessments. The randomization scheme will use a permuted block design. Interventionists, project managers, statisticians, and medical staff cannot be blinded to participant assignments, but the investigators and clinic assessment team will be blind to participant assignment and identity. Masking will only be revealed to the external medical monitors to ascertain relatedness of a serious adverse event or safety alert as necessary. A participant will be considered enrolled in the study when they complete baseline assessments, receive their randomly assigned group assignment, and complete the week 0 visit. Every effort will be made to start the intervention (i.e., conduct the week 0 visit) within 14 days of completing baseline (day 7 visit) assessments.

Treatment groups

The intervention approach for the trial will be detailed in a separate full-length publication in this issue (Kennedy et al., in press). Participants in all groups will be provided with a daily multivitamin containing ∼1000 IU of vitamin D and an oyster shell calcium supplement (500 mg elemental calcium; Nature Made Nutritional Products) and will be encouraged to follow moderate exercise guidelines for health promotion (e.g., brisk walking for ∼150 min/wk and strength training 2 d/wk) [49].

Ad libitum Control (usual diet) group

Consistent with prior trials of CR [6,50], participants randomly assigned to the Control group will continue their habitual eating habits ad libitum. They will not receive any guidance on when or what to eat.

Traditional CR

The Traditional CR intervention is based on the CR intervention from the Comprehensive Assessment of Long-term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy (CALERIE) 2 trial [6,50]. Participants will be prescribed an individualized energy intake that equates to 75% of their weight maintenance energy intake (i.e., 25% CR) measured at baseline. Participants in the traditional CR group will be provided with a bathroom scale, and they will be weighed at intervention sessions. Adherence to the CR goal will be quantified by tracking body weight in relation to each participant’s weight loss prediction. This process relies on previously described energy balance models [[51], [52], [53], [54]] and weight loss nomograms [55,56] to quantify and promote CR adherence. If the weight deviates from the expected trajectory, a priori established criteria will trigger specific intervention strategies. Participants will receive dietary counseling and a meal plan that includes 3 meals and snacks to be consumed throughout the day. Initially, the meal plan will prescribe ∼55% carbohydrate, ∼15% protein, and ∼30% fat, while emphasizing lean sources of protein and whole grains. Moderate alcohol intake will be accommodated, and the recommendations in the meal plan will rely heavily on easily available portion-controlled foods [6,50]. Dietary counseling will educate participants on how to modify macronutrients to accommodate specific dietary needs and to mitigate likely symptoms such as hunger [57]. Participants will record their dietary intake using paper records or a commercial application (e.g., MyFitnessPal) and may change the method of recording to foster maintenance of this behavior. To support behavior change, participants will attend weekly individual in-person counseling sessions during weeks 0 to 12. During weeks 13 to 24, weekly sessions will alternate between in-person and remote sessions, which we believe will be necessary in a trial of longer duration. The sessions will be guided by a nutrition and behavior change curriculum adapted from CALERIE 2 [6,58] to incorporate more recent clinical recommendations and to improve health literacy [59,60].

Adaptive CR

The Adaptive CR intervention will be identical to the Traditional CR intervention in 3 ways as it 1) targets 25% CR, 2) relies on the same energy balance models [[51], [52], [53], [54]] and weight loss nomograms [55,56] to quantify and promote CR adherence, and 3) follows the same session cadence and curriculum. However, the adaptive CR intervention will use mHealth technology to assess behavior remotely and follow JITAI principles [43,44] to identify the current needs of each participant and to systematically adapt counseling strategies to address these needs. Participants will receive an internet-connected scale (i.e., BodyTrace) and will be asked to weigh daily at home. These weights are plotted automatically onto each participant’s weight loss nomogram and displayed simultaneously within an electronic health (eHealth) platform for counselor and participant viewing. The eHealth platform will also track attendance at intervention sessions, self-monitoring, and behavioral/psychosocial challenges that could impede adherence or study completion. Dietary intake will also be tracked with the SmartIntake application, which quantifies food intake from photographs of meals captured with the application [61,62]. The SmartIntake application will be used as needed to promote adherence and at baseline, week 12, and follow-up to quantify dietary intake.

Traditional TRE

Participants in the Traditional TRE group will be instructed to eat within an 8-h period early in the day and fast for the other 16 hours on ≥6 d/wk (equating to 86% adherence). Participants will self-select a regular time to start eating between 06:00 and 10:00 that is no later than 4 hours after waking. They will be encouraged to maintain a regular (±30 min) start time (e.g., 07:00 to 08:00 for a 07:30 breakfast) to maintain consistency. We will prescribe early TRE to reap the benefits of both a longer daily fasting period and eating in alignment with metabolic circadian rhythms [63]. Adherence to the self-selected eating window will be tracked by self-report using a short REDCap [47,48] survey that captures the times that eating starts and stops and documents any reason(s) for nonadherence. This survey is completed each evening or the following morning. TRE participants can eat ad libitum and consume alcohol during the eating window but, as caffeine affects the phase of the central circadian clock, they will be asked to avoid caffeine after 14:00. During the fasting period, only water and noncaloric beverages are permitted. Traditional TRE participants will receive behavior change support to adhere to the eating window through 7 counseling sessions at weeks 0 and 2, which will be conducted in-person, and weeks 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20, which will alternate between in-person or virtual delivery. If 2-wk adherence (i.e., eating within specified time interval) falls below a mean of 5 d/wk, additional sessions will occur (remotely either via secure videoconference or phone) as frequently as every 2 wk.

Adaptive TRE

Participants in the Adaptive TRE group will receive the same eating window, adherence goal, and counseling sessions as participants assigned to the Traditional TRE group. However, treatment delivery will be personalized with mHealth technologies. Daily eating times will be captured with 4 methods that sync to an internet-based feedogram system developed for the trial as follows: 1) participants will record eating events, 2) interventionists may record eating events, 3) participants will use the SmartIntake application from which date and time stamps from food images will automatically populate into the feedogram, and 4) date and time stamps of predicted eating events identified with interstitial glucose measured by a continuous glucose monitor [Dexcom G6 continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) system; Dexcom] will be transmitted to the feedogram at intervention weeks 6, 12, and 18. The feedogram quantifies TRE adherence by plotting each eating event on a 24-h clock and in relation to the self-selected 8-h eating window. The feedogram assists interventionists and participants to detect patterns in adherence and to identify personalized strategies to support behavior change.

Standardization of intervention delivery

Lifestyle and behavior change counselors will be trained to deliver the intervention under the direct supervision of 1 principal investigator (CKM) and 2 co-investigators (GRD and CC). A treatment manual has been developed with a curriculum for each of the topics, and weekly supervision meetings will occur to ensure standardization. Additional information regarding intervention standardization and fidelity procedures, including approaches used to ensure consistent delivery across study sites, is provided in the companion rationale and design paper (Kennedy et al., in press).

Study visit overview

Participants will be enrolled in the study for ∼8 mo to complete baseline testing and the 24-wk intervention and outcome assessments (Table 1). Following screening, outcome assessments will be obtained at baseline and week 24. A shorter visit will occur during week 12 to help foster retention, measure weight, and assess safety surveillance measures. Participants will participate in a focus group or an individual interview within 4 and 8 weeks of completing the pilot study to collect intervention and trial feasibility data.

Trial outcomes are measured at baseline and week 24 during 2 fasting (overnight for ≥8 h) visits spanning ∼10 d to allow for measurement of total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) with doubly labeled water (DLW) and other outcomes including weight, vital signs (i.e., blood pressure, heart rate, and temperature), waist circumference, body composition, and bone mineral density [dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA)], skeletal muscle mass (D3-creatine), blood and urine collection (prespecified aging outcomes including biological age and hallmarks of aging), pulmonary function (spirometry test), physical activity (activPAL accelerometer), interstitial glucose (CGM), questionnaires for participant-reported outcomes, and dietary intake (SmartIntake application) and to collect data regarding adverse events. Between the 2 visits, participants will collect urine samples for the DLW and D3-creatine tests and a stool sample for future analysis of the gut microbiome.

For the brief visit during week 12, participants will have measurements of their vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, and temperature), weight, waist circumference, eating disorder symptoms, dietary intake, and interstitial glucose. Participant-reported outcomes including medication changes and adverse events will also be reported. Biospecimens will be collected for planned assays (CBC and chemistry 26 panel) and study archives. The study multivitamin and calcium supplements will be tracked and redispensed.

Procedures

Screening and safety outcomes

Regarding anthropometrics and vital signs, height will be measured with a calibrated wall-mounted stadiometer at screening. Body weight will be measured with participants wearing a hospital gown and underwear using a calibrated electrical scale. Waist and hip circumference are measured using a spring-loaded tape (Gulick II; Country Technology). Seated vital signs (pulse, blood pressure, and heart rate) will be measured after a 5-min rest. All anthropometric and vital sign measures will be recorded twice. If the 2 trials differ by >10%, a third measure will be obtained.

Bone mineral density of hip and lumbar spine will be measured with DXA (General Electric Lunar iDXA; General Electric) with the participant in the supine position. Participant-reported outcomes will be used at screening to assess eligibility criteria. These include a General Health Questionnaire, Lifestyle and Demographics Questionnaire to assess demographics, family history, social history (alcohol, tobacco), household income, education level, and the Beck Depression Inventory-II [46]. Eating disorder symptoms (binge eating, restrictive eating, purgative behavior, fear of fatness, avoidance of forbidden foods, and depression) will be evaluated with the MAEDS [64,65]. Participants who score >2 SDs (t-scores ≥ 70) on any of the 6 subscales of the MAEDS will complete the Eating Disorder Assessment for DSM-5 (www.eda5.org) [66] to assess the presence of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, other specified feeding or eating disorder, and unspecified feeding or eating disorder.

A physical examination will be conducted at screening, and a fasting blood and urine sample will be collected for measurement of the safety and outcome measures, including complete blood count (CBC) and a metabolic panel including blood lipids and urinalysis.

Primary outcome: intervention feasibility score

The primary outcome is an intervention feasibility score comprising intervention satisfaction ratings, retention, and adherence to the CR and TRE interventions. Intervention satisfaction ratings will be assessed by evaluating attrition rates (the difference in number of dropouts compared with the control arm) and via 3 independent reports from participants, including the following: a study feedback survey completed at screening to assess willingness to be randomly assigned to the 5 study groups and the perceived ability to adhere to each group over 24 wk and 5 y (i.e., the timeframe aligned with the planned longer-term grant) an intervention satisfaction questionnaire administered at week 24, and treatment-specific focus groups (or individual structured interviews). Focus group discussions will be facilitated by trained moderators using semistructured discussion guides pertaining specifically to each intervention group. Discussions will explore intervention acceptability, adherence barriers and facilitators, perceived burden, and perspectives on long-term implementation. Focus groups will include ∼4 to 6 participants per intervention arm and may be conducted either in-person or remotely; sessions will be audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and reviewed for themes or analyzed using a thematic analysis framework [67]. When focus group participation is not feasible, individual cognitive interviews will be completed using the same discussion framework.

Adherence to the CR interventions (%CR) will be determined over 24 wk by the intake-balance method [6]. We will sum the mean of TDEE measured at baseline and week 24 with the change in the amount of energy stored in fat-free mass (FFM) and fat mass (FM) over the same period. To measure TDEE, participants will consume a mixture of DLW containing 1 part deuterium (2H) and 19 parts 18O (dosed at 1 g/kg body weight) in the morning after an overnight fast of ≥8 h and collect 2 baseline urine samples. Two additional urine samples collected after ingestion on the dosing day, at home on days 4 and 7, and in the clinic on approximately day 10 will be analyzed for 18O and 2H abundance by isotope ratio mass spectrometry centrally at the PBRC Mass Spectrometry Core. TDEE will be calculated by multiplying rCO2 [68] by the energy equivalent of carbon dioxide for the mean respiratory quotient (RQ) of 0.86 [12]. Whole-body composition (FFM and FM) will be measured by DXA (General Electric Lunar iDXA; General Electric) with the participant wearing a hospital gown and in a supine position.

Adherence to the TRE interventions will be measured by the extent to which participants adhere to their 8-h eating window. Adaptive TRE adherence will be assessed with the feedogram, which will be populated with eating events from 4 sources: interstitial glucose measured with CGM, SmartIntake, participant-reported eating events, and counselor-reported eating events.

Each of the 4 intervention arms will be scored for adherence, attrition in relation to the control arm, and satisfaction, and these scores will be combined into an overall feasibility score. Thresholds for good (green), moderate (yellow), or poor (red) adherence, attrition, and intervention satisfaction were determined based on prior experience and consensus within the research team, and scores have been assigned to these elements to correspond with their importance in defining overall feasibility. Elements of our proposed traffic light strategy are shown in Figure 3. Combined with focus group feedback and the ability of the interventions to affect the outcome variables, this score will be used to inform the dietary interventions to deploy in the planned 5-y trial and to point to intervention areas needing refinement.

Overview of feasibility score. The primary outcome is an intervention feasibility score comprised of intervention satisfaction ratings, retention, and adherence to the calorie restriction (CR) and time-restricted eating (TRE) interventions.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcome is to assess the preliminary efficacy of the interventions on composite measures of biological age (i.e. ,Klemera–Doubal) [69] and health span (i.e., MSS) [70,71].

The Klemera–Doubal Biological Age [69] calculation requires a fasting blood and urine sample to measure serum albumin, alkaline phosphatase, high-sensitive C-reactive protein, glycated hemoglobin, total cholesterol, creatinine urea nitrogen, uric acid, white blood cell count (replacement for cytomegalovirus optical density), systolic blood pressure, and forced expiratory volume [10,72]. Forced expiratory volume will be measured by a standard pulmonary function test where ≥3 spirograms will be obtained from ≥5 forced expiration.

MSS [70,71] will be calculated for males and females separately and from HDL, triglycerides (TGs), waist circumference (WC), fasting plasma glucose, and mean blood pressure (MBP) as follows: for females, MSS = (45 − HDL)/SDHDLW+ (TG − 150)/SDTG + (WC − 88)/SDWCW + (FBG − 100)/SDFBG + (MBP − 100)/SDMBP; for males, MSS = (40− HDL)/SDHDLM+ (TG − 150)/SDTG + (WC − 102)/SDWCM + (FBG − 100)/SDFBG + (MBP − 100)/SDMBP.

Sample size and power calculations

Primary outcome

We have assessed our power for distinguishing between good (green) and poor (red) examples of adherence, attrition, and satisfaction, thus ensuring adequate inputs into the decision process (Table 3). The goals of these analyses are not to prove superiority or inferiority at the highest levels of statistical confidence but rather to identify signals of good compared with poor feasibility. As such, we will assess feasibility with 1-sided hypotheses, and we have conducted our power calculations accordingly. Our target sample size of ≥12 participants (completers) will be sufficient to distinguish between high and low adherence and intervention satisfaction, while recruitment of 18 participants will allow sufficient power to distinguish between high and low attrition scenarios. Our selected sample size is also ideal to estimate effect sizes for the secondary and exploratory outcomes to power the 5-y trial and is consistent with recommended pilot sample sizes of 12 participants (i.e., the rule of 12) for pilot studies, with larger sample sizes showing diminishing benefits for these estimations [88,89].

Statistical analysis plan

Statistical analyses will be completed using SAS/STAT software, version 9.4 of the SAS System for Windows by the trial biostatistician (SB). Descriptive statistics will be calculated for change from baseline at week 24 for all health span and aging outcomes. Where warranted, outcomes will be transformed to meet assumptions of normality. For the measures of intervention efficacy, participants will be analyzed according to the arms to which they are randomly assigned, regardless of adherence to the intervention; however, because most will only be available at baseline and end-of-study, participants who fail to complete their end-of-study visit (i.e., are lost through attrition) will not be able to be included in analyses (i.e., completers-only analyses). Participants who drop out before week 24 will not have outcome data imputed and will not be included in efficacy analyses, as dropout is itself an outcome for the feasibility analysis. Missing study outcome data for participants for whom some study outcome data for the relevant visit are available (e.g., data available for some but not all measures) will be imputed based on the available outcome data. Because intervention feasibility data will be collected throughout the trial, these data will be available for feasibility analysis from participants who drop out.

For secondary outcome efficacy analysis, linear mixed models will be used, with change from baseline to week 24 as the dependent variable; baseline value, sex, BMI stratum, and site as covariates; and treatment as the independent variable of interest. Because of our limited sample size and the exploratory nature of the analyses, statistical models will be simplified where justified (e.g., removal of covariates where balanced across completers). Because the purpose of these analyses can be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory, no adjustments will be made for multiple comparisons. For each outcome, estimated intervention effects across all 4 intervention arms will be calculated for 1) within-group change from baseline and 2) change from baseline compared with that of the control and will be summarized with mean and interval estimates (1-sided 80% CIs) and standardized effect sizes.

For exploratory efficacy analyses, we will use the same analytic framework and estimate contrasts of CR compared with TRE (pooled across traditional and adaptive intervention arms) and adaptive compared with traditional interventions (both pooled across CR and TRE intervention arms as well as within each intervention type), both for within-group changes from baseline and compared with control. For exploratory mediation analyses, because we will only have a single nonbaseline time point for these analyses, we will informally explore simple mediation by examining the indirect effects of the interventions through the mediators, as implemented by the PROCESS macro available for SAS [92,93]. For both CR and TRE interventions, changes in the psychological constructs of self-efficacy, disinhibition, restraint, executive function, and delayed discounting will be considered for mediation; weight change will also be explored as a mediator for both interventions. Additional exploratory mediation analyses may also be conducted.

Discussion

Nutritional interventions are 1 of the most promising nonpharmacologic approaches that attenuate aging. Continuous CR, the most studied intervention, extends lifespan in preclinical models (e.g., worms, flies, mice) and, in humans, slows biological aging and improves health span (e.g., disease risk factors and quality of life). In CALERIE 2, the largest clinical trial of CR in humans, we observed that adherence to 25% CR waned to ∼12% CR over 2 y, which questions the practicality of 25% CR interventions over longer periods [6,49,57]. However, CALERIE 2 did not benefit from a more advanced approach, namely a JITAI, which tailors and optimizes the intervention for each participant. JITAs strive to provide individuals with the right type and amount of personalized support when necessary by adapting the intervention delivery to the present adherence, needs, and environment of the participant [92]. Furthermore, newer dietary-based approaches, such as TRE, may also benefit human aging. Our short-term pilot studies of TRE showed that, independent of CR, eating over a 6-h window that began in the morning significantly improved diabetes and cardiovascular disease risk factors (health span) and biomarkers of primary aging (e.g., autophagy, oxidative stress, and nutrient sensing) [39]. The long-term feasibility of TRE is unknown, and it is unclear whether TRE interventions with JITAI designs enhance intervention feasibility and adherence in the context of healthy aging.

Our pilot trial has several strengths. First, we will measure composite measures of health span and biological age in addition to biomarkers representing the 9 hallmarks of aging. This approach was chosen in acknowledgment that aging is a complex process, usually defined as a systemwide decline in physiological integrity that leads to deteriorated physical function. This decline occurs due to interconnected events in the organism that have been classified into 9 hallmarks that underlie aging [94]. Of these, the primary hallmarks (i.e., genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, and loss of proteostasis) represent the foundational causes of cellular damage. As a result, compensatory hallmarks (i.e., deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular senescence) occur, and integrative hallmarks (i.e., stem cell exhaustion and altered intercellular communication) ultimately lead to functional decline with age. Importantly, most of these processes may be impacted by nutrition interventions [95]. In this pilot trial, we will collect biospecimens, including peripheral blood mononuclear cells and stem cells, to obtain preliminary data on these 9 hallmarks of aging. We also will measure telomere length, epigenetic age (determined by DNAm), cellular senescence, and autophagy. We acknowledge that selecting representative biomarkers of aging is a well-known debate in gerontology; for example, in addition to these hallmarks, 7 pillars of aging also were recently described [96]. To mitigate this concern, we will also be collecting additional biological specimens (i.e., blood, serum, plasma, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, stool, and urine) to be stored for future research.

Second, as detailed in the companion paper detailing the CR and TRE intervention arms, we will pioneer the integration of CGM and a food image–based application into a JITAI to promote real-time adherence to TRE. We will develop and test a novel adaptive TRE intervention using JITAI principles. We will use objective data to assess a participant’s adherence to their eating window through time-stamped eating events, captured through the remote food photography method [61,62] and SmartIntake application, and secondarily with CGM. By leveraging these 2 novel data streams, we aim to individualize intervention delivery in real time. In doing so, we will be, to our knowledge, among the first studies to develop and pilot an adaptive TRE intervention using a JITAI framework.

The obvious limitation is the generalizability of the trial and its eventual findings with the inclusion of a relatively young sample of participants without obesity who are essentially free of disease and medication use. Using NHANES data, we estimated that only ∼3.9% of the United States population would be eligible for this pilot trial based on a reduced set of eligibility criteria (e.g., age, BMI, free of chronic disease, and medication use) [97]. We also found that only 11 (2.2%) of 492 respondents to an online survey met the eligibility criteria for the planning project, which is consistent with our recruitment experience, where we recruit a maximum of ∼2% of screeners per month. This leads to the second limitation being recruitment and costly advertising and screening costs. The trial was designed following stringent criteria imposed by the funders who were seeking a mechanistic trial in humans with the age, BMI, and general health of our target sample. An alternative approach would be to use slightly more liberal eligibility criteria, primarily related to increasing the upper BMI limit, which is currently 30 kg/m2, and to enroll participants who use some medications or who have certain medical conditions. The advantages of this approach include better generalizability and the ability to recruit the study sample more quickly and cost-effectively. The downside is a more heterogeneous sample that could, for example, impose drug-nutrition interactions that cannot be sufficiently accounted for in the assessment of response variability and analysis.

In conclusion, the DiAL Health pilot trial builds on prior National Institute of Aging–sponsored trials on CR in healthy, young males and females without obesity and will gather important pilot data on the feasibility of longer-term CR and TRE interventions, the utility of JITAI-supported intervention delivery, and the preliminary effects of these approaches on a large number of important end points for assessing biological aging, health span, safety, and intervention adherence.
