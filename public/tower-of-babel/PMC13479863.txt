Patient‑specific 3D‑printed Ti‑6Al‑4 V sternum–rib implant for near‑total sternal resection: operational blueprint, implant design principles, and intermediate clinical outcome
Authors: Mayank Tripathi, Mohd Irfan Ansari, Reshma R. Balachandran, Vaibhav Kushwaha, Ankita Dubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge sternal resections jeopardize chest-wall rigidity and cardiopulmonary protection. Conventional reconstructions often trade anatomical conformity for convenience. Three-dimensional planning and patient-specific titanium (Ti-6Al-4 V) implants may reconcile oncologic goals with stable mechanics; however, detailed end-to-end workflows are seldom reported.MethodsWe implemented a CT-to-implant workflow - segmentation, virtual resection, CAD with lateral rib-coupling flanges and soft-tissue eyelets, polymer prototype rehearsal, additive manufacturing, and single-stage implantation - for a 28-year-old man with a sternal giant cell tumor. Near-total sternectomy was followed by rigid prosthetic fixation to a manubrial sliver, xiphoid remnant, and medial rib stumps. Double-layer polypropylene mesh (deep barrier and superficial buttress) and preserved pectoral muscle coverage enabled flap-less soft-tissue reconstruction.ResultsEstimated blood loss was 300 ml. The patient was extubated on table, the subcutaneous drain was removed on postoperative day 2, the intercostal drain on postoperative day 5, and total hospital stay was 5 days. Recovery was uncomplicated with oral-analgesic pain control and satisfactory early function. The cost of the prosthesis was INR 180,000 (approximately US$1,950). Final histopathology confirmed giant cell tumor with negative margins (R0 resection). Preoperative spirometry showed a restrictive pattern; repeat spirometry at approximately 1 month showed an overall improvement of about 20% in major parameters, although the original postoperative printout was not retrievable. At 6-month follow-up, the patient had resumed work and reported only intermittent mild pain not requiring analgesics.ConclusionWe present a pragmatic operational blueprint for patient-specific sternal reconstruction that highlights anchorage strategy, rib coupling, and soft-tissue suspension. The case demonstrates feasibility in a specialized multidisciplinary setting, but longer-term oncologic, functional, and implant-durability data remain necessary.

Introduction

Chest-wall reconstruction after oncologic resection pursues a triad of outcomes: oncologic clearance, mechanical stability, and soft-tissue integrity. When the sternum is largely resected, loss of the anterior keystone can trigger paradoxical motion, pain, and compromised ventilation [1–4]. Conventional strategies - polypropylene mesh-methyl-methacrylate (PMMA)[5, 6] sandwiches, plates, and intraoperatively contoured meshes [7] - remain widely used but can be difficult to shape accurately, may not reproduce native curvature, and may predispose to buckling, exposure, or suboptimal force transfer in large defects. Three-dimensional planning with additively manufactured titanium offers an alternative by allowing anatomical conformity, preplanned fixation points, and engineered features such as eyelets and lattice windows [8–13]. Despite growing interest, many reports still emphasize feasibility rather than the practical workflow from imaging to implantation. We therefore document our CT-to-implant workflow, implant design principles, operative sequence, and updated intermediate clinical outcome in a patient who underwent near-total sternectomy and immediate reconstruction.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old man presented with a 4-month history of progressively enlarging anterior chest-wall swelling associated with intermittent pain (ECOG performance status 1). On examination, an approximately 8 × 8 cm multiloculated, variably firm lesion was palpable over the sternal body and was not fixed to the skin. Core biopsy showed a giant-cell-rich lesion suggestive of giant cell tumor. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated an expansile lytic lesion centered in the sternal body with cortical breach and an extra-osseous component measuring approximately 8.2 × 5.0 × 9.6 cm. The lesion abutted the second to fourth costal cartilages bilaterally, involved only the overlying fibers of pectoralis major, extended anteriorly into the subcutaneous plane, and showed posterior intrathoracic extension with encasement of the bilateral internal mammary vessels(Fig. 1). Lungs were clear with no lymphadenopathy. In view of the local extent of disease and the anticipated structural defect following oncologic resection, the patient was planned for single-stage near-total sternectomy and immediate reconstruction with a patient-specific titanium prosthesis. Because this was a giant cell tumor, we aimed for an approximately 1 cm surgical margin while preserving as much uninvolved soft tissue and bone as possible for reconstruction.

Segmentation and virtual resection

Diagnostic multidetector CT DICOM data were imported into MySegmenter (JMS, USA) for segmentation. Thresholds used as guidance were approximately 150–1000 HU for bone, 70–574 HU for cartilage, and 1.57–72.25 HU for tumor/soft tissue, followed by manual refinement of the tumor-bone interface(Fig. 1). Virtual osteotomies were planned to preserve narrow superior (manubrial) and inferior (xiphoid) shelves where oncologically feasible so as to provide longitudinal load-sharing and purchase for fixation. A composite 3D model of the rib cage, cartilage, and tumor guided the resection perimeter and confirmed rib suitability for fixation(Fig. 2).

Design principles and prototype rehearsal

Surface refinement was performed in Freeform 22 and the definitive construct was designed in SolidWorks (Dassault Systemes). The implant replicated the expected anterior thoracic contour using preserved manubrial/xiphoid alignment together with the arc of the uninvolved adjacent ribs and the overall anterior chest contour on CT, rather than the dysmorphic tumor-bearing sternal body alone. The design incorporated lateral rib-coupling flanges, countersunk screw ports, and suture eyelets to facilitate rigid fixation and muscular suspension. Lattice windows were incorporated where feasible to reduce weight and permit tissue integration while preserving stiffness. Edge chamfers were used to minimize soft-tissue irritation. A polymer prototype was printed for physical rehearsal, implant orientation, and team briefing.

Manufacturing and quality checks

The definitive prosthesis was manufactured in medical-grade Ti-6Al-4 V using powder-bed fusion. Implant production time from finalized design to delivered prosthesis was approximately 2 weeks. Following support removal, cleaning, passivation, and sterilization, fit verification was performed on the prototype/chest wall model. The prosthesis cost was INR 180,000 (approximately US$1,950).

Anesthesia and perioperative care

General anesthesia with single-lumen endotracheal intubation and controlled ventilation was used. Standard hemodynamic monitoring and lung-protective strategies were applied. Multimodal analgesia without neuraxial catheter was employed. Antibiotic prophylaxis and antifibrinolytic use followed institutional pathways.

Operative technique

A midline incision from the suprasternal notch to the xiphisternum was made and skin flaps were raised in the subfascial plane while preserving viable perforators where feasible; a thick skin-subcutaneous flap was maintained to ensure robust prosthesis coverage. Near-total sternectomy was performed as a wide local excision including the involved medial rib ends bilaterally, with careful protection of the pleura and pericardium and ligation of the internal mammary vessels(Fig. 3). After resection, a deep polypropylene mesh sheet was placed as a protective barrier over mediastinal structures. The custom implant was then seated and fixed superiorly to the retained manubrial sliver and inferiorly to the xiphoid remnant, while the lateral flanges were secured to the corresponding rib remnants. Fixation used screws, sternal wires, and non-absorbable sutures (Ethibond) tailored to local tissue quality. A second, superficial mesh layer was placed as a broad soft-tissue buttress and suture platform in accordance with our institutional chest wall reconstruction practice(Fig. 3). Only the pectoralis fibers directly overlying the tumor were resected; most of the pectoralis muscle was preserved and advanced to cover the prosthesis. No formal muscle flap transfer was required. The patient was extubated on table.

Postoperative course

Estimated intraoperative blood loss was 300 mL. One subcutaneous drain placed around the mesh/soft tissues and one intercostal drain were used. The subcutaneous drain was removed on postoperative day 2 and the intercostal drain on postoperative day 5(Fig. 3). No clinically evident postoperative seroma or soft-tissue collection was observed. Total hospital stay was 5 days. Pain was controlled with oral analgesics, wound healing was primary, and sutures were removed on day 14.

Final histopathology confirmed giant cell tumor with negative margins (R0 resection). Preoperative spirometry showed a restrictive pattern with FVC 3.57 L (65% predicted), FEV1 2.93 L (65% predicted), and FEV1/FVC 82.13%. Repeat postoperative spirometry performed at approximately 1 month showed an overall improvement of about 20% in major parameters compared with baseline; however, the original postoperative printout could not be retrieved during revision, and exact parameter-wise postoperative values are therefore not tabulated (Table 1). At approximately 6-month follow-up, the patient was clinically well, had resumed work, and reported only intermittent mild pain not requiring analgesics.

Discussion

This case highlights that the principal value of patient-specific titanium reconstruction lies not merely in successful implantation but in the structured integration of imaging, engineering, and operative execution. For extensive central anterior defects, anatomical conformity, preplanned fixation points, and soft-tissue suspension features are difficult to reproduce reliably with hand-contoured conventional constructs [2, 3, 8, 14–16, 18].

In this case, several steps contributed to the reconstructive advantages. First, preoperative segmentation and virtual planning allowed preservation of mechanically useful superior and inferior remnants and informed a rib-based anchorage strategy. Second, patient-specific CAD enabled preplanned curvature, fixation points, and eyelets for muscle re-anchoring, reducing the need for major intraoperative improvisation. Third, the layered operative sequence—deep mesh barrier, rigid implant fixation, preserved pectoral muscle advancement, and superficial mesh buttress—facilitated mediastinal protection, stable fixation, and organized soft-tissue restoration.

Conventional alternatives such as PMMA sandwich reconstruction, plates, or mesh-based repairs would have been technically feasible, but were considered less attractive here because of the size and geometry of the central defect, the need for accurate contour restoration, and the requirement for multiple planned fixation and soft-tissue suspension points [5]. Titanium was selected because of its strength-to-weight ratio, corrosion resistance, biocompatibility, and compatibility with additive manufacturing [9, 10, 12, 13]. At the same time, we recognize the trade-off between rigidity and physiologic compliance; the design therefore incorporated a patient-specific contour and lattice features to reduce unnecessary bulk while preserving stability. High-performance polymers such as PEEK [19, 20] remain relevant alternatives and are discussed in the manuscript as materials with different modulus characteristics and imaging advantages [8].

The superficial mesh layer warrants specific explanation. Its purpose was not to provide additional rigid chest-wall support but to create a broad, low-tension suture platform over the prosthesis and to reinforce soft-tissue closure. In this case, only the pectoralis fibers directly overlying the tumor were resected; the majority of the pectoralis musculature was preserved and was advanced to cover the implant [5–7]. In addition, a thick skin-subcutaneous flap was raised to enhance soft-tissue coverage [11, 17]. This strategy avoided the need for flap transfer while still maintaining vascularized tissue coverage over the prosthesis.

This report has important limitations. It describes a single patient with approximately 6 months of follow-up, which is insufficient to draw conclusions regarding long-term recurrence, implant durability, fatigue behavior, or respiratory functional outcomes. Although repeat postoperative spirometry suggested an overall improvement of approximately 20% at 1 month, the original printout was not retrievable and exact parameter-wise postoperative values could not be presented. A standardized postoperative pain score was also not prospectively recorded. In addition, the workflow required specialized imaging analysis, engineering collaboration, and access to custom metal additive manufacturing, which may limit immediate applicability outside specialized multidisciplinary settings [21]. A publication-quality follow-up CT suitable for design-to-implant comparison was also unavailable.

Conclusion

We present a pragmatic CT-to-implant operational blueprint for patient-specific sternal reconstruction that emphasizes segmentation-guided planning, anatomy-informed implant design, rib-based anchorage, and structured soft-tissue restoration. In this case, the approach enabled reconstruction after near-total sternectomy with acceptable perioperative recovery and encouraging 6-month clinical status.(Fig. 4) Broader conclusions regarding long-term oncologic control, functional benefit, and implant durability require longer follow-up and larger experience.

Innovation statement

This report describes a structured CT-to-implant workflow for personalized sternal reconstruction that integrates segmentation, virtual resection planning, CAD-guided implant design, polymer rehearsal, additive manufacture, rigid fixation, and soft-tissue reconstruction using preserved muscle coverage and double-layer mesh.
