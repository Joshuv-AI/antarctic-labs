Introduction

Corn stover (stover) and corn grain stillage (stillage) generated at corn ethanol plants are
established sources of renewable electricity and biomethane (i.e., renewable natural gas, [RNG])
through the use of anaerobic digestion (AD) in Germany.1–3 In the United States (U.S.), stover is
typically left on fields after corn grain harvesting and stillage is typically processed into animal
feed.4 U.S. digesters more frequently process manure, food waste, and wastewater sludge. This
limited adoption of stover and stillage AD in the U.S. reflects, in part, uncertainty about the
technology’s RNG production potential, as well as life cycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions
under U.S. conditions. This uncertainty may be significant, as several U.S. policies, such as the
U.S. Renewable Fuels Standard (RFS), the federal Clean Fuel Production Credit (45Z), and state
clean fuel standards (CFS)5–10, provide incentives to RNG projects based on their life cycle GHG
emissions intensities. Thus, a transparent life cycle analysis (LCA) is necessary to estimate the
life cycle GHG emissions of RNG from stover and stillage in the U.S context.

To begin to address this gap, this work compiles life cycle inventory (LCI) data on an anonymous
industry-relevant stover and stillage AD project in the U.S. The LCI data cover biogas yields,
onsite energy requirements, and other metrics, ready for integration into a future release of the
Research and Development version of the Greenhouse Gases Regulated Emissions and Energy
Use in Technologies (R&D GREET) model. The empirical data for AD operations presented here
informs a comprehensive LCA of this technology that will leverage U.S.-relevant upstream and
downstream emissions factors for processes such as the alternative fates of stover (left on field)
and stillage (processed into animal feed), grid electricity usage, and RNG pipeline injection for
various end uses including use as a process fuel in industry and use as a transportation fuel.

Anaerobic Digestion of Corn Stover

Figure 1 illustrates the various stages involved in converting corn stover to RNG through AD. This
process is based on an operational biorefinery in the U.S., which has an annual RNG production
capacity of about 0.5 trillion Btu.11 Corn stover, which would have otherwise been left on the field
to enrich the soil, is gathered, baled, and transported by truck to the AD facility. There, it is milled,
mixed with fresh water and recycled liquid water from AD effluent (digestate) separation and fed
to the AD operating at mesophilic conditions. Biogas is produced during digestion, followed by
biogas cleaning stages and RNG upgrading to produce pipeline quality RNG. The digestate is
separated into solid and liquid fractions – the liquid fraction is recycled back to the inlet of the
digester to raise the moisture content of the incoming milled fresh stover, while the solid fraction
is stored onsite and distributed as a soil conditioner (Humus).

Corn stover counterfactual, gathering & baling, and truck transport to AD facility
Corn stover is typically left in the field after harvesting to prevent soil erosion and enrich the soil
as it decomposes.13 When stover is removed for use as a biofuel feedstock, essential nutrients
such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (as P2O5), and potassium (as K2O) are lost, reducing on-field
NO and N2O emissions and requiring the application of supplementary fertilizers to restore soil
fertility.14 R&D GREET accounts for the emissions and other environmental impacts of production
the aforementioned processes to compensate for the impact of stover removal.15

Corn stover milling, AD, and RNG production
As indicated in Figure 1, corn stover undergoes mechanical milling before being fed into the
digesters. At steady state, it is mixed with the liquid fraction obtained from the solid-liquid
separation of the digestate and a freshwater stream (0.82 ton water per ton corn stover as
received) to lower the solid content of the input stream to the digester to approximately 0.12 ton
TS/wet ton (wet digestion).11 The digesters operate under mesophilic conditions, with their
contents continuously mixed and heated. The total energy requirements for the corn stover size

It is important to note that the values shown represent methane (CH4) and biogas production prior
to accounting for leakages that occur during AD, upgrading, and humus storage operations. To
account for CH4 emissions during digestate operations, as well as the end-of-life of the carbon in
the humus, the carbon remaining in the humus needs to be estimated. The carbon in the humus
was determined by subtracting the carbon in the raw biogas from the total carbon in the feedstock.
The wet weight of humus was calculated with a mass balance on industry supplied data – the
balance assumes that the humus is equal to the mass of feedstock and water input to the digester
minus the mass of raw biogas produced.11 There is an 8% discrepancy in the overall mass
balance due to water, likely originating from small discrepancies in the data that was provided.
Importantly, this discrepancy has no impact on the carbon balance and would have no impact on
GHG results if an LCA were conducted. The digestible solids in the humus were estimated under
the assumption that the TS content in the effluent stream from the digester is reduced to 7% from
12% at the inlet. This approach implies a 48.7% decrease in VS between the influent and effluent
streams of the digester. The carbon content in the humus was estimated by subtracting the carbon
present in the biogas from the total carbon in the feedstock.11

Anaerobic Digestion of Grain Stillage

The process flow diagram for the conversion of de-oiled grain stillage (i.e., stillage after corn oil
extraction) to RNG is presented in Figure 2. Operational data and process specifications were
also provided by an operating U.S.-based facility with a RNG production capacity of about 2 trillion
Btu.11 De-oiled grain stillage, which would have otherwise been used to produce animal feed, are
mixed with recycled liquid digestate and are fed to the continuously mixed AD operating at
mesophilic conditions. Unlike corn stover, de-oiled grain stillage does not require preliminary
pretreatment before digestion. Instead, it is mixed with a recycled stream of the digestate and a
clean effluent backset (i.e., water) stream to control the water balance of the process. Biogas is
produced during digestion, followed by biogas cleaning stages and RNG upgrading to produce
pipeline quality RNG which is injected into a local distribution pipeline. Some of the digestate can
be directly recycled to mix with incoming de-oiled grain stillage. The remaining digestate is

separated into solid and liquid fractions within a decanter – the thin effluent leaves the system
boundary to be further processed into fertilizer products, while the solid fraction is stored onsite
and distributed as a soil conditioner (Humus).

Grain stillage counterfactual
Grain stillage is commonly utilized to produce distiller’s grains with solubles (DGS), which serve
as substitutes for conventional animal feed such as corn, soybean meal, and urea. In life cycle
analyses of corn ethanol, a displacement co-product handling method is often applied to the DGS
co-product, resulting in a displacement credit for the ethanol product. However, when grain
stillage is redirected from DGS production to RNG production, the displacement credit is forfeited
because the ethanol biorefinery no longer produces animal feed substitutes.

This analysis treats the forfeited displacement credit as a penalty for the RNG product, as the
benefit of animal feed displacement is lost due to the shift to RNG production. Consequently, de-
oiled grain stillage used for RNG production is assigned an embedded carbon intensity reflecting
the foregone credit. Importantly, this approach does not alter the carbon intensity of the ethanol
product, regardless of whether de-oiled grain stillage is used for DGS or RNG production. A dry
mass ratio of 1:1 is assumed between grain stillage and DGS, as solid losses during DGS
production in conventional ethanol refineries are minimal.19–21 It is estimated that 0.0714 dry tons
of DGS are forfeited per MMBtu (LHV) of raw biogas produced from anaerobic digestion of
diverted stillage.

R&D GREET assumes specific displacement ratios of conventional animal feed by DGS in
domestic and export markets, as outlined in Table 7. These displacement ratios, combined with
the carbon emissions associated with conventional animal feed, are used to calculate the
embedded carbon intensity of grain stillage.
