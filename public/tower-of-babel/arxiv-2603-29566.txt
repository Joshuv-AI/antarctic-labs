The Geometry of Polynomial Group Convolutional Neural Networks
Yacoub Hendi, Daniel Persson, Magdalena Larfors

Abstract
We study polynomial group convolutional neural networks (PGCNNs) for an arbitrary finite group G G . In particular, we introduce a new mathematical framework for PGCNNs using the language of graded group algebras. This framework yields two natural parametrizations of the architecture, based on Hadamard and Kronecker products, related by a linear map. We compute the dimension of the associated neuromanifold, verifying that it depends only on the number of layers and the size of the group. We also describe the general fiber of the Kronecker parametrization up to the regular group action and rescaling, and conjecture the analogous description for the Hadamard parametrization. Our conjecture is supported by explicit computations for small groups and shallow networks.

Introduction

Geometric deep learning
[
BBL+17
,
BBCV21
,
GAC+23
]
is the subfield in deep learning that studies equivariant architectures with respect to a given group
G
.
The main principle of geometric deep learning is that when training over a dataset that has a set of symmetries, we can introduce an informed bias towards equivariant and invariant models by using equivariant architectures such as convolutional layers or graph layers.
The benefit of doing this is a reduction of sample complexity; this can be seen since an equivariant architecture has fewer parameters due to weight-sharing, and fewer parameters need fewer training data points to determine their values and, henceforth, smaller sample complexity
[
DWZ+18
]
.

A classical example of equivariant architectures is convolutional neural networks (CNN), which are equivariant under discrete translations, corresponding to the group
G=\mathbb{Z}
.
A general theory for linear equivariant layers for finite groups and compact groups is presented in
[
CW16
,
CGW19
]
.
The core idea is that enforcing equivariance on linear maps, under given representation, results in weight-sharing constraints which
G
-convolutional layers have.
Hence all linear equivariant layers are convolutional and vice-versa.
In
[
NCWP25
]
, this approach is extended to non-linear equivariant layers.

Apart from designing equivariant models, another direction of geometric deep learning is concerned with understanding the expressivity and training dynamics of such models.
In this article we are interested in the latter direction, and to investigate this, we apply tools from the recently emergent field of
neuroalgebraic geometry

[
MSM+25
]
.

The main idea of neuroalgebraic geometry can be briefly understood as follows.
Given a fixed neural network architecture
\Phi
, one obtains a parametric family of functions
\Phi_{\theta}
indexed by the network parameters
\theta
.
We call the space of all realizable functions
the neuromanifold

\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
associated with the architecture
\Phi
.
The term “manifold” is a slight misnomer, as these functional spaces usually exhibit singularities.
Given a loss function
\mathcal{L}
and a dataset
\mathcal{D}
, then learning via stochastic gradient descent can be interpreted as a distance minimization problem over the neuromanifold to an objective function
f_{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D}}
in the ambient space, that depends on both the loss function
\mathcal{L}
and the dataset
\mathcal{D}
.
From this perspective, the geometric properties of the neuromanifold translate into statistical counterparts.
For example, dimension and curvature translate into sample complexity and expressivity, fiber classification translates into identifiability, and so on.

However, the analysis of these neuromanifolds is often intractable due to their compositional complexity and the use of nonlinear activation functions, which in some cases even fail to be analytic (e.g. the RELU function).
One useful simplification is to restrict to polynomial activation functions, which can be justified for three reasons. Firstly, polynomials are universal approximators by Weierstrass Approximation Theorem, which implies that neural networks with non-polynomial activation functions can be approximated by networks with polynomial activation functions.
Secondly, the neuromanifold becomes a subspace of the space of homogeneous polynomials of fixed degree and in a fixed number of variables.
More precisely, the neuromanifold becomes a semi-algebraic set (i.e. it can be described as a solution set of polynomial equations and inequalities in its finite dimensional ambient space), which can be analyzed using tools from algebraic geometry.
Thirdly, even though non-polynomial activations are more used in mainstream uses, neural networks with polynomial activation have performed well in many different applications (e.g healthcare, finance, computer vision, cryptography, theoretical physics)
[
DNM+16
,
FML+22
,
GHL11
,
NM18
,
YHN+21
,
CMB+21
,
HTG17
,
DLQ22
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the study of polynomial group convolutional neural networks (PGCNN) i.e. neural networks with
G
-convolutional layers and polynomial activation functions.
We formalize the construction of PGCNNs using the language of group algebras and describe the neuromanifolds as subspaces of these algebras.
The aim of this paper is to generalize the results in
[

]
which hold for polynomial CNNs to PGCNN for an arbitrary finite group
G

It is worth highlighting here that the results from

[

,

show that the neuromanifolds of polynomial neural networks (PNN) with fixed width have the expected (maximum) dimension. More precisely, for a

Since a PGCNN can be represented as a PNN with fixed width, then one might expect that the dimension of the neuromanifold of PGCNN can be inferred immediately from these results.
This is not true because a PGCNN enforces weight-sharing constraints that violate the required generality assumption. In terms of algebraic geometry, the neuromanifold of PGCNN is a specialization of the neuromanifold of PNN with fixed width, and general conditions do not necessarily hold over specializations.

Related works.

We review here works related to neuroalgebraic geometry and focus on those concerning convolutional and equivariant architectures.

Mehta et al.
[
MCTH22
]
presented one of the first attempts to use algebraic geometry to count critical points in the loss landscape for deep linear neural networks.
Building on this perspective, Traget et al.
[
TKB20
]
introduced the classification of critical points as spurious (due to parametrization) and pure (due to geometry).
In
[
KTB19
]
, Kileel et al. connected the dimension of the neuromanifold of PNNs to the expressivity of the model and established upper and lower bounds on layer width for neuromanifolds to be filling (i.e. they fill their ambient space).
In the same paper, they conjectured that for sufficiently high activation degrees, all PNN architectures are filling. This conjecture was later proven independently in each of
[
FRWY25
,
MM25
,
Cra25
]
.
Furthermore, questions about non-defectiveness and identifiability of PNNs were settled separately by
Usevich et al.
[
UBDC25
]
using techniques from tensor decompositions and by Massarenti and Mella
[
MM25
]
by analyzing secant varieties.

Turning to convolutional architectures, the neuromanifold of a linear convolutional network was first studied in
[
KMMT22
]
and
[
KMST24
]
.
In these works, convolution filters were identified with homogeneous bivariate polynomials and the convolution operation with polynomial multiplication. These allowed the authors to define the neuromanifold by factorization conditions and to show it is a semi-algebraic set; moreover, conditions under which it is filling are established.
Building on this line of work, Shahverdi
[
Sha24
]
developed recursive algorithms to construct the polynomial equations and inequalities that define the neural manifold for a linear convolutional network, and it relates the number of critical points of the square loss function to the generic Euclidean degree of the Segre variety.

The work most closely related to this article is
[
SMK25
]
. In this work, Shahverdi et al. compute the dimension, the degree and the generic Euclidean degree for polynomial convolutional networks in addition to classifying the general fiber and the singular locus. A central aspect of their analysis is based on the relation between the neuromanifold and the Segre-Veronese variety; in particular, they show that the neuromanifold for CNN is birational to the Segre-Veronese variety.
Our work generalizes their dimension result for PGCNNs.
In a later work
[
SMK26
]
, Shahverdi et al. considered neural networks and CNN for a generic polynomial as an activation functions, and they showed that for CNN the fibers are generally singletons and for fully connected neural networks fibers are finite.
Finally, in
[
SMBK26
]
, Shahverdi et al. treated identifiable equivariant models and showed that, under the condition of identifiability, these models are layerwise equivariant for some representation on latent spaces.
An interesting feature of this result is that it is architecture-agnostic.

2
Preliminaries on graded group algebras

In this section, we present graded group algebras, their regular representations, and how Kronecker and Hadamard products act on them.
These are the building blocks for polynomial group convolutional networks. The section can be skipped, in case the reader is familiar with these concepts from algebra, and returned to later when needed.

In the following,
G
and
H
are general finite groups with order
n=|G|
and
|H|
and
S:=\bigoplus_{i}S_{i}
is a graded commutative ring such as the ring of polynomials in
n
variables
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
For two elements
g,h\in G
we denote their group product by
gh
and for two elements
s,t\in S
we denote their ring product by
st
and their sum by
s+t
.

2.1
Graded group algebras and regular representations

Here we present graded group algebras and their regular representations, which describe the
G
action on group convolutional networks.
We show, under a fixed choice of basis, these representations are described by
G
-circulant matrices, which are a generalization of the circulent matrices in the case where
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

The graded group algebra
S[G]
is an algebra

S[G]:=\{\theta\ |\ \theta:G\to S\}\cong S^{|G|},

where
S^{|G|}
is the free
S
-module of rank
{|G|}
. The graded algebra bilinear product in
S[G]
is given by
the convolution operation,

(\theta\ast\psi)(g):=\sum_{h\in G}\theta(gh^{-1})\psi(h),

and the scalar multiplication is defined componentwise; i.e. for
s\in S
we define
(s\theta)(g):=s\theta(g)
.
A basis of
S[G]
is given by the group elements in
G
.
We call elements in
S[G]

S
-filters over
G
.

Remark 2.2
.

There are multiple equivalent ways to describe a filter
\theta\in S[G]
.
In the definition it is described as a function from
G
to
S
.
Another useful way to describe this filter is to identify it with the formal linear combination

\theta\equiv\sum_{g\in G}\theta(g)g.

Then the convolution defined above becomes just the product of such formal linear combinations.
Note that if
G=(\mathbb{Z},+)
, then the description of a finite filter as a formal linear combination identifies it with a polynomial in one variable, which is the identification of convolutional filters in linear CNN presented in
[
KMMT22
]
.

Also from here it is clear how
S[G]
is isomorphic to the free
S
-module of rank
{|G|}
.
Lastly, if we fix an order
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
on the elements of
G
, then we can also identify the filter
\theta
with the tuple
(\theta(g_{1}),\dots,\theta(g_{n}))
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{3}=\{0,1,2\}
be the cyclic group of order
3
(with addition modulo
3
),
and let
S=\mathbb{R}
concentrated in degree
0
.
Then

\mathbb{R}[G]=\{\theta:\mathbb{Z}_{3}\to\mathbb{R}\}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}.

A filter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}[G]
is determined by its values

\theta(0)=a,\qquad\theta(1)=b,\qquad\theta(2)=c,

and can be written as the formal linear combination

\theta=a\,0+b\,1+c\,2.

Similarly, let
\psi\in\mathbb{R}[G]
be given by

\psi(0)=x,\qquad\psi(1)=y,\qquad\psi(2)=z,\quad\text{so that}\quad\psi=x\,0+y\,1+z\,2.

The convolution
\theta\ast\psi
is the function
\mathbb{Z}_{3}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(\theta\ast\psi)(g)=\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\theta(g-h)\psi(h),

where subtraction is taken modulo
3
.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle(\theta\ast\psi)(0)

\displaystyle=ax+bz+cy,

\displaystyle(\theta\ast\psi)(1)

\displaystyle=ay+bx+cz,

\displaystyle(\theta\ast\psi)(2)

\displaystyle=az+by+cx.

Definition 2.4

(
S[G]
regular representation)
.

The regular algebra representation
\rho
of
S[G]
on itself is defined as

\displaystyle\rho:S[G]

\displaystyle\to\text{End}(S[G]),\qquad\rho_{\theta}(\psi)=\theta\ast\psi.

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\mapsto\rho_{\theta}

The algebra representation
\rho
is completely determined by the induced group representation of
G
on
S[G]
.

Definition 2.5

(
G
-circulent matrix)
.

Fix an order
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
of the elements of
G
. With respect to this basis, the map
\rho_{\theta}
is represented by an
n\times n

G
-circulent matrix

\text{Mat}_{\theta}
over
S
, whose entries are given by

(\text{Mat}_{\theta})_{ij}:=\theta(g_{i}g_{j}^{-1}).

So the vector representation of
\text{Mat}_{\theta}\psi
under this base is given by

(\text{Mat}_{\theta}\psi)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\text{Mat}_{\theta})_{ij}\psi(g_{j}),

which is just a rewriting of the convolution
(\theta\ast\psi)(g_{i})
.
In the special case
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the matrix
\text{Mat}_{\theta}
reduces to the classical circulent matrix.

Remark 2.6
.

Note that since
Mat
is the algebra representation
\rho
under a choice of base, it follows immediately that for any filters
\theta,\psi\in S[G]
we get

\text{Mat}_{\theta}\text{Mat}_{\psi}=\text{Mat}_{\theta\ast\psi}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \text{Mat}_{\theta}+\text{Mat}_{\psi}=\text{Mat}_{\theta+\psi}.

Also, the set
\{\text{Mat}_{g}\ |\ g\in G\}
defines a basis for
G
-circulent matrices over the algebra
S
, where we identify
g\in G
with the filter
g(h)=1
if
h=g
otherwise
g(h)=0
.

In the following proposition, we relate the invertibility of
G
-circulent matrices to the invertibility of filters as elements in the group algebra
S[G]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\theta\in S[G]
. If
\text{Mat}_{\theta}
is invertible, then its inverse is also
G
-circulent.
By Remark
2.6
,
\text{Mat}_{\theta}
is invertible if and only if
\theta
is invertible in
S[G]
.

Proof.

Since
S
is commutative, we can apply Cayley-Hamilton to the inverse
\text{Mat}_{\theta}^{-1}
to write it as a polynomial in
\text{Mat}_{\theta}
.
Since
\text{Mat}_{\theta}=\sum_{g\in G}\theta(g)\text{Mat}_{g}
, then
\text{Mat}_{\theta}^{-1}
is also a linear combination of the matrices
\text{Mat}_{g}
’s with coefficients in
S
. Therefore
\text{Mat}_{\theta}^{-1}
is also
G
-circulent.
∎

2.2
Kronecker and Hadamard products

Next we define the Kronecker product and the Hadamard product between filters and state propositions about their interactions with convolution.
These two operations will give us two ways to define polynomial activation functions.

Proposition 2.8

(Definition of Kronecker Product)
.

There is a canonical isomorphism
\iota
between
S[G]\otimes S[H]
and
S[G\times H]
given by

\displaystyle\iota:S[G]\otimes S[H]

\displaystyle\to S[G\times H]

\displaystyle\theta\otimes\psi

\displaystyle\mapsto\iota(\theta\otimes\psi)

where for
(g,h)\in G\times H
,
\iota(\theta\otimes\psi)(g,h):=\theta(g)\psi(h)
. By abuse of notation, we denote the filter
\iota(\theta\otimes\psi)
by
\theta\otimes\psi
and call it the Kronecker product of
\theta
and
\psi
.

Remark 2.9
.

For
r\geq 1
, and
\theta\in S[G]
we use the notation

\theta^{\otimes r}:=\underbrace{\theta\otimes\ldots\otimes\theta}_{r},

which is a filter in
S[G^{r}]
where
G^{r}
is the direct product of
r
copies of
G
.
Note that the Kronecker product respects the grading; i.e. if
\theta\in S_{k}[G]
and
\psi\in S_{l}[H]
then
\theta\otimes\psi\in S_{k+l}[G\times H]
and
\theta^{\otimes r}\in S_{rk}[G^{r}]
.

Definition 2.10

(Hadamard Product)
.

Let
\theta,\psi\in S[G]
, their
Hadamard product
is the filter denoted by
\theta\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\psi\in S[G]
and is given by

(\theta\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\psi)(g):=\theta(g)\psi(g).

Moreover, we denote the
r
th Hadamard power of
\theta
by
\sigma_{r}(\theta):=\underbrace{\theta\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\ldots\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\theta}_{r}
. The Hadamard product also respects the grading of
S
.

To justify our terminology, note that for any two filters
\theta
and
\psi
we have the identities

\text{Mat}_{\theta\otimes\psi}=\text{Mat}_{\theta}\otimes\text{Mat}_{\psi}

and

\text{Mat}_{\theta\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\psi}=\text{Mat}_{\theta}\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\text{Mat}_{\psi}

where on the right hand side in both of them, we use the Kronecker product and the Hadamard product between matrices, respectively.

Example 2.11
.

Let
G=H=\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
and
S=\mathbb{R}
.
Fix the ordered bases
\{0,1\}
of
\mathbb{R}[G]
and

\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
of
\mathbb{R}[G\times H]
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}[G]
and
\psi\in\mathbb{R}[H]
be given by

\theta(0)=a,\ \theta(1)=b,\qquad\psi(0)=x,\ \psi(1)=y.

A direct computation shows that left convolution by
\theta
and
\psi

is represented by the matrices

\text{Mat}_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&a\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{Mat}_{\psi}=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}.

The Kronecker product of these matrices is

\text{Mat}_{\theta}\otimes\text{Mat}_{\psi}=\begin{pmatrix}ax&ay&bx&by\\
ay&ax&by&bx\\
bx&by&ax&ay\\
by&bx&ay&ax\end{pmatrix}.

On the other hand, the filter
\theta\otimes\psi\in\mathbb{R}[G\times H]
satisfies

(\theta\otimes\psi)(g,h)=\theta(g)\psi(h)
, and convolution by

\theta\otimes\psi
on
\mathbb{R}[G\times H]
is represented, in the chosen basis,
by the same matrix.

The following proposition shows that the Kronecker product distributes over convolution.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in S[G]
and
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in S[H]
. Then
(\theta_{1}\otimes\psi_{1})\ast(\theta_{2}\otimes\psi_{2})=(\theta_{1}\ast\theta_{2})\otimes(\psi_{1}\ast\psi_{2})
.
By induction it holds that for
\theta_{i},\psi_{i}\in S[G_{i}]
for
1\leq i\leq r

(\theta_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\theta_{r})\ast(\psi_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\psi_{r})=(\theta_{1}\ast\psi_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes(\theta_{r}\ast\psi_{r}).

Proof.

Use definition of convolution, we get

\displaystyle(\theta_{1}\otimes\psi_{1})\ast(\theta_{2}\otimes\psi_{2})(g,h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime}}(\theta_{1}\otimes\psi_{1})(gg^{\prime-1},hh^{\prime-1})(\theta_{2}\otimes\psi_{2})(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})

(use definition of
\otimes
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime}}\theta_{1}(gg^{\prime-1})\psi_{1}(hh^{\prime-1})\theta_{2}(g^{\prime})\psi_{2}(h^{\prime})

(reorder the terms)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime}}\theta_{1}(gg^{\prime-1})\theta_{2}(g^{\prime})\psi_{1}(hh^{\prime-1})\psi_{2}(h^{\prime})

(split the sum into product of two sums)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{g^{\prime}}\theta_{1}(gg^{\prime-1})\theta_{2}(g^{\prime})\right)\left(\sum_{h^{\prime}}\psi_{1}(hh^{\prime-1})\psi_{2}(h^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=(\theta_{1}\ast\theta_{2})(g)(\psi_{1}\ast\psi_{2})(h)=(\theta_{1}\ast\theta_{2})\otimes(\psi_{1}\ast\psi_{2})(g,h)

∎

On the other hand, the Hadamard product does not distribute over convolution, and this is easy to check as follows.
Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in S[G]
then

\displaystyle(\theta_{1}\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\theta_{2})\ast(\psi_{1}\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\psi_{2})

\displaystyle(g)=\sum_{h\in G}\theta_{1}(gh^{-1})\theta_{2}(gh^{-1})\psi_{1}(h)\psi_{2}(h)

\displaystyle\neq\left(\sum_{h\in G}\theta_{1}(gh^{-1})\psi_{1}(h)\right)\left(\sum_{h\in G}\theta_{2}(gh^{-1})\psi_{2}(h)\right)=(\theta_{1}\ast\psi_{1})\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}(\theta_{2}\ast\psi_{2})(g).

The following proposition explains the relation between the Hadamard product, the Kronecker product, and convolution.
This is an important proposition, as it tells us how to move between the two descriptions of polynomial activation functions; see §
3
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
\theta_{1},\psi_{1},\theta_{2},\psi_{2}\in S[G]
. Then

(\theta_{1}\ast\psi_{1})\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}(\theta_{2}\ast\psi_{2})=\left((\theta_{1}\otimes\theta_{2})\ast(\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{2})\right)|_{G}.

It follows that for
\theta,\psi\in S[G]
and
r\geq 1
we get
\sigma_{r}(\theta\ast\psi)=(\theta^{\otimes r}\ast\psi^{\otimes r})|_{G}
, where
G
is identified with the diagonal subgroup in
G^{r}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.12
,

(\theta_{1}\otimes\theta_{2})\ast(\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{2})=(\theta_{1}\ast\psi_{1})\otimes(\theta_{2}\ast\psi_{2}).

Restricting the right hand side to the diagonal subgroup
G<G^{2}
gives us
(\theta_{1}\ast\psi_{1})\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}(\theta_{2}\ast\psi_{2})
, as required.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

If
H
is a subgroup of
G
then
S[H]
is a subalgebra of
S[G]
.
Therefore, for
\psi\in S[H]
and
\theta\in S[G]
we have
\theta\ast\psi,\psi\ast\theta\in S[G]
. Namely,

(\theta\ast\psi)(g)=\sum_{h\in H}\theta(gh^{-1})\psi(h)\ \text{and}\ (\psi\ast\theta)(g)=\sum_{h\in H}\psi(h)\theta(h^{-1}g).

In other words, any filter
\psi\in S[H]
can be extended to
G
by setting
\psi(g)=0
for all
g\notin H
.
In case we need to distinguish between
\psi\in S[H]
and its extension
\psi\in S[G]
we denote the extension by
\psi^{G}
and its associated matrix by
\text{Mat}_{\psi}^{G}
.

2.3
General conditions and symbolic ranks

To compute the dimension of the neuromanifold, which is the image of the parametrization map
\Phi
(see §
3
), we will need to compute the rank of the Jacobian of the parametrization map
\Phi
at a general set of parameters
\theta
.
In this subsection, we define what a general condition is, and we introduce the concept of symbolic matrix and symbolic ranks that will help us define general conditions.

Definition 2.15

(generality)
.

We say that a condition
\mathcal{P}
is
general
in
\mathbb{K}^{m}
if it holds over a dense set in
\mathbb{K}^{m}
equipped with the Zariski topology.

Another more intuitive way to understand generality, although not exactly equivalent, is to think of
\mathcal{P}
as holding everywhere in
\mathbb{K}^{m}
except for a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

Definition 2.16
.

A symbolic matrix
M
is a matrix over a ring of polynomials
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]
.
We say
M
is symbolic in terms of the indeterminates
(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
.
The symbolic rank of
M
is decided by the vanishing of its minors in the ring
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]
.

For a symbolic matrix
M
over
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]
and a point
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})\in\mathbb{K}^{m}
, we construct the matrix
M(p)
from
M
by substituting every
y_{i}
for
p_{i}
.
Then the condition on
p
to satisfy
\text{rank}(M)=\text{rank}(M(p))
is general in
\mathbb{K}^{m}
, because this condition fails if and only if
p
is the zero of a nontrivial minor of
M
.
We illustrate this in the following example.

Example 2.17
.

Consider the symbolic matrix over
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},y_{2}]

M=\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y_{2}\\
y_{2}&y_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

The determinant of
M
is the polynomial

\det M=y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}.

Hence, over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},y_{2}]
, the symbolic rank of
M
is
2
since the determinant is not the zero polynomial in
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},y_{2}]
. Therefore, a point
p=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{K}^{2}
satisfies
\text{rank}(M)=\text{rank}(M(p_{1},p_{2}))
if and only if
p_{1}^{2}-p_{2}^{2}\neq 0
which is a general condition in
\mathbb{K}^{2}
.
This example illustrates that the rank of a symbolic matrix is determined by polynomial conditions on the indeterminates and that rank deficiencies occur precisely on algebraic varieties defined by the vanishing of suitable minors.

2.4
Invertibility of filters

In this subsection, a general filter
\theta\in S[G]
is shown to be invertible in the group algebra.
Similar results hold for its extension to
G^{r}
, its
r
th Hadamard product, and its
r
th Kronecker product.
These results will be relevant in the proof of the main theorems. Since we also work in a general setting, we will be allowed to assume that the matrices we work with are full rank.

To comment on what is aimed for in these proofs, note that a general square matrix
M
has, of course, a full rank because its determinant defines a nontrivial polynomial in terms of its entries.
What we show in what follows is that for
G
-circulent matrices, which have weight-sharing constraints, the determinant still defines a nontrivial polynomial regardless of the group
G
.

Definition 2.18
.

For a filter
\theta\in S[G]
, we define its determinant to be the determinant of its associated matrix, i.e.
\det(\theta):=\det(\text{Mat}_{\theta})
.

In the following proposition, we relate the determinants of the three filters
\theta\in S[G]
and
\theta^{\otimes},\theta^{G^{r}}\in S[G^{r}]
.

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
\theta\in S[G]
, then we have

\det(\theta^{\otimes r})=\det(\theta)^{rn^{r}}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \det(\theta^{G^{r}})=\det(\theta)^{n^{r-1}}

where
n=|G|
.
This implies that
\theta\in S[G]
is invertible if and only if
\theta^{\otimes r}
and
\theta^{G^{r}}
in
S[G^{r}]
are both invertible.

Proof.

The first equality follows directly from the property of the Kronecker product for matrices.

The second equality follows from a standard argument in representation theory.
Since we identify
G
with the diagonal subgroup
\Delta<G^{r}
, we obtain that the restriction of the regular algebra representation of
S[G^{r}]
to
S[G]
has
|G^{r}/G|=n^{r-1}
unique
S[G]
-invariant sub-representation
V_{i}\subset S[G^{r}]
where
S[G^{r}]=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n^{r-1}}V_{i}
and
S[G]\cong V_{i}
for all
i
. Therefore, under a suitable choice of basis, we have
\text{Mat}_{\theta}^{G^{r}}
is a block diagonal matrix of
n^{r-1}
blocks where each block is equal to
\text{Mat}_{\theta}
.
This proves our claim.
∎

In the following proposition, we state that invertibility of filters is a general condition in
S[G]
.

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
\theta\in S[G]
then the following conditions are general for all
r\geq 1
:

1.

\det(\theta)\neq 0

2.

\det(\theta^{G^{r}})\neq 0

3.

\det(\theta^{\otimes r})\neq 0

4.

\det(\sigma_{r}(\theta))\neq 0
.

Proof.

Assume
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
and
\theta=(\theta_{g_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{g_{n}})
where
g_{1}=e
is the group identity.
We have
(\text{Mat}_{\theta})_{i,j}=\theta(g_{i}g_{j}^{-1})
. For fixed
g_{i}
(row) and
g_{k}
there is a unique
g_{j}
such that
g_{i}g_{j}^{-1}=g_{k}
, namely
g_{j}=g_{k}^{-1}g_{i}
.
Similarly for fixed
g_{j}
(column) and
g_{k}
there is a unique
g_{i}
such that
g_{i}g_{j}^{-i}=g_{k}
.
This means the element
\theta(g_{k})
occurs once and only once in each column and in each row in the matrix
\text{Mat}_{\theta}
.
Therefore by elementary rules of the determinant, the term
\theta(g_{k})^{n}
for all
g_{k}\in G
occurs once and only once in the determinant of
\text{Mat}_{\theta}
.
This implies the condition
\det(\text{Mat}_{\theta})=\det(\theta)\neq 0
in
S[G]
is general.
By Proposition
2.19
, the conditions 2 and 3 are general.

The same argument shows that
\det(\sigma_{r}(\theta))
is not the trivial polynomial because it includes the terms
\theta(g_{k})^{rn}
for all
g_{k}\in G
.
∎

Corollary 2.21
.

Propositions
2.19
and
2.20
imply that generally
\theta,\sigma_{r}(\theta)\in S[G]
and
\theta^{\otimes r},\theta^{G^{r}}\in S[G^{r}]
are invertible, for all
r\geq 1
. In particular, they are generally not zero divisors in their algebras.

3
Polynomial group convolutional neural networks

In this section, polynomial group convolutional networks and their neuromanifolds are defined, which are the main object of study here.
Recall that the main goal of this article is to compute the dimension of these neuromanifolds.

3.1
Definition of polynomial group convolutional neural networks

Definition 3.1

(GCNN and PGCNN)
.

A group convolutional neural network (GCNN) with the filters
\theta:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\in(\mathbb{K}[G])^{L}
and an activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{K}
is the map

\displaystyle\Phi_{\theta}:\mathbb{K}[G]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{K}[G]

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\sigma(\ldots(\sigma(\sigma(x\ast\theta_{1})\ast\theta_{2})\ast\ldots\ast\theta_{L-1})\ast\theta_{L},

where
\sigma
acts component wise.
When
\sigma=\sigma_{r}
, where
\sigma_{r}
denotes the
r
th Hadamard product, we call
\Phi_{\theta}
a polynomial group convolutional neural network (PGCNN).
We call
x
the input filter and
\theta_{l}
the
l
th weight filter for
1\leq l\leq L
.

The GCNNs are
G
-equivariant, where the
G
-action is the one given by the regular representation in Section §
2
, and equivariance follows directly from the associativity of convolution. Before discussing PGCNN further, we present a few remarks to connect our definition with existing work in the literature.

Remark 3.2
.

Convolutional neural networks, regardless of their name, are in the literature defined using the operation
cross correlation

\star
instead of convolution
\ast
. Nevertheless, these two operations are intrinsically connected and interchangeable via the relation
\theta\star\psi:=\theta\ast\overline{\psi}
, where the filter
\overline{\psi}(g):=\psi(g^{-1})
is called the involution of
\psi
.
Therefore these two conventions are equivalent in the sense that every neural network defined via convolution can be defined via cross correlation by involuting all its filters.
In this article, we adopt the convolutional convention for two reasons. Firstly, under this convention, the
G
-action translates to just one extra left convolution, and the equivariance property translates into associativity of the convolution operation. Secondly, by associativity of convolutions, we can study the interaction between the weight filters
\theta
away from the input filter
x
.

Remark 3.3
.

In machine learning literature, the input filter
x
is called the input signal, and the weight filters correspond to convolutional layers.
Moreover, the signal
x
is considered over a domain
\Omega
equipped with a
G
action. In other words,
x:\Omega\to\mathbb{K}
where for
t\in\Omega
and
g\in G
we have
(g\cdot x)(t):=x(g^{-1}\cdot t)
.
In this context, what we define above is a special case of GCNN where the domain
\Omega=G
.
This simplification is convenient because it keeps the group algebra structure at the first layer.

Remark 3.4
.

Notice that when
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is the
n
th cyclic group, the GCNN does
not
specialize to CNN considered, for example, in
[
KMMT22
]
and
[
SMK25
]
because these CNNs are
not equivariant
with respect to shifts.
The main reason is that in this CNN architecture, the domain is considered to be a sequence rather than a circle.
To see this, let
C_{k}
denotes the domain that is a sequence of length
k
.
Moreover, let
x
be a signal on
C_{n}
and
\theta
be a signal (or filter) over
C_{k}
where
k<n
.
Then their cross correlation with a step of length 1 will be the signal
x\star_{CNN}\theta
over
C_{n-k+1}
given by

(x\star_{CNN}\theta)(l):=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}x(l+j)\theta(j),\,\text{where}\,l<n-k+1.

Assume that
s_{d}
denotes the shift to the right with
d
steps; then

(s_{d}\cdot x)(l):=\theta(l-d\mod n)
, note importantly that the order of the modulo operation depends on the size of the signal
x
.
Given this, we have

s_{d}\cdot(x\star_{CNN}\theta)(l)=(x\star_{CNN}\theta)(l-d\mod(n-k+1))=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}x([l-d\mod(n-k+1)]+j)\theta(j),

and

(s_{d}\cdot x\star_{CNN}\theta)(l)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}s_{d}\cdot x(j+l)\theta(j)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}x([l-d\mod n]+j)\theta(j).

This implies
s_{d}\cdot(x\star_{CNN}\theta)\neq s_{d}\cdot x\star_{CNN}\theta
, unless
k=1
which means the convolution filter contains only one element.
Hence, standard discrete CNNs with finite filters are not equivariant with respect to cyclic shifts, except in the trivial case
k=1
.

3.2
Neuromanifolds of PGCNNs

From Definition
3.1
, we see that for any tuple of
L
filters
\theta:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\in(\mathbb{K}[G])^{L}
and activation function
\sigma_{r}
, the PGCNN
\Phi_{\theta}\in\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(x,r^{L-1})[G]
defines
n
homogeneous polynomials in
n
variables
x:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
with degree
r^{L-1}
. Moreover, each coefficient in the homogeneous polynomial
\Phi_{\theta}(g)
is itself a multihomogeneous polynomial with the multidegree
\mathbf{r}:=(r^{L-1},r^{L-2},\dots,1)
in the parameters of the weight filters
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
. Thus, these coefficients belong to the space
\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(\theta,\mathbf{r})
.

Definition 3.5

(Neuromanifold)
.

The
neuromanifold

\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
of the architecture of PGCNN of depth
L
and activation degree
r
is the image of the parameterization map
\Phi:\theta\mapsto\Phi_{\theta}
in
\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(x,r^{L-1})
; namely

\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}=\{\Phi_{\theta}\ |\ \theta\in(\mathbb{K}[G])^{L}\}\subset\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(x,r^{L-1})[G].

We define the associated
neurovariety

\mathcal{V}_{\Phi}
to be the Zariski closure of
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
in
\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(x,r^{L-1})[G]
.

Note that the projection map
\Phi_{\theta}\mapsto\Phi_{\theta}(e)
over the identity element is injective due to equivariance. Therefore we can consider the ambient space of the neuromanifold
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
to be
\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(x,r^{L-1})
rather than
\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(x,r^{L-1})[G]
.

As we have seen until now, the activation function is represented using the Hadamard product.
Another equivalent way is to replace the Hadamard product with the Kronecker product using Proposition
2.13
.
So in terms of the Kronecker product, the filter
\Phi_{\theta}
can be written as

\Phi_{\theta}=(x^{\otimes r^{L-1}}\ast\varphi_{\theta})|_{G},

where
\varphi_{\theta}=\theta_{1}^{\otimes r^{L-1}}\ast\theta_{2}^{\otimes r^{L-2}}\ast\dots\ast\theta_{L}\in\mathbb{K}[G^{r^{L-1}}]
. We define the parametrization map

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathbb{K}[G]^{L}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{K}[G^{r^{L-1}}]

\displaystyle\theta\mapsto\varphi_{\theta}

and we denote its image by
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=\{\varphi_{\theta}\ |\ \theta\in\mathbb{K}[G]^{L}\}\subset\mathbb{K}[G^{r^{L-1}}]
.

Both of the maps
\Phi
and
\varphi
are defined by multihomogeneous polynomials in the weight parameters
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
, and therefore, by the Tarski-Seidenberg Theorem, both
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
are semi-algebraic sets in their respective ambient spaces.
Moreover, they induce rational maps
\tilde{\Phi}
and
\tilde{\varphi}
on the projectivizations of their domains and codomains.
The relation between the rational maps
\tilde{\varphi}
and
\tilde{\Phi}
is shown in a commutative diagram, see Figure

, where

is a projective linear map (it consists of convolution with

and the restriction to
e\in G
).

Remark 3.6

(Linear GCNN)

In the linear case, where

\Phi_{\theta}=x\ast\varphi_{\theta}=x\ast\theta_{1}\ast\dots\ast\theta_{L}.

.

4
Main results on neuromanifolds of PGCNN

In this section, we state our main results about the neuromanifolds of PGCNN: we compute the dimensions
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
, in addition to give a description of the general fibers of
\varphi
and
\Phi
.

4.1
Dimension of
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}

We describe here broadly our technique to compute the dimension of the neuromanifold
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
and the dimension of
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
.

1.

We study the rank of the Jacobians of the parametrization maps
\Phi
and
\varphi
at general filters.
Note that for nonzero scalars
\lambda_{l}\in\mathbb{K}-\{0\}
, where
1\leq l\leq L-1
, and any tuple of filters
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\in\mathbb{K}[G]^{L}
, we have

\varphi_{\theta}=\varphi_{\psi}\ \text{and}\ \Phi_{\theta}=\Phi_{\psi},

where
\psi=(\lambda_{1}\theta_{1},\lambda_{1}^{r}\lambda_{2}\theta_{2},\dots,\lambda_{L-2}^{r}\lambda_{L-1}\theta_{L-1},\lambda_{L-1}^{r}\theta_{L})
.
Therefore the rank of the Jacobians
J_{\theta}\varphi
and
J_{\theta}\Phi
is bounded up by
nL-L+1=n(L-1)+1
.

2.

The vectors that lie in the kernels of
J_{\theta}\varphi
and
J_{\theta}\Phi
due to Eq (
1
) are all killed by the projectivization of the domain. Therefore if
J_{\theta}\tilde{\varphi}
and
J_{\theta}\tilde{\Phi}
are full rank, then the affine Jacobians
J_{\theta}\varphi
and
J_{\theta}\Phi
have the maximal rank
n(L-1)+1
.

3.

To compute the rank of the Jacobians at general filters
\theta
, we consider the Jacobians as symbolic matrices in terms of the parameters
\theta
and compute their symbolic rank.

Theorem 4.1
.

For activation degree
r\geq 2
, the rational map
\tilde{\varphi}
is
generally regular,
i.e. its Jacobian is full rank at a general point in its domain. This implies that
\dim(\mathcal{M}_{\varphi})=L(n-1)+1
, where
n=|G|
and
L
is the number of layers.

Proof.

Let
\theta:=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{L})
be
L
general filters on a group
G
with
n
elements.
The projectivized map
\tilde{\varphi}
is given by the following composition of maps,
\tilde{\varphi}=p\circ\varphi\circ f
,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{K}^{n}))^{L}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f}(\mathbb{K}^{n})^{L}\xrightarrow{\varphi}\mathbb{K}^{n^{r^{L-1}}}\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{K}^{n^{r^{L-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle([\theta_{1}],\ldots,[\theta_{L}])

\displaystyle\mapsto\underbrace{\left(\dfrac{\theta_{1}}{|\theta_{1}|},\ldots,\dfrac{\theta_{L}}{|\theta_{L}|}\right)}_{:=\dfrac{\theta}{|\theta|}}\mapsto\varphi\left(\dfrac{\theta}{|\theta|}\right)\mapsto\left[\varphi\left(\dfrac{\theta}{|\theta|}\right)\right]=[\varphi(\theta)],

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm for
V=\mathbb{K}^{n}
. Note that even though the Euclidean norm depends on the choice of an absolute value for the field
\mathbb{K}
, which exists for all fields, the proof holds regardless of this choice.

Let
[\theta]:=([\theta_{1}],\ldots,[\theta_{L}])
, then
J_{[\theta]}\tilde{\varphi}=J_{\varphi(\theta/|\theta|)}p\circ J_{\theta/|\theta|}\varphi\circ J_{[\theta]}f
. We have three observations:

•

\ker\left(J_{\varphi(\theta/|\theta|)}p\right)=\{k\varphi(\theta/|\theta|)\ |\ k\in\mathbb{K}\}
.

•

\ker\left(J_{\theta/|\theta|}\varphi\right)\cap\text{Im}(J_{[\theta]}f)=\{0\}
, this is because the basis of
\ker\left(J_{\theta/|\theta|}\varphi\right)
, as shown in Proposition
4.2
, consists of perturbations that do not alter the class
[\theta]
.

•

By Euler’s theorem for homogeneous functions, we have
(J_{\theta/|\theta|}\varphi)(\theta/|\theta|)=k\varphi(\theta/|\theta|)
for some constant
k\in\mathbb{K}
.

Assume
\dot{\theta}\in\ker\left(J_{[\theta]}\tilde{\varphi}\right)
then
(J_{\theta/|\theta|}\varphi\circ J_{[\theta]}f)(\dot{\theta})\in\ker J_{\varphi(\theta/|\theta|)}p
which implies
(J_{\theta/|\theta|}\varphi\circ J_{[\theta]}f)(\dot{\theta})=k\varphi(\theta/|\theta|)
. Hence

(J_{[\theta]}f)(\dot{\theta})=k\theta/|\theta|+\dot{\psi}
where
\dot{\psi}\in\ker\left(J_{\theta/|\theta|}\varphi\right)
. But
k\theta/|\theta|+\dot{\psi}
is a perturbation that does not alter the class
[\theta]
hence
\dot{\theta}=0
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.1
relies strongly on the next proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
L
be the number of layers and
r
be the degree of activation.
Let also
\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{L})
denote the tuple of filters that define
\varphi_{\theta}
.
Then for a general
\theta

\left((\theta_{1},-r\theta_{2},0,\ldots,0),(0,\theta_{2},-r\theta_{3},0,\ldots,0),\ldots,(0,\ldots,0,\theta_{L-1},-r\theta_{L})\right).

Proof.

We do a proof by induction over the number of layers

.
The hardest step in this inductive proof is to show that for a perturbation
\dot{\theta}\in\ker(J_{\theta}\varphi)
at a general point
\theta\in\mathbb{K}[G]^{L}
the last layer filter
\theta_{L}
and its corresponding perturbation filter
\dot{\theta}_{L}
are related by a scaling, i.e.
\dot{\theta}_{L}=\lambda\theta_{L}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\backslash\{0\}
.
For more details, see Appendix
A
.
∎

The next theorem states the same result for the dimension of the neuromanifold
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For activation degree

, the rational map
\tilde{\Phi}
is

\dim(\mathcal{M}_{\Phi})=L(n-1)+1

Proof.

The claim follows directly from Proposition

,
in the same manner that

in

.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
L
be the number of layers and

Let
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})

.
Then for a general

\bigl(\,(\theta_{1},-r\theta_{2},0,\dots,0),\ (0,\theta_{2},-r\theta_{3},0,\dots,0),\ \dots,\ (0,\dots,0,\theta_{L-1},-r\theta_{L})\,\bigr).

Remark 4.5
.

The package
PGCNNGeometry
allows us to perform sanity checks for the dimension results for small networks (groups with up to 13 elements and network depth up to 3).
However, the numerical computation becomes intractable for bigger groups and deeper network because the size of the Jacobian matrix, for both maps

and

, is at least quadratic in terms of the size of the group and exponential in terms of the number of layers.

4.2
General fiber of
\varphi

The fiber of
\varphi
at filters
\theta:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
is the set

\varphi^{-1}(\varphi_{\theta}):=\{\psi\in\mathbb{K}[G]^{L}\ |\ \varphi_{\psi}=\varphi_{\theta}\}.

In this subsection, the shape of the general fiber of
\varphi
is described. However, before we give this main result, we need to borrow a lemma about symmetric tensor ranks from
[
CGLM08
]
.

Lemma 4.6

(Lemma 5.1
[
CGLM08
]
)
.

Let
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
be linearly independent. Then the symmetric tensor

A:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{\otimes r}

has a symmetric rank
\text{rank}_{S}(A)=n
.

Theorem 4.7
.

For general filters
\theta:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
, we have
\tilde{\varphi}_{\theta}=\tilde{\varphi}_{\psi}
if and only if

\psi=(\theta_{1}\ast g_{1},\ g_{1}^{-1}\ast\theta_{2}\ast g_{2},\ \dots,\ g_{L-2}^{-1}\ast\theta_{L-1}\ast g_{L-1},\ g_{L-1}^{-1}\ast\theta_{L}),

where
g_{i}\in G
for
1\leq i\leq L-1
, up to rescaling each filter.

Proof.

We do a proof by induction over the number of layers

Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we presented a mathematical framework for polynomial group convolutional networks using graded group algebras.
Under this framework, we computed the dimension of the neuromanifold for a PGCNN where the group
G
is an arbitrary finite group.
We showed that the dimension does not depend on the group structure but only on the size of the group.
We also present a conjecture on the general fiber of these networks.

For future work, we aim to prove Conjecture
4.8
by studying the defining polynomials of the variety
VP_{n,k,d}
using the explicit construction provided in
[
AC14
]
.
Furthermore, to compute the degree and the generic Euclidean degree for the neuromanifold of PGCNN, we aim to generalize the techniques used in
[
SMK25
]
for the same computation for CNN.
In the CNN case, considered in
[
SMK25
]
, the parametrization map
\Phi
factors through Segre-Veronese embedding via a projective linear map.
Since the degree of a variety is invariant under projective linear morphism, the authors get that the degree of the Segre-Veronese variety naturally translates into the degree of the neurovariety of a CNN.
In contrast, in the case of PGCNN, the parametrization map
\Phi
factors through the Segre-Veronese embedding via a rational map with a nontrivial kernel rather than a projective linear morphism.
Therefore, in the case of PGCNN, we expect the Segre-Veronese factorization of the map
\Phi
to give an upper bound for both the degree and the generic Euclidean degree, rather than determining them exactly.

Another important finding of neuroalgebraic geometry is that points in the singular locus of the neuromanifold have a higher probability of being extrema under training
[
MSM+25
]
.
This makes the singular locus of the neuromanifold of PGCNN worthy of further investigation.
During our experimentation we observed that the rank of the Jacobian, for both maps
\varphi
and
\Phi
, drops when the matrix representation of any convolution layer is not full rank.
This suggests that there is a connection between singularities of layer, locally, and singularities of the network, globally.

For further generalization, we propose two natural directions. The first direction is to consider polynomial activation functions which are sums of Hadamard products.
This extension is interesting since, as we discussed before, PNNs are being used in multiple fields now.
Moreover, by Weierstrass Approximation Theorem, these networks can be used to approximate neural networks with non-polynomial activation functions.
This might enable a deeper investigation of the geometric structure underlying GCNNs with non-polynomial activations.

Another direction is to relax our restriction to finite groups, and study PGCNNs of compact groups.
In this case input signals and convolution filters are local sections of an associated vector bundle over a homogeneous space, and the convolution operation is formulated as an integral
[
CGW19
]
.
To consider this case, we either need to generalize our framework to use infinite dimensional graded group algebras, or to discretize compact groups to finite groups and reduce to the current case.
While in this setting one would have an infinite-dimensional generalization of a neuromanifold, relevant questions still remain about fibers and singularities.

\theta^{\prime}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L-1}),\qquad\dot{\theta}^{\prime}=(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L-1}).

Then:

1.

For any index
[g_{1},\dots,g_{r^{L-1}}]
we have

\Phi_{\theta}(g)[g_{1},\dots,g_{r^{L-1}}]=\sum_{\delta\in S_{r^{L-1}}}\varphi_{\theta}(g_{\delta(1)}^{-1}g,\dots,g_{\delta(r^{L-1})}^{-1}g)=\Phi_{\theta}(e)[g^{-1}g_{1},\dots,g^{-1}g_{r^{L-1}}],

where
S_{m}
denotes the permutation group on
m
elements.

2.

As proved in
[
SMK25
]
,

J_{\theta}\Phi(\dot{\theta})=r\bigl(J_{\theta^{\prime}}\Phi(\dot{\theta}^{\prime})\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})\bigr)\ast\theta_{L}\;+\;\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})\ast\dot{\theta}_{L}.

The Jacobian of
\Phi_{\theta}
is obtained by computing the Jacobian of each polynomial coefficient of
\Phi_{\theta}(g)
for all
g\in G
.

Proof.

Using (
B
), for any
g\in G
,

\Phi_{\theta}(g)=\sum_{h\in G^{r^{L-1}}}x^{\,r^{L-1}}(h)\,\varphi_{\theta}(h^{-1}g).

Since
x^{\,r^{L-1}}(h_{1})=x^{\,r^{L-1}}(h_{2})
whenever
h_{1},h_{2}
differ by a permutation, the formula (
1
) follows immediately.
∎

In other words, the first part of Lemma
B.1
states that the coefficients of the polynomial
\Phi_{\theta}(g)
are completely determined by the coefficients of the polynomial
\Phi_{\theta}(e)
at the group identity
e\in G
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{L-1},e)\in\mathbb{K}[G]^{L}
where
\theta_{i}
is a general filter for
1\leq i<L
and
e\in G\subset\mathbb{K}[G]
is the identity element. If
\dot{\theta}=(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L})\in\ker J_{\theta}\Phi
then
\dot{\theta}_{L}=\lambda e
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

Let
G=\{g_{1}=e,g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\}
,
\theta^{\prime}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L-1})
and
\dot{\theta}^{\prime}=(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L-1})
.
For brevity set

J:=J_{\theta^{\prime}}\Phi(\dot{\theta}^{\prime}).

Lemma
B.1

implies that
\dot{\theta}\in\ker J_{\theta}\Phi
if and only if

rJ\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})=-\,\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})\ast\dot{\theta}_{L}.

Evaluate the filters on both sides in Eq. (
B
) at
g=e
to get

(rJ\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}))(e)=-\sum_{h\in G}\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})(h)\dot{\theta}_{L}(h^{-1}).

Equation (
B
) is an equality of two homogeneous polynomials in
\text{Sym}_{\mathbb{K}}(x,r^{L-1})
.
Therefore for an index
[r_{1}g_{1},\dots,r_{n}g_{n}]
with
r_{1}+\dots+r_{n}=r^{L-1}
,
equation (
B
) gives the equation

(rJ\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}))(e)[r_{1}g_{1},\dots,r_{n}g_{n}]=-\sum_{h\in G}\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})(h)[r_{1}g_{1},\dots,r_{n}g_{n}]\dot{\theta}_{L}(h^{-1}),

which by Proposition
B.1
is equivalent to

(rJ\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}))(e)[r_{1}g_{1},\dots,r_{n}g_{n}]=-\sum_{h\in G}\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})(e)[r_{1}hg_{1},\dots,r_{n}hg_{n}]\dot{\theta}_{L}(h).

Since the above equation is a relation between the polynomials at only
e
, we remove
e
from the notation and write

(rJ\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}))[r_{1}g_{1},\dots,r_{n}g_{n}]=-\sum_{h\in G}\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[r_{1}hg_{1},\dots,r_{n}hg_{n}]\dot{\theta}_{L}(h).

These equations (
B
) form a system of equations in the unknowns
\dot{\theta}
; one equation for each multindex
[r_{1}g_{1},\dots,r_{n}g_{n}]
.
Note that on the one hand an unknown appears in the homogeneous polynomial
J
if and only if it comes from the first
L-1
filters in
\dot{\theta}
. On the other hand, the claim of this lemma concerns only the unknowns
\dot{\theta}_{L}
.
Therefore our goal is to eliminate the unknown homogeneous polynomial
J
from the system (
B
).
To achieve this, we need to express the coefficient of
J
in terms of
\dot{\theta}_{L}
. We do this step by step as follows.

First coefficients.

For the index
[r^{L-1}g_{i}]
, Eq (
B
) becomes

r(J\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}))[r^{L-1}g_{i}]=-\sum_{h\in G}\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[r^{L-1}hg_{i}]\,\dot{\theta}_{L}(h).

Unfolding the Hadamard product on the left hand side gives

r(J\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}))[r^{L-1}g_{i}]=rJ[r^{L-2}g_{i}]\,\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[(r-1)r^{L-2}g_{i}].

So we obtain

J[r^{L-2}g_{i}]=-\sum_{h\in G}\frac{\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[r^{L-1}hg_{i}]}{r\,\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[(r-1)r^{L-2}g_{i}]}\dot{\theta}_{L}(h).

Next coefficients.

For the multiindex
[g_{1},(r^{L-1}-1)g_{i}]
,

r(J\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}))[g_{1},(r^{L-1}-1)g_{i}]=-\sum_{h\in G}\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[hg_{1},(r^{L-1}-1)hg_{i})]\,\dot{\theta}_{L}(h).

The left side expands as

rJ[g_{1},(r^{L-2}-1)g_{i}]\,\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[(r-1)r^{L-2}g_{i}]+rJ[r^{L-2}g_{i}]\,\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[g_{1},((r-1)r^{L-2}-1)g_{i}],

so together with (
B
) this expresses

J[g_{1},(r^{L-2}-1)g_{i}]
linearly in
\dot{\theta}_{L}
.

Iterating.

Define for
0\leq m\leq r^{L-2}

O_{m}^{i}=\sum_{h\in G}\frac{\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[mhg_{1},(r^{L-1}-m)hg_{i}]}{r\,\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[(r-1)r^{L-2}g_{i}]}\dot{\theta}_{L}(h),

and

D_{k}^{i}=\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[kg_{1},((r-1)r^{L-2}-k)g_{i}].

Define
O_{m}
to be the square
n\times n
matrix where its rows are given by
O_{m}^{i}
. Namely, the entry
(i,h)
is given by

\frac{\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[mhg_{1},(r^{L-1}-m)hg_{i}]}{r\,\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[(r-1)r^{L-2}g_{i}]}.

For
0\leq m<k\leq r^{L-2}
set

C_{m,k}^{i}=\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})[(k-m)g_{1},((r-1)r^{L-2}+m-k)g_{i}]=D_{k-m}^{i},

and

C_{i}^{m,k}=\sum_{\{\alpha_{1}=m<\cdots<\alpha_{f}=k\}}\;\prod_{j=1}^{f-1}C_{\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j+1}}^{i},\qquad C_{i}^{k,k}=1.

Induction on
r_{1}
yields, for
0\leq r_{1}\leq r^{L-2}-1
and
1<i\leq n
:

J[r_{1}g_{1},(r^{L-2}-r_{1})g_{i}]=\sum_{m=0}^{r_{1}}O_{m}^{i}\,C_{i}^{m,r_{1}}.

Constructing the linear system only in
\dot{\theta}_{L}
.

Using (
B
) and (
B
), for each
i\neq 1
the coefficient equation (
B
) corresponding to
[r^{L-2}g_{1},(r-1)r^{L-2}g_{i}]
gives

r\sum_{r_{1}=0}^{r^{L-2}}J[r_{1}g_{1},(r^{L-2}-r_{1})g_{i}]\,D_{r^{L-2}-r_{1}}^{i}+rO_{r^{L-2}}^{i}D_{0}^{i}=0.

This is linear in
\dot{\theta}_{L}
, since each
J[\cdot]
and
O_{m}^{i}
is linear in
\dot{\theta}_{L}
.

These
n-1
equations (indexed by
i\neq 1
) form a
(n-1)\times n
symbolic matrix in the parameters
\theta^{\prime}
.
The entry in row
i
and column
h=g_{i}^{-1}
contains the term

\bigl(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}[r^{L-2}g_{1}])\bigr)^{\,r+1}\bigl(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}[r^{L-2}g_{i}]\bigr)^{\,r-1},

which is a polynomial in the parameters
\theta^{\prime}
(the term appears in the sum where
r_{1}=0
).
Excluding the entry at the column
h=e
, no other entry in this row contains a term that has the factor
\bigl(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}[r^{L-2}g_{1}])\bigr)^{\,r+1}
.

Hence the submatrix omitting the column
h=e
has a symbolically nonvanishing determinant, so the kernel is one–dimensional and spanned by
(1,0,\dots,0)
. Therefore
\dot{\theta}_{L}(h)=0
for
h\neq e
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We are finally ready to give the proof of Proposition
4.4

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

We write perturbations as
\dot{\theta}=(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L})
.
Set
\theta^{\prime}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L-1})
and
\dot{\theta}^{\prime}=(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L-1})
.
We proceed by induction on
L
.

Base case
L=1
.

For
L=1
we have
\Phi_{\theta}=x\ast\theta_{1}
, hence

J_{\theta}\Phi(\dot{\theta})=J_{\theta_{1}}(x\ast\theta_{1})(\dot{\theta}_{1})=x\ast\dot{\theta}_{1}.

This vanishes iff
\dot{\theta}_{1}=0
, so the kernel is trivial, consistent with the proposition.

Inductive step.

Assume the proposition holds for all networks with
L-1
layers.
We prove it for
L
layers.

For brevity set

J:=J_{\theta^{\prime}}\Phi(\dot{\theta}^{\prime}).

Lemma
B.1

implies that

\dot{\theta}\in\ker J_{\theta}\Phi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r\bigl(J\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\sigma_{r-1}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})\bigr)\ast\theta_{L}=-\,\sigma_{r}(\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}})\ast\dot{\theta}_{L}.

Let
\dot{\theta}_{L}=0
.
Since
\theta_{L}
is general, so by Corollary
2.21
we get
\theta_{L}
is not a zero divisor, and hence
J=0
. The required result follows by the inductive hypothesis.

Assume
\dot{\theta}_{L}\neq 0
, convolute both sides of (
B
) with the inverse of
\theta_{L}
, which exists because
\theta_{L}
is general, and let
\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{L}:=\dot{\theta}_{L}\ast\theta_{L}^{-1}
to get

rJ\mathrel{\text{\faIcon[regular]{dot-circle}}}\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\,r-1}\;=\;-\,\Phi_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\,r}\ast\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{L}.

(12)

Since
\theta^{\prime}
is general and
\dot{\theta}_{L}\neq 0
, then by Lemma
B.2
, we have
\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{L}=\lambda e
for some nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
.
This implies
\dot{\theta}_{L}=\lambda\theta_{L}
, and therefore any
\dot{\theta}\in\ker J_{\theta}\Phi
must have the form

\dot{\theta}=(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L-1},\;\lambda\theta_{L}).

Observe that the vector

(0,\dots,0,\;-\tfrac{\lambda}{r}\theta_{L-1},\;\lambda\theta_{L})

lies in the kernel as well (it is a direct check using the Jacobian composition rule). Subtracting this vector from
\dot{\theta}
yields

(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L-1}-(-\tfrac{\lambda}{r}\theta_{L-1}),\;0)\;=\;(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L-1}+\tfrac{\lambda}{r}\theta_{L-1},\;0)\in\ker J_{\theta}\Phi.

By Eq. (
B
) the vector

(\dot{\theta}_{1},\dots,\dot{\theta}_{L-1}+\tfrac{\lambda}{r}\theta_{L-1})\in\ker J_{\theta^{\prime}}\Phi,

and by the inductive hypothesis, it can be written as a linear combination of

\bigl((\theta_{1},-r\theta_{2},0,\dots,0),\,(0,\theta_{2},-r\theta_{3},0,\dots,0),\,\dots,\,(0,\dots,0,\theta_{L-2},-r\theta_{L-1})\bigr).

Adding back the previously subtracted vector

(0,\dots,0,-\tfrac{\lambda}{r}\theta_{L-1},\lambda\theta_{L})

shows that
\dot{\theta}
is a linear combination of the
L-1
vectors stated in the proposition.
This completes the induction and the proof.

∎
