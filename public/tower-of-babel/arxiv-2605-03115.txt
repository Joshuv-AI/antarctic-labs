Theorems of Bertini and Chevalley
János Kollár

Abstract
. We give a short proof of Chevalley’s theorem that every algebraic group is an extension of an Abelian variety by a linear algebraic group. Along the way we treat Bertini’s irreducibility theorem.



We revisit the following two well-known theorems.

Theorem 1

(Bertini’s irreducibility theorem)
.

Let
X
be a geometrically irreducible variety over an infinite field
k
, and

\pi:X\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
a dominant morphism,
n\geq 2
.
Let
H\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be a general hyperplane. Then

\pi^{-1}(H)
is geometrically irreducible.

Theorem 2

(Chevalley or Barsotti-Chevalley theorem)
.

Let
k
be a perfect field and

G
a connected, smooth, algebraic group over
k
.
Then
G
can be written as a group extension

1\to(\mbox{affine algebraic group})\to G\to(\mbox{proper algebraic group})\to 0.

The aim is to give proofs that use only results from Shafarevich’s textbook
[
Sha74
]
.
The proof of Bertini’s irreducibility theorem
given in Section
5
is such, and seems shorter than other proofs in the literature.

For Chevalley’s theorem we also need the
existence of the Jacobian of a smooth, projective curve; see Theorem
4
.
From algebraic group theory we use only Weil’s theorems on rational maps to algebraic groups, these are reproved in Section
3
.
See Remark
8
for other versions of Chevalley’s theorem.

The main message is that one should not be afraid of working with Weil divisors; they provide the optimal framework for some questions.

1.
Algebraic geometry background

We need only one result form algebraic geometry that is not treated in
basic textbooks:
the existence of the Jacobian, which is the group of degree 0 divisors modulo linear equivalence.

Theorem 4
.

Let
X
be a smooth, projective curve. Then
its Jacobian is a proper, algebraic group, denoted by
\operatorname{\mathbf{Jac}}(C)
. ∎

The proof in
[
Wei48
]
is very clear; so is the version in
[
Mil86
]
which uses contemporary language.
It was superseded by the construction of the Picard variety in
[
Gro62
]
; this viewpoint is presented in
[
Mum66
,
BLR90
]
.

Notation 5
.

Let
k
be a field with algebraic closure
\bar{k}
.

A
k
-variety
is an irreducible and reduced scheme
X
of finite type over
k
. Its
smooth locus
is denoted by
X^{\rm sm}
.

The adjective
geometrically
refers to properties over
\bar{k}
.

A
rational map
between varieties is denoted by a dotted arrow

\phi:X\dasharrow Y
. A
morphism
is everywhere defined, and denoted by a solid arrow

\phi:X\to Y
.

The
fiber

\phi^{-1}(y)
over
y\in Y
is usually denoted by
X_{y}
.

The notation for
linear systems
is discussed in Section
4
.

Let
S
be a
k
-scheme and
{\mathcal{P}}
a property that makes sense for
K
-points of
S
.
We say that
{\mathcal{P}}
holds for
general points
of
S
, iff there is a dense, open subset
S^{\circ}\subset S
such that

{\mathcal{P}}
holds for all
K
-points of
S^{\circ}
for every field extension
K\supset k
.

Let
G
be a connected, smooth, algebraic group with unit
e\in G
.
A
rational action
on a normal variety
X
is a rational map

\mu:G\times X\dasharrow X

such that
\mu(e,x)=x
and

\mu(g_{1}g_{2},x)=\mu\bigl(g_{1},\mu(g_{2},x)\bigr)

holds for
(g_{1},g_{2},x)
in a dense, open subset of
G\times G\times X
. One can identify
\mu
with the collection of birational maps

\mu_{g}:X\cong\{g\}\times X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu}}{{\dasharrow}}X;

these are defined for
g
in a dense, open subset of
G(\bar{k})
.

In classical terminology a morphism is called a ‘regular map.’ I try to avoid this, since both
[
Gro60
, IV.6.8.1]
and
[
Sta25
,
Tag 07R6
]
use ‘regular’ with a very different meaning.
However, if
\mu
is a morphism, it is usually called an
action

or
regular action
to emphasize the difference from a rational action.

Let
H,G
be algebraic groups and
\phi:H\dasharrow G
a rational map.
It is a
rational homomorphism
of algebraic groups if there are dense, open subsets

U\subset H
and
W\subset U\times U
,
such that
\phi(u_{1}u_{2})=\phi(u_{1})\phi(u_{2})
for
(u_{1},u_{2})\in W
.
If
\phi
is morphism, then we get the usual notion of
homomorphism or
regular homomorphism.

(We see in Lemma
11
that a rational group homomorphism is actually regular, but in Lemma
12
we will obtain a map

H\dasharrow\mathrm{PGL}_{N}
which is only a rational homomorphism to start with.)

2.
Proof of Chevalley’s Theorem

Notation 6
.

We work over a perfect field
k
with algebraic closure
\bar{k}
. Let
G
be a connected, smooth, algebraic group, with unit
e\in G
. The group operation is written multiplicatively.

A proper algebraic group is frequently denoted by
A
, and the group operation is written additively. In the proof these will always appear as
subgroups of products of Jacobians of smooth, projective curves.

7
Proof of Theorem
2
.

The proof is done in 5 steps.

Step
7
.1: Rational action on a projective variety.

The arguments are the simplest if we have a regular, faithful
G
-action on a normal, projective variety
X
. However, if we are willing to work with Weil divisors, then it is enough to use a
rational,

faithful
G
-action on a normal, projective variety
X
. These are easy to get.

Let
U\subset G
be any affine, open subset, and
X
the normalization of its closure in some projective space. Then
X
is normal and projective, with a rational, faithful
G
-action.

Step
7
.2: Algebraic map of
G
to an Abelian variety.

Let
G
be a connected, smooth, algebraic group acting rationally on a normal, projective variety
X
. We write the action as

\mu:G\times X\dasharrow X
.

Let
D
be a Weil divisor on
X
. The closure of the image of
G\times D
by
(1_{G},\mu)
is a Weil
divisor
{\mathbf{D}}
on
G\times X
. Let
{\mathbf{D}}_{g}
denote its restriction to
\{g\}\times X\cong X
; see Definition
13
.

For a smooth, projective curve
C\subset X^{\rm sm}
, let

\alpha_{C}:G\to\operatorname{\mathbf{Jac}}(C)
be the morphism that sends

g\in G
to
{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|_{C}-{\mathbf{D}}_{e}|_{C}
.

The Severi-Weil Lemma
28
says that there is a finite set of curves

\{C_{i}\subset X^{\rm sm}:i\in I\}
with product morphism

[formula]

\alpha_{X,D}:G\to\textstyle{\prod}_{i\in I}\operatorname{\mathbf{Jac}}(C_{i}),

such that
\alpha_{X,D}(g_{1})=\alpha_{X,D}(g_{2})
iff
{\mathbf{D}}_{g_{1}}
is linearly equivalent to
{\mathbf{D}}_{g_{2}}
.
Note that
\alpha_{X,D}(e)=0
.

At this point
\alpha_{X,D}
is a morphism of varieties, and we do not yet claim any naturality property for it.

Step
7
.3: Homomorphism of
G
to an Abelian variety.

The morphism
\alpha_{X,D}
obtained in (
7
.2.1) is
a group homomorphism, since any morphism from an algebraic group to a proper, algebraic group is a group homomorphism by

[
Wei48
]
. We recall the proof in Section
3
.

Let
H=H_{X,D}\subset G
denote the
identity component of the kernel of
\alpha_{X,D}
.
We aim to show that
H
is affine for suitable choice of
D
.

Step
7
.4: Linear representations of
H
.

We start with a special case when
the
G
-action on
X
is regular. Then translation by any
h\in G

gives isomorphisms of the complete linear systems

\mu_{h,g,D}:|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}|{\mathbf{D}}_{hg}|.

If
h\in H
then
|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|=|{\mathbf{D}}_{hg}|
, so for any fixed
g
we get a
linear representation

\tau_{g,D}:H\to\operatorname{Aut}\bigl(|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|\bigr)\cong\mathrm{PGL}_{N}.

The elements of the kernel map
D
to itself.
A sufficiently general
D
should have no automorphisms. If this holds then
\tau_{g,D}
is faithful, hence
H
is affine.

If the
G
-action on
X
is not regular, then the best
we can hope for is that the
\mu_{h,g,D}
are isomorphisms
for
(h,g)
in some open, dense subset of
H\times G
.
However, even this seems problematic, because of the following subtle difficulty.

Warning
7
.4.1.

Let
\{A_{i}:i\in I\}
be pairwise linearly equivalent divisors spanning the linear system
|A_{i}:i\in I|
. Even if the
g_{*}(A_{i})
are pairwise linearly equivalent divisors, the linear system spanned by the
g_{*}(A_{i})
is frequently different from the birational transform of the linear system
|A_{i}:i\in I|
. That is,

[formula]

g_{*}|A_{i}:i\in I|\neq|g_{*}(A_{i}):i\in I|,

see Example
17
.
However, Corollary
19
shows that equality holds in
(
7
.4.2) if the
A_{i}
are parametrized by a geometrically irreducible variety.

A solution is to replace the complete linear system
|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|
with a carefully chosen linear subsystem.

Step
7
.4.3: Linear representations of
H
, general case.

With
G\supset U\subset X
as in (
7
.1),
there is a dense, open subset
G^{*}\subset G
such that

{\mathbf{D}}_{g}
is the closure of its intersection with
U

for
g\in G^{*}
. Thus, if
g,hg\in G^{*}
, then

(\mu_{h})_{*}{\mathbf{D}}_{g}={\mathbf{D}}_{hg}
.
However, as we noted in (
7
.4.1), this does not imply that
(\mu_{h})_{*}|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|
and
|{\mathbf{D}}_{hg}|
are the same.

Fix now a
g\in G^{*}
. Let
H^{*}\subset H
be a dense, open subset such that
H^{*}\cdot g\subset G^{*}
.
Let
H^{**}\subset H^{*}
be any dense, open subset.
Then the linear subsystem

[formula]

|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|^{H}:=\bigl|{\mathbf{D}}_{hg}:h\in H^{**}\bigr|\subset|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|

is independent of
H^{**}
.
In particular,
\mu_{h}
maps a spanning set parametrized by

H^{*}\cap h^{-1}H^{*}
to the spanning set parametrized by

hH^{*}\cap H^{*}
.
Since
H
is geometrically irreducible,
Corollary
19
implies that

[formula]

(\mu_{h})_{*}|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|^{H}=|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|^{H}\quad\mbox{for}\quad h\in H^{*}.

Once (
7
.4.5) holds, Corollary
12
says that we get a
linear representation

[formula]

\tau_{g,D}:H\to\operatorname{Aut}\bigl(|{\mathbf{D}}_{g}|^{H}\bigr)\cong\mathrm{PGL}_{N}.

Its kernel is a subgroup of those elements of
H
that map
D
to itself.

Step
7
.5: Choosing
D
.

For ‘general’
D
we expect that the kernel
K
of

\tau_{g,D}
is trivial, but here is a concrete example.

Let
U\subset G
be an affine, open subset as in (
7
.1). Choose
D_{U}\subset U
to have an isolated singular point, and let
D\subset G
be its closure.

If a subgroup
K\subset H
fixes
D
, then it fixes
\operatorname{Sing}D
.
So, if
\operatorname{Sing}D
has an isolated point, then
K
is finite.
Then
\tau_{g,D}
is a linear representation of
H
with a finite kernel, hence
H
is affine.
This completes the proof of Theorem
2
. ∎

As a slight improvement,
if
\operatorname{char}k\neq 2
or
\operatorname{char}k=2
and
\dim G
is even, then
we can chose
D_{U}
such that its scheme theoretic singular set is a closed point. In this case we also get that
K
is reduced.
If
\operatorname{char}=2
, we can replace
G
by
G\times{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
.

Remark 8
.

The stronger form of Theorem
2
says that

G
has a unique, connected, affine, normal subgroup
H
such that
G/H
is proper.

Our proof shows that there is a minimal such
H^{\rm min}\subset G
.
This implies the stronger form, if we use
that the quotient of an affine, algebraic group by a normal, algebraic subgroup
is again an affine, algebraic group; see

[
Hum75
, Sec.11.5]
or
[
Bor91
, Thm.6.8]
.

3.
Maps to algebraic groups

These results are mostly due to Weil
[
Wei48
]
; see also
[
BLR90
, Sec.4.4]
.
The proofs are reproduced
for completeness.

Lemma 9
.

Let
(G,\cdot,e)
be a connected, algebraic group,
(A,+,0)
a proper, algebraic group and
\phi:G\to A
a morphism such that
\phi(e)=0
.
Then
\phi
is a group homomorphism.

Proof.
Let
\tau:G\times G\to A
be the map

\tau:(g_{1},g_{2})\mapsto\phi(g_{1}g_{2})-\phi(g_{2})-\phi(g_{1}).

We need to show that
\tau
is constant on a dense, open subset of
G\times G
. Choose an affine
e\in U\subset G
and a normal, projective

U\lhook\joinrel\to\bar{U}
. Given
g_{1},g_{2}\in U
choose
smooth, projective, irreducible curves

\{e,g_{i}\}\subset C_{i}\subset\bar{U}
.
Then
\tau
restricts to a rational map

\tau_{C}:C_{1}\times C_{2}\dasharrow A
, which is a morphism by (
10
).

Also,
\tau_{C}
maps both

\{e\}\times C_{2}
and
C_{1}\times\{e\}
to
0\in A
.
We apply the Rigidity lemma of
[
Mum70
, Sec.4]
and
[
Sha74
, Sec.III.4.3]
. We get that

\tau_{C}
maps both

\{c_{1}\}\times C_{2}
and
C_{1}\times\{c_{2}\}
to points for all
c_{i}
.
Thus
\tau_{C}
is constant. ∎

Lemma 10
.

Let
X
be a smooth variety and
A
a proper algebraic group. Then every rational map
\phi:X\dasharrow A
is a morphism.

Proof. Since
A
is proper, there is a closed subset
Z\subset X
of codimension
\geq 2
such that
\phi|_{X\setminus Z}
is a morphism.
Let
\Delta_{X}\subset X\times X
denote the diagonal, and
consider

\Phi:X\times X\dasharrow A\quad\mbox{given by}\quad\Phi(x_{1},x_{2}):=\phi(x_{1})-\phi(x_{2}).

Note that
\Phi
is a morphism on
(X\setminus Z)\times(X\setminus Z)

and maps
\Delta_{X\setminus Z}
to
0\in A
.

Let
0\notin D\subset A
be a Cartier divisor such that

A\setminus D
is affine. Let
\Phi^{*}(D)\subset X\times X
denote the closure of its preimage. Set
W:=(X\times X)\setminus\Phi^{*}(D)
.
Then
\Phi|_{W}
is a rational map to the affine variety
A\setminus D
,
and it is a morphism outside the codimension
\geq 2
set

(X\times Z)\cup(Z\times X)
. Thus
\Phi|_{W}
is a morphism.

Since
X\times X
is smooth,
\Phi^{*}(D)
is a Cartier divisor.
Thus
\Phi^{*}(D)\cap\Delta_{X}
has pure codimension 1 in
\Delta_{X}
.
On the other hand,
\Phi^{*}(D)\cap\Delta_{X}

is contained in
\Delta_{Z}
which has codimension
\geq 2
in
\Delta_{X}
. Thus
\Phi^{*}(D)
is disjoint from
\Delta_{X}
.
So
\Phi
is a morphism in a neighborhood of
\Delta_{X}
.

Finally note that

\phi(x)=\Phi(x,y)+\phi(y)
, and for every
x
there is a dense, open subset

U_{x}\subset X
such that
\Phi(x,y)
and
\phi(y)
are both defined for
y\in U_{x}
.
So
\phi
is a morphism. ∎

Remark
10
.1.
The proof gives the following stronger version.

Let
X
be a normal variety such that
X\times X
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-factorial, and
A
an algebraic group. Let
\phi:X\dasharrow A
be a rational map
that is a morphism outside a codimension
\geq 2
subset. Then
\phi
is a morphism everywhere.

Lemma 11

(Rational homomorphisms)
.

Any rational homomorphism of algebraic groups is a regular homomorphism.

Proof.
Let
\phi:H\dasharrow G
a rational homomorphism as in Notation
5
.
Consider the rational map

\Psi:(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\mapsto\phi(u_{1}u_{3})\phi(u_{3}^{-1}u_{2}).

It is independent of
u_{3}
on a dense, open subset, hence everywhere.
Thus
\Psi
depends only on the product
u_{1}u_{2}
, and gives the extension of
\phi
to
H
. ∎

Corollary 12

(Linear representations from rational actions)
.

Let
\mu:G\times X\dasharrow X
be a connected algebraic group acting rationally on a normal, projective variety.
Let
|D|
be a linear system on
X
such that

(\mu_{g})_{*}|D|=|D|
for general
g\in G(\bar{k})
. Then

g\mapsto(\mu_{g})_{*}
defines a linear representation

\tau_{|D|}:G\to\operatorname{Aut}(|D|)\cong\mathrm{PGL}_{N},\quad\mbox{where}\quad N:=\dim|D|+1.

Proof. Fix two point sets
\{p_{i}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{N}:i=1,\dots,N+2\}
and

\{q_{i}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{N}:i=1,\dots,N+2\}
in
linearly general position. Then there is a unique
\phi\in\operatorname{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{N})
such that
\phi(p_{i})=q_{i}
for every
i
.

Thus giving a rational map
\Phi:G\dasharrow\operatorname{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{N})
is the same as giving
N+2
rational maps
\phi_{i}:G\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
such that
the
\{\phi_{i}(g):i=1,\dots,N+2\}
are in
linearly general position over a dense, open subset
G^{*}\subset G
.

By assumption we have a rational map

\bar{\mu}:G\times|D|\dasharrow|D|.

We may assume that
\bar{\mu}
is defined on a dense, open subset of

\{e\}\times|D|^{\circ}\subset\{e\}\times|D|
. Choose
N+2
divisors
D_{i}\in|D|^{\circ}
in linearly general position. Then the

S_{i}:=\bar{\mu}\bigl(G\times[D_{i}]\bigr)
are
N+2
rational sections
of
\pi_{1}:G\times|D|\to G
.

There is thus a dense, open subset
G^{*}\subset G
such that
the
S_{i}
are sections in linearly general position over
G^{*}
.
Thus the
S_{i}
define a rational representation

\tau_{|D|}:G\dasharrow\operatorname{Aut}(|D|)
, which is regular
by Lemma
11
. ∎

4.
Linear systems of Weil divisors

Definition 13

(Weil divisors)
.

Let
X
be a normal variety. A
Weil divisor
on
X
is a finite
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear combination of irreducible, codimension 1 subvarieties
D:=\sum m_{i}D_{i}
.

Let
X^{\rm sm}\subset X
be the smooth locus. Then
D|_{X^{\rm sm}}
is a Cartier divisor, whose closure is
D
. One can thus identify Weil divisors on
X
with Cartier divisors on
X^{\rm sm}
; though this can be misleading for families of divisors.

A
family of Weil divisors
parametrized by a smooth variety
S
is a Weil divisor
{\mathbf{D}}
on
X\times S
whose support does not contain any of the fibers
X_{s}:=X\times\{s\}
. (This definition also works if
S
is normal, but needs more foundational work, see
[
Kol23
, Sec.4.3]
.)
The fiber
{\mathbf{D}}_{s}
over
s\in S
is defined as the the closure of the restriction of
{\mathbf{D}}
to

X_{s}^{\rm sm}
.

Let
U\subset X\times S
be any dense, open subset.
Then
{\mathbf{D}}_{s}\setminus U
has codimension
\geq 1
for general
s
. Thus

Claim
13
.1.

{\mathbf{D}}_{s}
is the closure of
{\mathbf{D}}_{s}\cap U
for all
s
in some dense, open
S^{\circ}\subset S
.

Definition 14

(Pull-back and birational transform)
.

Let
h:Y\to X
be a morphism of normal varieties.
Assume that there is a closed
Z\subset X
such that

D
is Cartier on
X\setminus Z
and

h^{-1}(Z)
has codimension
\geq 2
in
Y
. Then one can define the
pull-back

h^{*}(D)
as the closure of the Cartier divisor

h^{*}(D|_{X\setminus Z})
. If these condition do not hold, then one usually cannot define
the pull-back of
D
in a sensible way.

The pull-back preserves linear equivalence.

Let
X,Y
be normal varieties. Let
g:X\dasharrow Y
be a birational map that is defined outside a closed subset
Z\subset X
of codimension
\geq 2
. (For example,
Y
is proper.)
If
D
is a Weil divisor on
X
, we define its

birational
or
proper
transform on
Y
as
the closure of
g_{*}(D|_{X\setminus Z})
. We denote it by

g_{*}(D)
.

Warning.
Birational transform need not preserve linear equivalence. So we need to define the birational transform of a linear system separately in (
16
).

Definition 15

(Linear systems)
.

Let
X
be a normal, proper and geometrically integral
k
-variety.
We let
|D|
denote a not-necessarily complete linear system.
Its members are usually denoted by
D_{\lambda}
as divisors, and

[D_{\lambda}]
as points in the projective space
|D|
.

Warning.
In the literature
|D|
is used to denote either the
complete
linear system of a divisor
D
, or some not-necessarily complete linear system; in many cases without explicit mention.
I intend to say complete linear system when that variant is used.

If
\{D_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
is a set of linearly equivalent Weil divisors, we let
|D_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda|
denote the
(not-necessarily complete) linear system spanned by them.

The
incidence correspondence
of
|D|
is

{\mathbf{D}}:=\{(x,D):x\in D\}\subset X\times|D|.

If
|D|
has no fixed components
then
{\mathbf{D}}
is irreducible and its
first projection
\pi_{2}:{\mathbf{D}}\to X

is generically a hyperplane bundle.
Conversely, any such
{\mathbf{D}}
is the incidence correspondence of a unique linear system
|D|
without fixed components.

A 1-dimensional linear system is traditionally called a
pencil.

16
Birational transforms of linear systems.

Let
g:X\dasharrow Y
be a birational map between
normal, proper and geometrically integral
k
-varieties,
and
|D|
a linear system on
X
without fixed components.

Using the identity of
|D|
, we get a birational map

\bar{g}:X\times|D|\dasharrow Y\times|D|
. Then
\bar{g}_{*}({\mathbf{D}})

is a divisor on
Y\times|D|
, which corresponds to a linear system
g_{*}|D|
on
Y
. It is called the
birational
or
proper
transform of
|D|
.

The next example illustrates some of the subtleties that one needs to keep in mind.

Example 17
.

Let

\phi:(x{:}y{:}z)\mapsto(x^{-1}{:}y^{-1}{:}z^{-1})
be the standard quadratic transformation of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. The linear system of lines

|L|:=|ax+by+cz|
is mapped to the linear system of conics

|Q|:=|ayz+bxz+cxy|
. Let
W
be the set of lines that pass through a coordinate vertex. It is a union of 3 linear subsystems

|ax+by|\cup|by+cz|\cup|ax+cz|
.
Each of these is mapped to itself, for example

|ax+by|
is mapped to
|ay+bx|
.

Thus
W\subset|L|
is a connected subset that spans
|L|
, yet the
\phi
-images of its members do not span
|Q|=\phi_{*}|L|
.

If we change
\phi
to
\phi^{\prime}
so that its 3 base points are conjugate over
k
, then the corresponding
W^{\prime}
is irreducible over
k
.

In working with linear systems, we need to understand
when the birational transform of a member of a linear system
|D|
fails to be a
member of the birational transform of
|D|
.
The next lemma says that this happens only along linear subspaces.
As an immediate corollary we get that
general members of
irreducible spanning sets transform as expected.

Lemma 18
.

Let
g:X\dasharrow Y
be a birational map between normal
k
-varieties as in (
15
), and

|D|
a linear system on
X
without fixed components.
Then there is a finite set of linear subspaces
\{L_{i}\subsetneq|D|_{\bar{k}}:i\in I\}
defined over
\bar{k}
, such that
g_{*}(D_{\lambda})\in g_{*}|D|

for
[D_{\lambda}]\notin\cup_{i}L_{i}
.

Proof. The map
g
restricts to an isomorphism on suitable,
open, dense subsets
X\supset U_{X}\cong U_{Y}\subset Y
.
Let
E_{i}
be the codimension 1, irreducible components of

Y\setminus U_{Y}
, defined over
\bar{k}
.

Then

|D|
and
g_{*}|D|
restrict to the same set of divisors on

U_{X}
and
U_{Y}
. Thus if
[D^{X}_{\lambda}]\in|D|
, then
the corresponding member of
[D^{Y}_{\lambda}]\in g_{*}|D|
is of the form

D^{Y}_{\lambda}=g_{*}\bigl(D^{X}_{\lambda}\bigr)+\textstyle{\sum}m_{i,\lambda}E_{i}.

Let

L^{Y}_{i}\subset g_{*}|D|_{\bar{k}}
be the linear subspace of those divisors that contain
E_{i}
. Since
g_{*}|D|_{\bar{k}}
has no fixed components,
L^{Y}_{i}\neq g_{*}|D|_{\bar{k}}
.
We can then take
L_{i}
to be the corresponding linear subspace of
|D|_{\bar{k}}
. ∎

Corollary 19
.

Let
|D|
be a linear system on
X
without fixed components, and
g:X\dasharrow Y
a birational map. Let
W\subset|D|
be a geometrically irreducible subvariety that spans
|D|
.
Then there is a dense, open
W^{\circ}\subset W
such that

g_{*}|D|=\bigl|g_{*}(D_{\lambda}):[D_{\lambda}]\in W^{\circ}\bigr|.\qed

5.
Bertini theorems

Let
X
be a variety and
|H|
a linear system on
X
.
Bertini-type theorems say that if
X
satisfies certain property
{\mathcal{P}}
, and
|H|
is ‘large enough,’ then ‘general members’
[H_{\lambda}]\in|H|
also satisfy
{\mathcal{P}}
. Working over a field
k
, we usually want the
H_{\lambda}
to be defined over
k
.
This works whenever
k
is infinite.
For Bertini-type theorems over finite fields, see
[
CP16
]
.

We will use the case
{\mathcal{P}}=(\mbox{smooth})
, which is proved in many places, for example
[
Sha74
, Sec.II.6.2]
.

[
Har77
, II.8.18]
states it for
X
projective, but the proof is the same in general; we essentially give it in (
24
). (Note that, in the quasi-projective case, the set
\{H_{\lambda}:X\cap H_{\lambda}\mbox{ is smooth}\}
need not be open in
|H|
.)

Theorem 20

(Bertini’s smoothness theorem)
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
be a smooth, quasi-projective variety and

H\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
a general hyperplane. Then

X\cap H
is smooth. ∎

Similar results for other local properties
{\mathcal{P}}
are treated in
[
Gro60
, Sec.IV.12.1]
; see also
[
Kol23
, Sec.10.2]
.

We start the proof of Bertini’s irreducibility theorem with some lemmas.

Lemma 21
.

Let
Y
be an irreducible
k
-variety that has a smooth
k
-point
p
. Then
Y
is geometrically irreducible.

Proof. Let
k^{\rm s}
be the separable closure and
Y^{\rm s}_{i}
the irreducible components of
Y_{k^{\rm s}}
. One of them, say
Y^{\rm s}_{1}
contains
p
. Then all of its conjugates also contain
p
. Since
p
is smooth,
Y^{\rm s}_{1}
is

\operatorname{Gal}(k^{\rm s}/k)
-invariant, hence defined over
k
. So

Y^{\rm s}=Y^{\rm s}_{1}
. ∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
X
be a geometrically irreducible variety and

|M|
a pencil of divisors without fixed components. Assume that members of
|M|
are smooth at a
k
-point
x\in X
, and meet transversally. (That is, the scheme-theoretic base locus
\operatorname{Bs}|M|
is smooth and has codimension 2 at
x
.)
Then general members of
|M|
are geometrically irreducible.

Proof. We may assume that the base field is algebraically closed.
By shrinking
X
we may assume that
\operatorname{Bs}|M|
has pure codimension 2 in
X
.

Let
\bar{X}\subset X\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be the closed graph of
X
.
The first projection
\pi_{1}:\bar{X}\to X
is an isomorphism over

X\setminus\operatorname{Bs}|M|
, and a
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
-bundle over
\operatorname{Bs}|M|
.
Thus
\bar{X}
is (geometrically) irreducible.
Therefore the generic fiber
\bar{X}_{\eta}
of the second projection
\pi_{2}:\bar{X}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is
irreducible. Finally
\pi_{1}^{-1}(x)
gives a smooth
k(\eta)
-point on
\bar{X}_{\eta}
. Thus

\bar{X}_{\eta}
is geometrically irreducible by Lemma
21
.

Then general members of
|M|
are geometrically irreducible, see for example
[
Sha74
, Sec.II.6.1]
.
∎

23
Proof of Theorem
1
.

We may assume that the base field is algebraically closed.
By shrinking
X
we may also assume that each fiber of
\pi
has pure codimension
n
in
X
.

Assume first that
\pi
is generically smooth, and choose
p\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
such that
X_{p}
is generically smooth.
Let
|M|\subset|{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(1)|(-p)
be a general pencil and

|M|_{X}
its pull-back to
X
. Then members of
|M|_{X}
are smooth at
x
and meet transversally. Thus general members of
|M|_{X}
are
irreducible by (
22
).

If
\pi
is not generically smooth, then consider the
p^{r}
-th power Frobenius

F^{r}:{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{(r)}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. (In fact
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{(r)}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.) Let
X_{(r)}
denote the reduced underlying subscheme of

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{(r)}\times_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}X
. Then
X_{(r)}\to X
is a homeomorphism,
so
\pi^{-1}(H)
is geometrically irreducible iff its pull-back to
X_{(r)}
is. Finally

X_{(r)}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{(r)}
is generically smooth for
r\gg 1
, so the previous argument applies. ∎

Putting these together and using induction we get that
if
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
is a normal, projective variety of dimension
n
, and

H_{1},\dots,H_{n-1}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
are general hyperplanes,
then
X\cap H_{1}\cap\dots\cap H_{n-1}
is a smooth, irreducible curve.

We need a stronger version of this, where the
H_{i}
are not ‘fully general.’ To state it,
let
X
be a projective variety of dimension
n
, and

|H|
a linear system.
Let
\operatorname{Grass}(n{-}1,|H|)
denote the Grassmannian of
n{-}1
dimensional subspaces of
|H|
. Let
{\mathbf{C}}\subset X\times\operatorname{Grass}(n{-}1,|H|)
denote the universal subvariety of curve intersections. That is, the fiber
C_{L}
of
{\mathbf{C}}
over
L\in\operatorname{Grass}(n{-}1,|H|)
is the intersection of all members of
L
.

Corollary 24

(Bertini’s irreducibility theorem II)
.

Let
Y
be a projective variety such that
\operatorname{Sing}X\subset X
has codimension
\geq 2
. Let
A
be a very ample divisor and
|H|:=|mA|
, the complete linear system.
Then there are closed subsets
Z_{i}\subset\operatorname{Grass}(n{-}1,|H|)
of codimension
\geq i
such that

(24.1)

C_{L}
is smooth and irreducible if
m\geq 1
and
L\notin Z_{1}
,

(24.2)

C_{L}
is irreducible if
m\geq 2
and
L\notin Z_{2}
, except for
Y={\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and
|H|=|{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(2)|
.

Proof. It is enough to show that if
S\subset Y
is the intersection of

n{-}2
general members of
|H|
then the claims hold for
S
.
Note that
S
is irreducible by Theorem
1
, and has only
isolated singularities by (
20
).

Let
|H_{S}|
be the restriction of
|H|
to
S
.
Using Theorem
1
on
S
, we see that if

C\in|H_{S}|
is reducible, then
C
either passes through a singular point of
S
, or it is singular at a smooth point of
S
.

If
s\in\operatorname{Sing}S
then
|H_{S}|(-s)
is a hyperplane in
|H_{S}|
, and a general member of
|H_{S}|(-s)
is irreducible, unless

|H_{S}|(-s)
maps
S
to a curve. So
S
is a cone with vertex
s
, and

H
has degree 1 on the rulings. This cannot happen for
m\geq 2
.

If
s\in S\setminus\operatorname{Sing}S
then
|H_{S}|(-2s)
has codimension 3 in
|H_{S}|
.
A general member of
|H_{S}|(-2s)
is irreducible, unless

S
is a cone with vertex
s
, and

H
has degree
\leq 2
on the rulings.
This cannot happen for
m\geq 3
.

For
m=2
the only problem is when

S
is a cone with vertex
s

for every
s
. This happens only for
S\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. ∎

6.
Severi-Weil lemmas

Severi noted that linear equivalence of divisors on surfaces can be checked on finitely many curves
[
Sev1905
,
Sev1906
,
Sev1921
]
. This was extended by Weil to
normal varieties
[
Wei52
]
.

Note that while the restriction of a Weil divisor
D
to a curve
C
is usually not defined,
D
is Cartier on the smooth locus
X^{\rm sm}
,
thus
D|_{C}
is a well-defined linear equivalence class whenever

C\subset X^{\rm sm}
.

Given a morphism
\pi:Y\to S
, we would like to know when a divisor is
linearly equivalent to the pull-back of a divisor on
S
.
As first step, we look for divisors that do not dominate
S
.
Equivalently, they are disjoint from the generic fiber.
I call these
vertical.

Lemma 25
.

Let
Y,S
be normal varieties, and
\pi:Y\to S
a proper morphism whose general fibers are geometrically integral curves. Let
D
be a Weil divisor on
Y
. Then
D
is linearly equivalent to a vertical divisor iff

D|_{Y_{s}}\sim 0
for general
s\in S
.

Proof. If
D
is vertical, then it is disjoint from a general fiber
Y_{s}
, so
D|_{Y_{s}}=0
. Conversely, if
D|_{Y_{s}}\sim 0
for general
s\in S
,
then
D|_{Y_{g}}\sim 0
for the generic fiber.
This gives a rational section of
{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}(D)
whose divisor consists of
D
plus a vertical divisor. ∎

Lemma 26
.

Let
Y,S
be normal varieties, and
\pi:Y\to S
proper morphism of relative dimension 1.
Assume that there is a closed subset
Z_{2}\subset S
of codimension
\geq 2
, such that the fibers of
\pi
are
geometrically integral over
S\setminus Z_{2}
.

Let
D
be a Weil divisor on
Y
. Then
D
is linearly equivalent to the
pull-back of a divisor from
S
iff

D|_{Y_{s}}\sim 0
for general
s\in S
.

Proof. The assumption implies that every vertical divisor is the
pull-back of a divisor from
S
. ∎

Lemma 27
.

Let
X
be a geometrically normal, projective variety over an infinite field
k
, and
|H|
a very ample linear system on
X
.
Let
D
be a Weil divisor on
X
. The following are equivalent.

(27.1)

D\sim 0
.

(27.2)

D|_{C}\sim 0
for every
smooth, complete intersection curve

C:=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-1}
over
k
, where
H_{i}\in|2H|
.

Proof. It is clear that (
27
.1) implies
(
27
.2), and the equivalence is clear
for
X={\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.

For the converse
|2H|
gives an embedding
X\lhook\joinrel\to{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Choose a
general projection
\pi:X\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
whose base point set
P=\{x_{i}\}
is smooth.
We may assume that
P
is disjoint from
\operatorname{Supp}D
.
Blowing up
P
we get
X^{\prime}:=B_{P}X
and
D^{\prime}\cong D
.

The projection morphism

\pi^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
has relative dimension 1,
and satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
26
by Corollary
24
.
Thus, if (
27
.2) holds, then
D^{\prime}
is
linearly equivalent to the
pull-back of a divisor
D_{P}
from
{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
.

Over each
x_{i}\in P
we have an exceptional divisor
E_{i}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
, and

D^{\prime}
is disjoint from
E_{i}
. Thus the
pull-back of
D_{P}
is linearly trivial on
E_{i}
, so
D_{P}\sim 0
.
∎

Theorem 28

(Severi-Weil lemma)
.

Let
X
be a geometrically normal, projective variety over an infinite field,
S
a smooth variety and

D
a Weil divisor on
S\times X
.
Then there are finitely many smooth, projective curves

\{C_{i}\subset X^{\rm sm}:i\in I\}
such that, for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
,

D_{s_{1}}\sim D_{s_{2}}\Leftrightarrow D_{s_{1}}|_{C_{i}}\sim D_{s_{2}}|_{C_{i}}\quad\forall i\in I.

Proof.
Let
|H|
be very ample on
X
.
For any
C:=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-1}
as in (
27
.2)
restriction to
C
gives a morphism

\alpha_{C}:S\to\operatorname{\mathbf{Jac}}(C)
.
Set
\Gamma_{C}:=S\times_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Jac}}(C)}S\subset S\times S
.

By the Noetherian property, there is a finite set of curves

\{C_{i}:i\in I\}
such that
\cap_{i\in I}\Gamma_{C_{i}}
equals the intersection of all the
\Gamma_{C}
.

Thus, if
D_{s_{1}}|_{C_{i}}\sim D_{s_{2}}|_{C_{i}}
for every
i\in I
, then

D_{s_{1}}|_{C}\sim D_{s_{2}}|_{C}
for every
C=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-1}
, hence
D_{s_{1}}\sim D_{s_{2}}
by Lemma
27
.∎

7.
Further comments

I tried to simplify the proofs of Theorems
1
–
2
, thus several intermediate results are stated in weak forms, when this makes the proofs shorter.
Here I point out some more complete versions and connections. These are not used in the paper, but should be useful in general.

29
Compactifications.

As a refinement of (
7
.1),
choose a finite, affine cover
G=\cup_{i\in I}U_{i}
and embeddings

\tau_{i}:U_{i}\lhook\joinrel\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{i}}
. The product gives a rational map

G\dasharrow\times_{i\in I}U_{i}\lhook\joinrel\to\times_{i\in I}{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{i}}.

Let
X
be the normalization of the closure of the image.
The advantage is that
G\dasharrow X
is an open embedding outside a
closed subset
Z\subset G
of codimension
\geq 2
. Thus every divisor on
G
is the restriction of a Weil divisor on
X
, and

H^{0}(G,L)=H^{0}(G\setminus Z,L)
for every line bundle
L
on
G
.

Note that the stronger compactification theorems do not quite work for our purposes.
Nagata’s theorem
[
Nag62
]
says that any normal variety has a normal, proper compactification; see
[
Sta25
,
Tag 0F41
]
for a modern proof.
Weil’s regularization theorem
[
Wei55
]
says that any rational
G
-action is birational to a regular
G
-action on a proper, normal variety; see also
[
Sum74
,
Bri10
]
.
However, these either do not guarantee projectivity, or appear to use
Chevalley’s theorem in order to prove projectivity.

We use the projectivity in Section
6
to find families of projective curves on
X
that are contained in its smooth locus. There are proper, normal algebraic surfaces
S
such that every curve on
X
passes through a singular point.

30
Class group and Picard variety.

The morphism
\alpha_{X,D}
constructed in Theorem
28

sends
S
to an Abelian variety that depends on various choices.

Over arbitrary fields
Matsusaka proved that there is a universal choice for the target, called the Picard variety of
X
; see
[
Mat51
,
Mat52
]
. I denote it by
\operatorname{\mathbf{Pic}}^{\rm wm}(X)
for Weil and Matsusaka.

The definition of a Picard variety was changed in
[
Gro62
, VI]
, and since then
Picard variety
almost always means the Grothendieck version.
The 2 versions agree for smooth varieties in characteristic 0, but can have different dimensions in general.
I plan to discuss this in detail later.

Irreducible sets of generators.

There are other examples where geometrically irreducible sets of generators behave better than arbitrary sets of generators, see for example

[
Kol99
, Sec.3]
and
[
BK12
, Ques.14]
.
