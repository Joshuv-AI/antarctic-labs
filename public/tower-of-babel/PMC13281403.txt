Knowledge, Attitude, and Use of E‐Cigarettes Among Syrian University Students: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Mohammad Basheer Alameer, Omar Al Emam, Samira Almajzoub, Ghena Blah Alherch, Rana Hadakie, Bilal Aljebbeh, Rana Habeeb, Kenda Jawich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsStudies have shown that electronic cigarettes have gained immense popularity, particularly among university students, as they've been marketed as a safer, cleaner, and more acceptable alternative to traditional tobacco. However, little is known about the knowledge, attitude, and use of e‐cigarettes in a developing country such as Syria. This study aimed to investigate the knowledge, practices, and attitudes of Syrian university students regarding e‐cigarettes and the demographic factors related to these aspects.MethodsA web‐based survey was distributed to university students on social media. A total of 26 questions were used to collect demographic data and evaluate the knowledge, attitude, and use of e‐cigarettes.ResultsThe total number of participants was 1572. The prevalence of e‐smoking alone was 24%, while 9.5% smoked e‐cigarettes and traditional tobacco together. The most prevalent source of information about e‐cigarettes was friends, followed by media, while desirable taste, followed by fun, was the most prevalent reason for using e‐cigarettes. The average knowledge score was 5.20 ± 3.01, while the average attitude score was 21.04 ± 4.56. Male gender, parental smoking, and decreased attitude score were independently associated with e‐smoking, while studying a medical specialty and decreased knowledge score were associated with e‐smoking but not independently.ConclusionsSyrian university students' knowledge about e‐smoking is limited. Male gender, parental smoking, and lower attitude scores were independent predictors of e‐cigarette use. Academic specialty and knowledge were associated in univariate analysis but were not independent predictors. Awareness campaigns are imperative to counter the increased popularity of e‐smoking among the Syrian youth.Keywords: awareness, e‐cigarettes, Syria, university students

Background

E‐cigarettes are devices that are designed to heat a solution composed of nicotine, humectants (glycerol or propylene glycol), and, in many cases, flavoring agents to deliver the aerosol to be inhaled by users. E‐cigarettes do not involve the burning of tobacco; instead, they use a process that heats a liquid containing nicotine and other components, creating a vapor for inhalation. While the absence of burning likely limits the intake of harmful chemicals for e‐cigarette users as compared to traditional tobacco [1], the use of electronic cigarettes has seen a marked growth, especially among adults and younger adults over the last decade [2]. Advertising greatly affects young adults; e‐cigarettes are retailed as an effective approach for reducing conventional cigarette use and smoking cessation. Marketing messages claim that e‐cigarettes are safer, cleaner, and can be used in places where conventional smoking is restricted [3]. As its popularity and use increase, so does the concern about public health. Therefore, public awareness about the potential harmful effects of e‐cigarettes has been examined; it has been shown that the use of e‐cigarettes is not risk‐free. Users of e‐cigarettes are at risk of developing acute health conditions such as myocardial infarction, stroke, and coronary artery diseases [4]. Harmful substances such as formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, and acrolein were revealed in the analysis of e‐cigarettes' vapor [5]. Formaldehyde and acetaldehyde are classified as possibly carcinogenic materials [3], while acrolein causes nasal irritation as well as lung injury. E‐cigarettes use cause airway inflammation, ciliary dysfunction, alveolar injury, and increases mucus secretion [6]. Furthermore, evidence of a higher addictive potential with e‐cigarettes than conventional cigarettes among young adults were presented [7]

As the use of e‐cigarettes continues to increase along with uncertainty about their safety, it is of paramount importance to explore the patterns, perceptions, and attitudes among young adults represented as university students. E‐cigarettes gained considerable popularity in many developing countries. A recent multinational study that included many neighboring countries to Syria reported that 4.5% of dentistry students use e‐cigarettes and their knowledge about them was not enough despite having acceptable belfies and attitudes towards them [8]. In contrast, published data on e‐cigarette use in Syria remain limited, with only a few recent reports that have begun to document patterns of tobacco and e‐cigarette use in selected populations [9, 10]. However, no prior study has specifically examined knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward e‐cigarettes among Syrian university students.

In the current study, we will investigate the knowledge, attitude, and practice of e‐cigarettes amongst university students to provide guidance for Syrian community‐based tailored education campaigns.

Methods

This is an observational cross‐sectional study. A web‐based survey consisting of a 26‐item self‐reported questionnaire was adapted from a study from Saudi Arabia [11], which in‐turn was an Arabic version of a questionnaire used in a study from the U.S [12]. The survey was launched in January 2023; we published brief information about the survey and a direct link to the survey on social media. The brief information included an invitation to contribute and the name of the principal investigator, title and aims of the study, and the sponsoring university. After accessing the link, a more detailed explanation of the study was displayed, the aim of the study, the construction of the survey questionnaire, and the expected time required to answer.

The survey targeted University students in Syria and was written in Arabic. The survey was anonymous and could be reached via Google Forms. The survey was open for 50 days; no payment was provided to participants. The questionnaire took approximately 5 to 10 min to complete, and was reviewed to ensure that its content is readable by two authors.

The participant information sheet and the consent were collected on the first page of the survey. Upon providing consent, participants were moved to the next screen containing the full study questionnaire. Five sections covering 26 questions were used; the first section investigated the demographic data (age, gender, place of residence, average annual income, level of education, enrolled college), and the second section was formulated to assess smoking status and habits. E‐cigarette knowledge was evaluated in the third section. The knowledge section of the questionnaire consisted of 12 items presented in a yes, no, I don't know format. Knowledge score was calculated by assigning one point for each correct response, with possible scores ranging from zero to 12 points. The fourth section investigated the practice of e‐cigarettes and consisted of six questions to collect data about reasons to use e‐cigarettes, the frequency of its use, the dual use of e‐cigarettes and traditional tobacco, and time between waking up and using e‐cigarettes.

Attitude was evaluated in the last, the attitude score was derived based on Likert‐scale points, six statements were used; for each statement, participants were presented with five Likert‐based response options (1 = strongly disagree; 2 = disagree; 3 = neutral/unsure; 4 = agree; 5 = strongly agree); scores for the attitude sections ranged between 6 and 30. An elevated attitude score reflects a stronger agreement among participants with statements characterizing e‐cigarettes as harmful, unsafe, or ineffective as a smoking cessation aid.

Average annual income can be explained as follows: 1‐ poor: cannot buy necessities, 2‐ moderate: can only buy necessities, 3‐ good: can buy all necessities and some luxuries, 4‐ excellent: can buy all necessities and luxuries.

The inclusion criteria included university students in one of the Syrian universities. The exclusion criteria were non‐university students' young adults. Students studying medicine, pharmacy or dentistry were considered students in a medical specialty, while others were considered students of a non‐medical specialty.

Validation and Reliability

The evaluation of the questionnaire's validity and reliability was conducted by a professional committee of clinical pharmacists and a statistician, confirming its applicability to the Syrian population. The questionnaire was translated from English to Arabic and back‐translated by two senior academic staff members who were fluent in both languages. The questions were free from medical jargon or hard terminology. Then, the questionnaire was evaluated and validated via a pilot sample of 25 academics and 25 non‐academic participants during a month‐long pilot study to ensure comprehension, clarity, readability, and acceptability of the survey. Modifications to the questionnaire were made as needed. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient was tested for the study instrument and was found to be 0.837 for the knowledge section and 0.631 for the attitude section, with an overall score of 0.729, which indicates acceptable internal consistency and reliability.

Ethical Considerations

This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. Ethical approval to conduct this study was obtained from the Ethical Committee at the International University for Science and Technology (IUST) (ID‐PH‐24‐3) (Year:2024). Participants gave their informed consent before filling the questionnaire. The participation was completely voluntary and no names were taken to assure anonymous data collecting. A clinical trial number is not applicable.

Data Analysis

All data from the Google form were downloaded. The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS‐26. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical and continuous variables, presenting results through frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Shapiro‐Wilk test, along with visual inspection, was used to assess the normality of data. The Chi‐Square Test assessed relationships between categorical variables, specifically examining the associations between demographic characteristics and e‐smoking behaviors. The Mann‐Whitney U Test compared mean differences in knowledge and attitude scores across binary categories such as gender and academic specialization, while the Kruskal‐Wallis Test was applied for comparisons involving multiple groups, such as academic years and smoking statuses. To identify predictors of e‐cigarette use, Univariate and Multivariate Logistic Regression analyzes were performed. The Odds Ratio (OR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) was calculated to estimate the likelihood of e‐cigarette use based on independent variables. Variables associated with e‐cigarette use at p < 0.20 in univariate analysis, along with conceptually important covariates, were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), confirming that no variable exceeded acceptable thresholds. Model diagnostics, including goodness‐of‐fit evaluation using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and inspection of residuals and influential observations, were performed to ensure the adequacy and stability of the final model. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 (two‐tailed) was applied to determine statistically significant associations.

Results

A total of 1572 students participated in the study. Females represented 53.8% of the sample, and the most common age group was 21–23 years. Most participants lived in urban areas and reported a good or moderate income. Medical and non‐medical specialties were almost equally represented, and just over half of the sample were postgraduate students. Nearly all participants (98.5%) had heard of e‐cigarettes, most commonly through friends and social media. Full demographic characteristics and information sources are presented in Tables 1 and 2.

Current Smoking Status

Non‐smokers made up 49.4% (n = 777) of the sample, followed by e‐cigarette users at 24% (n = 377), then traditional cigarette smokers at 17% (n = 268), and then dual smokers at 9.5% (n = 150). The total number of e‐cigarette users was 527 (33.5% of the sample), with 150 of them being dual users of e‐cigarettes and traditional tobacco, while 210 of them smoked hookah with e‐cigarettes.

Out of e‐cigarette users 52.2%, (n = 275) of them used e‐cigarettes daily, 47.4% (n = 250) used them for less than 15 min for each session, 26.6% (n = 140) used them between 15 and 30 min, and 26% (n = 137) used them more than 30 min. Moreover, 51.6% (n = 272) of e‐cigarette users used it for more than 3 times a day, while 30.6% (n = 161) used it once, 9.1% (n = 48) used it twice, and 8.7% (n = 46) used it three times. 61.3% (n = 323) of e‐cigarette users reported using it after more than an hour after they wake up, while 20.7% (n = 109) used it after less than 30 min, and 18% (n = 95) used it after between an hour and 30 min. When asked whether they find it difficult to give up their first use session after waking up, 68.5% (n = 361) of e‐cigarette users responded with no, while 17.3% (n = 91) responded with yes, and 14.2% (n = 75) responded with sometimes.

The distribution of parental smoking was 50.5% (n = 794) for positive parental smoking, with 31.3% (n = 492) having the father only as the smoker, 6.6% (n = 106) having the mother only as the smoker, and 12.7% (n = 199) having both parents as smokers. compared to 49.5% (n = 778) with negative parental smoking.

Knowledge About E‐Cigarettes and Associated Factors

The average knowledge score was 5.20 ± 3.01 and ranged between 0 and 12. The average knowledge score for females was 5.37 ± 3.1 compared to 4.99 ± 3.06 for males. The average knowledge score for students of a medical specialty was 5.77 ± 3.01 compared to 4.65 ± 3.06 for non‐medical students. The average knowledge score for first, second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth‐year students, and postgraduates was 5.22 ± 2.9, 5.17 ± 2.93, 4.8 ± 3.02, 5.23 ± 3.07, 5.9 ± 2.96, 5.17 ± 3.38, and 5.12 ± 3.16, respectively. The average knowledge score was 5.67 ± 3.19 for non‐smokers, 5.07 ± 3.21 for traditional cigarette smokers, 4.55 ± 2.75 for e‐cigarettes users, and 4.57 ± 2.69 for smokers of both. The average knowledge score for those with low, moderate, good, and excellent income was 4.72 ± 3.51, 4.88 ± 3.28, 5.26 ± 2.93, and 5.73 ± 3.05, respectively. The average knowledge score for those living in the city was 5.29 ± 3.03 compared to 4.84 ± 3.29 for those living in the countryside.

Knowledge scores of those female (p = 0.013 < 0.05), studying a medical specialty (p < 0.001), and living in the city (p = 0.013 < 0.05) were significantly different when compared to scores of males, studying a non‐medical specialty, and living in the countryside, respectively. Non‐smokers scores were also significantly different when compared to the scores of traditional cigarette smokers and e‐cigarette users (p < 0.001). There is a statistically significant difference in knowledge according to income level, p < 0.001, while there was no significant difference between academic years in terms of knowledge about e‐cigarettes, p = 0.068 > 0.05.

Attitude Towards E‐Cigarettes and Associated Factors

The average attitude score for the entire sample was 21.04 ± 4.56 and ranged between 8 and 29. The average attitude score for males was 20.29 ± 4.89 compared to 21.68 ± 4.16 for females. The average attitude score for those studying a medical specialization was 21.3 ± 4.29 compared to 20.79 ± 4.79 for those studying a non‐medical specialty. The average attitude score for users of e‐cigarettes was 18.82 ± 4.42 compared to 22.15 ± 4.22 for non‐users.

There was a statistically significant difference between males and females, p < 0.001, and those who don't use e‐cigarettes compared to users, p = < 0.001 in attitude scores, while the difference was not statistically significant for academic specialization, p = 0.053 > 0.05.

Factors Associated With E‐Cigarette Use

The percentage of e‐smoking practice among males (43.2%) was greater than that among females (25.2%), while the percentage of e‐smoking practice among those studying a non‐medical academic specialty (37.4%) was greater than among those studying a medical specialty (29.4%). The percentage of e‐smoking practice differed according to the academic year, with 36.3%, 28.3%, 36.5%, 25.8%, 26.8%, 31%, and 36.6% of first, second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth‐year students being e‐cigarette users, while 36.6% of postgraduates were e‐cigarette users. The percentage of e‐smoking practice among those with positive parental smoking was 38% compared to 28.9% for those with negative parental smoking. Moreover, the percentage of e‐cigarette use among traditional tobacco smokers was 35.9% compared to 32.7% among non‐smokers. The average knowledge score among non‐e‐cigarette users was 5.52 ± 3.21 compared to 4.56 ± 2.73 among e‐cigarette users. The average attitude score among non‐e‐cigarette users was 22.15 ± 4.22 compared to 18.82 ± 4.42 among e‐cigarette users.

There was a statistically significant difference in the practice of e‐smoking according to gender, p < 0.001; academic specialization, p = 0.001 < 0.05; academic year, p = 0.030 < 0.05; and parental smoking, p < 0.001, while there was no statistically significant difference in the use of electronic cigarettes according to the practice of traditional smoking. p = 0.233 > 0.05.

Using a univariate logistic regression, being male Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 2.26 (1.82–2.79) p < 0.001, and positive parental smoking Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 1.51 (1.22 – 1.86) p < 0.001 increased the risk of e‐cigarette use. While the increase in attitude score Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 0.84 (0.82–0.86) p < 0.001, the increase in knowledge score Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 0.90 (0.87–0.93) p < 0.001, and studying a medical specialty Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 0.70 (0.57–0.86) were negatively associated with e‐cigarette use. Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 1.05 (0.99–1.10), p = 0.075 > 0.05, and traditional smoking were positively associated with e‐cigarette use, but this association was not statistically significant.

A multivariate logistic regression test was performed to study the effect of factors that were statistically significant with a univariate logistic regression test. The factors that remained influential on the practice of electronic smoking are gender Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 2.00 (1.59–2.52) p = p < 0.001, attitude score Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 0.85 (0.83–0.88) p < 0.001, and positive parental smoking Odds Ratio (with 95% CI) = 1.65 (1.31–2.08) p < 0.001. Discussion

Although e‐cigarettes are often marketed as a safer alternative to conventional cigarettes, e‐cigarettes are now recognized to cause several adverse health effects. Aerosol exposure has been linked to airway inflammation, impaired lung function, and increased oxidative stress. Cardiovascular studies show acute endothelial dysfunction, elevated sympathetic activity, and potential long‐term cardiometabolic risk. Severe acute toxicity has also been documented through cases of e‐cigarette or vaping‐associated lung injury (EVALI). Additionally, dual use with combustible cigarettes may increase overall toxicant exposure rather than reduce harm [13]. In Syria, where tobacco and e‐cigarette use are largely unregulated due to prolonged instability during the period when e‐cigarettes gained popularity. However, the transitional government has been implementing new regulations on the use of e‐cigarettes, at least in Damascus. Understanding patterns of use among young adults is essential to tailor these regulations to the Syrian population.

Participants in this study were aged 18–35, the demographic most likely to use e‐cigarettes [14, 15]. Nearly all participants had heard of e‐cigarettes, consistent with findings from Saudi Arabia [16] and Jordan [17], though higher than the 57.5% reported in Egypt, where knowledge varied by age group, and the age group similar to the one studied in the paper being the most knowledgeable about e‐cigarettes [18]. Friends and social media were the most common information sources, aligning with studies from Saudi Arabia [16, 19], Egypt [20], and Jordan [17]. This reflects the strong influence of peer networks and digital platforms on youth behavior [21, 22, 23].

The prevalence of e‐cigarette use in our sample (33.5%) was higher than that reported in Saudi Arabia (24%) [24] and Palestine (18.1%) [25], comparable to rates in the United States (27%–45%) and Europe (23%–31%) [26], yet lower than those in Australia, India, and the UK [15]. Taste and pleasure were the most common reasons for use, consistent with other regional and international studies [19, 27, 28]. Differences in prevalence between Syria and neighboring countries may reflect variations in product availability, enforcement of tobacco regulations, cultural norms around nicotine use, and the influence of social media marketing. The prolonged conflict in Syria may also contribute to higher stress‐related nicotine consumption among young adults, which could partially explain the elevated rates observed in our sample [29, 30]. Differences in methodology across studies may also contribute to the variation in reported prevalence. Several regional surveys used classroom‐based or campus recruitment, whereas our study relied on social‐media distribution, which may oversample digitally active students who are more exposed to e‐cigarette marketing. Definitions of “current use” also vary—some classify any use in the past 30 days as current use, while others require daily or weekly use—making direct comparisons difficult. In addition, many studies targeted specific faculties such as medicine or dentistry, while our sample included a broader university population, which may influence knowledge and attitude scores. These methodological and contextual differences may partly explain why our prevalence estimates are higher than those reported in some neighboring countries.

About one‐third of users believed e‐cigarettes are less harmful than traditional cigarettes, a lower proportion than reported in Saudi Arabia [19] and Poland [31]. Given the conflicting evidence regarding e‐cigarette safety [32, 33] and the presence of toxic components in many unregulated products [34], correcting misconceptions is crucial, especially as e‐cigarette use rises among individuals who have never smoked traditional tobacco [35].

Dual use was common: nearly 30% of e‐cigarette users also reported smoking traditional cigarettes, and approximately 40% of e‐cigarette users reported concurrent hookah use. Although some users adopt e‐cigarettes for cessation [19], only 36.2% in our sample reported this intention, similar to findings from Palestine [25] and other studies [36]. High daily use, difficulty refraining after waking, and the popularity of flavored products suggest a strong potential for nicotine dependence [37]. Traditional smokers' support for e‐cigarette use, as reported in US research [12], may further reinforce dual‐use patterns. Interestingly, traditional cigarette smoking was not significantly associated with e‐cigarette use in our sample. E‐cigarette use also varied significantly across academic years, although this pattern did not correspond to differences in knowledge levels, which were similar across years

Knowledge levels were low, consistent with studies from Saudi Arabia [19], Jordan [17], and Palestine [25]. Higher knowledge was associated with lower e‐cigarette use in the univariate analysis; however, this association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for other factors, similar to findings among Egyptian medical students [20]. Non‐smokers scored higher than smokers, reinforcing the role of accurate information in preventing initiation. Medical students demonstrated higher knowledge, likely because they are more exposed to health‐related information, while knowledge did not differ significantly across academic years. However, academic specialization was not independently associated with e‐cigarette use after adjustment for other variables. These findings underscore the need for educational interventions tailored to the Arab context, where family influence, peer networks, and community norms strongly shape smoking behaviors.

Knowledge was higher among city residents and those with higher income, which may reflect differences in access to information and educational resources [38]. Females scored higher than males, contrasting with findings from Saudi Arabia [19] and Egypt [18]. This may relate to evidence suggesting that women are more likely to seek health information and engage in preventive behaviors [39].

Attitude scores were relatively high, indicating that many students hold cautious or negative perceptions of e‐cigarettes despite limited factual knowledge. This pattern mirrors traditional smoking, where individuals may recognize general harm without understanding specific risks [40], possibly influenced by addiction, flavor appeal, or peer dynamics [41].

Males were over two times more likely to use e‐cigarettes compared to females. Studies show contrasting results regarding the effect of gender on e‐cigarette use [12, 19, 20]. Males who smoke are more likely to use other ways to get nicotine to maintain its levels in their bodies [42], and e‐cigarettes represent a quick and easy source of nicotine, which would work optimally for maintaining nicotine levels. Moreover, the social context is important when studying the effect of gender on smoking or e‐cigarette use in a conservative community like the Syrian community, where smoking for females is socially unacceptable compared to males [43]. Studies from similar conservative communities report similar results like the ones mentioned above, while studies from other communities, like the United States, might report a higher prevalence among females [44]. Additionally, evidence suggests that females tend to be more concerned with the health risks of smoking, which may also play a role in decreasing the prevalence of e‐smoking among them [45].

Public health measures should be gender‐tailored to maximize effectiveness. Interventions targeting male students may require a stronger focus on correcting misconceptions, addressing peer‐driven behaviors, and highlighting addiction risks. In contrast, female students, who showed higher awareness and more negative attitudes toward e‐smoking, may benefit from reinforcement‐based strategies that sustain their protective perceptions. Gender‐responsive messaging, peer‐led campaigns, and university‐based programs that acknowledge these behavioral and perceptual differences could enhance the impact of future prevention efforts.

Positive parental smoking was associated with increased risk of using e‐cigarettes in our study. People with parents who smoke are more likely to accept the use of e‐cigarettes as normal [46], hence why they were more likely to use e‐cigarettes.

Female gender and not using e‐cigarettes were associated with higher attitude scores. Additionally, a higher attitude score was associated with decreased e‐cigarette use. Similar results were found by other studies [19, 46]. As established before, these two factors were also associated with increased knowledge, which was associated with decreased e‐cigarette use. Although knowledge levels were relatively modest, improving awareness remains important for correcting misconceptions and supporting broader tobacco control efforts.

Conclusions

The findings of this study highlight both non‐modifiable and modifiable determinants of e‐cigarette use among Syrian university students, which should guide targeted public health strategies. Non‐modifiable factors such as gender and parental smoking—both independently associated with higher e‐cigarette use—indicate the need for gender‐responsive interventions and early preventive messaging for students from smoking households. Modifiable factors, including low knowledge levels, permissive attitudes, and misconceptions about the harms of e‐cigarettes, represent actionable targets for intervention. Universities and health authorities should implement structured awareness campaigns that correct false beliefs, emphasize addiction risks, and address the appeal of flavored products. Because males showed lower knowledge, less negative attitudes, and significantly higher use, prevention programs should prioritize male‐focused messaging and peer‐led initiatives. Social media, identified as a major information source, should be leveraged to disseminate accurate, youth‐friendly content. Given the high prevalence of dual use with hookah and traditional cigarettes, cessation support programs should address nicotine dependence comprehensively rather than focusing solely on e‐cigarettes. These tailored, evidence‐based measures can more effectively counter the rising popularity of e‐smoking among Syrian university students.

Limitations

The most prominent limitation is the limited access to participants outside of Damascus and its suburbs, and the overrepresentation of students in the urban setting compared to the rural setting, making results less generalizable to communities in other governorates. Moreover, these data cannot be generalized to other age groups. The Cronbach's alpha is borderline and should be interpreted cautiously. Electronic smoking devices are diverse in nicotine concentration and types. These factors could not be measured in this study.
