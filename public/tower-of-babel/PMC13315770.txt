From sustainable development to enduring development: Renewing ecological, social, and human foundations☆
Authors: Zuyi Xia
Published: 2026

Development has long stood at the core of human society, carrying the aspirations of billions of people for a better life. Yet industrialization has also exposed the limits of prevailing development models: environmental pollution, ecosystem degradation, resource overuse, and persistent inequalities within and across generations. Although sustainable development has become a broad global consensus, the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development has given the international community a shared framework for action. Yet delivery remains far from sufficient. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) Report 2025 shows that only 35% of assessed SDG targets are on track or making moderate progress, nearly half are moving too slowly, and 18% have regressed from the 2015 baseline [1].

These figures suggest that the challenge is not only implementation, but also how long-term development capacity is built. Climate change, biodiversity loss, public-health pressures, conflict, trade barriers, and fragmented governance are converging to make progress less secure. Enduring development should therefore be understood not as a replacement for SDGs, but as a further development of their underlying logic.

What we mean by “Enduring Development”

Enduring development refers to the capacity of a development system to remain viable by renewing the foundations on which it depends. While sustainable development asks whether present development can proceed without compromising the future, enduring development asks whether the ecological, social, and human conditions for development can be continually renewed across generations. It is not an alternative to sustainable development, but a deepening of its central concern: the long-term viability of human society within a living planet. Its emphasis is therefore less on maintaining a static balance than on enabling systems to adjust, renew themselves, and improve over time. This also helps explain why enduring development cannot be reduced to a single environmental indicator or economic target. It concerns the continuing capacity of a society to keep its life-support systems, institutions, and human capabilities functioning together.

A practical reading of harmony and coexistence

The principle of harmony and coexistence provides a compact framework for enduring development: relations between humanity and nature, among human beings, and within the inner life of individuals. In contemporary language, this framework asks whether development can preserve ecological balance, sustain cooperation and mutual trust, and cultivate the well-being and responsibility needed for long-term collective flourishing. The value of this framework lies in treating these relations not as separate moral claims, but as connected conditions for the durability of development itself. When one relation is persistently weakened, the others are also affected: ecological stress can undermine social stability, social fragmentation can delay environmental action, and poor well-being can reduce the motivation for long-term choices.

Harmony with Nature—Ecological Renewal. The first task is to move from damage control to measurable ecological renewal. Pollution control, carbon reduction, and resource efficiency remain essential, but they should be integrated into a broader transition that improves ecosystem quality, optimizes resource use, and accelerates greener patterns of production and consumption. It also implies that green transition should be embedded across development planning, from pollution prevention and ecosystem improvement to cleaner energy systems, long-term carbon-reduction goals, and lower-impact ways of living. Respecting ecological limits means placing ecological protection at the center of development decisions, rather than treating nature as an external resource to be consumed.

Harmony with Each Other—Cooperation and Trust. Many sustainability challenges cannot be addressed by individual actors alone. Tackling climate change, biodiversity conservation, pollution control, and addressing other sustainability challenges all depend on coordination across sectors, regions, and countries. Such coordination must also extend beyond the environmental sector, because ecological outcomes are shaped by economic, industrial, technological, and social decisions. Enduring development requires institutions that support dialogue and cooperation, uphold fair rules, provide more equal opportunities for participation, and enable shared responsibility in addressing global challenges. This is especially important when environmental risks cross borders and when the costs and benefits of transition are unevenly distributed. Long-term environmental progress, therefore, depends not only on scientific knowledge or technological capacity but also on governance arrangements that allow countries and communities to work through differences, share responsibilities, and maintain cooperation over time. Such cooperation depends on mutual trust, respect, and dialogue rather than zero-sum competition.

Harmony with the Self—Well-being and Long-term Capacity. The third dimension concerns the values, motivations, and time horizons that shape individual behavior. More than one billion people are living with mental health conditions worldwide [2]. Beyond this public-health burden, enduring development also depends on whether individuals and communities can sustain the sense of purpose, responsibility, and long-term orientation required for collective action. A narrow pursuit of material progress can erode this orientation by weakening responsibility, cooperation, and concern for the future. Conversely, mental well-being and a stable sense of purpose can strengthen responsibility, cooperation, and long-term thinking. Human well-being is therefore not separate from enduring development; it forms part of the human foundation that supports it. This perspective does not move environmental questions away from science; rather, it recognizes that the choices required for sustainability are made by people whose capacities, values, and expectations shape collective outcomes.

A self-reinforcing system

These three dimensions should be understood not as separate domains, but as mutually reinforcing conditions for enduring development. Inner well-being shapes how people relate to nature and to one another; a fair and cooperative society, together with a healthy environment, provides the external conditions for individual stability and growth. The logic also works at the collective level: ecological pressures often require social cooperation, while cooperation is more likely to endure when people have confidence, security, and a sense of shared responsibility. Enduring development, therefore, depends on a virtuous cycle among ecological balance, social cooperation, and human well-being.

Human civilization stands at a critical juncture. The move from sustainable development to enduring development is not simply a call to prolong existing patterns of growth, but to renew the conditions under which development remains possible. In this sense, harmony and coexistence provide a way to understand how development can remain balanced, stable, and viable across generations.
