From traumatic oral fibroma to fatal pneumonia: a multidisciplinary postmortem investigation in a long-term monitored Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops aduncus)
Authors: Ji-Hyung Park, Kyung Lee Lee, Young Min Lee, Ji Yeong Choi, Yu Ri Heo, Seung Mok Oh, Dasom Lee, Sunmin Kim, Heon Woo Lee, Cherry Tsz Ching Poon, Won Hee Hong, Hyo-Bang Moon, Sori Mok, Chung-Young Lee, Min-Ah Kim, Adams Hei Long Yuen, Seung Hyeok Seok, Byung Yeop Kim, Sang Wha Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops aduncus) in the coastal waters of Jeju Island, Republic of Korea, exhibited an oral mass and mandibular deformity over a documented 6-year period, including 3 years of intensive longitudinal monitoring by our research team. A multidisciplinary approach combining imaging, pathology, microbiology, and omics analyses was used to assess the dolphin.ResultsPost-mortem computed tomography confirmed a mandibular fracture at the oral mass site. Histopathological examination of the oral mass revealed prominent fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition. Fibropapillomas and desmoid tumors were excluded based on viral detection assays and β-catenin accumulation analysis, supporting a diagnosis of trauma-induced fibroma. Transcriptomic analysis of the tumor tissues identified highly expressed genes associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, myofibroblast activation, and epithelial differentiation, supporting a reactive fibrotic rather than malignant phenotype. Gross necropsy revealed multiple suppurative pulmonary lesions, abundant foamy fluid within the respiratory tract, and diatoms within the pulmonary tissue. Metagenomic sequencing revealed a polymicrobial infection, with Parvimonas micra as the predominant organism. Collectively, these findings are most consistent with aspiration pneumonia, with severe secondary pulmonary infection considered a major contributor to death. In addition, analysis of halogenated organic contaminants revealed accumulation levels consistent with those typically observed in aged individuals, and no evidence was identified indicating a direct causal role in the terminal disease process.ConclusionsTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize the pathological features and proposed pathogenic mechanism of traumatic fibroma in a marine mammal, and the first confirmed case of pulmonary abscessation associated with Parvimonas micra infection in this taxonomic group. Overall, these findings provide valuable baseline data for the health monitoring and conservation of marine mammal populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40850-026-00277-z.

Background

Marine mammals function as apex predators and sentinel species, reflecting the health of marine environments. In particular, resident populations are more closely influenced by localized environmental factors and disease dynamics. Historically, the reported incidence of tumors in marine mammals has been considered low; however, the number of reported cases has increased in recent years owing to expanded research efforts in marine mammal pathology. Previous studies have proposed associations between tumor occurrence and exposure to anthropogenic contaminants, raising broader concerns regarding environmental health [1, 2, 3]. Therefore, a thorough characterization of tumors in resident populations, even in a single well-documented case, has implications beyond individual diagnosis, informing population health and ecosystem assessments.

The Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin, Tursiops aduncus (Ehrenberg, 1833), inhabits the coastal waters of the Indian Ocean and western Pacific Ocean and is currently listed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List [4, 5]. In particular, the resident population inhabiting Jeju Island, with at least 100 individuals, represents an important conservation target in South Korea because of its strong site fidelity. Importantly, the population characteristics and behaviors of this group have been subject to ongoing monitoring by multiple research groups [6, 7, 8]. The animal ‘Tuckee’ (ID: TA25-0630) was first observed with a mandibular deformity and a prominent oral mass in October 2019 in Jeju Island, and was officially reported through the television documentary “MBC Special–Warning from the Ocean: The Vanishing Whales” [9]. Following this report, the individual attracted public attention and raised concerns about potential environmental pollution. Our research team initiated focused follow-up observations in 2022. After several years of continued monitoring in the wild, during which Tuckee was observed as recently as one day before its death, the individual was found stranded along the coast in the summer of 2025.

In this case report, we aimed to characterize the histological and molecular properties of an oral mass identified in a long-term monitored individual, determine whether it was malignant, and investigate the factors contributing to death. To this end, a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach was employed, encompassing pathology, diagnostic imaging, microbiology, molecular biology, and environmental toxicology. Transcriptomic analysis and computed tomography imaging of the head were performed for the differential diagnosis of the oral mass. In addition, we characterized pulmonary polymicrobial infections and evaluated the extent of environmental contaminant accumulation.

Methods

Long-term field monitoring and sample collection

Data were derived from an intensive field monitoring program that spanned 4 years. Tuckee and other individuals were observed for over 300 days annually, with an average daily observation session of 8 h. Shore-based observations were performed using high-resolution digital cameras (Sony A7M4 and Canon R5 Mark II) and telephoto lenses. Complementary aerial surveys were conducted using drones (DJI Mavic 3 Cine/Pro). To minimize disturbance, aerial surveys were conducted at altitudes of 20–30 m to document group movement patterns and notable behaviors. After the discovery of a stranded carcass in the summer of 2025, the specimen was transported ashore for visual examination and photographic documentation of the oral lesions. Oral mass samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (NBF), and the carcass was stored frozen at − 20 °C until analysis to minimize post-mortem degradation. Post-mortem computed tomography (PMCT) analysis

Briefly, the frozen carcass was transported to the Jeju National University Equine Hospital for post-mortem computed tomography (PMCT) scanning to assess the oral mass and adjacent cranial structures. Imaging was performed using a 16-row, 32-slice helical CT scanner (Aquilion Lightning, Canon Medical Systems, Japan). Owing to the size constraints of the gantry bore (780 mm in diameter), the animal was scanned in two segments: the rostrum-to-pectoral flipper and the caudal portion. The scan parameters were set at 120 kVp and 225 mAs with a 1 mm slice thickness and a 490 mm field of view. The acquired Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) dataset was post-processed using Synapse 3D (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan), with multiplanar reconstruction and three-dimensional volume rendering performed using the software’s built-in features.

Gross necropsy and age determination

The carcass was gradually thawed over 72 h at room temperature (~ 20 °C) with ice packs. Physical examination and gross necropsy were performed by experienced veterinary professionals. Necropsy was conducted following standard protocols with modifications appropriate for the condition of the specimen [10, 11]. Each organ was measured, weighed, and photographed, and tissue sections were collected for cryopreservation or fixation in 10% NBF. In addition, the chronological age of the dolphin was estimated using three complementary methods.

Validation of growth layer groups (GLGs)

Briefly, teeth were extracted from the left mandible and stored in 70% ethanol. After fixation in resin blocks, the teeth were sectioned longitudinally along the midline using a low-speed diamond cutter (Isomet 1000; Buehler, USA) and mounted on slides. A parallel section with a thickness of 5 mm was ground using a polisher (Ecomet 250; Buehler, USA) until the growth layers were clearly discernible. Growth layer groups (GLGs) were counted independently by two researchers and confirmed by a veterinarian [12].

Radiographic assessment of pectoral flipper bone maturation

Skeletal maturity was assessed based on pectoral flipper bone maturation using a method validated for bottlenose dolphins [13]. Maturity scores were assigned across 16 anatomical sites, including the distal radius and ulna, proximal and distal metacarpals II–IV, and phalanges of digits II and III, and composite scores were used to estimate chronological age.

Body surface speckle analysis

Photographs of lateral and ventral surfaces were obtained and analyzed for speckle distribution patterns. In particular, the jaw, throat, chest, abdomen, and genital regions were individually scored for speckle density, and the overall speckle phase was determined according to the criteria established by Yagi et al. [14].

Oral mass characterization (pathological and molecular diagnosis)

Histopathology

Briefly, formalin-fixed tissues were paraffin-embedded, cut into 4 μm-thick sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). In addition, Masson’s trichrome (MT) staining was performed to assess collagen density within the fibrous tissue. Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) staining was performed to exclude acid-fast bacterial infection.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin sections of the oral mass were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0). After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and nonspecific binding, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted 1:250 in blocking solution: β-catenin (Abcam, ab32572), vimentin (Invitrogen, PA5-27231), α-smooth muscle actin (Abcam, ab7817), and CD34 (Invitrogen, 14-0341-82). Subsequently, biotinylated secondary antibodies and diaminobenzidine were applied, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. The slides were digitized using an Aperio AT2 scanner (Leica Biosystems, USA) [15, 16, 17].

Mutational analysis

To differentiate the lesion from a desmoid tumor (aggressive fibromatosis) given its aggressive gross appearance, exon 3 of the CTNNB1 gene was PCR-amplified and directly sequenced to detect mutations [18, 19]. Penile mucosal tissue from the same individual was used as a reference control. The primers used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) oral mass tissue (tongue). RNA quality assessment yielded a RIN value of 2.1. Sequencing was performed using Quant-Seq 3′ mRNA-Seq (EBIOGEN Inc., Korea), which is suitable for partially degraded RNA derived from FFPE tissues. RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) generated 22,820,162 raw reads (101 bp read length), yielding 21,390,398 clean reads after preprocessing. Sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC, yielding a Q20 rate of 97.0% and GC content of 42.3%. Because a fully annotated gene transfer format (GTF) file for Tursiops aduncus was unavailable, reads were aligned to the reference genome of the common bottlenose dolphin, Tursiops truncatus (Montagu, 1821). A total of approximately 15.96 million reads were mapped, corresponding to an overall mapping rate of 74.6%. Given that only one fibroma sample was analyzed and no matched normal tissue was available, formal differential expression analysis was not performed. Therefore, the transcriptomic findings were interpreted as exploratory expression profiling rather than definitive tumor-specific differential expression. RNA-Seq data analysis and functional annotation of highly expressed genes using gene ontology (GO) terms were performed using the ExDEGA software (EBIOGEN Inc., Korea).

Bacteriological analysis

Bacterial culture and identification

Samples collected from pulmonary abscess lesions were plated on Blood Agar and 3% NaCl Tryptic Soy Agar and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Morphologically distinct single colonies were sub-cultured through three passages and identified using VITEK MS (bioMérieux, France) or 16S rRNA gene sequencing. In particular, the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 27 F and 1492R, followed by Sanger sequencing with the internal primers 785 F and 907R. The resulting sequences were analyzed using the NCBI BLAST (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST) tool for bacterial identification. The primer sequences are presented in Table 1.

Metagenome sequencing

Abscess material was collected from the left lung using cotton swab and subjected to 16S rRNA amplicon-based metagenomic sequencing (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea). Amplicon libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., USA), followed by bioinformatic processing and taxonomic classification.

Systemic distribution of key pathogens

Based on the results of metagenomic sequencing of the pulmonary abscess, specific primers targeting Parvimonas micra and Fusobacterium spp. were used to assess systemic dissemination across multiple organs using PCR. All PCR products were analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. The primer sequences are presented in Table 1.

Virological analysis

Virus detection

DNA extracted from the oral mass and lung tissues was subjected to nested PCR targeting herpesvirus and papillomavirus [23, 24]. Viral isolation from lung tissue was attempted using embryonated chicken eggs, followed by hemagglutination assay [25]. For RNA virus detection, lung tissue RNA was reverse-transcribed and sequenced using MinION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) and Illumina MiSeq (Illumina Inc., USA) platforms [26, 27]. Metagenomic classification was performed using CZID (czid.org) and Metabuli (metabuli.steineggerlab.com) [28, 29].

Transmission electron microscopy of the oral tumor

Formalin-fixed oral tumor tissues were reprocessed for transmission electron microscopy as previously described with minor modifications [30]. Tissues were washed in phosphate-buffered saline, refixed in 2% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50–100%), and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were then prepared using an ultramicrotome, mounted on copper grids, double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and subsequently examined using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400, JEOL, Japan) operated at 80 kV.

Halogenated organic contaminant (HOC) analysis

Briefly, levels of halogenated organic contaminants (HOCs), including legacy persistent organic pollutants (POPs; e.g., polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT)-related compounds) and alternative flame retardants, were measured in blubber, liver, melon, kidney, and stomach tissue as previously described [31]. Tissues extracts were prepared following homogenization, solvent extraction, lipid clean-up, and concentration procedures. Target compounds were quantified using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS; Agilent Technologies, USA) and tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS; Agilent Technologies, USA). Concentrations were normalized to extractable lipid weight (lw) and reported as ng/g lw. A complete list of the target analytes and limits of quantification is provided in Table S1.

Results

Animal characteristics and behavioral observations

Tuckee was a long-term resident of the Jeju coastal population, and its locomotor patterns and overall vitality remained comparable to those of conspecifics throughout the observation period. Tuckee consistently participated in the fission-fusion social dynamics of the population, characterized by transitions between large aggregations and smaller subgroups. Notably, the prominent oral mass resulted in persistent mouth opening while swimming. Importantly, this structural impairment likely affected prey capture, as the individual was observed employing an atypical foraging strategy, primarily targeting olive flounder, Paralichthys olivaceus (Temminck & Schlegel, 1846), and repeatedly inserting prey sideways through the narrow oral gap.

Behavioral anomalies were first detected three weeks before death, including lagging behind the group and extended periods of solitary activity. In particular, the individual was documented alongside two conspecifics using a drone the day before being stranded. No lip hemorrhage was apparent at that time, and vertical aerial imaging indicated that the lower jaw had deviated 25° to the left of the midline (Fig. 2A). A day later, the carcass was retrieved from the nearshore waters by the Korea Coast Guard (33.242673°N, 126.418391°E). Physical examination revealed a 278 cm, 208 kg male in fair nutritional condition. Age estimation based on GLG counts (19 years), pectoral flipper bone maturation (18 years), and body surface speckle phase (Phase IV; 16–18 years) collectively supported an estimated age of 18–19 years (Supplementary Material 1).

Oral mass and mandibular fracture

Compared with the initial observations 6 years prior, the oral mass showed minimal change in size. However, a permanent leftward deviation of the mandible and an open-mouth posture were consistently maintained throughout the observation period (Fig. 2B, C). Gross examination revealed a large, firm, and lichenified mass along the left mandibular gingiva. In addition, a small, firm, and lichenified mass was observed in the rostral right mandibular gingiva. Generally, the tongue was thickened with prominent bulging and lichenification at the apex, with rounded, protruding masses in the sublingual area (Fig. 3A).

PMCT revealed a soft-tissue oral mass originating from the left gingiva and extending into the oral cavity and esophagus, with poorly defined margins that blended with the adjacent soft tissue. The lesion exhibited an electron density similar to that of the gingiva on sagittal multiplanar reconstruction images, which was compatible with a gingival origin. The mass in this case was associated with severe chronic mandibular trauma. A comminuted fracture of the left mandible was observed, with the rostral fragment markedly displaced and rotated 80° to the left of its normal anatomical position. In addition, bone remodeling at the fracture site was consistent with chronicity. Similarly, the right mandible exhibited a comminuted fracture with deformity and evidence of chronic bone remodeling. Collectively, these bilateral mandibular fractures were consistent with previous high-impact blunt-force trauma, resulting in abnormal bone union. A complex fracture in the caudal right mandible was likely sustained post-mortem during retrieval. Dental abnormalities, including missing or cracked teeth, were observed ipsilateral to the mass. (Fig. 3A–D).

Histopathological examination of the oral mass revealed marked epithelial hyperplasia, severe inflammatory cell infiltration, and neovascularization. Dense collagen bundles and poorly circumscribed proliferation of fibroblasts were identified, which stained intensely blue upon MT staining, supporting a diagnosis of fibroma (Fig. 4A, B). In conjunction with the associated mandibular fracture and lesion distribution, these findings were considered most consistent with traumatic fibroma. Desmoid tumor was excluded by negative β-catenin immunostaining and the absence of CTNNB1 gene mutations (Fig. 4C–F). Transmission electron microscopy of three sites within the oral tumor revealed no viral particles. Potential histological artifacts associated with freezing and thawing were considered during pathological interpretation, and conclusions were based primarily on lesions considered unlikely to be substantially altered by freeze–thaw changes.

Transcriptomic analysis of FFPE tongue tissues using RNA-Seq suggested four major biological themes through GO annotation. Functional interpretation was derived from highly expressed genes ranked by read count. Secretory proteolysis– and digestive enzyme–related processes were prominently represented among the top-ranked genes. Extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and fibrotic tissue remodeling, along with cytoskeletal organization and contractile fiber-associated processes, were also identified. In addition, GO terms associated with epithelial differentiation and keratinization were detected in the expressed gene sets. Collectively, the transcriptional profile was consistent with secretory enzyme activity, stromal remodeling-associated pathways, cytoskeletal components, and epithelial differentiation signatures in the lesion.

Fatal pulmonary infection

Gross necropsy revealed abundant foamy fluid throughout the tracheal lumen. The left lung contained multiple suppurative nodular lesions concentrated in the caudoventral region, characterized by multifocal to coalescing nodules filled with yellow, creamy purulent material. Pleural adhesions were present on the left lung surface, and the regional lymph nodes surrounding the left lung were markedly enlarged compared with those on the right side, with multiple mediastinal lymph nodes also exhibiting lymphadenopathy. Overall, these findings were consistent with near-total functional loss of the left lung. Consistent with the pre-necropsy PMCT findings, the right lung appeared grossly unaffected by purulent lesions (Fig. 5A, B, F).

These gross findings were corroborated by PMCT imaging. Marked enlargement of the right hyoid and mediastinal lymph nodes was evident (maximum diameters 4.96 cm and 3.85 cm, respectively), possibly representing reactive inflammatory responses to the chronic oral mass. The left lung demonstrated diffuse consolidation and extensive ground-glass opacities in a “crazy-paving” pattern with pleural effusion, indicative of near-total loss of function. In contrast, the right lung showed a milder ground-glass opacity. The upper airway and digestive tract were notably compromised. PMCT revealed nasal sinus congestion with air-fluid levels and marked upper airway swelling, resulting in a non-patent trachea. In addition, the tumor mass partially obstructed the esophagus. Collectively, these findings indicate severe respiratory dysfunction attributable to chronic upper airway obstruction and secondary pulmonary infection (Fig. 5D, E).

Histopathological examination of the left lung was consistent with multifocal to coalescing, severe, chronic-active, suppurative and necrotizing pneumonia, characterized by extensive parenchymal destruction, abundant basophilic bacterial colonies, necrotic debris, and dense inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4G, H). The right lung exhibited compressed alveolar structures with diffuse septal thickening, uneven alveolar spaces partially filled with pale eosinophilic exudate, and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, with bacterial colony invasion in some bronchial walls (Fig. 4I, J). Acid-fast staining was negative in both lungs. An impression smear of the left lung cross-section revealed diatoms as an incidental finding on ZN staining (Fig. 4K). Focal erythema at the left laryngeal inlet, identified at necropsy, was histopathologically confirmed as an ulceration with an inflammatory response (Fig. 4L, M). Overall, laryngeal ulceration and the detection of diatoms within the pulmonary tissue support the likelihood of seawater aspiration and aspiration pneumonia as contributing factors.

Aerobic culture of lung swab samples yielded nine bacterial isolates: Proteus mirabilis, Leclercia adecarboxylata, Bacillus cereus group, Shewanella algae, Acinetobacter calcoaceticus, Glutamicibacter sp., Vagococcus fluvialis, Alcaligenes faecalis subsp. faecalis, and Leucobacter chromiireducens. However, metagenomic sequencing revealed a polymicrobial infection predominantly composed of the anaerobic bacterium Parvimonas micra (> 85%), followed by Fusobacterium spp. and Actinomyces spp. as secondary and tertiary components, respectively (Fig. 5). Only routine aerobic culture was performed at the time of necropsy, which may contribute to the discrepancy between culture-based and metagenomic findings. Parvimonas and Fusobacterium spp. were additionally detected by PCR in organs other than the lungs (Table 2).

HOCs accumulation

In this study, the concentrations of the target HOCs were consistently higher in blubber than in other tissues, reflecting the lipophilic nature of the analyzed compounds. Among the target groups, DDTs and PCBs were the dominant contaminants in blubber, with concentrations of 66,500 ng/g lw and 11,900 ng/g lw, respectively. Novel brominated flame retardants (NBFRs) and Dechlorane Plus (DP), which are considered more recently used flame retardants than legacy POPs, were detected at relatively low concentrations, whereas HCHs were not detected in blubber samples (Fig. 6A). Previous studies have revealed that the mean organochlorine concentrations in cetaceans vary considerably depending on the geographic region and species [32]. Therefore, the HOC concentrations observed in this individual were compared with those reported for dolphins inhabiting Asian waters at similar trophic levels [33, 34]. Similarly, DDTs were the predominant contaminant group reported in Hong Kong and Japanese waters, followed by PCBs and chlordanes (CHLs), with a compositional pattern consistent with that observed in this case study. Furthermore, the concentrations detected in this individual overlapped with the previously reported range for cetaceans from Asian coastal waters. The relative contributions of the compound groups to the total HOC burden were generally consistent across tissues (Fig. 6B).

Additional findings

A comprehensive review of whole-body PMCT imaging, necropsy, and histopathological findings revealed no evidence of metastasis to the brain or other organs. Multiple dead Anisakis spp. nematodes were identified in the forestomach of the dolphin. Gross mucosal damage was limited to a focal ulcerative lesion associated with parasite aggregation, and mildly prominent gastric rugae were observed in some areas of the forestomach. Trematodes of the genus Nasitrema were observed in the cranial cavity and eustachian tubes at levels considered within normal range. PMCT imaging of the caudal body segment revealed caudal vertebral bone erosion consistent with age-related degeneration.

Discussion

In marine mammal research, multidisciplinary post-mortem analyses of individual carcasses provide important data on species conservation and disease epidemiology, despite the inherent limitations of sample size. For the Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin, a locally resident species, the precise determination of the cause of death in even a single individual carries substantial scientific and practical significance for population-level conservation planning. In the present study, we combined long-term behavioral monitoring with post-mortem molecular analysis to characterize a pathological sequence linking traumatic fracture, reactive tumor formation, and fatal opportunistic infections. This integration of longitudinal field observation data with post-mortem findings is uncommon in wildlife medicine and provides valuable baseline information for evaluating the health status of dolphins as sentinel species in marine ecosystems. Accordingly, this case represents more than an individual-level mortality investigation and serves as an important model illustrating how physical trauma may evolve from chronic structural impairment into a life-threatening infectious disease.

Mandibular fractures in marine mammals are frequently reported and may result from intraspecific physical combat, interspecific predation such as shark bites, or anthropogenic trauma including entanglement in fishing gear and vessel strikes [1, 35, 36]. In the present case, the absence of trauma to the maxilla or other cranial structures, with the pathology confined to the rostral mandible, suggests that a highly localized blunt force was applied to the lower jaw while the mouth was open. Oremland et al. [37] reported that male-male competition during reproductive or social hierarchy contests frequently results in physical collisions, with fractures most commonly occurring in the structurally thinner and less dense caudal third of the mandible. In contrast, the present individual exhibited a severely comminuted fracture of the rostral mandible with 80° rotation, which differed from the fracture pattern reported for conspecific interactions. An anthropogenic source of trauma cannot be excluded, although the available evidence does not permit definitive attribution. Despite the severity of the injury, the individual survived for years after the initial trauma. Given that the mandible is critical for both foraging and acoustic reception, such long-term survival may reflect behavioral adaptation. Tuckee adapted its foraging behavior by targeting the flat-bodied olive flounder, which could be maneuvered through the displaced jaw, and maintained a fair nutritional condition until shortly before death.

Although fibromas are classified as benign neoplasms, oral fibromas are often regarded as reactive hyperplastic lesions arising from chronic mechanical stimulation rather than as true autonomous tumors [38]. The transcriptomic analysis revealed that secretory proteolysis-related transcripts were most prominently represented among the highly expressed genes, whereas ECM remodeling, cytoskeletal organization, and epithelial differentiation signatures were present but less prominent. In particular, the high expression of secretory proteolysis-associated genes may reflect contributions from salivary gland-derived cells or interactions with the surrounding inflammatory tissue, as such pathways are commonly reported in chronic periodontitis [39]. ECM remodeling and contractile fiber-associated gene expression—consistent with myofibroblast activity—also reflected ongoing tissue remodeling in response to chronic mechanical irritation. Overall, the transcriptomic profile appeared to be more consistent with mechanically reactive proliferation than with neoplastic transformation. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously, given the absence of paired normal controls. The possibility of a desmoid tumor, suspected based on the aggressiveness of the lesion, was excluded by negative β-catenin immunostaining and the absence of CTNNB1 mutations, indicating that the lesion was unlikely to represent a genetically driven neoplasm. Further studies incorporating species-specific genome annotation for T. aduncus, along with continued pathological investigations of stranded individuals, are necessary to refine the molecular interpretation of this population.

An important pathological finding in this case is the mechanism by which a non-metastatic fibroma ultimately may have contributed to the death of the animal. Permanent facial structural deformity associated with the mandibular fracture and a large fibroma prevented complete mouth closure, which could have facilitated continuous seawater ingress during swimming. Moreover, the detection of diatoms within the pulmonary tissue and ulcerative lesions at the left laryngeal inlet suggest the possibility that seawater and oral bacteria may have entered the lower respiratory tract. The predominantly caudoventral distribution of abscess lesions in the lungs suggests that seawater may have been displaced toward the caudoventral lung regions by the hydrodynamic forces generated during the dolphin’s rapid forward locomotion. Collectively, these findings support a potential contributory role for mandibular deformity in the development of aspiration pneumonia.

Parvimonas micra, the predominant taxon identified in the pulmonary abscesses of this individual, is generally regarded in human medicine as a weakly virulent oral commensal, capable of acting as an opportunistic pathogen in deep tissue infections [40]. In human clinical literature, P. micra has been implicated in severe pulmonary abscesses, with a notable predilection for elderly or immunocompromised patients with antecedent dental diseases [41, 42, 43]. Given that the lung is an oxygen-rich organ, anaerobic pathogens may be underestimated by routine aerobic culture, highlighting the value of NGS-based microbial detection [42]. In addition, PCR using P. micra-specific primers confirmed the presence of the organism within the oral tumor tissue, suggesting that the oral cavity may have served as the primary reservoir for bacterial aspiration into the lungs. The high expression of secretory proteolysis-related genes in the oral tumor may be mechanistically linked to the pathogenicity of P. micra detected in both the oral and pulmonary lesions. P. micra is characterized by high protease activity [44], which disrupts the host tissue architecture and fosters a chronic inflammatory microenvironment. Whether bacterial protease activity directly induced host proteolytic gene upregulation, or whether the pre-existing inflammatory microenvironment facilitated bacterial colonization, cannot be determined from this case alone and warrants further investigation. Published reports of P. micra in non-humans are scarce; to our knowledge, available descriptions are limited to bite wound infections in dogs and monkeys [45]. Consistent with its known propensity for polymicrobial infection rather than isolated pathogenicity [41, 46], the present case also demonstrated co-infection with Fusobacterium spp. and Actinomyces spp. The present case broadens the documented host and pathological context of this opportunistic pathogen and indicates that oral-origin anaerobic pneumonia observed in humans may also occur in free-ranging cetaceans. The cause of death in this individual should not be interpreted as the result of a single infectious agent, but as a multifactorial etiology in which structural deformity sustained over several years, advancing age, and HOC-mediated immunosuppression collectively lowered host resistance and permitted the proliferation of opportunistic pathogens.

The HOC concentrations observed in this individual were generally comparable to those reported in marine mammals occupying similar trophic levels in Asian waters [33, 34], suggesting that the measured contaminant burdens reflect long-term environmental exposure typical of upper-trophic-level predators. Previous studies on the closely related species T. truncatus have shown that adult males tend to accumulate higher concentrations of lipophilic contaminants than adult females because they lack maternal depuration pathways during gestation and lactation. Consequently, the HOC burdens in males generally increase with age and may reach substantial tissue concentrations over their lifetimes [47, 48, 49, 50].

Notably, the relative compositional patterns of the HOC groups were broadly consistent across all analyzed tissues, including the blubber and internal organs. The absence of marked redistribution argues against severe nutritional depletion or major metabolic disturbance prior to death, as lipid mobilization typically alters contaminant distribution patterns. For a contextual comparison, the blubber concentrations of DDTs and PCBs were evaluated against the toxicity reference values reported for marine mammals. Although interspecies comparisons should be made with caution, elevated blubber concentrations of DDTs are associated with adverse health effects, including epizootic-related mortality (3,400 ng/g lw) and impaired immune function (2,400 ng/g lw) in harbor seals, Phoca vitulina Linnaeus, 1758 [51, 52]. Similarly, elevated PCB concentrations have been linked to endocrine disruption and immunotoxicity in free-ranging harbor seals (1,300 ng/g lw) [53] and increased calf mortality in common bottlenose dolphins (10,000 ng/g lw) [54]. In the present study, the blubber concentrations of both DDTs and PCBs exceeded these literature-based toxicity reference values. A direct causal role of HOC burden in the neoplastic lesions or pneumonia remains unsubstantiated. However, chronic physiological effects, including possible immune dysregulation, which may increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections by P. micra, cannot be excluded.

Overall, our findings provide valuable insights relevant to the conservation management of the resident Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin population around Jeju Island. As a site-fidelity population, this group is inherently vulnerable to the cumulative effects of localized environmental stressors, anthropogenic trauma, and infectious diseases. This case indicates that a single traumatic event can initiate a cascade of pathological events that may ultimately result in death. Long-term photo-identification monitoring is important for documenting the lesion progression and behavioral adaptations, and should be continued as a core component of population health surveillance. Integrating field observations with pathological investigations may improve the detection of emerging health threats and support conservation efforts for this resident population.

Limitations

While this study provides preliminary insights, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. For instance, transcriptomic analysis was performed using the T. truncatus reference genome due to the unavailability of an adequately annotated GTF file for T. aduncus. Therefore, interspecies genomic divergence may affect the mapping efficiency and functional annotation accuracy. In addition, only a single fibroma sample was analyzed without matched normal tissues, precluding formal differential gene expression analysis and definitive discrimination between tumor-specific and baseline tissue expression patterns. Accordingly, the transcriptomic findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The carcass was frozen prior to necropsy, which may have introduced histological and microbiological artifacts despite efforts to account for freeze–thaw effects during interpretation. In addition, formal immunohistochemical antibody validation in dolphin tissues and dedicated positive and negative control tissues were not available, which may limit interpretation of the immunohistochemical findings. Finally, the findings are inherently limited by the single-subject nature of this case report.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive multidisciplinary analysis, including pathology, diagnostic imaging, molecular biology, and environmental toxicology, of a stranded Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin that had been monitored in Jeju Island for 6 years. The principal pathological findings included bilateral mandibular fractures, reactive oral fibroma arising from chronic mechanical irritation, and chronic suppurative and necrotizing pneumonia of the left lung. P. micra was identified as the predominant organism in the pulmonary abscesses, representing, to our knowledge, the first documented case of a fatal infection by this bacterium in marine mammals.

Overall, the death of this individual is interpreted as the outcome of a likely multifactorial pathological sequence in which mandibular fracture and tumor formation may have promoted chronic aspiration, thereby facilitating the development of a P. micra-dominant pulmonary infection. Beyond the individual case, these findings contribute to the scientific foundation for disease monitoring and conservation management of the resident Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin population in Jeju.
