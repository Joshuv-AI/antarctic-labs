1.0 DETAILED DESCRIPTION

1.1 Overview of Experiment

Pulsed-neutron die-away (PNDA) experiments were completed at Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory (LLNL). The goal of these experiments was to provide a benchmark to validate thermal
neutron scattering laws of H2O. The experiment was conducted with a deuterium-tritium (D-T)
neutron generator producing pulses of 14.1 MeV neutrons that impinged on a moderating target. The
neutrons scattered and thermalized within the target and were counted as a function of time using
Helium-3 (3He) detectors that surrounded the target. These data provide a time-decay profile of the
neutron population from which the time eigenvalue of the experiment is calculated. The time
eigenvalue quantity α represents the integral parameter of interest. This report describes the
measurements for H2O targets. The measurements were conducted over several days beginning on
October 23rd, 2023. All measurements were completed at the low-scatter facility at LLNL. The
evaluation identifier is FUND-LLNL-DT-H2O-PNDA-001.

1.2 Description of Experimental Configuration

Figure 1 shows a cross-sectional layout of the experiment. The layout includes the following: the
cadmium-lined borated-HDPE shielding box, 3He detectors along with their holders, steel rods that
are used to support the detectors, an aluminum base plate that the detector steel rods are mounted on,
the target centered over a hole in the bottom of the box, an aluminum T-strut fixture that supports the
box, the D-T neutron generator, and the trolly. Ten moderating targets consisting of H2O in aluminum
containers were measured. Due to the computational burden required to evaluate the measurements,
the 10 experiments were down selected to four benchmark cases that provide the most value for
validating thermal scattering laws (TSLs). Throughout this evaluation, each target is referenced by
its case number. Case 1 through Case 3 used the same container to be labeled “Container 1.”: A
container of a different size was used in Case 4 to be labeled “Container 2.” Table 1 provides the case
numbers and their associated container labels and the dimensions of the water in the container during
the experiment. The variations of a given sample size were created by manipulating the height of the
water in the container, and therefore the buckling of the system. The water height shown for each
case is a derived quantity and is described in detail in Section 2.

The experimental configuration consisted of a high-density, 5%-borated polyethylene shielding box
lined with cadmium to minimize the effect of room return. The cylindrical targets were centered on a
1.9890 in. (5.0521 cm) diameter hole which itself was centered in the bottom of the shielding box.
The neutron generator was placed underneath the box, aligned with the center line of the target. The
generator’s top surface was not touching the bottom of the box and a small unmeasured gap was left
between the two. The source plane inside the generator is 5 in. away from the top head of the generator

1.2.1 Low Scatter Facility

The experiments were conducted in Building 255, Room 183A at LLNL, which is a low-scatter
irradiation room. Figure 2 shows the experimental setup. Because the room is heavily shielded, the
generator was operated at maximum settings to produce the largest neutron source term. Furthermore,
the low-scatter facility helps reduce room-return background effects. Room 183A is a large concrete-
walled room with a high ceiling and a false floor made of aluminum grating. The PNDA experiments
were maintained at least 3.0 m away from the floor and ceiling by mounting the shielding box on a
table made of aluminum T-strut. The neutron generator was suspended below and perpendicular to
the box, above the false floor. Figure 3 shows the dimensions of the room as well as the location of
the box relative to the room for all measurements. In the figure, Dimension A was 251 cm. Dimension
B was 265 cm. The thickness of the concrete walls is 61 cm. The door to the room was located at the
south wall. Figure 4 shows the placement of the PNDA experiments setup relative to the concrete

1.2.2 Borated HDPE Box

Sheets of 5%-borated HDPE (Shieldwerx™ 201HD) were procured to construct the outer shielding
box walls. The borated HDPE serves as a neutron moderator and poison to minimize room return
from the facility walls and equipment. The interior of the box is lined with cadmium sheets to further
reduce room return. The neutron generator was placed directly below a hole in the bottom of the box
as shown in Figure 5. The shielding box is easily disassembled, making it portable and reconfigurable
to other geometries.

The length (L), width (W), and height (H) of the box were measured from the inner panels without
the Cd liner as shown in Figure 6. Each dimension was measured at three locations and the average
value was recorded. The thickness (T) was determined by taking the average of three measurements
for two panels. Figures 7-11 show the average and standard deviation of the average for each
dimension indicated on each of the panels. In Figure 7, a top-down view of the top panel is shown.
The dimensions shown are the average and the standard deviation in the average of the measurements.
The length and width of the top panel were measured twice. The thickness dimensions were measured
three times.

1.2.3 Cadmium Sheets

The interior panels of the borated HDPE box were lined with cadmium sheets. The sheets lining the
east, west, south, north, and bottom panels were designed to slide into the box with only friction used
to hold it in place. The sheets lining the inner side walls of the box sit on top of the cadmium sheet
lining the bottom of the box. The sheet lining the top panel is held in place with bolts. Each sheet
was also designed to have the same thickness with length and width varying slightly to fit all together
in the box. Three of the six sheets were measured multiple times to determine the overall average in
length, width, and thickness of all sheets. The measurements were performed at three locations for
each sheet. The average and standard deviation for these measurements is indicated (labeled by W,
L, and t) on Figure 12. The same measurements are labeled on Figure 13 which also shows the hole
dimensions in the east facing sheet on the right and the hole dimensions in the bottom sheet on the
left. For length and width measurements, a tape measure with a 0.0625 in. (0.1588 cm) precision was
used. For thickness and hole-diameter measurements, an electronic caliper with ± 0.0015 in. (0.0025
cm) accuracy and 0.00005 in. (0.00013 cm) precision was used.

1.2.7 Configuration Details

For each case, the target was centered in the box and the detectors were positioned at 90° intervals
around the cylinder radius. Figure 21 shows the detectors’ setup around Container 2. The lids were
kept in place during the experiments but removed in the figure for display. The distance between the
detectors and the target’s surface is equal to the length of the screws pinching the detectors and
touching the target’s surface as shown in Figure 22. The length of the screws was each measured
using an electronic caliper with a ± 0.025 mm accuracy. The average and standard deviation in the
average for these measurements was found to be 4.900 mm ± 0.270 mm. Each detector was held up
on one end of the detector holder with the screws pinching the detectors. On the other end of the
holder, a steel rod is held in place by hex nuts. During the experiment, the location of the detectors
was adjusted with each target size by sliding the steel rods over the slits in the Al-base so the screws
touched the surface of the target. Visual inspection of the detectors’ alignment with the target was

done before and after each experiment to ensure they did not tilt at any angle. For all cases, the
detectors were lowered to their bottommost point, where the bottom face touched the Al-base.

1.6 Measurement Technique

The control and monitoring of the generator is performed using a computer-based system. The neutron
detectors were connected to a combined system of high-voltage power supply, pre-amplifier,
amplifier, and discriminator through the PDT20A-SHV-12V-C model from Precision Data
Technology.

A single pulse from the generator had a ~100 µs pulse width for each case. The neutron detectors
operated at a 50 ns pulse width. The signals from the neutron detectors are recorded by an R7780
CAEN shift register. The register provides time tagged information with accuracy of 100 ns. The
neutron detectors were connected to the register’s channel input along with the trigger output of the
generator itself. Keeping track of the generator pulses allows for neutron counts of each of the four
detectors to be summed together with correspondence to the time-tagged generator pulse that created
them.

After a neutron pulse, many neutrons will scatter and/or get absorbed in the surrounding materials
inside the HDPE-borated box and in the box itself. A small portion will also lead to room return.
Some of the neutron population scattering within the target will slow down and reaches a thermal
equilibrium with a fundamental spatial mode. For the purpose of this benchmark and the design of
the PNDA experiments minimizing room return, Eq. 2 describes the behavior of the neutron
population at thermal equilibrium and in the fundamental mode. An additional room return term exists
to the right side of Eq. 2 when room return is not being minimized. Here, r is a spatial location, t is
time, and α [s-1] is the characteristic flux decay-time eigenvalue that represents the integral parameter
for the experiment.

The integral parameter (α) is calculated by fitting the parameters in Eq. 2 to the measured data as
described in Section 1.7. When the neutrons diffuse through the target material and travel to the
detectors, the counts exhibit exponential decaying behavior once equilibrium is reached. An
exponential fit with non-linear least square is performed on the neutron counts to determine α.

Certain characteristics of the target can impact the resulting α. Eq. 3 describes in further detail the
parameters of most influence on α. Here, D0 [cm2-s-1] is the asymptotic diffusion coefficienta in an
infinite medium of the target material, C [cm4-s-1] is the “cooling coefficient,” B02 [cm-2] is the
geometric buckling, v is the thermal neutron velocity, and Σa [cm-1] is the macroscopic absorption
cross section. The cooling coefficient captures a geometric effect where more high-energy neutrons
leak at the system boundaries, which causes the average neutron population to be at a temperature
lower than thermodynamic equilibrium.

The term 𝐶𝐵04 is often smaller relative to the absorption and diffusion terms and has a small impact
on α. The exception to this is when the geometric buckling is very large, and the B04 term becomes
very important. In most cases, α is a nearly linear function of B02 and the slope depends on the material
cross sections.
For a specific target material, B02 can be changed by altering a certain geometric dimension, which
would be done to manipulate how α is affected by different physics of the system. For instance, when
the target sample is large and B02 is small, α depends mostly on absorption (Σa), i.e. not on TSLs.
When the target sample is small, B02 is large and α depends primarily on D0, which itself is highly
influenced by the physics of thermal scattering. Fundamentally, the sensitivity of α to thermal
scattering increases with B02, or increases as the target sample size decreases.

Impurities in water were tested by the manufacturer and were all found to be below the detection
threshold as discussed in Section 1. The results shown in Table 9 were measured to parts per billion

have a negligible impact on α. Another source of water impurity to consider is the aluminum
containers which oxidize with water. Al oxidation rate with air is faster than with watera. This leads
to the formation of an oxidation layer on the surface of the containers protecting them from further
interacting with water. This is also especially true for the duration of the experiments (maximum 30
minutes).

2.2.2 Container Composition

The Al containers are made of Al-1050 alloy. The chemical composition found in this alloy is shown

container composition. The impact of using an Al-1050 alloy instead of a pure Al was found to be
insignificant to the calculation of α and thus pure Al was used.

2.5 Detector Efficiency

Determining α as described in section 1 is done when the neutron population is at thermal equilibrium
and is in the fundamental spatial mode. During this time, the neutron spectrum does not change. The
integrated average efficiency, therefore, remains constant for each case and its uncertainty is
considered to have a negligible impact on α.

2.6 Detector Dead Time

The decay curves are expected to be the most sensitive to dead time during the generator pulse and
immediately after. This is when the generator is turned on and the count rate is at its maximum. The
region where the integral parameter is determined for this benchmark is well after the neutron pulse
where the count rate decreases by several orders of magnitude. For the generator frequencies used in
all the measurements, the pulse periods are ~1 ms between each generator trigger. This means that
for a typical 3He detector, which has a dead time of a few microseconds, time-tagged events belonging
to different generator triggers are considered to be outside of the time interval impacted by dead time.

Figure 45 shows the regions where α is calculated for each decay curve from measured data. The
region in Case 2 shows the highest count rate used in calculating α. Another case to consider is when
the 3He detectors are exposed to the highest number of thermal neutrons when the generator is turned
off, which occurs for Case 4 being the largest target.

Revision: 0 Page 52 of 111

 Figure 45. Regions on the decay curves where each α is calculated highlighted in gold.
The following study was applied to Case 2 and Case 4. A list of the time difference between each
event for each generator trigger is first created. Next, a virtual deadtime of 5 µs (5 µs was chosen as
a conservative estimate) was imposed on the measured data list, removing detection events if they
occurred with 5 µs of the previous event. This process was done for each detector independent of the
other. The detection system can be characterized by either a paralyzable or non-parazayble behavior
when it is under deadtime. For this study, the paralyzable model is used, since it causes a larger loss
in the signal.

Figure 46 shows the decay curve for Case 2 from the deadtime-corrected and uncorrected data. The
artificial imposition of deadtime caused a significant loss of neutron events during the pulse width
and during the first ~0.25 ms after the pulse. However, the area of the die-away curve from which the
integral parameter is extracted shows very limited changes. When α was calculated from both
deadtime-corrected and uncorrected curves, the two α values agreed within 3 standard deviations. For
Case 4, no statistically significant change in α occurred. For this reason, deadtime corrections are
omitted from method to estimate the integral parameters of this experiment.

3.0 BENCHMARK SPECIFICATIONS

The benchmark model for each target consists of the borated-HDPE box, Cd liner, targets, and
detectors. All were modeled using the MCNP6.2 neutron transport code with nuclear data processed
from the ENDF/B-VIII.0 library. The following simplifications were made:

 1. Removal of electronics and SHV connectors.
 2. Removal of Al detector holders and steel rods.
 3. Removal of any air gaps between the Cd and box.
 4. Removal of all components outside the HDPE-borated box.
 5. The HDPE-borated box lid was modeled with the same width and length dimensions as the
 rest of the box.
 6. The distance between the surface-of-detector to surface-of-target was modeled as of 0.3175
 cm.
The simplifications resulted in negligible changes in the decay constant for the larger targets with
negligible defined as a difference that is less than 0.1%. For the smaller targets, Case 1 and Case 2,
the removal of the electronics, SHV connectors, and Al detector holders were taken as simplification
biases. Their impact is further described below. Another simplification is the removal of the alignment
rulers. This is considered to have an insignificant effect on α.

3.1.1 Room Return and Fixtures Outside the Box

Fixtures outside the box including generator body, T-strut, trolly, and room (walls, ceiling, floor,

dominate the decay curve at different times for each case and is impacted by the size of the target.
Figure 47 shows the neutron counts as a function of time for Case 1 from the benchmark model in

return here becomes the most prominent after ~0.6 ms. The fitting region (in red) used to determine

time region, the neutron population from room return is statistically negligible. For all cases, α is
determined in the region that is most linear (in log scale) and represents the fundamental mode.
Therefore, any room-return bias from not modeling features of the experimental room is considered
insignificant.

Figure B-1 shows a typical behavior that the neutron counts exhibit in the detector. The neutron pulse
ends at ~0.1 ms. As the neutrons thermalize and scatter in the target material, the higher-order spatial
modes decay and the neutrons reach the fundamental mode before background and room return
dominate the decay curve. This is the equilibrium region of the curve where the neutrons exhibit
purely exponential decay behavior and from which the integral parameter is calculated.

 APPENDIX C: UNCERTAINTY IN α

 APPENDIX E: LIMITATION IN DETERMINING α

The method presented herein to determine α demonstrates the authors’ best effort in isolating the
fundamental mode and quantifying the uncertainty in α. This makes α a sufficient integral parameter
to assess the uncertainties and biases between the experiments and the models. The method, however,
poses some restrictions, especially when it comes to small targets. While smaller targets are most
sensitive to the thermal scattering laws, they also experience the fastest neutron leakage, resulting in
lower statistics in the detectors. This issue is particularly pronounced in the target used for Case 1.
The methodology for determining α in this case, as described in Appendix B, has the following
caveats:

 1. Fit Window Selection: When choosing fit windows along the decay curve, it is important to
 ensure the window is small enough to isolate the fundamental mode from higher-order
 modes.

 2. Statistical Accuracy: The fit window must also contain enough data to ensure the
 calculated α is statistically reliable.

Currently, the parameters used to calculate α are first determined from the experimental data and then
kept the same for the model calculations. This approach means that both the fitting strategy and the
experimental statistics could introduce additional bias between the experimental α and the model-
derived α.
To address this, future analyses should independently determine the parameters for calculating the
experimental α and those for the model α. This separation will provide a more accurate assessment of
the true bias between the experimental and benchmark model values of α.
