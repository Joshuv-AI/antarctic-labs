Genomic distribution characteristics and interspecific differences of microsatellite landscapes in Felidae
Authors: Hailong Dou, Hongyuan Xi, Yujian Li, Haitao Yang, Honghai Zhang, Fang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicrosatellites within genomes play crucial roles in regulating gene expression, DNA replication, and chromosomal structure and function. Analyzing the composition and distribution patterns of microsatellites in closely related species not only reveals their evolutionary dynamics and adaptive mechanisms but also provides essential technical support for applications in genetic breeding, species conservation, and disease research. As one of the world’s most captivating animal groups, the landscape patterns of microsatellites across feline genomes remain to be systematically characterized.ResultsThis study utilized high-quality genomic data to conduct a systematic comparative analysis of microsatellite landscape distribution patterns across the genomes of 13 felid species. The findings revealed that microsatellite abundance and distribution exhibit species-specific characteristics, with a non-random genomic distribution and a negative correlation between microsatellite abundance and repeat length. The predominant distribution pattern followed the sequence: single > double > quadruple > triple > quintuple > sextuple nucleotide repeats. Microsatellite abundance peaked in intergenic regions, whereas trinucleotide repeats were more prevalent within exons. Coding regions showed a marked preference for trinucleotide and hexanucleotide repeats. Enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG pathways indicated that coding sequences containing microsatellites were primarily involved in transcription and translation processes.ConclusionsOur study elucidates the distribution patterns and characteristics of microsatellites across diverse feline species, providing significant insights into their evolutionary mechanisms and functional roles. Furthermore, these findings establish a valuable reference and foundational dataset for the future development of high-quality, species-specific microsatellite markers in felids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13118-7.

Background

Microsatellites, also known as simple sequence repeats (SSRs), are tandemly repeated motifs of 1–6 nucleotides that are widely distributed across eukaryotic, prokaryotic, and viral genomes [1, 2]. Microsatellites are frequently associated with retrotransposons, highlighting their dynamic nature within genomes [3]. Owing to their high polymorphism, codominant inheritance, and broad genomic distribution, microsatellites have become indispensable molecular markers for gene mapping, linkage map construction, marker-assisted selection, and tracing genetic relationships and evolutionary history [1, 4].Microsatellite abundance varies considerably among species and generally shows a positive correlation with genome size [5]. Their genome distribution is non-random, with marked differences between coding and non-coding regions, and certain repeat motifs display strong enrichment in specific genomic contexts [2]. Microsatellite accumulation is also closely linked to chromosomal heterochromatinization. In species with a ZW sex-determination system, substantial microsatellite accumulation on the W chromosome is considered a key factor in sex chromosome differentiation and evolution [6]. Although once regarded as non-functional “junk DNA”, microsatellites are now recognized as biologically significant elements with functions that are highly dependent on their genomic location [7, 8]. They influence gene regulation, development, and genetic variation at multiple levels. Microsatellites within coding regions can affect gene activation and protein expression, while those in non-coding regions may regulate transcriptional activity [9]. Intronic microsatellites have been associated with mRNA transport and alternative splicing [10] and repeat expansions within introns or 3’-Untranslated regions (UTR) can lead to gene silencing or loss of function [11, 12].

A defining characteristic of microsatellites is their high instability, manifested as frequent changes in repeat number and elevated mutation rates. Reported microsatellite mutation rates range from 10⁻² to 10⁻⁸ per locus per generation, based on estimates from yeast, Drosophila, and mammals [13]. These rates vary with factors such as allele length and repeat unit size. This instability affects microsatellite abundance and functional integrity and is strongly associated with the development and progression of numerous diseases [14, 15]. Current evidence indicates that DNA replication slippage, recombination during double-strand break repair, and retrotransposition are major molecular mechanisms driving microsatellite instability [16], reflecting the complex and multifactorial nature of their variability.

Advances in next-generation sequencing technologies and bioinformatic tools have largely replaced traditional library screening approaches for microsatellite identification, greatly enhancing the efficiency and accessibility of microsatellite marker development [1, 17]. Consequently, genome-wide analyses of microsatellite distribution have been conducted across diverse taxa, including humans [18], primates [19], birds [20, 21], reptiles [8, 22], fish [23], plants [24], fungi [18, 25], and viruses [26]. These investigations have substantially advanced our understanding of microsatellite biology and provided valuable insights into phylogenetic relationships and the mechanisms underlying microsatellite formation and diversification.

Felidae is one of the most species-rich families within the order Carnivora. However, many felid species are currently classified as endangered or threatened due to anthropogenic pressures and habitat loss [15, 27]. As apex predators, felids play an irreplaceable role in maintaining ecosystem stability and possess considerable ecological and cultural importance. Despite their value as models for studying genome evolution and genetic diversity, comprehensive analyses of genome-wide microsatellite distribution patterns in felids remain limited. Characterizing microsatellite landscapes across feline genomes is therefore essential for elucidating phylogenetic relationships, understanding molecular mechanisms of adaptive evolution, and supporting applications in genetic management of captive breeding programs, biodiversity conservation, and disease research.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a comparative analysis of microsatellite landscape features across 13 feline genomes. The specific objectives were to (1) compare microsatellite quantity, diversity, abundance, frequency, and GC content; (2) examine the relationship between repeat number and sequence length; (3) assess microsatellite distribution across different genomic regions, including intergenic regions, exons, introns, UTRs, and coding sequences (CDS); and (4) determine the biological pathways and functional categories associated with microsatellite-containing coding genes. These findings provide a comprehensive overview of microsatellite organization in felid genomes and offer a foundation for developing high-quality molecular markers for studies of genetic diversity, functional genomics, and conservation biology.

Materials and methods

Data sources

This study utilized genomic data from 13 felid species, representing seven genera (Table 1). Species were selected based on the availability of both a GenBank (GCA) and a RefSeq (GCF) reference genome assembly at the time of analysis; the domestic cat was excluded to maintain a focus on wild felids. All genomic sequences and corresponding annotation files were downloaded from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Annotation files were standardized and calibrated using TBtools [28], a bioinformatics toolkit whose GXF Fix function corrects structural inconsistencies in genomic annotation files.

Microsatellite identification and attribute survey

Microsatellite loci across the genomic sequences were identified using Krait v1.5.1 [29]. The thresholds for repeat number were as follows: mononucleotide repeats ≥ 12 (i.e., ≥ 12 bp), dinucleotide repeats ≥ 7, and trinucleotide, tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeats all ≥ 4. A custom Python script was used to integrate microsatellite identification results with genomic annotation data, enabling characterization of microsatellite distribution patterns across distinct functional genomic regions, including intergenic spaces, exons, introns, CDS, and UTR.

Compound microsatellites were defined as sequences comprising two or more perfect microsatellites separated by a spacer sequence not exceeding 100 bp. Repeat unit patterns exhibiting circular permutations or reverse complementarity were treated as the same repeat type. The following repeat types were classified: single-nucleotide repeats (A, C), dinucleotide repeats (AT, AG, AC, and GC), trinucleotide repeats (10 types), tetranucleotide repeats (33 types), pentanucleotide repeats (102 types), and hexanucleotide repeats (350 types), for a total of 501 repeat unit types.

To visualize microsatellite density distribution across the 13 feline genomes, a circular heatmap was constructed, adapting the method described by Zhong et al. [8] with minor modifications. The process involved three steps: (1) calculating and ranking the relative density (total SSR length in bp per Mb of genomic sequence) of each microsatellite type within each genome; (2) assigning a score based on density rankings—microsatellites in the top 5, 20, and 35 of density received scores of 3, 2, and 1, respectively, while remaining types were scored as follows: 0 for microsatellites with density > 1, -1 for microsatellites with density between 0 and 1, and − 2 for microsatellites with density = 0; and (3) generating the heatmap using the ChiPlot online tool (https://www.chiplot.online) to visualize cross-species microsatellite density. Additionally, a Python script was employed to statistically analyze the GC content of all identified microsatellites.

Scalability of microsatellite sequences

To assess the scalability of microsatellites, defined as their potential for length variation, we examined the correlation between repeat number and sequence length. Sequences were categorized into six length intervals: <24 bp, 24–50 bp, 50–100 bp, 100–150 bp, 150–200 bp, and > 200 bp. The relationship between repeat counts and sequence length was visualized using a heatmap.

Functional annotation of microsatellites within CDS

To investigate the potential functional roles of microsatellites within CDS, CDS regions were extracted from the annotated genome of each species. Functional annotation was performed using EggNOG-mapper software [30], against the eggNOG 5.0 database, including GO classification and KEGG pathway annotation. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using TBtools software [28], with the GO and KEGG annotations from EggNOG-mapper serving as the background dataset, and gene IDs from CDS- containing microsatellites serving as the foreground dataset. Enrichment significance was assessed using a hypergeometric test, and resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method; terms/pathways with an FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Separate GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed accordingly. Significant GO terms and KEGG pathways were visualized using heatmaps generated with OriginPro 2025.

Results

Global distribution and compositional characteristics of microsatellites

The effective genome lengths of the 13 felid species ranged from 2,297,559,483 bp (lion) to 2,466,981,241 bp (clouded leopard), with GC content between 41.58% (jaguar) and 41.91% (fishing cat) (Table 1).

The total number of SSRs across species varied from 871,299 (cheetah) to 1,087,474 (Canada lynx), with genome coverage spanning from 0.75% (cheetah) to 0.91% (Canada lynx) (Appendix Table S1). This variation was not evenly distributed among genera. Within the Panthera genus, SSR counts were relatively conserved, ranged from 899,832 (lion) to 945,556 (snow leopard), with coverage between 0.77% and 0.80%. Intraspecific variation was low (CV < 2.01%). In contrast, the two Lynx species included in this study exhibited stark differences: Canada lynx possessed 1,087,474 SSRs (coverage 0.91%), while bobcat had only 936,572 (coverage 0.77%), a difference of over 150,000 (16% variation). This divergence was driven primarily by mononucleotide repeats (~ 62% of the total count difference) and secondarily by dinucleotide repeats (~ 25%), whereas tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide repeats each contributed less than 8%. Within the Panthera genus, by comparison, both mononucleotide and dinucleotide repeat counts were tightly conserved across the five species (CV < 3% for each class). The two species of Prionailurus, the leopard cat and fishing cat, were nearly identical in both SSR count (929,211 and 939,671, respectively) and coverage (both 0.77%). Relative abundance and density analyses revealed the lowest values for the cheetah (366.49 loci/Mb and 7,464.32 bp/Mb) and the highest values in the Canada lynx (452.17 loci/Mb and 9,087.24 bp/Mb). Neither relative abundance (Pearson, r = 0.07) nor relative density (Pearson, r = 0.05) showed significant correlation with genome size (p > 0.05).

Despite marked differences in total microsatellite counts, the distribution of motif types was consistent across species. Mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant, followed by dinucleotide repeats, together accounting for over 80% of total microsatellites. The remaining repeat types were less abundant, ranked in descending order as follows: tetranucleotide repeats, trinucleotide repeats, pentanucleotide repeats, and hexanucleotide repeats (Supplementary Table S1). The proportion of mononucleotide repeats ranged from 43.47% to 50.20%, while dinucleotide repeats ranged from 32.08% to 36.94%. The difference in these proportions varied significantly across species, with the smallest difference in cheetah (6.54%) and the largest in Geoffroy’s cat (18.12%) (Supplementary Table S1).

Analysis of microsatellite density revealed that certain repeat types, such as mononucleotide (A)n, (C)n, dinucleotide repeats (AC)n, (AG)n, and tetranucleotide repeat (AAAT)n, were abundant across all species (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S2). However, certain repeat types exhibited species-specific distributions. For instance, the hexanucleotide repeat (ACCCGC)n had a density of 1.12 bp/Mb in the fishing cat, approximately tenfold greater than in other species (0.03–0.38 bp/Mb), while (AGCGGG)n exhibited a density of 0.35 bp/Mb, approximately four times higher than in other species (0.05–0.13 bp/Mb) (Supplementary Table S2).

Short microsatellites (repeat number < 20) dominated the genome, with proportions of mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide microsatellites exceeding 86.14%, 89.38%, 99.55%, 99.49%, 99.83%, and 99.70%, respectively. GC content and base composition characteristics

Microsatellite GC content ranged from 28.07% (Geoffroy’s cat) to 32.14% (cheetah), approximately 10% points lower than the genomic average (41.58%–41.91%), indicating an enrichment of AT-rich repeat sequences (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S3). GC content varied among repeat types. Dinucleotide microsatellites had the highest GC content, ranging from 45.59% (Geoffroy’s cat) to 46.05% (leopard cat). GC content in trinucleotide and hexanucleotide microsatellites was also elevated compared to the overall genome, while mononucleotide repeats had the lowest GC content (Fig. 2).

GC content of microsatellites across 13 felid genomes and different functional genomic regions

GC content distribution in microsatellites was highly uneven across functional genomic regions (Fig. 3). Within intron regions, microsatellite GC content was nearly identical to that of the overall genome. Within the UTRs, microsatellite GC content was closer to that of exon regions, with trinucleotide and hexanucleotide repeats displaying elevated GC content. Within the CDS, the GC content of all microsatellite types was markedly higher than that in other regions and showed the greatest variations. The average microsatellite GC content in CDS, UTR, and exon regions exceeded that in introns and the genome as a whole. Pentanucleotide repeats in CDS regions exhibited the greatest GC content variation among species, ranging from 35.05% (Canada lynx) to 89.68% (lion) (Supplementary Table S3).

Distribution patterns and characteristics of microsatellites

Microsatellite distribution across the genome is non-random (Fig. 4). Intergenic regions and introns were the most concentrated areas, accounting for 46.29% (cheetah) to 60.16% (Canada lynx) of the total microsatellites and 38.76% (Canada lynx) to 50.96% (cheetah) in introns (Supplementary Table S4). The CDS region had the fewest microsatellites. Relative abundance analysis revealed two distinct patterns: in three species (jaguarundi, Canada lynx, jaguar), microsatellites were most abundant in intergenic regions, followed by introns, UTRs, exons, and CDS; in the remaining species, the pattern was inverted, with introns having the highest abundance, followed by intergenic regions, UTRs, exons, and CDS.

Mononucleotide repeats predominated in both intergenic regions and introns, followed by dinucleotide, tetranucleotide, trinucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide (Supplementary Table S4). The motif composition showed distinct preferences: mononucleotide repeats were mainly composed of (A)n, while dinucleotide repeats were most abundant in (AG)n, with (CG)n being extremely rare. Trinucleotide repeats were predominantly (AAC)n and (AAT)n, while tetranucleotide to hexanucleotide repeats were dominated by high-AT motifs, such as (AAAT)n, (AAAAC)n, and (AAAAAC)n (Figs. 5A, B, Supplementary Table S5). A key distinction was observed in hexanucleotide repeats: (ACAGAG)n was more prevalent in introns, whereas (ATATAG)n was more common in intergenic regions.

Although microsatellites constitute only 0.91%–2.76% of total exons, their composition differed markedly from that in intergenic regions and introns (Supplementary Table S4). The proportion of trinucleotide repeats was significantly higher in exons, surpassing dinucleotide repeats in nine species and remaining substantially higher than in intergenic regions and introns in the other four species. Exon microsatellites were enriched in high-GC motifs, such as (CCG)n, (AGG)n, (ACG)n, and (AGC)n, contrasting with the AT-rich motifs prevalent in intergenic regions and introns (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table S5). The microsatellite composition within CDS regions was most distinctive: being almost entirely composed of trinucleotide repeats, with (CCG)n being the most abundant (Fig. 5D). Mono-, di-, tetra-, and pentanucleotide repeats within exons were primarily located in the UTRs (Supplementary Table S4). The 5’-UTR was particularly characterized by abundant trinucleotide repeats, especially (CCG)n, and high GC content, whereas the 3’-UTR was more similar to the intergenic regions and introns, albeit with a higher proportion of high-GC motifs (Fig. 6).

Functional enrichment analysis of microsatellites within CDS

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on CDS regions containing microsatellites. A total of 1,366 to 2,306 significantly enriched GO entries were identified across species (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Tables S6, S7). The enrichment patterns were consistent across species, with microsatellites within CDS predominantly enriched in the following GO categories: Molecular function (binding and transcription regulator activity), Cellular component (organelle, nucleus, membrane-enclosed lumen), and Biological process (biological regulation, macromolecule metabolic processes, gene expression (Fig. 7). KEGG pathway analysis revealed 11 significantly enriched pathways across all species (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S7). The most significantly enriched pathways included ‘Transcription factors’ (03000), ‘Spliceosome’ (03041), ‘Transcription machinery’ (03021), and ‘Chromosome and associated proteins’ (03036) (Fig. 8). Genes containing microsatellites were primarily clustered within core protein families, such as ZBTB, FOX, SOX, NKX, and HOX families within ‘Transcription factors’; ‘Spliceosome’, HNRNP, PRPF, and RBM families within ‘Spliceosome’; GTF, MED, CDK, and SMARCA families within ‘Transcription machinery’; and CHD, KDM/KMT, and ARID families within ‘Chromosome and associated proteins’ (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

Global distribution pattern of microsatellites

This study reveals significant variation in both the total number of microsatellites (871,299–1,087,474) and genomic coverage (from 0.75% to 0.91%) across felid species. When comparing across taxa, felid microsatellite coverage is similar to that observed in primates (0.4%–0.98%) [21, 31, 32], yet markedly lower than that of humans (approximately 3%) [33], insects (1.69%–7.23%) [5], and some reptiles (0.8%–8.19%) [8, 22], while higher than that of birds (0.13%–0.49%) [34] and fungi (0.04%–0.15%) [35]. Such cross-taxon differences largely reflect underlying disparities in genome architecture—including genome size, transposable element content, and GC composition—rather than directly mirroring evolutionary history [36–38]. Meaningful evolutionary inference is therefore better drawn from comparisons among closely related species with conserved genome architecture, such as those within the Felidae family [12, 23, 25].

Notably, within the Panthera genus, microsatellite content remains relatively consistent, whereas a significant variation (approximately 16%) is observed between the two Lynx species included in this study. Previous studies have demonstrated that microsatellite diversity is sensitive to historical events such as population bottlenecks [39], and this divergence within the Lynx genus may point to distinct population histories for the Canada lynx and bobcat. This rapid variation in microsatellite content among closely related species mirrors findings from other taxa, such as Drosophila, where microsatellite numbers also exhibit high instability over short evolutionary timescales [40]. Additionally, differences in genome assembly quality across species may also influence microsatellite detection outcomes [41].

Early studies generally suggested a positive correlation between microsatellite abundance or density and genome size [12, 42], a view supported by a limited number of eukaryotic studies [19]. However, the present study finds no significant correlation between microsatellite relative abundance or density and genome size in felids. Similar findings have been reported across diverse taxa, including mammals [32], birds [34], fish [23], insects [43], and fungi [44], whereas reptiles more often exhibit a negative correlation [8]. These conflicting results indicate that the relationship between genome size and microsatellite abundance or density is not universal but may vary significantly across different phylogenetic groups. Potential mechanisms underlying this phenomenon may relate to differences in DNA replication slippage efficiency and the functionality of DNA repair systems among taxa [12, 45].

The distribution pattern of microsatellites in felids aligns with typical eukaryotic trends, with mononucleotide repeats being the most abundant, followed by dinucleotide repeats [25]. Concurrently, tetranucleotide repeats are more numerous than trinucleotides in their genomes, consistent with patterns observed in other vertebrates. In contrast, some invertebrates exhibit a greater prevalence of pentanucleotide or hexanucleotide repeats [46, 47]. This distribution pattern cannot be explained by the simplistic notion that “longer repeat units are less abundant.” Studies suggest that microsatellite mutation rates display motif-specific biases [23, 48] and that different repeat types may be subject to distinct selective constraints. These biases lead to cumulative preferences for specific microsatellite types across species, resulting in fundamental interspecific differences [12, 34].

The genomic distribution of microsatellites reflects a dynamic equilibrium shaped by multiple molecular mechanisms during evolution. Evidence indicates that microsatellite abundance typically decreases as repeat length increases [20]. In the present study, microsatellite length distributions were highly consistent across species, with the vast majority containing fewer than 20 repeat units. This pattern may result from a balance equilibrium between repeat length mutations and point mutations [49]. Under such an equilibrium, microsatellite expansion is constrained; once repeats reach a critical length, genomic mechanisms such as large-scale deletions or repair processes act to limit further expansion [13, 50]. These length constraints likely have important biological implications. By maintaining repeat stability, they help preserve normal gene expression, allowing microsatellite length polymorphism to generate phenotypic variation while minimizing the risks associated with excessive expansion [15, 51]. Notably, we identified a small number of exceptionally long microsatellites (> 200 repeats), including mononucleotide repeats in clouded leopards and leopard cats and pentanucleotide repeats in Canada lynx and tigers. These unusually long loci may represent genomic instability hotspots or reflect specific functional requirements, such as roles in chromatin structure maintenance or replication origin specification. Further investigation of these regions may provide new insights into the functional mechanisms of microsatellites.

The specific distribution of microsatellite GC content and its functional implications

Consistent with most eukaryotes, AT-rich microsatellites predominate in feline genomes. This pattern is consistent with a ‘GC→AT bias,’ whereby GC-rich DNA sequences tend to accumulate AT substitutions over evolutionary time [31]. In felids, dinucleotide repeats exhibit the highest GC content, while mononucleotide repeats display the lowest, a pattern similar to that reported in rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) [31]. However, GC-content patterns differ across taxa: trinucleotide repeats show the highest GC content in bovids [52], while mononucleotide repeats are GC-enriched in many beetles [5]. Such interspecific variation likely reflects the long-term interplay between mutational pressures and selective constraints.

The biological significance of GC content extends beyond sequence stability. Expansion of GC-rich microsatellites is strongly associated with multiple human diseases [53], and GC-rich microsatellites often impose more severe functional consequences when amplified. Accordingly, they are subject to stricter constraints in functionally critical genomic regions such as CDS and 5’-UTR. Our data support this notion, revealing significantly higher GC content in microsatellites located within CDS compared with non-coding regions, forming a clear gradient: CDS > UTR > intron ≈ genome-wide background. This gradient likely reflects differential selective pressures across genomic compartments. Within CDS regions, elevated GC content and constrained repeat length appear to result from multiple selective forces. First, C ≡ G triple hydrogen bonds enhance local stability. Second, codon usage bias often favors GC-rich synonymous codons, contributing to improved translational efficiency and accuracy [54, 55]. Third, many GC-enriched trinucleotide repeats (e.g., CCG, AGG) correspond directly to codon triplets; changes in their repeat number alter amino acid composition and may disrupt protein function (3). Consequently, microsatellites in coding regions are subject to stronger purifying selection than those in introns. This selective pressure both elevates GC content and imposes stringent constraints on repeat length variation, eliminating most deleterious expansions to preserve protein integrity.

Non-random distribution patterns of microsatellites

Genome-wide analyses revealed pronounced regional specificity in microsatellite distribution. Among mononucleotide repeats, (A)n sequences are far more abundant than (C)n, with poly(A/T) repeats demonstrating marked predominance. This pattern likely results from two primary mechanisms. From a mutational perspective, (GC)n repeats exhibit lower replication slippage rates due to the greater stability conferred by triple hydrogen bonds, whereas poly(A/T) tracts are more prone to slippage-mediated expansion [52]. Concurrently, the high frequency of poly(A) sequences may correlate with the insertions of poly(A)-tailed retrotransposons and the formation of processed pseudogenes [56, 57].

Among dinucleotide repeats, (AG)n is the most abundant motif in felid genomes, followed by (AC)n. A similar pattern has been observed in tree shrews (Tupaia belangeri), indicating that this AG-dominant configuration is not restricted to felids [19]. In many other vertebrates, however, (AC)n repeats are most abundant [19, 42]. Given that GC-rich regions are generally more stable and less susceptible to replication slippage, whereas AT-rich regions are more prone to instability, microsatellite motifs may evolve toward equilibrium states shaped by both mutational bias and selective constraints [23]. Consequently, (AG)n and (AC)n repeats tend to be more prevalent than (AT)n or (CG)n repeats. This motif bias may also account for the relatively elevated GC content observed in feline dinucleotide repeats compared with other repeat types.

From a functional region perspective, microsatellite distribution patterns in feline intergenic regions and introns closely resemble those observed genome-wide, with a notably low proportion of trinucleotide repeats. Motifs such as (CCG)n and (ACG)n are particularly rare in these regions. This scarcity may arise from the susceptibility of CpG dinucleotides to methylation and subsequent C→T mutations, thereby disrupting repeat continuity [23, 58]. Concurrently, GC-rich trinucleotide repeats may be subject to negative selection due to their potential to form secondary structures, such as hairpins or G-quadruplexes, which can interfere with DNA replication or mRNA splicing [53, 59].

Within CDS, the near-exclusive presence of trinucleotide repeats is expected from the reading-frame constraint. Non-triplet repeats within exons are predominantly confined to UTRs, which exhibit distinct compositional differences between the 5’-UTR and 3’-UTR. Both CDS and 5′-UTR show a marked enrichment of (CCG)n. Composed entirely of G and C, (CCG)n is the most stable trinucleotide and thus preferentially retained under purifying selection in CDS. In the 5′-UTR, these GC-rich sequences may influence translation initiation efficiency by forming higher-order secondary structures, including hairpins and G-quadruplexes [60]. In contrast, the microsatellite composition of the 3′-UTR more closely resembles that of non-coding regions, with AT-rich repeats being dominating. This suggests that the 3’-UTR may exhibit greater evolutionary plasticity and greater tolerance for microsatellite variations [61].

Functional enrichment of microsatellite-containing genes and the role of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs)

GO functional enrichment analysis of microsatellite-containing CDS regions indicates that these genes are preferentially associated with “binding” and “biological regulation” in felids. A similar enrichment of these same GO categories has been reported for CDS microsatellites in reptiles [8]. Likewise, “Transcription factors” has been identified as a prominently enriched KEGG pathway in both felids and reptiles, suggesting that this functional association is not lineage-specific but may reflect a more general feature of protein-coding microsatellites across distantly related vertebrates.

Further KEGG analysis in felids reveals significant enrichment in additional core regulatory pathways, including “Spliceosome,” “Transcription machinery,” and “Chromosome and associated proteins.” These pathways constitute the central framework of gene expression regulation and are critical for developmental processes, metabolic homeostasis, and environmental responsiveness [62, 63]. Collectively, these findings suggest that microsatellite-containing genes may play important roles in modulating regulatory networks in felids. Notably, the ZBTB and FOX transcription factor families harbor the highest numbers of microsatellite-containing genes. Both families are well documented for their roles in immune regulation and metabolic control [64, 65]. Enrichment within spliceosomal components (e.g., HNRNP and RBM families) and chromatin-associated proteins further indicates that microsatellites may contribute to the regulation of alternative splicing and epigenetic modification.

Structural analysis reveals that proteins encoded by microsatellite-containing genes are rich in IDRs [49, 66, 67]. IDRs are characterized by conformational flexibility, dynamic interaction capacity, and tolerance to sequence length variation. They frequently mediate protein–protein interactions and regulatory complex assembly and are central to transcriptional regulation, RNA processing, and chromatin organization [67, 68]. Given that microsatellites within coding regions often encode repetitive amino acid tracts, length polymorphisms may modulate IDR conformation and interaction affinity without disrupting overall protein architecture. Such tunable variation could enable fine-scale adjustment of regulatory functions while preserving essential protein activity. This mechanism provides a plausible framework by which microsatellite polymorphism contributes to phenotypic plasticity and potentially to environmental adaptation in felids. However, experimental validation will be required to substantiate this hypothesis.

Overall, microsatellite distribution patterns cannot be attributed to a single evolutionary force. Although replication slippage is the principal mutational mechanism underlying microsatellite generation [23], their genomic organization more accurately reflects a dynamic balance between mutational pressure and selective constraint. Our findings indicate that microsatellite landscapes in felids are shaped by the combined effects of slippage dynamics, sequence context, functional constraints, codon usage, DNA repair efficiency, and genomic location. The remarkable similarity of microsatellite distribution patterns across 13 feline species suggests that these regulatory dynamics are evolutionarily conserved within the Felidae family. This conservation likely reflects a broader mammalian strategy that balances functional stability with the maintenance of adaptive variability.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive genome-wide comparative analysis of microsatellite landscape patterns across 13 feline species. Our results demonstrate that both the conservation and divergence of microsatellite characteristics, encompassing abundance, motif composition, GC content, length distribution, and functional localization, are shaped by the interplay of mutational processes and selective constraints during evolution. Collectively, these findings deepen our understanding of microsatellite evolutionary dynamics and offer new insights into the genomic architecture and evolutionary history of the Felidae.
