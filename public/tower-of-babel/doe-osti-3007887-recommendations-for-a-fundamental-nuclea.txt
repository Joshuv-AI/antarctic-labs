 ABSTRACT

A fundamental nuclear material control (FNMC) plan, which is required for all fuel fabrication facilities
that are authorized to possess more than 1 effective kg of special nuclear material, describes how material
control and accounting (MC&A) requirements will be met to comply with US Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) regulations. Tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel fabrication facilities are likely to
have issues in meeting MC&A requirements because of the new processes and fuel types their work
involves. This report provides recommendations for an FNMC plan specifically for a TRISO fuel
fabrication facility under NRC regulations and includes a draft outline of an FNMC plan.

This report was produced for the Materials Protection, Accounting, and Control Technologies (MPACT)
program under the Nuclear Fuel Cycle Technologies programs within the US Department of Energy’s
Office of Nuclear Energy.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The domestic material control and accounting (MC&A) requirements of the US Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) can be found in Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations Part 70
(10 CFR Part 70). Because 10 CFR Part 70 deals with all aspects of special nuclear material (SNM), it
contains the requirements for licensing, inspection, safety, and security in addition to MC&A
requirements.

The purpose of the fundamental nuclear material control (FNMC) plan is to describe what the licensee
will do to control and account for SNM and how the licensee plans to achieve the requirements, which
will vary depending on the amount and type of SNM.

An FNMC plan, also known as an MC&A plan, is required for all fuel fabrication facilities that are
authorized to possess more than 1 effective kg of SNM. The plan must be submitted as part of a license
application and describes how MC&A requirements will be met. To achieve this goal, the plan describes
how a facility will track the location and quantity of all SNM, regulate those who have access to nuclear
materials, and regulate where nuclear materials are used. Although each FNMC plan addresses the same
MC&A elements, the measures described in an FNMC plan may differ from facility to facility. The
measurement systems and overall process and methods for complying with regulations and requirements
will be similar, but FNMC plans will have nuanced differences depending on the overall layout and
particularities of each licensee’s fuel fabrication process.

Facilities using nuclear material (e.g., power reactors) or engaged in nuclear waste disposal may not be
required to have the same level of detail in their plans for meeting all the requirements because they
normally deal with sealed SNM sources.1 The plan for a fuel fabrication facility must demonstrate
compliance with the requirements in 10 CFR Part 74.2 Because the FMNC plan describes what a facility
will do to and how it will maintain a program that can effectively track and verify the quantities and
locations of all SNM within the facility, it serves as the cornerstone of a facility’s efforts to maintain
compliance with NRC regulations.

 2. ELEMENTS OF AN FMNC PLAN

The FNMC plan ensures the control and accounting of SNM at a facility and must describe the methods
for achieving the performance objectives outlined in 10 CFR 74.31 (MC&A for SNM of low strategic
significance, or Category III) and 10 CFR 74.41 (MC&A for SNM of moderate strategic significance, or
Category II). The performance objectives for a tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel fabrication facility are
as follows:

 1. Maintain accurate, current, and reliable information on the quantities and locations of SNM.
 2. Conduct investigations of and resolve any anomalies indicating possible loss of SNM.
 3. Enable rapid determination of whether an actual loss of SNM has occurred. A significant quantity
 of SNM is defined as follows:
 i. More than 1 formula kg of strategic SNM; or
 ii. 10,000 g or more of 235U contained in uranium enriched up to 20.00%.
 4. Generate information to support in the investigation and recovery of missing SNM in the event of
 an actual loss.3

An FNMC plan provides a full description of the MC&A program. A facility’s FNMC plan will usually
consist of 13 chapters that describe how compliance with applicable requirements will be accomplished.
The following chapters are recommended for inclusion in an FNMC plan for a TRISO fuel fabrication
facility:

 1. Introduction – The introduction includes general information and a description of the MC&A
 program.

 2. Performance Objectives – The performance objectives chapter includes information on topics
 such as regulatory requirements and system and program capabilities.

 3. Management Structure – The management structure chapter includes information on topics such
 as the site and MC&A organizational structure, training and qualification requirements, and the
 material control boundary structure.

 4. Measurements – The measurements chapter includes information about measurement points,
 measurement systems, measurement uncertainties, measurement procedures, and scrap control.

 5. Measurement Control – The measurement control chapter includes information about the
 measurement control organization and management, calibrations, control standards and control
 limits, the replicate program, sampling, and measurement control performance.

 6. Statistics – The statistics chapter includes information on the determination of measurement
 uncertainties and the determination of inventory statistics.

 7. Physical Inventories – The physical inventories chapter includes information on topics such as
 inventory procedures and responsibilities, typical inventory composition, conducting physical
 inventories, inventory difference limits, and inventory reporting.

 8. Item Control – The item control chapter includes information on topics such as item descriptions,
 a description of typical item strata, item identity controls, storage controls, item monitoring
 methodology and procedures, and investigating and resolving item discrepancies.

 9. Shipper–Receiver Differences – The shipper–receiver differences (SRDs) chapter includes
 information on receipts, shipments, determining the receiver’s quantities, and evaluating and
 resolving SRDs.

 10. Independent Assessment and Review of the MC&A Program – This chapter includes information
 on the assessment schedule and team, the assessment report, and management review and
 response to findings and recommendations.

 11. Resolving Indications of Missing Uranium: Methods and Procedures for Identifying Loss
 Indicators – This chapter includes relevant methods, systems, and procedures for identifying,
 investigating, and resolving loss indicators as well as response actions for unresolved indicators,
 notifications, and documentation requirements.

 12. Informational Aid to Assist in Investigating and Recovering Missing Uranium – This chapter lists
 relevant information for investigating loss of SNM and resolving indicators of missing uranium.

 13. Recordkeeping – The recordkeeping chapter describes records to be kept, their storage and
 provides information on program controls for an accurate and reliable record system.

These 13 chapters are followed by references and appendices. The appendices in an FNMC plan may
include process flow diagrams and other supplemental information about the facility and more detailed
technical information on measurement systems and the overall MC&A program.

 3. CONSIDERATIONS FOR TRISO FUEL FABRICATION FACILITIES

To support the envisioned deployment of reactors that will use high-assay low-enriched uranium
(HALEU), new fuel fabrication facilities using higher levels of enrichment will be needed. Considering
the higher enrichment levels involved in HALEU fuel manufacturing, MC&A will not only continue to be
a fundamental requirement for facility operations but also is crucial for ensuring that no material is lost.

The current nuclear reactor operating fleet mostly uses low-enriched uranium with an enrichment level of
235U ranging between 2%–5%. New reactor designs currently under development call for the use of

HALEU fuel that has an enrichment ranging from 10%–20%. Facilities handling HALEU will need more
specific requirements for internal controls, measurements, and inventory controls. Because of the
moderate strategic significance related to the quantities and enrichment of the 235U at facilities handling
HALEU, these facilities will be subject to requirements for Category II material, beyond the requirements
for Category III material.4

Category III materials, or SNM of low strategic significance, include up to 15 g of 235U at greater than
20% enrichment, less than 10 kg of 235U at 10%–20% enrichment, or any amount of uranium at less than
10% enrichment. Category II materials, or SNM of moderate strategic significance, include any quantity
of uranium at up to 20% enrichment, but with a limit of 1 kg of 235U in higher than 20% enrichment.5

Fulfilling the requirements for a TRISO fuel fabrication facility requires having accurate information on
the quantities of uranium in all stages of nuclear material handling, from receipts and shipments to
internal transfers and inventories. In many cases, measurements are required to ensure accurate
accounting for all SNM received, processed, and shipped. At TRISO fuel fabrication facilities, the
requirements for measurements and measurement controls are more extensive than they are at existing
commercial fuel fabrication facilities and include tighter limits for uncertainty associated with SNM.
Although the use of control and monitoring measures to prevent or detect loss when it occurs or soon after
it occurs are similar or even the same for traditional and TRISO fuel fabrication facilities, the application
of MC&A in TRISO fuel fabrication facilities will require a variety of measurements at different points in
the fuel manufacturing process.

 4. FUTURE WORK

4.1 COMPLETE GENERIC FNMC PLAN FOR TRISO FUEL FABRICATION FACILITIES

Work done in FY25 included developing an outline of a generic TRISO fuel fabrication FNMC plan,
which is provided in Appendix A. In FY26, the team will complete a generic FNMC plan that will have
more detail on what information to include in each subsection and how the requirements may be

4.2 NONDESTRUCTIVE ASSAY MEASUREMENTS

As part of this project, the team is working with industry to better understand the fuel fabrication process
and where holdup measurements are necessary in these types of facilities. The team is working with
industry partners to explore, demonstrate, and recommend measurement methods, detector types, and
locations for holdup measurements. Given the new fuel forms and fabrication processes in development,
this type of measurement is an area that still needs to be explored.

In addition, some TRISO fuel fabrication facilities are using new processes to dissolve and dispose of
their waste, so exploring measurements and measurement systems for various fuel and waste forms in the
future would also be useful.

4.3 RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY MEASUREMENTS

Raman spectroscopy is a nondestructive chemical analysis technique that provides detailed information
about the chemical structure of nuclear fuels, polymorphs, and crystallinity. Raman spectroscopy is based
on the interaction of light with the chemical bonds within a material. Raman spectra can be used for
chemical and structural characterization, so this method can provide a chemical fingerprint of the TRISO
fuel measured. It is, therefore, used to detect different chemistries and structures whenever unknown
materials in the fuel need to be identified. This measurement method is widely applicable for quality
control, sample identification, material characterization, failure analysis, and even interrogating a nuclear
material’s physical and chemical properties.

The benefits of using Raman spectroscopy for nondestructive assay measurements include enabling in-
line measurements and eliminating the need for sample preparation. Raman spectroscopy is excellent for
identifying the specific uranyl(VI) species in uranium solutions because the vibrational frequencies of the
uranyl ion (UO2+2 ) shifts depend on the coordinating ions (e.g., hydroxide, acetate) and pH; these
characteristics enable the use of Raman spectroscopy for monitoring and identifying different uranyl
complexes in situ. Uranyl peak intensity is directly proportional to uranium concentration. However,
spectral features resulting from complex and dynamic solution conditions must be accounted for.
