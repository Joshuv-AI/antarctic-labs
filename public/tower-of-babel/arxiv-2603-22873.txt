A Lavrentiev phenomenon in the neo-Hookean model
Marco Barchiesi, Duvan Henao, Carlos Mora-Corral, Rémy Rodiac

Abstract
. We exhibit a Lavrentiev gap phenomenon for the neo-Hookean energy in three- dimensional nonlinear elasticity. More precisely, we construct boundary data for which the infimum of the neo-Hookean energy over deformations satisfying a natural regularity and invertibility condition is strictly larger than the infimum over the weak H 1 H^{1} -closure of that class. The mechanism underlying the gap is a deformation with a dipole-type singularity.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview of the problem and statement of the result

The neo-Hookean model is one of the simplest models in nonlinear elasticity. In this model,
a deformation
\bm{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
of an elastic body in a reference
configuration
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is assumed to minimise an energy of the form

(1.1)

E(\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}\left[|D\bm{u}|^{2}+H(|\det D\bm{u}|)\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where
H:(0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a suitable convex function such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H(t)}{t}=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}H(s)=\infty.

The main mathematical difficulty is to identify an admissible class for which the
minimisation of
E
is both physically meaningful and analytically tractable. Physical admissibility requires the deformation to preserve orientation and exclude interpenetration of matter, so two natural mathematical restrictions for the deformation are that
\bm{u}
is one-to-one almost everywhere (a.e.), and
\det D\bm{u}>0
a.e. As a boundary condition, we prescribe a bi-Lipschitz orientation-preserving map
\bm{b}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In fact, for technical convenience, we impose the Dirichlet condition in a strong form: we fix a smooth bounded
domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
compactly contained in
\Omega
and require that each admissible deformation agrees with

\bm{b}
on the entire complement
\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
, not merely on
\partial\Omega
.
This leads naturally to the admissible class

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega},\bm{b}):=\{\bm{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3})\colon\bm{u}=\bm{b}\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\Omega},\,\bm{u}\text{ is one-to-one a.e.},&\\
\det D\bm{u}>0\text{ a.e.},\text{ and }E(\bm{u})<\infty&\}.\end{split}

However, due to the possibility of cavitation, i.e., the formation of voids in the material, it was shown in
[
5
]
that
E
is not lower semicontinuous in
\mathcal{A}
. Since the difficulty is the possible appearance of many cavities, one can try to add a term to penalize the formation of cavities in the energy (
1.1
), or to add a condition in the minimisation space to exclude cavitation. This is the approach followed by Müller and Spector
[
30
]
, where they also introduced the nowadays well-known condition INV in order to rule out some pathological examples of cavitation. Condition INV, which is a stronger form of invertibility than the property of being one-to-one a.e., informally means that after the deformation, matter coming from any subregion
U
remains
enclosed by the image of
\partial U
and matter coming from outside
U
remains exterior to the region enclosed
by the image of
\partial U
. An alternative approach was devised in
[
25
,
26
]
where, instead of condition INV, the authors work with one-to-one a.e. maps but penalize the formation of cavities by using the “divergence identities”. These are a generalisation of the identity
\Det D\bm{u}=\det D\bm{u}
, which holds in absence of cavitation, where
\Det D\bm{u}:=\frac{1}{3}\dive(\cof D\bm{u}^{T}\bm{u})
denotes the distributional Jacobian. Both approaches are successful in treating energies slightly more coercive than (
1.1
), for example energies like (
1.1
) where
|D\bm{u}|^{2}
is replaced by
|D\bm{u}|^{p}
for some
p>2
, but fail to provide minimisers for (
1.1
). The difficulty is that, although condition INV and the divergence identities are well defined in the limiting case
p=2
(see
[
13
,
27
]
), they are not sequentially closed with respect to the weak
H^{1}
convergence. This was demonstrated by an example of Conti and De Lellis
[
13
, Theorem 6.1]
, see also
[
8
,
14
,
10
]
. This lack of compactness prevents the application of the direct method of the calculus of variations.

The goal of this paper
is to show that this lack of compactness is also related to a
Lavrentiev phenomenon
.
More precisely, we investigate the minimisation of (
1.1
) in the space

(1.3)

\mathcal{A}^{r}=\mathcal{A}^{r}(\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega},\bm{b}):=\{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}\colon\Omega_{\bm{b}}=\imG(\bm{u},\Omega)\text{ a.e. and }\bm{u}^{-1}\in W^{1,1}(\Omega_{\bm{b}},\mathbb{R}^{3})\},

where
\Omega_{\bm{b}}:=\bm{b}(\Omega)
and
\imG(\bm{u},\Omega)
is the geometric image defined in Definition
2.2
.
Here, the superscript
r
stands for
regular
. Indeed, by using
[
9
, Lemma 2.10]
and
[
6
, Lemma 5.1]
, one can show that maps in
\mathcal{A}^{r}
are regular in the sense that they satisfy condition INV and the divergence identities (and, hence, they do not open any cavity). Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega=B(\bm{0},4)
, and assume that
H
is convex and satisfies (
1.2
) and

(1.4)

H(t)\leq c\,t^{-\alpha}\quad\text{as }t\to 0^{+}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H(t)\leq c\,t^{\beta}\quad\text{as }t\to\infty,

for some
\alpha<\tfrac{1}{3}
,
\beta<\tfrac{3}{2}
, and
c>0
. Then, there exist a subdomain
\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\subset\Omega
and a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
\bm{b}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

(1.5)

\inf\{E(\bm{u})\colon\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}^{r}\}>\inf\{E(\bm{u})\colon\bm{u}\in\overline{\mathcal{A}^{r}}\},

where
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{r}}
is the closure of
\mathcal{A}^{r}
with respect to the weak convergence in
H^{1}
.

Several remarks are in order concerning this theorem. First, it is not known if the infimum on the right-hand side of (
1.5
) is attained. This is because we do not know if
\det D\bm{u}_{n}\rightharpoonup\det D\bm{u}
weakly in
L^{1}
whenever
(\bm{u}_{n})_{n}\subset\mathcal{A}^{r}
is a minimising sequence for
E
. Nevertheless, with the same proof used for Theorem
1.1
we can provide slightly different Lavrentiev phenomena where the infimum in
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{r}}
is attained; see Section
4
. Indeed, it was shown in
[
15
, Theorem 5.3]
and
[
28
, Theorem 4.3]
that the infimum of
E
in the sequential weak closure in
H^{1}
of homeomorphisms in
H^{1}
satisfying
\det D\bm{u}\geq 0
a.e., Lusin’s condition (N) and agreeing with
\bm{b}
on
\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
is attained. However, a slight modification of Theorem
1.1
(see Theorem
4.2
) shows that, under assumption (
1.4
), the minimisers they have obtained must have some singularity. Hence, the classical strategy of the calculus of variations, which consists of finding a minimiser of the energy in a larger space and then showing that minimisers are more regular, cannot work in the presence of a Lavrentiev phenomenon. This is an argument for using a relaxed energy instead of the original energy
E
; this strategy was developed in
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

Second, we notice that if the convex function
H
in (
1.1
) satisfies (
1.2
) and if there exists
c>0
such that

(1.6)

c^{-1}H(t)\leq H(2t)\leq c\,H(t),\quad H(t)\geq\frac{1}{t^{2}},\text{ for all }t\in(0,\infty),

then a weak
H^{1}
limit of orientation preserving homeomorphisms in
\mathcal{A}
does satisfy condition INV; see
[
14
]
.
This suggests that, under the growth condition (
1.6
) there is no Lavrentiev gap phenomenon for
E
as the one in Theorem
1.1
.

Third, it was recently shown in
[
11
]
that even less coercive energies than (
1.1
) admit minimisers in the sequential weak closure of some Sobolev, orientation preserving homeomorphisms. These energies are of the form
E(\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}\left[|D\bm{u}|^{p}+H(\det D\bm{u})\right]
with
1<p<2
. It would be interesting to know if a Lavrentiev gap phenomenon holds in that case.

Fourth, we observe that there is a strong analogy between the problem of minimising the neo-Hookean energy among regular maps and the problem of minimising the Dirichlet energy for maps
\bm{u}:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
among continuous maps. This analogy was already observed in
[
21
,
22
]
and shows that the lack of compactness of both problems is caused by maps with dipole-like singularities. From that point of view, our Theorem
1.1
can be seen as an analogue of
[
23
, Theorem B]
. We also refer to
[
24
]
and
[
29
]
for more on the Lavrentiev gap phenomenon for harmonic maps, in particular we can ask if Lavrentiev gap phenomena such as the one in Theorem
1.1
hold for many boundary data, since it is the case in the context of harmonic maps, as shown in
[
29
]
.
In the next section we present several Lavrentiev gap phenomena discovered in nonlinear elasticity.

1.2.
Lavrentiev gap phenomena in elasticity

In nonlinear elasticity, the Lavrentiev phenomenon was first observed by Ball
[
2
]
for genuinely cavitating deformations, i.e., with cavities that actually open a hole.
This is not the mechanism underlying Theorem
1.1
, in which a cavity is formed and then filled from material coming from another cavity, so that
\Omega_{\bm{b}}=\imG(\bm{u},\Omega)
a.e.
From the analytical point of view, this is reflected in the fact that a true cavitation map cannot be approximated within
\mathcal{A}^{r}

under a uniform bound on the neo-Hookean energy, because such a bound would force the Jacobians to be equi-integrable.
By contrast, the dipole singularity relevant here does admit an approximation by maps in
\mathcal{A}^{r}
with equibounded neo-Hookean energy.

The question whether the Lavrentiev phenomenon can occur under growth conditions on the stored-energy density that imply
continuity of all finite-energy deformations was raised by Ball and Mizel
[
4
]
(see also Ball
[
3
]
).
This question was answered affirmatively in two dimensions by Foss
[
18
]
and
Foss, Hrusa and Mizel
[
19
,
20
]
, who constructed examples on a disk sector with a smooth,
polyconvex, frame-indifferent energy density
W
satisfying
W(F)\geq c\,|F|^{p}-C
for some
p>2
and

W(F)\to\infty
as
\det F\to 0^{+}
.
In those examples, the Lavrentiev gap is driven by the local behaviour of almost minimisers near the
tip of the sector, interacting with prescribed boundary conditions.

More recently, Almi, Krömer and Molchanova
[
1
]
produced Lavrentiev gaps in
dimensions
d=2
and
d=3
for a neo-Hookean-type density
W(F)=|F|^{p}+\gamma(\det F)^{-q}
with
p>d

and
1<q<p/(p-2)
.
Their admissible class consists of orientation-preserving
W^{1,p}
-maps satisfying the
Ciarlet–Nečas condition
[
12
]
, which (in the regime
p>d
, hence for continuous maps)
implies almost everywhere injectivity.
The gap in
[
1
]
separates
W^{1,p}
-deformations from
W^{1,\infty}
-deformations.
It is generated by a global self-contact mechanism: for a suitable choice of boundary conditions on a
disconnected domain, one can compress two cross-sections onto the same point (or line), creating
self-contact while keeping the energy arbitrarily small; by contrast, Lipschitz competitors satisfying
the Ciarlet–Nečas condition are necessarily injective everywhere (as a consequence of
Reshetnyak’s theorem for mappings of finite distortion) and must therefore “go around” the obstruction,
incurring a strictly positive energy cost.

The present paper exhibits a Lavrentiev phenomenon of a fundamentally different nature, distinguished
from the existing literature in two complementary respects:

(i) Natural coercivity exponent and physical energy.

We work in three dimensions with the standard neo-Hookean functional

E(\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(|D\bm{u}|^{2}+H(|\det D\bm{u}|)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

The coercivity exponent here is
p=2<d=3
, namely the physically natural regime in which Sobolev embedding does not yield continuity of finite-energy deformations.
By contrast, the examples in
[
1
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
are constructed in the continuity regime
p>d
.

(ii) Gap driven by inverse regularity and lack of weak closure.

In our setting, the energy gap does not arise from comparing two regularity levels of the forward
deformation
\bm{u}
within a fixed notion of injectivity.
Rather, it stems from the failure of the physically motivated class
\mathcal{A}^{r}
to be
sequentially closed under weak
H^{1}
-convergence.
Accordingly, the gap separates
\mathcal{A}^{r}
from its weak closure
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{r}}
and is
governed by the regularity of the
inverse
deformation (Sobolev versus
BV
), rather than an
additional regularity of
\bm{u}
itself.

This should be contrasted with
[
1
]
. There, the admissibility condition is stable under weak convergence, but the class remains broad enough to permit low-energy deep self-contact.
Here, by contrast, the admissible class rules out interpenetration but loses weak compactness.
Thus
[
1
]
points to an admissible class that is too large, whereas our example points to one that is too small.

1.3.
Strategy of the proof and organisation of the paper

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on results that appeared in the series of papers
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
and on the use of the Conti–De Lellis dipole map and its regularised sequence. More precisely, we have shown in
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
that any sequence of regular maps approximating the Conti–De Lellis map produces a jump of energy of at least
2\pi
in the limit. By using this observation and by constructing a suitable interpolation between the Conti–De Lellis map and a regularised sequence we can construct a boundary data for which we obtain the desired gap phenomenon.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2
recalls some results from
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
and the basic properties of the Conti–De Lellis map. These ingredients are used in Section
3
to prove our main result, Theorem
1.1
. Section
4
presents two variants in which the infimum over the
larger class is attained. The main technical ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the construction of a boundary data obtained from the Conti–De Lellis map and its regularised sequence. That construction is deferred to Appendix
C
, after Appendices
A
and
B
where the precise definitions of the Conti–De Lellis map and its regularised sequences are given.

2.
The relaxed energy and the Conti–De Lellis map

2.1.
The relaxed energy

In
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
the authors have proposed an approach to the minimisation problem for
E

based on relaxation, by providing a larger space
\mathcal{B}\supset\mathcal{A}^{r}
that is
compact
for sequences with
equibounded energy. In order to define this space we first define the
geometric image
of a map
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}
. We recall that maps in
H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3})
are approximately differentiable a.e.; see, e.g.,
[
16
, Theorem 6.4]
.
We denote the approximate gradient by
\nabla\bm{u}
and the distributional derivative by
D\bm{u}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\bm{u}
be approximately differentiable a.e. and such that
\det D\bm{u}\neq 0
a.e. We define
\Omega_{0}
as the set of
\bm{x}\in\Omega
for which the following are satisfied:

(1)

the approximate differential of
\bm{u}
at
\bm{x}
exists and equals
D\bm{u}(\bm{x})
,

(2)

there exist
\bm{w}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})
and a compact set
K\subset\Omega
of density
1
at
\bm{x}
such that
\bm{u}|_{K}=\bm{w}|_{K}
and
\nabla\bm{u}|_{K}=D\bm{w}|_{K}
,

(3)

\det\nabla\bm{u}(\bm{x})\neq 0
.

From
[
17
, Theorem 3.1.8]
,
Rademacher’s Theorem and Whitney’s Theorem we infer that
\Omega_{0}
is a set of full Lebesgue measure in
\Omega
, i.e.,
|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|=0
.

Definition 2.2
.

For any measurable set
A
of
\Omega
, the geometric image of
A
under an a.e. approximately differentiable map
\bm{u}
is defined by

\imG(\bm{u},A):=\bm{u}(A\cap\Omega_{0}),

with
\Omega_{0}
as in Definition
2.1
.

The notion of geometric image is used in the definition of the space

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}:=\{\bm{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3})\colon&\bm{u}=\bm{b}\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\Omega},\,\bm{u}\text{ is one-to-one a.e.},\,\det D\bm{u}\neq 0\text{ a.e.},\\
&\Omega_{\bm{b}}=\imG(\bm{u},\Omega)\text{ a.e.},\,\bm{u}^{-1}\in BV(\Omega_{\bm{b}},\mathbb{R}^{3}),\text{ and }E(\bm{u})<\infty\}.\end{split}

On this set
\mathcal{B}
it is possible to define a
lower semicontinuous
energy
F
extending
E
, namely

F(\bm{u}):=E(\bm{u})+2\|D^{s}\bm{u}^{-1}\|\text{ for }\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}.

Here
D^{s}\bm{u}^{-1}
is the singular part of the distributional gradient of the inverse (which is a matrix-valued Radon measure),
and
\|D^{s}\bm{u}^{-1}\|
is its norm.
Then, by using the direct method of the calculus of variations, one can obtain that the energy
F
admits
a minimiser
\bm{u}
on
\mathcal{B}
. In this way the existence problem of a minimiser for
E
is reduced to showing
that
\bm{u}
belongs to
\mathcal{A}^{r}
. This is summarised in the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
9
, Theorem 1.1]

Let
\{\bm{u}_{j}\}_{j}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{B}
such that
\{F(\bm{u}_{j})\}_{j}
is equibounded.
Then there exists
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}
such that, up to a subsequence,

\bm{u}_{j}\rightharpoonup\bm{u}
in
H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3})
and

\liminf_{j\to\infty}F(\bm{u}_{j})\geq F(\bm{u}).

In particular, the energy
F
has a minimiser in
\mathcal{B}
.

2.2.
The Conti–De Lellis map

Since the singular map
\bm{v}
of Conti–De Lellis plays a central role in our construction,
we briefly describe it and provide pictures here; for the details we refer the reader to Appendix
A
.
The Conti–De Lellis map is an axisymmetric map defined in
B(\bm{0},4)
with boundary condition equal to the identity.
We remark that
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{B}
and
\det D\bm{v}>0
a.e., but

\bm{v}\notin\mathcal{A}^{r}
.
Indeed, the third component of the inverse is not Sobolev, but it belongs to the class
SBV

(special functions of bounded variation).
The key feature of
\bm{v}
is that it brings into contact two portions of the body that were initially
at unit distance apart, namely, the half-balls

a:=\{\bm{x}:\,x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}<1,\,x_{3}<0\}\quad\text{and}\quad e:=\{\bm{x}:\,x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+(x_{3}-1)^{2}<1,\,x_{3}>1\}

(see Figure
1
)
are put in contact with each other across the “bubble”

\Gamma:=\{(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):\,y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+(y_{3}-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}=(\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\},

which in turn comes entirely from only two singular points: the origin
\bm{0}
and
\bm{0}^{\prime}=(0,0,1)
.
At
\bm{0}^{\prime}
a cavity is nucleated and subsequently filled by material originating from the half-ball
a
,
which passes through the origin.
We refer to this structure as a
dipole
.
The jump set of the third component of the inverse coincides with the sphere
\Gamma
, and the amplitude of
the jump is given by the distance between the poles
\bm{0}
and
\bm{0}^{\prime}
.
Consequently
F(\bm{v})=E(\bm{v})+2\pi
.

The rough idea of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to use
\bm{v}
as a boundary data to force approximate minimisers of the energy
E
to have the same singularities as
\bm{v}
. However, the map
\bm{v}
is not one-to-one: the segment
S:=\{(0,0,x_{3})\colon x_{3}\in(0,1)\}
,
the punctured disks
D:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},0)\colon 0<x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\leq 1\}
and
D^{\prime}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},1)\colon 0<x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\leq 1\}
,
and the semisphere
\{\bm{x}:\,x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=1,\,x_{3}<0\}

are contracted onto the origin. Nevertheless, outside the region

(2.1)

\displaystyle U:=\{\bm{x}:\,x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}\leq 1,\,x_{3}\leq 0\}\cup S\cup D^{\prime}

it is regular and it can be “patched”. This means that we can construct a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism from
B(\bm{0},4)
that agrees with
\bm{v}
outside a small neighbourhood of
U
. This construction is the main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We now proceed to the proof of Theorem
1.1
and start by a result containing the patching process alluded to in the previous section, where
\bm{v}
is modified in the neighbourhood

U_{\delta}:=\{\bm{x}\in B(\bm{0},4)\colon\dist(\bm{x},U)<\delta\},\qquad\delta\in(0,1]

of
U
(see Figure
3
, in blue).

Theorem 3.1
.

For each
\delta\in(0,1]
there exists a bi-Lipschitz orientation-preserving homeomorphism
\bm{b}_{\delta}:B(\bm{0},4)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

such that
\bm{b}_{\delta}=\bm{v}
on
B(\bm{0},4)\setminus U_{\delta}
.
Moreover, if
H
is convex and satisfies (
1.2
) and (
1.4
)
for some
\alpha<\tfrac{1}{3}
,
\beta<\tfrac{3}{2}
, and
c>0
,
then

\{E(\bm{b}_{\delta})\}_{\delta>0}
is equibounded.

Because the proof is technical,
we defer it to Appendix
C

Proof of Theorem

Given the complexity of the proof, we first outline our overall strategy.

(i)

Starting point:
The Conti–De Lellis dipole

is a singular map

Outside any neighborhood

By the construction in
[

with uniformly bounded energy, i.e.,

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}E(\bm{u}_{\varepsilon})<\infty

(ii)

Definition of
\bm{b}_{\delta}
:

For each
\delta\in(0,1]

\varepsilon>0

sufficiently small so that
\delta\geq c_{0}\varepsilon^{\gamma}

large enough.
The scale

(C.1)

\bm{b}_{\delta}(\bm{x}):=\begin{cases}\bm{u}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})&\text{if }\dist(\bm{x},U)\leq\delta/2,\\[4.0pt]

The key point is that the interpolation introduces gradient perturbations of order
\varepsilon^{\gamma}/\delta

which remain controlled thanks to the scaling

e

|D\bm{u}-D\bm{v}|\leq c\,\varepsilon^{\gamma}
.

Step 2: uniform bound on the energy and orientation-preserving character
.

We have to show that the energy contribution coming from the transition layer is bounded independently of
\delta
.

Energy region
b
.

In
b\cap T_{\delta}
we write

\bm{x}=\rho\sin\varphi\,\bm{e}_{r}(\theta)+\rho\cos\varphi\,\bm{e}_{3},\qquad\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\varphi\leq\pi,\qquad s:=\rho-1=\dist(\bm{x},U)\in\Big[\frac{\delta}{2},\delta\Big].

Recall that in
b

\bm{b}_{\delta}(\bm{x})=\Big(s+\chi_{\delta}(s)\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon^{\gamma}\Big)\,\bm{d}(\varphi,\theta)+\chi_{\delta}(s)\,\varepsilon^{\gamma}\,\bm{e}_{3},\qquad\bm{d}(\varphi,\theta):=\sin\Phi(\varphi)\,\bm{e}_{r}(\theta)+\cos\Phi(\varphi)\,\bm{e}_{3},

with
\Phi(\varphi):=\frac{\varphi+\pi}{2}
.
Set

r_{\delta}(s):=s+\chi_{\delta}(s)\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon^{\gamma},\qquad z_{\delta}(s):=\chi_{\delta}(s)\varepsilon^{\gamma},\qquad\rho=1+s.

Since
\chi_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)=-2/\delta
on
[\delta/2,\delta]
and
\delta\geq c_{0}\varepsilon^{\gamma}
(see (
C.2
)),
we have

|r_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)|\leq 1+c\,\frac{\varepsilon^{\gamma}}{\delta}\leq c,\qquad|z_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)|\leq c\,\frac{\varepsilon^{\gamma}}{\delta}\leq c.

Moreover,

\partial_{s}\bm{b}_{\delta}=r_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)\,\bm{d}+z_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)\,\bm{e}_{3},\qquad\partial_{\varphi}\bm{b}_{\delta}=\frac{1}{2}\,r_{\delta}(s)\big(\cos\Phi\,\bm{e}_{r}(\theta)-\sin\Phi\,\bm{e}_{3}\big),\qquad\partial_{\theta}\bm{b}_{\delta}=r_{\delta}(s)\sin\Phi\,\partial_{\theta}\bm{e}_{r}(\theta).

Using the metric factors of spherical coordinates and
\rho=1+s\simeq 1
on
T_{\delta}
,
and using that, for
\frac{\pi}{4}\leq\varphi\leq\pi
,

(C.4)

\frac{\sin^{2}\Phi}{\sin^{2}\varphi}=\frac{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)}{\sin^{2}\varphi}=\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)}\leq 2,

we find that

|D\bm{b}_{\delta}(\bm{x})|^{2}\leq c\,\Big(|\partial_{s}\bm{b}_{\delta}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}|\partial_{\varphi}\bm{b}_{\delta}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}|\partial_{\theta}\bm{b}_{\delta}|^{2}\Big)\leq c\,\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2\gamma}}{\delta^{2}}\Big),

and therefore

\int_{T_{\delta}\cap b}|D\bm{b}_{\delta}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\leq c\,\Big(\delta+\frac{\varepsilon^{2\gamma}}{\delta}\Big)\leq c.

For the determinant, in the coordinates
(s,\varphi,\theta)
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Appendix B.3]

\displaystyle\det D\bm{b}_{\delta}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\det(\partial_{s}\bm{b}_{\delta},\partial_{\varphi}\bm{b}_{\delta},\partial_{\theta}\bm{b}_{\delta})}{\rho^{2}\sin\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{r_{\delta}(s)^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\,\frac{\sin\Phi(\varphi)}{\sin\varphi}\Big(r_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)+z_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)\cos\Phi(\varphi)\Big)

(C.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{r_{\delta}(s)^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\,\frac{\sin\Phi(\varphi)}{\sin\varphi}\Big(1+\varepsilon^{\gamma}\chi_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)\big(\sqrt{2}+\cos\Phi(\varphi)\big)\Big).

Since
\frac{\sin\Phi(\varphi)}{\sin\varphi}=\frac{1}{2\sin\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)}
(see (
C.4
)) is bounded above and below on
[\pi/2,\pi]
,
choosing

) large enough yields

1+\varepsilon^{\gamma}\chi_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)\big(\sqrt{2}+\cos\Phi(\varphi)\big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{for }s\in[\delta/2,\delta].

We infer from (

\det D\bm{b}_{\delta}(\bm{x})\geq c\,s^{2}

T_{\delta}\cap b
.
Hence,

\int_{T_{\delta}\cap b}H(\det D\bm{b}_{\delta})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\leq c\,\int_{\delta/2}^{\delta}s^{-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq c,

independently of
\delta
.

Energy region
d
.

Recall that in
d\cap T_{\delta}
by (
C.2
) we have
r\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\geq 2\sqrt{2}\varepsilon^{\gamma}

By the formula for the gradient in

(C.6)

Let us first study the tangential stretch

\displaystyle w_{r}(\bm{x})=\frac{\delta-s(r,x_{3})}{\delta/2}\omega_{\varepsilon}\big(z(r,x_{3})\big)+s(r,x_{3})\sin\varphi(z(r,x_{3}))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad s\sin\varphi\leq w_{r}(\bm{x})\leq\frac{\delta/2}{\delta/2}\cdot 6\varepsilon^{\gamma}+s\sin\varphi

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\frac{s\sin\varphi}{r}\leq\frac{w_{r}(\bm{x})}{r}\leq 2+\frac{s}{r}\sin\varphi.

The lower bound for

with
\det D\bm{v}

By construction of
\bm{b}_{\delta}

.

Regarding the

\frac{\partial(w_{r},w_{3})}{\partial(r,x_{3})}=\frac{\partial(w_{r},w_{3})}{\partial(s,z)}\frac{\partial(s,z)}{\partial(r,x_{3})}.

Note also that

c^{-1}\frac{\partial(w_{r},w_{3})}{\partial(s,z)}\leq\frac{\partial(w_{r},w_{3})}{\partial(r,x_{3})}\leq c\,\frac{\partial(w_{r},w_{3})}{\partial(s,z)}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(w_{r},w_{3})}{\partial(s,z)}

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}-\tfrac{2}{\delta}\omega_{\varepsilon}(z)+\sin\varphi&2\frac{\delta-s}{\delta}\omega_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(z)+\tfrac{\pi}{12}s\cos\varphi\\
-\cos\varphi&\frac{\pi}{12}s\sin\varphi\end{vmatrix}

(C.8)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\pi}{12}s-\tfrac{2}{\delta}\omega_{\varepsilon}(z)\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{12}s\sin\varphi+\underbrace{\cos\varphi\cdot 2\tfrac{\delta-s}{\delta}\omega_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(z)}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\geq\tfrac{\pi}{12}s\Bigg(1-2\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(z)}{\delta}\sin\varphi\Bigg)\underset{\text{\eqref{eq:boundOmegaEps}}}{\geq}\tfrac{\pi}{12}s\Bigg(1-2\frac{6\varepsilon^{\gamma}}{\delta}\sin\varphi\Bigg)\underset{\text{\eqref{eq:delta-eps}}}{\geq}c\,s.

Since

Similarly, from
(
B.8
),
(
C.6
),
(

and
(
C.8
),
we obtain the upper bound

Therefore,
\int_{T_{\delta}\cap d}H(\det D\bm{b}_{\delta})\leq c
independently of
\delta

(compare with (
A.6
)).
After a change of coordinates from
(r,x_{3})
to
(s,z)
,
using (
B.7
)
and (
B.8
),
each term on
D\bm{b}_{\delta}
other than the tangential stretch
\tfrac{w_{r}}{r}

can be controlled.

Energy region
e
.

In this region the map
\bm{v}
degenerates at the boundary
\varphi=\pi/2
.
We use spherical coordinates. The radial component is given by

b_{\rho}=\chi_{\delta}u_{\rho}+(1-\chi_{\delta})v_{\rho}
.
Since the angular components coincide (
b_{\varphi}=v_{\varphi}=u_{\varphi}=\varphi
), the Jacobian
3
3

3

See, e.g.,
[
8
, Appendix B.3]
is

\det D\bm{b}_{\delta}=\frac{(b_{\rho})^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{\rho}b_{\rho}.

We must check that
\partial_{\rho}b_{\rho}>0
. Differentiating the interpolation gives

\partial_{\rho}b_{\rho}=\chi_{\delta}\partial_{\rho}u_{\rho}+(1-\chi_{\delta})\partial_{\rho}v_{\rho}+\chi^{\prime}_{\delta}(u_{\rho}-v_{\rho})\partial_{\rho}\dist(\bm{x},U).

In this region,
\dist(\bm{x},U)=\rho\cos\varphi
, so that
\partial_{\rho}\dist(\bm{x},U)=\cos\varphi

Specifically, we have the following estimates:

(i)

For the convex combination term: since
\partial_{\rho}u_{\rho}\geq\cos\varphi

(see
[
8
, p. 39, Eq. (3.33)]
) and
\partial_{\rho}v_{\rho}=\cos\varphi
, we have

\chi_{\delta}\partial_{\rho}u_{\rho}+(1-\chi_{\delta})\partial_{\rho}v_{\rho}\geq\cos\varphi.

(ii)

For the perturbation term: we have
\chi^{\prime}_{\delta}=-2/\delta
and
|u_{\rho}-v_{\rho}|\leq 7\varepsilon^{\gamma}
.
Indeed,
writing

\displaystyle u_{\rho}(\rho,\varphi)-v_{\rho}(\rho,\varphi)=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\varepsilon}(u_{\rho}(\varepsilon,\varphi)-\cos\varphi)+\frac{\rho-\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot 6\varepsilon^{\gamma}-\frac{\varepsilon(1-\rho)}{1-\varepsilon}\cos\varphi

and considering that

\cos\varphi\leq u_{\rho}(\varepsilon,\varphi)\leq\cos\varphi+6\varepsilon^{\gamma}
(see
[
8

),
it is possible to see that

\displaystyle u_{\rho}(\rho,\varphi)-v_{\rho}(\rho,\varphi)\geq\frac{-\varepsilon(1-\rho)}{1-\varepsilon}\cos\varphi\geq-2\varepsilon\cos\varphi.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle u_{\rho}-v_{\rho}\leq\frac{1-\rho}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot 6\varepsilon^{\gamma}+\frac{\rho-\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot 6\varepsilon^{\gamma}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\varepsilon^{\gamma}\cdot 1\leq 7\varepsilon^{\gamma}.

Consequently,

\chi^{\prime}_{\delta}(u_{\rho}-v_{\rho})\partial_{\rho}\dist(\bm{x},U)\geq-\frac{2}{\delta}(7\varepsilon^{\gamma})\cos\varphi=-\frac{14\varepsilon^{\gamma}}{\delta}\cos\varphi.

Combining these, we factor out the term
\cos\varphi

\partial_{\rho}b_{\rho}\geq\cos\varphi\left(1-\frac{14\varepsilon^{\gamma}}{\delta}\right).

Since
\cos\varphi>0
for

up to a strictly positive multiplicative constant,

in (
C.2
).
In addition, the lower bound on

established above gives

\displaystyle b_{\rho}=\chi_{\delta}u_{\rho}+(1-\chi_{\delta})v_{\rho}=v_{\rho}+\chi_{\delta}(u_{\rho}-v_{\rho})\geq v_{\rho}-2\varepsilon\cos\varphi=(1+\rho-2\varepsilon)\cos\varphi.

Since

\varepsilon

b_{\rho}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}(1+\rho)^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi=\frac{1}{4}\,v_{\rho}^{2}

Hence

We now bound the

Since

Therefore, from the integrability of

\int_{T_{\delta}\cap e}H(\det D\bm{b}_{\delta})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\leq c

independently of
\delta

Energy region

In

\bm{b}_{\delta}=(1-\chi)\bm{v}+\chi\bm{u},\quad\chi(\bm{x}):=\frac{\delta-\dist(\bm{x},U)}{\delta/2}\in[0,1],\quad|\nabla\chi|=\frac{2}{\delta}.

Since

and, by the explicit formulas in Appendices

Since the dipole

For the determinant, note that

For the

) gives

.
In particular,

independently of

.

We prove that the map
\bm{b}_{\delta}
constructed in Step 1 is bi-Lipschitz on
B(\bm{0},4)
.
Recall that
\bm{b}_{\delta}=\bm{u}
on
U_{\delta/2}
and
\bm{b}_{\delta}=\bm{v}
on
B(\bm{0},4)\setminus U_{\delta}
.
Moreover,
\bm{b}_{\delta}
is continuous across all interfaces by construction.

(i)
\bm{b}_{\delta}

The domain
B(\bm{0},4)
is partitioned into the following subdomains: the core
U_{\delta/2}
, the exterior

, and the four pieces

,

,

On each of these pieces,
\bm{b}_{\delta}

We apply the elementary estimate

|\bm{A}^{-1}|\leq\frac{c\,|\bm{A}|^{2}}{\det\bm{A}},\qquad\text{for any }\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\text{ with }\det\bm{A}>0

(iii) Injectivity and Lipschitz continuity of the inverse.

We next argue that
\bm{b}_{\delta}

On
