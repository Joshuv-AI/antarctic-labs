Leveraging Community Voice to Address Environmental Resilience: A Case Study from the US Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor
Authors: Llewellyn J. Cornelius, Hee Yun Lee, Helen R. Ladson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined environmental justice concerns and community-led resilience strategies in historically marginalized communities within the Gullah Geechee Heritage Corridor following the 2023 Hercules-Pinova plant fire in Brunswick, Georgia. The incident, which released hazardous chemicals, prompted civic reflection and mobilization among residents of Glynn and McIntosh Counties. A mixed-methods design grounded in the Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) framework was employed. Quantitative data were collected through a survey completed by 177 residents; qualitative data were derived from six semi-structured interviews conducted by community members who were trained by university researchers. The survey assessed health impacts, community priorities, and civic concerns, while interviews provided deeper context regarding environmental challenges and locally-driven solutions. Survey results revealed that 86.5% of respondents experienced health symptoms following the explosion, and 60.9% expressed concern about potential long-term effects. Environmental issues, including water and air pollution, were the most frequently cited concerns (66.1%), followed by civic engagement (48%), public safety (34.5%), and youth development (32.8%). Interview analysis revealed key themes including poor water quality, air pollution, legislative disempowerment, and the need for community-driven strategies such as digital outreach. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating community voices into environmental health research. The study offers a model for academic-community partnerships and contributes to scholarship in the field of environmental social work.

Introduction

This study is situated within the Georgia segment of the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor, which includes the city of Brunswick – a community marked by its rich cultural heritage and ongoing racial and environmental injustices. Brunswick gained national attention in 2000 following the racially motivated murder of Ahmaud Arbery, a 25-year-old Black man. Against this backdrop, this Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) study examined the concerns of community residents following a chemical explosion at the Hercules-Pinova plant in Brunswick, Georgia. Drawing from six in-depth interviews and 177 surveys, the study examines the impact of the explosion on residents living in Glynn and McIntosh counties. Survey findings indicate that a large majority of the survey respondents (86.5%) experienced some health issues associated with the 2023 explosion. Participants (66.1%) identified exposure to water and air pollution as primary concerns. Qualitative interview data highlighted the importance of community involvement in addressing on-going social and environmental justice impacts. This study highlights the importance of participatory approaches to mitigating water and air pollution, and promoting the well-being of affected residents.

Background

The Gullah Geechee people reside within what is formally designated by the United States (US) government as the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor (National Heritage Areas Act, 2006; see Figure 1). The region honors the enduring cultural traditions established by enslaved Africans brought to the US from the Sierra Leone-Liberian coastlines, as well as the Kongo-Angola region of Central Africa (Gerald, 2005). The corridor historically extended from Wilmington, North Carolina, to Jacksonville, Florida, covering the Atlantic barrier islands and stretching up to 35 miles inland. It includes key cities such as Jacksonville, Florida; Brunswick and Savanna, Georgia; Charleston, Hilton Head, Myrtle Beach, and Beaufort, South Carolina; and Wilmington, North Carolina. The Corridor was formally created in 1865 via Special Field Order 15 at the end of the Civil War. Relative geographic isolation within these Sea Island communities contributed to the “survival of rich African traditions of folklore, religion, arts and music, land use, food ways, architecture, and health practices” (Correa & Graves, 2015, p. 1).

Although the Gullah Geechee communities remained largely intact following the Civil War, the 20th Century ushered in significant economic and social changes. During this period, large portions of land were purchased by outside investors seeking to develop coastal property, often displacing the traditional residents of the corridor (Sanjak & Donovan, 2016). The 20th century also brought intensified land dispossession and ethnic tourism, both of which have lasting impacts on the region. In particular, the 1920s and 1930s marked the first significant wave of acquisition within the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor, as outside investors purchased defunct rice plantations from bankrupt owners, often using legal loopholes to acquire land from residents. Homes in the corridor that were collectively owned by families – often without formal documents of land ownership – increasing their vulnerability to dispossession (Green, 2013). Today, residents of the corridor continue to face longstanding structural challenges related to land dispossession while also contending with escalating environmental threats, including air and water pollution and the growing impacts of climate change.

This case study examines the perceived impact of the 2023 chemical explosion and fire at the Hercules-Pinova plant on residents living in two counties within the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor: Glynn County, Georgia, located approximately 35 miles north of the Florida state line along the Atlantic Coast, and McIntosh County, the neighboring county to the north. The Hercules-Pinova plant began operations in 1912 in Brunswick, GA (Glynn County), producing rosin and turpentine (Science History Institute, n.d.). However, the plant also released benzene and other toxic chemicals into the groundwater, contaminating nearby properties and rendering the water undrinkable and increasing residents’ risk of cancer (Hercules, n.d.). Beginning in the 1980s, Hercules engaged in a series of environmental remediation activities spanning several decades (Hercules, n.d.). Since 2018, the Georgia Environmental Protection Division initiated four formal enforcement actions against the facility for Clean Air Act violations (Landers, 2023). On April 15, 2023, a significant fire at the plant prompted city officials to issue a shelter-in-place order for the Brunswick community. At the height of the blaze, cellphone footage captured a large plume of black smoke rising into the skyline, underscoring the severity of the fire (Tanyos, 2023).

The chemical fire was the second incident at a chemical plant in the area within a year (Siqueira, 2023). Industrial runoff and emissions from the now-closed Hercules-Pinova plant are believed to have affected the residents living in nearby communities. The region also has experienced saltwater contamination due to declining groundwater levels, as documented by the U.S. Geological Survey (Priest, 2004). The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, 2013/2017) has highlighted significant health risks associated with contaminated drinking water in Glynn and McIntosh Counties, particularly for children, who are more vulnerable to gastrointestinal illnesses, developmental disorders, and cancer due to their higher water intake relative to body weight (EPA, 2013/2017). Residents of Glynn and McIntosh Counties experience poorer overall health outcomes compared to the national averages (U.S. News and World Report, 2024a, 2024b). At the same time, access to healthcare providers is limited. The state average of primary care doctors is 1.26 per 1,000 persons. McIntosh County has reported only 0.27 primary care doctors per 1,000 residents, while Glynn County has 1.04 per 1,000 residents (US News and World Report, 2024a). Repeated exposure to environmental hazards in this community led to what Davis et al. (2024) called “resiliency fatigue” – physical and emotional exhaustion – as well as a persistent fear of future hazards.

This unique history and accumulation of factors contributed to a community mobilization process that examined and addressed long-term environmental health problems. This study has two aims: (1) to explore the perceived impacts of the 2023 Hercules-Pinova plant fire among residents of Glynn and McIntosh Counties in the Gullah Geechee Heritage Corridor; and (2) examine locally driven solutions and strategies for advancing environmental health and community education to strengthen collective action in historically marginalized coastal communities.

This study builds upon more than four decades of environmental justice research and advocacy that emerged following the 1979 Bean v. Southwestern Waste Management Corp. lawsuit, which challenged the siting of a waste facility in a marginalized community in Houston, Texas, as documented by environmental justice pioneer Robert. D. Bullard (Bullard, 1990, 2021). Over several decades, Bullard has demonstrated that poor people and persons of color are disproportionately exposed to environmental hazards, including toxic waste facilities, landfills, air pollution, and pesticide exposures with close to half of the population of color living near one of the 1,857 Superfund sites in the U.S (Bullard, 2021). Other research in this area has documented increased cancer risks associated with pesticide exposure (Bassil et al., 2007) and air pollution (Wang et al., 2025) as well as elevated risks of illnesses resulting from exposure to waste and other water pollutants (Mengistie et al., 2025; Sukhram et al., 2025).

Scholarship on environmental justice and community practice has also documented the evolution of an expansive framing of environmental justice that encompasses both the natural environment and socio-ecological factors. For example, in a concept analysis of articles focused on environmental social work, Ramsay and Boddy (2017) emphasize the importance of reverence for the natural environment and highlight the interconnected relationships among humans, non-human species and ecological systems. They underscore the importance of embracing cultural traditions from across the planet that collectively protect the environment. Similarly, in their analysis of environmental social work articles published between 1991 and 2025, Krings et al. (2020) found an emphasis on topics like the natural environment, food insecurity, climate change, conservation, and sustainability. Ongoing environmental justice organizing includes advocacy among social workers (Abu-Ras et al., 2026; Jenkins et al., 2026) and community stakeholders (Bullard, 2021).

This study draws from two environmental resilience frameworks to examine the issues of environmental justice: ecological research and community-led resilience (social-ecological resilience). As noted by Allen et al. (2016), ecological resilience refers to the capacity of humans and natural systems to withstand environmental pressures. This ecological resilience is typically measured by indicators related to extreme weather or natural disasters. In contrast, the community-led resilience framework aligns with the collective survival strategies framework articulated by Bell et al. (2019), which builds on the strengths of oppressed communities while emphasizing power, culture, and historical context.

Methods

Research design

This project stems from a sustained engagement between the University of Georgia School of Social Work, Center for Social Justice, Human and Civil Rights, and Heritage Works (led by Helen Ladson). The project, which began in 2017, was based on the Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) model (Israel et al., 2010) where community stakeholders (facilitated by our community partner, Heritage Works) and the University collaborated to prioritize work that would benefit the community. The community stakeholders involved in this effort included seniors from the local high school Human Ecology Club, county-level elected officials, ministers, administrative staff from the local libraries, and community advocates and leaders of local nonprofit organizations and residents. The CBPR implementation effort centered on co-creating the study design elements, with the University team providing technical assistance in design and execution, and the community stakeholders leading the process of prioritizing their needs and fostering a space for dialogue and discovery.

In 2017 (seven years prior to this study), this collaboration first focused on enhancing ecological resilience among coastal residents in response to persistent flooding issues. Between 2017 and 2023, the team focused on select EUROSTAT (2024) ecological resilience dashboard indicators: fossil fuel emissions (carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide emissions), pesticide use in farming, protection of the coastal fresh waterways, water stress (flooding), water management by public and private entities, and the protection of renewable energy. During that time, our team met quarterly to address the short- and long-term needs of the community.

The 2023 explosion led to a dramatic shift in the engagement process to include another type of resilience activity, community-led resilience. activities addressing individual and family mental stress, social environmental, and physical health impacts following the direct exposure to the Pinova explosions. This led to a series of bi-weekly meetings and outreach activities over 18 months focusing on: (1) consumer education relating to water and air quality concerns and (2) mobilizing the community both to advocate for the closure of the Pinova plant and conducting a broad survey of the communities impacted by the explosion.

Data collection and analysis

Quantitative research data collection

A quantitative survey was developed prior to scheduling a series of planning meetings with community stakeholders, co-directed by the UGA research team and our community partner Heritage Works, which were held at local libraries. Before the planning meetings, eleven community volunteers were trained to obtain consent from respondents for the overall study and to assist with data collection. Immediately after each briefing, volunteers invited attendees to consent to and complete an anonymous survey via a QR code that directed them to a Qualtrics survey on their smartphones. If they did not have a smartphone, they were provided with a hard copy of the survey. The Qualtrics survey included demographic information and questions exploring residents’ concerns related to exposure to the chemical explosion (e.g., breathing, drinking, or seeing). It also asked them to prioritize their needs for the next step in the community engagement process. After completing the survey, participants were encouraged to share the QR code with other community members. This process resulted in the completion of 177 surveys over three months (April 2024 through June 2024).

Following the completion of the data collection activities, descriptive analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS v 31. The descriptive analyses focus on computing frequencies to examine the demographic characteristics of respondents and the types of environmental health problems they encountered following the explosions. The purpose of this part of the analysis was to profile the study population’s characteristics and the overall priorities identified by the study population. Except for the question “What are the top three most important things you believe need to be addressed in your community,” all questions in the survey were fixed single-response questions.

Qualitative research data collection and analysis

The development and analysis of the qualitative data followed the recommended COREQ checklist (Tong et al., 2007) to ensure the validity and integrity of qualitative findings. The COREQ checklist includes items focused on the characteristics of the research team and research flexibility (including the relationship between the researchers and the population, interviewer characteristics, and interviewers’ knowledge), the study design, and data collection activities.

After the completion of the survey, persons who were interested in participating in one on one individual interviews were given the following topics (previously developed by the stakeholder team) to choose for an interview: (1) Memories of living in Glynn/McIntosh County before 1980; (2) Keeping youth from leaving Glynn/McIntosh County; (3) Discussing the meaning of living in Glynn/McIntosh County today; (4) Honoring Ahmaud Arbery four years after his death in Glynn County (2.23.20); (5) Water/environmental issues in Glynn/McIntosh County; or (6) Air quality and personal health in Glynn McIntosh counties.

While 26 interviews were conducted, this paper focuses on the six individual interviews (N = 6) that addressed environmental resilience issues central to this paper (Topics 5 and 6). For topics five and six, they were asked:

Can you share with us how water and other environmental resilience issues (e.g., flooding, runoffs, drinking water quality, pollution, waste) affected the quality of life for those living in McIntosh County?

Individual interviewees also were asked to describe how the exposure to the chemical fire affected the residents of their community and what solutions they would recommend to address these issues.

Interviews were audio-recorded using digital recorders for an average of 25 minutes. Transcripts from the audio recorders were used for qualitative data analyses.

Qualitative interview recordings were transcribed verbatim using the VoiceBase platform (2018) to ensure accurate capture of participant narratives.

Thematic analysis (Clarke et al., 2015) was employed to examine the data systematically. The qualitative data analyses were conducted by the first author, a cisgender African American male (LJC), and two MSW graduate research assistants (one white cisgender female and one African American cisgender female) trained in qualitative research methods. Initial coding and theme development were conducted independently, followed by multiple consensus meetings to finalize the themes. An iterative coding process was employed to identify emergent themes, allowing for a nuanced interpretation of recurring patterns and community insights. The quotes were placed is a triangulation matrix, using Microsoft Word, sorted by interview question on each row, where the columns, moving from left to right, contained the original quotes, followed by the extracted quote from each analyst, followed by reflection and a thematic theme for all the, which was completed via a consensus review of the completed matrix by the team. Saturation occurred in the iterative process after the analysis revealed commonalities in their reflections, as reported in the grid. The process was supervised by a senior researcher (LJC) with over 18 years of experience in qualitative research, 25 years in CBPR research, and 40 years in quantitative research. The final audit of the transcripts was conducted by the community volunteer interviewers, who also annotated the inaudible words and phrases. This auditing process ensured “member checking” by involving the community in the data analysis (McKim, 2023). The authors incorporated elements of reflexivity into the project as noted by Olmos-Vega et al. (2023).

While this study focused on the experiences of persons living in the Gullah Geechee Heritage Corridor, neither the senior author, a Black male academic researcher (LJC) nor his MSW graduate assistants, nor the Black female community representative (HL) were born in the Corridor. As residents were particularly leery of people who were not Gullah/Geechee, as well as university researchers, we addressed their concerns by centering all that we were doing around the self-identified needs of the community. We also used a self-reflection journal to check in with ourselves and our teammates through the project.

The results presented here reflect synthesized thematic summaries, supported by illustrative quotes drawn directly from the transcripts. To preserve participants voice authenticity, only minimal grammatical adjustments were made to enhance clarity while maintaining the original context and meaning. Although one of the interviewees was between the ages of 14 and 17, that participant’s quotes were not included in the analyses as their responses primarily focused on participation in a S.T.E. A.M. (Science, Technology, Engineering, Art, and Math) class rather than on environmental health issues.

The Institutional Review Board of the University of Georgia approved this human subject research. As per the IRB protocol, consent forms and assent forms for the youth were provided to the respondents to read and sign before they were provided with access to the survey or participated in qualitative interviews. Since minors were involved in the data collection for this study, extra precautions were taken to protect their interests, in accordance with the University of Georgia’s Policies and Procedures related to working with minors (University of Georgia, Programs and Activities Serving Minors https://programsforminors.uga.edu/). For example, for all interviews involving minors, parents were present in the room during the sessions. Interviewers were instructed in advance that no adult should be left alone with a child in a room. Accordingly, all child interviews were conducted with both an interviewer and an adult assistant present in the room in addition to the child’s parent(s).

Limitations

While this study offers important insights into environmental health perceptions within the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor, several limitations must be noted. First, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, potentially overrepresenting those already affected or concerned about the Hercules-Pinova fire. Therefore, findings cannot be generalized to the broader population. Second, the qualitative data, drawn from only six interviews, limit the diversity of perspectives and may be affected by social desirability bias. Third, because the survey responses are based on self-report, we are not aware of respondents’ medical knowledge regarding the impact of exposure to chemicals, air pollution, and water toxicity on their health. Fourth, reliance on self-reported symptoms without clinical verification or geospatial data introduces recall and attribution bias, hindering localized exposure analysis. Lastly, the cross-sectional design, conducted a year after the incident (April – June 2024), precludes the assessment of longer-term shifts in health, perception, or community action. Longitudinal research is needed to understand the evolving impact fully.

Results

Socio-demographics of the participants from the qualtrics survey

While the explosion took place in 2023, the data collection process occurred a year later (2024) as the team worked with community stakeholders to ensure that the study design was reflective of the priorities of the community. A wide range of persons in the two affected Georgia Coast Counties completed the survey a year after the exposure to the Hercules-Pinova Chemical explosion (Table 1). Slightly over 70% (70.3%) of the respondents were female. At the same time, 30.9% of the respondents were between the ages of 14 and 17, and 69.1% were between the ages of 18 and 74. Over 90% of the respondents were either White (41.2%) or African American (51.4%). Approximately one-third (33.7%) had less than a high school education, while the remaining two-thirds had at least a high school diploma. Finally, more than half of the respondents (54.6%) lived more than five miles from the explosion. By comparison, in 2023, the Brunswick population (the location of the Pinova Explosion) consisted of 61.4% African American and 28.4% non-Hispanic white (DataUSA, n.d.-a). At the same time, the Darien Georgia Population (McIntosh County-the County next to the explosion–35.0% of the population were African American, 52.5% were non-Hispanic white (DataUSA, n.d.-b).

2024 Qualtrics survey findings: social demographic factors and perceived environmental health impacts and concerns

In terms of the impact of the explosion on these residents, most of the 2024 survey respondents (86.5%) reported some health symptoms following the 2023 explosion (Table 2). A year later, nearly all respondents expressed concern about the blast’s long-term impact on human health, with 60.9% indicating they were very concerned. When asked to select the top three issues that need to be addressed in their communities, 66.1% of participants identified environmental issues such as water and air pollution as a primary concern, underscoring the prominence of ecological hazards in daily life. In addition to environmental health, respondents emphasized the need for stronger civic engagement and structural support systems. Nearly half (48%) of the respondents viewed voting as a crucial community priority, suggesting an awareness of political empowerment as a means to address environmental and social injustices. Family and community well-being were also central concerns, with 37.1% of participants identifying empowerment as a top issue. Education and youth development (32.8%) and job and economic development (31.1%) were also frequently mentioned, reflecting an interest in building local capacity for long-term sustainability. Furthermore, 34.5% of participants listed public safety as a pressing issue, and 27.1% emphasized access to healthy food, indicating how environmental and infrastructural stressors intersect with daily living conditions. Together, these findings illustrate that community-defined resilience involves not only addressing environmental degradation but also strengthening civic infrastructure, enhancing youth opportunities, and ensuring access to basic needs.

Quantitative socio-demographics of interview participants

Table 3 summarizes the demographics of the six interview participants. Half of the interviewees lived within 10 miles of the explosion, while the other half lived 11 miles or more away. Most were female (66.7%) and African American (66.7%). The majority (66.7%) were between 25 and 64 years old, with smaller proportions aged 14–17 and 18–24 (16.6% each).

Qualitative findings: human environmental health issues, industrial pollution, coalition building and policy engagement

Qualitative data from the individual interviews with residents revealed a pattern of deeply rooted concerns about environmental health in their communities that mirrored the previously presented literature on this issue relating to environmental crises, water and air pollution and the importance of engaging in community action to sustain the environment. These discussions underscored not only the immediate effects of recent environmental events but also a longstanding history of structural neglect. Four themes emerged from the individual interviews: (1) poor water quality, (2) air pollution, (3) political disempowerment and legislative vulnerability, and (4) solutions and strategies for environmental health issues. Selective quotes from these themes are presented below.

Poor water quality

A consistently recurring theme was poor water quality, primarily attributed to aging infrastructure and industrial runoff contaminating local water sources.

This was poignantly illustrated by respondents who described the sensory evidence of water pollution and the uncertainty surrounding its safety:

You will know when you reach this community by the Atlantic Ocean, when you smell either the egg water or the sewage smell from the water factories, or the smell of the sewers, as the sewer companies and their pipes lead into the pipes that bring the water in. So that’s cross-contamination right there. So, you don’t know if you’re probably drinking somebody’s pee water or poop water. You don’t know what you’re drinking.

And I’m going to use this strong word just raping nature and part of that will include water specifically water, which is again a necessity, not a commodity. And so, they deposit, they, meaning industry. deposits a lot of poisons, and excesses into the water streams. which, of course, recycles itself into nature. It takes decades, if not generations, to subsequently dissipate and the environment becomes healthy again. I mean, that’s theoretical. but that’s what’s going on. And they dump, the elicit dumping occurs in traditionally black communities or working people, communities and things. That’s been cited throughout the country, from Alabama to Louisiana, the Georgia coast, New York, and so on, and so forth.

Residents also raised concerns about water stress (flooding), which not only exacerbates existing water issues but also disrupts daily life. One youth respondent emphasized the infrastructural challenges during heavy rains:

When it rains in this rural community by the Atlantic Ocean, it can cause flooding in some nearby cities. It also backs up the drains because, once too much water enters the sewer at one time, it can’t drain continuously.

Air pollution

Beyond water, air quality emerged as a concern. A community organization leader shared the personal toll of air pollution and its connection to surrounding industrial sites:

Here, due to air pollution and some of these Superfund sites located on the coastline, especially when I visit this County, the air quality is one of the issues. I got sick from. I just automatically knew that issues were going on with my body, and I could not tell. There’s more industrialized stuff here.

A second community organization leader shared talked about the immediate impact of the explosion:

I didn’t know anything about the explosion. So my husband called in a panic and said, Hey, hey! Where are you? Do you see anything? Do you smell anything? And I said, No, what’s going on? And he told me that the factory had had an explosion or a fire, and he wasn’t really sure but it was still going on. So I immediately called my friend. I said, Girl, where you at? You got tested? She said. No, I’m already home. But what happened? And then, we told her … So when I went outside, you couldn’t see directly, because it’s about 8 to 10 miles south of where I live. And you can see weird clouds like it’s just smoke clouds, you can see, but you know it’s not a cloud. But you know I’m like, that’s not a cloud that’s smoke, you know. But there was no call, no shelter in place, and I thought that was really odd.

Political disempowerment and legislative vulnerability

Environmental degradation also was closely tied to political disempowerment. Residents raised alarms about political disempowerment and the lack of transparency and public input around legislation, particularly the State of Georgia’s House Bill 1146 (2024) (a law that permits private companies to regulate the distribution of water in local communities, which concerns community residents) and how unchecked growth and resource exploitation have compounded the community’s vulnerability:

Unless these things happen directly in our communities, the black communities, our brown communities, we are slow to move towards the idea of being proactive. People must be cautious about how they protest, as there can be negative consequences associated with it. This area is still predominantly mills and manufacturing plants. So, if your supervisor finds out that you’re out there leading some movement or even participating in it. Next thing you know, your hours are reduced from 40 hours a week to 20, and then you are phased out.

[in talking about H.B. 1146] You know, just unprecedented growth without oversight, you know, competition with public water providers, which is very, very scary to me. Lack of responsible planning. Water is already a limited resource that most people use every day, yet they often fail to realize its importance and the actual scarcity of this resource. Therefore, the public is not aware of what is happening at the legislative level … The depletion of water, depletion of aquifers, and pollution.

We already have pollution from industry and water related to new industry. Here on the coast, that residents were not prepared for, that was shelved through without their oversight and without their again, when you have a super majority in the legislature that doesn’t really need your input and can get those bills through quickly.

Solutions and strategies for environmental health issues

Residents also shared strategies for moving forward – many of which centered on strengthening community voice and grassroots leadership. For example, youth participants advocated for using communication platforms to raise awareness and foster engagement:

What would it take? I think hearing you speak out would help a lot more, just getting the word out about how the water could affect this in 10 to 20 years.

I would say on social media. That’s where everybody is, and everybody, you know, is other youth. Connecting online on social media can bring people together.

Finally, community leaders emphasized the importance of tailored, region-specific strategies across the Gullah Geechee Heritage Corridor. They called for collaborative planning that honors the unique circumstances of each community while recognizing shared struggles across the Corridor:

Georgia is distinct from South Carolina, North Carolina, and Florida. That’s one of the things I have learned along the way. We gotta do a different strategy, and nobody’s better than the next person, because the same thing that’s happening on Sapelo or on Jekyll, or on Sunbury, or wherever else that’s along the court. It’s all the same story with land loss, medical health, and quality basic taxes.

And then we came together. We have a scribe. We scribe it out, map it out like we had, like a strategy session. And then, remember, Georgia is different from South Carolina, North Carolina, and Florida. It just so happens that we all a part of the corridor and a Federal corridor, that you are supposed to do something about government. Thus, have to do a different strategy, and nobody’s better than the next person, because the same thing that’s happening on Sapelo or on Jekyll, or on Sunbury, or wherever else that’s along the court. It’s all the same story with land loss, medical health and quality basic taxes.

Together, these narratives convey a compelling vision of environmental resilience, one shaped by lived experience, cultural continuity, and a drive for community-led solutions.

Discussion

This study examined environmental health perceptions, community-identified concerns, and grassroots strategies for resilience in the aftermath of the 2023 Hercules-Pinova plant fire within a segment of the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor. The findings reveal a strong sense of vulnerability among respondents, with over 86% reporting health symptoms and more than 60% expressing grave concern about the long-term consequences of the explosion. These responses highlight not only the immediate health impacts but also the cumulative burden of environmental exposures that marginalized coastal communities have historically endured (Bullard et al., 2008; Mohai et al., 2009). Along with connecting their concerns to environmental justice (Mohai et al., 2009), participants connected industrial contamination to deeper structural issues, including water insecurity, political marginalization, and generational land loss, that intensify their exclusion from decision-making processes (Taylor, 2014).

Both the survey findings (Tables 2 and 3) and the qualitative data reinforced the notion that the Hercules-Pinova chemical explosion was a pivotal moment in community engagement, mobilizing the community to address public health issues and reflect on longstanding environmental injustices. Respondents shared symptoms consistent with chemical exposure (e.g., eye issues and concerns about water contamination). However, these health issues were not viewed in isolation. Participants situated their experiences within a broader narrative of environmental neglect and systemic inequality. This meant worrying that they might even lose their jobs because of speaking out about these issues. Furthermore, because of the use of the CBPR methodology to drive the development of the study questions, the respondents expanded the list of community defined priorities from the EUROSTAT (2024) resilience indicators to also include social environmental issues. This aligns with findings by Kang et al. (2019), who reported that individuals often interpret ecological risks through the lens of their wider socio-economic and racialized realities.

Our findings provide a multidimensional understanding of community priorities in the aftermath of environmental trauma. While environmental issues like air and water pollution were most frequently cited (66.1%), nearly half of respondents (48%) also identified civic engagement, particularly voting, as a top priority, reflecting a collective awareness of political empowerment as a pathway to justice. Concerns about family well-being (37.1%), youth development (32.8%), and economic opportunity (31.1%) further underscore a community-driven vision for resilience that extends beyond immediate disaster response. These findings are supported by the Ecological Social Theory of Health (Krieger, 2011), which argues that the intersection of social, political, and ecological forces shapes health outcomes.

Our findings also noted that community members demonstrated clear agency in envisioning locally grounded resilience strategies. Respondents emphasized the importance of utilizing social media and youth outreach to promote awareness and stimulate civic dialogue. These preferences are consistent with best practices in community-based participatory research (CBPR) (Israel et al., 2010), which advocate for the co-creation of knowledge, equitable engagement, and actionable solutions (Muhammad et al., 2015; Wallerstein & Duran, 2010). Additionally, community members emphasized that strategies must be tailored to the unique geographic and historical contexts of the Georgia portion of the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor. The integration of both quantitative and qualitative methods provided a complementary lens through which to understand the community’s multifaceted experiences. The structured survey allowed for the identification of broad patterns and pressing priorities, while the in-depth individual interviews added rich narrative detail and contextual insight. Together, this mixed methods approach enhanced the rigor and relevance of the findings, offering a more comprehensive understanding of how environmental exposures, structural barriers, and local strengths intersect to shape community-defined pathways toward resilience.

Implications for community practitioners, education, research, and practice

This case study of two communities in the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor underscores the urgent need for the social work profession to deepen its engagement with environmental justice, particularly in historically marginalized and environmentally burdened communities. From an educational standpoint, social work curricula should more systematically integrate content on ecological and environmental justice. As Hudson (2019) argues, opportunities exist to embed these issues into foundational courses such as Human Behavior in the Social Environment (HBSE), and into electives focused on sustainability, policy, and advocacy. Courses should encourage students to assess the environmental determinants of health, such as water and air quality, and their intersection with race, class, and geographic inequalities.

On the research front, this study exemplifies the value of community-academic partnerships grounded in the principles of Community Based Participatory Research (CBPR) (Israel et al., 2010). Through a co-led survey and mixed-methods approach, researchers and community stakeholders were able to document localized concerns and elevate resident voices in identifying culturally relevant solutions.

In practice, community practitioners must recognize the structural and environmental dimensions of client vulnerability. The findings from this study highlight the interlinkage between health concerns, civic disempowerment, and economic insecurity, as well as their connection to broader ecological degradation. Community practitioners should expand psychosocial assessments to include questions about environmental hazards, infrastructure stressors, and access to clean water, food, and air. Furthermore, policy practice must be amplified: as noted by one participant reflecting on H.B. 1146, lack of public oversight in environmental policymaking exacerbates inequities. Community practitioners can play a critical role in advocating for inclusive policy development and supporting grassroots organizing efforts to reclaim decision-making power.

Regarding the Social Work Grand Challenges and the US Sustainable Development Goals (SDG), this case study profiles ways that residents of two counties that have been exposed to a series of long term structural environmental problems (e.g., land dispossessions) and environmental health problems, are mobilizing start to develop a collective voice to address these concerns discover ways to transform their community. In this case, via their co-leading a survey process with an academic institution. This case study contributes to the Grand Challenges for Social Work, particularly the challenge “Creating social responses to a changing environment” (Grand Challenges for Social Work, n.d.), as the study highlights the social and environmental inequities within the communities in the study and documents a process that can lead to increased community involvement in social change efforts.

The findings from this case study speak to the importance of capturing what Kang et al. (2019) called the “localized perception of the environment” in conducting a survey. In their qualitative interviews, Kang and colleagues noted that while they explored the impact of air pollution, climate change, and water contamination on the lives of the residents of a midwestern city, the residents noted that other structural environmental issues in their community (violence and racism) were more pressing than the environmental issues. They said that “our findings show that despite some disconnection from pollution and climate change, many study participants applied an environmental justice lens to their experiences” (Kang et al., 2019, p. 328). As in the Kang study, although most respondents reported experiencing health symptoms from exposure to the chemical fire explosion, they also believed that future community engagement activities should focus on broader environmental justice activities such as voting, safety, and economic development. These findings suggest a need to adopt a broader ecosystem perspective in developing strategies to transform their environment, that includes both a focus on the human/non-human biological ecosystem and social environmental transformation (e.g. academic/community partnership collaboration, policy engagement and so on).

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that people living near the Pinova plant were affected by the explosion. They also indicated a desire to continue mobilizing to address other environmental needs, in tandem with the long history of cultural self-determination in the Gullah Geechie Cultural Heritage corridor. For example, two years following the Pinova explosion (2025), residents continue their cultural presentation activities by black landowners on Sapelo Island (McIntosh County) and won a State of Georgia Supreme Court case that threatened to displace the indigenous residents (Tueton et al., 2025). It also is reflected in the Brunswick residents (Glynn County) advocating for the creation of a Superfund Research Center in this area to continue examining the effects of contaminants from the explosion on the community’s metabolic health (Mecke, 2025). As such, the momentum generated through this community-academic partnership has catalyzed meaningful next steps for advancing environmental justice in the Gullah Geechee Heritage Corridor. Building on the findings of this survey and individual interviews, local stakeholders are now preparing to host county-wide listening sessions in Glynn and McIntosh Counties. These sessions will offer residents the opportunity to envision a more just and sustainable environmental future collectively, and to express their interest in ongoing engagement and advocacy efforts.
