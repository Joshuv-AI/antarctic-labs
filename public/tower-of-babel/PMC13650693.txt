The feasibility and toxicity of the second course of boron neutron capture therapy in recurrent head and neck cancer patients
Authors: Yoshiaki Takagawa, Yoshihiro Takai, Takashi Ono, Ichiro Seto, Kanako Takayama, Shinya Komori, Ryohei Kato, Akihiko Takeuchi, Yuhei Yamazaki, Tomoaki Motoyanagi, Takahiro Kato, Michitaka Honda, Koji Kono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe feasibility and safety of a second course of boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) are unknown. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the treatment outcomes and toxicities in recurrent head and neck cancer (RHNC) patients who underwent BNCT twice at Southern TOHOKU BNCT Research Center. In total, 33 patients who underwent BNCT twice for RHNC at Southern TOHOKU BNCT Research Center between June 2020 and March 2024 were included. The indications for second BNCT were local recurrence or residual tumor after the first course. Eligible patients either declined salvage surgery or had no curative treatment options other than second BNCT. The median age was 65 years (range, 23–84 years), and the median interval from the first to the second BNCT was 4.1 months (range, 2.3–14.2 months). The median follow-up period after second BNCT was 27.3 months (range, 3.8–60.4 months), and the median survival time after second BNCT was 33.6 months. The 1-year/2-year overall survival and local control after second BNCT were 81.8%/57.1% and 38.3%/22.3%, respectively. The response rates to the first and second courses of BNCT were 81.8% and 70%, respectively. Acute and late Grade 3 or higher toxicities were observed in 5 (15.1%) and 20 patients (60.6%), respectively. Only one patient developed Grade 5 aspiration pneumonia. A second course of BNCT developed a high incidence of late Grade 3 or higher toxicities with limited clinical benefit. Therefore, it may be considered only in carefully selected patients without alternative treatment options.Keywords: BNCT, head and neck cancer, re-irradiation, toxicity

INTRODUCTION

Radiotherapy (RT) plays an important role in curative local treatment for head and neck cancer. The use of chemoradiotherapy (CRT) has been increasing as initial treatment for head and neck cancer patients due to its potential for organ preservation over the last 30 years; however, approximately 50% of patients with locally advanced head and neck squamous cell cancer develop recurrence after initial treatment [1]. Management of recurrent head and neck cancer (RHNC) remains challenging due to the difficulty of balancing tumor control with organ preservation. Although salvage surgery is the most curative treatment option for RHNC, it often results in substantial functional impairment and deterioration in quality of life (QOL) due to tissue loss. Although modern advances in RT such as intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) and proton beam therapy (PBT) enable re-irradiation for RHNC [2], the treatment outcomes are poor.

Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) is a novel particle radiation therapy based on the 10B(n,α)7 Li reaction. Since tumor cells preferentially accumulate intracellular 10B compared with normal tissues BNCT enables tumor-selective irradiation and may provide a less toxic re-irradiation strategy in RHNC patients with a history of RT. Hirose et al. reported a phase II study (JHN002) on the efficacy and toxicity of BNCT [3], based on which BNCT for RHNC became eligible for national health insurance coverage in Japan in June 2020. Although previous studies have reported favorable outcomes under the national health insurance system [4, 5], some RHNC patients develop further local recurrence after initial BNCT. In these situations, curative treatment options are limited. Moreover, most patients decline salvage surgery after the first course of BNCT. Given the radiobiological and physical advantages of BNCT, we hypothesized that a second course may offer a potential curative option for selected patients with local recurrence after initial BNCT. However, the feasibility and safety of a second course of BNCT are unknown. In the present study, we retrospectively analyzed the treatment outcomes and toxicities in RHNC patients who underwent BNCT twice at Southern TOHOKU BNCT Research Center.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patients

A total of 33 patients who underwent BNCT twice for RHNC at Southern TOHOKU BNCT Research Center between June 2020 and March 2024 were included. The eligibility criteria for the second course of BNCT were as follows: (1) presence of local recurrence or residual tumor confined to the previously irradiated area after the first course; (2) refusal of salvage surgery or absence of other curative treatment options apart from a second course of BNCT; and (3) provision of written informed consent after understanding the potential risk of severe adverse events. This study was approved by the institutional ethics review board (approval number: 789).

Boron neutron capture therapy

The detailed BNCT procedure is described in our previous reports [3–6]. Patients were placed in either the supine or seated position according to tumor location using patient-specific immobilization devices [7, 8]. Computed tomography (CT) images for treatment planning were acquired in the treatment position. Treatment planning was performed using the RayStation® treatment planning system (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) and dose calculations were conducted using the NeuCure® Dose Engine (Sumitomo Heavy Industries Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) [9]. The relative biological effectiveness values for gamma rays, fast neutrons and thermal neutrons were set to 1.0, 2.4 and 2.9, respectively [10]. The compound biological effectiveness for borofalan (10B) (Stella Pharma, Osaka, Japan) in tumor, mucosa, skin and other normal tissues was set to 4.0, 4.9, 2.5 and 1.34, respectively [11–13]. The tumor-to-blood boron concentration ratio was defined constantly as 3.5 [14]. The prescribed dose was set with the maximum dose to the pharyngeal mucosa limited to 12–20 Gy-Eq, with the dose parameter V20 Gy-Eq ≤ 5 cm3 to the oral mucosa. The goal minimum dose to the GTV (Gross tumor volume) was greater than 25 Gy-Eq. Borofalan was administered intravenously at 200 mg/kg/h beginning 2 h before neutron irradiation and continued at 100 mg/kg/h during irradiation Neutron irradiation was performed by NeuCure®. Whole-blood 10B concentration was measured using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy, and irradiation time was determined based on the measured whole-blood 10B concentration in each patient.

Assessment

The efficacy of the first BNCT was evaluated as previously described [3–6]. Treatment response was assessed 3 months after the first and second courses of BNCT using 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) according to the Positron Emission Tomography Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (PERCIST) version 1.0 [15, 16]. In patients who were unable to undergo 18F-FDG PET, contrast-enhanced CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [17]. 18F-FDG PET was conducted following a standardized institutional protocol. Patients fasted for at least 4 h before the intravenous injection of 18F-FDG (3.7 MBq/kg), and scans were initiated 90 min post-injection. 18F-FDG PET images were acquired using the Discovery IQ® (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA). Response rate was calculated as the rate of complete response (CR)/complete metabolic response (CMR) and partial response (PR)/partial metabolic response (PMR). Patients who achieved CR/CMR or near-complete response were followed at our institution whenever feasible. Patients with stable disease (SD)/stable metabolic disease (SMD) or progressive disease (PD)/progressive metabolic disease (PMD) were referred to local institutions for additional management, including salvage surgery, palliative chemotherapy or best supportive care. For these patients, we conducted follow-up using questionnaires or telephone interviews with the physician-in-charge at the local hospital, the patient or their family. Acute and late toxicities were assessed using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0, based on the consensus of two radiation oncologists.

Statistical analysis

Overall survival (OS) and local control (LC) were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves. Follow-up began on the date of the second course of BNCT. OS was defined as the time from the second course of BNCT to death or last follow-up. LC was defined as the absence of tumor progression within the irradiated field after the second course of BNCT. Prognostic factors were evaluated using the Cox proportional hazards model. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata®, version 11.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Patient and treatment characteristics

Patient and treatment characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Detailed characteristics of all patients at the first BNCT were summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The median age was 65 years (range, 23–84 years). Twenty-five patients were male and eight were female. The median cumulative prior RT dose before the first course of BNCT was 70 Gy (range, 52–166 Gy; EQD2 Gy; α/β = 10). The targets after the first course of BNCT were as follows: 19 patients had local recurrence alone, 11 patients had lymph node recurrence alone and three patients had both local and lymph node recurrence. The median GTVs in the first and second courses of BNCT were 14.07 cm3 (range, 1.21–135.57 cm3) and 6.45 cm3 (range, 1.03–90.57 cm3), respectively. The median GTV minimum doses in the first and second courses of BNCT were 30.9 Gy-Eq (range, 17.1–65.3 Gy-Eq) and 34.1 Gy-Eq (range, 14.0–53.9 Gy-Eq), respectively. The cumulative doses for organ at risk (OAR) (brain, skin, pharynx and oral mucosa) of all patients between prior RT and the first/second course of BNCT are described in the Supplementary Table S2. The median GTV SUVmax on 18F-FDG PET before the second course of BNCT (n = 32) was 7.9 (range, 3.7–19.5). Only one patient did not undergo 18F-FDG PET before the second course of BNCT due to rapid tumor progression. The median interval time from the first to second course of BNCT was 4.1 months (range, 2.3–14.2 months). The median follow-up period after the second course of BNCT was 27.3 months (range, 3.8–60.4 months). Four patients remain alive without recurrence or metastasis (three did not undergo salvage therapy after the second course of BNCT), seven patients are alive with cancer, eighteen patients died from cancer, four patients died from causes other than the primary cancer, four patients underwent salvage surgery after the second course of BNCT, and 19 patients underwent salvage chemotherapy after the second course of BNCT. Most of these patients received immune checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab or pembrolizumab). The clinical course of all patients was summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Clinical efficacy

The median survival time after the second course of BNCT was 33.6 months. The 1-year/2-year OS and LC after the second course of BNCT were 81.8%/57.1% and 38.3%/22.3%, respectively (Fig. 1). The response rates to the first and second courses of BNCT were 81.8% and 70%, respectively (Table 2). Four and 10 patients did not undergo 18F-FDG PET for treatment assessment after the first and second courses of BNCT, respectively. No imaging was performed after the second course of BNCT in three patients due to progressive disease, acute toxicity and personal reasons. Univariate analysis (UVA) of the prognostic factors for OS and LC is summarized in Table 3. The patients with local recurrence alone at the first course of BNCT had better LC (P = 0.05) than those with lymph node recurrence. The patients with CR/CMR after the first and second courses of BNCT had better LC (P = 0.08 and 0.059, respectively) than those with non-CR/non-CMR. The patients with an interval of ≥4 months between the first and second courses of BNCT had slightly better OS than those with an interval < 4 months (P = 0.083). Salvage surgery or chemotherapy after the second BNCT did not significantly improve OS (P = 0.20). As a result, no significant differences in OS and LC were observed among the prognostic factors.

Toxicity

Acute toxicities during the second course of BNCT were as follows: Grade 0/1/2/3 oral or pharyngeal mucositis was observed in 4/14/13/2 patients, Grade 0/1/2/3 nausea was observed in 11/3/19/0 patients and Grade 0/1/2/3 anorexia was observed in 11/11/8/3 patients, respectively. There were no Grade 4 or higher acute toxicities and all acute toxicities were manageable. Grade 3 or higher late toxicities were observed in 20 patients (60.6%). Only one patient developed Grade 5 aspiration pneumonia due to dysphagia after the second course of BNCT. The details of late toxicities are shown in Table 4 and Supplementary Table S4. The most frequent Grade 3 or higher toxicities were aspiration, dysphagia, skin ulceration and brain necrosis. Five patients developed aspiration pneumonitis (Grade 3) and required hospitalization. Ten patients developed Grade 3 dysphagia; eight required gastrostomy, one required a feeding tube and one required central venous nutrition. Of note, nine patients developed Grade 3/4 skin ulceration, which negatively affected the QOL due to severe pain and exudation from the ulcer surface. The median maximum doses to the OAR in the prior RT, the first/second courses of BNCT are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2.

Representative case of the patient with brain necrosis

A representative case of a 52-year-old female (Patient #4) with squamous cell carcinoma of the right external ear canal (cT2N0M0) is shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs S1 and S2. She initially underwent CRT (Supplementary Figs S1A and S2A), but developed local recurrence. Although salvage surgery was performed, further local recurrence occurred (Fig. 2A). The first course of BNCT for local recurrence was carried out with a minimum dose of 26.8 Gy-Eq to the GTV (Fig. 2B). 18F-FDG PET performed 3 months after the first course revealed CMR, but 6 months post-BNCT, further local recurrence was detected on 18F-FDG PET (Fig. 2D). There was no distant metastasis. After informing the patient about the potential risk of severe late toxicities and obtaining informed consent, we initiated the second course for further local recurrence, delivering a minimum dose of 46.6 Gy-Eq to the GTV (Fig. 2E). 18F-FDG PET performed 3 months after the second course again demonstrated CMR. Although no further local recurrence was noted (Fig. 2F), brain necrosis was detected by follow-up contrast-enhanced MRI 16 months after the second course of BNCT. Brain necrosis continued to expand, and neurological symptoms appeared 3 months later (Fig. 2G). Corticosteroids were effective in controlling the progression of brain necrosis and her neurological symptoms improved (Fig. 2H). The patient no longer requires corticosteroids to control brain necrosis. The dose volume histograms between prior RT and the first/second BNCT are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

DISCUSSION

Feasibility of the second BNCT

We investigated the feasibility and toxicities of a second course of BNCT for RHNC. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such report. Despite advances in CRT using IMRT, 15–50% of patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma develop recurrence [18], and the prognosis of patients with RHNC after curative CRT is poor. Re-irradiation is one treatment option with curative potential. Although several retrospective studies of re-irradiation using different radiation modalities reported 1-year OS rates of 45–90% [19–27], most prospective clinical trials reported rates of approximately 50% [28–36]. This discrepancy may be due to patient selection bias at the time of re-irradiation. Furthermore, re-irradiation using IMRT, SBRT or PBT is mechanistically different from that using BNCT. The first course of BNCT for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma provides favorable clinical outcomes, with reported two-year OS rates ranging from 58% to 79.8% [3–6]; however, some patients experience recurrence. Moreover, because of the risk of exceeding normal tissue tolerance, evidence supporting a second course of re-irradiation for head and neck malignancies is limited. Xu et al. reported the efficacy and safety of three or more courses of radiation using three-dimensional conformal photon therapy, IMRT or PBT in 33 patients with head and neck carcinoma treated at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center [37]. The median local recurrence-free survival after the final course of radiation was 9.1 months and the median OS was 10 months. On the other hand, the 1-year/2-year OS and one-year/two-year LC after the second course of BNCT in our study were 81.8%/57.1% and 38.3%/22.3%, respectively, with a median survival of 33.6 months. However, more than half of the patients underwent salvage chemotherapy for residual tumor or recurrence after the second course of BNCT (Supplementary Table S3); thus, caution is needed in interpreting these clinical results. Although no significant difference was observed in survival due to salvage therapy, these results may still be influenced by salvage chemotherapy, especially by the administration of immune checkpoint inhibitors after the second course of BNCT. In randomized phase III trials, nivolumab and pembrolizumab significantly improved OS in patients with recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma who experienced progression during platinum-based chemotherapy [38, 39]. Therefore, there is a possibility that the first and second courses of BNCT increased the tumor-immune response at the time of salvage chemotherapy, thereby improving its effectiveness. Although three patients had no tumor recurrence without salvage therapy, our clinical outcomes should be interpreted as reflecting the combined effect of two courses of BNCT followed by salvage chemotherapy. The response rates to the first and second courses of BNCT were 81.8% and 70%, respectively. Thus, the residual or recurrent tumor after the first course of BNCT responded sufficiently to the second course. According to UVA, although no significant prognostic factors were identified, patients with local recurrence alone without lymph node recurrence at the first course of BNCT, with a response of CR or CMR thereafter, and with an interval from the first to second course of BNCT of over 4 months showed a trend toward clinical benefit from a second course of BNCT.

Late toxicities of the second BNCT

The rate of Grade 3/4 late toxicities in our study (60.6%) was comparable to that reported by Xu et al. (66%) [37]. It is difficult to determine whether late toxicity after re-irradiation is caused by prior RT or by re-irradiation. For example, nearly half of the patients (14 patients) had already had dysphagia before the first BNCT (Supplementary Table S1). However, the negative effects of severe late toxicities on QOL resulting from cumulative RT and BNCT cannot be ignored. In this study, the late adverse events varied and depended on the location of the recurrent tumor (Table 4 and Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Grade 3/4 late toxicities were observed in 20 patients. Nearly half of these patients had severe dysphagia, and most required gastrostomy due to difficulty swallowing. In relation to dysphagia, five patients developed Grade 3 aspiration pneumonitis, and one developed Grade 5 aspiration pneumonitis. As such, regardless of gastrostomy, microaspiration was inevitable due to severe dysphagia.

The primary pathophysiology of radiation-induced skin injury is hypoxia due to the progressive obliteration of the microvasculature (progressive endarteritis), as well as fibrosis [40]. In cases of severe skin ulcer or soft tissue necrosis, surgical resection of the affected tissue may be indicated, with reconstruction of the defect using a free flap or split-thickness skin graft. In our study, although some patients underwent optimal surgical resection or debridement, wound healing at the operation site was poor and persistent exudation was often observed, leading to substantial impairment of QOL (Supplementary Table S4). Hyperbaric oxygen treatment is one of the most common applications for delayed radiation injuries [41]; however, several courses are required, and administration is difficult for patients with skin ulcers or soft tissue necrosis.

Brain necrosis was the fourth most common late Grade 3/4 toxicity in this study, occurring in five patients. The primary tumors in these patients were located in the external auditory canal (two patients), oral cavity (one patient), maxillary sinus (one patient) or parotid gland (one patient). Radiation-induced brain necrosis is often refractory to treatment, associated with permanent neurological deficits, and may occasionally be fatal [42, 43]. The optimal management of brain necrosis involves follow-up by MRI and medication for brain edema. In our representative case (Patient #4), the brain dose in the second course of BNCT was the highest among all cases (13.53 Gy-Eq) (Fig. 2E and Table 1). Brain necrosis was successfully managed using corticosteroids (Fig. 2H). Although most patients respond to steroids, some develop progressive edema and necrosis. Bevacizumab use is strongly recommended in selected patients with corticosteroid-refractory brain necrosis [44]. In BNCT, precise adjustment of the neutron beam is more challenging than in IMRT, SBRT or PBT (Fig. 2B and E). Therefore, to minimize radiation exposure to the pharynx, skin and brain, it is necessary to reduce the prescribed dose or develop a more sophisticated neutron beam irradiation technique.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective study in a small cohort of 33 patients. Therefore, the results from the statistical analysis in this study were limited. Second, accurate cumulative dose calculation for the target and OARs was difficult due to fundamental differences in dosimetric methods between prior RT and BNCT. The prescribed doses for BNCT depend on the 10B uptake by tumor cells. Furthermore, there is no consensus on whether the linear-quadratic model is suitable for BNCT, which delivers a high dose in a single fraction. Third, the actual rate of late toxicities after the second course of BNCT may have been underestimated. Although we performed follow-up using a questionnaire or telephone interview for the patients and their families who were unable to come to our institution after the second course of BNCT, these follow-up methods did not allow objective clinical assessment. Moreover, it is possible that some patients died before any late adverse events developed due to the poor prognosis of RHNC. Furthermore, as all patients had a history of previous RT and initial BNCT, it is impossible to accurately determine whether these late toxicities were caused by the second course of BNCT alone. These limitations are common issues in studies on re-irradiation and are difficult to eliminate. However, in this study, some patients and their families experienced more pain and a further decline in QOL due to radiation-induced late toxicities than from the symptoms of residual tumors or tumor progression (Supplementary Table S4). As such, due to the accumulation of radiation damage in normal tissues, radiation oncologists should pay great attention to possible late toxicities and confirm that patients and their families have a clear understanding of the symptoms and management of late toxicities to provide informed consent in the case of re-irradiation. This is the first report on the feasibility and toxicity of a second course of BNCT in RHNC. Further investigations regarding efficacy and safety are warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

A second course of BNCT developed a high incidence of late Grade 3 or higher toxicities with limited clinical benefit. Therefore, it may be considered only in carefully selected patients without alternative treatment options.
