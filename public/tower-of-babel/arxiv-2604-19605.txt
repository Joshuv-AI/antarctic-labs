Tuning in to Frequencies: How Global Assets Align U.S. Put–Call Parity Residuals
Useong Shin

Abstract
Put–call parity is a risk-neutral identity, but enforcing it is path-dependent and capital-using. I study the carry gap, the annualized wedge between option-implied and OIS discount factors, in SPX and RUT options. Because parity enforcement ties up scarce capital, its opportunity cost may reflect outside investment opportunities. Adding low-frequency global asset-return components to an OIS-based baseline improves in-sample and leave-one-year-out out-of-sample R 2 R^{2} , with gains robust to broad-dollar neutralization, alternative asset blocks, and nested horizon selection. The evidence indicates reduced-form P–Q alignment: the carry gap is not empirically separable from physical-measure outside-option proxies, rather than behaving as a purely OIS-contained wedge.

1
Introduction

Put–call parity is the most basic no-arbitrage relation for European
options. Viewed solely through its terminal payoff, it is a model-free
identity, and under the risk-neutral measure its residual is zero by
construction. Enforcing this identity in practice, however, is not only
a pricing operation but also a capital-allocation problem. A parity
position must be maintained through the pre-maturity path, exposing the
arbitrageur to interim mark-to-market losses, margin requirements, and
finite-capital constraints.
Shin 2026
documents that this
implementation burden manifests as a systematic wedge between
option-implied and OIS discount factors—the
carry gap
—and
characterizes it as a reduced-form function of a GBM path-risk term,
trading frictions, and financial conditions.

This distinction motivates the present paper. The carry gap is a
Q-measure object constructed from the discount structure embedded in
option prices. Yet the capital committed to enforcing parity competes
with alternative uses of capital under the physical measure. The
opportunity-cost component of parity enforcement may therefore contain
information not fully summarized by the risk-free rate or by OIS-based
discounting alone. If scarce arbitrage capital is allocated across
competing investment opportunities, low-frequency returns on major
asset classes may proxy for variation in the outside-option component
of the enforcement burden. The empirical question is therefore whether
this Q-measure carry-space residual is separable from P-measure
outside-option proxies.

This paper documents robust reduced-form evidence of such P–Q
alignment in the SPX and RUT options markets. Augmenting the OIS-based
baseline specification with low-frequency global asset-return
components raises the explanatory power of the carry gap by

\Delta R^{2}=0.090
in SPX and
\Delta R^{2}=0.080
in RUT. The
improvement is not confined to in-sample fit. Pooled
leave-one-year-out out-of-sample
R^{2}
rises from 0.221 to 0.379 in
SPX and from 0.171 to 0.318 in RUT, with positive gains in 8 of 10
holdout years for SPX and 7 of 10 for RUT. The result also survives
broad-dollar neutralization, alternative asset-allocation blocks, and
nested horizon selection in which the asset lookback windows are chosen
using only the training sample within each holdout fold.

The asset-return extension is deliberately restricted by an
asset-allocation taxonomy rather than selected from an unrestricted
predictor universe. Each specification contains one equity proxy, one
sovereign-bond proxy, and gold. These three asset classes form a
conventional liquid core of asset allocation and represent economically
distinct outside opportunities: equities proxy for risk-bearing capital,
sovereign bonds for duration and safe-asset demand, and gold for a
non-credit safe-haven asset. Within equities and bonds, I vary the
regional block along a standard benchmark-index dimension—U.S.,
developed ex-U.S., and emerging markets. This design keeps the
specification interpretable and prevents the asset block from becoming
an unrestricted search over unrelated predictors.

The main specification uses IEFA, IGOV, and IAU, representing developed
ex-U.S. equity, developed ex-U.S. sovereign bonds, and gold. Their
effective horizons differ sharply: IEFA aligns with the carry gap at a
relatively short horizon, while IGOV and IAU operate at longer
low-frequency horizons. Introducing the asset-return block also makes
the long-horizon OIS-based GBM term empirically redundant, while the
short-horizon OIS-based component remains informative. This pattern
does not imply that OIS-based opportunity costs are irrelevant. Rather,
it indicates that the short-rate OIS component remains useful, while
slow-moving variation in the carry gap is better summarized by the
asset-return outside-option block than by the long-horizon OIS term
alone.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it shows that a
put–call-parity-based Q-measure residual is not empirically separable
from P-measure outside-option proxies. This result should be read as
reduced-form evidence of P–Q alignment, not as a structural
P
\to
Q transmission model. Second, it shows that the relevant
outside-option information is more visible in foreign-tilted
asset-allocation blocks than in U.S.-only blocks, although all
asset-augmented specifications improve on the OIS-only baseline.
Third, it documents horizon heterogeneity across asset classes,
indicating that the opportunity-cost component of parity enforcement
does not collapse to a single representative asset or common frequency.

2
Related Literature

This paper connects to five strands of literature: put–call parity,
limits to arbitrage, CIP deviations, risk-neutral objects and
physical-measure information, and option-implied discount factors. The
common theme is that textbook no-arbitrage relations impose terminal
pricing restrictions, whereas their real-world enforcement can be
path-dependent and capital-using. The contribution of this paper is to
study whether a carry-space object constructed from option-implied and
OIS discount factors is empirically separable from low-frequency
physical-measure outside-option proxies.

2.1
Put–Call Parity and Empirical Deviations

Put–call parity was systematically formalized by
Stoll 1969
, and
the subsequent empirical literature has examined whether observed
deviations represent genuine arbitrage opportunities or reflect
execution costs and constraints
(
Gould and Galai 1974
;
Klemkosky and Resnick 1979
;
Ackert and Tian 2001
)
. The shared
conclusion is that observed deviations should not be interpreted
mechanically as failures of no-arbitrage logic, because transaction
costs, short-sale constraints, and execution frictions can generate
empirical departures from the frictionless benchmark.

This paper shares that perspective but studies a different object. The
focus is not on whether visible price-space deviations from parity are
large enough to constitute immediate arbitrage opportunities. Instead, I
aggregate parity information into a daily carry-space object and ask
whether its dynamics are related to state variables outside the options
market. In this sense, the paper shifts attention from the existence of
quoted parity deviations to the low-frequency structure of the
option-implied discounting wedge.

2.2
Limits to Arbitrage and Implementation Risk

This perspective connects naturally to the limits-to-arbitrage
literature. Following
Shleifer and Vishny 1997
, subsequent work shows that
arbitrage is exposed to funding constraints, margin requirements,
path-dependent profits and losses, and finite-capital problems, so that
theoretical price deviations need not be eliminated immediately

(
Gromb and Vayanos 2002
;
Brunnermeier and Pedersen 2009
;
Mitchell and Pulvino 2012
)
. In the options-market context,
Ofek et al. 2004

show that short-sale constraints and limits to arbitrage can be linked
to parity deviations.
Muravyev et al. 2025
further show that much of the
predictive power for individual stock returns attributed to
implied-volatility spreads and skews in single-stock options reflects a
measurement artifact arising from the omission of stock borrow fees in
the implied-volatility calculation.

The SPX and RUT contracts examined here are index options, for which
borrow-fee effects are much less direct. Moreover, the carry gap is
constructed from the wedge between option-implied and OIS discount
factors rather than from implied-volatility spreads. The paper therefore
inherits the implementation-risk logic of this literature, but studies a
different object: a systematic carry-space wedge in index options rather
than a stock-borrow-related implied-volatility artifact. The relevant
question is not whether a visible arbitrage gap remains open, but
whether the carry-space wedge is systematically related to variables
that proxy for the cost of allocating scarce arbitrage capital.

2.3
Conceptual Connection to CIP Deviations

Du et al. 2018
document that, since the global financial crisis, CIP
deviations have been persistent across G10 currencies, with magnitudes
linked to quarter-end balance-sheet constraints, intermediary
balance-sheet costs, and fixed-income liquidity spreads. Their results
show that deviations from textbook no-arbitrage relations may reflect
systematic intermediation costs and capital constraints rather than
purely transitory frictions.

This paper studies an analogous enforcement problem in equity index
options, but the analogy is conceptual rather than identificational.
Whereas
Du et al. 2018
examine the cross-currency basis in FX swap
markets, I construct a carry gap from the wedge between option-implied
and OIS discount factors. The measurement object, institutional setting,
and market participants differ. The common economic theme is narrower:
a no-arbitrage relation can be terminally valid while its enforcement
requires balance-sheet capacity, margin support, and capital commitment
along the path.

2.4
Risk-Neutral Objects and Physical-Measure Information

The motivating question also relates to the literature on the
relationship between risk-neutral objects extracted from option prices
and physical-measure expected returns or risk premia.
Ross 2015

provides conditions under which physical probabilities can be recovered
from state prices;
Bollerslev et al. 2009
show that the variance risk premium can
be linked to expected stock returns; and
Martin 2017
shows that
risk-neutral variance obtained from option prices can carry information
about expected market returns. The common direction of this literature
is information extraction from Q-measure objects to P-measure
quantities.

This paper is complementary but distinct. I do not recover physical
probabilities from option prices, nor do I estimate a structural mapping
from physical-measure returns to risk-neutral prices. Instead, I ask
whether a Q-measure carry-space residual is empirically separable from
P-measure outside-option proxies. The evidence is best interpreted as
reduced-form P–Q alignment: physical-measure asset-return components
align with the carry gap, but the paper does not identify a structural
P
\to
Q transmission mechanism.

The structure I uncover—multiple asset classes loading on the carry
gap at distinct horizons—is also conceptually consistent with the
ICAPM of
Merton 1973
, in which investors hedge variation in the
future investment-opportunity set using multiple state variables. A
structural ICAPM test lies beyond the scope of this paper. The narrower
claim is that the carry gap behaves as if the opportunity cost of
parity-enforcement capital reflects multiple low-frequency components
of the outside investment-opportunity set.

2.5
Option-Implied Discount Factors and Closely Related Work

Azzone and Baviera 2021
estimate option-market-implied discount factors from
European put–call parity and propose an empirical pipeline for
comparing them against an exogenous discount benchmark. I adopt this
approach directly to construct the carry gap and use it as the starting
point of the analysis.

This paper also extends
Shin 2026
.
Shin 2026
documents the
existence and basic state-dependence of the carry gap and introduces a
GBM path-risk term that summarizes the reduced-form structure of the
enforcement burden. Building on that baseline, I reinterpret the
rate-like component of the GBM term as an opportunity-cost component and
introduce physical-measure asset-return components as outside-option
proxies. The contribution is therefore not merely to add predictors, but
to test whether an OIS-only representation is empirically sufficient for
summarizing the opportunity-cost component of parity enforcement.

3
Framework and Baseline Specification

3.1
Identification of Option-Implied Discount Factors and the Carry Gap

I estimate option-market-implied discount factors following the
identification procedure of
Azzone and Baviera 2021
. For European calls and puts
at time
t
sharing maturity
T
and strike
K
, put–call parity can
be written as

C_{t}(K,T)-P_{t}(K,T)=B_{t}(T)\bigl(F_{t}(T)-K\bigr),

(1)

where
B_{t}(T)
is the market-implied discounting factor and
F_{t}(T)

the forward value at the same maturity.

The key idea of
Azzone and Baviera 2021
is to use the synthetic forward

\mathcal{G}_{t}(K,T)=C_{t}(K,T)-P_{t}(K,T)

(2)

to identify the discount factor
\hat{B}_{t}(T)
that renders the
recovered forward value flat across strikes. I apply this procedure
repeatedly to the full SPX and RUT samples, producing a panel of
option-implied discount factors indexed by market, date, and maturity.

The benchmark discount factor is taken from the OIS curve. The carry
gap is defined as the annualized wedge between the option-implied and
OIS discount factors. Letting
\tau_{t}(T)=T-t
,

CG_{t}(T)=\frac{1}{\tau_{t}(T)}\log\!\left(\frac{D_{t}^{\mathrm{OIS}}(T)}{\hat{B}_{t}(T)}\right),

(3)

and in the empirical analysis I use the basis-points version

CG_{t}^{bp}(T)=10^{4}\cdot CG_{t}(T).

(4)

In the regression analysis, the daily-aggregated market-level carry gap
is denoted
CG_{i,t}^{bp}
.

3.2
Construction of the GBM Term

I introduce the GBM term as a reduced-form component summarizing the
path-dependent risk and capital commitment of parity enforcement.
Although a parity position is deterministic at the terminal payoff,
maintaining it before maturity is not frictionless: the trader remains
exposed to interim mark-to-market losses, margin requirements, and the
possibility of additional capital injections. The economic burden of
enforcement may therefore vary systematically with volatility, time to
maturity, and the opportunity cost of capital.

A simple way to formalize this intuition is to approximate the
normalized interim profit-and-loss process by

X_{t}=\sigma B_{t},

where
B_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion and
\sigma
denotes
annualized volatility. Let
L_{t}
denote the minimum cumulative capital
support needed to keep the position solvent throughout
[0,T]
. Under
the minimal support rule,

L_{t}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(-X_{s})^{+}.

A standard property of Brownian motion implies that the expected support
at horizon
t
scales as

\mathbb{E}[L_{t}/N]=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2t}{\pi}},

so the required support is proportional to volatility and rises with the
square root of time. Averaging over the life of the trade gives the
representative capital commitment

\bar{B}(T)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[L_{t}/N]\,dt=\frac{2}{3}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2T}{\pi}}.

If the opportunity cost of tied-up capital is summarized by a rate-like
object
r_{t}
, the implied scaling is
r_{t}\bar{B}(T)
. I interpret

r_{t}
broadly as an opportunity-cost component rather than narrowly
as the risk-free rate. In the baseline specification, OIS rates provide
the natural risk-free opportunity-cost proxies. In the asset-return
extension below, the same slot is used to proxy for outside investment
opportunities faced by a finite-capital parity enforcer. The GBM term
used in the empirical specification is this path-risk scale multiplied
by an opportunity-cost component and translated into basis points.

Accordingly, for market
i\in\{\mathrm{SPX},\mathrm{RUT}\}
, I define

GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{OIS},xY}=10^{4}\cdot\frac{OIS_{t}^{xY}}{100}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{Vol_{i,t}}{100}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\tau_{i,t}}{\pi}},\qquad x\in\{1,10\},

(5)

where

Vol_{i,t}=\begin{cases}VIX_{t},&i=\mathrm{SPX},\\
RVX_{t},&i=\mathrm{RUT}.\end{cases}

GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}
and

GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}
are therefore baseline OIS-based
opportunity-cost components at shorter and longer horizons,
respectively. They provide a risk-free benchmark for the opportunity
cost of parity-enforcement capital, but need not exhaust that cost for a
finite-capital arbitrageur. The purpose here is not to estimate a
structural model of parity enforcement, but to embed in the empirical
specification the core functional form suggested by path-dependent
implementation risk: a path-risk scale multiplied by an
opportunity-cost component.

3.3
Baseline Regression

The baseline specification is a separate regression that directly
allows for cross-market heterogeneity. For each market

i\in\{\mathrm{SPX},\mathrm{RUT}\}
,

CG_{i,t}^{bp}=\alpha_{i}+\phi_{1,i}GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}+\phi_{10,i}GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}+\beta_{i}\frac{BA_{i,t}^{med}}{\tau_{i,t}}+\gamma_{i}NFCI_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t},

(6)

where
BA_{i,t}^{med}
is the median ATM bid–ask spread and
NFCI_{t}

is the Chicago Fed National Financial Conditions Index.

This baseline, established in
Shin 2026

the systematic link between the carry gap, the GBM term, trading
frictions, and financial conditions.

4
Asset-Return Extension and Derivation of the 3ETF Specification

4.1
Motivation for Asset-Return Components

The central question raised in the introduction is whether the carry
gap, although constructed as a Q-measure object from option-implied and
OIS discount factors, is empirically separable from physical-measure
investment opportunities. I study this question by extending the
baseline GBM specification. The extension does not add generic asset
returns as unrestricted predictors. Instead, it replaces the
opportunity-cost component of the GBM term with low-frequency
outside-option return proxies.

The baseline GBM term can be written conceptually as

\text{GBM term}=\text{path-risk scale}\times\text{opportunity-cost component}.

In the OIS baseline, the opportunity-cost component is proxied by OIS
rates. This is the natural benchmark for a frictionless or
risk-free-funded agent. For a finite-capital arbitrageur, however,
capital committed to parity enforcement competes with alternative uses
of capital. The relevant opportunity cost may therefore reflect the
shadow return on outside investment opportunities rather than the OIS
rate alone. This motivates replacing the rate-like component of the GBM
term with low-frequency return components from major asset classes.

To construct these outside-option proxies, I use the OLS slope of

\log
prices over a lookback window of
n
trading days. The slope is
a low-frequency proxy for the asset’s continuously compounded return.
Annualizing it would multiply the regressor by an approximately constant
factor, which is absorbed by the estimated regression coefficient. I
therefore use the unannualized slope directly. To preclude look-ahead
bias, the slope used at date
t
is computed only from information
through
t-1
, excluding the contemporaneous price. Formally, the
low-frequency return component for asset
a
,

\tilde{r}_{a,t}^{(n)}
, is the slope of a linear approximation to the
most recent
n
-day log-price path ending at
t-1
. This construction
filters high-frequency noise while retaining persistent outside-option
variation.

The asset universe is deliberately restricted by an asset-allocation
taxonomy rather than selected from an unrestricted predictor set. Each
candidate specification contains one equity proxy, one sovereign-bond
proxy, and gold. These three asset classes form a conventional liquid
core of global asset allocation and represent economically distinct
outside opportunities: equities proxy for risk-bearing capital,
sovereign bonds for duration and safe-asset demand, and gold for a
non-credit safe-haven asset. Within equities and bonds, I vary the
regional block along a standard benchmark-index dimension: U.S.,
developed ex-U.S., and emerging markets. This design keeps the
specification economically interpretable and prevents the exercise from
becoming an unrestricted search over unrelated ETFs.

The empirical question is therefore narrow: within this constrained
asset-allocation design, which outside-option component, at which
lookback horizon, aligns most closely with the carry gap? I answer this
question with asset-by-asset horizon scans and then evaluate the selected
specification in sample, out of sample, and under additional robustness
checks including broad-dollar neutralization, alternative regional
blocks, and nested horizon selection.

4.3
Selection of the 3ETF Specification under a Minimalist
View

Among the candidate assets, the main specification restricts attention
to three ETF components, balancing explanatory power against
parsimony. Following the conventional asset-allocation taxonomy, I
select one equity, one bond, and one gold proxy, and consider three
combinations along a regional axis: a U.S.-centered combination
(VTI, BND, IAU), a developed ex-U.S. combination (IEFA, IGOV, IAU),
and an emerging-market-centered combination (IEMG, EBND, IAU). The
developed ex-U.S. combination delivers the strongest in-sample
explanatory power overall and is therefore adopted as the main
specification; the other two are revisited as alternative-asset
robustness checks in Section
6.3
.

The selected assets are thus IEFA (developed ex-U.S. equity), IGOV
(international government bond), and IAU (gold). They represent
distinct economic categories, reducing explanatory overlap while
spanning a broad outside-option set. All three deliver meaningful
incremental
R^{2}
over the baseline in single-asset scans, repeatedly
in both markets, and among the strongest candidates the non-U.S.
assets tend to deliver larger incremental
R^{2}
than their U.S.
counterparts. This pattern suggests that the outside-option channel
aligned with the carry gap is connected to a global investment-
opportunity set broader than U.S. domestic asset classes. The main
specification is therefore not the result of mechanically adding many
assets, but of combining a minimal set of strong-explanatory non-U.S.
candidates with heterogeneous frequencies.

The final lookback windows are 70 trading days for IEFA, 441 for
IGOV, and 315 for IAU. These values preserve a single specification
applicable to both markets while representing the effective horizon
band at which each asset aligns most strongly with the carry gap.
IEFA captures the relatively fast-moving foreign-equity component,
IGOV captures a much slower international sovereign-bond component,
and IAU captures an intermediate gold component.

faster channel coexists with the slower dynamics of IGOV and IAU,
supporting a multi-channel structure. The improvement delivered by
the 3ETF specification is thus unlikely to be a mere indirect
restatement of a single global factor, and the cross-asset frequency
heterogeneity documented earlier is visible not only at the
R^{2}

4.4
The Final 3ETF Regression

Building on these results, the final extended specification augments
the baseline separate regression with three asset-return-based GBM
terms. For asset
a
and lookback window
n
, I construct

GBM_{i,t}^{a,n}=10^{4}\cdot\tilde{r}_{a,t}^{(n)}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{Vol_{i,t}}{100}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\tau_{i,t}}{\pi}}.

(7)

This term replaces the OIS rate in the opportunity-cost component of
the baseline GBM term with a low-frequency outside-option return proxy.
The slope
\tilde{r}_{a,t}^{(n)}
is left in daily log-slope units.
Annualizing it would multiply the regressor by an approximately
constant factor, which is absorbed by the estimated coefficient

\theta_{a,i}
. The economic content of the term is therefore a
path-risk scale multiplied by an outside-option opportunity-cost proxy.

Relative to the baseline specification, the main 3ETF specification
excludes the OIS 10Y GBM term. This choice is empirical rather than
conceptual. The horizon-search results show that the IGOV component is
strongly aligned with the long-horizon opportunity-cost variation
previously captured by the OIS 10Y term. Including both terms induces
multicollinearity and disturbs the coefficient structure of the
asset-return specification. Once the OIS 10Y term is removed, the
explanatory power is essentially unchanged, while the coefficient signs
and magnitudes become more stable. I therefore retain OIS 1Y as the
short-horizon OIS-based opportunity-cost component, but drop OIS 10Y
from the main 3ETF regression.

The final regression for market
i\in\{\mathrm{SPX},\mathrm{RUT}\}
is
then

\displaystyle CG_{i,t}^{bp}={}

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}+\phi_{1,i}GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}

(8)

\displaystyle+\theta_{E,i}GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{IEFA},70}+\theta_{G,i}GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{IGOV},441}+\theta_{A,i}GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{IAU},315}

\displaystyle+\beta_{i}\frac{BA_{i,t}^{med}}{\tau_{i,t}}+\gamma_{i}NFCI_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}.

Here
GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{IEFA},70}
,

GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{IGOV},441}
, and

GBM_{i,t}^{\mathrm{IAU},315}
are constructed from the 70-day,
441-day, and 315-day log OLS slopes of IEFA, IGOV, and IAU,
respectively. The final specification therefore layers a disciplined
outside-option block—foreign equity, foreign sovereign bonds, and
gold—on top of the short-horizon OIS-based opportunity-cost component
retained from the baseline. The next sections examine the in-sample fit,
out-of-sample performance, and time-series fit of
equation (
8
).

The baseline columns retain both OIS-based GBM terms, while the 3ETF

OIS 10Y component with the IGOV-based asset-return term. This
specification choice reflects the empirical multicollinearity between
OIS 10Y and IGOV: including both terms disturbs the coefficient
structure, whereas dropping OIS 10Y leaves explanatory power essentially
unchanged and improves coefficient stability.

The most prominent common result is that the IEFA term carries a
consistently negative sign and strong statistical significance in
both markets. Foreign equity is therefore the asset-return-based
channel that aligns most stably with the carry gap. The IGOV term
loads with a positive coefficient in both markets, but is statistically
significant only in SPX. The IAU term is positive in both markets and
statistically significant in both, with a stronger coefficient in RUT.
Thus, the most robust common asset-return channels are IEFA and IAU,
while IGOV operates more visibly in SPX and is weaker in RUT.

The sign structure is consistent with the paper’s interpretation of
the carry gap as an implementation-risk premium. A positive carry gap
in put–call parity means that the option-implied forward is priced
rich relative to the OIS benchmark; this premium is paid by the
arbitrageur enforcing parity through a forward-sale leg—the agent
bearing path-dependent mark-to-market losses and capital
commitment—to the forward buyer. Under this interpretation, the
negative IEFA coefficient is consistent with periods in which strong
low-frequency global risk-asset returns expand the enforcer’s
risk-bearing capacity, compressing the premium required for a given
path risk. The positive IAU and IGOV coefficients are consistent with
the opposite case: in risk-off or safe-asset-demand episodes, the
enforcer’s capacity contracts and the premium widens. The asset terms
therefore capture different phases by asset class but admit a single
mechanism: variation in the enforcer’s risk-bearing capacity is
reflected in the implementation-risk premium.

The coefficient structure also clarifies the role of the OIS-based
GBM terms. In the baseline,
GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}
is negative and

GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}
is positive in both markets. Once the 3ETF
components are introduced and OIS 10Y is removed, the OIS 1Y term
remains negative and statistically significant in both SPX and RUT,
but its magnitude shrinks sharply. This indicates that the short-
horizon OIS-based opportunity-cost channel is not fully displaced,
while much of the long-horizon variation previously captured by
OIS 10Y is better represented by the asset-return components,
especially IGOV.

The trading-friction and financial-conditions variables continue to
play important roles.
BA^{med}/\tau
remains positive and
statistically significant in both markets and both specifications,
indicating that the trading-friction channel operates on the carry
gap independently of the asset-return components.
NFCI
is
significant only at the 5% level in the baseline but, under the
3ETF specification, takes on a larger negative coefficient with
strong significance in both markets. Broad financial conditions
therefore continue to explain residual variation in the carry gap
even after controlling for the asset-return-based low-frequency
components.

The overall message survives lag 21 HAC inference. The dynamics of
the carry gap, a Q-measure object, are not adequately summarized by
OIS-based rate components inside the risk-neutral framework alone.
Low-frequency asset-return components reflecting P-measure investment
opportunities provide significant additional explanatory power, while
the exclusion of the collinear OIS 10Y term yields a cleaner and more
stable coefficient structure.

6.1

To assess whether the explanatory power of the foreign-asset-based GBM
terms merely reflects common variation in the U.S. dollar in disguise,
this subsection conducts a robustness analysis using the broad-dollar
index
DTWEXBGS
. Specifically, I divide the price indices of
IEFA, IGOV, and IAU by the broad-dollar index to construct
FX-neutralized series and recompute each asset’s
\log
OLS slope from
those series. Because
DTWEXBGS
does not align perfectly with
the trading-day calendar of the asset price data, missing values are

GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}
but
drops
GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}
once the asset-return components are
introduced. The motivation is that the long-rate OIS term overlaps
conceptually and empirically with slow-moving foreign-bond and
dollar-cycle components. The baseline specification remains unchanged
and includes both OIS-based GBM terms.

GBM^{\mathrm{IEFA,FXN},70}
is negative and highly
significant, while
GBM^{\mathrm{IAU,FXN},315}
is positive and highly
significant. The IEFA component therefore captures a robust
risk-asset outside-option channel, whereas the IAU component captures a
robust safe-haven or risk-off channel. These two components remain
strong even after the broad-dollar component is removed.

The IGOV component behaves differently. Although

GBM^{\mathrm{IGOV,FXN},441}
retains a positive point estimate in both
SPX and RUT, it is not statistically significant in either market under
lag 21 HAC inference. This result weakens the interpretation of IGOV
as a robust stand-alone FX-orthogonal outside-option channel. The
foreign-sovereign-bond component is therefore better read as a
selective or less stable channel whose explanatory power in the
non-neutralized specification is likely intertwined with the
broad-dollar cycle and long-rate variation.

Removing
GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}
from the extended specification does
not eliminate the role of the OIS-based path-risk block. Instead,

GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}
remains negative and significant in both
markets. This indicates that a short-horizon OIS-based opportunity-cost
component continues to explain residual variation in the carry gap even
after the FX-neutralized asset-return terms are added. The absorption
pattern is therefore more nuanced than a complete displacement of
OIS-based variables: the long-rate OIS term becomes redundant in the
extended specification, but the short-rate OIS term remains useful.

The trading-friction and financial-conditions controls also remain
stable.
BA^{med}/\tau
is positive and significant in both markets,
indicating that trading frictions widen the carry gap independently of
the asset-return components.
NFCI
is negative and highly significant
in both extended specifications, showing that broad financial
conditions continue to explain variation in the carry gap after
controlling for both FX-neutralized asset returns and the remaining
OIS-based GBM term.

Overall, the coefficient evidence supports a heterogeneous P
\to
Q
alignment rather than a single homogeneous channel. The robust
FX-orthogonal component is carried primarily by foreign equity and
gold: IEFA enters with a stable negative sign, while IAU enters with a
stable positive sign. IGOV retains the expected positive sign but lacks
cross-market statistical strength once broad-dollar exposure and the
long-rate OIS component are removed. The parsimonious FX-neutralized
specification therefore identifies IEFA and IAU as the main robust
asset-return channels, with IGOV serving as a weaker and more
conditional foreign-bond component.

6.2
Principal-component and residualization tests

This subsection examines whether the explanatory power of the 3ETF
block is merely a disguised single common factor. The concern is that
IEFA, IGOV, and IAU may all be indirectly capturing the same global
cycle, such as a broad-dollar or risk-off component. I address this
concern in two complementary ways. First, I apply principal-component
analysis to the three standardized asset-slope series. Second, I replace
the raw asset terms with mutually orthogonal principal components and
verify whether the explanatory power of the model is preserved.

The pairwise correlations among the three slope series are modest. The
correlation between IEFA and IGOV is only 0.088, the correlation between
IEFA and IAU is 0.170, and the correlation between IGOV and IAU is 0.381.
Thus, even before applying PCA, the three asset components do not behave
as a single tightly collinear block.

principal component captures an important common slow-moving component,
but it does not dominate the asset block. A distinct IEFA-dominant
component and a residual IGOV–IAU component remain quantitatively
substantial, and the carry-gap regression retains its explanatory power
after orthogonalization. The 3ETF specification is therefore better read
as a multi-channel low-frequency outside-option structure rather than as
a repeated proxy for one latent global factor.

7
Discussion

This section consolidates the economic content of the empirical
results and discusses both their interpretive scope and their
limitations. The emphasis is not only on the explanatory gain itself,
but also on what the asset-return extension absorbs from the
baseline, which channels remain robust under HAC inference and FX
neutralization, and why the alignment differs across SPX and RUT.

7.1
Empirical Implications of P–Q Alignment

The central implication of the analysis is that the carry gap,
although constructed from option-implied and OIS discount factors, is
not empirically separable from physical-measure outside-option proxies.
The result should be interpreted as reduced-form P–Q alignment rather
than as evidence of a structurally identified transmission channel. The
baseline specification summarizes the carry gap using OIS-based GBM
terms, trading frictions, and financial conditions. Once low-frequency
asset-return components are added, however, a substantial part of the
variation previously associated with the long-horizon OIS component is
better summarized by outside-option proxies.

This finding does not diminish the usefulness of the risk-neutral
framework. Rather, it clarifies the empirical boundary of an OIS-only
representation of parity-enforcement costs. The risk-neutral framework
prices terminal payoffs, whereas maintaining a parity-enforcement
position before maturity exposes the arbitrageur to mark-to-market
losses, margin requirements, and finite-capital constraints. The capital
committed to this enforcement path competes with other uses of capital
under the physical measure. If those outside opportunities vary
systematically over time, then the carry gap may display reduced-form
alignment with P-measure asset-return components even though it is
constructed from Q-measure pricing objects.

The absorption pattern should therefore be read carefully. The
asset-return extension does not mechanically eliminate all OIS-based
content. In the final 3ETF specification,
GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}
is
excluded because it overlaps with the slow-moving foreign-bond component
and destabilizes the coefficient structure when included jointly with
IGOV. By contrast,
GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}
remains in the model and
continues to load negatively and significantly in both markets. The
P–Q alignment documented here is therefore not a complete displacement
of the OIS block. It is a partial substitution in which the
long-horizon OIS component becomes redundant once the asset-return block
is introduced, while the short-horizon OIS component remains useful.

This distinction is important for interpretation. The evidence does not
imply that OIS-based opportunity costs are irrelevant. It implies that
the OIS-only baseline is empirically incomplete: the short-rate
component remains informative, but the low-frequency component of the
carry gap is more closely aligned with asset returns that proxy for
outside investment opportunities. The carry gap is therefore best read
as a Q-measure object whose empirical dynamics are partly aligned with
P-measure outside-option proxies.

7.2
Mechanism of the GBM Loading

The coefficients on the GBM terms should not be interpreted as
structural primitive effects. They are reduced-form net loadings that
combine several margins of parity enforcement. This point is important
because the GBM term is designed to summarize the path-dependent
capital burden of enforcing a terminal no-arbitrage relation, not to
isolate a single causal channel.

First, put–call parity can be enforced in both directions. A trader
may take the synthetic-forward-minus-futures leg,
+C-P-F
, or the
opposite leg,
-C+P+F
. Both positions are exposed to path-dependent
mark-to-market losses and capital support requirements, but they move
the measured carry gap in opposite directions. The observed coefficient
on a GBM term therefore depends on the net directional imbalance
between the two enforcement pressures.

Second, the GBM term contains a path-risk scale effect. Holding the
direction and participation margin fixed, higher volatility, longer
maturity, or a larger rate-like opportunity-cost component increases
the capital burden associated with sustaining the arbitrage position.
This is the mechanical channel captured by the GBM construction.

Third, the same state variable may also affect the intensity with
which arbitrage capital is deployed. When outside investment
opportunities become more attractive, or when the cost of funding
rises, enforcing parity becomes less attractive relative to alternative
uses of capital. In that case, the per-unit path risk of an arbitrage
position may increase, while the amount of arbitrage capital willing
to bear and transmit that path risk into the carry gap may decline.
The observed loading is then the net result of a scale effect and an
arbitrage-intensity, or pass-through, effect that can work in opposite
directions.

The signs and magnitudes of the GBM coefficients should therefore be
interpreted with caution. A positive coefficient does not mean that
the corresponding state variable mechanically raises the primitive cost
of every parity trade, and a negative coefficient does not mean that
path risk itself falls. Rather, each coefficient reflects the
equilibrium net effect of directional imbalance, the scale of
path-dependent capital support, and the degree to which arbitrage
capital transmits that burden into the observed carry gap. The present
evidence therefore supports the GBM term as a useful reduced-form
state variable for parity-enforcement costs, while leaving the
structural decomposition of these margins for future research.

7.3
Relative Strength of Foreign-Asset Explanatory Power

As Figure
4.1
shows, the pattern of foreign assets
delivering relatively stronger explanatory power than U.S. domestic
assets is already visible at the single-asset stage. In equities, IEFA
and IEMG yield larger incremental
R^{2}
than VTI; in bonds, IGOV exceeds
BND and SCHP; and in real estate, VNQI exceeds VNQ.

This pattern is confirmed more systematically in the alternative-
combination analysis of Section
6
.
Table
6.8
shows that the U.S.-only
specification (VTI + BND + IAU) provides a meaningful improvement over
the baseline but is weaker than both the developed ex-U.S. specification
(IEFA + IGOV + IAU) adopted in the main analysis and the emerging-market
specification (IEMG + EBND + IAU). The outside-option information
aligned with the carry gap therefore appears more visible in global
asset blocks than in U.S. domestic asset classes alone.

It would be an overreading, however, to conclude that all foreign assets
are uniformly strong. Under HAC-based inference and FX neutralization,
robustness varies clearly across foreign assets. IEFA and IAU retain
stable signs and significance across both markets, whereas IGOV is
stronger in SPX in the main specification but weakens after broad-dollar
neutralization. The core of the foreign-asset result is therefore not
that foreign assets are generically superior predictors, but that
foreign equity and gold reappear as particularly robust outside-option
proxies.

The precise mechanism behind this relative strength lies beyond the
scope of this paper. One possibility is that foreign assets capture
components of the global investment-opportunity set that are less
collinear with variables internal to the U.S. options market. Another is
that global asset-allocation proxies better summarize the opportunity
set faced by marginal arbitrage capital than U.S. domestic assets alone.
The greater instability of the IGOV channel further suggests that the
foreign-bond component combines several sources of variation, including
currency exposure, dollar-cycle variation, duration, and long-rate
dynamics.

7.4
Common Structure and Cross-Market Heterogeneity

SPX and RUT share a common carry-gap structure, but the channels through
which that structure appears are not identical. In both markets, the
IEFA component is negative and statistically robust, and the IAU
component is positive and statistically robust. This common sign pattern
is consistent with an interpretation in which the carry gap is related
to the risk-bearing capacity of parity enforcers: foreign equity proxies
for a risk-asset outside-option channel, whereas gold proxies for a
safe-haven or risk-off channel.

The two markets differ in the strength of the slower bond-like
component and in the extent to which asset-return terms substitute for
OIS-based variation. In SPX, the main developed ex-U.S. block is the
strongest overall specification, and IGOV is more visibly active in the
main non-neutralized regression. In RUT, IGOV is weaker, IAU is strongly
significant, and the emerging-market alternative performs best out of
sample. These differences suggest that the same reduced-form alignment
may be projected through different outside-option proxies depending on
the underlying index.

The stronger out-of-sample performance of the emerging-market
specification in RUT is economically plausible. RUT is a small-cap
index, and the capital committed to enforcing parity in RUT options may
be more sensitive to broad risk appetite and liquidity conditions than
the corresponding SPX position. Emerging-market assets are not merely
regional equity or bond proxies; they also contain information about
global risk tolerance, dollar funding conditions, external financing
constraints, and the willingness of investors to hold high-beta or
liquidity-sensitive assets. These variables are closely related to the
marginal value of risk-bearing capital.

Under this interpretation, the emerging-market block performs well in
RUT because it captures a global-risk and liquidity-sensitive component
of the outside-option set more sharply than the developed ex-U.S. block.
SPX, by contrast, is more closely connected to large-cap, developed-
market, and institutional allocation channels, so the developed ex-U.S.
specification delivers the strongest overall performance there. The
difference should not be read as evidence that the structural channel is
different in kind across the two markets. Rather, it suggests that a
common reduced-form alignment can appear through different empirical
proxies across index-option markets.

The OIS-based component also shows cross-market nuance. The final 3ETF
specification drops
GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}
in both markets, but

GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}
remains negative and significant. Thus, the
relevant distinction is not simply “OIS versus assets” but horizon.
The short-horizon OIS-based opportunity-cost component continues to
matter, while the long-horizon OIS component is better summarized by the
low-frequency asset-return block.

The two markets are therefore best understood as sharing a common
implementation-risk interpretation while differing in the outside-option
proxies through which the slow component appears. IEFA and IAU form the
robust cross-market core. IGOV is more selective, more visible in SPX,
and less robust after FX neutralization. RUT, by contrast, appears to
admit a broader foreign-tilted or emerging-market risk component. These
differences point to heterogeneity in the empirical proxy structure,
without requiring a fully identified market-specific structural model.

7.5
A Reduced-Form Asset-Pricing Interpretation

The observed P–Q alignment admits an asset-pricing interpretation,
although the evidence remains reduced-form. The most important feature
is the coexistence of multiple low-frequency components with different
effective horizons. IEFA aligns with the carry gap at a relatively short
horizon of 70 trading days, IGOV at a much slower horizon of 441 trading
days, and IAU at an intermediate horizon of 315 trading days. This
horizon heterogeneity suggests that the opportunity-cost component of
parity enforcement does not collapse to a single representative asset or
a single frequency.

This structure is conceptually consistent with an ICAPM-style view in
which investors care about changes in the investment-opportunity set

(
Merton 1973
)
. In the present context, the parity enforcer is not
only exposed to a terminal payoff relation under
\mathbb{Q}
; the
enforcer must also allocate capital across competing opportunities while
surviving the path of mark-to-market losses under
\mathbb{P}
. If
alternative investment opportunities vary at different frequencies, then
the reduced-form implementation premium reflected in the carry gap may
load on multiple outside-option proxies with heterogeneous horizons.

The sign pattern should be interpreted as a reduced-form equilibrium
outcome rather than as a one-to-one mapping from asset returns to
primitive arbitrage costs. The negative IEFA coefficient is consistent
with states in which favorable global risk-asset conditions expand
risk-bearing capacity or reduce the pass-through of path risk into the
carry gap. The positive IAU coefficient is consistent with risk-off
states in which safe-haven demand and scarce arbitrage capital are
associated with a wider implementation premium. IGOV has the same
positive sign in the main specification, but is less stable after FX
neutralization, suggesting that the foreign-bond component mixes
duration, currency, and dollar-cycle channels rather than representing a
clean stand-alone outside-option factor.

This interpretation is deliberately reduced-form. The results do not
identify the structural stochastic discount factor of parity
enforcement, nor do they prove that the asset-return components are
priced state variables in the ICAPM sense. They show that the carry gap
behaves as if the cost of enforcing a risk-neutral parity relation is
related to physical-measure investment opportunities. Identifying the
exact structural mechanism remains a task for future research.

7.6
Implications for Future Work Beyond Put–Call Parity

The interpretation developed here may be relevant for other parity-
enforcement settings, but such extensions are beyond the formal scope of
this paper. More generally, arbitrage strategies involving futures or
forward legs may expose intermediaries to interim mark-to-market losses,
variation margin, and finite-capital constraints. These features are not
unique to equity index options, so related implementation-risk terms may
also arise in other markets.

Preliminary evidence from CIP residuals is consistent with this broader
possibility, but should be treated only as suggestive. In a post-GFC
sample, an exploratory regression of government-bond CIP deviation
residuals on a fixed asset-based specification scaled by trailing FX
volatility yields non-trivial explanatory power. The measurement target,
market structure, and institutional environment differ from the main
analysis, so these results should not be read as validation of the same
mechanism. They only motivate future work on whether similar
outside-option alignment appears in other capital-using parity-
enforcement problems involving futures, forwards, variation margin, and
finite intermediary capital.

7.7
Limitations and Future Research

This paper is a reduced-form empirical analysis and therefore does not
fully establish the causal pathway behind the observed alignment. The
asset-return terms are interpretable as outside-option proxies, but they
are not structural state variables identified from a full equilibrium
model. The results should therefore be read as documenting a robust
empirical alignment and an economic interpretation consistent with that
alignment, not as providing a complete structural model of parity
enforcement.

A second limitation is that the observed alignment is heterogeneous
across assets and markets. IEFA and IAU are comparatively robust across
SPX and RUT and remain strong after broad-dollar neutralization. IGOV is
more conditional: it is useful in the main specification, especially for
SPX and for replacing the long-horizon OIS component, but it weakens
after FX neutralization and lacks cross-market statistical strength.
This heterogeneity does not weaken the central result. Rather, it shows
that the opportunity-cost structure of parity enforcement is unlikely to
reduce to a single representative outside asset.

A third limitation concerns the very long end of the maturity structure.
The 3ETF specification improves the medium-maturity range most strongly,
especially around 5–10 months. The far long end is less consistently
improved, and in RUT the 21-month-plus bin deteriorates under the
parsimonious specification. This suggests that additional slow-moving or
maturity-specific components may remain outside the present three-asset
block.

Three directions follow naturally. First, future work should decompose
the foreign-bond channel into currency, duration, safe-asset demand, and
global rate components to clarify why IGOV is less stable than IEFA and
IAU. Second, the analysis should be extended to other parity relations,
especially FX and fixed-income settings where futures, forwards,
collateral, and balance-sheet constraints play central roles. Third, a
structural model is needed to determine whether the heterogeneous
horizon loadings documented here correspond to an ICAPM-style
investment-opportunity-state structure or to a different
implementation-risk mechanism.

8
Conclusion

This paper documents reduced-form P–Q alignment in put–call-parity
carry gaps. The carry gap is constructed from option-implied and OIS
discount factors, and is therefore a Q-measure carry-space object.
Nevertheless, augmenting an OIS-based baseline with low-frequency global
asset-return components substantially improves the explanation of the
carry gap in both SPX and RUT options. The evidence does not identify a
structural P
\to
Q transmission mechanism. It shows instead that the
carry gap is not empirically separable from physical-measure
outside-option proxies.

The main 3ETF specification, built from IEFA, IGOV, and IAU, raises
in-sample
R^{2}
from 0.312 to 0.402 in SPX and from 0.281 to 0.361 in
RUT. The improvement is not confined to in-sample fit. Under
leave-one-year-out validation, pooled out-of-sample
R^{2}
rises from
0.221 to 0.379 in SPX and from 0.171 to 0.318 in RUT. These gains are
obtained even though the extended specification removes

GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},10Y}
, indicating that slow-moving variation
previously associated with the long-horizon OIS component is better
summarized by the low-frequency asset-return block. The result is also
robust to broad-dollar neutralization, alternative asset-allocation
blocks, and nested horizon selection.

The results contribute to the literature in three ways. First, the paper
shows that a put–call-parity-based Q-measure residual is empirically
aligned with P-measure investment-opportunity proxies. Whereas much of
the existing literature studies the Q
\to
P direction—recovering
P-measure information from Q-measure objects—this paper examines the
reverse empirical question: whether a Q-measure carry-space residual is
separable from P-measure outside-option variables. The answer is no in
the data. The carry gap is constructed from discount factors inside the
options market, yet its dynamics are partly organized by
physical-measure asset-return components.

Second, the evidence clarifies what the asset-return block absorbs from
the OIS baseline. The asset-return extension does not eliminate all
OIS-based content:
GBM^{\mathrm{OIS},1Y}
remains useful in the
asset-augmented specification. The long-horizon OIS component, however,
becomes redundant once the low-frequency outside-option block is
introduced. This pattern suggests that the short-horizon OIS-based
component and the longer-horizon asset-return component capture distinct
parts of the carry-gap dynamics.

Third, the effective horizon at which each asset class aligns with the
carry gap differs sharply across assets. IEFA aligns at a relatively
short horizon, IGOV at a much slower horizon, and IAU at an intermediate
horizon. This horizon heterogeneity suggests that the outside-option
structure relevant for the carry gap does not reduce to a single
representative asset or common frequency, but is better summarized by
multiple low-frequency components.

The strongest and most robust components are foreign equity and gold.
IEFA enters with a stable negative sign and IAU with a stable positive
sign, including after broad-dollar neutralization. IGOV remains useful
as a slow foreign-bond component in the main specification, especially
in SPX, but weakens after FX neutralization and is therefore best read
as a more conditional component that mixes foreign-bond, dollar-cycle,
and long-rate information. The alternative-combination analysis
reinforces the broader pattern: U.S.-only assets improve on the
baseline, but foreign-tilted specifications perform more strongly
overall, with the developed ex-U.S. block strongest in SPX and the
emerging-market block especially strong in RUT.

The interpretation should remain deliberately reduced-form. The GBM
coefficients should be read as equilibrium net loadings rather than
primitive structural effects, because they combine directional arbitrage
imbalance, the scale of path-dependent capital support, and the
intensity with which arbitrage capital transmits that burden into the
carry gap. The results are consistent with an opportunity-cost
interpretation of parity enforcement, but they do not identify the
structural stochastic discount factor of the enforcement problem.
Determining whether the heterogeneous horizon loadings documented here
correspond to an ICAPM-style investment-opportunity-state structure or
to a different implementation-risk mechanism requires further structural
modeling and testing.

The central empirical message is therefore narrower than a full
structural theory. Risk-neutral pricing remains the correct language for
terminal payoffs, but it is silent on the capital-using,
path-dependent process through which parity is enforced. Once that
enforcement path is taken seriously, the residual of a model-free
no-arbitrage relation need not be empirically separable from
physical-measure outside opportunities. The carry gap provides evidence
that this alignment is systematic, economically meaningful, and visible
across global asset-return components.

Appendix A
Data and Methodological Details

This appendix collects the implementation details of the data,
identification procedure, variable construction, sample cleaning, and
regression evaluation used in the main text. The body of the paper
focuses on the core intuition and main results; this appendix
supplements that with the concrete procedures needed for replication.

A.1
Data and Analysis Sample

I identify market-implied discounting factors from SPX and RUT
options and construct the carry gap by comparing them with the OIS
discounting factor. The implied discount factors are identified using
the synthetic-forward procedure of
Azzone and Baviera 2021
, the central
advantage of which is that the discount factor implicitly applied by
the market can be recovered from European call and put prices alone
at a common maturity.

Option quote data are minute-level NBBO from ThetaData. The option
data extend through December 31, 2025, but the analysis sample is
restricted to January 4, 2016 through October 31, 2025 to match the
availability of the OIS data. All results in the body of the paper
are based on the common sample on which both the option-market
information and the OIS discount curve are simultaneously observable.

SPX and RUT options are European-style index options, so they carry
no early-exercise premium, which removes a source of institutional
noise from parity-based discount-factor identification.

The empirical analysis is implemented in MATLAB R2025b.

In a
parallel computing environment, the full pipeline ran in roughly one
hour for the two markets combined.

A.2
Identification of Option-Implied Discount Factors

The basic identification logic follows
Azzone and Baviera 2021
. For European
calls and puts at time
t
sharing maturity
T
and strike
K
,
put–call parity is

C_{t}(K,T)-P_{t}(K,T)=B_{t}(T)\bigl(F_{t}(T)-K\bigr),

(9)

where
B_{t}(T)
is the market-implied discounting factor and
F_{t}(T)

the forward value at the same maturity.

Defining the synthetic forward as

\mathcal{G}_{t}(K,T)=C_{t}(K,T)-P_{t}(K,T),

(10)

no-arbitrage requires the forward value to be independent of
K
, so
the market-implied discounting factor is identified as the value that
makes

F_{t}(T)=\frac{\mathcal{G}_{t}(K,T)}{B_{t}(T)}+K

(11)

flat across strikes.

In implementation, for each date–maturity combination I jointly
estimate
\hat{B}_{t}(T)
and
\hat{F}_{t}(T)
from the linear relation
between the synthetic forward and strike across the cross-section of
strikes. The option-implied discount factor is therefore the
discounting coefficient that makes the forward price recovered from
the synthetic forward flat across strikes.

This procedure reduces dependence on a particular ATM contract or any
chosen moneyness range, removes the need to treat dividend estimates
as a separate exogenous input, and mitigates the non-synchronicity
problems that arise when spot, futures, interest-rate, and dividend
data must be combined separately.

A.3
OIS Curve Construction and Carry-Gap Computation

The benchmark is the OIS discounting factor. Since the global
financial crisis, the OIS curve has become the standard discounting
benchmark for derivatives, and
Azzone and Baviera 2021
likewise measure the
funding spread against it.

I apply standard bootstrapping to daily OIS data to recover
discounting factors and zero rates by maturity, then construct
maturity-matched OIS discounting factors aligned with the option
maturities and compare them directly to
\hat{B}_{t}(T)
.

The carry gap is the annualized wedge between the two. Letting

\tau_{t}(T)=T-t
,

CG_{t}(T)=\frac{1}{\tau_{t}(T)}\log\!\left(\frac{D_{t}^{\mathrm{OIS}}(T)}{\hat{B}_{t}(T)}\right),

(12)

where
D_{t}^{\mathrm{OIS}}(T)
is the OIS-based discounting factor and

\hat{B}_{t}(T)
the option-implied discounting factor.
CG_{t}(T)>0

indicates that the options market embeds a higher implied carry than
the OIS benchmark.

In the empirical analysis I use the basis-points version

CG_{t}^{bp}(T)=10^{4}\cdot CG_{t}(T),

(13)

and the daily-aggregated market-level carry gap is denoted

CG_{i,t}^{bp}
.

A.4
Sample Cleaning and Daily Aggregation

The purpose of preprocessing is to drop observations with very low
liquidity or unstable price information so that the cross-sectional
regression remains stable. I retain only call–put pairs that share
the same strike and maturity, and exclude observations with abnormally
low prices or excessive bid–ask spreads. I also exclude maturities
with too few effective strikes for stable cross-sectional
identification, and dates on which OIS curve construction fails or
the term structure is anomalous.

The final sample consists of observations for which (i) the option-
implied discounting factor is identifiable and (ii) the OIS
discounting factor can be stably constructed at the same date and
maturity. For each of SPX and RUT, I build a date
\times
maturity
panel and aggregate to the daily series using the median of the
eligible observations on each date. This approach reduces sensitivity
to outliers and transitory noise while still tracking the central
movement of the carry gap.

A.5
Construction of the Asset-Return-Based Low-Frequency
Component

The asset-return extension in the main text uses ETF price data
representing the major asset classes to construct the low-frequency
return component. The ETFs are VTI, IEFA, IEMG, BND, SCHP, IGOV,
EBND, IAU, VNQ, and VNQI, chosen to represent U.S. equity, developed
ex-U.S. equity, emerging-market equity, U.S. aggregate bond, U.S.
inflation-linked bond, international government bond, emerging-market
sovereign bond, gold, U.S. REITs, and ex-U.S. REITs, respectively.

For each ETF, I estimate the low-frequency component by OLS on the
log-price path over the most recent
n
trading days through
t-1
.
The
n
-day low-frequency return component for asset
a
is the slope

\beta_{a,t}^{(n)}
from

\log P_{a,t-1-j}=\alpha_{a,t}^{(n)}+\beta_{a,t}^{(n)}j+u_{a,t-1-j},\qquad j=0,1,\dots,n-1.

(14)

For brevity I refer to this as the
\log
OLS slope. Computing it
from information through
t-1
only, excluding the contemporaneous
price at date
t
, precludes look-ahead bias.

I use the
\log
OLS slope rather than a simple cumulative return
because it reflects persistent trend components more stably than
transient spikes. This component then serves as the rate-like object
of the asset-return-based GBM term.

A.6
Regression Estimation and Performance Evaluation

The baseline and 3ETF extended specifications are both estimated as
separate regressions for SPX and RUT. The baseline includes the
OIS-based GBM terms, the bid–ask term, and NFCI; the extended
specification adds the asset-return-based GBM terms for IEFA, IGOV,
and IAU.

Inference on the regression coefficients uses date-based HAC
(Newey–West) standard errors, which jointly mitigate
heteroskedasticity and possible low-order autocorrelation in the
residuals of a daily time-series regression. Unless otherwise noted,
all parenthesized standard errors and significance markers in the
coefficient tables of the main text and appendix are based on these
HAC standard errors. In the implementation, the maximum lag length
is set to 21.

Out-of-sample evaluation uses leave-one-year-out (LOYO) validation.
For each year, I exclude that year as the holdout sample, estimate the
regression coefficients on the remaining years, and compute fit and
prediction errors on the excluded year.

The performance metrics are in-sample
R^{2}
, adjusted
R^{2}
, RMSE,
and MAE, together with year-by-year out-of-sample
R^{2}
, mean
R^{2}
,
median
R^{2}
, pooled
R^{2}
, mean RMSE, and mean correlation under
the LOYO scheme. The in-sample and out-of-sample fit metrics
themselves summarize point-estimate performance; the HAC adjustment
applies primarily to standard errors and significance assessment.
All OOS results reported in the main text and appendix are based on
this procedure.

Appendix B
Nested Horizon-Selection Robustness

The main specification uses fixed lookback horizons for the three asset-return
components: IEFA at 70 trading days, IGOV at 441 trading days, and IAU at
315 trading days. Although these horizons are motivated by the asset-by-asset
horizon profiles in the main text, a natural concern is that the reported
out-of-sample gains may partly reflect ex-post horizon selection. This appendix
addresses that concern with a nested leave-one-year-out exercise.

For each holdout year, I first exclude that year from the sample. Using only the
remaining training years, I select the IEFA, IGOV, and IAU lookback windows by
maximizing the equal-weighted in-sample
R^{2}
across SPX and RUT. The search
starts from the main-specification horizons and combines a coarse grid search
with a local hill-climb refinement. After the horizons are selected on the
training sample, the baseline and 3ETF specifications are estimated on the same
training years and evaluated on the excluded holdout year. Thus, the holdout
year is not used either to estimate the regression coefficients or to choose the
asset-return horizons.

The exercise is designed as a robustness check rather than as a replacement for
the main specification. Its purpose is to test whether the short foreign-equity
component and the slower foreign-bond/gold components reappear when horizon
selection is performed inside the training sample only.
