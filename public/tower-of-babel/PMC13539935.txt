Lobectomy for right lower lobe lung cancer with anomalous systemic arterial supply and partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection: a case report
Authors: Yuki Hirai, Takashi Anayama, Hironobu Wada, Kentaro Hirohashi, Yuki Onozato, Toshiko Kamata, Ichiro Yoshino, Shigetoshi Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnomalous systemic arterial supply to the basal lung (ASASB) and partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection (PAPVC) are rare congenital anomalies; their coexistence in a patient with lung cancer has not been previously reported.Case presentationA 68-year-old woman was referred for a right lower lobe mass detected on screening. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed an aberrant artery from the abdominal aorta supplying segments 9–10 and drainage of all right upper pulmonary veins into the superior vena cava. Qp/Qs on echocardiography was 1.3. Three-dimensional reconstruction clarified the vascular anatomy and guided thoracoscopic right lower lobectomy. The aberrant artery was doubly ligated at its intrathoracic origin and divided with a vascular stapler. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. Histology revealed papillary-predominant adenocarcinoma (pT2aN0M0). Twelve-month surveillance showed no recurrence or stump aneurysm; Qp/Qs decreased to 1.1.ConclusionsCareful cardiopulmonary evaluation and three-dimensional imaging permitted safe minimally invasive resection in this rare condition. However, the mechanisms underlying postoperative changes in Qp/Qs remain unclear and require cautious interpretation.

Background

Anomalous systemic arterial supply to the basal lung (ASASB) is an extremely rare congenital pulmonary vascular anomaly, characterized by abnormal systemic arteries arising from the descending aorta or other systemic sources supplying the basal segment of the lung, despite normal bronchopulmonary structures [1]. PAPVC is another congenital cardiovascular anomaly in which part of the pulmonary veins drain into the systemic venous circulation, such as the superior vena cava or brachiocephalic vein, instead of the left atrium. The incidence of PAPVC has been reported to be 0.4–0.7% in autopsy series [2]. Although often asymptomatic, significant left-to-right shunting can lead to clinical symptoms such as exertional dyspnea or arrhythmia.

Here, we report an extremely rare case of right lower lobe lung cancer associated with both ASASB and PAPVC. To our knowledge, no such case has been previously reported. Preoperative three-dimensional vascular reconstruction and cardiopulmonary evaluation enabled safe thoracoscopic resection. We present this case with a review of the literature.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old woman

Chief complaint: None (abnormal shadow on chest imaging).

Past medical history: Hypertension, dyslipidemia.

Smoking history: Never smoker.

Present illness

An abnormal mass shadow in the right lower lobe was incidentally detected during a routine health check-up. Bronchoscopy was performed at a previous hospital, during which 450 mL of airway bleeding occurred during brushing cytology. Subsequent contrast-enhanced CT revealed an abnormal artery arising from the abdominal aorta feeding the right lower lobe. Additionally, all right upper pulmonary veins were found to be draining into the superior vena cava. The patient was diagnosed with anomalous systemic arterial supply to the basal segment of the lung and partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection (PAPVC). Cytology was classified as Class IV, raising suspicion of right lung cancer associated with cardiovascular anomalies, and the patient was referred to our department for surgical management.

Physical findings on admission

Height: 151 cm; Weight: 57 kg; Body temperature: 36.5 ℃; Blood pressure: 126/68 mmHg; Heart rate: 75 bpm; SpO₂: 97%; No asymmetry in breath sounds.

Laboratory findings

No notable abnormalities in blood count, biochemistry, or coagulation profiles. Tumor markers (CEA, CYFRA, ProGRP) were within normal limits.

Arterial blood gas analysis

pH: 7.433; pO₂: 94.2 Torr; pCO₂: 40.1 Torr; sO₂: 98.2%.

Chest contrast-enhanced CT

A mass measuring 5.0 cm in total diameter, partially showing ground-glass opacity, was observed in segments S9 and S10 of the right lower lobe (Fig. 1).

Mild accumulation with SUVmax increasing from 3.1 to 3.5 was observed in the lesion. No signs of distant metastasis were found.

Pulmonary function test

FVC: 2.62 L (114.9% predicted); FEV₁: 2.01 L (110.4% predicted); FEV₁%: 76.71%.

Echocardiography

Ejection fraction: 65%. No wall motion abnormalities or intracardiac anomalies were found. Qp/Qs was 1.3. Mild enlargement of the superior vena cava and right heart was observed without evidence of pulmonary hypertension.

Preoperative diagnosis

Bronchoscopy, including cytology and tissue biopsy, was performed at the previous hospital. Although cytology was classified as Class IV, the tissue biopsy did not reveal definitive malignancy. Based on imaging and clinical findings, a diagnosis of suspected right lung cancer (cT2bN0M0, Stage IIA) with associated ASASB and PAPVC was made, and surgical resection was planned.

Given that echocardiography revealed a Qp/Qs ratio of 1.3, indicating a small left-to-right shunt, it was determined that vascular reconstruction was not necessary.

Intraoperative findings

The patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position, and video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) with four ports was initiated. All right upper pulmonary veins were confirmed to drain into the superior vena cava (Fig. 3a). The right lower lobe showed discoloration, scarring of lung parenchyma, and subpleural capillary engorgement and tortuosity (Fig. 3b) Also, an abnormal artery was visualized through the pulmonary ligament (Fig. 3c). The pulmonary ligament was dissected, and the aberrant artery was exposed (Fig. 3d). No calcification was noted in the aberrant vessel. To minimize formation of a blind-end stump, the vessel was ligated with non-absorbable sutures at its root within the thoracic cavity and the distal portion was divided using a stapling device. Subsequently, A6 and A8 branches of the pulmonary artery originating from the main trunk were dissected. The inferior pulmonary vein and bronchus to the lower lobe had no anatomical abnormalities and were divided with staplers. The interlobar fissure was divided, completing the right lower lobectomy.

Pathological findings

The resected tumor was diagnosed as pulmonary adenocarcinoma (pT2aN0M0, Stage IB). The aberrant artery from the abdominal aorta was a true elastic artery, with no accompanying bronchus, and no cystic changes in the surrounding lung tissue, consistent with anomalous systemic arterial supply rather than sequestration.

Postoperative course

The chest drain was removed on postoperative day 1, and the postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 7. She is currently on adjuvant oral chemotherapy with UFT, and no evidence of recurrence or metastasis has been observed. Ongoing surveillance includes monitoring for potential aneurysmal changes near the proximal stump of the ligated aberrant artery.

Discussion

The authors report a rare case of thoracoscopic right lower lobectomy for lung cancer in a patient with coexisting ASASB and PAPVC. Postoperatively, vital capacity (VC) decreased from 2.70 L to 2.41 L, and forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁) decreased only modestly from 2.01 L to 1.81 L, a 10.0% reduction. This limited decline is likely attributable to resection of the hypoplastic right lower lobe, which had received aberrant systemic arterial supply. Additionally, the Qp/Qs ratio improved from 1.3 to 1.1 after surgery, may reflect favorable postoperative hemodynamic changes.

ASASB is a rare congenital anomaly in which an aberrant artery arising from the aorta supplies the basal segment of the lung despite the presence of normal bronchopulmonary structures. This condition was previously classified as type I pulmonary sequestration according to Pryce [1]. But it has since been recognized as an independent entity due to the absence of sequestrated lung tissue [3]. While patients may present with initial symptoms such as hemoptysis, recurrent respiratory infections, or pneumonia, it can also be asymptomatic and incidentally detected during health screening.

PAPVC is a congenital anomaly in which part of the pulmonary venous return drains into the systemic venous circulation, resulting in a left-to-right shunt and increased pulmonary blood flow. PAPVC is often asymptomatic and may be incidentally discovered in adulthood.

Scimitar syndrome, a variant of PAPVC, is characterized by a scimitar vein draining the right lung into the inferior vena cava and is known to be associated with various other anomalies such as pulmonary sequestration, cardiac malformations, and diaphragmatic abnormalities [4]. The present case exhibited both anatomical and hemodynamic features resembling Scimitar syndrome, including anomalous pulmonary venous return, anomalous systemic arterial supply, and signs of right heart volume overload. However, unlike Scimitar syndrome, the anomalous pulmonary vein drained into the superior vena cava (SVC) rather than the inferior vena cava (IVC), and typical findings such as right lung hypoplasia and dextroposition of the heart were absent. The presumed mechanism of this anomaly involves a combination of abnormal pulmonary venous development and the persistence of systemic arterial branches during embryogenesis. However, this hypothesis is based on assumptions regarding embryological development and remains speculative.

In this case, the coexistence of lung cancer necessitated a detailed preoperative assessment of the vascular anomalies and hemodynamics to ensure safe surgical planning. When PAPVC is confined to the lobe being resected, the shunt can be eliminated through lobectomy. However, such as in this case, the PAPVC was located in a different lobe, the risk of increased shunt flow or pulmonary hypertension must be considered, and the need for vascular reconstruction must be evaluated based on predicted postoperative hemodynamic changes. A Qp/Qs ratio > 1.5 is considered an indication for vascular reconstruction [5]. In this case, preoperative Doppler echocardiography revealed a Qp/Qs of 1.3, leading to the decision not to perform vascular reconstruction. Although balloon occlusion tests using right heart catheterization have been reported as a method to predict postoperative Qp/Qs [6], because pulmonary blood flow to the resected side may decrease over time after surgery, the predicted Qp/Qs value may fluctuate. Moreover, because this patient also had ASASB, we determined that accurate hemodynamic prediction via catheterization would be difficult and did not perform the test. This decision was based on the relatively small shunt (Qp/Qs 1.3), absence of pulmonary hypertension, and the complexity introduced by anomalous systemic arterial supply, which was expected to limit the accuracy of occlusion-based prediction.

Although resection of the right lower lobe could theoretically reduce systemic blood flow (Qs) by eliminating anomalous systemic arterial supply, postoperative deterioration of the Qp/Qs ratio did not occur (Table 1). In the present case, Qp/Qs decreased from 1.3 to 1.1 after surgery. This finding may be explained by a reduction in systemic arterial inflow to the resected lobe as well as a decrease in shunt-related pulmonary blood flow. However, detailed hemodynamic mechanisms could not be fully evaluated in this case because no invasive assessment, such as right heart catheterization, was performed. Therefore, the exact mechanism underlying the postoperative change in Qp/Qs remains speculative.

In the absence of established criteria for ASASB-associated shunts, management decisions were guided by principles applied to analogous left-to-right shunt conditions. Although postoperative worsening of shunt hemodynamics cannot be completely excluded in theory, the preoperative echocardiographic finding of a mild shunt (Qp/Qs = 1.3) without pulmonary hypertension was considered acceptable for surgical resection without invasive occlusion testing, consistent with established practice in analogous shunt conditions such as atrial septal defect.

Additionally, the lung cancer in this case was located in the right lower lobe, which was supplied by an aberrant artery. Therefore, management of the anomalous artery was required during lobectomy. There is no established standard for the management of aberrant vessels, and various methods have been reported, including ligation and division, division using a surgical stapler, and suture ligation and division. Since the residual stump of an aberrant systemic artery is exposed to systemic arterial pressure, it poses a risk of aneurysmal change, and complete division without leaving a blind stump is recommended. However, in this case, the aberrant artery originated from the abdominal aorta, and division within the thoracic cavity resulted in a residual blind stump within the abdominal cavity. There have been reports of aneurysm formation at the site of arterial division [7]. Although no aneurysmal change was observed during the 12-month follow-up, long-term surveillance is required.

Furthermore, contrast-enhanced CT and three-dimensional CT angiography allowed accurate assessment of the course and extent of the aberrant vessels, contributing to the safe completion of surgery. While it is routine to evaluate the vasculature and bronchial anatomy of the resected area before pulmonary resection, the possibility of coexisting anomalies should always be considered. A thorough preoperative evaluation of not only the target lobe but also the contralateral lung’s vasculature and bronchial anatomy is essential to avoid life-threatening postoperative complications. In this case, the patient experienced massive endobronchial bleeding during bronchoscopy, indicating that assessment of vascular anomalies should be conducted not only preoperatively but also prior to performing invasive procedures such as bronchoscopy. Black et al. reported a case of fatal right heart failure after right pneumonectomy in a patient with unrecognized contralateral PAPVC [8], emphasizing the importance of preoperative recognition of PAPVC. As demonstrated in our case, pre-procedural understanding of the anomalous vessels’ course and distribution is crucial from the standpoint of preventing complications. The massive airway bleeding (approximately 450 mL) during brushing cytology represents an important clinical finding. This event likely reflects fragile and high-pressure vascularization in the affected lung segment supplied by the anomalous systemic artery. In such cases, bronchoscopic procedures may carry a substantial risk of bleeding, particularly during brushing or biopsy. Therefore, when anomalous systemic arterial supply is suspected, pre-procedural contrast-enhanced CT or vascular imaging should be considered. Careful selection or avoidance of invasive bronchoscopic maneuvers may be warranted.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of lung cancer with coexisting PAPVC and ASASB. Further accumulation and investigation of such cases are warranted in the future.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of lung cancer with coexisting ASASB and PAPVC. Preoperative cardiopulmonary assessment and three-dimensional imaging contributed to safe surgical management. However, the mechanisms underlying postoperative changes in Qp/Qs remain unclear, and careful interpretation is required. Further accumulation of similar cases is necessary to better understand the hemodynamic implications of such combined anomalies.
