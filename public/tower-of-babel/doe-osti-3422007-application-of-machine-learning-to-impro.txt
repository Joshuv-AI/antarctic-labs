 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The Agile Biofoundry (ABF) is a multi‐national lab consortium funded by the DOE Bioenergy
 Technologies Office that has developed a biofoundry that enables the rapid deployment of
 bioproducts into the market. Working with four ABF laboratory members (National Laboratory
 of the Rockies (NLR), Pacific Northwest National Laboratory(PNNL), Lawrence Berkeley
 National Laboratory (LBNL), and Argonne National Laboratory (ANL)), Kalion will use
 advanced high throughput techniques, advanced analytics, and machine learning to optimize the
 production environment for glucaric acid. Low-cost, high-purity glucaric acid enables products
 in a wide range of fields to improve the chemical properties of products and applications ranging
 from water treatment, polymers & textiles, coatings, detergents, and pharmaceuticals. With its
 broad range of applications, glucaric acid has the potential to demonstrate how such bio-based
 materials offer great benefit to the US economy.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Summary Introduction

 Kalion, Inc. (Kalion) has developed a bioprocess in which E coli strain 4032 converts myo-
 inositol to glucaric acid, an important building block chemical. Two challenges prevent
 penetration into pharmaceutical markets: 1) robust production has only been achieved in the
 presence of yeast extract, the second-most expensive media component after myo-inositol, and 2)
 productivity has been limited by a slow rate of production after the first 48 hours.

 We confronted the first challenge by attempting to identify, through metabolomics and machine
 learning, the components in yeast extract critical to optimal performance. Removing the
 requirement of expensive complex media such as yeast extract would reduce the overall process
 cost of the medium and make the process more economically favorable.

 Task 1-Bioreactor cultivations and analytical work (NLR)

 A series of experimental campaigns will be designed to support machine learning approaches.

 Subtask 1.1. High-throughput cultivations for media optimization: Work will be conducted
 in a micro-bioreactor system (BioLectorPro) to mimic near-real process conditions. Samples will
 be collected at the end of the experiment and the data will be provided to LBNL for machine
 learning work. We plan to conduct at least 5 iterations between NLR and LBNL to optimize the
 media. Multiple sample sets of final time point cultivation samples will be analyzed.

 We hypothesized that the observed reliance of E. coli strain 4032 on yeast extract for favorable
 performance was due to some specific components of the yeast extract, not necessarily yeast
 extract as a whole; identification of these critical components would allow us to supplement the
 fermentation medium with just these compounds and alleviate the need to use whole yeast
 extract, which is the second most expensive component of the fermentation medium, after myo-
 inositol.

 To identify which components of yeast extract are critical to E. coli strain 4032 performance, we
 conducted a bioreactor campaign that compared glucaric acid production under optimal
 production conditions (with 10 g/L yeast extract (YE)) to production under sub-optimal
 conditions (reduced yeast extract, or other complex media) (Figure 1). Samples spanning the
 first 43 hours of production were collected and provided to Task 2.2 for metabolomic analysis
 (see task 2.2 for further details). Resulting metabolic data was accessed for compounds of
 interest as described under Subtask 2.2. This approach allowed for the identification of 12
 potentially critical compounds for further testing (Table 1).

 We proceeded to develop a pipeline for testing the effects of these 12 compounds on glucaric
 acid production in a high throughput manner via the BioLector Pro, a microbioreactor platform.
 However, due to a combination of supply chain shortages and months of instrument downtime,
 we pivoted to testing these compounds in bioreactors. The low throughput nature of bioreactor
 operation, as well as limitations on reagents and person-hours, necessitated the grouping of these
 12 compounds into 6 groups, with the compounds in each group to be tested in aggregate (Table
 1). Groups were rationally selected based on the chemistry of the identified compounds.

 The six groups of compounds were tested for their effect on glucaric acid production in 0.5L
 bioreactors. This experimental campaign, as well as all others described in this report, was
 carried out as follows: temperature, pH, and DO were held constant at 34ºC, pH 7, and 30%,
 respectively; sparging rate was set as a minimum of 0.6 vvm; a glucose exponential feed was
 delivered over the first 24 hours of the cultivation; and pH control was provided through both
 ammonium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide. The compound within each group were added at
 a final concentration of either 1 or 10 mM each (Table 1) to a sub-optimal base medium
 containing 5 g/L yeast extract (Table 2). Performance of E. coli 4032 in the presence of each
 group is shown in Figure 2. In all cases, the addition of the compound groups either reduced or
 failed to significantly improve final titer, however, Group 2a appeared to improve the rate of
 glucaric production (Figure 2a). In two follow-up bioreactor campaigns assessing the effect of
 Group 2a on glucaric acid production, Group 2a improved final titers once and had no effect on
 performance in the second campaign. Table 3 summarizes final titers for all bioreactor
 campaigns involving Group 2a.

 Based on these inconsistent results, we hypothesized that 5 g/L yeast extract, while sub-optimal,
 may still be too rich to allow us to detect the effect of Group 2a compounds on final titer, if there
 is any effect. To investigate this possibility, we compared E. coli strain 4032 performance in the
 presence of Group 2a using a base media with no yeast extract. To further investigate the effect
 of Group 2a compounds specifically, which are all sugars, to any benefit that may be being
 imparted by general sugar addition, we also included a control condition that was supplemented
 with 30 mM glucose. Results (Figure 3) showed that sugar in general was beneficial to
 performance and the condition with Group 2a performed no better than the glucose control,
 though both did outperform the control with neither Group 2a or glucose added (Figure 3, 0 g/L
 YE). Interestingly, both experimental conditions performed as well as the YE2 control. This
 suggests that glucose addition has the potential to recover some of the performance lost in
 reducing yeast extract in the medium.

 In addition to glucose, we hypothesized that components in the original medium recipe other
 than yeast extract (Table 4) could influence performance if added in greater or lesser quantity.
 Rationally selected concentration ranges of each base media component were provided to
 Subtask 3.1 (Table 4). Due to the high quantity of data required for machine learning
 approaches, it was necessary to generate data in a high-throughput system like the BioLector Pro.
 The aforementioned maintenance issues with the instrument meant that this experimental
 campaign had to be delayed by several months. Furthermore, the amount of glucose added
 during the exponential feeding phase, while hypothesized to be important to the process, was
 unable to be included among the variables to be tested. This is because increasing the glucose fed
 results in significantly increased cell growth which increases the oxygen demand of the system
 beyond what the instrument can support. Performance in a total of 21 media compositions,
 generated as described in Subtask 3.1, was evaluated in the BioLector Pro system. However,

 despite performing the experiments in quadruplicate, we were unable to generate data suitable
 for machine learning, as the variability introduced by position in the BioLector Pro plate
 exceeded the variability generated by altering the media compositions. Instead, the dataset was
 analyzed by doing manual regressions between each media component and final glucaric acid
 titers, myo-inositol consumed, and glucaric acid produced per unit of OD. This analysis showed

 we were able to perform manual regressions of single variables versus performance, we were
 unable to investigate any combinatorial effects the different media components may have had, as
 would have been possible if we were able to utilize machine learning, as originally intended.

 Batch glucose and fed glucose were tested at three concentrations each, for a total of 9
 experimental conditions (Table 5). For better comparison with previous experiments, we
 selected a medium with 5 g/L yeast extract as the control comparison condition. Results showed
 that no experimental condition resulted in performance similar to that observed in the 5 g/L yeast
 extract control (Figure 5). Of the experimental conditions, a 1.1X glucose feed with normal (2
 g/L) batch glucose was slightly better, though this difference is not statistically significant. These
 data motivated the design of one more experimental campaign (Table 6) in which the increased
 feed rate was tested in combination with yeast extract supplementation at a reduced
 concentration of 1 g/L. Additionally, the experimental conditions were supplied with 5X iron,
 based on experimental results generated by Kalion (data not shown) and ICP data showing iron
 to be an important component of yeast extract. Results (Figure 6) show that supplementation of
 1g/L yeast extract had a significant impact on performance, with glucaric acid titers reaching
 79.3% of those reached in the YE5 condition.

 In conclusion, while we successfully identified several compounds that were depleted in sub-
 optimal media, addition of these compounds proved insufficient to compensate for removal of
 yeast extract from the system. The best-performing medium composition (Figure 6c) resulted in
 the production of 79.3% of the glucaric acid titer achieved by the control condition containing 5
 g/L yeast extract. Due to the exceedingly high cost of myo-inositol, however, this 5-fold
 reduction in yeast extract represents only a 1.8 % reduction in the cost of the medium, thus, is
 not economically advisable given the nearly 20% reduction in final product titer.

 We hypothesized that analysis of biocatalyst performance in sub-optimal media compositions
 can reveal which media components may be limiting in media formulations that do not contain
 10 g/L yeast extract. Fermentation broth samples spanning the first 43 hours of fermentation in
 both optimal and non-optimal media formulations (Figure 1) were provided by Subtask 1.1. A
 total of 179 metabolites were detected in this dataset, of which 72 were identified and 107
 unidentified. A heatmap analysis of detected metabolites (Figure 7) showed that some
 compounds were being consumed between the first two timepoints (T0 and T14), while others
 remained throughout the fermentation. These data were used to identify compounds that could be
 critical to glucaric acid production under the fermentation conditions studied. Compounds that
 were 1) identified, 2) gave a high intensity response in media with yeast extract but a low
 response in recipes without yeast extract, and/or 3) were more depleted at the 14-hour timepoint
 in the 5 g/L yeast extract condition than in the 10 g/L condition, were considered as candidates
 for critical media components. The high number of unidentified media components means that
 potentially critical compounds were excluded from the analysis. A final set of 12 compounds that
 meet these criteria (Table 1) was utilized by Subtask 1.1 in experimental validation campaigns
 (Figure 2, Figure 3).

 Overall, the metabolomics strategy did not allow for the identification of all potential compounds
 of interest, as many of the differentially utilized compounds remained unidentified. Additionally,
 the relative quantification of metabolomics data meant that there is no information regarding the
 actual concentrations of these compounds in the media recipes. This means that when we
 supplemented the media with the selected compounds of interest, we utilized a “physiological
 best guess” concentration for each compound (Table 1); therefore, it is possible that we did not
 test optimal amounts of the compounds in our subsequent analyses.

 Two separate machine learning approaches will be used to address glucaric acid productivity and
 media issues.

 Subtask 3.1.-AI towards optimizing the cultivation media (LBNL): The Automated
 Recommendation Tool (ART) from LBNL will be utilized to propose a series of defined media

 between NLR and LBNL to optimize the media.

 Deliverable: Report on a set of media optimized compositions to be used in the successive

 At the start of the campaign, we hypothesized that 1) differences among media recipes will be
 large enough to drive measurable changes in performance, and 2) differences in performance will
 largely be the result of media recipe differences and not stochastic or biological variability.
 Subtask 1.1 provided subtask 3. 1 with a list of base media components to be included in media
 composition testing (Table 3). These components, their solubility limits, the interval ranges of
 interest, and maximum well volume were all used to generate optimal stock media
 concentrations, which were provided to Subtask 1.1. Next, a total of 21 experimental media
 recipes and 3 control recipes (differing no more than +/- 10% of the original starting
 concentrations of each compound) were generated using ART’s Latin Hypercube sampling
 capability (Figure 8). These recipes were provided to Subtask 1.1 for cultivation experiments in
 the BioLector Pro system.

 Summary Conclusion

 This project aimed to determine which components of yeast extract, an expensive medium
 additive, were responsible for good bacterial performance when converting myo-inositol to
 glucaric acid. Good performance had not been seen in the absence of yeast extract. Throughout
 the duration of the project, we determined that adding additional sources of carbon to the media,
 such as glucose or other sugars, could alleviate some of the performance reduction observed
 when yeast extract is not provided. Nevertheless, maximum performance could not be attained
 without yeast extract, even when additional carbon was added. The increased carbon, however,
 does support the reduction of total yeast extract needed, thus reducing the overall cost of the
 medium. In addition to increased carbon, it may be necessary to increase iron in medium
 formulations that utilize less yeast extract to compensate for reduced iron, which is typically
 provided by the yeast extract. While the cost of yeast extract may not be relevant to processes
 that utilize commercial myo-inositol, it becomes an important component of total cost for
 processes that use in-house generated side streams of myo-inositol.

 Some learnings from this work include that the microbioreactor system (BioLector Pro), which
 has been successfully applied in the past to assess gross differences in performance, is not
 suitable for detecting small changes in performance. This is because variability introduced by the
 system itself overpowers differences in performance caused by experimentally manipulated
 process changes, if the effect of those changes is small. Secondly, since machine learning
 requires a high-quality input dataset, this strategy cannot be applied to datasets that suffer from a
 lot of non-process related variability, as was the case in this project.

 In sum, this project serves to highlight the challenges faced when making bioprocesses
 commercially viable. Furthermore, we identified the limitations of both microbioreactor systems
 and data input for machine learning. Now that these limitations have been identified, they can be
 averted in future research endeavors by first assessing the inherent variability of microbioreactor
 systems and then determining whether this variability in within acceptable ranges to serve as
 input for machine learning.
