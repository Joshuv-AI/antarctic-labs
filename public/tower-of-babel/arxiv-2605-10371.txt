Approximate Envy-Free Allocations up to any k k Goods
Aris Filos-Ratsikas, Georgios Kalantzis, Fangxiao Wang

Abstract
We study the problem of finding approximate envy-free allocations up to any k k goods ( α \alpha -EFkX), when agents have additive values over goods in a bundle. As our main result, we show that for any k > 2 k>2 , k + 1 k + 2 \frac{k+1}{k+2} -EFkX allocations exist for any number of agents, and can be computed in polynomial time, via an appropriate generalization of the 3PA algorithm of [ 6 ] . An immediate corollary of this result is that 3 / 4 3/4 -EF2X allocations exist for any number of agents; in contrast, 2 / 3 2/3 -EFX allocations are only known to exist for up to 7 agents. We improve this latter result by devising an algorithm that achieves 2 / 3 2/3 -EFX for 8 agents. We also consider EFkX graph orientations; we prove that such orientations do not always exist, and that deciding their existence is NP-complete, thereby generalizing the corresponding result of [ 15 ] for k = 1 k=1 .



1
Introduction

The area of computational fair division studies the problem of allocating resources to agents in a fair manner, based on those agents’ heterogeneous and often contrasting preferences over how the resources should be allocated. Amongst its many variants, the one that is currently on the forefront of the associated literature is the existence of fair allocations of
indivisible goods
, also known as
discrete fair division

[
4
]
. This literature has studied multiple notions of fairness for this setting, but arguably the most intriguing out of all those is that of
envy-freeness up to one good (EFX)

[
13
]
; this notion stipulates that the envy of one agent for another agent’s allocated bundle is eliminated, once we remove the least valuable good, from the envious agent’s perspective, from the bundle of the envied agent.

The question of whether EFX allocations always exist, and, if they do, whether they can be computed in polynomial time, is still open. In fact, it would be fair to say that this is the most important question, or the “holy grail”, of this very active research area. In the absence of a definitive answer, the literature has produced a plethora of results on meaningful special cases, which can be seen as important building blocks towards tackling the main question. For example, in a breakthrough result,
14
showed the EFX allocations exist for three agents (strengthening a previous existence result for two agents due to
22
, see also
[
2
]
).
5
showed the EFX allocations exist and can be computed in polynomial time on instances where agents can have one of two possible values for the goods, and
15
showed a corresponding result when the values can be represented by a graph.
22
showed an EFX existence result when the agents’ values induce the same ordering over goods.

Achieving
exact
EFX allocations has proven to be quite challenging for settings that go beyond the aforementioned special cases. For this reason, the literature has applied further relaxations to the EFX notion, two of the most prominent of which are the following:

-

approximate-EFX or
\alpha
-EFX
, i.e., a notion that stipulates that after the removal of any good from the envied agent’s bundle, the remaining envy is bounded multiplicatively by a factor
\alpha
.

-

EFkX
, i.e., a notion that stipulates that the envy is eliminated after the removal of any
k
goods from the envied agent’s bundle.

Obviously, when
\alpha=1
and
k=1
, we recover the definition of EFX allocations. The notion of
\alpha
-EFX was notably studied by
7
, who proved the (currently best known) approximation of
\alpha=0.618
; this bound was established also by
17
with a different algorithm.
21
improved the factor to
\alpha=2/3
when all of the
n
agents agree on the
n
most valuable goods. In a work that is most relevant to us,
6
obtained
2/3
-EFX allocations for three important cases, namely when (i) there are at most
7
agents, or (ii) when the values can be represented by a multigraph, or (iii) when the agents have one of three possible values.

The notion of EFkX allocations was defined by
3
, who, among other results, proved the existence of EF2X allocations for a rather restricted class of values (namely when each agent has a value in
\{0,v_{g}\}
for some good
g
). Recently,
8
showed the EF2X allocations exist for up to
4
agents. To the best of our knowledge, prior to our work, results for
k>2
were not known in the literature.

1.1
Our Contribution

In this work, for the first time we combine the two aforementioned lines of investigation, and consider
approximate-EFkX allocations
. This combination allows us to achieve significant improvements to the approximation factor
\alpha
compared to the state-of-the-art results for EFX, without any restrictions on the number of agents or their values. Our main result is given by the following theorem, informally stated below.

Informal Theorem 1
.

For any
k\geq 2
, and any number of agents with additive values, a
\frac{k+1}{k+2}
-EFkX allocation exists and can be computed in polynomial time.

To put
1
in context, we compare its instantiation for
k=2
with the aforementioned results of
3
and
8
, which establish the existence of EF2X allocations for rather restricted valuation classes, and only for
4
agents, respectively. By allowing an approximation of
\alpha=3/4
, we are able to achieve
(3/4)
-EF2X allocations for
any
number of agents and
any
additive valuation functions. We state the corresponding corollary below.

Informal Corollary 1
.

For any number of agents with additive values, a
(3/4)
-EF2X allocation exists and can be computed in polynomial time.

1
does not apply when
k=1
. For this case, we manage to improve on the result of
[
6
]
by showing the existence of
2/3
-EFX allocations for
8
agents. Although the improvement from
7
to
8
agents may not seem that significant at first glance, the extension reveals some of the inherent difficulties of moving beyond the case of
7
agents, and introduces new techniques to achieve that. We state the corresponding theorem informally below.

Informal Theorem 2
.

A
2/3
-EFX allocation exists and can be computed in polynomial time for instances with up to
8
agents.

We also consider EFkX allocations on graphs, extending the setting introduced by
15
in the context of EFX allocations. In this setting, every good (corresponding to an edge of the graph) is positively valued by only two agents (the endpoints of the edge in the graph). We are particularly interested in
orientations
of the edges, i.e., allocations such that each good is assigned to one of the two agents that values it positively.
15
showed the EFX orientations may not exist, and deciding their existence is an NP-complete problem. We strengthen their results to EFkX orientations, for any meaningful value of
k
.

Informal Theorem 3
.

EFkX orientations on graphs do not always exist. Furthermore, given an instance of the problem, deciding whether it admits an EFkX orientiation is NP-complete.

1.1.1
An overview of our techniques

First, as a warm-up, in
Section

3.1
, we present a rather simple algorithm which achieves the weaker guarantee of
\frac{k}{k+1}
-EFX. Then, for our main result in
Section

3.2
, we generalize the approach taken in
[
6
]
. The
\frac{k+1}{k+2}
-EFkX allocation in
1
is obtained in three steps.

First, we obtain a partial allocation
\mathbf{X}^{1}
which satisfies a certain set of desirable properties (see
(a)
,
(b)
,
(c)
,
(d)
,
(e)
and
(f)
in
Section

3.2.1
); the most important of those properties are that the set of unallocated goods (called “the pool”) contains a limited number of highly-valued (or “critical”) goods for each agent, and only for agents whose bundles are singletons. This is achieved by the
Generalized Property Preserving Allocation algorithm (
G3PA
)
, an appropriate generalization of the
3PA
algorithm of
6
, which was designed for the case of
k=1
. Compared to the original design, the algorithm works for any value of
k\geq 1
and it is also conceptually simpler, as it uses only one type of envy graph (coined the “modified graph”) rather than the two variants (the “reduced” and “enhanced” envy graphs) used in
[
6
]
, as well as fewer subroutines. The use of the modified graph also allows us to prove the polynomial running time of the algorithm, correcting an oversight of
6
which was idenfied by
19
(see
Remark

B.1
in
Appendix

B
). The proof of correctness of the algorithm follows closely that of the original version, with the necessary technical adaptations to make it work for the more general case of any
k
.

In the second step, we devise an algorithm which inputs the partial allocation
\mathbf{X}^{1}
and produces a partial allocation
\mathbf{X}^{2}
, which is
\frac{k+1}{k+2}
-EFkX, and for which the pool does not have any critical goods for any agent. In
[
6
]
, this was only achieved for special cases of the problem, e.g., for instances with up to
7
agents. For
k\geq 2
, our novel algorithm, coined
AllocateAndEliminateCritical
, achieves this allocation for any number of agents. The crucial insight that allows us to establish this result is that, after allocating a number of critical goods to an agent, the remaining goods in the pool are no longer critical for the agent, since her utility in the meantime has increased. This approach falls short for
k=1
; in this case we still manage to strengthen the state-of-the-art results from
7
to
8
agents. While the number of agents increases by only one, our newly introduced algorithm for allocating the critical goods is much more involved compared to the corresponding one in
[
6
]
, and handles the critical goods differently depending on the number of
contested
critical goods and the number of sources in the modified envy graph.

Finally, in the third step, we use the Envy cycle elimination algorithm of
[
20
]
to transform the partial allocation
\mathbf{X}^{2}
to a full allocation
\tilde{X}
which is
\frac{k+1}{k+2}
-EFkX. This is enabled by a crucial lemma, which establishes that this is possible, assuming the absence of any critical goods from the pool. This lemma was first formally stated and proved for
k=1
by
21
, but it was used implicitly earlier by
7
;
17
. Our version is an appropriate generalization for any value of
k
; we refer to this lemma as the
Partial-to-Full-Allocation Lemma (P2FA)
, see
Lemma

2.4
.

We consider EFkX graph orientations in
Section

4
. First, we construct an instance in which graph orientations do not exist for any constant value of
k
, and then prove the corresponding NP-completeness result. Our NP-hardness result proceeds via induction on
k
and uses the NP-hardness result of
15
for
k=1
as the base case. Then, by carefully introducing new graph gadgets in the construction of our reduction we show that we can decide the existence of EFkX orientations if and only if we can decide the existence of EFk-1X orientations on any input graph instance.

1.2
Related Work

We now provide a more extensive overview of the relevant literature on the problem of fairly allocating indivisible goods. More specifically, we focus on related work on exact and approximate envy-free allocations up to one good, other relaxed fairness notions and restricted settings. For further results and progress on fair allocations, we refer the reader to the survey by
4
.

More specifically, on the topic of exact EFX allocations,
14
showed existence for three agents with additive valuations.
[
2
]
improved this to a slightly more
general class.
5
showed the EFX allocations exist and can be computed in polynomial time on instances where agents can have one of two possible values for the goods.
15
showed a corresponding result when the values can be represented by a graph.
22
showed an EFX existence result when the agents’ values induce the same ordering over goods and
18
show existence when agents have one of three additive valuations.

On approximate EFX allocations,
7
showed the existence of
0.618
-EFX allocations,
21
improved the factor to
2/3
when all agents
n
agree on the ordering of the
n
most valuable goods. Furthermore,
6
showed the existence of
2/3
-EFX allocations for
7
agents and some other restricted valuation classes.

Regarding relaxed fairness notions, EF1 allocations, introduced by
11
, which on contrast with EFX allow the removal of some good, were shown by
20
that always
exist, even for general monotone valuations, and can be computed efficiently. Stronger than notions than EF1 are EFL introduced by
10
, EFR by
17
, EEFX and MXS by
12
, and their combinations, such has existence of MXS and EFL by
9
). Lastly, more closely to our work EF2X allocations were proven to exist by
8
for four agents and by
3
for a restricted additive class.

Lastly on orientations,
15
showed the non-existence and the NP-completeness of deciding EFX orientations.
16
showed that EF1 orientations always exist when agents have monotone valuations and proved that deciding the existence of EFX orientations remains NP-complete even for special classes of graphs.
23
characterized the existence of EFX orientations. Lastly,
1
studied EFX orientations in
(bipartite) multigraphs.

2
Preliminaries

We consider the problem of fairly allocating a set
M
of
m
indivisible goods to a set
N
of
n
agents. Each agent
i
has preferences over the goods, which are expressed by terms of an
(additive)
valuation function
v_{i}:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that assigns non-negative values to each good; the value of an agent for a set (or a
bundle
)
X\subseteq M
of goods is defined as
v_{i}(X)=\sum_{g\in X}v_{i}(g)
. A partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
is defined as a tuple
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, where
X_{i}
denotes the bundle allocated to agent
i
, such that (a) for every
i\in N
, it holds that
X_{i}\subseteq M
and that (b) for any
i,j\in N
such that
i\neq j
, it holds that
X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset
. A partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
is a
full allocation
, or simply, an
allocation
, if additionally, it holds that
\bigcup_{i\in N}X_{i}=M
. For a partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
, the set
M\setminus\bigcup_{i\in N}X_{i}
will be referred to as
the pool
, and will be denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})
.

The notion of fairness that we will be concerned with in this work is that of
approximate envy-freeness up to
k
goods
, which stipulates that an agent can only envy another agent’s bundle up to a multiplicative factor
\alpha
, after the removal of any
k
goods from the latter agent’s bundle. We provide the formal definition below.

Definition 2.1

(
\alpha
-EFkX)
.

Given a partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
, a
k\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
, an
\alpha>0
, and two agents
i,j\in N
, we will say that agent
i
is
\alpha
-EFkX towards agent
j
if, for any
Y\in X_{j}
such that
|Y|=k
, it holds that
v_{i}(X_{i})\geq\alpha\cdot v_{i}(X_{j}\setminus Y)
. We will say that
\mathbf{X}
is
\alpha
-EFkX if every agent
i\in N
is
\alpha
-EFkX towards any other agent
j\in N
.

When
\alpha=1
, or
k=1
, these will be omitted from the notation, i.e., we will instead refer to EFkX (when
\alpha=1
),
\alpha
-EFX (when
k=1)
, and EFX (when both
\alpha=1
, and
k=1
). When
k=0
, instead of
\alpha
-EF0X, we will say that an agent does not
\alpha
-envy
another agent, and
\mathbf{X}
is
\alpha
-envy-free. Extending the definitions above, we will also need to compare the value of an agent for a bundle of goods
Y
which is not necessarily the bundle of some other agent in
\mathbf{X}
. In that case, when
v_{i}(Y)\geq a\cdot v_{i}(X_{i})
, we will say that agent
i

prefers
Y
by a factor of at least
\alpha
.

Following the terminology of
[
6
]
, we will refer to goods in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})
that an agent
i
values significantly compared to her current allocation as
critical goods
.

Definition 2.2

(
\beta
-critical good)
.

Given a partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
, a good
g\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})
is
\beta
-critical
for agent
i\in N
, if
v_{i}(g)\geq\beta\cdot v_{i}(X_{i})
.

Envy-graphs

A central concept in the literature of fair division with indivisible goods is that of an
envy graph

[
20
]
. Given a partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
, its
envy graph

G(\mathbf{X})=(N,E(\mathbf{X}))
is a directed graph that has one node for each agent, and a directed edge
(i,j)
if and only if agent
i
envies agent
j
, i.e.,
v_{i}(X_{i})\geq v_{i}(X_{j})
. For the
3PA
algorithm,
6
, defined two different variants of the envy graph, namely
reduced envy graphs
and
enhanced envy graphs
. Our generalization simplifies their approach and only makes use of a single variant, that we call the
modified envy graph
.

Definition 2.3

(Modified Envy Graph
\alpha
-
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
)
.

Given a partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
and an
\alpha\in[0,1]
, its modified
\alpha
-envy graph
\alpha
-
G_{\mathrm{mod}}(\mathbf{X})=(N,E(\mathbf{X}))
is a directed graph that has one node for each agent, and a directed edge
(i,j)
if and only if:

-

|X_{i}|=|X_{j}|
and
v_{i}(X_{j})\geq v_{i}(X_{i})

(exact) envy

-

|X_{i}|=1
and
|X_{j}|>1
and
v_{i}(X_{j})\geq\alpha\cdot v_{i}(X_{i})

\alpha
-approximate (weak) envy

-

|X_{i}|>1
and
|X_{j}|=1
and
v_{i}(X_{j})\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot v_{i}(X_{i})

\frac{1}{\alpha}
-approximate (strong) envy

An alternative but useful definition of the
\alpha
-
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
graph can be obtained via the following
proxy valuation function
:

\tilde{v}_{i}(Y)=\begin{cases}v_{i}(Y),&|Y|=1,\\
\frac{1}{\alpha}v_{i}(Y),&|Y|>1.\end{cases}

Then, the modified envy graph
G_{\mathrm{mod}}(\mathbf{X})
is simply the envy graph
G(\mathbf{X})
on an instance with valuation functions
\tilde{v}_{i}(\cdot)
rather than
v_{i}(\cdot)
, for any
i\in N
.

When it is clear from context, we will drop the partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
from the notation and simply write
G
, and
\alpha
-
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
for the envy graph and the modified envy graph, respectively. Notice also that
G
is simply
\alpha
-
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
, when
\alpha=1
. Since
\alpha
will be fixed for all of our results where
\alpha
-
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
is used (as a function of
k
), we will refer to the modified envy-graph
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
(dropping the
\alpha
) instead.

2.2
Partial to Full Allocation (P2FA) Lemma

As we discussed in the Introduction, in the heart of our approach lies a simple that useful lemma, which allows us to translate partial allocations that satisfy certain properties to full approximate EFkX allocations. Such lemmas were used (implicitly) in the past by other works
[
7
;
17
]
), but the first explicit statement appeared in
[
21
]
, see also
[
6
, Lemma 4.2]
. The statement in
[
21
]
only concerns EFX allocations (rather than EFkX); we state the appropriate generalization below. Its proof is a straightforward adaptation, but we include it here for completeness.

Lemma 2.4

(Partial-to-Full-Allocation (P2FA) Lemma)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
. Let
\mathbf{X}
be an
\alpha
-EFkX partial allocation such that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})
does not contain any
\beta
-critical good for any agent. Then a
\min\{\alpha,\frac{1}{\beta+1}\}
-EFkX allocation
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{X}
in polynomial time.

Proof.

The full allocation
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
will be obtained from
\mathbf{X}
by allocating the goods from the pool
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})
using the
EnvyCycleElimination
algorithm (see Algorithm
6
in
Appendix

A
). First, from the definition of
EnvyCycleElimination
, it follows that, during the run of the algorithm, the utility of an agent
i\in N
never decreases (see
[
20
]
and
[
6
, Lemma 4.1]
). Consider any agent
i\in N
and any agent
j\in N\setminus\{i\}
. If suffices to prove that in
\tilde{X}
, agent
i
is
\min\{\alpha,\frac{1}{\beta+1}\}
-EFkX towards agent
j
. We consider two cases:

-

Case 1:

\tilde{X}_{j}=X_{j}^{\prime}
, i.e., the final bundle of agent
j
in allocation
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
was the exact bundle of some (possibly other) agent
j^{\prime}
in partial allocation
\mathbf{X}
, without adding any items from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})
. Then, since
v_{i}(\tilde{X}_{i})\geq v_{i}(X_{i})
as discussed above, and since agent
i
was
\alpha
-EFkX towards agent
j^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{X}
, she will now be
\alpha
-EFkX towards agent
j
in
\tilde{X}
.

-

Case 2:
One or more goods from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})
have been added to
X_{j}
during the execution of the algorithm. Let
g
be the last good that was added to
\tilde{X}_{j}
, and let
j^{\prime}\in N
be the agent to whom the bundle was assigned to before the addition of good
g
. Also, let
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{t}
be the partial allocation produced by the algorithm at that point in the execution; hence
\tilde{X}_{j^{\prime}}^{t}=\tilde{X_{j}}\setminus\{g\}
. By definition of the algorithm, agent
j^{\prime}
was a source of
G(\mathbf{X}^{t})
, and hence

\displaystyle=\frac{2k}{(k+1)^{2}}\cdot v_{i}(X_{i})>\frac{k+1}{k(k+2)}v_{i}(X_{i})\geq v_{i}(g_{k}),

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We initiated the study of approximate envy-free allocations up to
k
goods. Namely, we showed the existence of
\frac{k+1}{k+2}
-EFkX allocations for any number of agents by constructing the G3PA Algorithm; a careful generalization of the algorithm that gives the state-of-the art approximate EFX allocation for a few agents by
6
. The G3PA algorithm could potentially be applied to other frameworks and valuation classes, such as multigraphs, three valued instances, etc.. Furthermore, we improved upon the result of
6
and showed the existence of
2/3
-EFX allocation for
8
agents. Lastly, we showed that EFkX orientations do not always exist and deciding their existence is NP-complete. An interesting direction, also posed by
16
, is settling the complexity of EF1 orientations.

Appendix B
A note on the running time of the
G3PA
algorithm

In this section, we provide some useful discussion related to the running time of the
G3PA
algorithm. To this end, we provide the following remark regarding the proof of its running time, as presented in
[
6
]
.

Remark B.1
.

The proof of polynomial running time in
[
6
]
, as stated, is technically incorrect. This is due to an oversight in the argument about the termination of the
AllCyclesResolution
subroutine when applied to the “enhanced graph” that the authors use in their version of their algorithm. In particular,
6
state that the
EnvyCycleElimination
procedure, when applied to the enhanced graph, will always result in a reduction in the number of edges. This was proved to not be the case by
19
via an appropriate counter-example.
19
also provide a fix, which however only results in a pseudopolynomial-time algorithm, rather than a polynomial-time one. In our modified envy graph
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
, the statement about the reduction in the number of edges is true, and an almost straightforward adaptation of the argument in
[
6
]
goes through. Intuitively, this is because, the new edges that can appear in the enhanced graph in the counter-example of
19
are already present in the modified graph
G_{\mathrm{mod}}
. Indeed, this oversight in the analysis in
[
6
]
is not a feature of the algorithm, which still runs in polynomial time.

Initially, regardless of whether the
G3PA
or
3PA
algorithm is used, the number of possible bundles that an agent can have is at most
\binom{m}{k}+\binom{m}{1}+1
(where, for
3PA
,
k=2
).
In every step except
AllCyclesResolution
of the
G3PA
and
3PA
algorithms, the proxies of some agents increase while those of the other agents remain the same.
Whenever the
CycleResolution
subroutine is executed in the modified envy graph, enhanced envy graph, or reduced envy graph, the proxies of some agents increase while those of the others remain the same.

AllCyclesResolution
can be regarded as a sequence of
CycleResolution
subroutines, so we can simply consider how many times
CycleResolution
is executed during the algorithm (
G3PA
or
3PA
).
Therefore, there are at most
n\cdot m^{k}
iterations of all the steps except Step
4
, including the
CycleResolution
subroutines.
The complexity of each step (except
AllCyclesResolution
) and the
CycleResolution
subroutine, is
\mathcal{O}(n)
.
Hence, both
G3PA
and
3PA
run in polynomial time.
