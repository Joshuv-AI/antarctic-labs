Relationship Between Radiocapitate Distance as a Structural Parameter and Clinical Outcomes After Volar Locking Plate Fixation of Distal Radius Fractures
Authors: Takafumi Hosokawa, Tsuyoshi Tajika, Morimichi Suto, Junkoh Okada, Ayako Nagai, Hirotaka Chikuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRadiocapitate distance (RCD) has been proposed as a radiographic parameter reflecting sagittal carpal alignment after distal radius fracture. However, its clinical significance following volar locking plate (VLP) fixation remains unclear. This study evaluated RCD at 1 year after VLP fixation and investigated its association with radiographic parameters and clinical outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 145 patients who underwent VLP fixation for distal radius fracture and completed 1-year follow-up. RCD was measured on lateral wrist radiographs as the perpendicular distance from the longitudinal axis of the volar radial cortex to the center of the capitate. Correlation analyses were performed between RCD and conventional radiographic parameters. Univariate and multivariable regression analyses were conducted to assess the association between RCD and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) score, grip strength, and wrist range of motion.ResultsRCD was evaluated in 137 patients with complete functional data, and 126 patients were included in the primary analysis for QuickDASH score. The mean final RCD was −2.4 ± 3.0 mm. Final RCD correlated with palmar tilt and radial inclination but not with ulnar variance. In exploratory analyses, ulnar variance was the only radiographic parameter significantly associated with QuickDASH score. RCD was significantly associated with wrist flexion but not with grip strength or wrist extension. In multivariable analysis adjusting for age, sex, and hand dominance, RCD was not an independent predictor of QuickDASH score but remained independently associated with wrist flexion.ConclusionsRCD was associated with wrist flexion at 1 year after VLP fixation but was not an independent predictor of QuickDASH score. RCD appears to reflect sagittal carpal alignment dependent on radial reduction quality and may be a candidate structural marker that warrants further investigation.Type of study/level of evidenceRetrospective cohort study/Ⅲ.Key words: Distal radius fracture, QuickDASH, Radiocapitate distance, Volar locking plate

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) are among the most frequently encountered fractures in daily clinical practice, and open reduction and internal fixation using a volar locking plate (VLP) has become widely accepted as the standard treatment in recent years. However, even in cases in which radiographically acceptable reduction is considered to have been achieved, postoperative functional impairments—such as restricted wrist range of motion and decreased grip strength—may occur. In addition, complications including carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS)1,2 and flexor tendon rupture3,4 have been reported. The underlying factors contributing to these adverse outcomes remain incompletely understood.

Traditionally, radiographic parameters such as radial inclination, palmar tilt (PT), and ulnar variance (UV) have been used to assess the quality of reduction following treatment of DRF.5, 6, 7, 8 However, deformity of the distal radius has been reported to exert secondary effects on carpal alignment, particularly at the midcarpal joint level, leading to malalignment and adaptive changes.9, 10, 11 Dorsal angulation and malunion after DRFs have been associated with midcarpal instability and carpal malalignment, which in turn are related to wrist dysfunction.9, 10, 11 Furthermore, corrective osteotomy restoring carpal alignment has been shown to improve clinical symptoms and functional outcomes,12,13 suggesting the importance of appropriately evaluating and restoring the relative relationship between the radius and the carpals.

Bickerstaff and Bell defined displacement of the capitate relative to the longitudinal axis of the radius as an index—termed radiocapitate displacement—to capture changes in carpal alignment that are difficult to assess using angular parameters alone, and advocated the need for distance-based evaluation.14 Based on these findings, Watanabe15 defined the relative position between the radius and the capitate as the radiocapitate distance (RCD) in patients who underwent VLP fixation for DRFs, and reported that changes in RCD were correlated with wrist flexion range of motion and decreased grip strength. In that study, RCD in the uninjured wrist showed a relatively constant value regardless of age, sex, or laterality. In contrast, postoperative dorsal displacement of the capitate was observed in a substantial number of cases, suggesting that RCD may reflect carpal malalignment not fully captured by conventional angular parameters. However, reports regarding RCD remain limited, and its clinical significance, as well as its association with long-term postoperative outcomes and complications, remain unclear. In particular, further studies are needed to clarify the extent to which RCD may serve as a reduction parameter or a prognostic predictor in the treatment of DRF.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the RCD at 1-year follow-up in patients with DRFs treated with VLP fixation and to clarify its radiographic characteristics and its association with clinical outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This study was a single-center retrospective observational study. Two hundred twenty patients who underwent surgical treatment with VLP fixation for DRFs at our institution between April 2014 and March 2021 were identified using medical records and the institutional radiographic database. The present cohort included patients who had previously been analyzed for factors associated with the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) score at 1 year after surgery. While the prior report comprehensively evaluated factors influencing the QuickDASH score, the current study specifically focused on RCD, a novel radiographic parameter.

This study was conducted with approval from our hospital's institutional review board. Because of the retrospective design, informed consent was obtained using an opt-out approach.

Surgical treatment was indicated in cases in which maintenance of reduction was considered difficult with nonsurgical treatment based on initial plain radiographs. The inclusion criteria comprised patients who met at least one of the following radiographic parameters after reduction: radial inclination ≤ 10°, PT ≤ −10° or ≥ 20°, UV ≥ 3 mm, an intra-articular gap or step-off ≥ 2 mm, or the presence of metaphyseal or epiphyseal cortical comminution. Even when postreduction radiographs were within acceptable limits, surgery was performed in patients who desired early functional use or wished to ensure maintenance of optimal alignment. Patient selection, exclusion criteria, and the final study cohort are summarized in a flowchart (Fig. 1). Patients who completed 1-year follow-up were included in the outcome analysis. Of these, 137 had complete radiographic and functional data. Functional outcomes were available for 137 patients for grip strength, 135 for range of motion, and 126 for the primary QuickDASH analysis. The final sample size for each analysis was determined by data completeness.

Patient flowchart of patient selection and inclusion in the study.

Sex was defined as biological sex and categorized as male or female based on the medical records. Trauma energy was categorized as low-energy (fall from standing height) or high-energy (fall from a height, traffic accident, sports injury, or industrial injury).

All procedures were performed by or under the supervision of hand surgeons or orthopedic surgeons with more than 10 years of surgical experience. In all cases, a trans–flexor carpi radialis approach was used, and internal fixation was achieved using a VLP. Several types of commercially available anatomically contoured VLPs were used. The choice of implant and the details of the fixation technique were determined at the discretion of the operating surgeon, based on the fracture pattern and intraoperative findings. After surgery, the wrist was immobilized with a short arm cast, and wrist range-of-motion exercises were initiated at 1 week after surgery. Most patients underwent outpatient rehabilitation for approximately 3 months.

Radiographic evaluation and measurement of radiocapitate distance

Plain radiographs of the wrist obtained before surgery, immediately after surgery, and at 1 year after surgery were evaluated using measurement tools in the picture archiving communication system. The radiographic parameters measured included RI, PT, and UV. RCD was measured on the lateral wrist radiograph as the perpendicular distance from a reference line drawn along the longitudinal axis of the volar cortex of the radial shaft to the center of the head of the capitate (Fig. 2).15 In addition, the difference between the affected and contralateral sides (ΔRCD = affected-side RCD − contralateral-side RCD) was calculated and used for analysis.15 All radiographic evaluations and measurements were performed independently by the first author.

Outcomes

Functional outcomes at 1 year after surgery were recorded. The evaluation parameters included the QuickDASH score, grip strength, and active wrist range of motion. Grip strength was measured using a Smedley-type dynamometer (Matsumiya Ika Seisaku-sho). Measurements were obtained with the patient in a seated position, the shoulder adducted, and the elbow flexed at 90°. Grip strength was expressed as percent grip strength (%GS), calculated as the ratio relative to the contralateral side. Range of motion was measured in the same position using a goniometer and included wrist flexion, wrist extension, forearm supination, and pronation.

Statistical analyses

To assess the intraobserver reliability of RCD measurements, the first author randomly selected 20 patients and performed the measurements twice with a 1-week interval. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to evaluate intraobserver agreement. Additionally, interobserver reliability was assessed in 20 randomly selected patients by two independent examiners who were blinded to the clinical outcomes.

To investigate the association between RCD and radiographic parameters, correlation analyses were performed between RCD and RI, PT, and UV.

Exploratory univariate analyses were performed. To evaluate the impact of each radiographic parameter on the QuickDASH score, simple linear regression analyses were conducted with RCD, RI, PT, and UV entered separately as independent variables. Furthermore, to assess the association between RCD and other clinical outcomes, additional simple linear regression analyses were performed with %GS, wrist flexion, and wrist extension as dependent variables and the final RCD as the independent variable.

For the primary outcome, the QuickDASH score was entered as the dependent variable in a multiple linear regression model, with RCD as the primary independent variable and age, sex, and injury to the dominant16 hand included as covariates based on clinical relevance. This analysis was performed to determine the independent effect of RCD on patient-reported functional outcomes. For the secondary outcomes, %GS, wrist flexion, and wrist extension were each entered as dependent variables in similar multiple linear regression models. In additional exploratory analyses, the same regression procedures were repeated using ΔRCD in place of RCD. A two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 220 patients underwent surgical treatment for DRFs during the study period. Of these, 75 were excluded according to predefined criteria, and 145 completed the 1-year follow-up. Grip strength, range of motion, and QuickDASH scores were available in 137, 135, and 126 patients, respectively. RCD was evaluated in 137 patients with complete 1-year functional data (Table 1). Of these, 104 patients overlapped with those included in our previous report.

Postoperative complications are presented in Table 2. Complications occurred in 24 patients within 1 year after surgery. In this study, median neuropathy was defined as the presence of subjective symptoms in the median nerve distribution. Among these patients, CTS was diagnosed based on positive clinical findings (e.g., Tinel-like sign and Phalen test) in conjunction with electrophysiological evidence of delayed nerve conduction. As a result, nine cases met the diagnostic criteria for CTS. The remaining six cases were considered to represent transient median neuropathy. Six patients underwent plate removal and carpal tunnel release at a mean of 6.5 months after surgery. Other complications included extensor pollicis longus tendon rupture and flexor pollicis longus tendon adhesion, which were treated with plate removal combined with extensor indicis proprius tendon transfer and tenolysis, respectively. The mean final RCD in patients who developed CTS was −3.4 mm (range, −7.1 to 0 mm).

Discussion

RCD was significantly associated with wrist flexion at 1 year after surgery; however, it was not an independent predictor of the QuickDASH score. These findings suggest that RCD is related to specific aspects of wrist motion but does not directly influence comprehensive patient-reported outcomes. Consistent with previous reports, UV was the only radiographic parameter significantly associated with the QuickDASH score in our exploratory analyses, supporting prior observations that postoperative Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand/QuickDASH scores are influenced by UV.7,17, 18, 19 Functional outcomes after DRF are multifactorial and may be affected by variables beyond radiographic alignment.16,20, 21, 22, 23 Prior studies have suggested that the influence of radiographic parameters on functional outcomes may diminish over time, particularly in elderly patients.24, 25, 26, 27, 28

Watanabe reported that decreased ΔRCD was weakly correlated with reduced wrist flexion and GS at 3 to 6 months after surgery. In contrast, in our study, RCD was associated only with wrist flexion at 1 year and showed no association with GS or QuickDASH score. These differences may be attributable to differences in study design, such as the timing of evaluation and the methods used to express functional outcomes. Specifically, Watanabe analyzed the contralateral difference in RCD (ΔRCD) and expressed wrist motion and GS as side-to-side differences, whereas our study evaluated the absolute range of wrist motion and GS as a percentage of the contralateral side.

Carpal malalignment following DRF has been understood as a secondary adaptive change associated with radial deformity.9, 10, 11 Taleisnik et al10 demonstrated that midcarpal malalignment accompanying distal radius malunion may be associated with pain and instability. Gupta et al11 reported that carpal displacement represents an adaptive response to radial deformity and does not necessarily indicate intrinsic ligament injury. RCD reflects the sagittal positional relationship between the radius and the capitate and may capture carpal malalignment not fully appreciated by conventional angular parameters.14,15 In the present study, final RCD correlated with PT and RI, supporting the concept that RCD is dependent on the quality of radial reduction rather than representing an independent morphological parameter.

The magnitude of postoperative dorsal displacement in our cohort was 2.4 mm, similar to the 2.6 mm reported by Watanabe. Even when RI, PT, and UV were restored within acceptable ranges, the capitate remained dorsally positioned relative to the contralateral side. These findings suggest that restoration of PT within traditional radiographic limits does not necessarily ensure complete anatomical repositioning of the carpus.15 Nevertheless, this residual dorsal displacement was not associated with worse QuickDASH scores at 1 year after surgery. In surgically treated patients in whom major alignment parameters are adequately corrected, mild residual dorsal shift of the capitate may have limited short-term clinical impact. However, a previous report has suggested that radiocarpal malalignment associated with distal radius malunion may become fixed over time and difficult to fully correct by osteotomy.29 Therefore, the long-term structural implications of persistent dorsal displacement should be interpreted cautiously.

RCD was not associated with patient-reported outcomes. However, RCD may still have clinical relevance as a structural risk indicator. Watanabe and Ota30 reported that decreased RCD was associated with delayed CTS after conservative treatment of DRF and identified a threshold value of −9.9 mm (odds ratio, 21). In our cohort, the mean RCD among patients who developed CTS was smaller than the overall mean, suggesting that decreased RCD may be associated with an increased risk of CTS. In this study, the mean RCD in patients who developed CTS was −3.4 mm. This finding may suggest that a more negative RCD is associated with an increased risk of CTS. However, only nine cases of CTS were observed, which is insufficient for statistical analysis, and therefore this should be interpreted as an exploratory observation.

In addition, Park et al31 demonstrated that sagittal carpal alignment—a measurement concept closely related to RCD—was an independent predictor of flexor tendon rupture after VLP fixation. Each 1-mm increase in sagittal carpal alignment was associated with a 1.56-fold increase in rupture risk. Because dorsal migration of the capitate may increase mechanical stress on the flexor tendons, these findings support the interpretation of RCD as a structural risk indicator rather than a functional predictor.

From a surgical standpoint, the correlation between RCD and PT underscores the importance of meticulous anatomical reduction of PT and stable fixation to prevent secondary displacement. Because RCD reflects the relative sagittal alignment between the radius and carpus, incomplete restoration of sagittal alignment may result in persistent dorsal positioning of the capitate. In intra-articular fractures, imperfect reduction of the joint surface may influence carpal position, and RCD may therefore serve as an indirect indicator of articular congruity. However, whether complete restoration of RCD to match the contralateral side is always necessary remains unclear, particularly when conventional radiographic parameters are within acceptable limits.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study and may be subject to selection bias. Second, the interobserver reliability of RCD measurement was moderate (ICC [2,1] = 0.68), indicating potential variability in measurement. This variability may have influenced the accuracy of the outcome assessment and could limit the clinical applicability of the findings. Furthermore, standardization of the measurement technique, including consistent definition of the position of the longitudinal axis against the volar cortex, determination of the center point of the capitate, and establishment of a measurement protocol, including observer training, is necessary before clinical implementation. Third, contralateral radiographs were not available in all patients, limiting the sample size for ΔRCD analyses. Fourth, RCD was measured on 2-dimensional lateral radiographs and may have been influenced by forearm rotation or radiographic positioning. Fifth, follow-up was limited to 1 year, and the long-term structural and clinical consequences of residual dorsal displacement remain uncertain. Whether persistent dorsal displacement contributes to late degenerative changes or delayed complications requires further longitudinal investigation. Finally, although multivariable analyses were performed, the number of covariates included in the models was limited. Therefore, residual confounding by unmeasured or inadequately adjusted factors—such as fracture characteristics, postoperative rehabilitation, and pain—cannot be excluded. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders are warranted.

In conclusion, RCD was associated with wrist flexion at 1 year after surgery but was not an independent predictor of patient-reported functional outcomes. RCD should be interpreted as a secondary structural parameter reflecting sagittal carpal alignment dependent on the quality of radial reduction. Although it does not appear to determine short-term functional outcomes, it may be a candidate structural parameter that warrants further investigation.
