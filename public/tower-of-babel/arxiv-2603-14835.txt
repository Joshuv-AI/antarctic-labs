On the evaluation of time-to-event, survival time and first passage time forecasts
Robert J. Taggart, Nicholas Loveday, Simon Louis

Abstract
Time‑to‑event forecasts are essential when decisions depend on event timing. This article develops a framework for evaluating such forecasts when the event (a) has not yet occurred or (b) is not predicted within the forecast horizon. We introduce a theory of provisional evaluation, in which each forecast is assessed against its right‑censored realization, defined as the minimum of the event time and the evaluation time. For probabilistic forecasts, we show that strictly proper scoring rules induce provisionally strictly proper scoring rules, whose expected score – computed from the right‑censored realization – is optimized under truthful forecasting. Threshold‑weighted versions of the continuous ranked probability score and the logarithmic score satisfy this property. We also develop a theory for scoring point (single‑valued) forecasts under right‑censoring. Quantile and interquartile range forecasts are shown to be provisionally elicitable, meaning that scoring functions exist for which these functionals uniquely minimize the expected score, whereas the expectation functional is not provisionally elicitable. A synthetic experiment demonstrates that the proposed scores correctly rank forecasters. Diagnostic tools, including Murphy diagrams and reliability diagrams, extend naturally. Applications to operational time‑to‑flood and time‑to‑strong‑wind forecasts illustrate the approach. Keywords: Consistent scoring function; Decision theory; Elicitability; First passage time forecast; Forecast evaluation; Forecast ranking; Forecast verification; Proper scoring rule; Survival analysis; Time-to-event forecast.



1

Introduction

Time-to-event forecasts are critical in domains where the timing of an event — not merely its occurrence — has major implications for decision making, planning, and resource allocation. Such forecasts play important roles across diverse fields, including healthcare, engineering, emergency management, human resources, finance, and the physical and life sciences. Examples include survival time for cancer patients
(
Llobera et al., 2000
)
, failure time for mechanical devices
(
Wang et al., 2015
)
, timing of geo-mechanical failure and landslides
(
Mufundirwa et al., 2010
)
, tsunami arrival time
(
Greenslade et al., 2019
)
, flood peak arrival
(
Langridge et al., 2021
)
, creep rupture time of polymers
(
Spathis & Kontou, 2012
)
, customer churn
(
Huang et al., 2012
)
, and time to loan default
(
Sarlija et al., 2009
)
.

Given their importance, such forecasts should be evaluated using rigorous methods to identify superior forecasters or models, promote continuous improvement, and ultimately support better decision making. While a robust suite of evaluation techniques and diagnostic tools has emerged over the past two decades (e.g.,
Gneiting & Raftery, 2007
;
Gneiting, 2011a
;
Ehm et al., 2016
;
Dimitriadis et al., 2024
), two challenges commonly arise in the evaluation of time-to-event forecasts, requiring extensions of existing tools. First, at evaluation time, some cases lack realized event times because the event has not yet occurred. Second, the forecasting model may have a finite prediction horizon, and the event lies beyond that horizon for some cases. This paper develops evaluation methods to address both challenges — or explains why such methods cannot exist in some situations.

We consider two main forms of time-to-event forecasts: (i) predictive distributions, which specify the likelihood of event timing over all possible future times, and (ii) single-value (‘24 months’) or interval (‘20 to 30 months’) forecasts, often representing summary statistics such as the mean or interquartile range (IQR) of a predictive distribution. Predictive distributions provide the most comprehensive representation of uncertainty in event timing. They are common in survival analysis, often expressed as survival functions and generated using statistical techniques
(
Kaplan & Meier, 1958
;
Cox, 1972
;
Wang et al., 2019
)
. Ensemble prediction systems in weather and hydrology
(
Mureau et al., 1993
;
Molteni et al., 1996
;
Schaake et al., 2007
;
Cloke & Pappenberger, 2009
)
have enabled the increased generation of probabilistic forecasts, occasionally including time-to-event predictions
(
Roksvåg et al., 2023
)
. Probabilistic first-passage-time forecasts also appear in physics, biology, and finance
(
Weiss, 1967
;
Patie, 2004
;
Iyer-Biswas & Zilman, 2016
)
.

To assess and compare the accuracy of different predictive methods, we evaluate the forecasts they generate against the corresponding observed outcomes. Scoring rules provide summary measures by assigning a numerical score
S(F,t)
to a predictive distribution
F
when compared with the corresponding realized event time
t
. A well-developed theory of scoring rules has emerged over the past two decades
(
Gneiting & Raftery, 2007
;
Gneiting & Katzfuss, 2014
)
, advocating the use of
proper scoring rules
. Proper scoring rules incentivize honest forecasts based on careful probabilistic assessment and reward predictive distributions that are sharp yet calibrated
(
Gneiting & Raftery, 2007
)
. They enable the ranking of forecasters by mean score over a common set of cases. When a scoring rule is not proper, it may assign better scores to inferior forecasts, leading to misguided inferences.

The theory of proper scoring rules assumes evaluation against the realized event time
t
. However, at evaluation time
\tau
, the event may not yet have occurred, in which case
t
is unknown and
S(F,t)
cannot be computed. The survival analysis literature addresses this by replacing the unknown
t
with the known right-censored realization
\min(t,\tau)
and by modifying the scoring rule accordingly. We call this approach
provisional evaluation
and develop a corresponding theory of scoring rules for this setting. A scoring rule is
provisionally strictly proper
on the time interval
[0,\tau]
if the only way a forecaster can optimize their expected score under evaluation against
\min(t,\tau)
is to issue a predictive distribution that aligns with their true probabilistic assessment on
[0,\tau)
(Definition
4
). We show that provisionally strictly proper scoring rules can be constructed from strictly proper scoring rules for predictive distributions (Theorem
1
) and predictive densities (Theorem
2
; cf.
Holzmann & Klar, 2017
). Consequently, we establish that threshold-weighted versions of the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)
(
Gneiting & Ranjan, 2011
)
and logarithmic score
(
Diks et al., 2011
)
are provisionally strictly proper (Corollaries
1
and
2
). This builds on related work by
Rindt et al., 2022
, who show that the threshold-weighted logarithmic score is provisionally proper, while some other scores used in survival analysis — such as the integrated Brier score
(
Graf et al., 1999
)
and survival-CRPS
(
Avati et al., 2020
)
— are not. Measures of discrimination such as the concordance index
(
Harrell Jr et al., 1996
)
are also not proper
(
Blanche et al., 2019
;
Rindt et al., 2022
)
. We illustrate the theory using well-designed synthetic experiments that confirm that (provisionally) strictly proper scoring rules rank forecasters correctly, while improper rules can mislead.

We extend this framework to single-valued and interval forecasts. To guide forecasters in issuing such predictions,
Murphy & Daan, 1985
,
Engelberg et al., 2009
, and
Gneiting, 2011a
argue that either the scoring function should be specified in advance or a specific
functional
of the predictive distribution (e.g., mean, median, or 90th percentile) should be requested. A scoring function is
strictly consistent
for a functional if the only way a forecaster can optimize their expected score is to report the true functional value of their predictive distribution
(
Gneiting, 2011a
)
. For example, squared error is strictly consistent for the expectation (i.e., mean), and absolute error for the median. A functional is
elicitable
if it admits a strictly consistent scoring function
(
Lambert et al., 2008
)
. The mean, median, and quantiles are elicitable
(
Savage, 1971
;
Thomson, 1978
;
Saerens, 2000
)
, whereas the mode and conditional value-at-risk are not
(
Heinrich, 2014
;
Gneiting, 2011a
)
.

For provisional evaluation, we say that a scoring function is
provisionally strictly consistent
for the functional on the time interval
[0,\tau]
if the only way a forecaster can optimize their expected score, given evaluation against the right-censored realization
\min(t,\tau)
, is to report the functional of their predictive distribution, ‘modulo right-censorship’ (Definition
5
). A functional is
provisionally elicitable
if it admits a provisionally strictly consistent scoring function (Definition
6
). Quantile functionals are provisionally elicitable (Theorem
3
) via threshold-weighted variants of quantile loss
(
Taggart, 2022a
)
. Predictive intervals such as the IQR, where endpoints are quantiles, are also elicitable (Theorem
4
) via threshold-weighted variants of the classical interval score
(
Dunsmore, 1968
;
Winkler, 1972
)
, which we introduce in this paper. Synthetic experiments confirm that (provisionally) strictly consistent scoring functions rank forecasters correctly, while inconsistent scoring functions can mislead. We also give general conditions under which a functional is not provisionally elicitable (Theorem
5
), and use these to show that the expectation functional is not provisionally elicitable (Corollary
3
). This imposes serious limitations on the evaluation of expected time-to-event forecasts in situations where events may take years to materialize.

Recall the second challenge for time-to-event evaluation: events may not occur within the model’s prediction horizon. This challenge arises for infrequent meteorological events when using numerical weather prediction models, whose prediction horizons are typically less than 15 days. The threshold-weighted scores addressing the first challenge are invariant to right-censoring of forecasts, permitting coherent evaluation of predictive distributions, quantile forecasts, and predictive intervals censored to the model’s prediction horizon. No such solution exists for expected time-to-event forecasts.

To complement ranking methods based on provisionally strictly proper and consistent scores, we show that several other important diagnostic tools apply to censored data. Brier score decompositions
(
Schumacher et al., 2003
)
and Murphy diagrams
(
Ehm et al., 2016
)
provide graphical diagnostics of performance across decision thresholds, and can reveal cases where one method outperforms another for decision making at different time scales despite similar mean scores. Reliability diagrams for quantile forecasts, derived via isotonic regression (cf.
Barlow et al., 1972
;
Dimitriadis et al., 2021
;
Jordan et al., 2022
), expose conditional biases and enable optimal in-sample recalibration under monotonicity constraints, even with right-censoring.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews proper scoring rules and consistent scoring functions for uncensored data and illustrates them using a synthetic experiment. Section
3
adapts this theory for right-censored realizations and forecasts, again illustrated with synthetic data with the aid of Murphy diagrams. Section
4
applies the theory to operational time-to-flood and time-to-strong-wind forecasts from the Australian Bureau of Meteorology, with particular emphasis on reliability diagrams and recalibration. Section
5
concludes with a discussion of extensions to left- and interval-censoring. Proofs of new results appear in the appendix.

2

Evaluation of time-to-event forecasts when both forecasts and realizations are known

This section reviews established evaluation methods for time-to-event forecasts when both forecasts and realizations are known. We focus on the key concepts of strictly proper scoring rules, strictly consistent scoring functions, and elicitability, as these form the foundation for the modifications introduced later to handle right-censored data. To illustrate these concepts – and new concepts introduced later – we also present a synthetic experiment for time-to-event forecasts.

The general mathematical set-up and notation is as follows. The set of possible times at which a well-defined event could occur will be represented by an interval
I
of the real line taking the form
[0,\infty)
,
[0,b)
or
[0,b]
for some positive
b
. The interior of
I
consists of
I
excluding its endpoints. The time-to-event random variable will typically be denoted by
T
and takes values in
I
, and its realization (or observation) will typically be denoted by
t
. (For now we do not consider the case where there is positive probability that the event never materializes, though an extension of the theory to this case is discussed in Section
3.3
.)

We adopt a measure-theoretic framework for probability distributions and probability density functions (PDFs). To make the body of this paper as accessible as possible, technical terms related to measure theory are restricted to this paragraph and the appendix. Suffice to say, the setting described below is very general and includes virtually every probability distribution and PDF that arises in practical applications. Throughout, a time-to-event predictive distribution (i.e. probabilistic forecast)
F
is assumed to be a Borel probability measure on
I
, and a class
\mathcal{F}
of time-to-event predictive distributions is assumed to be contained within the set of Borel probability measures on
I
. A predictive distribution
F
will be identified with its cumulative distribution function (CDF), such that given any time
s
in
I
,
F(s)
is the forecast probability that
T
does not exceed
s
. Throughout we tacitly assume that scoring functions
S
and associated weight functions
w
are Borel measurable. Occasionally we work with classes
\mathcal{F}
of probability measures that have densities (i.e. PDFs); in this case each probability measure in
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

2.1

Proper scoring rules

A scoring rule assigns a numerical score
S(F,t)
to each pair
(F,t)
, where
F
is a time-to-event predictive distribution and
t
is the time-to-event realization. In this paper we adopt the convention that scoring rules are negatively oriented, so that the score represents a penalty and a lower score is better.

Given a random variable
T
and probability distributions
F
and
G
, we write
\mathbb{E}_{F}S(G,T)
to denote the expectation of the score
S(G,T)
given that the random variable
T
is distributed according to
F
, assuming tacitly that the expectation exists.

Definition 1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a class of probability distributions on
I
.
A scoring rule
S:\mathcal{F}\times I\to(-\infty,\infty]
is said to be
proper
relative to the class
\mathcal{F}
if

\mathbb{E}_{F}S(F,T)\leq\mathbb{E}_{F}S(G,T)

(1)

for all
F
and
G
in
\mathcal{F}
. It is
strictly proper
if equality in (
1
) implies that
F=G
.

Proper scoring rules offer several advantages. If a time-to-event process
T
follows distribution
F
, then
F
achieves the best possible expected score under a proper scoring rule, and uniquely so under a strictly proper scoring rule. Moreover, if forecaster A makes optimal use of their available information and forecaster B has access to additional information and uses it optimally, then forecaster B will attain a better expected score when the scoring rule is strictly proper
(
Holzmann & Eulert, 2014
)
. Finally, if a forecaster genuinely believes the time-to-event process has distribution
F
, then issuing
F
optimizes their expected score under a proper scoring rule. Thus, score optimization aligns with honest forecasting, avoiding the forecaster’s dilemma
(
Lerch et al., 2017
)
.

We give two examples of scoring rules that are proper relative to classes
\mathcal{F}
defined on the interval
I
and one that is not. The continuous ranked probability score
\mathrm{CRPS}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{CRPS}(F,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\left(\mathds{1}\{s\geq t\}-F(s)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

(2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{F}|T-t|-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{F}|T-T^{\prime}|,

(3)

where
\mathds{1}
denotes the indicator function so that
\mathds{1}\{s\geq t\}
takes the value
1
if
s\geq t
and
0
otherwise, and where in the last term
T
and
T^{\prime}
are independent random variables with CDF
F
. The
\mathrm{CRPS}
is strictly consistent relative to the class
\mathcal{F}
of probability measures with finite first moment
(
Matheson & Winkler, 1976
;
Gneiting & Raftery, 2007
)
. The logarithmic score
\mathrm{LogS}
, defined by

\mathrm{LogS}(F,t)=-\ln F^{\prime}(t),

(4)

is strictly proper relative to the class
\mathcal{F}
of probability distributions whose members
F
have a probability density function (PDF)
F^{\prime}
(i.e., a Lebesgue density)
(
Good, 1952
;
Gneiting & Raftery, 2007
)
, and is closely related to maximum likelihood estimation. In contrast, the linear score
\mathrm{LinS}
, given by

\mathrm{LinS}(F,t)=-F^{\prime}(t)

for predictive distributions
F
with a probability density function
F^{\prime}
, is not a proper scoring rule, but rewards overprediction at the modes of a forecaster’s true predictive density
(
Winkler, 1969
;
Gneiting & Raftery, 2007
)
.

We illustrate these properties using a simple synthetic experiment involving gamma distributions. Appendix
B
gives a definition of the gamma distribution
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
with shape parameter
\alpha
, rate parameter
\beta
, and support on
[0,\infty)
. To set up the experiment, suppose that for each forecast case
i
, where
1\leq i\leq 10000
, the survival time

of the

W_{1}+W_{2}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\beta)
whenever
W_{j}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\beta)
. It follows that the marginal distribution of

is

2.2

Measures of discrimination

We briefly examine two commonly used discrimination measures that often serve as primary tools for evaluating survival-time prediction models. These are the concordance index, also known as the c-index or c-statistic
(
Harrell et al., 1982
;
Harrell Jr et al., 1996
;
Pencina & D’Agostino, 2004
)
, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC), where the curve is the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (e.g.
Heagerty et al., 2000
).

Following
Blanche et al., 2019
, consider a positive time
s
. For each forecast case
i
, denote by
p_{i}
the predictive probability that the event time
T_{i}
does not exceed
s
. Let
t_{i}
denote the corresponding realized event time. Let
\tau
denote the censoring time, which can be
\infty
if censoring is not required as is the case at present. For a set of
n
forecast–realization pairs
(p_{i},t_{i})
, the (empirical) c-index is given by

\text{c-index}=\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\mathds{1}\{p_{i}>p_{j}\}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}<t_{j}\}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}\leq\tau\}+\mathds{1}\{p_{i}=p_{j}\}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}<t_{j}\}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}\leq\tau\}/2\right)}{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}<t_{j}\}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}\leq\tau\}}.

(6)

In a nutshell, the c-index measures the probability that, for two randomly chosen subjects, the model’s predicted survival risk maintains the appropriate relative order to their true survival times, conditioned on the lower true survival time not exceeding
\tau
. The (empirical)
\mathrm{AUC}_{s}
, defined by

\mathrm{AUC}_{s}=\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\mathds{1}\{p_{i}>p_{j}\}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}\leq s<t_{j}\}+\mathds{1}\{p_{i}=p_{j}\}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}\leq s<t_{j}\}/2\right)}{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{t_{i}\leq s<t_{j}\}},

(7)

measures the probability that, for two randomly chosen subjects, the model’s predicted survival risk maintains the same appropriate relative order to the realized binary event status at time
s
. These measures are positively oriented, with a scale ranging from 0 to 1, where 0.5 indicates no discrimination ability and 1 indicates perfect ability to discriminate between cases with different outcomes.

Byrne, 2016
establishes a framework under which emprical
\mathrm{AUC}_{s}
can be interpreted as a positively oriented scoring function. He shows, however, that empirical
\mathrm{AUC}_{s}
is generally not proper, becoming proper only under restrictive assumptions that typically fail in forecasting applications.
Blanche et al., 2019
further argues that the theoretical analogue of empirical
\mathrm{AUC}_{s}
is proper, whereas the theoretical c-index is not. Nonetheless, as
Byrne, 2016
emphasises, the assumptions underpinning this theoretical setting rarely hold in practical forecasting contexts.

2.3

Consistent scoring functions

While a predictive distribution
F
for a time-to-event variable
T
provides the most complete representation of uncertainty, in many practical settings a summary – such as the mean, a specific percentile, or the interquartile range (IQR) – offers greater utility for decision making, communication, or reporting. 3

Evaluation with right-censored observations and forecasts

We now consider the case where time-to-event forecasts are evaluated at time
\tau
, but for some instances the event has not yet occurred. As noted in the introduction, even when realization time
t
is unknown because the event has not materialized by
\tau

\min(t,\tau)
is available and could be used for evaluation. Throughout, assume that the right-censoring time
\tau
is positive and belongs to the interior of the time interval
I

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}

I

denote their right-censored counterparts as follows:

\displaystyle[t]_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\min(t,\tau)

\displaystyle[x]_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\big(\min(x_{1},\tau),\ldots,\min(x_{k},\tau)\big)

\displaystyle[T]_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\min(T,\tau)

\displaystyle[A]_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\{\min(a,\tau):a\in A\}

\displaystyle[F]_{\tau}(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F(s),&s<\tau,\\
1,&s\geq\tau.\end{cases}

To develop sound methods for provisional evaluation, we require an analogue of strictly proper scoring rules, adapted so that a predictive distribution
G

\mathbb{E}_{F}S(G,[T]_{\tau})

Definition 4
.

A scoring rule
S:\mathcal{F}\times I\to(-\infty,\infty]
is
provisionally proper
on the interval
[0,\tau]
relative to the class of probability distributions
\mathcal{F}
if

\mathbb{E}_{F}S(F,[T]_{\tau})\leq\mathbb{E}_{F}S(G,[T]_{\tau})

F
and

[G]_{\tau}=[F]_{\tau}

Thus a provisionally strictly proper scoring rule

[0,\tau]
encourages forecasters to quote any distribution that aligns with their best judgment on the interval

Our definition of provisionally proper is closely related to ideas of

\mathrm{survCRPS}_{\tau}(F,t)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{CRPS}(F,t)&\text{if }t<\tau\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}F(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s&\text{if }t\geq\tau.\end{cases}

The following theorem allows one to construct provisionally strictly proper scoring rules from strictly proper scores. It can be used to show that threshold-weighted versions of the CRPS, in contrast to survival-CRPS, are provisionally strictly proper.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}_{*}
are two classes of distributions on

\mathrm{twCRPS}(F,t;w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\mathds{1}\{s\geq t\}-F(s))^{2}\,w(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

for all

in
I

F
for which the integral exists and is finite.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle v(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,x)}w(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

•

3.3

Dealing with right-censored forecasts

Sections
3.1
and
3.2
addressed forecast evaluation using right-censored realizations. We now consider evaluation of right-censored time-to-event forecasts. Such forecasts arise naturally in numerical weather prediction (NWP) models, which provide atmospheric and surface forecasts with horizons typically less than 15 days. If the event is the first time, from the model ‘initial condition time’, that a typhoon appears in the South China Sea then frequently the event will not be forecast within the prediction horizon
\tau
of the model. In such cases, one can say that the time-to-event forecast
x
is greater than
\tau
, so that
[x]_{\tau}
is known even if the model does not produce a value for
x
.

Fortunately, the provisionally strictly proper scoring rules and provisionally strictly consistent scoring functions of Sections
3.1
and
3.2
are invariant with respect to right-censorship of the forecast. That is, the threshold-weighted CRPS (
15
,
18
) and threshold-weighted logarithmic score (
23
,
24
) can be calculated if the values
F(s)
of the predictive distribution are only known for
0\leq s<\tau
, so that
[F]_{\tau}
is known even if
F
is unknown. Similarly, the values of the provisionally strictly consistent scores (
26
,
27
,
28
) for quantile and interval forecasts are unchanged if the forecast
x
is replaced with
[x]_{\tau}
. The CRPS decomposition and Murphy diagram diagnostic tools illustrated in Figure
2
are also invariant with respect to right-censorship of the forecast, provided that one restricts the diagrams to decision thresholds from
0
to
\tau
.

In summary, right-censored CDF, quantile and interval forecasts can be evaluated using the threshold-weighted scores introduced earlier. As illustrated in Section
4
, such forecasts can also be derived from ensembles of time-series forecasts with finite prediction horizons. However, right-censored mean (expected value) forecasts cannot be derived from an ensemble if the event fails to occur in any member within the model’s prediction horizon.

Right-censorship of forecasts at time
\tau
can also be used in situations where the predicted probability that the event never occurs is positive, so that the outcome space is the extended half real line
[0,\infty]
and
\mathbb{P}(T=\infty)>0
, where
T=\infty
denotes the possibility that the event never occurs. In such cases, a predictive distribution
F
, defined by
F(s)=\mathbb{P}(T\leq s)
whenever
s\in[0,\infty]
, has right-censored counterparts
[F]_{\tau}
that can be used for provisional evaluation as discussed above.

Finally, note that while only the values of right-censored forecasts are required for these evaluation methods, it is useful for communication to indicate whether a forecast has been censored. For example, if the censoring time is 12 months and the right-censored median forecast equals 12 months, one could state:

•

“The median survival time is 12 months” if the median is known to be exactly 12 months;

•

“The median survival time is at least 12 months” if the true median is unknown;

•

“The median survival time is greater than 12 months” if the true median is unknown but known not to equal the censoring time.

This example illustrates the use of isotonic regression as a diagnostic for conditional biases in time-to-event forecasts and as a tool for forecast calibration. Both forecasts and realizations are right-censored.

Discussion

Evaluating time-to-event forecasts is challenging when realizations are unknown because the event has not materialized, or forecasts are incomplete because the event did not occur (or occur with certainty) within the prediction horizon. To address these challenges, we proposed evaluation methods that combine right-censoring concepts from survival analysis with proper scoring rules and consistent scoring functions from mathematical statistics.
Specifically, we advocate provisional evaluation using provisionally strictly proper scoring rules (e.g., threshold-weighted CRPS and logarithmic score) and provisionally strictly consistent scoring functions (e.g., threshold-weighted quantile and interval scores). These scores avoid misleading inferences about forecast quality under right-censoring, as illustrated by the synthetic example. Importantly, Theorems
1
and
2
open up the possibility of constructing broad classes of new provisionally strictly proper scoring rules, induced from existing strictly proper scoring rules. While threshold-weighted logarithmic scores have previously been applied to survival data
(
Rindt et al., 2022
)
, the use of threshold-weighted CRPS, quantile loss, and interval scores for this purpose appears novel. The threshold-weighted CRPS is especially well suited to ensemble-based forecast distributions, as frequently encountered in meteorological and hydrological applications, because it admits a computationally efficient closed form and naturally accommodates distributional discontinuities. The threshold-weighted CRPS avoids the impropriety issues associated with survival‑CRPS
(
Rindt et al., 2022
;
Avati et al., 2020
)
, and our approach to scoring quantile forecasts is substantially less restrictive than a recent attempt to render Portnoy’s estimator proper
(
Yanagisawa, 2023
;
Portnoy, 2003
)
. Conversely, we showed that the mean (expectation) functional is not provisionally elicitable and provided sufficient conditions for identifying other functionals that share this fundamental limitation.

Threshold-weighted CRPS and quantile scores also admit decompositions into Brier and elementary scores, enabling graphical diagnostics for interpreting provisional rankings through a decision-theoretic lens, even under right-censoring. Although not illustrated here, a similar diagnostic exists for the threshold-weighted interval score, since it is the sum of two quantile scores. We are unaware of an analogous decomposition for the threshold-weighted logarithmic score. This paper also introduced reliability diagrams for quantile forecasts under right-censoring using isotonic regression, which supports CORP-style decompositions
(
Dimitriadis et al., 2021
;
Dimitriadis et al., 2024
)
into uncertainty, miscalibration, and discrimination components. It is likely that isotonic distributional regression
(
Henzi et al., 2021
)
could similarly be applied to time-to-event predictive distributions.

Our results show that predictive distributions, quantiles, and intervals for time-to-event forecasts can be evaluated soundly under right-censoring. However, the provisional non-elicitability of the expectation functional implies that single-valued forecasts targeting the mean cannot be scored soundly using right-censored data. This may be inconsequential for short-term events. However, it is problematic for events that may take years to occur and questions the practice of training models on squared loss in such cases. While long-term expected time-to-event forecasts may remain useful for some applications, their quality must be inferred from proxies such as provisional evaluation of predictive distributions, which cannot fully assess tail behavior and thus cannot guarantee mean-value accuracy.

Interestingly, threshold-weighted scores were originally motivated by the need to evaluate predictive performance with emphasis on extreme events
(
Diks et al., 2011
;
Gneiting & Ranjan, 2011
;
Taggart, 2022a
;
Allen et al., 2023
)
. Here, we identify another important role: handling right-censored data.
The use of left-censored and interval-censored data is of interest in the survival literature
(
Lindsey & Ryan, 1998
;
Gomez et al., 1992
)
and elsewhere.
Although not explored in this paper, we expect that extensions of our results to left- and interval-censoring are feasible by adapting threshold weights to the specific censoring structure.
