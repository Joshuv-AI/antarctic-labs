Support-Conditioned Flow Matching Is Kernel Smoothing
Daniel Matsui Smola

Abstract
Generative models are often conditioned on a small set of examples via cross-attention. Under the Gaussian optimal-transport path, we show that the exact velocity field induced by a finite support set is a Nadaraya–Watson kernel smoother whose bandwidth decreases with flow time, from broad averaging at early steps to nearest-neighbor at late steps. A single Gaussian-kernel attention head exactly computes this field, connecting cross-attention conditioning to classical kernel theory. The theory predicts three failure regimes: nearest-neighbor collapse of the kernel at high dimension, mismatch between the isotropic kernel and the data geometry, and insufficient support for nonparametric estimation. Experiments on Gaussian mixtures, spherical shells, and DINOv2 ImageNet features confirm that learned conditioning improves in precisely these regimes, and that IP-Adapter’s cross-attention implements approximate NW smoothing in practice.

Introduction

Text alone often cannot specify the visual attributes one wants to generate in an image: a particular face, a beloved pet, art style, or object appearance. The standard solution is to condition on reference examples via cross-attention: IP-Adapter
(
Ye et al. 2023
)
, Context Diffusion
(
Najdenkoska et al. 2024
)
, SuTI
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
, and few-shot diffusion models
(
Giannone et al. 2022
)
all condition the generative process by attending to a set of reference image features.

However, this mechanism is understood only empirically. One may ask:
What is the mathematical structure of the velocity field that a finite reference set induces?

What governs the success or failure of cross-attention conditioning?

We answer these questions in the flow matching setting. Given a finite support set of samples from an unknown target distribution, the task is to learn a velocity field that generates fresh samples via ODE integration without parameter updates at test time. We show that the exact velocity field induced by such a support set under the Gaussian optimal-transport path is a Nadaraya–Watson (NW) kernel smoother
(
Nadaraya 1964
;
Watson 1964
)
. This amounts to a weighted average of the support points, with Gaussian kernel weights that concentrate more heavily on nearby points as the flow progresses. A single Gaussian-kernel cross-attention head computes this field exactly (Theorem
3
), for any support set and any flow time.

Classical kernel smoothing has well-characterized failure modes, and each appears in the conditioning setting:

1.

Nearest-neighbor collapse.
At high dimension, concentration of measure collapses the Gaussian kernel weights to a single nearest neighbor. The exact field reduces to nearest-neighbor interpolation, and using it as an inductive bias becomes harmful.

2.

Geometry mismatch.
The isotropic kernel smoothes uniformly in all directions. When variation in the data is concentrated along a low-dimensional subspace, the kernel under-smoothes the informative directions while wasting bandwidth on uninformative ones, yielding high-variance velocity estimates independently of support size.

3.

Support scarcity.
At small support sizes, nonparametric estimation is data-starved. Learned models improve by amortizing over a meta-distribution, exploiting cross-task structure that single-task kernel estimation cannot access.

We verify each failure mode experimentally on Gaussian mixtures, spherical shells, and DINOv2 ImageNet features, and show that learned conditioning improves precisely where the theory predicts the exact field is deficient. IP-Adapter’s attention weights approximate NW kernel weights in practice (§
5
), suggesting the framework applies beyond our controlled setting.

Relation to prior work.

Tsai et al. 2019
and
Goel & Bartlett 2024
show that attention
can
implement kernel regression. We show that the OT-FM velocity field
is
a kernel smoother—the attention construction is one implementation of a structure already present in the velocity.
Fukumizu et al. 2025
and
Kunkel & Trabs 2025
derive minimax rates from the endpoint KDE connection;
Zhou et al. 2026
analyzes the empirical velocity and discretization bias. We derive the full-path velocity-field decomposition, of which the endpoint KDE is a special case, and experimentally verify its predicted failure modes.

Background and setup

Flow matching learns a velocity field
v(x,t)
that transports samples from a noise distribution (
t=0
) to a target data distribution (
t=1
) via ODE integration
\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t=v(x,t)
. To generate, one draws noise
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and integrates forward to obtain a sample at
t=1
.

Optimal-transport (OT) flow matching.

Given a data distribution
q
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and base
p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, the OT conditional path
(
Lipman et al. 2023
)
is

X_{t}=t\,X_{1}+\sigma_{t}\,X_{0},\qquad\sigma_{t}=1-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}})\,t,

(1)

where
X_{1}\sim q
,
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, and
0<\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\leq 1
. The conditional flow matching objective regresses the conditional velocity target
Y_{t}=X_{1}-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}})X_{0}
.

We define the
de-scaled bandwidth

h(t)=\sigma_{t}/t
, which is strictly decreasing on
(0,1]
, sweeping from
\infty
(at
t\to 0
) to
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
(at
t=1
). As we will show, this quantity connects flow matching time to kernel density estimation bandwidth.

Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression.

For bandwidth
h>0
and support set
S=\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, the Gaussian kernel weights and local mean are

w_{i}(x)=\frac{\phi_{h}(x-s_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{h}(x-s_{j})},\qquad m_{h}(x;S)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(x)\,s_{i},

(2)

where
\phi_{h}(z)=(2\pi h^{2})^{-d/2}\exp(-\|z\|^{2}/2h^{2})
. The effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}
measures how many support points meaningfully contribute.

Problem: support-conditioned generation.

Given
S=\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}q
from an unknown
q
, the goal is to learn a velocity field
v_{\theta}(x,t,S)
that generates fresh samples from
q
via ODE integration, without parameter updates at test time. We call this
In-Context Flow Matching
(ICFM) and train with the support-conditioned loss:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\Pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{S\sim q^{m}}\;\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}\sim q,\,X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\,t\sim\tau}\bigl[\|v_{\theta}(X_{t},t,S)-Y_{t}\|^{2}\bigr],

(3)

where
\Pi
is a meta-distribution over data distributions and
\tau=\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1]
.

We distinguish three velocity fields. The
population field

u_{t}^{q}
is the marginal velocity induced by the true
q
. The
empirical plug-in field

u_{t}^{S}
replaces
q
by
\hat{q}_{S}=m^{-1}\sum_{i}\delta_{s_{i}}
and is exactly computable for any support set via a single Gaussian-kernel attention head (§
3.3
). The
learned field

v_{\theta}
is trained over tasks from
\Pi
. The empirical question is whether
v_{\theta}
improves over
u_{t}^{S}
, and if so, whether the improvement can be explained by identifiable failure modes of the plug-in field.

3
The NW velocity identity

Full proofs are in Appendix
A
; we state the key results with proof sketches here.

3.1
The OT path as a bandwidth sweep

We show that the OT probability path through a finite support set is a kernel density estimate (KDE) whose bandwidth decreases with time. The key step is
de-scaling
: dividing
X_{t}=tX_{1}+\sigma_{t}X_{0}
by
t
gives
\tilde{X}_{t}\coloneqq X_{t}/t=X_{1}+(\sigma_{t}/t)\,X_{0}
. Since
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, this is
X_{1}
plus Gaussian noise at standard deviation
h(t)=\sigma_{t}/t
. When the data distribution is the empirical measure
\hat{q}_{S}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\delta_{s_{i}}
(uniform point masses at the support points), the de-scaled density is a Gaussian KDE:

Proposition 1

(De-scaled empirical OT path is a KDE)
.

Under the Gaussian OT conditional path (
1
), for every
t\in(0,1]
, the de-scaled empirical density
\tilde{p}_{t}^{S}(\tilde{x})\coloneqq t^{d}\,p_{t}^{S}(t\tilde{x})
equals

\tilde{p}_{t}^{S}(\tilde{x})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{h(t)}(\tilde{x}-s_{i}),\qquad h(t)=\frac{\sigma_{t}}{t}.

(4)

As
t
traverses
(0,1]
,
h(t)
sweeps from
\infty
to
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
: the OT path through a support set is a continuous multi-resolution KDE, from maximum smoothing to near-delta peaks at the support points.

Proof.

Using the Gaussian scaling identity
t^{d}\,\phi_{\sigma_{t}}(t\,z)=\phi_{\sigma_{t}/t}(z)
:

t^{d}\,p_{t}^{S}(t\tilde{x})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}t^{d}\,\phi_{\sigma_{t}}(t\tilde{x}-ts_{i})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\phi_{h(t)}(\tilde{x}-s_{i})
.
∎

Implication.
At each flow time
t
, the generative process maintains a KDE of the support set at bandwidth
h(t)
. The ODE generates samples from a continuously sharpening density estimate: broad smoothing at early times, near-delta peaks at the support points as
t\to 1
. The noise schedule
\sigma_{t}
therefore determines the bandwidth schedule
h(t)=\sigma_{t}/t
: for practitioners, designing the noise schedule for support-conditioned generation is equivalent to selecting a time-varying kernel bandwidth, a problem with a rich classical literature. The velocity field that drives this sharpening is characterized next.

3.2
The exact velocity field is a NW smoother

The marginal velocity field is
u_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}})X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x]
. For the Gaussian OT path, the Tweedie identity
(
Efron 2011
)
connects this to the score, and de-scaling converts the score into NW form.

Proposition 2

(Exact empirical OT-FM velocity field)
.

Under the Gaussian OT conditional path (
1
) with
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, the exact empirical velocity field induced by support set
S
is:

u_{t}^{S}(x)=\tilde{x}+\frac{m_{h(t)}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x}}{\sigma_{t}},\qquad\tilde{x}=x/t,\quad h=h(t),

(5)

where
m_{h(t)}(\tilde{x};S)=\sum_{i}w_{i}(\tilde{x})\,s_{i}
is the NW local mean with Gaussian kernel weights at bandwidth
h(t)=\sigma_{t}/t
.

Proof sketch.

Applying Tweedie’s identity to the signal-plus-noise decomposition
X_{t}=tX_{1}+\sigma_{t}X_{0}
gives
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]=x/t+(\sigma_{t}^{2}/t)\,\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
. Substituting into the marginal velocity and simplifying yields the velocity–score relation:

u_{t}(x)=\frac{x}{t}+\frac{\sigma_{t}}{t}\,\nabla\log p_{t}(x).

(6)

Differentiating the KDE from Proposition
1
gives the score in NW form:
\nabla\log\tilde{p}_{t}^{S}(\tilde{x})=(m_{h}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x})/h^{2}
. Evaluating (
6
) at
x=t\tilde{x}
and substituting yields:

u_{t}^{S}(t\tilde{x})=\tilde{x}+\frac{\sigma_{t}}{t^{2}}\cdot\frac{m_{h}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x}}{h(t)^{2}}.

Since
h(t)=\sigma_{t}/t
, we have
\sigma_{t}/(t^{2}h^{2})=1/\sigma_{t}
, yielding (
5
).
∎

Implication.
The velocity in (
5
) consists of an inertia term
\tilde{x}
and a correction
(m_{h}-\tilde{x})/\sigma_{t}
that steers toward the NW mean of nearby support points. As
t\to 1
and
\sigma_{t}\to\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
, this correction dominates: at late flow times, the field pulls samples strongly toward the kernel-weighted average of their neighbors. The exact velocity is therefore entirely determined by the NW estimator
m_{h}
. If the kernel weights degenerate (e.g., collapsing to a single neighbor) or the kernel shape mismatches the data geometry, the velocity field inherits these defects—it steers samples incorrectly, degrading generation quality. We characterize these failures in §
4
.

3.3
Attention realization

Since the NW local mean is a weighted average with softmax-normalized weights, it can be computed by a cross-attention head with appropriately chosen logits:

Theorem 3

(Attention realization of the empirical OT-FM field)
.

Under the Gaussian OT conditional path (
1
), consider a single cross-attention head with query
\tilde{x}
, keys and values
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, and Gaussian-kernel logits
\ell_{i}=-\|\tilde{x}-s_{i}\|^{2}/(2h(t)^{2})
. Define the affine post-map
A_{t}(\tilde{x},z)=\tilde{x}+(z-\tilde{x})/\sigma_{t}
. Then

A_{t}\!\bigl(\tilde{x},\;\mathrm{Attn}(\tilde{x},t,S)\bigr)=u_{t}^{S}(t\,\tilde{x}).

(7)

That is, one Gaussian-kernel cross-attention head followed by an affine map exactly implements the marginal velocity field induced by
S
.

Proof sketch.

The softmax weights with logits
\ell_{i}=-\|\tilde{x}-s_{i}\|^{2}/(2h^{2})
satisfy
\alpha_{i}=\exp(\ell_{i})/\sum_{j}\exp(\ell_{j})
. Since
\phi_{h}(z)\propto\exp(-\|z\|^{2}/2h^{2})
, the normalization constant
(2\pi h^{2})^{-d/2}
cancels between numerator and denominator:
\alpha_{i}=\phi_{h}(\tilde{x}-s_{i})/\sum_{j}\phi_{h}(\tilde{x}-s_{j})=w_{i}(\tilde{x})
, the NW weights. So
\mathrm{Attn}(\tilde{x},t,S)=\sum_{i}w_{i}\,s_{i}=m_{h}(\tilde{x};S)
, and
A_{t}(\tilde{x},m_{h})=\tilde{x}+(m_{h}-\tilde{x})/\sigma_{t}=u_{t}^{S}(t\tilde{x})
by Proposition
2
.
∎

Implication.
The exact NW field can be embedded as a frozen, zero-parameter attention head inside any learned model, providing a built-in nonparametric baseline. This gives a concrete experimental tool: if the exact head helps, the NW signal is useful at that operating point; if it makes no difference, the signal is uninformative and the model learns to ignore it. We use this probe in §
4.2
.

3.4
Multi-head NW decomposition

In practice, the learned model uses multiple cross-attention heads with trained projection matrices alongside the frozen exact head. The NW framework extends naturally to this setting:

Proposition 4

(Multi-head attention as NW ensemble)
.

Multi-head cross-attention with
H
heads computes
\mathrm{MHA}(q,Z)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}W_{O}^{h}\,m_{K_{h}}\!\bigl(q;\,\{(z_{i},\,W_{V}^{h}z_{i})\}\bigr)
, where each
m_{K_{h}}
is a generalized NW estimator with kernel
K_{h}(x,s)=\exp(x^{\top}W_{Q}^{h\top}W_{K}^{h}s/\sqrt{d_{k}})
. The logit bilinear form has rank
\leq d_{k}=d_{\mathrm{model}}/H
, so each head’s weights depend on at most
d_{k}
independent linear functionals of the query and keys.

The proof follows directly from the definition of multi-head attention (Appendix
B
).

Implication.
Each learned head computes a NW-type velocity correction in a
d_{k}
-dimensional subspace. Since the isotropic kernel degrades with ambient dimension (as we will show in §
4.2
), projecting into lower-dimensional subspaces allows each head to maintain effective smoothing where the full-dimensional kernel would collapse. The learned component of the model is therefore an ensemble of
H
adaptive smoothers, each avoiding the full curse of dimensionality by operating in a learned
d_{k}
-dimensional projection of the support set. Formal convergence rates and an anisotropic extension are given in Appendices
B
and
C
.

4
Failure modes and what learning corrects

The NW identity from §
3
establishes that cross-attention conditioning implements a principled nonparametric estimator. However, the specific isotropic Gaussian kernel underlying the plug-in field has well-known limitations. We now test three predictions from classical NW theory and show what the learned model corrects in each case.

4.1
Setup

All experiments train the ICFM model from (
3
). The velocity network
v_{\theta}(x,t,S)
is a transformer (
d_{\mathrm{model}}=128
, 3 layers) that conditions on the support set via
H=4
learned cross-attention heads with trainable
W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}
projections (the generalized NW smoothers from Proposition
4
). Separately, an optional
frozen exact head
computes the plug-in velocity from Theorem
3
using Gaussian-kernel attention and feeds it as an additional signal into the output MLP. The key experimental manipulation is enabling or disabling this head:

•

Plug-in:
the exact NW velocity field integrated alone (no learned parameters).

•

Learned (OFF):
learned heads only. This is closest to what practical systems (IP-Adapter, Context Diffusion) do.

•

Learned (ON):
learned heads + frozen exact head. Our experimental probe, testing whether explicitly providing the NW signal as an inductive bias helps.

If ON outperforms OFF, the NW velocity is a useful inductive bias. If ON matches OFF, the signal is uninformative. Sample quality is measured by MMD
2
(unbiased Gaussian-kernel U-statistic) and C2ST on 100 held-out tasks (
\geq\!4
seeds per condition). Full training details are in Appendix
D.1
.

4.2
Collapse: the isotropic kernel becomes nearest-neighbor

The isotropic Gaussian kernel in Proposition
2
is subject to the curse of dimensionality. Concentration of measure makes pairwise distance contrasts vanish as
O(1/\sqrt{d})

(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
, and the exponential kernel amplifies even small relative differences into overwhelming weight ratios
(
Bengio et al. 2005
)
. The NW weights should therefore collapse to a single nearest neighbor at high
d
.

To see why this degrades the velocity, recall that the plug-in error is
u_{t}^{S}-u_{t}^{q}=(m_{h}^{S}-m_{h}^{q})/\sigma_{t}
(from Proposition
2
). The NW variance scales as
O(1/(m\,h^{d}\,f(\tilde{x})))

(
Tsybakov 2009
)
, and dividing by
\sigma_{t}^{2}
gives a velocity MSE of order
O(t^{d}/(m\,\sigma_{t}^{d+2}\,f(\tilde{x})))
, which grows exponentially with
d
(proof in Appendix
A.4
). At high
d
, the kernel concentrates on a single neighbor (
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\to 1
, where
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
measures how many support points contribute) and the velocity becomes a volatile nearest-neighbor lookup. This is the memorization phenomenon documented in trained diffusion models
(
Kim et al. 2025
)
. For practitioners, the
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
quantity provides a closed-form, per-timestep diagnostic of this effect without requiring model training. With the kernel collapsed, the signal from the exact head becomes uninformative, and we expect the ON condition to converge toward OFF as
d
increases.

Experiment.
We train on Gaussian mixtures at
d=2,4,8,16
with the frozen exact head ON vs. OFF (
\geq\!4
seeds per condition).

Results.

(left) shows the exact head benefit declining monotonically from

The learned heads avoid collapse by projecting into

4.3
Geometry mismatch: the isotropic kernel wastes bandwidth

The isotropic kernel can also fail when its shape does not match the data geometry, independently of whether
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is healthy. An isotropic bandwidth smoothes equally in all directions; when variation in the data is concentrated along a low-dimensional subspace, this wastes capacity on uninformative directions while under-smoothing informative ones
(
Lepski et al. 1997
)
. On distributions with anisotropic structure, the learned model (which can adapt its kernel via
W_{Q},W_{K}
projections; Proposition
4
) should dramatically outperform the plug-in, even when the plug-in’s kernel weights are well-distributed.

Experiment.
We test on spherical shells (distributions where all variation is radial, embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

d=16

collapse, we also evaluate on DINOv2+PCA ImageNet features
(
Oquab et al. 2024
)
(pre-computed DINOv2 ViT-S/14 class features projected via PCA) and a whitened variant of the same features where whitening makes the covariance isotropic, artificially collapsing
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. If
n_{\mathrm{eff}}

4.4
Support scarcity: when the plug-in is data-poor

The plug-in field has an intrinsic quality ceiling. By Proposition
1
at
t=1
, its ODE flow produces samples distributed as
\hat{q}_{S}*\phi_{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}
. This is a Gaussian KDE at bandwidth
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
; at small
m
, it is data-poor regardless of kernel shape or dimensionality.

Experiment.
We use the DINOv2+PCA ImageNet features introduced in §
4.3
at
d=16
, because neither collapse nor geometry mismatch is active at this dimension. This isolates support size as the remaining variable. We train separate models at each support size
m\in\{1,5,10,25,50\}
, comparing the learned model (OFF) to the plug-in.

What learning corrects.
The plug-in velocity is the Bayes-optimal predictor for a single task under the empirical measure
\hat{q}_{S}
(Appendix
A.6
), but it cannot use information from other tasks. The ICFM loss (
3
) trains over many tasks drawn from
\Pi

v^{*}(x,t,S)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid X_{t}=x,t,S]
, which integrates over all distributions consistent with
S
under the meta-distribution. When
m
is small and many distributions are consistent with
S
, this posterior predictive is substantially better than the empirical plug-in because it exploits shared structure across tasks
(
Meunier et al. 2025
)
. This meta-learning advantage is what the learned model provides and what single-task kernel estimation cannot access.

4.5
Controls

Extension to practical conditioning

The theory above is derived for Gaussian-kernel attention under the Gaussian OT path. Do practical conditioning systems, which use scaled dot-product attention and different noise schedules, exhibit similar NW structure?

Related work

Attention as kernel smoothing.

Tsai et al. 2019
interpret attention through kernel smoothing;
Katharopoulos et al. 2020
and
Choromanski et al. 2021
develop kernelized attention variants;
Goel & Bartlett 2024
show a transformer can represent a Kalman filter, constructing an attention-based kernel regression in the process. Our result is different in kind: the OT-FM velocity field
is
a NW smoother; the attention construction is one implementation of a structure already present in the empirical velocity.

Flow matching theory.

Lipman et al. 2023
and
Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden 2023
establish the flow matching framework.
Fukumizu et al. 2025
and
Kunkel & Trabs 2025
derive minimax rates via the endpoint KDE connection;
Zhou et al. 2026
analyzes the empirical velocity and discretization bias;
Liu et al. 2025
identify oracle-velocity regimes at late timesteps. We derive the full-path bandwidth family
h(t)=\sigma_{t}/t
and the velocity-field NW decomposition, of which the endpoint KDE is a special case (§
3.1
).

Nonparametric estimation theory.

Each failure mode in §
4
has classical roots. The 1-NN collapse follows from concentration of measure
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
and exponential kernel amplification
(
Bengio et al. 2005
)
. The geometry-mismatch failure connects to anisotropic minimax theory
(
Lepski et al. 1997
)
. The support-scarcity crossover reflects the gap between single-task NW rates
(
Stone 1982
;
Tsybakov 2009
)
and meta-learning rates that exploit cross-task structure
(
Meunier et al. 2025
)
. The 1-NN collapse also connects to recent memorization analyses in diffusion models:
Kim et al. 2025
show that empirical score models behave as nearest-neighbor maps in the low-noise regime;
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
gives a closed-form version of this phenomenon for support-conditioned flows.

FMNP
(
Abu Hamad & Rosenbaum 2025
)
is closest in problem setting but models conditional function laws, not density transport. DiScoFormer
(
Ilin & Sushko 2025

(

7
Discussion and conclusion

Summary.

The NW identity answers both sides of the question posed in §
1
. Cross-attention conditioning works because it implements kernel smoothing—a consistent nonparametric estimator whose quality improves with support size. On well-structured features, the plug-in field is already near-optimal. But the identity also reveals when conditioning fails: 1-NN collapse in high dimensions, geometry mismatch on anisotropic data, and support scarcity at small
m
. In each case, the learned model overcomes the failure—by maintaining multi-neighbor smoothing through adaptive projections (§
4.3
), and by amortizing over the meta-distribution to compensate for limited support (§
4.4
). The NW structure persists approximately in IP-Adapter (§
5
), suggesting the framework extends to practical systems. For reference-conditioned image generation systems that condition on only a few reference images, the support scarcity regime is most relevant: the meta-learning advantage is largest at small
m
.

The role of
\Pi
.

The learned model’s advantage depends on the meta-distribution
\Pi
. To test this, we train on three of the four
\mathbb{R}^{2}
families and evaluate on the held-out family (Appendix
D.3
). When the held-out family shares geometric structure with the training families (e.g., spirals held out from GMMs, rings, and moons), the learned model transfers well (
2.3\times
better than the plug-in). When the held-out family is structurally dissimilar (e.g., cluster-based GMMs held out from curve-based families), transfer fails catastrophically (
37\times
worse). The three curve-based families (rings, moons, spirals) generalize to each other, but cluster-based GMMs do not transfer to or from curves (Figure
7
). One possible explanation is shared manifold structure: families whose supports lie on topologically similar low-dimensional manifolds may provide useful priors for each other, while families with fundamentally different support geometry (clusters vs. curves) do not. Formalizing this hypothesis and characterizing which properties of
\Pi
enable amortization remain open problems.

Scope and extensions.

The formal results assume Gaussian-kernel attention and the Gaussian OT path. Extending to VP paths is natural: any linear Gaussian path
X_{t}=\mu_{t}X_{1}+\sigma_{t}X_{0}
produces a Gaussian convolution of the scaled data distribution, so a NW-like velocity decomposition may exist with bandwidth
h(t)=\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t}
. Formalizing the connection to SDPA, which we demonstrate empirically in §
5
, is another natural direction. Two open problems remain: providing a formal bound on the learned model’s improvement over the plug-in, and determining the optimal noise schedule for finite support sets, balancing over-smoothing at early times against 1-NN collapse at late times.

Proofs

with
\sigma_{t}=1-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}})t

A.1
Building blocks

The following intermediate results are used in the proofs of Propositions
1
and
2

Proposition 5

(Marginal density)
.

The marginal density of
X_{t}
is
p_{t}(x)=(q_{t}*\phi_{\sigma_{t}})(x)
where
q_{t}(y)=t^{-d}q(y/t)
.

Proof.

X_{t}\mid X_{1}=x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(tx_{1},\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{d})
. Marginalizing over
X_{1}\sim q
and substituting
z=tx_{1}
, we obtain
p_{t}(x)=\int\phi_{\sigma_{t}}(x-z)\,t^{-d}q(z/t)\,dz=(q_{t}*\phi_{\sigma_{t}})(x)
.
∎

Proposition 6

(Tweedie identity for OT path)
.

\mathbb{E}[tX_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]=x+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
.

Proof.

Write
X_{t}=M+\sigma_{t}Z
with
M=tX_{1}
and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Differentiating
p_{t}(x)=\int\phi_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\mu)\,q_{t}(\mu)\,d\mu
, we get
\nabla p_{t}(x)=\frac{p_{t}(x)}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}(\mathbb{E}[M\mid X_{t}=x]-x)
. Dividing by
p_{t}(x)
and rearranging gives the result.
∎

Proposition 7

(Velocity–score identity)
.

u_{t}(x)=x/t+(\sigma_{t}/t)\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
.

Proof.

The marginal velocity is
u_{t}(x)=(\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}})x)/\sigma_{t}
. Substituting
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]=x/t+(\sigma_{t}^{2}/t)\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
from Proposition
6
(divided by
t
), we get

u_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}\Bigl(\frac{x}{t}+\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x)-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}})x\Bigr).

The coefficient of
x
is
(1/\sigma_{t})(1/t-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}))=(1/\sigma_{t})(\sigma_{t}/t)=1/t
, using
\sigma_{t}=1-(1-\sigma_{\mathrm{min}})t
.
∎

Proposition 8

(De-scaled marginal and score)
.

Define
\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})=t^{d}p_{t}(t\tilde{x})
. Then: (i)
\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})=(q*\phi_{h(t)})(\tilde{x})
, and (ii)
\nabla_{\tilde{x}}\log\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})=(t/h(t))(u_{t}(t\tilde{x})-\tilde{x})
.

Proof.

(i) Using
t^{d}\phi_{\sigma_{t}}(tz)=\phi_{\sigma_{t}/t}(z)
, we get
t^{d}p_{t}(t\tilde{x})=\int\phi_{h(t)}(\tilde{x}-x_{1})\,q(x_{1})\,dx_{1}
. (ii) Chain rule gives
\nabla_{\tilde{x}}\log\tilde{p}_{t}=t\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(t\tilde{x})
. By Proposition
7

\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=(tu_{t}(x)-x)/\sigma_{t}
, so at
x=t\tilde{x}
:
t\cdot t(u_{t}(t\tilde{x})-\tilde{x})/\sigma_{t}=(t/h(t))(u_{t}(t\tilde{x})-\tilde{x})
.
∎

\nabla\log\tilde{p}_{t}^{S}(\tilde{x})=(m_{h}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x})/h^{2}

A.4
Velocity MSE bound (§
4.2
)

Let
S=\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}q
and fix
t\in(0,1]
. Write
h=h(t)
and let
\tilde{x}
be an interior point of
\mathrm{supp}(q)
. We require:

(i)

q
has a Lebesgue density
f
with
f(\tilde{x})>0
.

(ii)

f
is continuous in a neighborhood of
\tilde{x}
.

(iii)

The regime
m\,h^{d}\to\infty
(enough support points per kernel volume).

Under (i)–(iii), the NW local mean
m_{h}^{S}(\tilde{x})
is a ratio of two sample means whose denominators converge to
f_{h}(\tilde{x})=(f*\phi_{h})(\tilde{x})>0
. The variance of the numerator is
O(1/(m\,h^{d}))
by the law of large numbers for kernel averages
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
, and the delta method for ratios gives
\mathrm{Var}(m_{h}^{S}(\tilde{x}))=O(1/(m\,h^{d}\,f(\tilde{x})))
. Since
u_{t}^{S}(t\tilde{x})-u_{t}^{q}(t\tilde{x})=(m_{h}^{S}(\tilde{x})-m_{h}^{q}(\tilde{x}))/\sigma_{t}
by Proposition
2
, the velocity MSE is
\mathrm{Var}(m_{h}^{S})/\sigma_{t}^{2}=O(t^{d}/(m\,\sigma_{t}^{d+2}\,f(\tilde{x})))
. No smoothness beyond continuity is needed because both the sample and population NW means use the same bandwidth; there is no bias term.

The softmax weights with logits

are

, so

. Applying

:

by Proposition

∎

Corollary 9

(Dot-product realization)
.

The Gaussian-kernel logits equal unscaled dot products via a nonlinear feature lift into
\mathbb{R}^{d+2}
. Define
Q(\tilde{x},t)=[\tilde{x}/h,\;-\|\tilde{x}\|^{2}/(2h^{2}),\;1]

A.6
Bayes optimality and plug-in distributional rate (§
4.4
)

The ICFM loss (
3
) is a squared-error regression with conditioning on

. The unconstrained minimizer is the conditional expectation
v^{*}(x,t,S)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid X_{t}=x,t,S]
—the posterior mean velocity given the support set and current state. The plug-in field (Proposition

for a single task, but generally not under the meta-distribution

(Plug-in distributional rate)
.

The ODE flow of
u_{t}^{S}
from

Proof.

By Proposition

p_{1}^{S}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\phi_{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}(x-s_{i})
. The velocity

generates

via the continuity equation, so the ODE flow transports

∎

Appendix B
Multi-head theory

Definition 11

(Generalized NW estimator)
.

For any positive kernel
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
, the generalized NW estimator is
m_{K}(x;\{(x_{i},y_{i})\})=\sum_{i}w_{i}^{K}(x)\,y_{i}
with
w_{i}^{K}(x)=K(x,x_{i})/\sum_{j}K(x,x_{j})
.

Proof of Proposition
4
.

By the MHA definition,
\text{MHA}(q,Z)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}W_{O}^{h}\,\text{Head}_{h}(q,Z)
. Each head’s attention weights
\alpha_{i}^{(h)}=\exp((W_{Q}^{h}q)^{\top}W_{K}^{h}z_{i}/\sqrt{d_{k}})/\sum_{j}(\cdots)=w_{i}^{K_{h}}(q)
with
K_{h}(x,s)=\exp(x^{\top}W_{Q}^{h\top}W_{K}^{h}s/\sqrt{d_{k}})
. The output
\text{Head}_{h}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{(h)}W_{V}^{h}z_{i}=m_{K_{h}}(q;\{(z_{i},W_{V}^{h}z_{i})\})
.
The logit matrix
A_{h}=W_{Q}^{h\top}W_{K}^{h}/\sqrt{d_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}\times d_{\mathrm{model}}}
has
\text{rank}(A_{h})\leq d_{k}
.
∎

Proposition 12

(Spherical NW rate)
.

Under QK-normalization (
\|\tilde{q}\|=\|\tilde{k}_{i}\|=R
for all
i
,
d_{k}\geq 2
), the attention kernel is vMF-zonal on
S^{d_{k}-1}(R)
. With tunable concentration
\kappa_{m}\asymp m^{2/(d_{k}+3)}
, the NW estimator achieves
\text{MSE}^{*}=O(m^{-4/(d_{k}+3)})
, the Stone (1982) minimax rate in
d_{k}-1
intrinsic dimensions.

Proof.

Under spherical normalization, the logits become
\tilde{q}^{\top}\tilde{k}_{i}/\sqrt{d_{k}}=\kappa\cos\theta_{i}
where
\theta_{i}
is the geodesic angle between
\tilde{q}
and
\tilde{k}_{i}
on
S^{d_{k}-1}(R)
, and
\kappa=R^{2}/\sqrt{d_{k}}
. The kernel
K(\theta)=\exp(\kappa\cos\theta)
is the von Mises–Fisher (vMF) density up to normalization, which is zonal on the sphere.

For the NW bias: the kernel average
m_{K}(q)=\sum_{i}w_{i}y_{i}
with
w_{i}\propto\exp(\kappa\cos\theta_{i})
concentrates on a spherical cap of angular radius
O(1/\sqrt{\kappa})
. A Laplace approximation of the vMF integral gives an effective bandwidth
h_{\text{eff}}\sim 1/\sqrt{\kappa}
on the
(d_{k}-1)
-dimensional sphere, yielding bias
O(h_{\text{eff}}^{2})=O(1/\kappa)
for Lipschitz targets.

For the NW variance: the effective number of points in the cap scales as
m\cdot\text{cap volume}\sim m\cdot h_{\text{eff}}^{d_{k}-1}=m/\kappa^{(d_{k}-1)/2}
, giving variance
O(\kappa^{(d_{k}-1)/2}/m)
.

Balancing bias
2
and variance:
1/\kappa^{2}=\kappa^{(d_{k}-1)/2}/m
gives
\kappa^{*}\asymp m^{2/(d_{k}+3)}
, and MSE
{}^{*}=O(m^{-4/(d_{k}+3)})
.
∎

Appendix C
Anisotropic extension

For
M\succ 0
, define the anisotropic NW weights
w_{i}^{(h,M)}(x;S)=\exp(-\|x-s_{i}\|_{M}^{2}/2h^{2})/\sum_{j}\exp(-\|x-s_{j}\|_{M}^{2}/2h^{2})
with
\|z\|_{M}^{2}=z^{\top}Mz
, and the anisotropic local mean
m_{h,M}(x;S)=\sum_{i}w_{i}^{(h,M)}(x;S)\,s_{i}
.

Theorem 13

(Anisotropic attention realization)
.

Let
M\succ 0
. Under the OT path with base
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,M^{-1})
, the anisotropic empirical velocity field is:

u_{t}^{S,M}(t\tilde{x})=\tilde{x}+\frac{m_{h(t),M}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x}}{\sigma_{t}}.

(8)

This has exactly the same affine form as the isotropic case, with
m_{h,M}
replacing
m_{h}
. A Mahalanobis-kernel cross-attention head with logits
\ell_{i}=-\|\tilde{x}-s_{i}\|_{M}^{2}/(2h^{2})
, followed by the same post-map
A_{t}
, exactly realizes this velocity.

Proof.

Under the anisotropic base
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,M^{-1})
, the marginal at time
t
is
p_{t}^{S,M}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\mathcal{N}(x\mid ts_{i},\sigma_{t}^{2}M^{-1})
. The Tweedie identity for this model gives
\mathbb{E}[tX_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]=x+\sigma_{t}^{2}M^{-1}\nabla\log p_{t}^{S,M}(x)
, so the velocity–score identity becomes:

u_{t}^{S,M}(x)=\frac{x}{t}+\frac{\sigma_{t}}{t}M^{-1}\nabla\log p_{t}^{S,M}(x).

De-scaling (
\tilde{x}=x/t
) and differentiating the anisotropic KDE
\tilde{p}_{t}^{S,M}(\tilde{x})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\phi_{h,M}(\tilde{x}-s_{i})
give:

\nabla\log\tilde{p}_{t}^{S,M}(\tilde{x})=\frac{M}{h^{2}}(m_{h,M}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x}).

Substituting into the velocity–score identity, the
M^{-1}
cancels the
M
:

u_{t}^{S,M}(t\tilde{x})=\tilde{x}+\frac{\sigma_{t}}{t^{2}h^{2}}(m_{h,M}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x})=\tilde{x}+\frac{m_{h,M}(\tilde{x};S)-\tilde{x}}{\sigma_{t}},

which is the same affine post-map
A_{t}
as in the isotropic case.
∎

Low-rank extension.

When
M=L^{\top}L
with

and

, the theorem as stated requires

and does not directly apply. However, the Mahalanobis distance
\|\tilde{x}-s_{i}\|_{M}^{2}=\|L(\tilde{x}-s_{i})\|^{2}
depends only on the

-dimensional projections
L\tilde{x}
and

; adding a small ridge
\epsilon I
to
M
recovers positive definiteness while preserving the low-rank kernel structure in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
. The effective estimation dimension is then
r
, not

.
