Reply to Letter “Immune Polarization in Coexisting Rheumatoid Arthritis and Asthma: Insights into Th2 Dominance and Unresolved Confounding”
Authors: Sana S. Almutairi, Fawaz Y. Azizieh, Adeeba A. Al-Herz, Tahany E. Al-Shemary, Ahmed R. Alsaber, Raj Raghupathy
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Nedim Kaban [1] for his interest in our article “Immunological Profile of Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis and Asthma” [2] which was aimed at ascertaining whether the immunological profile of patients with both rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and asthma is different from those with RA without asthma. In response to the comments on methodology and the confounding effect of disease modifying anti-RA drugs and steroid, we would like to clarify that detailed treatment stratification was not included in our publication because based on sub-analysis, no statistically significant findings were detected between the studied groups. RA patients with recent disease flare or asthmatics with recent exacerbation were not included in the study. This information was not included in our paper because of page number limitation as per journal’s guidelines. As for the comment that the standardized measures of disease activity and duration were not reported for either RA or asthma, we have mentioned that patients with disease flare or exacerbation of either asthma or RA were excluded.

We agree that asthma sub-phenotypes, including atopic versus non-atopic and obesity-related forms, were not specified; however, in the Discussion section, we did mention that our analysis suggests that the BARA group is similar to the BA group both in terms of spirometry readings and responses to the questionnaire, and do not show immune activation typically associated with a chronic neutrophilic inflammatory profile. This rules out the presence of confounding groups of obesity-related or neutrophilic inflammatory response which are usually present among non-type 2 (Low T2) asthmatics who have airways neutrophilia, are paucigranulocytic, or have obesity-associated asthma with poor response to steroids and lack response to target type 2 inflammation [3].

In the Conclusion section, we have stressed that our study is exploratory in nature and has a few limitations. One of these is the relatively small sample size; despite meeting power requirements, our results should be confirmed with larger sample sizes. A second limitation is the fact that we measured only 7 cytokines which included Th1, Th2, and Th17 cytokines; it would be of interest to also study the levels of other pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines as well as regulatory cytokines as they play important roles in rheumatoid arthritis and bronchial asthma. Thirdly, this cross-sectional study focused on blood samples obtained at a single time point; as immune reactivity and cytokine profiles are dynamic, it would be of interest to measure cytokine levels at different time points.

Our thanks to Dr. Kaban for the insightful comments.
