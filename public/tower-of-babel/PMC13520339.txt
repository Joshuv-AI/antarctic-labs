Chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis presenting as fever of unknown origin in a child: a diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Xianglin Yang, Shuolan Jing, Shihao Li, Yuqing Zhang, Liqun Dong, Tingting Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis (CNO), also referred to as chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis (CRMO), is a rare autoinflammatory bone disorder in children and adolescents. Bone pain is the most common presenting symptom, whereas prolonged recurrent fever of unknown origin is uncommon and may mimic infection or malignancy, leading to extensive diagnostic evaluations, including invasive procedures.Case presentationWe report a 12-year-old girl who presented with recurrent fever as the predominant symptom, accompanied by delayed and intermittent musculoskeletal pain. Extensive infectious, rheumatologic, and oncologic investigations, including repeated cultures, metagenomic next-generation sequencing, and bone marrow examination, were unrevealing. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated multifocal bone marrow edema, and positron emission tomography–computed tomography showed multifocal FDG-avid skeletal lesions, with a maximum SUV of 6.85 among the focal skeletal lesions, raising concern for malignancy. Histopathological examination of a femoral bone biopsy revealed lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with focal fibrosis and no evidence of infection, granulomatous inflammation, necrosis, or malignancy. Based on the clinical course, imaging findings, exclusion of infection and malignancy, and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of CNO/CRMO was established. The patient improved after stepwise treatment with naproxen, methotrexate, and prednisone.ConclusionThis case illustrates an uncommon fever-dominant presentation of pediatric CNO/CRMO with multifocal skeletal lesions mimicking malignancy. CNO/CRMO should be considered in children with fever of unknown origin accompanied by delayed musculoskeletal symptoms or multifocal bone marrow lesions. Introduction

Chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis (CNO), also referred to as chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis (CRMO), is a rare autoinflammatory bone disorder that predominantly affects children and adolescents and represents the most common form of non-infectious osteitis in the pediatric population [1]. First described by Giedion et al. in 1972 [2], CNO/CRMO is characterized by sterile bone inflammation with a highly heterogeneous clinical course. Although its pathogenesis is not fully understood, dysregulation of innate immunity and pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling has been implicated [3].

The clinical presentation of CNO/CRMO is variable, ranging from unifocal to multifocal bone involvement. Bone pain, localized tenderness, and swelling are typical manifestations, whereas systemic symptoms such as fever are less prominent. Due to the absence of disease-specific biomarkers, CNO/CRMO is frequently misdiagnosed as infectious osteomyelitis, malignancy, or other rheumatologic diseases [4]. Diagnostic frameworks, including the Bristol and Jansson criteria, support diagnosis by combining clinical manifestations, multifocal imaging findings, exclusion of infection and malignancy, and, when necessary, histopathological evidence [5, 6]. These frameworks are particularly useful in determining whether bone biopsy can be avoided in typical cases or remains necessary in atypical presentations.

Although fever-dominant CNO/CRMO has been described, it remains diagnostically challenging in children with fever of unknown origin, particularly when musculoskeletal symptoms are delayed or subtle. Here, we report a pediatric case of CNO/CRMO presenting primarily as fever of unknown origin and discuss the structured diagnostic approach required to distinguish this condition from infection and malignancy.

Case report

A 12-year-old girl from Huili County, Liangshan Yizu Autonomous Prefecture, Sichuan Province, was admitted with fever of unknown origin for 41 days and joint pain for 3 days. At disease onset, the patient presented primarily with intermittent fever, with a maximum temperature of 38℃, accompanied by mild upper respiratory symptoms. She was initially treated symptomatically for presumed upper respiratory tract infection at a local clinic. Although the fever transiently subsided, it recurred repeatedly.She was subsequently hospitalized several times at local institutions and received multiple courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics, including levofloxacin, cefixime, and piperacillin–tazobactam, without sustained clinical improvement.Three days before admission, she developed migratory musculoskeletal pain involving the metacarpophalangeal, ankle, and knee regions. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs provided only limited symptomatic improvement. Since disease onset, she had experienced an unintentional weight loss of approximately 2.5 kg. Five days before symptoms onset, she had fallen from a bicycle and sustained a left elbow fracture, which was treated with plaster immobilization at a local hospital. The cast was removed one month later.

On physical examination, mild lumbar scoliosis was noted. During febrile episodes, tenderness was present over the right ankle and the left fourth metacarpophalangeal region, accompanied by a mildly abnormal gait. No significant joint swelling, muscle weakness, or neurological deficits were observed.

Laboratory investigations revealed leukopenia (3.7 × 10⁹/L) with mild neutropenia (1.21 × 10⁹/L), elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (33 mm/h), and increased C-reactive protein (10.3 mg/L). Serological testing suggested past Epstein–Barr virus infection. Liver and renal function, electrolytes, procalcitonin, ferritin, thyroid function, autoantibodies, and antistreptolysin O levels were all within normal limits (Table 1).

After admission, empirical oxacillin treatment was initiated, but the fever persisted. Given the patient’s epidemiological background from Liangshan Yi Autonomous Prefecture, where scrub typhus may occur, together with fever, musculoskeletal pain, and leukopenia, scrub typhus was considered in the differential diagnosis, and empirical doxycycline was added. However, the fever did not improve. As musculoskeletal pain emerged during the disease course, an inflammatory rheumatic disease, including juvenile idiopathic arthritis, was also considered. Nevertheless, treatment with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs failed to achieve sustained clinical improvement. (suppl.1 Fig. 1)

Because of the persistent fever and poor response to antimicrobial therapy, an extensive infectious workup was performed. Blood, urine, and stool cultures; fungal and mycobacterial studies; polymerase chain reaction assays for common pathogens; and metagenomic next-generation sequencing of blood were all negative. Bone marrow aspiration revealed no evidence of infection or leukemia. Cytokine analysis demonstrated elevated interleukin-6 and interferon-γ levels, suggesting systemic immune activation. Rheumatologic evaluations, including ANA profile, extractable nuclear antigen antibodies, ANCA, HLA-B27, and antiphospholipid antibodies, were unremarkable.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the hands, ankles, and knees revealed multifocal bone marrow edema involving multiple metacarpals and phalanges of both hands, the bilateral distal tibiae, several tarsal bones, and the distal femur. The lesions showed a multifocal and partially bilateral distribution pattern, without aggressive cortical destruction, soft-tissue mass, abscess formation, or marked synovial proliferation (Figures A–F ). These imaging features suggested multifocal inflammatory osteitis. However, given the prolonged fever, unintentional weight loss, mild leukopenia, and multifocal skeletal abnormalities, malignancy remained an important differential diagnosis. Tumor markers and contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest and abdomen were normal.

Whole-body positron emission tomography–computed tomography was performed to further evaluate the multifocal skeletal lesions and to exclude occult malignancy. PET-CT demonstrated heterogeneous and multifocal FDG uptake in multiple skeletal sites, including multiple metacarpal and phalangeal bones of both hands, bilateral tibiae, the distal shaft of the right femur, and several small bones of the feet. The highest SUVmax among these focal skeletal lesions was 6.85, with corresponding mildly increased bone marrow density on CT. Additional patchy and linear FDG uptake was observed in parts of the axial skeleton and proximal appendicular skeleton, which was interpreted as possible reactive bone marrow changes. No generalized lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, or visceral hypermetabolic lesions suggestive of systemic malignancy were identified (Figure G). Although the PET-CT findings raised concern for hematologic malignancy or other marrow-infiltrative disorders, increased FDG uptake is not specific for neoplastic disease and required correlation with bone marrow examination, MRI findings, and histopathology.

After multidisciplinary consultation with Orthopedics, Hematology, Rheumatology, and Radiology, bone biopsy was recommended. Although biopsy may be avoided in typical cases of CNO/CRMO with characteristic clinical and imaging findings, this patient had an atypical fever-dominant presentation, unintentional weight loss, mild leukopenia, and multifocal hypermetabolic skeletal lesions on PET-CT. Therefore, biopsy was performed primarily to exclude malignancy, infectious osteomyelitis, and other infiltrative bone marrow disorders rather than merely to confirm CNO/CRMO. An open biopsy of a femoral lesion was performed. Histopathological examination revealed fragmented bone tissue infiltrated by lymphocytes and plasma cells with focal fibrous connective tissue proliferation, consistent with chronic inflammatory changes. No granulomas, necrosis, malignant cells, or infectious organisms were identified (Figure H-I).

Based on the clinical course, multifocal imaging findings, histopathological features, and exclusion of infectious and malignant conditions, a diagnosis of CNO/CRMO was established. The diagnosis was supported by established diagnostic frameworks, including the Bristol and Jansson criteria: the disease course exceeded six weeks; imaging demonstrated multifocal bone lesions; microbiological investigations were negative; and bone biopsy showed chronic inflammatory changes without evidence of malignancy, granulomatous inflammation, necrosis, or identifiable pathogens.

Initial treatment with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (naproxen 200 mg orally twice daily) was initiated; however, the clinical response was limited. Methotrexate was subsequently introduced at a dose of 15 mg orally once weekly, with folic acid supplementation (5 mg orally once weekly on a different day) to reduce potential adverse effects. Due to persistent systemic inflammation and inadequate symptom control, oral prednisone was added at a total daily dose of 30 mg (15 mg twice daily). Supportive therapy, including calcium and vitamin D supplementation, was also provided.

Following initiation of combined immunosuppressive therapy, the patient’s fever resolved rapidly, and musculoskeletal pain significantly improved. Inflammatory markers gradually decreased.

Prednisone was tapered according to clinical response and laboratory improvement. At the two-month follow-up, the prednisone dose had been reduced to 20 mg/day. The patient remained afebrile, with complete resolution of pain and normalization of inflammatory markers. Methotrexate therapy was continued, and no disease relapse was observed during the available follow-up period.

Discussion and conclusion

CNO/CRMO is an autoinflammatory bone disorder with heterogeneous clinical presentation, which can pose significant diagnostic challenges in pediatric practice. In particular, presentations dominated by fever of unknown origin (FUO), with delayed or subtle musculoskeletal symptoms, may lead to misdiagnosis and prolonged diagnostic pathways [1].The present case illustrates an atypical presentation of pediatric CNO/CRMO in which fever was the dominant initial symptom, while musculoskeletal manifestations were delayed and relatively subtle. This clinical pattern overlaps significantly with infectious, malignant, and systemic inflammatory conditions, often leading to extensive and invasive diagnostic investigations.

In this patient, repeated microbiological testing, metagenomic next-generation sequencing, and bone marrow examination failed to identify an infectious or malignant etiology. These findings highlight a key diagnostic challenge: in the absence of disease-specific biomarkers, the diagnosis of CNO/CRMO relies on a structured exclusion of alternative causes.

In this context, established diagnostic frameworks, such as the Bristol and Jansson criteria, provide important guidance [5, 6]. These criteria integrate clinical features, imaging findings, exclusion of infection and malignancy, and histopathological evidence when available. Before biopsy, the diagnosis of CNO/CRMO was considered but could not be confidently established due to the atypical presentation and concern for malignancy. In the present case, the prolonged disease course (> 6 weeks), multifocal bone lesions, negative microbiological investigations, and histopathological findings consistent with chronic inflammation collectively supported the diagnosis of CNO/CRMO according to these frameworks.

Imaging played a central role in the evaluation of suspected CNO/CRMO. Magnetic resonance imaging is particularly sensitive for detecting bone marrow edema and identifying multifocal involvement [7].In this case, MRI demonstrated multifocal lesions involving the appendicular skeleton without evidence of aggressive cortical destruction, soft-tissue mass, or abscess formation, supporting a non-infectious inflammatory process. However, the PET-CT findings of multifocal FDG-avid lesions with a maximum SUV of 6.85 raised concern for malignancy. It is important to note that increased FDG uptake is not specific for neoplastic disease and may also be observed in inflammatory conditions [8]. The absence of lymphadenopathy, organomegaly, or visceral hypermetabolic lesions in this patient reduced the likelihood of systemic malignancy. Therefore, imaging findings must be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and laboratory data to avoid misdiagnosis.

The indication for bone biopsy in CNO/CRMO remains a matter of clinical judgment. In typical cases with characteristic clinical and imaging features, biopsy may be avoided. However, in patients with atypical presentations—such as persistent fever, weight loss, hematologic abnormalities, or malignancy-like imaging findings—histopathological evaluation is often necessary. In the present case, bone biopsy was performed primarily to exclude malignancy and infectious osteomyelitis rather than to confirm CNO/CRMO. The findings of lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with fibrosis, in the absence of granulomas, necrosis, malignant cells, or pathogens, were consistent with chronic sterile inflammation and supported the diagnosis.

From a pathophysiological perspective, CNO/CRMO is increasingly recognized as an autoinflammatory disorder driven by dysregulation of innate immunity [9]. Abnormal activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, has been implicated in disease pathogenesis [3, 9]. In the present case, elevated levels of interleukin-6 and interferon-γ further supported the presence of systemic inflammatory activation, although these findings are not specific.

Management of CNO/CRMO is primarily empirical and based on disease severity. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are generally considered first-line therapy. In patients with inadequate response, disease-modifying agents such as methotrexate or sulfasalazine may be introduced, and short-term glucocorticoids can be used for rapid control of inflammation. In refractory cases, biologic agents targeting tumor necrosis factor-α or interleukin-1, as well as bisphosphonates, have shown therapeutic benefit [10]. In the present case, combined therapy with naproxen, methotrexate, and prednisone resulted in rapid clinical improvement, supporting the effectiveness of stepwise treatment escalation.

In conclusion, although CNO/CRMO is traditionally considered rare, it is likely underrecognized, particularly in patients with fever-dominant presentations. This case highlights the importance of considering CNO/CRMO in the differential diagnosis of pediatric FUO, especially when imaging reveals multifocal bone lesions. A structured diagnostic approach incorporating clinical evaluation, imaging, exclusion of infection and malignancy, and selective use of biopsy is essential for timely diagnosis. Increased awareness of atypical presentations may reduce diagnostic delay, avoid unnecessary investigations, and improve patient outcomes.

This case further highlights the importance of a structured diagnostic approach in pediatric FUO, particularly in distinguishing CNO/CRMO from malignancy and guiding appropriate use of bone biopsy.
