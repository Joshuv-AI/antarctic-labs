Generative artificial intelligence implementation in REDCap

Background and significance

REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a no-code, secure web-based application designed to help biomedical research teams plan, collect, and manage data for clinical studies and trials.1 Since its initial development at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC) in 2004, REDCap has been licensed and adopted by more than 8000 institutions in 163 countries and territories. Local adoption of REDCap requires local IT, informatics, and researcher support capacity. The global community of academic, nonprofit, and government institutions and institutional experts fostering collaborative development of REDCap as an electronic data capture (EDC) tool is known as the REDCap Consortium.2 The REDCap Consortium’s collaborative structure facilitates the continuous development and dissemination of REDCap’s features to meet the evolving needs of research teams worldwide.

The potential of generative artificial intelligence (AI) across a wide breadth of biomedical research applications makes it a promising direction for expanding feature development in REDCap.3 Generative AI refers to deep learning models that can generate content, including text, images, and audio, based on user input. These models have demonstrated their ability to effectively summarize qualitative data,4,5 improve clinical documentation efficiency,6,7 and optimize participant identification and recruitment to clinical trials,8,9 among others. Despite their promise, implementing generative AI-powered capabilities in REDCap and other EDC systems requires thoughtful consideration of factors such as data governance, security safeguards, institutional policy adherence, usability, workflow adaptability, cost, and long-term compatibility.

Objective

We describe the REDCap Consortium’s initial implementation of three generative AI within the REDCap platform: AI-assisted writing helper, qualitative data summarization, and language translation. Our approach focused on integrating generative AI capabilities within REDCap to promote secure and responsible adoption by partner institutions.

Methods

Establishing guiding principles for a minimum viable product

Based on our 20-year experience developing and deploying software for the global REDCap Consortium, we understood there would be diverse opinions among REDCap administrators regarding the types of AI features that should be deployed for unsupervised use by local research teams. We wanted to encourage adopting institutions to establish secure AI software tenants for data security and privacy reasons. Specifically, each institution links its REDCap instance with its own sandboxed version of the AI software to minimize the risk of unintended data leakage. When we began brainstorming generative AI features for REDCap in early 2023, Vanderbilt and other institutions were starting to develop local policies for using generative AI in research. Moreover, organizations’ central IT teams were still developing scalable models for delivering secure internal AI tenants for research use. Given this environment, we created and released a minimum viable product (MVP) with a small set of useful but non-controversial generative AI features, encouraging REDCap administrators to work with their IT departments and establish AI subscription services. We required use of local generative AI resources rather than a centralized REDCap-wide subscription for all Consortium partners to reduce privacy risks associated with data transfer and ensure associated costs could be covered locally.

The MVP guiding principles were: (1) adoption of and local researcher access to the REDCap generative AI service should be optional and left to the discretion of local REDCap administrators; (2) individual researcher-focused features within the new generative AI service should also be optional and left to the discretion of local REDCap administrators; (3) features should primarily focus on low-risk, “researcher helper” use cases with human oversight; (4) features only needed to accommodate a single generative AI tenant option (ie, OpenAI ChatGPT), a convenience-driven selection because it was the first generative AI tenant solution adopted by VUMC; and (5) features should support independent adoption by the researcher without extensive tutorials.

MVP feature selection

After a small set of technical feasibility pilots, assessment of developer capacity, and a virtual ideation session with REDCap administrators from the REDCap Consortium, we selected three researcher-facing features for the MVP release.

The first MVP feature was a “writing helper” allowing researchers to use AI to support the creation or refinement of text (eg, survey invitations, case report form field labels, report introductory language) anywhere in the REDCap application with rich text editing (Figure 1). The researcher controls the process and is fully responsible for the accuracy and relevance of any text creation or modification.

AI Writing Assistant feature. (A) Researchers can launch the AI Writing Assistant by clicking the magic wand icon in any standard rich text editor. (B) Once launched, the AI Writing Assistant screen is shown, and the text is automatically checked for correct grammar. Researchers can select from existing operations (eg, change the length, tone, or reading level) or compose a custom prompt. Researcher can then choose to accept, revise, or discard AI-suggested text, and return to the rich text editor.

The second MVP feature was a “qualitative data summarization” tool designed to process and summarize unstructured free-text data in REDCap reports (Figure 2). Manual analysis of free text data is often laborious and time consuming, whereas AI performs the same process more efficiently and at scale. This feature appears next to unstructured text fields on reports and summarizes report responses. While a default prompt to the AI service is provided (ie, “summarize the following information in 150 words or less”), users can specify a custom prompt and save it for repeated use within the report as data are added or changed over time.

AI Qualitative Data Summarization Feature. (A) Researchers can launch the AI Qualitative Data Summarization Feature in a REDCap report by clicking the magic wand icon for any un-validated text field. Alternatively, they can click a button at the top of the report page to open a multi-field version. (B) Researchers can use the default AI prompt, “Summarize the following information in 150 words or less,” or compose and save a custom prompt. After clicking on “Execute the action,” the AI-generated summary will be outputted in the same screen.

The third feature focused on language translation within an existing REDCap module, Multi-Language Management, which allows case report forms and surveys to be deployed in many languages simultaneously and provides users with a “select language” button in the interface (Figure 3). During multi-language forms creation, users typically translate forms or survey text manually, a process that is often time intensive. The new AI-powered auto-translation tool within the Multi-Language Management module streamlines the workflow by sending all untranslated text to the AI service which returns translations for user review. Researchers may choose to further modify the text or accept the translation.

Multi-Language Management Auto-Translation Feature. (A) Researchers can launch the Multi-Language Management Auto-Translation Feature on the language setup page when designing a project. Researchers can click the “Translate using AI” to automatically translate project elements (eg, survey questions, user interface elements, etc.). (B) AI-translated text will be displayed in each element’s respective text box for review and saving.

To unify the user experience, all 3 generative AI features were represented with a magenta “magic wand” icon in buttons and form elements.

Security and safe use considerations

Security and safe use were a central design principle guiding both architecture and use case selection. The three MVP features (writing helper, summarization, and translation) were chosen as low-risk applications based on the following criteria: (1) they operate on user-initiated or easily accessible text, (2) outputs are assistive and allow human review before use, and (3) they do not directly modify underlying research data. All generative AI functionality was implemented using the Microsoft Azure OpenAI service within VUMC’s enterprise cloud environment, which operates under a Business Associate Agreement. This “walled garden” approach ensures that API calls remain within an institutionally managed, access-controlled environment. At the system level, generative AI features are enabled by REDCap system administrators, ensuring allowance for local institutional oversight and governance. At the application level, REDCap enforces authenticated access and role-based permissions for each project. This design ensures that in the course of operation any data transmitted to the generative AI service is already accessible to the authenticated user initiating the request. Prior to deployment at VUMC, the implementation underwent institutional cybersecurity review, including evaluation of system architecture, authentication and access controls, and compliance with institutional policies for protected data. This approach reflects a risk-based implementation strategy that prioritizes minimizing sensitive data exposure, restricting access to trusted users, and maintaining human oversight of all model outputs.

In addition, REDCap provides concrete guidance to REDCap system administrators, who are responsible for enabling and configuring generative AI features. During configuration, administrators are presented with recommendations for selecting an appropriate AI service and deployment model, including a preference for institutionally managed, enterprise-grade environments to support security and data privacy. REDCap also outlines alternative deployment options, such as other cloud-hosted or locally hosted services, while clearly noting that REDCap and VUMC cannot vouch for the security of these external services. Accordingly, administrators are advised to independently evaluate vendor security practices and documentation before enabling such configurations. Importantly, generative AI features are disabled at the system level by default and can only be activated by administrators, ensuring that institutions retain full control over whether and how these capabilities are deployed.

Tracking of initial use

To evaluate uptake of the new AI-based features, we assessed usage in two ways. Using our local REDCap installation at VUMC, we tracked both the number of projects using generative AI features and the number of API calls to our local AI tenant. Given REDCap’s decentralized distribution model, we were limited in the information we could collect from the REDCap Consortium partners. With approval from local REDCap administrators, REDCap installations report high-level usage data to the REDCap Consortium, including REDCap version numbers, counts of features enabled, and general feature usage. These metrics were used to assess adoption of the generative AI features consortium wide.

Results

At VUMC, we deployed all three generative AI features in January 2025. Since the initial rollout, we recorded use of at least one generative AI feature across 958 unique projects. A total of 215 projects used the AI writing helper, 754 the qualitative data summarization tool, and 24 the multi-language translation feature. We also recorded the number of API calls to the AI writing helper (n = 2801), qualitative data summarization tool (n = 2720), and multi-language translation feature (n = 200). All usage metrics reflect activity on the VUMC enterprise production REDCap server; however, because REDCap production projects originate from development-phase work and users may also create exploratory or test projects, these metrics likely include a combination of early exploratory use, project development use, and operational production use.

The REDCap generative AI features were disseminated broadly to the REDCap Consortium as part of REDCap Standard release 15.0.0 (9 January 2025) and REDCap Long-Term Support release 15.0.9 (30 January 2025). REDCap Consortium socialization included announcements on a REDCap Administrator Community of Practice website, presentations and discussion at weekly Consortium Administrator All-Hands virtual meetings, and monthly calls supporting REDCap administrators in the Europe/Africa and Asia/Oceania time zones.

During the first seven months of release (January to August 2025) to the REDCap Consortium, 21 REDCap institution servers activated the optional generative AI module. Among these, three were deemed to be test servers based on metadata from the high-level statistics reporting available to our team. Among the 18 non-test servers, 22% were domestic US institutions (ie, three academic medical centers, one government organization), and the remainder were international institutions from Austria, Brazil, Chile, China, Colombia, Ghana, Portugal, Switzerland, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and Vietnam. Among the eight institutions (three domestic, five international) with at least five projects using the AI module, there were a total of 1171 unique projects, with a median of 12 projects per site (range: 6, 958). Based on the reported usage statistics, all eight institutions used the AI writing helper, seven the qualitative data summarization feature, and five the multi-language translation feature.

Discussion

This first phase of implementing generative AI features in REDCap represents a meaningful step toward enhancing researcher workflows, data utility, and eventual opportunities for automation. Despite limited awareness generation across the REDCap Consortium, we saw uptake from both domestic and international partners. Adopting institutions spanned lower-middle (Ghana, Vietnam), upper-middle (Brazil, China, Colombia), and high-income economies (Austria, Chile, Portugal, Switzerland, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and the United States) according to the World Bank 2025 data.10 Nevertheless, global adoption appeared to be slower, likely due to needing a locally approved and supported AI tenant infrastructure to enable the generative AI module within REDCap.

We saw early adoption of the generative AI features within the VUMC REDCap user community. The multi-language translation module was used less than the other two features, as only some projects required rendering in non-English languages. From supporting almost 1000 unique projects, we saw significantly fewer projects using the AI writing helper feature compared to the qualitative data summarization feature, though the number of API calls was similar. We postulate that the imbalance in project numbers supported by each module was due to workflow, as the AI writing helper was mostly used in setting up new projects while the data summarization feature can be used any time after data collection began. Given VUMC’s primary instance of REDCap is supporting thousands of active projects, it’s likely that more users are discovering the new qualitative data summarization feature than the writing helper feature. The similarity in the number of API calls indicates that there were more calls per project with the AI writing helper feature.

Despite promising initial adoption, our implementation has several limitations. It was limited to a single generative AI service (ie, OpenAI ChatGPT), and other services may have been more readily adopted by institutional IT departments. Reported usage metrics may underestimate true adoption, as central monitoring did not capture every REDCap installation. Finally, though the three features were selected as part of an MVP strategy with input from the REDCap Consortium, they may not reflect the features most relevant or desirable to end users.

To build upon the first three AI features, we plan to expand REDCap’s generative AI capabilities to extract information from unstructured text fields in surveys or clinical notes retrieved by the REDCap Clinical Data Interoperability Services module.11 New features could support a wide range of biomedical natural language processing (NLP) and research applications, including information extraction (eg, extracting clinical entities from free text), text classification (eg, categorization of patient reported outcomes or adverse event narratives), thematic analysis of free-text survey responses, generation of structured clinical summaries from narrative inputs, or text de-identification for privacy-preserving sharing. We already have made provisions allowing flexibility for REDCap administrators to select other generative AI models (eg, Google’s Gemini) and established the ability to invoke connectivity with these AI models at the global or project level, which will help with project-level research billing if desired. Finally, our experience offers lessons for other software teams considering generative AI integration. Conclusion

The initial release of generative AI features in REDCap marks a significant milestone that demonstrated both the technical feasibility and value to researchers of AI integration in EDC systems. By starting with low-risk, human-in-the-loop features, we were able to launch without requiring extensive institutional approvals while also establishing an infrastructure to support future expansion. With uptake across the VUMC community and international partners, we have a foundation to build upon as we expand to more complex use cases, including NLP of unstructured fields and integration with different AI model providers. Our future priorities include developing new features for a second release, improving the user experience to ensure broader awareness, and strengthening outreach to partners across the REDCap Consortium.
