I
Introduction

The exponential Boltzmann factor
e^{-\beta E}
is one of the central
mathematical structures of equilibrium statistical mechanics. It fixes
the canonical probability measure, determines the Planck distribution
of blackbody radiation, enters the thermal description of Hawking
emission, and provides the equilibrium kernel from which many
nonequilibrium fluctuation identities are constructed
huang1987
;
jarzynski1997
;
crooks1999
;
seifert2012
;
esposito2009
. For this reason,
any modification of the Boltzmann weight must be treated with
particular care. A change in the thermal factor is not a merely formal
replacement; it can modify spectral occupations, thermodynamic
response functions, high-energy emission rates, and the structure of
work relations. At the same time, the physical consequences of such a
modification should be separated from model-dependent assumptions, so
that the results that follow directly from the deformed weight are not
confused with interpretations that require an additional microscopic
theory.

Non-exponential thermal weights have been widely investigated in
systems where the assumptions leading to the Gibbs distribution are
modified or only effectively satisfied. Tsallis statistics and
superstatistics, for example, provide useful descriptions of systems
with long-range correlations, temperature fluctuations, memory effects,
or non-standard phase-space structures
tsallis1988
;
tsallis2009
;
beck2003
;
beck2001
;
wilk2000
. Such approaches have been applied to
gravitating systems
plastino1993
, high-energy particle spectra

bediaga2000
;
cms2010
;
alice2013
;
wong2013
, and dissipative
optical lattices
douglas2006
. Other departures from the
standard exponential law arise in contexts inspired by generalized
uncertainty principles
maggiore1993
;
kempf1995
and discrete-time
mechanics
lee1983
;
jaroszkiewicz1997
. These examples show that
the study of modified thermal weights is physically meaningful, but
they also show that each deformation has to be analyzed on its own
terms.

The present work examines a specific deformation proposed by Chung,
Hassanabadi, and Boumali
chung2022
, in which the inverse
temperature is defined on a discrete lattice,

\mathcal{B}=\{\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}+nb\}
, and the ordinary Boltzmann factor
is replaced by

B_{E}(\beta_{n})=(1-bE)^{n}.

(1)

Here
b
is a positive parameter with dimensions of inverse energy, and
the continuum exponential is recovered in the correlated limit

b\to 0
,
n\to\infty
, with
nb=\beta
fixed. The most important
feature of this deformation is not simply that it changes the
high-energy tail; rather, positivity imposes the condition
E<1/b
.
Thus the deformed thermal weight has compact support in energy. This
bounded support is the basic mechanism behind the spectral,
black-hole, and nonequilibrium effects studied in this paper.

Our objective is to determine which consequences follow directly from
this compactly supported thermal weight. The analysis is organized
around three connected questions. First, how does the discrete factor
modify the occupation of bosonic modes and the corresponding spectral
integrals? Second, what is the leading effect of this modification on
Hawking emission from a Schwarzschild black hole, and how should the
resulting endpoint of the thermal description be interpreted? Third,
how are nonequilibrium work identities modified when the thermal
weight is no longer exponential and therefore no longer multiplicative
in the energy difference? These questions are treated within the same
formal framework, which makes it possible to identify the precise role
played by the parameter
b
in equilibrium, gravitational, and
nonequilibrium settings.

A central point of the paper is that the discrete weight must not be
treated as an exact finite-
b
version of a geometric Boltzmann factor.
For a bosonic mode with occupation number
k
, one has

(1-bk\varepsilon)^{n}\neq[(1-b\varepsilon)^{n}]^{k}
at finite
b
.
Consequently, the usual derivation of the Bose–Einstein distribution
from a geometric series is not exact. We therefore introduce an
effective discrete Bose–Einstein occupation law and compare it with
the exact truncated partition sum. This comparison determines the
domain in which the effective law is reliable and shows that it
captures the part of the spectrum that dominates the Hawking
luminosity integral.

The black-hole application is then developed as a controlled
consequence of the modified spectral occupation. The bounded thermal
weight suppresses high-energy modes and yields a finite-
b
correction
to the Stefan–Boltzmann luminosity. When the deformation scale is
calibrated at the Planck energy, the thermal channel terminates at a
Planckian mass. This endpoint should be understood as the limit of
validity of the thermal description generated by the discrete weight,
not as a proof of an absolutely stable remnant. The same distinction
is important in the entropy calculation: the brick-wall analysis gives
a logarithmic shift linear in
b
, but its numerical coefficient
depends on the chosen near-horizon regulator. We therefore use the
entropy result as a diagnostic of the deformation rather than as a
universal prediction.

The nonequilibrium part of the analysis reveals a different aspect of
the deformation. In the ordinary exponential case, the ratio of
thermal weights along a protocol depends on the work through

e^{-\beta W}
. For the discrete weight, however, the natural ratio is

(1-bE_{f})^{n}/(1-bE_{i})^{n}
, which depends separately on the initial and
final energies. This loss of exponential multiplicativity has two
important consequences. An exact Jarzynski-type identity can still be
proved for deterministic measure-preserving protocols, but the
corresponding Crooks ratio cannot be reduced to a function of work
alone at finite
b
. Moreover, the first-order Jensen inequality
contains the mixed moment
\langle E_{i}W\rangle
rather than only work
cumulants. Hence the discrete framework preserves an integral
fluctuation identity while changing the statistical information needed
to characterize nonequilibrium transformations.

The main results of the paper can be summarized as follows. We derive
an effective discrete Bose–Einstein law and establish its validity by
comparison with the exact truncated partition function. We obtain the
leading finite-
b
correction to the Schwarzschild luminosity and show
that, for the Planck-scale calibration
b=8\pi/E_{\mathrm{P}}
, the thermal
description reaches an endpoint at
M_{\mathrm{cut}}=m_{\mathrm{P}}
. We prove an
exact discrete Jarzynski identity for the discrete work functional

\mathcal{W}_{d}
and show that no work-only Crooks ratio exists at finite
b
.
We also derive the corresponding first-order second-law inequality and
identify the mixed moment
\langle E_{i}W\rangle
as the characteristic
correction. Finally, we compute the brick-wall entropy diagnostic and
make explicit its regulator dependence.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II

introduces the discrete framework, the partition function, and the
effective Bose–Einstein law. Section
III
applies the formalism
to black-hole radiation, including the luminosity correction, the
Planckian endpoint, and the entropy diagnostic. Section
IV
develops the nonequilibrium theory, proves the
discrete Jarzynski identity, establishes the work-only obstruction, and
discusses possible observable implications. Section
V

summarizes the conclusions and outlines open problems. The technical
details of the effective Bose–Einstein approximation and of the
brick-wall computation are given in Appendices
A
and

B
, respectively.

II
Discrete framework

We collect here the basic structures used throughout the paper. The
parameter
b
has dimensions of inverse energy;
\hbar
and
c
are
kept explicit in dimensional formulas but set to unity in purely
statistical expressions;
k_{\mathrm{B}}
appears only when converting
\beta

to thermodynamic temperature,
T=1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}\beta)
. We distinguish
carefully between exact identities (valid at any finite
b
) and
controlled effective approximations valid for
b\to 0
in the
correlated continuum limit.

II.1
Discrete Boltzmann factor and continuum limit

The inverse-temperature lattice is

\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}+nb,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots,

(2)

with
b>0
; we set
\beta_{0}=0
throughout. The discrete Boltzmann
factor is
chung2022

B_{E}(\beta_{n})=(1-bE)^{n},

(3)

which is positive iff
0\leq E<1/b
, defining the energy cutoff

E_{\max}=1/b.

(4)

The standard exponential is recovered through the correlated limit

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\to 0,\;n\to\infty\\
nb=\beta\end{subarray}}(1-bE)^{n}=e^{-\beta E},

(5)

which is the elementary identity
(1-x/n)^{n}\to e^{-x}
at
x=\beta E
.
At finite
b
, the weight obeys the lattice recursion

B_{E}(\beta_{n+1})-B_{E}(\beta_{n})=-bE\,B_{E}(\beta_{n}),

(6)

the forward-difference analogue of

\partial_{\beta}e^{-\beta E}=-Ee^{-\beta E}
. The deformation thus
replaces continuous cooling in
\beta
by a stepwise evolution along
the lattice, with the compact-support condition acting already at the
level of this finite-difference dynamics.

Lattice vs continuum statements.

Exact statements are made at integer
n
. When we write
n=\beta/b

and expand in
b
, we are taking the correlated large-
n
continuum
approximation with
\beta
fixed. Several results below are

exact
for the lattice formulation, whereas others are
controlled effective approximations valid at small
b
.

Comparison with Tsallis statistics.

The Tsallis
q
-exponential
tsallis1988
is

e_{q}(x)=[1+(1-q)x]^{1/(1-q)},\qquad q\neq 1.

(7)

Setting
q=1-bE
formally relates
e_{q}(-\beta E)
to
Eq. (
3
), but the two are
not
identical: the Tsallis
exponent is the continuous real number
1/(1-q)
, whereas the discrete
exponent is the non-negative integer
n
. The relation is therefore
an asymptotic correspondence in the correlated continuum limit; the
discrete framework should not be presented as an exact finite-
b

realization of Tsallis statistics.

II.2
Discrete partition function and free energy

For a system with discrete energies
\{E_{k}\}
and degeneracies

g_{k}
, the discrete partition function is

Z_{d}(n)=\sum_{k:\,E_{k}<1/b}g_{k}\,(1-bE_{k})^{n},

(8)

and the discrete free energy
chung2022
reads

F_{d}=-\frac{1}{b}\,\ln\!\left[\frac{Z_{d}(n+1)}{Z_{d}(n)}\right].

(9)

For two protocol values
\lambda_{i}
,
\lambda_{f}
, the
corresponding discrete free-energy difference
\Delta F_{d}
is fixed by
the equivalent natural identity

\bigl(1-b\,\Delta F_{d}\bigr)^{n}\equiv\frac{Z_{d}^{(f)}}{Z_{d}^{(i)}},

(10)

which agrees with
-(1/\beta)\ln(Z^{(f)}/Z^{(i)})
in the continuum
limit but differs from it at
\mathcal{O}(b)
. We use
Eq. (
10
) as the operational definition of

\Delta F_{d}
throughout, since it is the form that appears in the
exact discrete Jarzynski identity (Theorem
2
).
The discrete internal energy is

U_{d}=\frac{1}{b}\!\left[1-\frac{Z_{d}(n+1)}{Z_{d}(n)}\right]=\frac{1}{b}\bigl[1-e^{-bF_{d}}\bigr],

(11)

and the discrete heat capacity is the second finite difference,

C_{d}^{(b)}=\frac{U_{d}(n+1)-U_{d}(n)}{T_{n+1}-T_{n}}=\frac{b\,\Delta_{n}U_{d}}{1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}\beta_{n+1})-1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}\beta_{n})}.

(12)

In the continuum limit,
U_{d}\to\langle E\rangle
and

C_{d}^{(b)}\to k_{\mathrm{B}}\beta^{2}\mathrm{Var}(E)
, recovering the standard
fluctuation–dissipation relation. At finite
b
, however, compact
support implies
U_{d}\leq 1/b
and the sign of
C_{d}^{(b)}
remains
model-dependent.

The bounded support changes the qualitative structure of the canonical
ensemble: every thermodynamic sum is taken over a compact domain, so
all moments of
E
are finite by construction. The high-temperature
regime is then governed by how the occupation weight reorganizes
within
0\leq E<1/b
, which is the common origin of the spectral,
Hawking, and nonequilibrium modifications discussed below.

II.3
Effective discrete Bose–Einstein law

The single-mode partition function for a bosonic mode of energy

\varepsilon
is the truncated sum

Z_{d}^{\mathrm{BE,exact}}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{\max}}(1-b\,k\varepsilon)^{n},\quad k_{\max}=\lfloor 1/(b\varepsilon)\rfloor.

(13)

Unlike
e^{-\beta k\varepsilon}=(e^{-\beta\varepsilon})^{k}
, the
discrete weight does
not
factorize:

(1-bk\varepsilon)^{n}\neq[(1-b\varepsilon)^{n}]^{k}
for
k\geq 2
.
Eq. (
13
) therefore admits no closed-form geometric
sum at finite
b
. A direct expansion gives

(1-bk\varepsilon)^{n}=[(1-b\varepsilon)^{n}]^{k}\Bigl[1+\tfrac{1}{2}nb^{2}\varepsilon^{2}k(k-1)+\mathcal{O}(b^{3})\Bigr],

(14)

so factorization is restored to leading order whenever

nb^{2}\varepsilon^{2}=b\beta\varepsilon\cdot b\varepsilon\ll 1
.

Motivated by this, we
define
the effective discrete
Bose–Einstein occupation by formal analogy with the geometric-series
closure that holds in the exponential case,

\langle n_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{d}\equiv\frac{1}{(1-b\varepsilon)^{-n}-1}

(15)

This definition has three properties that justify its use below:
(i) it reduces to the Planck distribution in the continuum limit;
(ii) it incorporates the single-mode cutoff at
\varepsilon=1/b
;
(iii) it captures the leading
\mathcal{O}(b)
correction to the
exact occupation throughout the Wien regime.

Lemma 1

(Validity of the effective BE law)
.

For
b\varepsilon\leq 0.01
and
\beta\varepsilon\geq 1
, the
relative error

\bigl|\langle n_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{d}-\langle k\rangle_{\mathrm{exact}}\bigr|/\langle k\rangle_{\mathrm{exact}}

is bounded by
2\%
. In the Rayleigh–Jeans regime

\beta\varepsilon\lesssim 0.3
, the effective law underestimates the
true suppression by a factor
\sim 4n_{P}+1
, where

n_{P}=1/(e^{\beta\varepsilon}-1)
.

The proof, by direct comparison of the
\mathcal{O}(b)

expansions of the exact and effective occupations, is given in
Appendix
A
. The relevance of Lemma
1
is
that the Hawking luminosity integral is dominated by modes with

\beta\varepsilon\gtrsim 1
(cf. Sec.
III.1
), so the
deep-infrared limitation does not affect (R2).

Writing
n=\beta/b
and expanding to first order in
b
,

\langle n_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{d}\approx\frac{1}{e^{\beta\varepsilon}-1}-\frac{b\beta\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\,\frac{e^{\beta\varepsilon}}{(e^{\beta\varepsilon}-1)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}).

(16)

The correction is negative for all
\varepsilon>0
, confirming that
the effective discrete distribution
systematically suppresses

high-energy occupation. Two points are worth emphasizing.
Eq. (
15
) should be read as an effective single-mode law,
justified by Lemma
1
, rather than as an exact
finite-
b
identity. The
\mathcal{O}(b)
correction in
Eq. (
16
) scales as
b\beta\varepsilon^{2}
well before
the cutoff is reached, so discreteness effects switch on smoothly
rather than only at the endpoint.

Figure
1
illustrates this behavior on a logarithmic
vertical scale, using the dimensionless combination

x=\beta\varepsilon
for a representative ratio
b/\beta
. Three
features are simultaneously visible: (i) near-continuum behavior at
small
x
; (ii) progressive suppression at intermediate
x
; and
(iii) exact truncation at
x_{\max}=\beta/b
.

Figure 1:
Dimensionless comparison of the continuum Bose–Einstein
factor and its discrete counterpart, plotted against

x=\beta\varepsilon
for
b/\beta=0.04
(
n=25
). The vertical axis
is logarithmic so the infrared overlap and the ultraviolet
suppression are visible in a single panel. Three features control

x
, and exact truncation of the

x_{\max}=\beta/b
.

III
Black-hole sector

We now apply the discrete framework to the most direct gravitational
arena: the Hawking spectrum, in which thermal occupation numbers
appear explicitly. Compact support of the discrete weight feeds
directly into the spectrum, the integrated luminosity, and the
interpretation of the terminal stage of evaporation.

For a Schwarzschild black hole of mass
M
, the Hawking
temperature
hawking1975
is

T_{H}=\frac{\hbar c^{3}}{8\pi GMk_{\mathrm{B}}},\qquad\beta_{H}=\frac{8\pi GM}{\hbar c^{3}}.

(17)

Placing
\beta_{H}
on the lattice
\beta_{n}=nb
and identifying the mass
spacing
\delta M
with the lattice quantum—as motivated by
Bekenstein’s area
quantization
bekenstein1973
;
bekenstein1974
and by loop quantum
gravity
rovelli2004
;
thiemann2007
;
dreyer2003
—fixes

b=\frac{8\pi G\,\delta M}{\hbar c^{3}}.

(18)

The Planck-scale calibration
\delta M=m_{\mathrm{P}}=(\hbar c/G)^{1/2}
then
gives

b=\frac{8\pi}{E_{\mathrm{P}}},

(19)

with
E_{\mathrm{P}}=m_{\mathrm{P}}c^{2}\approx 1.22\times 10^{19}\,\mathrm{GeV}
. The
energy cutoff is then

E_{\max}=E_{\mathrm{P}}/(8\pi)\sim 5\times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{GeV}
. The integer

n=M/\delta M
ranges from
n\sim 10^{38}
for a solar-mass black hole
down to
n=1
at the cutoff.

Remark
.

The identification of
b
with a Planckian mass spacing is a
phenomenological calibration of the deformation scale, not an
independent derivation from a microscopic theory of quantum gravity.
The statistical construction itself only requires
b>0
and
E<1/b
.

III.1
Spectrum, luminosity, and thermal cutoff

Substituting Eq. (
15
) into the Planckian Hawking spectrum
yields the discrete occupation

\langle n_{\omega}\rangle_{d}=\frac{1}{(1-b\hbar\omega)^{-n}-1}.

(20)

We discuss its key features in turn.

Ultraviolet cutoff.

The spectrum vanishes for
\omega\geq\omega_{\max}=1/(b\hbar)
. At
Planck-scale
b
, this removes trans-Planckian modes from the
effective thermal spectrum. The cutoff should be read as a
regularization of the thermal channel only, not as a complete
solution of trans-Planckian issues in dynamical
backgrounds
jacobson1991
;
unruh1995
;
martin2001
.

Infrared agreement.

For
b\hbar\omega\ll 1
, expansion of Eq. (
20
) reproduces
the Planck spectrum at leading order, with a negative subleading
correction

\langle n_{\omega}\rangle_{d}\approx\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}-1}-\frac{b\beta_{H}(\hbar\omega)^{2}}{2}\,\frac{e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}}{(e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}-1)^{2}}.

(21)

Grey-body modification.

With a frequency-dependent grey-body factor
\Gamma_{\ell}(\omega)

for partial wave
\ell
, the differential luminosity per mode reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\ell}}{\mathrm{d}\omega}=\frac{\Gamma_{\ell}(\omega)\,\hbar\omega^{3}}{2\pi}\,\frac{1}{(1-b\hbar\omega)^{-n}-1}.

(22)

The deformation modifies only the occupation factor; standard
grey-body corrections still encode propagation through the curvature
potential. In the analytic estimate below we use the constant-
absorption
s
-wave approximation, which keeps the coefficient
transparent. Frequency-dependent factors
page1976
would alter
the numerical prefactor but neither the compact-support statement nor
the sign of the leading depletion.

Spin-dependent emission.

For fermions,

\langle n_{\omega}\rangle_{d,\mathrm{FD}}=1/[(1-b\hbar\omega)^{-n}+1]
, with
the same UV cutoff. The fermionic-to-bosonic ratio is

\frac{\langle n_{\omega}\rangle_{d,\mathrm{FD}}}{\langle n_{\omega}\rangle_{d,\mathrm{BE}}}=\tanh\!\Bigl[\tfrac{n}{2}\ln\bigl(1/(1-b\hbar\omega)\bigr)\Bigr],

(23)

which reduces to
\tanh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)
as
b\to 0
.

Luminosity correction

In the constant-absorption
s
-wave approximation, the total emitted
power is

L_{d}=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{abs}}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1/(b\hbar)}\!\!\frac{\hbar\omega^{3}\,\mathrm{d}\omega}{(1-b\hbar\omega)^{-n}-1}.

(24)

Using the expansion

\frac{1}{(1-b\hbar\omega)^{-n}-1}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}-1}-\frac{b\beta_{H}(\hbar\omega)^{2}\,e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}}{2(e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}-1)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),

(25)

substituting into Eq. (
24
), and applying the integral identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{x^{5}\,e^{x}}{(e^{x}-1)^{2}}=120\,\zeta(5),

(26)

we find

L_{d}=L_{\mathrm{SB}}\!\left[1-\frac{900\,\zeta(5)}{\pi^{4}}\,bk_{\mathrm{B}}T_{H}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})\right]\!,

(27)

where
L_{\mathrm{SB}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{abs}}\pi^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}^{4}T_{H}^{4}/(120\hbar^{3})

and
\zeta(5)\approx 1.037
. Numerically the coefficient is

900\zeta(5)/\pi^{4}\approx 9.58
. The negative sign confirms that the
discrete spectrum radiates
less power
than the Planckian one at
the same Hawking temperature.

Physically, Eq. (
27
) expresses the fact that the
compact-support deformation systematically depletes the ultraviolet
modes that dominate the Stefan–Boltzmann integral. The numerical
coefficient is not universal (it depends on the constant-absorption

s
-wave approximation), but the negative sign and the linear scaling
with
bk_{\mathrm{B}}T_{H}
are robust. This is one of the clearest quantitative
consequences that follows directly from the thermal deformation,
independent of any additional assumption about microscopic gravity.

III.2
Planckian endpoint of the thermal description

The mass-loss rate
\mathrm{d}M/\mathrm{d}t=-L_{d}/c^{2}
combined with
Eq. (
27
) gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{C}{M^{2}}\!\left[1-\frac{900\,\zeta(5)}{\pi^{4}}\,\frac{b\hbar c^{3}}{8\pi GM}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})\right]\!,

(28)

where
C>0
. The correction reduces the thermal evaporation rate as

M
approaches the cutoff scale, but Eq. (
28
) is a
first-order result in
bk_{\mathrm{B}}T_{H}
and should not be extrapolated all
the way to the Planck regime.

A more conservative nonperturbative statement follows from the
lattice itself. With
n=M/\delta M
and
\delta M=m_{\mathrm{P}}
, the smallest
positive lattice value
n=1
corresponds to

M_{\mathrm{cut}}=\frac{\hbar c^{3}b}{8\pi G}=\delta M=m_{\mathrm{P}},

(29)

equivalently
k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{H}=1/b
. The discrete
thermal description

therefore reaches a Planckian endpoint when the characteristic
Hawking energy is of the same order as the compact-support scale.

Remark
.

Eq. (
29
) does not establish the existence of an
absolutely stable remnant. It states only that the continuum thermal
evaporation picture has reached the boundary of the finite-
b

description: the spectrum is strongly depleted, the energy domain is
compact, and the mass lattice has no smaller positive point.
Nonthermal tunneling, pair creation beyond the occupation-number
picture, and other quantum-gravitational
processes
almheiri2013
;
penington2020
could still be relevant.

The mechanism here is therefore comparable to remnant
scenarios
adler2001
;
scardigli1999
;
cavaglia2004
;
chen2015
in
which evaporation slows near a Planckian scale. Compared with
generalized-uncertainty-principle and noncommutative
approaches
nicolini2006
;
smailagic2003
, the distinguishing
feature here is that the slowdown is traced directly to the compact
support of the thermal weight, rather than to a modified uncertainty
relation or a deformed near-horizon geometry. Establishing an
actually stable remnant would require a separate dynamical analysis
beyond the thermal sector, including the degeneracy issues
emphasized in the remnant literature
chen2015
;
unruh2017
.

III.3
Brick-wall entropy diagnostic (non-universal)

The entropy discussion must be interpreted with more caution than the
luminosity calculation. The aim here is not a microscopic derivation
of black-hole entropy from the discrete weight, but the narrower
question: if the usual near-horizon thermal mode count is deformed by
the bounded occupation factor, what type of subleading correction is
induced in a standard brick-wall computation?

Replacing the continuum thermal weight in the brick-wall free energy
by the discrete one (see Appendix
B
for details), the
leading divergence still renormalizes the area term, and the first
discreteness correction generates an additional logarithmic piece.
A representative first-order result is

S^{(d)}_{\mathrm{BH}}=\frac{A}{4\ell_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}+\bigl(\alpha_{0}+\delta\alpha_{b}^{(\mathrm{bw})}\bigr)\,\ln\!\left(\frac{A}{\ell_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\right)+s_{0}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),

(30)

with
\alpha_{0}=-1/2
recovering the standard continuum
value
carlip2000
;
kaul2000
;
meissner2004
and

\delta\alpha_{b}^{(\mathrm{bw})}\propto b
a regulator-dependent
discreteness shift. The structural message is that a bounded thermal
weight induces a logarithmic correction
linear in
b
; the
explicit normalization, derived in Appendix
B
under
specific brick-wall conventions, should be read as an
order-of-magnitude indicator rather than a universal prediction. We
do
not
promote the numerical value of

\delta\alpha_{b}^{(\mathrm{bw})}
to the status of a physical result.

In the continuum limit only the standard logarithmic term with

\alpha_{0}=-1/2
survives. At finite
b
, any specific value of

\delta\alpha_{b}^{(\mathrm{bw})}
obtained within the brick-wall
scheme should be compared with coefficients produced by loop quantum
gravity
kaul2000
;
meissner2004
and conformal
techniques
carlip2000
as a consistency check rather than as a
prediction. What the estimate provides is a diagnostic: the linear
dependence on
b
originates from the same low-order expansion that
controls the spectral suppression, while the numerical coefficient
inherits the regulator-sensitive content of the near-horizon density
of states.

IV
Nonequilibrium work relations

We now turn to the nonequilibrium sector, which probes the
statistical structure most directly. The Jarzynski
equality
jarzynski1997
and the Crooks
theorem
crooks1999
link nonequilibrium work measurements to
equilibrium free-energy differences and underpin the second law at
the fluctuation level
seifert2012
;
esposito2009
. The standard
derivations rely on the multiplicativity

e^{-\beta W}e^{-\beta E_{i}}=e^{-\beta(W+E_{i})}
, which
(1-bx)^{n}

does not satisfy. We construct the discrete generalization
accordingly.

IV.1
Discrete work functional and exact Jarzynski identity

For a trajectory
\gamma:i\to f
connecting an initial microstate of
energy
E_{i}^{(\lambda_{i})}
to a final microstate of energy

E_{f}^{(\lambda_{f})}
, define the
discrete work functional

\mathcal{W}_{d}[\gamma]\equiv\left(\frac{1-bE_{f}^{(\lambda_{f})}}{1-bE_{i}^{(\lambda_{i})}}\right)^{\!n}.

(31)

In the continuum limit
b\to 0
with
nb=\beta
fixed,

\mathcal{W}_{d}\to e^{-\beta W}
where
W=E_{f}^{(\lambda_{f})}-E_{i}^{(\lambda_{i})}
.

Theorem 2

(Exact discrete Jarzynski identity)
.

Let the driving protocol be implemented by a deterministic bijection
(hence measure-preserving)

\Phi:i\mapsto f=\Phi(i)
on the finite state space
\{E_{k}<1/b\}
,
with initial distribution

p_{i}^{(d)}=\frac{(1-bE_{i}^{(\lambda_{i})})^{n}}{Z_{d}^{(i)}}.

(32)

Then

\bigl\langle\mathcal{W}_{d}\bigr\rangle=\frac{Z_{d}^{(f)}}{Z_{d}^{(i)}}\equiv(1-b\,\Delta F_{d})^{n},

(33)

where
\Delta F_{d}
is the discrete free-energy difference defined
through Eq. (
9
).

Proof.

By definition,

\langle\mathcal{W}_{d}\rangle=\sum_{i}p_{i}^{(d)}\,\mathcal{W}_{d}[i\!\to\!\Phi(i)]=\frac{1}{Z_{d}^{(i)}}\sum_{i}(1-bE_{\Phi(i)}^{(\lambda_{f})})^{n}.

(34)

Since
\Phi
is a bijection of the finite state space, the substitution

f=\Phi(i)
rewrites the sum without reweighting,

\langle\mathcal{W}_{d}\rangle=\frac{1}{Z_{d}^{(i)}}\sum_{f}(1-bE_{f}^{(\lambda_{f})})^{n}=\frac{Z_{d}^{(f)}}{Z_{d}^{(i)}}.

(35)

The final equality follows from Eq. (
9
).
∎

Equation (
33
) is the exact identity that survives the
loss of multiplicative exponential structure. Instead of averaging

e^{-\beta W}
, one averages a ratio of discrete weights between
trajectory endpoints: the natural object measures how the statistical
weight assigned to a trajectory changes under the protocol when
inverse temperature lives on a lattice.

The deterministic measure-preserving assumption is the discrete
analogue of the setting in which the ordinary Jarzynski equality
admits its simplest proof. We sketch a stochastic generalization
below.

First-order expansion.

Expanding
\ln\mathcal{W}_{d}
to first order in
b
,

\ln\mathcal{W}_{d}=-\beta W-\frac{b\beta}{2}\bigl(2E_{i}W+W^{2}\bigr)+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}).

(36)

The extra term depends separately on the trajectory-specific initial
energy
E_{i}
, not only on the work
W
. Consequently, it is

not
legitimate to replace the exact ratio
\mathcal{W}_{d}
by the simpler

(1-bW)^{n}
: the latter differs already at order
b
. In particular,

\langle(1-bW)^{n}\rangle=(1-b\,\Delta F)^{n}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})
is

not
valid in general; the discrepancy is of order
b
, not

b^{2}
. Only the full ratio
\mathcal{W}_{d}
satisfies the exact identity. This
is structural: because
(1-bE)^{n}
lacks the multiplicative property
of
e^{-\beta E}
, work alone is no longer sufficient to describe the
deformation—one must keep the trajectory endpoints explicitly.

IV.2
Stochastic generalization (sketch)

Theorem
2
extends naturally to stochastic dynamics
satisfying a discrete form of local detailed balance. Let

T_{i\to f}^{(\lambda)}
be the transition kernel under instantaneous
protocol value
\lambda
, with stationary measure

p_{i}^{(d,\lambda)}=(1-bE_{i}^{(\lambda)})^{n}/Z_{d}^{(\lambda)}
.
Detailed balance with respect to the discrete weight reads

\frac{T_{i\to f}^{(\lambda)}}{T_{f\to i}^{(\lambda)}}=\left(\frac{1-bE_{f}^{(\lambda)}}{1-bE_{i}^{(\lambda)}}\right)^{\!n}.

(37)

For a discrete-time protocol
\lambda_{0}\to\lambda_{1}\to\cdots\to\lambda_{N}

generating a stochastic trajectory
\gamma=(i_{0},i_{1},\dots,i_{N})
,
the natural multi-step work functional is the telescoping product

\mathcal{W}_{d}[\gamma]=\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{1-bE_{i_{k+1}}^{(\lambda_{k+1})}}{1-bE_{i_{k+1}}^{(\lambda_{k})}}\right)^{\!n},

(38)

which for a deterministic bijection collapses to Eq. (
31
).
A standard trajectory-summation argument, using
Eq. (
37
) at each protocol step, yields

\langle\mathcal{W}_{d}\rangle=Z_{d}^{(\lambda_{N})}/Z_{d}^{(\lambda_{0})}=(1-b\,\Delta F_{d})^{n}
,
recovering Theorem
2
. We do not develop the full
construction here; the point is that the algebraic structure required
is precisely Eq. (
37
), with no further use of exponential
factorization.

IV.3
Discrete Crooks ratio: the work-only obstruction

For forward (
F
) and time-reversed (
R
) protocols, the path-weight
ratio is

\frac{P_{F}[\gamma]}{P_{R}[\bar{\gamma}]}=\frac{(1-bE_{i})^{n}}{(1-bE_{f})^{n}}\cdot\frac{Z_{d}^{(f)}}{Z_{d}^{(i)}}.

(39)

In the exponential case,

e^{-\beta E_{i}}/e^{-\beta E_{f}}=e^{\beta W}
depends only on
W
.
The discrete ratio, by contrast, depends on
E_{i}
separately.

Proposition 3

(No work-only Crooks at finite
b
)
.

For any
b>0
, the path-weight ratio
P_{F}[\gamma]/P_{R}[\bar{\gamma}]

in Eq. (
39
) is
not
a function of
W[\gamma]

alone. Equivalently, there exist pairs of trajectories
\gamma_{1}
,

\gamma_{2}
with
W[\gamma_{1}]=W[\gamma_{2}]
but

P_{F}[\gamma_{1}]/P_{R}[\bar{\gamma}_{1}]\neq P_{F}[\gamma_{2}]/P_{R}[\bar{\gamma}_{2}]
.

Proof.

Expanding the logarithm of Eq. (
39
) to first order
in
b
gives

\ln\!\frac{P_{F}[\gamma]}{P_{R}[\bar{\gamma}]}=\beta(W-\Delta F)+b\beta E_{i}W+\tfrac{b\beta}{2}(W^{2}-\Delta F^{2})+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),

(40)

in which
E_{i}
refers to the initial energy of the specific
trajectory
\gamma
. At fixed
W\neq 0
, varying
E_{i}
changes the
right-hand side at order
b
, which proves the claim.
∎

If one nevertheless wishes to compare with the standard Crooks
formulation, the observable distributions are

P_{F}(W)=\sum_{\gamma:W[\gamma]=W}P_{F}[\gamma],

(41)

and similarly for
P_{R}(-W)
. The ratio
P_{F}(W)/P_{R}(-W)
involves
a conditional average of
(1+b\beta E_{i}W)
over trajectories
producing work
W
, and reduces to a clean analytic expression only
under additional assumptions about the joint distribution of

(E_{i},W)
.

Worked example: two-level system.

Consider a single two-level system with energies

\{0,\varepsilon(\lambda)\}
, whose gap is driven from

\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon(\lambda_{i})
to

\varepsilon_{f}=\varepsilon(\lambda_{f})
by a deterministic
bijection that maps each level of the initial Hamiltonian to the
corresponding level of the final one. The two trajectories have work
values
W_{1}=0
(ground branch) and
W_{2}=\varepsilon_{f}-\varepsilon_{i}

(excited branch). The discrete partition functions are

Z_{d}^{(i)}=1+(1-b\varepsilon_{i})^{n}
and

Z_{d}^{(f)}=1+(1-b\varepsilon_{f})^{n}
, and Theorem
2

is verified by direct evaluation. For the excited branch,

\frac{P_{F}[\gamma_{2}]}{P_{R}[\bar{\gamma}_{2}]}=\left(\frac{1-b\varepsilon_{i}}{1-b\varepsilon_{f}}\right)^{\!n}\frac{Z_{d}^{(f)}}{Z_{d}^{(i)}},

(42)

whose continuum limit is
e^{\beta(W_{2}-\Delta F)}
as expected.
Expanding the logarithm to first order in
b
and using
\beta=nb
,

\begin{split}\ln\!\frac{P_{F}[\gamma_{2}]}{P_{R}[\bar{\gamma}_{2}]}&=\beta(W_{2}-\Delta F_{d})\\
&\quad+\frac{b\beta}{2}(2\varepsilon_{i}W_{2}+W_{2}^{2}-\Delta F_{d}^{2})+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),\end{split}

(43)

matching Eq. (
40
) with
E_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}
. The
correction
b\beta\varepsilon_{i}W_{2}
is the clean manifestation of
the work-only obstruction: keeping
W_{2}
fixed and varying

\varepsilon_{i}
changes the right-hand side at order
b
. Direct
numerical evaluation of Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
)
for
b=10^{-2}
,
n=20
,
\varepsilon_{i}\in[0.5,10]
,
W_{2}=1

confirms a linear deviation with slope
b\beta\varepsilon_{i}W_{2}

and residuals of order
b^{2}
.

IV.4
First-order Jensen inequality

A valid finite-
b
inequality follows from applying Jensen’s
inequality to the exact identity Eq. (
33
). Since

-\ln x
is convex for
x>0
,

\langle-\ln\mathcal{W}_{d}\rangle\geq-\ln\langle\mathcal{W}_{d}\rangle=-n\ln(1-b\,\Delta F).

(44)

Using the expansion Eq. (
36
), the left-hand side reads

\langle-\ln\mathcal{W}_{d}\rangle=\beta\langle W\rangle+b\beta\langle E_{i}W\rangle+\tfrac{b\beta}{2}\langle W^{2}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),

(45)

while the right-hand side reads

-n\ln(1-b\,\Delta F)=\beta\Delta F+\tfrac{b\beta}{2}\Delta F^{2}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}).

(46)

Combining and rearranging yields the first-order-in-
b
inequality

\langle W\rangle\geq\Delta F-b\langle E_{i}W\rangle-\tfrac{b}{2}\bigl(\langle W^{2}\rangle-\Delta F^{2}\bigr)+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),

(47)

valid under the same assumptions as Theorem
2
. Three
points are noteworthy. The zeroth-order content is the standard

\langle W\rangle\geq\Delta F
. The
\mathcal{O}(b)
correction depends on the
mixed moment
\langle E_{i}W\rangle
and the second moment
\langle W^{2}\rangle
, so
it does not reduce to a function of the variance or skewness of
W

alone. Finally, unlike a cumulant-truncation “second-law correction”
of the form
\langle W\rangle\geq\Delta F+(\beta/2)\mathrm{Var}(W)
, which is valid only
in the Gaussian limit, Eq. (
47
) follows directly from
Jensen and from the exact identity Eq. (
33
) without
cumulant truncation.

The mixed moment
\langle E_{i}W\rangle
is not an artifact of the derivation
but a structural feature of the discrete framework, consistent with
Proposition
3
.

IV.5
Observable consequences and experimental regimes

The first-order correction in Eq. (
40
) suggests that
the natural scale of the finite-
b
deviation from the standard
Crooks relation is

\bigl|\delta\ln(P_{F}/P_{R})\bigr|\sim b\beta\langle E_{i}W\rangle.

(48)

The interpretation depends on the meaning of
b
.

Universal Planck-suppressed parameter.

For
b\sim 10^{-28}\,\mathrm{eV}^{-1}
(Planck-suppressed) and

\langle W\rangle\sim\langle E\rangle\sim k_{\mathrm{B}}T
at room temperature in single-molecule
experiments
liphardt2002
, the order-of-magnitude estimate of
the fractional deviation is
b\beta\langle E_{i}W\rangle\sim 10^{-27}
, far
below experimental resolution. Fluctuation-theorem experiments are
then conceptually important but not competitive as direct probes of
fundamental quantum gravity.

Analogue or effective realization.

Bounded weights may also arise in engineered or coarse-grained systems
with an emergent
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
unrelated to the universal Planck
scale
collin2005
. Much larger apparent shifts could then be
realized, and the discrete Jarzynski framework would become testable
as an effective statistical theory. Because the exact relation
retains explicit
E_{i}
dependence, a quantitative comparison with
data would require either a protocol controlling the
E_{i}
–
W

correlation, or a direct measurement of
\mathcal{W}_{d}
rather than only the
work distribution.

Estimator bias.

The first-order expansion of Eq. (
33
) implies that the
modified Jarzynski estimator carries a systematic bias of order

\bigl|\Delta F^{(d)}_{\mathrm{est}}-\Delta F^{(0)}_{\mathrm{est}}\bigr|\sim b\,\langle W^{2}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}).

(49)

For Planck-suppressed
b
this bias is negligible; in analogue
implementations with larger
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
it may become relevant,
and its exact coefficient depends on the joint distribution of

(E_{i},W)
.

V
Summary and outlook

We have analyzed a single structural feature of the discrete
Boltzmann factor
B_{E}(\beta_{n})=(1-bE)^{n}
: unlike the canonical
weight, it has compact support in energy. Once that point is kept at
the center of the discussion, the consequences fall into a clear
hierarchy.

Thermal sector.
The bounded weight suppresses occupation
numbers progressively before the cutoff and removes them entirely at

E=1/b
. In the Hawking application, this depletes the spectrum in
its intermediate and ultraviolet parts, lowers the integrated
luminosity by the universal-sign correction

-(900\zeta(5)/\pi^{4})bk_{\mathrm{B}}T_{H}
, and brings the thermal description to
a Planckian endpoint at
M_{\mathrm{cut}}=m_{\mathrm{P}}
for the Planck-scale
calibration
b=8\pi/E_{\mathrm{P}}
. Whether the associated terminal
configuration corresponds to an absolutely stable remnant, a
long-lived quasi-static phase, or a regime in which nonthermal
channels dominate is a question the thermal framework alone cannot
settle.

Nonequilibrium sector.
Defining the discrete work functional
as a ratio of thermal weights yields an exact Jarzynski-type identity
for deterministic measure-preserving protocols
(Theorem
2
), which extends naturally to stochastic
dynamics satisfying a discrete local detailed balance
(Sec.
IV.2
). The Crooks ratio cannot be a function of
work alone at finite
b
(Proposition
3
), and the
correct first-order Jensen inequality involves the mixed moment

\langle E_{i}W\rangle
rather than work cumulants
[Eq. (
47
)]. The loss of exponential multiplicativity
forces trajectory endpoints to be tracked explicitly—a structural
consequence of the deformation that is coherent with the thermal
analysis.

Entropy sector.
A brick-wall computation produces a
logarithmic shift linear in
b
, but its normalization inherits the
regulator dependence of the scheme; it should be read as a
diagnostic, not as a universal prediction.

Experimental status.
For universal Planck-suppressed
b
,
direct corrections to fluctuation theorems are unobservable. Analogue
or engineered systems with emergent
b_{\mathrm{eff}}
could test the
formal structure of the discrete nonequilibrium theory without
probing fundamental quantum gravity.

Several questions remain open.
(i) A microscopic derivation of the entropy correction bypassing the
brick-wall regulator.
(ii) A complete construction of the stochastic discrete Jarzynski
identity sketched in Sec.
IV.2
, including dissipation
identities and a discrete fluctuation theorem for entropy
production.
(iii) Extension of the two-level worked example to multi-level systems
and continuous driving, where the conditional distribution

p(E_{i}\mid W)
becomes nontrivial and a closed-form
distribution-level Crooks generalization may be possible.
(iv) A systematic comparison between
b
and the deformation scales
of generalized uncertainty principle
models
maggiore1993
;
kempf1995
;
scardigli1999
and other
quantum-gravity-inspired frameworks.
(v) Translation of bounds on the extragalactic CMB
spectrum
fixsen1996
and the projected sensitivity of
next-generation spectrometers such as PIXIE
kogut2011
into
upper bounds on
b
, an avenue that is in principle open but
quantitatively challenging given the Planck suppression expected in
the fundamental interpretation.

truncated partition sum

1

\mathcal{O}(b)

A.1
Exact
\mathcal{O}(b)
correction

Expanding the discrete single-state weight
(1-bk\varepsilon)^{n}

around the exponential gives

(1-bk\varepsilon)^{n}=e^{-\beta k\varepsilon}\Bigl[1-\tfrac{1}{2}b\beta\varepsilon^{2}k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})\Bigr],

(50)

with
\beta=nb
. The associated correction to the exact occupation

\Delta\langle k\rangle_{\mathrm{exact}}\equiv\langle k\rangle_{\mathrm{exact}}-n_{P}=-\tfrac{1}{2}b\beta\varepsilon^{2}\bigl[\langle k^{3}\rangle_{P}-n_{P}\langle k^{2}\rangle_{P}\bigr]+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),

(51)

where averages are taken with the standard Planck distribution and

(52)

\mathcal{O}(b)

\beta\varepsilon\gg 1

•

\beta\varepsilon\ll 1

grows: the effective law

This proves the second clause of Lemma
1
; the first

), summarized in

(a).

Appendix B
Sketch of the brick-wall entropy estimate

This appendix records the assumptions and intermediate steps behind
Eq. (
30
). The aim is not to claim a microscopic derivation
but to make explicit where the linear-in-
b
logarithmic shift
originates.

B.1
Discrete brick-wall free energy

Starting from the bosonic free-energy expression in a black-hole
background, we replace the standard thermal weight by the discrete
one, obtaining schematically

F_{d}\approx\frac{1}{\beta_{H}}\int_{0}^{1/(b\hbar)}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,g(\omega)\,\ln\!\bigl[1-(1-b\hbar\omega)^{n}\bigr],

(54)

where
g(\omega)
is the brick-wall density of states and the upper
limit implements the compact support of the discrete factor. For

\beta_{H}=nb
and
b\hbar\omega\ll 1
,

\begin{split}\ln\!\bigl[1-(1-b\hbar\omega)^{n}\bigr]&=\ln(1-e^{-\beta_{H}\hbar\omega})\\
&\quad+\frac{b\beta_{H}}{2}\,\frac{(\hbar\omega)^{2}}{e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}-1}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),\end{split}

(55)

so
F_{d}=F_{0}+\delta F_{b}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})
with

\delta F_{b}\propto b\int\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,g(\omega)\,\frac{(\hbar\omega)^{2}}{e^{\beta_{H}\hbar\omega}-1}.

(56)

B.2
Density of states and entropy operation

The near-horizon density of states contains an area-law term and
subleading pieces:

g(\omega)=c_{3}\,A\,\omega^{2}+c_{1}\,\omega^{0}+\cdots,

(57)

where
c_{3}
and
c_{1}
depend on the radial cutoff and on the
background geometry. The
\omega^{2}
piece renormalizes the area
term, while the
\omega^{0}
piece yields a logarithmic contribution
under the standard entropy operation

S=\beta_{H}^{2}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\beta_{H}}.

(58)

Using the standard integrals

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{x^{3}}{e^{x}-1}=\frac{\pi^{4}}{15},\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},

(59)

the discreteness correction multiplies the same geometric structure
that produces the standard logarithm. With the conventional
brick-wall normalization adopted in Sec.
III.3
,

\delta\alpha_{b}^{(\mathrm{bw})}=\frac{bE_{\mathrm{P}}}{8\pi}.

(60)

Because
c_{3}
and
c_{1}
are regulator-sensitive and
Eq. (
55
) is only a first-order deformation of a
regulated calculation, this should be read as a representative
estimate of a linear-in-
b
shift, not as a universal prediction.
