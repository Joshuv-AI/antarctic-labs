A divided difference identity for a class of multiple integrals
Michael S. Floater

Abstract
We derive an identity that relates a class of multiple integrals involving Vandermonde polynomials to divided differences. Alternatively the identity can be viewed as an integral formula for divided differences. As part of the derivation we show that both sums of pure partial derivatives and mixed partial derivatives of Vandermonde polynomials are zero, which might be of independent interest.



Keywords:
multiple integral,
Vandermonde determinant, Vandermonde polynomial, divided difference.

Math Subject Classification:

15A15, 65D05, 65F40.

1
Introduction

In this paper we show that there is a surprisingly simple formula
for a certain type of multiple integral involving
Vandermonde polynomials.
This formula arose while working on bounds for the determinant
of an exponential matrix, and will be used for that
purpose in
[
5
]
.
Here, we will focus purely on a proof of the formula
(Theorem
1
).
The formula can alternatively be viewed as an integral
formula for a divided difference (at distinct points)
which is different to the
Genocchi-Hermite formula and to the B-spline integral formula of
Curry and Schoenberg
[
2
]
.
For a survey of divided differences and their role
in interpolation theory, see de Boor
[
3
]
,
and for some basic theory, see
Isaacson and Keller
[
6
, Section 6.1]

and Steffensen
[
8
]
.

For some
n\geq 1
, let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n+1})
be a real increasing
sequence and consider the rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

R(\mathbf{x}):=[x_{1},x_{2}]\times[x_{2},x_{3}]\times\cdots\times[x_{n},x_{n+1}].

(1)

Let
\phi(\mathbf{t})=\phi(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})
be a
continuous function
\phi:R(\mathbf{x})\to\mathbb{R}
. Then its integral
over
R(\mathbf{x})

is

\int_{R(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\mathbf{t})\,d\mathbf{t}:=\int_{x_{n}}^{x_{n+1}}\cdots\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{3}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\phi(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2}\cdots dt_{n}.

Let us denote by
V(\mathbf{t})
the Vandermonde polynomial

V(\mathbf{t})=V(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}):=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(t_{j}-t_{i}).

(2)

We note that Vandermonde polynomials
appear in the study of generalized Vandermonde
determinants and Schur
polynomials
[
7
,
4
,
1
]
.
Let

y_{i}:=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n+1-i}+x_{n+3-i}+\cdots+x_{n+1},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n+1.

(3)

Thus,
y_{i}
is the sum of the
x_{j}
except the ‘
i
-th to last’,

x_{n+2-i}
,
and we can alternatively write

y_{i}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}\Big)-x_{n+2-i}.

(4)

Thus,

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n},

(5)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=x_{2}+\cdots+x_{n+1},

and by (
4
),

y_{i+1}-y_{i}=x_{n+2-i}-x_{n+1-i}>0

and so
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}
are increasing.
It also follows from (
5
) that for
\mathbf{t}\in R(\mathbf{x})
,

y_{1}\leq t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots+t_{n}\leq y_{n+1}.

(6)

We will show

Theorem 1

For
f\in C^{n}[y_{1},y_{n+1}]
,

\int_{R(\mathbf{x})}V(\mathbf{t})f^{(n)}(t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots+t_{n})\,d\mathbf{t}=V(\mathbf{x})[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n+1}]f,

(7)

where

[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n+1}]f
denotes the divided difference
of
f
at the points
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n+1}
.

For example, for
n=1
,

\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}f^{\prime}(t_{1})\,dt_{1}=(x_{2}-x_{1})[y_{1},y_{2}]f=(x_{2}-x_{1})[x_{1},x_{2}]f,

and for
n=2
,

\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{3}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}(t_{2}-t_{1})f^{\prime\prime}(t_{1}+t_{2})\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2}=(x_{2}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})[y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}]f,

where

y_{1}:=x_{1}+x_{2},\quad y_{2}:=x_{1}+x_{3},\quad y_{3}:=x_{2}+x_{3}.

In
[
5
]
, we will use both Theorem
1
and the
following corollary.
Letting
f(\alpha):=\alpha^{n}/n!
shows:

Corollary 1

\int_{R(\mathbf{x})}V(\mathbf{t})\,d\mathbf{t}=\frac{1}{n!}V(\mathbf{x}).

For example, in the case
n=2
,

\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{3}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}(t_{2}-t_{1})\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(x_{2}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2}).

2
A divided difference viewpoint

The identity of Theorem
1
can be
expressed purely as a formula for the divided difference

[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n+1}]f
for given

y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{n+1}
.
To see this observe first that from (
4
),

y_{j}-y_{i}=x_{n+2-i}-x_{n+2-j},

and so
V(\mathbf{x})=V(\mathbf{y})
, where
\mathbf{y}:=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n+1})
.
Next observe that treating
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
as column vectors,
they are related by the matrix equation
\mathbf{y}=M\mathbf{x}
, where

M
is the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix

M:=\left[\begin{matrix}1&1&\cdots&1&0\\
1&1&\cdots&0&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&0&\cdots&1&1\\
0&1&\cdots&1&1\end{matrix}\right].

It is easy to check that
M
has the inverse

M^{-1}=\frac{1}{n}\left[\begin{matrix}1&1&\cdots&1&1-n\\
1&1&\cdots&1-n&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&1-n&\cdots&1&1\\
1-n&1&\cdots&1&1\end{matrix}\right],

and therefore,

x_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}y_{j}-ny_{n+2-i}\Big),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n+1.

(8)

So, by Theorem
1
,

[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n+1}]f=\frac{1}{V(\mathbf{y})}\int_{R(\mathbf{x})}V(\mathbf{t})f^{(n)}(t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots+t_{n})\,d\mathbf{t},

with
\mathbf{x}
given by (
8
).
For example, in the case
n=2
,

[y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}]f=\frac{1}{V(\mathbf{y})}\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{3}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}(t_{2}-t_{1})f^{\prime\prime}(t_{1}+t_{2})\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2},

where

V(\mathbf{y})=(y_{2}-y_{1})(y_{3}-y_{1})(y_{3}-y_{2}),

and

x_{1}:=\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}-y_{3}}{2},\quad x_{2}:=\frac{y_{1}-y_{2}+y_{3}}{2},\quad x_{3}:=\frac{-y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}}{2}.

3
Partial derivatives of
V(\mathbf{t})

The rest of this paper is devoted to proving Theorem
1
.
A key element is the fact that the sums of mixed partial derivatives
of any fixed order of
V(\mathbf{t})
are zero:
Lemma
5
.
As the first step towards that,
we consider a polynomial of the form

\omega(t):=(t-t_{1})(t-t_{2})\cdots(t-t_{m}),

(9)

where
t,t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
.
We want to express its derivatives in terms of products of
the differences
t-t_{i}
.

3.1
Derivatives of
\omega

To compute derivatives of
\omega
in (
9
),
consider the elementary symmetric polynomials.
These are defined, for
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, as

e_{0}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}):=1,

and for
k=1,\ldots,m
,

e_{k}(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{m}):=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m}t_{i_{1}}t_{i_{2}}\cdots t_{i_{k}}.

(10)

Lemma 1

For
k=1,\ldots,m
,

\frac{d}{dt}e_{k}(t-t_{1},\ldots,t-t_{m})=(m-k+1)e_{k-1}(t-t_{1},\ldots,t-t_{m}).

Proof.

By (
10
),

e_{k}(t-t_{1},\ldots,t-t_{m})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m}(t-t_{i_{1}})\cdots(t-t_{i_{k}}),

and its derivative with respect to
t
is

\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m}\sum_{r=1}^{k}(t-t_{i_{1}})\cdots(t-t_{i_{r-1}})(t-t_{i_{r+1}})\cdots(t-t_{i_{k}}).

(11)

We can rewrite this as a sum over
products of the form

(t-t_{j_{1}})\cdots(t-t_{j_{k-1}})

(12)

where

1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k-1}\leq m.

Since there are
m-k+1
elements in the complement
of
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k-1}\}
in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
the product (
12
) appears
m-k+1

times in the sum in (
11
) and so

\frac{d}{dt}e_{k}(t-t_{1},\ldots,t-t_{m})=(m-k+1)\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k-1}\leq m}(t-t_{j_{1}})\cdots(t-t_{j_{k-1}}).

\Box

This lemma enables us to find the derivatives of
\omega
in (
9
).

Lemma 2

Let
\omega
be the polynomial in (
9
). Then
for
k=0,1,\ldots,m
,

\omega^{(k)}(t)=k!\,e_{m-k}(t-t_{1},t-t_{2},\ldots,t-t_{m}).

Proof.

Since
\omega(t)=e_{m}(t-t_{1},t-t_{2},\ldots,t-t_{m})
,
applying Lemma
1
iteratively gives

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=e_{m-1}(t-t_{1},t-t_{2},\ldots,t-t_{m}),

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=2!e_{m-2}(t-t_{1},t-t_{2},\ldots,t-t_{m}),

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=3!e_{m-3}(t-t_{1},t-t_{2},\ldots,t-t_{m}),

and so on.

\Box

3.2
Pure partial derivatives of
V

Using Lemma
2
,
we next derive a formula for the ‘pure’ (not mixed) derivatives
of the Vandermonde polynomial
V(\mathbf{t})
in (
2
)
when
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
are distinct.

Lemma 3

Let
V(\mathbf{t})
be the Vandermonde polynomial
of (
2
) with
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
distinct.
For each
i=1,\ldots,n
and
k=1,\ldots,n-1
,

\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i}^{k}}V(\mathbf{t})=k!\,V(\mathbf{t})\,e_{k}\Big(\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}},\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i+1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{n}}\Big).

Proof.

We can write
V(\mathbf{t})=c\omega(t_{i})
, where

c:=(-1)^{n-i}V(t_{1},\ldots,t_{i-1},t_{i+1},\ldots,t_{n}),

which is independent of
t_{i}
, and

\omega(t):=(t-t_{1})\cdots(t-t_{i-1})(t-t_{i+1})\cdots(t-t_{n}).

Then

\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i}^{k}}V(\mathbf{t})=c\omega^{(k)}(t_{i})=V(\mathbf{t})\frac{\omega^{(k)}(t_{i})}{\omega(t_{i})}.

By Lemma
2
,

\omega^{(k)}(t)=k!\,e_{n-1-k}(t-t_{1},\ldots,t-t_{i-1},t-t_{i+1},\ldots,t-t_{n}),

and therefore,

\frac{\omega^{(k)}(t_{i})}{\omega(t_{i})}=k!e_{k}\Big(\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}},\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i+1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{n}}\Big).

\Box

The lemma covers the cases
k=1,\ldots,n-1
.
Since
V(\mathbf{t})
is a polynomial of degree
n-1

with respect to
t_{i}
, it follows that

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial t_{i}^{n}}V(\mathbf{t})=0.

(13)

3.3
Sums of pure derivatives

Next we show that the sums of pure derivatives of
V(\mathbf{t})
are zero.

Lemma 4

Let
V(\mathbf{t}):=V(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
for any
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,n
and
k=1,\ldots,n
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i}^{k}}V(\mathbf{t})=0.

Proof.

For
k=n
this is implied by (
13
).
For
1\leq k\leq n-1

it is sufficient to prove it for distinct
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
.
Then, by Lemma
3
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i}^{k}}V(\mathbf{t})=k!\,V(\mathbf{t})S,

where

S:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m\\
i\not\in\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(t_{i}-t_{i_{j}})}.

By considering the sequence of length
k+1

formed by
i
and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
,
we can rewrite
S
as

S=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k+1}\leq m}S(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k+1}),

where

S(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k+1}):=\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}\frac{1}{\prod_{s=1,s\neq r}^{k+1}(t_{j_{r}}-t_{j_{s}})}.

We recognize
S(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k+1})

as the divided difference of order
k
of the constant function
1

over the
k+1
points
t_{j_{1}},\ldots,t_{j_{k+1}}
,
which is zero.

\Box

3.4
Sums of mixed derivatives

We now show that the sums of mixed derivatives of
V(\mathbf{t})
are also zero.
For
k=1,\ldots,n
, let
E_{k}
be the differential operator

E_{k}:=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial t_{i_{k}}}.

(14)

Lemma 5

Let
V(\mathbf{t})=V(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
for
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then
E_{k}V(\mathbf{t})=0
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

Let
P_{k}
denote the differential operator

P_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i}^{k}}.

We have already shown that
P_{k}V(\mathbf{t})=0
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
.
The operators
P_{k}
and
E_{k}
are analogous to the
power sum and elementary symmetric polynomials and thus
obey Newton’s identities:

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=P_{1},

\displaystyle 2E_{2}

\displaystyle=E_{1}P_{1}-P_{2},

\displaystyle 3E_{2}

\displaystyle=E_{2}P_{1}-E_{1}P_{2}+P_{3},

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle kE_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{i-1}E_{k-i}P_{i}+(-1)^{k-1}P_{k}.

Thus, since
P_{1}V(\mathbf{t})=\cdots=P_{k}V(\mathbf{t})=0
,
it follows that
E_{k}V(\mathbf{t})=0
as well.

\Box

4
The sum function

For
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
s:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the sum

s(\mathbf{t}):=t_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}.

(15)

We note that
s
has the property that

\frac{\partial s}{\partial t_{i}}=1,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

It then follows from the chain rule that if
f\in C^{1}(s(\Omega))
for some
domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}\big(f(s(\mathbf{t}))\big)=f^{\prime}(s(\mathbf{t})),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Building on this fact, we next compute the highest order mixed derivative
of the product of
f\circ s
with some other function
\psi
.
Recall the notation of (
14
) and define
E_{0}:={\rm id}
.

Lemma 6

Suppose that
\psi\in C^{n}(\Omega)
and
f\in C^{n}(s(\Omega))

for some domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define the product

\phi(\mathbf{t}):=\psi(\mathbf{t})f\big(s(\mathbf{t})\big),\qquad\mathbf{t}\in\Omega.

Then,

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\phi(\mathbf{t})=E_{n}\phi(\mathbf{t})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}E_{k}\psi(\mathbf{t})f^{(n-k)}\big(s(\mathbf{t})\big),\quad\mathbf{t}\in\Omega.

(16)

Proof.

We show more generally that

\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{m}}\phi(\mathbf{t})=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial t_{i_{k}}}\psi(\mathbf{t})f^{(m-k)}\big(s(\mathbf{t})\big)

(17)

for all
m=0,1,\ldots,n
by induction on
m
.
This is true for
m=0
by the definition of
\phi
.
Suppose it holds for some
m
,
0\leq m\leq n-1
.
Differentiation with respect to
t_{m+1}
gives

\frac{\partial^{m+1}}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{m+1}}\phi(\mathbf{t})=a+b,

where

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m}\frac{\partial^{k+1}}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial t_{i_{k}}\partial t_{m+1}}\psi(\mathbf{t})f^{(m-k)}\big(s(\mathbf{t})\big),

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial t_{i_{k}}}\psi(\mathbf{t})f^{(m+1-k)}\big(s(\mathbf{t})\big).

We can write
a
as

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k-1}\leq m}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial t_{i_{k-1}}\partial t_{m+1}}\psi(\mathbf{t})f^{(m+1-k)}\big(s(\mathbf{t})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}=m+1}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial t_{i_{k}}}\psi(\mathbf{t})f^{(m+1-k)}\big(s(\mathbf{t})\big),

and adding this to
b
yields the case
m+1
of (
17
).

\Box

Now let
V(\mathbf{t})
be the Vandermonde polynomial in (
2
).
By Lemma
5
,

E_{k}V(\mathbf{t})=0
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
, while
E_{0}V(\mathbf{t})=V(\mathbf{t})
.
Therefore, letting
\psi=V
in Lemma
6
implies

Lemma 7

Suppose that
f\in C^{n}(s(\Omega))
for some domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then,

V(\mathbf{t})f^{(n)}(s(\mathbf{t}))=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\big(V(\mathbf{t})f(s(\mathbf{t}))\big),\qquad\mathbf{t}\in\Omega.

This observation will simplify the integration in Theorem
1
.

5
Integration on a rectangle

Let us now look at integration on a rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Such a rectangle has the form

R:=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times[a_{2},b_{2}]\times\cdots\times[a_{n},b_{n}],

and has
2^{n}
vertices which we can denote by

\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}:=((1-\epsilon_{1})a_{1}+\epsilon_{1}b_{1},(1-\epsilon_{2})a_{2}+\epsilon_{2}b_{2},\ldots,(1-\epsilon_{n})a_{n}+\epsilon_{n}b_{n}),

for
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
It is easy to integrate the highest order mixed derivative
of a function of
n
variables on
R
.
Similar to (
15
) we define

s({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}):=\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\cdots+\epsilon_{n}.

Lemma 8

For any
\phi\in C^{n}(R)
,

\int_{R}\frac{\partial^{n}\phi}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{n-s({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})}\phi(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}).

(18)

Proof.

We show more generally that the integral can be written as

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{k-s({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})}\int_{a_{n}}^{b_{n}}\cdots\int_{a_{k+1}}^{b_{k+1}}\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t_{k+1}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\phi(\mathbf{v}_{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},k})\,dt_{k+1}\cdots dt_{n}

(19)

for any
k=0,1,\ldots,n
, where

\mathbf{v}_{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},k}:=((1-\epsilon_{1})a_{1}+\epsilon_{1}b_{1},\ldots,(1-\epsilon_{k})a_{k}+\epsilon_{k}b_{k},t_{k+1},\ldots,t_{n}).

The case
k=0
of (
19
) is the left hand side of (
18
).
Suppose (
19
) holds for some
k
,
0\leq k\leq n-1
.
Then its inner integral is

\frac{\partial^{n-k-1}}{\partial t_{k+2}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\phi(\mathbf{v}_{({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},1),k+1})-\frac{\partial^{n-k-1}}{\partial t_{k+2}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\phi(\mathbf{v}_{({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},0),k+1})\\
=\sum_{\epsilon_{k+1}=0}^{1}(-1)^{1-\epsilon_{k+1}}\frac{\partial^{n-k-1}}{\partial t_{k+2}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\phi(\mathbf{v}_{({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\epsilon_{k+1}),k+1}),

and moving this summation out of the remaining integrals
yields the case
k+1
of (
19
).
Thus (
19
) also holds for
k=n
, which
is the right hand side of (
18
).

\Box

Suppose next that
R
is the sequential rectangle
R(\mathbf{x})

of (
1
)
and that
\phi
has the zero property

\phi(\mathbf{t})=0\quad\hbox{whenever $t_{i}=t_{i+1}$
for any $i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}$.}

(20)

Then the sum in (
18
) is reduced from
2^{n}
terms to
n+1
.

Lemma 9

If
\phi\in C^{n}(R(\mathbf{x}))
and
\phi
has the zero property
(
20
) then

\int_{R(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\partial^{n}\phi}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{n}}\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{n+1-i}\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i}),

(21)

where

\mathbf{v}_{i}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1-i},x_{n+3-i},\ldots,x_{n+1}).

(22)

Proof.

We apply Lemma
8
to the sequential rectangle

R=R(\mathbf{x})
, whose vertices are

\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}:=((1-\epsilon_{1})x_{1}+\epsilon_{1}x_{2},(1-\epsilon_{2})x_{2}+\epsilon_{2}x_{3},\ldots,(1-\epsilon_{n})x_{n}+\epsilon_{n}x_{n+1})

for
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
By the zero property (
20
),

\phi(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})=0
whenever
