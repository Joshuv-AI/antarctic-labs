Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair Using a Cable Suture‐Bridge Technique
Authors: Jingjie Zhang, Qi Xiao, Huixiang Jiang, Huiyun Deng, Qingquan Wu, Jiapeng Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRotator cuff tear is one of the most common diseases in sports medicine, and arthroscopic anchor suture and knotting are the main treatment. However, poor tendon‐bone healing or retear after surgery remains the main challenge in rotator cuff repair. Herein, we describe a technique for arthroscopic repair of rotator cuff tear: “cable‐bridge” suture. This is a rotator cuff repair technique based on the biomechanical concept of the rotator cuff cable and on the principle of bridge suture. First, different knots of medial row anchors are locked with each other to form a main cable similar to the rotator cuff structure. The sutures are cross‐linked and fixed with the lateral row anchors to form a “crossed‐bridge” cable structure. The medial row suture locking plus lateral row bridge fixation enables all sutures to form a single overall structure like a cable bridge, achieving an optimized mechanical distribution at the tendon‐bone interface, forming a continuous compression band at the tendon‐bone interface, evenly distributing the tension, reducing single‐point stress concentration, and converting linear tension into circular pressure, which is similar to the principle of the cable bridge, with improved surgical reliability.

The entire operation process of “cable‐bridge” suture technique is as follows: 1. Insertion of medial row anchors: At the junction of the humeral head joint surface and the bone bed, 2‐sutures anchors are inserted. The anchor space is approximately 5 to 10 mm in diameter. Multiple medial row anchors are placed according to the size of rotator cuff tear, matching the width of rotator cuff tear. 2. Suture process: The Grasper is used to determine the suture position and tension of the rotator cuff. The suture shuttle is used to guide the suture from the articular surface side of the torn rotator cuff to the subacromial side. For the 2 edges of torn rotator cuff, the suture shuttle makes 1 stitch to guide out 1 suture. For the middle part of torn rotator cuff, the suture shuttle makes 1 stitch to guide out 2 different‐color sutures from the same anchor or 2 different‐color sutures from 2 separate anchors. That is, except for the suture sites on the 2 edges of torn rotator cuff where there is only 1 suture, the middle suture sites have 2 different‐color sutures. 3. Knotting: For the medial row of anchors, the same color sutures are used to thread the cannula. Before knotting, the suture passer is used to cross‐link other different‐color sutures that are paired with the suture to be knotted around the suture to be knotted. After passing through the middle of 2 knotted sutures, the knot is tightened. This procedure is repeated to achieve mutual locking of all the suture knots in the of medial row of anchors, forming an integrated cable structure. Attention should be paid to suture management to prevent entanglement that may affect the knot‐tying of medial row anchors. Over‐tightening of the suture anchors should be avoided during knot placement to prevent excessive tension and potential compromise of vascular supply, which may affect healing. 4. Insertion of lateral row anchors: One suture is taken from each of the knotted sutures. The sutures are passed through the lateral row anchor. According to the number of sutures, 2 lateral row anchors are inserted at the distal end of the greater tuberosity to make the sutures cross each other, forming a “crossed‐bridge” cable structure. Rotator cuff tear is one of the most common shoulder joint diseases. Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair is the main treatment. Common techniques include single‐row suture, double‐row suture, suture‐bridge suture, etc. The clinical outcomes are satisfactory, but poor tendon‐bone healing or retear after surgery remains the main challenge. A study has shown that the retear rate after rotator cuff repair is as high as 10% to 40%, and in some cases, it even reaches 90%. There are many factors affecting rotator cuff healing, including tear size, tendon quality, patient's age, obesity, underlying diseases, corticosteroid injection, etc. 1 In addition, surgical technique is one of the important factors affecting rotator cuff healing. 2 To reduce the retear rate after rotator cuff suture surgery, surgeons have designed various lock‐edge suture techniques, such as double‐lock suture technique, 3 blended suture‐bridge technique, 4 parachute suture technique, 5 double‐row antitear technique, 6 shoelace technique, 7 double‐layer loop ring technique, 8 “double‐triangle” suture technique, 9 and double‐pulley 3‐row technique. 10 A study has shown that the application of various locking‐loop suture techniques during surgery can effectively reduce the failure rate and prevent retearing of the rotator cuff during the suturing process. 11 However, different suture methods have different biomechanical properties. 12 For degenerative rotator cuff tears or poor‐quality tendon, the experienced surgeon is usually crucial for the successful surgery and the operation time is often longer in order to achieve reliable locking suture. This study describes a “cable‐bridge suture technique” based on the biologic cable mechanical structure. The surgical procedures include lateral knotting and circumferential locking between the medial row anchor suture lines as well as the lateral row anchor bridging fixation to form a “cable‐bridge” suture structure, which makes the surgery relatively simple.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Trained arthroscopic surgeons can quickly master this cable bridge‐style suture technique. This technique does not require any special instruments, but can be accomplished using the regular suture anchor.

Step 1: Patient Position

The surgery is performed under brachial plexus anesthesia and general anesthesia. The patient is positioned in the lateral recumbent position on the healthy side and fixed on the standard operation table using a pelvic fixator. All bony prominences are protected with sponge pads. The nonoperative arm is flexed at 90°, the knee is bent, and the head is kept in a neutral position. Under anesthesia, the range of motion of the shoulder joint and the condition of joint capsule adhesions are evaluated through preoperative examinations. Then, the surgical arm is placed in a foam traction sleeve, and a simple traction frame is used to apply a traction force of 3 kg. The trunk is tilted backward by 30°, and the surgical arm is abducted at 70° and flexed at 20°. Then, the surface positioning landmarks of the shoulder joint are marked. 13

Step 2: Establishment of the Arthroscopic Approach

First, a 2 cm posterior incision is made below the posterior‐lateral angle of the acromion, 1 cm medial to it. A 30° arthroscope is used through this access for the initial arthroscopic examination. Based on the positioning guide, an anterior incision is made 4 cm below the anterior acromion angle and 1 cm lateral to the coracoid process. A 2 cm lateral incision is made along the supraspinatus tendon axis from the distal end of the acromion. If necessary, an additional auxiliary lateral incision is made. 13

Step 3: Shoulder Joint Assessment

The glenohumeral joint is examined through the posterior access. If the long head of the biceps tendon is torn or there is evident inflammation, an anterior access is established. The patient's age and clinical findings are used to determine whether the long head of the biceps tendon should be repaired or severed. If severe adhesions of the joint capsule are found, a release is necessary, and other pathological changes within the joint should also be repaired.

Step 4: Preparation for Subacromial Decompression and Rotator Cuff Repair

When the subacromial space is observed from the posterior access, the arthroscopic shaver (Smith & Nephew, Mansfield, MA) enters from the lateral access to remove the damaged and inflamed tissues in the thickened bursa, and the radiofrequency system (Smith & Nephew, Mansfield, MA) is used for hemostasis. Then, the field of view is turned to the acromion. If shoulder acromial hypertrophy and impingement are observed, the lateral side of the acromion is marked. The arthroscopic radiofrequency system is used to remove the hypertrophic soft tissue in the subacromial space, and the burr (Smith & Nephew, Mansfield, MA) is employed to remove the hypertrophic bone in the subacromial area. After the above procedures, the cancellous bone surface is smoothed, followed by hemostasis. Then, the anterior acromial angle is handled from the posterior outer access. The size, shape, and quality of the rotator cuff tear are evaluated. The rotator cuff tear is released and the tension condition and suture method are assessed. The arthroscopic burr is used to prepare the tendon footprint, and to expose the cortical bone of the greater tuberosity, until the bone surface is fresh enough to promote tendon‐bone healing.

Step 6: Postoperative Rehabilitation

A reasonable postoperative rehabilitation plan is crucial to prevent retears. Early vigorous activities or lack of proper physical therapy may increase the risk of retears. Patients are usually asked to avoid vigorous activities in the first few weeks after surgery and receive appropriate rehabilitation guidance. 14 , 15 Postoperative rehabilitation involves the fixation of the arm in an arm sling for 6 weeks. The forearm should be kept in a neutral rotation and mild abduction position to reduce the tension on the repaired rotator cuff. Passive movements are initiated 2 weeks after surgery. Passive and active assisted movements should be monitored in the postoperative recovery for 6 weeks. Weight‐bearing or strengthening exercises are allowed 12 weeks later.

DISCUSSION

In an aging population, the incidence of rotator cuff tears is increasing in patients with joint degeneration and osteoporosis. For relatively minor rotator cuff tears, conservative treatment is usually recommended; however, nonsurgical treatment may lead to aggravation of fat infiltration and reduction in the distance between the acromion and the humeral head, thereby limiting the function of shoulder joint and increasing pain. 16 With the development of arthroscopic technology, various methods can be employed for the rotator cuff repair, but retearing after rotator cuff repair remains a major challenge, especially during 6 weeks to 3 months after surgery. 17 The causes of retears may be multifactorial, and they are related to age, tendon quality, and surgical technique. Therefore, generally, the appropriate method for rotator cuff repair is based on the patient's age, the shape, size, retraction degree, and fatty degeneration of the rotator cuff, as well as the surgeon's personal preferences. Studies have shown that arthroscopic repair of rotator cuff tears is associated with an increased retear rate.

It has been confirmed that locking suturing can improve the success rate of repairing torn rotator cuffs without causing significant degeneration or deterioration of the rotator cuff. 5 , 8 , 18 However, there is evidence showing that most of the locking suturing techniques may increase the operation time and difficulty, limiting their application. The cable‐bridge suturing technique in the present study is easy to operate and has a short learning curve. Surgeons with certain experience in shoulder surgery can accomplish it, and it is also more suitable for beginners in shoulder arthroscopy. Pearls and Pitfalls of Cable‐Bridge Suture Technique

Numerous studies have confirmed the significance of the cable structure of the rotator cuff for force‐couple balance. The cable structure within the rotator cuff tendon is a crucial mechanical support. 19 Specifically, it refers to the bow‐shaped fiber bundles within the rotator cuff tendon, which run horizontally and resemble cables. It plays a vital role in maintaining the force‐couple balance of the rotator cuff. During shoulder joint movement, the cable structure serves as the core for stress transmission in the rotator cuff, effectively distributing loads and reducing stress at the distal end of the tendon. Particularly during shoulder abduction, it maintains the balance of the downward and upward forces on the humeral head through its rigid support, preventing abnormal displacement. The cable structure becomes increasingly important and serves as a main stabilizer of the rotator cuff tendon. 20 If the cable structure is damaged or undergoes degeneration, local stress may concentrate at the edge of the tendon, causing rotator cuff tears or mechanical decompensation, disrupting the force‐couple balance, and subsequently leading to subacromial impingement and instability of the shoulder joint. A study has shown that the presence or absence of the cable structure is significantly correlated with the degree of rotator cuff tears. 21 Therefore, the integrity of the cable structure is crucial for the biomechanical function of the rotator cuff, and its protection and repair are of great significance for restoring dynamic stability of the shoulder joint. The “cable‐bridge” suture technique, based on the principle of suture‐bridge suturing, first locks the different knotting points of the medial row anchor with each other to form a main cable similar to the cable structure of the rotator cuff. The sutures are cross‐linked and fixed with the lateral row anchor to form a “crossed‐bridging” cable structure. The interlocking of the medial row sutures and the bridging fixation of the lateral row fix all sutures into an overall structure similar to a cable bridge. This achieves an optimized mechanical distribution of the tendon‐bone interface, forms a continuous compression band at the tendon‐bone interface, evenly disperses the tension, reduces single‐point stress concentration, and converts linear tension into circular pressure, similar to the cable‐bridge principle, thereby improving surgical reliability.
