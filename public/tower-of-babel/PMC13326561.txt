On the inevitability of microvascular failure in septic shock and other vasodilatory conditions
Authors: Ivor Popovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microcirculatory dysfunction is a defining feature of septic shock and is strongly associated with mortality, yet its relationship to macrocirculatory haemodynamics remains poorly understood. In particular, the persistence of heterogeneous capillary perfusion despite restoration of blood pressure and cardiac output (termed haemodynamic incoherence) lacks a coherent mechanistic explanation. I developed a conceptual and computational model of the microcirculation in which network behaviour is constrained by three interacting variables: cardiac output, vasomotor state, and shear stress regulation. A network of one million parallel arterioles was simulated using physiologically plausible distributions of vessel radius. For each vessel, flow requirements were determined by an apparent shear target, reflecting endothelial sensing of shear rather than absolute physical values. Total cardiac output required to maintain network-wide shear was calculated as the sum of individual vessel demands. The model demonstrates that, for a given shear target, total flow requirements increase in proportion to the sum of vessel radii cubed, such that even modest global vasodilation produces a substantial increase in required cardiac output. Increasing the apparent shear target further amplifies this demand. When cardiac output is insufficient to meet these requirements, vessels experience low shear and undergo functional derecruitment, reducing total flow demand but resulting in marked heterogeneity and reduced functional capillary density. These behaviours reproduce key features of septic physiology, including the hyperdynamic circulation and microvascular shunting observed in severe sepsis. The model provides a unifying framework in which microcirculatory dysfunction emerges as an inevitable consequence of the interaction between vasodilation, flow limitation, and shear regulation, rather than as an independent pathological process. It further predicts that therapies which reduce global vasodilation or lower the apparent shear target may restore microvascular coherence without requiring supranormal cardiac output. This framework generates testable hypotheses and offers a physiologically grounded basis for reinterpreting haemodynamic management in septic shock.

Background

Sepsis is the most common cause of circulatory failure treated in the intensive care unit. Despite this, a consistent understanding of the pathophysiology has been elusive. At first, it was thought sepsis was a standard low output shock state. Widespread use of the pulmonary artery catheter in the 60s and 70s first revealed the more common hyperdynamic, low resistance situation [1, 2]. At the same time animal and ex-vivo studies showed widespread arteriolar vasodilation and hypo-responsiveness of micro-vessels to catecholamines and other vasoconstricting agents [3, 4]. The next several decades of therapeutic theory focussed on enhancing oxygen delivery through targeting ScvO2 and supranormal cardiac index, which ultimately proved fruitless [5, 6].

Since the turn of the millennium access to new imaging techniques like handheld videomicroscopy has revealed the importance of microcirculatory dysfunction. Capillary perfusion is heterogeneous, with reduced functional capillary density and perfused vessel density, and increased stop flow capillaries [7, 8]. These changes may persist despite normalisation of macrocirculatory variables such as blood pressure and cardiac output and predict mortality; the so-called haemodynamic incoherence [9]. A satisfactory explanation for this disconnect has not emerged. Why a global vasodilatory process should lead to such a patchy locally heterogeneous problem is unclear. While it is possible that the two phenomena are completely separate, purely vasodilatory conditions such as calcium channel blocker overdose also result in the same microvascular impairment despite high global flow [10].

Shear stress is the force exerted by flowing blood on the endothelium, in the direction parallel to the vessel wall. Shear signalling in normal endothelium involves feedback that maintains shear stress within vessels within a given range. Shear is increased by flow and by a reduction in vessel size. When shear is excessive, the signalling pathway will result in local release of nitric oxide (NO), which dilates the vessel and reduces the shear stress back to an acceptable range. Low shear produces endothelin, which constricts the vessel. Sensing of shear occurs via mechanotransduction proteins in the endothelial glycocalyx and gap junctions. Shear regulation is prominent within the microcirculation [11, 12].

Shear signalling is known to be abnormal in septic shock. Excessive expression of inducible NO synthase (iNOS) results in elevated levels of NO [13]. At the same time endothelial damage disrupts mechanotransducers and inflammatory cytokines inhibit signalling pathways, resulting in impairment of shear sensing [14, 15]. These observations have been linked generally to the development of microvascular dysfunction, but not through a mechanistic hypothesis.

The aim of this paper is to put forward a hypothesis that explains these disparate findings, and model how the microcirculation is constrained by three main factors; total cardiac output, vasomotor state, and shear regulation. Predictable behaviour of the microcirculation naturally emerges from changes in these variables.

Methods

A microcirculatory network consisting of one million parallel arterioles was simulated in Python using JupyterLab (v 4.3.4). The distribution of vessel radii was simulated with a truncated normal distribution (with lower and upper physiological bounds of 0 and 100 microns). The mean vessel radius was varied between 20 and 30 microns, and the standard deviation between 1.5 and 3 microns, consistent with the typical size of arterioles in the microcirculation [16]. Typical numbers of parallel arterioles in the human body are unknown exactly, but probably closer to 100 million (based on roughly 1010 capillaries and each arteriole feeding a bed of 100 capillaries [16, 17]), however the simulation was scaled for ease of computation, as explained further below.

Model output

The model calculated Q0 for each vessel in the one million vessel network based on the set apparent shear target and the distribution of vessel sizes. It displayed the minimum cardiac output required to satisfy the global shear target plotted against the mean vessel radius, with sliders to adjust the standard deviation of vessel radii between 1.5 and 3 microns, and apparent shear target between 10 and 20 Pa. Rightward movement along the x-axis therefore represents attempted uniform vasodilatation. Plots of vessel size distribution at each standard deviation, for a mean of 25 microns, were also produced. Units were converted from the SI units used in the shear equation to mL·min− 1 for flow and microns for vessel radius.

If cardiac output is not able to meet the minimal requirement, some vessels will experience shear below their target and constrict in response. Hypothetically, vessel de-recruitment should occur and vessels will be lost from the network, decreasing the overall flow requirement until an equilibrium is achieved again, at a reduced functional capillary density. Such an idealised response was implemented in this model in order to study network flow shunting behaviour, accepting that such regulation is not perfect in real biological systems. The model therefore also simulated a fixed cardiac output of 5000 mL·min− 1, with standard deviation 1.5 microns and shear target 20 Pa. As mean vessel radius was increased from 20 to 30 microns, it iteratively removed the smallest vessels from the distribution, updating the distribution until total shear requirements were satisfied. It displayed a plot of vessel distribution, the total number of remaining vessels out of the initial million, and the relative resistance of the network compared to a baseline mean radius of 20 microns (where there was no vessel dropout). Results

Over the studied vessel range the minimum cardiac output required to maintain the global shear target increased almost linearly with the mean vessel radius. The required cardiac output, at a shear target of 20 Pa and standard deviation of 1.5 microns, increased from approximately 5 L per minute at a mean radius of 25 microns, to approximately 8.5 L per minute at a radius of 30 microns (Fig. 1). Discussion

I have illustrated a hypothetical model of the microcirculation that is constrained by three elements; cardiac output, shear regulation, and vasomotor state. The model produces the characteristic microcirculatory changes seen in vasodilatory shock. When the microcirculation dilates (increases the sum of its radii cubed), cardiac output must increase in proportion to the sum of radii cubed in order to preserve the same level of shear. If this cannot be achieved, then vasoconstrictive responses triggered by low shear will close off branches of the microcirculation, reducing functional capillary density. While it is unlikely that the baseline or target shear can be restored, the network will therefore tend towards achieving equilibrium in cases of relatively inadequate flow through increased microvascular heterogeneity.

In the model, at a mean vessel radius of 25 microns the normal resting cardiac output of 5 L per minute emerges. Increase of mean vessel radius to 30 microns results in a cardiac output requirement of 8–9 L per minute, similar to the hyperdynamic values commonly observed in survivors of septic shock in the supranormal oxygen delivery era [6]. If cardiac output is fixed at 5 L per minute, then the same change in vessel radius results in dropout of almost 50% of vessels, similar to what is seen experimentally [7, 8]. While vessel number was reduced by a factor of one hundred from normal in this model, to aid with computational ease, a scaled shear target parameter was chosen that produced physiologically consistent results and was also scaled up by approximately the same factor from shear observed in capillaries, which lie close to arterioles.

This model therefore posits that microvascular dysfunction is an inevitable response in a vasodilated system where cardiac output does not rise proportionately. It provides a potential mechanistic explanation for the haemodynamic incoherence seen in septic shock. The latter has been traditionally explained by assuming that the macro and microcirculations are truly dissociated and that microvascular dysfunction is a function of local thrombosis and endothelial dysfunction, rather than that the two systems constrain each other [7, 14]. Without linking the two circulations, it is impossible to explain why vasoconstrictors help in septic shock, or why purely vasodilatory conditions like L-type calcium channel blocker overdose also result in microvascular shutdown. Total blood flow is elevated in all organs in vasodilatory shock, including the splanchnic circulation [20]. If all vessels dilated evenly, then mean arterial pressure (MAP) would be irrelevant. It has been recognised that MAP is important precisely because flow shunting occurs. Why it occurs has been a mystery, as has the question of why vasoconstriction doesn’t just further clamp down small vessels and worsen shunting. This model links aspects of known physiology in a novel manner, providing a theoretical mechanism whereby attempted global vasodilation produces competing vasoconstrictory signals and shunting as a necessity under certain conditions.

Under this paradigm, an adequate MAP is therefore simply a crude signal that flow matches the dilated state. In our model, the resistance in the network at a mean of 30 microns (without vessel dropout) is about half that of the network at 25 microns, and the required total cardiac output is almost double. These nearly opposite and equal relative changes mean a stable MAP. This is consistent with the fact that healthy exercising humans, who experience a drop in systemic vascular resistance greater than or equal to that in septic shock, do not die of microvascular failure. Cardiac output rises to often more than 15 L per minute, and blood pressure does not fall, therefore shear targets are able to be maintained. In fact, new capillary beds are recruited during exercise [21], so the physiology is able to cope with an increase in shear demand. This is possible due to the large decrease in venous capacitance [22] that occurs which allows the maintenance of such high cardiac outputs. In sepsis and vasodilatory diseases, the cardiac output, though elevated, may still be insufficient. The barrier to further increase is the massive increase in venous capacitance, so flow is limited by venous return, as well as by likely myocardial dysfunction.

Attempting to restore the microcirculation by increasing cardiac output may therefore not be successful. Patients who managed to achieve supranormal targets in the therapeutic trials done in the 90s with simple therapies like fluid had excellent survival [6], however overall mortality was worsened as many patients were not able to achieve the targets despite exposure to inodilators that increased myocardial oxygen demand and further worsened vasodilatation. Fluid therapy may also not result in sustained increases in cardiac output but expose patients to the risks of fluid overload and pulmonary oedema. The model provides two further insights into therapy that may restore the microcirculation. Decreasing the global vasodilatory burden through the use of vasoconstrictors such as noradrenaline, vasopressin, methylene blue and newer agents such as angiotensin II, may reduce the cardiac output required to satisfy shear, paradoxically eliminating the locally vasoconstricting stimulus of low shear. Vasoconstrictors are of course standard therapy in vasodilatory conditions, but this provides novel insight into their mechanism of action and why they have been shown to improve the microcirculation even when cardiac output does not change [23]. This is a more satisfactory explanation than that traditionally provided of “restoring perfusion pressure” by increasing the resistance in those very vessels that the perfusion pressure is supposed to supply. However, at excessive doses vasoconstrictors may amplify constriction in low shear vessels more than they combat the global vasodilation, worsening functional capillary density. Where this balance lies may be different depending on the agent.

Microvascular dysfunction may persist despite normalisation of blood pressure for reasons other than excessive vasoconstriction. Increasing the shear target in our model dramatically increases the cardiac output required and accentuates the vasoconstrictory influence of low shear. Shear regulation involves three components; the actual physical level of shear (described with Eq. 1), the afferent pathway sensing this shear, and the efferent pathway mediating the response. The afferent pathway involves an intact endothelial glycocalyx, mechanosensitive calcium channels such as Piezo1 and TRPV4, and mechanotransduction proteins such as PECAM-1, VE-cadherin, and VEGFR2. The efferent pathway utilises eNOS and prostacyclins as the response to high shear, and Endothelin-1 for low shear. The interaction of this triad may be misaligned due to the effect of septic toxins; sepsis itself results in endothelial damage and impaired shear sensing [13–15]. This will increase the apparent shear target as shear will be interpreted as low even when physically it is adequate, and the locally vasoconstrictory response to low shear will remain active. The role of shear may therefore be the missing link in haemodynamic incoherence and microvascular failure and an avenue ripe for future therapeutic investigation. The testable hypothesis would be that enhancing the afferent limb of shear sensing improved microvascular function. Indirect experimental evidence for methods to achieve this is by increasing pulsatility. Even if mean flow and therefore mean shear remains constant, the endothelium has been shown to respond to increased time varying shear during pulses by increasing production of local smooth muscle relaxing autocoids [24]. We also know that pulsatile extracorporeal circuits achieve better microcirculatory recruitment than constant flow circuits [25]. Clinically one may improve pulsatility by increasing stroke volume and reducing heart rate. Direct pharmaceutical approaches could involve activation of the aforementioned mechanotransduction proteins or their downstream signalling pathways. Activation of the efferent pathway may also override the functionally high shear target and could be considered, although may be a less targeted approach. Inhibition of vasoconstrictors involved in the response to low shear is part of this efferent modulation; endothelin antagonists have shown promise in experimental animal studies [26]. Lastly, viewing under-sensing of shear as a critical part of septic physiology allows us to entertain alternative explanations for the haematological abnormalities seen in extreme cases of microvascular failure such as purpura fulminans. Adequate vessel shear levels are required to inhibit thrombosis and coagulation.

The larger strength of this model is that it provides a theoretical integration of several aspects of the circulation that could form the basis for future experimental work. With further validation, it may add to the bedside intensivist’s understanding of septic physiology and help guide individualised assessment of the patient. It suggests that both vasodilatory and vasoconstrictory signals operate to produce microcirculatory dysfunction, and therefore combinations of vasoactive agents with opposing effects targeting different parts of the microcirculation may be physiologically sound; generalised vasoconstriction combined with targeted dilation in low shear vessels. A complex approach centred on shear signalling will likely be required to further advance haemodynamic support in severe septic shock. While shear is the main focus of regulatory signalling, the model still allows a unification of this with the other known components of vascular homeostasis. Metabolic control is represented in the global vasodilatory signal, while myogenic control, which occurs further upstream in larger arteries [27] and regulates organ inflow, helps to control total available flow. This provides a more simplified, pragmatic view of how these three control mechanisms interact. There is a need to bridge the gap between complex mathematical models found in the physiology and engineering literature, and the practical needs of the clinician.

Of course, this model lacks experimental validation. It is rather a hypothesis that attempts to unify paradoxical findings in the septic circulation. Limitations of the model include the underlying assumptions. I have ignored the shear thinning behaviour of blood and chosen a constant viscosity for the ease of computation. Variation in viscous effects is considered negligible relative to the effect of vessel sizes, which are raised to the third power, although I cannot rule out a significant effect on the model. Laminar shear is assumed in the microcirculation, which is a valid assumption as Womersley numbers are well below one. I have assumed that vessel sizes are normally distributed, which is an assumption based on biology, but one that remains unproven. The total number of arterioles in the circulation is unknown but derived from evidence on total capillary number and arteriole-capillary branching structure. In the vessel dropout model, I assume that vessels disappear from the left side of the distribution, and have adopted a binary simulation of arterioles either closing or remaining in their original state. This is unlikely to be the biological reality; rather it is more likely that the entire distribution would shift in an unknown way. This is a simplification for the sake of the model which still allows us to demonstrate the broad principles of network behaviour. I have ignored effects such as plasma skimming, Fahraeus-Lindquist and conservation of momentum at branch points in the microcirculation which may also explain maldistribution of vessel perfusion. I have not explicitly modelled other factors that regulate vessel tone such as myogenic and metabolic control, as discussed above. Perhaps the main weakness is in the modelling of shear regulation. I assume an identical shear target for all vessels for ease, accepting that shear targets will vary even across the same level of the network. This ultimately would change some of the values that appear in the model output, but would not change the behaviour of the model overall. The bigger weakness is in the assumed strength of shear regulation. The model assumes that shear signalling closes vessels until total shear returns to normal. This is untrue- measured shear values in the septic circulation remain low [19]. What if the system simply accepts a lower shear? I concede that shear responses will not fully restore the system. However modelling it in this way allows us to analyse the tendency of the system to respond to the level of shear, and its consequences, even if the magnitude of response is not realistic. There is no evidence to suggest that shear regulation is completely ablated, and in fact the available evidence suggests that the apparent shear target is amplified, as discussed above. I am also at this stage not able to propose how the model interacts with other known behaviours of the microcirculation, such as vascular waterfalls or critical closing pressure.

In summary, the current model proposes that microvascular function is constrained by three elements; total cardiac output, vasomotor state, and shear targeting. When vasodilation occurs, inadequate response in the cardiac output, or excessive shear targets, produces microvascular heterogeneity as a natural response to regulate the network. This hypothesis has the potential to explain the long standing mystery of haemodynamic incoherence and to provide insights into the physiology of circulatory failure for bedside intensivists as well as future therapeutic avenues.
