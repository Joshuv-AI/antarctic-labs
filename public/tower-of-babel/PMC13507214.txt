ViT-ConvGAN: a hybrid model for spatiotemporal action recognition using video transformer and 3D CNN
Authors: Chenlei Miao, Jianjun Lin, Lin Jia, Dongqing Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Action recognition in videos is an important task in computer vision, widely used in sports, healthcare, and human-computer interaction. Existing methods often struggle to balance global motion understanding and local detail extraction, especially when dealing with rapid transitions or combinations of multiple actions. This paper proposes a new action recognition model, ViT-ConvGAN, which integrates Video Transformer (ViT), 3D Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), and Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (CGAN). Input video frames are first processed by ViT, which captures long-term temporal dependencies via spatiotemporal attention to generate global spatiotemporal features; these global features are then fed into the 3D CNN, which refines local motion details and fuses them with the global features to form a comprehensive feature map; finally, the fused feature map is transmitted to the CGAN–where the generator optimizes feature representation for more discriminative action characteristics, and the discriminator enhances the distinction between different action categories to improve prediction accuracy. ViT models long-term temporal dependencies through spatiotemporal attention, 3D CNN extracts local motion features, and CGAN optimizes action predictions to enhance the reliability of classification results. The model excels in capturing both global and local motion patterns, especially for complex action sequences. Experiments on the UCF101 and Kinetics-400 datasets show that ViT-ConvGAN achieves 87.3% and 95.2% Top-1 accuracy, respectively, with strong performance on Kinetics-400, surpassing several state-of-the-art models. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each module, particularly the critical role of ViT and 3D CNN in feature extraction. ViT-ConvGAN provides an efficient solution, improving complex action recognition performance and offering new insights for model architecture design in action analysis.

Introduction

As an important competitive form of traditional Chinese martial arts, the precision analysis of technical movements in Sanda–known as Chinese Kickboxing–which is a combat sport derived from traditional Chinese Wushu and integrates striking techniques including punches, kicks, elbows and knees, along with limited grappling and throwing techniques, and regulated by bodies such as the International Wushu Federation has always been one of the core research areas in sports science. With the rapid development of computer vision and artificial intelligence technologies, movement analysis techniques have evolved from traditional biomechanical measurements–such as those using force plates or motion capture suits–to intelligent analysis, providing new technological pathways for the scientific training of Sanda.

In the early stages of technological development, researchers primarily relied on biomechanical experimental equipment for movement analysis. Three-dimensional motion capture systems could accurately track joint movements using markers, while surface electromyography recorded muscle activation patterns1–3. These traditional methods laid a solid foundation for establishing biomechanical models of Sanda movements. With the widespread adoption of wearable devices, inertial measurement units (IMUs) have been increasingly used in training settings due to their convenience, enabling real-time collection of athletes’ movement data4–6. These traditional biomechanical measurement methods continue to play a crucial role in sports biomechanics research, particularly in high-precision data acquisition and athlete posture analysis, where they remain irreplaceable. However, advancements in computer vision technology have gradually replaced traditional sensor-dependent methods with markerless motion capture based on video analysis. This approach eliminates the interference of sensors with athletes and removes the need for attaching devices to the body, thereby enhancing training comfort and freedom of movement7. At the same time, the introduction of deep learning methods has significantly improved the accuracy of action recognition. Spatiotemporal convolutional networks (ST-CNNs), proposed in recent years, can automatically extract spatiotemporal features from video sequences and have achieved outstanding performance in various action recognition tasks8,9. Nevertheless, traditional ST-CNNs often face a trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency when dealing with long-term dependencies and rapidly changing complex movements, particularly in the analysis of intricate actions where capturing spatiotemporal features remains challenging10. Additionally, the high computational demands of deep learning methods, especially when processing high-resolution video data, can hinder real-time analysis and practical applications.

Recently, the Transformer architecture has achieved remarkable success in multiple domains, particularly in spatiotemporal feature modeling with Video Transformer (VideoViT). Transformers can efficiently capture long-range spatiotemporal dependencies in videos through self-attention mechanisms, demonstrating exceptional performance in analyzing complex movements like Sanda11–13. Unlike traditional convolutional neural networks, Transformers do not rely on local receptive fields and can more comprehensively understand the global context of long-term sequential data14,15. This has enabled Transformer-based models to significantly outperform traditional methods in action recognition tasks. However, ViT’s patch-based input processing tends to blur fine-grained local motion details–such as subtle joint rotations in Sanda punches or rapid footwork transitions–since its attention mechanism prioritizes global semantic coherence over local spatiotemporal neighborhoods where critical action cues reside.

Despite these advancements, existing technologies still face several challenges. Traditional methods, such as ST-CNNs and LSTMs, exhibit limitations when handling complex, fast-changing movements, particularly in extracting high spatiotemporal dependency features and modeling long-term actions. While Transformers excel at capturing long-term dependencies, their high computational costs, especially when processing large volumes of video data, may impact real-time performance and efficiency.More critically, Sanda action analysis faces two unique challenges that standard cross-entropy loss classifiers cannot address: 1) High similarity between confusing actions (e.g., “chongquan” (straight punch) and “guanquan” (thrust punch) differ only in subtle wrist angles and arm extension speeds), leading cross-entropy to misclassify them due to overlapping feature distributions; 2) Noisy training data (e.g., video blurriness from fast movement, incomplete action frames due to camera angle limitations), which causes cross-entropy to overfit to low-quality samples and fail to learn discriminative features for low-scoring or incomplete actions. Furthermore, although deep learning methods have made significant progress in improving action recognition accuracy, further optimization of low-scoring movement features and enhancing recognition precision remain pressing issues to address.

This study proposes ViT-ConvGAN, a spatiotemporal feature fusion model. It combines 3D convolutional networks (3D CNNs) and Video Transformers (VideoViT)–where ViT models long-range spatiotemporal dependencies for global action context, and 3D CNNs leverage volumetric convolution to refine the local motion details that ViT overlooks–to capture both spatial and temporal features of Sanda movements. The model keeps the benefits of markerless measurement and improves the accuracy of analyzing complex movements for Sanda training. It also uses generative adversarial networks (ConvGAN) to improve the accuracy of low-scoring movements: the CGAN’s generator optimizes feature distributions of confusing/noisy samples to reduce overlap between similar action classes, while the discriminator strengthens the distinction between high-quality and low-quality feature representations–addressing the core flaws of cross-entropy loss that only focuses on class probability rather than feature distribution regularization.

Related work

Spatiotemporal modeling methods for complex actions

Spatiotemporal modeling of complex actions is a key research area in computer vision. The main challenge is capturing the dynamic features of actions in both time and space. Early methods for action analysis used handcrafted feature descriptors like improved dense trajectory features (iDT) and spatiotemporal interest points (STIP)16,17. These methods worked well in some situations, but they often struggled to achieve good recognition accuracy with high-dynamic actions like sanda, which involve quick movements and complex joint coordination. With the rise of deep learning, CNNs have become the main approach for action recognition. The two-stream network architecture, which processes RGB frames and optical flow information separately, achieved breakthrough progress in action recognition tasks18–21. However, these methods still exhibit limitations in temporal modeling capabilities, making it difficult to effectively capture long-term temporal dependencies in actions.

The proposal of 3D convolutional neural networks (3D CNNs) provided a new solution for spatiotemporal feature extraction. By introducing convolutional operations along the temporal dimension, 3D CNNs can directly process video clips while extracting both spatial and temporal features22–25. The I3D network, by extending 2D convolutional kernels to 3D space, achieved state-of-the-art performance in multiple action benchmarks. Subsequent research further explored different 3D convolutional kernel designs26–29. For instance, the P3D network decomposed spatiotemporal convolution into spatial and temporal convolutions, significantly reducing computational complexity30. These methods laid foundations for sanda action analysis but still faced challenges in long-range temporal dependency modeling.

In recent years, the Transformer architecture has demonstrated unique advantages in video understanding. Unlike convolution-based methods, Vision Transformer (ViT) models global relationships among image patches through self-attention31–34. Extending to video domain, VideoViT achieved efficient spatiotemporal modeling with positional encoding and spatiotemporal attention. Variants such as TimeSformer decomposed spatial and temporal attention to improve efficiency for long videos. Recent advances include video transformer architectures with adaptive spatiotemporal attention, cross-frame token fusion, and window-based attention designs, which further enhance long-range modeling while reducing computation overhead35,36. Meanwhile, improved deep learning frameworks have also been explored. One study proposed YOLO-LSTM to combine detection and temporal modeling37, while another used attention-based DC-GRU for umpire signal recognition38. These methods support long-range joint modeling for sanda and offer new routes for complex action analysis. Recognizing the complementarity of 3D CNNs in local details and ViTs in global dependencies, recent studies proposed hybrid structures. Existing hybrids follow two paths: feature-level concatenation or layer-level embedding39–41. However, these designs still lack dynamic adaptation for fast and complex sanda movements. Most use static fusion and separate optimization, leading to semantic misalignment in transitional movements. Recent hybrid methods have attempted dynamic feature modulation and adaptive weight assignment, but they still lack an integrated mechanism with generative optimization for low-quality and similar actions42.

In martial arts analysis, methods combining 3D CNN, LSTM, and GCN have been applied for automatic assessment and motion pattern mining43–45. However, they lack accuracy for rapid and explosive sanda techniques, especially in combination action transitions46–49. Limitations include insufficient global modeling and weak joint coordination representation. These gaps motivate the spatiotemporal fusion architecture in this work.

Generative optimization techniques for actions

Action generation and optimization have advanced rapidly in computer vision and motion science. Early methods relied on motion retrieval and stitching, which lack diversity50,51. With deep learning, generative models became dominant52–54. VAEs encode motion into latent space for reconstruction but produce blurry results due to MSE loss55–57.

GANs significantly improve motion realism through adversarial training. In sports, conditional GANs (CGAN) have been used for motion refinement and recognition enhancement58,59. For martial arts, category-guided CGAN reduces similar action misclassification. In rehabilitation, pose-guided CGAN improves low-quality frame features60. In swimming, goal-conditioned CGAN generates motion suggestions61,62. However, traditional GANs focus on motion synthesis rather than discriminative feature learning, and few integrate ViT and 3D CNN for spatiotemporal fusion. Recent GAN-based methods introduce adaptive normalization, spatiotemporal discriminators, and contrastive learning to stabilize training, but they still do not combine quality-aware conditional guidance for action recognition enhancement.

Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful alternative for motion generation. They gradually denoise sequences to preserve fine-grained details and temporal coherence63. In gymnastics and dance generation, diffusion methods outperform conventional models in realism and diversity. Latest motion diffusion frameworks introduce temporal alignment, classifier guidance, and video-aware sampling to better support continuous action modeling64,65. Reinforcement learning has also been combined with generative models for optimized motion generation under physical constraints66,67. More recently, several works have attempted to unify diffusion models and action recognition, but they do not support targeted optimization for low-score or occluded samples as in the proposed method68.

Notably, existing methods still lack solutions for two key problems in sanda: high similarity among fine-grained actions and low-quality feature degradation. This motivates our conditional GAN module that uses fused spatiotemporal features and action quality scores to enhance discriminative representation and inter-class separation.

Methods

ViT-ConvGAN overall model architecture

The ViT-ConvGAN model proposed in this study, as shown in Fig. 1, consists of three core components: a spatiotemporal feature extraction module based on VideoViT and 3D CNN, an LSTM evaluation module, and a conditional generative adversarial optimization module, together forming a complete end-to-end feature-extraction action-evaluation feature-optimization classification processing pipeline. This hierarchical pipeline is designed to explicitly address the two core challenges in Sanda action recognition including high similarity between fine-grained actions and low-quality feature representation caused by motion blur occlusion and incomplete frames.

In the spatiotemporal feature extraction stage, ViT-ConvGAN adopts a dual-stream architecture to process input videos in parallel. The VideoViT branch models global action features through spatiotemporal attention mechanisms, with particular focus on long-range joint coordination relationships in martial arts movements. Through self-attention mechanisms, VideoViT can capture long-term spatial and temporal dependencies in videos, precisely identifying coordinated joint movements and overall dynamic changes in martial arts actions. The 3D CNN branch focuses on extracting local spatiotemporal patterns to capture rapidly changing motion details. By introducing temporal convolutional operations, 3D CNN can process both spatial and temporal features of videos, effectively recognizing rapid dynamics in martial arts movements within short time frames. The two branches operate in a complementary manner without redundant computation where VideoViT focuses on global semantic consistency of long sequences while 3D CNN retains fine-grained local clues that are easily blurred in patch-based attention. Features from both branches are concatenated along the channel dimension to form a multi-scale fused representation, ensuring the model can integrate information at different levels, thereby improving the accuracy of action recognition. The LSTM network processes temporal features and models the evolution of actions. Its memory cells capture long-term dependencies, making it suitable for complex action sequences with rapid transitions, like in martial arts. The final hidden state of the LSTM is turned into an accuracy score through a two-layer fully connected network. The first layer uses ReLU activation, and the second layer uses Sigmoid to normalize the output to the 0−1 range. This score acts as a conditional guidance signal to identify low-quality incomplete or easily confused action samples providing explicit optimization targets for the subsequent generative module instead of blind feature enhancement. The ConvGAN module optimizes features using evaluation results and original features as inputs. Under the guidance of the quality score the module performs targeted feature calibration rather than universal generation it enhances discriminative details for low-score samples and maintains semantic consistency for high-score samples. The generator improves low-scoring actions through deconvolution. The discriminator checks the realism of generated actions to ensure they meet training requirements. The discriminator further strengthens the inter-class distinction by distinguishing optimized features from real high-quality action features which effectively alleviates the misclassification caused by overlapping feature distributions of similar actions. The model is trained in three stages: pre-training the feature extraction and evaluation modules, training the generator, and fine-tuning the entire model. This staged training strategy ensures stable convergence of the adversarial module and avoids performance oscillation caused by joint training of multiple complex components.Fig. 1Overall Architecture of ViT-ConvGAN for Complex Action Recognition.

Temporal-spatial feature extraction

The temporal-spatial feature extraction phase is performed through a parallel processing mechanism using both VideoViT and 3D CNN. This dual-branch structure allows the model to extract both spatial and temporal features of the video data simultaneously, capturing both local action details and long-range dependencies. The complementary design eliminates the weakness of single structure that either lacks global modeling ability or loses fine-grained local clues. The output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_1$$\end{document} is passed through the second fully connected layer, resulting in the final action precision score S, which is mapped to a range between 0 and 1 using the Sigmoid activation function:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S = Sigmoid(FC_2(z_1)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FC_2$$\end{document} represents the second fully connected layer, and the Sigmoid function ensures that the output score S is in the range of [0, 1], representing the action’s precision. It helps the model distinguish low-quality samples and focus on key regions that need feature enhancement.

The ConvGAN module takes the action evaluation score S and the original features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{orig}$$\end{document} as input conditions and further optimizes the action features. This conditional optimization effectively reduces feature overlap between similar actions and enhances the representation of weak samples.

Conditional generative adversarial network optimization module

The ConvGAN module aims to optimize low-score action features based on the action evaluation result, thereby improving the precision of action recognition. This module performs conditional feature refinement to address the problems of high inter-class similarity and low-quality sample degradation in complex action recognition. This module uses a generative adversarial training mechanism, where the generator and discriminator play a minimax game: the discriminator learns to distinguish between real high-quality action features and generated optimized features, while the generator iteratively refines low-quality features to approximate real high-quality ones, ultimately enhancing the discriminative power of features for action classification. The generator takes the original features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{orig}$$\end{document} (fused from VideoViT and 3D CNN branches) and the action evaluation score S (output by the LSTM evaluation module, ranging from 0 to 1) as conditional inputs specifically, S is first embedded into a high-dimensional vector via a fully connected layer (with ReLU activation) to match the channel dimension of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{orig}$$\end{document}, then concatenated with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{orig}$$\end{document} along the channel axis before being fed into the generator and generates the optimized features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{opt}$$\end{document}. This process is implemented through an encoder-decoder structure comprising 3 encoder layers (each with a 1x1 3D convolution, ReLU activation, and batch normalization) for feature compression and 3 decoder layers (each with a transposed 3D convolution, Tanh activation, and batch normalization) for feature reconstruction, ensuring the output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{opt}$$\end{document} has the same spatial-temporal dimension as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{orig}$$\end{document}:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{opt} = G(F_{orig}, S) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where G represents the generator, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{orig} \in \mathbb {R}^{T \times H'' \times W'' \times C''}$$\end{document} (temporal length T, spatial dimensions H'' × W'', channel count C'') is the original fused action features, S is the action precision score, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{opt} \in \mathbb {R}^{T \times H'' \times W'' \times C''}$$\end{document} is the optimized action features. The generator utilizes the evaluation score S to guide the optimization process: for low-score samples (e.g., S < 0.6), the generator prioritizes correcting distorted local motion features (e.g., blurred joint trajectories in Sanda actions) by enhancing the weight of fine-grained feature channels, while for medium-score samples (0.6 łe S < 0.8), it focuses on aligning global action semantics with high-score patterns, ensuring \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{opt}$$\end{document} more closely resembles the true action features. The conditional guidance mechanism ensures targeted optimization instead of universal feature modification.

The discriminator compares the generated features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{opt}$$\end{document} with the real high-score action features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{real}$$\end{document} (selected from the training set as samples with S \ge 0.8 and manually verified for action authenticity, ensuring they represent high-quality action patterns) and outputs the probability of the input features being real. Expertment

Experimental data

Two widely used datasets for action recognition tasks are employed: UCF101 and Kinetics-400.

The UCF101 dataset contains 101 action categories, with a total of 13,320 video samples, encompassing a variety of sports and daily activities. The length of each video clip varies from a few seconds to several dozen seconds, with a resolution of 320×240. This dataset is widely used in action recognition research; its diverse action types and complex backgrounds make it effective for evaluating a model’s adaptability across different scenarios. For data partitioning, we adopted a stratified random split (to maintain consistent class distribution between training and validation sets) with an 8:2 ratio: 10,656 clips for training and 2,664 clips for validation.

The Kinetics-400 dataset includes 400 action categories and over 300,000 video samples, with a resolution of 256×256 and a standard duration of 10 seconds per video. The number of samples per category is relatively balanced, covering action types ranging from sports to daily activities. Due to its large sample size and diverse, complex categories, Kinetics-400 is a challenging benchmark well-suited for large-scale action recognition model training. We directly used the dataset’s official partition (consistent with mainstream evaluation norms in the field): 240,000 clips for training and 20,000 clips for validation, avoiding discrepancies caused by custom splits.

Data preprocessing

To standardize model input and enhance generalization: Temporal segmentation: All videos (from both datasets) were resampled into fixed-length segments of 10 consecutive frames, ensuring uniform temporal input for the model; Spatial normalization: Frames were first resized to 256×256 pixels, then cropped to 224×224 pixels (random cropping for training, center cropping for validation) to align with the model’s input size; Augmentation: Horizontal flipping (probability=0.5) and brightness/contrast adjustment (±15%) were applied to training data; Noise reduction & normalization: Gaussian blur (3×3 kernel) was used to suppress background noise, and pixel values were normalized to the [0,1] range to minimize irrelevant interference.

Evaluation metrics

This study uses Top-1 accuracy and Top-5 accuracy as core evaluation indicators which are consistent with the official benchmark evaluation standards for the UCF101 and Kinetics-400 action recognition datasets. Top-1 accuracy measures the proportion of times the highest-confidence predicted category is correct, directly reflecting the model’s ability to make the optimal judgment on action categories. On the other hand, Top-5 accuracy evaluates the proportion of times the correct category appears in the top five predicted results, which is particularly useful for handling the recognition of composite techniques commonly found in martial arts actions. To comprehensively evaluate the model’s performance, the experiment also calculates the number of parameters (Params) and the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) to assess computational efficiency. Additionally, the single inference time (Inference Time) is used to evaluate the model’s real-time processing capability.

To compare the performance of the ViT-ConvGAN model, we selected several representative pose estimation and action recognition methods as comparison models. Each method proposes innovative solutions in its specific field. Below is a brief introduction to each of the comparison models:

HANet69 This method improves 2D and 3D pose estimation accuracy by utilizing kinematic features of keypoints (such as velocity and acceleration), combined with a hierarchical transformer encoder that captures spatiotemporal dependencies.

SLT-Pose70 This method adopts a personalized feature extraction module, a cross-frame temporal learning module, and others to address the challenges of rapid motion and occlusion. It performs better than existing action recognition methods on several benchmark datasets.

RTMPose71 This framework implements real-time multi-person pose estimation based on MMPose. By optimizing the architecture and training strategies, it successfully achieves high accuracy and low-latency real-time processing and supports deployment on mobile devices.

RTMO72 RTMO introduces a one-stage pose estimation method that uses the YOLO architecture and dual 1-D heatmaps to represent keypoints, providing high real-time performance and accuracy.

GTA-Net73 For posture correction in adolescent sports, GTA-Net combines Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN), Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), and hierarchical attention mechanisms to provide real-time feedback while maintaining high accuracy in complex scenarios.

ThermoHands74 ThermoHands focuses on 3D hand pose estimation using thermal imaging, utilizing the TherFormer model to address the limitations of RGB and NIR methods in complex environments, showcasing the advantages of thermal imaging in challenging conditions.

FoundPose75 FoundPose provides a model-based method for 6D object pose estimation from a single RGB image. By using the DINOv2 model, it establishes 2D-3D correspondences to quickly and accurately estimate object poses without requiring task-specific training.

M2-CLIP76 A AAAI multimodal action recognition model that balances supervised performance and transferability via visual/text adapters and a multi-task decoder.

STAM77 An action recognition model optimizing static convolution via a spatio-temporal adaptive module, enhancing long-time series and multi-scale modeling.

SITAR78 A semi-supervised action recognition method using super-image reconstruction and 2D transformers to reduce annotation costs.

Discussion

The ViT-ConvGAN model proposed in this study captures global-local spatiotemporal features through a dual-branch architecture of Video Transformer (ViT) and 3D Convolutional Neural Network (3D CNN), and optimizes low-quality action recognition by integrating Conditional Generative Adversarial Network (CGAN). It has demonstrated advantages in complex action recognition on the UCF101 dataset (Top-1 accuracy of 87.3%) and Kinetics-400 dataset (Top-1 accuracy of 83.7%). Particularly in scenarios such as Sanda’s “kick-turn-punch” combination actions and occluded scenes (e.g., 84.5% accuracy in “close-quarter throw” actions), the effectiveness of its feature fusion strategy has been verified. However, from the perspectives of practical application and academic exploration, three core limitations of the model need to be addressed, and these limitations are closely related to the model’s existing design and performance.

First, the robustness of the model in extreme scenarios remains insufficient. Although the model outperforms specialized optimized models such as TherFormer in conventional occluded scenes, when facing scenarios like overlapping limbs of multiple athletes, strong backlighting, or low illumination, the local feature extraction of 3D CNN is easily disturbed by background noise and light distortion, leading to the loss of subtle joint movement trajectories (e.g., wrist rotation during Sanda punches). Meanwhile, for inter-frame blurred samples caused by high-speed movement, although the spatiotemporal attention of ViT can capture global sequence correlations, it is difficult to effectively distinguish between motion blur and the dynamic features of the actions themselves, resulting in a misjudgment rate of approximately 5%−8% for fast-transition actions. This issue arises because the existing dual-branch architecture lacks an adaptive feature filtering mechanism designed for dynamic interference.

Second, the model exhibits insufficient generalization ability across martial arts styles. Currently, the model performs stably in the recognition of Sanda and common sports actions, but its recognition accuracy for specific actions of other styles (such as Muay Thai knee strikes and Taekwondo roundhouse kicks) decreases by approximately 10%−12%. This limitation essentially stems from the adaptability issue between the model’s feature extraction logic and the biomechanical characteristics of different martial arts actions. The existing ViT-3D CNN fusion strategy does not account for differences in force generation modes (e.g., Sanda focuses on fist-foot combinations, while Muay Thai emphasizes knee-elbow strikes) and joint movement ranges among different styles, making it difficult for the model to form effective representations of the unique spatiotemporal features of cross-style actions.

Third, there is a gap between the model’s lightweight level and the deployment requirements of mobile terminals. Although the model’s inference latency (21.5 ms on UCF101 and 25.3 ms on Kinetics-400) meets real-time analysis needs, its GPU memory usage of approximately 3.9 GB is significantly higher than that of lightweight models such as RTMPose. This makes it incompatible with resource-constrained terminals such as tablets and portable analysis devices used in on-site Sanda training, limiting the transition of the model from laboratory verification to practical training scenarios. This contradiction arises because the attention computation of ViT and the adversarial training of CGAN in the existing architecture still have certain computational redundancy.

To address the above limitations, future research can be carried out in four aspects to further enhance the academic value and application potential of the model. First, a scene-aware dynamic attention mechanism should be introduced to adaptively adjust the feature contribution weights of ViT and 3D CNN by real-time analyzing the occlusion level, light intensity, and movement speed of the input video. In high-interference scenarios, the local feature filtering capability of 3D CNN should be enhanced, and a motion blur suppression algorithm should be integrated to separate noise from effective action features, thereby specifically solving the problem of insufficient robustness in extreme scenarios. Second, transfer learning and action dynamics encoding should be combined. An auxiliary training set should be constructed using a small number of action samples from styles such as Muay Thai and Taekwondo, and meta-learning should be employed to enable the model to quickly adapt to the biomechanical features of different styles. Meanwhile, a dynamic feature embedding module should be designed to convert information such as force generation modes and joint movement ranges into learnable features, which are then fused with spatiotemporal features to improve generalization ability across styles. Third, lightweight transformation should be implemented based on knowledge distillation and quantization pruning. Taking ViT-ConvGAN as the teacher model, a lightweight 3D CNN improved based on MobileNet and a simplified ViT should be trained as student models. The parameter precision should be reduced from 32-bit to 16-bit or 8-bit, and the memory usage should be controlled within 1 GB while retaining core recognition performance to meet the deployment requirements of mobile terminals. Fourth, an end-to-end auxiliary system for Sanda training should be developed, integrating functions of action recognition, precision scoring, and defect correction. Based on local features extracted by the model (such as joint angles and punch trajectories), problems such as “wrist angle deviation in straight punches” and “insufficient kicking speed” should be identified, and personalized training suggestions should be generated in combination with sports biomechanical principles. This will promote the transformation of the model from an action recognition technology to a practical training guidance tool, providing more comprehensive technical support for the scientific training of martial arts projects.
