Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

Continued verification, validation, and improvement of Distant Observer software for quality
control of newly installed parabolic troughs. Besides specific trough adjustments, the potential
for improvements to the software speed, user interface, and photogrammetry algorithms will be
improved.
CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:
 • Assists laboratory in achieving programmatic scope
 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies
 • Enhances U.S. competitiveness by utilizing DOE developed intellectual property and/or
 capabilities.

 1

Purpose:
Collaboration between NREL and Solar Dynamics on parabolic trough metrology is essential to
ensure software development in the laboratory can benefit the concentrated solar industry. Solar
Dynamics is at the forefront of parabolic trough technologies and plans to deploy several projects
in the next few years. The parabolic trough industry has an immediate need for fast quality
assurance of newly assembled troughs in the field. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s
(NREL) Distant Observer software (DO) is currently being adapted to fill this need. This
continued collaboration ensures that DO results in specific adjustments useful to Solar
Dynamics’ SunBeam trough, an interface that will allow it to be operated by a trough assembly
team, and other improvements that will be most valuable for commercial use.
Summary of Research Results:
Distant Observer (DO) is a metrology software used to measure the shape of both the absorber
tube and mirror surface of parabolic trough modules used in concentrated solar thermal (CST)
fields. DO was first developed by NREL in 2011 [1], [2], though similar work began several
years earlier [3]. More recently, DO was adapted for aerial measurement using unmanned aerial
systems (UAS), specifically small, inexpensive consumer drones with integrated high-resolution
cameras. It was also adapted with advanced machine learning and computer vision capabilities
for larger scale processing at Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) plants and was used to measure
over 100 parabolic trough modules at an operating plant [4].
Solar Dynamics is a leading developer of next-generation parabolic trough models, for solar
thermal heat and power. Their current SunBeam trough prototype module is located at their
SolarTAC facility and is shown in Figure 1 below.

 Figure 1: The SunBeam trough at SolarTAC.

 2

NREL and Solar Dynamics have partnered on the DO software development to bring this
laboratory-developed metrology technology into industry to benefit new and existing CST plants.
Solar Dynamics has been working on developing various small-scale parabolic trough CST
projects for industrial process heat. In these installations, traditional warehouse quality assurance
and quality control (QAQC) techniques are cost inhibitive and too time consuming. Solar
Dynamics plans to assemble SunBeam trough modules directly in the field. For this to work, it is
essential to have a way to measure the troughs as they’re being installed, ensure the optics meet
the requirements for good plant performance, and make fine-tuning adjustments if necessary.
While DO can measure trough modules in the field, before this project there had not been any
attempts to turn these measurements into actionable adjustments plant technicians could make.
The three tasks below are designed to demonstrate this process and make the necessary software
improvements to DO for it to be used effectively as a QAQC tool with high confidence and
relatively little training.
Since the start of the CRADA project, the work performed was adjusted to meet Solar
Dynamics’ measurement needs. The most important effort was making sure DO measurements
were consistent, that adjustments to the trough could be measured with DO, and measuring
changes in trough optics based on residual stresses; shape change when rotated from west to
zenith and east to zenith.
Task 1: Real-world application and further validation
Further verify the DO Quality Control (QC) system’s adjustment recommendations through real-
world inspection and correction cycles (measure, report errors and suggest corrections, adjust,
remeasure) using a variety of precisely calibrated physical adjustments to the module.
The deliverable for this task was to report on SunBeam adjustment and correction cycle,
including example DO case and output adjustment report.
A series of Distant Observer (DO) measurements were collected on the novel SunBeam
parabolic trough demonstration module, located at Solar Dynamics’ SolarTAC facility. These
were collected by Solar Dynamics, using their in-house UAS. NREL assisted in completing the
necessary procedure for camera lens calibration. Data was collected on five occasions over the 1-
year project, with a total of 23 DO measurements analyzed. A memo outlining the results like the
one shown in Figure 2 was delivered to Solar Dynamics.

Developing this report involved discussions with Solar Dynamics about how troughs would be
adjusted during field commissioning. First, the absorber is adjusted to correct any misalignment.

Task 2: Software improvement
Investigate potential improvements to the DO software’s underlying algorithms, to improve
measurement accuracy and/or reduce the sensitivity to real-world environmental effects. Improve
the software interface for commercial pilot testing, for use in large-scale projects.
The deliverable for this work was a report detailing improvements to the software, and an
updated software user manual. In general, these improvements are focused on making the
software generate additional outputs and reports to make it more useful for different
measurement applications.
The main portion of this work was also the DO validation campaign described under the section
for Task 3, and include Lessons Learned have been incorporated into the DO software manual
and future measurement procedures. In addition to the report memos, DO software was improved
to generate an automated output report as shown in Figure 5. For this to work, a new
TroughModel.csv file needs to be provided to DO, with facet dimensions and mounting point
positions and absorber support pylon locations so the software can determine what adjustments
to make at each support pylon or facet mounting point.

Task 3: Additional Tasks
Contractor will perform other work at the direction of the Participant, consistent with the scope
and subject to the availability of funding.
As we began collecting data, a major new effort began to validate DO in a way that would allow
Solar Dynamics to confidently use it to measure and adjust troughs on new commercial projects.
This effort led to the work in the sections below, first to validate DO by alternative measurement
methods, and second, to improve our DO procedure and requirements, addressing some issues
that were leading to inconsistent DO results.
 1. DO Validation and Alternative Verification Methods: Initial DO results collected as part
 of this CRADA award showed a dramatic change to the trough’s receiver position,
 compared to previous measurement. This seemed to have occurred after an adjustment
 but necessitated careful analysis to understand this change before proceeding. Several
 secondary measurement methods are used to check the absorber position, including
 photogrammetry studies by both NREL and SD, and a physical test with wires to measure
 absorber movement during trough rotation.
 2. UAS and Camera Challenges and Lessons Learned: DO requires highly precise
 measurement of camera focal length, active sensor area, and camera lens distortion
 parameters. Over the course of these measurements, we diagnosed several issues related
 to camera quality and error. Some main lessons learned are listed below.
 A. Certain UAS cameras generated much lower uncertainty results. Specifically,
 Solar Dynamics Air2S performed much more consistently than their Matrice
 UAS. It is still not entirely clear why this is. One potential hypothesis is that the
 longer focal length of the Matrice resulted in less accurate camera calibrations.
 This would be because the UAS needs to be further from the targets to
 accommodate the more zoomed image, resulting in calibration images at lower
 angles. It could also be because of some “focus breathing” effects present in the
 Matrice’s longer focal lengths. Basically, the camera’s focal length changes with
 focusing distance, leading to a different focal length when focusing on calibration
 targets (closer), and the trough during measurement (further). There could also be
 some onboard image processing software present in this UAS we are unable to
 account for. We would like to perform further testing to investigate this, but for
 now it will primarily measure with the Air2S.
 B. Improved laboratory camera calibration procedure using more images.
 C. Better guidelines for when to collect data to avoid glare obscuring part of the
 reflection. Cloudy conditions, of towards the beginning or end of the day are best.
