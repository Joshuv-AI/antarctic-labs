Executive Summary
Bidirectional charging and Vehicle-to-Home (V2H) technologies enable the energy stored in a
plug-in hybrid electric vehicle’s (PHEV) or battery electric vehicle’s (BEV) onboard battery to
supply residential loads during grid outages and support broader energy management objectives.
Conventional backup solutions, such as Uninterruptible Power Supplies (UPS) and standalone
inverter systems, are limited in capacity and scalability, whereas PHEV/BEV-based storage offers
substantially greater energy reserves that can sustain household loads for extended periods.

V2H systems are still in the early stages of commercialization in the North American market, with
only a handful of offerings available from automotive OEMs. Three currently commercially
available backup systems are the Tesla Powershare Home Backup System, the GM Energy Home
System, and the Ford Intelligent Home Backup System. Understanding the real-world performance
of these systems, beyond what is disclosed in manufacturer specifications, is of direct relevance to
homeowners seeking to enhance the reliability and continuity of their residential power supply.

A structured performance characterization study of these three proprietary V2H systems was
conducted under the “V2X Connect” project, sponsored by the U.S. Department of Energy's
Transportation Technologies Office. The evaluation encompassed common residential backup use
cases, power backup and grid restoration latency measurements, maximum power output
characterization, and inverter efficiency assessment. The systems under evaluation include both
AC and DC V2H configurations: The Tesla Powershare, wherein the Cybertruck's onboard
inverter performs DC-AC power conversion within the vehicle, and the GM Energy and Ford
Home Backup systems, wherein DC power drawn from the vehicle battery is converted to AC by
an external inverter housed within the home backup unit. Some key performance findings for each
system are summarized in the table below:

Across all three systems, several common limitations were identified. Each solution features a
tightly integrated, proprietary architecture designed exclusively for specific vehicle models from
a single OEM, with no interoperability across platforms. Additional hardware requirements,
installation complexity, and (in some cases) the need for OEM-certified installers contribute to
considerable system costs and physical footprint, limiting economic attractiveness for typical
residential customers.

Apart from these limitations, the evaluated V2H systems demonstrated meaningful backup
capability and represented a significant step toward the broader integration of battery-powered
vehicles into residential energy infrastructure. Continued development toward standardized,
interoperable, platform-agnostic architectures and improved transfer response times will be critical
to realizing the full potential of V2H technology as a resilience resource for residential customers.

1 Vehicle-to-Home (V2H) Systems
Vehicle-to-Home (V2H) technology represents a promising extension of bidirectional charging
capabilities, enabling the energy stored in a plug-in hybrid electric vehicle’s (PHEV) or battery
electric vehicle’s (BEV) onboard battery energy storage system (BESS) to serve residential loads
during grid outages. As onboard battery capacities continue to grow, the prospect of leveraging
these mobile energy reserves for stationary backup applications has attracted interest from
researchers and industry.

1.1 V2H Capabilities
The need for V2H systems is driven by growing concerns over grid supply reliability, rising
residential energy demand, and the increasing penetration of distributed energy resources such as
rooftop solar systems. Key to enabling V2H operation is recognizing that personal vehicles spend
a majority of the time parked, leaving large, underutilized energy reserves connected to the home.

By integrating dedicated power electronics, bidirectional power transfer hardware, and control
interfaces, energy can flow from the vehicle to residential electrical installations in a controlled
manner, effectively transforming the vehicle into a mobile backup power source. Conventional
backup solutions, including uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) and inverter-based systems,
have traditionally served this role, but are often constrained by limited energy storage capacity,
poor scalability, and restricted operational flexibility. PHEVs/BEVs offer substantially higher
energy reserves capable of sustaining household loads for extended durations, presenting a
compelling alternative to dedicated stationary storage.

Beyond outage support, V2H systems may provide additional value through peak demand
reduction, load shifting, and improved alignment between local consumption and renewable
generation, making them a versatile solution for enhancing both energy resilience and efficiency
at the residential level.

1.2 Existing V2H Technologies
V2H is an application within the broader vehicle-to-everything (V2X) framework. V2X
encompasses several modes of bidirectional energy exchange. Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) technology
involves energy transfer from the vehicle to the utility grid, enabling its participation in grid
services such as frequency regulation, demand response, and peak shaving at the electrical network
level. V2H, by contrast, operates at the home or building level where energy is discharged from
the vehicle directly to on-site residential loads, typically behind the utility meter and in isolation
from the grid during outage conditions.

The broader V2X landscape has seen meaningful progress in standardization, particularly for V2G
applications. Established protocols such as ISO 15118-20, which governs vehicle-to-charger

 1

communication, and SAE J2847/2, which defines requirements for communication between plug-
in vehicles and the off-board DC chargers, have provided a foundation for interoperable V2G
implementations. Grid interconnection requirements are further addressed through standards such
as IEEE 1547 and UL 1741. However, equivalent standardization efforts for V2H applications
remain considerably less mature. The absence of a unified communication protocol,
interconnection standard, or certification framework specific to V2H has left OEMs without a
common technical baseline to adhere to.

In the North American market, available V2H solutions are limited in number, and those that do
exist are primarily offered by automotive OEMs. These platforms are often tightly integrated
proprietary architectures that pair specific vehicle make/models with a single bidirectional charger
that requires supplemental hardware such as power inverters, automatic power transfer switches,
energy management units, dark-start batteries, etc., that are often accompanied by a proprietary
mobile or web-based user interface for power flow monitoring and system control.

Consequently, the unique implementations of current commercial V2H offerings exhibit a
significant lack of interoperability. This fragmentation restricts broader V2H adoption and
complicates efforts toward standardized, platform-agnostic V2H deployment.

1.3 Need for Performance Characterization
Given the novelty and limited commercial deployment of V2H systems, there is an opportunity
for systematic performance characterization to better understand their operational behavior,
practical constraints, and real-world applicability. Current understanding is largely derived from
manufacturer specifications and promotional disclosures, which may not accurately reflect system
performance under realistic operating conditions. For residential users seeking to enhance home
power supply reliability through V2H adoption, a rigorous and objective understanding of system
availability, functionality, operational boundaries, and actual performance characteristics is
essential.

To address this gap, a structured performance characterization study of commercially available
V2H systems has been conducted under the “V2X Connect” project, sponsored by the U.S.
Department of Energy's Transportation Technologies Office. The evaluation encompasses
common residential backup scenarios as well as edge cases such as extended outage conditions,
power backup and grid restoration latency measurements, maximum power output
characterization, and inverter efficiency assessment.

The objective of this study is to provide an unbiased and comprehensive assessment of the V2H
system architecture, integration, and operational characteristics. The findings are intended to
support informed decision-making for end users, while also identifying key limitations and
opportunities for improvement, thereby contributing to the broader advancement and adoption of
V2H technology.

1.4 Typical V2H System Operation
V2H systems can be broadly classified into two configurations based on the location of the power
conversion stage, as shown in Figure 1. In AC V2H systems, the vehicle is equipped with an
onboard inverter that converts the DC battery output to AC power within the vehicle, which is then
supplied to residential loads. In DC V2H systems, the power conversion is performed outside the
vehicle. DC power is drawn from the vehicle battery and converted to AC by an inverter housed
within the V2H bidirectional charging equipment. The use of external power conversion hardware
in DC V2H systems introduces additional component and installation costs; however, it also offers
greater flexibility in inverter design and can yield improved conversion efficiency. The V2H
systems evaluated in this report are both DC V2H and AC V2H.

 Figure 1. AC V2H and DC V2H

As illustrated in Figure 2, V2H hardware is typically installed between the utility grid, vehicle
charging inlet, and the home electrical loads. Under normal grid-connected conditions, the V2H
system facilitates vehicle charging while simultaneously allowing the grid to supply residential
electrical loads.

In the event of a grid outage, as shown in Figure 3, the system disconnects from the utility grid
and transitions to backup operation. This function is typically performed by an integrated power
transfer switch. The inverter, either onboard the vehicle or housed externally in the home backup
unit, converts DC energy from the vehicle battery into a regulated single-phase 240VAC, 60Hz
supply for residential loads, consistent with standard North American residential electrical
requirements.

At the moment of a grid outage, most V2H systems experience a brief blackout during which keep-
alive circuitry maintains communication between the vehicle and charger. Powered by an auxiliary
dark-start battery or a 12V signal tapped from the vehicle, this circuit facilitates the initial
communication handshakes required to engage backup power and regulates ongoing power
delivery for the duration of the outage.

2 Tesla Powershare Home Backup System (AC V2H)
Tesla's Powershare Home Backup System is a commercially available V2H solution developed
for the Tesla Cybertruck. It enables energy stored in the vehicle's battery to power residential loads
during grid outages, delivering up to 11.5kW of continuous AC power to the home. The bundle
comprises a Tesla Universal Wall Connector (EVSE) and a Powershare Gateway (V2H system
hardware), providing a complete, integrated home backup solution.[1]

2.1 Performance Testing
2.1.1 Test Bench

To characterize the performance of the Tesla Powershare Home Backup system, a test bench
assembly was built at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), adhering to Tesla installation
guidance, technician build support, and lab-conducted safety inspection guidelines. Figure 4
presents the line diagram of the test bench installed and evaluated at ANL.

The grid supply was routed through a Schneider Electric 200A double-pole single-throw (DPST)
disconnect switch, protected by 200A fuses on lines L1 and L2, before entering the Tesla
Powershare unit. On the input terminals of the Powershare unit, an Eaton CSR2200N main breaker
provided overcurrent and short-circuit protection. On the load side, the Powershare load-side
busbar was equipped with a 125A circuit breaker serving the home loads and a dedicated 60A
breaker for the Tesla Wall Connector. The home load supply was further distributed through a load
subpanel, where it was divided across two 60A, 2-pole sub-breakers, each terminating at a
NEMA14-60 240V outlet. MeasureLogic power meters were installed to record electrical
parameters on the grid input, vehicle charging, and home loads.

2.1.2 Mobile App-based User Interface to Powershare Unit

Upon installation, commissioning using a custom tool developed exclusively for Tesla-certified
installers, known as the “Tesla One” app, was required. The commissioning process was found to
be time-intensive and procedurally involved; however, for a typical residential customer, this step
would be completed before handover from a Tesla-approved installer and would not represent a
difficult user experience.

Following commissioning, the Powershare unit becomes accessible through the standard Tesla
Mobile app, the platform used by Tesla owners to monitor and control their products. The app
provides three primary functions for the Powershare unit: enrolling the system in Powershare grid
support programs, control features such as software-triggered grid disconnections, and energy
flow/consumption data metrics monitoring, as shown in Figure 6.

The Tesla Mobile app presents system status and power flows through a graphical interface that is
visually clear and effective for basic monitoring, conveying real-time power flow and system state
in an accessible manner. A notable shortfall observed during testing relates to connectivity. The
app communicates with the Powershare unit over the unit's dedicated Wi-Fi network, and
intermittent connection losses were encountered on multiple occasions, requiring manual
reconnection to restore app functionality.

2.1.3 Performance Characterization Tests and Test Results

The following section presents the performance characterization results from the tests conducted
on the Tesla Powershare Home Backup system. All tests were carried out on the previously
described test bench, using a 2024 Tesla Cybertruck as the test vehicle.

Grid to Home Only: Verified the Powershare unit operated without a connected vehicle and could
supply residential loads entirely from the grid. This test confirmed that the Powershare unit reliably
passed grid power through to home loads under normal grid-available conditions.

Grid to Vehicle Charging Only (V1G): Verified the Powershare unit's ability to charge the
Cybertruck from the grid in the absence of any home loads, confirming normal vehicle charging
functionality through the unit.

 7

Grid to Vehicle Charging + Home Loads: Evaluated simultaneous vehicle charging and home
load supply under grid-available conditions. The Cybertruck was charged at 240VAC, 48A (11.5
kW), while home loads consisted of a 120VAC, 40A (4.8kW) resistive load and a 240VAC, 48A
(11.5kW) separate vehicle charging load. The results confirm that the Powershare unit is capable
of concurrently managing both single-phase 120VAC and split-phase 240VAC loads on the home
side.

V2H Short-Duration Backup during Physical Grid Outage: Evaluated the backup power
capability of the Powershare unit under a simulated grid outage condition. Before the outage, the

V2H Backup Overload Response during Physical Grid Outage: This test examined the
behavior of the Powershare system when home load demand exceeds the Powershare unit's backup
power capacity during an actual physical grid outage. The Tesla Powershare unit is rated for a
maximum backup output of 240VAC, 48A (11.5 kW). When the home load demand was
intentionally driven beyond this threshold during V2H backup operation, the Powershare unit
detected the overload condition and shut down backup power output entirely. Upon load reduction
to within the rated backup capacity, the unit restored power to the home loads. The Powershare
system was observed to periodically poll the load demand during the overload condition to
determine whether the load had returned to within acceptable limits before attempting restoration,
as illustrated in Figure 20.

V2H Backup Overload Response during App-Triggered Outage: This test replicated the
overload scenario described previously, but under a software-triggered grid outage condition
initiated via the Tesla app. When home load demand exceeded the 11.5 kW backup capacity
threshold, the Powershare unit's response differed notably from the physical outage case. Rather
than shutting down backup output, the unit detected the existence of an app-triggered outage and
an available grid supply. It autonomously restored grid power, as seen in Figure 21, to serve the
home loads, without any user intervention or confirmation. This behavior highlights an important
distinction in the system's overload handling logic, depending on the nature of the outage
condition.

V2H Backup with Non-Compliant Vehicle: This test examined the Powershare system's
response when a Tesla vehicle other than the Tesla Cybertruck was connected and charging at the
time of a grid outage. Upon loss of grid power, the Powershare unit lost output entirely, and no

3 GM Energy V2H Home System (DC V2H)
The GM Energy V2H Home system delivers backup power capabilities with a range of GM BEVs.
The system, as shown in Figure 23, comprises a GM Powershift Charger for vehicle connectivity,
a DC-to-AC power inverter for energy transfer, and a dark-start battery to sustain communication
and control of the system during a grid outage. An optional GM Energy PowerBank is available
to extend backup duration. The GM Energy PowerBank operates at a nominal voltage of 150VDC
and incorporates an internal DC boost converter to step up its output to 400VDC at the inverter
input, matching the voltage level of the vehicle battery.[3]

The Powershift Charger has a CCS Type 1 handle that utilizes J1772 pins during charging for AC
Level 2, and uses DC pins during discharging for V2H application. The J1772 AC Level 2 charging
has a maximum current capacity of 80A, supporting a maximum charging power of 19.2kW, while
DC V2H has a maximum discharge capacity of 9.6kW as limited by the DC-to-AC inverter. Under
normal grid-connected conditions, the Home Hub closes its isolation contactor, connecting the grid
to supply home loads and charge the vehicle through the Powershift Charger. If a GM Energy
PowerBank is present, it is also charged during this period in preparation for potential grid outage
conditions.

Upon loss of grid power, the Home Hub opens its isolation contactors, disconnecting the system
from the utility grid. The bidirectional inverter BDI, rated for 9.6kW, then operates in grid-forming
mode, providing a regulated 240VAC, 60Hz supply for critical home loads. Power during this
mode is sourced either from the onboard vehicle battery via DC reverse power transfer (V2H
operation) or from the GM Energy PowerBank, which has a rated storage capacity of 10.6 kWh
and can contribute up to 6kW to the backup supply.

Each system demonstrated a maximum backup power output of 9.6kW, sufficient to support
essential residential loads. Tesla Powershare exhibited the fastest backup transfer delay of
approximately 3 seconds, benefiting from its AC V2H architecture, while the GM Energy Home
and Ford Home Backup systems, which rely on DC V2H configurations, recorded longer transfer
delays of approximately 35 seconds and 60 seconds, respectively. Inverter efficiency was strong
across both DC V2H systems, with GM Energy Home exceeding 95% above 2kW and Ford Home
Backup achieving a peak efficiency of 94.5% at 6.5kW. The Ford system was the only platform to
support a grid-following mode via its Intelligent Power Transfer function, enabling continuous
load offset during desirable predetermined times of the day.

 40

Across all three systems, several common limitations were identified. Each solution is built on a
tightly integrated, proprietary architecture designed exclusively for specific vehicle make/models
from a single OEM, with no cross-platform interoperability. Additional hardware requirements,
installation complexity, and, in some cases, the need for OEM-certified installers contribute to
considerable system costs and physical footprint, limiting economic attractiveness for typical
residential customers. Overload handling also remains an area of concern, with two of the three
systems exhibiting voltage sag and instability rather than controlled shutdown under excess load
conditions.

Despite these limitations, the evaluated V2H systems represent a meaningful step toward the
broader integration of battery-powered vehicles into residential energy infrastructure,
demonstrating viable backup capability and increasing feature sophistication. Continued
development toward standardized, platform-agnostic, interoperable architectures and improved
transfer response times will be critical to realizing the full potential of V2H technology as a
resilience resource for residential customers, alongside the advancement of relevant V2H
standards and protocols.

Building on the findings of this evaluation, the following next steps are recommended to advance
the understanding and development of residential V2H technology:
 - Inverter efficiency characterization for Tesla Powershare: Conduct dedicated
 efficiency measurements across the full backup output range for the Tesla Powershare
 system to enable consistent cross-platform performance comparison.
 - Vehicle-side battery data recording: Instrument and record vehicle-side battery
 parameters, including SOC, cell voltage, temperature, and estimated degradation, during
 V2H operation across all platforms to assess long-term battery health implications.
 - Overload protection improvement assessment: Investigate whether controlled
 shutdown mechanisms can be implemented to replace the observed voltage sag and
 instability response under overload conditions.
 - Transfer delay reduction for DC V2H systems: Explore strategies to reduce backup
 transfer delays, including optimization of communication session negotiation protocols
 (e.g., DIN 70121:2012) and vehicle pre-conditioning approaches.
 - Non-OEM and cross-platform interoperability testing: Evaluate V2H system
 performance with non-native, V2H-compatible vehicles to assess interoperability and
 consistency of behavior across different vehicle platforms.
 - V2H standards and regulatory landscape development: Monitor and engage with
 ongoing development of V2H standards and grid interconnection requirements to identify
 implications for system design, certification, and broader residential deployment.
