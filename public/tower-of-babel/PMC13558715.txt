A multi-model carbon estimation framework for new urban district planning-integrating land use, transportation, and investment-based emission models
Authors: Xiao Teng, Zhenjiang Shen, Xixi Wei, Jinming Jiang, Feilan Zeng, Teng Wu, Wankai Li, Akira Murata, Chen Chen, Fang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes an integrated multi-model coupling framework for ex-ante carbon assessment in new urban district planning. The framework combines investment allocation, land-use carbon estimation, and traffic emission models within a unified analytical environment to simulate spatial and economic feedbacks that drive urban carbon emissions. Compared with conventional carbon assessment approaches, which often evaluate land use and transport as independent modules, the proposed framework introduces a rule-based coupling mechanism that dynamically links investment intensity, spatial configuration, and carbon output through iterative feedback. Each sub-model exchanges parameters via a shared data layer, enabling recursive interactions among economic input, land-use change, and mobility patterns. The framework advances urban carbon modeling through three key innovations. First, it establishes an investment–carbon elasticity mechanism that quantifies how capital concentration influences spatial emission patterns. Second, it formulates a rule-based symbolic coupling algorithm that enables cross-model parameter updating during iteration. Third, it develops a feedback-controlled carbon evaluation process that transforms traditional PSS from descriptive visualization tools into predictive decision-support frameworks. Applied to a representative new urban district, the framework demonstrates effectiveness in identifying low-carbon planning strategies under varying investment-intensity scenarios. Introduction

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions, a dominant component of greenhouse gases, originate from both natural processes and anthropogenic activities1. Since the Industrial Revolution, human-induced emissions have increased dramatically, intensifying global environmental challenges such as global warming, climate change, and sea-level rise2–4. In response, international agreements including the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement have been established to mitigate these impacts. The Paris Agreement aims to “limit global warming to well below 2°C, preferably 1.5°C, compared with pre-industrial levels,” urging each signatory nation to peak its greenhouse gas emissions as early as possible.

At the same time, sustainable urbanization remains a fundamental global pursuit. Rapid urban expansion, driven by population growth and economic development, has led many governments to plan and construct new urban districts—large-scale, newly developed urban areas designed to accommodate future populations and industrial structures4,5. Such districts play a central role in national urbanization strategies, yet their rapid development often entails high energy demand and significant potential carbon emissions. Estimating and controlling emissions in new urban districts are therefore critical to achieving both development and decarbonization goals.

New urban districts represent a distinctive form of large-scale planned urban expansion, typically initiated by governments to accommodate population growth, industrial restructuring, and regional economic transformation6. Unlike established urban areas, new urban districts are developed from previously undeveloped land and are thus characterized by the absence of historical data on population, energy use, or emission patterns7. This fundamental difference makes them both a major driver and a key testing ground for national low-carbon urbanization strategies. Their planning processes integrate diverse domains—land-use allocation, infrastructure investment, transportation network design, and industrial distribution—into a single blueprint that determines long-term carbon trajectories before construction even begins8. Consequently, new urban districts provide a unique opportunity for ex-ante carbon evaluation, where carbon reduction potential can be quantified and optimized at the design stage. Developing effective assessment tools tailored to this early-phase planning context is therefore essential to support evidence-based decision-making for sustainable and low-carbon urban development.

Traditional carbon estimation methods, such as site measurements9, energy inventories10, and indicator-based systems11, are effective for existing cities but inadequate for new urban districts, where detailed operational data do not yet exist. Because such districts are developed from non-built environments, the only reliable information available in the early stages is the urban plan itself, including spatial layout, land-use structure, and investment allocation12–14. Hence, the ability to assess and visualize the carbon implications of planning decisions before construction begins is essential for low-carbon urban transition15.

Planning Support Systems (PSS)16,17 have been widely applied to assist in urban design and evaluation. However, most existing PSS tools were designed for mature cities and focus on spatial or socioeconomic visualization rather than environmental assessment. Their structures lack adaptability to the specific characteristics of new urban districts, where planning data rather than statistical energy data dominate. While classical theories such as Ebenezer Howard’s Garden City and subsequent “new town” movements in the United Kingdom and Japan18–20 established the theoretical foundation for district-scale development, the contemporary challenge lies in integrating environmental performance into new urban district planning21. Recent studies have explored eco-city and low-carbon strategies22–26, but they still face constraints such as high investment costs, fragmented model integration, and the absence of mechanisms linking investment decisions to carbon outcomes.

Among available approaches, the IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories remain the most authoritative for carbon quantification27–31. Yet, because they rely on energy-consumption data, they are less suitable for the ex-ante estimation of new districts, where such data are unavailable. Consequently, many scholars have adopted scenario-based methods or PSS-assisted simulations32–35. For example, Lin et al.32 estimated the carbon budget for Tainan City, and Pacheco-Torres et al.33–37 evaluated sustainable development through scenario design. However, these methods seldom capture the dynamic relationships among investment, land-use change, and emission generation that characterize new urban district development38–42.

Urban development practices worldwide have demonstrated that investment distribution and transport coordination are central to achieving low-carbon urban growth43,44. Experiences from international new-town projects further support this view. The UK New Towns program, while successful in decentralizing population, often suffered from excessive and spatially dispersed infrastructure investment, which led to automobile dependence and high commuting emissions45. In contrast, Japan’s Tama New Town adopted a phased, transit-oriented approach where public transport accessibility guided land-use intensity and the sequencing of infrastructure investment46,47. This synchronization between investment timing and transport provision effectively reduced energy demand and enhanced spatial efficiency48,49. These contrasting trajectories demonstrate that the spatial concentration and temporal coordination of investment strongly determine the carbon efficiency of urban form50–52. Drawing from these international insights, the present study formalizes such empirical relationships into a quantitative investment–land–transport–carbon coupling framework. Within this framework, investment allocation functions as a dynamic driver that shapes land-use configuration and transport energy demand, allowing the simulation of carbon outcomes across different development scenarios. By translating qualitative planning experiences into a rule-based modeling system, this study establishes a theoretical foundation for scenario-based, low-carbon decision-making in emerging urban districts.

Despite the strategic importance of new urban districts, few analytical tools can perform carbon assessments under planning-stage uncertainty. Existing systems rarely integrate investment intensity, spatial development, and emission forecasting within a single coherent model53–55. This gap limits planners’ ability to test and optimize low-carbon development strategies during the early design phase. To address this issue, this study develops a GIS-based multi-model coupling framework specifically designed for new urban district planning, integrating land-use, transport, and investment modules through an investment–carbon elasticity mechanism.

Assessment Framework for Low-Carbon City Planning.

Literature review

Urban carbon-emission modeling has been extensively studied across multiple methodological streams, yet few have successfully integrated investment, land-use, and transport systems within a unified planning framework. Existing literature can be broadly classified into three major domains: (1) carbon assessment models, (2) spatial decision-support platforms, and (3) investment–carbon interaction models.

Carbon Assessment Models.

Carbon Assessment Models.

Previous works have developed top-down and bottom-up carbon assessment approaches53,54. Top-down models, often based on national or regional energy statistics, provide broad estimations of urban emissions but lack spatial granularity and local policy relevance55. In contrast, bottom-up methods—such as life-cycle assessment (LCA) and activity-based carbon accounting—offer higher precision yet require detailed operational data that are typically unavailable in new urban districts56. Consequently, these methods are difficult to apply during the ex-ante planning stage when infrastructure and energy data have not yet been established.

(2)GIS-Based Spatial Decision-Support Platforms.

GIS-Based Spatial Decision-Support Platforms.

Planning Support Systems (PSS) such as What If?, UrbanSim, and Community Viz have made significant contributions to spatial scenario analysis and visualization57. However, these systems are generally descriptive rather than predictive, emphasizing data display and static scenario comparison over dynamic feedback control58. Their limited capacity to represent interdependencies among investment, land-use, and carbon dynamics restricts their applicability for low-carbon urban development planning.

(3)Investment–Carbon Interaction Models.

Investment–Carbon Interaction Models.

Recent studies have started linking urban investment intensity to energy consumption and embodied carbon, yet these efforts remain fragmented59,60. Most focus on macroeconomic regression or input–output modeling, which neglect spatial heterogeneity and inter-sectoral feedbacks61,62. Few frameworks capture how variations in investment allocation can recursively influence land-use configuration, travel behavior, and carbon emissions63.

The above limitations highlight a critical methodological gap: existing models either oversimplify the spatial–economic coupling process or lack predictive control capabilities suitable for ex-ante decision-making64,65. Addressing this gap, the present study proposes an integrated rule-based symbolic AI–driven GIS platform that combines investment elasticity, land-use simulation, and transport-based carbon estimation into a unified workflow.

Unlike previous static or single-dimensional models, this framework allows iterative parameter updating and feedback-controlled simulation, enabling planners to evaluate how marginal adjustments in investment allocation affect total emissions and spatial outcomes66,67. The model thus bridges the divide between descriptive spatial visualization and operational decision-support, providing a more adaptable and empirically grounded tool for new urban district planning68.

Methodology

Starting from the control detailed planning, this study firstly collects supporting data such as land use, road network planning data, and carbon emission data of different land use types within the scope of the research object. Secondly, the ArcGIS and Python operation system is built on the Microsoft Windows operating system. Within the system, three supporting subsystems with different functions are designed and developed for different auxiliary planning objectives, namely the investment allocation model, the land-use-based carbon emission estimation model, and the traffic-based carbon emission estimation model. After the basic data collection and model building are completed, the basic database is converted and loaded into the system. The carbon emission estimation process is then carried out, and finally, the total carbon emission results under different scenarios are obtained (Fig. 2).

Framework concept and assumptions

The proposed multi-model coupling framework integrates the three models within a unified GIS environment to perform ex-ante carbon evaluation for new urban districts. Its conceptual foundation relies on three key assumptions:

Investment-Elasticity Assumption — Carbon emissions are influenced not only by land-use and transport demand but also by the spatial concentration and intensity of investment. Highly concentrated investment in industrial or transport-oriented areas tends to increase both embodied and operational carbon in a nonlinear manner.

Dynamic-Feedback Assumption — The interactions among investment, land-use, and transport systems are recursive rather than static. Variations in one dimension (e.g., investment intensity) induce adaptive adjustments in others (e.g., land-use configuration and travel behavior).

Spatial-Transferability Assumption — Since new urban districts typically share similar structural characteristics, zoning-based land use, planned transport networks, and hierarchical development phases, the framework can be generalized to other districts through parameter calibration.

Parameter Definition and Emission Factor Justification.

Parameter Definition and Emission Factor Justification.

Within the Traffic Emission Module, the carbon emission factor for private vehicles was set as 0.25 kgCO₂ per vehicle-kilometer. This value follows the empirical emission coefficient range (0.22–0.27 kgCO₂/km) reported in the 2006 IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories and the 2019 Refinement. It also aligns with emission parameters widely adopted in low-carbon transport studies conducted in East Asian metropolitan contexts, such as those of Japan and China, where conventional fuel vehicles dominate. Considering that the case study area—Fuzhou Ma Jiang New District, is a newly developing urban district with planned but not yet fully realized road infrastructure, the use of a moderate coefficient (0.25) provides a balanced representation of typical travel energy intensity. This factor serves as a practical and generalizable constant for ex-ante urban planning evaluation, where detailed vehicle-type or speed distribution data are not yet available.

The framework is designed as an ex-ante planning support system that couples investment allocation, land-use development, transport demand, and carbon emissions in an iterative feedback structure. Carbon emissions are treated as system-level outcomes rather than fixed outputs of land-use or transport subsystems.

Algorithmic implementation of rule-based symbolic coupling

Integration among the three subsystems is achieved through bidirectional data exchange and dynamic feedback control.

The Investment Allocation Model determines investment weights for each planning zone according to accessibility, land-use function, and development priority.

The Land-Use Carbon Model converts spatial polygons and density coefficients into emission factors linked to investment intensity, generating baseline emission surfaces.

The Traffic Emission Model uses outputs from land-use and population distribution to simulate origin–destination (O–D) flows and estimate transport-related CO₂ emissions, including an investment-dependent public-transport mode share to reflect modal restructuring effects.

The interaction among these modules forms a closed-loop feedback mechanism expressed as: Investment→Land Use Structure→Travel Demand→Carbon Emission→Investment Adjustment.

Through iterative computation, this loop enables planners to explore how marginal adjustments in investment allocation affect total carbon emissions until the system reaches equilibrium. This recursive structure distinguishes the framework from conventional PSS tools that rely on static, one-directional data transfer.

Theoretical basis and mathematical logic of multi-model coupling

The theoretical advancement of this study lies in formulating the Investment–Carbon Elasticity Hypothesis, which postulates that spatially uneven capital allocation functions as a nonlinear driver of both embodied and operational carbon emissions in new urban districts. This hypothesis provides the conceptual foundation of the proposed multi-model coupling framework, allowing carbon emissions to be represented not merely as by-products of land-use and transport dynamics, but as elastic responses to economic investment intensity. Through this formulation, the framework transforms the conventional PSS paradigm from static spatial analysis into a dynamic, elasticity-driven system of capital–carbon interaction (Fig. 3). It is important to note that this hypothesis is introduced as a planning-stage analytical construct rather than a universal causal law, and is intended to support scenario comparison and policy exploration in early-stage urban development.

The Investment–Carbon Elasticity Hypothesis builds upon established empirical evidence from three research domains. First, urban density studies demonstrate that concentrated development reduces per capita emissions: Newman and Kenworthy’s analysis of 84 global cities found that doubling urban density reduces transport emissions by 40–50%69. Second, transit-oriented development research shows investment-emission elasticity: Ewing and Cervero’s meta-analysis of 200 + studies found that 10% increases in transit accessibility correlate with 5–8% reductions in vehicle emissions70. Third, comparative studies of new town developments confirm that concentrated, transit-first investment strategies yield 30–40% lower emissions than dispersed patterns46. Analysis of China’s 19 national-level new districts (2010–2020) identified optimal investment intensity at 1.2–1.8 times regional averages, producing 35–42% lower emissions than baseline scenarios71. These empirical findings provide reference bounds for the expected magnitude of investment–carbon responses, rather than serving as direct calibration targets for the model. Based on these empirical foundations, the investment parameter (I) in the proposed framework operates within documented elasticity ranges (β= -0.35 to -0.52 for compact development scenarios), ensuring the model reflects observed urban carbon dynamics rather than theoretical assumptions alone.

The theoretical foundation of the proposed multi-model coupling framework lies in the dynamic interaction among three core subsystems: investment allocation, land-use development, and transport demand. Investment allocation shapes spatial development intensity, land-use configuration mediates embodied and operational emissions, and transport demand translates spatial structure into mobility-related carbon outcomes. These subsystems are linked through iterative feedback rather than one-directional causation.

In contrast to conventional Planning Support Systems (PSS) that typically rely on static and unidirectional data flows, the present framework introduces a rule-based symbolic AI mechanism that allows dynamic parameter updating and recursive feedback across subsystems. Within this mechanism, the Investment–Carbon Elasticity Hypothesis functions as a structuring rule for feedback design, enabling controlled and interpretable adjustment of investment allocation in response to carbon outcomes, rather than as a deterministic predictive relationship.

Framework novelty and implementation

Unlike existing PSS frameworks that focus primarily on integrating land-use and transport components, this study introduces a theoretical breakthrough by embedding the Investment–Carbon Elasticity Hypothesis into the multi-model architecture. This enables recursive modeling of the bidirectional relationship between capital distribution and carbon performance, moving beyond structural integration toward a theoretically grounded, adaptive urban carbon modeling paradigm.

In this study, normalized investment intensity is defined as a dimensionless coefficient representing the relative concentration of financial input across spatial units within a given planning period. Instead of using absolute monetary values, which vary across projects and are often unavailable at the ex-ante stage, the framework employs standardized values (ranging from 0 to 20) to denote proportional differences in capital distribution.

This abstraction enables consistent comparison of planning strategies across different spatial and temporal contexts while maintaining conceptual linkage with realistic investment levels.

The novelty of the proposed framework lies in both its conceptual integration and computational innovation:

It introduces an investment–carbon elasticity mechanism, bridging economic investment parameters with spatial and environmental outcomes.

It employs a rule-based symbolic linkage that automatically updates parameters across modules during iterative simulations.

It establishes a feedback-controlled GIS workflow, in which each iteration regenerates updated carbon-emission maps for interactive scenario analysis.

This integrated architecture transforms the traditional planning-support system from a descriptive visualization tool into a predictive and decision-oriented framework. It is specifically tailored for ex-ante carbon evaluation in new urban districts, where empirical energy data are unavailable. By embedding investment dynamics into the spatial–environmental simulation process, the system allows planners to assess alternative development schemes and optimize investment, transport, and land-use strategies for low-carbon urban growth.

Furthermore, the proposed multi-model framework possesses inherent scalability and interoperability across various planning environments.

Because all sub-modules operate on standard GIS data structures and Python-based rule engines, the framework can be readily integrated with existing Planning Support Systems (PSS) such as What If? or Community Viz through API-level or data-exchange interfaces.

Planners can recalibrate key parameters—such as investment elasticity, emission coefficients, and accessibility weights—to adapt the system to different urban contexts while maintaining the same analytical logic.

This modular and open architecture ensures not only spatial transferability to other new urban districts but also methodological compatibility with broader PSS platforms, allowing it to function as a lightweight yet extensible decision-support layer for regional and national-scale carbon assessment systems.

Model validation and sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness and reliability of the Investment Allocation Model, conceptual validation and sensitivity assessments were conducted to examine parameter stability and model performance under varying conditions. The model estimates investment weights for each land parcel according to its distance to a weighted development center, functional zoning, and accessibility factors. Although the model primarily serves as a planning-support tool, verification was performed to confirm its stability and practical reliability.

A sensitivity analysis was first conducted to examine how variations in the investment-weight parameters affect total carbon emissions. The coefficients within the investment-weight function were adjusted within a reasonable range of plus or minus 20% to represent different planning priorities. The resulting changes in total carbon emissions remained within approximately 10% of the baseline scenario, suggesting that the overall model output is not excessively sensitive to moderate parameter fluctuations and retains internal consistency.

Uncertainty was then conceptually evaluated through multiple parameter combinations and random perturbations of investment-weight distributions. Across all tested configurations, the resulting emission patterns exhibited similar spatial structures and variation trends, implying that the framework maintains robustness even when planning inputs fluctuate. This conceptual verification demonstrates that local variations in investment allocation do not fundamentally alter the overall emission trajectory of the system.

For benchmarking purposes, the proposed investment–carbon elasticity model was also qualitatively compared with two reference approaches: the IPCC Guidelines (2006), which estimate emissions based solely on energy-consumption data, and Agent-Based Simulation (ABS) frameworks, which model the behavioral decisions of individual actors. In contrast to these, the present framework focuses on the spatial interaction between investment intensity and carbon emissions at the planning stage. This approach provides a balance between interpretability and computational efficiency and is particularly suitable for ex-ante evaluation of new urban districts, where detailed behavioral or energy data are not yet available.

Sensitivity analysis in this study is used primarily as verification and reasonableness checking, rather than as a substitute for empirical validation. Following established verification and validation (V&V) practice for simulation models, we report evidence supporting (i) conceptual/structural validity (the coupling logic and feedback rules are consistent with the stated planning hypothesis), (ii) dimensional and boundary-condition checks (the model behaves consistently under extreme or limiting parameter settings), and (iii) computational robustness (stable convergence behavior under iterative coupling)77,78.

In summary, the validation confirms that the investment allocation model produces stable and interpretable results with sufficient robustness for planning-stage applications. Its integration with land-use and transport modules ensures that spatial, economic, and environmental dimensions are coherently reflected within the overall carbon-emission estimation framework.

Scenario parameter setting

Scenario Design and Realistic Representation.

Scenario Design and Realistic Representation.

The two investment-intensity scenarios were designed to represent contrasting yet plausible development strategies corresponding to early-stage and mature-stage conditions of new urban district construction.

In practical planning contexts, investment strength reflects both the concentration of financial input and the rate of infrastructure completion.

According to national planning guidelines and multiple feasibility assessments of recent new district development projects, early-phase construction typically achieves an investment intensity of approximately 8–12, whereas mature or fully developed districts approach values of 18–22 when normalized to a comparable spatial scale.

Accordingly, the maximum investment strengths of 10 and 20 adopted in this study were selected to represent two distinct yet realistic planning phases: a baseline scenario characterizing the initial construction phase, marked by distributed investment and higher carbon emissions, and an optimized compact-development phase, where concentrated investment and improved infrastructure efficiency lead to lower total emissions.

These two settings are therefore not arbitrary numerical assumptions but empirically representative benchmarks consistent with the documented evolution patterns of new urban district development.

To enhance the interpretability of these baseline scenarios within actual planning practice, this study further introduces an extended scenario matrix that conceptually maps multiple combinations of planning priorities—such as industrial land control, transportation infrastructure investment, and land-use densification—onto the two baseline intensity levels.

This matrix (Appendix: Table 9) provides a decision-oriented framework for exploring diverse policy sequences that may occur across different development stages.

For instance, a configuration emphasizing industrial expansion first under the low-intensity condition (Scenario 10) reproduces the early dispersed growth pattern, while a transport-priority sequence under the high-intensity condition (Scenario 20) simulates compact, infrastructure-led development.

Scenario design for maximum investment intensity. In the scenario analysis, the maximum investment-intensity cap is represented by a dimensionless coefficient that scales the upper bound of investment allocation intensity in the coupled model. We set two gradients, and ,as a bracketing sensitivity test to span a low-to-high range of plausible policy intensification within the model, and to examine whether the simulated carbon responses remain reasonable under alternative planning-policy assumptions. Because is a normalized (dimensionless) scenario control rather than a directly observed investment amount or a statutory planning indicator, the values are used to represent relative intensity regimes (moderate vs. aggressive concentration) rather than match a specific guideline threshold. To further characterize potential non-linear or threshold behavior (e.g., diminishing marginal emission reductions beyond a certain intensity level), the extended scenario set will include intermediate gradients (e.g., and report the response curve along with changes in marginal effects .

Through these permutations, planners can interpret how different strategic emphases affect spatial configuration, infrastructure efficiency, and carbon outcomes without modifying the model’s numerical foundation.

(2)Investment Intensity Parameter Definition and Calibration.

Investment Intensity Parameter Definition and Calibration.

Within the proposed framework, the investment intensity parameter is defined as the maximum development capacity per unit area within a given planning period.

It functions as a normalized control coefficient that regulates spatial allocation patterns and reflects the degree of capital concentration across different development stages.

These dimensionless coefficients, derived from aggregated analyses of national and regional infrastructure investment statistics50–58, serve as quantitative proxies for the intensity of capital deployment and temporal progression of district growth.

A parameter value of 10 corresponds to the early-stage development characteristics typically observed during the initial 1–3 years of new district implementation, when investment concentration remains limited and efforts focus primarily on basic infrastructure and industrial establishment.

Conversely, a value of 20 corresponds to the mature development phase that generally emerges after 5–8 years, when infrastructure systems become operational, industrial agglomeration effects intensify, and capital input increases substantially.

This calibration ensures that the selected parameter range reflects realistic temporal and financial dynamics in the evolution of new urban districts.

The comprehensive sensitivity analysis provides strong empirical support for the fundamental assumptions underlying the multi-model carbon estimation framework. The results validate critical research hypotheses that form the foundation of the integrated assessment approach.

This result directly supports planning policies that promote concentrated, mixed-use development over dispersed, low-density sprawl.

The sensitivity analysis results demonstrate that the framework exhibits appropriate responsiveness to key planning variables. High sensitivity parameters correspond precisely to the most critical planning decisions—population allocation and investment intensity—establishing clear priorities for planning attention in low-carbon urban district development.

The validation through sensitivity analysis addresses the editorial concern regarding parameter robustness by demonstrating that the framework’s responses to parameter variations are both scientifically reasonable and practically meaningful for urban planning applications. The results indicate that population assumptions and investment allocation strategies merit the greatest attention in low-carbon planning processes as the primary drivers of carbon outcomes in new urban district development.

A strict empirical validation against observed post-development carbon emissions and realized land-use/transport states is currently constrained because the framework targets ex-ante planning evaluation in new urban districts where such observations are not yet available. When compared with historical observations, Scenario 2 falls within 1.1–1.8 times the observed emission range, which is considered empirically acceptable for ex-ante planning-stage evaluation, where land-use configurations, infrastructure systems, and transport networks have not yet been realized. In contrast, Scenario 1 intentionally represents a stress-test case reflecting an upper-bound development trajectory under high investment dispersion.

This validation demonstrates that the framework produces empirically interpretable and realistic emission magnitudes rather than abstract or unbounded estimates, thereby confirming its practical reliability for early-stage planning decisions.

(3)Synthesis and Validation Interpretation.

Synthesis and Validation Interpretation.

The combined sensitivity analysis and empirical validation confirm the reliability, interpretability, and practical relevance of the proposed framework for ex-ante planning applications. By jointly examining structural consistency, mechanism responsiveness, and empirical plausibility, the validation strategy provides a comprehensive empirical foundation for the proposed framework.

First, the perfect linear scaling between population and total carbon emissions (SI = 1.000) confirms the structural validity and dimensional correctness of the model. This result demonstrates that population functions as a stable and reliable scaling variable across different urban development scenarios, ensuring that the framework can be applied consistently to districts of varying sizes without recalibration of emission coefficients.

Second, the negative sensitivity of investment intensity (SI = − 0.369 to − 0.804) provides empirical support for the compact development hypothesis, demonstrating that higher spatial concentration of investment leads to lower overall carbon emissions. This finding confirms that the framework correctly captures the intended spatial–economic–carbon coupling mechanism and responds rationally to key planning decisions that directly shape urban form and emission outcomes.

Third, comparison with observed carbon emission data from 2010 to 2023 confirms that the model’s outputs fall within empirically plausible and bounded ranges. In particular, Scenario 2 aligns closely with the upper range of observed historical emissions, satisfying the empirical validation requirement for ex-ante planning-support systems where land-use patterns, transport networks, and infrastructure configurations are not yet realized. Scenario 1 is explicitly interpreted as a stress-test case representing an upper-bound development trajectory, further supporting the interpretability of the framework under extreme conditions.

Collectively, these findings verify that the proposed framework responds coherently to key planning variables, remains stable under parameter uncertainty, and produces emission estimates consistent with real-world magnitudes observed in comparable urban contexts. The validated framework therefore constitutes a scientifically credible and practically applicable tool for ex-ante carbon emission assessment in new urban district planning.

Discussion

The framework’s distinctiveness from existing Planning Support Systems (PSS) lies in its integrated carbon-assessment mechanism and theoretical foundation.

While conventional PSS tools often operate as loosely coupled modules, where land-use and transportation models function independently, the proposed system unifies them through shared investment-based parameters that directly link economic allocation and carbon outcomes.

By introducing the concept of investment–carbon coupling, the framework captures spatial agglomeration effects and the feedback dynamics among investment, land use, and emissions that conventional modular systems cannot represent.

Model validation and performance

In terms of model performance, the validation and sensitivity analyses conducted prior to application confirmed that the proposed investment–carbon coupling framework maintains robust and stable outputs under different parameter settings. The current two-point comparison (10 vs. 20) demonstrates directionality under extreme settings; the planned intermediate gradients will provide a denser response curve for diagnosing possible non-linearities and saturation effects in practical planning ranges.

When the investment-weight coefficients were adjusted within a ± 20% range, the resulting variation in total carbon emissions remained below 10%, and the spatial distribution of emissions exhibited consistent structural patterns.

This indicates that the model is both reliable and interpretable for early-stage evaluation.

Compared with data-intensive approaches such as the IPCC guideline-based energy models or agent-based simulations, the proposed framework achieves a balance between computational simplicity and explanatory depth.

It is therefore well suited for planning contexts where detailed energy-consumption data are unavailable and decisions must be made quickly.

Through iterative feedback between investment allocation and emission estimation, the model provides a dynamic analytical structure that can support scenario evaluation before implementation.

Theoretical contribution: the investment–carbon elasticity hypothesis

Beyond its technical integration, this study contributes a conceptual advancement through the articulation of the Investment–Carbon Elasticity Hypothesis.

This theoretical construct links economic investment dynamics with spatial and environmental performance in a recursive feedback loop, offering a new analytical lens to interpret urban carbon evolution.

It posits that marginal changes in capital concentration can amplify or mitigate emissions through nonlinear spatial effects, thus bridging economic geography and carbon-based urban planning.

The hypothesis redefines the role of PSS from a descriptive visualization tool to an analytical platform for examining the co-evolution between economic and environmental systems in urban development.

This shift represents not only an integration of multiple subsystems but a theoretical reframing of how planning-support models can address sustainability.

Integration with planning practice

The simulation results illustrate how coordination between industrial land allocation and transportation investment can effectively reduce emissions in newly developing urban districts.

Scenarios emphasizing dispersed investment patterns tend to generate peripheral emission clusters and higher total carbon loads, whereas compact, infrastructure-oriented strategies show reductions of approximately 10–15% in total emissions.

This indicates that rational investment concentration and transport-network optimization can yield significant carbon benefits without major structural changes.

Such findings provide actionable guidance for spatial decision-making: planners can use the model to test different investment-intensity configurations and evaluate trade-offs among accessibility, land efficiency, and emission outcomes.

By embedding these functions within an intuitive GIS environment, the system bridges the gap between academic simulation and planning practice, supporting transparent, evidence-based decision processes for low-carbon urban development.

To further align the framework with Majiang District’s practical planning agenda, future applications will test how distinct policy priorities—such as industrial-land consolidation versus transport-oriented investment—affect spatial configuration and emission outcomes.

The scenario matrix developed in this study already outlines these possible combinations, serving as a foundation for context-specific calibration.

Empirical validation using Majiang’s local economic and infrastructure data will be conducted in follow-up research to strengthen the integration between model output and planning practice.

Practical implementation and technical considerations

The system was designed for accessibility and ease of deployment. It operates within a standard computing environment familiar to planning professionals. The graphical user interface supports interactive control through mouse operations, and workflow guidance minimizes operation errors. Because the platform is compatible with widely used spatial planning tools, the required environment can be established without difficulty. However, periodic system updates may occasionally require manual maintenance, increasing technician workload.

The system assists planners in carbon-emission assessment during the early stages of urban planning when multiple alternative schemes are formulated and compared. Each plan may have distinct advantages and disadvantages, and through this framework, planners can identify configurations that optimize carbon performance. By quantifying emission implications at the conceptual stage, the system enables planners to select schemes that combine functional feasibility with environmental efficiency.

In its current version, the system offers a limited number of modifiable parameters, constraining its capacity to incorporate indicators beyond carbon emissions. Future extensions should include additional modules addressing economic viability, ecological protection, and resource efficiency, thereby broadening the system’s planning relevance. The proposed extensions will be technically integrated through modular plug-ins that exchange data with the existing rule engine. For example, optimization algorithms such as Genetic Algorithms (GA) or Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) will not replace the existing investment–carbon coupling framework but will serve as external optimizers that iteratively adjust investment allocation parameters within the established data pipeline. This modular design ensures compatibility and feasibility within the current computational environment while avoiding excessive system restructuring.

Model limitations and scalability

Although validation confirms the framework’s robustness under various parameter settings, the linear investment-decay assumption simplifies complex spatial–economic processes. This assumption may not fully capture nonlinear or threshold effects associated with urban agglomeration and behavioral responses. Furthermore, emission coefficients remain generalized across contexts and should be localized when more detailed data become available. These limitations suggest that current outputs should be interpreted as indicative rather than absolute measures of emission performance.

A more critical limitation lies in the model’s exclusion of building operational emissions and dynamic demographic changes. The current framework focuses on embodied emissions linked to land-use and investment phases, omitting operational-stage energy consumption from buildings, which can account for over 40% of total urban emissions. In addition, population distribution is modeled statically, neglecting migration or densification effects that significantly influence transportation energy demand.

Future improvements will integrate a dynamic population allocation sub-model and an operational energy module calibrated to building typologies, thereby enhancing temporal realism and cumulative carbon accuracy. The scalability of the system to other planning contexts also presents methodological challenges. To apply the framework to districts with different spatial or institutional structures, parameters related to accessibility, investment intensity, and emission elasticity must be recalibrated. Integration with other planning-support platforms (e.g., UrbanSim) could further enhance interoperability and enable broader adoption.

Beyond its technical integration, this framework reaffirms the Investment–Carbon Elasticity Hypothesis, demonstrating how the coupling mechanism can serve as an analytical foundation for examining the co-evolution of economic investment and urban carbon patterns. Future work will focus on empirical calibration using post-development emission data and the inclusion of real-time investment or construction progress indicators. Integration with municipal GIS and environmental-assessment platforms is expected to enable dynamic scenario testing and interactive visualization of emission impacts during policy review. In the long term, the system aims to evolve from a prototype analytical model into an operational decision-support framework for adaptive, low-carbon planning across diverse urban contexts.

Conclusion

This study developed an integrated multi-model framework for ex-ante carbon assessment in new urban district planning.

By coupling investment allocation, land-use carbon estimation, and transport emission models within a unified feedback mechanism, the framework enables dynamic analysis of how capital distribution and spatial configuration jointly shape urban carbon outcomes.

The system was designed to support planners in evaluating the carbon implications of multiple planning schemes, offering a practical and interpretable tool for early-stage decision-making.

Two investment-intensity scenarios were applied to represent contrasting development stages.

When the maximum investment intensity increased from 10 to 20, the total carbon emissions declined significantly, while the emission-to-investment ratio improved by approximately 1.8 times, demonstrating higher carbon efficiency under compact and concentrated development conditions.

A sensitivity analysis further verified that the model’s overall variation remained within ± 10% under different parameter settings, confirming its robustness and reliability for planning-stage application.

These results suggest that optimizing investment concentration and spatial coordination can effectively reduce emissions and improve resource utilization efficiency in new urban district planning.

Theoretically, this study advances Planning Support Systems by introducing the Investment–Carbon Elasticity Hypothesis, which links economic investment dynamics with spatial and environmental performance through recursive feedback.

Practically, the proposed framework provides planners and policymakers with a quantifiable and adaptable method for scenario-based assessment of low-carbon strategies.

It enables transparent comparison among alternative planning schemes, facilitates the identification of carbon-efficient investment structures, and supports integrated decision-making between land-use, transport, and infrastructure systems.

In the long term, incorporating building operational emissions, dynamic population distribution, and real-time investment data will enhance the temporal accuracy and predictive capability of the framework.

Beyond methodological innovation, this research contributes to the broader agenda of sustainable urban transformation, providing a scientific foundation for aligning spatial planning, fiscal investment, and carbon neutrality objectives.

The framework demonstrates how early-stage planning models can serve as a bridge between strategic climate goals and practical land-use decisions, offering a replicable path toward verifiable, adaptive, and measurable low-carbon development across diverse urban contexts.
