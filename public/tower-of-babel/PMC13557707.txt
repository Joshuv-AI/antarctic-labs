Just Semantics? How Diagnostic Criteria Influence Physician-Patient Narratives in Alzheimer’s Disease
Authors: Alice Accorroni, Christine Clavien, Giovanni B. Frisoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe current diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is shaped by the Alzheimer’s Association (AA) and the International Working Group (IWG) criteria. While both frameworks lead to similar physician-patient conversations when disclosing biomarker results to patients with cognitive impairment, they diverge significantly for cognitively unimpaired individuals. The aim of this paper is to explore these differences and their associated epistemological and ethical implications, through simulated, case-based physician-patient conversations.MethodsThe conversations were developed on the basis of clinical experience, exchanges with patients and carers, insights from a targeted narrative literature review and were subsequently refined with large language model assistance.ResultsOur analysis highlights that the AA framework reinforces a deterministic narrative by equating amyloid biomarker positivity with disease and raising a misunderstanding of illness. In contrast, the IWG framework is consistent with the incomplete penetrance of AD pathology and frames biomarker positivity in cognitively unimpaired individuals as a risk condition. We acknowledge that the IWG framework enables more complex but also more realistic physician-patient conversations.ConclusionPhysician-patient conversations employing a carefully selected lexicon, reflecting patient-centred outcomes, may promote patient engagement, compliance with medical recommendations, and, ultimately, societal trust in medicine and physicians.

Introduction

Narratives are the basic tools of human communication, creating intersubjective realities and enabling cooperation on a large scale [1]. In medicine, disease narratives play an important role in the physician-patient relationship. Narratives also shape patients’ understanding of their condition, fostering engagement with medical recommendations and treatment plans and influencing public trust in medicine [2].

The field of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) exemplifies this delicate dynamic and exposes potential pitfalls arising from divergent disease narratives. The International Working Group (IWG) [3] and the Alzheimer’s Association Workgroup (AA) [4] criteria are the diagnostic frameworks currently shaping the way the condition is conceptualized, diagnosed, and discussed between patients and physicians.

The AA framework, grounded in the canonical amyloid hypothesis, assumes that β-amyloid deposition is both necessary and sufficient to initiate the disease cascade, with dementia marking its final stage [4]. While this conceptualization is well suited to describe the natural history of autosomal dominant AD and Down syndrome, where a single genetic mutation is necessary and sufficient to trigger the pathological cascade leading to cognitive impairment, it fails to capture the complexities of sporadic AD [5–7]. In fact, although the AA framework conceptualizes that co-pathology and resilience factors influence clinical presentation, it defines AD biologically using Core 1 biomarkers, including both amyloid and AD-specific tau, while in practice allowing amyloid positivity alone to suffice in certain contexts [4]. The IWG instead embraces a probabilistic framework, defining AD as a clinical-biological entity and considering isolated amyloid biomarker positivity as a risk factor for AD [3]. This difference has significant implications for physician-patient narratives, particularly in the context of the numerous secondary-prevention initiatives launched worldwide [8]. It also shapes how biomarker positivity is interpreted and influences the ethical dimensions of diagnostic disclosure.

Further complexity arises from semantic differences across languages and cultural contexts, with the English language making a differentiation between disease (denoting the biomedical condition) and illness (the patient's experience of disease), and others where the term disease includes both concepts (e.g., malattia in Italian, maladie in French, enfermedad in Spanish, Krankheit in German) [9, 10]. As a result, the lack of linguistic separation between the disease label and its lived experience may create semantic dissonance, increasing the risk of misunderstanding the meaning, severity, and implications of the diagnostic label.

This risk is made even greater by the growing availability of biomarker tests for AD pathology through direct-to-consumer services. In such contexts, individuals may rely on lay narratives and culturally embedded interpretations of what it means to “have Alzheimer’s disease” – interpretations that may equate disease with manifest cognitive impairment rather than with asymptomatic biological abnormality – potentially overinterpreting the results or misjudging their prognosis [11–14].

Furthermore, the question of how AD should be defined is situated within a longstanding debate in the philosophy of medicine concerning the nature of disease and the role of conceptual and narrative frameworks in its construction and communication [15]. Some theoretical accounts conceptualize disease as a biologically grounded condition that can be identified independently of social interpretation, whereas others argue that disease concepts are inherently normative and shaped by cultural expectations, linguistic practices, and lived experience [16–19]. This study aimed to assess the epistemic and ethical impact of the IWG and AA criteria on physician-patient conversations through the use of simulated conversations developed on the assumptions and implications embedded within each diagnostic framework. The conversations are situated in the context of disclosing AD biomarker test results to cognitively unimpaired and impaired individuals in specialized services.

Methods

Study Design

This study employs a qualitative, case-based approach. The analysis is informed by: (1) personal clinical experience, (2) the application of large language models (LLM), and (3) a targeted review of the literature as detailed below.

Clinical Experience

To illustrate the practical impact of key points of convergence and divergence of the AA and IWG frameworks and their influence on physician-patient conversations, we developed three simulated physician-patient conversations reflecting distinct clinico-biological profiles of a hypothetical 65-year-old person with vascular risk factors and a family history of late-onset AD dementia. Specifically, the scenarios represent the following clinical contexts: (1) a cognitively impaired individual with positive β-amyloid and tau biomarkers; (2) a cognitively unimpaired individual with both biomarkers positive; and (3) a cognitively unimpaired individual with only β-amyloid positivity (Table 1). The scenarios were deliberately stylized to highlight key distinctions in narratives under the AA and IWG assumptions. The narratives were formulated by the authors, informed by clinical experience and ongoing exchanges with patients and carers.

Use of LLMs

Following initial drafting, the clinician-patient dialogues were refined with the assistance of ChatGPT-5 (OpenAI) [20], which was used to fine-tune the wording, tone, and flow of the exchanges. ChatGPT-5 was not used to generate the conceptual distinctions between frameworks, determine diagnostic positioning, or contribute to the ethical or analytical interpretation of the scenarios. An example of this fine-tuning process – illustrating how ChatGPT-5 was used to evaluate and adjust the clinician-patient dialogues for realism and consistency – is presented in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552764). All AI-assisted outputs underwent expert review by the three authors to ensure conceptual coherence, clinical validity, and alignment with the diagnostic frameworks under analysis.

Literature Review

A targeted narrative literature review was conducted using PubMed and Scopus to provide the theoretical framework for constructing the simulated physician-patient conversations and interpreting their implications. The review had three primary objectives: (1) to clarify how the epistemic distinctions between disease, illness, and risk states play a role in the definition of physician-patient conversations in the context of AD diagnosis under the AA and IWG frameworks; (2) to identify communication challenges in disclosing biomarker results to cognitively unimpaired individuals; and (3) to assess the ethical implications of the different frameworks in shaping patient’s understanding of their condition, their engagement with the proposed care, and the public trust and understanding of AD. Relevant literature was identified through keyword searches in PubMed and Scopus (see online suppl. Table 2) and selected based on conceptual relevance to one or more of the review’s aims. To enhance the completeness of the search strategy, LLM platforms – Scholar Deep Research [21] and PubMedGPT [22] – were also employed to identify additional relevant keywords and adjacent thematic domains, which informed iterative refinement of the search strings applied in PubMed and Scopus. The LLM platforms were not used to filter, screen, or rank articles from the retrieved records. The initial search yielded 302,298 records. The first author screened titles and abstracts for relevance and selected 291 articles for full-text read and review (see online suppl. Table 3). Reasons for exclusion included languages other than English, French, Italian, or German; unrelated medical speciality; or lack of topical relevance.

Results

Physician-Patient Conversations: Differences between Diagnostic Frameworks

Current scientific literature (full texts of 291 articles reviewed; see final list in online suppl. Table 3 [2–5, 7–12, 14, 16–19, 23–299]) and clinical experience indicate that the AA and IWG frameworks converge substantially when applied to patients presenting with cognitive impairment. This is reflected in Scenario 1 (Table 1), in which biomarker positivity for both β-amyloid and tau pathology leads both frameworks to classify the case as AD. In such cases, the clinical conversation centres on disease prognosis and therapeutic strategies.

The two frameworks diverge when applied to cognitively unimpaired individuals. Our simulated physician-patient conversations (Table 1, Scenarios 2 and 3) and the current scientific evidence, particularly concerning the differentiation between disease and risk states and the challenges of biomarker disclosure in cognitively unimpaired individuals, highlight two forms of epistemic divergence: one largely semantic, with minimal clinical consequence, and one with more significant practical implications.

First Divergence

Cognitively unimpaired individuals with biomarker evidence of both β-amyloid positivity and neocortical tau positivity are classified as having preclinical AD in the AA framework and presymptomatic AD in the IWG framework. As illustrated in Table 1, scenario 2, this semantic distinction has little impact on clinical communication. In both cases a diagnostic label is applied, and the underlying assumed clinical trajectory is highly similar, thereby shaping overlapping narratives across both frameworks.

This strong alignment is supported by current scientific evidence. Longitudinal studies consistently show that cognitively unimpaired individuals who exhibit both β-amyloid and neocortical tau positivity are at markedly elevated risk of progression, with approximately 60–75% developing mild cognitive impairment or dementia due to AD within a 7-year period [23–26]. These findings indicate a robust and relatively consistent association of this profile with future AD-related cognitive impairment. In this context, a predominantly disease-oriented communicative framing may be proportionate to the magnitude and temporal proximity of the estimated risk, while still acknowledging residual uncertainty. Importantly, our literature review did not identify a fixed probability threshold that determines when disease-oriented communication is warranted; rather, such framing appears to be grounded in the strength of the available prognostic evidence.

Second Divergence

Consistent with themes identified in the literature regarding risk disclosure and diagnostic framing in AD, a more consequential discrepancy arises for cognitively unimpaired individuals who present with isolated β-amyloid positivity. Under the AA framework, such individuals are labelled as having preclinical AD, a designation that implicitly reinforces the presence of established disease. In our simulation (Table 1, Scenario 3), this framing orients the conversation toward disease confirmation, the availability of β-amyloid-targeting therapies, and the importance of managing vascular risk factors, even while acknowledging that such measures cannot reverse the underlying process.

By contrast, the IWG framework categorizes the same clinico-biological profile as asymptomatic at risk for AD. This formulation allows clinicians to contextualize the biomarker finding without assigning a disease label. The resulting conversation focuses on probabilistic risk, the substantial uncertainty inherent in isolated amyloid positivity, and strategies that may mitigate or delay the onset of AD-related cognitive impairment.

Current evidence underscores the limitations of the AA framework in this scenario. Indeed, population-based longitudinal studies indicate that cognitively unimpaired 65-year-old individuals with β-amyloid positivity have an estimated lifetime risk of only 20–40% for developing AD-related clinical symptoms [27, 28] – a level of risk that is increased relative to amyloid-negative individuals and that remains probabilistic rather than deterministic. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of clinical trajectories associated with isolated β-amyloid positivity and indicate that amyloid status alone does not provide a definitive prediction of clinical progression [29, 30].

In our simulated dialogue, the tension in the third scenario arises precisely from this disjunction: the physician, guided by a biologically grounded definition of disease, uses terminology that may be perceived as alarming or difficult to contextualize by some patients. In contrast, the IWG-guided conversation allows for a more transparent narrative, where risk is acknowledged, but the disease label is withheld in the absence of cognitive impairment and when the likelihood of developing AD-related symptoms remains probabilistic rather than deterministic.

Discussion

This study explored how the AA and IWG diagnostic frameworks shape clinical communication and patient experience in scenarios involving AD biomarker disclosure. Through a comparative analysis of simulated physician-patient dialogues across three clinico-biological profiles, we conclude that the most significant epistemic and communicative divergences arise in cases involving cognitively unimpaired individuals with isolated β-amyloid positivity. These differences are not merely semantic but reflect distinct assumptions about the purpose of diagnosis and have ethical implications that are detailed below.

Understanding the Role of Diagnosis within the Two Frameworks

Our assessment of the clinical scenarios and the epistemic assumptions embedded in each framework suggests that the two approaches diverge in their conceptualization of the broader communicative role of diagnosis in clinical practice. More specifically, they structure the clinician-patient interaction in different ways by orienting disclosure toward distinct epistemic and relational objectives. In fact, the AA framework adopts a biologically reductionist model, defining AD as a pathophysiological process that begins with β-amyloid deposition, regardless of the subsequent clinical trajectory. This formulation enables early identification and stratification in research contexts and supports biomarker-based classification in clinical trials. In clinical practice, the dialogue, within the AA criteria, tends to orient amyloid biomarker positivity toward confirmation of diagnostic status, anticipated disease trajectory, and integration into disease-management models. This framework detaches diagnosis from the patient’s lived experience and societal intersubjective realities, which in preclinical and low-risk stages may be one of normal function and minimal concern [31]. This mismatch may contribute to confusion and psychological distress when individuals are labelled as having a disease despite the absence of perceived impairment and the lack of established treatment options for cognitively unimpaired individuals.

By contrast, the IWG framework conceptualizes diagnosis as a clinical-biological construct. In doing so, it integrates the biological presence of disease with the individual’s subjective awareness of potential illness manifestations. Under this framework, amyloid biomarker positivity in cognitively unimpaired individuals is framed as a risk condition rather than as definitive disease. This framing shifts the communicative focus toward probabilistic forecasting, explicit acknowledgment of uncertainty, longitudinal monitoring, and discussion of modifiable factors. Furthermore, the IWG framework implicitly treats diagnosis not only as a biomedical categorization but also as a communicative and normative act that can contribute to the definition of a person's current and future personal, psychological, and social states. This perspective draws from and aligns with sociological understandings of diagnosis as a discourse that shapes personal identity, social roles, and access to care [31, 32].

These dual roles of diagnosis, as both a biomedical classification and a socially meaningful process, are increasingly acknowledged in contemporary clinical discourse. While traditional models often emphasized pathophysiology alone, a broader conception has gained increasing acceptance in several fields, including psychiatry. Notably, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) has incorporated dimensional approaches and contextual considerations that move beyond purely categorical diagnosis [33]. For example, disorders are now more often understood along spectrums, and diagnostic formulation encourages attention to psychosocial stressors, environmental context, and cultural identity [34, 35]. Although the DSM-5 remains grounded in a biomedical paradigm, these developments signal a shift toward recognizing diagnosis as a tool that not only reflects biological processes but also structures clinical communication and carries social consequences.

From this perspective, diagnostic communication is not a neutral transmission of biomedical information but a relational process. The way diagnostic information, including biomarker findings, clinical assessments, and diagnostic labels, is framed influences how individuals and patients interpret their present condition, anticipate their future, and negotiate identity and agency within the clinical encounter. Disease and risk narratives, therefore, do not merely describe medical reality; they structure the epistemic and experiential horizon of the consultation.

Applying this lens to AD, the IWG framework – by withholding the diagnostic label in cognitively unimpaired individuals with limited risk of developing AD-related cognitive impairment – allows for a more contextualized and transparent narrative in which risk is acknowledged, but disease identity is not imposed. This mitigates the risk of misalignment between scientific language and patient understanding [17, 32], as also indicated in our simulated scenario. Furthermore, it may also support patient trust and autonomy, fostering a more collaborative and transparent physician-patient relationship [31]. In this respect, the IWG framework represents an ethically informed diagnostic model that is patient-centred and that tries to reconcile the epistemic objectives of disease disclosure with its broader social implications.

Communication Challenges: Psychological Harm, Societal Implications, and Potential Erosion of Public Trust

One of the most significant ethical concerns surrounding the AA framework is the psychological burden placed on individuals diagnosed with AD based solely on amyloid biomarker positivity, despite the uncertainty of whether they will ever develop cognitive impairment, let alone dementia.

Indeed, patients, their families, and the broader public often equate an AD diagnosis with inevitable cognitive decline, increasing dependency, and loss of autonomy [12]. These concerns are reinforced by societal narratives that predominantly portray AD as a terminal and debilitating condition, focusing almost exclusively on its final stages [12, 36]. As a result, the lay perception of AD diverges significantly from the clinical and the more biologically oriented understandings of the disease [13].

When biomarker positivity is communicated within a disease-oriented frame, these pre-existing narratives may be activated prematurely, reinforcing expectations of deterioration even in clinically stable individuals. In the absence of a clear therapeutic course for asymptomatic individuals, such framing may feel abstract or disempowering and may make it more difficult for individuals to contextualize their risk, potentially leading to disengagement, decisional conflict, or persistent worry [37–39].

The communicative challenges explored above may be heightened in linguistic contexts where no distinction exists between disease – a biomedical condition – and illness – the subjective experience of that condition. This is the case for many languages, including Italian (malattia), Spanish (enfermedad), French (maladie), and German (Krankheit), where one term often encompasses both concepts [17]. In the context of biomarker-based diagnosis, this linguistic structure becomes particularly relevant. When isolated β-amyloid positivity is framed as disease in languages where the same term conventionally denotes both the biomedical condition and symptomatic cognitive decline, the distinction between probabilistic biological risk and manifest clinical illness may be less explicit in the exchange with physicians [17, 31, 40]. In such cases, diagnostic terminology may inadvertently narrow the perceived gap between biomarker abnormality and anticipated cognitive deterioration. This consideration does not imply that patients are unable to distinguish conceptual categories. Rather, it highlights how linguistic conventions may interact with diagnostic framing and culturally embedded narratives, particularly given the strong societal association of AD with progressive cognitive decline, to shape how biomarker findings are interpreted and integrated into personal understanding.

In this context, risk-based communication may encourage shared decision-making, align better with patient values, and maintain hope without fostering false expectations [39]. Such an approach aligns with findings from van der Schaar et al. [41], who reported that individuals receiving risk-based explanations responded with proactive engagement and constructive future planning.

In addition, the AA framework introduces a structural tension that may further erode patients’ and public trust. On one hand, it defines isolated biomarker positivity as sufficient for diagnosing AD; on the other, it explicitly advises against biomarker testing in asymptomatic individuals due to limited clinical utility and potential psychological harm [4]. This tension – between diagnostic assertion and clinical caution – can create confusion not only for patients but also for clinicians navigating how to responsibly communicate results. Furthermore, when patients discover they have been classified as having a disease with non-deterministic risk to develop symptoms and without approved therapeutic options, trust in the diagnostic process and in the scientific community may be eroded [31].

Moreover, the AA framework risks attributing the societal implications and burden of an AD diagnosis to patients who may never develop AD-related cognitive impairment. In fact, multiple studies have reported that receiving an AD diagnosis can lead to public and internalized stigma, difficulties in disclosing the diagnosis to family or employers, identity disruptions [31, 32, 42], and important long-term care insurance consequences [43–46].

In this context, the IWG framework, assuming a probabilistic approach, allows clinicians to engage in a transparent dialogue focused on modifiable risk, cognitive resilience, and individualized monitoring. By orienting the conversation toward uncertainty and future contingencies rather than fixed diagnostic categories, it supports patients in interpreting biomarker information without prematurely internalizing a disease identity. This may help mitigate both the psychological burden and broader societal implications of receiving an AD diagnosis within a context of non-deterministic clinical trajectories [32]. It may also foster a more accurate public understanding of current scientific knowledge and help preserve trust in medical communication and the scientific community [31, 32, 41]. Nevertheless, probabilistic disclosure introduces its own challenges. Empirical research demonstrates that limitations in health literacy and numeracy are prevalent among the general public, patients, and physicians and can significantly affect comprehension of statistical risk information, potentially leading to misunderstanding, heightened anxiety, or decisional conflict [47–49]. Moreover, the absence of consensus-driven guidelines on how to effectively communicate probabilistic biomarker findings may undermine the above-mentioned benefits.

In addition, the difficulty lies not only in the format of disclosure but also in unresolved conceptual questions regarding the meaning of disease, its distinction from illness, and the social implications of diagnostic labelling. In the absence of shared standards addressing both statistical uncertainty and the normative dimensions of disease attribution, patients may receive heterogeneous or ambiguously framed information, potentially affecting interpretation, decision-making, and trust in healthcare systems [48, 50].

Ultimately, the ethical and clinical merits of a risk-oriented framing will depend on the implementation of structured, evidence-based risk communication strategies – such as transparent discussion of uncertainty, use of absolute risk formats, and provision of longitudinal follow-up [43].

Emerging care models – such as the Brain Health Service (BHS) patient journey [51, 52] – may help address these issues. Rooted in risk stratification, lifestyle-based prevention, and shared decision-making, BHS promotes the responsible use of biomarker information to support patient empowerment, behaviour change, and advance care planning without reducing individuals to disease categories. Studies suggest that such risk-oriented conversations can be well-tolerated, foster motivation, and minimize psychological harm – particularly when supported by structured communication protocols and follow-up care [48, 53].

Limitations and Future Directions

We acknowledge that the narratives we propose are not the only possible ones and that our prior involvement in the development and application of the IWG diagnostic framework may have introduced bias. To mitigate this, the design of the conversations was informed by a scoping literature review and refined through our clinical experience and feedback from patients and carers. Furthermore, an LLM was used for linguistic refinement, enhancing the clarity, tone, and internal consistency of the dialogues. It is also important to note that there are still significant gaps in current research on risk disclosure within clinical settings [38, 48, 53, 54]. In particular, there is limited evidence-based guidance on communication strategies that effectively convey probabilistic risk while minimizing patient distress and misinterpretation [43, 51, 55]. Addressing these issues, especially in the context of direct-to-consumer availability of blood-based biomarkers, will require new clinical guidelines, stronger regulatory oversight, as well as new models of care such as the BHS. Furthermore, there is a need to establish interdisciplinary working groups involving representatives of patients and carers, clinicians, scientists, ethicists, and communication experts. These forums will provide a space to critically examine the use and implications of AD diagnostic criteria in clinical practice and research. Moreover, it will be important to include contributors from both the IWG and AA frameworks in these discussions, with the goal of fostering a more integrated and patient-informed approach to the development and communication of AD diagnoses.

Conclusions

In this paper, we emphasized that the differences between the AA and IWG diagnostic frameworks are not merely semantic; they have important implications for patient narratives and clinical transparency, and they can influence public trust in medicine. Diagnostic frameworks should thus be viewed as communicative and ethical instruments. In this light, the IWG approach, by deferring disease labelling in asymptomatic individuals and adopting a risk-based paradigm, may better preserve epistemic integrity, support patient autonomy, and foster public trust.
