COMPOSE: Composing Future Theorems from Citations and Formal Structure
David Busbib, Michael Werman

Abstract
A plausible future mathematical claim must satisfy two constraints: it should follow the direction of prior work and respect the formal dependencies that constrain what can validly follow. Existing approaches typically model only one of these sources, producing claims that are either weakly grounded or insufficiently motivated. We introduce grounded future mathematical generation, where the goal is to generate a plausible future theorem-like claim for an anchor paper using two complementary sources of context: its scientific citation graph and aligned formal theorem dependency graph. To address this setting, we propose COMPOSE, a dual-graph framework that conditions a language model on both scientific citation context and formal theorem structure. To support this setting, we construct a dataset of 108K paired scientific-formal graph examples from arXiv and Mathlib, together with a benchmark of 47K future papers from 2024–2025. Experiments show that COMPOSE outperforms strong baselines on retrieval to real future papers and achieves the best overall performance under LLM-judge evaluation, producing more grounded and mathematically richer outputs. These results show that future mathematical generation benefits from combining scientific context with formal structure. Project page is available at https://david-busbib.github.io/COMPOSE-page/ .

This challenge is reflected in two complementary sources of mathematical knowledge: scientific papers, which capture how ideas develop across the literature and indicate which directions are worth pursuing

, and formal theorem libraries such as Lean’s Mathlib, which encode mathematical results together with explicit dependency structure.

(

;
47
;

. Yet existing approaches tackle only one side: models trained on scientific text alone can identify plausible research directions but may generate claims that are not grounded in formal mathematical dependencies

theorem-proving systems

In this work, we propose
COMPOSE
, a system for generating plausible future mathematical claims from an anchor paper. This is carried out by jointly modeling scientific context and formal mathematical structure. To represent these two sources, we construct two complementary graphs:
(i)
a scientific graph built from the anchor paper, selected cited papers, and extracted theorems, and

(ii)
a formal graph built by aligning those theorems to corresponding formal theorems and their dependency structure. These two graphs are encoded separately and fused into a shared representation that conditions an LLM decoder on both the scientific direction of the papers and the formal structure of the underlying mathematics.
Figure
1
provides an overview of the full pipeline.
To learn this representation effectively, training proceeds in two stages: we first optimize the graph encoders and fusion module with alignment objectives, and then fine-tune the decoder for graph-conditioned generation. The model then generates a theorem-like mathematical text that represents a plausible contribution in the same research direction, grounded in both the evolution of ideas across papers and the dependency structure of formal mathematics.

To evaluate this setting, we construct a benchmark of 108K paired samples from arXiv mathematics using training papers collected from the pre-2023 corpus, together with a test set of 47K papers from late 2024 and 2025.

Empirically,
COMPOSE
achieves the strongest retrieval performance in our main evaluation setting and performs best overall under LLM-judge evaluation, with ablations confirming that both graph sources contribute.

We make the following contributions:

•

We study grounded future mathematical generation, where the goal is to predict plausible future mathematical claims using both scientific citation context and formal theorem dependencies.

•

We construct a dataset of 108K paired scientific-formal graph samples from arXiv and Mathlib, using an informal-to-informal alignment strategy via FrenzyMath, and establish a benchmark of 47K future papers from 2024-2025 for evaluation.

•

We propose
COMPOSE
, a system for grounded future mathematical generation that combines scientific citation context with formal theorem structure to generate plausible future mathematical claims.

Related Work

Future Scientific Generation.

Prior work studies future scientific generation in several ways.
One line of work generates future research directions from prior literature using retrieval or iterative retrieval-and-reasoning over related papers
(
5
;
6
;
31
)
. Another line of work uses citation structure or temporal supervision for the task
(
39
;
42
)
. GIANTS
(
16
)
uses citation-based scientific context to guide generation, although it targets future scientific summaries rather than theorem-like claims. Some work also explores structure-aware scientific generation using external graphs or chain-of-ideas frameworks
(
14
;
45
)
. Across these settings, existing systems rely on textual, citation-based, or structured scientific context, without combining the formal theorem structure that constrains what can validly follow from prior results.

Formal Mathematical Reasoning.

Formal proof libraries such as Lean’s Mathlib
(
27
)
, Isabelle’s Archive of Formal Proofs
(
30
)
, and Coq-based libraries
(
7
;
32
)
encode large collections of verified theorems together with their formal dependency structure
(
22
)
. This dependency structure is not just a record of what has been proved; it also helps determine what can be proved next. Retrieval-augmented and premise-selection systems use this structure to identify relevant premises for proof search, as in ReProver
(
46
)
and related work
(
1
;
41
;
28
)
. Formal theorem provers also use this library context to guide proof generation and search, including systems such as Baldur
(
11
)
and DeepSeek-Prover
(
44
)
, as well as more recent work
(
37
;
10
)
. Beyond proof search, formal libraries have also been used to conjecture what should be proved in the first place: Lemmanaid
(
2
)
proposes new lemmas by drawing analogies across existing theories, while related work studies conjecture generation, verification, and blueprint construction from library context
(
29
;
20
;
23
;
48
)
. Together, these systems show that formal dependency structure can inform not only how to prove but also what to prove. However, they operate entirely within the formal world, requiring formal input and producing formal output, without modeling the informal mathematical context found in research papers.

Scientific Informal-Formal Alignment.

In our setting, the alignment problem is to match an informal theorem from a research paper to its corresponding theorem in Mathlib. A natural approach is to translate informal mathematics written in natural language into formal proof-assistant syntax. Early work used large language models to map informal theorems into formal representations
43
;
4
;
26
. Later work improved Autoformalization with stronger training, retrieval-based methods, and math-specialized models, including Kimina
(
37
)
, Draft, Sketch, and Prove
(
19
)
, and ProofBridge
(
18
)
. Despite these advances, coverage on real paper-level mathematics remains limited because of the large vocabulary gap between informal exposition and formal syntax
47
. As an alternative, FrenzyMath
(
13
)
takes a different route by providing human-written informal descriptions for over 136K Mathlib theorems, enabling informal-to-informal retrieval as a pragmatic alignment strategy that avoids formalization altogether.
Recent work also suggests that linking informal theorem to formal libraries benefits from stronger retrieval and intermediate structure
(
26
;
19
;
18
)
.
Unlike these works, we do not treat informal-formal alignment as the primary objective. Instead, we use the resulting aligned structure to support grounded generation of future mathematical claims.

3
Method

Given an anchor paper, the goal is to generate a plausible future mathematical claim using its scientific and matched formal context. Our approach has three components. First, we construct a dataset that links scientific papers with formal theorem graphs
3.1
. Second, we introduce
COMPOSE
, a dual-encoder architecture that jointly models scientific and formal mathematical structure
3.2
. Third, we present our two-stage training
3.3
.

Inference overview.

At inference time, given an anchor paper, the model constructs two graphs: a scientific citation graph
G_{s}
, built from the paper and its contextually relevant cited papers, and a formal theorem dependency graph
G_{f}
, built from Mathlib theorems aligned to extracted mathematical theorems. Each graph is independently encoded by a dedicated GNN encoder. The two representations are then fused via cross-attention. The fused graph embeddings are passed as conditioning to our language model decoder, which generates a free-text theorem proposal.

3.1
Data Curation and Construction

Training our dual-graph model requires paired examples linking scientific context with formal theorem-dependency structure. We construct this dataset by linking mathematical papers collected via S2ORC
(
24
)
with formal theorems from Mathlib
(
27
)
.

On the scientific side, we begin with an anchor paper and construct outgoing citation edges for up to two hops, retaining at most 5 cited papers at the first hop and at most 3 cited papers per node for the second hop. Cited papers are selected by citation-context relevance, favoring citations that are discussed in important parts of the paper or used directly in its proofs or main results, rather than by including the full citation list; full selection details are in Appendix
A.1
. The graph contains
abstract
nodes for all included papers, together with
theorem
nodes extracted from papers in the first two citation hops. Each
theorem
node is connected either to its corresponding
abstract
node or to a parent
theorem
node. We use typed edges to distinguish citation links, paper-to-theorem links, and theorem-to-theorem links, allowing the graph to capture both external and internal citation context. For each example, we also identify a target paper: a paper that cites the anchor and was published after it, excluded from the input graph. Its main mathematical claim serves as the generation target.

On the formal side, we align extracted theorems to Mathlib theorems using dense retrieval over the FrenzyMath corpus
(
13
)
, which provides informal descriptions for 140k Mathlib theorems (see Figure
2
).
For each extracted theorem, we keep the top retrieved theorem only if its similarity is above a threshold
\tau
; weak matches are discarded. Each retained theorem serves as the root of the local formal dependency graph, which is expanded for a fixed number of hops using directed theorem-dependency edges extracted from Mathlib with LeanDojo
(
46
)
. Root nodes are marked with a distinct node type, while the remaining nodes represent dependency neighbors. A comparison with alternative alignment strategies and additional details about
\tau
is provided in Appendix
A.4
.

Starting from a corpus of 500K mathematical papers published between 2000 and 2023, this process yields approximately 108K paired paper-theorem subgraphs.
In addition, we define a temporally held-out benchmark of 47K mathematical papers from late 2024 and 2025, using their
abstract
and extracted main claim, as future target texts for evaluation.
Full details of the alignment procedure, target construction, filtering steps, and dataset statistics are provided in Appendix
A
.

3.2
COMPOSE

We first introduce notation, then present the encoder, fusion, and decoder components.

Formally, each example is represented as

x=(G_{s},G_{f},\mathcal{P}),\qquad G_{s}=(V_{s},E_{s}),\qquad G_{f}=(V_{f},E_{f}),

where
x
is the input context and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T})
is the target output sequence.

The scientific graph contains two types of nodes:

V_{s}=\{v^{\mathrm{abs}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\cup\{u^{\mathrm{thm}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},

where
v^{\mathrm{abs}}_{j}
denotes an abstract node corresponding to a paper in the scientific graph, and
u^{\mathrm{thm}}_{i}
are extracted theorem nodes. The edge set
E_{s}
contains directed citation edges between abstract nodes and structural edges connecting each theorem node either to its corresponding abstract node or to a parent theorem node.

The formal graph contains theorem nodes,

V_{f}=\{v^{\mathrm{thm}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{k},

where each node corresponds to a Mathlib theorem, and edge set
E_{f}
is given by dependency relations extracted from Mathlib.

Let
V_{s}^{\mathrm{thm}}=\{u^{\mathrm{thm}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
denote the set of theorem nodes in
G_{s}
.
The alignment set is defined as

\mathcal{P}\subseteq V_{s}^{\mathrm{thm}}\times V_{f},

where each pair
(u^{\mathrm{thm}},v^{\mathrm{thm}})\in\mathcal{P}
links an informal theorem node in
G_{s}
to its matched formal theorem node in
G_{f}
.

Each graph is processed by a dedicated GNN encoder. The two encoders share the same overall message-passing architecture
15
but differ in their node type and input initialization. In
G_{s}
, nodes are heterogeneous, with abstract and theorem nodes initialized from E5 embeddings
(
40
)
. In
G_{f}
, nodes correspond to formal Lean theorems and are initialized from DeepSeek-Math embeddings
(
33
)
of theorem signatures, chosen for their stronger representation of formal mathematical content. Both encoders use directed, edge-type-specific message passing, with incoming and outgoing messages aggregated separately. The resulting updates are combined with a learned gated residual connection to mitigate over-smoothing, followed by layer normalization. For each node, the GNN produces a structural vector that is concatenated with the node’s original text embedding, yielding contextualized representations
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{s}
and
\mathbf{h}_{j}^{f}
that capture both semantic content and graph structure. Full architectural details are in Appendix
B
.

Since
G_{s}
and
G_{f}
operate in different representation spaces, we project
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{s}
and
\mathbf{h}_{j}^{f}
into a shared latent space using the same learned MLP. Type embeddings are then added after projection to distinguish nodes from
G_{s}
and
G_{f}
. We apply a single bidirectional cross-attention block between the two node sets, with separate Q/K/V projections for the scientific-to-formal and formal-to-scientific directions, and residual connections with layer normalization applied after each directional update. The resulting fused node representations are denoted by
\{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|V_{s}|+|V_{f}|}
.

The fused node representations
\{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}\}
are provided as graph inputs to a pretrained math-specialized language model decoder adapted with LoRA
17
. Cross-attention layers are inserted at regular intervals, covering approximately 20% of the decoder layers. In these layers, the decoder hidden states serve as queries, while the fused graph node representations, projected to the decoder hidden dimension, serve as keys and values. This allows each generation step to attend over the full set of fused graph nodes, conditioning the output sequence
y
on both the scientific context of
G_{s}
and the formal structure of
G_{f}
.

3.3
Two-Stage Training

We train the model in two stages. The first learns graph representations that capture both within-graph structure and alignment between the scientific and formal domains, without using the decoder. The second adds the decoder and fine-tunes the full model for generation while preserving graph conditioning.

In the first stage, we train the scientific and formal graph encoders, together with the bridge MLP and fusion module, while keeping the pretrained text embeddings frozen. The resulting graph modules are then used to initialize the second stage. The first-stage objective is:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stage}_{1}}=\lambda_{link}\mathcal{L}_{link}+\lambda_{align}\mathcal{L}_{align}+\lambda_{cross}\mathcal{L}_{cross}

(1)

To ensure both encoders capture graph structure rather than collapsing to uniform representations, we supervise them with a link prediction loss. For each graph
G\in\{G_{s},G_{f}\}
, we define
a_{ij}=1
if
(v_{i},v_{j})\in E
and
a_{ij}=0
for non-edges , and optimize

\mathcal{L}_{link}=-\mathbb{E}\left[a_{ij}\log\sigma(\mathbf{h}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{j})+(1-a_{ij})\log\left(1-\sigma(\mathbf{h}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{j})\right)\right].

(2)

where
\mathbf{h}_{i}
and
\mathbf{h}_{j}
denote representations in the graph under consideration, and
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.

To encourage the fused graph representation to align with the scientific and formal content of the target, we use a contrastive alignment loss
(
36
)
. Let

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{G}=\mathrm{AttnPool}(\{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|V_{s}|+|V_{f}|})

(3)

denote the fused graph representation obtained by attention pooling over the fused node representations.

We construct a positive target representation
\mathbf{e}^{+}
from the held-out target paper by combining the embeddings of its abstract and main mathematical claim, after projecting both into the shared latent space. For each example, we construct negative target representations
\mathcal{B}^{-}
from papers that don’t cite papers in the current scientific subgraph, together with their main mathematical claim.

This objective encourages the fused graph representation to be close to the true held-out target and far from unrelated target papers. We then optimize

\mathcal{L}_{align}=-\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{G},\mathbf{e}^{+})/\tau)}{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{G},\mathbf{e}^{+})/\tau)+\sum_{\mathbf{e}^{-}\in\mathcal{B}^{-}}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{G},\mathbf{e}^{-})/\tau)}

(4)

where
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity.

Finally, to bridge the informal and formal spaces,
\mathcal{L}_{cross}
aligns each informal theorem node
u_{i}^{\mathrm{thm}}\in V_{s}
with its matched theorem node
v_{j}^{\mathrm{thm}}\in V_{f}
, where
(u_{i}^{\mathrm{thm}},v_{j}^{\mathrm{thm}})\in\mathcal{P}
. For each positive pair, we sample a set of random negative theorem nodes
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subset V_{f}
. We then optimize

\mathcal{L}_{cross}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(u_{i}^{\mathrm{thm}},v_{j}^{\mathrm{thm}})\in\mathcal{P}}\log\frac{\exp(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{s}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{j}^{f}/\tau)}{\exp(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{s}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{j}^{f}/\tau)+\sum_{v_{l}^{\mathrm{thm}}\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\exp(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{s}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{l}^{f}/\tau)}.

(5)

where
\tau
is the temperature hyperparameter.

In the second stage, we add a pretrained math-specialized decoder adapted via LoRA
(
17
)
and fine-tune the full model for graph-conditioned generation using two objectives: an autoregressive generation loss and a graph margin loss that prevents the decoder from ignoring the graph context. The autoregressive loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{CE}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},G_{s},G_{f}).

(6)

To prevent the decoder from ignoring the graph context and relying purely on language patterns, we additionally use a graph margin loss
\mathcal{L}_{margin}
that requires the model to assign lower generation loss to the correct graph pair
(G_{s},G_{f})
than to a mismatched pair
(G_{s}^{\prime},G_{f}^{\prime})
sampled from a different citation graph within the same mathematical domain.

\mathcal{L}_{margin}=\max\left(0,\,\mathcal{L}_{CE}(G_{s},G_{f})-\mathcal{L}_{CE}(G_{s}^{\prime},G_{f}^{\prime})+\delta\right)

(7)

The second stage objective is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stage}_{2}}=\lambda_{ce}\,\mathcal{L}_{CE}+\lambda_{margin}\,\mathcal{L}_{margin}

(8)

Additional alignment and training details are provided in Appendix
B
.

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

Data and Temporal Split.

The 108K paired scientific-formal graph samples are split 80/10/10 into training, validation, and test sets. To evaluate temporal generalization, we construct a future-paper benchmark from a corpus of 47K mathematics papers published in late 2024-2025. We retain only test-set anchor papers that are cited by at least one paper in the 47K future-paper pool, yielding 2K test samples. For each anchor, future papers that cite it are treated as targets.

Retrieval Evaluation.

Existing mathematical embedding models cluster future papers so tightly that the similarity gap between true target papers and randomly sampled unrelated papers is too small to reliably distinguish generation methods (see Appendix
D.1
). We therefore fine-tune a DeepSeek-Math embedding model on the 47K benchmark papers using a margin-ranking objective that pulls together papers with overlapping citation context and pushes apart unrelated papers. The resulting fixed model is then used as a shared retrieval evaluator for all compared methods.

Metrics.

We report Tgt-Sim, the mean cosine similarity between the generated text and its corresponding ground-truth future papers; Neg-Sim, the mean cosine similarity between the generated text and 500 sampled papers from unrelated subfields of the future pool; Gap = Tgt-Sim
-
Neg-Sim, which measures how much closer the generated text is to the correct future papers than to unrelated ones in the pool; and H@
k
, the fraction of examples for which a relevant future paper appears in the top-
k
retrieved results.
Exp-Sim measures similarity to the broader cluster of future papers that share citation context with the target, capturing whether the generated text lands in the correct research neighborhood rather than an unrelated area.
We also report Novel Mathematical Claim Prediction (NMCP) metrics for theorem-level grounding: Precision, the fraction of outputs aligned with the formal theorem target derived from the future paper; Match, the fraction of outputs aligned with the natural-language theorem target from the future paper; and Math, the ratio of mathematical tokens in the output.
To complement embedding-based metrics, we also evaluate generation quality using an LLM judge (see Section
D
).

Internal Variants.

We compare
COMPOSE
against several internal variants that remove or simplify parts of the proposed system: (1) Paper-graph-only, which uses only the scientific graph
G_{s}
; (2) Bag-of-Papers, which replaces the citation graph with a set of abstract and theorem nodes, removing graph structure and bypasses the two graph encoders; (3) Text-only (LoRA), which fine-tunes the decoder on our dataset using only a textual prompt, without any graph input; and (4) Prompt-only, which uses a richer prompt but does not use graph input or fine-tuning.

We evaluate all variants with both DeepSeek-Math 7B and Mistral 7B decoders to demonstrate that the gains from dual-graph conditioning are consistent across different backbone architectures.

Baselines.

Since no prior work addresses exactly the same task, we compare against the closest systems for future scientific generation and future-paper prediction. These include (1) GIANTS
(
16
)
, which uses its own Qwen-based decoder; (2) DeepSeek-Math-7B-based systems, including FutureGen
(
5
)
, GoAI
(
14
)
, and KG-CoI
(
45
)
, which use the raw DeepSeek-Math-7B decoder; and (3) GPT-4.0-based variants of GoAI, CoI, and ResearchAgent
(
6
)
, which we include as API baselines.
We also include a simple retrieval baseline: Fixed NN, which retrieves by nearest-neighbor similarity over abstracts and main theorems.
For all external baselines, we use the authors’ released code and follow the prompts described in their papers, adapting only the output request toward a mathematical claim.

6
Limitations and Future Work

Our method relies on approximate informal-formal alignment, which introduces noise into the formal graph encoder. More importantly, generated claims are not verified by a proof assistant, so retrieval and LLM-based evaluation remain proxy signals rather than direct tests of formal correctness. In addition, the benchmark depends on automatically identifying a main claim from each future paper, which may be noisy for papers with multiple results or less standardized theorem statements.

A natural next direction is to incorporate proof-aware verification so that generated claims can be checked directly against a proof assistant. This framework could also support iterative conjecture generation and proof attempts.

7
Conclusion

We introduced grounded future mathematical generation, a task that combines scientific citation context with formal theorem structure to generate future mathematical claims. To support this setting, we constructed a paired scientific-formal dataset and a temporally grounded benchmark over 47K future papers, and proposed
COMPOSE
, a dual-graph framework for graph-conditioned generation.

Experiments show that
COMPOSE
produces outputs more similar to real future claims than the baselines do, and LLM-judge evaluation supports this finding. Ablations further show that both graphs are necessary: the scientific graph identifies relevant research directions, while the formal graph keeps predictions grounded in valid mathematical dependencies.

A.1
Citation Subgraph Construction

Starting from the anchor paper, we follow outgoing citation edges hop by hop. We retain up to 5 cited papers at the first hop and up to 3 cited papers per node for the last hops. Candidate neighbors are ranked by citation-context relevance rather than sampled uniformly. Nodes without publication year metadata are discarded. Each paper node is represented by its abstract, encoded with E5-large-v2
40
.

Since not all citations are equally informative, we rank candidate neighbors at each expansion step relative to the current paper node. The ranking uses citation-context heuristics intended to capture relevance to the surrounding scientific development, including appearances in the abstract, introduction, main result claim, or proof-related passages; explicit dependency cues such as “using,” “by,” or “follows from”; repeated technical mentions; and proximity to extracted theorem. At each hop, we retain only the top-k candidates, yielding a compact citation subgraph that preserves the most relevant local scientific context.

A.2
Proof-Flow Graph Extraction

For papers within the first two citation hops, we extract a proof-flow graph from the full text. We identify theorem nodes corresponding to five types:
theorem
,
lemma
,

,

. Directed edges are added between theorem nodes when the text contains explicit dependency references, detected with regular expressions matching patterns such as “by Lemma 2.1” or “using Theorem 3.4”. We filter out malformed statement spans, including nodes that begin mid-sentence. Papers with fewer than three valid theorem nodes after filtering are discarded. The resulting graph is a directed acyclic graph that approximates the proof-flow structure of the paper.

A.5
Training Target Types

We use the same target-construction pipeline for both the training dataset and the future-paper retrieval benchmark. For each paper, we first try to extract its main mathematical claim from the body text using a set of heuristics, including regular-expression matching for phrases such as “we prove,” “we show,” “Main Theorem,” and early numbered theorem statements. When no single main claim can be identified reliably, we fall back to alternative targets such as the abstract or a supporting theorem with clear local context. This yields a consistent target-construction process across both training and evaluation, while handling different ways of stating the main claim.

Each training sample is paired with several target types derived from the same graph. These targets expose the model to different forms of mathematical output from the same underlying context, helping it learn richer graph representations instead of specializing to a single output style.

A
Claim
is the main mathematical result of the paper, extracted from sentences beginning with “we prove,” “we show,” or identified as Theorem 1 or Main Theorem in the text. An
Abstract
target is the full paper abstract. A
Lemma in claim
is a supporting lemma together with the neighboring theorems it depends on, providing local proof flow context.
Lean only
targets consist of the formal Lean signatures of the matched Mathlib theorems, and
Informal + Lean
combines the informal claim with those signatures. Training on this mixture encourages the model to learn graph representations that generalize across output types, which benefits Claim generation at evaluation time.

Appendix B
GNN Architecture Details

Both GNN encoders use the same architecture with separate parameters.
Each encoder consists of
L=2
message-passing layers with hidden dimension
d=1024
.
At each layer
\ell
, incoming and outgoing neighbor messages are mean-pooled separately
and summed to produce an aggregated message
\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\ell)}
:

\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\ell)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{in}}(i)|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{in}}(i)}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(\ell)}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{out}}(i)|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{out}}(i)}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(\ell)}.

(9)

The message is transformed by a linear layer followed by ReLU activation:

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{(\ell)}=\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{msg}}^{(\ell)}\,\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right).

(10)

The node state is then updated via a learned scalar gated residual connection:

g_{i}^{(\ell)}=\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{gate}}^{(\ell)}\!\left[\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}\;\|\;\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right]\right),\qquad\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}=g_{i}^{(\ell)}\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{(\ell)}+(1-g_{i}^{(\ell)})\,\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)},

(11)

followed by layer normalization. After
L
layers, a final linear projection maps each node
to a structural vector of dimension 128, which is concatenated with the frozen text embedding
to form the final node representation. Table
10
shows the training hyperparameters.

Appendix D
LLM Judge Evaluation

The LLM judge is based on five dimensions of mathematical quality: Mathematical Content, Technical Depth, Novelty, Precision, and Specificity.

Mathematical Content measures whether the output contains substantive mathematical statements rather than only prose. Technical Depth measures whether the claims are non-trivial and mathematically meaningful. Novelty measures whether the prediction introduces non-obvious ideas rather than simple restatements. Precision measures whether assumptions, objects, and conclusions are stated clearly and correctly. Specificity measures whether the output gives concrete mathematical details rather than vague high-level descriptions. Together, these scores measure both the mathematical content and the quality of the generated text. We report the average score for each dimension, along with the overall mean across them. The judge receives the generated prediction together with the input paper context and assigns scores on these five dimensions. The full prompt is shown in Figure
6
.

We use a GPT-4.0 version released before the benchmark period and apply the same prompt and scoring rubric uniformly to all compared models.

D.1
Embedding Space Analysis

Raw cosine similarity in mathematical embedding spaces is not a reliable retrieval signal in our setting. As shown in Table
13
, different embedding models produce broadly similar Tgt-Neg margins, and no single model consistently outperforms the others. Across embedding models, target and unrelated future papers remain too similar to separate generation methods cleanly.
In Table
13
, we report results using the DeepSeek-based COMPOSE variant rather than the Mistral variant.

This motivates us to fine-tune a DeepSeek-Math embedding model on the 47K benchmark papers for retrieval evaluation, using a margin-ranking objective that pulls together papers with overlapping citation context and pushes apart unrelated papers. Specifically, positive pairs consist of papers that cite the same anchor paper, while negatives are sampled from unrelated citation groups. The model is trained with a contrastive cross-entropy loss over cosine similarities scaled by temperature
\tau=0.05
