Polystyrene nanoplastics induce size-associated oxidative-inflammatory stress, hepatic insulin-signaling impairment and metabolic disturbances in normal and diabetic mice
Authors: Fang Xu, Yuyang Jin, Zhiqiang Sha, Yadi Yang, Shangwen Wang, Ying Chen, Jianzhong Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNanoplastics are emerging pollutants that can cross biological barriers and perturb metabolic homeostasis. Polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) are frequently detected in food and drinking water; however, their size-dependent metabolic effects and the modifying role of pre-existing diabetes remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate whether subchronic oral exposure to PS-NPs of different particle sizes altered hepatic insulin signaling and glucose-lipid homeostasis in normal and diabetic mice, and whether oxidative-inflammatory imbalance was associated with these responses.ResultsWe found that PS-NP exposure induced size-dependent disturbances in metabolic and hepatic signaling endpoints. Among the tested particle sizes, 25 nm PS-NPs generally showed the largest apparent changes across several inflammatory, oxidative-stress, glycemic and insulin-signaling outcomes, although direct between-size differences were not uniformly established for all endpoints. PS-NP exposure increased serum levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, CRP and TNF-α) and altered hepatic oxidative-stress markers, including the lipid peroxidation marker malondialdehyde (MDA) and antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, SOD and GSH-Px), suggesting systemic inflammatory activation accompanied by hepatic oxidative-stress imbalance. These changes were associated with suppression of the hepatic IRS1-AKT-PPARγ axis and downregulation of GLUT4 expression, consistent with impaired insulin signaling and metabolic regulation. Compared with normal mice, diabetic mice exhibited exacerbated oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, and metabolic disturbance upon PS-NP exposure, suggesting higher susceptibility of metabolically compromised hosts to PS-NPs. Exploratory SEM provided supportive evidence for an association between oxidative-inflammatory imbalance and hepatic insulin-signaling impairment, while downstream links to systemic metabolic disturbance require further validation. Exploratory multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and random forest (RF), suggested response patterns associated with metabolic background and particle size, but these analyses should be interpreted as supportive pattern-recognition tools rather than validated predictive models.ConclusionsThis study suggests that PS-NPs may act as metabolic stressors under the present experimental conditions, with their effects influenced by particle size and host metabolic background. By integrating classical toxicological methods with exploratory computational modeling, our findings provide mechanistic clues and preliminary data-driven evidence that PS-NP exposure may be associated with hepatic insulin-signaling impairment and metabolic disturbance, underscoring the need to assess nanomaterial safety in metabolically vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00867-4.Keywords: Polystyrene nanoplastics, Oral exposure, Oxidative-inflammatory, Metabolic disturbance, Structural equation modeling, Multivariate modeling

Background

Plastics production has increased continuously worldwide, and the environmental degradation of plastic waste has made microplastics and nanoplastics ubiquitous pollutants in modern ecosystems [1]. Polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) possess high surface reactivity and can interact with biological interfaces, which may enable them to cross intestinal barriers and reach the liver after oral exposure [2]. Nanoplastics have been detected in drinking water, table salt, processed foods, atmospheric dust and human biological samples, indicating that ingestion is an important route of exposure [3, 4]. Accumulating evidence suggests that nanoplastics may disturb metabolic and immune homeostasis [5, 6]; however, their organ-specific biological effects, particularly those related to hepatic metabolic regulation, remain incompletely understood. In addition, how particle size and host metabolic background jointly shape biological responses to PS-NPs remains unclear.

Experimental studies indicate that nanoplastics can induce oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, mitochondrial dysfunction and endocrine-related disturbances, while epidemiological evidence suggests possible associations with metabolic and inflammatory alterations [7]. The liver may be a relevant target organ for PS-NPs because of its central role in glucose and lipid homeostasis [8]. Ingested PS-NPs have been reported to cross intestinal and vascular barriers, accumulate in metabolic organs such as the liver, and interfere with insulin-signaling pathways-including IRS1, AKT, PPARγ, and GLUT4-that regulate glycogen synthesis, glucose utilization, and lipid metabolism [9, 10]. Previous mechanistic studies have reported that PS-NPs activate NF-κB signaling, inhibit Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant defenses, reduce AKT phosphorylation, and decrease GLUT4 abundance, thereby potentially affecting hepatic insulin responsiveness [11]. Animal experiments further show that exposure to micro- and nanoplastics may be associated with impaired glucose tolerance and altered lipid profiles [12]. Together, these findings support the hypothesis that PS-NPs may act as metabolic stressors.

Despite the rapid growth of research on PS-NPs, several important knowledge gaps remain [13, 14]. First, it is not yet clear whether PS-NPs produce size-associated or non-linear metabolic responses in vivo. Although non-linear size-effect relationships are increasingly recognized in nanomaterials research, in vivo evidence related to metabolic regulation remains limited. Second, obesity and diabetes are known to increase susceptibility to environmental pollutants, but it is unknown whether pre-existing metabolic dysfunction enhances PS-NP- associated oxidative stress and hepatic insulin-signaling impairment. Third, few studies have integrated conventional toxicological endpoints with exploratory analytical frameworks, such as structural equation modeling (SEM) and multivariate pattern-recognition approaches, to organize multi-endpoint datasets and generate mechanistic hypotheses. Consequently, the relationships among PS-NP exposure, oxidative-inflammatory imbalance, hepatic insulin signaling and metabolic disturbance require further clarification.

In this context, the present study systematically evaluated the metabolic and molecular effects of oral exposure to vehicle control or PS-NPs with nominal diameters of 25, 50 and 100 nm in normal and diabetic mice. By integrating biochemical measurements, indices of oxidative and inflammatory status, hepatic insulin-signaling analysis and targeted gene-expression assays, we characterized size-associated response patterns after PS-NP exposure. SEM was used as an exploratory framework to assess associations among oxidative-inflammatory status, hepatic insulin signaling and metabolic disturbance, while principal component analysis (PCA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA) and random forest (RF) were applied as supportive pattern-recognition tools. By directly comparing normal and diabetic animals within a unified experimental framework, this study aimed to evaluate whether PS-NP particle size and host metabolic background modify susceptibility to PS-NP-associated metabolic disturbance.

Materials and methods

Characterization of PS-NPs

The 25-, 50- and 100-nm PS-NPs used in this study were purchased from Zhongke Leiming (Beijing, China). All particles were suspended in normal saline. The physicochemical characterization of the PS-NPs is presented in Figure S1. Dynamic light scattering (Figures S1 A-C) revealed narrow, single-peak size distributions consistent with the nominal diameters. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Figures S1 D-F) showed a spherical morphology and uniform dispersion without apparent aggregation. All PS-NP suspensions carried negative zeta potentials ranging from − 13.9 to − 15.9 mV, indicating sufficient electrostatic repulsion for colloidal stability under aqueous conditions (Figures S1 G-I). Endotoxin levels were below the detection limit (< 0.05 EU·mL⁻¹). These measurements supported the suitability of the suspensions for subsequent comparative analyses.

Animal exposure system and experimental design

Six-week-old male ICR mice of specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Kunming Medical University and housed under SPF conditions at 22 ± 1 °C and 55 ± 10% relative humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunming Medical University (Approval No. kmmu20231118).

Normal and diabetic metabolic backgrounds were established before PS-NP exposure. To establish the diabetic model, mice were first fed a high-fat diet (HFD; D12492, 60% kcal from fat) for 3 weeks to induce insulin resistance. They then received intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ) at 30 mg/kg/day for 5 consecutive days [15]. One week after the last STZ injection, metabolic status was assessed after a 2‑h fast. Mice with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, together with impaired oral glucose tolerance and elevated HOMA‑IR, were defined as diabetic.

To confirm successful model establishment, fasting blood glucose, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR were compared between the normal and diabetic metabolic-background groups. Because model validation was performed before PS-NP exposure assignment, mice later allocated to the vehicle, 25-nm, 50-nm and 100-nm groups were pooled within each metabolic background for this comparison. Diabetic mice exhibited significantly higher fasting blood glucose, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, confirming successful induction of hyperglycemia and insulin resistance (Figure S2). Age-matched mice receiving standard chow and vehicle injections served as the normal metabolic-background group. After metabolic characterization, mice were randomly assigned to eight groups using a computer‑generated random number table: normal vehicle, normal 25-nm PS-NPs, normal 50-nm PS-NPs, normal 100-nm PS-NPs, diabetic vehicle, diabetic 25-nm PS-NPs, diabetic 50-nm PS-NPs and diabetic 100-nm PS-NPs. Investigators responsible for biochemical measurements, histological evaluation and molecular analyses were blinded to group allocation throughout data collection and outcome assessment.

PS-NPs with nominal diameters of 25, 50 and 100 nm were administered by oral gavage at a dose of 30 mg/kg/day (0.2 mL/10 g body weight) for 42 consecutive days [16]. Based on upper‑bound estimates of human dietary intake of micro‑ and nanoplastics and doses commonly used in rodent studies, the gavage dose of 30 mg/kg/day was selected to model a high‑exposure scenario and to facilitate detection of potential size‑associated effects of PS‑NPs. Vehicle-control mice received an equal volume of normal saline (vehicle control). Nanoplastic suspensions were vortexed and sonicated immediately before administration to ensure uniform dispersion. Throughout the exposure period, normal mice were maintained on standard chow, whereas diabetic mice were maintained on the high‑fat diet.

After the 6-week exposure period, mice were fasted overnight before sample collection. Blood samples were collected via retro‑orbital sinus puncture under isoflurane anesthesia. Serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. After euthanasia under anesthesia, liver tissues were rapidly excised, briefly rinsed with cold saline and blotted dry. Portions of liver tissue were fixed for histological analysis, whereas the remaining tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent biochemical and molecular analyses.

Biochemical assays

Six mice per group were used for biochemical assays (n = 6 biological replicates per group). The following parameters were measured in serum: FPG, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin, C‑peptide, and the inflammatory cytokines interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), C‑reactive protein (CRP), and tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α).

Approximately 50 mg of snap-frozen liver tissue was homogenized on ice and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Using commercial colorimetric kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions, the following parameters were measured in liver supernatants: hepatic lipid-related markers, including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C); oxidative-stress markers, including malondialdehyde (MDA), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px); and hepatic glucose-handling indicators, including hepatic glycogen content, glucokinase (GCK) activity and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) activity. All assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols, with two technical replicates for each sample.

Histological analysis

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The stained sections were evaluated in a blinded manner. Histological assessment was performed using a modified Ishak scoring system, namely the Histological Activity Index (HAI; total score, 0–18), which included components such as hepatocellular necrosis, ballooning degeneration and inflammatory infiltration. Detailed scoring criteria are provided in Table S1.

Western blot analysis of hepatic insulin signaling

For western blot analysis, three mice per group were randomly selected for assessment (n = 3 biological replicates per group). Liver tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes and probed with antibodies against IR, IRS1, AKT, PPARγ, GLUT4 and their phosphorylated forms. β-Actin was used as the loading control. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and phosphorylation ratios, including p-IRS1/IRS1, p-AKT/AKT and p-PPARγ/PPARγ, were calculated. Detailed antibody information is provided in Table S2. The representative cropped western blot bands shown in the main figures were derived from the corresponding uncropped blots provided in the Supporting Information.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

For qPCR analysis, three mice per group were randomly selected (n = 3 biological replicates per group). Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue using TRIzol reagent. RNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry, with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial reverse-transcription kit. qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System using SYBR Green chemistry to measure hepatic mRNA levels of IRS1, AKT, IR, PPARγ and GLUT4. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. β-Actin served as the internal control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Three technical replicates were run for each sample. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Statistical and multilevel computational analyses

Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the main effects of particle size and metabolic background, as well as their interaction effects. When overall effects were significant, Tukey’s post hoc test was performed for pairwise comparisons. In the figures, asterisks indicate comparisons between each PS-NP-exposed group and the corresponding vehicle group within the same metabolic background, unless otherwise specified. Direct between-size comparisons among the 25-, 50- and 100-nm exposure groups were not used as the primary basis for defining a formal toxicity peak; therefore, size-related findings are described as apparent response patterns. For integrated SEM and multivariate analyses, only animals with complete matched biochemical, western blot and qPCR data were included. Therefore, the effective sample size for models incorporating signaling and gene-expression variables was n = 3 biological replicates per group. No group-mean substitution was used. For SEM, composite indices were constructed to represent broader biological domains, including oxidative-inflammatory disturbance, hepatic insulin signaling and metabolic disturbance. Each index was calculated as the mean of the z-scored biomarkers assigned to that domain. For antioxidant enzymes, values were reverse-scored to ensure that higher scores consistently indicated greater disturbance. To avoid cross-loading and statistical circularity, each biomarker was assigned to only one index.

PCA, LDA and RF were used as exploratory pattern-recognition approaches to examine multilevel response patterns associated with PS-NP exposure. PCA was performed on scaled variables to visualize overall variation patterns. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was applied to evaluate the classification stability of the LDA model across different metabolic backgrounds. LOOCV was selected because it is commonly used in small-sample classification settings and can reduce optimism in accuracy estimation. The RF model was used primarily for variable-importance ranking rather than for building a validated predictive model. The RF model was built with 500 trees and default mtry settings. Out-of-bag (OOB) error rates were recorded as internal estimates of model performance and were interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size.

Results

PS-NPs induce systemic inflammatory responses and hepatic oxidative-stress alterations

To assess inflammatory and oxidative-stress responses to PS-NP exposure, we measured serum inflammatory cytokines and hepatic oxidative-stress markers across different particle sizes and metabolic backgrounds.

In both metabolic backgrounds, serum IL-6, CRP and TNF-α increased after PS-NP exposure, with the largest apparent mean values generally observed in the 25-nm groups (Fig. 1A-C). In normal mice, the 25-nm group showed the largest apparent increases in IL-6 and CRP levels, and a similar trend was observed for TNF-α. In diabetic mice, IL-6 increased in the 25-nm group and partially declined at 50 and 100 nm, although levels remained elevated relative to vehicle controls. By contrast, CRP and TNF-α showed sustained elevations at larger particle sizes. Two-way ANOVA confirmed significant main effects of metabolic state and particle size (p < 0.01), with moderate-to-large effect sizes (partial η² = 0.18–0.42; Table S4).

(A-C) Serum levels of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6, CRP and TNF-α. (D-G) Hepatic oxidative-stress biomarkers, including MDA, CAT, SOD and GSH-Px, expressed per mg protein. Bars represent mean ± SD (n = 6 biological replicates per group), and individual animals are shown as “×”. Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences compared with the corresponding vehicle control within the same metabolic state (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Vehicle control indicates physiological saline without PS-NPs

Oxidative-stress indices showed a partially overlapping but endpoint-specific response pattern (Fig. 1D-G). In both metabolic backgrounds, MDA levels increased significantly, with the largest apparent elevation observed in the 25-nm groups and sustained elevations also observed at 50 and 100 nm. CAT activity also showed the largest apparent increase in the 25-nm groups, followed by a partial decline at larger particle sizes. In contrast, SOD and GSH-Px generally showed mild and non-significant reductions across particle sizes, with limited recovery at 50–100 nm (Table S4). Particle size exerted strong effects on MDA and CAT (partial η² = 0.34–0.64, p < 0.001), whereas SOD and GSH-Px showed weak particle-size effects and non-significant interactions (Table S4).

Taken together, these findings indicate that PS-NP exposure was associated with systemic inflammatory activation and hepatic oxidative-stress alterations. Several inflammatory and oxidative-stress endpoints showed larger apparent responses in the 25-nm groups, particularly under diabetic conditions. These results suggest a potential association between inflammatory activation, hepatic oxidative-stress imbalance and PS-NP-associated metabolic disturbance, although the temporal sequence and causal relationships require further validation.

PS-NPs alter systemic glycemic indices, hepatic lipid-related markers and hepatic glucose-handling indicators

Following oral PS-NP exposure, changes in systemic glycemic indices, hepatic lipid-related markers and hepatic glucose-handling indicators were observed. Both FPG and HbA1c showed size-associated changes, with the largest apparent mean elevations observed in the 25-nm groups (Fig. 2A, B). Two-way ANOVA showed that particle size had moderate-to-large effects on FPG and HbA1c (partial η² = 0.516 and 0.302, respectively), whereas metabolic state exerted strong effects on these endpoints (partial η² = 0.927 for FPG and 0.361 for HbA1c). These findings suggest that both particle size and diabetic status contributed to glycemic disturbance (Table S5).

Hepatic lipid-related markers. PS-NP exposure altered hepatic lipid-related parameters. After oral exposure, hepatic TG and TC levels increased, with the largest apparent mean increases generally observed in the 25-nm groups, followed by partial attenuation at 50–100 nm (Fig. 2C, F). Particle size had a larger effect on TG (partial η² = 0.517), whereas metabolic state strongly affected TC and HDL-C (partial η² = 0.343 and 0.667, respectively). LDL-C exhibited a non-linear response pattern, with larger apparent reductions observed at both 25 and 100 nm in diabetic mice (Fig. 2D). HDL-C changed with particle size, and a significant metabolic state × particle size interaction was detected (p = 0.003, partial η² = 0.291), suggesting that the HDL-C response differed by metabolic background (Fig. 2E).

Systemic insulin-related indices and hepatic glucose-handling markers. PS-NP exposure was also associated with changes in systemic insulin-related indices and hepatic glucose-handling markers. In normal mice, fasting insulin levels decreased after PS-NP exposure, whereas in diabetic mice they remained relatively stable or showed slight increases. C-peptide levels changed only modestly across exposure groups (Fig. 3A, B). Because fasting insulin and C-peptide alone cannot distinguish altered insulin secretion from altered insulin clearance or β-cell compensation, these findings were interpreted as evidence of altered insulin homeostasis rather than as direct evidence for a specific mechanism. Hepatic glycogen content decreased after PS-NP exposure, with the largest apparent reduction observed in the 25-nm groups, suggesting reduced hepatic glucose storage (Fig. 3C). GCK activity increased in normal mice but showed limited change or reduced activity in diabetic mice, indicating metabolic-state-dependent regulation (Fig. 3D). G6PD activity increased after PS-NP exposure in both metabolic backgrounds, with larger apparent changes observed in diabetic mice (Fig. 3E), suggesting altered redox-related glucose metabolism.

Overall, PS-NP exposure was associated with alterations in systemic glycemic indices, hepatic lipid-related markers and hepatic glucose-handling indicators. The magnitude of these changes varied with particle size, and 25-nm PS-NPs generally showed the largest apparent changes across several metabolic endpoints in the present dataset. These findings suggest that PS-NP exposure was accompanied by hepatic metabolic disturbance, particularly under diabetic conditions. The ANOVA results highlighted two main features: first, particle size had a strong effect on glycemic and lipid-related outcomes (partial η² = 0.516; Table S5); second, diabetic status was associated with stronger overall responses to PS-NP exposure (partial η² = 0.927; Table S5).

These findings are consistent with a response pattern in which systemic inflammatory activation and hepatic oxidative-stress alterations were accompanied by hepatic metabolic disturbance after PS-NP exposure.

Hepatic histopathological alterations

H&E staining showed exposure-associated histopathological alterations, including ballooning degeneration, focal necrosis and localized inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4). These alterations appeared more evident in the 25-nm groups, particularly under diabetic conditions.

To further assess histopathological alterations associated with PS-NP exposure, the HAI scoring method was applied to H&E-stained liver sections from each group. Within the current dataset, HAI scores tended to be higher in the 25-nm groups than in the other particle-size groups, suggesting an association between this exposure condition and more evident histological alterations under the present experimental conditions (Fig. 5). Alterations in hepatic insulin-signaling pathways

Western blotting (WB) and qPCR analyses indicated exposure-associated alterations in hepatic insulin-signaling pathways following oral PS-NP exposure (Fig. 6). Two-way ANOVA at the protein level (Table S7) indicated significant main effects of both particle size and metabolic state on phosphorylation indices and key signaling proteins. Consistent with these analyses, size-associated reductions in phosphorylation-related signaling events were observed. The p-IRS1/IRS1 and p-AKT/AKT ratios decreased significantly in the PS-NP-exposed groups, with the largest apparent reductions observed in the 25-nm groups (partial η² = 0.46 and 0.83, respectively). Total IR abundance showed a similar response pattern, with a larger apparent reduction in the 25-nm groups and partial recovery at larger particle sizes (partial η² = 0.89). GLUT4 and p-PPARγ/PPARγ also showed non-linear response patterns, with the largest apparent suppression generally observed in the 25-nm groups within the current dataset. These reductions were consistent with impaired downstream glucose transport and transcriptional regulation.

At the transcriptional level, the qPCR results (Table S8) were generally consistent with the protein findings. Two-way ANOVA showed that the mRNA expression of PPARγ and GLUT4 was significantly influenced by particle size and metabolic state. The largest apparent reductions were observed in the 25-nm groups (PPARγ partial η² = 0.93; GLUT4 partial η² = 0.77). Transcripts of IRS1, IR and AKT also showed exposure-associated reductions across PS-NP-exposed groups, suggesting coordinated transcriptional alterations in insulin-signaling-related genes.

These results indicate that, under the conditions of the present study, the 25-nm groups generally showed the largest apparent changes in the hepatic IRS1-AKT-PPARγ axis and GLUT4 expression. These findings suggest that PS-NP exposure may weaken hepatic insulin signaling and downstream glucose transport capacity, particularly under diabetic conditions. Diabetic background was associated with greater overall impairment of hepatic insulin-signaling endpoints.

Exploratory SEM suggests an association between oxidative-inflammatory disturbance and impaired hepatic insulin signaling

To explore potential relationships among particle size, oxidative-inflammatory disturbance, hepatic insulin signaling and systemic metabolic disturbance, we constructed an SEM based on composite indices derived from standardized biomarker panels. This analysis was intended to generate testable hypotheses rather than to confirm deterministic causal relationships. A schematic representation of the final model, including standardized coefficients and significance levels, is shown in Fig. 7.

Exploratory SEM illustrating associations among PS-NP particle size, oxidative-inflammatory disturbance, hepatic insulin-signaling impairment and systemic metabolic disturbance. Particle size exposure was modeled as the exogenous variable, including vehicle control and PS-NPs with nominal diameters of 25, 50 and 100 nm. The oxidative-inflammatory index, hepatic insulin-signaling index and metabolic disturbance index were constructed as composite indices derived from standardized biochemical and signaling indicators. Path coefficients (β) are shown along each arrow, with solid lines indicating statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) and dashed lines indicating marginal or non-significant paths. The clearest observed association was the negative association between the oxidative-inflammatory index and hepatic insulin signaling (β = −0.698, p < 0.001). Marginal but non-significant associations of particle size with the oxidative-inflammatory index (β = 0.242, p = 0.085) and hepatic insulin signaling (β = 0.207, p = 0.059) were also observed. The model should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as confirmatory causal evidence

The SEM path coefficients are summarized in Table S10. A statistically significant negative association was observed between the oxidative-inflammatory index and hepatic insulin signaling (standardized β = −0.698, p < 0.001). The associations of particle size with the oxidative-inflammatory index (β = 0.242, p = 0.085) and insulin signaling (β = 0.207, p = 0.059) were marginal but did not reach statistical significance. The paths from the oxidative-inflammatory index (β = 0.313, p = 0.106) and insulin signaling (β = 0.047, p = 0.801) to the metabolic disturbance index, as well as the direct path from particle size to metabolic disturbance (β = −0.002, p = 0.987), were also non-significant.

To examine the stability and plausibility of the proposed structure, we compared four alternative models (Table S11). The final model (M1) was a just-identified model (df = 0) that provided a perfect fit by construction; therefore, its fit indices (CFI = TLI = 1.00, RMSEA = SRMR = 0.00) reflect its saturated nature rather than empirical confirmation. Reversing the direction between oxidative stress and insulin signaling (M2) resulted in poorer fit (χ² = 3.42, df = 1, RMSEA = 0.11). Adding a direct particle size-to-metabolic disturbance path (M3) produced a saturated model that was not parsimonious. A parallel predictor model (M4) showed acceptable but less interpretable fit (χ² = 2.88, df = 1, RMSEA = 0.09). Among these models, M1 was retained as the most parsimonious representation of the data. Consistent with its exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature, the model should be interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory.

Machine-learning analyses as supportive pattern-recognition approaches summarize metabolic response patterns associated with PS-NP exposure

Multivariate and machine-learning analyses, including PCA, LDA and RF, were used as supportive pattern-recognition approaches to complement conventional statistical analyses (Fig. 8). These analyses suggested metabolic response patterns associated with PS-NP exposure, metabolic background and particle size. Considering the modest effective sample size of the integrated dataset, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Unsupervised pattern discovery by PCA. The PCA plot showed separation between normal and diabetic mice. PC1 explained 37.6% of the total variance and mainly captured variation attributable to metabolic background. PC2 explained 18.0% of the total variance and reflected variation in oxidative-inflammatory markers, primarily driven by high loadings of MDA, CAT and related variables. The PCA loading matrix is provided in Table S12. These results suggest that metabolic background was the primary source of inter-group variation, whereas oxidative-inflammatory markers contributed to a secondary dimension of variation. Particle size appeared to contribute less strongly to the overall PCA structure, as most variance was attributable to metabolic background rather than particle size.

Supervised discrimination of metabolic background by LDA. LDA achieved a training accuracy of 64.6% and a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) accuracy of 62.5% in discriminating normal from diabetic metabolic backgrounds, representing moderate classification performance. The confusion matrices for the training set and LOOCV are presented in Table S13. Despite the moderate classification accuracy, these results suggest that the integrated molecular and metabolic profiles contained detectable metabolic-background-related signals. This finding requires further validation in larger sample sets.

Variable-importance ranking by RF. RF analysis was used for variable-importance ranking rather than for building a validated predictive model. In the RF1 model comparing normal and diabetic mice, FPG, GLUT4, MDA, HDL-C, PPARγ and insulin-signaling proteins, including IR, IRS1 and AKT, were ranked among the variables with higher importance. In the RF2 model for exposure-group classification, including vehicle control and 25-, 50- and 100-nm PS-NP exposure groups, variables with higher importance included p-PPARγ, AKT phosphorylation, GLUT4, PPARγ mRNA, inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6 and TNF-α) and G6PD. The OOB error rate was 0% for RF1 and 4.17% for RF2 (Table S14). Given the modest effective sample size of the integrated dataset and the absence of external validation, these low OOB error rates should be interpreted cautiously and may overestimate generalizable predictive performance.

Integrated interpretation. Taken together, these exploratory analyses suggest that metabolic status primarily shaped the overall clustering pattern, whereas oxidative-stress and insulin-signaling markers contributed substantially to group separation. Particle size, particularly 25-nm PS-NPs within the current dataset, was associated with a particle-size-associated molecular pattern superimposed on the metabolic background. However, these findings are exploratory in nature and require independent validation.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated how oral exposure to different particle sizes of PS-NPs was associated with glucose-lipid metabolism in normal and diabetic mice within an integrated toxicological framework. By combining biochemical measurements, histological evaluation, hepatic signaling analyses, exploratory SEM and supportive machine-learning analyses, we observed a consistent response pattern in which PS-NP exposure was associated with systemic inflammatory activation, hepatic oxidative-stress alterations, hepatic insulin-signaling impairment and metabolic disturbances. The amplified responses observed in diabetic mice suggest that pre-existing metabolic dysfunction may increase susceptibility to PS-NP-associated metabolic disturbance. These findings are consistent with a possible link among inflammatory activation, hepatic oxidative-stress imbalance, hepatic insulin-signaling impairment and metabolic disturbance, although the temporal sequence and causal relationships require further validation.

PS-NP exposure was associated with coordinated molecular and metabolic alterations. Specifically, PS-NP exposure increased serum inflammatory cytokines and altered hepatic oxidative-stress markers, with 25-nm PS-NPs generally showing the largest apparent responses across several endpoints. These changes were accompanied by impaired hepatic insulin-signaling markers, characterized by reduced phosphorylation of IRS1 and AKT, downregulation of the IRS1-AKT-PPARγ axis and decreased GLUT4 expression. Similar links among oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, insulin-signaling impairment and metabolic dysregulation have been reported for other environmental pollutants and metabolic stressors [17, 18]. Consistent with altered hepatic insulin signaling, we also observed reduced hepatic glycogen content and changes in GCK and G6PD activities, suggesting alterations in hepatic glucose handling and redox-related metabolism [18]. Together, these findings support a possible link among systemic inflammatory activation, hepatic oxidative-stress imbalance, hepatic insulin-signaling impairment and metabolic disturbance.

Our results suggest an apparent size-associated response pattern, with 25-nm PS-NPs showing larger apparent biological responses within the current dataset. Previous studies have reported that nanoparticles of approximately 20–40 nm may exhibit relatively efficient cellular uptake, often through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and may interact with subcellular organelles such as mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum [19, 20]. Larger particles (> 50 nm) may show lower uptake efficiency unless specific receptor-mediated pathways are strongly activated [19, 20]. Among the tested particle sizes, 25-nm PS-NPs generally showed larger apparent changes in inflammatory, oxidative-stress and insulin-signaling endpoints in the current dataset. This response pattern suggests that particle size may influence PS-NP-associated biological responses through several intertwined physicochemical and biological mechanisms. Under the present experimental conditions, 25-nm PS-NPs may fall within a particle-size range associated with relatively efficient cellular interaction, potentially increasing intracellular stress-related responses. In addition, smaller particle size increases the surface-area-to-volume ratio, which may increase the availability of reactive surface sites for protein adsorption and redox interactions [21]. Particle size may also influence the composition and thickness of the protein corona, thereby modulating subsequent biological interactions [22]. Smaller particles have been reported to form more heterogeneous coronas that may facilitate cellular recognition, whereas larger particles may form more compact coronas with comparatively lower reactivity [21, 22]. These mechanisms may help explain the relatively prominent responses observed in the 25-nm groups under the present experimental conditions.

The results of this study suggest that host metabolic background influences PS-NP-associated biological responses, particularly those related to glucose-lipid metabolism. Compared with normal mice, diabetic mice showed a higher oxidative-stress burden, reduced insulin-signaling responsiveness and lower metabolic buffering capacity, which may contribute to their greater susceptibility to PS-NP-associated metabolic disturbance. Inflammatory, oxidative-stress and insulin-signaling responses were more evident in diabetic mice, suggesting that host metabolic status may modify the biological effects of PS-NP exposure. This interpretation is consistent with recent animal studies. Zhang et al. reported that oral PS-NP exposure further impaired beige adipose function, aggravated energy metabolism disorders and promoted hepatic steatosis in high-fat-diet-fed mice, suggesting that PS-NP-associated interference may be enhanced under increased metabolic load [14]. Similarly, Wang et al. found that PS-NPs with different functionalized surfaces worsened insulin resistance, disrupted AKT signaling, reduced hepatic glycogen synthesis and induced more evident glucose-tolerance abnormalities in a mouse model of type 2 diabetes, whereas these effects were less apparent under normal metabolic conditions [23]. These findings suggest that metabolic dysfunction may act not only as a background condition but also as a susceptibility-modifying factor in nanoplastic-associated metabolic responses. In addition, Hsiao et al. emphasized that micro-/nanoplastic exposure may contribute to diabetes and metabolic syndrome through oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, mitochondrial damage and lipid-metabolism dysregulation, and that individuals with pre-existing metabolic abnormalities may be more vulnerable to these pathways [9]. In the present study, the enhanced oxidative-inflammatory response, greater insulin-signaling impairment and more evident hepatic metabolic disturbance observed in diabetic mice were consistent with these proposed mechanisms. Taken together, these findings support the view that PS-NP-associated metabolic responses may be influenced by both particle-size characteristics and host metabolic background, and that metabolically vulnerable conditions, such as diabetes, obesity and high-fat-diet exposure, may increase susceptibility to nanoplastic-associated metabolic disturbance.

This study provides an exploratory integrative framework that combines classical toxicological endpoints with statistical and computational modeling to organize multi-endpoint responses after PS-NP exposure. First, the study compared vehicle control and three PS-NP particle sizes, namely 25, 50 and 100 nm, within a unified exposure framework and assessed their associations with glucose-lipid metabolic indicators. Within the current dataset, 25-nm particles generally showed larger apparent biological responses across several endpoints. Second, the study directly compared two metabolic backgrounds and found that diabetic mice showed greater PS-NP-associated responses, suggesting metabolic-state-dependent susceptibility. Third, the study integrated multiple analytical layers, including biochemical profiling, histological assessment, hepatic signaling analysis, exploratory SEM, PCA, LDA and random forest modeling, to summarize response patterns rather than to establish a definitive causal pathway. Together, these findings provide a useful exploratory framework for interpreting how particle size, host metabolic background and oxidative-inflammatory imbalance may jointly contribute to PS-NP-associated biological responses.

The interdisciplinary approach used in this study provided complementary support for interpreting multi-endpoint response patterns. Classical biochemical assays were used to assess metabolic disturbances at systemic and hepatic levels, whereas histological evaluation, western blotting and qPCR provided information on liver histopathological alterations and hepatic insulin-signaling endpoints. SEM, as an exploratory tool, enabled the simultaneous assessment of oxidative-inflammatory disturbance, hepatic insulin signaling and metabolic outcomes, thereby generating hypothesis-driven associations within an integrated framework. However, SEM was not intended to establish a confirmed causal pathway. Machine-learning methods, used as supportive pattern-recognition tools, provided complementary analytical perspectives. PCA showed separation mainly driven by metabolic background, with oxidative-inflammatory variables contributing to secondary variation. LDA distinguished normal and diabetic groups with moderate accuracy, suggesting that the integrated molecular and metabolic profiles contained detectable metabolic-background-related signals. RF further ranked the importance of individual variables and identified several markers associated with group separation, including oxidative-stress indicators, insulin-signaling-related proteins and glucose-lipid parameters. Taken together, the combined use of PCA, LDA and RF provided a multi-angle exploratory framework for summarizing response patterns associated with PS-NP exposure, particle size and metabolic background. Nonetheless, the SEM and machine-learning results should be interpreted with caution. The near-perfect fit of the final SEM reflects its just-identified structure and limited degrees of freedom rather than empirical confirmation of a causal model. Alternative model specifications, presented in the Supporting Information, generally showed poorer fit or reduced parsimony, supporting the selected structure as one parsimonious representation of the current dataset. Similarly, PCA, LDA and RF were used to complement conventional analyses and should not be interpreted as externally validated predictive models.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size differed across endpoint categories. Biochemical endpoints were assessed using six biological replicates per group, whereas western blotting, qPCR and integrated analyses involving these variables were based on a smaller effective sample size. This modest sample size may have limited the ability to detect subtle interactions, especially for models incorporating signaling and gene-expression variables. Second, the LDA model was built on a restricted integrated dataset and therefore its classification performance should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than predictive. Third, the oxidative-inflammatory construct in the SEM was inferred mainly from inflammatory cytokines, lipid peroxidation markers and antioxidant enzyme activities rather than from direct redox measurements. Future work should incorporate direct ROS quantification, more comprehensive redox indicators, such as the GSH/GSSG ratio, and redox-omics approaches. Fourth, the exposure duration represents a subchronic scenario and may not fully capture chronic, low-level exposure patterns under real-world human conditions. We also did not assess nanoparticle biodistribution, gut-liver axis alterations, microbiome shifts or epigenetic modifications, although these factors may influence metabolic responses. To refine the proposed mechanistic framework, future studies would benefit from longitudinal designs, environmentally relevant lower-dose exposure settings and multi-organ integrative approaches.

The oral exposure dose used in this study (30 mg/kg/day) is higher than current estimates of human environmental intake. Nevertheless, similar or even higher doses are commonly used in mechanistic nanoplastic toxicology studies to explore potential biological mechanisms rather than to directly mimic environmental exposure levels. At low doses, nanoplastics may undergo rapid clearance and show limited gastrointestinal retention; therefore, higher doses are often used in experimental models to increase the detectability of biological responses [24–26]. Human biomonitoring studies have detected plastic particles, including nanoscale fractions, in blood and placental tissue, suggesting that systemic dissemination may occur under chronic exposure conditions. In addition, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling suggests that repeated ingestion may lead to tissue accumulation of nanoplastics, especially for smaller particles [27–29]. Although the present dose should be interpreted within a mechanistic high-exposure context, the observed size-associated response patterns may help inform future low-dose and chronic-exposure studies relevant to environmental risk assessment.

Nanoplastic contamination is likely to increase globally because of the continued growth of plastic production and the fragmentation of legacy plastic waste. At the same time, the prevalence of diabetes, obesity and other metabolic disorders is increasing worldwide. The convergence of these trends raises the possibility that nanoplastic exposure and metabolic vulnerability may interact and contribute to metabolic health risks. Our findings suggest that PS-NPs may warrant consideration not only as environmental pollutants but also as potential metabolic stressors, particularly under metabolically susceptible conditions. Further elucidation of the combined effects of nanoplastic exposure and metabolic disorders, as well as evaluation of whether specific particle-size ranges, such as 25 nm under the present experimental conditions, reproducibly show enhanced biological responses, may help refine nanosafety assessment and inform future prevention-oriented research in metabolically vulnerable populations.

Conclusion

The present study shows that oral PS-NP exposure is associated with systemic inflammatory activation, hepatic oxidative-stress alterations, hepatic insulin-signaling impairment and metabolic disturbances in mice. Among the tested particle sizes, 25-nm PS-NPs generally showed the largest apparent effects across several endpoints under the present experimental conditions. Diabetic mice exhibited greater adverse responses, suggesting that pre-existing metabolic dysfunction may increase susceptibility to PS-NP-associated metabolic disturbance. By integrating conventional toxicological measurements with exploratory SEM and multivariate analyses, our findings provide supportive evidence that PS-NP-associated metabolic responses are influenced by particle size and host metabolic background. Further studies using direct biodistribution analysis, targeted mechanistic validation, formal size-response modeling and environmentally relevant exposure designs are needed.
