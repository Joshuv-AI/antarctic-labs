Executive Summary
  • Ethanol is clearly a viable fuel for ships, based on the introduction of methanol dual-fuel
 engines more than a decade ago.
 o Properties are very similar to those of methanol, such that methanol infrastructure
 and experience can accelerate ethanol adoption.
 o Ethanol has significant advantages over methanol, including 35% higher energy
 content, lower toxicity and corrosivity, and more rapid biodegradation if spilled
 into the ocean. Additionally, bioethanol is available today at scale and at a much
 lower price than biomethanol.
 o Large dual-fuel methanol engines can operate on ethanol with no changes. The
 approximately 112 to 120 methanol dual-fuel ships in operation today could
 consume 6 million metric tons (mt) or 2 billion gallons of ethanol annually if
 operated full time on this fuel.
 o Several trial voyages were conducted using ethanol in late 2025 and 2026. In the
 summer of 2026, commercial ship-to-ship bunkering of ethanol was initiated.
  • The global market for fuels in marine shipping is massive, with an estimated 259 million
 mt (86 billion gallons assuming 330 gal/mt for heavy fuel oil) consumed in 2023.
  • A significant driver for the use of alternative fuels in shipping, including ethanol, is the
 International Maritime Organization’s Net-Zero Framework. This framework recognizes
 170 different fuel pathways, including 3 ethanol pathways. Regional regulations such as
 Fuel EU Maritime are also significant drivers.
  • An International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standard for ethanol to be used
 in shipping is needed. ISO has already initiated work on this standard. U.S. ethanol
 industry participation is critical to ensure that the standard is based on modern scientific
 information on corn ethanol.
  • A key uncertainty is an assessment of needed supply chain infrastructure development,
 supply chain volatility, and costs:
 o What infrastructure is needed to supply ethanol at key U.S. and international
 ports?
 o What are key U.S. ports for ethanol bunkering?

Introduction
Roughly 80% of global trade occurs via marine shipping, primarily utilizing heavy fuel oil
(HFO) or very low sulfur fuel oil (VLSFO) [1]. Marine shipping is by far the most efficient and
lowest-cost approach to moving cargo. In part, this efficiency is achieved because of the huge
scale in modern shipping, with the largest container ships capable of carrying more than 20,000
twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU; a single 20-foot-long container) [2]. These can carry up to 5
million gallons (16,000 metric tons [mt] 1) of HFO for propulsion of the ship [3].

Marine shipping is regulated by the International Maritime Organization (IMO), a United
Nations agency charged with maritime safety, security, and prevention of marine pollution from
ships; reducing air pollutant emissions such as sulfur oxides (SOx), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and
particulate matter (PM, or soot) [4]; reducing the impact of fuel spills on the environment; and
potentially in the future reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions [5].

A potentially low-cost approach to meeting all these objectives is to switch from conventional
HFO to cleaner biofuels. For example, biodiesel is approved for use as B100 in many ships [6]
and in the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 8217:2024 standard for marine
diesel and residual fuels. Additionally, methanol and ethanol are approved for use in vessels
modified or built specifically to handle, store, and burn them [7, 8, 9]. This document provides a
summary of U.S. ethanol production and uses, reviews information on the use of ethanol in
shipping, and summarizes remaining challenges and barriers.

IMO estimates that shipping overall consumed 4.2 million barrels per day or 64 billion gallons
(211 million mt) of fuel per year in 2023 [10]. However, this number is for international
shipping, and ships greater than 5,000-mt gross weight, that are required to report fuel use to
IMO. This number is underreported in that only 28,620 ships out of 35,143 (81.4%) provided
data to IMO in 2023. Extrapolation to full reporting suggests a total of 259 million mt (86 billion
gallons). Other estimates place the total at more than 300 million mt annually because the IMO
estimate does not include smaller ships or domestic shipping [11].

Accounting for the large difference in energy content between HFO and ethanol, in the extreme
case of all ships running on ethanol (less 5% for liquefied natural gas [LNG] ships and less 5%
for diesel/fuel oil pilot to ignite the ethanol), the 259-million-mt market would be as large as 355
million mt of ethanol (118 billion gallons). While this extreme case is not likely to be possible,
clearly the marine market represents a huge opportunity for ethanol.

IMO Net-Zero Framework
The IMO Net-Zero Framework (NZF) is based on IMO’s 2023 GHG strategy, which was
adopted by the Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC.377(80)) [12]. Targets
include reducing carbon intensity by at least 40% by 2030 against a 2008 baseline, decreasing
total annual GHG emissions by no less than 20% by 2030 and 70% by 2040, and achieving net
zero for total emissions by around 2050. The strategy requires at least 5% of energy used in
shipping to be supplied by zero- or near-zero-GHG technology or fuel by 2030.

The framework includes a global fuel standard that gradually reduces how much GHG is emitted
for each unit of energy used on a life cycle basis, a pricing mechanism allowing ship owners to
earn credit if they exceed the GHG emission limits, and purchase credits if they fail to meet the
requirements [5]. To be used for compliance with the IMO NZF, fuel life cycle emissions must
be verified using procedures recognized by IMO’s Marine Environmental Protection Committee
[13, 14].

Ship emissions are quantified in terms of GHG fuel intensity (GFI), a measure of GHG
emissions per unit of energy that is very similar to what has been called carbon intensity in other
contexts. It has units of grams of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) per megajoule, where CO2eq
means that it includes methane and nitrous oxide emissions. A zero- or near-zero-emissions fuel
is defined as having a GFI of no more than 19.0 gCO2eq/MJ—80% lower than the current
average of 93.3 gCO2eq/MJ. The NZF is a primary driver for increased use of lower-GFI fuels
such as ethanol, along with regional regulations such as Fuel EU Maritime. Notably, there may
be some adoption of alternative fuels in harbor craft and inland marine vessels to reduce air
pollutant emissions such as SOx, NOx, and PM.

These regulations would apply to international shipping and ships greater than 5,000 mt gross
weight. These ships are believed to emit 85% of GHGs emitted by marine shipping. As with all
IMO regulations, the NZF would be enforced by the 176 IMO member states.

For a fuel to be considered in the IMO NZF, it must have a GFI calculated from well-to-wake
life cycle analysis using IMO-approved methodology [14]. There are currently 170 pathways that
IMO is considering for default the GFI, including 3 ethanol pathways.

Ethanol Background
Ethanol, also known as ethyl alcohol, is produced from biomass and used for various purposes in
transportation, chemical, and pharmaceutical industries. It is produced by fermentation of sugar,
which can be derived from starch or cellulose. In the United States, ethanol is mostly produced
from corn starch. Ethanol has a long history as a fuel component. For example, the original Ford
Model T, produced in 1908, was a flexible-fuel vehicle with carburetors that could be adjusted to
use ethanol, gasoline, or a mixture of the two [15]. More recently, the energy crisis of the 1970s
led to the introduction of gasoline with 10 vol % ethanol, and legislation exempted ethanol
blends from part of the federal highway tax. The phaseout of tetraethyl lead in gasoline during
the 1980s generated interest in the use of ethanol as a high-octane blendstock. In the 1990s,
oxygenates, including ethanol, were mandated in gasoline to reduce carbon monoxide and
volatile organic compound emissions. However, methyl tert-butyl ether dominated the oxygenate
market until it was phased out beginning in March 2000 [16]. Since that time, ethanol blending
has grown to the point where more than 95% of gasoline consumed in the United States contains
10% ethanol [17]. In January 2011, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency approved the use
of E15 in 2001 and newer model year automobiles [18], and use of E15 is becoming more
common [19, 20].

Global fuel ethanol production exceeded 31 billion gallons (117 billion liters, or 93 million mt)
in 2024 [21] and is forecast to exceed 140 million mt by 2034 [22]. The United States was the
largest producer at 16 billion gallons, with Brazil in second place at 9 billion gallons. India, the
European Union, and China each produced more than 1 billion gallons.

The United States has about 2 billion gallons per year of idled production capacity (nearly 6
million mt). Ethanol is primarily manufactured in the Midwestern United States and transported
nationwide by rail, commonly as unit trains of 100 × 30,0000-gal ethanol tank cars
(approximately 9,000 mt). About 1.8 billion gallons were exported in the marketing year from
September 2023 to August 2024, and more than 2.1 billion gallons in 2025. Canada, the
European Union, India, and the United Kingdom are the largest destinations [23]. Exports by
ship were predominantly from the Houston area. The United States imports very little ethanol;
for example, only 4 million gallons were imported in 2024, primarily from Brazil [24]. Brazil
exports around 700 million gallons (2.6 billion liters) of ethanol annually, with South Korea and
the Netherlands as major customers [25].

A 2021 review examined many studies of the carbon intensity of corn ethanol in the United
States, highlighting several factors that have changed over time [26]. The study found that
market factors had led to reduced fertilizer and diesel fuel use on farms and more efficient use of
energy in ethanol plants and presented a state-of-the-art assessment of land use change effects.
The study concluded that a best estimate of ethanol carbon intensity is 51.4 gCO2eq/MJ—46%
lower than for petroleum fuels. The largest component was CO2 from ethanol production, where
about one-third of the carbon in feedstock sugar is converted to CO2. Ethanol producers are
increasingly adopting sequestration of CO2, which qualifies for a recently enacted tax credit.
Sequestration may reduce carbon intensity by as much as 30 gCO2eq/MJ [27].

Current Status of Marine Fuel Usage and Regulations
Conventional and Alternative Marine Fuels
IMO estimates total consumption of all fuels for international shipping in 2023 was 211–259
million mt, or approximately 65–80 billion gallons (5,000 mt gross weight and larger vessels)
[10]. Other sources suggest this number is much higher at more than 300 million mt for all
shipping, but these additional ships are not subject to IMO regulation [11].

As shown in Figure 1, since 2020 more than half the fuel used has been HFO, with an increasing
proportion of light fuel oil brought about by the reduction of allowable sulfur content from 3.5
wt % to 0.5 wt % beginning in 2020 (although 3.5% sulfur fuel can be used if the ship is
equipped with a SOx scrubber, which is very common for large container ships). HFO is also
known as residual fuel oil because it is the material that does not boil off during the distillation of
crude oil. Property limits are described in ISO 8217:2024. It can have very high viscosity, up to
500 mm2/s at 50°C for the heaviest grade. Room temperature viscosity may be as high as 10,000
mm2/s, and this material has been described as a semisolid tar. This very tarry substance must be
heated on board the ship to reduce its viscosity for it to be pumpable before injection into the
engine. Light fuel oil is also a residual fuel but has a lower viscosity and may have lower sulfur
content. MGO is a distillate fuel that is similar to diesel fuel.

Figure 2 shows that bulk carriers, container ships, and tankers consume most of the marine fuel.
The largest alternative fuel used was LNG, and the primary ships that use LNG are LNG
carriers—in many cases fueled in part by the LNG cargo boiloff. Very small amounts of other
alternative fuels were used in the 2021 to 2023 period, as shown in Figure 3. Biofuel in this
figure likely comprises primarily biodiesel and renewable diesel. In 2023, IMO reports that 4,000
mt of ethanol was used to fuel large bulk carriers, but little additional information appears to be
available [10]. The fuels shown in Figure 3 represented less than 0.5% of fuel used in shipping in
this time frame, but it is likely that use of LNG and methanol has increased significantly since
2023. As of the end of 2025 LNG remained the most widely adopted alternative fuel in marine
shipping, with about 10% of the global fleet (in service and on order) by deadweight tonnage
[31]. Methanol-capable ships are the second largest category after LNG, with a total of 112–120
vessels in operation, including 38 new deliveries in 2026 [32]. Approximately one-third of these
vessels are methanol carriers, and one-half are container ships. A further 700 ships are methanol
ready (purpose built for later conversion to methanol dual-fuel), and 300–350 are on order.

Bunkering
Ship crews carefully monitor the amount of fuel on board using a combination of automated tank
sounding gauges, pressure sensors, and flow meters. In modern ships the crew can track the
weight of fuel in each tank in real time. This is especially critical before taking on board
additional fuel (bunkering) to ensure that there is no spillage or overflow.

Bunkering is the process of supplying fuel to ships, which is typically brought to the ship by a
bunker barge (ship-to-ship transfer), but can also be accomplished via pipe or hose from a land-
based terminal or from trucks. Because of the large tonnages involved, as well as safety,
environmental, and financial risk, ship bunkering is a complex and meticulously planned and
regulated operation. Because alternative fuels such as methanol, ethanol, and LNG have
significantly different properties than conventional fuels, bunkering procedures are also different,
and these operations require the use of bunker barges designed specifically for handling these
fuels.

The largest tonnage bunkering port is Singapore at 56.2 million mt in 2025—as much as 25% of
bunker fuel used globally [33, 34]. All other ports have much smaller totals, with Rotterdam in
second place at 9.8 million mt. Total bunkering tonnage for all ports in the United States is
estimated at 18 million mt annually [35]. This is consistent with U.S. residual fuel oil production
of around 18 million mt annually, as shown in Figure 4. To identify U.S. ports where bunkering
is likely to occur, we can look at rankings for cargo tonnage, which show that Houston is the
largest U.S. port overall, followed by several other ports on the U.S. Gulf Coast. Aside from
methanol carriers, container ships are the most likely to be using methanol (or ethanol) in the
near term, and the Port of New York and New Jersey is the largest container port, followed by
Los Angeles, Long Beach, Savannah, and Houston [36].

Air Pollutant Emissions
Regulations
The International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL) was
adopted by the IMO and sets international standards for preventing ship-source pollution. Annex
VI of MARPOL specifically addresses air pollution, which includes NOx [38], SOx, PM [39],
volatile organic compounds [40], and ozone-depleting emissions [41]. It sets standards for vessel
and engine manufacture, certification, and operation, along with standards for fuel quality. These
standards are for implementation worldwide, while stricter standards apply for Emission Control
Areas (ECAs). An ECA is a body of water designated by IMO where ships must follow stricter
emissions regulations for SOx, PM, NOx, or all three. Current ECAs include the Baltic Sea, North
Sea, Mediterranean Sea, and the North American ECA that includes the entire coast of the
United States and much of Canada. Annex VI sets the following requirements:

 1. NOx emission standards apply for marine diesel engines above 130 kW of power. The
 requirements are for both main propulsion engines and auxiliary engines with NOx
 emission levels as specified in Table 2 and Figure 5.
 A. Tier I and Tier II limits apply worldwide. Tier II standards can generally be
 achieved by optimization of the engine combustion process. Changes in
 parameters such as fuel injection timing, pressure, rate (rate shaping), fuel nozzle
 flow area, exhaust valve timing, and cylinder compression volume allow engine
 operation to comply with these regulations.
 B. Tier III limits apply only in ECAs. Tier III standards typically necessitate the use
 of advanced NOx control technology. These may include measures like water
 injection into the combustion process (via fuel, scavenging air, or directly into the
 cylinder), exhaust gas recirculation, or selective catalytic reduction systems. For
 the large two-stroke engines powering the largest ships and consuming most
 maritime fuels, main engine maximum speed (N) will be less than 130 rpm.

Fuel Spillage
The International Convention on Civil Liability for Bunker Oil Pollution Damage (2001) is the
main instrument covering spills of fuel from all seagoing vessels. It established a system of strict
liability for shipowners for pollution damage, including environmental damage, and requires
ships over 1,000 gross tons to maintain compulsory insurance.

When HFO is spilled into the sea, there can be large environmental impacts, as it can form a
thick film on the surface that spreads slowly due to its high viscosity. Depending on the density,

 9

HFO might float on the surface or be suspended under the surface if the density is greater than 1
g/mL. Further, because of its slow evaporation rate, most of the HFO remains in the environment
for extended periods of time. HFO readily forms stable emulsions of oil in water and water in oil
that are resistant to natural breakdown and difficult to recover. Slow oxidation of HFOs can also
occur, leading to changes in properties such as viscosity, density, and pour point [42, 43]. If
spills are not properly contained, the impact on coastlines can be high. Cleanup for even a small
spill can cost millions of dollars. HFO contains compounds such as polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbons, which are harmful to aquatic organisms and bioaccumulate in the food chain.

Spilling MGO in marine environments differs from HFOs, primarily due to its lower density and
higher volatility. Gas oil is considerably less viscous, allowing it to quickly spread on the water
surface when it is released. Unlike HFO, MGO contains a high concentration of volatile
hydrocarbons that lead to extensive evaporation, such that the MGO can evaporate within the
first 24–48 hours of release, especially with warm and windy weather. This evaporation reduces
the amount of oil remaining on the surface, but volatile organic compounds that are toxic can
impact marine organisms and create related inhalation risks to response operators. MGO also
disperses more easily into the water, and with the assistance of chemical dispersants,
biodegradation can be enhanced, but there may be a risk of short-term aquatic toxicity. MGO
does not emulsify as easily or stably as HFO. However, due to its solubility and toxicity, MGO
poses acute environmental risks for marine organisms and coastal environments. It has low
persistence in seawater compared to HFO, but the initial impact can be extreme. Shore pollution
is less common and less problematic to remove.

Marine Engines
Large Two-Stroke Engines
Large commercial ship engines are low-speed (100–300 rpm) two-stroke engines owing to their
higher efficiency, higher torque at low speeds, and ability to burn low-cost heavy fuels.
Compared to on-land diesel engines that are four-stroke engines delivering power in every two
revolutions, marine engines produce power in every revolution (i.e., two strokes), with
scavenging (the process of replacing exhaust with fresh intake mixture), compression,
combustion, and exhaust occurring in one revolution. Two-stroke engines provide a higher
power-to-weight ratio compared to four-stroke engines. Low-speed two-stroke marine diesel
engines are highly efficient, with peak thermal efficiency greater than 50%. Fuel consumption
can be as low as 160–170 g/kWh with design based on very long periods of steady operation on
HFO. Output powers range from 1,100 kW to more than 97,000 kW, enabling them to power the
biggest commercial vessels.

The low rotational speed of these engines allows direct mechanical coupling between the
propeller shaft and the crankshaft without the need for reduction gearing (i.e., a transmission).
The absence of a complicated gear system also increases the power-to-weight ratio and reduces
mechanical complexity and production cost. In addition, the two-stroke layout is mechanically
simpler because intake valves are not used, which reduces the number of components prone to
mechanical failure. Instead, the cylinder liner has inlet ports located about two-thirds of the way
down from the top that are opened and closed by the piston motion.

Marine engines for commercial vessels can be 10 to 15 meters tall to accommodate the large
pistons and crankshafts needed for propelling ships. Two-stroke diesel engines require forced
induction (turbocharging and/or supercharging) for air charging as well as scavenging of the
cylinder.

The operating cycle can be described as commencing from the bottom dead center of the piston.
As the piston rises, it covers and closes the inlet ports and compresses the trapped air, with
compression ratios in the range of 17:1 to 23:1, and pressures on the order of 40 bar at the end of
the compression stroke (first stroke). Fuel is directly injected through several injectors in the
cylinder head at 10 to 20 crank angle degrees before the top of the compression stroke. Due to
the high temperature of the compressed air, the injected fuel autoignites and burns as a spray.
This results in rising cylinder pressure, which typically peaks in the range of 180 to 195 bar. This
pressure pushes the piston down in the power stroke (second stroke), and work is transmitted
through the connecting rod through the crosshead bearing to the crankshaft, converting linear
motion into rotational energy. As the piston continues down, it uncovers the inlet ports, allowing
air into the cylinder at pressures of 30–70 kPa above ambient (about 1.3–1.7-atm absolute
pressure), and the exhaust valve is opened. Air flow pushes the exhaust gases out. At the bottom
of the power stroke, the piston starts its upward stroke—covering the inlet ports and beginning a
new compression process.

Smaller Four-Stroke Engines
Medium and small marine vessels that include ships, boats, ferries, and tugs usually operate with
four-stroke diesel engines. Auxiliary engines used for power generation on larger ships may also

 11

be four-stroke. Diesel engines are typically used as they are efficient, durable, and are able to
provide reliable torque during variable operating conditions. These smaller vessels usually burn
MGO or ultra-low-sulfur diesel (ULSD), as opposed to the HFO or VLSFO burned by most large
oceangoing commercial vessels. MGO is a refined distillate fuel similar to land-based diesel but
formulated for use in marine applications and has higher fuel density. It has a much lower sulfur
content than traditional marine fuels and therefore allows vessels to meet the strict international
emission standards, especially for operation in ECAs. ULSD is a fuel that contains sulfur levels
typically below 15 ppm. It is widely available because it is used in both on-road and off-road
land-based diesel engines and thus can be easily obtained by small vessels refueling in ports
shared between marine and land-based customers. ULSD is compatible with modern emission
control technologies, including diesel particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction systems.
There are a few operational advantages with the use of MGO or ULSD in marine diesel engines.
However, MGO and ULSD have a higher cost per ton than HFO, requiring maximizing engine
efficiency for economic purposes.

Dual-Fuel Engines
Dual-fuel two-stroke engines have emerged as an important engine technology in the marine
sector for burning low-cetane-number alternative fuels while maintaining the high power-density
and reliability required for long-haul transport. These engines have the capability to operate in
two modes: one on conventional HFO or MGO, and a second where HFO or MGO is used as a
pilot to ignite a low-cetane-number fuel such as LNG, methanol, or ethanol. The flexibility to
operate on either conventional or alternative fuels is considered a major advantage by shipping
companies as they strive to ensure the relevance of these long-lived, high-cost assets in an
uncertain regulatory environment.

Marine engine manufacturers have advanced dual-fuel concepts to a mature, widely adopted
technology spanning ultra-large container vessels, tankers, and bulk carriers, as well as smaller
ships. Figure 6 shows a diagram of injector layout (similar diagrams have been presented by
equipment manufacturers) [44]. When air enters the cylinder, it creates a swirling flow field.
There are typically two or three sets of pilot and low-cetane-number fuel injectors, with the pilot
located upstream of the primary fuel relative to the swirling air motion. This air motion pushes
the burning pilot spray into the primary fuel jet, causing it to ignite and burn as a spray.

 12

 Figure 6. Pilot and primary fuel injector layout for dual-fuel engines (graphic by N. Naser, NLR).

LNG dual-fuel engines are the most widely deployed dual-fuel engines and use a lean burn
strategy in which vaporized natural gas is injected into the cylinder and ignited by a small
quantity of pilot liquid fuel (MGO or HFO) injected at high pressure, maintaining a diesel-like
compression-ignition process but with LNG as the main energy carrier. The pilot fuel fraction is
usually in the order of 1%–5% of the total energy input, which is sufficient to ensure stable
ignition and combustion over a wide load range. When LNG is unavailable [45], or under certain
operating conditions such as low-load, transient maneuvers, the same engine can be operated
with the conventional liquid fuel alone without hardware changes, providing operational
flexibility and redundancy. This is achieved by advanced engine management systems, including
in-cylinder pressure measurement-based control, that control pilot injection timing, and air
management to avoid misfiring and other engine combustion issues while maintaining target
power output and speed. For LNG, SOx is eliminated due to the very low sulfur content in
natural gas, and PM emissions are low due to the absence of heavy aromatics. NOx reductions of
85%–90% compared with conventional HFO engines are possible, although these engines
require additional NOx abatement to meet Tier III emissions requirements.

Methanol dual-fuel engines, like the Everllence ME-LGIM [46], the WinGD X-DF-M [47], and
those from Wartsila [48] and Caterpillar [49], can use either methanol or conventional marine
fuels while employing the same diesel engine design. For methanol operation, a small amount of
pilot fuel (1%–5% of total energy input) is injected to initiate combustion [50]. Methanol is
injected at high pressure for better spray atomization, and after injection it burns as a spray.

Methanol in Marine Shipping
Methanol is a widely used chemical and solvent with production of 92.8 million mt (34 billion
gallons) worldwide in 2024. By 2030 production is projected to grow to 116.3 million tons [51].
Methanol is a commodity that is shipped as cargo all over the world, with 35% of production
exported in 2023 [52]. While methanol is almost entirely made from natural gas, it can be made
from many feedstocks including coal, biomass, and waste CO2. Depending on how it is
produced, methanol can be assigned a color name:

 • Green methanol is made from biomass or captured CO2 and green hydrogen (made by
 water electrolysis using renewable electricity).
 • Blue methanol is made using captured CO2 and blue hydrogen (made from natural gas by
 steam methane reforming with capture and sequestration of CO2).
 • Gray methanol is made from natural gas.
 • Brown methanol is made from coal.
Almost all methanol produced today is gray methanol.

Methanol is approved as a marine fuel in the sense that IMO has published interim guidelines on
safety requirements for use of methanol and ethanol, including fuel system design, ventilation,
leak detection, and firefighting [9]. These interim guidelines are being integrated into the
mandatory International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea section entitled “International
Code of Safety for Ships Using Gases or Other Low-Flashpoint Fuels” [53]. There are a total of
112 to 120 methanol-capable vessels in operation, including 38 new deliveries in 2026 [32]. A
further 700 are methanol ready (purpose built for later conversion to methanol dual-fuel), and
300 to 350 are on order. The first orders were for methanol-carrying tankers, but today most
methanol-capable ships on order are ultra-large container ships (>14,500 TEU) and new
Panamax container ships (10,000 to 14,500 TEU)—highlighting the strong interest in the
container shipping sector in alcohol fuels [54]. Methanol-capable ships have undergone
significant development since their first introduction [55]. All of these vessels are capable of
operating on conventional fuel oil or methanol (in dual-fuel mode with MGO or HFO as a pilot
ignition source) and today operate primarily on conventional fuel oil. 2

Methanol has several advantages over conventional marine fuels for large commercial vessels.
Methanol is essentially sulfur-free, so that SOx emissions are effectively eliminated. As an
oxygenate it produces very low soot emissions and burns cooler, reducing NOx, although
additional reduction is required to meet IMO Tier III limits [56, 57, 58]. For example, Everllence
reports a 30%–50% NOx reduction relative to MGO for their ME-LGIM engine [59]. In the event
of a large methanol spill, the fuel would quickly dissolve and be rapidly dispersed and
biodegraded, in contrast to the high-cost negative impacts of a large HFO spill [60, 61].

Methanol bunkering requires a dedicated barge or methanol tanker. As of 2025, there were 12
dedicated methanol bunker barges worldwide, with most in Singapore [54]. Shipboard bunkering

supply chain, including dedicated barges, develops [59]. An added complexity for methanol
bunkering compared to HFO is the need to maintain inert gas coverage both in the barge and on
the ship. This is accomplished by creating a closed-loop system where displaced nitrogen-

the limited availability of green methanol, methanol bunkering has remained limited. For
example, in 2025 10,000 mt of green methanol was bunkered in Shanghai, 3,000 mt in Singapore
[69], and 12,000 mt in Rotterdam [70]. For comparison, total annual tonnage for all fuels
bunkered in Singapore was 56.2 million mt in 2025 [34].

Methanol-capable engines are available for new-build ships from the major engine original
equipment manufacturers (e.g., Everllence, Wartsila, WinGD, Caterpillar), including both large
two-stroke main engines and smaller auxiliary engines for power generation that may be four-
stroke. Ships in service can also be retrofitted to be dual-fuel methanol. Retrofitting is primarily
applicable to ships that are between 5 and 10 years old with electronic engine control. Retrofits
for dual-fuel methanol operation are a major undertaking that can involve replacing machine
parts, hull expansion, adding new fuel tanks, and replacing the fuel preparation room and fuel
supply system, along with controls, alarms, and monitoring [71]. For the Maersk Halifax this
involved cutting the ship in half to extend the hull by 15 meters [72].

Comparison of Ethanol and Methanol
The use of both methanol and ethanol is covered in the IMO interim guidance [9]. However,
methanol is more mature because of the existence of an ISO standard for fuel quality, port-level
standards such as the TR 129 standard for methanol bunking in Singapore [82], and
implementation experience with methanol having been used in many ships. Because both
methanol and ethanol are alcohols and have similar properties, ethanol may develop very rapidly
because of the experience with methanol and the potential for shared infrastructure and a two-
alcohol strategy.

Physical Properties and Flammability
Both methanol and ethanol are alcohols and have very similar, but not identical, chemical
properties. These are highlighted in Table 6. While similar, the change from one carbon atom in
methanol (CH3OH) to two carbon atoms in ethanol (C2H5OH) has a relatively small effect on
boiling point, density, and flash point but a large effect on LHV. The LHV for ethanol is 35%
higher. While methanol’s LHV is 54% lower than MGO, ethanol’s LHV is only 37% lower.
When examined in terms of energy density (on a volume basis), onboard fuel storage must be 2.4
times larger for methanol but only 1.8 times larger for ethanol to have the same vessel range as
operation on MGO (38-GJ/m3 and 850-kg/m3 density, respectively). Figure 7 shows relative
volume required for various alternative fuels compared to MGO [60]. Notably, ethanol requires
less fuel storage volume than both methanol and LNG.
Because of their similar flash point, ethanol must be handled using similar procedures for
methanol in terms of nitrogen blanketing, fire detection, and ventilation. Fire detection is
important because both alcohols burn with a nearly invisible flame that is difficult to detect by
human eye. Autoignition temperatures are in the same range as for MGO (250°C–500°C),
providing similar safety from the standpoint of ignition from contact with hot metal surfaces.
Methanol’s wider flammability range may pose a greater risk of fire or explosion should the inert
gas system fail.

Specifications
ISO published ISO 6583:2024, “Methanol as a fuel for marine applications – General
requirements and specifications,” which outlines purity and properties for methanol to be used in
the marine market. An ISO specification for ethanol to be used in marine shipping is currently in
development, as ISO has created preliminary work item 6583-2, “Alcohols fuel for marine
applications – Part 2: Ethanol.” It will likely be based on specifications for ethanol promulgated
by ASTM International; the Brazilian National Agency of Petroleum, Natural Gas and Biofuels
(ANP); and the European Committee for Standardization (CEN), which are compared to the ISO
6583 methanol specification for grade MMC in Table 7. The MMC grade has the widest range of
impurities of the various methanol grades. Because the methanol specification is based on the
methanol chemical specification (IMPCA), the allowable levels of impurities are significantly
lower than for the ethanol specifications intended for use as a blendstock to make gasoline. The
limits on methanol in the various ethanol specifications are because automotive fuel system
materials may not be compatible with higher levels, because methanol has a large effect on
increasing vapor pressure of gasoline at low blending levels, and methanol can have limited
solubility in hydrocarbons. Maximum gasoline vapor pressure is typically regulated to reduce
evaporative emissions. These considerations may not be applicable to marine shipping, where
engines and fuel supply systems are using nearly pure ethanol or methanol. The methanol ISO
specification limit on ethanol concentration of 150 ppm does not appear to have a technical basis,
as physical and combustion properties are unlikely to be affected by ethanol concentrations of
several volume percent or higher. While ethanol has a higher allowable acidity, experience has
shown that ethanol does not increase corrosion rates in common grades of steel. While inorganic
chloride at the point of ethanol production is near zero, specifications allow up to 6.7 mg/kg
because of contamination in the supply chain. Experience has shown that this low chloride level
does not present a corrosion issue. As described in the next section, ethanol producers and
distributors routinely add corrosion inhibitor additives to ethanol to protect infrastructure and end
user fuel systems.

ASTM D4806, ANP No. 19, and EN15376 require a denaturant to make ethanol unsuitable for

The need for and impact of denaturants in ethanol for marine shipping is still under discussion. If

requirements of ASTM D4806). Alternatively, undenatured fuel ethanol as described in ASTM
D8651 can be used.

Because of the similarities of methanol and ethanol, it is possible to imagine a marine fuel
market where both are used almost interchangeably as alcohol fuel. This would have the benefit
of allowing alcohol-specific bunker barges to switch from one fuel to the other without cleaning
and allow ships to load one alcohol on top of a tank partially filled with the other. This is made
possible because ship crews closely monitor the amount of fuel in the ship’s tanks using
soundings and other measurements on a weekly or even daily basis. Careful measurement of the
amount of fuel in a tank prior to bunkering new fuel is a critical part of any fuel bunkering
operation and allows the captain to calculate the energy content of the mixture of
methanol/ethanol in the tank and hence the vessel sailing range.

Materials Compatibility
Generally, methanol is more challenging from a materials compatibility perspective than ethanol.
Methanol is more polar and has a higher hydrogen-bonding propensity, which—combined with
its smaller molecular size compared to ethanol—leads to a higher degree of interaction with
elastomers and plastics. This interaction leads to higher swelling and degradation of the
elastomer/plastics physical properties. These properties of alcohols can be quantified in terms of
solubility parameters and molar volume [84], as shown in Table 8.

From the standpoint of metal corrosion, it is notable that methanol is more acidic than water,
while ethanol is less acidic [85]. Additionally, the breakdown product from methanol (formic
acid) is a much stronger acid than the breakdown product from ethanol (acetic acid). In the
presence of low levels of water, methanol will be more corrosive than ethanol. This is consistent
with the recommendation that stainless steel be used for storing methanol (or mild steel tanks
having an interior coating) [59], while mild steel may be acceptable for storing ethanol [60]—
especially given the common use of corrosion inhibitor additives. Both alcohols are likely
corrosive to aluminum alloys and lead and can also promote galvanic corrosion when different
metals are in electrical contact.

While not required by specification, it is common to add corrosion inhibitor additives to fuel
ethanol at the point of production to protect against corrosion throughout the supply chain,
including end user equipment [86]. Many ethanol corrosion inhibitors also include a pHe

buffering agent to neutralize acids that may form under some conditions [87]. An evaluation
protocol for determining the appropriate concentration of the additive has been published [88].

Systems developed for storing and handling methanol should easily be compatible with ethanol.

Methanol and Ethanol Spillage Into the Environment
Kass et al. [43] examined the potential environmental impacts of significant spills or releases of
alternative marine fuels, including ethanol and methanol. While these fuels are very similar,
there are important differences in their impact when spilled into the ocean. These are highlighted
in Figure 9.

Ethanol boils at 78°C and is highly volatile, so it will eventually vaporize if it is left to air. It is
completely soluble in water, and if dissolved, its volatility is significantly reduced. When
discharged into surface waters, a portion of the ethanol can briefly form a light nonaqueous
liquid film on the water’s surface, as shown in Figure 9. During and immediately after the spill,
ethanol vapors will form over this surface layer, and being denser than air, will displace oxygen
near the release point. Ultimately, these vapors will disperse, creating a mixture within the
flammable range that can be ignited. But with ethanol’s high solubility, this surface layer is
quickly removed, primarily by dissolution in the water and secondarily by evaporation. Upon
dissolving, ethanol spreads rapidly within the water and then decomposes through microbial
activity. The biodegradation half-life of ethanol in water is 6.5 to 26 h [89]. Although ethanol is
harmful to marine organisms, its fast dispersion avoids any detrimental effects on those animals
or organisms that are nearest to the location of discharge. In addition, ethanol has a low
bioconcentration factor—i.e., it does not get accumulated in the food chain.

 Figure 9. Marine ethanol spill scenario [61]
Because of their similar properties, a methanol spill will have similar behavior. However,
methanol has a higher vapor pressure and will evaporate more quickly—methanol vapors will
rise and disperse more quickly than is the case for ethanol, such that methanol accumulation on
the water surface is unlikely [61]. While there remains a risk of fire during a spill event, most of
the methanol will quickly dissolve in water, where it will biodegrade with a half-life of 1 to 7
days—significantly more slowly than ethanol [89].

 24

Thus, while there is a significant risk of fire for spillage of both alcohols, this risk is more
quickly abated by evaporation and dispersion into air, and dissolution into water, for methanol.
However, once dissolved in water, ethanol will biodegrade more quickly than methanol.

Due to the high miscibility of methanol and ethanol in water, current spill detection technologies
may not be able to detect spills. Infrared technologies for oil spill detection that leverage thermal
and polarization differences of oil and water cannot be adopted for methanol/ethanol due to
similar properties with water. Radar technologies that can detect an oil spill may not be able to
detect methanol/ethanol spills as surface wave heights differ. Ultraviolet technologies could be
applicable with suitable adaptations [61].

Implementation of Ethanol in the Marine Industry
Regulatory
Ethanol is an approved fuel based on the IMO interim guidelines that include safety
requirements, fuel system design, ventilation, leak detection, and firefighting [9]. Maritime
classification societies set and enforce technical rules for ship design, construction, and operation
that are more detailed than high-level IMO regulations, focusing on the structural and
mechanical integrity of vessels throughout their life. Multiple classification societies have
published detailed technical requirements for the use of methanol and ethanol including DNV,
American Bureau of Shipping, Lloyd’s Register, Bureau Veritas, Class NK, and China
Classification Society.

For ethanol to be used broadly in shipping, an ISO standard describing its properties needs to be
developed. This standard is currently in development by ISO/TC 28/SC 4/WG 18. This group is
targeting release of a final standard in 2027.

A driver for alternative fuel use in the maritime industry is the IMO NZF, and the adoption of
this framework as legally binding on IMO member states will also be a major driver for use of
ethanol in marine shipping. An IMO-recognized pathway and default GFI for U.S. corn-based
ethanol is required for a fuel to be recognized under the IMO NZF. A 2021 review found that the
carbon intensity of U.S. corn ethanol was 51.4 gCO2eq/MJ—46% lower than for petroleum fuels
[26]. U.S. ethanol producers are investing in technology to reduce energy consumption and
improve ethanol yields, further reducing this number. Several producers are adopting
sequestration of CO2, which qualifies for a U.S. tax credit and may reduce carbon intensity by as
much as 30 gCO2eq/MJ [27].

Engine Status and Trail Voyages
Engine Performance
Both methanol and ethanol are members of the alcohol family, and most of their physical
properties are similar. Because methanol is more challenging from a materials compatibility
perspective, ethanol is compatible with methanol fuel storage, handling, and injection systems. It
is increasingly acknowledged that methanol-capable vessels can operate on ethanol with no or
minor modifications, although fully optimized engines would show slightly better performance
[90, 91, 92, 93, 94].

In 2025, Everllence confirmed that its large-bore two-stroke ME-LGIM engine designed for
methanol dual-fuel could run successfully on ethanol at all load points. The ME-LGIM engine
entered service in 2016 in methanol tankers and is one of the more common methanol dual-fuel
engines, with 50 engines in operation and 225 on order [95]. A four-cylinder test engine (4S90-
LGIM) designed for methanol dual-fuel was operated on both methanol and hydrous ethanol
with no major changes to the engine. Performance was acceptable on ethanol, although there was
potential to optimize injection and improve thermal efficiency. A second full-scale methanol
dual-fuel engine (7G95-LGIM) was also operated on anhydrous denatured ethanol with no
changes to the engine with only minor changes to performance relative to methanol. Everllence

 26

has also demonstrated its four-stroke L21/31 in ethanol dual-fuel operation [96]. This engine is
in common use for power generation on large ships.

WinGD has tested methanol and ethanol extensively for marine engine applications [97]. The
ethanol tests were conducted on an engine designed to operate on methanol. WinGD also
conducted spray combustion chamber experiments, where it was shown that the spray
propagation of methanol and ethanol is very similar to that of diesel fuel. Results indicated that
combustion properties, performance, and emissions are closely comparable and that the same
design and safety principles can be applied to both fuels. Because ethanol has a higher LHV than
methanol, the engine needs to compensate for the difference in injection quantity between
ethanol and methanol to optimize combustion. The injection system originally designed for
methanol operation is sufficient to cover ethanol operation without modification. However,
nozzle tip geometry can be optimized depending on whether the engine will be operating on
methanol or ethanol. WinGD now offers dual-fuel engines that can operate on ethanol, methanol,
or a mixture of the two [97]. They have recently accepted orders for two six-cylinder 6X82DF-
M/E engines designed to operate primarily on ethanol [98].

Similar studies by Wärtsilä confirm that ethanol and methanol have similar characteristics,
meaning the same fuel system and safety precautions as those developed for ethanol can be
utilized in current methanol engine platforms with very few modifications [94]. Safety practices
and training for operators developed for methanol will also work for ethanol. Studies conducted
on a Wärtsilä W6L32M multicylinder engine originally meant for methanol used anhydrous
ethanol meeting EN 15376, with slight water content and including 3% isobutanol as a
denaturant. Ethanol gave a slightly higher brake-specific energy consumption and lower heat
release rates compared to methanol for different loads without hardware modifications and
without changes in the control system settings.

Caterpillar has also acknowledged that it is exploring the use of ethanol in its dual-fuel methanol
marine four-stroke engines [99].

Alcohol dual-fuel engines easily meet Tier I and Tier II emission requirements, including NOx,
when running in dual-fuel mode. Meeting Tier III NOx emissions requires additional controls
such as urea selective catalytic reduction.

Trial Voyages
Public reports of sea trials using ethanol dual-fuel systems are limited to the trials on Laura
Maersk—the world’s first methanol-capable container ship powered by an Everllence 6G50ME-
C9.9-LGIM engine. From October to December of 2025, trial voyages were conducted in the
Baltic Sea using a blend of 10% ethanol in methanol, which confirmed that this blend could be
used without compromising engine performance. A second trial using a 50% ethanol blend was
initiated in December 2025 and was also successful [100]. A third trial using 100% anhydrous
denatured ethanol was conducted in early 2026 and was also successful, with no challenges to
the use of ethanol noted [101].

Bunkering and Availability in Ports
Recently, there have been major milestones in commercial ethanol bunkering. In May 2026
Laura Maersk completed barge-to-ship bunkering of 2,000 mt (670,000 gal) of ethanol in

 27

Rotterdam [102]. Also during May in Rotterdam, the X-Press Feeders container ship Eco Levant
separately bunkered 90% biomethanol and 10% ethanol in a barge-to-ship transfer, with the two
alcohols mixed in the onboard tank [103]. In July, the 16,000-TEU container ship Antonia
Maersk bunkered 2,800 mt (938,000 gal) of ethanol in a barge-to-ship transfer in Barcelona
[104]. All of these ships have then operated on the bunkered fuel in commercial operation.
Efforts to bunker ethanol in the United States are in the planning stages [105].

Ethanol is available in ports where it is exported or imported. The specific ports used for export
and import of ethanol in the United States may be significant for use of ethanol as a marine
shipping fuel, as these ports have ethanol storage and handling facilities that presumably could
supply ethanol to ships. Nearly half of U.S. ethanol exports are out of Houston, with another
12% out of New Orleans, and significant exports through New York and Seattle as well [24].
Ethanol imports, although very small, were almost entirely to the Port of Savannah in 2024.

A challenge in understanding how to implement ethanol as a maritime fuel in the United States is
a lack of data on bunkering tonnage in U.S. ports, and what types of vessels are bunkering.
Sources suggest that the total U.S. bunkering market in 2025 was approximately 18 million mt
[35]. The Greater Houston area is thought to have the largest market at 4–5 million mt annually.
Port statistics from Los Angeles, one of the few U.S. ports offering such data, suggest a total
bunker tonnage of 1 million mt in 2025 [106].

We can estimate a cap on the near-term market by looking at potential ethanol consumption by
the current operating fleet of 112 methanol dual-fuel ships. Assuming that these are on average
large container ships that consume 100 mt of fuel oil per day, this translates into 160 mt per day
of ethanol. Annually, ethanol consumption would be nearly 6 million mt or nearly 2 billion
gallons (for 330 days of operation) under this scenario.

Summary and Remaining Barriers
This report examines whether ethanol can become a viable marine fuel and compares it
extensively with methanol, which is already gaining adoption in shipping. The report concludes
that ethanol is technically feasible for marine use and could leverage much of the infrastructure,
engine technology, and operational experience already developed for methanol. However, large-
scale adoption will depend on regulatory progress, fuel standards, bunkering infrastructure, and
recognized life cycle carbon accounting.

Marine shipping currently employs HFO and VLSFO, consuming roughly 211–259 million mt of
fuel annually for IMO-regulated international shipping, with broader estimates exceeding 300
million mt globally. Shipping emissions are regulated by IMO, including SOx, NOx, and PM.
Alternative fuels such as biodiesel, methanol, and ethanol can help to meet these emissions
requirements and are being evaluated as pathways toward IMO decarbonization goals.

A central finding is that ethanol shares many important properties with methanol. Existing
methanol-capable dual-fuel marine engines can operate on ethanol with little or no modification.
Major engine developers, including Everllence, WinGD, Wärtsilä, and HD Hyundai have
successfully tested ethanol in methanol-designed engines. Trials demonstrated acceptable
combustion behavior, comparable emissions characteristics, and stable operation across engine
loads. WinGD has announced production of ethanol-capable dual-fuel engines. Commercial
trials have occurred aboard Maersk vessels using Everllence engines.

The report highlights several advantages of ethanol relative to methanol. Ethanol has
approximately 35% higher energy content than methanol, lower human toxicity, and potentially
lower life cycle greenhouse gas emissions when produced from bio-based feedstocks. A cited
study estimated U.S. corn ethanol at approximately 51.4 gCO2eq/MJ, about 46% lower than
petroleum fuels, with additional reductions possible through carbon capture and sequestration at
ethanol plants.

At the same time, ethanol presents operational and safety considerations similar to those for
methanol. Its flash point of 14°C requires inert gas blanketing and specialized handling during
storage and bunkering. Like methanol, ethanol is fully miscible with water and highly
flammable. Ethanol spills in marine environments will disperse and biodegrade relatively
quickly, with biodegradation half-lives between 6.5 and 26 hours in water. Although ethanol is
harmful to marine organisms at high concentrations, rapid dissolution and low bioaccumulation
reduce long-term environmental persistence compared to conventional oil spills. Methanol
behaves similarly but biodegrades more slowly once dissolved.

The report also reviews fuel standards and regulatory development. IMO interim safety guidance
already permits the use of methanol and ethanol aboard appropriately designed ships. ISO has
finalized a marine methanol specification (ISO 6583:2024), while a corresponding ethanol
marine fuel standard, ISO 6583-2, is under development. Existing ethanol standards were largely
created for automotive gasoline blending rather than marine fuel applications, so additional
specification work is needed.

 31

Economically, the report identifies marine shipping as a potentially large future market for
ethanol producers. Theoretical maximum demand for ethanol in IMO-regulated shipping could
reach approximately 355 million mt (118 billion gallons) annually, although the report
acknowledges that full displacement of conventional fuels is unlikely. Even partial adoption
could create demand measured in billions of gallons per year. The report estimates that the
currently operating methanol dual-fuel fleet alone could consume roughly 2 billion gallons of
ethanol annually under high-substitution scenarios.

Ethanol is technically viable as a marine fuel and benefits from compatibility with methanol
infrastructure and engines. Remaining barriers include development of ethanol bunkering
infrastructure at ports, completion of international fuel specifications, supply chain investment,
and establishment of IMO-recognized life cycle greenhouse gas intensity values for ethanol.
