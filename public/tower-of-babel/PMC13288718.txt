Association between serum iodine and thyroid function in Malagasy adults: a cross-sectional study in a tertiary-level hospital in Antananarivo, Madagascar
Authors: Sitraka Angelo Raharinavalona, Rija Eric Raherison, Thierry Razanamparany, Maminirina Ratojoniaina, Andrianirina Dave Patrick Rakotomalala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIodine status and thyroid disorders remain understudied subjects in low-income countries such as ours. The objectives of the present study were to assess the serum iodine (SI) level and thyroid function in Malagasy adults seen at the Soavinandriana Hospital Center in Antananarivo and to determine the association between high levels of serum iodine (HLSI) and dysthyroidism. This cross-sectional study was conducted over the course of two years (September 1, 2021 to August 31, 2023). The SI was used as a proxy for iodine status in the absence of urinary assays. Thyroid function was assessed by thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and free tetra-iodothyronine (fT4). The HLSI was defined by the total serum iodine >100 µg/L. Dysthyroidism was defined by abnormal TSH and/or fT4 levels indicative of hypo- or hyperthyroidism. Among 195 patients, the prevalences of serum iodine excess and dysthyroidism were 12.8% and 15.4%, respectively. HLSI was significantly more common in subjects <60 years old (p-value < 0.0001), and less common in those with hypertension (p-value < 0.0001), dyslipidemia (p-value < 0.0001), diabetes mellitus (p-value = 0.0082) and ex-smoking (p-value < 0.0001). Adjusted for age and gender, the association between HLSI and dysthyroidism was significant (OR = 108 [17–680]). HLSI and dysthyroidism were prevalent among Malagasy adults. The presence of one condition would encourage the search for the other. Their management is primarily preventive through their periodic monitoring.Keywords: Dysthyroidism, high level of serum iodine, Malagasy, serum iodine, thyroid function

Introduction

Iodine is an essential trace element for the biosynthesis of thyroid hormones. Thyroid dysfunction is prevalent, readily discernible, and amenable to treatment. However, if it goes undiagnosed or remains untreated, it can have deleterious consequences [1]. One of the severe complications is cardiovascular, even for subclinical forms [2]. In adults, iodine deficiency can lead to goiter, eu- or hypothyroidism, cognitive disorders, infertility, hypercholesterolemia and cardiovascular risk [3,4]. The implementation of universal salt iodization around the world has reduced the incidence of iodine deficiency, but 30% of the global population remains at risk [5]. While the urinary iodine concentration (UIC) is the gold standard for assessing an individual's iodine status [6], the serum iodine (SI) has a strong nonlinear correlation with the UIC. A high level of SI has been identified as a risk factor for thyroid diseases [7]. Furthermore, the iodine food frequency questionnaire (I-FFQ) has been validated by several studies to assess iodine intake in pregnant women and children [8–10]. On a global scale, half of all pregnant adults and adolescents have insufficient or excessive iodine intake, leading to dysthyroidism in both mothers and newborns [11,12]. A recent study showed that the SI could be an indicator of iodine status in pregnant women and has a significant effect on maternal free tetra-iodothyronine (fT4) and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) [13]. In sub-Saharan Africa, iodine status varies from one population to another. Iodine deficiency disorders and iodine excess remain significant public health concerns, requiring a sustainable prevention strategy for each country [14].

In Madagascar, a national program to combat iodine deficiency disorders was developed in 1993 through the distribution of iodized capsules. In 1995, compulsory salt iodization was mandated by Decree No. 95‐587 on 05/09/1995. In 2014, Decree No. 2014-1771 further reinforced this by adding salt fluoridation [15]. Despite these efforts, a national iodine and salt survey in 2016 found that 75.8% of the population had moderate iodine deficiency, and household coverage of adequately iodized salt was only 21.3% (95% CI: 15.5%–27.1%) [16].

Very few studies have been conducted on this subject in Madagascar, with a focus on children and women of reproductive age [16–18]. However, Malagasy adults may also have a specific iodine status and thyroid function, even 30 years after the implementation of this national program. The objectives of the present study were to assess SI levels and thyroid function in Malagasy adults seen at the Soavinandriana Hospital Center in Antananarivo and to determine the association between high levels of serum iodine (HLSI) and dysthyroidism.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Cardiovascular Diseases and Internal Medicine Departments of the Soavinandriana Hospital Center (Military Hospital) in Antananarivo, the capital of Madagascar. The aforementioned departments have gained renown for their expertise in the management of cardiovascular, internal medicine, metabolic, and endocrine diseases in the capital, as well as cases referred from other regions of the big island. In Madagascar, regarding the level of iodization, there are three strata 1, 2 and 3, are defined by the proportions of adequately available iodized salt in households <20%, 20% to 60%, and 60% or more, respectively [16]. The study setting is located in stratum 1. The study covered a two-year period from September 1, 2021, to August 31, 2023.

Study population

The study population consisted of adult patients followed externally during the period studied. We included all adults attending outpatient visits, aged over 18 years who had had a measurement of SI, TSH and fT4. We excluded patients with known thyroid disorders, pregnant and lactating women, those taking antithyroid medications or thyroxine or amiodarone, those with recent CT scans with injections of iodinated contrast media and/or those with incomplete records.

The random selection of the population ensured the representativeness of the sample. The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula: n = (Z₁—α/2)² P(1—P)/d². This calculation was based on the assumptions of a 95% confidence level (Z₁—α/2 = 1.96), a 5% margin of error (d), and a 15.7% prevalence (P) of thyroid diseases, as reported by Jin et al. [7] in the absence of local data on this subject. The sample size for the study was 204. In light of a non-response rate of 10% (20 patients), the final estimated sample size for the study was determined to be 224 patients who consented to participate.

Clinical and laboratory data

The study encompassed demographic profile (age, gender), cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, overweight/obesity, dyslipidemia, diabetes, menopause, current and ex-smoking), total serum iodine, thyroid assessment (TSH, fT4, euthyroidism, dysthyroidism, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism), other biological assessments (corrected calcemia, phosphoremia, total cholesterol, high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLc), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLc), triglyceride.

The presence of hypertension is certified by self-declaration of patients already taking antihypertensives or a blood pressure reading of 140/90 mmHg or greater, based on the average of at least two separate measurements. A body mass index (BMI) of 25 kg/m² or greater is indicative of overweight status, whereas a BMI of 30 kg/m² or greater indicates obesity. Dyslipidemia is defined by LDLc outside the European Society of Cardiology targets [19] or using a lipid-lowering agent. Patients with self-reported diabetes mellitus taking anti-diabetic drugs or who met the criteria of the American Diabetes Association were considered diabetic [20].

The entirety of the biological testing was conducted within a single reference medical laboratory located in Antananarivo. The determination of total serum iodine (or protein iodine) is carried out in two stages: mineralization, leading to obtaining iodide ions, and then the Sandell and Kolthoff reaction, which consists of assessing the catalytic power of iodide for the reduction of cerium sulfate with arsenious anhydride. The normal reference values are between 40 µg/L and 100 µg/L (315 nmol/L and 788 nmol/L). The HLSI is defined by total serum iodine >100 µg/L [7]. TSH and fT4 were assessed by the chemiluminescence technique on Alinity i from Abbott 8596. Their reference values were 0.35 µIU/mL to 4.94 µIU/mL and 9 pmol/L to 19.04 pmol/L, respectively. Euthyroidism is defined as normal TSH and fT4 reference values, hyperthyroidism as TSH levels below 0.35 µIU/mL and fT4 levels above 19.04 pmol/L; subclinical hyperthyroidism as TSH levels below 0.35 µIU/mL and normal fT4, hypothyroidism as TSH levels above 4.94 µIU/mL and fT4 levels below 9 pmol/L, and subclinical hypothyroidism as TSH levels above 4.94 µIU/mL and normal fT4. ‘Dysthyroidism’ encompasses both hypo- and hyperthyroid states (including subclinical forms) [21,22]. Calcemia was measured by Alinity C 8501 colorimetric assay, corrected according to albuminemia. The reference values for calcemia are 2.10 mmol/L to 2.55 mmol/L, and those for phosphoremia are 0.87 mmol/L to 1.45 mmol/L.

Statistical analysis

The data were coded, cleaned, entered and analyzed with the help of Epi Info™ version 3.5.4® software (United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in Atlanta, Georgia). Continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) 25% and 75%. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. To compare the proportions of normal and high serum iodine levels, we used Fisher's exact test. The association between iodine status and thyroid function was determined by the odds ratio (OR) in univariate and age-adjusted multivariate analysis, with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance was set at a p-value of < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to their involvement in the study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The patients' anonymity and confidentiality were respected. The Review Board of Soavinandriana Hospital approved this study.

Results

During the study period, 29 patients were excluded because of incomplete data, so 195 adult patients who underwent total serum iodine measurement and thyroid assessment at the same time were included (Figure 1). The median SI was 81 µg/L (IQR 65.9–91.4). The median TSH and fT4 were 1.2 µIU/mL (IQR 0.92–1.66) and 13.55 pmol/L (IQR 11.92–64.35), respectively. Discussion

In the present study, the assessment of iodine was done by measuring total serum iodine. One of the reasons is because the UIC assay was not available in our laboratories during the study period. Additionally, direct analysis of circulating serum iodide may also provide more in-depth information on iodine status and thyroid function than urine biomonitoring. Given the rapid renal filtration and excretion of iodine, serum iodine is a more reliable indicator of long-term iodine intake than is the urinary iodine concentration [7,23,24].

Level of serum iodine and thyroid function

Iodine status most likely depends on iodine intake. In the present study, the serum iodine was high in 12.8% of cases, with a median of 81 µg/L. According to a national survey on iodine and salt in Madagascar in adult women in 2016, the iodine excess was 14.8%, 3.7%, and 8.2% in strata 1, 2 and 3, respectively [16]. In a Sudanese study, 3% to 5% of patients had excess iodine [25]. A Chinese study findings indicate that the median serum iodine levels were 76.8 µg/L [IQ 67.9–84.8], 82.9 µg/L [IQ 71.6–96.7] and 84.8 µg/L [IQ 74.8–100.6] in groups with adequate, sufficient and excess iodine intake, respectively [7].

In the present study, the prevalence of dysthyroidism was 15.4%. Likewise, it was 15.7% in the study conducted in China [7]. However, it was less common in the cohorts of Camargo et al. in Brazil (9.86%) [26], Ajlouni et al. in Jordan (11.9%) [27] and Mehran et al. in Iran (12%) [28]. This could be linked to the differences in iodine intake and food habits of each study population. Consequently, the ongoing monitoring of iodine levels in fortified foods or salt available in the country, in conjunction with the assessment of the thyroid health of the population, is imperative. This finding suggests a necessity to re-evaluate the current guidelines for iodine intake in our country.

The HLSI in our patients was positively associated with age less than 60 years and elevated serum calcium. While the opposite was found for hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, ex-smoking cessation, elevated triglyceridemia and LDLc. Studies carried out in Spain [29] and China [30] have also shown this positive association between young age and iodine excess. Indeed, young people would probably consume much more salt than older people. In addition, patients at high cardiovascular risk would probably be more educated about reducing salt consumption, thus minimizing the occurrence of iodine excess. In addition, according to a study conducted by Liu et al. in China, excessive iodine intake was significantly associated with elevated glycaemia and blood pressure [31]. Tayie and Jourdan had objectivized that salt restriction was associated with iodine deficiency in women. However, this was not the case for men. The search for other alternative sources of iodine was suggested [32]. Furthermore, a low UIC was associated with increased odds for dyslipidemia [33]. Iodine supplementation may improve the lipid profile of patients with iodine deficiency [27].

Association between high level of serum iodine (HLSI) and dysthyroidism

Our findings indicate that dysthyroidism and subclinical hyperthyroidism are associated with HLSI. Also, Yu et al. established that the SI was positively correlated with total T4, total T3 and fT4 [24]. In addition, Xu et al. found that men with an SI above the reference value had a higher risk of subclinical hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism. Women with SI below the reference value had a higher risk of subclinical hypothyroidism [34]. The thyroid's secretory capacity was affected by iodine excess [35]. Thus, the SI can be used as an indicator to assess nutritional iodine status and thyroid dysfunction.

Strengths and limitations of the study

To our knowledge, the present study was the first study carried out on SI and thyroid function in Madagascar. It allowed us to determine the SI level and thyroid function in hospital Malagasy adults and the association between the HLSI and dysthyroidism. All of this will compact the country's databases and improve patient care. It also promotes national-scale work so that it is representative for all Malagasy population.

Our study has limitations. The cross-sectional study design precluded the establishment of causal inference, thereby influencing the determination of risk factors. Certain medical records were missing potential confounders risk factors, including dietary iodine intake, salt consumption, socioeconomic factors, and medication use, which may affect both iodine status and thyroid function. The use of the standard unit instead of the universal reference unit has the potential to impede the generalization of the study's findings and the reproducibility of its results. The exclusion of certain participants resulted in a reduction in the sample size, thereby introducing selection bias and limiting the generalizability of the findings. The limited sample size of the study and sparse data imposed constraints on the statistical analysis, which were further compounded by the occurrence of abnormally high odds ratios.

Public health implications

The findings of the present study indicate that disorders of iodine status and thyroid function persist as prevalent public health issues in low-income countries, a phenomenon that is also observed in our nation. It is imperative to monitor these issues closely and on a regular basis. Serum iodine testing could represent an alternative for measuring urinary iodine concentration that is easier, less burdensome, and more reproducible.

Conclusion

Serum iodine excess and dysthyroidism in adults were quite common. Their associations were significant. This implies that screening for dysthyroidism should be systematic in the face of serum iodine excess, and vice versa. Despite its limitations, this work calls for the development of protocols for monitoring iodine status and thyroid function in adults of all genders. Therapeutic education on reducing salt consumption is also important, especially among young people. Periodic monitoring and control of salt iodization quality appear to be essential elements in this prevention.
