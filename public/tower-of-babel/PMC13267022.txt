Capsaicin inhibits cariogenic Streptococcus mutans–Candida albicans cross-kingdom biofilms
Authors: Xinyan Huang, Jianhang Bao, Jiaqi Zhu, Shuangyue Wu, Yuankun Zhai, Yanfang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis research aimed to determine whether capsaicin (CAP), a phytochemical in chilli peppers, inhibits cariogenic Streptococcus mutans–Candida albicans cross-kingdom biofilms and their key virulence factors, and to explore underlying mechanisms.DesignThe anti-caries potential of CAP was assessed in S. mutans–C. albicans co-cultures by quantifying viable cell counts, biomass and biofilm metabolic activity. Biofilm microstructure was visualized by confocal laser scanning microscopy with COMSTAT quantification, and CAP-induced transcriptional changes were profiled by qRT-PCR. Matched ethanol vehicle controls were included.ResultsCAP inhibited the growth of both organisms (MIC = 320 µg/mL). At 1/2 MIC (160 µg/mL) and MIC, CAP significantly suppressed biofilm formation, disrupted pre-formed biofilms and decreased acid production and extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) accumulation. In mature biofilms, CAP downregulated key virulence genes associated with EPS biosynthesis (gtfB, gtfC, gtfD) and fungal pathogenicity (HWP1, ERG4, CHT2, SOD3), suggesting potential interference with glucosyltransferase activity, hyphal morphogenesis and membrane-cell wall integrity.ConclusionCAP attenuated key virulence attributes of S. mutans–C. albicans biofilms in vitro, suggesting its potential as a natural adjunct in caries management. However, its efficacy and deliverability require optimization of oral formulations and rigorous in vivo/clinical validation.

Introduction

Biofilm-dependent dental caries, a chronic disease affecting more than 2.6 billion individuals worldwide, remains a significant research focus [1,2]. Growing evidence highlights the synergistic interaction between Streptococcus mutans and Candida albicans, whose intertwined biofilms significantly elevate caries risk, particularly in early childhood caries and root caries [3,4]. The cornerstone of this cross-kingdom relationship lies in the binding of S. mutans GtfB to the N-/O-mannans of C. albicans, which facilitates rapid surface migration, active communication and nutrient cross-feeding. These interactions enhance biofilm tolerance to environmental stress and increase the risk of extensive caries lesions, posing significant clinical challenges [5–7]. Although broad-spectrum chlorhexidine (CHX) has been widely used for caries management, its long-term use is limited by several well-documented drawbacks, including the induction of resistance and, most critically, disruption of oral microbiome homeostasis. Evidence indicates that CHX use can induce an ecological shift to a caries-associated community [8–11] and may potentially increase the risk of hypertension [12]. As a result, caries management strategies grounded in ecological principles [13] now prioritize plant-derived and pharmaco-food interventions that are microbiome-friendly and capable of targeting the synergistic interactions between cariogenic microbes.

Capsaicin (CAP), a pungent bioactive compound derived from Capsicum species and historically used for culinary and medicinal purposes since 7000 BC, has long been recognized as a valuable analgesic agent [14,15] and is known for its analgesic properties despite its characteristic algogenic effects. Emerging evidence has repositioned CAP as a pharmaco-nutrient with multifaceted biological activities, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antitumor and circadian rhythm-modulating effects, while also functioning as a microbiome and metabolic modulator [16–19]. Additionally, extensive clinical evidence supports the association between regular chili pepper consumption and reduced risks of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality across diverse populations [20,21]. Importantly, during consumption of chilli peppers, CAP is delivered directly to the oral cavity and can transiently contact saliva, dental biofilms and mucosal surfaces, making the oral microbiome an immediate target. Recent national oral health surveys in China have shown an association between chili pepper consumption and caries prevalence, with lower caries prevalence in high-consumption regions compared to regions with lower chili pepper intake [22,23].

Given its widespread availability, eco-friendly nature and intrinsic antimicrobial potential, CAP represents a promising candidate for anti-caries therapy. Limited studies have reported that CAP inhibits the growth of S. mutans and C. albicans in single-species settings that attenuate environmental pH drops, reduce mono-species biofilm formation [24–27] and suppresses fungal hyphae development [26]. However, inconsistencies have been reported among the studies, including notably high minimum inhibitory concentrations (≥1,500 μg/mL) required for activity against C. albicans [28] and a lack of inhibitory effects on S. mutans in some instances [29]. The reported MIC values can vary substantially depending on the microbial strain, whether Capsicum-derived extracts or purified CAP are used, and the assay conditions [27]. Importantly, there remains a critical gap in understanding the effects of CAP in clinically more relevant dual-species models of S. mutans–C. albicans, as most existing research focuses solely on mono-species settings.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the inhibitory effects of CAP on S. mutans–C. albicans dual-species growth in both planktonic and biofilm states, assess its impact on key virulence factors and preliminarily explore underlying mechanisms, thereby providing insight into CAP's potential as a natural anti-caries agent.

Materials and methods

Microorganisms and capsaicin

S. mutans UA159 and C. albicans SC5314 (Oral Biobank of China, Sichuan; kind gifts from Dr. Xin Xu) were used throughout. Capsaicin (CAP; ≥99% purity; MCE) was dissolved in 100% ethanol at 10 mg/mL, aliquoted, protected from light, and stored at −20 °C until use.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination

MICs of CAP were determined using twofold broth microdilution according to CLSI guidelines (fungi: M27; bacteria: M07/M100). RPMI 1640 was used for C. albicans and TSBYE for S. mutans [30,31]. Considering the broad MIC variability reported and our pilot screening, CAP was tested at 10–1,280 µg/mL. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration showing no visible turbidity after 24 h. To visualize dose-dependent growth inhibition, growth curves were performed across the same CAP range using an initial inoculum of 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL in TSBYE for each microorganism.

Planktonic culture establishment

Colonies of C. albicans from YPD agar (Sangon Biotech, China) and S. mutans from blood agar (Quanlong, China) were subcultured twice in YPD broth (Sangon Biotech, China) or TSBYE broth (3% tryptic soy and 0.5% yeast extract; Solarbio, China) supplemented with 1% glucose, respectively, at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until the target inoculum concentration was reached [5]. To mimic a high-caries-risk environment, C. albicans and S. mutans were co-inoculated at 10³ CFU/mL and 10⁵ CFU/mL, respectively [5,30,32,33]. Mono-species time-kill assays were conducted in parallel using the same initial inoculum as the dual-species model (C. albicans: 10³ CFU/mL; S. mutans: 10⁵ CFU/mL) to enable direct comparison. Viable counts were measured at 0, 6 and 20 h, and pH was recorded at 0, 2, 4, 6 and 20 h using a calibrated microelectrode (Mettler Toledo, USA), with or without CAP.

Dual-species biofilm model establishment

Biofilms were established on saliva-coated 24-well plates (Corning, USA), with initial inoculum concentration matching those used in planktonic cultures. To assess effects on biofilm formation (biofilm inhibition), CAP was added at inoculation in TSBYE with 1% (w/v) sucrose to model a high-sucrose cariogenic challenge and incubated for 24 h (37 °C, 5% CO₂). To assess the ability to eradicate pre-formed cariogenic biofilms (biofilm destruction), dual-species biofilms were allowed to form without interference for 24 h in TSBYE containing 0.1% (w/v) sucrose to facilitate controlled early biofilm establishment [5,30,32]. At 24 h and 48 h, the spent medium was carefully aspirated and replaced with fresh TSBYE supplemented with 1% (w/v) sucrose, with or without CAP, to replenish nutrients and maintain a consistent sucrose challenge. No washing step was performed between medium changes to minimize unintentional mechanical disruption of the biofilm architecture. Immediately before harvesting, biofilms were gently rinsed with sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) and then harvested and sonicated at predefined time points for microbiological and molecular analyses. Untreated groups served as growth controls, and ethanol at the corresponding concentration served as vehicle controls.

Crystal violet (CV) staining and tetrazolium reduction assay (MTT metabolic assay) of biofilms

Total biomass was quantified using the CV staining method. At the endpoints of biofilm inhibition (24 h) and destruction (72 h) models, biofilms were washed twice with PBS (Gibco™, USA), air dried, stained with 0.1% (w/v) CV (Solarbio, China) for 20 min, and then eluted with 95% (v/v) ethanol. Metabolic activity was assessed using the MTT assay (0.2 mg/mL; Solarbio, China). After twice PBS washes, biofilms were incubated in the dark with MTT, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 100% DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm (CV) and 570 nm (MTT) using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Biomass and metabolic activity were expressed as percentages relative to untreated growth controls.

Biofilm imaging by laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscopy (LCSFM)

Biofilms were grown simultaneously in confocal dishes (Nest, China) using the same media schemes. Exopolysaccharides were labeled throughout growth with 1 μM Alexa Fluor® 647-dextran (Molecular Probes, USA; 647/668 nm), and microbial cells were labeled at endpoints with 2.5 μM SYTO® 9 green fluorescent nucleic acid stain (Molecular Probes, USA; 485/498 nm) [32]. Five random fields per dish were imaged using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope with a 60× oil objective. Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated in Imaris, and biomass (μm3/μm2) and average thickness (μm) were quantified using COMSTAT2 [34].

RNA extraction and real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Biofilms were stabilized in RNAlater (Applied Biosystems/Ambion, USA) for 1 h, scraped, and total RNA was purified using the MasterPure Complete kit (Lucigen, USA). RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis prior to reverse transcription. cDNA was synthesized from 0.2 μg of purified RNA using PrimeScript™ (Takara, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed with TB Green® (Takara, Japan) on a QuantStudio™ 3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Housekeeping genes of S. mutans (gyrA) and C. albicans (ACT1) were used as internal reference for comparative expression calculation. Amplification specificity was confirmed by melting-curve analysis, and only primer pairs showing a single melting peak were included.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism (v10.4.2). All experiments were performed in at least three independent repeats with duplicate wells. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, multiple comparisons were tested using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied to data that were not normally distributed. Statistical tests were two-sided with a significance level of 5%.

Results

Capsaicin inhibited the growth of S. mutans and C. albicans in planktonic co-cultures

Capsaicin (CAP) effectively inhibited the growth of S. mutans and C. albicans, with minimum inhibitory concentration (MICs) of 320 µg/mL for both strains (Figure S1A and B). Growth kinetic assays further showed dose-dependent growth suppression of C. albicans across the same CAP range (Figure S1D). In mono-species time-kill assays initiated with inocula matched to the dual-species model, CAP induced a significant reduction in S. mutans and C. albicans viability compared to the mono-species growth controls (Figure S1E and F). Notably, for C. albicans, CAP at MIC reduced viability by >3 logs relative to the initial inoculum (Figure S1F). In the dual-species planktonic high-caries-risk model, 1/2 MIC and MIC of CAP significantly suppressed S. mutans growth by over 2 and 3 logs, respectively, at 6 h (p < 0.0001), compared to untreated controls (Figure 1A). Notably, at 20 h, CAP at MIC maintained a potent inhibitory effect, achieving a >6-log reduction in bacterial viability, whereas 1/2 MIC resulted in only a ~1-log reduction. Vehicle controls confirmed that the observed suppression was mainly due to CAP treatment.

(C) Medium pH changes in dual-species. Vehicle controls: 1.6 and 3.2% ethanol (E); *p < 0.05 compared to growth control, #p < 0.05 compared to other CAP concentration. A dotted line indicates a non-detectable level (<20 CFU/mL). n = 3 independent experiments; data are presented as mean ± SEM. One-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc tests.

In this dual-species co-culture, CAP at both 1/2 MIC and MIC resulted in significant reductions in viable C. albicans by 2 and 3 logs, respectively, at 6 h (p < 0.0001) (Figure 1B). At 20 h, CAP at MIC continued to exert sustained inhibition, reducing C. albicans to undetectable levels (<20 CFU/mL). In contrast, C. albicans treated with 1/2 MIC of CAP displayed regrowth but remained significantly suppressed, with a 2-log reduction compared to controls.

Both CAP concentrations significantly alleviated medium acidification in the cariogenic dual-species model (Figure 1C). Medium pH was elevated in all treated groups relative to controls, with the highest pH (~6.9) observed in the MIC-treated group.

CAP interfered with biofilm formation by S. mutans and C. albicans

Co-incubation with CAP at 1/2 MIC and MIC for 24 h in the biofilm inhibition model, significantly reduced both the biomass and metabolic activity of the S. mutans and C. albicans dual-species biofilms (Figure 2A and B). At MIC, biofilm formation was reduced to only 8.8% of that observed in untreated controls, with a >99% decrease in metabolic activity. While CAP at 1/2 MIC was less effective, it still led to a 62.4% reduction in biomass and 72.1% in metabolic activity compared to untreated controls. Notably, CAP at both concentrations demonstrated acid-neutralizing capacities, a critical factor in caries prevention, with higher medium pH values of 6.2 (1/2 MIC) and 6.9 (MIC), both above the critical threshold for enamel demineralization. In addition, CAP treatment resulted in a significant decrease in the viability of S. mutans and C. albicans within the biofilms (Figure 2D–F).

Intriguingly, vehicle controls showed comparable biofilm formation, medium pH and enhanced metabolic activities of biofilms at 24 h. Since biofilm formation is generally more susceptible than mature biofilms, and the vehicle controls did not induce distinct alterations in biofilm formation, these groups were excluded from further analysis.

CAP effectively disrupted pre-formed S. mutans and C. albicans biofilms

Following two consecutive treatments, results of CV staining, MTT assays and viable cell counts substantiated the biofilm-disrupting effects of CAP (Figure 3). Although CAP at 1/2 MIC and MIC diminished biomass by 17.1 and 24.7%, respectively, a significant decline in metabolic activity of 39% (1/2 MIC) and 71.5% (MIC) was observed. CAP significantly diminished S. mutans and C. albicans viability, compared to untreated dual-species biofilms (Figure 3D and E). Specifically, CAP inhibited S. mutans growth by 2.5 and 3 logs at 48 h and 72 h, respectively. However, in mature biofilms, CAP's efficacy against C. albicans was less pronounced than in biofilm inhibition models, showing a ~1 log reduction at both 48 h and 72 h under the MIC of CAP. Moreover, CAP at the MIC counteracted medium acidification, reaching caries-safe pH levels of 5.97 at 72 h.

Microscopic observations of inhibited cariogenic biofilm with CAP

CAP at 1/2 MIC and MIC altered the biofilm structures in both inhibition and destruction models (Figure 4). Compared to the dense and compact cariogenic dual-species biofilms in untreated controls, CAP treatment induced dose-dependent thinning of biofilms, with sparse EPS and microbial cells. The biomass of EPS decreased by 60.7 and 95.0% under CAP at 1/2 MIC and MIC, respectively, accompanied by 96.1 and 99.9% reduction in microbial biomass. In mature biofilms at 72 h, while untreated biofilms displayed a more intertwined structure, CAP still successfully thinned biofilms, reduced EPS accumulation and reduced microbial cell numbers. Notably, the difference in eradication efficacy between CAP concentrations was minimal, with a less than 10% variation.

CAP downregulated S. mutans and C. albicans virulence genes in pre-formed dual-species biofilms

To investigate the transcriptional regulation of virulence genes in S. mutans–C. albicans mature biofilms treated with CAP, qRT-PCR was conducted on biofilms at 72 h (Figure 5). CAP treatment significantly downregulated S. mutans genes related to EPS biosynthesis (gtfB, gtfC and gtfD) by approximately 29–57%, compared to untreated controls. Interestingly, CAP at 1/2 MIC exhibited a greater reduction in the expression of the gtfD gene than groups treated with CAP at MIC (p = 0.0046). Additionally, lacG and lacC genes, involved in lactose metabolism, were significantly down-regulated in CAP-treated biofilms. Conversely, a significant up-regulation of atpD, encoding a subunit of ATP synthase, was observed in 1/2 MIC CAP-treated groups.

CAP altered expression of key virulence genes in dual-species cariogenic biofilms. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001,***p < 0.0001. n = 3 independent experiments; data are presented as mean ± SEM. One-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc tests.

For C. albicans, CAP treatment at 1/2 MIC and MIC resulted in a 3.6-fold and 1.9-fold decrease in the expression of the HWP1 gene, associated with yeast-hypha formation, respectively, compared to untreated controls. Meanwhile, genes involved in fungal cell wall chitin remodeling (CHT2) and oxidative stress protection (SOD3) were significantly downregulated. While not statistically significant, ERG4, a gene related to the synthesis of ergosterol, an essential component of the fungal cell membrane, showed a decreasing trend under CAP treatment, relative to controls. In contrast, HSP90 and CDR1 genes, involved in protein folding, stress protection and encoding drug efflux, were significantly upregulated upon CAP treatment.

Discussion

The findings of this study show that capsaicin (CAP) exerts potent anti-cariogenic effects against S. mutans–C. albicans dual-species communities by suppressing microbial growth, acid production and biofilm development. CAP also disrupted mature biofilms and reduced exopolysaccharide (EPS) accumulation, accompanied by downregulation of key virulence genes in both species. These findings indicate that CAP can reduce cross-kingdom biofilm pathogenicity through multiple pathways, highlighting its potential as an effective natural agent for ecological caries management.

In this study, CAP's minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for both S. mutans and C. albicans was 320 μg/mL. Prior reports span a wide MIC range for S. mutans (1.25–50 μg/mL in some studies [24,25] and no inhibition up to 2,000 μg/mL [29] and for C. albicans (2–25 μg/mL for laboratory strains and 12.5–50 μg/mL for oral isolates [26,35], with ≥1,500 μg/mL for ATCC strains in one study [28]. Such dispersion likely reflects differences in strain background, source and purity of CAP (fruit extracts vs. purified compound), underscoring the need for strain-specific responses and standardized CAP formulations. Within this context, the MIC in current study indicates moderate planktonic susceptibility. Importantly, CAP exerted antibiofilm effects at 1/2MIC, supporting a primarily biofilm-modulatory role. Any consideration for topical oral use will require cytocompatibility assessment and formulation-dependent exposure optimization.

Most previous work evaluated CAP in single-species settings [25,26,35], whereas our study addresses more clinically relevant S. mutans–C. albicans interactions that drive oral dysbiosis and cariogenicity [36]. Co-cultivation of these organisms amplifies cariogenic virulence, enhancing EPS and acid production and tolerance to stress [5,37–39], emphasizing agents that retain efficacy in dual-species biofilms are more likely to translate clinically. Consistent with this concept, CAP-associated killing was attenuated in co-culture relative to mono-species conditions. To our knowledge, this is among the first demonstrations that CAP inhibits dual-species biofilm development and destabilizes established biofilms, accompanied by reductions in biomass, metabolic activity and viable counts, dampened acidification and EPS accumulation, two key drives of caries development.

At the transcriptional level, CAP significantly downregulated gtfB, gtfC and gtfD genes, consistent with the observed decrease in EPS biomass, suggesting that altered gtf transcription may contribute in part to reduced matrix production. Notably, gtfB and gtfC, associated with insoluble glucan-rich scaffold formations [40–42], showed comparable suppression between the 1/2 MIC and MIC groups, whereas gtfD gene, linked to soluble glucans synthesis, functioning mainly as primers and potential carbon reserves [41], was slightly more downregulated at 1/2 MIC. Therefore, the greater EPS biomass reduction at MIC likely reflects a broader overall inhibition on net biofilm accumulation and matrix development in the dual-species community, rather than proportional changes in gtf transcripts alone.. Moreover, the altered carbohydrate-metabolism genes (lacC and lacG) may indicate metabolic adaptation toward lactose utilization in response to CAP exposure.

In addition to its effects on S. mutans, CAP also modulated the expression of several key virulence- and stress-associated genes in C. albicans, including downregulating the hyphal formation gene (HWP1) and ergosterol synthesis gene (ERG4), both of which are central to fungal pathogenicity, in line with mono-species observations reporting CAP-associated suppression of hyphae formation and ergosterol synthesis [26]. Furthermore, CAP repressed CHT2 and SOD3 genes expression, linked to cell-wall remodeling [43] and oxidative-stress tolerance [44]. Taken together, CAP appears to act on three principal fungal vulnerability axes: (1) pathogenicity—by inhibiting hyphal adhesion and biofilm formation (HWP1) [45]; (2) membrane and cell-wall integrity—through suppression of ERG4 [46] and CHT2 genes; and (3) stress resistance—via repression of SOD3 gene. These multifaceted effects likely converge to weaken fungal persistence within mixed biofilms.

Notably, following two consecutive CAP exposure cycles, a modest induction of HSP90 (~2-fold) and a pronounced induction of CDR1 (~7–10-fold) may indicate activation of compensatory stress-adaptation and efflux-associated programs in residual biofilm fraction [47,48]. HSP90 encodes a central molecular chaperone in diverse stress-response circuits, and as a key enabler of antifungal tolerance phenotypes [49,50]. However, transcriptional upregulation at a single endpoint does not demonstrate stable, heritable resistance, as CDR1 can be transiently induced by environmental stress [51]. Nevertheless, such responses could favor subpopulations with heightened stress buffering and efflux capacity, potentially undermining long-term CAP durability and promoting cross-protection to antifungals. These observations motivate longitudinal studies testing whether HSP90/CDR1 elevation persists after CAP withdrawal and contributes functionally to tolerance, and support evaluating combination strategies, such as CAP paired with conventional antifungals, or adjuvants that attenuate Hsp90-dependent stress buffering or efflux activity.

CAP is a food-derived small molecule with a long record of human exposure and generally good tolerability in topical or oral formulations [52,53]. At 1/2 MIC (160 µg/mL), CAP produced robust anti-biofilm effects with a non-lethal and anti-virulence profile, consistent with the ecological perspective. To contextualize this effective in vitro level, CAP contents reported for some chilli condiments are in a similar order of magnitude on a mass basis [54,55]; for instance, canned chipotle contains 196.8 ± 23.5 μg/g CAP. However, intraoral exposure during dietary consumption is transient, subjected to rapid salivary dilution and clearance. Therefore, these food-content measurements are cited strictly to reflect order-of-magnitude context for routine human exposure and tolerability, rather than to imply sustained therapeutic concentrations following dietary intake. Importantly, commercial condiments contain additional bioactive capsaicinoids, like dihydrocapsaicin, meaning that an equivalent CAP-only dose would likely impose a lower pungency burden than the total capsaicinoid content of these products. Beyond direct antibiofilm activity, CAP acutely stimulates salivary flow [56], potentially{Shin, 2016 #48} enhancing oral clearance of fermentable substrates and strengthening bicarbonate-mediated buffering. Formulation advances, such as nano-carriers, may enhance CAP's bioaccessibility while reducing irritation, thereby improving clinical acceptability [57,58].

However, a limitation of this study is the exclusive use of reference strains, and responses may differ among clinical isolates. Future studies should evaluate diverse clinical panels, assess the impacts of CAP on commensal microbiota, and validate efficacy and safety in vivo and in clinical settings, including formulation-dependent exposure and cytocompatibility.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, CAP attenuated cariogenicity in dual-species S. mutans and C. albicans biofilms by disrupting the virulent cross-kingdom interaction, reducing EPS production destabilizing the biofilm structure, and suppressing acid production. These effects may involve interference with glucosyltransferase-associated EPS synthesis, C. albicans hyphal development and membrane/cell-wall integrity. Collectively, these findings suggest that CAP could serve as an adjunctive approach for ecological caries management. However, translation will require cytotoxicity assessment, evaluation of CAP's impact on commensal oral microbiota and overall ecological balance, validation in in vivo caries models and optimization of an appropriate oral formulation prior to clinical evaluation.
