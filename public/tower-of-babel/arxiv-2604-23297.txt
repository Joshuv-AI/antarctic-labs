Noise spectroscopy of insulating and itinerant altermagnets
Lucas V. Pupim, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
One of the central goals in the emergent field of altermagnetism is the unambiguous experimental identification and characterization of altermagnetic order across a variety of compounds. This motivates exploring tools that can clearly distinguish altermagnets from antiferromagnets, based on symmetry signatures, and offer access to the dominant orbital character (e.g., d d -wave vs. g g -wave) of the magnetic order parameter. In this work, we theoretically explore the potential of noise magnetometry for this task, studying contributions from both magnons and itinerant electrons in different regimes and scenarios. While altermagnetism and antiferromagnetism also lead to different noise spectra for magnons, we find the most striking and symmetry-sensitive signatures in the charge fluctuations of itinerant altermagnets. Both for the homogeneous bulk case and in the presence of strain and/or around domain walls, we identify noise contributions that are only permitted by symmetry in the altermagnet and, thus, provide a unique signature of altermagnetism. Furthermore, the angular dependence of noise around domain walls also offers access to the orbital character of the altermagnet. On a more technical note, we discuss the role and relevance of lattice effects related to the dipole tensor. We hope that our work will help pave the way towards the clear experimental identification of altermagnetism across a wide range of candidate materials.

The spins

As such, the noise will probe the magnon modes, which differ in AFMs and AMs as a result of their distinct symmetries. In particular, a well established fact is that AMs have split magnon bands
40
;
58
;
12

We focus on the regime where the magnon spectra are only partially gapped, due to an applied external magnetic field, and one band remains gapless. This means that the magnetic noise is dominated by single-magnon processes rather than two-magnon scattering and other higher processes. The contrasting “diffusive regime”, where the spectrum is fully gapped (i.e., the probe frequency lies inside the magnon gap due to an easy-axis term) and single magnon processes do not contribute to the noise spectra was studied in
6

While charge-carrying electronic excitations can be neglected in insulating AMs, such that magnons provide the key contributions to the noise spectrum, this is not necessarily the case in the metallic regime. In fact, one of the central properties of altermagnetic order is the resultant non-trivial splitting of the electronic bands and Fermi surfaces. As such, it is naturally expected to lead to non-trivial consequences for the noise arising from charge fluctuations.
To study this, we investigate an electronic tight-binding model on the same checkerboard lattice, Fig.

\boldsymbol{J}_{j}(t)

\boldsymbol{B}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\sum_{j}\frac{\boldsymbol{J}_{j}\times\boldsymbol{r}_{j}}{r_{j}^{3}}.

(3)

More specifically, we look into how time-reversal symmetry breaking paired with other perturbations can give contrasting noise signatures for AFMs and AMs. This contrast, building on the approach of De
et al.

\delta\Gamma

II
Insulating altermagnets

II.1
Model and Magnons

To describe altermagnetism and contrast it with antiferromagnetism in a minimal setting, we use a Heisenberg model on the checkerboard lattice, with Hamiltonian

H=J_{1}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\boldsymbol{S}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{j}+J_{2}\sum_{\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle}\boldsymbol{S}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{j}-\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{i}\,\,\,.

(4)

The sum over
\langle i,j\rangle
includes nearest neighbors (connecting different sublattices) and the sum over
\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle
corresponds to the second nearest neighbors (connecting sites in the same sublattice) indicated as black dashed lines in Fig.
1
(a). We consider the distance between sites of the same sublattice to be
a=1
. In addition, we take the Zeeman field
\boldsymbol{h}=h\,\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
to be uniform and pointing out-of-plane. Notably, this model has been studied in the context of planar pyrochlore
4
;
5
;
52
;
36
;
22
;
8
;
64
and layered titanades
56
. More recently it has been used (as we do here) as a minimal model for altermagnets
73
;
61
;
14
;
12
;
6
;
32
;
31
.

We parameterize the coupling constants as
J_{1}=\eta^{2}
and
J_{2}=-(1-\eta^{2})
for
0\leq\eta\leq 1
, so that
|J_{1}|+|J_{2}|=1
and consequently, the width of the magnon bands is independent of
\eta
. This will capture three different scenarios: when
\eta=1
(i.e.,
J_{1}=1,J_{2}=0
), we effectively obtain a 2D (square-lattice) antiferromagnet with nearest neighbor exchange couplings; when
\eta=0
(i.e.,
J_{1}=0,J_{2}=-1
), the system decouples into 1D (chains in the
x
and
y
directions) ferromagnets; finally, if
0<\eta<1
, we have an altermagnet since, when both
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are finite, the two sublattices in Fig.
1
(a) are not related by translation but, instead, by a rotation.
As the ferromagnetic chain limit is rather artificial and not of interest to us here, we here exclude this case, i.e. focus on
\eta\neq 0
. In fact, we will only consider
\eta\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
, to keep
|J_{1}|\geq|J_{2}|
. We note that the Zeeman field
\boldsymbol{h}
also affects these orders by canting them out of the plane, the details of this effect will be discussed further on.

As we are interested in the noise due to spin fluctuations, we consider the linear spin wave theory (LSWT) regime and calculate the magnon spectra of our model. To obtain these magnons we first perform a Holstein-Primakoff transformation
26
.
This can be done
55
by first rewriting the spin operators using a local basis as
\boldsymbol{S}_{i}=\boldsymbol{m}_{i}S^{\parallel}+\boldsymbol{S}^{\perp}_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}
is the direction of the local magnetization,
S^{\parallel}
and
\boldsymbol{S}^{\perp}_{i}
are the spin components parallel and perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}
, respectively. The perpendicular spin components are parameterized as
\boldsymbol{S}^{\perp}_{i}=\sum_{\nu=\pm}\boldsymbol{e}^{s}_{i}\,S^{\nu}_{i}
, with
\boldsymbol{e}^{\pm}_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{1,i}\mp i\boldsymbol{e}_{2,i}\right)
. Here, the unit vectors
\boldsymbol{e}_{1,i}
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{2,i}
, together with
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}
, locally form a right-handed coordinate system in the sense that
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}=\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{1,i}\times\boldsymbol{e}_{2,i}\right)
.

Now we use the Holstein-Primakoff transformation
\hat{S}^{\parallel}_{i}=S-\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{i}
and
\hat{S}^{+}_{i}=(\hat{S}^{-}_{i})^{\dagger}\simeq\sqrt{2S}\,\hat{b}_{i}
, where
\hat{b}_{i}
are bosonic annihilation operators. We plug these expressions into Eq. (
4
) (for the detailed calculation, see Appendix
A
) and find the direction of
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}
by minimizing the classical energy. Writing
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}=\varrho_{i}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\cos{\theta_{m}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\sin{\theta_{m}}
with
\varrho_{i}=\pm 1
for different sublattices, this yields

\theta_{m}\;=\;\begin{cases}\arcsin\!\left(\dfrac{h}{8J_{1}S}\right),&|h|\leq 8J_{1}S,\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{\pi}{2},&|h|>8J_{1}S.\end{cases}

(5)

Here we see explicitly the canting effect that the Zeeman field has, i.e., it tilts the compensated (in-plane) local magnetization out of the plane and generates an uncompensated component
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}=\sin{\theta_{m}}
.

:
(a) Antiferromagnetic (
\eta=1

Within LSWT the spin-model Hamiltonian in Eq. (
4
) is approximated by a quadratic bosonic theory,
H\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
, which can be compactly written in momentum (
\boldsymbol{k}
) space and in matrix form. We label operators
\hat{b}_{a}
from different sublattices with the lower index
a=1,2
, and use the Bogoliubov basis
\hat{\psi}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\left[\hat{b}_{1,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\,\,\hat{b}_{2,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\,\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{1,-{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{2,-{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right]^{T}
, so
\mathcal{H}/S=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\hat{\psi}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\,\,h_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,\,\hat{\psi}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
,

h_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}4J_{1}\cos{2\theta_{m}}+A_{x}&-C\,\sin^{2}{\theta_{m}}&0&C\cos^{2}{\theta_{m}}\\
-C\,\sin^{2}{\theta_{m}}&4J_{1}\cos{2\theta_{m}}+A_{y}&C\cos^{2}{\theta_{m}}&0\\
0&C\cos^{2}{\theta_{m}}&4J_{1}\cos{2\theta_{m}}+A_{x}&-C\,\sin^{2}{\theta_{m}}\\
C\cos^{2}{\theta_{m}}&0&-C\,\sin^{2}{\theta_{m}}&4J_{1}\cos{2\theta_{m}}+A_{y}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{h}{S}\sin{\theta_{m}}\mathbb{I}.

(6)

For clarity in the expression, we suppressed the
\boldsymbol{k}
dependence of the terms on the right-hand side and defined the momentum dependent factors
A_{i}=2J_{2}(\cos{k_{i}}-1)
and
C=4J_{1}\cos{(k_{x}/2)}\cos{(k_{y}/2)}
. We note that, without the Zeeman field, one could simplify the Hamiltonian to effectively become
2\times 2
(akin to a superconductor with or without spin-orbit coupling) making it straightforward to diagonalize it analytically, c.f.
8
;
6
.
However, in the “full” problem, there are no compact analytical expressions available. Hence, we diagonalize our quadratic bosonic Hamiltonian numerically through the Colpa method
13
;
65
. We give a concise summary of this method in Appendix
B
.

In Fig.
2
, we can see the magnon modes for the antiferromagnetic (a) and altermagnetic (b) cases. The two phases share some features for small momentum, e.g., the linear Goldstone mode around
\Gamma
and the gapped mode due to the Zeeman field. The substantial difference is seen closer to the edge of the Brillouin zone (around the
X
point), where the two magnonic modes are degenerate for the AFM, while for the AM they are split; similar to the spin splitting of the electronic bands, this is a characteristic feature of magnons in altermagnets
58
;
44
.

Another fact that these AM materials and models have is the pair of Van Hove singularities pinned at the edge of the Brillouin zone. As we will see in more detail in the next section, this feature can be important for noise spectroscopy, since this method is not momentum resolved and primarily sensitive to the DOS.

II.2
Computation of Noise Tensor

Now, we need to relate the spin waves from our Hamiltonian model to the magnetic noise sensed through the qubit dephasing (decoherence) or relaxation. For concreteness, we focus on the dephasing rate; however, a similar derivation follows for the relaxation rate, see Appendix
C
. Let us start with the qubit quantization axis
\boldsymbol{n}_{q}
along the
z
direction, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{n}_{q}=(0,0,1)^{T}
, such that the associated rate is
1/T_{2}\propto\mathcal{N}_{zz}
with magnetic noise tensor components
\mathcal{N}_{ab}
defined in Eq. (
1
). We can rotate this axis to an arbitrary direction
\boldsymbol{n}_{q}=(\sin\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta)^{T}
, see Fig.
1
(b), by a pair of rotations through the
z
and
x
axes
R_{\text{tot}}=R_{z}(\phi)R_{x}(\theta)
, such as
\mathcal{N}\rightarrow R_{\text{tot}}^{T}\,\mathcal{N}\,R_{\text{tot}}
. The relevant component becomes

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{zz}\xrightarrow{R_{\text{tot}}}\,\,&\mathcal{N}_{zz}\cos^{2}\theta+\sin(2\theta)\,\sin\phi\,\,\mathcal{N}_{xz}\\
&+\sin^{2}\theta\left(\mathcal{N}_{yy}\cos^{2}\phi+\mathcal{N}_{xx}\sin^{2}\phi\right).\end{split}

(7)

Each of these tensor components can, in turn, be written as functions of the spin-spin correlators, by inserting Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
1
). For conciseness, we here write down explicitly only
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
and leave the detailed calculation for Appendix
C
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{zz}&=\frac{1}{2}\coth{\frac{\omega}{2T}}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\\
&\quad\times\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\,\,e^{-2kd}k^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{zz}+\frac{\mathcal{C}_{xx}+\mathcal{C}_{yy}}{2}),\end{split}

(8)

where
T
is the temperature and

\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)=\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}\text{Im}\left[\,i\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}\langle[S_{a}^{i}(\boldsymbol{k},t),S_{a^{\prime}}^{j}(-\boldsymbol{k},0)]\rangle\,\right].

(9)

A well known property of the expression (
8
), which also applies to the other components of the magnetic noise tensor, is the presence of a momentum filter
e^{-2kd}k^{2}
that peaks at
k\sim 1/d
while suppressing other values. One can picture this filter as the spatial averaging out of noise from short wavelength (
k\gg 1/d
) modes while long wavelength magnons (
k\ll 1/d
) are nearly uniform over the sensor scale and therefore generate little noise.

We also note that to calculate these spin-spin correlators, we need the spin operators in the lab frame, which read in terms of our Holstein-Primakoff bosons as

\begin{split}S^{x}_{a}(\boldsymbol{k})&\simeq\varrho_{a}\left(\sqrt{2S}\sin\theta_{m}\frac{\hat{b}_{a,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\hat{b}_{a,-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}}{2}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad+\left.\cos\theta_{m}(S-\hat{b}_{a,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{a,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}})\right),\\
S^{y}_{a}(\boldsymbol{k})&\simeq i\,\varrho_{a}\sqrt{2S}\frac{\hat{b}_{a,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}-\hat{b}_{a,-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}}{2},\\
S^{z}_{a}(\boldsymbol{k})&\simeq-\varrho_{a}\sqrt{2S}\cos\theta_{m}\frac{\hat{b}_{a,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\hat{b}_{a,-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}}{2}\\
&\quad\,+\sin\theta_{m}(S-\hat{b}_{a,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{a,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}),\end{split}

(10)

where
a=1,2
again labels the two sublattices.
We use the diagonalizing (Bogoliubov) transformation to rewrite our bosonic operators, i.e.

\hat{\psi}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=T_{\boldsymbol{k}}\hat{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
,
with

\hat{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\left(\hat{\gamma}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{+,\boldsymbol{k}},\hat{\gamma}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{-,\boldsymbol{k}},\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}_{+,-\boldsymbol{k}},\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}_{-,-\boldsymbol{k}}\right)^{T}
, which also allows to conveniently state their form in the Heisenberg picture,
\hat{\gamma}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}t}\hat{\gamma}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}(0)
, where
\omega_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}
are the associated magnon-band energies. The transformation
T_{\boldsymbol{k}}
can be chosen to have all its entries being real numbers and we write

T_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k})&\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{k})\\
\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{k})&\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k})\end{pmatrix},

(11)

where
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are
2\times 2
matrices. Denoting their components by
u_{ij}=[\mathcal{U}(k)]_{ij}
and
v_{ij}=[\mathcal{V}(k)]_{ij}
, the spin-spin correlators become

\begin{split}&C_{xx}=\frac{S}{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{m}\,\left[\delta(\omega-\omega_{+,\boldsymbol{k}})(u_{11}-u_{12}+v_{11}-v_{21})^{2}\right.\\
&+\left.\delta(\omega-\omega_{-,\boldsymbol{k}})(u_{12}-u_{22}+v_{12}-v_{22})^{2}\right],\\
\\
&C_{yy}=\frac{S}{2}\,\,\left[\delta(\omega-\omega_{+,\boldsymbol{k}})(u_{11}-u_{12}-v_{11}+v_{21})^{2}\right.\\
&+\left.\delta(\omega-\omega_{-,\boldsymbol{k}})(u_{12}-u_{22}-v_{12}+v_{22})^{2}\right],\\
\\
&C_{zz}=\frac{S}{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{m}\,\left[\delta(\omega-\omega_{+,\boldsymbol{k}})(u_{11}-u_{12}+v_{11}-v_{21})^{2}\right.\\
&+\left.\delta(\omega-\omega_{-,\boldsymbol{k}})(u_{12}-u_{22}+v_{12}-v_{22})^{2}\right],\end{split}

(12)

where we suppressed the
\boldsymbol{k}
dependence of the coherence factors
u_{ij}
and
v_{ij}
on the right-hand side for notational simplicity.
We note that these expressions are proportional to the spectral density of each band. This feature will give us qualitative differences between the AM and the AFM cases. We further note that the noise is dominated (in leading order) by single magnon processes due to our
(1/S)
expansion. Hence, there is no contribution from the two-point correlator terms proportional to
\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\delta\!\left(\pm\omega_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}\pm\omega_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\omega\right)\left(n_{\boldsymbol{k}\pm\boldsymbol{q}}\pm n_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)
.

II.3
Results

In the expressions of Eq. (
12
), we can see that the spin-spin correlators
C_{ij}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)
are all proportional to
\delta(\omega-\omega_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}})
. Hence, the relevant components of
\mathcal{N}
, which involve integrals of
C_{ij}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)
over
\boldsymbol{k}
, are crucially determined by the density of states of the magnon bands
\omega_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}
. This fact will be the cause of the main qualitative difference in the noise spectrum between AFMs and AMs to be discussed next.

Recall from Fig.
2
(a) that the antiferromagnetic magnon spectrum has a Van-Hove singularity only at the highest energy (
\omega_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}=4
in our dimensionless units) and pinned to the edges of the Brillouin zone. On the other hand, the altermagnetic magnon bands are split and therefore exhibit Van-Hove singularities at two different energies. For our specific model and choice of parameters (
\eta^{2}=1/2
) used in the numerics, the second Van-Hove energy is at half of the bandwidth (
\omega_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}=2
in our dimensionless units) and also pinned at the edge of the Brillouin zone.

This difference in the density of states is directly reflected in the decoherence rate
1/T_{2}
when our probe qubit is very close to the sample, as shown in Fig.
3
. We can see that for both AFM and AM, the frequency
\omega

1/T_{2}
is crucially determined by the DOS (dashed curves) behavior for
d<a
(here we forego our convention of
a=1
for clarity in the scales involved).
Consequently, for an AFM,
1/T_{2}(\omega)
has a single peak in its decoherence (and also relaxation) profiles as one scans
\omega
, while altermagnetism leads to two peaks. We note that measuring
T_{2}
(as opposed to
T_{1}
) allows us to scan
\omega
by using different dynamic decoupling protocols
69
;
15
;
2
;
17
;
74
(frequency filters), e.g., the Hahn spin echo
25
.

Of course, the premise is that one can place the probe close enough to the sample and reach high frequencies. Unfortunately, this leads to practical issues that arise with this method of distinguishing an AFM and an AM. If one places the qubit farther and farther away from the magnetic sample, the different signatures of AFMs and AMs fade away. This problem comes from the term
e^{-2kd}k^{2}
in the noise expression Eq. (
8
). As mentioned before, it acts as a momentum filter, favoring momenta with
k\sim 1/d
. This filter makes the effects that come from the edges of the Brillouin zone effectively invisible for distances
d\gg a
. Hence, the distinction between having one and two VHS in our model cannot be probed with qubits at distances larger than the lattice scale.
We note that this issue will become less severe in systems with Van-Hove singularities away from the edges of the Brillouin zone and, thus, at smaller momenta. Another possibility of resolving such complications lies in exploring systems where the lattice spacing
a
is large enough, e.g., moiré altermagnets
38
;
51
, so that

Note that, in the regime where

since the Fourier transform of the magnetic dipole, Eq. (

\omega=0
, while larger

We also note that for AFMs and AMs, the modes at the edge of the Brillouin zone typically have frequencies on the order of THz

12

As such, systems with smaller magnonic bandwidth are more naturally probed with noise spectroscopy, given the current status of the field. In a moiré system, this issue might be resolved due to the rescaling of effective interactions.

III
Noise from itinerant electrons

Having scrutinized the noise contributions from the magnetic order parameter in insulating AMs, we next generalize to itinerant systems. Here, the additional noise contributions from the gapless electronic excitations need to be taken into account. This is the focus of this section.

III.1
Model

To describe itinerant electrons, we replace the Heisenberg model with an electronic tight-binding model on the checkerboard lattice in Fig.
1
(a). Instead of only spin degrees of freedom, electrons, as described by the creation operators
c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}
,
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
, can now occupy the different sites
i=(\boldsymbol{R},a)
, labeled by the Bravais lattice points
\boldsymbol{R}
and the sublattice index
a=1,2
. We include nearest-neighbor hopping (with amplitude
t_{1}
) along the solid diagonal bonds in Fig.
1
(a) as well as next-nearest-neighbor hopping; the associated amplitudes are given by
t_{2}+\delta t_{2}
and
t_{2}-\delta t_{2}
along the green and black dashed bonds, respectively. In the limit
\delta t_{2}\rightarrow 0
, the checkerboard lattice becomes a square lattice with nearest- and next-nearest-neighbor hopping. To add antisymmetric spin-orbit coupling, we impose the
C_{4v}
point-group symmetries and restrict ourselves to nearest-neighbor processes, such that spin-orbit coupling is described by a single parameter
\alpha_{R}
.

Introducing the associated momentum-space operators
c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},a,\sigma}
, the Hamiltonian can be written as
H_{\text{it}}=H_{0}+H_{\text{m}}
, where
H_{0}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}h_{0,\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
with the
4\times 4
Bloch-Hamiltonian in sublattice (
\tau_{j}
) and spin space (
s_{j}
),

h_{0,\boldsymbol{k}}=(\tau_{0}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\tau_{x}t_{x,\boldsymbol{k}}+\tau_{z}t_{z,\boldsymbol{k}})s_{0}+(g_{x,\boldsymbol{k}}s_{x}+g_{y,\boldsymbol{k}}s_{y})\tau_{x}.

(13)

Here,
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-2t_{2}[\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}]-\mu
is the sublattice-independent part of the dispersion,
t_{z,\boldsymbol{k}}=-2\delta t_{2}[\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}]
the sublattice antisymmetric part, and
t_{x,\boldsymbol{k}}=4t_{1}\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}\cos\frac{k_{y}}{2}
couples the two sublattices. The two contributions
g_{x,\boldsymbol{k}}=2\alpha_{R}\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}\sin\frac{k_{y}}{2}
and
g_{y,\boldsymbol{k}}=-2\alpha_{R}\cos\frac{k_{y}}{2}\sin\frac{k_{x}}{2}
lock the spin and spatial degrees of freedom.
The term describing long-range magnetism is given by
H_{\text{m}}=J\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{R}}\boldsymbol{s}\tau_{z}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{R}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{R})
, where the unit vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{R})
defines the local orientation of the staggered magnetization. As expected,
h_{0,\boldsymbol{k}}
commutes with
\tau_{x}
for
\delta t_{2}=0
, which corresponds to the presence of a translational symmetry relating the red and blue sublattices in Fig.
1
(a).

Let us first focus on spatially uniform magnetism,
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{R})=\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
. If the strength
J
of the altermagnetic order parameter is large enough, there is a gap between the two lower and the two upper bands. For chemical potentials within the energetic range of, say, the lower pair of bands, we obtain the two Fermi surfaces shown in Fig.
5
(a), which are split as a result of the altermagnetic order parameter and spin-orbit coupling. To understand the origin better, we can project the Bloch Hamiltonian
h_{0,\boldsymbol{k}}+J\tau_{z}s_{z}
onto the lower eigenspace of
J\tau_{z}s_{z}
, where spin and sublattice are anti-correlated,
\tau_{z}s_{z}=-1
. Denoting the effective Pauli matrices describing this spin-sublattice locked
2\times 2
subspace by
\sigma_{j}
, the resulting effective two-band model reads as

h_{\text{eff},\boldsymbol{k}}=\sigma_{0}\frac{\boldsymbol{k}^{2}}{2m}-\delta t_{2}\sigma_{z}(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2})+\alpha_{R}(k_{y}\sigma_{x}-k_{x}\sigma_{y}),

(14)

where we have already performed a gradient expansion—only keeping terms up to leading order in momentum in each term—and set
(2m)^{-1}=t_{2}
. The continuum approximation will be essential for our discussion of spatially varying altermagnetic textures in Sec.
III.3
below. Equation (
14
) makes the altermagnetic spin splitting manifest in the Hamiltonian and the fact that a finite hopping imbalance
\delta t_{2}\neq 0
is essential for this effect; it vanishes for
\delta t_{2}=0
since the two sublattices are related by translation in this limit and the magnetic order reduces back to antiferromagnetism.

,

. Note that the relevant contribution to the decoherence difference
\delta\Gamma
comes from momenta
\boldsymbol{q}
with
|\boldsymbol{q}|=1/2d
.

Similar to many of the features in the noise of Sec.
II
related to magnons, we also expect that there will be quantitative differences in the noise spectrum when comparing AM and AFM on the level of Eq. (
14
). We, however, focus here on more striking symmetry signatures that, if observed, would allow us to uniquely distinguish between AMs and AFMs.

Although it is not directly the Berry curvature
\Omega(\boldsymbol{k})
that will play the central role, we can still use it to illustrate the key symmetry argument, as
\Omega(\boldsymbol{k})
provides a way of quantifying the degree (and sign) of broken time-reversal symmetry in the orbital channel at momentum point
\boldsymbol{k}
. We obtain
\Omega(\boldsymbol{k})=0
for the AFM limit
\delta t_{2}=0
; meanwhile, the Berry curvature is non-zero for the AM as is also shown in Fig.
5
(a). It still averages to zero,
\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\Omega(\boldsymbol{k})=0
, as a result of
C_{4z}
rotational symmetry combined with time reversal
\Theta=i\sigma_{y}\mathcal{K}
(
\mathcal{K}
is complex conjugation), making it not directly visible in isotropic local probes like NV noise spectroscopy. However, when perturbing the system in a way that breaks
C_{4z}
, the crucial difference between AM and AFM will become visible, as we discuss next.

III.2
Strain

The first type of such rotational-symmetry-breaking perturbation we will consider is strain, which may either be applied uniformly and intentionally in experiment (see, e.g.,
37
;
66
in the context of AM candidate materials) or be naturally present locally in different regions of the sample. Focusing on uniaxial strain, the associated (
2\times 2
) strain tensor
\mathcal{E}=R^{T}_{\varphi}\text{diag}(-\varepsilon,\nu\varepsilon)R_{\varphi}
, with
R_{\varphi}
denoting the orthogonal matrix rotating two-component vectors by an angle
\varphi
, depends on two independent parameters—the strength
\varepsilon
and direction
\varphi
; the Poisson ratio
\nu
, in turn, is a material-dependent constant.

On the level of the model in Eq. (
14
), we incorporate the effect of strain by bond stretching. We picture the original lattice model, Eq. (
13
), being distorted along the
x
axis (
\varphi=0
above) such as the lattice constants become direction dependent. Hence the couplings
t_{2},\delta t_{2},\alpha_{R}
become anisotropic, too; for instance,
t_{2}\boldsymbol{k}^{2}\rightarrow t_{2}[(1+\varepsilon)k_{x}^{2}+(1-\nu\varepsilon)k_{y}^{2}]
. The remaining couplings transform in the same fashion. This clearly breaks the
C_{4z}\Theta
and the reflection symmetry
\sigma_{d}
along the diagonal. As such, there is no symmetry anymore forcing the angular average of
\Omega(\boldsymbol{k})
to vanish.

To probe this symmetry reduction, which we just illustrated using
\Omega(\boldsymbol{k})
as an example, in the noise spectrum, we follow Ref.
16
and compare
T_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})
with
T_{1}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})
. Note that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{T_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}})}&\propto\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{xx}(\omega_{\text{q}})+\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{yy}(\omega_{\text{q}})\\
&+\,\text{Im}[\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}})-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{yx}(\omega_{\text{q}})],\end{split}

(15)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}=R_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}}}\mathcal{N}R^{T}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}}}
is the rotated noise tensor, with
R_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}}}
rotating
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
onto the qubit quantization axis
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}}
. As such, the difference
\delta\Gamma=T^{-1}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})-T^{-1}_{1}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})
is proportional to
\text{Im}[\mathcal{N}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}})-\mathcal{N}_{yx}(\omega_{\text{q}})]
. As time-reversal
\Theta
acts as complex conjugation on
\mathcal{N}
(see Appendix
E
),
\delta\Gamma\neq 0
implies that
\Theta
is broken as well as
C_{4z}\Theta
and
\sigma_{d}
. Adding strain to Eq. (
14
), all of these symmetries are broken. However, in the AFM limit, we still obtain
\Theta
combined with a lattice translation as a symmetry, which implies
\delta\Gamma\rightarrow 0
(at least for
d\gg a
).

To demonstrate this, we explicitly compute
\delta\Gamma
with noise coming from the current fluctuations coupling to the qubit through Eq. (
3
). As expected from being proportional to
\text{Im}[{\mathcal{N}}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}})-{\mathcal{N}}_{yx}(\omega_{\text{q}})]
it is related to the Hall conductivity
\sigma_{\text{H}}
of the system; as shown explicitly in Ref.
16
, it holds

\delta\Gamma\simeq\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}\omega_{\text{q}}\coth\beta\omega/2}{16\pi^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}q\,qe^{-2qd}\int\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\text{Im}\left[\sigma_{\text{H}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega_{\text{q}})\right],

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{q}=q(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)^{T}=q\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\varphi}
. We here neglected the momentum dependence of the screening, as this does not crucially alter our results, and focused on the limit (
d\gg a
) where no sublattice resolution needs to be taken into account in the Fourier transform. For an effectively non-interacting model, like in Eq. (
14
), we can compute the Hall conductivity as
\sigma_{H}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{xy}-\sigma_{yx})
and

\sigma_{ij}(\mathbf{q},\omega_{q})=\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar(\omega+i\eta)}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int d^{2}k\,\sum_{n,m}\frac{f(E_{n}^{\mathbf{k}})-f(E_{m}^{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}})}{E_{n}^{\mathbf{k}}-E_{m}^{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}+\hbar\omega_{q}+i\hbar\eta}\;\bigl[v_{i}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})\bigr]_{nm}\,\bigl[v_{j}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})\bigr]_{nm}^{*},

(17)

where
[v_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})]_{nm}=\langle n\mathbf{k}|\,V_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})\,|m,\mathbf{k}{+}\mathbf{q}\rangle
with the velocity operator,

V_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=(\frac{\partial h_{\text{eff},\boldsymbol{k}}}{\partial k_{\alpha}}\big|_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{\partial h_{\text{eff},\boldsymbol{k}}}{\partial k_{\alpha}}\big|_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}})/2
, and
f(E)
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution. In our numerical evaluation of the expression in Eq. (
17
), we will use a small broadening
\eta
.

As already mentioned above, the factor
qe^{-2qd}
in Eq. (
16
) is peaked at
q=\frac{1}{2d}
, so that
\delta\Gamma
is primarily determined by
\braket{\text{Im}\left[\sigma_{\text{H}}(\frac{1}{2d},\omega_{\text{q}})\right]}:=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi}\text{Im}\left[\sigma_{\text{H}}(\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\varphi}}{2d},\omega_{\text{q}})\right]
.
As before in Sec.
II
, this alludes to small momenta with respect to the Brillouin zone for realistic distances
d
. However, we are now probing the behavior of electrons, and
\boldsymbol{q}
is the momentum transfer relative to their Fermi surface, which is generically at sizable momenta and, thus, sensitive to the altermagnetic symmetry reduction. Indeed, by computing
\sigma_{\text{H}}
from the model in Eq. (
14
), with the result shown in Fig.
5
(b), we can clearly see how local strain
\varepsilon
can induce such an asymmetry, already in the limit
d\gg a
. As is clear by symmetry (translations combined with
\Theta
), this effect is not present in AFMs, as we have also checked explicitly within our model. We note that the values for
k_{F}d
are chosen in our numerics so
d
corresponds to values in the 10-100 nm range, for
k_{F}\simeq\sqrt{\mu}=5\,\text{nm}^{-1}
.

Note that the symmetry considerations that allow us to connect time-reversal symmetry breaking to the imaginary part of cross terms in the noise tensor are general and do not depend on the type of system studied. In particular, they also apply to the magnons studied in Sec.
II
. However, we note that
\delta\Gamma
quickly vanishes as a function of distance
d/a
within our Holstein-Primakoff approach. Only for
d\lesssim a
, where the difference between the lattice and continuum Fourier-transform of the dipole tensor matters,
\delta\Gamma
can be probed. But this would require bringing the NV qubit very close to the sample surface. We detail these aspects in Appendix
C.2
.

III.3
Domain walls

Another class of perturbations that reduce the symmetries locally and will allow us to distinguish between AMs and AFMs are domain walls in the order parameter
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r})
. They are ubiquitous and important in magnets (including AMs
72
;
3
;
23
;
28
;
67
;
75
;
54
), and their impact on the electronic properties of AMs was recently studied in
54
in a related two-sublattice model.

To leading (zeroth) order in
1/J
and focusing on coplanar textures
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, the impact of spatially varying magnetic order parameters is described by the additional terms,

\Delta h_{\text{eff}}=\frac{1}{2m}\text{tr}[g(\boldsymbol{r})]\sigma_{0}-\delta t_{2}\text{tr}[\eta g(\boldsymbol{r})]\sigma_{z}

(18)

to the effective two-band Hamiltonian in Eq. (
14
).
Here
g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})=\partial_{i}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\partial_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}/4
encodes the spatial variation of the texture and can be thought of as the quantum metric of the associated spin-
1/2
, two-level system. Furthermore,
\eta=\text{diag}(1,-1)
is the metric tensor of the
d
-wave AM considered, since
k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2}=\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\eta\boldsymbol{k}
.

While the first of the two terms in Eq. (
18
) simply renormalizes the chemical potential and, hence, does not affect the symmetries of the system, the second one can be thought of as an emergent local Zeeman field. For a circular domain wall, it inherits the
d
-wave nature of the AM, as can be seen in Fig.
5
(c). Since this effect vanishes in the AFM limit,
\delta t_{z}\rightarrow 0
, it is a hallmark of the AM, as pointed out in Ref.
54
. Here, we show that this not only leads to signatures in the local magnetization but also in the noise.

First, notice that the additional term
\propto\sigma_{z}
breaks both
C_{4z}\Theta
and
\sigma_{d}
and should thus induce finite
\delta\Gamma
. This is indeed the case, as can be seen in our results presented in Fig.
5
(d), where we show the angular dependence at a fixed distance from the domain wall. We can further see that the sign of
\delta\Gamma
follows the
d
-wave nature of
\text{tr}[\eta g(\boldsymbol{r})]
coming from the
k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2}
dependence of the projected altermagnetic order parameter. Since a
g
-wave AM will also give rise to a
g
-wave pattern in
\text{tr}[\eta g(\boldsymbol{r})]
along a domain wall
54
and due to the locality of Eq. (
18
),
\delta\Gamma
will exhibit a
g
-wave behavior too. This shows that the noise spectrum along a domain wall not only differs qualitatively between AMs and AFMs, but also provides access to the dominant orbital nature of the AM.

Apart from
\Delta h_{\text{eff}}
in Eq. (
18
), there is a second modification in the Hamiltonian that has already been taken into account in Fig.
5
(d). To understand its origin and form, let us first recall how
\Delta h_{\text{eff}}
arises.
We obtain
\Delta h_{\text{eff}}
by rotating the effective Hamiltonian in Eq. (
14
) into the co-rotating reference frame
70
;
43
;
54
, i.e.,
U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r})\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}U(\boldsymbol{r})=\sigma_{z}
. Since we start with a Hamiltonian with spin-orbit coupling, the orientations in spin space and real space are locked. Consequently, we distinguish between Bloch-like and Néel-like domain walls. For the former, a natural choice would be
U=\exp(i\frac{\phi(r)}{2}(\sigma_{x}\cos\alpha+\sigma_{y}\sin\alpha))
, where
\phi(r)
interpolates between
0
and
\pi
and
\alpha
is the (direction) polar angle. In this case,
\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{r})
rotates perpendicular to the domain wall. For a Néel-like domain wall, the order rotates parallel to the domain wall, corresponding to
U=\exp(i\frac{\phi(r)}{2}(-\sigma_{x}\sin\alpha+\sigma_{y}\cos\alpha))
. We here neglect the non-coplanarity of the resulting texture (no derivative with respect to
\alpha
entering
g
), which makes sense in the limit of small curvature of the domain wall (in our case,
R\rightarrow\infty
). In this limit, one can think of
\alpha
also simply as the orientation of a straight domain wall with respect to the lattice/altermagnetic order parameter. An important consequence is that
U^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\nabla}U
is entirely off-diagonal in spin space and, thus, vanishes when projected to the low-energy subspace with fixed
\tau_{z}s_{z}
. As such, the emergent gauge fields
70
;
43
;
54
(and not only the associated orbital magnetic field) vanish for these specific choices of
U
.

However, in addition to the emergent Zeeman field in Eq. (
18
), the rotation
U(\boldsymbol{r})
also transforms the spin-orbit coupling in Eq. (
14
) according to
\sigma_{x,y}\rightarrow\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}(\boldsymbol{r})
where

\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}(\boldsymbol{r})=\sum_{j^{\prime}=x,y}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}\text{tr}[U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r})\sigma_{j}U(\boldsymbol{r})\sigma_{j^{\prime}}]/2,

(19)

see Appendix
D
. The consequences of the modifications to the spin-orbit coupling term can be seen especially right at the domain wall (
\delta r=0
). There, we find the largest effective potential
\Delta h_{\text{eff}}
. However, we also have
\phi(R)=\pi/2
and the
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
is in the plane of the system. This implies that the spin-orbit coupling term becomes
\alpha(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\perp}=\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
. Consequently, the Bloch vector is confined to a plane (spanned by
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
) across all momenta, which gives a vanishing Berry curvature. Therefore, right at the wall, when the effective potential is the largest, we have
\sigma_{H}=0
. This results in a sign change of the
\delta\Gamma
through the domain wall (at fixed angle
\alpha
), i.e., when increasing
r
from
r<R
to
r>R
, which is also visible in Fig.
5
(d). This shows that the noise through
\delta\Gamma
is expected to display a qualitatively different signature than
V_{z}
, which is a consequence of the fact that the former is determined by both the emergent Zeeman field
V_{z}
and the (renormalized) spin-orbit coupling.

III.4
Symmetry considerations

Hitherto, we mainly discussed the difference in the relaxation rate of anti-aligned qubits with
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{q}}=\pm\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
. Similarly to what we presented in Sec.
II.2
, and more specifically in Fig.
4
, one can investigate the more general dependence of the relaxation and decoherence rates on the qubit orientation
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}}
for itinerant electrons as well.
As we have seen with
\delta\Gamma
as a concrete example above, the finite size of Fermi surfaces makes the noise related to the itinerant electrons generically susceptible to any symmetry reduction in the system. We therefore discuss additional features of these quantities based on general symmetry arguments.

The two equations that define the symmetry constraints for the noise tensor are

\begin{split}&\mathcal{N}(\Omega)=R^{T}(g)\mathcal{N}(\Omega)R(g),\\
&\mathcal{N}(\Omega)=R^{T}(g)\mathcal{N}^{T}(\Omega)R(g).\end{split}

(20)

In the first equation,
R(g)
is the appropriate pseudo-vector representation of the point-group symmetry
g
. In the second equation, the consequence of a magnetic symmetry is considered, i.e.,
g\Theta
with time-reversal
\Theta
. A detailed discussion regarding these equations can be found in Appendix
E
.

Now, we can take into account the appropriate symmetries for an AFM and an AM. For concreteness, let us assume the magnetic order is along the
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
direction, that spin-orbit coupling is present, and that a magnetic field
B
is applied along the
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
direction.
Then a square-lattice AFM has the magnetic mirror symmetry
\sigma_{y}\Theta
and
C_{2z}
, while an AM only has
\sigma_{y}\Theta
. We emphasize that all symmetries here are understood as spinful transformations, where not only the lattice coordinates but also the spins are transformed accordingly. In the first case, this implies that

\mathcal{N}_{\text{AFM}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{N}_{xx}&\mathcal{N}^{B}_{xy}&0\\
-\mathcal{N}_{xy}^{B}&\mathcal{N}_{yy}&0\\
0&0&\mathcal{N}_{zz}\\
\end{array}\right)

(21)

where the off-diagonal term is purely imaginary and only present when
B\neq 0
, as indicated by the superscript. More precisely, we use the notation that all components with (without) superscript
B
are odd (even) in
B
. This behavior follows from the presence of the symmetry given by translation combined with
\Theta
and
B\rightarrow-B
in the AFM. Importantly, this symmetry (just like
C_{2z}
) is broken in the AM. More terms are possible and one finds

\mathcal{N}_{\text{AM}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{N}_{xx}&\mathcal{N}_{xy}^{B}&\mathcal{N}_{xz}\\
-\mathcal{N}_{xy}^{B}&\mathcal{N}_{yy}&\mathcal{N}^{B}_{yz}\\
-\mathcal{N}_{xz}&\mathcal{N}^{B}_{yz}&\mathcal{N}_{zz}\\
\end{array}\right).

(22)

Similarly to the AFM,
\mathcal{N}^{B}_{xy}
and
\mathcal{N}_{xz}
are purely imaginary. The remaining off-diagonal term,
\mathcal{N}^{B}_{yz}
, is real.
Note that, in principle, these statements are also true for the calculations in Sec.
II
; however, the dipolar magnetic field and the magnon spectra within our leading-order Holstein-Primakoff approach impose further constraints and affect the angular dependence of the noise.

Using the form of the noise tensor in Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
), one can readily derive the general form of
1/T_{1,2}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{q}})
, see Appendix
E
. First, we find that
\delta\Gamma=0
if
B=0
for both AFM and AM; just like in our analysis above, additional perturbations are needed to contrast the two types of orders (we also see that applying
B
would not help since then
\delta\Gamma\neq 0
for both).
However, we notice the presence of the anti-symmetric components
\mathcal{N}_{xz}
in the AM, which are absent for the AFM. This term can be probed in the noise, e.g., from the rotated asymmetry term
\widetilde{\delta\Gamma}=T^{-1}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{y}})-T^{-1}_{1}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{y}})
: one finds
\widetilde{\delta\Gamma}\propto\mathcal{N}_{xz}\neq 0
for the AM (even at
B=0
) while
\widetilde{\delta\Gamma}=0
for the AFM. But we emphasize that
\mathcal{N}_{xz}
also enters for other generic directions of
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{q}}
. Most importantly, this shows that the noise also allows access to unique symmetry signatures of AM even without additional perturbations (like the strain or domain walls discussed above).

Finally, we point out that there are also unique signatures of AMs in
T_{2}
. As follows from Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
),
1/T_{2}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{q}})
has a
B
-linear contribution (for generic
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{q}}
) only in the AM, which is related to the
\mathcal{N}^{B}_{yz}
term and is thus absent for the AFM. This is also the only term (and thus only non-vanishing in the AM) leading to differences in
1/T_{2}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{q}})
for
\phi
and
\phi+\pi
[cf. parameterization in Sec.
II.2
].

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we explored the potential of noise magnetometry, e.g., as implemented via NV centers, to probe and characterize altermagnets. We have calculated and compared the magnetic noise spectrum of an antiferromagnet and altermagnet, realized in the two-sublattice model illustrated in Fig.
1
(a). We considered both the fluctuations in the magnetic order parameter (magnons) as well as the charge fluctuations of itinerant electrons in the bands that are reconstructed by the magnet.

We first focused on the fluctuations related to magnons, which are the central contribution for insulating systems. We employed a Heisenberg model and studied the regime with gapless magnons, i.e., when the noise is dominated by single-magnon processes. We identified quantitative differences between altermagnets and antiferromagnets in the noise spectrum. While in principle key features of the magnon bandstructure—the splitting of the bands in the altermagnetic case—can also be seen in the noise spectrum, it requires access to large energy ranges and the qubit being very close to the sample. We note that this regime might be accessible in moiré systems
38
;
51
, where the effective energy scales and lattice constants are reduced. We discussed the relevance of taking into account lattice effects in the Fourier transform of the dipolar interaction kernel in this regime.

In the second part, we studied the additional contribution of charge fluctuations relevant to itinerant altermagnets, which we captured starting from a tight-binding model. This is a particularly important contribution since the non-trivial impact of the magnetic order parameter on the itinerant charge carriers is the central feature of altermagnets and the Fermi surface is typically at sizable momenta, making the noise spectra generically susceptible to altermagnetic symmetries. To illustrate this, we computed
[1/T_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})-1/T_{1}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})]\propto\sigma_{H}(\omega_{q})
in the presence of distortions that lift the rotational symmetry
C_{4z}
and the mirror symmetry
\sigma_{d}
. We studied uniaxial strain and domain walls as such symmetry-breaking distortions; this allows a finite
\sigma_{H}
only for the altermagnet, while antiferromagnets show no finite response, therefore, giving us a qualitative difference between the two phases. What is more, the angular dependence of the noise around domain walls further contains information about the dominant orbital character of the altermagnet. We showed by a symmetry analysis that there are also contributions in the noise spectrum that distinguish altermagnets from antiferromagnets without additional perturbations, such as
T^{-1}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{y}})-T^{-1}_{1}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{y}})
(assuming in-plane magnetic order).

Overall, our work shows that noise magnetometry can provide crucial insights into altermagnetism, in particular in the itinerant case. It further leads to many natural follow-up questions, such as the impact not only of static strain but also of fluctuations in it
62
;
9
. It would also be interesting to generalize our symmetry analysis and model calculations to altermagnetic and unconventional magnets on other Bravais lattices, and take into account the emergent spin-orbit coupling in inhomogeneous altermagnets
54
;
43
.

Appendix A
Holstein-Primakoff transformation in the local basis

As mentioned in the main text, we parametrize
\boldsymbol{S}_{i}=\boldsymbol{m}_{i}S^{\parallel}+\boldsymbol{S}^{\perp}_{i}
, with
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}^{\perp}_{i}=0
, and rewrite the Heisenberg Hamiltonian as

H=J_{1}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left[(\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{j})S_{i}^{\parallel}S_{j}^{\parallel}+\boldsymbol{S}_{i}^{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{j}^{\perp}\right]+J_{2}\sum_{\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle}\left[(\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{j})S_{i}^{\parallel}S_{j}^{\parallel}+\boldsymbol{S}_{i}^{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{j}^{\perp}\right]-\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\,\,S^{\parallel}+\mathcal{O}(1/S^{0})\,\,\,.

(23)

Within our
1/S
expansion, we can neglect terms with
\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{i}^{\perp}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{i}^{\perp}\cdot S_{j}^{\parallel}
. Now, we use the Holstein-Primoakoff transformation in the local basis
(\boldsymbol{e}_{1,i},\boldsymbol{e}_{2,i},\boldsymbol{m}_{i})
(also defined in the main text and see
55
) and obtain the “classical” contribution to the Hamiltonian

H_{\text{cl}}=J_{1}S^{2}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{j}+J_{2}S^{2}\sum_{\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle}\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{j}-S\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{i}=\mathbbm{1}E_{\text{cl}}[\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\}]

(24)

and the
\mathcal{O}(S)
correction, which is quadratic in the bosonic operators and reads as

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}/S&=J_{1}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left[-\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{j}(n_{i}+n_{j})+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{+}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{+})b_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{+}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{-})b_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{j}^{\dagger}+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{-}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{+})b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{-}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{-})b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\dagger}\right]\\
&+J_{2}\sum_{\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle}\left[-\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{j}(n_{i}+n_{j})+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{+}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{+})b_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{+}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{-})b_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{j}^{\dagger}+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{-}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{+})b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+2(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{-}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{-})b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\dagger}\right]\\
&+\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\,\,n_{i},\end{split}

(25)

where we introduced
n_{i}=b_{i}^{\dagger}b^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{i}
.

For our choice of parameters (
\boldsymbol{h}=h\hat{z}
;
J_{1}>0
dominating the energetic preference of
J_{2}
),
E_{\text{cl}}[\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\}]
is minimized by a magnetization of the form
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}=\varrho_{i}\cos{\theta}\,\,\hat{x}+\sin{\theta}\hat{z}
with
\varrho=\pm 1
depending on the sublattice. The classical part becomes

H_{\text{cl}}=NS\,\,(-h\sin{\theta}-J_{1}S\cos{2\theta}+J_{2}S).

(26)

The minimum is reached for

\theta_{m}\;=\;\begin{cases}\arcsin\!\left(\dfrac{h}{8J_{1}S}\right),&|h|\leq 8J_{1}S,\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{\pi}{2},&|h|>8J_{1}S.\end{cases}

(27)

We can now write down the explicit local basis

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{e}_{1,i}&=\varrho_{i}\sin{\theta_{m}}\,\,\hat{x}-\cos{\theta_{m}}\,\,\hat{z};\\
\boldsymbol{e}_{2,i}&=\varrho_{i}\,\,\hat{y};\\
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}&=\varrho_{i}\cos{\theta_{m}}\,\,\hat{x}+\sin{\theta_{m}}\hat{z}.\end{split}

(28)

The dot products in
\mathcal{H}
are

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{e}^{\pm}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}^{\pm}_{j}&=\begin{cases}0,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\text{if $i,j$ are in the same sublattice,}\\
\frac{\cos^{2}{\theta_{m}}}{2}\quad\quad\text{if $i,j$ are in different sublattice,}\end{cases}\\
\\
\\
\boldsymbol{e}^{\pm}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}^{\mp}_{j}&=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2},\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\text{if $i,j$ are in the same sublattice,}\\
-\sin^{2}{\theta_{m}}\quad\quad\text{if $i,j$ are in different sublattice,}\end{cases}\\
\\
\\
\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}_{j}&=\begin{cases}1,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\text{if $i,j$ are in the same sublattice,}\\
-\cos{2\theta_{m}}\quad\quad\text{if $i,j$ are in different sublattice.}\end{cases}\\
\end{split}

(29)

Hence the Hamiltonian is

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}/S&=J_{1}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left[\cos{2\theta_{m}}\,\,(n_{i}+n_{j})-\sin^{2}{\theta_{m}}\,\,(b_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{j}^{\dagger}+b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}})+\cos^{2}{\theta_{m}}\,\,(b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\dagger}+b_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}})\right]\\
&+J_{2}\sum_{\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle}\left[-(n_{i}+n_{j})+\,\ b_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}b_{j}^{\dagger}+\,\ b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\right]\\
&+\sum_{i}\frac{h}{S}\sin{\theta_{m}}\,\,n_{i}.\end{split}

(30)

Now, if we use the Nambu basis defined in the main text, we obtain directly the matrix form in Eq. (
6
).

Appendix B
Colpa method

Within the linear spin wave approximation, we want to numerically obtain the Bogoliubov coefficients and spectrum. However they are too complicated to be written in a reasonable analytical form. Hence, we need to numerically diagonalize
h_{\boldsymbol{k}}
from (
6
), a task that is not as straightforward as it is with fermions due to the bosonic commutation relations.

A common way to proceed is through the Colpa method
13
. The first step is to define the metric of our system
\Sigma=\text{diag}(1,1,-1,-1)
. Now we write down our Hamiltonian as
h=K^{\dagger}K
(suppressing the momentum dependence in the following). This form is always possible, given that
h
is positive definite. For our case, at
k=0
, we always have a Goldstone mode with zero energy, hence we can add a small diagonal shift, e.g.,
\epsilon\sim 10^{-5}
, that makes the problem numerically stable.

The next step is to obtain
K
, which is done through a Cholesky decomposition. Then we solve the eigenproblem
K\Sigma K^{\dagger}
and obtain the unitary transformation
U
to the diagonal basis, i.e., the transformation is such that
L=U^{\dagger}K\Sigma K^{\dagger}U
is diagonal. The eigenvalues of this problem are not the physical energies, instead
E=\Sigma L
gives the correct energies (it transforms the negative energy bands into positive). Furthermore, the eigenvectors of the physical problem are the columns of

T=K^{-1}UE^{1/2}.

(31)

It is straightforward to check that
T\Sigma T^{\dagger}=\Sigma
, such that the transformation safeguards the bosonic commutation relations, while simultaneously diagonalizing the Hamiltonian,
T^{\dagger}hT=E
.

The transformation matrix
T
follows the symmetries of a Bogoliubov transformation by definition and can be written as

T=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{U}&\mathcal{V}\\
\mathcal{V}&\mathcal{U}\end{pmatrix},

(32)

with the gauge choice where all the entries are real, which is possible because
h_{k}
is real.

Appendix C
Noise tensor expressions

We start with our expressions for the magnetic noise tensor in real space and the magnetic field expression for a set of dipoles

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int dte^{i\omega t}\langle\{B_{\alpha}(t),B_{\beta}(0)\}\rangle,

(33)

and

\boldsymbol{B}=\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\sum_{j}\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{S}_{j}}{r_{j}^{3}}-\frac{3\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\right)\boldsymbol{r}_{j}}{r_{j}^{5}}\right].

(34)

As stated in the main text
\boldsymbol{r}_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j},d)
corresponds to the distance between the spin in the
(x_{j},y_{j},0)
position and the qubit at
(0,0,d)
. We first use the dissipation-fluctuation result
\langle\{,\}\rangle_{T}\rightarrow-\coth{\frac{\omega}{2T}}\,\,\theta(t)\,\,\text{Im}\langle-i[,]\rangle
. We also note that we can rewrite the magnetic field in Eq. (
34
) in terms of the dipole tensor

\begin{split}B_{\alpha}&=\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\sum_{\eta}\sum_{j}\sum_{\gamma}D^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\eta})\,S^{\gamma}_{\eta,j},\\
D^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})&=\frac{\delta^{\alpha\gamma}}{r_{j}^{3}}-\frac{3r_{j}^{\alpha}r_{j}^{\gamma}}{r_{j}^{5}}\\
&=-\partial^{\alpha}\partial^{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+z^{2}}}\right)\Bigg|_{z=d},\end{split}

(35)

where
\rho
is the norm of the in-plane position vector
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(x,y)^{T}
. The index
\eta
labels the two different sub-lattices, it also indicates the precise spin position
\boldsymbol{r}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\eta}
, with
\boldsymbol{\eta}=\hat{x}/2
or
\hat{y}/2
.

Now we can write the expectation value with a commutator

\begin{split}\langle[B_{\alpha}(t),B_{\beta}(0)]\rangle=\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\sum_{\eta,\xi}\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\gamma\delta}D^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\eta})D^{\beta\delta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\xi})\langle[S^{\gamma}_{\eta,i}(t),S^{\delta}_{\xi,j}(0)]\rangle,\end{split}

(36)

and rewrite the spin operators in momentum space
S_{\eta,i}^{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\eta})}S_{\eta,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\gamma}
. We note that the spins “live” in the plane so transform
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i}
instead of
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
. Now the expectation value is

\begin{split}\langle[B_{\alpha}(t),B_{\beta}(0)]\rangle=\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\eta,\xi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\gamma\delta}\left[\sum_{i}D^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\eta})e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\eta})}\right]\left[\sum_{j}D^{\beta\delta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\xi})e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\xi})}\right]\langle[S^{\gamma}_{\eta,\boldsymbol{k}}(t),S^{\delta}_{\xi,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(0)]\rangle,\end{split}

(37)

For
d\gg a
a small-
\boldsymbol{k}
expansion suffices (we discuss the case
d\lesssim a
later on). Then the expressions in-between square brackets become just the 2D Fourier transform
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{k})=\mathcal{F}\{D^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})\}(\boldsymbol{k})
of the dipole tensor and we can compactly write

\begin{split}\langle[B_{\alpha}(t),B_{\beta}(0)]\rangle=\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\sum_{\eta,\xi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\gamma\delta}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{k})\mathcal{D}^{\beta\delta}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\langle[S^{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}(t),S^{\delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\xi}(0)]\rangle.\end{split}

(38)

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{k})\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{2\pi}{k}\,e^{-kd}\,k_{\alpha}k_{\gamma},&\alpha,\gamma\in\{x,y\},\\[8.0pt]
\,i\,2\pi\,e^{-kd}\,k_{\alpha},&\alpha\in\{x,y\},\ \gamma=z\ \text{or}\ \alpha=z,\ \gamma\in\{x,y\},\\[8.0pt]
-2\pi\,k\,e^{-kd},&\alpha=\gamma=z.\end{cases}

(39)

We note that within this expansion, the dependence on the sub-lattices is compensated by a variable shift in the continuum Fourier transformation and therefore it does not appear explicitly in the dipole expression.

Now we take into account the translational invariance of our system (momentum conservation),
\langle[S^{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(t),S^{\delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(0)]\rangle\propto\delta_{\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
, and go to thethermodynamic limit which allows us to replace
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rightarrow\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}
,

\begin{split}\langle[B_{\alpha}(t),B_{\beta}(0)]\rangle=\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\sum_{\eta,\xi}\sum_{\gamma\delta}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\,\,D^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{k})D^{\beta\delta}(-\boldsymbol{k})\langle[S^{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}(t),S^{\delta}_{-\boldsymbol{k},\xi}(0)]\rangle.\end{split}

(40)

Hence, the noise tensor is

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\coth{\frac{\omega}{2T}}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\sum_{\gamma\delta}\int\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}D^{\alpha\gamma}(\boldsymbol{k})D^{\beta\delta}(-\boldsymbol{k})\,\,\mathcal{C}_{\gamma\delta}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega),

(41)

with

\mathcal{C}_{\gamma\delta}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)=-\sum_{\eta,\xi}\text{Im}(-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}\langle[S^{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}(t),S^{\delta}_{-\boldsymbol{k},\xi}(0)]\rangle).

(42)

Decoherence rate

d\gtrsim a

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{zz}=\frac{1}{2}\coth{\frac{\omega}{2T}}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\,\,\sum_{\eta,\xi}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\left[\mathbb{D}^{z\gamma}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\eta})\mathbb{D}^{z\delta}(-\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbb{C}^{\eta\xi}_{\gamma\delta}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)\right],\end{split}

(53)

since the same symmetry constraints hold, we can write

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{zz}=\frac{1}{2}\coth{\frac{\omega}{2T}}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\,\,\sum_{\eta,\xi}&\left[\mathbb{D}^{zx}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\eta})\mathbb{D}^{zx}(-\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbb{C}^{\eta\xi}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)+\mathbb{D}^{zy}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\eta})\mathbb{D}^{zy}(-\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbb{C}^{\eta\xi}_{yy}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)\right.\\
&\left.+\mathbb{D}^{zz}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\eta})\mathbb{D}^{zz}(-\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbb{C}^{\eta\xi}_{zz}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)\right],\end{split}

(54)

and

\begin{split}\mathbb{C}^{11}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)&=(2S)\,\sin^{2}\theta_{m}\frac{1}{4}\left[(u_{11}+v_{11})^{2}\,\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{+,\boldsymbol{k}})+(u_{12}+v_{12})^{2}\,\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{-,\boldsymbol{k}})\right]\\

&\left.+(u_{12}+v_{12})(u_{22}+v_{22})\,\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{-,\boldsymbol{k}})\right]\\
\mathbb{C}^{22}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)&=(2S)\,\sin^{2}\theta_{m}\frac{1}{4}\left[(u_{21}+v_{21})^{2}\,\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{+,\boldsymbol{k}})+(u_{22}+v_{22})^{2}\,\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{-,\boldsymbol{k}})\right].\\
\end{split}

(55)

For the remaining correlators, one finds similar expressions.

C.2
Relaxation rate

Similarly to the decoherence rate, we can relate the relaxation rate to a noise component. In this case, the transversal noise is the relevant one, e.g., for the qubit quantization axis along
\hat{z}
we have
1/T_{1}\propto\mathcal{N}_{-+}

(\pm)=\hat{x}\pm i\hat{y}

&+2i\left[\,\mathcal{N}_{xy}(\cos\theta-1)+\,\mathcal{N}_{yz}\sin\theta\sin\phi\right]-\mathcal{N}_{xz}\sin 2\theta\,\sin\phi\end{split}

(56)

Here, we already omitted the vanishing terms proportional to
(\mathcal{N}_{xy}+\mathcal{N}_{yx}),(\mathcal{N}_{xz}-\mathcal{N}_{zx})
and
(\mathcal{N}_{yz}+\mathcal{N}_{zy})
, which are zero due to
\mathcal{N}_{xy}
,
\mathcal{N}_{yz}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{xz}
being purely imaginary and real, respectively.

Appendix D
Transformation of spin-orbit coupling

Almost all of the terms we use for the effective semi-classical Hamiltonian around the domain wall have been derived in Ref.
54
. The only exception is the effect of the slowly varying texture on the spin-orbit coupling term
\propto\alpha_{R}
in Eq. (
14
). To discuss this, we first note that the unitary transformations used in this work obey

\boldsymbol{A}:=iU^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\nabla}U\propto s_{x},s_{y},\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad P_{-}\boldsymbol{A}P_{-}=0,

(59)

where
P_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}\mp s_{z}\tau_{z})
projects onto the low- or high-energy subspace. This is the reason why there are no emergent gauge fields for the first two terms in Eq. (
14
). Intuitively, this is connected with the fact that the texture is effectively co-planar in the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
(negligible curvature of the domain wall) that we focus on here. As such, there is no emergent magnetic field and a gauge exists where the associated emergent gauge field vanishes.

With this in mind, we now consider the projection of the rotated spin-orbit term, which is of the form (here
\hat{k}_{j}=-i\partial_{j}
is the momentum operator)

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=x,y}\alpha_{j,k}P_{-}U^{\dagger}\tau_{x}\hat{k}_{j}s_{k}UP_{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=x,y}\alpha_{j,k}P_{-}U^{\dagger}\tau_{x}\{\hat{k}_{j},s_{k}\}UP_{-}

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=x,y}\alpha_{j,k}P_{-}\tau_{x}\{U^{\dagger}\hat{k}_{j}U,\tilde{s}_{k}\}P_{-},\quad\tilde{s}_{k}=U^{\dagger}s_{k}U

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=x,y}\alpha_{j,k}P_{-}\tau_{x}\{\hat{k}_{j}-A_{j},\tilde{s}_{k}\}P_{-},\,\,A_{j}=-U^{\dagger}(\hat{k}_{j}U)

(62)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=x,y}\alpha_{j,k}P_{-}\tau_{x}\left(\{\hat{k}_{j},\tilde{s}_{k}^{\perp}\}-\{A_{j},\tilde{s}_{k}^{\parallel}\}\right)P_{-},

(63)

where we suppressed the explicit dependence on
\boldsymbol{r}
in
U
and
\tilde{s}_{k}
for brevity.
In the last line, we wrote
\tilde{s}_{k}=\tilde{s}_{k}^{\parallel}+\tilde{s}_{k}^{\perp}
with
\tilde{s}_{k}^{\parallel}\propto s_{z}
and
\tilde{s}_{k}^{\perp}\propto s_{x},s_{y}
, or explicitly

\tilde{s}_{k}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=x,y}s_{j}\text{tr}[\tilde{s}_{k}s_{j}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=x,y}s_{j}\text{tr}[U^{\dagger}s_{k}Us_{j}]

(64)

and
\tilde{s}_{k}^{\parallel}=\tilde{s}_{k}-\tilde{s}_{k}^{\perp}
. We further used Eq. (
59
). The latter in turn also implies
\{A_{j},\tilde{s}_{k}^{\parallel}\}=0
. In total and in the semi-classical limit (see, e.g.,
54
), we therefore get a spin-orbit term of the form

\alpha_{R}\left(k_{y}\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}(\boldsymbol{r})-k_{x}\widetilde{\sigma}_{y}(\boldsymbol{r})\right),\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=x,y}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}\text{tr}[U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r})\sigma_{j}U(\boldsymbol{r})\sigma_{j^{\prime}}],

(65)

after transformation and projection.

Appendix E
Symmetry constraints on noise

E.1
General remarks

As already discussed in detail in the main text, the central object that NV noise magnetometry is sensitive to is the correlator

\mathcal{N}_{ab}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{i\Omega t}\braket{\{B_{a}(t),B_{b}(0)\}}

(66)

of the time-dependent magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}(t)
at the location of the NV center. We begin by discussing some symmetry constraints on it. First, let us assume that the system is
stationary
. Then

\mathcal{N}_{ab}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{i\Omega t}\braket{\{B_{a}(0),B_{b}(-t)\}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{-i\Omega t}\braket{\{B_{b}(t),B_{a}(0)\}}=\mathcal{N}_{ba}(-\Omega).

(67)

Complex conjugation of the definition (
66
) leads to
\mathcal{N}^{*}_{ab}(\Omega)=\mathcal{N}_{ab}(-\Omega)
and, thus, the
reality condition

\mathcal{N}^{\dagger}(\Omega)=\mathcal{N}(\Omega).

(68)

Finally, under
time-reversal symmetry
, it holds

\mathcal{N}_{ab}(\Omega)\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{i\Omega t}\braket{\{-B_{a}(-t),-B_{b}(0)\}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{-i\Omega t}\braket{\{B_{a}(t),B_{b}(0)\}}=\mathcal{N}_{ab}^{*}(\Omega)=\mathcal{N}_{ba}(\Omega),

(69)

where we used Eq. (
68
) in the last step; this makes the action of time-reversal symmetry very natural as it just corresponds to transposition akin to Onsager relations.

To understand the relation to the symmetries in the material that is probed, we have to take into account the relation between
\boldsymbol{B}(t)
and the material properties, which are coupled through Eq. (
2
) or Eq. (
3
), depending on the type of fluctuations. Let us assume that the material is invariant under the symmetry transformation
g
which is not broken (at least approximately) by the position of the NV center (e.g., elementary lattice translations are approximately preserved for
d\gg a
). We then have

\mathcal{N}(\Omega)=R^{T}(g)\mathcal{N}(\Omega)R(g),

(70)

where
R(g)
is the appropriate pseudo-vector representation of
g
. Similarly, if the system exhibits a magnetic symmetry, i.e.,
g\Theta
involving time-reversal
\Theta
, we have

\mathcal{N}(\Omega)=R^{T}(g)\mathcal{N}^{T}(\Omega)R(g).

(71)

Denoting the qubit axis by
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
, the relaxation rates can be obtained as follows:

\frac{1}{T_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})}\propto\int\mathrm{d}\Omega\,f(\Omega)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{T}\mathcal{N}(\Omega)\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},

(72)

where
f
is some protocol-dependent filter function, that does not affect our symmetry analysis here. Furthermore, it holds

\frac{1}{T_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\text{q}})}\propto\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{xx}(\omega_{\text{q}})+\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{yy}(\omega_{\text{q}})+2\,\text{Im}[\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}})],

(73)

where
\omega_{\text{q}}
is the qubit frequency and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}=R_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}\mathcal{N}R^{T}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}
with
R_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}
rotating
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
onto
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
.

With this at hand, it is straightforward to derive the symmetry constraints on the components of
\mathcal{N}
and the resulting features of the relaxation rates.
For instance, we get
\delta\Gamma\propto\text{Im}[\mathcal{N}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}})-\mathcal{N}_{yx}(\omega_{\text{q}})]=2\text{Im}[\mathcal{N}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}})]
as stated in the main text. We now have, for instance,

R(\sigma_{d})=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{such that}\quad\sigma_{d}:\,\,\mathcal{N}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}})\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{yx}(\omega_{\text{q}})=\mathcal{N}^{*}_{xy}(\omega_{\text{q}}).

(74)

Consequently, the presence of
\sigma_{d}
implies that
\delta\Gamma=0
. The same holds for
C_{4z}\Theta
.
