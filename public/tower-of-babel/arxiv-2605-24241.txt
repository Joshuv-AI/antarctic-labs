The petrol station game: the regional average price. A mean field analysis
Fabio Bagagiolo, Federico Pontiroli, Ivan Romanò

Abstract
In January 2023, the obligation for petrol stations to display the average fuel price calculated on a regional basis was introduced by the Italian Government. A mean field game model is here proposed to describe the evolution of the fuel price. In particular, each petrol station decides its own price, for the next day, taking account of an estimate of the average price, to be communicated by the Local Authority. The existence and the evolution of a mean field equilibrium are analytically investigated. Some simulations are compared with real data from the Autonomous Province of Trento, Italy.



1
Introduction

In the spring of 2023, at the petrol stations in Italy, displaying the average regional fuel prices to the costumers become mandatory. Actually, such obligation was suspended during 2024. However, data collection and processing continued, available on
osservaprezzi
. This was due to an Italian law designed, among other goals, to increase
transparency and fuel price control. The underlying concept
is: the “rational” consumer will avoid filling up at outlets
whose prices are significantly above the average, knowing that for every gas station that has
above-average prices, there is at least another one that has cheaper prices.
In practice, the issue is more complex, because other factors may influence the choice of the customers, such as the presence of a bar, a car wash, a repair shop, etc..
The average price to be displayed is updated daily based on the individual
price that each gas station has communicated to the Ministry of Enterprise and Made in Italy (MIMiI)
the day before. It is then a quantity subject to a certain degree of uncertainty, until it is published, and every gas station attendant
must somehow estimate it in order to understand what price is best for her/him.

In this paper we interpret the problem as a a mean-field game, in which the mean field is the average price. In particular, in the formulation of the problem, we get inspiration from a simple model that regulates the start time
of a company meeting, “When does the meeting start”, which is contained in
liolasgue
. It is assumed that the
gas station attendants, and consequently the consumers, are rational decision makers: in practice, this means
that the players’ decisions are assumed to minimize some cost function.
In our main model we assume that the cost of the raw material is constant and is the same for
everyone. This may be justified since the evolution of the average price is considered in a rather short windows of days (see also Remark
1
).

The model then aims to describe the dynamics of decisions, that is
the evolution of prices, and therefore to rationally interpret the process that
leads each gas station attendant to choose a certain price, in order to be capable to possibly predict future behaviors, suitably fitting the parameters.

This Introduction continues with some further details on the legislative context, on the available data and with a first notational description of the price evolution. Then, in Section
2
, an analytical model is constructed and studied for what concerns equilibria and their convergence. In Section
3
some simulations are presented, comparing with data from the Autonomous Province of Trento and after a possible clustering of the petrol stations with respect to some of their specificities.
As general references for the numerical and statistical tools, we refer to
nowrig
,
hastibfri
.

Gas station attendants
must display a sign showing the average prices
for the types of fuel available at their point of sale,
ensuring that it is updated daily.

Let us suppose that we have fixed a data collection day. We are going to use the following notation:
\overline{p}
as the average price displayed on that day (and communicated by the Ministry early in the morning);
p_{i}^{+}
as the price decided by the
i
-th player (the
i
-th petrol station) for the next day for her/his own station;
\overline{p}^{+}
as the average price of the next day calculated as arithmetic mean of all prices
p_{i}^{+}
. The transition scheme is then

\overline{p}\ \longrightarrow\ \left(p_{i}^{+}\right)_{i}\ \longrightarrow\ \overline{p}^{+}

(1)

From the game theory point of view (see for example
pet
), we have a non-cooperative game
with imperfect information (no one knows the prices that
others will set, and consequently the average price, until the following day) and complete information
(ignorance of the others’ moves is the same for each player; the average price of the
current day on which to base estimates is known to all).

The data has been collected and made available as a .csv file since 2015 on the website
dati
.
Actually, starting in 2023, MIMiI has performed a data processing function and
made a summary available. Just considering only data referring to Petrol (Benzina) self-service (outside the highways), we can observe in Figure 1 the evolution of the average price during the year 2023 for the Autonomous Province of Trento.

2
The analytical model

In this section we introduce the costs faced by the agent (player)
i
-th when the price of the next day
p_{i}^{+}
must be decided on the estimated average price
\overline{p}^{+}
and describe how the transition (
1
) may be generated.

What is uncertain in the decision process of the
i
-th player is the knowledge of the average price of the next day,
\overline{p}^{+}
. We then assume that the single agent
i
-th, in its decision process, uses a personal estimate of
\overline{p}^{+}
as

\tilde{p}_{i}:=\overline{p}+\sigma^{i}\varepsilon_{i}

(2)

where
\sigma_{i}>0
is a fixed coefficient and
\varepsilon^{i}\sim N(0,1)
is a normally distributed random variable with
\varepsilon^{i}
and
\varepsilon^{j}
independent when
i\neq j
. We now introduce the costs faced by the agent
i
-th in its decision process for
p_{i}^{+}
. In the following,
[r]_{+}=\max\{r,0\}
is the positive part, and the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta>0
are fixed. We also introduce the notation
p_{i}^{-}
for the price that agent
i
-th is making the actual day.

i) Competition cost.
This cost takes account of the fact that choosing a value for
p_{i}^{+}
lower than the (estimated) average means that the agent earns less (in average) than its competitors:

c_{1}(\overline{p},p_{i}^{+})=\alpha[\tilde{p}_{i}-p_{i}^{+}]_{+}.

(3)

ii) Fidelity cost.
This cost takes account of the fact that choosing a value for
p_{i}^{+}
greater than the (estimated) average means that the number of customers probably decreases:

c_{2}(\overline{p},p_{i}^{+})=\beta[p_{i}^{+}-\tilde{p}_{i}]_{+}.

(4)

iii) Reputation cost.
This cost takes account of the fact that changing the price often and significantly is seen by the customers as a bad behavior:

c_{3}(p_{i}^{-},p_{i}^{+})=\frac{\gamma}{2}(p_{i}^{+}-p_{i}^{-})^{2}.

(5)

iv) Absolute price cost.
It penalizes excessively high values of the price in absolute terms, independently from the estimated average price:

c_{4}(p_{i}^{+})=\frac{\delta}{2}(p_{i}^{+})^{2}.

(6)

The global cost to be minimized is then given by

c(p_{i}^{-},\overline{p},p_{i}^{+})=c_{1}(\overline{p},p_{i}^{+})+c_{2}(\overline{p},p_{i}^{+})+c_{3}(p_{i}^{-},p_{i}^{+})+c_{4}(p_{i}^{+})

(7)

and the minimization process of the
i
-th agent is given by

p_{i}^{+}={\rm argmin}_{p\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[c(p_{i}^{-},\overline{p},p)].

(8)

where
\mathbb{E}
is the expectation value of the random variable
c
(where the randomness is in the choice of the estimated average
\tilde{p}_{i}
as in (
2
)).

Remark 1

The cost terms (
5
), (
6
), in particular the latter, will play an essential role because they ensure the existence of an equilibrium, see Subsection
2.2
. Indeed, as shown in Remark
2
, when
\gamma=\delta=0
an equilibrium exists only if
\alpha=\beta
. Note that the parameter
\delta
may somehow encode the information about the general price of petroleum: the bigger the second the smaller the first (less cost (
7
) in applying higher prices at the station). Concerning the minimization process (
8
) we see that it is posed on the whole real line
p\in\mathbb{R}
. We refer to Remark
3
for the positivity of the prices.

2.1
Well-posedness

Considering the agent
i
,
p_{i}^{-}
and
\overline{p}
as fixed, we analyze the expectation value of the cost function (
7
) as function of
p\in\mathbb{R}
, denoting it by
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}(p)
(dropping the index
i
). In the following
N(\cdot)
is the distribution function of
\varepsilon^{i}
and
N^{\prime}(\cdot)
its derivative, that is its density.

Proposition 1

The function
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}(p)=\mathbb{E}[c(p_{i}^{-},\overline{p},p)]
is derivable and

\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}^{\prime}(p)=-\alpha\left(1-N\left(\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\right)+\beta N\left(\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)+\gamma(p-p_{i}^{-})+\delta p

(9)

Moreover, it is strictly convex with second derivative

\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}^{\prime\prime}(p)=\frac{\alpha}{\sigma_{i}}N^{\prime}\left(\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)+\frac{\beta}{\sigma_{i}}N^{\prime}\left(\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)+\gamma+\delta

(10)

Proof

We have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}(p)=\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha[\tilde{p}_{i}-p]_{+}+\beta[p-\tilde{p}_{i}]_{+}+\frac{\gamma}{2}(p-p_{i}^{-})^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}p^{2}\right]=\\
\displaystyle\alpha\int_{\overline{p}+\sigma_{i}x-p>0}(\overline{p}+\sigma_{i}x-p)N^{\prime}(x)dx+\beta\int_{p-\overline{p}-\sigma_{i}x>0}(p-\overline{p}-\sigma_{i}x)N^{\prime}(x)dx+\\
\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}(p-p_{i}^{-})^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}p^{2}=\alpha\int_{\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}}^{+\infty}(\overline{p}+\sigma_{i}x-p)N^{\prime}(x)dx+\\
\displaystyle\beta\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}}(p-\overline{p}-\sigma_{i}x)N^{\prime}(x)dx+\frac{\gamma}{2}(p-p_{i}^{-})^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}p^{2}.\end{array}

The integrals in the right-hand side are
p
-dependent. Note that when
x=(p-\overline{p})/\sigma_{i}
the integrand function vanishes in both integrals. Deriving with respect to
p
we then get (the term with the derivative of the boundary of the interval of integration vanishes, for what said above)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}^{\prime}(p)=-\alpha\int_{\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}}^{+\infty}N^{\prime}(x)dx+\beta\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}}N^{\prime}(x)dx+\gamma(p-p_{i}^{-})+\delta p=\\
\displaystyle-\alpha\left(1-N\left(\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\right)+\beta N\left(\frac{p-\overline{p}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)+\gamma(p-p_{i}^{-})+\delta p.\end{array}

(11)

From formula (
9
) we see that
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}
is also twice derivable and we get (
10
). Since
\alpha,\beta,\sigma_{i},\gamma,\delta>0
and
N^{\prime}>0
, we eventually get the strict convexity.

Corollary 1

The function
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}
has one and only one minimum point
p_{i}^{+}
which is identified as the unique point such that
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}^{\prime}(p_{i}^{+})=0
.

Proof

The thesis immediately follows from Proposition
1
and the fact that
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}
is coercive:
\lim_{p\to\pm\infty}\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}(p)=+\infty
.

By Corollary
1
, the minimization process (
8
) is well posed for each single agents
i
and for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta>0,\sigma_{i}>0
and random variable
\varepsilon^{i}
.

2.2
Mean field equilibrium

We label the discrete time evolution by
t_{n}=n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with the meaning of the passing of the days. At the first day we are given the average price
\overline{p}_{0}
and the collection of actual prices, one per every agent
i
,
\{p_{i,0}^{-}\}_{i}
, with
\overline{p}_{0}=(1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i,0}^{-}
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}
is the number of agents. Then, better specializing the transition (
1
), we have the transition

\mathbb{R}^{m}\ni(p_{i,n}^{-})_{i}\longrightarrow\left((p_{i,n}^{-})_{i},\overline{p}_{n}\right)\longrightarrow(p_{i,n}^{+})_{i}=(p_{i,n+1}^{-})_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(12)

where
\overline{p}_{n}=(1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i,n}^{-}
and the values
p_{i,n}^{+}
are constructed by the minimization process (
8
). We denote by
G:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
the function described in (
12
) which is well defined by the results in Subsection
2.1
.

Definition 1

A mean-field equilibrium
(p_{i})_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a fixed point of the map
G
,
G((p_{i})_{i})=(p_{i})_{i}
.

Remark 2

The terms (
5
) and (
6
) are introduced not only for analytical reasons, such as coercivity of the minimization problem, but also to avoid a structural degeneracy of the equilibrium problem. Indeed, if
\gamma=\delta=0
, then (
9
) gives at equilibrium

N((p_{i}-\overline{p})/\sigma_{i})=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)
, hence

p_{i}=\overline{p}+\sigma_{i}q
, with
q=N^{-1}(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta))
. Averaging with respect to
i
yields

q\,m^{-1}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}=0
, and since
\sigma_{i}>0
for every
i
, necessarily
q=0
.
Thus
\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)=N(0)=1/2
, namely
\alpha=\beta
. Hence, if

\alpha\neq\beta
, the unregularized problem cannot admit an equilibrium; if

\alpha=\beta
, the equilibrium is simply the actual value
\overline{p}
.

Proposition 2

The function
G
is a contraction map on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, endowed with the infinity norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, and hence it has a unique fixed point. The game has then a unique mean-field equilibrium.

Proof

For every
i^{\prime}=1,...,m
,
p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}=G_{i^{\prime}}((p_{j,n}^{-})_{j})
is the unique real number that makes
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}^{\prime}
in (
9
) vanish, with
\overline{p}=\overline{p}_{n}
and
p_{i^{\prime}}^{-}=p_{i^{\prime},n}^{-}
. Differentiating the equality
\varphi_{\gamma,\delta}^{\prime}(p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}((p_{i,n}^{-})_{i}))=0
we get for
i\neq i^{\prime}
and
i=i^{\prime}
respectively

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}}{\partial p_{i,n}^{-}}=\frac{\frac{1}{m}\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}N^{\prime}\left(\frac{p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}-\overline{p}_{n}}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}\right)}{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}N^{\prime}\left(\frac{p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}-\overline{p}_{n}}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}\right)+\gamma+\delta}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}}{\partial p_{i^{\prime},n}^{-}}=\frac{\gamma+\frac{1}{m}\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}N^{\prime}\left(\frac{p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}-\overline{p}_{n}}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}\right)}{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}N^{\prime}\left(\frac{p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}-\overline{p}_{n}}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}\right)+\gamma+\delta}\end{array}

(13)

Since all the entries in (
13
) are non-negative, for every row
i^{\prime}
of the Jacobian matrix of
G
, namely
JG
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\frac{\partial p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}}{\partial p_{i,n}^{-}}\right|=\frac{\gamma+\frac{1}{m}A_{i^{\prime}}}{A_{i^{\prime}}+\gamma+\delta}+(m-1)\frac{\frac{1}{m}A_{i^{\prime}}}{A_{i^{\prime}}+\gamma+\delta}=\frac{\gamma+A_{i^{\prime}}}{A_{i^{\prime}}+\gamma+\delta}<1,

where
\displaystyle A_{i^{\prime}}=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}N^{\prime}\left(\frac{p_{i^{\prime},n}^{+}-\overline{p}_{n}}{\sigma_{i^{\prime}}}\right).
Since
N^{\prime}
is bounded, this estimate is uniform, hence there exists
L<1
such that
\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\!|\!|JG(\xi)|\!|\!|_{\infty}\leq L<1.

It easily follows that
G
is a contraction on
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, which is a complete metric space, and then it has a unique fixed point (see for instance
ortrhe
).

Remark 3

By Proposition
2
, the map
G
is contractive and therefore the mean-field equilibrium is unique; in fact it can be explicitly characterized, together with its uniqueness,. At a fixed point
p_{i}^{+}=p_{i}^{-}=p_{i}
, hence the reputation term in (
9
) vanishes and one has

-\alpha(1-N((p_{i}-\overline{p})/\sigma_{i}))+\beta N((p_{i}-\overline{p})/\sigma_{i})+\delta p_{i}=0
.
Arguing as in Remark
2
, set
q_{i}=(p_{i}-\overline{p})/\sigma_{i}
, so that
p_{i}=\overline{p}+\sigma_{i}q_{i}
and
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}q_{i}=0
.
The previous condition becomes

(\alpha+\beta)N(q_{i})+\delta\sigma_{i}q_{i}=\alpha-\delta\overline{p}=:\lambda.

(14)

For each
i
the left-hand side is strictly increasing in
q_{i}
and goes to
\pm\infty
as
q_{i}\to\pm\infty
. Therefore for each
\lambda
there exists a unique solution
Q_{i}(\lambda)
with
\lambda\mapsto Q_{i}(\lambda)
strictly increasing too. The equation
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}Q_{i}(\lambda)=0
then admits at most one solution
\lambda
.
Since
\lambda=(\alpha+\beta)/2
gives
Q_{i}(\lambda)=0
for every
i
, then it is the solution and
p_{i}=\overline{p}
for all
i
. Substituting in (
14
),
-\alpha/2+\beta/2+\delta\overline{p}=0
, hence the unique equilibrium is

p_{i}=(\alpha-\beta)/(2\delta)
for every
i
.
In particular, this equilibrium is positive, and therefore physically meaningful, if and only if
\alpha>\beta
.
Moreover, the equilibrium value does not depend on
\gamma
, since the reputation term vanishes at a fixed point, nor on the coefficients
\sigma_{i}
. Obviously, they anyway affect the off-equilibrium best replies and hence the rate of convergence. Also observe that Proposition
2
gives stability of such equilibrium and guarantees that it can be reached by succesive iterations. In Figure 2 some behaviors of the nest-replay function for a fixed agent
i
are reported for different values of the parameters. Note that the case
\gamma=\delta=0
is also there reported. Indeed, the results of Subsection
2.1
, in particular Corollary
1
, also hold in that case and moreover one easily gets the convergence of minima as
(\gamma,\delta)\to(0,0)
for
(\overline{p},p_{i}^{-})
fixed.

3
Simulations

To make the model more realistic, we may want to take into account more than one type of petrol station. Namely, this means to fix a number of clusters,
K
, and fit a model with
K
4-uples of parameters
(\alpha_{\nu},\beta_{\nu},\gamma_{\nu},\delta_{\nu})
. Each cluster has its numerosity
m_{\nu}
and the average price is still calculated on the total number of agents
m=m_{1}+\cdots+m_{K}
. The proof of the existence of a unique mean-field equilibrium, Proposition
2
, is the same.

Over the years, the Agenzia delle Entrate has considered a station classification procedure,
Age
, which grouped petrol stations according to their available services (e.g. bars, mechanical assistance, car washing facilities, etc.), with the aim of predicting their annual revenue, suggesting up to 7 groups and also giving their numerosity. This does not necessarily translate into 7 clusters for the price, but can be used as an upper bound on
K
.

This increases the flexibility of the model. To avoid the risk of overfitting,
3
clusters only were used, for a total of
12
parameters. Each
\sigma_{i}
was estimated from the data, as the standard deviation of the prices for that specific agent over the quarter
1
1

1

For those petrol stations with standard deviation smaller than
10^{-3}
, we used the standard deviation of the mean price time series 0.027
. Starting from real data of the Autonomous Province of Trento, clusters were obtained through the
k
-means algorithm (see Figure 3(b)).

Parameter estimation was then performed via constrained nonlinear least squares, forcing the parameters to lie in
[0.10,100]
. The relatively large admissible range was chosen to avoid introducing strong a priori constraints on the dynamics. The initial condition
(\bar{p},(p_{i}^{-})_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{194+1}
was taken from real data. Repeated iterations of the LS method were performed, using a subsample of the time series
\bar{p}_{t}
to fit the dynamic of the model. Finding a global optimum is numerically challenging, since the 12-dimensional parameter vector must reproduce a time series of 77 observations, where each observation results from the interaction of 194 individual optimization problems. Some further constraints could be imposed, for example, on
\frac{\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}}{\delta_{i}}
.
