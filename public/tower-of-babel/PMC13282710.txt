Bartonella-associated aortitis: expanding the differential diagnosis of large-vessel inflammation

Bartonella infections should be considered in the differential diagnosis of blood culture-negative large-vessel inflammation.

Dear Editor, Aortitis, defined as inflammation of the aortic wall, can arise from a variety of infectious and non-infectious aetiologies [1]. Infectious aortitis is more common in patients with cardiovascular comorbidities and more frequently leads to structural complications such as saccular aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm formation [2]. Bartonella species are facultative intracellular fastidious bacteria with marked endothelial tropism [3]. Bartonella quintana and Bartonella henselae are well-recognized causes of infective endocarditis. In this context, they are known to mimic small-vessel vasculitis [3]. However, their role in large-vessel inflammation remains poorly characterized [2, 3]. We report, to our knowledge, the first case of B. henselae-associated aortitis without structural complications in a healthy young adult.

A 27-year-old male with no known medical history presented with three weeks of fever up to 39.6 °C, progressive fatigue, and painful swelling in the left axilla and epitrochlear region. He reported regular contact with feral cats. Physical examination revealed tender lymphadenopathies in the left axilla (up to 4 cm) and elbow (2–3 cm). Biochemistry showed lymphocytosis and a rising CRP (from 2.6 to 84.7 mg/l). Initial Bartonella serology showed positive IgM (titer of 1/100) and negative IgG. Follow-up serology two weeks later showed marked seroconversion with high-titer IgG (>1/5120). Ultrasound-guided aspiration of an axillary lymph node tested positive for B. henselae by PCR. A PCR on whole blood (EDTA), performed several days after the initiation of antibiotic therapy, was negative. Transthoracic echocardiography with excellent visualization did not show any signs of endocarditis. Despite empirical treatment with azithromycin (500 mg per day for 2 weeks), the patient’s fever and lymphadenitis persisted. A trial of corticosteroids (oral methylprednisolone, 32 mg/day, tapered over 10 days) led to transient symptomatic and biochemical improvement; however, fever recurred upon tapering. An 18F-FDG PET/CT scan (Fig. 1) demonstrated heterogeneous FDG uptake in the aortic arch, the proximal descending aorta (SUVmax 4.4), and subclavian arteries (SUVmax 3.6), along with focal hypermetabolism in the liver, spleen, and the previously known axillary and epitrochlear lymph nodes. A 12-week course of cotrimoxazole (800/160 mg BID) and rifampin (300 mg BID) was initiated, resulting in resolution of fever, regression of lymphadenopathies, and normalization of CRP. Follow-up 18F-FDG PET/CT (Fig. 1) after 12 weeks of treatment showed visual resolution of the vascular FDG uptake, with decreased SUVmax values in the subclavian arteries (1.9) and aorta (2.9). The patient subsequently returned to full activity with no recurrence at 12 months.

To date, eight cases of Bartonella infection involving large vessels have been reported in the literature (Table S1, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice Online). These cases involved elderly or immunocompromised patients with cardiovascular comorbidities, presenting with classical features of infectious aortitis, such as mycotic aneurysms, or with vascular graft infections. In contrast, our patient had non-aneurysmal inflammation of native large vessels in the absence of immunosuppression or pre-existing vascular disease, making the presentation a mimic of primary large-vessel vasculitis.

Indeed, the underlying mechanism of vascular inflammation in our patient is open to discussion. While Bartonella species can cause direct vascular infection, an immune-mediated process cannot be ruled out. Various viral and bacterial pathogens have been implicated as potential triggers of large-vessel vasculitis [4]. In this respect, Bartonella may be compared with Coxiella burnetii, another intracellular pathogen with notable endothelial tropism. C. burnetti can give rise to two phenotypes of vascular inflammation: classical infectious aortitis and a clinical, histopathological and imaging presentation mimicking primary large-vessel vasculitis [3]. To our knowledge, no comparable cases of large-vessel inflammation attributed to Bartonella have been reported to date. Although a vascular biopsy was not obtained in our patient, the initial symptomatic improvement following corticosteroid administration suggests a significant inflammatory or immune-mediated component. However, as has been hypothesized by Beydon et al. for C. burnetti, it is probably in situ infection of the large-vessel wall that induces a granulomatous inflammatory response resembling primary large-vessel vasculitis. Therefore, antimicrobial therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment [3]. Immunosuppressive therapy was thus discontinued in favour of a prolonged course of antibiotics. Regimens with intracellular activity are generally preferred, classically doxycycline combined with rifampin for complicated B. henselae infections [5]. In this case, cotrimoxazole was selected instead of doxycycline through shared decision-making, as the patient works outdoors and the risk of doxycycline-associated photosensitivity was considered undesirable. In addition, cotrimoxazole, often in combination with rifampin, has shown efficacy in hepatosplenic bartonellosis in case reports and case series [6].

Furthermore, our report underscores the potential role of 18F-FDG PET/CT in identifying vascular involvement and monitoring treatment response. In resource-rich settings, screening for large-vessel inflammation using 18F-FDG PET/CT could be considered in patients with Bartonella infections and persistent systemic symptoms, particularly when standard investigations remain inconclusive.

Overall, this case report suggests considering Bartonella infections in the differential diagnosis of blood culture-negative large-vessel inflammation. Timely diagnosis is essential to guide appropriate antimicrobial therapy and avoid potentially harmful immunosuppressive therapy.
