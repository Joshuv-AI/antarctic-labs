Domain-robust vision transformer with hierarchical swin encoding for explainable low-latency driver drowsiness detection
Authors: Al Rafy, Md Najmul Gony, Md Mashfiquer Rahman, Mohammad Shahadat Hossain, Sd Maria Khatun Shuvra, Rezaul Haque, Md Redwan Ahmed, S M Masfequier Rahman Swapno, Tahani Jaser Alahmadi, Mohammad Ali Moni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDriver drowsiness is a significant cause of road accidents worldwide, often leading to fatalities due to delayed reaction times and loss of vehicle control. Traditional fatigue detection systems face several limitations, including poor generalization across various conditions, a lack of model transparency, and an inability to operate on low-power platforms. To address these issues, we propose Enhanced Multi-path Token-Fusion Vision Transformer (ViT), Light-VTD, a novel lightweight architecture designed for accurate and explainable fatigue detection from facial images. Unlike existing transformer-based methods, Light-VTD features a fused-MBConv block for efficient local feature extraction, a position-aware token mixer for capturing global context, and a multi-path token fusion mechanism that enhances spatial consistency across different resolutions. Furthermore, we adapt Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) to generate interpretable attention heatmaps that align with clinically relevant fatigue indicators. Our experimental models are trained on a large-scale dataset comprising over 167,000 labeled images, compiled from four diverse public datasets: MRL Eye, nthuDDD2, and UTA-RLDD. The Light-VTD model achieves an intra-dataset accuracy of 99.2% and at least 93% in cross-dataset transfer, outperforming current ViT baselines by 2–4%. It supports inference with <120 ms latency on low-power devices (Raspberry Pi 4). Moreover, we have integrated the model within a web-based application that provides live predictions and visual explanations for fatigue monitoring. This study contributes a robust, interpretable, and deployable solution with potential applications in fleet-wide Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS), workplace safety platforms, and portable telehealth diagnostics.Keywords: Drowsiness detection, Vision transformer (ViT), Explainable AI (XAI), Token fusion, Embedded systemsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Driver drowsiness is a major factor contributing to road traffic accidents worldwide. It leads to delayed reaction times, impaired situational awareness, and a loss of vehicle control. According to the World Health Organization, road traffic crashes result in approximately 1.3 million fatalities each year1, with driver fatigue accounting for an estimated 20–25% of highway-related deaths2,3. In addition to safety concerns, fatigue-related incidents create noteworthy economic and societal burdens4, particularly in sectors such as transportation, logistics, and emergency services, where prolonged working hours and limited monitoring are common. To address this issue, governments and regulatory bodies are increasingly mandating in-vehicle driver monitoring systems5,6. The European Union’s General Safety Regulation (GSR) requires Driver Drowsiness and Attention Warning (DAW) systems in new vehicles, while similar initiatives are being adopted by the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration within ADAS7,8.

Early approaches to detecting drowsiness relied on physiological signals, such as electroencephalography (EEG), electrooculography (EOG), and photoplethysmography (PPG). While these methods can capture subtle neurological and cardiovascular indicators of fatigue, they are often intrusive, sensitive to motion artifacts, and impractical for long-term use in real-world situations9,10. In contrast, vision-based approaches offer a non-invasive alternative by analyzing facial cues, such as eye closure, blinking patterns, yawning, and head pose. Traditional computer vision methods11–13 that use handcrafted features-such as PERCLOS, the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR), and the Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR)-have shown promise but often perform poorly under varying lighting conditions, occlusion, head movement, and demographic diversity14–16. Deep neural networks have demonstrated superior performance on still image datasets, with architectures such as VGG, ResNet, and MobileNet often fine-tuned for binary alertness classification. However, many studies17–19 are limited by small dataset sizes, leading to overfitting and poor generalization across varying real-world conditions. Another major concern is the lack of transparency in these models. Transfer learning models often function as black boxes, providing limited explanations for their predictions. This lack of interpretability poses major challenges in regulated fields such as transportation safety and clinical assessment. More recently, ViTs have appeared as a promising alternative. They can model long-range dependencies and global spatial relationships across image patches. While ViTs have achieved state-of-the-art results in general computer vision tasks, their application in drowsiness detection is still in its early stages.

Several critical gaps remain unaddressed in the current literature. Most existing approaches20,21 are trained on the MRL Eye or nthuDDD2 datasets, without validating their performance on external corpora. As a result, these models tend to be highly sensitive to domain-specific biases and struggle to generalize to unseen samples. Moreover, due to their quadratic attention mechanisms, ViTs are not suitable for resource-constrained environments22,23. Many transformer-based models also lack optimization for low-latency performance, which restricts their use in wearable systems. Although ViTs inherently produce attention maps, these are seldom utilized effectively for post hoc explainability in drowsiness detection24. Current studies do not correlate model attention with physiologically meaningful regions (such as eyelid movement and periocular texture), which reduces trust in the model’s decisions. This lack of interpretable output is a major barrier in safety-critical applications that require clinical or regulatory validation25,26. Additionally, most existing works rely on fundamental or arbitrary data augmentation strategies. Without an evolution from simple to complex visual transformations-like motion blur, occlusion, and low-light distortion-models are less likely to develop the visual resilience needed for robust performance.

There is also a general absence of systematic ablation studies assessing individual model components’ contribution. This omission complicates the diagnosis of bottlenecks and hinders effective optimization of architectures. Finally, few studies benchmark models based on deployment metrics such as inference speed, power consumption, and frame-rate performance on embedded systems. Real-time fatigue monitoring requires accuracy and efficient inference pipelines that satisfy strict timing and energy constraints. The lack of such deployment-focused evaluations makes it challenging to determine the practical feasibility of current methods. Therefore, this study aims to develop a reliable, efficient, and user-friendly system for detecting driver drowsiness using camera-based image analysis. Related works

Physiological signal-based approaches

Early studies on drowsiness detection primarily relied on physiological signals, which capture neurological and ocular indicators of fatigue. Awais et al.27 proposed a hybrid EEG–ECG framework that reduced the number of electrodes while achieving 80.9% accuracy in a simulated driving environment. However, its reliance on controlled settings and intrusive sensors limits real-world applicability. Liu et al.28 employed EEG and EOG features with a fuzzy SVM classifier, reporting 95.11% accuracy on the SEED-VIG dataset and integrating an IoT-based warning mechanism. Despite strong performance, signal instability and motion artifacts pose challenges in mobile driving scenarios. Kołodziej et al.29 analyzed blink-related EOG signals during office tasks, achieving up to 93% accuracy in user-dependent settings, though limited subject diversity restricted generalizability. Sharma et al.30 introduced a wavelet–Tsallis entropy model using EEG, EMG, and EOG data from a large cohort, but the approach suffered from high computational cost and reduced sensitivity to imbalanced fatigue stages. While physiological approaches offer high sensitivity under controlled conditions, their intrusive nature, susceptibility to noise, and limited scalability hinder deployment in real-world driver monitoring systems.

Vision-based approaches

Vision-based methods provide a non-intrusive alternative by analyzing facial cues such as eye closure, blinking frequency, yawning, and head pose. Spandana et al.31 utilized MobileNet-SSD for eye-state detection, achieving 97% accuracy on a small-scale dataset, but lacked validation under diverse environmental conditions. Albadawi et al.32 combined handcrafted facial features with a Random Forest classifier, reporting 99% accuracy on nthuDDD2; however, performance degraded under low illumination and occlusion due to reliance on HOG-based face detection. Safarov et al.33 focused on eye gaze and head movement analysis in video sequences, achieving strong head-lean detection but struggling with sunglasses and occlusions. Li et al.34 employed multi-feature fusion with transfer learning to reach real-time performance, though robustness declined under poor lighting and facial accessories. Classical feature-based approaches such as that of Owen et al.35 demonstrated moderate accuracy in controlled environments but failed to generalize across subjects. Akrout et al.36 extended facial cue analysis to multiple datasets, achieving around 96% accuracy, yet reported false alarms caused by head motion and illumination variability. These studies highlight the practicality of vision-based systems but also reveal limitations in robustness, generalization, and interpretability when deployed in unconstrained environments.

Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have significantly improved drowsiness detection by learning discriminative facial representations directly from data. Almazroi et al.37 proposed a MobileNet-based multi-task system for drowsiness and distraction detection, achieving high accuracy and F1 scores, though evaluation was restricted to favorable lighting conditions. Vijaypriya et al.38 introduced a multi-channel CNN optimized via Flamingo Search Optimization, reporting strong performance on benchmark datasets but lacking real-world validation. Li et al.39 designed a lightweight CNN for wearable glasses, achieving 93.9% blink detection accuracy, albeit on a small dataset with limited subject diversity. Venkateswarlu et al.40 proposed a similar CNN-based multi-alert framework, but robustness under adverse weather and nighttime conditions remained untested. Florez et al.41 evaluated multiple CNN backbones for eye-state classification, achieving near-perfect accuracy on the NITYMED dataset, though the limited number of video samples constrained generalizability. Despite strong local feature extraction, CNN-based methods often struggle with capturing long-range spatial dependencies and maintaining robustness across datasets with varying capture conditions.

ViTs have recently emerged as a powerful alternative by modeling global spatial relationships through self-attention mechanisms. Jarndal et al.42 applied ViTs to facial images and achieved high accuracy on nthuDDD2 and UTA-RLDD, but performance degraded under severe occlusion. Xiao et al.43 combined handcrafted facial parameters with a residual Swin Transformer, achieving competitive accuracy at the cost of a large model size that limits edge deployment. Hassan et al.44 integrated ViT and Swin Transformer architectures for real-time detection, demonstrating strong accuracy across multiple datasets but incurring high computational overhead. Gao et al.45 proposed a CLIP-based transformer framework, introducing semantic alignment at the expense of handcrafted prompts and reduced efficiency. El-Nabi et al.46 combined diffusion models with Swin Transformers to enhance robustness, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy but with prohibitive computational cost for low-power devices. Zhang et al.47 explored transformer-based fatigue detection using EEG signals, presenting methodological novelty but limited real-time applicability. While ViT-based models improve global context modeling, most existing approaches remain computationally expensive for deployment in resource-constrained environments.

Explainable and real-time systems

With the increasing deployment of driver monitoring systems in safety-critical environments, explainability and real-time efficiency have become essential requirements alongside accuracy. Explainable AI (XAI) aims to provide transparent decision evidence by identifying which visual or physiological cues drive a model’s prediction, improving user trust, regulatory acceptance, and clinical interpretability48. In the specific context of drowsiness and fatigue detection49, explainability is particularly important for verifying whether models attend to physiologically meaningful regions such as the eyelids, periocular area, and facial muscle tension, rather than spurious correlations50.

Recent studies primarily employ post hoc51 explainability layered on top of predictive models. Iglesias et al.50 investigated driver drowsiness detection with XAI and emphasized interpretable outputs to support human-understandable decisions; however, many such XAI integrations remain qualitative and are not systematically validated for cross-domain consistency. Similarly, Kiruba and Sofia49 presented an XAI-based driver fatigue detection framework to promote safer driving, yet common limitations in this line of work include limited external validation and incomplete reporting of how explanations correlate with clinically relevant fatigue cues. More broadly, Alharthi et al.48 presented XAI for sensor-signal interpretation in health monitoring and highlighted recurring challenges that also apply to explanation faithfulness, robustness under noise, and the need for robust evaluation protocols. Consistent with these observations, several works apply saliency mapping or attention visualization as interpretability proxies without quantitative validation or cross-dataset consistency analysis52. Further, Chengula et al.53 used XAI to enhance driver anomaly detection and argued that interpretable decisions can support actionable alerts; nevertheless, many attention-guided studies54 still do not explicitly demonstrate alignment between highlighted regions and physiologically grounded fatigue indicators. Wearable and sensor-driven XAI systems for driving-related cognitive monitoring have also been explored55, but these approaches often shift the challenge toward sensor reliability and user compliance. Moreover, yagin et al.56 targetted fatigue-related conditions outside driving domains, reinforcing the general observation that interpretability is increasingly expected, yet domain-specific physiological validation remains underdeveloped.

Real-time deployability remains a major barrier for fatigue monitoring on embedded platforms. Facial analysis pipelines can achieve operational frame rates by using shallow backbones, reduced input resolution, or simplified feature extraction57–59. However, these optimizations may reduce robustness under occlusion, illumination changes, or cross-domain shifts, which are common in real driving environments. Transformer-based models60,61 often incur higher computational cost due to self-attention operations, creating deployment challenges without specialized acceleration. Efficiency-oriented designs attempt to mitigate this issue, such as hybrid ViT models62–64 with attention mechanisms designed to reduce redundancy while improving interpretability, and optimized deep hybrid learning approaches for multi-channel EEG-based drowsiness detection65. Nonetheless, deployment evaluations are frequently limited to simulated conditions and often omit critical reproducibility details such as power consumption, operating modes, and hardware configurations. Furthermore, while hardware-oriented studies demonstrate feasibility in non-driving fatigue domains66, translating these insights into robust in-vehicle monitoring requires explicit validation under realistic conditions and transparent reporting of trade-offs.

Existing systems tend to treat explainability and real-time efficiency as separate design objectives. Comprehensive reviews67–69 of fatigue detection methodologies present that the field still lacks unified frameworks that jointly optimize generalization, interpretability, and deployment efficiency with transparent evaluation protocols. Recent XAI frameworks in adjacent healthcare analytics similarly stress the importance of interpretable yet robust modeling under practical constraints70,71. These gaps motivate the development of lightweight architectures that embed explainability into the model design and evaluation pipeline while maintaining low-latency inference suitable for edge deployment. In this context, lightweight hybrid ViT models with integrated feature explanations represent a promising direction for practical and trustworthy driver drowsiness monitoring systems.

Methodology

Data description

We used three publicly available datasets to capture fatigue-related visual patterns in both static and dynamic conditions, enabling us to create adaptable deep learning models for real-world scenarios. The first dataset, MRL Eye Dataset72 includes 84,898 high-resolution images-41,946 with closed eyes and 42,952 with open eyes-captured in various lighting conditions and from different camera angles and subjects. This diversity improves the model’s robustness, allowing it to adapt to changing environments. It is crucial in aviation and logistics, where operators experience extended periods of strain and variable lighting. Sample images from the experimental datasets are shown in Fig. 2.

Sample image from each class of both (a) MRL eye, (b) nthuDDD2, and (c) RLDD datasets.

To incorporate dynamic visual cues, for second dataset, we utilized the nthuDDD244, which contains 66,521 labeled video frames from real driving sessions. Of these, 36,030 frames are marked as “Drowsy” and 30,491 as “Not Drowsy.” This video-based dataset helps models learn key fatigue indicators such as yawning and slow eye blinks, essential for real-time driver assistance systems that monitor alertness and prevent accidents. Lastly, the UTA-RLDD (Real-Life Drowsiness Dataset)73 includes 11,787 facial sequences labeled as either Fatigue (4557) or Active (7230). Captured in uncontrolled environments, it features challenges like occlusion and variable lighting. This dataset’s realistic conditions are perfect for training models for practical applications like in-car systems and mobile fatigue detection, ensuring effective real-time context-aware performance.

Data preprocessing

All face images were standardized to a resolution of 224 × 224 prior to tokenization. To improve visibility of subtle ocular boundaries under uneven illumination, we applied CLAHE. It improves local contrast around the periocular region, which is critical for distinguishing partial eyelid closure under challenging lighting. We verify gains via ablation (Table 11). For region localization, we used a cascaded detection pipeline consisting of HOG+linear SVM followed by RetinaFace for non-frontal faces and occlusion handling74. The cascaded HOG–RetinaFace pipeline is applied only during offline preprocessing; runtime inference operates solely on cropped facial regions. The extracted facial ROI preserves upper-face cues (e.g., eyelid aperture and gaze direction) that are highly discriminative for fatigue inference. Finally, all cropped ROIs were normalized to the [0,1] range. For video-based datasets (nthuDDD2 and UTA-RLDD), we reduced redundancy via temporal sub-sampling (every k-th frame) and generated overlapping windows to preserve short-term behavioral dynamics. Figure 3 shows an example of the final preprocessed output used by the transformer backbone. Here, CLAHE differs from global histogram equalization by operating on small local tiles and incorporating contrast limiting. This approach prevents the amplification of noise while enhancing the visibility of features such as partial eyelid closure, iris boundaries, and periocular texture. This enhancement is particularly useful for low-light or low-contrast images75, like those in the MRL Eye dataset, where signs of fatigue may be difficult to distinguish. Results indicate that our preprocessing pipeline consistently enhances the PR-AUC on the other two datasets, highlighting its effectiveness in retaining critical ocular features for accurate drowsiness detection.

Data augmentation

Preprocessing ensures input consistency, while data augmentation enhances the training distribution. Therefore, they are handled separately. A structured augmentation strategy was applied solely to the training data to boost robustness and minimize overfitting, leaving the validation and test sets unchanged. Augmentation occurred in three stages: (1) geometric transformations, (2) photometric changes, and (3) complex distortions. Motion blur was applied after spatial and photometric transformations to maintain facial structure before adding sensor and motion artifacts. CutMix was utilized last in the curriculum after applying motion blur and Cutout, combining each augmented image with a second randomly selected training image. The mixing coefficient λ was derived from a Beta distribution, and the training label was adjusted using the formula . This sequence ensures that CutMix is applied to already modified samples, acting as a distribution-level regularizer and preventing early label noise, while ensuring geometric and photometric realism before mixing regions from different samples. For temporal datasets, frame sub-sampling and temporal jittering were included to enhance robustness against speed variations and motion-induced noise. Baseline models

We compare the proposed Light-VTD with four representative ViT backbones that represent complementary design choices: multi-path aggregation (MPViT), hierarchical multiscale token pyramids (MViT v2), adaptive token reduction (TokenLearner ViT), and region-aware token interaction (RegionViT). All baseline models are trained and evaluated using the same data splits, input resolutions, and training protocols as Light-VTD. MPViT is an efficient multi-path ViT that aggregates information across multiple receptive-field scales through parallel feature streams. This approach is particularly relevant for capturing both fine periocular cues and broader facial context76. We use MPViT as the frame-level backbone for drowsiness classification and report its performance under the same training protocol as Light-VTD. MViT v2 constructs a hierarchical token pyramid with multiscale attention, striking a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency by progressively reducing spatial resolution while increasing channel capacity77. This multiscale hierarchy serves as a strong baseline for subtle facial dynamics, as it preserves both local detail and global structure. TokenLearner ViT adaptively selects a compact set of informative tokens, reducing redundancy and minimizing background noise78,79. This mechanism is relevant for driver monitoring because it prioritizes salient facial regions while lowering attention costs. RegionViT introduces complementary local and regional token streams with region-to-local interaction, aiming to retain fine details while incorporating stable coarse context80,81. We include RegionViT to benchmark region-aware modeling against the region-focused design choices in Light-VTD.

Proposed Light-VTD

Design objectives and scope

Light-VTD is a deployment-oriented drowsiness detector that is designed to (i) preserve fine periocular cues even under challenging lighting conditions, (ii) efficiently capture multi-scale facial context, and (iii) produce explanation maps suitable for safety-critical monitoring. Specifically, Light-VTD combines a lightweight convolutional stem for capturing local facial structures with a hierarchical transformer encoder for contextual reasoning. It also integrates an adaptive multi-path fusion module to combine complementary spatial scales without adding significant computational overhead. The architecture is applied to cropped face ROIs at a fixed input size of 224×224 pixels and outputs a dataset-specific binary label, while maintaining a consistent backbone across different benchmarks. As shown in Fig. 4 and Algorithm 1), the model utilizes a five-stage pipeline for facial analysis. The process begins with a MobileOne-derived convolutional stem that extracts local facial features such as eyelid boundaries, iris edges, and periocular textures. Next, it employs patch embedding to convert these features into tokens. Following this, hierarchical Swin encoding facilitates efficient contextual interactions among tokens and progressive patch merging. The fourth stage involves adaptive multi-path token fusion, which aggregates multi-scale token representations using learnable weights to enhance the model’s understanding. Finally, a lightweight classification head, which consists of LayerNorm, global pooling, and a fully connected layer, generates the final prediction.

Evaluation metrics

To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed models in multiclass drowsiness classification tasks across diverse datasets, we employed a diverse set of quantitative metrics, each designed to capture different aspects of predictive performance. One of the core metrics was Micro Accuracy, mathematically described in Eq. (7). This metric calculates the ratio of correctly classified samples to the total number of samples, without considering the class distribution. It is particularly suitable for class-imbalanced settings, as it aggregates all true predictions across classes uniformly. In this context, represents the count of instances from actual class i predicted as class j, while on the diagonal corresponds to accurate predictions.

To complement this, we employed the Micro F1-score, shown in Eq. (8), providing a harmonic mean of micro-averaged precision and recall. This metric ensures a balanced view of the classifier’s performance by accounting for both false positives and false negatives across all classes. To effectively evaluate model performance with varying classification thresholds, especially in imbalanced data scenarios, we used the Micro-Averaged PR AUC. This metric averages precision and recall values across all classes to create a precision-recall curve and calculates the area under it, highlighting the balance between sensitivity and specificity. Additionally, for a comprehensive evaluation of prediction quality across all confusion matrix outcomes, we employed the MCC. The binary form of MCC, stated in Eq. Discussion

The Light-VTD model showed superior performance across all evaluated datasets, consistently outperforming both convolutional and transformer-based baselines. On the MRL Eye dataset, the model outperformed the best-performing ViT-Adapter baseline by a margin of 2.3% in accuracy and 0.028 in MCC. This level of performance was maintained across other still-image datasets, where Light-VTD exhibited high recall and precision even under complex lighting and head-pose variations. The model also showed strong robustness in cross-domain settings. When trained on a single dataset and tested on previously unseen corpora, Light-VTD achieved an accuracy of 93% or higher on all target datasets, outperforming five state-of-the-art baselines by margins ranging from 2% to 4%. This suggests that the proposed architecture effectively captures domain-invariant visual cues related to indicators of ocular and periocular fatigue. The model’s effectiveness comes from integrating curriculum-based augmentation and multi-scale token fusion. The leave-one-dataset-out evaluation strategy confirmed its strong generalization, as Light-VTD consistently performed well across different datasets with varying subject demographics and environmental conditions.

The results indicate that each component of the proposed architecture significantly contributed to its overall performance. The fused-MBConv stem effectively extracted robust local features from the periocular regions, improving early convergence and enhancing noise tolerance. The Enhance–Integrate token mixer allowed the model to maintain spatial consistency while learning attention-weighted interactions among features across the image. In particular, the multi-path token fusion module was crucial for aggregating context from various spatial windows, thereby boosting the model’s capability to detect subtle variations in eyelid closure and facial posture. Ablation studies further validated these design choices. When the token fusion module was removed, there was a 3.7% drop in the MCC, along with a decrease in class-specific recall, especially for mildly drowsy states. Moreover, excluding curriculum augmentation led to worse performance in occlusion and motion blur scenarios.

The performance improvement between ×2 and ×6 augmentation was most significant in video-based datasets such as nthuDDD2 and UTA-RLDD, where fatigue cues are often subtle and degraded over time. On these datasets, recall for the “Fatigue” class increased by 5.2% between ×2 and ×6, and confusion between the “Active” and “Drowsy” states decreased by nearly 50%. This suggests that the model became better at distinguishing the progression of fatigue under challenging viewing conditions. Additionally, utilizing four diverse public datasets greatly enhanced the model’s ability for domain-invariant learning. These datasets varied in capture modalities (still images vs. video), participant demographics, sensor quality, and annotation granularity. Training on this multi-source corpus helped prevent overfitting to features specific to any dataset, enabling the model to generalize effectively to previously unseen data. This was demonstrated in cross-dataset experiments, where the Light-VTD achieved an accuracy of 93% or higher, even when tested on completely out-of-distribution samples. Moreover, ablation studies on augmentation levels indicated a saturation effect beyond ×6, where adding further complexity resulted in only marginal or no improvements. This suggests that the training regimen struck an optimal balance between variability and stability. Notably, simpler models (e.g., ViT-Small or MobileNetV2) did not benefit from aggressive augmentation due to their capacity limitations.

The low variance in predictions across different target domains highlights its reliability. Class confusion matrices showed minimal drift in decision boundaries, and the model consistently focused on periocular regions, even when faced with varying resolutions, background clutter, or facial diversity. This consistency is crucial for maintaining performance in monitoring multi-fleet vehicles, managing public transportation hubs, and ensuring occupational safety. Moreover, the generalization capability was achieved without increasing inference costs. Unlike domain adaptation techniques that necessitate retraining or fine-tuning during testing, Light-VTD enables zero-shot transfer, making it highly suitable for plug-and-play deployment scenarios. Its performance on unseen datasets demonstrates the model’s readiness for real-world applications.

The qualitative analysis of all four datasets reveals a consistent and meaningful attention pattern learned by Light-VTD. For Alert predictions, the model primarily concentrates on the iris boundary and the curvature of the upper eyelid. In contrast, for Drowsy and Fatigue states, the attention shifts toward the eyelid crease, drooping eyelashes, periocular texture, and the upper cheek region, areas commonly linked to visual fatigue in human assessments. To quantitatively evaluate the reliability of these explanations, we calculated the Intersection-over-Union (IoU) between Grad-CAM heatmaps and manually annotated periocular regions, achieving an average IoU exceeding 0.71 across the datasets. This high degree of overlap confirms a strong alignment between the model’s focus and clinically relevant facial cues. Importantly, these attention patterns remain stable across datasets with varying camera viewpoints, resolutions, lighting conditions, and subject demographics. This indicates that Light-VTD learns domain-invariant fatigue cues rather than overfitting to dataset-specific artifacts. The activation maps consistently suppress irrelevant background areas (such as the seat or dashboard) and focus on fatigue-related facial structures, even under challenging conditions like partial eye closure or head tilt. Compared to conventional CNN-based models, which often produce diffuse or noisy saliency maps, Light-VTD generates more precise and stable explanations due to its attention-based token fusion and hierarchical modeling. The coherence between these qualitative observations and the strong cross-dataset performance further supports the conclusion that Light-VTD captures robust and generalizable facial indicators of drowsiness across diverse recording environments.

The integration of Grad-CAM into the Flask-based web interface significantly enhances the system’s transparency. Users are not only able to view prediction outcomes, but they also receive a visual explanation in just 120 milliseconds. This combination of interpretability and speed makes Light-VTD a suitable solution for interactive safety systems. The use of a fused-MBConv stem notably reduces the number of parameters and FLOPs required during early feature extraction. Unlike standard ViT models that apply full self-attention to all tokens, Light-VTD utilizes multi-path token fusion and position-aware channel attention. This approach minimizes attention overhead while maintaining spatial awareness, resulting in a model that balances representational power and inference efficiency on resource-constrained platforms. This capability is crucial for applications in aftermarket fatigue-monitoring systems, telemedicine dashboards, and occupational safety kiosks, where quick feedback and platform interoperability are essential. Unlike many studies that only report offline evaluation results, Light-VTD showcases its readiness for full-stack deployment, from training on large-scale heterogeneous datasets to edge-compatible inference with real-time explainability.

The proposed Light-VTD architecture has several limitations. It is trained and evaluated solely on RGB visual inputs, which means it lacks multimodal integration with biosignal modalities such as EEG, EOG, or PPG. This exclusion of neurophysiological data limits the model’s sensitivity to subtle fatigue states, like cognitive drowsiness or microsleep episodes, which can occur before visible signs appear. Consequently, Light-VTD is restricted in applications that require high precision, such as aerospace cockpit monitoring, clinical fatigue screening, and detecting task-induced lapses in vigilance. Additionally, Light-VTD operates under a frame-wise inference paradigm and does not incorporate temporal encoding or sequential attention mechanisms. While this approach is practical for static image classification, it hinders the model’s ability to capture temporal dependencies, such as changes in blink rate, eye closure duration, and progressive head tilt. The absence of temporal self-attention, 3D convolutions, or transformer decoders with positional recurrence restricts the system’s ability to interpret longitudinal visual cues in video sequences.

Despite extensive data augmentation, the dataset composition remains biased toward controlled acquisition settings with limited variability in environmental conditions. For instance, the UTA-RLDD dataset includes fewer than 25 unique subjects and lacks diverse imagery, which limits Light-VTD’s ability to generalize in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. Moreover, the training data does not adequately represent different skin tones, age groups, and facial structures, increasing the risk of demographic bias and overfitting to the source conditions. Although Grad-CAM is used for post-hoc explainability, the current visualization focuses only on activations from the final token-fusion layer. This reduces spatial resolution and may obscure more nuanced attention shifts captured in earlier stages of the transformer encoder. The model does not provide uncertainty-aware explanations, vital for generating calibrated confidence scores and incorporating risk-sensitive decision thresholds in mission-critical applications. The tests were conducted in controlled lab conditions with isolated inference pipelines. However, in real-world scenarios, factors like thermal throttling, concurrent input/output loads, camera frame buffering, and multi-threaded process interference can affect system latency-conditions not rigorously evaluated in this study. Finally, the memory footprint and computational performance under low-voltage or battery-powered conditions remain unquantified.

Demographic bias remain a concern, as detailed demographic annotations. such as age groups, ethnicity, skin tone, and facial morphology, are not consistently available for our experimental datasets. The absence of explicit demographic labels limits the ability of Light-VTD to conduct a fine-grained analysis of performance disparities among underrepresented populations. This gap may impact fairness and reliability our application. Although robustness experiments have addressed common issues like motion blur, partial occlusion, and low light conditions, scenarios such as heavy rain, night driving with strong glare, extreme shadows, and severe sensor noise have not been tested. Performance in these situations may differ from the reported results, indicating a need for further investigation using real-world datasets. Lastly, our model lacks explicit temporal modeling. It primarily operates on frame-level representations, which limits its capability to model fatigue progression over extended driving periods. Although short-term dynamics are indirectly addressed through data sampling and augmentation, incorporating sequence-aware modules could improve its sensitivity to gradual drowsiness onset.

Future work will focus on three main areas to enhance reliability, fairness, and real-world applicability for driver monitoring. Firstly, we will collect training resources with clear demographic descriptors such as age, skin tone, and facial features. We will evaluate performance based on subgroup metrics, including worst-group accuracy and subgroup PR-AUC. To mitigate bias, we will explore strategies like distributionally robust optimization and adaptive reweighting, ensuring that our data collection process preserves privacy. In addition, we will assess performance using real-world driving datasets that encompass challenging conditions such as night glare, rain, fog, and sensor issues. To enhance safety, we will integrate uncertainty-aware decision mechanisms that allow for dynamic threshold adjustments or abstaining from predictions when inputs fall outside the training distribution. Finally, we will transition from frame-level analysis to temporal reasoning to better understand fatigue progression. This will involve using efficient sequence modeling techniques, such as temporal transformers and lightweight recurrent models, to improve early detection of drowsiness while minimizing false alarms. Additionally, we will implement federated learning for on-device adaptation and expand our explainability methods beyond Grad-CAM to support human validation.

Conclusion

The proposed Light-VTD model combines local and global feature extraction using fused MBConv stems, multi-path token fusion, and curriculum-based augmentation to achieve state-of-the-art automated driver fatigue detection performance across multiple public datasets. This architecture shows strong cross-corpus generalization, demonstrating its ability to detect drowsiness in real-world situations reliably. Additionally, its integration of transformer-compatible Grad-CAM provides clinically meaningful visual explanations, enhancing interpretability and fostering user trust. However, the current system has significant limitations. It relies solely on single-modality RGB inputs and lacks temporal modeling, with limited testing conducted under extreme occlusion and night-time conditions. Moreover, aspects like uncertainty quantification and multimodal fusion, which could improve detection reliability, have not yet been implemented. Future directions for Light-VTD include incorporating temporal context through video-based transformer models, integrating physiological modalities for enhanced sensitivity, employing uncertainty-aware predictions, and exploring federated learning for personalized fatigue detection. Addressing these areas will further strengthen the practical impact and robustness of Light-VTD in intelligent transportation and occupational safety environments.
