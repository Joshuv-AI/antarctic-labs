Machine-Verifying Toom-Cook Multiplication with Integer Evaluation Points
Srihari Nanniyur, Siddhartha Jayanti

Abstract
We present a machine-verified proof of the correctness of Toom-Cook multiplication with generalized integer evaluation points. Toom-Cook is a class of fast multiplication algorithms parameterized by a triple ( k x , k y , v → ) \displaystyle(k_{x},k_{y},\vec{v}) consisting of two positive integer split sizes k x , k y \displaystyle k_{x},k_{y} and a vector v → \displaystyle\vec{v} of distinct evaluation points . As part of our proof, we verify that for any selection of k x + k y − 1 \displaystyle k_{x}+k_{y}-1 distinct integer evaluation points, we can compute a threshold function θ ⁡ ( k x , k y , v → ) \displaystyle\theta(k_{x},k_{y},\vec{v}) such that, if the algorithm’s base-case problem size is set above this threshold, then the algorithm’s termination is guaranteed regardless of the values of the operands. The threshold formula, which we derive by obtaining upper bounds on the subproblem sizes produced by the Toom-Cook recurrence, does not depend on the operands; it depends only on k x \displaystyle k_{x} , k y \displaystyle k_{y} , v → \displaystyle\vec{v} , and the base b \displaystyle b in which we operate. We write the proof in Lean 4, making use of the Mathlib library. We formalize the algorithm, our base case threshold formula, and our key lemma statements in Lean. We then use the AI theorem prover Aristotle to assist in completing the machine verification of the algorithm’s correctness. This proof, through its synthesis of human input and AI assistance, demonstrates the considerable power of AI to automate the machine verification process.

In the common case where

1.1
Multiplication algorithms

The standard
long multiplication
(a.k.a.
grade-school multiplication
) algorithm for integers has been known since antiquity.
The earliest recorded explicit specification of this algorithm, to our knowledge, is contained in Brahmagupta’s
Brahma-sphuta-siddhanta
(c. 7th century CE)
[
5
]
.

The quadratic running time of this algorithm was conjectured to be asymptotically optimal by Kolmogorov
[
14
]
.
In 1962, Karatsuba
[
15
]
refuted this conjecture by devising a divide-and-conquer algorithm that reduces multiplication of two
\displaystyle n
-digit operands to three multiplications with operands that have roughly
\displaystyle\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}
digits, thus achieving a running time of
\displaystyle O\left(n^{\log_{2}3}\right)
.

Toom
[
19
]
and Cook
[
6
]
vastly generalized Karatsuba’s approach, showing that by dividing each problem into more subproblems of smaller size, multiplication can be performed in
\displaystyle O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
time for arbitrarily small
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
.
The Toom-Cook class of algorithms is the primary subject of this paper; we detail it in Section
2
.

Schönhage and Strassen showed that multiplication can be performed in
\displaystyle\tilde{O}(n)
time using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)
[
18
]
.
The exact running time of their algorithm is
\displaystyle O(n\log n\log\log n)
; but they conjectured that
\displaystyle O(n\log n)
would be achievable and optimal.
A recent series of papers
[
8
,
11
,
10
,
12
]
in this domain made steady progress on this conjecture.
This line of work culminated in the celebrated algorithm of Harvey and van der Hoeven
[
13
]
, which uses more complicated FFT-based techniques to achieve
\displaystyle O(n\log n)
time complexity.
However, this algorithm is galactic, i.e., it is only efficient at problem sizes too large for any practical context
[
13
]
.
The Schönhage-Strassen conjecture remains open, since there is no proof of asymptotic optimality.
In fact, we are not aware of any non-trivial general lower bounds on the asymptotic time complexity of integer multiplication.

1.2
Lean, Aristotle, and AI-assisted theorem proving

Our Toom-Cook proof is specified in Lean, a language for formalizing and machine-verifying mathematical proofs. Given a Lean formalization of a proof, the Lean backend is able to check that proof’s correctness. Lean provides a large number of
tactics
, or commands specialized for solving particular types of proof goals. We also make extensive use of Mathlib, a library of mathematical tools and lemmas implemented in Lean.

Aristotle, an AI tool developed by Harmonic, is capable of generating valid Lean proofs by itself. Given a piece of Lean code containing unfilled lemma headers, Aristotle will attempt to fill in the proofs of those lemmas, making no changes to the rest of the code. Harmonic’s October 2025 white paper on Aristotle
[
1
]
provides more information on the tool’s internals. At the core of Aristotle is a reinforcement learning-based system that generates natural-language proofs of lemmas, renders them in Lean code, and then progressively refines them using feedback from the Lean proof checker. Thus, Aristotle knows when its code does not pass the checker, and it can amend the code accordingly. However, Aristotle also has limitations: it does not always succeed at reaching the goal, and even when it does, its proofs often require emendations to work in other Lean environments.

A significant portion of our proof code is Aristotle-generated. In accordance with the spirit of this project, we also made judicious use of GPT-5 to find Mathlib lemmas and help structure our code. We also note that not all of Aristotle’s proof passed our local Lean checker initially; we attribute this to subtle differences between Aristotle’s Lean backend and ours. The emendations needed to fix this typically involved rewriting tactic calls into more explicit proofs.

Related work

To our knowledge, we are the first to machine-verify Toom-Cook.

\displaystyle k_{x}
,
\displaystyle k_{y}

\displaystyle\vec{v}

\displaystyle\vec{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k_{x}+k_{y}-1}

Cook

Modern Computer Arithmetic

[
4

[
20

provides an analysis of the case where
\displaystyle k_{x}\neq k_{y}
, in particular of Toom–2.5 (that is, of
\displaystyle k

Bodrato
[
3

, both on his own and in collaboration with Zanoni
[
2
]
, works towards developing heuristics towards optimizing evaluation-point selection. Bodrato and Zanoni
[
2

[

1.4
Challenges and our approach

While the Toom-Cook family of algorithms is well understood, expositions on the general case have omitted the
base case threshold

\displaystyle\theta
, i.e., an exact specification of the problem size at which to switch from the recursive case to the base case.
It turns out that, in the case of generalized evaluation points, this threshold cannot be chosen as an absolute constant (see the illustrative example in Section
3.1.1
).
This is because the threshold required to ensure termination actually increases based on the splitting parameters (
\displaystyle k_{x}
,
\displaystyle k_{y}
) and evaluation points (
\displaystyle\vec{v}
).

Writing machine-verifiable proofs is time-consuming, since it requires spelling out low-level details.
In the case of recursive algorithms like Toom-Cook, one of the most time-consuming steps in machine verification is proving termination.
The challenge lies in stating explicit bounds on the sizes of problems and resultant subproblems, and also in showing that the sizes are actually strictly decreasing between successive recursion depths above the base case.
We adopt an AI-driven approach to this task, taking advantage of the existence of an AI proof assistant that can push user-specified lemma goals through to completion.

Our first main contribution is the derivation of an explicit base case threshold formula
\displaystyle\theta(k_{x},k_{y},\vec{v})
, such that Toom-Cook-(
\displaystyle k_{x}
,
\displaystyle k_{y}
,
\displaystyle\vec{v}
) can use the recursive case until the
problem size
—the digit count of the operand with larger absolute value—no longer exceeds
\displaystyle\theta
. The algorithm switches to the base case when the problem size is
\displaystyle\leq\theta(k_{x},k_{y},\vec{v})
. (Note that the base
\displaystyle b
in which we work is also implicit in the formula; all operations and representations in the algorithm take place in base
\displaystyle b
. Thus, we do not explicitly notate
\displaystyle b
in the parameter list.)

The derivation of
\displaystyle\theta
requires an explicit formulation of the problem size as a function of the digit counts of all the components that contribute to it.
To obtain our upper bound on the asymptotic behavior of the problem size, we separate out those components that depend on the operands from those that do not; the result is a clean, immediately usable formula for a valid base-case threshold in terms of those components that do
not
depend on the operands.

Our contributions

We make the following contributions in this paper.

on the base case threshold of Toom-Cook-

.
Notably, the value of this upper bound depends only on these three parameters and on the fixed base

.
Notably, we make use of the AI theorem prover Aristotle to aid the proof process.

2
Toom-Cook multiplication

We give a brief overview of the algorithm, building on the descriptions given by Brent and Zimmermann in
Modern Computer Arithmetic

[
4
]
and by Zanoni in his paper on iterative Toom-Cook methods
[
20
]
.

We work in a fixed base
\displaystyle b\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
\displaystyle b>1
. All digit counts taken from this point forward are in base
\displaystyle b
. For the remainder of this paper, we work entirely in base
\displaystyle b
; thus, we leave
\displaystyle b
implicit in the parameter list of
\displaystyle\theta(k_{x},k_{y},\vec{v})
(see Section
3.2
).

Let
\displaystyle\delta_{b}(a\in\mathbb{Z})
denote the number of digits needed to represent an integer
\displaystyle a
in base
\displaystyle b
. The operands to our multiplication are
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{N}
. We define splitting parameters
\displaystyle k_{x},k_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\displaystyle k_{x}>1
and
\displaystyle k_{y}>1
. We define
\displaystyle P_{x,y}=\delta_{b}(\max\{x,y\})
as the
problem size
; or, to be more precise, the digit count in base
\displaystyle b
of the operand with the greater magnitude. (For the extension to integer operands, we define
\displaystyle P_{x,y}=\delta_{b}(\max\{|x|,|y|\})
for
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
.) Finally, let
\displaystyle\vec{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k_{x}+k_{y}-1}
be the vector of
\displaystyle k_{x}+k_{y}-1
distinct integer evaluation points.

Let
\displaystyle B=b^{i}
for some
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{N}
chosen such that
\displaystyle x<B^{k_{x}}
,
\displaystyle y<B^{k_{y}}
,
\displaystyle x
has roughly
\displaystyle k_{x}
digits in base
\displaystyle B
, and
\displaystyle y
has roughly
\displaystyle k_{y}
digits in base
\displaystyle B
. It is important to choose
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle B
such that the relative sizes of the chunks resulting from splitting an operand are kept as balanced as possible.

Our specification uses an optimized choice of
\displaystyle i
: namely,
\displaystyle i=\max\{\lceil\delta_{b}(x)/k_{x}\rceil\ ,\lceil\delta_{b}(y)/k_{y}\rceil\}
. We do not further generalize the selection of
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle B
, as our aim is to show that there exists at least one such selection for which we can derive a base case threshold satisfying termination and correctness for generalized
\displaystyle\vec{v}
. Our threshold derivation (Section
3.2
) is carried out with respect to this choice of
\displaystyle i
, although it is also correct for any
\displaystyle i
that produces chunks of digit length at most
\displaystyle\lceil P_{x,y}/\min\{k_{x},k_{y}\}\rceil
in base
\displaystyle b
.
Notably, Cook’s thesis
[
6
]
specifies that
\displaystyle i
(which he terms
\displaystyle q
) should satisfy a similar chunk-size bound.

We define the polynomials
\displaystyle p,q
where
\displaystyle x=p(B)
and
\displaystyle y=q(B)
:

p(u\in\mathbb{Z})=\sum_{j=0}^{k_{x}-1}(\lfloor(x/B^{j})\rfloor\mod B)u^{j}

q(u\in\mathbb{Z})=\sum_{j=0}^{k_{y}-1}(\lfloor(y/B^{j})\rfloor\mod B)u^{j}

For each entry
\displaystyle v_{i}
of
\displaystyle\vec{v}
, we recursively multiply
\displaystyle p(v_{i})q(v_{i})
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle P_{x,y}
and store the evaluated results in the vector
\displaystyle\vec{w}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k_{x}+k_{y}-1}
.

We take the Vandermonde matrix
\displaystyle V
of
\displaystyle\vec{v}
. Since the values in
\displaystyle\vec{v}
are distinct, this Vandermonde matrix is invertible provided the inversion and interpolation are performed over a field. We multiply
\displaystyle V^{-1}\vec{w}
to get the vector
\displaystyle\vec{r}
, which consists of the
\displaystyle k_{x}+k_{y}-1
coefficients of
\displaystyle p\cdot q
.

Let
\displaystyle h
be the polynomial of degree
\displaystyle k_{x}+k_{y}-2
whose coefficients are the values contained in
\displaystyle\vec{r}
. The construction of the Vandermonde interpolation step ensures that
\displaystyle h=p\cdot q
. We evaluate
\displaystyle h(B)=(p\cdot q)(B)=p(B)\cdot q(B)=x\cdot y
to get the product of
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
.

The procedure as specified above is for operands in
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
. However, it is easy to extend it to operands in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
by multiplying those operands’ absolute values and then determining the result’s sign based on their original signs. This is the approach we have taken.

3
Base case threshold formula

3.1
Motivation for deriving the formula

In practical implementations of Toom-Cook, the evaluation points are small enough (and the base-case
\displaystyle P_{x,y}
large enough) that termination is not in question. For instance, one version of Toom–3 implemented in the GNU GMP uses a fixed threshold of 74 digits
[
9
]
with efficient points. However, if
\displaystyle\vec{v}
is allowed to contain arbitrarily large integer points, then it becomes necessary to derive a formula for a base case threshold
\displaystyle\theta(k_{x},k_{y},\vec{v})
. We demonstrate this necessity below.

3.1.1
Toom–3 example with large evaluation points

To show why arbitrary point values make the proof of termination nontrivial, we provide a small example. Let
\displaystyle b=10
,
\displaystyle x=123
,
\displaystyle y=456
,
\displaystyle k_{x}=k_{y}=3
, and
\displaystyle\vec{v}=\langle 10^{10},10^{10}+1,10^{10}+2,10^{10}+3,10^{10}+4\rangle
. Then
\displaystyle p(u)=1u^{2}+2u+3
and
\displaystyle q(u)=4u^{2}+5u+6
. Thus, the recursive sub-multiplications in the pointwise multiplication step are:

\displaystyle\displaystyle 100000000020000000003\cdot 400000000050000000006

\displaystyle\displaystyle 100000000040000000006\cdot 400000000130000000015

\displaystyle\displaystyle 100000000060000000011\cdot 400000000210000000032

\displaystyle\displaystyle 100000000080000000018\cdot 400000000290000000057

\displaystyle\displaystyle 100000000100000000027\cdot 400000000370000000090

The initial problem size is
\displaystyle\delta_{b}(\max\{|x|,|y|\})=3
. But one step deeper in the recursion, the largest problem size is 21, as all the recursive sub-multiplications multiply 21-digit numbers.

The question of how the problem size behaves as the recursion depth approaches infinity is thus vital for determining a base-case threshold that ensures the termination of the algorithm. As it turns out, we can calculate a finite upper bound
\displaystyle\theta
on that threshold. (Necessarily, the formula for
\displaystyle\theta
cannot depend on the operands; as it turns out, it does not.)

Indeed, we can prove (see Section
3.3
) that as long as the current problem size
\displaystyle P>\theta
, then the largest subproblem size at the next recursion depth is always less than
\displaystyle P
. That is, problem size above
\displaystyle\theta
strictly decreases from one recursion depth to the next.

3.2
Derivation

We continue to use the notation established in Section 2. We work entirely in base
\displaystyle b\in\mathbb{N}
where
\displaystyle b>1
; this base is implicit in the entire derivation of
\displaystyle\theta
. Recall that
\displaystyle\delta_{b}(x\in\mathbb{Z})
denotes the number of digits (in base
\displaystyle b
) of an integer
\displaystyle x
. Recall as well that we defined
\displaystyle P_{x,y}=\delta_{b}(\max\{|x|,|y|\})
; that is,
\displaystyle P_{x,y}
is the initial “problem size” with respect to the two operands
\displaystyle x,y
.

Given a pair of operands
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
, it suffices to demonstrate this derivation for
\displaystyle|x|,|y|\in\mathbb{N}
, as we can multiply the absolute values of the operands and then determine the result’s sign afterwards.

3.2.1
Analysis of the polynomial evaluation step

Let
\displaystyle v_{\max}\in\mathbb{N}
be the absolute value (not the raw value) of the entry with the highest magnitude in
\displaystyle\vec{v}
. That is, we have that

v_{\max}=\max_{1\leq i\leq k_{x}+k_{y}-1}|v_{i}|

(1)

For an operand
\displaystyle z\in\{x,y\}
, we define
\displaystyle p_{z}(v_{i})
as the evaluation at
\displaystyle v_{i}
of the polynomial obtained by splitting
\displaystyle|z|

\displaystyle k_{z}
ways. This polynomial is of the following form:

p_{z}(v_{i})=s_{k_{z}-1}v_{i}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{0}v_{i}^{0}

(2)

By (
1
), for each entry
\displaystyle v_{i}
of
\displaystyle\vec{v}
,
\displaystyle|v_{i}|\leq v_{\max}
. We have that
\displaystyle|x|,|y|\in\mathbb{N}
and so
\displaystyle\{s_{0},\cdots,s_{k_{z}-1}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
. Recall that we defined
\displaystyle\delta_{b}(a\in\mathbb{Z})
as the number of digits in
\displaystyle a

\displaystyle b
representation. Since the split is performed on
\displaystyle\{|x|,|y|\}
, the polynomials’ coefficients are all non-negative. By the non-negativity of all these
\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
, we can state:

\displaystyle\delta_{b}\left(s_{k_{z}-1}v_{i}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{0}v_{i}^{0}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=\delta_{b}\left(|s_{k_{z}-1}v_{i}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{0}v_{i}^{0}|\right)\text{ as $\displaystyle\delta_{b}(n)=\delta_{b}(|n|)$ $\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}$}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{b}\left(s_{k_{z}-1}|v_{i}|^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{0}|v_{i}|^{0}\right)\text{ by triangle inequality and non-negativity of $\displaystyle s$-terms}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{b}\left(s_{k_{z}-1}v_{\max}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{0}v_{\max}^{0}\right)\text{ by monotonicity of digit count over $\displaystyle\mathbb{N}$}

We aim to separate out whatever depends on
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
from whatever does not depend on them. A natural way to proceed with this is to define an expression for the coefficient of maximum magnitude. We can then (by the non-negativity of all the terms in the polynomial when it is evaluated at
\displaystyle v_{\max}
) factor out a single term that contains within it the operand-dependent contribution to the evaluated result’s digit count. We thus define the coefficient of maximum magnitude
\displaystyle s_{\max,z\in\{x,y\}}\in\{s_{0},\cdots,s_{k_{z}-1}\}
:

s_{\max,z}=\max_{s_{i}\in\{s_{0},\cdots,s_{k_{z}-1}\}}s_{i}

(4)

Note that we do not need the absolute value notation here as we did with
\displaystyle v_{\max}
, as
\displaystyle\{s_{0},\cdots,s_{k_{z}-1}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
. By (
2

3

\delta_{b}\left(s_{k_{z}-1}v_{\max}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{0}v_{\max}^{0}\right)\leq\delta_{b}\left(s_{\max,z}v_{\max}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{\max,z}v_{\max}^{0}\right)

(5)

And by (
1
), (
3
), and (
5
), we can state:

\delta_{b}\left(s_{k_{z}-1}v_{i}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{0}v_{i}^{0}\right)\leq\delta_{b}\left(s_{\max,z}v_{\max}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{\max,z}v_{\max}^{0}\right)

(6)

Using the fact that
\displaystyle\forall c,d\in\mathbb{N},\delta_{b}(c\cdot d)\leq\delta_{b}(c)+\delta_{b}(d)
, we can state the following about the right side of (

\displaystyle\delta_{b}\left(s_{\max,z}v_{\max}^{k_{z}-1}+\cdots+s_{\max,z}v_{\max}^{0}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=\delta_{b}\left(s_{\max,z}\sum_{j=0}^{k_{z}-1}v_{\max}^{j}\right)\leq\delta_{b}(s_{\max,z})+\delta_{b}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k_{z}-1}v_{\max}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{x,y}
is the maximum digit count of any term passed to the sub-multiplications at the next recursion depth. That is, it is the digit count of the polynomial-evaluation result with the highest absolute value. Thus, the term

\delta_{b}\left(s_{\max,z}\right)+\delta_{b}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k_{z}-1}v_{\max}^{j}\right)

serves as an upper bound on the digit count of the evaluated result of
\displaystyle p_{z}(v_{i})
for any component
\displaystyle v_{i}
of
\displaystyle\vec{v}
. If we weaken this bound somewhat, we can put it in a form that enables us to derive an asymptotic upper bound on the problem size as the recursion depth approaches infinity.

A well-formed upper bound on
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
given
\displaystyle P

Let
\displaystyle k_{\min}=\min\{k_{x},k_{y}\}
. By split construction,
\displaystyle\delta_{b}(s_{\max,z\in\{x,y\}})\leq\lceil\delta_{b}(\max\{|x|,|y|\})/k_{\min}\rceil

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\delta_{b}(\max\{|x|,|y|\})
is exactly our definition of
\displaystyle P_{x,y}
. Thus, accounting for the slack from the ceiling function:

\delta_{b}(s_{\max,z\in\{x,y\}})\leq P_{x,y}/k_{\min}+1

(8)

We now define the terms
\displaystyle C_{x}
,
\displaystyle C_{y}
, and
\displaystyle C
as follows:

\displaystyle C_{x}=\delta_{b}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k_{x}-1}v_{\max}^{j}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle C_{y}=\delta_{b}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k_{y}-1}v_{\max}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle C=\max\{C_{x},C_{y}\}+1

And therefore, by (
7
), (
8
), and the definition of
\displaystyle C
:

P^{\prime}_{x,y}\leq P_{x,y}/k_{\min}+C

(10)

3.2.3
Calculating
\displaystyle\theta

Let
\displaystyle P_{0}
denote the initial
\displaystyle P_{x,y}
, and let
\displaystyle P_{n}
denote the maximum problem size among all sub-multiplications being performed at recursion depth
\displaystyle n\geq 1
. Equation (
10
) still applies to
\displaystyle P_{n}
, as that equation also applies to every subproblem at depth
\displaystyle n-1
. That is, given that
\displaystyle n\geq 1
:

P_{n}\leq P_{n-1}/k_{\min}+C.

The above inequality holds by Equation (
10
) and our definition of
\displaystyle P_{n}
. We can now begin to express the progression of problem sizes over the course of the recursion:

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{1}\leq P_{0}/k_{\min}+C,

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{2}\leq P_{1}/k_{\min}+C=\frac{P_{0}/k_{\min}+C}{k_{\min}}+C=\frac{P_{0}}{k_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{C}{k_{\min}}+C,

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{3}\leq P_{2}/k_{\min}+C=\frac{\frac{P_{0}}{k_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{C}{k_{\min}}+C}{k_{\min}}+C=\frac{P_{0}}{k_{\min}^{3}}+\frac{C}{k_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{C}{k_{\min}}+C,

and so on. We can write the upper bound on
\displaystyle P_{n}
as follows:

P_{n}\leq\frac{P_{0}}{k_{\min}^{n}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{C}{k_{\min}^{j}}

The validity of the above bound follows by induction on the recursion depth. We can now express an asymptotic upper bound on
\displaystyle P
as the recursion depth approaches infinity:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}P_{n}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{P_{0}}{k_{\min}^{n}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{C}{k_{\min}^{j}}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{P_{0}}{k_{\min}^{n}}\right)+\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{C}{k_{\min}^{j}}\right)

The first term in the addition disappears:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P_{0}}{k_{\min}^{n}}=0

As for the summation term, we can rewrite it as an infinite geometric series and (given that
\displaystyle k_{\min}>1
) apply the standard sum formula:

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{C}{k_{\min}^{j}}=C\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k_{\min}}\right)^{j}=C\frac{1}{1-1/k_{\min}}=C\frac{1}{(k_{\min}-1)/k_{\min}}=\frac{Ck_{\min}}{k_{\min}-1}

We define
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{Q}
, where

\theta=\frac{Ck_{\min}}{k_{\min}-1}

as an asymptotic upper bound on
\displaystyle P
as the recursion depth approaches infinity. In the Lean proof, we machine-verify that if the base case is fixed at some
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
where
\displaystyle N>\theta
, then the recursion’s eventual termination is guaranteed.

3.3
Strict problem-size decrease above
\displaystyle\theta

Lean requires us to prove that, above the base case, some measure within the algorithm’s logic is strictly decreasing between recursive calls. As it turns out,
\displaystyle P
is such a measure. Here is a proof of its decrease property above
\displaystyle\theta
.

Theorem 1
.

\displaystyle P>\theta\implies P^{\prime}<P
.

Proof.

Assume
\displaystyle P>\theta
. We can state:

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{\min}>1,\text{ }\theta=C\frac{k_{\min}}{k_{\min}-1},\text{ }C=\frac{k_{\min}-1}{k_{\min}}\theta\text{ by construction}

\displaystyle\displaystyle P^{\prime}\leq P/k_{\min}+C\text{ by Equation \eqref{eq:10}}

Using these statements, we can rewrite the expression
\displaystyle P/k_{\min}+C
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle P/k_{\min}+C=P/k_{\min}+\frac{k_{\min}-1}{k_{\min}}\theta

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{P+k_{\min}\theta-\theta}{k_{\min}}=P/k_{\min}+\theta-\theta/k_{\min}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{P-\theta}{k_{\min}}+\theta

Trivially,

\displaystyle\displaystyle P-\theta+\theta=P

We know that
\displaystyle P>\theta
and therefore
\displaystyle P-\theta>0
. Then, given that
\displaystyle k_{\min}>1
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{P-\theta}{k_{\min}}+\theta<P

We thus have the following:

\displaystyle\displaystyle P^{\prime}\leq P/k_{\min}+C

\displaystyle\displaystyle P/k_{\min}+C=\frac{P-\theta}{k_{\min}}+\theta

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{P-\theta}{k_{\min}}+\theta<P

And so it follows that
\displaystyle P^{\prime}<P
.
∎

Conclusion

We have presented a machine-verified correctness proof of the Toom-Cook multiplication algorithm given arbitrary integer evaluation points. The proof requires a formula for a base-case threshold that ensures the algorithm’s termination for any integer values of the operands
\displaystyle x,y
. By analyzing the behavior of the recurrence’s subproblem sizes over time, we derive an upper bound
\displaystyle\theta(k_{x},k_{y},\vec{v})
on the base-case threshold required for termination. We machine-verify that this
\displaystyle\theta
—which can be expressed entirely in terms of our working base
\displaystyle b
, the point vector
\displaystyle\vec{v}
, and the splitting parameters
\displaystyle k_{x}
and
\displaystyle k_{y}
—is a valid threshold.
Our proof, which uses the assistance of Aristotle, demonstrates the power of AI to automate the most time-intensive (and often tedious) parts of the machine verification process.
Our results suggest that the synthesis of human analysis and AI tools can facilitate the machine verification of several useful algorithms.
