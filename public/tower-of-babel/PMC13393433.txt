Fulminant liver dysfunction after endovascular pharmacomechanical thrombectomy for peripheral arterial occlusive disease
Authors: Ho-Ching Leung, Dexter Yak-Seng Chan, Yiu Che Chan, Stephen W Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDerangement of liver function is a rare, but known, complication of percutaneous pharmacomechanical thrombectomy (PMT). PMT can cause intravascular hemolysis, which results in cell-mediated and direct hepatocellular toxicity. We report the case of a patient who suffered such a complication, and recovered with close monitoring of liver function in an intensive care setting. Further studies will elucidate the risk factors for and mechanisms of post-thrombectomy acute hepatitis, and providers should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients who have recently undergone PMT.Keywords: Acute hepatitis, Thrombectomy, Peripheral arterial disease, Arterial occlusive disease, Haemolysis

Percutaneous pharmacomechanical thrombectomy (PMT) has been used increasingly in both arterial and venous thrombotic diseases. The Angiojet Peripheral Thrombectomy System (Boston Scientific) uses high-pressure (7000-12,000 PSI) saline jets, with or without thrombolytic agents, to break up thrombi in target vessels (termed rheolysis) and aspirates fragments via the Venturi effect to prevent distal embolism. Rheolytic devices are known to cause more hemolysis than those that work through aspiration alone.1

Severe derangement of liver function is rare after PMT; only two cases have been reported in contemporary literature and not after peripheral arterial revascularization. Here, a patient who developed this complication after peripheral arterial PMT is presented.

Case history

A 77-year-old lady with a history of hypertension and chronic kidney disease presented with progressive debilitating left leg intermittent claudication. Duplex ultrasound study showed total occlusion of the left superficial femoral artery (SFA) and above-knee popliteal arteries with chronic emboli. Thus, the lesion was angioplastied, and in view of recoil and arterial dissection, stented with the Supera Peripheral Stent System (Abbott Vascular), an interwoven nitinol stent (Fig 1, A). Postprocedure, the leg was warm with palpable pulses. She remained in hospital for a few days awaiting a cardiac consultation.

On postprocedure day 4, she experienced a sudden onset of pain and coldness in her left foot. Emergency duplex ultrasound study and subsequent intraoperative angiogram showed acute thrombosis of the stented SFA, with arterial dissection of the distal common femoral artery and proximal SFA. Initial attempts to pass a Fogarty embolectomy balloon failed with persistent passage into the false lumen, necessitating groin exploration with patch angioplasty. This was followed by balloon angioplasty of the involved segment with further stenting to cover the dissection via an “up-and-over” approach from the contralateral groin. Twenty-three milligram recombinant tissue plasminogen activator was delivered intra-arterially, and six to eight thrombectomy passes to the mid-to-distal SFA, and anterior tibial artery was performed with the Angiojet system. The procedure was uneventful, and the leg was revascularized (Fig 1, B). Total thrombectomy time was 240 seconds with blood loss of 500 mL. The patient was hemodynamically stable throughout.

However, she developed malaise over the next 2 days with a drop of hemoglobin from 11.0 g/dL preoperatively down to 6.5 g/dL, and 2 units of packed red cells were transfused. Lactate dehydrogenase rose to 2478 U/L. Serum alanine transaminase and aspartate transaminase rose to 80-fold the upper reference range on day 3 (Fig 2). Serum bilirubin remained normal with a moderate elevation in alkaline phosphatase. Serum creatinine rose from 79 μmol/L (estimated glomerular filtration rate, 62 mL/min/1.73 m2) preoperatively to 236 μmol/L (estimated glomerular filtration rate, 16 mL/min/1.73 m2) on day 2, returning to baseline 10 days later. She did not develop hyperkalemia or uremia, and did not require renal replacement therapy (Table I).

Hepatobiliary specialists were consulted, and contrast computed tomography showed no radiologic abnormality accounting for the deranged liver function. Hepatitis serologies were negative. The impression of possible ischemic hepatitis. She was monitored closely in the intensive care setting, and her liver function improved over the next 5 to 10 days.

The patient was discharged on day 14 from the index operation. She was well on follow-up, with a well-perfused leg and normal liver enzymes. This patient gave written consent for her case to be published.

Discussion

PMT is increasingly used in vascular surgery for arterial and venous thrombotic disease. Hemoglobinuria and its relationship with acute kidney injury are well documented.2,3 Our patient had acute kidney injury in addition to severe liver dysfunction after lower limb revascularization. However, reports of acute hepatitis post-PMT are rare; only two exist, both after treatment of thrombosed arteriovenous grafts4,5 (Table II). Pre-existing chronic kidney disease and hemolysis-related acute kidney injury may worsen liver dysfunction due to impaired clearance of free hem and other byproducts from blood.

Fortunately, post-thrombectomy, hemolysis-induced acute hepatitis appears self-limiting, improving with expectant management. It is advisable to serially monitor liver enzymes, minimize additional liver insults, rule out alternate causes, and manage concomitant organ dysfunction such as acute kidney injury or pancreatitis. Lastly, caution should be taken, in the course of working up the liver dysfunction, to avoid contrast-induced acute kidney injury, including adequate hydration and close monitoring of renal function.

Treatment of bilateral disease and aggressive single session treatment are documented risk factors for severe hemolysis and consequent end-organ dysfunction due to the high thrombus load.6 In our patient, the entire left SFA, popliteal artery and anterior tibial artery were thrombectomised.6 To ensure clearance of thrombus, we operated the Angiojet for 240 seconds, the longest recommended thrombectomy time according to manufacturer instructions for use (IFU). Remaining within the IFU thrombectomy time (ie, 240s with flow and 480s without flow) and avoiding overzealous single-session treatment of higher thrombus loads is advised to reduce the excessive hemolysis that precipitates liver injury. Operating the thrombectomy catheter in short bursts, while limiting passes in already-treated segments, may also reduce unnecessary hemolysis.

The mechanism of hepatic injury is related to reactive oxygen species and free radicals created by the ferric ion; these upregulate and sensitize cells to proinflammatory cytokines (such as tumor necrosis factor α, platelet activating factor, and adhesion molecules) and neutrophils, causing hepatocellular injury.7 This proinflammatory effect is observed in other diseases characterized by overwhelming intravascular hemolysis such as malaria. In malaria mouse models, the level of free hem correlated with the activation of common transcription factor nuclear factor kappa-light-chain enhancer of activated B cells, promoting neutrophil chemotaxis and infiltration into liver sinusoids. In the same model, neutrophil depletion prevented apoptosis of hepatocytes and reduced serum liver enzymes, demonstrating the relationship between free heme and hepatocellular toxicity.8 Free hemoglobin may promote intravascular coagulation and vasoconstriction by inhibiting endothelium-derived relaxing factor, seen in studies on post-PMT pancreatitis. This same phenomenon may explain ischemic liver injury after PMT.9 Moreover, reactive oxygen species cause oxidative damage to lipid membranes and intracellular proteins, perturbing cellular function in hepatocytes.

Specific proteins protect cells against free hem-related cytotoxicity, such as hemopexin (HPX). HPX is produced in the liver and binds hem for catabolism. In overwhelming hemolysis, the catabolic capacity of HPX is exceeded, allowing unchecked entry of cytotoxic hem into hepatocytes.7 Another protein, hem oxygenase-1, breaks down free hem into the ferrous iron, carbon monoxide and bilirubin. Although hem oxygenase-1 is inducible by oxidative stress, excess free hem can still overwhelm this system.10 Lastly, free heme induces α-1-microglobulin in the liver, which scavenges radicals and mitigates endothelial damage. That these protective mechanisms become unable to protect cells against the toxicity of large quantities of hem highlights the importance of minimizing thrombus load in thrombectomy.

Conclusions

Derangement of liver function is exceedingly rare after endovascular PMT. This potentially serious complication after lower limb arterial revascularization emphasizes the importance of close monitoring and multidisciplinary management involving liver specialists. Minimizing intraprocedural hemolysis, by avoiding single-session treatment of high thrombus loads and remaining within the IFU for thrombectomy duration, helps to minimize incidence of post-PMT liver dysfunction. Future research will elucidate the exact mechanisms and risk factors for post-thrombectomy acute hepatitis, but awareness, early diagnosis and management remain paramount.
