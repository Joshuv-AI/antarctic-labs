Acute Hyponatremia as a Telling Complication of Necrotizing Pancreatitis and Perforated Cholecystitis After Percutaneous Cholecystostomy
Authors: Jasmine Lee, Alan Kubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute hyponatremia is an uncommon complication of percutaneous cholecystostomy drainage with minimal literature to date. We present a case of a patient with necrotizing pancreatitis causing perforated cholecystitis, requiring percutaneous cholecystostomy that was complicated by acute hyponatremia. High biliary solute and volume loss led to the hyponatremia and elucidated downstream biliary obstruction. Fluid resuscitation, biliary stenting, and endoscopic drainage corrected the patient's sodium and symptoms in this multidisciplinary case.

INTRODUCTION

Percutaneous cholecystostomy (PC) is increasingly used to treat cholecystitis in high-risk surgical patients.1 Well-known complications are infection, hemorrhage, biliary leakage, bowel perforation, peritonitis, and dislodged catheter.2 There is minimal literature on the risk of electrolyte disturbances after catheter placement. We present a surprising case of acute symptomatic hyponatremia from high biliary output. Hyponatremia should be considered in patients with high volume drainage and may be a sign of obstruction, warranting further evaluation and potential endoscopic intervention.

CASE REPORT

A 41-year-old man with alcohol use disorder (1 pint of liquor daily) and remote perforated diverticulitis presented with epigastric pain and vomiting. Laboratory test results showed lactate 6.2 mmol/L, white blood count 10.4 × 103/uL, lipase 501 U/L, total bilirubin 0.7 mg/dL, and triglyceride level 7,056 mg/dL. Computed tomography (CT) with contrast revealed hepatic steatosis and necrotizing pancreatitis with a small ill-defined fluid collection (Figure 1). He was treated for shock in the intensive care unit and transferred to the floor on day 5. Hypertriglyceridemia improved with insulin infusion, omega-3-acid ethyl ester, and fenofibrate.

Necrotizing pancreatitis on admission.

On day 7, he developed intermittent low-grade fevers with transaminitis (aspartate aminotransferase 160 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 113 U/L, and alkaline phosphatase 290 U/L) with total bilirubin 9.6 mg/dL. The admission specimen to assess liver function was hemolyzed, likely from lipemia. Infectious workup was negative. Repeat CT showed improvement in pancreatitis with no biliary dilatation, as did an abdominal ultrasound. Laboratory values were attributed to pancreatitis, hepatic steatosis, and possible drug-induced liver injury from fenofibrate. The Infectious Diseases service was consulted. Laboratory values and symptoms improved, and patient wished to discharge home. On day 13, a surveillance CT was obtained early to assess the evolution of necrosis in the setting of mild epigastric pain and to ease outpatient logistics. Remarkably, it showed a perforated gallbladder and common bile duct (CBD) obstruction from pancreatic head collections (Figure 2).

Repeat computed tomography with distended gallbladder and pericholecystic fluid collection (arrow) suggesting perforation and distended bile ducts adjacent to extensive pancreatic necrosis and fluid.

Patient was started on intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole. Surgery recommended no acute operation. Interventional Radiology placed a PC catheter with 2 L of drainage in the first 24 hours and 11 L over 4 days. Two days in, the patient developed dizziness, tinnitus, and drowsiness. He felt dehydrated. Laboratory test results showed an acute kidney injury (creatinine 1.97 mg/dL from baseline 1.00 mg/dL) and Na 118 mEq/L (Figure 3, Table 1) with measured serum osmolality 286 mOsm/kg, calculated serum osmolality 258 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 255 mOsm/kg, and urine Na <20 mEq/L. Given the discordance between measured and calculated serum osmolality, we suspect the measured value may have been confounded by a delay in stored specimen processing and/or unmeasured osmoles, e.g., iohexol contrast. Initial workup of hyponatremia was unrevealing with unremarkable protein (8.4 g/dL), glucose (150 mg/dL), urea (37 mg/dL), and triglyceride (274 mg/dL) levels making pseudohyponatremia unlikely. There were also no culprit medications. We sampled our patient's drainage sodium level, 126 mEq/L, and gave fluids. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion was ultimately excluded based on the patient's hypovolemic examination, low urine sodium, and improvement in serum sodium after fluids. Bilirubin had decreased to 1.7 mg/dL, but the CT on day 13 had shown new biliary dilatation and distal CBD compression which, given the patient's high drain output over days, raised concern for complete obstruction requiring endoscopic intervention.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) confirmed a distal CBD stricture from adjacent pancreatic fluid. A sphincterotomy and fully covered self-expanding metal stent were executed. In addition, the peripancreatic fluid collection was endoscopically drained via placement of a lumen-opposing metal stent between the stomach and walled-off necrosis. Output waned, and sodium normalized. After 7 days of antibiotics, he was discharged home on day 22.

DISCUSSION

In patients with cholecystitis, cholangitis, or biliary obstruction who are not surgical candidates, a PC catheter can be placed percutaneously into the gallbladder lumen under imaging guidance for decompression. Prevalence of PC has increased 10-fold from 1994 to 2004 to 3% of all gallbladder procedures performed.1 Complications of PC typically occur in the first few days and include tube malfunction, infection, and hemorrhage.3 Hyponatremia (defined as serum sodium level <135 mEq/L) is considered a minor complication but can be clinically significant.3 Symptoms of hyponatremia usually occur in the acute setting (<48 hours) due to the brain's adaptation to hypotonicity-induced cerebral edema over time. Symptoms range widely from life-threatening seizure and cardiac arrest to most commonly vomiting, weakness, and cognitive impairment in mild chronic hyponatremia.4

A systematic review found bile to have the highest sodium concentration (185 ± 24 mEq/L) of all body fluids including plasma, pancreatic fluid, and diarrhea.5 Bile is composed mainly of water and bile acids, which are made in the liver from cholesterol and are crucial to fat absorption and general metabolism. About 95% of intestinal bile acids are recycled multiple times daily via the enterohepatic circulation, being primarily reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and returning to the liver through the portal vein.6 Average basal flow rate of bile in humans is 620 mL per day.7 Our patient drained 3–4 times this amount. Furthermore, while plasma sodium is regulated by antidiuretic hormone secretion and renal excretion to maintain osmotic balance, bile sodium is not independently regulated. As conjugated bile acids are actively secreted into the bile canaliculi, sodium enters to maintain electrical neutrality and in turn provides the driving force for bile salt reuptake via sodium-dependent membrane transporters.8

We found a handful of cases of chronic hyponatremia after biliary drainage: a patient in Turkey with an obstructive, inoperable Klatskin tumor status post external biliary drain who presented 3 months later with emesis, lethargy, and Na 108 mEq/L that recovered with salt supplementation9; one with cirrhosis and acalculous cholecystitis requiring PC who presented 2 months later with lethargy and Na 116 mEq/L that recovered after catheter removal10; and one with advanced cirrhosis and biliary tree mass requiring PC who presented 1 week later with jaundice, incidentally found to have Na 122 mEq/L that resolved after drain capping.11 In one cohort study, 22% of 208 patients developed hyponatremia after choledochotomy and T tube drainage.12 To our knowledge, this is the first report of acute, symptomatic hyponatremia after high-output PC due to CBD obstruction that reversed with ERCP and stenting.

Our patient demonstrated hypovolemic hyponatremia. The patient's hypovolemic examination and low urine sodium (<20 mEq/L) suggested low effective arterial blood volume from extrarenal causes. His volume loss may have been multifactorial from vomiting and pancreatitis-associated systemic inflammation, third spacing, and insensible losses, which are exacerbated by necrosis.13 However, he ultimately had excessive drainage due to a CBD stricture. The most common area of blockage in cholecystitis is the cystic duct, typically causing low drainage due to its isolation from the rest of the biliary system. CBD involvement promoted downstream pressure and potentially biliary pancreatitis creating a cycle of inflammation and uncontrolled infection.14 Fluid repletion corrected the hyponatremia, but stents placed in the CBD and between the stomach and walled-off necrosis were necessary for stabilization and source control.

Postprocedural care of a biliary drainage catheter includes monitoring for sepsis, abnormal laboratory values, and catheter issues.15 A PC catheter is usually maintained for 2–6 weeks based on clinical improvement and follow-up imaging.16 In cases of high volume or purulent drainage, workup includes ultrasound, CT, and/or ERCP. Our case highlights the importance of trending electrolytes after biliary drain placement and investigating for obstruction when there is extraordinary output.
