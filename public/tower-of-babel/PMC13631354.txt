When silence is safer: a review and decision-theoretic framework for LLM abstention in healthcare
Authors: Oriana Presacan, Alireza Nik, Jaya Ojha, Vajira Thambawita, Bogdan Ionescu, Michael A. Riegler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) are designed to generate answers to user prompts, which often drives them to respond even when uncertainty is high, information is incomplete, or a refusal would be more appropriate. In healthcare, this tendency can be dangerous: confidently stated but inaccurate medical advice can cause significant harm, making the ability to abstain especially important. In this paper, we review studies investigating LLM abstention behaviors in healthcare. The literature highlights two main motivations: (1) uncertainty-driven abstention, where the model withholds a response when confidence is low, and (2) safety-driven abstention, where the model declines to provide potentially harmful information. Most existing mechanisms are extrinsic and rely on auxiliary tools to determine when to abstain. We find that state-of-the-art LLMs still struggle to refuse inappropriate prompts, and that few benchmarks evaluate abstention in realistic medical scenarios, where performance lags behind other domains. Building on these findings, we introduce a decision-theoretic formalization of abstention that models the trade-off between answering and withholding responses under uncertainty and potential harm. Based on this formulation, we present MedSAFE, a framework for evaluating abstention in clinical dialogs, and demonstrate its operationalization through a proof-of-concept pilot across clinical scenarios derived from the review.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in healthcare by both patients and clinicians, supporting tasks such as clinical summarization, patient dialog, and diagnostic reasoning1–3, and in some cases outperforming human experts4–6. Despite these advances, LLMs exhibit a fundamental behavioral limitation: they often produce answers even when they should not7. This problem stems from multiple underlying causes. First, LLMs lack reliable mechanisms for recognizing and acting on uncertainty. Their inability to know what they do not know often leads to overconfident but incorrect responses, a behavior described as hallucination8. Researchers argue that hallucinations are inevitable9 and link them to training and evaluation practices that reward speculation instead of admitting uncertainty10. Models are not trained to respond with “I do not know” or to express uncertainty unless explicitly prompted, and prompting alone does not overcome their intrinsic inability to recognize uncertainty11. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can mitigate hallucinations, but a full reduction is not possible at the moment10. Second, beyond uncertainty, LLMs must also navigate safety, ethical, and policy constraints. Even when a model possesses the relevant knowledge, it may need to refuse to respond if doing so could cause harm, violate privacy, or exceed its professional scope.

Uncertainty and safety are general limitations of current AI systems, however, their consequences are especially severe in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, where incorrect or unsafe outputs can directly jeopardize patient well-being. In this context, LLMs may use professional-sounding clinical language while offering incorrect advice or treatment suggestions12. Recent studies show that models such as GPT-4 can fabricate clinical details13, recommend unsafe treatments such as contraindicated drugs for pregnant patients14, or even produce biased and inconsistent treatment recommendations in psychiatric contexts when patient race is indicated, raising serious ethical concerns15. Furthermore, LLMs are also vulnerable to adversarial misuse. Users can employ AI-jailbreaking techniques to bypass safety mechanisms and elicit harmful or inappropriate outputs16. For instance, models can be manipulated into providing instructions for drug misuse or generating fabricated medical certificates even when they were explicitly trained to refuse such requests17,18. While these issues have been explored in the context of general AI safety, they received far less attention in medical settings19,20.

Addressing these gaps requires models that can withhold responses when appropriate. They may refuse to answer by indicating a lack of knowledge (e.g., “I do not know”) or by explicitly declining to respond (e.g., “I cannot answer that”). They may also abstain from providing a final answer by requesting clarification or by deferring to qualified human expertise (e.g., “Please consult a doctor”). As illustrated in Fig. 1, when a patient asks which pill to take, the model should either seek further information or refrain from answering entirely, since the correct decision depends on individual factors such as age and medical history. However, while knowing how to abstain is relatively straightforward, determining when to do so remains far more challenging. The idea that AI systems should be able to abstain from answering is not new and originates in the broader machine learning literature. Early work on classification with a reject option and selective prediction introduced the principle that models should refrain from producing outputs when confidence is low, formalized through the risk-coverage trade-off between prediction accuracy and the fraction of inputs answered21,22. More recently, abstention has re-emerged in the context of LLMs, where AI safety and alignment research has explored refusal mechanisms to prevent harmful or inappropriate outputs; a comprehensive survey of these developments is provided by Wen et al.23.

Healthcare abstention, however, raises questions that are closely related to, but distinct from, general-purpose LLM refusal. In clinical settings, both answering and not answering can have consequences: incorrect answers may create safety risks, while unnecessary abstention may delay care, withhold useful guidance, or increase patient anxiety. Uncertainty is also often contextual, since safe responses may depend on missing symptoms, history, medications, test results, or examination findings. As a result, appropriate behavior may involve clarification-seeking, triage guidance, or clinical deferral, rather than a binary choice between answering and refusing. This review complements existing work by examining how abstention mechanisms manifest, are implemented, and evaluated specifically in healthcare settings, where clinical risk, ethical constraints, and domain-specific safety norms introduce challenges that are not fully captured by general-purpose frameworks.

Although recent work has begun to examine LLM refusals as a strategy for reducing hallucinations and improving model safety in healthcare19,24, research in this area remains limited. Most studies still evaluate how well models answer questions rather than how effectively they abstain. A recent abstention benchmark11 spanning 17 datasets found that, aside from unanswerable questions, frontier LLMs perform poorly in most abstention settings, with the weakest results in medical domains. Similarly, another study25 observed that models rarely refuse to answer when they should, with the lowest refusal rates occurring in medical tasks. These findings highlight the need for a more healthcare-specific account of when LLMs should answer, ask for more information, defer, or abstain.

To address these gaps, we review existing work on LLM abstention within healthcare applications. To our knowledge, no prior study has systematically examined these behaviors within medical contexts. We synthesize the underlying motivations, current methods, and evaluation benchmarks. Building on these findings, we develop a decision-theoretic formalization of abstention and introduce MedSAFE, a framework for operationalizing and evaluating abstention behavior in clinical dialog settings. Ultimately, ensuring that LLMs know when not to answer is as essential to patient safety as improving the accuracy of the answers they provide.

Results

This section reviews the literature on LLM abstention in healthcare, organized around four research questions: (RQ1) what abstention entails and the motivations behind it (why the model does not answer), (RQ2) how non-response behaviors manifest in current LLMs, (RQ3) which technical methods are used to implement abstention mechanisms (how the model does not answer), and (RQ4) what benchmarks are used to evaluate these behaviors. The section concludes with a conceptual framework for assessing whether models appropriately withhold responses in the presence of uncertainty or safety risks.

Motivations for LLM abstention in healthcare

The term abstention in the context of LLMs is relatively recent and not yet consistently defined in the general AI literature. Different studies interpret the term in varying ways. Madhusudhan et al.7 suggest that LLMs should abstain whenever uncertainty is high, whereas Feng et al.26 argue that abstention is necessary when questions are inherently unanswerable; for example, when required information does not exist. Wen et al.23 broaden this definition by incorporating alignment with human values, including ethical, privacy, and safety considerations. In contrast, Kirichenko et al.11 frame abstention strictly as reasoning under uncertainty, explicitly excluding safety-related cases.

In this paper, we use abstention as an umbrella term encompassing all deliberate non-response behaviors by LLMs. However, we clearly differentiate between two distinct motivations for such behavior: uncertainty-driven abstention, rooted in epistemic uncertainty, and safety-driven abstention, arising from compliance constraints. Although both result in a withheld answer, they reflect fundamentally different reasons for non-response.

Uncertainty-driven abstention occurs when the model lacks sufficient knowledge or confidence to provide a reliable answer. This behavior is epistemic in nature: the model recognizes uncertainty in its internal representations or the absence of relevant information in its training data. Safety- and ethics-driven abstention, in contrast, arises when the model possesses relevant knowledge but determines that responding could cause harm, violate ethical or legal norms, or disclose sensitive information. These refusals are normative rather than epistemic, motivated by the need to prevent unsafe or unethical behavior. Throughout the paper, we use the terms abstention and refusal interchangeably to refer to all forms of deliberate non-response, including direct non-answers, deferrals, and clarification questions (see Fig. 1).

In machine learning, uncertainty refers to a model’s limited ability to determine a correct answer when knowledge or information is incomplete27. This uncertainty is often categorized into two types: epistemic uncertainty, which stems from incomplete knowledge or model capacity and can in principle be reduced by acquiring more or better data; and aleatoric uncertainty, which arises from inherent randomness or variability in the data-generation process (for example, when predicting patient outcomes that naturally vary between individuals despite similar data) and so cannot be entirely eliminated by more data28.

Across the reviewed studies, we identified six common manifestations of uncertainty (see Fig. 3): low-confidence predictions, incomplete or underspecified user queries, false or ambiguous questions, inherently unanswerable prompts, out-of-distribution, and out-of-domain inputs. Most of these reflect epistemic uncertainty that arises from limited knowledge or representation within the model. In contrast, inherently unanswerable prompts correspond to aleatoric uncertainty, as no definitive answer exists.

For example, in ref. 29, the authors investigate the ability of LLMs to identify incomplete information in a user query and ask follow-up questions, showing that handling such cases remains a challenging and non-trivial task. Out-of-distribution (OOD) cases, where the model encounters unfamiliar or atypical data, are addressed by Li et al.30, who use the Mahalanobis distance to quantify how far a given chest X-ray deviates from the training distribution. A large distance indicates that the input lies outside the model’s learned distribution and that the model should abstain from making a prediction. Study31 estimates model certainty using a hidden-state signal derived from the final layer of the LLM. When this signal falls below a predefined threshold, the model’s prediction is treated as low-confidence and deferred to human experts, whereas high-confidence cases are handled autonomously by the LLM. In32, the authors explore how input manipulation affects answerability by adding irrelevant context or removing key information from prompts to make questions unanswerable, providing a controlled setting to study abstention behavior. Finally, unanswerable questions are addressed by Jerrin et al.33, who examine text-to-SQL systems that determine whether a question is answerable given the database schema and abstain when sufficient schema coverage is lacking.

Safety-driven abstention is motivated by the need to ensure ethical integrity and prevent harm. It occurs when an LLM recognizes that generating or sharing certain information could breach ethical, legal, or professional boundaries. In healthcare, this includes protecting patient privacy, avoiding unverified or potentially harmful medical advice, and maintaining professional responsibility in line with established medical and regulatory standards.

In the reviewed studies, safety has been examined from multiple perspectives. For example, Han et al.19 define safety in accordance with the American Medical Association’s (AMA) Principles of Medical Ethics, such as beneficence (acting in the patient’s best interest), nonmaleficence (avoiding harm), respect for autonomy, and justice. Similarly20, integrates the AMA principles, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) privacy standards, and prior LLM safety frameworks to derive eight clinical safety principles addressing issues such as privacy, bias, misinformation, self-harm, and professional conduct. In34, the authors examine unsafe model behavior through unqualified medical advice, such as drug recommendations. Beyond clinical safety35, targets broader categories of harmful queries, such as violence, self-harm, discrimination, or jailbreak attempts. Finally36, explores ethically sensitive scenarios in nephrology, evaluating how models handle questions that could involve bias or discrimination in medical decision-making.

Observed LLM abstention behavior

Understanding how LLMs behave in situations where they should withhold an answer provides essential context for developing effective refusal strategies. Empirical evaluations in the medical domain consistently show that most models generate confident answers even when information is incomplete, ethically sensitive, or uncertain. These findings underscore the limitations of current systems in recognizing when not to respond. Study25 found that GPT-4 appropriately abstained in only 10% of unanswerable medical queries. Similarly36, examined ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 in ethically sensitive scenarios, finding that the former never refused to respond, whereas the latter declined only 16.3% of cases. Likewise, Kaur et al.37 assessed LLMs on health-related claim verification tasks and observed that these seldom refrained when confronted with false or misleading medical statements, often agreeing with the user instead. Extending this analysis to the multimodal domain, Qin et al.38 evaluated multiple vision LLMs on an ophthalmology benchmark and identified six major failure modes, one being failure to abstain, which accounted for 18% of glaucoma diagnosis errors.

Technical approaches to implementing refusals

To address the inconsistent abstention behavior observed in existing models, recent research has explored a range of technical approaches to improve or enable refusal capabilities. These approaches differ in where and how the abstention decision is made, either within the model’s own reasoning process or through external mechanisms or components. We categorize these techniques as intrinsic or extrinsic. Intrinsic methods allow the LLM to decide to refuse as part of its internal generation process, while extrinsic methods rely on an external module or information to determine when refusal should occur, without altering the LLM itself. Among the reviewed studies, extrinsic strategies were the more common approach.

Fine-tuning (indicated as (1) in Fig. 4) is the only intrinsic method identified in the studies we reviewed. It involves adapting a pre-trained model to a domain-specific dataset to refine its behavior and improve task performance. In this context, fine-tuning was primarily used for alignment purposes. Models were trained on curated examples of appropriate, non-harmful responses to encourage safety-motivated refusals. This approach is commonly implemented through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) in alignment research39. Notably, none of the reviewed studies incorporated refusal behavior during the initial pretraining phase of the model.

Several studies demonstrate how fine-tuning can strengthen or, in some cases, weaken safety alignment. For example, in19, medical LLMs were fine-tuned on pairs of harmful questions and safe responses, effectively reducing their willingness to follow unsafe instructions. However, Lyu et al.40 found that when an already safety-aligned model is further fine-tuned using templates with explicit safety instructions, its safety can actually deteriorate, possibly due to overfitting to the initial templates. They recommend a Pure Tuning, Safe Testing strategy, namely fine-tuning without the safety prompt and only adding safety instructions at inference time to maintain alignment. Conversely41, examine refusals from a malicious perspective, showing through their Subversive Alignment Injection method that even minimal poisoning of fine-tuning data (1%) can cause targeted refusals towards specific topics. This reveals that abstention behaviors may reflect malicious manipulation rather than actual safety.

Extrinsic strategies comprise prompt engineering, which modifies the input to encourage the model to express or act on uncertainty; consistency-based sampling, applied after generation to assess the reliability of outputs; and the use of auxiliary models, which can be integrated either before the LLM (for input filtering) or after it (for post-hoc confidence evaluation). Some studies also combine two or more of these techniques.

Prompt engineering is the most straightforward approach, as it only requires modifying the input query. For instance, the model can be explicitly instructed to abstain when uncertain (indicated as (2) in Fig. 4), allowing the LLM to decide whether to provide an answer or withhold based on its self-assessed confidence. Study11 evaluated this approach across multiple LLMs, showing that prompting models with abstention scenarios and instructions improves abstention accuracy in both reasoning and standard LLMs. Avnat et al.42 investigated the impact of modifying prompts to explicitly include an “I don’t know” response option in GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus. Although this adjustment increased the frequency of abstentions, the authors observed no significant correlation between abstention rate and accuracy, suggesting that abstention prompting alone does not necessarily enhance model performance. Conversely, Wen et al.32 examined prompt modifications that removed, replaced, or augmented contextual information to control question answerability. Their findings indicate that substituting or appending irrelevant context to the gold context can improve abstention behavior while simultaneously enhancing task performance.

Another prompt-engineering approach involves asking LLMs to verbalize their confidence (indicated as (3) in Fig. 4). After generating a response, a threshold is applied to the reported confidence to decide whether the model should abstain. In29, the authors tested several verbalized confidence-based abstention strategies, including numerical (confidence score between 0 and 1), binary (yes/no confidence), and scale variants (five-point confidence scale), along with extensions using rationale generation (RG) and self-consistency (SC) sampling. Their results show that RG yields the largest individual performance gains, while combining the scale-based method with RG and SC achieves the best overall results. Moreover, Strong et al.31 combine verbalized confidence with a hidden-state estimate from the final layer in a deferral framework, where an LLM classifies spinal MRI reports for abnormal findings, deferring uncertain cases to clinicians. They report that this combination improves both classification accuracy and deferral performance compared to using either confidence signal alone.

Consistency sampling (indicated as (4) in Fig. 4) is another common extrinsic method. The approach generates multiple responses from an LLM at high temperature to encourage diversity and evaluates their similarity. High similarity indicates model confidence, whereas low similarity triggers abstention based on a predefined threshold. For instance, Farquhar et al.43 sampled ten outputs per query, clustered them by semantic similarity, and computed entropy over these clusters to detect inconsistencies. High semantic entropy indicated potential confabulation, prompting abstention. Likewise, Wang et al.44 generated a deterministic reference and multiple temperature-sampled reports from a vision LLM for chest X-rays, measuring report- and sentence-level uncertainty to improve factuality. Their framework achieved a 10% increase in factuality by rejecting 20% of high-uncertainty reports using the RaDialog model. In45, consistency sampling was evaluated alongside verbalized confidence. While consistency sampling achieved better results, the overall gains were modest, and the model remained overconfident. Moreover, Li et al.29 found that consistency sampling alone degrades performance unless it is combined with other methods.

Moving to methods that employ auxiliary models, some studies use lightweight models to filter inputs before passing them to the main LLM (indicated as (5) in Fig. 4). For instance46, employs a small LLM prompted to reject out-of-domain queries prior to main model inference. Likewise20, fine-tunes Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct to detect jailbreaking attempts, serving as a classifier that filters inputs before they reach the primary LLM. Shrivastava et al.47 enhance confidence estimation by combining the main LLM’s verbalized confidence with probability outputs from a smaller surrogate LLM. Feng et al.26 introduce a multi-LLM collaboration framework where models either cooperate or compete to detect uncertainty, triggering abstention when instability arises and achieved a 19.3% improvement in abstention accuracy compared to the baseline.

Some studies employ non-LLM auxiliary models (indicated as (5) in Fig. 4). For example, Shankar et al.48 propose an energy-based model to classify queries as in- or out-of-domain in obstetrics and gynecology. In49, the authors address arrhythmia and sleep stage classification using an AI system that estimates uncertainty and interpretability, routing confident cases to a multimodal LLM that interprets the data and uncertain ones to human experts. Finally, Song et al.50 apply a post-processing step that evaluates the model’s confidence after it generates an answer (indicated as (7) in Fig. 4). The system analyses the model’s stated reasoning and confidence, uses a secondary model to assess reliability, and produces a final score that determines whether the system should provide an answer or abstain.

Beyond these main categories, some studies explore alternative strategies. Wang et al.35 propose directly adjusting a model’s internal representations to influence its behavior, without requiring additional training (see (6) in Fig. 4). These adjustments are derived by comparing how a smaller, safety-focused model responds to harmful versus harmless prompts. During inference, this guidance encourages safer outputs, reducing attack success while maintaining task accuracy. Similarly, Berkowitz et al.51 analyzed the hidden activations of safety-tuned models that refuse to provide medical advice. They found that while safety alignment reduces accuracy on medical QA benchmarks, much of the underlying knowledge remains intact. By analyzing the model’s internal signals with a lightweight classifier, they recover most of the performance of an unaligned model, suggesting that alignment restricts the model’s willingness to respond rather than erasing its medical knowledge. Finally, study52 employs an external knowledge base of verified information to guide model responses. The system is designed to answer only questions that can be supported by this trusted source and to refuse others. However, maintaining and validating such a knowledge base requires continuous effort and oversight.

MedSafetyBench19 includes 1800 unsafe medical prompts designed to violate the AMA principles, all of which models are expected to refuse. Furthermore, SORRY-Bench34 includes 9240 unsafe prompts across 40+ safety categories, such as hate speech, crime, and medical harm, created via linguistic and formatting augmentations, with around 7000 model responses human-labeled for refusals.

In contrast to safety-related abstention, MediQ29 focuses on uncertainty-driven abstention. The authors argue that, unlike static benchmarks that assume complete patient information, MediQ simulates more realistic clinical settings by presenting incomplete data and evaluating whether models can identify missing information and request it before answering. Built by converting MEDQA54 and CRAFT-MD55 into interactive formats, MediQ reveals only age, gender, and chief complaint at the start, and models choose whether to respond or ask follow-up questions. Performance is measured by interaction efficiency (number of questions asked) and final diagnostic accuracy. AbstentionBench11 broadens abstention evaluation to 20 datasets across domains. Its medical subset is derived from MediQ and includes 3500 instances with explicit abstention labels. Abstention performance is evaluated using recall, precision, and F1-score.

Focusing on out-of-scope and unanswerable clinical queries, ASTRID56 presents an automated framework for evaluating retrieval-augmented clinical QA systems. It measures model performance using three metrics: context relevance, conversational faithfulness, and refusal accuracy. The dataset includes 238 real question-answer pairs from post-operative cataract surgery interactions, supplemented with additional out-of-scope questions curated by ophthalmic experts to assess refusal behavior.

Discussion

Our synthesis revealed that LLMs in healthcare exhibit a persistent weakness in recognizing when to withhold an answer. Even advanced systems tend to generate confident responses despite uncertainty, incomplete information, or ethical constraints. In clinical contexts, this behavior is not a peripheral flaw but a central reliability problem. Appropriate abstention, knowing when not to respond, is important for trustworthy decision support. In this section, we interpret abstention as a problem of calibrated decision-making, linking the conceptual findings of our review with the decision-theoretic formulation and the MedSAFE evaluation framework.

Across the reviewed studies, two primary motivations for abstention were evident. The first is epistemic uncertainty, in which the model recognizes a lack of reliable knowledge. The second is normative constraint, where answering could cause harm or violate ethical, legal, or professional boundaries. Behaviorally, abstention can take the form of an explicit refusal, a clear non-answer, or a mitigated engagement, such as requesting clarification or deferring to human expertise. These dimensions of motivation and response mode yield four archetypal behaviors summarized in Table 3. This typology captures abstention not as a binary outcome but as a calibrated behavior balancing epistemic and ethical risk. To connect this conceptual structure with measurable performance, we formalize abstention within a standard decision-theoretic framework.

The decision-theoretic formulation introduced earlier reframes abstention as a calibration problem. At each interaction, a model implicitly weighs the expected benefit of answering against the risks of misinformation, unsafe guidance, or overstepping professional boundaries. In this view, abstention is not merely a refusal mechanism but a decision policy balancing epistemic uncertainty, potential harm, and the cost of withholding information. This perspective provides a unified lens through which both uncertainty-driven and safety-driven abstention can be evaluated.

MedSAFE operationalizes this perspective in an interactive dialog setting. The scenario design distinguishes between benign queries and risky or uncertain situations, while rubric-based scoring captures whether abstention behaviors are appropriate, excessive, or insufficient. This structure allows both uncertainty calibration and safety discrimination to be evaluated within a single conversational framework.

The empirical record reveals two opposing pathologies: under-refusal and over-refusal. Both reflect a miscalibration in the model’s internal decision threshold. Effective abstention therefore requires balancing safety and utility: models must avoid unsafe responses while maintaining sufficient coverage of low-risk interactions. In clinical dialog, this balance is inherently context-dependent. Dialog history, patient intent, and the availability of human oversight may all influence whether answering or abstaining is the safer choice. Reporting R-C and AOC curves stratified by scenario type makes these trade-offs explicit and enables reproducible evaluation. The pilot evaluation (Table 5) illustrates both pathologies in practice: selective risk fell well below the no-abstention baseline (AURC = 0.093 vs. 0.163), and the AUAOC of 0.929 confirms reliable discrimination between risky and benign inputs across the full threshold range.

Advancing abstention research requires unified models that integrate epistemic and normative signals into joint abstention predictors and domain-specific, multi-turn training data capturing realistic ambiguity. Empirical studies should examine how clinicians and patients interpret refusals and whether these behaviors affect trust or workflow. Further work should also assess disparities in refusal behavior across languages, specialities, and demographics to ensure equitable system performance. Together, these directions define a coherent research agenda for the study of abstention in medical AI. Rubric design should further distinguish between explicit refusal and normative mitigated engagement: in our pilot evaluation, the model produced appropriate non-directive guidance on an ethical disclosure scenario (justification quality: 0.90; empathic alignment: 0.85) yet the binary appropriate-abstention dimension scored it as an unanswered query, obscuring a clinically correct behavior. Future evaluation instruments should score these response modes separately, in accordance with the epistemic, normative, and explicit, mitigated taxonomy presented in Table 3.

Finally, abstention should be recognized as a positive marker of competence rather than a limitation. In clinical reasoning, the capacity to acknowledge uncertainty and defer appropriately is integral to professionalism. Analogously, an AI system that can identify and communicate the limits of its reliability exhibits meta-cognitive alignment, a quality empirically observable in the pilot, where six risky scenarios elicited clinically reasoned abstentions (mean justification quality: 0.92) rather than reflexive refusals, including the adversarial and false-premise scenarios, which were correctly identified despite their indirect framing. Accuracy remains necessary but not sufficient: safety-critical systems must also optimize a cost-sensitive abstention policy that aligns epistemic self-assessment with ethical responsibility. The integration of conceptual, formal, and behavioral perspectives presented here establishes a foundation for that goal.

Methods

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic search across PubMed, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, and Semantic Scholar to identify relevant studies. In addition, we performed a manual search using Google Scholar and the Google search engine. The final search was conducted on September 23, 2025, and covered publications from the last five years (January 2021-September 2025).

Searches were performed in the title, abstract, and metadata fields using database-specific Boolean expressions that combined three concept groups: terms related to abstention and refusal behaviors (e.g., abstention, refusal, selective prediction, uncertainty estimation, model calibration, hallucination, factual consistency, truthfulness, misinformation detection); LLM-related terms (e.g., large language model, LLM, GPT, ChatGPT, generative AI); and healthcare-related terms (e.g., medical, clinical, digital health). Complete Boolean search strings for each database are provided in the Supplementary Material. We included both peer-reviewed and preprint papers. Because abstention and related behaviors have been studied under varied terminology, we used a broad keyword set to capture relevant studies even when the term abstention did not appear explicitly.

Screening and selection

All records retrieved from the database searches and manual searches were imported into the reference management and screening platform Rayyan and deduplicated. A total of 1,046 unique records were screened by title and abstract, of which 954 were excluded for irrelevance. The remaining 92 articles were assessed in full text, of which 28 met all inclusion criteria. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies that examined abstention within medical, clinical, or healthcare contexts and incorporated LLMs in their AI systems. We considered papers that analyzed observed LLM refusals, proposed abstention mechanisms, discussed ethical or safety rationales for abstention, or provided empirical benchmarks. We included all reported motivations for refusals, including uncertainty, safety, and out-of-domain considerations. Studies were excluded if they were unrelated to healthcare applications or focused solely on non-LLM models.

Data extraction and synthesis

For each eligible study, we extracted information on the publication year, medical domain, medical task, and how abstention or refusal behavior was elicited and implemented. We also noted whether the study applied an abstention technique or simply evaluated LLM refusals, along with the reasons for abstention.

Abstention motivations were coded using a codebook developed iteratively around two main categories: epistemic (uncertainty-driven) and normative (safety-, ethical-, legal-, or policy-driven). When reported, we also coded the model’s response mode as explicit refusal or mitigated engagement. Two reviewers collaborated on developing and refining the codebook by jointly coding an initial set of studies and resolving discrepancies through discussion. This process informed the taxonomy in Table 3, while recognizing that published descriptions of model behavior are often incomplete and may blur the line between epistemic and normative motivations.

Study quality and heterogeneity

The included studies exhibited substantial heterogeneity in design, model access, and evaluation realism. Many evaluations were conducted on synthetic or curated benchmarks rather than prospective, real-world clinical use, and only a minority reported preregistered protocols or error analyses beyond aggregate accuracy. We therefore did not attempt a formal quantitative risk-of-bias assessment. Instead, we qualitatively appraised each study along three dimensions: benchmark realism (e.g., synthetic vignettes versus real-world clinical notes or patient queries), transparency of model configuration and prompting, and clarity of abstention or refusal definitions. This heterogeneity limits the comparability of reported performance estimates and likely biases the literature toward optimistic results on narrow, well-specified tasks. In interpreting the synthesis, we emphasize patterns that appear robust across these differences and avoid treating any single study as definitive evidence of abstention behavior in deployed clinical systems.

Decision-theoretic formalization of abstention

Motivation

The systematic review surfaced two recurring and opposing failure modes across the literature. The first is under-refusal: models answer despite high uncertainty, incomplete clinical context, or potential for harm. The second is over-refusal: models decline benign queries unnecessarily, reducing their practical utility and obstructing access to reliable information. These patterns consistently appeared across the reviewed studies. In addition, existing refusal benchmarks reveal that abstention is typically operationalized as a binary outcome (refuse vs. answer), and are largely composed of static, single-turn question-answer pairs (see Table 2). This binary framing conceals the calibration problem at the heart of abstention: the model must balance two competing risks simultaneously.

The review also established that abstention arises from two structurally distinct motivations: epistemic uncertainty, where the model lacks sufficient knowledge or confidence to answer reliably, and normative constraint, where answering could cause harm or violate ethical, legal, or professional boundaries (see Fig. 3). Although both produce a withheld response, they reflect different sources of risk and require different mechanisms to detect and manage. Current technical approaches treat these motivations largely in isolation: intrinsic methods address uncertainty through confidence estimation, while safety-aligned models address norm violations through training and filtering. Neither framework provides a principled unified criterion for deciding when to abstain.

The formalization below addresses this gap. It provides a unified, decision-theoretic account of the abstention decision that integrates both epistemic and normative signals, connects directly to the evaluation design of MedSAFE, and operationalizes the under-refusal/over-refusal trade-off as a measurable curve.

Problem statement

We model abstention as a two-action decision problem with an explicit reject option, following the classical formulation of selective prediction with reject21,22. At each interaction, the model observes an input x (e.g., a patient query or clinical prompt) and must choose between two actions: producing an answer, or withholding a response. The key challenge is that the correct choice depends on quantities that the model cannot observe directly: the true answer y, the harm that a given response might cause, and the cost of failing to respond. A good abstention policy must integrate available signals about these quantities into a principled decision rule.

Models with better uncertainty calibration achieve lower selective risk at any given coverage level: they successfully route the hardest queries to abstention while continuing to answer the queries they handle well. The area under the risk-coverage curve (AURC) provides a single scalar summary; lower AURC indicates better calibration of the uncertainty signal. Confidence calibration itself can be quantified using the Expected Calibration Error (ECE), which measures the average discrepancy between the model’s stated confidence and its observed accuracy. Reporting R-C curves stratified by scenario type (e.g., uncertainty-driven vs. safety-driven) makes threshold trade-offs explicit and enables reproducible comparison across systems.

Abstention operating characteristic (AOC) curve

The R-C curve captures uncertainty calibration but does not directly measure the trade-off between appropriate safety refusals and inappropriate over-refusals. This distinction is critical in clinical settings where both errors carry patient-facing consequences: a missed harmful query endangers the patient, while an unjustified refusal on a benign query obstructs care.

To make this trade-off explicit, we adapt the receiver operating characteristic framework to abstention and define the Abstention Operating Characteristic (AOC) curve. In contrast to a standard ROC, the positive class here is the should-abstain decision, and the curve traces the trade-off between correct abstention on risky inputs and incorrect abstention on benign ones as the decision threshold varies. The AOC curve makes a precise distinction that is implicit but unresolved in the reviewed benchmarks. MedSafetyBench19 and CARES20 measure only the AAR component (how often the model refuses harmful prompts) but not IAR (how often the model refuses benign ones). Conversely, AbstentionBench11 reports abstention recall and precision in uncertainty-driven scenarios but does not explicitly separate correct refusals from over-refusal on benign prompts. The AOC curve unifies these perspectives, providing a single visualization that exposes both failure modes simultaneously and enables comparison across models and thresholds. The summary statistic AUAOC is analogous to the AUROC in binary classification and inherits its interpretability: AUAOC = 0.5 indicates no discriminative ability between risky and benign inputs, while AUAOC = 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination.

MedSAFE: a conceptual framework for evaluating abstention

Motivation

Existing refusal evaluation benchmarks exhibit several important limitations. First, most are single-turn, whereas real-world clinical interactions with LLMs typically unfold as multi-turn dialogs in which uncertainty and risk are addressed progressively throughout the conversation. Second, they operationalize abstention as a binary refusal outcome, rather than as a nuanced behavior that may include clarification, deferral, or proportionate caution. Third, many benchmarks are static and frequently derived from pre-existing datasets or synthetically generated prompts. This raises concerns about data leakage and overfitting, while their fixed design limits adaptability to evolving model capabilities and emerging safety risks. Therefore, evaluating abstention requires a shift from single-turn accuracy metrics to dynamic, dialog-based assessments that better reflect realistic human-AI interactions. To support this shift, we introduce MedSAFE, a conceptual framework for designing future abstention benchmarks in healthcare.

MedSAFE is designed to characterize abstention across the scenario types identified in our review (e.g., incomplete information, ambiguous queries, false premises, harmful content, adversarial prompts), spanning explicit refusal, deferral, and clarification-seeking. By representing abstention as a structured behavioral dimension rather than an incidental refusal, it provides a foundation for adaptive, safety-aware benchmarks that evolve with the capabilities of contemporary AI systems.

Architecture

MedSAFE implements a three-agent evaluation pipeline that simulates realistic clinical dialog. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the framework is organized around three LLMs with distinct roles: an auditor, a target model, and a judge. The auditor generates the user side of a short medical dialog based on a scenario seed, producing natural user turns that initiate the interaction. These turns are then passed to the target model, which represents the system under evaluation and produces assistant responses. Together, the user turns and model replies form a complete conversation transcript. The transcript is subsequently evaluated by the judge model, which assigns scores according to a rubric designed to capture multiple aspects of model behavior. The benign control produced Δ(x) = 0.00, a boundary case arising because the judge correctly assigned zero justification-quality score (no abstention was present to justify), which placed the scenario exactly on the decision threshold; the policy tie was resolved to abstain by convention rather than by model failure. The ethical disclosure scenario (normative, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{R}}$$\end{document}R) produced Δ(x) = + 0.90 and was classified as answered, yet the model’s response was appropriate: it offered non-directive guidance, declined to make a personal decision for the user, and maintained empathic alignment (empathic alignment: 0.85; justification quality: 0.90). This case reveals a limitation of the binary appropriate-abstention rubric dimension, which does not distinguish between explicit refusal and normative mitigated engagement. As a result, clinically appropriate responses may be misclassified. We return to this issue in the Discussion, where we outline potential refinements to the evaluation rubric.

Limitations

Importantly, this implementation is not a validated benchmark in its current form. Rather, it serves as an illustrative prototype demonstrating that dialog-based abstention evaluation is feasible and offering a foundation upon which more extensive and rigorously validated benchmarks can be built. The pilot results should therefore be interpreted as a feasibility demonstration, not as evidence of reliability, robustness, or comparative model performance.

Several limitations should be noted. First, our MedSAFE prototype is currently implemented with only a small number of synthetic scenarios per category. Expanding both the number and the clinical diversity of seeds (e.g., across specialties, patient populations, and languages) is an important direction for future work. Second, both the user and the judge are simulated. This setup can introduce potential biases; for example, an LLM judge may share failure modes with the evaluated model family. We use LLM judges here because they offer practical advantages in terms of cost, scalability, and time efficiency, and prior work has shown alignment between LLM-based and human assessments in similar evaluation settings11,58,59. Nonetheless, validating these results through systematic human-LLM agreement studies remains an essential area for future research.

Prospects and future work

Looking ahead, MedSAFE is intended as a foundation for a broader research program on abstention and calibrated clinical interaction in medical LLMs. The prospects for this work are to develop MedSAFE from a proof-of-concept framework into a validated benchmark for medical abstention and uncertainty-aware interaction. Future work will expand the scenario set across specialties, risk types, and real-data-derived clinical scenarios; and validate judge-model scores against expert human ratings through collaboration with physicians. We also plan to refine the rubric to better distinguish clarification-seeking, explicit refusal, clinical deferral, and mitigated engagement. Ultimately, MedSAFE is intended to support ongoing evaluation of whether medical LLMs are not only accurate, but also appropriately calibrated in when to answer, ask for more information, or abstain.
