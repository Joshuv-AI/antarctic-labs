Ion‑Responsive in situ Gel of Quercetin‑Loaded PEGylated Liposomes for Anti‑inflammatory Treatment of Dry Eye Disease
Authors: Xiaping Yao, Xue He, Zhen Liang, Fudan Dong, Zhen Zhang, Guojuan Pu, Jian Ye, Tianyang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDry eye disease (DED) is a global health burden with limited effective and safe treatments. Quercetin (Qu) possesses potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, but its poor solubility and short half-life restrict ophthalmic use.MethodsQu‑loaded PEGylated liposomes (Qu‑PL) were prepared by thin‑film hydration and incorporated into gellan gum to form an ion‑responsive in situ gel (Qu‑PL‑ISG). The formulation was characterized for particle size (PS), zeta potential (ZP), polydispersity index (PDI), encapsulation efficiency (EE), short-stability, in vitro release, cytotoxicity, ocular irritation, mucoadhesion, ocular surface retention, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic efficacy in a BAC‑induced DED mouse model. Network pharmacology and molecular docking were used to generate testable hypotheses on possible mechanisms of Qu in DED.ResultsQu‑PL‑ISG had a PS of 173.33 ± 1.27 nm, a ZP of −46.87 ± 0.95 mV, and an EE of 88.85 ± 1.41%. It released 46% of Qu over 72 h and remained stable for 21 days at 4 °C, with no cytotoxicity or irritation. Compared with Qu‑PL, Qu‑PL‑ISG exhibited higher mucoadhesive force (3783.5 ± 125.2 vs. 1986.2 ± 145.7 dyne/cm2) and longer ocular surface retention (45 vs. 20 min). Ocular bioavailability (AUC0‑180) in cornea, conjunctiva, and tears increased 2.79-, 1.47- and 1.44-fold, respectively. In DED mice, 0.2% Qu‑PL-ISG restored tear secretion, reduced corneal staining, repaired epithelial thickness, increased goblet cell density, and lowered corneal IL-1β and TNF-α levels, with efficacy comparable to 0.05% cyclosporine A. Network pharmacology and molecular docking were used as computational tools to predict that Qu may act on DED through multi‑target interactions (TNF, NF‑κB, STAT3, IL-1β, AKT1, IL-6, Src) and pathways (IL-17, PI3K-Akt, TNF signaling), though these findings require experimental validation.ConclusionQu‑PL‑ISG is a safe, ion‑activated in situ gel that significantly enhances Qu bioavailability and anti‑inflammatory efficacy, showing promise for DED treatment.Keywords: dry eye disease, quercetin, PEGylated liposome, gellan gum, in situ gel

Introduction

With the widespread use of air conditioning and the increasingly frequent use of computers and smartphones, dry eye disease (DED) has become the most prevalent multifactorial ophthalmic disease in modern society, affecting up to 50% of the population worldwide.1,2 The core pathological features of DED are sustained ocular inflammation and tear film instability, which result in ocular discomfort and blurred vision, severely disrupting patients’ daily lives. Tear hyperosmolarity and oxidative stress trigger the excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, creating a self-sustaining inflammatory loop that further disrupts corneal barrier integrity and locks the disease into a vicious cycle.3–5 Therefore, inhibiting ocular surface inflammation is an essential strategy to halt the progression of DED.

Current first-line topical treatments include artificial tears and anti-inflammatory agents. Artificial tears primarily provide symptomatic relief by improving ocular surface humidity and reducing evaporation, but they fail to address the underlying inflammatory progression of DED.6,7 Topical corticosteroids and cyclosporine A (CsA) have demonstrated efficacy in interrupting the inflammatory cycle of DED, but their long-term use is restricted by safety concerns: steroid carry the risk of cataract or glaucoma, while CsA frequently causes ocular burning and has a delayed onset of action, which compromises patient compliance.8–10 Against this backdrop, there is an urgent need to develop novel formulations that can rapidly and effectively target the inflammatory cascade in DED, while offering a favorable safety profile.

Dietary-derived plant polyphenols have received increasing attention for their anti‑inflammatory and antioxidant activities.11 Among them, quercetin (3,3,4,5,7-pentahydroxyflavone, Qu), one of the most commonly consumed flavonoid polyphenolic compounds in the human diet and widely distributed in fruits, vegetables, and medicinal plants,12 has demonstrated therapeutic potential in several ocular surface diseases, including corneal disease, conjunctivitis, anterior uveitis, cataract and glaucoma.13 In the context of DED, Qu has been shown to reduce hypertonicity‑induced inflammatory injury in human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) via the PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway and to lower the levels of interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) and tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α).14 Topical application of Qu can also improve corneal surface regularity and enhance tear production.15,16 These findings suggest that Qu might have the potential to treat DED. However, the clinical application of Qu is severely limited by its poor water solubility (less than 10 μg/mL), chemical instability, and short biological half-life.17 Traditional ophthalmic drops, usually water-based, undergo rapid clearance from the ocular surface, thereby yielding short retention times and low bioavailability (generally less than 5%).18

Overcoming these limitations requires an advanced drug delivery system that can simultaneously improve Qu solubility and prolong its residence on the ocular surface. Liposomes are spherical vesicles (25 nm to 10 μm in diameter) composed of phospholipid bilayers that can encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. They are particularly attractive for ocular delivery because their structure resembles that of biological membranes, which facilitates close contact with the cornea and conjunctiva, thereby enhancing drug penetration and prolonging ocular surface residence.19,20 Furthermore, lipid-based eye drops have been shown to be well tolerated and more effective than those containing hydroxypropyl methylcellulose or sodium hyaluronate at enhancing tear film stability and lowering tear osmolarity.21 Moreover, the surface functionalization of liposomes with poly (ethylene glycol) (PEG) chains represents a well‑established strategy to improve their stability, enhance trans‑corneal permeation and modulate their interaction with the mucosal barrier.22 Additionally, PEGylated liposomes may also display moderate mucoadhesive properties on the ocular surface, as the flexible PEG chains can interpenetrate with the mucus gel network and increase the overall contact time with the cornea and conjunctiva.23 Furthermore, incorporating PEGylated lipids into the bilayer enhances storage stability by providing steric stabilization, thereby preventing particle aggregation.24

In situ gel (ISG) formulations overcome challenges such as rapid corneal clearance and poor ocular surface retention by undergoing a stimuli-responsive sol-gel phase transition in response to factors such as pH, temperature, or ionic strength at the administration site.25,26 Among the various stimuli‑responsive polymers, gellan gum (GG) is particularly well-suited for ophthalmic use. GG is an anionic polysaccharide that forms clear, cohesive and transparent gels in the presence of physiological cation concentrations (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca2⁺) found in tear fluid.27,28 ISGs based on GG not only extend the precorneal contact time but also offer shear‑thinning (pseudoplastic) properties that facilitate uniform spreading of the formulation over the ocular surface during blinking.29 Combining liposomes with GG create a synergistic system that leverages the dual benefits of enhanced drug solubility/permeability from liposomes and sustained release/prolonged residence from the gel matrix, thus addressing the key limitations of rapid clearance in conventional ophthalmic formulations.30

Based on the above rationale, we hypothesized that integrating Qu-loaded PEGylated liposomes into a GG-based ion-sensitive ISG would produce a synergistic hybrid system that could address the multiple shortcomings of conventional Qu eye drops. This design capitalized on the enhanced drug solubility, stability and corneal permeability of PEGylated liposomes combined with the prolonged ocular surface retention and sustained release provided by the GG gel matrix. Although liposomes and ISGs had each been explored individually for ocular drug delivery and a few studies had combined them for other drugs, the application of such a hybrid system for Qu in the treatment of DED has not, to the best of our knowledge, been reported. Accordingly, we set out to develop and thoroughly characterize the Qu-PL-ISG formulation, assessing its physicochemical properties, stability, safety and pharmacokinetic profile. The therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in a BAC-induced mouse model of DED, and the underlying mechanism was predicted using a combination of network pharmacology and molecular docking.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Adult male C57BL/6J mice (6–7 weeks, 18–20 g) and New Zealand white rabbits (2.0–2.5 kg) were purchased from Huaxing Laboratory Animal Company (Henan, China). The animals were kept under a 12 h light-dark cycle in standard laboratory conditions at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1°C and relative humidity of 40% ± 5%, with free access to food and water all the time. All animal protocols were approved by the Ethical Committee of Experimental Animal Care of the Henan Eye Institute (approval no. HNEECA-2025-14) and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Resolution.

Specific-pathogen-free sterile chicken embryos (50–60 g) were obtained from Jinan Spafas Poultry Co., Ltd.

Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (USA), Rat conjunctival epithelial cells (RConEpiCs) were obtained from Cellverse (Shanghai, China).

Drugs Preparation

Qu (Q817162) and GG (G821481) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Qu analytical reference material (B20527) was purchased from Yuan ye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). DSPE-PEG2000 (PS1-E1-2K) was purchased from Ponsure Biotech, Inc. (Shanghai, China). Cholesterol (166023–21-8) and hydrogenated soybean phosphatidylcholine (HSPC, 92128–87-5) were purchased from AVT Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Tween 80 was purchased from Jinshan Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Sichuan, China). Benzalkonium chloride (BAC) was purchased from Hubei Gedian Humanwell Pharmaceutical Excipients Co.,Ltd (Hubei, China). 0.05% CsA eye drops were purchased from Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Shenyang, China). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (ELISA) for mouse IL-1β and TNF-α were purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from APExBIO Technology LLC (Beijing, China). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade methanol was purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). All unspecified reagents were from commercial resources.

Preparation of Qu-Loaded PEGylated Liposome (Qu-PL)

PEGylated liposomes encapsulating Qu were prepared by the thin film hydration method, as described previously, with some modifications.31 Briefly, HSPC, cholesterol, DSPE-PEG2000, and Tween 80 at a weight ratio of 30:5:5:0.75 (total lipid 500 mg) and Qu (5% of total lipid, 25 mg) were dissolved in 5 mL of absolute ethanol in a round-bottom flask. The organic solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator at 50 °C and 60 rpm to form a thin lipid film. The film was further dried under vacuum overnight to remove residual solvent. The dried film was hydrated with 10 mL of deionized water at 60 °C for 1 h under stirring (80 rpm). The resulting dispersion was sonicated using a probe sonicator (40 W, 15 min, ice bath), followed by extrusion within 0.45 μm pore-sized polycarbonate membranes to achieve a homogeneous particle size.

Preparation of Qu-PL-ISG

To formulate the ISG matrix, GG was dissolved in deionized water by heating to 90°C to ensure complete polymer dissolution and subsequently cooled until it reached ambient temperature. The concentrated Qu-PL was then introduced dropwise into the ISG matrix under continuous magnetic stirring at 500 rpm to ensure homogeneous mixing and form the 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG formulation. The GG concentrations were 0.4% (w/v), 0.5% (w/v) and 0.6% (w/v), respectively. The optimal concentration of GG was determined based on the viscosity of the formulations.

Physicochemical Characterization

Particle Size (PS), Polydispersity Index (PDI) and Zeta Potential (ZP)

The average PS, PDI and ZP of the formulations were determined using the dynamic light scattering (DLS) method (Zetasizer, NanoZS90, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Measurements were performed at 25 °C with a detection angle of 90°, following appropriate dilution of the samples with purified water. Each sample was measured three times.

Determination of Encapsulation Efficiency (EE)

The entrapment efficiency of Qu-PL was determined indirectly using ultracentrifugation.32 Briefly, 2 mL of Qu-PL was ultracentrifuged at 10000 rpm for 1 h using a centrifuge (Centrifuge 5910Ri, Eppendorf, Germany). HPLC Analysis of Qu

The Qu content was quantified by HPLC using a Waters 2695 system (Milford, MA, USA) equipped with an X-Bridge C18 column (150 mm × 3.0 mm, 3.5 μm; XTerra® MS, Waters, Ireland) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase, composed of 0.2% phosphoric acid and methanol (35:65, v/v), was delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Detection was performed at 371 nm with an injection volume of 20 μL. A standard curve was constructed for quantification. Specifically, a stock solution of Qu was prepared in methanol and then serially diluted with the mobile phase to obtain working standard solutions.

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) of Qu-PL

The morphology of Qu-PL was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200C, Thermo Scientific, USA). Briefly, the sample was diluted with deionized water, and a drop of the suspension was loaded onto a carbon-coated copper grid. Excess liquid was then blotted off with filter paper, followed by negative staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid aqueous solution and natural air-drying at room temperature. TEM observation was performed at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) of Qu-PL-ISG

The surface morphology of Qu-PL-ISG and simulated tear fluid (STF, pH 7.4)-containing Qu-PL-ISG samples were examined using SEM (Prisma E, Thermo Scientific, USA). The Qu-PL-ISG solution and the sample obtained by crosslinking Qu-PL-ISG solution with STF at a volume ratio of 40:28 was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve their microstructure, followed by freeze-drying for 24 h. The dried samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of platinum to enhance conductivity. Subsequently, each sample was mounted on a brass stub using double-sided adhesive tape and observed under SEM at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Viscosity Measurement of Qu-PL-ISG

The viscosity of the samples was measured using a cone-and-plate viscometer (Brookfield, DVNXLVCP, USA). 500 μL of Qu-PL-ISG was placed in the sample holder, and the gap between the CP51 spindle and the plate was set. Measurements were performed at different speeds (5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 RPM) at 25 °C. Similarly, the 500 μL hydrogel formed by mixing Qu-PL-ISG and STF at a volume ratio of 25:7 was analyzed at the same rotational speeds but at 34 °C.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometry (FTIR)

An FTIR spectrometer (Alpha ii, Bruker Co., Germany) was used to evaluate the compatibility between drug and other excipients by studying the Qu-Susp, GG, blank-PL, Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG through attenuated total reflection mode. The recorded spectral range was acquired from 4000 to 600 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Stability Studies

The preliminary stability of the Qu-PL-ISG formulation was evaluated by storing it in polypropylene eye drop bottles under two conditions: 4°C/25% RH and 25°C/60% RH. Over a 21-day period, key physicochemical parameters, including PS, PDI, ZP, pH, viscosity and drug content, were monitored at predetermined time points (0, 1, 3, 7, 14 and 21 days).

Qu-PL-ISG Release Studies in vitro

In vitro Release Studies

Mechanism of Drug Release

The release kinetics and mechanism of Qu from Qu-Susp, Qu-PL, and Qu-PL-ISG formulations were fitted to four distinct mathematical models including zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer-Peppas. The correlation coefficient (R2) was employed as the primary criterion for evaluating the goodness-of-fit. The model with the highest correlation R2 was selected as the best representation of the release kinetics.

Ex vivo Mucoadhesive Study

The Qu-PL-ISG formulation was evaluated for its mucoadhesive force by the method described in previously published literature using a modified physical balance method.34 Briefly, a freshly excised rabbit cornea was mounted on a vial with the epithelial side facing outward and secured using an aluminum cap and a rubber stopper. The prepared vial was equilibrated at 37 °C for 5 min. Another identical cornea-mounted vial was then inverted and attached to the arm of a balance as the upper vial, while the first vial served as the lower vial and was placed on a height-adjustable platform. An appropriate amount of Qu-PL or Qu-PL-ISG (pre-mixed with STF at a 25:7 ratio) was uniformly applied onto the corneal surface of the lower vial, and the platform was raised carefully to bring the two corneal surfaces into intimate contact, which was maintained for 10 min. Then, water was added to the pan to increase the weight until the vials detached. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Ocular Surface Resident Behavior

To evaluate the residence behavior of the eye drops on the ocular surface, the fluorescent tracer method was conducted.35 Fluorescein sodium (FLU), FLU-loaded Qu-PL (Qu-PL-FLU) and FLU-loaded Qu-PL-ISG (Qu-PL-ISG-FLU) were prepared with a final FLU concentration of 0.2% (w/v) to serve as drug tracer. A 50 μL volume of the free FLU solution was administered to the left eyes, while the right eye received 50 μL of either the Qu-PL-FLU or Qu-PL-ISG-FLU formulation. At predetermined time intervals, the ocular surface fluorescent images were captured using slit-lamp microscopy (Chongqing Kanghua S&T Co., Ltd., China) with cobalt blue light.

Ocular Pharmacokinetics in Rabbit Eyes

Grouping and Dosing

For the ocular pharmacokinetic study, the methods and sample sizes used were referred to in previously published literature.36 Sixty-three rabbits were divided into three treatment groups (n = 63 per group) receiving a single instillation of the same dose of Qu-Susp, Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG. Each group was sampled at seven time points (5, 15, 30, 60, 90, 120 and 180 min), with three rabbits (six eyes) sacrificed per time point. Specifically, 50 μL dose of the respective formulation was instilled into the inferior conjunctival sac of both eyes in each rabbit, followed by gently holding the eyelids closed for 15s. Tear fluid was sampled at predetermined time points by placing a sterile 7 mm diameter filter paper disk under the superior conjunctival sac for 30s (the disk was weighed immediately after removal. Note: Since tear density≈1.0 g/mL, the value in μg/g was numerically equivalent to μg/mL; tear concentrations were reported as μg/g for consistency with the weight-based quantification of cornea and conjunctiva). Subsequently, rabbits were euthanized by intravenous injection of propofol solution (7.5 mg/kg) and potassium chloride solution (100 mg/kg) via the ear margin vein. The ocular surfaces were then rinsed with saline, and the corneal and conjunctival tissues were carefully excised. After a saline rinse and blot-drying to remove residual moisture, the harvested tissues were precisely weighed and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis.

Analysis of Ocular Tissues

Drug extraction and quantification from ocular tissues and tear fluid were performed based on a previously established method.37 Each harvested sample (the tear-saturated filter paper disks, cornea or conjunctiva) was minced and immersed in 300 μL of methanol. The samples were then kept at 4 °C for 24 h to ensure complete drug extraction. After centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 10 min (Eppendorf, Germany), the supernatant was collected, diluted with methanol as necessary, and vortexed for 30s. The resulting solution was then analyzed by HPLC. The aforementioned samples were analyzed on a Waters X-Bridge C18 column (150 mm × 3.0 mm, 3.5 μm) with mobile Phase 0.2% phosphoric acid-methanol (35:65, v/v) at 0.3 mL/min, 40 °C, injection volume 20 μL and 371 nm detection. To construct the calibration curves, working solutions were prepared by serial dilution of the drug standard and then adding them to the respective blank rabbit tissue matrices to mimic the sample conditions. The pharmacokinetic parameters were derived by processing the concentration-time profiles with DAS 2.0 software (BioGuider Medicinal Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

In vitro Cytotoxicity Assay

The in vitro cytotoxicity of the Qu-PL-ISG formulation was assessed on HCECs and RConEpiCs using the CCK-8 assay. The methods and sample sizes used were based on previously published studies.38 Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL of DMEM/F12 medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin double antibiotic solution, then incubated for 24 h at 37°C under 5% CO2. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing Blank-PL-ISG/Qu-PL-ISG at various concentrations (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4 and 8 μg/mL) for 1, 2, 4 and 24 h. Wells containing cells with culture medium alone served as the control group, while wells containing only culture medium served as the blank. After treatment, the cells were rinsed twice with serum-free medium and incubated with 100 μL of CCK-8 solution for 3 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Rabbit Ocular Irritation Test

The ocular safety of Qu-PL-ISG was evaluated in six New Zealand albino rabbits. The rabbits were divided into two groups (n = 3): one group received a single 50 μL dose of Qu-PL-ISG in the right eye, while the other group received five 50 μL doses at 15 min intervals in the right eye. The left eyes of all rabbits received normal saline as controls. Ocular irritation was examined at 1, 2, 4, 24, 48 and 72 h post-administration using slit-lamp microscopy (Chongqing KangHua S&T Co., Ltd., China) to assess conjunctival hyperemia, corneal edema, iris changes and secretions. Corneal epithelial integrity was evaluated by fluorescein staining. Irritation scores were determined according to the modified Draize test, where 0–3 indicates no irritation, 4–8 slight, 9–12 moderate and 13–16 severe irritation.39 Upon completion of the study, the rabbits were euthanized by an overdose of 4% pentobarbital sodium via ear vein injection, and the eyes were enucleated. After rinsing with saline, the eyeballs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h. The corneas were then processed for histology by paraffin embedding, sectioning into 5 μm slices, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for evaluation.

Hen’s Egg Test-Chorioallantoic Membrane (HET-CAM)

The potential of the Qu-PL-ISG formulation to cause irritation was evaluated using the CAM model. Fertilized chicken eggs were kept at 37.8°C and 60% relative humidity for 9 days, with automatic turning three times daily to prevent the embryo from sticking to one side of the shell. Selected fertilized chicken embryos were candled to mark the air sac position on the eggshell. The eggshell over the air sac region was carefully removed using ophthalmic serrated curved forceps to expose the inner shell membrane, ensuring the membrane remained intact. A sterile 0.9% NaCl solution was applied with a pipette to fully moisten the inner shell membrane, after which the solution was poured off. The fibrous layers over the shell membrane and the CAM were carefully removed with forceps, avoiding damage to the vascular membrane. The vascular structure was then re-examined to assess its integrity and suitability for the experiment. A volume of 0.3 mL of the test substance (0.9% saline, 0.1 M NaOH and Qu-PL-ISG) was evenly applied to the surface of the CAM, ensuring coverage of at least 50% of the area. After 5 min of exposure, the CAM surface was gently rinsed with physiological saline for 30s. Residual liquid was removed, and toxicological endpoints such as hemorrhage, lysis or coagulation were observed under a stereomicroscope. The level of irritation to the CAM was evaluated objectively using a standardized irritation score, where scores of 0.0–0.9 indicate no irritation, 1.0–4.9 indicate slight irritation, 5.0–8.9 indicate moderate irritation, and 9.0–21.0 indicate severe irritation.40

In vivo Therapeutic Efficacy Assessment on DED

Animal Models of DED

The DED model was established, and the sample sizes used were based on previously described methods.41 Briefly, C57BL/6 mice received topical administration of 5 μL of 0.2% BAC eye drops (w/v) in each eye twice daily (8:00 am and 8:00 pm) for 7 consecutive days. After successful model induction, the mice were randomly assigned to six groups as follows: sham group, and five treatment groups receiving 5 μL of topical eye drops three times per day of Blank-PL-ISG (control), 0.05% Qu-PL-ISG, 0.1% Qu-PL-ISG, 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG and 0.05% CsA (positive control) to the ocular surface for 7 days. It should be noted that Qu is a compound with poor water solubility (<10 μg/mL), the concentration of 0.2% is the maximum concentration at which the Qu-PL-ISG, obtained through formulation screening, can maintain favorable physicochemical properties. Therefore, 0.2% was set as the highest administered concentration, and two lower concentrations, 0.1% and 0.05%, were also prepared to explore the concentration-effect relationship.

Tear Secretion Assessment

Tear secretion was quantified using phenol red-impregnated cotton threads (Tianjin Jingming New Technological Development Co., Ltd.) according to the instructions. The threads were carefully placed on the lower eyelid palpebral conjunctiva at 1/3 of the distance from the lateral canthus. The length (mm) of the wetted cotton thread was determined as the millimeters after 20s without anesthesia.42 Clinical assessments were performed on days 0, 4 and 7 of treatment.

Fluorescein Staining

Corneal integrity was assessed using fluorescein staining as described previously.4 Briefly, corneal epithelial damage was evaluated using corneal fluorescein staining after sodium fluorescein was instilled into the conjunctival sac of the mice, and the cornea was observed under the slit-lamp microscope with a cobalt blue filter. The cornea was divided into five areas (central, nasal, temporal, superior, and inferior), and each area was scored as follows: 0 = no staining, 1 = slight punctate staining, 2 = distinct punctate or slight coalescent staining, 3 = distinct coalescent or slight patchy staining, and 4 = distinct patchy staining. Clinical assessments were performed on days 0, 4 and 7 of treatment. Two independent observers who were unaware of the group allocation performed a blinded assessment of the fluorescein staining scores.

Histopathological Analysis

Mice were intraperitoneally injected with diazepam injection (5 mg/kg) and butorphanol tartrate injection (0.8 mg/kg for analgesia), and then euthanized by cervical dislocation, then the ocular globes and associated adnexa were collected and fixed in FAS eyeball fixative (Servicebio, China) and embedded in paraffin, cut into sagittal sections (5 μm thick), and stored at room temperature. Eye sections underwent H&E staining for regular histological analysis, while periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was utilized to observe and count conjunctival goblet cells.

Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Corneal levels of IL-1β and TNF-α were evaluated by ELISA. On days 4 and 7 of the treatment period, five mice per group were euthanized, and corneal tissues were surgically excised and stored at −80°C. For analysis, frozen samples were first equilibrated on ice for 30 min. The tissues were then homogenized, and the supernatant was collected following a standard centrifugation protocol. The concentrations of IL-1β and TNF-α in the supernatants were determined strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for the respective ELISA kits. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Network Pharmacology and Molecular Docking

We explored the potential targets and mechanisms of action of Qu in the treatment of DED based on network pharmacology.43 The targets of Qu were screened from SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/; probability > 0), BATMAN 2.0 database (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/; Score cutoff > 0.84) and TCMSP database (https://www.tcmsp-e.com/#/home). The disease targets of DED were predicted via Genecards Database (https://www.genecards.org/; GeneCard (Relevance score > 10). With the help of Venny online mapping website, the intersection targets of Qu and DED were screened out, and the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed through the String database (https://string-db.org/; minimum required interaction score: 0.4). The topological analysis method of Cytoscape 3.8.2 was used to screen out the core targets and Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the core targets were preformed using Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html). Molecular docking was performed using the AutoDock Vina (Auto Protein-Ligand Blind Docking) and PyMOL (https://autodock.scripps.edu/; molecular visualization software).

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism Software Version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA) was used to conduct statistical analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk test or the D’Agostino and Pearson test was performed to assess the normality of samples. The F-test or Brown-Forsythe or Bartlett’s test was used to evaluate equality of standard deviations. The t-test (for normally distributed variables) or Mann–Whitney test was used to compare the means of two data groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for comparisons among three or more groups, followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. The p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data are presented as mean ± SD, unless otherwise indicated in figure legends (mean ± SEM is noted where used).

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG

Although Qu treatment (Figure 1A) showed some effectiveness in reducing corneal damage caused by DED, its therapeutic potential was significantly restricted due to poor water solubility and rapid clearance, leading to inadequate bioavailability.44 To overcome these challenges, we constructed a PEGylated liposome for delivery of Qu (Qu-PL), and further functionalized with GG to form the Qu-PL-ISG system. It is worth noting that we incorporated TW80 into this formulation, as the combination of nonionic surfactants and phospholipids can enhance the stability of the dispersion system.45 The preparation of Qu-PL-ISG was accomplished via a two-step procedure. As shown in Figure 1B, Qu-PL was prepared by the classical thin-film hydration method, a self-assembly process. Notably, Qu-PL exhibited a high EE of (88.85 ± 1.41) % (Figure 1C). Subsequently, the final Qu-PL-ISG formulation with a Qu concentration of 0.2% was obtained by blending the Qu-PL dispersion with GG, forming a gel matrix in which the liposomes are homogeneously dispersed (Figure 1D).

Given that viscosity plays a critical role in ocular surface retention performance, the optimal concentration of GG was selected based on the viscosity of Qu-PL-ISG containing different concentrations of GG (0.4%, 0.5%, 0.6%) before and after gelation. As illustrated in Figure 1E, at 25 °C without STF and a speed of 5 RPM, the viscosity of Qu-Pl-ISG with 0.6% GG exceeded 70 cP, whereas those with 0.4% and 0.5% GG were below 50 cP, which facilitated administration of topical ophthalmic solutions.27 Under STF-activated conditions at 34 °C, the viscosity of Qu-Pl-ISG with 0.5% GG (248.57 ± 5.53 cP) was higher than that with 0.4% GG (173.50 ± 11.70 cP) at a speed of 5 RPM, and this higher viscosity might contribute to greater potential for prolonged ocular retention. Therefore, 0.5% GG was identified as the optimal concentration.

Subsequently, the physicochemical properties of the Qu-PL-ISG formulation were thoroughly characterized, as these attributes are governing factors for its efficacy, and stability as a topical ophthalmic solution. Following incorporation into the GG gel matrix, the Qu-PL-ISG formulation exhibited a more negative ZP (−46.87 ± 0.95 mV) than that of Qu-PL (Figure 1F). This was driven by the ionized carboxyl groups of GG, and the more negative ZP contributes to electrostatic stabilization rather than to improve mucoadhesion.46 Correspondingly, the average PS of Qu-PL-ISG increased to 173.33 ± 1.27 nm (Figure 1G), indicating partial aggregation or polymer adsorption, while remaining smaller than that of unadorned Qu-loaded liposome (274.06 ± 26.98 nm) reported previously.47 Moreover, the PDIs of Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG were below 0.3 (Figure 1H), which are considered acceptable for drug delivery applications using lipid-based carriers, indicating a relatively narrow size distribution,48 and their volume distributions exhibited a single narrow peak (Figure S1A and B), indicating that the vast majority of vesicle volumes fall within a tight size range. TEM imaging analysis revealed that Qu-PL possessed a clear spherical morphology (Figure 1I). However, the larger particles that might occasionally be observed in TEM images either arise from drying artifacts (eg., flattening or aggregation),49,50 or represent an extremely minor population that contributes negligibly to the total vesicle volume, considering the single narrow peak and fact that the PDI was below 0.3.

The morphological characteristics of Qu-PL-ISG were further investigated by SEM (Figure 1J). The Qu-PL-ISG prior to STF addition exhibited a spherical morphology, following incubation with STF, the structure largely retained its integrity and formed an interconnected porous network, a morphology known to facilitate drug encapsulation and sustained release.35

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to identify potential interactions between the drug and excipients, an assessment critical for ensuring the stability and efficacy of the formulation system. As shown in Figure 1K, the FTIR spectrum of pure Qu (Qu-Susp) exhibited characteristic absorption bands: a broad O-H stretch at 3392 and 3267 cm−1, a carbonyl C=O stretch at 1663 cm−1, and aromatic C=C vibrations at 1606 and 1517 cm−1. Additional peaks included C-O stretching between 1257–1167 cm−1, collectively confirming the functional groups consistent with the Qu structure.51 For Qu-PL, the coexistence of Qu-derived bands and the lipid-specific peaks at 1735 cm−1, with no noticeable shifts in the Qu-related bands, indicated successful physical incorporation of Qu into the liposomal bilayer. GG exhibited the characteristic peaks, including O-H stretching at 3251–3550 cm−1, C-H stretching at 2902 cm−1, carboxylate group vibrations between 1555–1452 cm−1, and C-O stretching at 1052 cm−1.52 The FTIR profile of Qu-PL-ISG largely overlapped with those of Qu-PL and GG, showing no significant shift or loss of characteristic peaks, which suggested the absence of chemical interactions and supported the formation of a physical gel encapsulating the liposomes.

Based on the above evidence, it can be concluded that a smart ocular drug delivery system based on GG-modified, long-circulating liposomes loaded with Qu was successfully developed. The system retained the liquidity of eye drops for easy administration while undergoing cation-triggered gelation in the tear film.

Short-Term Stability

Formulation stability is a critical development focus to ensure the preservation of efficacy during storage. The stability of Qu-PL-ISG was evaluated by monitoring its particle size, PDI, zeta potential, pH, viscosity, and drug content of Qu-PL-ISG over 21 days at 4 °C and 25 °C (Figure 2A).

The short-term stability of Qu-PL-ISG in 4°C and 25°C. (A) Schematic illustration depicting the short-term stability of Qu-PL-ISG, created by Figdraw. (B) Zeta potential. (C) Particle size. (D) PDI value. (E) PH value. (F) Viscosity. (G) Drug content. All data are represented as the mean ± SD, n = 3.

As shown in Figure 2B, the zeta potential of Qu-PL-ISG remained strongly negative (approximately −45 to −40 mV) throughout the study, supporting adequate electrostatic stabilization. Furthermore, its particle size remained within a narrow range of approximately 173–177 nm over 21 days at 4 °C, while it increased to approximately 182 nm by day 21 at 25 °C (Figure 2C). The PDI of Qu-PL-ISG increased from ~0.22 to ~0.25 at 4 °C, while it reached up to ~0.3 at 25 °C (Figure 2D), but remained within the typical limit (< 0.3), which is considered acceptable for lipid‑based nanocarriers.48 The PDI slightly increased, which is likely merely an indication of slight heterogeneity within the liposome gel network, rather than of severe particle fusion or precipitation. Moreover, the formulation maintained a consistent pH between 5.6 and 5.8 under both storage conditions, a range that was well-tolerated by the ocular surface and essential for ensuring patient compliance during topical application (Figure 2E). Viscosity measurements revealed that the viscosity of Qu-PL-ISG remained relatively stable at 4°C, whereas at 25°C, it gradually decreased, during the 21-day period (Figure 2F). Furthermore, the drug content at 4 °C decreased by about 3% over 21 days, while it decreased by about 9% at 25 °C, confirming that lower temperatures better preserve the drug payload (Figure 2G). These results collectively indicated that Qu-PL-ISG maintained acceptable stability over 21 days at 4 °C rather than at 25 °C, and 4 °C is recommended as the preferred storage condition. Meanwhile, as shown in Figure S2A–D, during the same 21-day period, regardless of whether the temperature was 4 °C or 25 °C, the stability of Qu-PL-ISG in terms of zeta potential, particle size, PDI, and drug content was better than that of Qu-PL, confirming the stabilizing effect of the GG matrix on liposomes.

However, the 21‑day short-term evaluation provided only preliminary evidence of formulation stability, further longer‑term stability studies could be conducted in the future to obtain more comprehensive stability data.

In vitro Drug Release

In vitro drug release is a cornerstone of pharmaceutical formulation development and quality control.53 Drug release profiles were analyzed using a dialysis bag method (Figure 3A).

As depicted in Figure 3B, Qu-Susp displayed a rapid initial release, reaching a low cumulative percentage of 10.10 ± 1.35%, which plateaued early. This was indicative of the limited dissolution and poor solubility of the unformulated crystalline drug. In contrast, the amounts of cumulative drug released from Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG formulations were 73.04 ± 3.43% and 46.09 ± 2.11%, respectively. The prolonged release profile may result from the nanostructured lipid matrix slowing down drug diffusion and the ionically crosslinked hydrogel network limiting molecular movement.54

As shown in Table 1, among the four kinetic models tested, the first-order model yielded the highest R2 value (>0.99) for Qu-PL-ISG formulations, which was consistent with previously reported release behavior of liposomal delivery systems.46 However, the R2 alone was not sufficient to definitively establish the release mechanism, additional model selection criteria would be needed for a more rigorous comparison. Furthermore, the release exponent (n) obtained from the Korsmeyer-Peppas model was 0.45 for Qu-PL-ISG. According to the classical criterion for hydrophilic polymer matrices (n ≤ 0.5 indicates Fickian diffusion),55 this value suggests Fickian diffusion-controlled process, which is consistent with previous findings that highly porous GG matrices exhibited typical Fickian behavior.56 This structural basis was confirmed by the interconnected porous network observed by SEM after exposure to simulated tear fluid, as such a highly porous network architecture has been reported for GG-based ocular hydrogels.52,57 Thus, the structure may allow dissolved Qu molecules to gradually diffuse through the continuous water-filled channels of the gel matrix, continuously supplying the drug to the cornea and conjunctiva over an extended period.

It should also be noted that the dialysis bag method, while widely employed for comparative release studies of nanoparticle and in situ gel formulations, has inherent limitations. The dialysis membrane itself imposes a permeation barrier, so the apparent release profile measured in the receiver compartment depends on both drug release from the carrier and drug diffusion across the membrane.58 For lipophilic compounds such as Qu, the membrane may partially control the observed release rate, potentially causing the true release kinetics to be underestimated. However, our comparative experimental design (all formulations tested under identical membrane conditions) ensured that relative differences among Qu-Susp, Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG remained interpretable Future studies should employ more advanced release testing methods, such as the reverse dialysis bag approach, which minimizes or eliminates the membrane diffusion interference and yields more accurate characterization of drug release kinetics from the liposome-hydrogel composite system.

Mucoadhesive Force and Ocular Surface Retention

To evaluate the potential of Qu-PL-ISG for ocular drug delivery, we systematically investigated its bioadhesive properties and corneal retention capability.

Mucoadhesive force refers to the strength of binding between a formulation and the corneal surface. The parameter is critical for ocular ISG systems, as their ability to prolong precorneal residence depends not only on gelation upon instillation but also on the mucoadhesive capacity of the formed gel.59 Accordingly, the mucoadhesive properties of Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG were assessed using a modified physical balance method. In this assay, the formulations were placed between two rabbit corneas mounted in a face-to-face configuration, and the detachment force required to separate the surfaces was measured as a direct indicator of mucoadhesive performance (Figure 4A). As shown in Figure 4B, Qu-PL-ISG demonstrated a significantly higher mucoadhesive force (3783.5 ± 125.2 dyne/cm2) than did Qu-PL (1986.2 ± 145.7 dyne/cm2) (***p < 0.001), suggesting that ISG formation effectively improves adhesion to the corneal surface. This value also compares favorably with previously reported values for ocular in situ gel systems. The mucoadhesive force of Qu-PL-ISG (3783.5 dyne/cm2) is markedly higher than that of non-PEGylated ion-sensitive ISG for ocular delivery (<3000 dyne/cm2),54 and also higher than the value of 3412.3 ± 442 dyne/cm2 reported by Eldesouky et al for a chitosan-containing thermoresponsive gel on rabbit cornea,34 confirming the advantage of our hybrid system. The superior mucoadhesion may be mechanistically attributed to interpenetration between the flexible PEG chains and the mucus network,60 synergistically combined with the robust three‑dimensional scaffold provided by ion‑activated GG.

(C) Schematic illustration of the procedure for assessing ocular surface residence time using slit lamp microscope under CBL, created by Figdraw. Ocular drug delivery faces the challenge of rapid drug elimination caused by blinking and tear turnover, which significantly reduces bioavailability. Thus, the efficacy of formulations largely depends on their ocular surface retention time, making prolonged retention a key determinant of successful drug delivery and therapeutic outcomes.20 To evaluate this property in vivo, we loaded FLU, a hydrophilic fluorescent dye, into Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG, and subsequently tracked and visualized it using a slit lamp under cobalt-blue light (CBL) (Figure 4C). In the FLU group, the green fluorescence diminished rapidly within 5 min due to tear clearance and became nearly undetectable within 10 min. In the Qu-PL-FLU group, a small residue remained concentrated at the ocular corner at 15 min, with only trace green fluorescence visible in the conjunctival sac by 20 min. In contrast, after administration of Qu-PL-ISG-FLU, most of the formulation was concentrated in the conjunctival sac and gradually gelled upon contact with tear fluid and maintained fluorescence for 30 min observed under the CBL. Notably, faint green fluorescence could still be visually discerned at the lower eyelid at 45 min. The blinking reflex and tear dilution contribute to a gradual decrease in fluorescence intensity over time, facilitating the drainage of the drug through the nasolacrimal duct. In contrast, the GG-based gel system significantly delays this process in the Qu-PL-ISG formulation, consistent with the previously measured superior mucoadhesive force of Qu-PL-ISG (Figure 4D).

Based on the experimental evidence, the synergistic effect of in situ gelation and enhanced mucoadhesion in Qu-PL-ISG may contribute to prolonged ocular residence.

In vivo Ocular Pharmacokinetics in Rabbits

Ocular drug absorption, distribution and elimination following topical instillation are complex and highly dynamic processes. Drug absorption is constrained by multiple ocular surface clearance mechanisms, including nasolacrimal drainage, systemic absorption via the conjunctiva and continuous tear turnover, as well as anatomical barriers such as the corneal and conjunctival epithelia.61 Therefore, reliable evaluation of ocular drug behavior requires pharmacokinetic analysis. In this study, we conducted a comparative pharmacokinetic study of Qu delivered via Qu-Susp, Qu-PL, and Qu-PL-ISG in rabbit cornea, conjunctiva and tears (Figure 5A).

It is worth noting that the tear fluid concentration of Qu following instillation of Qu-PL-ISG showed relatively high interindividual variability (Figure 5B). The variability is likely attributable to several factors, including individual differences in tear ion composition affecting GG gelation kinetics, inherent variations in basal tear volume and turnover, differences in blinking frequency and nasolacrimal drainage,62,63 as well as the fact that the drug concentration gradient between the upper and lower fornix may vary with blinking pattern and gel spreading contributing to sampling variability. Meanwhile, at some points in time, corneal Qu concentrations were significantly elevated in the Qu-PL-ISG group versus both Qu-PL and Qu-Susp (Figure 5C), and Qu-PL-ISG also produced significantly higher drug concentration in the conjunctiva than Qu-PL and Qu-Susp (Figure 5D).

Key pharmacokinetic parameters are summarized in Table 2. Qu-PL-ISG achieved higher Cmax values than Qu-PL and Qu-Susp across all tissues: in tears (466.40 ± 288.03 vs. 272.69 ± 81.38, 212.72 ± 60.85 μg/g); cornea (2.51 ± 0.54 vs. 0.98 ± 0.15, 0.17 ± 0.03 μg/g), conjunctiva (3.41 ± 0.82 vs. 1.61 ± 0.59, 0.24 ± 0.07 μg/g). Notably, the higher Cmax of Qu-PL and Qu-PL-ISG observed in the conjunctiva compared to the cornea is attributed to the inherent structural and functional advantages of the conjunctiva, including a larger surface area, richer vascularization and greater permeability, collectively enhancing the efficiency of drug absorption.64 Furthermore, Qu-PL provided significantly greater ocular exposure under the concentration-time curve (AUC) compared to Qu-Susp, with AUC0-180 min values that were 4.18-, 5.94- and 1.29-fold higher in the cornea, conjunctiva and tears, respectively. Strikingly, Qu-PL-ISG further enhanced this effect of Qu-PL, achieving a 2.79-, 1.47- and 1.44-fold AUC increase, indicating substantially enhanced precorneal residence time.

Interestingly, in our study, the mean residence time (MRT) of Qu-Susp in the conjunctiva was numerically longer than that of Qu-PL-ISG. However, this phenomenon is not unique to our study, several other reports have also found similar phenomena.39,53 The possible explanation was that micron-sized drug particles from a suspension physically reside in the conjunctival cul-de-sac, an anatomical reservoir where larger particles can be retained for extended periods.65 In conventional tissue homogenate analysis, both undissolved particles and truly absorbed drug molecules are quantified together. Therefore, the relatively long MRT in the conjunctiva following Qu-Susp administration does not necessarily reflect enhanced pharmacological exposure; it was largely driven by the physical persistence of retained particles. Our Qu‑PL‑ISG formulation, by contrast, achieved substantially higher AUC and Cmax, confirming superior bioavailability despite a relatively short MRT.

In vitro Cytotoxicity

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using HCECs and RConEpiCs (Figure 6A), as these cell types were critically involved in the pathophysiology of DED and serve as primary targets for topical therapeutics.66 It should be noted that the concentration range (0.25–8 μg/mL) used in this study was determined based on the in vivo measured peak concentrations in the cornea (2.51 ± 0.54 μg/g) and conjunctiva (3.41 ± 0.82 μg/g), covering and exceeding the actual tissue exposure levels, thereby ensuring the clinical relevance of the study. And the 24 h exposure is widely used time point in standard biocompatibility testing (ISO 10993‑5), which not intended to mimic the real residence time, but rather to evaluated whether the formulation exhibits delayed or cumulative cytotoxicity.

The cytotoxicity assessment revealed a wide safety margin for Qu-PL-ISG. In HCECs, both Blank-PL-ISG and Qu-PL-ISG exhibited excellent biocompatibility after 1, 2 and 4 h of exposure across all tested concentrations (0.25 to 8 μg/mL), with cell viability consistently exceeding 90% (Figure 6B–D). After a 24 h incubation, the 8 μg/mL Qu-PL-ISG (85.92 ± 2.64%) group showed a decrease compared to Blank-PL-ISG (89.86 ± 2.72%; *p<0.05), yet the viability remained above 80% (Figure 6E). The consistent results in RConEpiCs further confirmed the low cytotoxicity of Qu-PL-ISG (Figure 6F–I).

Collectively, the results demonstrated that Qu-PL-ISG possesses a favorable safety profile, showing high biocompatibility with both HCECs and RConEpiCs.

In vivo Ocular Irritation in Rabbits

Ocular safety is a paramount concern in the development of ophthalmic drug delivery systems. The well-established heightened sensitivity of rabbits to irritants compared to humans makes them a predictive model, allowing the safety profile for human eyes to be reasonably extrapolated from these animal studies.64 Thus, to further evaluate the potential corneal toxicity of Qu-PL-ISG, an in vivo ocular irritation study was conducted in rabbits. Clinical observations were performed using slit-lamp microscopy, supplemented by sodium fluorescein staining, before administration and at 1, 2, 4, 24, 48 and 72 h after single or multiple instillations. Histopathological evaluation via H&E staining was also carried out on the cornea, conjunctiva, and iris to assess tissue integrity (Figure 7A).

Whether administered topically once or multiple times, ocular structures including the cornea, iris and conjunctiva remained clearly visible in both the saline and Qu-PL-ISG groups. In addition, no conjunctival discharge, chemosis, hyperemia or corneal epithelium injury was observed at any time point (Figure 7B and C). Furthermore, H&E staining revealed well-preserved tissue architecture and integrity in all examined ocular structures, with no significant inflammatory cell infiltration, edema, or other histopathological alterations (Figure 7D and E).

Collectively, Qu-PL-ISG produced no ocular irritation in rabbits, demonstrating the tolerability and ocular safety of the formulation.

In vitro Ocular Irritation Testing Using HET-CAM

Due to the well-developed vascularization of the CAM, which comprises arteries, veins and capillaries, as well as its ability to respond to injury with an inflammatory reaction similar to that observed in rabbit conjunctival tissue,40 Qu-PL-ISG was evaluated for its short-term acute toxicity using the HET-CAM model (Figure 8A).

As illustrated in Figure 8B, the negative control (0.9% NaCl) caused no significant alteration in the existing vascular network, confirming that the experimental procedure itself did not induce irritant response. In contrast, the positive control (0.1 M NaOH) led to pronounced vascular disruption and severe irritation. Overall, the Qu-PL-ISG formulation exhibited no significant irritant potential in the HET-CAM model, together with the other safety evaluations, this supports the favorable ocular safety profile of Qu-PL-ISG and its potential for long-term application.

In vivo Therapeutic Effects on DED

We evaluated the therapeutic potential of Qu-PL-ISG in a DED model that was constructed by topical administration of 0.2% BAC. As a frequently used preservative in ocular formulations, BAC can cause dry eye symptoms by damaging corneal epithelial cells and hastening the breakdown and evaporation of the tear film.67

The mice were administered 0.2% BAC twice daily for a week to develop the model, and the successfully modeled mice exhibited tear film instability, ocular surface inflammation, corneal epithelial damage, and goblet cell loss. These clinical manifestations and histopathological changes closely resemble those observed in human DED.10 Subsequently, the successfully modeled mice were randomly assigned to different treatments, including sham, Blank-PL-ISG, various concentrations of Qu-PL-ISG (0.05%, 0.1%, 0.2%) and 0.05% CsA, three times a day for 7 days. 0.05% CsA is generally applied twice a day, to ensure consistency with the formulation group’s administration frequency and to allow comparability, modifications were made based on existing literature.68 The changes in tear secretion and corneal fluorescein staining (CFS) served as the basis for the evaluation of disease status in our experiments, and we assessed them on days 0, 4 and 7 of the treatment period (Figure 9A).

The phenol red cotton thread test was utilized to measure tear secretion volume as a crucial sign of lacrimal gland function.41 The results showed that baseline tear volume in normal mice (4.96 ± 0.66 mm) was higher than that in DED mice (2.00 ± 0.48 mm; ***p < 0.001), confirming successful induction of dry eye in the model. Tear volume remained relatively high in the normal group on day 4 (4.92 ± 0.48 mm) and day 7 (5.04 ± 0.36 mm). Meanwhile, compared with the normal group at the corresponding time points, both the sham group (2.42 ± 0.68 mm on day 4 and 3.00 ± 0.95 mm on day 7) and the Blank-PL-ISG group (2.54 ± 0.59 mm on day 4 and 3.14 ± 0.76 mm on day 7) exhibited markedly reduced tear secretion (***p < 0.001), indicating persistent DED and a lack of therapeutic effect from the vehicle alone. In contrast, Qu-PL-ISG treatment led to dose-dependent improvements in tear secretion. The 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG group demonstrated the most substantial recovery, with tear volume reaching 4.56 ± 0.47 mm by day 7 (Figure 9B).

The corneal epithelium of healthy eyes generally lacks defects and showed negligible fluorescein staining, unlike dry eyes, which have defects with positive staining.4 Slit-lamp examination with CFS directly visualizes corneal epithelial damage (Figure 9C). As depicted in Figure 9D, no significant changes were detected in the Blank-PL-ISG group compared to the sham group, indicating that the blank vehicle alone lacked therapeutic efficacy. Interestingly, corneal damage progressively diminished with higher Qu-PL-ISG concentrations, and the 0.2% group demonstrated the strongest therapeutic effect by day 7, significantly reducing the fluorescein sodium score (4.00 ± 1.00) compared with the sham group (8.00 ± 1.22; **p < 0.01) and Blank-PL-ISG group (7.80 ± 1.30; **p < 0.01).

To further evaluate the efficacy of Qu-PL-ISG in DED model mice treated for 7 days, we proceeded with histopathological evaluations to examine the structural and morphological alterations in ocular tissues following different treatments. The tear film consists of mucus, aqueous and lipid layer from the innermost to the outermost layer, which are secreted by the meibomian glands, lacrimal glands, and conjunctival goblet cells, respectively.10 H&E staining was used to evaluate the overall structure and cell morphology of the cornea. As shown in Figure 9E, while the corneal cells of the normal mice appeared densely packed and orderly, those in sham-and Blank-PL-ISG-treated mice were disrupted and shed, leading to a marked thinning in morphology, consistent with previous studies.10 Notably, the corneal cell morphology and structure showed limited improvement after treatment with 0.05% (26.78 ± 1.76 μm) and 0.1% (27.62 ± 2.77 μm) Qu-PL-ISG, whereas the 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG group recovered to a large extent (30.04 ± 3.28 μm) compared with the sham (22.64 ± 2.03 μm; ***p < 0.001) and Blank-PL-ISG (24.40 ± 2.39 μm; *p < 0.05) groups. These results further confirmed the excellent therapeutic outcomes of 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG, which were similar to those of the 0.05% CAS group (30.22 ± 0.90 μm). The innermost tear film layer is the mucus layer, which is primarily secreted by goblet cells that released mucins for ocular surface lubrication, and their absence is a significant characteristic in mice suffering from DED.69 We then conducted PAS staining on the conjunctiva to examine the number of goblet cells. As shown in Figure 9F, the conjunctival fornix of normal corneas (45.00 ± 3.94) contained a high number of PAS-positive goblet cells, whereas in DED model mice, the goblet cell count was significantly reduced (22.20 ± 4.49; ***p < 0.001) and the cells exhibited a small and shriveled morphology. Blank-PL-ISG treatment failed to ameliorate this loss, with a cell count (23.00 ± 4.47) similar to that of the sham group (22.20 ± 4.49). In contrast, treatment with Qu-PL-ISG dose-dependently restored goblet cell numbers, as evidenced by the counts in the 0.05% (26.60 ± 5.03), 0.1% (30.80 ± 3.70), and 0.2% (37.80 ± 3.96; ***p < 0.001) groups, with the 0.2% formulation achieving optimal results comparable to the 0.05% CsA group (38.80 ± 3.56).

In DED, reduced tear secretion causes hyperosmotic stress, which triggers signaling in epithelial and immune cells, leading to inflammation.10,70 We investigated inflammatory cytokine levels in the cornea to further explore the anti-DED mechanism of Qu-PL-ISG. The effects of different treatments on pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in the cornea were assessed using ELISA (Figure 9G). After 4 and 7 days of continuous administration, 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG significantly reduced the corneal levels of IL-1β (243.69 ± 21.19 pg/mg on day 4 and 201.46 ± 18.38pg/mg on day 7), compared with the sham group (357.56 ± 60.48 pg/mg on day 4 and 294.23 ± 19.39 pg/mg on day 7; **p < 0.01) (Figure 9H). For TNF-α, 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG likewise exerted a significant reduction compared with the sham group. Specifically, the Qu-PL-ISG group showed levels of 352.69 ± 33.61 pg/mg on day 4 (vs. sham: 520.75 ± 31.06 pg/mg; **p < 0.01) and 446.27 ± 54.35 pg/mg on day 7 (vs. sham: 352.69 ± 33.61 pg/mg; ***p < 0.001) (Figure 9I). These results suggested that 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG achieved the most effective suppression.

Notably, although only 46% of Qu was cumulatively released from Qu-PL-ISG over 72 h, this value represents merely the amount of drug that diffused out of the dialysis bag under the experimental conditions and does not equate to the total amount of drug available to ocular tissues in vivo. By cross-referencing the significantly improved ocular pharmacokinetic profile and the excellent pharmacodynamic outcomes of Qu-PL-ISG, we confirmed that the amount of Qu actually released and absorbed from this formulation in vivo was fully sufficient to exert a marked anti-inflammatory effect.

Despite the 7-day treatment period revealing significant therapeutic effects of 0.2% Qu-PL-ISG in the DED model, future studies in larger animal models with extended observation windows are required to evaluate its longer-term safety and efficacy. Nevertheless, even within this short treatment window, our delivery system demonstrated clear advantages over previously reported formulations. Oh et al evaluated 0.5% Qu suspension topically in a DED model and observed therapeutic outcomes similar to those in our study.44 In contrast, Qu suspension suffers from rapid precorneal clearance due to limited solubility and a lack of retention mechanisms, a limitation that our Qu-PL-ISG explicitly overcomes. Importantly, we achieved comparable efficacy with 0.2% Qu (relative to 0.05% CsA), whereas Oh et al used 0.5% Qu, indicating a dose-sparing effect attributable to enhanced bioavailability. These short-term gains thus provide a strong rationale for the proposed long-term studies in larger animal models.

Network Pharmacological Analysis and Molecular Docking

Network pharmacology has become an essential strategy for deciphering the pharmacological mechanisms of active compounds derived from traditional Chinese medicine. By integrating multi-omics data and constructing systemic interaction networks, this approach allows for the systematic identification of drug-target interactions, biological pathways, and disease-related processes. As a promising paradigm in modern drug discovery, network pharmacology provides a holistic framework to uncover potential therapeutic targets and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Molecular docking is a computational technique that predicts ligand-target interactions and visualizes molecular conformations, making it widely useful for drug discovery and pharmacological studies.71,72 Importantly, all findings from network pharmacology and molecular docking are computational predictions only and do not constitute experimental evidence of mechanism. They serve to generate hypotheses for future mechanistic studies, rather than to establish causal relationships.

Screening for Potential Therapeutic Targets of Qu for DED

Through searches conducted across the TCMSP, BATMAN, and SwissTargetPrediction databases, 339 non-redundant targets related to Qu were identified. Meanwhile, a total of 3,460 targets associated with DED were retrieved from the GeneCards database. Using Venny analysis, the intersection between these two sets revealed 246 overlapping targets, as illustrated in Figure 10A.

Analysis of the targets associated with Qu and DED. (A) Venn diagram of Qu and DED-associated targets. (B) The top 30 hub genes within PPI network of Qu anti-DED.

To elucidate the mechanism of Qu in DED, a PPI network was constructed using the 246 overlapping targets via the STRING database. The top 30 hub genes were subsequently identified from this network using the Maximal Clique Centrality algorithm through the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. Key targets among them included NF-κB, STAT3, IL-6, BCL2, JUN, TP53, AKT1, TNF, IL-1β, Src and others (Figure 10B). These core targets are known to participate in critical inflammatory pathways (eg., NF-κB, cytokine signaling), and cell survival and apoptosis (eg., BCL2) pathways.73 Collectively, these in silico predictions raise the hypothesis that Qu may affect DED through a theoretically multi-target mechanism.

GO and KEGG Pathway Enrichment Analysis

To elucidate the core biological functions and signaling pathways through which Qu exerts its effects in DED, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis revealed that the potential targets of Qu in DED were significantly enriched in several key biological processes (BPs), such as response to lipopolysaccharide, response to molecules of bacterial origin, oxidative stress, xenobiotic stimulus, and regulation of neuron death (Figure 11A). The consistent enrichment of these hub genes across these diverse processes supports a poly pharmacological mechanism of action for Qu, primarily involving anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which collectively may ameliorate DED pathology.74 Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested that Qu may influence DED through multiple signaling pathways, including IL-17, PI3K-Akt, and TNF signaling pathway (Figure 11B).

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. (A) GO enrichment analysis. (B) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Bubble size indicates the number of genes enriched in the pathway.

Molecular Docking

Based on prior evidence implicating the STAT3-PI3K-Akt-NF-κB axis in the inflammatory response of DED,75 together with enrichment results from GO and KEGG analyses, we selected key inflammatory-related targets, such as NF-κB, STAT3, IL-6, AKT1, TNF, IL-1β and Src for molecular docking with Qu. Binding energy serves as a key indicator of docking effectiveness, with more negative values reflecting higher stability. Generally, binding energies below −5.0 kcal/mol suggest moderate affinity, while those below −7.0 kcal/mol indicate strong binding and a high likelihood of biological activity.76 In our analysis, the binding affinity of TNF to Qu was −6.6 kcal/mol (Figure 12A), whereas the other targets (NF-κB, STAT3, AKT1, Src, IL-1β and IL-6) exhibited high-affinity interactions, with binding energies of −7.0, −8.2, −8.2, −8.2, −7.5 and −7.8 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 12B–G). Collectively, the molecular docking results suggested that Qu may form stable complexes with multiple core inflammatory regulators, providing structural evidence for its potential multi-target mechanism against DED.

Molecular docking models of Qu with possible core anti-DED targets. Binding affinities (kcal/mol) of Qu to: (A) TNF: −6.6; (B) NFκB: −7.0; (C) STAT3: −8.2; (D) AKT1: −8.2; (E) SRC: −8.2; (F) IL-1β: −7.5; (G) IL-6: −7.8.

While these predictions of network pharmacology and molecular docking are consistent with our experimentally observed reduction in IL‑1β and TNF‑α levels, they do not prove causal relationships. Direct experimental validation (eg., using gene‑knockout models or pathway‑specific inhibitors) is necessary before any mechanistic claim can be established.

Limitations

While these findings are promising, this study has several limitations. First, the EE of Qu-PL was determined by an indirect ultracentrifugation method, which might not fully separate nanosized PEGylated liposomes from unencapsulated Qu. Consequently, the EE value (88.85 ± 1.41%) could be overestimated, but it was only slightly higher than the 81.5% ± 0.05% encapsulation rate of Qu-loaded liposomes reported in the literature.47 This potential bias did not affect the main conclusions of our study: the in vitro release kinetics and the in vivo pharmacokinetic comparisons (all groups received the same nominal Qu concentration) remained valid. Moreover, the dramatic improvements in ocular bioavailability and anti-inflammatory efficacy observed with Qu-PL-ISG indicated that, despite a potentially high absolute EE value, the relative trend and the actual delivery performance of the formulation remained reliable. Nevertheless, future studies should employ more rigorous separation techniques, such as size-exclusion chromatography or ultrafiltration, to accurately quantify EE.

Second, the rabbit remains the standard preclinical model for comparative ocular pharmacokinetics, although its naturally low blink rate is substantially lower than that in humans.77,78 The prolonged retention of our GG-based in situ gel primarily relies on cation-triggered gelation upon contact with tear fluid (Ca2⁺, K⁺, Na⁺), and this process is largely independent of blinking frequency.79 More importantly, published studies have confirmed that similar GG systems exhibited rapid viscosity recovery and excellent self‑healing even after exposure to high shear stress that mimics the mechanical force of human blinking, shear-thinning can facilitate uniform spreading of the gel during blinking and minimize mechanical irritation.25,54 Consequently, the gel’s structural integrity and prolonged ocular residence time are expected to be maintained under the higher shear stress conditions of the human eye. Future work should perform a three-interval thixotropy test using oscillatory time-sweep rheology under simulated human blinking conditions to directly characterize the shear-recovery behavior of Qu-PL-ISG. In addition, while our formulation showed increased viscosity upon mixing with STF, we did not quantitatively characterize the gelation time, gel strength, or ion-triggered transition kinetics. Therefore, future work should employ time-sweep rheology to fully establish the gelation kinetics of the Qu-PL-ISG system.

Third, considering the sterility of this liposome‑hydrogel composite ophthalmic formulation during large‑scale manufacturing, we propose an aseptic mixing process under strict GMP (Grade B+A) conditions in future studies. Specifically, since GG is known to withstand autoclaving (121°C, 15–20 min), as previously reported without losing its gelling properties,80 it can be sterilized individually by autoclaving. The liposomes can be sterile‑filtered through a 0.22 μm hydrophilic membrane,81 then the two sterile components can be mixed aseptically and filled into pre‑sterilized containers. The proposed aseptic mixing approach is already technically feasible.

Conclusion

The persistent challenges in DED therapy, namely poor ocular bioavailability, safety concerns, and suboptimal patient compliance, demand advanced delivery strategies. In response, this study developed an ion‑responsive, mucoadhesive in situ gel system (Qu‑PL‑ISG) that synergistically combines quercetin‑loaded PEGylated liposomes with a GG matrix (Figure 13A).

Construction of Qu-PL-ISG and the predicted mechanism of Qu in DED. (A) Illustration of construction of Qu-PL-ISG. (B) Schematic diagram of Qu treatment DED mechanism prediction based on network pharmacology and molecular docking. Created by Figdraw. The downward arrow indicates a decrease in the level of inflammatory factors.

Unlike conventional eye drops or simple liposomal formulations, Qu‑PL‑ISG leverages tear-triggered gelation and enhanced mucoadhesion to substantially prolong ocular surface residence. This translates into markedly improved drug delivery efficiency, as evidenced by superior pharmacokinetic profiles across the cornea, conjunctiva, and tears. In a BAC‑induced DED mouse model, the 0.2% Qu‑PL‑ISG formulation restored tear secretion, preserved corneal epithelial integrity, replenished goblet cells, and suppressed key pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑1β and TNF‑α), achieving efficacy comparable to that of 0.05% CsA, but without detectable cytotoxicity or ocular irritation.

Moreover, complementary network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses provide a computationally derived, testable hypothesis that Qu may act through multiple inflammatory targets and pathways (Figure 13B). These findings, while requiring experimental validation, offer a mechanistic framework for understanding the polypharmacological action of Qu in DED. Taken together, Qu‑PL‑ISG represents a promising, patient‑friendly platform for topical delivery of poorly soluble anti‑inflammatory agents.
