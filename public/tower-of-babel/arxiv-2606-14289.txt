Abstract
. Population-based and distributional optimization methods, from evolution strategies and consensus-based optimization to covariance-matrix adaptation and stochastic gradient methods viewed as distributional dynamics, are widely used for nonconvex or black-box problems, yet their convergence analyses remain fragmented across algorithm-specific techniques. We introduce an operator calculus in which a broad class of such methods, after choosing an appropriate state space and, where necessary, augmenting the state by memory or strategy variables, is described as a composition of three elementary operators ( mutation , selection , and recombination ) acting on probability measures. Under explicit stability and regularity conditions, the composite operator admits a pre-generator whose continuous-time limit is a transport–reaction–jump (TRJ) PDE that preserves the operator splitting. On this foundation we establish a modular Lyapunov principle. If a state-space Lyapunov function both dissipates under the full generator and controls the relevant search-space gauges, then the state-space Lyapunov functional and the induced search errors decay exponentially. The additive generator structure allows dissipation estimates to be assembled operator by operator, providing a toolkit for certifying convergence of composite mean-field algorithms.



1.
Introduction

Population-based and distributional optimization methods form a broad family,
from evolution strategies and consensus-based optimization (CBO) to
covariance-matrix adaptation (CMA-ES), particle swarm methods, and stochastic
gradient algorithms viewed as distributional dynamics

[
2
,
10
,
17
,
15
]
. Despite their empirical success, convergence analyses remain fragmented. Existing approaches include finite-state Markov chains for discrete genetic algorithms
[
32
]
, infinite-population dynamical systems
[
39
]
, runtime analysis on combinatorial domains
[
25
,
6
]
, and algorithm-specific mean-field limits for CBO
[
27
,
13
]
. To the best of our knowledge, no existing theory provides a common analytical language that covers both derivative-free population methods and gradient-based optimizers within a single framework.

This paper introduces such a framework. The central observation is that a wide range of population-based methods, from classical evolutionary algorithms to adaptive gradient methods, share a common structure when viewed at the level of probability measures. Each generation applies a composition of three elementary operators:
mutation
(stochastic perturbation),
selection
(fitness-dependent reweighting), and
recombination
(mixing of parent information). By studying the small-step limit of this composition, we obtain a continuous-time evolution equation, the transport–reaction–jump (TRJ) equation, that decomposes additively into a transport term from mutation, a reaction term from selection, and a jump term from recombination. This decomposition gives each operator a well-defined infinitesimal generator, and the generator of the full algorithm is the sum of the component generators.

On this foundation we build a modular Lyapunov convergence theory. If a
state-space Lyapunov function satisfies a closed dissipation inequality for
the full generator and controls the relevant objective-gap and concentration
gauges on the search space, then the Lyapunov functional and the induced
optimization errors decay exponentially. The key practical advantage is
modularity: because the generator is additive, dissipation estimates can be
verified operator by operator and then closed by summation. This provides a concrete toolkit for certifying convergence of existing algorithms and for analyzing new ones. A practitioner modifying the selection mechanism, for instance, needs to only re-verify the selection generator bound.

An important design feature is the distinction between the algorithm’s internal
state space
and the
search space
where the objective is evaluated. A sampling kernel maps internal states to candidate distributions, placing parametric methods (CMA-ES, NES, EDAs) on equal footing with nonparametric ones (genetic algorithms, evolution strategies, differential evolution). A detailed discussion of related work, including how representative algorithm families (CBO, CMA-ES, recombinative ES, and stochastic gradient methods) fit into the present framework, is given in Appendix
B
. The present submission focuses on the operator calculus and the mean-field Lyapunov theorem; finite-particle discovery bounds and detailed verification of the closed dissipation hypothesis for individual algorithm families are outside the scope of this paper and are deferred to future work.

We consider the problem of minimizing an objective
f\colon{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}

\varepsilon
-optimal sublevel set. We impose two sets of regularity conditions on the landscape.

Assumption 2.1

(Fitness landscape regularity)
.

There exist constants
L_{f},c_{\ell},c_{u}>0
,
s\geq 0
, and
u\geq\ell>0
such that for all
y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
:

(i)

Polynomially weighted Lipschitz continuity:
|f(y)-f(y^{\prime})|\leq L_{f}(1+\|y\|+\|y^{\prime}\|)^{s}\|y-y^{\prime}\|
.

(ii)

Growth control:
f_{*}>-\infty
, and
c_{\ell}(\|y\|^{\ell}-1)\leq f(y)-f_{*}\leq c_{u}(1+\|y\|^{u})
.

Assumption
2.1

[
4
,
27
]
. The polynomial weight in (i) accommodates objectives that grow at infinity;

recovers the globally Lipschitz case, while larger

allows polynomial growth, covering e.g. quartic double-well landscapes. Condition (ii) ensures that sublevel sets are bounded and that the population maintains finite moments, which is essential for propagation-of-chaos estimates (Section
4
).

Assumption 2.2

(Basin regularity)
.

There exist a minimizer
y_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{*}
, a basin radius
R_{0}>0
, a sharpness exponent
\nu\in(0,\infty)
, and constants
\eta,f_{\infty}>0
such that:

(i)

Sharpness near the basin.
\|y-y_{*}\|\leq\eta^{-1}[f(y)-f_{*}]^{\nu}
for all
y\in B_{R_{0}}(y_{*})
.

(ii)

Strict gap.
f(y)-f_{*}>f_{\infty}
for all
y\notin B_{R_{0}}(y_{*})
.

Assumption
2.2
appears in CBO analyses
[
13
]
. Sharpness (i) is an inverse-continuity condition, requiring that within the basin
B_{R_{0}}(y_{*})
an individual whose objective value is close to the optimum must also be spatially close to
y_{*}
. The exponent
\nu
controls the sharpness of this relationship (
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
recovers quadratic growth near the minimum; larger

, isolating the global basin from competing local minima. Together, the two assumptions guarantee that when the Lyapunov functional is small, the population is concentrated near
y_{*}
both in objective value and in space. Figure
1

State space versus search space.

The algorithm maintains a population described by a (possibly unnormalized) measure
\mu_{t}
on an internal state space
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
, which may differ from the search space
{\mathcal{S}}
where
f
is evaluated (in general
d_{x}\neq d
). We fix a moment order
q\geq 2
and write
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
for the cone of nonnegative Borel measures on
{\mathcal{X}}
with finite
q
-th moment and positive total mass. The normalization
\bar{\mu}_{t}:=\mu_{t}/\mu_{t}({\mathcal{X}})
is a probability measure representing the population distribution. A
sampling kernel

K\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{S}})\to[0,1]
maps each internal state
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
to a probability distribution
K(x,\cdot)
on
{\mathcal{S}}
, and the
induced search law
is

(1)

\nu_{t}:=\Pi[\bar{\mu}_{t}],\qquad\Pi[\bar{\mu}](A):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}K(x,A)\,\bar{\mu}(dx).

For nonparametric methods where the state is the candidate solution itself,
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{S}}
and
K(x,\cdot)=\delta_{x}
, so
\nu_{t}=\bar{\mu}_{t}
. For parametric methods such as CMA-ES,
{\mathcal{X}}
is a parameter space (encoding the mean, covariance, and step size of a Gaussian), and
K(x,\cdot)=\mathcal{N}(m(x),\sigma(x)^{2}C(x))
is the corresponding sampling distribution. This two-level structure, in which internal states govern the search distribution, is what allows parametric and nonparametric methods to be treated uniformly. The one-sample
\varepsilon
-success probability
p_{\varepsilon}(t):=\nu_{t}({\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon})
measures the probability that a single draw from the current search law falls within the
\varepsilon
-optimal set; see Figure
2
for an illustration.

Definition 2.3

(Mean-field convergence modes)
.

The mean-field curve achieves:

(MF1)

\varepsilon

p_{\varepsilon}(t)\to 1
for every
\varepsilon>0
;

(MF2)

\int f\,d\nu_{t}\to f_{*}
;

(MF3)

{\mathcal{S}}_{*}=\{y_{*}\}
and
W_{2}(\nu_{t},\delta_{y_{*}})\to 0
, where

denotes the
2

).

The modes form a natural hierarchy. Consensus (MF3) implies objective convergence (MF2), which implies
\varepsilon

2.1
–
2.2
. Consensus is the strongest mode but requires a unique minimizer. Objective convergence guarantees that the expected fitness approaches

but says nothing about spatial concentration. The weakest yet most operationally useful mode is
\varepsilon

{\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon}
. Our Lyapunov framework targets all three modes simultaneously (Theorem
4.1
), without requiring uniqueness of the minimizer or restrictive initializations.

3.
Operator Calculus and PDE Characterization

Rather than tracking individual candidates, we track the probability law of the population on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
. The key device is the
pre-generator
, a nonlinear generalization of the infinitesimal generator from classical semigroup theory
[
12
]
. In the familiar linear setting, the generator of a Markov process encodes the Kolmogorov forward (Fokker–Planck) equation; here, the pre-generator plays the same role for population-level dynamics, but depends on the current population measure
\mu
, a hallmark of McKean–Vlasov-type mean-field models
[
1
,
3
]
. The composition theorem (Theorem
3.2
) shows that the generator of the full algorithmic macro-step is the sum of the component generators, yielding a transport–reaction–jump (TRJ) PDE amenable to Lyapunov and Grönwall arguments.

3.1.
Population metric

An algorithmic macro-step is a composition of
J\geq 1
elementary operators,
T_{\tau}:=T_{\tau}^{(J)}\circ\cdots\circ T_{\tau}^{(1)}
, where each
T_{\tau}^{(j)}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
is one of the three primitives and
\tau>0
is the step size.

To study the small-step limit we need a metric on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
that captures both spatial rearrangement of individuals (caused by mutation and recombination) and changes in total mass (caused by selection, which upweights fit individuals and downweights unfit ones). The
2
-Wasserstein distance
W_{2}

[
1
,
37
]
, the standard metric for gradient flows in optimal transport, captures spatial rearrangement but is defined only for measures of equal total mass, so it cannot see mass changes from selection. We therefore supplement it with the
weighted bounded-Lipschitz distance

(2)

d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu):=\sup\bigl\{|\langle\varphi,\mu-\nu\rangle|\colon\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1\bigr\},

with test-function norm

(3)

\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}:=\sup_{x}\frac{|\varphi(x)|}{1+\|x\|^{q}}\;+\;\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{\|x-y\|\,(1+\|x\|^{q-1}+\|y\|^{q-1})}.

The first term bounds the pointwise size of
\varphi
relative to the
q
-th moment weight, and the second bounds its Lipschitz constant with a compatible polynomial weight. This distance metrizes weak convergence together with convergence of
q
-th moments, and is well defined for measures of different total mass.

The
population metric
combines both:

(4)

d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu):=d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)+W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

Two populations are close in
d_{q,2}^{*}
only when they agree both in their total-mass profile (captured by
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}
, which is sensitive to the mass changes introduced by selection) and in the spatial arrangement of the normalized distribution (captured by
W_{2}
, which reflects the displacements from mutation and recombination). Neither component alone suffices:
W_{2}
cannot distinguish populations that differ only in total mass, while
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}
is insensitive to fine spatial rearrangements.

3.2.
Pre-generators

Rather than studying the operator
T_{\tau}^{(j)}
directly, we work with its infinitesimal effect on population averages. For a test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathcal{D}:=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
denotes the space of bounded, three-times continuously differentiable functions on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:=(\mathcal{D})^{\prime}
denotes its topological dual — so that an element of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is a continuous linear functional on test functions, paired with
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
via
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
(see Appendix
A
) — the
pre-generator
of
T_{\tau}^{(j)}
is the functional

G_{j}[\mu](\varphi)\;:=\;\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0}\frac{\langle\varphi,\,T_{\tau}^{(j)}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau},

which captures the instantaneous rate of change of the average
\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle
under the action of operator
j
. In the classical setting of a Markov diffusion, the generator is the second-order differential operator from Itô’s formula, whose dual is the Fokker–Planck operator
[
12
]
. The notation
G_{j}[\mu]
emphasizes that, unlike this classical case, the pre-generator depends on the entire population
\mu
through law-dependent coefficients (for instance, the consensus point in CBO, the selection pressure, or the population-dependent diffusion matrix), precisely as in McKean–Vlasov SDEs. This nonlinearity is the hallmark of mean-field interaction and is what distinguishes the present framework from standard Markov semigroup theory.

Assumption 3.1

(Composition-admissible operator splitting)
.

Each component
T_{\tau}^{(j)}
satisfies the following uniformity and stability conditions on the moment sublevels

{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}:=\{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})\colon\int(1+\|x\|^{q})\,d\mu\leq r\},

with the intermediate measures defined by
\mu^{(0)}:=\mu
,
\mu^{(j)}:=T_{\tau}^{(j)}[\mu^{(j-1)}]
for
j=1,\dots,J
.

(A1)

Existence of component pre-generators (uniform on sublevels).
For each
j
there exists
G_{j}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
such that, for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
and every
r<\infty
,

\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}}\bigl|\tau^{-1}(\langle\varphi,T_{\tau}^{(j)}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle)-G_{j}[\mu](\varphi)\bigr|\to 0

as
\tau\downarrow 0
.

(A2)

Moment stability (and positivity) along the splitting.
For every
r<\infty
there exist
r^{\prime}(r)\in(r,\infty)
and
\tau_{0}(r)>0
such that
\mu^{(j)}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r^{\prime}(r)}
for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}(r)]
,
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
, and
j=0,1,\dots,J
.

(A3)

Near-identity (small-step) property with component rates.
For every
r<\infty
and each
j
there exist
r^{\prime}(r)\in(r,\infty)
,
\tau_{0}(r)>0
,
a constant
C_{j}(r)<\infty
, and an exponent
\alpha_{j}\in(0,1]
such that for all

\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}(r)]
,

\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r^{\prime}(r)}}d_{q,2}^{*}\!\left(T_{\tau}^{(j)}[\mu],\mu\right)\ \leq\ C_{j}(r)\,\tau^{\alpha_{j}}.

(A4)

Local Lipschitz continuity of the pre-generators in
d_{q,2}^{*}
.
For every
r<\infty
, each
j
, and each fixed
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
there exists
L_{j,\varphi}(r)<\infty
such that
|G_{j}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{j}[\nu](\varphi)|\leq L_{j,\varphi}(r)\,d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu)
for all
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r^{\prime}(r)}
.

Intuition behind (A1)–(A4).

These four conditions are the discrete-time analogue of the standard well-posedness assumptions for an ordinary differential equation with a Lipschitz right-hand side, lifted to the space of populations. Loosely: (A1) says each operator has a well-defined “time derivative” at
\tau=0
, so the discrete step
T_{\tau}^{(j)}
admits a meaningful continuous-time counterpart
G_{j}
(the analogue of the vector field in an ODE). (A2) guarantees that one step does not blow moments up to infinity, i.e., a population with finite spread stays in a bounded region of population space; without this the pre-generator could be unbounded along the trajectory. (A3) controls the step size: a
\tau
-step moves the population by at most
O(\tau^{\alpha_{j}})
in the population metric, so short steps produce small changes and the
\tau\downarrow 0
limit is meaningful. (A4) is the usual Lipschitz condition: the infinitesimal update
G_{j}[\mu]
varies continuously with the population state
\mu
, which prevents trajectories from branching and is what lets us compose operators by summing their generators in Theorem
3.2
.
Verifying them for a concrete operator is a routine calculation that we carry out once and for all for mutation, selection, and recombination in Appendix
C.2
.

3.3.
Composition theorem

The central result of the operator calculus is that the generator of a composed operator is the sum of the component generators, an analogue of the Lie–Trotter formula for nonlinear, measure-valued operators. This additivity is what makes the subsequent Lyapunov analysis modular: each operator’s contribution to dissipation can be analyzed independently and then summed.

Theorem 3.2

(Generator of the composition)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the composite operator
T_{\tau}
admits a pre-generator
G[\mu]:=\sum_{j=1}^{J}G_{j}[\mu]
, in the sense that for every
r<\infty
and every test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
,

\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}}\left|\frac{\langle\varphi,T_{\tau}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}-\sum_{j=1}^{J}G_{j}[\mu](\varphi)\right|\xrightarrow[\tau\downarrow 0]{}0.

The proof (Section
6.1
) telescopes through intermediate measures
\mu^{(j)}:=T_{\tau}^{(j)}[\mu^{(j-1)}]
. For each
j
, the difference
\langle\varphi,\mu^{(j)}\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu^{(j-1)}\rangle
is approximated by
\tau G_{j}[\mu^{(j-1)}](\varphi)
via (A1). The key difficulty is that
G_{j}
is evaluated at
\mu^{(j-1)}
rather than at the original
\mu
; the near-identity property (A3) and Lipschitz continuity (A4) control this drift, yielding an
O(\tau^{\alpha_{\min}})
residual that vanishes in the limit.

3.4.
General evolution equation and induced search-law dynamics

In the continuous-time limit, Theorem
3.2
shows that the measure-valued curve
\mu_{t}
satisfies the
general evolution equation

(5)

\frac{d}{dt}\langle\varphi,\mu_{t}\rangle=G[\mu_{t}](\varphi)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}G_{j}[\mu_{t}](\varphi)

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
. This is the
\mathcal{D}
-weak
form of a nonlinear PDE on the space of measures, generalizing the Kolmogorov forward equation. Here “
\mathcal{D}
-weak” means tested against
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
, which is stronger than the classical distributional (tested against
C_{c}^{\infty}
) notion and adapted to measures that need not admit densities; see Section
6.2
for the precise definition. When only a single diffusion-type operator is present, one recovers the classical Fokker–Planck equation; the additive structure
\sum_{j}G_{j}
makes the multi-operator setting tractable. The following corollary translates the state-space dynamics to the search space.

Corollary 3.3

(Induced evolution on
{\mathcal{S}}
)
.

Let
(\bar{\mu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\subset{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
be a probability-valued solution of (
5
), let
\nu_{t}:=\Pi[\bar{\mu}_{t}]
be the search law (
1
), and let

(K_{*}\phi)(x):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\phi(y)\,K(x,dy)
denote the kernel lift.
Then for every
\phi\in C_{b}({\mathcal{S}})
such that
K_{*}\phi\in\mathcal{D}
, the map
t\mapsto\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle
is locally absolutely continuous and

\frac{d}{dt}\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle=G[\bar{\mu}_{t}](K_{*}\phi)\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\geq 0.

The corollary says that search-space statistics evolve by applying the state-space generator to the lifted observable
K_{*}\phi
whenever the normalized evolution itself is probability-valued. For nonparametric methods the lift is the identity; for CMA-ES, parameter updates propagate through the Gaussian kernel to reshape the candidate distribution. This two-level view is what allows the Lyapunov analysis in Section
4
to work uniformly across both families; see Section
6.3
for details.

3.5.
Canonical operators and the TRJ equation

Equation (
5
) holds for any splitting that satisfies the regularity conditions of Assumption
3.1
. When the resulting solution curve is probability-valued, Corollary
3.3
transfers the same generator to the induced search law. We now specialize to the canonical three-operator splitting that covers the vast majority of population-based optimizers. Mutation perturbs individuals stochastically, selection biases the population toward fitter regions, and recombination mixes information from multiple parents. Each of these defines a measure-valued operator
T_{\tau}^{(j)}[\mu]
that maps a population to its updated version after one
\tau
-step. Figure
3
illustrates the three signatures on a one-dimensional bimodal population: mutation shifts and broadens the density, selection reshapes it toward low-fitness regions, and recombination concentrates mass around the blend of parent modes. We describe the three operators below; their formal definitions, regularity assumptions, and representative examples are collected in Section
5
, and the pre-generator derivations and verification of (A1)–(A4) are in Appendix
C.2
.

Mutation.

Given a population
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
with normalization
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})
, the mutation operator
M_{\tau}
is the pushforward induced by an SDE-style update with population-dependent drift
b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})
and diffusion coefficient
B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})
:

x^{+}=x+\tau\,b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})+\sqrt{\tau}\,B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\,\psi,\qquad M_{\tau}[\mu](A):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{\tau}(x,A;\mu)\,\mu(dx),

where
\psi
is a centered noise increment with
{\mathbb{E}}[\psi\psi^{\top}]=\Sigma
, and
p_{\tau}(x,\cdot;\mu)
is the law of
x^{+}
. The diffusion matrix is
a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu}):=B_{M}\Sigma B_{M}^{\top}
(Fig.
3
, left). This is a single step of a McKean–Vlasov SDE, directly analogous to the noisy gradient step in Langevin dynamics and SGLD:
b_{M}
plays the role of the drift (e.g., a gradient or consensus pull),
a_{M}
of the noise covariance. The dependence on
\bar{\mu}
is the source of the mean-field nonlinearity (in CBO, the drift targets a population-level consensus point; in CMA-ES, drift and diffusion depend on the adapted covariance). Its pre-generator is the second-order operator familiar from Itô calculus,
G_{M}[\mu](\varphi)=\int(\nabla\varphi\cdot b_{M}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}D^{2}\varphi))\,d\mu
. Setting
B_{M}=0
recovers deterministic gradient methods. See Section
5.1
for the formal definition, regularity assumptions, and examples, and Appendix
C.2.1
for the pre-generator verification.

Selection.

The selection operator
S_{\tau}
reweights individuals by fitness without moving them in space, scaling mass by
\exp(-\tau\Phi)
for a (bounded) pressure
\Phi(x;\bar{\mu})
so that fitter individuals gain weight (Fig.
3
, middle):

S_{\tau}[\mu](dx):=e^{-\tau\,\Phi(x;\bar{\mu})}\,\mu(dx),\qquad S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]:=\frac{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}{S_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})}\,S_{\tau}[\mu],

the latter being the mass-preserving
balanced
variant. This is the same mechanism as importance reweighting and the softmax/Gibbs measures used to sharpen distributions in variational inference and policy optimisation; the temperature
\tau
controls selective pressure. The balanced variant produces a replicator-type zero-sum reallocation
[
39
]
that unifies tournament, truncation, utility, and softmax selection. Writing
\bar{\Phi}(\bar{\mu}):=\int\Phi(\cdot;\bar{\mu})\,d\bar{\mu}
for the population-mean pressure, the resulting pre-generator
G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)=-\int(\Phi-\bar{\Phi})\varphi\,d\mu
is a Fisher-Rao-type
reaction
term analogous to the source terms used in unbalanced optimal transport
[
5
]
. See Section
5.2
for the formal definition, regularity assumptions, and examples, and Appendix
C.2.2
for the pre-generator verification.

Recombination.

The recombination operator
R_{\tau}
mixes parent pairs into offspring. It is specified by a normalized
pair law

\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\!\times\!{\mathcal{X}})
with both marginals equal to
\bar{\mu}
, a measurable
mixing rule

b\colon{\mathcal{X}}\!\times\!{\mathcal{X}}\!\times\!\Xi\to{\mathcal{X}}
producing offspring
b(x,y;\xi)
with
\xi\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}
, and an amplitude
\beta(\bar{\mu})>0
. Writing
p_{b}((x,y),A):={\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}(b(x,y;\xi)\in A)
for the offspring kernel, the
instantaneous offspring laws
and the
\tau
-step operator are

\displaystyle R^{\rm bal}[\mu](A)

\displaystyle:=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\!\iint_{{\mathcal{X}}\!\times\!{\mathcal{X}}}\!p_{b}((x,y),A)\,\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}(dx,dy),\qquad R[\mu]:=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,R^{\rm bal}[\mu],

\displaystyle R_{\tau}[\mu]

\displaystyle:=(1-\tau)\mu+\tau R[\mu],

with the balanced variant
R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
obtained by setting
\beta\equiv 1
(Fig.
3
, right). This covers crossover operators in GAs and DE, parameter-update steps in CMA-ES and NES, and has a direct analogue in mixup-style data augmentation, which takes convex combinations of pairs of training examples. Its pre-generator
G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)=\langle\varphi,R[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle
is a
jump
term, structurally reminiscent of Boltzmann-type collision operators in kinetic theory, in the limited sense that both encode nonlocal mass redistribution generated by interaction rules
[
38
]
. Under a Lipschitz condition on the mixing map (Assumption
5.13
),
R_{\tau}
is a bounded perturbation of the identity; for algorithms without crossover this term is absent. See Section
5.3
for the formal definitions, regularity assumptions, and examples, and Appendix
C.2.3
for the pre-generator verification.

A more detailed comparison with related particle and measure-valued approaches is deferred to Table
1
(Appendix
B
).
The purpose of this comparison is not to give an exhaustive survey, but rather to locate the present framework among several adjacent lines of work: classical Wasserstein gradient-flow theory, unbalanced optimal-transport formulations, McKean–Vlasov mean-field limits, consensus-based optimization, and mirror or preconditioned descent methods on spaces of measures.
The main distinction is that our perspective treats the particle dynamics and its limiting transport–reaction structure within a unified variational language, while retaining the algorithmic interpretation needed for optimization.
This viewpoint clarifies which parts of the analysis are inherited from existing metric-gradient-flow theory and which parts are specific to the consensus-driven, nonlocal, and potentially non-mass-preserving nature of the dynamics.

Transport-reaction-jump (TRJ) equation.

For the canonical balanced splitting
T_{\tau}=M_{\tau}\circ R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}\circ S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
— following the conventional EA order “select, then recombine, then mutate”, with the composition read right-to-left — each component satisfies Assumption
3.1
(verified in Appendix
C.2
). Since the composition theorem (Theorem
3.2
) gives the generator as the sum of the component generators, the TRJ equation below is invariant under any reordering of
M_{\tau}
,
S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
,
R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
. Applying the composition theorem yields the following named decomposition.

Theorem 3.4

(Transport–reaction–jump decomposition)
.

Let
q\geq 3
. Suppose the mutation, selection, and recombination
components satisfy the regularity assumptions verified in
Appendix
C.2

(Propositions
C.2
,
C.3
,

C.4
), so that Assumption
3.1

holds for
T_{\tau}=M_{\tau}\circ R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}\circ S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}

on every sublevel
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}
. Then:

(i)

(Additive pre-generator.)

T_{\tau}
has pre-generator
G[\mu]=G_{M}[\mu]+G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu]+G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\mu]
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

(ii)

(Mean-field equation.)
The pre-generator
G
admits the explicit form recorded in Definition
6.1
; equivalently, the associated
\mathcal{D}
-weak evolution is
\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=G[\mu_{t}]
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, that is, the
balanced TRJ equation

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\nabla\!\cdot\!\bigl(b_{M}(\cdot;\bar{\mu}_{t})\mu_{t}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}\textstyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ij}\!\bigl(a_{M,ij}(\cdot;\bar{\mu}_{t})\mu_{t}\bigr)}_{\text{transport--diffusion (mutation)}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\,\bigl(\Phi(\cdot;\mu_{t})-\bar{\Phi}(\mu_{t})\bigr)\mu_{t}}_{\text{reaction (selection)}}+\underbrace{R^{\rm bal}[\mu_{t}]-\mu_{t}}_{\text{jump (recombination)}}

in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=(C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}}))^{\prime}
, in the sense of Definition
6.1
, where
a_{M}=B_{M}\Sigma B_{M}^{\top}
and
\bar{\Phi}(\mu):=\tfrac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int\Phi(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)
.

Equation (
6
) is understood in the
\mathcal{D}
-weak sense, with
\mathcal{D}=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
. Its terms are defined by transposition against
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
as

\langle\varphi,-\nabla\!\cdot(b_{M}\mu_{t})\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu}_{t})\,\mu_{t}(dx)
and

\langle\varphi,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}(a_{M,ij}\mu_{t})\rangle:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl(a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu}_{t})D^{2}\varphi(x)\bigr)\,\mu_{t}(dx)
,
and similarly for the reaction and jump terms. These identities agree with
classical integration by parts whenever sufficient smoothness and boundary
conditions are available; for the bounded, non-compactly supported class

C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
they serve as the definition of the weak action. Finiteness follows
from the moment condition
q\geq 3
, the polynomial growth assumptions on

b_{M},a_{M}
, and the stated boundedness and moment assumptions on selection
and recombination.

The three terms have the usual PDE interpretations. Mutation gives a nonlinear
Fokker–Planck transport–diffusion operator with drift
b_{M}
and covariance

a_{M}
. Selection gives a Fisher–Rao-type replicator reaction driven by the
centered pressure
\Phi-\bar{\Phi}
, where
\bar{\Phi}
enforces mass conservation.
Recombination gives a Boltzmann-type gain–loss jump operator, in the limited
sense that a pairwise kernel replaces parent states by offspring states in weak
form, without implying conservation of kinetic invariants beyond balanced mass.
With only mutation, and for the CBO coefficients, one recovers the nonlinear
mean-field Fokker–Planck equation of CBO
[
27
,
13
]
.
With mutation removed, one obtains a mass-preserving reaction–jump equation
of replicator type, the continuous-time mean-field analogue of
recombination–selection mechanisms in the simple genetic algorithm
formalism
[
39
]
. Thus the TRJ equation
collects mutation, selection, and recombination into a single operator-splitting
structure. Corollary
3.3
gives the induced evolution of the
offspring law
\nu_{t}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
, while Section
6.2

contains the precise
\mathcal{D}
-weak formulation and the scalar
absolute-continuity lemma.

4.
Lyapunov Principle and Convergence

A
search-space gauge

\Psi\colon{\mathcal{S}}\to[0,\infty]
measures optimization error and vanishes on
{\mathcal{S}}_{*}
(e.g.
\Psi(y)=f(y)-f_{*}
or
\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
). A
state-space Lyapunov function

\Upsilon\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to[0,\infty)
tracks dissipation along the internal evolution. The sampling kernel connects the two via the
lifted gauge

\ell_{\Psi}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Psi\,dK(x,\cdot)
. We write
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}):=\int\Upsilon\,d\bar{\mu}
for the state-space Lyapunov functional and
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu}):=\int\ell_{\Psi}\,d\bar{\mu}
for the search error.
In the convergence theorem below we use two gauges in parallel: the objective-gap gauge
\Psi_{f}(y):=f(y)-f_{*}
and, relative to a chosen minimizer
y_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{*}
, the quadratic gauge
\Psi_{*}(y):=\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
. For nonparametric methods (
K=\delta
) one takes
\Upsilon=\Psi
, and the Lyapunov functional and the search error coincide; for parametric methods such as CMA-ES the natural Lyapunov function
\Upsilon(x)=\|m(x)-y_{*}\|^{2}+\sigma(x)^{2}\mathop{\rm Tr}C(x)
equals the expected quadratic search error under the Gaussian sampling kernel, so again
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}=\mathcal{E}_{\Psi_{*}}
.

4.1.
The mean-field convergence theorem

Theorem 4.1

(Mean-field convergence)
.

Let
q\geq 3
, and let
(\bar{\mu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\subset{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
solve the
balanced TRJ equation (
6
) with uniformly controlled
q
-th
moments. Let
\Upsilon\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to[0,\infty)
satisfy

\Upsilon\in C^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
and, for
k=0,1,2,3
,

\|D^{k}\Upsilon(x)\|_{k}\leq C_{k}(1+\|x\|^{(q-k)_{+}}).

Assume also that
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{0})<\infty
. Fix

y_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{*}
and define

\Psi_{f}(y):=f(y)-f_{*}
and
\Psi_{*}(y):=\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
.
Suppose there exist constants
C_{\mathrm{obj}},C_{\mathrm{quad}}\geq 0

and
\lambda>0
such that

(7)

\displaystyle\ell_{\Psi_{f}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{obj}}\,\Upsilon(x)

\displaystyle\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}},

\displaystyle\ell_{\Psi_{*}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{quad}}\,\Upsilon(x)

\displaystyle\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}},

\displaystyle G[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle\forall\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}}).

Then the state-space Lyapunov functional decays exponentially,

(8)

\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})\leq e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{0}),

and, under Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
, the three search-space convergence modes follow:

(a)

\varepsilon
-concentration (MF1).  For every
\varepsilon>0
, with
L_{0}:=L_{f}(1+2\|y_{*}\|+2R_{0})^{s}
and
r_{\varepsilon}:=\min\{R_{0},\varepsilon/L_{0}\}
,

1-p_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq C_{\mathrm{quad}}\,r_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\,e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{0}),

where
p_{\varepsilon}(t):=\nu_{t}({\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon})
is the one-sample
\varepsilon
-success probability;

(b)

Objective-gap decay (MF2).
\int f\,d\nu_{t}-f_{*}\leq C_{\mathrm{obj}}\,e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{0})
;

(c)

Consensus (MF3).
W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{t},\delta_{y_{*}})\leq C_{\mathrm{quad}}\,e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{0})
when
{\mathcal{S}}_{*}=\{y_{*}\}
.

The first two conditions in (
7
) are
search-space compatibility
for the objective-gap and quadratic gauges, respectively, while the third is the
closed dissipation inequality
driving
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
downward at rate
\lambda
. Since
\Upsilon
may be unbounded, the proof of (
8
) requires a truncation argument that approximates it by smooth cutoffs and passes to the limit via dominated convergence (Section
6.4
); the three convergence modes then follow by transferring the decay of
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
to
\nu_{t}=\Pi[\bar{\mu}_{t}]
via the compatibility inequalities, together with Markov’s inequality and the basin geometry (Section
6.4
).

Interpretation of the hypotheses.

The two compatibility inequalities are statements about the sampling kernel alone. They require that the lifted search errors are dominated, pointwise on the state space, by the internal Lyapunov function, and they calibrate the constants
C_{\mathrm{obj}}
and
C_{\mathrm{quad}}
appearing in the final bounds; they fail precisely when progress of the internal state need not translate into better candidates, which is the first thing to check when a new parametrization is introduced. The dissipation inequality is, by contrast, a statement about the dynamics. Along any solution of the TRJ equation,
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})=G[\bar{\mu}_{t}](\Upsilon)
for a.e.
t
(Lemma
6.5
), so the inequality requires the instantaneous decrease of the Lyapunov functional to dominate a multiple of the functional itself; this is a population-level analogue of the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (gradient-domination) condition of smooth optimization, and the exponential decay then follows from Grönwall’s lemma. For pure diffusions the same mechanism is the entropy–entropy-production argument behind the exponential equilibration of Fokker–Planck equations
[
18
,
1
]
; for CBO-type transport–diffusion dynamics, inequalities of this form underlie the variance-decay estimates of
[
4
,
13
]
. Theorem
4.1
isolates the mechanism from any particular algorithm: it reduces the convergence problem to the verification of one functional inequality for the full generator
G=G_{M}+G_{S}^{\rm bal}+G_{R}^{\rm bal}
. Although the dissipation condition is stated for all
\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
, the proof invokes it only along the solution curve, so it suffices to verify it on any class of laws known to contain the trajectory.

It is also worth recording which assumption enters where. The exponential decay (
8
) uses only the closed inequality together with the growth and moment conditions on
\Upsilon
and
(\bar{\mu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
; no landscape assumptions are involved at this stage. Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
enter only when the state-space decay is converted into the three search-space modes: the objective-gap mode (MF2) uses only the finiteness of
f_{*}
, the conversion radius
r_{\varepsilon}=\min\{R_{0},\varepsilon/L_{0}\}
in (MF1) is built from the Lipschitz envelope and the basin radius, and uniqueness of the minimizer is needed only for consensus (MF3).

Quantitative reading.

All three modes decay at the common rate
\lambda
and differ only in the gauge and in the multiplicative constant. The
\varepsilon
-concentration bound has a direct operational meaning:
p_{\varepsilon}(t)
is the probability that a single draw from the current search law is
\varepsilon
-optimal, so solving
1-p_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\delta
shows that

t\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\log\!\Bigl(\frac{C_{\mathrm{quad}}\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{0})}{r_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\delta}\Bigr)

suffices for one sample from
\nu_{t}
to be
\varepsilon
-optimal with probability at least
1-\delta
. The accuracy
\varepsilon
and the confidence
\delta
enter only logarithmically (the former through the conversion radius
r_{\varepsilon}
), the initialization enters through
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{0})
, and the dissipation rate
\lambda
sets the time scale of the search. This is also the prediction tested numerically in Section
4.3
: the quantities plotted in Figure
4
are instances of
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
for CBO, CMA-ES, and a recombinative ES, and the slopes of the semi-log profiles are empirical estimates of the corresponding rates
\lambda
.

4.2.
Modular verification of the dissipation condition

A crucial feature is that the closed dissipation condition in (
7
) can be verified operator by operator: if each canonical operator satisfies
G_{j}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-\lambda_{j}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}+c_{j}\mathfrak{b}_{j}
with bias functionals
\mathfrak{b}_{j}\leq\kappa_{j}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
, then
G[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-\lambda\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu})
with
\lambda=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}-\sum_{j}c_{j}\kappa_{j}>0
(Proposition
4.2
, proved in Section
6.4.2
). This modularity is what turns Theorem
4.1
into a practical verification toolkit: dissipation evidence assembled separately for mutation, selection, and recombination can be combined to certify convergence of the composite algorithm.

Proposition 4.2

(Operator-wise closure)
.

Suppose that the generator of each canonical operator can be bounded separately:

G_{M}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-\lambda_{M}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}+c_{M}\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon}
,

G_{S}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-\lambda_{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
,

G_{R}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-\lambda_{R}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
,
where
\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon}\geq 0
is a bias functional satisfying
\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon}\leq\kappa_{\Upsilon}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
.
If
\lambda:=\lambda_{M}+\lambda_{S}+\lambda_{R}-c_{M}\kappa_{\Upsilon}>0
, then the dissipation bound
G[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-\lambda\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu})
in (
7
) holds with rate
\lambda
.

Reading the dissipation budget.

Proposition
4.2
turns the additive generator structure into an accounting identity: each operator contributes its own rate, and the rates add. The individual bounds also have transparent meanings for the canonical operators of Section
3.5
, which we illustrate for the quadratic choice
\Upsilon(x)=\|x-y_{*}\|^{2}
in the nonparametric setting
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{S}}
. For mutation,

G_{M}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(2\langle x-y_{*},\,b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\rangle+\mathop{\rm Tr}a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\bigr)\,\bar{\mu}(dx),

so a drift aligned with
y_{*}-x
contributes contraction, while the diffusion contributes the nonnegative trace term: this is the expansive part collected by the bias functional
\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon}
. For balanced selection,
G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)=-\int(\Phi-\bar{\Phi})\Upsilon\,d\bar{\mu}
is the negative covariance, under
\bar{\mu}
, of the selection pressure and the Lyapunov value: selection dissipates exactly when states far from the minimizer carry above-average pressure, that is, when the fitness signal “sees” the Lyapunov function. For recombination with uniform independent pairing and midpoint crossover (the rule illustrated in Figure
3
), a one-line computation gives
G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)=-\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm Var}(\bar{\mu})
, where
{\rm Var}(\bar{\mu}):=\int\|x-m_{\bar{\mu}}\|^{2}\,\bar{\mu}(dx)
and
m_{\bar{\mu}}:=\int x\,\bar{\mu}(dx)
: averaging-type mixing dissipates half of the population variance per unit jump rate, but leaves the offset
\|m_{\bar{\mu}}-y_{*}\|^{2}
untouched, which must therefore be removed by mutation or selection.

The closure condition
\lambda=\lambda_{M}+\lambda_{S}+\lambda_{R}-c_{M}\kappa_{\Upsilon}>0
is an exploration–exploitation trade-off in quantitative form: the certificate survives as long as the combined contraction outweighs the amplified exploration bias
c_{M}\kappa_{\Upsilon}
. The hypothesis
\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon}\leq\kappa_{\Upsilon}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
asks the injected noise to be self-limiting. In CBO, for instance, the noise amplitude is proportional to the distance to the consensus point, so the trace term above is dominated by a multiple of
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
once the consensus point is quantitatively controlled — the content of the Laplace-principle estimates in the CBO convergence literature
[
27
,
13
]
. Verifying the three operator-wise bounds with explicit constants for concrete algorithm families is the algorithm-dependent step discussed in Section
7
; the framework reduces it to three decoupled calculations.

5.
Canonical Operators: Definitions, Regularity, and Examples

This section collects the formal definitions, regularity assumptions, and representative examples for the canonical mutation, selection, and recombination operators introduced in Section
3.5
. The verification that each operator satisfies conditions (A1)–(A4) of Assumption
3.1
is carried out in Appendix
C.2
.

Each subsection follows the same template: we define the operator as a map on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
, isolate a regularity class — diffusion-admissible mutation kernels (Assumption
5.2
), bounded-pressure selection rates (Assumption
5.7
), and Lipschitz recombination data (Assumption
5.13
) — discuss the induced generator, and illustrate the class with standard algorithmic mechanisms. The assumptions are calibrated to a single purpose: they are what is needed to verify, operator by operator, the four conditions (A1)–(A4) of Assumption
3.1
. This verification is carried out in Propositions
C.2
,
C.3
, and

). The growth and boundedness parts of the assumptions are used a second time in the convergence analysis, where they justify integrating the TRJ equation against polynomially growing Lyapunov functions (Lemma

5.1.
Mutation operator

Mutation is the only canonical operator that transports mass continuously through the state space, and it is modelled accordingly as one step of a Markov transition whose kernel may depend on the current population. The diffusion-admissible form below should be read as an Euler–Maruyama step of a McKean–Vlasov SDE: over a step of length
\tau
the drift acts at order
\tau
and the noise at order
\sqrt{\tau}
. This parabolic scaling is what makes drift and noise contribute at the same order to the pre-generator, producing the second-order transport–diffusion term of the TRJ equation (
6
); any other scaling would let one of the two effects vanish or dominate in the limit. The population dependence of the coefficients is essential for the intended applications: it encodes, for example, the consensus pull of CBO and the adapted covariance of CMA-ES-type flows.

Definition 5.1

(Mutation kernel and operator)
.

A
mutation kernel family
is a collection
\{p_{\tau}(x,\cdot;\mu)\}_{\tau>0}
of Markov kernels on
{\mathcal{X}}
, parametrised by the current population
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
. The associated
mutation operator

M_{\tau}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
is the mass-preserving Markov transition

(9)

M_{\tau}[\mu](A)\;:=\;\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{\tau}(x,A;\mu)\,\mu(dx),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{X}}),

or equivalently, in integrated form,
\langle\varphi,M_{\tau}[\mu]\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!\!\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(y)\,p_{\tau}(x,dy;\mu)\,\mu(dx)
for every bounded measurable
\varphi
.

Assumption 5.2

(Diffusion-admissible mutation kernel)
.

Let
\psi
be a centered noise increment on a probability space
(\Xi,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
with covariance
{\mathbb{E}}[\psi\psi^{\top}]=\Sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{\prime}\times d^{\prime}}
symmetric and positive semidefinite, and
{\mathbb{E}}\|\psi\|^{3}<\infty
. The mutation kernel family
\{p_{\tau}(x,\cdot;\mu)\}_{\tau>0}
is
diffusion-admissible on
{\mathcal{M}}_{3,+}^{r}
if there exist coefficient maps
b_{M}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathscr{P}}_{3}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
(drift) and
B_{M}\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathscr{P}}_{3}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}\times d^{\prime}}
(diffusion coefficient) such that
p_{\tau}(x,\cdot;\mu)
is the law of

(10)

m_{\tau}(x;\mu,\xi):=x+\tau\,b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})+\sqrt{\tau}\,B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\,\psi(\xi),

with the coefficients satisfying, on each
{\mathcal{M}}_{3,+}^{r}
with constants depending on
r
:

(i)

Linear growth.
\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|_{F}\leq C(r)\,(1+\|x\|)
.

(ii)

Joint Lipschitz continuity. There exists
L(r)<\infty
such that

\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-b_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\leq L(r)\bigl(\|x-y\|+W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\bigr)

for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{3}({\mathcal{X}})
.

Role of the assumptions.

The two conditions play distinct roles in the verification of (A1)–(A4). The linear-growth bound (i), combined with the moment conditions on the noise, is the driving estimate behind the pre-generator limit, the moment stability, and the near-identity bounds, that is, (A1)–(A3) of Proposition
C.2
; the third absolute moment
{\mathbb{E}}\|\psi\|^{3}<\infty
controls the cubic Taylor remainder in the pre-generator limit, and the choice of the test class
\mathcal{D}=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
is dictated by precisely this third-order expansion. The joint Lipschitz condition (ii) is needed only for the local Lipschitz continuity of the pre-generator (A4), the property that allows mutation to be composed with the other operators in Theorem
3.2
; the pre-generator identity itself holds without it (see the remark preceding the proof of Proposition
C.2
). Outside the operator calculus, the growth bound (i) is used once more to dominate the mutation term when the TRJ equation is tested against unbounded Lyapunov functions in the proof of Theorem
4.1
(Lemma
6.5
).

Transport–diffusion (mutation).

The mutation contribution
\int\nabla\varphi\cdot b_{M}\,d\mu_{t}+{}

\tfrac{1}{2}\int\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}D^{2}\varphi)\,d\mu_{t}
is a
Fokker–Planck operator
acting on the population measure. The first-order term
\nabla\varphi\cdot b_{M}
encodes deterministic drift: in CBO, this drift steers each particle toward the weighted consensus point
m_{\beta}(\bar{\mu}_{t})
; in CMA-ES, it encodes the mean-update rule; in SGD, it is the negative gradient
-\nabla f
. The second-order term
\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}D^{2}\varphi)
encodes stochastic exploration: in CBO, this is the isotropic noise scaled by the distance to consensus; in CMA-ES, it is the adapted covariance; in SGD with gradient noise, it is the mini-batch variance. When
a_{M}=0
, the mutation contribution reduces to a pure transport equation (first-order PDE), as in deterministic optimization.

The population dependence of
b_{M}
and
a_{M}
on
\bar{\mu}_{t}
is what makes the equation a
nonlinear
PDE (of McKean–Vlasov type): the drift and diffusion felt by each individual depend on where the rest of the population sits. This is the mathematical manifestation of the collective intelligence that distinguishes population-based methods from independent random search.

Naming convention for the next examples.

The labels in the next three examples are analytical descriptors of the induced generator, not standard evolutionary-computation terminology. In particular, the first example is the usual Gaussian mutation of evolution strategies
[
28
,
2
,
10
]
; the word “Boltzmann” only refers to the heat-semigroup/Boltzmann-entropy interpretation familiar from Wasserstein gradient flows
[
18
,
1
]
. Likewise, “KL mutation” below means a Langevin mutation whose population-level Fokker–Planck equation dissipates
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\,\|\,\gamma)
; it is not meant as a separate standard EA operator.

Example 5.3

(Gaussian heat (Boltzmann) mutation)
.

Assume for simplicity that
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. A mutation adding centered Gaussian noise with variance scale
2\varepsilon\tau
is obtained from

m_{\tau}^{\rm heat}(x;\xi)=x+\sqrt{2\varepsilon\tau}\,\psi(\xi),\qquad{\mathbb{E}}\psi=0,\qquad{\mathbb{E}}[\psi\psi^{\top}]=\Sigma.

In the diffusion-admissible form (
10
), this corresponds to

b_{M}^{\rm heat}(x;\bar{\mu})=0,\qquad B_{M}^{\rm heat}(x;\bar{\mu})=\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,I_{d}.

The coefficients are constant, hence Assumption
5.2
holds with constants independent of
r
, for instance
C(r)=\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\|I_{d}\|_{F}
and
L(r)=0
. The operator is mass-preserving and its pre-generator is

G_{M}^{\rm heat}[\mu](\varphi)=\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl(\Sigma D^{2}\varphi(x)\bigr)\,\mu(dx).

When
\Sigma=I_{d}
, this reduces to
G_{M}^{\rm heat}[\mu](\varphi)=\varepsilon\int\Delta\varphi\,d\mu
, the weak generator of the heat equation
\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\varepsilon\Delta\mu_{t}
. In density form, this is the
W_{2}
-gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy
\rho\mapsto\int\rho\log\rho
, which motivates the parenthetical terminology. In evolutionary-computation language the same step should simply be called Gaussian mutation.

Example 5.4

(Langevin mutation)
.

Let
\gamma(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{-U(x)}\,dx
be a reference probability measure with
U\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and assume that
\nabla U
is globally Lipschitz, hence of at most linear growth. For a fixed positive semidefinite preconditioner
\Sigma
, define

m_{\tau}^{\rm KL}(x;\xi):=x-\varepsilon\tau\,\Sigma\nabla U(x)+\sqrt{2\varepsilon\tau}\,\psi(\xi),\qquad{\mathbb{E}}\psi=0,\qquad{\mathbb{E}}[\psi\psi^{\top}]=\Sigma.

Equivalently, in (
10
),

b_{M}^{\rm KL}(x;\bar{\mu})=-\varepsilon\,\Sigma\nabla U(x),\qquad B_{M}^{\rm KL}(x;\bar{\mu})=\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,I_{d}.

The linear-growth and Lipschitz conditions in Assumption
5.2
follow from the corresponding assumptions on
\nabla U
. The induced operator is mass-preserving and has pre-generator

G_{M}^{\rm KL}[\mu](\varphi)=\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl(\Sigma D^{2}\varphi(x)\bigr)\,\mu(dx)-\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot\Sigma\nabla U(x)\,\mu(dx).

For
\Sigma=I_{d}
the forward equation is

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\varepsilon\Delta\rho_{t}+\varepsilon\nabla\!\cdot(\rho_{t}\nabla U)=\varepsilon\nabla\!\cdot\bigl(\rho_{t}\nabla\log(\rho_{t}/\gamma)\bigr),

which is the Wasserstein gradient flow of
\mathrm{KL}(\rho\,\|\,\gamma)

[
18
,
1
]
. For general constant
\Sigma
, the same formula gives the anisotropic or preconditioned Langevin equation, i.e. the corresponding gradient flow in the preconditioned transport geometry. Replacing
\nabla U
by an unbiased mini-batch estimator gives the stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics step used in Bayesian learning
[
40
]
.

Example 5.5

(Consensus-based optimization (CBO) mutation)
.

Let
f\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the objective, fix
\alpha,\lambda,\sigma>0
, and let
h\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]
be a bounded modulation function, often
h\equiv 1
or a smooth approximation of the Heaviside function. For
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}})
, define the Boltzmann-weighted consensus point

v_{\alpha}(\mu):=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{X}}x\,e^{-\alpha f(x)}\,\mu(dx)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\,\mu(dx)}.

The CBO mutation map is

m_{\tau}^{\rm CBO}(x;\mu,\xi):=x-\tau\lambda\bigl(x-v_{\alpha}(\mu)\bigr)h\!\bigl(f(x)-f(v_{\alpha}(\mu))\bigr)+\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,\|x-v_{\alpha}(\mu)\|\,\psi(\xi).

For identity-covariance noise, this corresponds to

b_{M}^{\rm CBO}(x;\bar{\mu})=-\lambda h\!\bigl(f(x)-f(v_{\alpha}(\bar{\mu}))\bigr)\bigl(x-v_{\alpha}(\bar{\mu})\bigr),\qquad B_{M}^{\rm CBO}(x;\bar{\mu})=\sigma\|x-v_{\alpha}(\bar{\mu})\|I_{d}.

The resulting mass-preserving pre-generator is

\displaystyle G_{M}^{\rm CBO}[\mu](\varphi)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{X}}h\!\bigl(f(x)-f(v_{\alpha}(\mu))\bigr)\bigl(x-v_{\alpha}(\mu)\bigr)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x-v_{\alpha}(\mu)\|^{2}\,\Delta\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx).

For a general noise covariance
\Sigma
, the Laplacian in the last display should be replaced by
\mathop{\rm Tr}(\Sigma D^{2}\varphi)
. The pre-generator identity itself only uses boundedness of
h
and the moment bounds; the full diffusion-admissibility assumptions used in Proposition
C.2
require additional regularity, for instance a Lipschitz
h
, enough regularity of
f
, and uniform control of
v_{\alpha}
on moment sublevels. Under these standard CBO regularity conditions the mean-field equation reduces to the nonlinear CBO Fokker–Planck equation
[
27
,
13
]
.

5.2.
Selection operator

Selection changes the weights of individuals without moving them in space. The exponential form of the multiplier is not an arbitrary choice: for a frozen pressure it composes consistently across time steps,
s_{\tau}\,s_{\tau^{\prime}}=s_{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}
, it keeps all weights strictly positive, and its small-
\tau
expansion produces the linear reaction term of the TRJ equation. Allowing the pressure
\Phi
to depend on the population is what brings rank-based, tournament, and truncation-type schemes — whose pressures are functionals of the empirical fitness distribution — into the same framework. The balanced variant renormalizes total mass and thereby turns selection into a zero-sum reallocation: individuals compete for a fixed mass budget, which is the measure-level version of the replicator picture.

Definition 5.6

(Selection rate, multiplier, and selection operator)
.

Let
\Phi\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable
selection rate
, and define the associated
selection multiplier

(11)

s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\;:=\;\exp\bigl(-\tau\,\Phi(x;\mu)\bigr),\qquad x\in{\mathcal{X}},\ \mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}}),\ \tau>0.

The associated (unbalanced)
selection operator

S_{\tau}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
acts by pointwise reweighting,

(12)

S_{\tau}[\mu](dx)\;:=\;s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx),

and the
balanced (mass-preserving) variant
is obtained by renormalization,

S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]\;:=\;\frac{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}{S_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})}\,S_{\tau}[\mu],\qquad S_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})>0.

Assumption 5.7

(Selection-rate assumptions)
.

Fix
q\geq 2
. The selection rate
\Phi\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
in Definition
5.6
is measurable and satisfies:

(i)

Bounded pressure. For every
r<\infty
there exists
C_{\Phi}(r)<\infty
such that

|\Phi(x;\mu)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
.

(ii)

Selection-rate regularity.
\Phi(\cdot\,;\mu)
is measurable in
x
, and for each
r<\infty
there exists
L_{\Phi}(r)<\infty
such that

\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}|\Phi(x;\mu)-\Phi(x;\nu)|\leq L_{\Phi}(r)\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\quad\forall\,\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r},

where
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})
and
\bar{\nu}:=\nu/\nu({\mathcal{X}})
.

(iii)

Spatial regularity of the selection rate. For every
r<\infty
there exists
K_{\Phi}(r)<\infty
such that for all
\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
and all
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

|\Phi(x;\nu)-\Phi(y;\nu)|\leq K_{\Phi}(r)\,\|x-y\|\,\bigl(1+\|x\|^{q-1}+\|y\|^{q-1}\bigr).

Role of the assumptions.

The three conditions are calibrated to Proposition
C.3
. Bounded pressure (i) alone yields the pre-generator, the moment stability, and the near-identity estimates (A1)–(A3): it confines the multipliers between the deterministic bounds
e^{\pm\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}
, so mass and moments can change only at a controlled exponential rate. The two regularity conditions are needed only for the local Lipschitz continuity (A4): condition (ii) controls how the pressure responds to a
W_{2}
-perturbation of the population, and condition (iii) controls how it varies across the state space; together they make the reaction term
-(\Phi-\bar{\Phi})\mu
Lipschitz in the population metric
d_{q,2}^{*}
. This division of labour is visible in the examples below: a bounded Lipschitz objective pressure such as
\Phi=\beta f
satisfies all three conditions, whereas exact rank-based pressures satisfy (i) — hence (A1)–(A3) — but generally violate the spatial regularity (iii) for empirical populations, which is why the rank-stability hypothesis (
13
) and the smoothed rank are introduced below. Boundedness of
\Phi
is also what the convergence analysis uses to control the reaction term against unbounded Lyapunov functions (Lemma
6.5
).

The sign convention in (
56
) is simple: points with
\Phi>\bar{\Phi}
lose relative weight, while points with
\Phi<\bar{\Phi}
gain relative weight. This is the only connection to Fisher–Rao reaction geometry that is used here. A variational Fisher–Rao gradient flow has a birth–death source of the form
-\rho(\delta\mathcal{F}/\delta\rho-\langle\delta\mathcal{F}/\delta\rho\rangle_{\rho})
, whereas the present selection operator merely has the same centered source structure. The function
\Phi
is specified by the algorithm and need not be a variational derivative
[
5
,
22
]
.

It is useful to separate two meanings of selection. An
objective-value
rule depends on the numerical values of the objective, whereas a
rank-based
rule depends only on their ordering and is invariant under strictly increasing transformations of the objective. The words “exponential”, “Boltzmann”, and “Gibbs” below are descriptive labels for the analytic form of the multiplier, not conventions that we import from evolutionary-algorithm terminology. Rank-based selection, tournament selection, and
(\mu,\lambda)
truncation selection are standard in evolutionary-algorithm analysis; NES and CMA-ES use rank utilities or fitness shaping for closely related invariance reasons
[
6
,
41
,
15
]
. The following examples should therefore be read as continuous-time, measure-level analogues of familiar finite-population rules, not as claims that the finite empirical rank map itself is smooth.

Example 5.8

(Exponential objective-value reweighting)
.

Let
f\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the objective to be minimized. If
f
is bounded and Lipschitz on
{\mathcal{X}}
, define

\Phi(x;\bar{\mu}):=\beta f(x),\qquad\beta>0.

Equivalently, one may replace
f
by
f-\int f\,d\bar{\mu}
, since the balanced generator subtracts
\bar{\Phi}
anyway. Then
s_{\tau}(x;\bar{\mu})=\exp(-\tau\beta f(x))
, so low-objective points receive larger weight. The assumptions of Proposition
C.3
are immediate: boundedness of
f
gives boundedness of
\Phi
,
\Phi
is independent of the population law so Assumption
5.7
(ii) holds with
L_{\Phi}=0
, and Lipschitz continuity of
f
gives the spatial regularity in (iii). If
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
f
is unbounded, this example is outside the bounded-log-multiplier framework as stated. A direct admissible variant is obtained by using a bounded clipped or saturated version of
f
, for instance

\Phi_{K}(x;\bar{\mu}):=\beta\,\operatorname{clip}(f(x)-c,-K,K),

with fixed
K<\infty
and a fixed reference level
c
. The Boltzmann or Gibbs wording sometimes used for such formulas refers only to the exponential form of the multiplier. By contrast, standard CBO uses exponential weights inside the consensus point; unless an explicit reweighting step is added, that mechanism belongs to the mutation or drift component in the present splitting rather than to a separate selection operator.

We next record three rank-based examples. For a normalized population
\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
, set

F_{\bar{\mu}}(a):=\bar{\mu}\{x:f(x)\leq a\},\qquad u_{\bar{\mu}}(x):=F_{\bar{\mu}}(f(x))\in[0,1].

The map
u_{\bar{\mu}}
is the normalized rank of
x
, with small values corresponding to better objective values. Exact ranks are often discontinuous, especially for empirical measures. To obtain the full local-Lipschitz conclusion (A4), one must either assume the rank-stability estimate

(13)

\sup_{a\in{\mathbb{R}}}|F_{\bar{\mu}}(a)-F_{\bar{\nu}}(a)|\leq C_{\rm rk}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\qquad\text{on the relevant moment ball,}

or replace
F_{\bar{\mu}}
by a smoothed rank function

F_{\bar{\mu}}^{\eta}(a):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\kappa_{\eta}(a-f(y))\,\bar{\mu}(dy),\qquad u_{\bar{\mu}}^{\eta}(x):=F_{\bar{\mu}}^{\eta}(f(x)),

where
\kappa_{\eta}
is bounded and Lipschitz. If
f
is Lipschitz, then

\sup_{a}|F_{\bar{\mu}}^{\eta}(a)-F_{\bar{\nu}}^{\eta}(a)|\leq\operatorname{Lip}(\kappa_{\eta})\operatorname{Lip}(f)W_{1}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\leq\operatorname{Lip}(\kappa_{\eta})\operatorname{Lip}(f)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}),

so Assumption
5.7
(ii) follows. Moreover,
x\mapsto u_{\bar{\mu}}^{\eta}(x)
is uniformly Lipschitz with constant at most
\operatorname{Lip}(\kappa_{\eta})\operatorname{Lip}(f)
, which gives Assumption
5.7
(iii) after composition with a Lipschitz rank utility. Without such a condition, the examples below still satisfy the bounded-pressure hypotheses needed for (A1)–(A3), but not the full local-Lipschitz assumption (A4).

Example 5.9

(Rank-utility selection)
.

Choose a bounded, nonincreasing utility
U\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
and let
\bar{U}:=\int_{0}^{1}U(v)\,dv
. Assume
U\not\equiv\bar{U}
and define

\chi(u):=\frac{U(u)-\bar{U}}{\|U-\bar{U}\|_{\infty}}\in[-1,1],\qquad\Phi(x;\bar{\mu}):=-\chi(u_{\bar{\mu}}(x)).

Then
s_{\tau}(x;\bar{\mu})=\exp(\tau\chi(u_{\bar{\mu}}(x)))
, and better ranks receive larger multipliers when
U
is larger near
u=0
. Since
|\Phi|\leq 1
, Proposition
C.3
gives the pre-generator, moment stability, and near-identity estimate. The local-Lipschitz statement additionally requires regularity of the rank map: for exact ranks one can assume (
13
) and uniform spatial Lipschitz continuity of
x\mapsto F_{\bar{\mu}}(f(x))
, for instance under a uniform density bound for the fitness pushforward together with Lipschitz
f
; alternatively, use the smoothed rank
u^{\eta}_{\bar{\mu}}
. If
U
and
f
are Lipschitz, the smoothed-rank construction above gives both the population and spatial regularity required in Assumption
5.7
(ii)–(iii). For empirical exact ranks,
F_{\bar{\mu}}
has jumps and the spatial regularity in (iii) generally fails; then only (A1)–(A3) are justified within the present proposition. This is the mean-field analogue of the rank-utility weights used in CMA-ES and in NES fitness shaping
[
41
,
15
]
.

Example 5.10

(Smoothed tournament selection)
.

Assume first that the fitness pushforward
f_{\#}\bar{\mu}
has a continuous CDF, so
u_{\bar{\mu}}(X)
is uniform on
[0,1]
for
X\sim\bar{\mu}
. In an
m
-tournament, the best rank among
m
independent draws has density

g_{m}(u)=m(1-u)^{m-1},\qquad u\in[0,1].

A strictly positive smoothed tournament multiplier is

w_{m}(u):=\delta+(1-\delta)g_{m}(u),\qquad\delta\in(0,1),

and we set
\Phi(x;\bar{\mu}):=-\log w_{m}(u_{\bar{\mu}}(x))
. For fixed
m
and
\delta
,
\Phi
is bounded because
\delta\leq w_{m}(u)\leq\delta+(1-\delta)m
. Hence Proposition
C.3
gives (A1)–(A3). Since
u\mapsto-\log w_{m}(u)
is Lipschitz for fixed
(m,\delta)
, the full local-Lipschitz conclusion follows under the same rank-stability or smoothing assumptions described above. The unsmoothed case
\delta=0
is outside the bounded-pressure framework for
m>1
, because
w_{m}(1)=0
and the logarithmic pressure becomes unbounded. Tournament and ranking selection are standard finite-population mechanisms in runtime analysis of genetic algorithms
[
6
]
.

Example 5.11

(Smoothed approximation of truncation selection)
.

Hard truncation selects the best fraction
\theta\in(0,1)
of the population and corresponds formally to a multiplier proportional to
\mathbf{1}\{u_{\bar{\mu}}(x)\leq\theta\}
, which is neither strictly positive nor represented by a bounded function
\Phi=-\log w
. A bounded-log-multiplier approximation is obtained by fixing
T>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
and defining

h_{T}(u):=\frac{1}{1+\exp((u-\theta)/T)},\qquad w_{\theta,T,\delta}(u):=\delta+(1-\delta)h_{T}(u),

Equivalently,
h_{T}(u)=\sigma((\theta-u)/T)
with
\sigma(r)=(1+e^{-r})^{-1}
. Thus
h_{T}
is only a smooth approximation to the hard cutoff
\mathbf{1}\{u\leq\theta\}
, not a separate convention from evolutionary-algorithm terminology. It replaces the discontinuity at the truncation boundary by a transition layer of width
O(T)
; ranks well below
\theta
receive a multiplier close to one, whereas ranks well above
\theta
receive a multiplier close to zero. The floor
\delta
is an analytic device that prevents complete deletion of mass, so that
w_{\theta,T,\delta}
stays strictly positive and
\Phi=-\log w_{\theta,T,\delta}
remains bounded. In the formal limit
T\downarrow 0
, followed by
\delta\downarrow 0
after balanced normalization, this recovers the usual hard truncation multiplier.
We then set

\Phi(x;\bar{\mu}):=-\log w_{\theta,T,\delta}(u_{\bar{\mu}}(x)),\qquad s_{\tau}(x;\bar{\mu})=w_{\theta,T,\delta}(u_{\bar{\mu}}(x))^{\tau}.

For fixed
(\theta,T,\delta)
,
\Phi
is bounded by
|\Phi|\leq|\log\delta|
and is Lipschitz as a function of
u
, with constants deteriorating as
T\downarrow 0
or
\delta\downarrow 0
. Thus Proposition
C.3
gives (A1)–(A3), and (A4) follows under the rank-stability or smoothed-rank hypotheses above. The exact
(\mu,\lambda)
truncation rule is recovered only as a singular limit, for instance
T\downarrow 0
and
\delta\downarrow 0
after balanced normalization; that limit is deliberately outside the bounded-log-multiplier proposition and should be treated separately if needed. Truncation selection is the standard survivor-selection mechanism of evolution strategies
[
28
,
2
,
10
]
.

5.3.
Recombination operator

Recombination mixes parents into offspring. It is specified by two components: a mating mechanism that selects parent pairs, and a mixing rule that produces an offspring from each pair. This separation mirrors algorithmic practice, where mating selection and the crossover operator are chosen independently, and it isolates the two places where regularity can fail. Requiring both marginals of the pair law to equal
\bar{\mu}
expresses that mating by itself does not bias the parent distribution; fitness-biased mate choice should instead be modelled inside the selection operator (see the discussion of bistochasticity at the end of this subsection). The
\tau
-step operator below performs a recombination event with probability
\tau
, so recombination is a jump mechanism: unlike mutation, it transports mass over finite distances at a finite rate, and its pre-generator is the plain difference
\langle\varphi,R[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle
with zero remainder in
\tau
(Proposition
C.4
(i)), which makes recombination the simplest of the three operators in the limit
\tau\downarrow 0
.

Definition 5.12

(Mating kernel and pair law)
.

Suppose
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2,+}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\mu({\mathcal{X}})>0
, and let
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}})
. We call a bounded, non-negative, symmetric function
\Lambda(\cdot,\cdot;\bar{\mu})
a
mating kernel
, and define the
unnormalized pair measure

\Gamma^{\mu}(dx,dy):=\Lambda(x,y;\bar{\mu})\,\bar{\mu}(dx)\,\bar{\mu}(dy).

Let
\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}:=\Gamma^{\mu}/\Gamma^{\mu}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
denote the corresponding
normalized pair law
. Finally, let
\beta\colon{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}})\to(0,\infty)
be the
amplitude
(the expected number of matings per step).

Assumption 5.13

(Recombination admissibility)
.

Fix
q\geq 2
. Let
(\Xi,\mathscr{F},{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi})
be a probability space, and let
b\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\times\Xi\to{\mathcal{X}}
be a measurable map. Assume that:

(i)

Pair-law compatibility and Lipschitz continuity.
For every
\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Lambda(x,y;\bar{\mu})\,\bar{\mu}(dy)=1\quad\text{for }\bar{\mu}\text{-a.e.\ }x,

so that the normalized pair law
\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}
has both marginals equal to
\bar{\mu}
. Moreover, there exists
L_{\Lambda}<\infty
such that for all
\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}^{\prime}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
,

W_{2}\bigl(\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\mu}}},\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\mu}^{\prime}}}\bigr)\leq L_{\Lambda}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}^{\prime}).

(ii)

Lipschitz mixing reproduction rule.
There exists
L_{b}<\infty
such that for all
x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\bigl[\|b(x,y;\xi)-b(x^{\prime},y^{\prime};\xi)\|^{2}\bigr]\leq L_{b}\bigl(\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{\prime}\|^{2}\bigr).

(iii)

q
-moment growth of offspring.
Denote
w_{q}(x)=1+\|x\|^{q}
. There exists
C_{b}^{(q)}<\infty
such that for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\bigl[w_{q}(b(x,y;\xi))\bigr]\leq C_{b}^{(q)}\bigl(w_{q}(x)+w_{q}(y)\bigr).

(iv)

Bounded amplitude and Lipschitz continuity on moment balls.
For every
r<\infty
there exists
\beta_{r}<\infty
such that

0<\beta(\bar{\mu})\leq\beta_{r}\qquad\text{for all }\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})\text{ with }\int w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}\leq r.

Moreover,
\beta
is Lipschitz in
W_{2}
on these sets:
|\beta(\bar{\mu})-\beta(\bar{\mu}^{\prime})|\leq L_{\beta}(r)\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}^{\prime})
.

Role of the assumptions.

Each condition has a definite role in Proposition
C.4
. The marginal compatibility in (i) is the bookkeeping identity that makes the balanced map mass-preserving, and the
W_{2}
-Lipschitz dependence of the pair law on
\bar{\mu}
is one of the three ingredients of the local Lipschitz continuity (A4); the Lipschitz mixing rule (ii) and the Lipschitz amplitude in (iv) are the other two. The moment-growth condition (iii) guarantees that offspring inherit
q
-th moments from their parents; it gives the moment stability (A2), underlies the near-identity estimates (A3), and reappears in the convergence analysis, where it dominates the recombination term when the TRJ equation is tested against polynomially growing Lyapunov functions (Lemma
6.5
). The amplitude bound in (iv) matters only for the unbalanced variant, where it controls the birth–death scaling of the total mass.

Many standard crossover operators satisfy Assumption
5.13
(ii). Arithmetic and BLX-
\alpha

[
11
]
crossover are recovered by
b(x,y;\xi)=x+\alpha(\xi)(y-x)
with
\alpha(\xi)\sim U(0,1)
(arithmetic) or
\alpha(\xi)\sim U(-\alpha,1+\alpha)
(BLX-
\alpha
); the map is
1
-Lipschitz in
(x,y)
for each
\xi
. SBX
[
7
]
corresponds to
b(x,y;\xi)=\tfrac{1}{2}[(1+\beta(\xi))x+(1-\beta(\xi))y]
and is Lipschitz whenever
\beta(\xi)
has finite second moment. Differential-vector blending, as used in differential evolution, is
b(x,y;\xi)=x+F(\xi)(y-x)
with bounded
F
.

Definition 5.14

(Offspring kernel and instantaneous recombination maps)
.

Let
b\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\times\Xi\to{\mathcal{X}}
be a measurable mixing rule. The associated
offspring kernel
is

p_{b}((x,y),A):=\int_{\Xi}\mathbf{1}_{\{b(x,y;\xi)\in A\}}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}(d\xi),\qquad A\in\mathscr{B}({\mathcal{X}}).

For
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2,+}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\mu({\mathcal{X}})>0
, the
balanced
and
unbalanced
instantaneous recombination maps are

R^{\rm bal}[\mu](A):=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\iint_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}p_{b}((x,y),A)\,\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}(dx,dy),\qquad R[\mu]:=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,R^{\rm bal}[\mu],\quad A\in\mathscr{B}({\mathcal{X}}),

so that
R^{\rm bal}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=\mu({\mathcal{X}})
and
R[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,\mu({\mathcal{X}})
. In particular,
R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
. We extend by
R[0]=R^{\rm bal}[0]=0
. The associated
\tau
-step recombination operators are the convex combinations

(14)

R_{\tau}[\mu]:=(1-\tau)\mu+\tau R[\mu],\qquad R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]:=(1-\tau)\mu+\tau R^{\rm bal}[\mu],\quad\tau\in[0,1],

modelling a recombination event with probability
\tau
(offspring drawn from
R[\mu]
) and no event with probability
1-\tau
.

Jump (recombination).

The recombination contribution

\langle\varphi,R[\mu_{t}]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu_{t}\rangle

is a pure-jump redistribution operator: a parent configuration is replaced,
at the level of the population law, by offspring generated from a mating
kernel and a mixing rule. Unlike mutation, which moves mass continuously,
and unlike selection, which reweights mass without changing locations,
recombination transports mass discontinuously from the pre-recombination
population law to the offspring law.

If recombination is absent, this term vanishes. The resulting equation is a
transport–reaction equation when selection remains present, and reduces
further to the CBO-type transport–diffusion equation when only the CBO
mutation operator is retained. The examples below verify the Lipschitz
mixing and moment-growth assumptions for standard real-coded mixing
rules. In each case, the verification concerns the offspring map; the pair law
must still satisfy the marginal-compatibility condition in
Assumption
5.13
(i).

Example 5.15

(Affine real-coded blending)
.

Let

b_{A}(x,y;\xi)=(I-A(\xi))x+A(\xi)y,

where
A(\xi)
is either a scalar coefficient or a diagonal matrix of
coordinatewise coefficients. For two parent pairs
(x,y)
and

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
,

b_{A}(x,y;\xi)-b_{A}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime};\xi)=(I-A(\xi))(x-x^{\prime})+A(\xi)(y-y^{\prime}).

If the coefficients of
A
lie in a bounded interval
[a_{-},a_{+}]
, then

\|b_{A}(x,y;\xi)-b_{A}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime};\xi)\|^{2}\leq L_{b}\bigl(\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{\prime}\|^{2}\bigr),

with

L_{b}\leq\sup_{a\in[a_{-},a_{+}]}\bigl((1-a)^{2}+a^{2}\bigr).

Thus arithmetic or segment blending, where
a\in[0,1]
, satisfies

L_{b}\leq 1
. The standard BLX-
\alpha
operator is obtained
coordinatewise by sampling each coefficient from

[-\alpha,1+\alpha]
; in this case

L_{b}\leq(1+\alpha)^{2}+\alpha^{2}=1+2\alpha+2\alpha^{2}.

The
q
-moment growth bound follows from

\|b_{A}(x,y;\xi)\|^{q}\leq 2^{q-1}\bigl(\|I-A(\xi)\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{q}\|x\|^{q}+\|A(\xi)\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{q}\|y\|^{q}\bigr),

and hence holds whenever the coefficient distribution has bounded

q
-th moment. This also covers two-parent intermediate recombination;
the usual multi-parent intermediate recombination in ES is obtained by
the analogous finite-parent affine map. In particular, ES intermediate
recombination is an arithmetic centroid of the selected parental vectors,
while discrete recombination selects coordinates from parents
coordinatewise
[
2
]
.

Example 5.16

(Simulated binary crossover)
.

The simulated binary crossover (SBX)
[
7
]
can be written, for one offspring branch, as

b(x,y;\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[(1+\varepsilon(\xi)\beta_{\rm SBX}(\xi))x+(1-\varepsilon(\xi)\beta_{\rm SBX}(\xi))y\bigr],

where
\varepsilon(\xi)\in\{-1,+1\}
selects one of the two symmetric
offspring branches. Setting

a(\xi)=\frac{1+\varepsilon(\xi)\beta_{\rm SBX}(\xi)}{2},

this is again an affine map. Since

a(\xi)^{2}+(1-a(\xi))^{2}=\frac{1+\beta_{\rm SBX}(\xi)^{2}}{2},

Assumption
5.13
(ii) holds with

L_{b}=\frac{1+{\mathbb{E}}[\beta_{\rm SBX}^{2}]}{2},

provided the spread factor has finite second moment. Likewise the

q
-moment growth bound holds whenever

{\mathbb{E}}|\beta_{\rm SBX}|^{q}<\infty
. For the usual unbounded SBX
distribution this moment condition imposes the corresponding restriction
on the distribution index; bounded implementations satisfy it automatically.

Example 5.17

(Differential-vector blending)
.

In differential evolution
[
33
]
, the donor or
mutant vector has the form

v=x_{r}+F(x_{s}-x_{t}),

with distinct population indices in the finite-
N
algorithm. Crossover
with the target vector then forms the trial vector, and greedy replacement
compares the trial vector with the target.

There are two useful ways to embed this mechanism. A two-parent
differential-style surrogate,

b(x,y;\xi)=x+F(\xi)(y-x),

is an affine blending map and inherits the preceding bounds when

F
has bounded or sufficiently many finite moments. The literal DE donor
uses a three-parent map

b(x_{r},x_{s},x_{t};\xi)=x_{r}+F(\xi)(x_{s}-x_{t}).

For bounded deterministic
F
,

\|b(x_{r},x_{s},x_{t})-b(x_{r}^{\prime},x_{s}^{\prime},x_{t}^{\prime})\|^{2}\leq(1+2F^{2})\bigl(\|x_{r}-x_{r}^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|x_{s}-x_{s}^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime}\|^{2}\bigr).

If
F
is random, the same bound holds with
1+2{\mathbb{E}}F^{2}
. Moreover,

\|b(x_{r},x_{s},x_{t})\|^{q}\leq 3^{q-1}\bigl(\|x_{r}\|^{q}+|F|^{q}\|x_{s}\|^{q}+|F|^{q}\|x_{t}\|^{q}\bigr),

so the three-parent moment condition holds when
F
has finite

q
-th moment. The product law
\bar{\mu}^{\otimes 3}
is the
with-replacement mean-field idealization; the usual finite-population rule
with mutually distinct indices is represented exactly by a without-replacement
finite-
N
law and converges to the product law as
N\to\infty
.

Pair law and amplitude.

The examples above specify only the mixing map
b
. To satisfy
Assumption
5.13
(i), the mating law must
also have the prescribed marginals. The simplest admissible choice is
uniform independent pairing,

\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}=\bar{\mu}\otimes\bar{\mu},

or
\bar{\mu}^{\otimes 3}
in the three-parent DE idealization. Then

W_{2}^{2}(\bar{\mu}\otimes\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\otimes\bar{\nu})\leq 2W_{2}^{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}),

and similarly the product triple law gives the constant
\sqrt{3}
.

More general mating kernels are admissible only when they are

\bar{\mu}
-bistochastic, namely

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Lambda(x,y;\bar{\mu})\,\bar{\mu}(dy)=1\quad\text{for $\bar{\mu}$-a.e. }x,

and, together with symmetry, therefore preserve both marginals of the pair
law. Generic fitness-proportional or rank-based mating kernels do not
satisfy this condition, because they change the marginal distribution of
selected parents. Such mechanisms should either be absorbed into the
selection operator or treated with a separate recombination assumption
allowing non-preserved marginals.

For ordinary mass-preserving recombination the canonical amplitude is

\beta\equiv 1
, so
R=R^{\rm bal}
. Other positive bounded amplitudes are
mathematically admissible under Assumption
5.13
(iv),
but they describe an unbalanced birth–death scaling of the recombination
event rather than standard crossover.

In what follows we work with

By telescoping, we can write the increment as

For each component

R_{j,\tau}(\eta;\varphi):=\frac{\langle\varphi,T_{\tau}^{(j)}[\eta]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\eta\rangle}{\tau}-G_{j}[\eta](\varphi),\qquad\eta\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}}).

Subtracting

Bound on

By (A2), for
\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}]

\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}

Since

(17)

|I_{2}|\;\leq\;\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}}\Bigl|\sum_{j=1}^{J}R_{j,\tau}(\mu^{(j-1)};\varphi)\Bigr|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{\eta\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r^{\prime}}}|R_{j,\tau}(\eta;\varphi)|\;\xrightarrow[\tau\downarrow 0]{}\;0.

Bound on
I_{1}

drifts from

In particular, this is
o(1)
uniformly over
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}}|G_{j}[\mu^{(j-1)}](\varphi)-G_{j}[\mu](\varphi)|

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
3.4
(
\mathcal{D}
-weak formulation and TRJ decomposition)

This subsection collects the relevant definitions for the TRJ equation, including the precise notion of
\mathcal{D}
-weak solution, and gives the full proof of Theorem
3.4
. Throughout, “
\mathcal{D}
-weak” refers to the measure-valued formulation obtained by testing against
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
. We emphasise that this is not the same object as a classical
weak (distributional) solution
of a Fokker–Planck-type PDE: the latter is a function
p_{t}(x)
satisfying an identity against
C_{c}^{\infty}
test functions, whereas a
\mathcal{D}
-weak solution is a measure-valued curve
(\mu_{t})
which need not admit a density. The two coincide when
\mu_{t}(dx)=p_{t}(x)\,dx
with sufficient regularity, but the measure-valued formulation is the natural level for the mean-field TRJ equation.

Definition 6.1

(
\mathcal{D}
-weak balanced TRJ solution)
.

Let
q\geq 3
,
\mathcal{D}:=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
, and for
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
write
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})
,
\bar{\Phi}(\mu):=\frac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\mu)\mu(dx)
, and
a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu}):=B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\Sigma B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})^{\top}
. For
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, define the balanced TRJ action

G[\mu](\varphi):=\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!\bigl(\nabla\varphi\cdot b_{M}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}D^{2}\varphi)\bigr)d\mu}_{\text{transport--diffusion (mutation)}}-\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!(\Phi-\bar{\Phi})\varphi\,d\mu}_{\text{reaction (balanced selection)}}\\
+\underbrace{\bigl(\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle\bigr)}_{\text{jump (balanced recombination)}}.

A curve
t\mapsto\mu_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
is a
\mathcal{D}
-weak solution of the balanced TRJ equation
if, for every
T<\infty
,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1+\|x\|^{q})\,\mu_{t}(dx)<\infty,\qquad\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}\mu_{t}({\mathcal{X}})>0,

and, for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, the map
t\mapsto G[\mu_{t}](\varphi)
is integrable on
[0,T]
and

\langle\varphi,\mu_{t}\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu_{s}\rangle=\int_{s}^{t}G[\mu_{\ell}](\varphi)\,d\ell\qquad\text{for all }0\leq s\leq t\leq T.

Equivalently in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
: for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, the map
t\mapsto\langle\varphi,\mu_{t}\rangle
is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
and
\frac{d}{dt}\langle\varphi,\mu_{t}\rangle=G[\mu_{t}](\varphi)
for a.e.

. In the normalized case
\mu_{t}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
, the lower mass condition is automatic.

Remark 6.2

(Interpretation of the displayed TRJ equation)
.

For
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, the right-hand side of the balanced TRJ equation (
6
) is understood by duality on
\mathcal{D}=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
through

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\varphi,-\nabla\!\cdot\!\bigl(b_{M}(\cdot;\bar{\mu})\mu\bigr)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\,\mu(dx),

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\varphi,\tfrac{1}{2}\textstyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ij}\!\bigl(a_{M,ij}(\cdot;\bar{\mu})\mu\bigr)\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl(a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})D^{2}\varphi(x)\bigr)\,\mu(dx),

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\varphi,-\bigl(\Phi(\cdot;\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\mu)\bigr)\mu\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(\Phi(x;\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\mu)\bigr)\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx),

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\mu]-\mu\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle:=\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle.

For
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}})\subset\mathcal{D}
, these identities agree with the usual distributional formulas whenever those are legitimate. For the actual test class
\mathcal{D}=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
, they
define
the action of the displayed operator in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 6.3

(Scalar absolute continuity of TRJ observables)
.

Under Assumptions
5.2
,
5.7
, and
5.13
, for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
and every
r,m_{0}>0
there exists
C_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}<\infty
such that

|G_{M}[\mu](\varphi)+G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)+G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)|\leq C_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}\qquad\text{for all }\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}.

Consequently, if
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a
d_{q,2}^{*}
-Borel curve contained in some
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}
and satisfying, for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
,

\langle\varphi,\mu_{t}\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu_{s}\rangle=\int_{s}^{t}\bigl(G_{M}[\mu_{\ell}](\varphi)+G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu_{\ell}](\varphi)+G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\mu_{\ell}](\varphi)\bigr)\,d\ell,\qquad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

then

is Lipschitz on

Proof.

Let
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}
. Since
q\geq 3
, the elementary bounds
w_{2}\leq 2w_{q}
and
w_{3}\leq 2w_{q}
imply

\mu({\mathcal{X}})\leq r,\qquad\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{2}\,d\mu\leq 2r,\qquad\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{3}\,d\bar{\mu}\leq\frac{2r}{m_{0}}.

Thus the normalized laws
\bar{\mu}
remain in a fixed third-moment sublevel.

Mutation contribution.
By Assumption
5.2
(i), applied on the normalized moment sublevel with radius
2r/m_{0}
,

\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|_{F}\leq C(2r/m_{0})(1+\|x\|).

Since
a_{M}=B_{M}\Sigma B_{M}^{\top}
,
\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|_{F}\leq\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}C(2r/m_{0})^{2}(1+\|x\|)^{2}
. Using
(1+\|x\|)\leq 2w_{q}(x)
and
(1+\|x\|)^{2}\leq 4w_{q}(x)
,

\displaystyle|G_{M}[\mu](\varphi)|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\!\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|\,\mu(dx)+\tfrac{1}{2}\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}\!\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|_{F}\,\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\leq 2r\,C(2r/m_{0})\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}+2r\,\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}C(2r/m_{0})^{2}\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}.

Selection contribution.
Assumption

(i) gives
|\Phi(x;\mu)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)
for

, hence

, and

|G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)|\leq 2C_{\Phi}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\,\mu({\mathcal{X}})\leq 2rC_{\Phi}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

Recombination contribution.
By mass preservation of the balanced recombination map,
R^{\rm bal}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=\mu({\mathcal{X}})
. Consequently,

|G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\bigl(R^{\rm bal}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})+\mu({\mathcal{X}})\bigr)\leq 2r\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

Adding the three estimates yields the asserted bound, for instance with

C_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}:=2r\,C(2r/m_{0})\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}+2r\,\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}C(2r/m_{0})^{2}\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}+2r\bigl(C_{\Phi}(r)+1\bigr)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

The formulas for the component pre-generators and the
d_{q,2}^{*}
-Borelness of the curve give measurability of the right-hand side along
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
; along any curve contained in
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}
this right-hand side is bounded in absolute value by
C_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}
. The integrated identity then gives

|\langle\varphi,\mu_{t}\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu_{s}\rangle|\leq C_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}|t-s|,\qquad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

which proves the Lipschitz, and hence the local absolute-continuity, claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Part (i): Additive pre-generator.

(i), Proposition

(i), and Proposition

(i), the balanced mutation, selection, and recombination steps admit pre-generators

6.3.
The induced evolution corollary: from parameters to candidates

We prove Corollary
3.3
, then provide intuition for its role in the framework.

Proof of Corollary
3.3
.

For every
t\geq 0
, by the definition of the search law (
1
) and Fubini’s theorem,

\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\phi(y)\,\Pi[\bar{\mu}_{t}](dy)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\phi(y)\,K(x,dy)\,\bar{\mu}_{t}(dx)=\langle K_{*}\phi,\bar{\mu}_{t}\rangle.

Since
K_{*}\phi\in\mathcal{D}
, the
\mathcal{D}
-weak formulation of the general evolution equation (
5
) for
(\bar{\mu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, tested against
K_{*}\phi
, yields

\langle K_{*}\phi,\bar{\mu}_{t}\rangle-\langle K_{*}\phi,\bar{\mu}_{s}\rangle=\int_{s}^{t}G[\bar{\mu}_{\ell}](K_{*}\phi)\,d\ell,\qquad 0\leq s\leq t.

Using the identity
\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle=\langle K_{*}\phi,\bar{\mu}_{t}\rangle
,

\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle-\langle\phi,\nu_{s}\rangle=\int_{s}^{t}G[\bar{\mu}_{\ell}](K_{*}\phi)\,d\ell.

In particular,
t\mapsto\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle
is locally absolutely continuous. Since the integrand belongs to
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
(Lemma
6.3
), the fundamental theorem of calculus for absolutely continuous functions gives

\frac{d}{dt}\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle=G[\bar{\mu}_{t}](K_{*}\phi)\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\geq 0.\qed

Corollary
3.3
answers a natural question. If a probability-valued internal population evolves according to the general evolution equation (
5
) on state space
{\mathcal{X}}
, what PDE does the distribution of
evaluated candidates
satisfy on search space
{\mathcal{S}}
?

The key object is the
kernel lift

(K_{*}\phi)(x):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\phi(y)\,K(x,dy)
, which converts a search-space observable
\phi
into a state-space function by averaging over the sampling distribution at each state
x
. For instance, if
\phi(y)=\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
measures quadratic distance to the optimum and
K(x,\cdot)=\mathcal{N}(m(x),\sigma(x)^{2}C(x))
is the CMA-ES sampling kernel, then
K_{*}\phi(x)=\|m(x)-y_{*}\|^{2}+\sigma(x)^{2}\mathop{\rm Tr}(C(x))
, i.e. the expected squared error of a candidate drawn from the current search distribution.

The corollary says that
\frac{d}{dt}\langle\phi,\nu_{t}\rangle=G[\bar{\mu}_{t}](K_{*}\phi)
: the rate of change of any search-space statistic is obtained by applying the
state-space generator
to the lifted version of that statistic. This means we never need to derive a separate PDE on
{\mathcal{S}}
; instead, we can analyze everything through the state-space TRJ equation and “read off” the search-space consequences through
K_{*}
. This factorization is what makes the Lyapunov analysis in Section
4
simultaneously applicable to nonparametric methods (where
K=\delta
and the lift is trivial) and parametric methods (where the lift encodes the nontrivial map from distribution parameters to candidate distributions).

6.4.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Roadmap.

The proof proceeds in five stages. First, the
search-space compatibility
lemma (Section
6.4.1
) reduces search-space integrals to state-space expectations, providing the transfer mechanism from
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
to
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}
. Second, the
operator-wise closure
proposition (Section
6.4.2
) packages the modular verification of the closed dissipation hypothesis. Third, a
testing lemma
(Section
6.4.3
) handles the technical issue that the Lyapunov function
\Upsilon
is unbounded while the TRJ equation is formulated against bounded test functions; a truncation-and-limit argument extends the
\mathcal{D}
-weak identity to polynomial-growth
\Upsilon
. Fourth, combining the testing identity with the closed dissipation hypothesis and Grönwall’s lemma yields
Lyapunov decay
for
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
(Section
6.4.4
). Finally, the three search-space modes (a)–(c) follow from
basin conversion
and Markov’s inequality applied to
\nu_{t}
(Section
6.4.5
).

6.4.1.
Search-space compatibility

The following lemma formalizes the connection between the state-space Lyapunov functional and the search-space error functional. The key identity
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell_{\Psi}(x)\,\bar{\mu}(dx)
reformulates a search-space integral as a state-space expectation, after which the Lyapunov decay can be transferred via the pointwise bound
\ell_{\Psi}\leq C_{K}\,\Upsilon
.

Lemma 6.4

(Search-space compatibility)
.

Let
\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
, let
\nu:=\Pi[\bar{\mu}]\in\mathscr{P}({\mathcal{S}})
, and let
\Psi\colon{\mathcal{S}}\to[0,\infty]
be measurable with
\Psi\in L^{1}(\nu)
. Then

(20)

\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Psi(y)\,\nu(dy)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell_{\Psi}(x)\,\bar{\mu}(dx).

Moreover, the following statements hold:

(i)

If
f_{*}>-\infty
and
\Psi(y)=f(y)-f_{*}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}f(y)\,\nu(dy)-f_{*}
.

(ii)

If
{\mathcal{S}}_{*}=\{y_{*}\}
,
\Psi(y)=\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})<\infty
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=W_{2}^{2}(\nu,\delta_{y_{*}})
.

(iii)

If, for some
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
c_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
\Psi(y)\geq c_{\varepsilon}
for all
y\in{\mathcal{S}}\setminus{\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon}
, then

1-\nu({\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon})=\nu({\mathcal{S}}\setminus{\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon})\leq c_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu}).

In particular, if this holds for every
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu}_{t})\to 0
, then the induced search laws achieve
\varepsilon
-concentration.

Consequently, if
\ell_{\Psi}(x)\leq C_{K}\,\Upsilon(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})\leq C_{K}\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu})
for every
\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
.

Proof.

By definition of
\Pi[\bar{\mu}]
and Tonelli’s theorem,

\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Psi(y)\,\Pi[\bar{\mu}](dy)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!\!\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Psi(y)\,K(x,dy)\,\bar{\mu}(dx)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell_{\Psi}(x)\,\bar{\mu}(dx),

which establishes (
20
), since
\nu=\Pi[\bar{\mu}]
also gives
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Psi\,d\nu
.

Part (i)
is immediate:
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}(f(y)-f_{*})\,\nu(dy)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}f(y)\,\nu(dy)-f_{*}
.

Part (ii):
the unique coupling in
\Gamma(\nu,\delta_{y_{*}})
is
\nu\otimes\delta_{y_{*}}
, hence

W_{2}^{2}(\nu,\delta_{y_{*}})=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{S}}}\|y-z\|^{2}\,(\nu\otimes\delta_{y_{*}})(dy,dz)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}\,\nu(dy)=\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu}).

Part (iii):

\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Psi(y)\,\nu(dy)\geq\int_{{\mathcal{S}}\setminus{\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon}}\Psi(y)\,\nu(dy)\geq c_{\varepsilon}\,\nu({\mathcal{S}}\setminus{\mathcal{S}}_{*}^{\varepsilon}),

which gives the claim.

The Lyapunov-domination corollary:
when
\ell_{\Psi}\leq C_{K}\,\Upsilon
, integrating against
\bar{\mu}
in (
20
) yields
\mathcal{E}_{\Psi}(\bar{\mu})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell_{\Psi}\,d\bar{\mu}\leq C_{K}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Upsilon\,d\bar{\mu}=C_{K}\,\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu})
.
∎

6.4.2.
Operator-wise closure

We prove Proposition
4.2
, stated in Section
4
, which formalizes the modular verification strategy that is central to the practical applicability of the framework.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Since
G[\bar{\mu}]=G_{M}[\bar{\mu}]+G_{S}[\bar{\mu}]+G_{R}[\bar{\mu}]
by Theorem
3.2
, we add the three operator-wise bounds:

\displaystyle G[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)

\displaystyle=G_{M}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)+G_{S}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)+G_{R}[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)

\displaystyle\leq(-\lambda_{M}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}+c_{M}\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon})+(-\lambda_{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon})+(-\lambda_{R}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon})

\displaystyle=-(\lambda_{M}+\lambda_{S}+\lambda_{R})\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}+c_{M}\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon}.

Using
\mathfrak{b}_{\Upsilon}\leq\kappa_{\Upsilon}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
,
G[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-(\lambda_{M}+\lambda_{S}+\lambda_{R}-c_{M}\kappa_{\Upsilon})\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}=-\lambda\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}
.
∎

6.4.3.
Testing the TRJ equation against unbounded Lyapunov functions

The main technical challenge is that the Lyapunov function
\Upsilon
may be unbounded while the TRJ equation is formulated for bounded test functions. The following lemma resolves this via truncation.

Lemma 6.5

(Testing the balanced TRJ equation with polynomial-growth Lyapunov functions)
.

For
k\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, and
f\in C^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
, write
\|D^{0}f\|_{0}:=|f|
,
\|D^{1}f\|_{1}:=\|Df\|
,
\|D^{2}f\|_{2}:=\|D^{2}f\|_{F}
,
\|D^{3}f\|_{3}:=\|D^{3}f\|_{\rm op}
. Under the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
, suppose
\Upsilon\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to[0,\infty)
has polynomial growth of order
q
, in the sense that
\Upsilon\in C^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
,
\|D^{k}\Upsilon(x)\|_{k}\leq C_{k}(1+\|x\|^{(q-k)_{+}})
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
k=0,1,2,3
. Then
t\mapsto\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})
is absolutely continuous and
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})=G[\bar{\mu}_{t}](\Upsilon)
for a.e.
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

The TRJ equation (
6
) is a
\mathcal{D}
-weak identity (Definition
6.1
), valid when tested against
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
; the Lyapunov function
\Upsilon
may grow as
\|x\|^{q}
and is therefore not in
\mathcal{D}
. We use a truncation-and-limit argument that follows the same three-step scheme as in the main manuscript: (i) multiply
\Upsilon
by a smooth spatial cutoff
\vartheta_{\bar{r}}
to produce a bounded test function
\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\in\mathcal{D}
for which the weak formulation applies; (ii) derive uniform-in-
\bar{r}
domination bounds on each generator term using the polynomial growth of
\Upsilon
and the moment assumption; (iii) send
\bar{r}\to\infty
and apply dominated convergence.

For
k\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, define
g_{k}(x):=1+\|x\|^{(q-k)_{+}}
. Polynomial-growth admissibility of
\Upsilon
of order
q
then reads

(21)

\|D^{k}\Upsilon(x)\|_{k}\leq C_{\Upsilon}\,g_{k}(x),\qquad x\in{\mathcal{X}},\ k=0,1,2,3,

where
C_{\Upsilon}:=\max(C_{0},C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})
.

Step 1: construction of the truncated sequence.
Choose
\vartheta\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}})
with
0\leq\vartheta\leq 1
,
\vartheta\equiv 1
on
B_{1}(0)
and
\vartheta\equiv 0
on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus B_{2}(0)
. For
\bar{r}\geq 1
, set
\vartheta_{\bar{r}}(x):=\vartheta(x/\bar{r})
and
\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}(x):=\vartheta_{\bar{r}}(x)\,\Upsilon(x)
. Then
\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\in C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})=\mathcal{D}
,
\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}=\Upsilon
on
B_{\bar{r}}(0)
, and
\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}=0
on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus B_{2\bar{r}}(0)
. For
m\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, set
M_{m}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|D^{m}\vartheta(x)\|_{m}<\infty
. By scaling,
\|D^{m}\vartheta_{\bar{r}}(x)\|_{m}\leq M_{m}\,\bar{r}^{-m}
. Moreover, for
m\geq 1
,
\operatorname{supp}D^{m}\vartheta_{\bar{r}}\subset A_{\bar{r}}:=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\bar{r}\leq\|x\|\leq 2\bar{r}\}
.

Set
\kappa_{0}:=1
and
\kappa_{m}:=1+2^{m}
for
m=1,2,3
. Then for every
1\leq m\leq k\leq 3
,

(22)

\bar{r}^{-m}\,g_{k-m}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{\bar{r}}}(x)\leq\kappa_{m}\,g_{k}(x).

Indeed, if
a:=q-k\geq 0
, then on
A_{\bar{r}}
we have
\bar{r}^{-m}\|x\|^{a+m}=(\|x\|/\bar{r})^{m}\|x\|^{a}\leq 2^{m}\|x\|^{a}
; if
a<0
, then
a\in[-1,0)
and
\|x\|\geq 1
on
A_{\bar{r}}
, so
\bar{r}^{-m}\|x\|^{a+m}\leq 2^{m}
.

Step 2: uniform derivative estimates.
For scalar
f,g\in C^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
, the product rules for
D,D^{2},D^{3}
yield, for
k=0,1,2,3
,

(23)

\|D^{k}(fg)(x)\|_{k}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom{k}{m}\|D^{m}f(x)\|_{m}\,\|D^{k-m}g(x)\|_{k-m},\qquad x\in{\mathcal{X}},

using
D^{2}(fg)=fD^{2}g+gD^{2}f+Df\otimes Dg+Dg\otimes Df
for
k=2
, the trilinear Leibniz formula for
k=3
, and
\|D^{2}h\|_{\rm op}\leq\|D^{2}h\|_{F}
. Applying (
23
) with
f=\vartheta_{\bar{r}}
,
g=\Upsilon
, and combining (
21
) with (
22
),

\|D^{k}\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}(x)\|_{k}\leq C_{\Upsilon}\Bigl(M_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\binom{k}{m}M_{m}\kappa_{m}\Bigr)\,g_{k}(x)=:\widetilde{C}_{k}\,g_{k}(x),

uniformly in
\bar{r}\geq 1
and
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, for
k=0,1,2,3
. In particular,
\|D^{0}\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}(x)\|_{0}\leq\widetilde{C}_{0}\,w_{q}(x)
uniformly, and
\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\to\Upsilon
,
D\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\to D\Upsilon
,
D^{2}\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\to D^{2}\Upsilon
pointwise as
\bar{r}\to\infty
.

Step 3: integrated identity.
Fix
0\leq s\leq t
. Since
\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\in\mathcal{D}
, the
\mathcal{D}
-weak formulation (
6
) gives

(24)

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\,d\bar{\mu}_{t}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}\,d\bar{\mu}_{s}=\int_{s}^{t}\Bigl(G_{M}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})+G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})+G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})\Bigr)du.

Step 4: uniform domination of generator terms.
Let
M_{q}^{*}:=\sup_{u\geq 0}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u}<\infty
by the assumed uniform moment control.

Mutation.
Since
(\bar{\mu}_{u})_{u\geq 0}
stays in a fixed
q
-moment ball and
q\geq 2
, Assumption
5.2
yields
L_{M}<\infty
such that

\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu}_{u})\|+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu}_{u})\|_{F}\leq L_{M}(1+\|x\|),\qquad\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu}_{u})\|_{F}\leq 2L_{M}^{2}(1+\|x\|^{2}),

where
a_{M}=B_{M}\Sigma B_{M}^{\top}
and
\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}
is absorbed into
L_{M}
. Using
g_{1}(x)(1+\|x\|)\leq 4w_{q}(x)
and
g_{2}(x)(1+\|x\|^{2})\leq 4w_{q}(x)
,

|G_{M}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})|\leq 4\bigl(\widetilde{C}_{1}L_{M}+\widetilde{C}_{2}L_{M}^{2}\bigr)\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u}=:C_{M}^{*}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u}.

Pointwise convergence of
D\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}
,
D^{2}\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}
and dominated convergence give
G_{M}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})\to G_{M}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon)
for every
u\geq 0
.

Selection.
Bounded pressure (Assumption
5.7
(i)) on the same moment ball yields
C_{\Phi}^{*}<\infty
with
|\Phi(x;\bar{\mu}_{u})|+|\bar{\Phi}(\bar{\mu}_{u})|\leq C_{\Phi}^{*}
. Therefore

|G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})|\leq 2C_{\Phi}^{*}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|D^{0}\Upsilon_{\bar{r}}(x)\|_{0}\,\bar{\mu}_{u}(dx)\leq 2C_{\Phi}^{*}\widetilde{C}_{0}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u}=:C_{S}^{*}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u},

and
G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})\to G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon)
by dominated convergence.

Recombination.
Write
R_{u}^{\rm bal}:=R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}]
. By the
q
-moment growth of the offspring law (Assumption
5.13
(iii)),

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}(z)\,R_{u}^{\rm bal}(dz)\leq 2C_{b}^{(q)}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u}.

Hence

|G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})|\leq\widetilde{C}_{0}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR_{u}^{\rm bal}+\widetilde{C}_{0}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u}\leq\widetilde{C}_{0}(2C_{b}^{(q)}+1)\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u}=:C_{R}^{*}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}_{u},

and dominated convergence under both
R_{u}^{\rm bal}
and
\bar{\mu}_{u}
gives
G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon_{\bar{r}})\to G_{R}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}_{u}](\Upsilon)
.

Step 5: passage to the limit.
Set
C_{*}:=C_{M}^{*}+C_{S}^{*}+C_{R}^{*}
. Then

7.
Discussion

Conclusion.

We have presented a unified mean-field framework built on operator-splitting
calculus and a modular Lyapunov convergence principle, covering direct and
parametric population-based methods as well as degenerate gradient-based
limits. An algorithm is specified by composing three elementary measure-valued
operators — mutation, selection, and recombination — and the generator of the
composite step is the sum of the component pre-generators
(Theorem
3.2
); for the canonical splitting this
produces the transport–reaction–jump equation
(Theorem
3.4
), a mean-field PDE whose terms retain
their algorithmic meaning. The Lyapunov principle
(Theorem
4.1
) then reduces convergence to a single
closed dissipation inequality that can be assembled operator by operator
(Proposition
4.2
) and yields exponential decay together
with the three search-space convergence modes of Section
2
, while
the separation of state space and search space makes the same statement apply
to nonparametric methods and to parametric ones such as CMA-ES. The numerical
experiments of Section
4.3
are consistent with the
predicted geometric decay, and the regularity classes and examples of
Section
5
show that standard algorithmic mechanisms
fall within the scope of the theory.

Limitations.

The results are mean-field and continuous-time: the paper does not prove
finite-particle propagation-of-chaos estimates, finite-time discovery bounds,
or discretization error bounds for concrete implementations. The main
convergence theorem is conditional on the closed Lyapunov inequality
(
7
) and on search-space compatibility; verifying
these hypotheses with explicit constants is algorithm-dependent and is only
illustrated here, not carried out exhaustively for all families in
Table
1
. The operator calculus also imposes regularity
conditions, including polynomial moment bounds, Lipschitz dependence of
mean-field coefficients, bounded-pressure selection, and Lipschitz
recombination kernels. These assumptions exclude some practical variants,
such as discontinuous replacement rules, unbounded selection pressures, or
biased mating schemes unless they are regularized, absorbed into the
selection operator, or treated by an augmented model. Finally, the numerical
experiments are intended as sanity checks of the predicted Lyapunov decay,
not as a benchmark study of optimizer performance.

Outlook.

Future work will address finite-particle discovery and
evaluation-complexity bounds, sharper propagation-of-chaos estimates,
fully traceable verification of concrete algorithm families with explicit
constants, multimodal extensions, and finite-time tradeoff analysis.

Appendix A
Notation and Preliminaries

We collect here the notation and background material used throughout the paper, to make the appendix self-contained. The conventions agree with the main manuscript.

Measure spaces.

Throughout,
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
is a Polish subspace (typically
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
); the search space is
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d_{x}\geq d
in general. We write
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{X}})
for the space of finite signed Borel measures on
({\mathcal{X}},\mathscr{B}({\mathcal{X}}))
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{+}({\mathcal{X}})
for nonnegative finite measures, and
{\mathscr{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
for probability measures. Since
{\mathcal{X}}
is Polish, every
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{X}})
is Radon. The total variation of
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{X}})
is
|\mu|:=\mu^{+}+\mu^{-}
, where
\mu^{+},\mu^{-}
are the positive and negative parts. For an exponent
q\geq 2
, the weight function is
w_{q}(x):=1+\|x\|^{q}
, and the cone of nonnegative measures with finite
q
-th moment and strictly positive total mass is

{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}}):=\bigl\{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{+}({\mathcal{X}}):\;\textstyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}(x)\,\mu(dx)<\infty,\ \mu({\mathcal{X}})>0\bigr\}.

Similarly,
{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
denotes the space of Borel probability measures on
{\mathcal{X}}
with finite
q
-th moment, which becomes a metric space when endowed with the
p
-Wasserstein distance for
p\leq q

[
1
]
. The moment sublevel is
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}:=\{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}}):\int w_{q}\,d\mu\leq r\}
, and, when a uniform lower bound on total mass is required, the mass-restricted sublevel

{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m}\;:=\;\bigl\{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}}):\;\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m,\;\textstyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu\leq r\bigr\},\qquad m>0.

The normalization is
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
whenever
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
. The pairing of a measurable function
\varphi
with a measure
\mu
is
\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle:=\int\varphi\,d\mu
.

Test function spaces and derivatives.

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
C_{b}^{k}({\mathcal{X}})
be the Banach space of
k
-times continuously differentiable functions with bounded derivatives up to order
k
, endowed with the
C^{k}
-norm
\|\varphi\|_{C_{b}^{k}}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}\varphi\|_{\infty}
, where
\|\partial^{\alpha}\varphi\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}|\partial^{\alpha}\varphi(x)|
. Unless otherwise stated, the test function space is
\mathcal{D}:=C_{b}^{3}({\mathcal{X}})
, and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
denotes its topological dual endowed with the weak topology
\sigma(\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{D})
; the operator norm on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is denoted
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
. We interpret
D\varphi(x)=\nabla\varphi(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
,
D^{2}\varphi(x)
as the Hessian in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}\times d_{x}}
, and
D^{3}\varphi(x)
as the symmetric third-order derivative tensor. For vectors
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
, we write
u\otimes v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}\times d_{x}}
for the outer product with entries
(u\otimes v)_{ij}=u_{i}v_{j}
; in particular,
\|u\otimes v\|_{F}=\|u\|\,\|v\|
. For the Hessian we use the Frobenius norm
\|D^{2}\varphi(x)\|_{F}
or the operator norm
\|D^{2}\varphi(x)\|_{\rm op}:=\sup_{\|u\|=1}\|D^{2}\varphi(x)u\|
; for third derivatives,
M_{3}(\varphi):=\max_{|\alpha|=3}\|\partial^{\alpha}\varphi\|_{\infty}
, and
\|D^{3}\varphi(x)\|_{\rm op}
denotes the trilinear operator norm induced by the Euclidean norm, so that
|D^{3}\varphi(x)[h,h,h]|\leq\|D^{3}\varphi(x)\|_{\rm op}\,\|h\|^{3}
. For any tensor field
T(x)
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, we use the convention

\|T\|_{\rm op,\infty}\;:=\;\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|T(x)\|_{\rm op},\qquad\|T\|_{F,\infty}\;:=\;\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|T(x)\|_{F},

i.e. the supremum over
{\mathcal{X}}
of the pointwise operator (resp. Frobenius) norm. The pointwise bound
\|D^{3}\varphi(x)\|_{\rm op}\leq d_{x}^{3/2}M_{3}(\varphi)
holds on
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
.

Bounded-Lipschitz norms and the weighted BL metric.

Let
\mathrm{BL}({\mathcal{X}})\subset C_{b}({\mathcal{X}})
denote the space of bounded Lipschitz functions with norm
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL}}:=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+|\varphi|_{L}
, where
|\varphi|_{L}:=\sup_{x\neq y}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|/\|x-y\|
. The dual bounded-Lipschitz (Dudley) norm on
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{X}})
is
\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{BL}}^{*}:=\sup\{\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle:\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1\}
, which metrizes weak convergence on
{\mathcal{M}}_{+}({\mathcal{X}})
when
{\mathcal{X}}
is Polish.

To handle polynomial growth, the weighted BL norm for
q\geq 2
(used in (
2
)) is
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}:=\|f\|_{0,q}+[f]_{1,q-1}
, where

\|f\|_{0,q}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{|f(x)|}{w_{q}(x)},\qquad[f]_{1,q-1}:=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\|x-y\|(1+\|x\|^{q-1}+\|y\|^{q-1})},

so
f
grows at most polynomially of order
q
with Lipschitz modulus of order
q-1
. The weighted BL distance (
2
) on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
is
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu):=\sup\{|\langle f,\mu-\nu\rangle|:\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1\}
. On any subset
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}\int\|x\|^{q}\,\mu(dx)<\infty
and
\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}\mu({\mathcal{X}})<\infty
,
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}
metrizes weak convergence together with convergence of the
q
-th moment
[
26
]
. Since
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
is bounded and globally Lipschitz with
\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi)\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}
, we have
\varphi\in\mathrm{BL}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
and

|\langle\varphi,\mu-\nu\rangle|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\,d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu),\qquad\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}.

Wasserstein distances.

For
p\geq 1
and
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{p}({\mathcal{X}})
, the
p
-Wasserstein distance
is

(26)

W_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\!\left(\int_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}\|x-y\|^{p}\,\gamma(dx,dy)\right)^{\!1/p},

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
(joint distributions with the prescribed marginals). Intuitively,
W_{p}
measures the minimal transport cost of rearranging the mass of
\mu
into the shape of
\nu
. The
p
-Wasserstein distance is monotone in
p
: for
1\leq p\leq q
and
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
,

(27)

W_{p}(\mu,\nu)\;\leq\;W_{q}(\mu,\nu),

by Jensen’s inequality applied to the optimal coupling. For a point mass,
W_{p}^{p}(\nu,\delta_{y_{*}})=\int\|y-y_{*}\|^{p}\,d\nu
, so consensus in
W_{p}
is equivalent to concentration of the
p
-th moment around
y_{*}
; the case
p=2
is our default.

Population metric and its role.

To control evolutionary operators on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
we need to simultaneously control linear observables
\mu\mapsto\int\varphi\,d\mu
for polynomially growing
\varphi
, and nonlinear dependence on the normalized population law
\bar{\mu}=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})
that appears in mean-field coefficients (consensus points, pair laws, rank-based selection, law-dependent drifts and diffusions). The weighted BL metric
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}
is convenient with test functions
\mathcal{D}
because it directly bounds test-function increments; stability conditions for law-dependent coefficients, in contrast, are naturally expressed in Wasserstein distance—typically
W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
, which is the canonical metric for Lipschitz dependence on the law in McKean–Vlasov-type dynamics. Accordingly, we endow
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
with the
population metric

(28)

d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu)\;:=\;d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)+W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}),\qquad\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}}),\;\bar{\nu}:=\nu/\nu({\mathcal{X}}),

which is well defined whenever
q\geq 2
(so that
\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}})
) and
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
(so that both total masses are positive).

Noise increments.

Where mutation kernels are built from a random increment
\psi
on a probability space
(\Xi,\mathscr{F},{\mathbb{P}})
taking values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{\prime}}
, we assume
{\mathbb{E}}[\psi]=0
,
{\mathbb{E}}[\psi\psi^{\top}]=\Sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{\prime}\times d^{\prime}}
symmetric and positive semidefinite, and
{\mathbb{E}}\|\psi\|^{3}<\infty
(see Assumption
5.2
). The covariance
\Sigma
is allowed to be singular; the scalar Gaussian case
\Sigma=I_{d_{x}}
is a special case.

B.1.
Convergence theory for population-based optimization

Early theoretical analyses for population-based methods were largely
algorithm-specific. Schema theory
[
16
]
gave an
influential early account of schema propagation in binary genetic algorithms, but should not be read as a general convergence theorem. Rechenberg’s
1/5
-success rule introduced an early principle for mutation-strength adaptation in evolution strategies, while subsequent progress-rate analyses developed scaling laws for Gaussian mutation models and strategy-parameter control
[
28
,
2
]
. A more probabilistic line models finite-population algorithms as homogeneous finite Markov chains. Rudolph
[
32
]

studied a specific binary genetic algorithm with mutation, crossover, and proportional reproduction, which he calls the canonical genetic algorithm; this terminology is local to that literature and is unrelated to our later use of canonical operator forms. Rudolph proved that this particular non-elitist chain does not converge to the global optimum, whereas variants that maintain the best solution found so far do converge.

A large body of work studies the number of fitness evaluations needed to
reach an optimum, or more generally a target set, from simple algorithms on pseudo-Boolean functions
[
25
]

to population-based processes and refined runtime-analysis techniques

[
6
,
8
]
. Drift analysis bounds hitting times by estimating the expected one-step change of a potential function, and is therefore conceptually close to Lyapunov arguments, although it is used in the runtime-analysis literature for discrete-time stochastic search processes rather than for limiting PDEs. Runtime analysis can yield sharp algorithm-specific bounds. By contrast, our mean-field framework provides structural, algorithm-independent statements that trade such sharpness for
modularity and generality; the two perspectives are therefore complementary.

A particularly close existing mean-field theory among derivative-free optimizers is the theory of consensus-based optimization (CBO)

[
27
]
. In CBO, interacting particles are driven toward a weighted consensus point defined through a Laplace-principle mechanism. Subsequent work developed a PDE-based analytical framework
[
4
]
and later established global convergence and quantitative convergence estimates under suitable assumptions
[
13
]
. In the present notation, CBO corresponds to the transport–diffusion component
G=G_{M}
, after choosing the CBO drift and covariance. The key extension here is that we also accommodate explicit reaction-type selection and recombination jump operators, which are absent from the basic CBO dynamics but central to many evolutionary algorithms. Thus CBO serves both as a building block and as a point of departure: its nonlinear Fokker–Planck generator reappears as a special case of the mutation component of the TRJ equation, while the selection and recombination generators extend the dynamics beyond the standard CBO
model.

B.2.
Algorithm families and their operator decompositions

A central motivation of the present work is the observation that the vast majority of population-based optimizers, despite their apparent algorithmic diversity, share a common structure at the level of their evolutionary operators. In this subsection we survey the main algorithm families covered by our framework, highlighting for each one how its generation cycle decomposes into mutation, selection, and recombination (the three canonical primitives formalized in Section
3.5
). The decompositions are consolidated in Table
1
and the corresponding pre-generators are derived in Appendix
C
.

Direct population-based methods: GAs, ES, DE, and PSO.

In direct population-based methods the update acts on candidate solutions, or on an augmented state containing candidate solutions together with auxiliary variables such as velocities, strategy parameters, or memory. Real-coded genetic algorithms instantiate the familiar selection–mutation–recombination cycle in continuous search spaces. Common choices include fitness-based parent selection,
Gaussian or polynomial mutation, and real-coded crossover operators such as simulated binary crossover (SBX)
[
7
]
; arithmetic recombination gives another standard mixing mechanism.

Evolution strategies provide a more tightly structured real-valued family. In a
(\mu,\lambda)
-ES, selection is truncation selection from the offspring population: the next parent population consists of the best
\mu
individuals among the
\lambda
offspring. Recombination, when used, may be discrete or intermediate; in the latter case the recombinant is an arithmetic mean of selected
parental components. Mutation is typically Gaussian, with the isotropic case as the simplest model and with step-size, coordinate-wise, or covariance adaptation in more refined variants
[
28
,
2
]
.

Differential evolution
[
33
]
is naturally
population-dependent, but it should not be described as mean-field in the original algorithmic formulation. Trial vectors are generated by adding a scaled difference of two randomly chosen population members to a third one, then combined with the target vector through binomial or exponential crossover, and retained if they improve upon the target. In the present framework, this update can be interpreted as an operator-splitting mechanism: differential mutation and crossover define an
empirical-law-dependent proposal kernel, while greedy replacement defines an objective-based selection step. A McKean–Vlasov or Fokker–Planck limit is therefore a possible modelling limit under an appropriate scaling regime, not a result contained in the original DE analysis.

Particle swarm optimization
[
19
]
is also
population-dependent, but its state includes both positions and velocities, together with memory variables such as personal-best and social-best locations. The update combines inertial motion with attraction toward these remembered best positions, while random acceleration coefficients provide stochastic exploration. Thus PSO
is not simply a selection–recombination mechanism in the same sense as a genetic algorithm. In the present TRJ perspective, DE and PSO are best viewed as examples whose elementary update components can be represented by suitable proposal, memory, and selection operators, after enlarging the state space where necessary; Table
1
records this structural correspondence, while Appendix
C
gives the associated pre-generator formalism under the assumptions imposed in this paper.

Parametric methods: CMA-ES, NES, and EDAs.

Parametric methods motivate the state-space/search-space distinction central to our framework. The internal state
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
parameterizes a sampling kernel
K(x,\cdot)
on the candidate space
{\mathcal{S}}
. Candidates are sampled and evaluated in
{\mathcal{S}}
, while the persistent population state is updated through the parameters of the sampling law.

CMA-ES
[
15
]
adapts a Gaussian
search distribution by combining rank-based selection, weighted
recombination of selected steps, covariance adaptation, and cumulative
step-size control. It is therefore a particularly transparent instance of the parametric structure: Gaussian sampling provides the proposal mechanism, fitness ranking selects informative samples, weighted recombination updates the distribution mean, and covariance and step-size adaptation update the shape and scale of the sampling law. NES
[
41
]

uses the same broad distributional-search viewpoint, but updates the
parameters of the search distribution by natural-gradient ascent on expected fitness, typically estimated through score-function, or log-likelihood-gradient, identities. EDAs
[
23
,
21
]

replace explicit crossover and mutation operators by estimation and sampling from a probabilistic model fitted to selected individuals. Thus CMA-ES, NES, and EDAs share a parametric population state, but differ in the parameter-update rule: CMA-ES uses rank-based weighted recombination together with covariance and step-size adaptation; NES uses natural-gradient updates of the search-distribution parameters; and EDAs use maximum likelihood, marginal-frequency, graphical-model, or smoothed estimation updates depending on the chosen model class.

Diversity-preserving methods.

Niching genetic algorithms modify selection or survivor replacement so as to maintain population diversity across multiple basins
[
14
,
31
]
. Fitness sharing does this by replacing the raw fitness with a shared fitness that penalizes crowded niches, whereas crowding-based methods use similarity-dependent replacement rules to prevent offspring from eliminating individuals in distant niches. In the classical sharing and crowding variants, the underlying mutation and recombination
operators may be kept the same as in the base GA; the niching mechanism enters primarily through the selection or replacement pre-generator. This illustrates the modularity of the operator-splitting approach: modifying one pre-generator is enough to represent a broad class of diversity-preserving variants.

Gradient-based methods as a degenerate case.

The convergence theory of stochastic gradient methods and adaptive variants such as AdaGrad and Adam is extensive

[
30
,
9
,
20
,
29
]
. These methods fit into the present formalism only as degenerate, non-population limits: the sampling kernel is
K(x,\cdot)=\delta_{x}
, so the algorithmic state itself is the candidate state, and there is no explicit population-level selection
or recombination. For SGD this gives a single-particle transport or stochastic approximation dynamics driven by gradient-oracle noise. For Adam one must enlarge the state to include the first- and second-moment variables, so that the update becomes a multi-channel state dynamics rather than a pure position update. This is structurally reminiscent of covariance or step-size adaptation in evolution strategies, in the limited sense that both augment the search state by adaptive variables controlling the scale or geometry of subsequent moves. The distinctive role of the present framework, however, lies in the explicit selection
and recombination generators, which have no direct analogue in ordinary
gradient methods.

B.3.
Mathematical infrastructure

Gradient flows and optimal transport.

Gradient flows in metric spaces
[
1
]

and the JKO scheme
[
18
]

provide a variational framework for dissipative measure-valued dynamics;
in particular, the JKO scheme identifies the Fokker–Planck equation as a Wasserstein gradient flow of the free energy. Unbalanced optimal transport extends the transport geometry to measures with unequal mass by adding a source term to the continuity equation, leading to Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao or Hellinger–Kantorovich type geometries

[
5
,
22
]
. Our population metric
d_{q,2}^{*}
is inspired by this line of work, but the present framework is not variational: the TRJ equation is not derived as the gradient flow of a single energy functional, and convergence is proved by Lyapunov estimates rather than by a metric-gradient-flow structure.

McKean–Vlasov equations and mean-field methods.

McKean–Vlasov SDEs
[
35
,
3
]

describe stochastic dynamics whose coefficients depend on their own law,
and they arise as mean-field limits of interacting particle systems. Propagation of chaos
[
35
]
is the classical mechanism by which mean-field limits can be related to finite-population systems, when the corresponding approximation theorem is available. In our setting, the mutation operator gives a McKean–Vlasov-type transport–diffusion dynamics, while selection and recombination add reaction and jump terms beyond the classical diffusive McKean–Vlasov SDE setting. Related measure-valued dynamics also
appear in mean-field analyses of neural-network training

[
17
]
and in mean-field games

[
3
]
; the former focus on gradient
or Langevin training in parameter space, while the latter focus on strategic control and equilibrium. The operator-calculus viewpoint developed here is different: it is designed for derivative-free population-based search and separates mutation, selection, and recombination at the generator level.

Operator splitting and dissipation.

Operator-splitting methods, including the Lie–Trotter product formula and Strang splitting
[
36
,
34
]
, are classical in numerical PDE. Our generation step

T_{\tau}=M_{\tau}\circ R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}\circ S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
,
corresponding to selection, then recombination, then mutation, is a
Lie–Trotter-type splitting of the TRJ mechanisms. Theorem
3.2

shows that, in the infinitesimal limit, the generator of the composition is the sum of the component generators. The key difference from classical splitting is that our operators act on finite measures and include a mass-conservative but mass-redistributing selection component. The closed Lyapunov inequality (
7
) plays the role of a population-level Polyak–Łojasiewicz or entropy-dissipation condition:

G[\bar{\mu}](\Upsilon)\leq-\lambda\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu})

directly yields exponential decay. The modular verification in
Proposition
4.2
is tailored to the additive generator structure of the TRJ equation.

B.4.
Synthesis: the operator decomposition across algorithm families

The preceding discussion reveals a common organizing principle: the
algorithm families surveyed above can be represented, sometimes after
degenerate identifications or augmentation of the state, in terms of mutation,
selection, and recombination-type mechanisms. Table
1

consolidates this observation by displaying one operator decomposition for
nine representative families.

The decomposition is not unique. For instance, covariance adaptation in
CMA-ES can be assigned either to the proposal mechanism, since it changes
the Gaussian sampling law, or to the recombination/adaptation mechanism,
since it aggregates information from selected samples. The splitting shown
in Table
1
is the one most convenient for deriving the
pre-generators and checking the regularity assumptions (A1)–(A4) in
Assumption
3.1
. For direct population-based
methods such as GAs and DE, the basic operators act on candidate solutions
in
{\mathcal{S}}
. For ES and PSO, the Markovian state is more naturally enlarged:
ES may include strategy parameters such as step sizes or covariance variables,
while PSO includes velocities and memory variables. For parametric methods
such as CMA-ES, NES, and EDAs, the persistent state lies in a parameter
space
{\mathcal{X}}
for a sampling kernel
K(x,\cdot)
on
{\mathcal{S}}
; candidates are
sampled and evaluated in
{\mathcal{S}}
, but the long-term update acts on the
parameters of the search distribution.

Each pre-generator derived in Appendix
C

corresponds to one of the three columns in Table
1
. In the
diffusive regularity class considered here, the mutation pre-generator is a
nonlinear Fokker–Planck operator. The selection pre-generator is a
replicator-type reaction driven by a bounded centered pressure, and the
recombination pre-generator is a pure-jump redistribution operator generated
by a mixing kernel. This correspondence is not accidental; it reflects the
three sufficient regularity classes used in the analysis: mean-field diffusion
mutation (Assumption
5.2
), bounded-pressure
selection (Assumption
5.7
), and recombination
through a Lipschitz mixing map
(Assumption
5.13
). Under these assumptions,
the Lie–Trotter composition in
Theorem
3.2
yields a well-defined infinitesimal
TRJ generator. Thus the table should be read as a structural embedding of
algorithmic building blocks into the regular operator classes used in this
paper, rather than as a claim that every implementation of every listed
method satisfies the assumptions without augmentation or regularization.

Definition C.1

(Pre-generator)

\displaystyle=\|x+\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{3}\leq 4\|x\|^{3}+4\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{3},

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq 4\tau^{3}\|b(x)\|^{3}+4\tau^{3/2}\|B(x)\psi\|^{3}\leq 4\tau^{3}\|b(x)\|^{3}+4\tau^{3/2}\|B(x)\|_{F}^{3}\|\psi\|^{3}.

Taking expectations in
\psi
and using the assumption
{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\psi\|^{3}<\infty
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{3}\leq 4\tau^{3}\|b(x)\|^{3}+4\tau^{3/2}\|B(x)\|_{F}^{3}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\psi\|^{3}.

Hence for
\tau\leq 1
, we can use the linear growth assumption for
b
and
B
to find
C^{\prime}(r)<\infty
such that

(37)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{3}\leq C^{\prime}(r)\tau^{3/2}(1+\|x\|^{3}).

Then, we can choose
C_{0}(r)<\infty
to obtain the following bound for all
\tau\in(0,1]
:

{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\!\bigl[1+\|m_{\tau}(x)\|^{3}\bigr]\leq 1+4\|x\|^{3}+4C^{\prime}(r)\tau^{3/2}(1+\|x\|^{3})\leq C_{0}(r)(1+\|x\|^{3}).

Therefore, setting
r_{M}(r):=\max\{C_{0}(r)r,\,r+1\}
and
\tau_{0}(r):=1
gives

(38)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1+\|y\|^{3})\,M_{\tau}[\mu](dy)\leq C_{0}(r)\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1+\|x\|^{3})\,\mu(dx)\leq C_{0}(r)\,r,

which shows that (A2) holds.

Part (iii): Near-identity in
d_{3,2}^{*}
(A3).

To show the claim, we divide the proof into two parts following the definition of the population distance.

(iii.a) Bound for
d_{\mathrm{BL},3}
.
Let
f\in\mathrm{BL}_{3}
be a function such that
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}\leq 1
, where the norm
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}
is given by

\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}:=\sup_{x}\frac{|f(x)|}{1+\|x\|^{3}}+\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\|x-y\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})}.

Therefore, if
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}\leq 1
, the second term in the sum must be
\leq 1
, which directly implies the inequality

|f(y)-f(x)|\leq\|y-x\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}).

Hence, we have that

\bigl|{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[f(m_{\tau}(x))-f(x)\bigr]\bigr|\leq{\mathbb{E}}|f(m_{\tau}(x))-f(x)|\leq{\mathbb{E}}\!\bigl[\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|\bigl(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|m_{\tau}(x)\|^{2}\bigr)\bigr].

Using this inequality, we can now write

\displaystyle|\langle f,M_{\tau}[\mu]-\mu\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\bigl[f(m_{\tau}(x))-f(x)\bigr]\,\mu(dx)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\!\bigl[\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|m_{\tau}(x)\|^{2})\bigr]\,\mu(dx).

From
\|m_{\tau}(x)\|^{2}=\|x+\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{2}\leq 2\|x\|^{2}+2\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{2}
, we have

1+\|x\|^{2}+\|m_{\tau}(x)\|^{2}\leq 1+3\|x\|^{2}+2\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{2},

and therefore

(39)

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|m_{\tau}(x)\|^{2})\leq\underbrace{3(1+\|x\|^{2})\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|}_{=:I_{1}}+\underbrace{3\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{3}}_{=:I_{2}}.

Next, let us find bounds for
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
. Noting that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\psi\|<\infty
follows from
{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\psi\|^{3}<\infty
(Assumption
5.2
), we can now use the linear growth assumption for
b
and
B
to find
C(r)<\infty
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|\leq\tau\|b(x)\|+\sqrt{\tau}\|B(x)\|_{F}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\psi\|\leq C(r)\sqrt{\tau}\,(1+\|x\|)\quad\text{for }\tau\leq 1.

Hence, together with (
37
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[I_{1}]

\displaystyle=3(1+\|x\|^{2})\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|\leq C_{1}(r)\sqrt{\tau}\,(1+\|x\|^{3}),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[I_{2}]

\displaystyle=3\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\Delta_{\tau}(x)\|^{3}\leq C_{2}(r)\tau^{3/2}(1+\|x\|^{3})

for some constants
C_{1}(r),C_{2}(r)<\infty
. Hence, for
\tau\leq 1
and
C_{3}(r):=C_{1}(r)+C_{2}(r)
,

|\langle f,M_{\tau}[\mu]-\mu\rangle|\leq C_{3}(r)\sqrt{\tau}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1+\|x\|^{3})\,\mu(dx)\leq C_{3}(r)\sqrt{\tau}\,r_{M}(r),

since
\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1+\|x\|^{3})\,\mu(dx)\leq r_{M}(r)
for
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{3,+}^{r_{M}(r)}
. Now, defining
C_{M}^{\mathrm{BL}}(r):=C_{3}(r)\,r_{M}(r)
and taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}\leq 1
yields

(40)

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{BL},3}(M_{\tau}[\mu],\mu)\leq C_{M}^{\mathrm{BL}}(r)\sqrt{\tau}.

(iii.b) Bound for
W_{2}\bigl(\overline{M_{\tau}[\mu]},\bar{\mu}\bigr)
.
Let us start by showing that normalization commutes with mutation. Because
p_{\tau}(\cdot,\cdot;\mu)
depends on
\mu
only through
\bar{\mu}=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})
, and because
M_{\tau}
is linear in
\mu
, we have for any
c>0
that
M_{\tau}[c\mu]=c\,M_{\tau}[\mu]
. Moreover, mass preservation gives
M_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=\mu({\mathcal{X}})
. Therefore,

\overline{M_{\tau}[\mu]}=\frac{M_{\tau}[\mu]}{M_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})}=\frac{M_{\tau}[\mu]}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}=M_{\tau}\!\left[\frac{\mu}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\right]=M_{\tau}[\bar{\mu}].

So the Wasserstein term is
W_{2}\bigl(\overline{M_{\tau}[\mu]},\bar{\mu}\bigr)=W_{2}(M_{\tau}[\bar{\mu}],\bar{\mu})
.

Next, let us construct a synchronous coupling bound for
W_{2}
. Let
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})\in{\mathscr{P}}_{3}({\mathcal{X}})
and define a coupling between
X\sim\bar{\mu}
and
Y\sim M_{\tau}[\bar{\mu}]
by using the same noise

Y=X+\tau\,b_{M}(X;\bar{\mu})+\sqrt{\tau}\,B_{M}(X;\bar{\mu})\,\psi,\quad\psi\sim\operatorname{Law}(\psi),\ {\mathbb{E}}[\psi]=0,\ {\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}[\psi\psi^{\top}]=\Sigma.

Then, by definition of
W_{2}
,

W_{2}^{2}(M_{\tau}[\bar{\mu}],\bar{\mu})\leq{\mathbb{E}}\|Y-X\|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\|\Delta_{\tau}(X)\|^{2},\qquad\Delta_{\tau}(X):=\tau b(X)+\sqrt{\tau}B(X)\psi,

where we can use the convexity of
\|\cdot\|^{2}
to obtain

{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|\Delta_{\tau}(X)\|^{2}\leq 2\tau^{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|b(X)\|^{2}+2\tau\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|B(X)\psi\|^{2}.

Using
{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|B(X)\psi\|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\bigl[\mathop{\rm Tr}(B(X)\Sigma B(X)^{\top})\bigr]
when
{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}[\psi\psi^{\top}]=\Sigma
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi}\|B(X)\psi\|^{2}\leq\|\Sigma\|_{F}\,{\mathbb{E}}\|B(X)\|_{F}^{2}.

Now from the linear growth assumption with constant
C(r)<\infty
, we get

\|b(X)\|+\|B(X)\|_{F}\leq C(r)(1+\|X\|).

Hence, we can find
C^{\prime}(r)<\infty
such that

\|b(X)\|^{2}+\|\Sigma\|_{F}\|B(X)\|_{F}^{2}\leq C^{\prime}(r)(1+\|X\|^{2}).

Since
\bar{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{3}({\mathcal{X}})
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}\|X\|^{2}<\infty
, and on the moment sublevel, as
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{3,+}^{r_{M}(r),m}
, hence
\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m>0
, we have a uniform bound
{\mathbb{E}}[1+\|X\|^{2}]\leq C^{\prime\prime}(r,m)
, where we write
r=r_{M}(r)
for short. Therefore

{\mathbb{E}}\|\Delta_{\tau}(X)\|^{2}\leq 2\tau^{2}C^{\prime}(r)\,{\mathbb{E}}[1+\|X\|^{2}]+2\tau C^{\prime}(r)\,{\mathbb{E}}[1+\|X\|^{2}]=:C_{*}(r,m)\tau

for
\tau\leq 1
absorbing the
\tau^{2}
term. Thus,

W_{2}(M_{\tau}[\bar{\mu}],\bar{\mu})\leq\sqrt{C_{*}(r,m)}\sqrt{\tau}=:C_{M}^{W}(r,m)\sqrt{\tau}.

Returning to
\mu
, we have shown

(41)

\displaystyle W_{2}\bigl(\overline{M_{\tau}[\mu]},\bar{\mu}\bigr)=W_{2}(M_{\tau}[\bar{\mu}],\bar{\mu})\leq C_{M}^{W}(r,m)\sqrt{\tau}.

Now combining (
40
) and (
41
) gives

\displaystyle d_{3,2}^{*}(M_{\tau}[\mu],\mu)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{BL},3}(M_{\tau}[\mu],\mu)+W_{2}\bigl(\overline{M_{\tau}[\mu]},\bar{\mu}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(C_{M}^{\mathrm{BL}}(r)+C_{M}^{W}(r,m)\bigr)\sqrt{\tau}=:C_{M}(r,m)\sqrt{\tau},

which shows that (A3) holds.

Part (iv): Local Lipschitz continuity of
G_{M}
(A4).

Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
and
r<\infty
. For
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{3,+}^{r_{M}(r)}
with
\mu({\mathcal{X}}),\nu({\mathcal{X}})>0
, write
\bar{\mu}=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})
,
\bar{\nu}=\nu/\nu({\mathcal{X}})
, and

G_{M}[\mu](\varphi)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}F(x;\bar{\mu})\,\mu(dx),

where

F(x;\bar{\mu}):=\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl(a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})D^{2}\varphi(x)\bigr).

Then

(42)

\displaystyle G_{M}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{M}[\nu](\varphi)=\int\underbrace{(F(x;\bar{\mu})-F(x;\bar{\nu}))}_{=:T_{1}}\mu(dx)+\int\underbrace{F(x;\bar{\nu})}_{=:T_{2}}(\mu-\nu)(dx).

(iv.1) Growth bound for the integrand
T_{2}
of form
|F(x;\bar{\nu})|\lesssim 1+\|x\|^{2}
.

Because
\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{3,+}^{r_{M}(r)}
, the coefficient bounds from Assumption
5.2
apply with constants depending on the sublevel
r_{M}(r)
; i.e., there exist
C=C_{r_{M}(r)}<\infty
and
L=L_{r_{M}(r)}<\infty
such that for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

\displaystyle\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\leq C(1+\|x\|),

\displaystyle\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-b_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\leq L\|x-y\|.

Also, since
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, we use the bounded derivative norms

\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}<\infty,\quad\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}<\infty,\quad\|D^{3}\varphi\|_{\rm op,\infty}<\infty.

Starting with the first term, we have

(43)

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})|\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\,2C(1+\|x\|^{2}),

since
\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|\leq C(1+\|x\|)
and
1+\|x\|\leq 2(1+\|x\|^{2})
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. This follows from the elementary inequality
t\leq 1+t^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
, hence
1+t\leq 1+(1+t^{2})=2+t^{2}\leq 2(1+t^{2})
. For the second term in
F(x;\bar{\nu})
, we have

\bigl|\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})D^{2}\varphi(x))\bigr|\leq\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\,\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}\leq\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}^{2}\,\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}.

By linear growth,
\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\leq C(1+\|x\|)
, hence

(44)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})D^{2}\varphi(x))\bigr|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}C^{2}(1+\|x\|)^{2}\leq C^{2}(1+\|x\|^{2})\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}.

Combining (
43
) and (
44
) gives the claimed growth:

(45)

\displaystyle|F(x;\bar{\nu})|\leq\bigl(2C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}+C^{2}\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}\bigr)(1+\|x\|^{2})=:C_{T_{2}}(r,\varphi)(1+\|x\|^{2}).

Then, for the weighted sup part of
\|F\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}
, we have

(46)

\displaystyle\|F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})\|_{0,3}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{|F(x;\bar{\nu})|}{1+\|x\|^{3}}\leq C_{T_{2}}(r,\varphi)\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{1+\|x\|^{2}}{1+\|x\|^{3}}\leq 2C_{T_{2}}(r,\varphi),

since
(1+\|x\|^{2})/(1+\|x\|^{3})\leq 2
for all
\|x\|\geq 0
.

(iv.2) Weighted Lipschitz seminorm.

To show that
\|F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}<\infty
, we also need to establish
|F(x;\bar{\nu})-F(y;\bar{\nu})|\lesssim\|x-y\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})
by finding bounds for the terms
A
and
B
in the decomposition

\displaystyle F(x;\bar{\nu})-F(y;\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-\nabla\varphi(y)\cdot b_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})}_{=:A}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}\underbrace{\bigl(\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})D^{2}\varphi(x))-\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})D^{2}\varphi(y))\bigr)}_{=:B}.

By adding and subtracting
\nabla\varphi(y)\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})
, we can write

A=\underbrace{(\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(y))\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})}_{=:A_{1}}+\underbrace{\nabla\varphi(y)\cdot(b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-b_{M}(y;\bar{\nu}))}_{=:A_{2}}.

Using the mean value theorem, we can bound the gradient difference by
\|\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(y)\|\leq\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}\|x-y\|
to obtain

(47)

\displaystyle|A_{1}|=|(\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(y))\cdot b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})|\leq\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}\|x-y\|\,C(1+\|x\|).

For the
b_{M}
-difference, we have

\displaystyle|A_{2}|

\displaystyle=|\nabla\varphi(y)\cdot(b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-b_{M}(y;\bar{\nu}))|\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-b_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\,L\|x-y\|.

Combining the bounds (
47
) and (
C.2.1
), and noting that
1+\|x\|\leq 2(1+\|x\|^{2})\leq 2(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})
, gives

\displaystyle|A|

\displaystyle\leq|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|\leq\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}\|x-y\|\,2C(1+\|x\|^{2})+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\,L\|x-y\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(2C\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}L\bigr)\|x-y\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})

(49)

\displaystyle=:C_{A}(r,\varphi)\|x-y\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}).

Next, adding and subtracting
\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})D^{2}\varphi(x))
, we can write

B=\underbrace{\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl((a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu}))D^{2}\varphi(x)\bigr)}_{=:B_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl(a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})(D^{2}\varphi(x)-D^{2}\varphi(y))\bigr)}_{=:B_{2}}.

For the first term in
B
, we have the bound

|B_{1}|=\bigl|\mathop{\rm Tr}\!\bigl((a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu}))D^{2}\varphi(x)\bigr)\bigr|\leq\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\,\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}.

Now, since
a=B\Sigma B^{\top}
,

\displaystyle a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle=B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\Sigma(B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu}))^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+(B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu}))\Sigma B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})^{\top},

we obtain

\displaystyle\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma\|_{F}\bigl(\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}+\|B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\bigr)\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})-B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma\|_{F}\bigl(C(1+\|x\|)+C(1+\|y\|)\bigr)L\|x-y\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\Sigma\|_{F}\,CL(1+\|x\|+\|y\|)\|x-y\|.

Because
\|x\|\leq 1+\|x\|^{2}
and
\|y\|\leq 1+\|y\|^{2}
,

1+\|x\|+\|y\|\leq 1+(1+\|x\|^{2})+(1+\|y\|^{2})=3+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}\leq 3(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}),

hence the bound for
B_{1}
becomes

\displaystyle|B_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq 6\|\Sigma\|_{F}\,CL\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})\|x-y\|

(50)

\displaystyle=:C_{B,1}(r,\varphi)(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})\|x-y\|.

For the second term in
B
, the boundedness of third derivatives together with the mean value theorem for the Hessian gives

\|D^{2}\varphi(x)-D^{2}\varphi(y)\|_{F}\leq d^{3/2}M_{3}(\varphi)\|x-y\|.

This follows from noting that each second derivative
\partial_{ij}\varphi
is Lipschitz with constant

\max_{k}\|\partial_{ijk}\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq M_{3}(\varphi).

Hence, summing over
i,j
yields the Frobenius bound with factor
d^{3/2}
. Since also

\|a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\leq\|\Sigma\|_{F}\|B_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\Sigma\|_{F}C^{2}(1+\|y\|)^{2},

we obtain

\displaystyle|B_{2}|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\mathop{\rm Tr}(a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})(D^{2}\varphi(x)-D^{2}\varphi(y)))\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\|a_{M}(y;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\,\|D^{2}\varphi(x)-D^{2}\varphi(y)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma\|_{F}C^{2}(1+\|y\|)^{2}d^{3/2}M_{3}(\varphi)\|x-y\|

\displaystyle\leq 5\|\Sigma\|_{F}C^{2}d^{3/2}M_{3}(\varphi)(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})\|x-y\|

(51)

\displaystyle=:C_{B,2}(r,\varphi)(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})\|x-y\|,

where we have used
1+\|y\|\leq 2(1+\|y\|^{2})\leq 2(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})
and
\|D^{3}\varphi(x)\|_{\rm op}\leq d^{3/2}M_{3}(\varphi)
. Hence, combining (
C.2.1
) and (
C.2.1
) and denoting
C_{B}(r,\varphi)=C_{B,1}(r,\varphi)+C_{B,2}(r,\varphi)
gives

(52)

\displaystyle|B|\leq|B_{1}|+|B_{2}|\leq C_{B}(r,\varphi)(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})\|x-y\|.

Therefore, combining (
C.2.1
) and (
52
),

\displaystyle|F(x;\bar{\nu})-F(y;\bar{\nu})|\leq|A|+|B|\leq(C_{A}(r,\varphi)+C_{B}(r,\varphi))\|x-y\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}),

which implies

(53)

\displaystyle[F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})]_{1,2}:=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|F(x;\bar{\nu})-F(y;\bar{\nu})|}{\|x-y\|(1+\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})}\leq C_{A}(r,\varphi)+C_{B}(r,\varphi).

Together (
46
) and (
53
) ensure that

\|F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}=\|F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})\|_{0,3}+[F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})]_{1,2}

and

\sup_{\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{3,+}^{r_{M}(r)}}\|F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}\leq 2C_{T_{2}}(r,\varphi)+C_{A}(r,\varphi)+C_{B}(r,\varphi)=:C_{F}(r,\varphi).

Hence, we obtain a bound for the second term in (
42
) as

(54)

\displaystyle\left|\int F(x;\bar{\nu})\,(\mu-\nu)(dx)\right|\leq\|F(\cdot;\bar{\nu})\|_{\mathrm{BL},3}\,d_{\mathrm{BL},3}(\mu,\nu)\leq C_{F}(r,\varphi)\,d_{\mathrm{BL},3}(\mu,\nu).

(iv.3) Bound for the integral of
T_{1}
in terms of
W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
.

By Assumption
5.2
(ii) with
x=y
,

\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\leq L(r)\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

Also

\displaystyle\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma\|_{F}\bigl(\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})\|_{F}+\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\bigr)\|B_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-B_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\Sigma\|_{F}\,C(r_{M}(r))L(r_{M}(r))(1+\|x\|)\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}),

using linear growth and Lipschitz continuity. Consequently,

\displaystyle|F(x;\bar{\mu})-F(x;\bar{\nu})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\|b_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-b_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\|a_{M}(x;\bar{\mu})-a_{M}(x;\bar{\nu})\|_{F}\,\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}L(r_{M}(r))W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle\quad+C(r_{M}(r))L(r_{M}(r))(1+\|x\|)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}L(r_{M}(r))+C(r_{M}(r))L(r_{M}(r))(1+\|x\|)\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{F,\infty}\Bigr)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle=:C_{T_{1}}(r,\varphi)(1+\|x\|)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

Integrating against
\mu
and using
\int(1+\|x\|)\,\mu(dx)\leq 2\int(1+\|x\|^{3})\,\mu(dx)\leq 2r_{M}(r)
, since
\|x\|\leq 1+\|x\|^{3}
implies that
1+\|x\|\leq 2(1+\|x\|^{3})
, we obtain

(55)

\displaystyle\int|T_{1}|\,\mu(dx)=\int|F(x;\bar{\mu})-F(x;\bar{\nu})|\,d\mu(x)\leq 2C_{T_{1}}(r,\varphi)\,r_{M}(r)\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

By combining (
55
) and (
54
), we obtain a bound for (
42
) as

\displaystyle|G_{M}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{M}[\nu](\varphi)|

\displaystyle\leq\int|T_{1}|\,\mu(dx)+\left|\int F(x;\bar{\nu})\,(\mu-\nu)(dx)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{T_{1}}(r,\varphi)\,r_{M}(r)\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})+C_{F}(r,\varphi)\,d_{\mathrm{BL},3}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(2C_{T_{1}}(r,\varphi)r_{M}(r)+C_{F}(r,\varphi)\bigr)\bigl(d_{\mathrm{BL},3}(\mu,\nu)+W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\bigr)

\displaystyle=:L_{M,\varphi}(r)\,d_{3,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu),

which establishes (A4) and completes the proof.
∎

C.2.2.
Selection operator: pre-generator verification

Proposition C.3

(Selection pre-generator and regularity)
.

Suppose Assumption
5.7
(i) holds, and let
r<\infty
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
. For any
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
with
\mu({\mathcal{X}})>0
, define

\bar{\Phi}(\mu):=\frac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx),

and set
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m}:=\{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}:\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m\}
for
m>0
(with
m=1
when
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
). Then the following statements hold.

(i)

Pre-generator (A1).
The pre-generators for the balanced and unbalanced selection operators,
S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
and
S_{\tau}
, are given by

(56)

\displaystyle G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0}\frac{\langle\varphi,S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\Phi(x;\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\mu))\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx),

\displaystyle G_{S}[\mu](\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0}\frac{\langle\varphi,S_{\tau}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\mu)\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx),

with convergence uniform on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
.

(ii)

Moment stability (A2).
For all
\tau\in(0,1]
and all
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
,

S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{\rm bal}},\quad S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\quad\text{for }r_{\rm bal}:=e^{2C_{\Phi}(r)}r,

and
S_{\tau}[\mu]\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r^{\prime}}
for
r^{\prime}:=e^{C_{\Phi}(r)}r
.

(iii)

Near-identity in
d_{q,2}^{*}
(A3).
Suppose either
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
or
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m}
for some
m>0
. There exists
C_{S}(r,m)<\infty
such that for all
\tau\in(0,1]
,

d_{q,2}^{*}(S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu],\mu)\leq C_{S}(r,m)\,\tau^{1/2}.

If
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
, this holds with
m=1
. Since
\overline{S_{\tau}[\mu]}=\overline{S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]}
, the
W_{2}
-part of
d_{q,2}^{*}
is identical for
S_{\tau}
and
S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
.

(iv)

Local Lipschitz continuity of
G_{S}^{\rm bal}
(A4).
Suppose additionally Assumption
5.7
(ii) and (iii) hold, and let
\mu,\nu
be as in (iii). For every fixed
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
there exists
L_{S,\varphi}(r,m)<\infty
such that

|G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\nu](\varphi)|\leq L_{S,\varphi}(r,m)\,d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu).

For the unbalanced operator, the same estimate holds on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
with a constant
L_{S,\varphi}(r)
independent of the mass lower bound
m
.

Proof.

Throughout, fix
r<\infty
,
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
, and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
.

Part (i): Pre-generator (A1).

Define

N_{\tau}(\mu;\varphi):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx),\qquad Z_{\tau}(\mu):=\frac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx),

so that
\langle\varphi,S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]\rangle=N_{\tau}(\mu;\varphi)/Z_{\tau}(\mu)
.

By Assumption
5.7
(i), on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
we have
|\Phi(x;\mu)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)
. Hence, for fixed
(x,\mu)
, we can apply the second-order Taylor expansion of
t\mapsto e^{-t\Phi(x;\mu)}
at
t=0
evaluated at
t=\tau
to obtain

e^{-\tau\Phi(x;\mu)}=1-\tau\Phi(x;\mu)+r_{\tau}(x;\mu),

with the remainder in Lagrange form

r_{\tau}(x;\mu)=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\Phi(x;\mu)^{2}e^{-\theta\tau\Phi(x;\mu)}\quad\text{for some }\theta\in(0,1).

For any
\tau\in(0,1]
it holds that

|r_{\tau}(x;\mu)|\leq C_{1}(r)\tau^{2}\quad\text{uniformly in }x\in{\mathcal{X}},\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r},

where
C_{1}(r):=\tfrac{1}{2}C_{\Phi}(r)^{2}e^{C_{\Phi}(r)}
.

Using the above, we can proceed by writing an expansion for
N_{\tau}
as

\displaystyle N_{\tau}(\mu;\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\bigl(1-\tau\Phi(x;\mu)+r_{\tau}(x;\mu)\bigr)\,\mu(dx)

(57)

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle-\tau\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\Phi(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)+R_{N}(\mu;\varphi,\tau),

where
R_{N}(\mu;\varphi,\tau):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)r_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)
. Since
\varphi
is bounded,
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}<\infty
, we obtain

|R_{N}(\mu;\varphi,\tau)|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}C_{1}(r)\tau^{2}\mu({\mathcal{X}})\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}C_{1}(r)\tau^{2}r,

as
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
by assumption. Likewise, for
Z_{\tau}
, we get the expansion

Z_{\tau}(\mu)=\frac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)=1-\tau\bar{\Phi}(\mu)+R_{Z}(\mu;\tau),

where
R_{Z}(\mu;\tau):=\frac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}r_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)
with bound
|R_{Z}(\mu;\tau)|\leq C_{1}(r)\tau^{2}
uniformly in
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
.

Let us next find an expansion for the inverse
1/Z_{\tau}(\mu)
around
1
. As above, write
Z_{\tau}(\mu)=1-a_{\tau}(\mu)
with
a_{\tau}(\mu)=\tau\bar{\Phi}(\mu)-R_{Z}(\mu;\tau)
. Then

|a_{\tau}(\mu)|\leq\tau C_{\Phi}(r)+C_{1}(r)\tau^{2}.

Choose
\tau_{0}>0
so that
|a_{\tau}(\mu)|\leq 1/2
for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}]
and
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
. Then, by Taylor’s theorem for
h(b)=(1-b)^{-1}
around
0
, we get
h(b)=h(0)+h^{\prime}(0)b+R_{h}(b)
with
R_{h}(b)=h^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\tau}(\mu))b^{2}/2
for some
\xi_{\tau}(\mu)\in(0,a_{\tau}(\mu))
, where
h^{\prime}(b)=(1-b)^{-2}
and
h^{\prime\prime}(b)=2(1-b)^{-3}
. Hence we obtain

(58)

\frac{1}{Z_{\tau}(\mu)}=1+a_{\tau}(\mu)+R_{h}(a_{\tau}(\mu))=1+\tau\bar{\Phi}(\mu)-R_{Z}(\mu;\tau)+R_{h}(a_{\tau}(\mu)).

Since
|a_{\tau}(\mu)|\leq 1/2
implies
|1-\xi_{\tau}(\mu)|\geq 1/2
, we get
|h^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\tau}(\mu))|\leq 2/(1-|\xi_{\tau}(\mu)|)^{3}\leq 2\cdot 2^{3}=16
. Then,

|R_{h}(a_{\tau}(\mu))|=\Bigl|\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\tau}(\mu))}{2}a_{\tau}(\mu)^{2}\Bigr|\leq 8\,a_{\tau}(\mu)^{2}\leq 8(C_{\Phi}(r)+C_{1}(r)\tau_{0})^{2}\tau^{2}=:C_{2}(r)\tau^{2}.

For the combined remainder
\widetilde{R}_{Z^{-1}}(\mu;\tau):=R_{h}(a_{\tau}(\mu))-R_{Z}(\mu;\tau)
, we obtain

|\widetilde{R}_{Z^{-1}}(\mu;\tau)|\leq|R_{h}(a_{\tau}(\mu))|+|R_{Z}(\mu;\tau)|\leq C_{2}(r)\tau^{2}+C_{1}(r)\tau^{2}=:C_{3}(r)\tau^{2}

uniformly in
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
.

We now combine (
C.2.2
) and (
58
) to obtain

\frac{N_{\tau}(\mu;\varphi)}{Z_{\tau}(\mu)}=\bigl(\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle-\tau\langle\varphi\Phi,\mu\rangle+R_{N}(\mu;\varphi,\tau)\bigr)\bigl(1+\tau\bar{\Phi}(\mu)+\widetilde{R}_{Z^{-1}}(\mu;\tau)\bigr).

To simplify the expression, we define
R_{\rm sel}
as the collection of
O(\tau^{2})
terms of the form

\tau^{2}\bar{\Phi}\langle\varphi\Phi,\mu\rangle,\quad R_{N},\quad R_{N}\tau\bar{\Phi},\quad R_{N}\widetilde{R}_{Z^{-1}},\quad\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle\widetilde{R}_{Z^{-1}},

each bounded by a constant times
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\tau^{2}
uniformly on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
. Then

\langle\varphi,S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]\rangle=\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle-\tau(\langle\varphi\Phi,\mu\rangle-\bar{\Phi}(\mu)\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle)+R_{\rm sel}(\mu;\varphi,\tau),

where
|R_{\rm sel}(\mu;\varphi,\tau)|\leq C_{4}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\tau^{2}
uniformly on
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
. Therefore

\frac{\langle\varphi,S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\Phi(x;\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\mu))\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx)+\frac{R_{\rm sel}(\mu;\varphi,\tau)}{\tau}.

Since
|R_{\rm sel}(\mu;\varphi,\tau)|/\tau\leq C_{4}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\tau\to 0
uniformly in
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
, this proves (
56
) together with the uniform weak-
*
convergence required by the pre-generator definition.

The proof in the case of the unbalanced operator
S_{\tau}
is obtained without ratio arguments. For
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
,

\langle\varphi,S_{\tau}[\mu]\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)e^{-\tau\Phi(x;\mu)}\,\mu(dx),

so that

\frac{\langle\varphi,S_{\tau}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\frac{e^{-\tau\Phi(x;\mu)}-1}{\tau}\,\mu(dx)\to-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\Phi(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx),

and boundedness of
\Phi
on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
gives uniformity, which establishes (A1).

Part (ii): Moment stability (A2).

By Assumption
5.7
(i), there exists
C_{\Phi}(r)<\infty
such that
|\Phi(x;\mu)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
. Then, if
\tau\leq 1
,

(59)

e^{-\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\leq s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\leq e^{\tau C_{\Phi}(r)},

and integrating gives

e^{-\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\mu({\mathcal{X}})\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)\leq e^{\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\mu({\mathcal{X}}).

Thus, for the balanced operator
S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu](dx)=s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\mu(dx)/Z_{\tau}(\mu)
, the scaling term satisfies
Z_{\tau}(\mu)\in[e^{-\tau C_{\Phi}(r)},e^{\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}]
. Let us define
g_{\tau}(x;\mu):=s_{\tau}(x;\mu)/Z_{\tau}(\mu)
. Combining the bounds on
s_{\tau}
and
Z_{\tau}
gives

(60)

e^{-2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\leq g_{\tau}(x;\mu)\leq e^{2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}.

Therefore,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dS_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}(x)g_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)\leq e^{2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu\leq e^{2C_{\Phi}(r)}r,

which yields moment stability with
r_{\rm bal}:=e^{2C_{\Phi}(r)}r
. Mass preservation is immediate from the definition of the balanced operator.

For the unbalanced operator
S_{\tau}[\mu](dx)=s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\mu(dx)
, the pointwise bound (
59
) gives for
\tau\leq 1

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dS_{\tau}[\mu]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}(x)s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)\leq e^{\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu\leq e^{C_{\Phi}(r)}r.

Hence we can choose
r^{\prime}(r):=e^{C_{\Phi}(r)}r
for the unbalanced operator, which completes the proof of (A2).

Part (iii): Near-identity in
d_{q,2}^{*}
(A3).

We check the claim in two parts by considering the
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}
and
W_{2}
components separately.

(iii.a) BL component.
Let
f
satisfy
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1
. By definition of
\|f\|_{0,q}
, we have
|f(x)|\leq w_{q}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Write
S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu](dx)=g_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx)
with
g_{\tau}=s_{\tau}/Z_{\tau}
as in (ii). Then, by (
60
),

\sup_{x}|g_{\tau}(x;\mu)-1|\leq e^{2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}-1\leq 2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)e^{2C_{\Phi}(r)}=:K_{1}(r)\tau,\quad\tau\leq 1.

Hence, if
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
,

|\langle f,S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]-\mu\rangle|\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}|g_{\tau}(x;\mu)-1|\int_{\mathcal{X}}|f|\,d\mu\leq K_{1}(r)\tau\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu\leq K_{1}(r)\,r\,\tau,

and taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1
gives

(61)

d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu],\mu)\leq K_{1}(r)r\tau,

uniformly on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
.

(iii.b) Wasserstein component.
Assume
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m}
with
q\geq 2
, set
m_{\mu}:=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m
and
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/m_{\mu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
. Write

S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu](dx)=g_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx),\qquad\bar{\mu}_{\tau}:=\overline{S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]}.

Since
S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}
preserves mass,
\bar{\mu}_{\tau}
is given by

\bar{\mu}_{\tau}(dx)=g_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\bar{\mu}(dx),\qquad\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{\tau}(x;\mu)\,\bar{\mu}(dx)=1.

By bounded pressure, for
\tau\leq 1
we have the uniform bounds

e^{-2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\leq g_{\tau}(x;\mu)\leq e^{2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}.

Define
\epsilon_{\tau}:=1-e^{-2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\in(0,1)
and the non-negative measure on
{\mathcal{X}}
by

\pi_{\tau}^{\rm res}(dx):=\frac{g_{\tau}(x;\mu)-e^{-2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}}{\epsilon_{\tau}}\,\bar{\mu}(dx).

Then
\pi_{\tau}^{\rm res}\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
and the normalized update admits the convex decomposition

\bar{\mu}_{\tau}=(1-\epsilon_{\tau})\bar{\mu}+\epsilon_{\tau}\pi_{\tau}^{\rm res}.

Construct a coupling
(X,Y)
of
(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\tau})
as follows: sample
X\sim\bar{\mu}
, sample
Z\sim\pi_{\tau}^{\rm res}
independently, sample
B\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\epsilon_{\tau})
independently, and set
Y:=X
if
B=0
and
Y:=Z
if
B=1
. Then
Y\sim\bar{\mu}_{\tau}
and

W_{2}^{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\tau})\leq{\mathbb{E}}\|X-Y\|^{2}=\epsilon_{\tau}\,{\mathbb{E}}\|X-Z\|^{2}\leq 2\epsilon_{\tau}\bigl({\mathbb{E}}\|X\|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\|Z\|^{2}\bigr).

Moreover, by the upper bound on
g_{\tau}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\pi_{\tau}^{\rm res}}{d\bar{\mu}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{\tau}(x;\mu)-e^{-2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}}{\epsilon_{\tau}}\leq\frac{e^{2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}-e^{-2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}}{1-e^{-2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}}

\displaystyle=1+e^{2\tau C_{\Phi}(r)}\leq 1+e^{2C_{\Phi}(r)}=:C_{g}(r),

hence
{\mathbb{E}}\|Z\|^{2}\leq C_{g}(r){\mathbb{E}}\|X\|^{2}
. Since
q\geq 2
,

{\mathbb{E}}\|X\|^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|^{2}\,\bar{\mu}(dx)\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}(x)\,\bar{\mu}(dx)=\frac{1}{m_{\mu}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu\leq\frac{r}{m}.

Therefore

W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\overline{S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]})=W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\tau})\leq C_{S}(r,m)\sqrt{\epsilon_{\tau}}\leq\widetilde{C}_{S}(r,m)\sqrt{\tau},

for constants
C_{S}(r,m),\widetilde{C}_{S}(r,m)<\infty
. Combining with (
61
) yields

d_{q,2}^{*}(S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu],\mu)\leq C_{S}(r,m)\tau^{1/2}

for
\tau\in(0,1]
. Because
\overline{S_{\tau}[\mu]}=\overline{S_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]}
, the same
W_{2}
estimate holds for the unbalanced operator, which establishes (A3).

Part (iv): Local Lipschitz continuity of
G_{S}^{\rm bal}
(A4).

By part (i),

G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\Phi(x;\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\mu))\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx),\qquad\bar{\Phi}(\mu):=\frac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\mu)\,\mu(dx).

Let us now decompose the generator difference as

(62)

G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\nu](\varphi)=-(I_{\mu}-I_{\nu})+(J_{\mu}-J_{\nu}),

where

I_{\mu}:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\mu)\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx),\qquad J_{\mu}:=\bar{\Phi}(\mu)\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx)=\bar{\Phi}(\mu)\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle,

and find bounds for
|I_{\mu}-I_{\nu}|
and
|J_{\mu}-J_{\nu}|
separately.

(iv.1) Bound on
I_{\mu}-I_{\nu}
.
Adding and subtracting
\int\Phi(x;\nu)\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx)
gives

I_{\mu}-I_{\nu}=\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\Phi(x;\mu)-\Phi(x;\nu))\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx)}_{=:A}+\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\nu)\varphi(x)(\mu-\nu)(dx)}_{=:B}.

By Assumption
5.7
(ii), the selection rate satisfies
|\Phi(x;\mu)-\Phi(x;\nu)|\leq L_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Hence

\displaystyle|A|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}|\Phi(x;\mu)-\Phi(x;\nu)|\,|\varphi(x)|\,\mu(dx)\leq L_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\mu({\mathcal{X}})

(63)

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}rL_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}),

where the last inequality follows from noting that
\mu({\mathcal{X}})\leq\int w_{q}\,d\mu\leq r
for
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
.

Next, considering the term
B
, we have by bounded pressure Assumption
5.7
(i) that
|\Phi(x;\nu)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)
for all
x
and
\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
. Thus the integrand
h_{\nu}(x):=\Phi(x;\nu)\varphi(x)
is bounded by
C_{\Phi}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
. Moreover,
h_{\nu}\in\mathrm{BL}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
with

\|h_{\nu}\|_{0,q}\leq\sup_{x}\frac{|h_{\nu}(x)|}{w_{q}(x)}\leq\sup_{x}|h_{\nu}(x)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, since
\Phi
satisfies Assumptions
5.7
(i) and (iii), and
\varphi
is continuously differentiable by assumption and hence Lipschitz, we have for any
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
:

\displaystyle|h_{\nu}(x)-h_{\nu}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|\Phi(x;\nu)||\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|+|\varphi(y)||\Phi(x;\nu)-\Phi(y;\nu)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Phi}(r)\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\|x-y\|+\|\varphi\|_{\infty}K_{\Phi}(r)\|x-y\|\bigl(1+\|x\|^{q-1}+\|y\|^{q-1}\bigr).

As
1\leq 1+\|x\|^{q-1}+\|y\|^{q-1}
, the previous display implies that

[h_{\nu}]_{1,q-1}\leq C_{\Phi}(r)\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\varphi\|_{\infty}K_{\Phi}(r).

Hence

\|h_{\nu}\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq C_{\Phi}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+C_{\Phi}(r)\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\varphi\|_{\infty}K_{\Phi}(r)=:C_{h}(r,\varphi).

By the dual representation of
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}
, the bound for
B
is given by

(64)

|B|=\Bigl|\int_{\mathcal{X}}h_{\nu}(x)(\mu-\nu)(dx)\Bigr|\leq\|h_{\nu}\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)\leq C_{h}(r,\varphi)d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu).

Putting (
C.2.2
) and (
64
) together, we get

(65)

|I_{\mu}-I_{\nu}|\leq C_{h}(r,\varphi)d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)+\|\varphi\|_{\infty}rL_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

(iv.2) Bound on
J_{\mu}-J_{\nu}
.
By adding and subtracting
\bar{\Phi}(\mu)\langle\varphi,\nu\rangle
, we can write

(66)

J_{\mu}-J_{\nu}=\bar{\Phi}(\mu)(\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle-\langle\varphi,\nu\rangle)+(\bar{\Phi}(\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\nu))\langle\varphi,\nu\rangle=:C+D.

First,
|\bar{\Phi}(\mu)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)
because
\bar{\Phi}(\mu)
is an average of
\Phi(\cdot;\mu)
under the probability
\bar{\mu}
. Also
\varphi\in\mathrm{BL}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}
. Hence

\displaystyle|C|

\displaystyle\leq|\bar{\Phi}(\mu)||\langle\varphi,\mu-\nu\rangle|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)

(67)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Phi}(r)(\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty})d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu).

Next, by adding and subtracting terms, we can write
D
in (
66
) via

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}(\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\Bigl(\int\Phi(x;\mu)\mu(dx)-\int\Phi(x;\nu)\nu(dx)\Bigr)}_{=:D_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}-\tfrac{1}{\nu({\mathcal{X}})}\Bigr)\int\Phi(x;\nu)\nu(dx)}_{=:D_{2}}.

To control
D_{1}
, we further split

\int\Phi(x;\mu)\mu(dx)-\int\Phi(x;\nu)\nu(dx)=\int(\Phi(x;\mu)-\Phi(x;\nu))\mu(dx)+\int\Phi(x;\nu)(\mu-\nu)(dx).

The first term is bounded by
L_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\mu({\mathcal{X}})
, the second term by
C_{\Phi}(r)\|1\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)=C_{\Phi}(r)d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)
. Therefore

\Bigl|\int\Phi(x;\mu)\mu(dx)-\int\Phi(x;\nu)\nu(dx)\Bigr|\leq L_{\Phi}(r)\mu({\mathcal{X}})W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})+C_{\Phi}(r)d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu).

Dividing by
\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m
(or
\mu({\mathcal{X}})=1
in the probability case) gives

(68)

|D_{1}|\leq L_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})+\frac{C_{\Phi}(r)}{m}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu).

Next, for
D_{2}
, since
|\int\Phi(x;\nu)\nu(dx)|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)\nu({\mathcal{X}})
,

\displaystyle|D_{2}|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\frac{\nu({\mathcal{X}})-\mu({\mathcal{X}})}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})\nu({\mathcal{X}})}\Bigr|\Bigl|\int\Phi(x;\nu)\nu(dx)\Bigr|\leq\frac{|\mu({\mathcal{X}})-\nu({\mathcal{X}})|}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}C_{\Phi}(r)

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\Phi}(r)}{m}|\mu({\mathcal{X}})-\nu({\mathcal{X}})|.

Then, by combining (
68
) and (
C.2.2
),

|\bar{\Phi}(\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\nu)|\leq L_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})+\frac{2C_{\Phi}(r)}{m}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu).

Since
|\langle\varphi,\nu\rangle|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\nu({\mathcal{X}})\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}r
,

(70)

|D|\leq|\bar{\Phi}(\mu)-\bar{\Phi}(\nu)||\langle\varphi,\nu\rangle|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}r\Bigl(L_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})+\frac{2C_{\Phi}(r)}{m}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)\Bigr).

Thus, combining (
C.2.2
) and (
70
) gives

\displaystyle|J_{\mu}-J_{\nu}|

\displaystyle\leq|C|+|D|\leq C_{\Phi}(r)(\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty})d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)

(71)

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\varphi\|_{\infty}rL_{\Phi}(r)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})+\frac{2\|\varphi\|_{\infty}rC_{\Phi}(r)}{m}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu).

Finally, collecting the bounds (
65
) and (
C.2.2
), there exists a constant
L_{S,\varphi}(r,m)<\infty
depending on
r
,
\varphi
and
m
such that

|G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{S}^{\rm bal}[\nu](\varphi)|\leq L_{S,\varphi}(r,m)\bigl(d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)+W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\bigr)=L_{S,\varphi}(r,m)d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu),

which verifies (A4) for the balanced operator.

For the unbalanced selection operator
S_{\tau}[\mu](dx)=s_{\tau}(x;\mu)\mu(dx)
, the pre-generator is

G_{S}[\mu](\varphi)=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\mu)\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx).

Unlike the balanced case, there is no normalization term
\bar{\Phi}(\mu)
and hence no division by
\mu({\mathcal{X}})
. Consequently, on each moment sublevel
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
the Lipschitz estimate in
d_{q,2}^{*}
follows directly from the decomposition

G_{S}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{S}[\nu](\varphi)=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\Phi(x;\mu)-\Phi(x;\nu))\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x;\nu)\varphi(x)(\mu-\nu)(dx),

together with Assumption
5.7
(ii) for the first term and the duality bound
|\langle h,\mu-\nu\rangle|\leq\|h\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)
for the second term, where
h(x):=\Phi(x;\nu)\varphi(x)
is uniformly bounded on
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}
by bounded pressure. In particular, for every fixed
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
there exists
L_{S,\varphi}(r)<\infty
such that

|G_{S}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{S}[\nu](\varphi)|\leq L_{S,\varphi}(r)d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu),\qquad\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r},

and no lower mass bound
\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m
is needed for the unbalanced pre-generator. This completes the proof.
∎

C.2.3.
Recombination operator: pre-generator verification

For
r<\infty
and
m_{0}>0
, recall the collection
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}({\mathcal{X}}):=\{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}}):\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m_{0},\ \int w_{q}\,d\mu\leq r\}
, and similarly write
{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r}({\mathcal{X}}):=\{\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}}):\int w_{q}\,d\mu\leq r\}
for the corresponding moment ball in the probability case.

Proposition C.4

(Recombination pre-generator and regularity)
.

Fix
q\geq 2
and
r<\infty
. Suppose Assumption
5.13
holds. If either
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r}({\mathcal{X}})
or there exists
m_{0}>0
such that
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}({\mathcal{X}})
, then there exist constants
\tau_{0}(r)\in(0,1)
and
r_{R}(r,m_{0})\in(r,\infty)
such that, for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}(r)]
, the following statements hold (with the convention
r_{R}(r):=r_{R}(r,1)
in the probability case).

(i)

Pre-generator (A1).
For
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\mu({\mathcal{X}})>0
and all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
,

(72)

G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\,\beta(\bar{\mu})\iint_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}\Bigl[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(z)\,p_{b}((x,y),dz)\Bigr]\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}(dx,dy)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx),

with convergence exact (zero remainder) in
\tau
; that is,

G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)=\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0}\frac{\langle\varphi,R_{\tau}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}=\langle\varphi,R[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle.

The balanced pre-generator
G_{R}^{\rm bal}
is obtained by setting
\beta\equiv 1
.

(ii)

Moment stability (A2).
For all
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}({\mathcal{X}})
we have
R_{\tau}[\mu]\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0})}({\mathcal{X}})
and

R_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=(1-\tau)\mu({\mathcal{X}})+\tau\beta(\bar{\mu})\mu({\mathcal{X}})=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\bigl((1-\tau)+\tau\beta(\bar{\mu})\bigr).

In the balanced case (
\beta\equiv 1
,
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r}({\mathcal{X}})
), the same moment bound holds without any mass restriction, with
R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r_{R}(r)}({\mathcal{X}})
.

(iii)

Near-identity in
d_{q,2}^{*}
(A3).
There exists
C_{R,\mathrm{BL}}(r,m_{0})<\infty
such that, for all
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}({\mathcal{X}})
and all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}(r)]
,

Moreover, for every
m_{0}>0
there exists

such that

In the balanced case,
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r_{R}(r)}({\mathcal{X}})
, the same bound holds with the same proof and with
\beta\equiv 1
.

(iv)

Local Lipschitz continuity of
G_{R}
(A4).
Suppose furthermore that Assumption
5.13
(ii) holds. Then, for every fixed
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, there exists
L_{R,\varphi}(r,m_{0})<\infty
such that, for all
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}({\mathcal{X}})
,

|G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{R}[\nu](\varphi)|\leq L_{R,\varphi}(r,m_{0})\,d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu).

In the balanced case,
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r_{R}(r)}({\mathcal{X}})
, the same bound holds with the same proof and with
\beta\equiv 1
.

Proof.

Throughout, fix
r<\infty
,
m_{0}>0
,
\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}(r)]
with
\tau_{0}(r)=1/2
, and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
. For
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}
we write
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
for the normalized population.

Part (i): Pre-generator (A1).

By definition of
R_{\tau}
,

\frac{\langle\varphi,R_{\tau}[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}=\frac{(1-\tau)\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle+\tau\langle\varphi,R[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle}{\tau}=\langle\varphi,R[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle,

which is independent of
\tau
. Hence the limit is exact and equals
\langle\varphi,R[\mu]-\mu\rangle
. Using
R[\mu]=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,R^{\rm bal}[\mu]
together with Definition
5.14
and Fubini–Tonelli (applicable because
\varphi
is bounded),

\langle\varphi,R[\mu]\rangle=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\,\beta(\bar{\mu})\iint_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}\Bigl[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(z)\,p_{b}((x,y),dz)\Bigr]\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}(dx,dy),

which gives (
72
). For
\mu=0
,
R[0]=0
by convention, hence
G_{R}[0](\varphi)=0
. This establishes (A1).

Part (ii): Moment stability (A2).

The mass identity is immediate from (
14
), since
R[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,\mu({\mathcal{X}})
:

R_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})=(1-\tau)\mu({\mathcal{X}})+\tau\beta(\bar{\mu})\,\mu({\mathcal{X}})=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\bigl((1-\tau)+\tau\beta(\bar{\mu})\bigr).

For the
q
-moment, by linearity and positivity,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR_{\tau}[\mu]=(1-\tau)\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu+\tau\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR[\mu].

The second term is estimated as follows. Using
R[\mu]=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,\mu({\mathcal{X}})\,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]
, Fubini–Tonelli, and Assumption
5.13
(iii),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[w_{q}(b(x,y;\xi))]\,\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}(dx,dy)

\displaystyle\leq C_{b}^{(q)}\iint(w_{q}(x)+w_{q}(y))\,\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}(dx,dy)=2C_{b}^{(q)}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu},

the last equality using that
\overline{\Gamma^{\mu}}
has both marginals equal to
\bar{\mu}
(Assumption
5.13
(i)). Now, if
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r,m_{0}}
, then

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\bar{\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu({\mathcal{X}})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu\leq\frac{r}{m_{0}},

and Assumption
5.13
(iv) yields
\beta(\bar{\mu})\leq\beta_{r/m_{0}}
. Combining the previous displays,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR[\mu]

\displaystyle=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,\mu({\mathcal{X}})\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]\leq 2C_{b}^{(q)}\,\beta_{r/m_{0}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{b}^{(q)}\,\beta_{r/m_{0}}\,r=:C_{R}(r,m_{0}).

Therefore, for
\tau\leq 1
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR_{\tau}[\mu]\leq(1-\tau)\,r+\tau\,C_{R}(r,m_{0})\leq r_{R}(r,m_{0}):=\max\{r,C_{R}(r,m_{0})\},

and, since
\tau\leq\tau_{0}(r)=1/2
,
R_{\tau}[\mu]({\mathcal{X}})\geq m_{0}/2
. This proves moment stability on the mass-restricted sublevel. In the balanced case (
\beta\equiv 1
,
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r}({\mathcal{X}})
), the same moment bound holds without any mass restriction, with
R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\mu]\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r_{R}(r)}({\mathcal{X}})
.

Part (iii): Near-identity in
d_{q,2}^{*}
(A3).

We treat the
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}
and
W_{2}
components separately, with
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}
throughout.

BL component.
By part (ii), and in particular by the bound established for
\int w_{q}\,dR[\mu]
above, both
R[\mu]
and
R_{\tau}[\mu]
belong to
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
whenever
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}({\mathcal{X}})
. For any
f\in\mathrm{BL}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1
,

\langle f,R_{\tau}[\mu]-\mu\rangle=\langle f,(1-\tau)\mu+\tau R[\mu]-\mu\rangle=\tau\langle f,R[\mu]-\mu\rangle.

Taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1
yields

(73)

d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(R_{\tau}[\mu],\mu)=\tau\,d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(R[\mu],\mu).

Next we claim that on the moment sublevel
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}
, both
\mu
and
R[\mu]
have uniformly bounded
q
-moments and mass, hence
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(R[\mu],\mu)\leq C_{R,\mathrm{BL}}(r,m_{0})
for some constant depending only on the sublevel. To justify this, recall the weighted BL norm on test functions,

\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}=\|f\|_{0,q}+[f]_{1,q-1},\qquad\|f\|_{0,q}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{|f(x)|}{w_{q}(x)},\qquad w_{q}(x)=1+\|x\|^{q}.

If
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1
then automatically
\|f\|_{0,q}\leq 1
, hence
|f(x)|\leq w_{q}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, and so

|\langle f,R[\mu]\rangle|\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR[\mu]\quad\text{and}\quad|\langle f,\mu\rangle|\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu.

As
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}
, we have
\int w_{q}\,d\mu\leq r_{R}(r,m_{0})
. Using Assumptions
5.13
(iii) and
5.13
(iv), and following the same steps as in part (ii) but with
r
replaced by
r_{R}(r,m_{0})
, we get, since
\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq m_{0}
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR[\mu]\leq\beta(\bar{\mu})\cdot 2C_{b}^{(q)}\cdot r_{R}(r,m_{0}).

Bounded amplitude on the corresponding moment ball gives
\beta(\bar{\mu})\leq\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}
, so

\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR[\mu]\leq 2C_{b}^{(q)}\,\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}\,r_{R}(r,m_{0})=:C_{R}^{(q)}(r,m_{0}).

Therefore

|\langle f,R[\mu]-\mu\rangle|\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,dR[\mu]+\int_{\mathcal{X}}w_{q}\,d\mu\leq C_{R}^{(q)}(r,m_{0})+r_{R}(r,m_{0})=:C_{R,\mathrm{BL}}(r,m_{0}).

Taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq 1
yields
d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(R[\mu],\mu)\leq C_{R,\mathrm{BL}}(r,m_{0})
, and combining with (
73
),

(74)

d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(R_{\tau}[\mu],\mu)\leq C_{R,\mathrm{BL}}(r,m_{0})\,\tau.

Wasserstein component.
Writing
\mu=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\,\bar{\mu}
and using
R[\mu]=\beta(\bar{\mu})\,R^{\rm bal}[\mu]
, we have
R_{\tau}[\mu]=(1-\tau)\,\mu({\mathcal{X}})\,\bar{\mu}+\tau\,\beta(\bar{\mu})\,R^{\rm bal}[\mu]
, hence the normalized measure is the convex mixture

\overline{R_{\tau}[\mu]}=\theta_{\tau}(\mu)\,\bar{\mu}+(1-\theta_{\tau}(\mu))\,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],\qquad\theta_{\tau}(\mu):=\frac{1-\tau}{(1-\tau)+\tau\beta(\bar{\mu})}.

Let
\rho:=R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]
. Consider the coupling between
\bar{\mu}
and
\theta\bar{\mu}+(1-\theta)\rho
that, with probability
\theta
, couples
\bar{\mu}
with itself by the identity, and, with probability
1-\theta
, couples
\bar{\mu}
with
\rho
by an optimal
W_{2}
coupling. This gives the standard bound

W_{2}^{2}(\theta\bar{\mu}+(1-\theta)\rho,\bar{\mu})\leq(1-\theta)\,W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\bar{\mu}).

Applying with
\theta=\theta_{\tau}(\mu)
:

W_{2}\bigl(\overline{R_{\tau}[\mu]},\bar{\mu}\bigr)\leq\sqrt{1-\theta_{\tau}(\mu)}\,W_{2}(R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],\bar{\mu}).

On
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}
, both
\bar{\mu}
and
R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]
have uniformly bounded second moments (by part (ii) and
q\geq 2
), so
W_{2}(R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],\bar{\mu})\leq C(r,m_{0})
. Moreover,
\beta(\bar{\mu})\leq\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}
, and for
\tau\leq 1/2
,

1-\theta_{\tau}(\mu)=\frac{\tau\beta(\bar{\mu})}{(1-\tau)+\tau\beta(\bar{\mu})}\leq\frac{\tau\,\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}}{1-\tau}\leq 2\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}\,\tau.

Therefore

(75)

W_{2}\bigl(\overline{R_{\tau}[\mu]},\bar{\mu}\bigr)\leq C(r,m_{0})\sqrt{2\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}}\,\sqrt{\tau}=:C_{R,W}(r,m_{0})\sqrt{\tau}.

Combining (
74
) and (
75
) gives, for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}(r)]
,

d_{q,2}^{*}(R_{\tau}[\mu],\mu)\leq C_{R,\mathrm{BL}}(r,m_{0})\,\tau+C_{R,W}(r,m_{0})\sqrt{\tau},

which establishes (A3). In the balanced case (
\beta\equiv 1
,
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r_{R}(r)}({\mathcal{X}})
) the same argument applies without a mass restriction and yields
d_{q,2}^{*}(R_{\tau}^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],\bar{\mu})\leq C_{R}(r)\sqrt{\tau}
.

Part (iv): Local Lipschitz continuity of
G_{R}
(A4).

Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
and let
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}
with
\bar{\mu}:=\mu/\mu({\mathcal{X}})
and
\bar{\nu}:=\nu/\nu({\mathcal{X}})
. Recalling that

G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)=\langle\varphi,R[\mu]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle,\qquad R[\mu]=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\,\beta(\bar{\mu})\,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],

we write

G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{R}[\nu](\varphi)=\langle\varphi,\mu({\mathcal{X}})\beta(\bar{\mu})R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]-\nu({\mathcal{X}})\beta(\bar{\nu})R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}]\rangle-\langle\varphi,\mu-\nu\rangle,

so that
|G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{R}[\nu](\varphi)|\leq T_{1}+T_{2}
, where

T_{1}:=|\langle\varphi,\mu({\mathcal{X}})\beta(\bar{\mu})R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]-\nu({\mathcal{X}})\beta(\bar{\nu})R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}]\rangle|,\qquad T_{2}:=|\langle\varphi,\mu-\nu\rangle|.

Bound on
T_{2}
.
Since
\varphi
is bounded and globally Lipschitz with
\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi)\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}
, we have
\varphi\in\mathrm{BL}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}=:C_{\varphi}
, and

(76)

T_{2}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}\,d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)\leq C_{\varphi}\,d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu).

Bound on
T_{1}
.
Decompose

\displaystyle\mu({\mathcal{X}})\beta(\bar{\mu})R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]-\nu({\mathcal{X}})\beta(\bar{\nu})R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}]

\displaystyle=(\mu({\mathcal{X}})-\nu({\mathcal{X}}))\beta(\bar{\mu})R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]

\displaystyle\quad+\nu({\mathcal{X}})(\beta(\bar{\mu})-\beta(\bar{\nu}))R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]

\displaystyle\quad+\nu({\mathcal{X}})\beta(\bar{\nu})\bigl(R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]-R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}]\bigr).

Hence

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq|\mu({\mathcal{X}})-\nu({\mathcal{X}})|\,\beta(\bar{\mu})\,|\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]\rangle|+\nu({\mathcal{X}})\,|\beta(\bar{\mu})-\beta(\bar{\nu})|\,|\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]\rangle|

\displaystyle\quad+\nu({\mathcal{X}})\,\beta(\bar{\nu})\,|\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]-R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}]\rangle|.

On
{\mathcal{M}}_{q,+}^{r_{R}(r,m_{0}),m_{0}}
,
|\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]\rangle|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
(since
R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
), and
\beta(\bar{\mu}),\beta(\bar{\nu})\leq\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}
. Moreover, by Assumption
5.13
(iv),
|\beta(\bar{\mu})-\beta(\bar{\nu})|\leq L_{\beta}(r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0})\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
.

For the difference
R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]-R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}]
, we use the Lipschitz mixing assumption and pair-law Lipschitzness to obtain a
W_{2}
-Lipschitz bound on
R^{\rm bal}
. Let
(X,Y)\sim\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\mu}}}
and
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\sim\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\nu}}}
be coupled optimally for
W_{2}
on
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
(with product metric), and share
\xi
across the two systems. Then, by Assumption
5.13
(ii),

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}])

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\|b(X,Y;\xi)-b(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime};\xi)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq L_{b}\,{\mathbb{E}}(\|X-X^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|^{2})=L_{b}\,W_{2}^{2}\bigl(\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\mu}}},\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\nu}}}\bigr).

By Assumption
5.13
(i),
W_{2}\bigl(\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\mu}}},\overline{\Gamma^{\bar{\nu}}}\bigr)\leq L_{\Lambda}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
, hence

W_{2}(R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}])\leq\sqrt{L_{b}}\,L_{\Lambda}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

Recall that by the monotonicity of the
p
-Wasserstein distance,
W_{1}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\leq W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
for all
\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}})
. Hence, by the previous two displays and the fact that
\varphi
is Lipschitz,

\displaystyle|\langle\varphi,R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}]-R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}]\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi)\,W_{1}(R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}])\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi)\,W_{2}(R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\mu}],R^{\rm bal}[\bar{\nu}])

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi)\sqrt{L_{b}}\,L_{\Lambda}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

Finally, since the constant function
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathrm{BL}_{q}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\|_{\mathrm{BL},q}=1
,

|\mu({\mathcal{X}})-\nu({\mathcal{X}})|\leq d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu).

Collecting these bounds,

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)\,\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}\,\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\nu({\mathcal{X}})\,L_{\beta}(r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0})\,\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle\quad+\nu({\mathcal{X}})\,\beta_{r_{R}(r,m_{0})/m_{0}}\,\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi)\sqrt{L_{b}}\,L_{\Lambda}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})

(77)

\displaystyle=:C^{\prime}_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}\,d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)+C_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}),

upon absorbing
\mu({\mathcal{X}}),\nu({\mathcal{X}})\leq r_{R}(r,m_{0})
into the constants.

Combining.
Adding (
76
) and (
C.2.3
),

\displaystyle|G_{R}[\mu](\varphi)-G_{R}[\nu](\varphi)|

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}\,d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)+C_{\varphi,r,m_{0}}\,W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})+C_{\varphi}\,d_{\mathrm{BL},q}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq L_{R,\varphi}(r,m_{0})\,d_{q,2}^{*}(\mu,\nu),

which establishes (A4). The balanced case follows identically by taking
\beta\equiv 1
and
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{q}^{r_{R}(r)}({\mathcal{X}})
(no mass restriction). This completes the proof.

Appendix D
Numerical verification

In this appendix, we perform a numerical sanity-check of Theorem
4.1
for three different EAs. The example algorithms are CBO, CMA-ES and recombinative ES. CBO has established existing theory, which makes it an appealing starting point. CMA-ES is an example of a parametric EA. The third example utilizes selection, recombination and mutation operators, simultaneously demonstrating the different types of operators in one algorithm.

Experimental protocol and reproducibility.

All numerical experiments are synthetic sanity checks; no datasets, train/test
splits, or pretrained models are used. The objective is the
d=4

Statistical variability.

The numerical experiments are illustrative sanity checks rather than benchmark
comparisons, so we do not perform hypothesis tests or report confidence
intervals. For each of the three methods we run ten independent seeds,
which vary both the random initialization and the algorithmic randomness.
Figure
4
plots all ten seed trajectories directly
on a semi-log scale, rather than only a mean curve with error bars. Thus the
displayed variability is seed-to-seed variability across complete optimization
runs.

D.1.
CBO

In CBO, the population is updated using a mutation. We denote the population by
(X_{k}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
. We compute a Boltzmann-weighted consensus point

v_{\alpha_{k}}(\mu_{k})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{k}^{i}e^{-\alpha_{k}(f(X_{k}^{i})-\min_{j}f(X_{k}^{j}))}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\alpha_{k}(f(X_{k}^{i})-\min_{j}f(X_{k}^{j}))}},

where
\alpha_{k}
is a temperature hyperparameter and
N
is the population size. The population members are then updated according to

X_{k+1}^{i}=X_{k}^{i}-\lambda\bigl(X_{k}^{i}-v_{\alpha_{k}}(\mu_{k})\bigr)\Delta t+\sigma\|X_{k}^{i}-v_{\alpha_{k}}(\mu_{k})\|\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi_{k}^{i},

where
\Delta t
time step,
\lambda
a hyperparameter that controls how strongly population members are driven toward the consensus point.

In the case of CBO experiments, we choose
\Psi(y)=\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
. The Lyapunov functional and search error both reduce to
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|X_{k}^{i}-y_{*}\|^{2}
.
Figure
4
a shows the development of the Lyapunov functional
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{k})
across optimization runs with 10 different seeds. The experiments use exponentially increasing
\alpha_{k}
to help with convergence, because using a fixed
\alpha
caused runs to stagnate somewhere in the neighborhood of the optimum. Figure
4
a exhibits exponential convergence, which is consistent with Theorem
4.1
.

D.2.
CMA-ES-type parameter flow

In this example, we use rank-
\mu
CMA-ES-type flow similar to
[
15
]
. This is an example of a parametric method.

Let
w_{1}\geq\cdots\geq w_{\mu}>0
satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_{i}=1
;
in the experiments we use logarithmic weights

w_{i}\propto\log(\mu+1/2)-\log i
. Define the effective selection size
\mu_{\rm eff}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_{i}^{2}\Big)^{-1}.

Let
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\lambda})
, where
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.

For each generation, we sample solution candidates

Y_{i}=m+\sigma C^{1/2}Z_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,\lambda.

Let
z_{1:\lambda}=\pi_{Y}(Z)
, where
\pi_{Y}
is a permutation that arranges
(f(Y_{i}))_{i=1}^{\lambda}
in an ascending order.
Write

\displaystyle z_{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_{i}z_{i:\lambda},\qquad y_{w}=C^{1/2}z_{w},\qquad M_{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_{i}z_{i:\lambda}z_{i:\lambda}^{\top}.

Fix hyperparameters
c_{m},c_{\sigma},c_{c},\eta_{\sigma},c_{1},c_{\mu}>0
. The model parameters are updated according to

\displaystyle m^{+}

\displaystyle=m+\tau c_{m}\sigma y_{w},

\displaystyle p_{\sigma}^{+}

\displaystyle=p_{\sigma}+\tau\Big(-c_{\sigma}p_{\sigma}+\sqrt{c_{\sigma}(2-c_{\sigma})\mu_{\rm eff}}\,z_{w}\Big),

\displaystyle p_{c}^{+}

\displaystyle=p_{c}+\tau\Big(-c_{c}p_{c}+\sqrt{c_{c}(2-c_{c})\mu_{\rm eff}}\,y_{w}\Big),

\displaystyle\sigma^{+}

\displaystyle=\sigma+\tau\eta_{\sigma}\sigma\Big(\frac{\|p_{\sigma}\|^{2}}{d}-1\Big),

\displaystyle C^{+}

\displaystyle=C+\tau\Big(c_{1}(p_{c}p_{c}^{\top}-C)+c_{\mu}(C^{1/2}M_{w}C^{1/2}-C)\Big).

In the case of CMA-ES, choosing
\Psi(y)=\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
yields Lyapunov functional of the form

\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})=\|m-y_{*}\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(C).

Numerical results on the development of
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})
using CMA-ES on a 4D Ackley function are presented in Figure
4
b. The results show exponential convergence, which is consistent with Theorem
4.1
.

D.3.
Recombinative ES

In this example, we use a recombinative ES with learned local gradient mutation. Let us denote the population by
(X_{k}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
. We compute Boltzmann weights as

\omega_{k}^{i}=\frac{\exp\{-\alpha_{\rm sel}(f(X_{k}^{i})-\min_{j}f(X_{k}^{j}))\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\exp\{-\alpha_{\rm sel}(f(X_{k}^{\ell})-\min_{j}f(X_{k}^{j}))\}}.

Parents are drawn independently according to these weights. Arithmetic recombination is performed as

Z_{k}^{i}=\xi_{k}^{i}X^{I_{k}^{i}}_{k}+(1-\xi_{k}^{i})X_{k}^{J_{k}^{i}},\quad\xi_{k}^{i}\sim\operatorname{Beta}(b_{1},b_{2})

where
I_{k}^{i}
and
J_{k}^{i}
represent the indices of the parents and
b_{1},b_{2}
are parameters of Beta-distribution.
The next generation is computed by applying mutation on the offspring,

X_{k+1}^{i}=Z_{k}^{i}-\beta\hat{g}(Z_{k}^{i},X_{k})+\sigma\eta_{k},\quad\eta_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),

where
\beta
denotes step size and
\hat{g}(Z_{k}^{i},X_{k})\approx\nabla f(Z_{k}^{i})
computes a local approximation of the gradient of the objective function following the approach by
Mukherjee et al. 2006
.

As with the CBO experiments earlier, we choose
\Psi(y)=\|y-y_{*}\|^{2}
, which yields
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|X_{k}^{i}-y_{*}\|^{2}
.
Figure
4
c shows the development of
\mathcal{V}_{\Upsilon}(\bar{\mu}_{t})
across optimization runs with 10 different seeds. We observe that the Lyapunov functional decays at an exponential rate, as predicted by Theorem
4.1
.
