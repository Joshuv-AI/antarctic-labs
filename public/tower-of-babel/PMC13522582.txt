SPP1-positive myeloid cell subpopulations associated with resistance to PD-1/L1 immunotherapy in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Ran Zhang, Cancan Wang, Xiaomei Ma, Xinxia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors have significantly improved outcomes in Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), yet many patients remain unresponsive due to resistance. This study aimed to identify key cellular subpopulations associated with immunotherapy resistance in LUAD. Clinical data from 197 LUAD patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors were retrospectively analyzed, including 79 with response and 118 with resistance. Fresh tumor tissues from three patients per group underwent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and one sample per group was analyzed using spatial transcriptomics. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) were used to validate protein expression and overlapping spatial signals. scRNA-seq revealed a distinct SPP1⁺ myeloid subpopulation enriched in resistant tumors. Pseudotime analysis suggested these cells are in a differentiation state linked to tumor progression. SCENIC analysis indicated activation of transcriptional networks potentially contributes to immune suppression. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed SPP1⁺ myeloid cells were localized near tumor nests in resistant tissues, suggesting an association with immunosuppressive microenvironment. IHC and IF further validated increased SPP1 expression and its overlapping spatial signals with macrophage markers CD68 and CD163, confirming their tumor-associated macrophage identity. This study identifies SPP1⁺ myeloid cells as a subpopulation closely associated with LUAD immunotherapy resistance. They may serve as a potential candidate biomarker for predicting PD-1/PD-L1 treatment outcomes and represent targets for further functional exploration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57503-4.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases1. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common pathological subtype of NSCLC, with an average 5-year overall survival rate of 19% for patients2. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies, has brought durable clinical benefits to a subset of patients and reshaped the treatment landscape of advanced NSCLC3. However, clinical data show that only about 20% of NSCLC patients exhibit a significant response to ICIs, while the majority display either primary or acquired resistance4,5. Thus, elucidating the factors and potential associations underlying ICI resistance remains a central scientific challenge in LUAD immunotherapy.

Recent studies have highlighted that the composition and dynamic remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) are significantly associated with immunotherapy response and resistance6. Among TIME components, myeloid-derived immune cells, especially tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), can exert either pro- or anti-immunogenic functions depending on their activation state. They are involved in various immune regulatory processes including T cell suppression, impaired antigen presentation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion7. Although TAMs have been implicated in modulating immunotherapy efficacy, their phenotypic and functional heterogeneity has hindered systematic characterization of their subtypes, states, and spatial localization in the NSCLC immune context8.

The rapid development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) has enabled unprecedented resolution in profiling the cellular composition, functional states, and spatial organization of immune cells within tumor tissues9,10. These technologies offer powerful tools to dissect the microenvironmental basis of immunotherapy response and resistance, and to identify key immune cell subsets and their regulatory mechanisms.

In this study, we employed an integrated multi-omics approach combining scRNA-seq and ST to systematically compare immunotherapy-responsive and -resistant LUAD tumors treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. We constructed detailed immune cell heterogeneity and spatial immune atlases, with a focus on the myeloid lineage. Our analysis identified an immunosuppressive SPP1⁺ macrophage subpopulation that is correlated with immunotherapy resistance. Through immunohistochemistry and multiplex immunofluorescence, we validated the spatial enrichment and localization of SPP1⁺ macrophages in resistant tumor tissues. This study uncovers potential mechanisms of myeloid cell remodeling and spatial immune dysfunction in the TIME of ICI-resistant LUAD, and provides novel insights and a potential rationale for developing SPP1⁺ cell-directed combination immunotherapies.

Materials and methods

Clinical patients

Patients enrolled in this study were diagnosed with LUAD and received PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy at the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between January 2018 and June of 2025. The clinical data extraction and sample collection were conducted between June and July of 2025. All 197 patients had a confirmed pathological diagnosis and met the diagnostic and therapeutic criteria outlined in the 2021 edition of the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines for LUAD. All patients were immunotherapy-naïve prior to initiation of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy. Depending on clinical indication and line of treatment, some patients had previously received platinum-based chemotherapy or targeted therapy before immunotherapy. In contrast, fresh tumor tissue samples used for scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics were collected prospectively from selected patients at the time of biopsy or surgery following ethical approval. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University Affiliated Tumor Hospital (K-2025062). Written informed consent was obtained from patients undergoing prospective biospecimen collection. The retrospective analysis of clinical data was approved by the institutional ethics committee in accordance with relevant guidelines.

Inclusion criteria: Age ≥ 18 years, regardless of sex; Pathologically confirmed LUAD; Clinical stage (I–IV) disease; Received standardized PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy or combination therapy for at least three cycles (≥ 9 weeks; PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors together with platinum-based chemotherapy during the same treatment cycle); Complete clinical imaging and hematological data available. Exclusion criteria: Presence of other concurrent malignancies; History of severe autoimmune or immunodeficiency disorders; Concurrent systemic therapy without PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors; Incomplete clinical data or inability to evaluate treatment response.

Treatment response was evaluated radiologically according to RECIST version 1.1. Patients who achieved complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) were classified as the response group. Patients with stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) were classified as the resistance group (non-responders), as both categories represent the absence of objective tumor regression under RECIST 1.1 criteria. Patients with a ≥ 30% reduction in the sum of the diameters of target lesions compared to baseline were classified as treatment-sensitive. A total of 79 patients were included in this group. Patients with either stable disease (no significant tumor change) or progressive disease (≥ 20% increase in lesion diameter) were considered resistant to treatment. A total of 118 patients were included in this group. Clinicopathological data were also collected for all enrolled patients.

Tissue sample collection

From the enrolled patient cohort, fresh tumor tissues from three randomly selected patients in each group (response and resistance) were collected for scRNA-seq analysis, clinical characteristics were shown in Table S1. Additionally, one fresh tumor tissue sample from each group was randomly selected for ST analysis. All biopsy-derived tumor tissues were also fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin (FFPE) for histological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from tumor tissues using a combination of mechanical dissociation and enzymatic digestion. Approximately 0.5–1.0 cm³ of fresh tumor tissue was rinsed with cold PBS to remove blood and necrotic regions, then placed immediately into ice-cold RPMI-1640 medium. The tissue was finely minced and digested in an enzymatic solution containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (0.2 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min in a water bath. After complete digestion, the cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove undigested debris, followed by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (BD Biosciences) for 3 min at room temperature. The resulting single-cell suspension was assessed using trypan blue staining under a microscope, and only samples with ≥ 85% cell viability were used for downstream processing.

Single-cell transcriptome profiling was conducted using the DNBelab C4 scRNA-seq platform (BGI, China). High-quality single-cell suspensions were diluted to a target concentration of approximately 1000 cells/µL and mixed with barcoded beads containing cell-specific barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). These mixtures were processed through a microfluidic chip to encapsulate single cells with beads, followed by cell lysis and mRNA capture via hybridization to barcoded oligonucleotides on the beads. Reverse transcription within the chip generated barcoded cDNA, which was subsequently amplified by PCR. The amplified cDNA was subjected to library construction using the MGIEasy Single-Cell Library Preparation Kit, involving end-repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, and library enrichment. The final libraries were sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform using PE150 mode, targeting a sequencing depth of approximately 30,000–50,000 reads per cell.

scRNA-seq data analysis

Raw gene expression matrices were processed using the Scanpy package (v1.10.3) in Python (v3.9.21). Single-cell data underwent rigorous quality control filtering with the following criteria:1≥ 200 genes detected per cell;2 each gene expressed in at least 3 cells;3 mitochondrial gene content < 10% of total UMI counts per cell; and4< 4000 genes detected per cell to exclude potential doublets. After filtering, a total of 64,142 high-quality cells across all samples were retained for downstream analysis. The median number of detected genes per cell was 902, and the median UMI count per cell was 1557.

Global normalization was performed using the Normalize_total function, scaling gene expression counts in each cell to a relative abundance multiplied by 10,000, followed by log-transformation using the Log1p function.

Batch correction was performed using the Harmony algorithm to minimize patient-specific variation, and the successful integration of datasets was visually confirmed via UMAP dimensionality reduction prior to clustering. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using the highly_variable_genes function, selecting the top 2000 genes with the highest variance across all cells for downstream analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then applied to the HVG expression matrix for dimensionality reduction and to determine the major axes of variation (principal components, PCs). A K-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph was constructed based on Euclidean distance, and unsupervised clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm with a default resolution parameter of 1.0 to identify major cellular subpopulations. To visualize the relationships between different clusters, dimensionality reduction was further performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Cell type annotation was conducted by comparing cluster-specific marker genes with reference data from the CellMarker 2.0 database. Differential gene expression analysis between clusters was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to identify signature marker genes defining each cell population. To statistically evaluate compositional changes in cell types between the response and Resistance groups, differential abundance testing was performed on the sample-level cell count matrix using the edgeR package. Log2 fold changes (log2FC), exact P-values, and false discovery rates (FDR) were calculated to assess effect sizes and statistical significance.

Myeloid cell subpopulation analysis

Myeloid cell subsets were extracted for further analysis. To evaluate the stemness and relative differentiation states of the myeloid subpopulations, we employed CytoTRACE 2 (Cellular (Cyto) Trajectory Reconstruction Analysis using gene Counts and Expression, version 2). Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using the Monocle2 package to infer the developmental differentiation pathways of myeloid subpopulations and explore their potential roles in tumor progression and immune evasion. To infer and visualize intercellular communication networks, we utilized the R package CellChat. Additionally, transcription factor regulatory network analysis was performed using the single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) pipeline to uncover the specific transcriptional regulatory programs activated in key myeloid cell subsets.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis

Spatial transcriptomic sequencing was performed using the STOmics platform developed by BGI. Tumor tissues were first snap-frozen and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, followed by cryosectioning at a thickness of 10 μm. Sections were mounted onto specially treated STOmics spatial chips (Chip ID: B01613D2; chip size: 1 cm × 1 cm; spatial resolution: 500 nm). After tissue attachment, fixation and permeabilization were carried out to allow mRNA released from the tissue to hybridize with spatially barcoded capture probes on the chip. In situ reverse transcription was then performed to generate spatially resolved cDNA. The resulting cDNA was amplified, subjected to library construction, and sequenced using the DNBSEQ high-throughput sequencing platform (MGISEQ-2000) in PE150 mode.

Raw sequencing data were processed using the STOmics Analysis Workflow (SAW v5.1.4) for quality control and preprocessing. High-quality clean reads (Q20 > 98%, Q30 > 92%) were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38), and reads mapped to exonic and intronic regions were quantified. After removal of duplicate reads, an expression matrix based on molecular identifier (MID) counts was generated.

Gene expression based on spot-level at each spatial location (DNA nanoball, DNB) was quantified using bins as the basic unit of analysis, with a bin size of 50 selected for statistical evaluation. Differential gene expression analysis between spatial clusters was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to identify marker genes for each spatial cell cluster. Finally, the SingleR package (v1.6.1) was used to annotate spatially defined cell types by comparing their expression profiles to reference single-cell transcriptome datasets. Cell-type abundance was estimated using cell2location, which models absolute cell abundance per spot using a Bayesian hierarchical framework and returns posterior mean estimates.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues corresponding to the spatial transcriptomics sections were used for H&E staining. Tissue blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm using a rotary microtome and mounted on glass slides. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, and 75%) to distilled water. Slides were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water, and differentiated in acid alcohol, followed by bluing in ammonia water. Subsequently, sections were counterstained with eosin for 1–2 min, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. H&E-stained sections were examined and imaged using a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Baseline tumor tissues were defined as samples collected before any exposure to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Paraffin-embedded tissue Sect. (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized by immersion in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in an EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 8.0) using a pressure cooker (heated on high power until the pressure valve vented, followed immediately by medium heat for 2 min and 30 s).

Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating sections in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature, followed by washing in PBS. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum at 37 °C for 30 min.

Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with the following primary antibodies (all diluted in antibody diluent): anti-Ki67 (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing; Catalog No. ZM-0166; 1:150), anti-PD-L1 (Abcam; Catalog No. AB205921; 1:200), anti-SPP1 (Abcam; Catalog No. AB63856; 1:200), anti-CD68 (ZSGB-BIO; Catalog No. ZM-0060; 1:150), anti-CD86 (Abcam; Catalog No. AB220188; 1:100), and anti-CD163 (ZSGB-BIO; Catalog No. ZM-0428; 1:150). After washing with PBS, slides were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO) at 37 °C for 30 min. DAB chromogen solution (ZSGB-BIO) was used for color development at room temperature. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for approximately 1 min, differentiated in acid alcohol, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted using neutral resin. Staining was visualized and imaged under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Paraffin sections underwent the same deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval procedures as described in IHC. After washing three times with PBS (5 min each), sections were blocked with 5% goat serum. A mixture of primary antibodies against SPP1 (Abcam; Catalog No. AB63856; 1:200), CD68 (ZSGB-BIO; Catalog No. ZM-0060; 1:150), and CD163 (ZSGB-BIO; Catalog No. ZM-0428; 1:150) was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies (TRY-520Plus, RY-570Plus, and TRY-690Plus; ZSGB-BIO, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence signals were assigned as follows: green for SPP1, red for CD68, and magenta for CD163. After washing, sections were mounted with Vectashield Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories) and stored in the dark at room temperature. Images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.2), and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association between clinical variables and resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy. Results were reported as Odds Ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics and immunotherapy response in LUAD patients

To evaluate the association between clinical and pathological features and response to PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy in LUAD patients, a total of 197 cases were included, with 79 in the response group and 118 in the resistance group (Table 1). Comparative analysis of clinical characteristics revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender, smoking history, distant metastasis, or pleural invasion, suggesting that these conventional clinical parameters are insufficient predictors of immunotherapy response.

Pathologically, there were no significant differences in tumor T stage, N stage, M stage, or overall clinical stage between the response and resistance groups. Although tumors in the resistance group tended to be more poorly differentiated, the difference was not statistically significant, indicating that traditional TNM staging and histological differentiation may not reliably predict immunotherapy outcomes.

In terms of immune-related markers, the expression level of Ki-67 was significantly higher in tumor tissues from the resistance group compared to the response group (t=-2.07, p = 0.040). Further analysis of macrophage markers within the tumor microenvironment revealed that SPP1 was significantly enriched in the resistance group (χ²=4.76, p = 0.029), while CD68 (a well-established pan-macrophage marker to assess total macrophage infiltration) expression was higher in the response group (χ²=6.40, p = 00.011). In contrast, CD86 and CD163 showed no significant difference between the two groups, suggesting their immunoregulatory roles require further investigation. Additionally, infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells did not differ significantly between the groups, although a decreasing trend in CD8⁺ T cells was observed in the resistance group.

To determine whether the observed association between SPP1⁺ macrophages and immunotherapy resistance is independent of other clinical and pathological confounders, we performed a straightforward logistic regression analysis (Table S2). In the univariate analysis, factors including M stage (reflecting distant metastasis/tumor burden), Ki-67, and high SPP1 expression were significantly associated with an increased risk of treatment resistance. Importantly, upon adjusting for selected key covariates in the multivariable model (including M stage, Ki-67, and PD-L1 status), high SPP1 expression remained a robust independent predictor of resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy (OR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.01–4.26, p = 0.048). Furthermore, M1 stage was also identified as an independent risk factor (OR = 3.36, 95% CI: 1.44–7.81, p = 0.005).

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals LUAD tissue heterogeneity and immunotherapy response characteristics

We performed scRNA seq on representative LUAD tumor samples from both response and resistant patients. A total of 20 cell clusters were identified through PCA (Figure S1), the removal of batch effects was visually confirmed using UMAP, showing a homogenous intermixing of cells from different batches (Figure S1B). Then unsupervised clustering using UMAP dimensionality reduction (Fig. 1A). Based on canonical marker gene expression, the clusters were annotated into 13 major cell types: Alveolar Type 2 cells (AT2), B cells, Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts (CAFs), CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, Ciliated cells, Club cells, Epithelial cells, Mast cells, Myeloid cells, Plasma cells, Vascular Endothelial Cells (VECs), and Vascular Smooth Muscle Cells (VSMCs) (Fig. 1B, C).

Cell composition analysis revealed that epithelial cells showed a trend of enrichment in the response group, whereas the proportions of AT2, CD4⁺T and myeloid cells were relatively higher in the resistance group (Fig. 1D, E). However, due to the limited number of biological replicates (n = 3 per group), these compositional shifts did not reach formal statistical significance (FDR > 0.05; Table S3). These findings suggest that the tumor microenvironment in resistant patients is more immunosuppressive, suggesting that myeloid cells are potentially involved in the altered immune microenvironment during to immunotherapy.

Reconstruction of myeloid cell subpopulations reveals subsets associated with immunotherapy resistance

To characterize the myeloid compartment within the tumor microenvironment, we performed unsupervised clustering of CD45⁺ myeloid-lineage cells. Myeloid cells include macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells (DCs), which play key roles in antigen presentation, inflammation, and immune regulation. A total of 9 functionally distinct myeloid subpopulations (Fig. 2A), including Alveolar Macrophage, Macro-CCL18, Macro-CXCL3, Macro-FABP4, Macro-IL1B, Macro-MARCO, Macro-SPP1, and DCs subsets (cDC1-BATF3 and cDC2-CD1C) (Fig. 2B, C). Differential abundance analysis revealed that Macro-SPP1 and Macro-IL1B were enriched in the resistance group, whereas Macro-CXCL3 and Macro-MARCO were more abundant in the response group (Fig. 2D, Figure S2).

Functional enrichment analysis showed that the Macro-SPP1 cluster was enriched in pathways related to T cell activation and regulation of adaptive immune response, while Macro-IL1B was involved in response to unfolded protein and response to topologically incorrect protein. In contrast, Macro-CXCL3 and Macro-MARCO were associated with immune activation and homeostasis maintenance (Figure S3).

Interestingly, SPP1 was not only highly expressed in the Macro-SPP1 cluster (Figure S4A) but also notably present in fibroblast populations (Figure S4B), suggesting its cross-lineage expression as a potential hallmark of immunosuppressive remodeling and therapeutic resistance.

Further differential gene expression analysis of the Macro-SPP1 cluster revealed co-expression of SPP1, LDHA, PGK1, DDIT4, C3, and F13A1. KEGG enrichment showed this cluster was involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, HIF-1 signaling, and the PI3K-Akt pathway (Fig. 2E), suggesting a phenotype characterized by high metabolic activity, low antigen presentation, and strong immunosuppressive potential.

To provide solid biological evidence for the developmental states of these myeloid subsets, we first investigated their cellular stemness using CytoTRACE 2 (Figure S5). As expected for mature immune populations, the vast majority of myeloid cells were categorized as “Differentiated” or “Unipotent,” confirming they are mature tissue-infiltrating or resident cells rather than stem/progenitor cells (Figure S5A). Interestingly, Macro-SPP1 cells exhibited relatively higher transcriptional plasticity (slightly elevated potency scores) compared to quiescent subsets like Macro-CXCL3 (Figure S5B), aligning with their highly active metabolic and immunosuppressive profile.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis (Figure S6) revealed that Macro-IL1B cells were predominantly located in earlier to intermediate pseudotime regions, Macro-SPP1 cells tended to be enriched toward distal regions of the myeloid differentiation trajectory, while still being distributed across multiple pseudotime states. This pattern suggests that Macro-SPP1 cells are associated with later transcriptional states characterized by immunosuppressive and metabolically active programs.

Identification of transcriptional regulatory networks in myeloid subpopulations

Using SCENIC analysis, we characterized the transcriptional regulatory landscape across myeloid subpopulations and identified distinct activity patterns of key transcription factors. In the Macro-SPP1 cluster, MYCN and CEBPD were markedly activated. The Macro-IL1B cluster showed elevated activity of ATF3 and CEBPD. In Macro-CXCL3, JUN and FOSB were upregulated, while STAT1 and MYCN were active in Macro-MARCO (Fig. 3A). Notably, the transcription factors MYCN and CEBPD exhibited robust and highly specific activation in the Macro-SPP1 subpopulation compared to other clusters (Fig. 3B). Gene regulatory network analysis revealed that MYCN directly targets and regulates SPP1, establishing it as a potential upstream factor associated with the Macro-SPP1 phenotype (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, MYCN actively co-regulates a suite of critical functional genes, including key glycolytic transporters and enzymes (SLC2A3, PGK1), as well as matrix remodeling factors (MMP14). Pathway enrichment analysis of these targeted networks demonstrated a strong convergence on specific signaling cascades, predominantly Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, the HIF-1 signaling pathway, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 3D). These findings indicate transcriptional heterogeneity within the myeloid compartment, with distinct subpopulations exhibiting divergent immune-related programs. These patterns likely reflect transcriptional heterogeneity of myeloid subsets within the tumor microenvironment.

Spatial transcriptomics reveals the spatial enrichment pattern of SPP1⁺ immune cells

To improve spatial interpretability, corresponding H&E-stained images were included for each spatial transcriptomics section, allowing alignment of histological architecture with spatial gene expression patterns (Figure S8). Spatial deconvolution and cell-type annotation revealed distinct regional enrichment patterns of epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and myeloid-derived populations (Fig. 4). Notably, SPP1⁺ myeloid cells were preferentially localized to regions adjacent to tumor nests and stromal areas in the resistant sample, whereas such spatial aggregation was less evident in the response sample.

IHC and multiplex IF confirm the spatial localization of SPP1⁺ macrophages

IHC was performed to assess the expression of Ki-67, PD-L1, SPP1, CD68, CD163, and CD86 (Fig. 5A). The results showed higher expression levels of Ki-67, PD-L1, SPP1, and CD163 in tumor tissues from the resistance group compared to the response group at baseline, while CD68, and CD86 expression was markedly reduced, indicating an enhanced immunosuppressive microenvironment. IHC slides were independently evaluated by two board-certified pathologists who were blinded to clinical response data. SPP1 expression was defined as high when ≥ 23% of cells showed positive staining. Tumor-cell and stromal expression were evaluated separately based on morphological criteria, with tumor cells identified by cytological features and glandular architecture. In cases of scoring discrepancy, slides were reviewed jointly and consensus was reached. Quantitative IHC scoring was performed for all tumors in the cohort, and the summarized values are presented in Table 1.

Discussion

With the widespread adoption of ICIs in LUAD, immunotherapy has become a critical component of treatment strategies. However, the majority of patients fail to achieve durable clinical responses, and the overall response rate remains limited with considerable interpatient variability11. Characterizing the factors associated with immunotherapy resistance, particularly the immunosuppressive cell populations and molecular mechanisms within the TIME, may help to improving the efficacy of ICIs. In this study, we employed an integrative multi-omics approach that combined retrospective clinical analysis, single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and tissue-level immunostaining to decipher the microenvironmental basis of PD-1/PD-L1 resistance in LUAD. Although our data suggest that SPP1⁺ macrophages are associated with immunotherapy resistance, we did not comprehensively adjust for other established biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, or oncogenic driver mutations. Therefore, SPP1 should be interpreted as a potential contributor within a multifactorial resistance landscape rather than a standalone determinant.

Our analysis of 197 LUAD patients treated with ICIs revealed no significant differences in conventional clinical parameters, including sex, smoking history, TNM stage, and tumor differentiation, between the response and resistance groups. It should be noted that mutational status of key immune-modulating genes such as KRAS, STK11, and KEAP1 was not comprehensively available for all cases. These genomic alterations are known to influence immune composition and therapeutic response in LUAD and may represent confounding factors. This underscores the limited predictive power of traditional clinical features and supports growing skepticism regarding the utility of single biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression12. These findings reinforce the concept that structural and functional remodeling of the TIME may be the true determinant of ICI efficacy.

At the molecular level, we observed significantly elevated Ki-67 expression in tumors from the resistance group, suggesting a link between increased tumor cell proliferation and immune evasion. Previous studies have also implicated early expansion of Ki-67⁺ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in both immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and immunotherapeutic failure13. Notably, although CD68 staining was higher in response tumors, indicating greater overall macrophage infiltration, resistant tumors exhibited a relative enrichment of the SPP1⁺ macrophage subset. This suggests that resistance is not associated with increased total TAM burden, but rather with a shift in macrophage functional states toward an SPP1⁺ phenotype, highlighting the potential role of TAM heterogeneity in differential treatment responses14.

Our single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed a complex landscape of cellular heterogeneity in LUAD, with notable differences between responsive and resistant tumors. The response group exhibited a higher proportion of epithelial cells, while the resistance group showed increased infiltration of fibroblasts and myeloid cells, suggesting a shift toward a “cold tumor” phenotype dominated by immunosuppressive components. Previous reports have demonstrated that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) can impair T cell infiltration and function by enhancing extracellular matrix deposition, inducing hypoxia, and upregulating PD-L1 expression15. Notably, the co-existence of FAP⁺ CAFs and SPP1⁺ TAMs has been associated with T cell exclusion and poor clinical outcomes16. However, although SPP1 expression was enriched in the macrophage cluster at the single-cell level, we cannot exclude contributions from tumor cells or stromal components at the bulk tissue level.

It is noteworthy that the intra-lineage heterogeneity of myeloid immune cells plays a crucial role in remodeling the TIME in LUAD. In our study, we identified nine functionally distinct myeloid subpopulations, among which Macro-SPP1 and Macro-IL1B were enriched in the resistance group, whereas Macro-CXCL3 and Macro-MARCO were predominant in the response group. Previous studies have reported that SPP1⁺ TAMs tend to localize at the tumor invasive margins and are significantly associated with high tumor burden and poor treatment response17,18.

KEGG pathway analysis further revealed that the Macro-SPP1 cluster was highly enriched in glycolysis, HIF-1 signaling, and PI3K-Akt pathways, indicating a prototypical “high-metabolism, low-antigen presentation, high-immunosuppression” resistant TAM phenotype. SPP1⁺ macrophages primarily rely on glycolysis as their metabolic program, which are associated with angiogenesis and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis19. Overexpression of SPP1 has been shown to enhance HIF-1α expression and promote cancer progression via activation of the PI3K signaling pathway20,21. The association between SPP1 and HIF-1α observed in this study was primarily derived from transcriptomic enrichment within macrophage subsets in the single-cell dataset. However, given that HIF-1α is broadly induced under hypoxic conditions in both tumor and immune cells, we cannot exclude the possibility that tumor-cell–derived HIF-1α may also contribute to SPP1 regulation within the tumor microenvironment.

Moreover, pseudotime trajectory analysis placed most Macro-SPP1 at the terminal end of the myeloid differentiation trajectory, suggesting that this immunosuppressive phenotype likely arises from tumor- linked reprogramming of myeloid cells. It should be noted that pseudotime analysis infers transcriptional continua rather than definitive lineage endpoints. Therefore, the enrichment of Macro-SPP1 cells toward later pseudotime regions likely reflects a shift toward specific functional states, rather than irreversible terminal differentiation.

Importantly, prior evidence has shown that patients with high SPP1 (Secreted Phosphoprotein 1) expression derive limited benefit from anti-PD-L1 therapy22. In our study, SPP1 emerged as a key marker of resistance, not only markedly upregulated in the Macro-SPP1 population, but also expressed at high levels in fibroblast clusters, indicating a cross-lineage expression pattern. This suggests that SPP1 may be associated with immune resistance through interactions across multiple cell types. A similar phenomenon has been observed in hepatocellular carcinoma, where SPP1⁺ TAMs and tumor-associated fibroblasts cooperatively remodel the ECM structure, restrict T cell infiltration, and enhance immune evasion23.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed that SPP1 and IL1B formed distinct regions of enriched expression in tumor tissues from the resistance group, particularly enriched at the invasive front. Multiplex immunofluorescence further confirmed the overlapping spatial signals of SPP1⁺ with CD68⁺ and CD163⁺ macrophages, supporting their potential structural and spatial association with immune resistance. SPP1⁺ macrophages may contribute to the formation of spatial immune barriers by secreting ECM-related factors and promoting connective tissue proliferation, thereby limiting T cell infiltration and activation, and fostering an immune-tolerant microenvironment24. Our findings suggest that SPP1⁺ macrophages might act as functional components of immunosuppression and participate in spatial immune exclusion, contributing to the establishment of a resistant tumor immune landscape. However, it should be noted that spatial transcriptomics spot resolution captures mixed-cell populations, and therefore does not allow definitive cell-type–specific spatial localization. The enriched expression of SPP1 likely represent composite expression signals.

From a therapeutic perspective, accumulating pre-clinical evidence suggests that the osteopontin/SPP1 axis is pharmacologically actionable and may modulate anti-tumor immunity. Neutralization of osteopontin/SPP1 using blocking antibodies has been explored in tumor models, providing proof-of-concept that interfering with this pathway can restrain tumor progression and reshape immune function25. In lung cancer–relevant settings, recent work has further suggested that anti-SPP1 strategies may alleviate T-cell dysfunction and enhance anti-tumor immune responses26. In addition, targeting downstream interactions such as SPP1–integrin/CD44 signaling has been proposed as an alternative approach to disrupt SPP1-driven immunosuppressive programs and potentially improve responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors27. Importantly, our current study is observational and does not establish causality. Therefore, future functional experiments, including genetic or pharmacologic perturbation of SPP1 signaling and macrophage reprogramming assays in LUAD models, will be required to determine whether modulation of this axis can causally enhance immunotherapy efficacy.

Moreover, SCENIC analysis revealed distinct transcriptional regulatory programs in myeloid subpopulations. We observed strong activation of MYCN and CEBPD in the Macro-SPP1 cluster, while Macro-IL1B was regulated by ATF3 and CEBPD. Transcription factors previously implicated in immune tolerance, pro-inflammatory responses, and macrophage polarization28–30. These findings provide potential factors that may be associated with immunotherapy resistance and suggest potential upstream targets for reprogramming the myeloid compartment. MYCN activation has been associated with enhanced metabolic and proliferative programs, potentially supporting cellular adaptation within the tumor microenvironment31. CEBPD is a stress-responsive transcription factor implicated in inflammatory signaling and macrophage functional polarization, and its activation may reflect transcriptional programs related to cytokine production and immune modulation32. Notably, the co-existence of FAP⁺ CAFs and SPP1⁺ TAMs has been associated with T cell exclusion and poor clinical outcomes. Several recent studies have elegantly demonstrated the intimate relationship between FAP⁺ fibroblasts and SPP1⁺ macrophages in the tumor microenvironment22,33.

Although our integrative single-cell and spatial analyses reveal robust associations between SPP1⁺ macrophages, stromal remodeling, and immunotherapy resistance, the present study is observational in nature. Therefore, functional experiments, such as genetic perturbation or co-culture assays, will be required to establish causal relationships between these cell populations and therapeutic outcomes. This study has several limitations remain. First, while we observed biologically meaningful trends in immune cell compositional shifts (such as the relative enrichment of SPP1⁺ macrophages in resistant tumors), our formal differential abundance testing using edgeR did not reach statistical significance. This is primarily constrained by the limited number of biological replicates (n = 3 per group) in the scRNA-seq cohort, which reduces statistical power. Therefore, our single-cell compositional findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, warranting future validation in larger independent cohorts. Second, the functional roles of the Macro-SPP1 population have not been directly confirmed through in vitro assays or in vivo models, and the causal relationship with resistance remains to be clarified. Functional validation will be required to determine whether SPP1⁺ macrophages causally contribute to resistance. Then, spatial transcriptomics was performed on one representative sample per group, which may introduce sample-level variability. Therefore, spatial analyses were used to provide contextual and supportive evidence for the single-cell findings rather than to draw definitive quantitative conclusions. Additionally, although single-cell datasets contain large numbers of cells, trajectory reconstruction may still be influenced by sample-level variability when derived from a limited number of biological replicates. Therefore, the pseudotime structure presented here should be interpreted as representing transcriptional state continua within this cohort, rather than definitive lineage hierarchies or universal differentiation pathways. Future studies incorporating larger and independent cohorts will be necessary to validate the robustness and reproducibility of the inferred trajectories. Several important clinical variables that may influence immunotherapy outcomes, such as ECOG performance status, detailed treatment regimens, line of therapy, baseline tumor burden, specific metastatic organ involvement, and comprehensive molecular alterations (e.g., KRAS, STK11, KEAP1), were not consistently available in the retrospective dataset. The absence of these variables may introduce potential confounding and limits the ability to perform fully adjusted multivariable analyses. Although we analyzed SPP1 expression, comprehensive survival follow-up data were not available to support Kaplan–Meier or Cox regression analyses. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive biomarker validation. Prospective studies with long-term follow-up will be required to establish the prognostic and predictive value of SPP1 in immunotherapy-treated LUAD. The spatial transcriptomics analysis in this study is descriptive and intended to provide anatomical context for the single-cell findings. Given the limited number of spatial samples and spot-level resolution, we did not perform advanced spatial modeling or neighborhood interaction analyses. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive spatial mechanistic evidence. Finally, TTF-1 has been reported to correlate with immunotherapy outcomes in NSCLC. However, additional TTF-1 staining was not feasible in the current retrospective cohort due to limited remaining tissue material.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant association between SPP1⁺ immunosuppressive macrophages and LUAD immunotherapy resistance from both single-cell and spatial perspectives. This subpopulation exhibits high metabolic activity, a functionally polarized state, and spatial enrichment, along with a distinct transcriptional patterns and potential immunosuppressive properties. These findings provide a novel perspective for understanding the mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance in LUAD and offer a theoretical basis and potential targets for the development of precise and targeted combination immunotherapy strategies.
