Quantitative assessment of the 30-second sit-to-stand test using computer vision technology for physical therapists
Authors: Chin-Ying Hsu, Ying-Po Hsu, Tien-Lung Sun, Chia-Hsuan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls are a leading cause of disability and mortality in older adults. The 30-second sit-to-stand test (30STS) traditionally relies on repetition counts, limiting its ability to capture subtle postural control deficits. We applied computer vision and machine learning to extend this test into a multidimensional assessment framework for functional status discrimination.MethodsThirty-two community-dwelling older adults (18 normal, 14 weak) performed two 30STS tests at baseline (June–July 2022) and follow-up (January–February 2023). Smartphone videos were processed using Keypoint R-CNN to extract 17 joint coordinates. Center of mass (CoM), kinematic parameters, and multiscale sample entropy (MSE) were derived. Background

Falls are a leading cause of disability, loss of independence, and mortality among older adults [1]. Age-related declines in postural control are reflected in larger and more irregular center-of-pressure sway in older compared with young adults [2]. Their consequences include substantial healthcare costs and long-term deterioration in quality of life [3, 4]. The 30-second sit-to-stand test (30STS) is widely used in geriatric and rehabilitation practice as a simple indicator of lower-limb strength, endurance, and functional mobility [5]. Prior work has shown that 30STS performance is closely associated with balance and fall risk and can serve as an associated with future fall risk in prior prospective studies [6–10]. Age- and sex-specific normative thresholds proposed by Jones and Rikli allow clinicians to classify older adults into functionally normal and weak performance groups based on the number of repetitions completed within 30 s [11]. The clinical relevance and validity of sit-to-stand performance as an indicator of lower-limb function have been further supported by subsequent studies [12]. However, in routine practice, assessment still relies almost exclusively on this repetition count. This unidimensional metric compresses the complexity of motor control into a single index and does not capture movement quality, including postural stability of the centre of mass (CoM), temporal coordination, or compensatory strategies. Beyond gait, recent work has shown that altered, sagittal-plane CoM movement strategies during sit-to-walk are associated with functional status discrimination in older adults [13]. As a result, subtle or subclinical impairments in postural control may remain undetected until overt functional decline or falls occur. There is therefore a clear need to transform the conventional 30STS from a purely quantitative count into a multidimensional assessment that can be implemented with everyday devices and directly inform functional status discrimination detection and clinical decision-making.

Advances in sensing and neuroengineering have created new opportunities to address these limitations. Wearable inertial measurement units provide high-resolution kinematic data and have been used to estimate balance, cognitive function, and functional status discrimination during sit-to-stand and gait tasks [14–17]. However, they require body-worn sensors, which may reduce comfort, adherence, and scalability in community settings. In contrast, computer vision enables non-contact, low-cost, and scalable motion analysis using ordinary cameras. Recent studies integrating sit-to-stand or gait tests with video-based motion capture and machine learning have reported improved validity for functional status discrimination identification [18–20]. Deep learning–based human pose estimation allows automatic extraction of two-dimensional joint trajectories from monocular video, enabling derivation of CoM motion and joint kinematics without markers or wearable devices [21, 22]. Preliminary work has combined pose-derived temporal signals with multiscale sample entropy (MSE), a nonlinear measure of temporal irregularity across scales, to characterize movement complexity, highlighting the potential of pose-based complexity measures as candidate digital biomarkers of balance control [23, 24]. Nonetheless, much of this literature remains confined to single-session, cross-sectional designs, often focusing on gait or five-times sit-to-stand rather than the clinically ubiquitous 30STS, and frequently relying on research-grade cameras or laboratory installations [25–28]. Few studies have explicitly combined smartphone-based pose estimation, established 30STS normative cut-offs, and longitudinal follow-up in community-dwelling older adults.

To address these gaps, the present study conducted a six-month longitudinal evaluation of the 30STS test in community-dwelling older adults using smartphone-based markerless pose estimation [18, 25, 29, 30]. Participants completed two assessments separated by six months and were classified as Normal or Weak according to established age- and sex-specific normative thresholds [11, 12]. From 17 body keypoints, we reconstructed whole-body CoM trajectories using standardized anthropometric models derived from classical segmental mass distribution frameworks [31–35]. Based on these trajectories, we derived kinematic features—including displacement, velocity, and acceleration—and quantified temporal complexity using MSE following established methodological guidelines for postural control analysis [36–39]. Variability- and distribution-based CoM indices were treated as surrogate markers of movement regulation and balance control [17, 40–42].

This study aimed to determine whether CoM-derived kinematic and entropy-based features can (1) differentiate Normal and Weak older adults based on movement quality rather than repetition count alone; (2) demonstrate region-specific sensitivity consistent with proximal–distal coordination models of postural control [31–33, 43–48]; and (3) achieve subject-level classification performance independent of repetition count when used as inputs to supervised machine-learning models [49–55]. By anchoring functional grouping to established repetition-based thresholds while excluding repetition count from model inputs, the study evaluates whether pose-derived CoM dynamics provide discriminative information not fully represented by conventional repetition-based assessment.

Methods

Study design and participants

This longitudinal study included two 30STS assessments conducted at baseline (June–July 2022) and at follow-up (January–February 2023). A total of 55 community-dwelling older adults were recruited (17 men, 38 women; mean age 74.5 ± 5.5 years), of whom 32 (9 men, 23 women; mean age 75.6 ± 6.1 years) completed both sessions and were included in the final analysis.

Between baseline and follow-up, attrition was attributable to loss of contact, health-related limitations, or inability to attend the second assessment, and was not related to recording quality or protocol adherence. All trials were reviewed during acquisition to ensure standardized task execution and were subsequently inspected during post-processing by overlaying detected keypoints on the original video to verify stable tracking throughout the 30-s sequence. No trials were excluded due to pose-estimation failure, tracking instability, or improper task performance.

Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 65 years; independent ambulation without walking aids; intact cognitive function. Exclusion criteria were: inability to stand or walk; major cardiovascular or hematological disease; major surgery within the past six months (lower limb, spine, or brain); severe visual impairment; or determination of ineligibility by a healthcare professional.

All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taoyuan General Hospital, Ministry of Health and Welfare, Taiwan (IRB no. TYGH109089).

Experimental setup and procedure

Videos were recorded using a smartphone (Apple iPhone 8; Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA; released in 2017; operating system: iOS 11) in a standardized indoor environment with consistent lighting conditions. The camera was positioned 2 m in front of the from the participant at approximately hip height. Video resolution was set to 1920 × 1080 pixels (1080p Full HD) at 60 frames per second. Participants performed repeated STS cycles for 30 s from an armless chair (floor-to-seat height: 0.435 m), following a standardized verbal cue, without using their arms for assistance. A licensed physical therapist supervised each trial to ensure safety and recorded the number of completed repetitions as the conventional 30STS outcome measure. Video processing and feature extraction

Pose estimation was performed using Keypoint R-CNN configured for the COCO 2017 17-keypoint scheme (nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles), as shown in Fig. 2. Because this scheme does not include fingertips or toe tips, pseudo-distal landmarks were defined to approximate distal segment endpoints. For the hand, a pseudo landmark was positioned from the wrist toward the elbow at 75% of the forearm length (k_hand = 0.75), and for the foot, from the ankle toward the knee at 60% of the shank length (k_foot = 0.60) [31]. Sensitivity analysis indicated that varying k by ± 20% had negligible influence on whole-body CoM estimation, reflecting the relatively small mass fractions of distal segments.

All processed videos underwent visual quality control. Predefined exclusion criteria included gross tracking failure or sustained keypoint loss; however, no trials met these criteria. Minor frame-to-frame jitters were attenuated using temporal smoothing prior to feature extraction. Segmental centers of mass were estimated using standardized anthropometric parameters based on Ae et al. [33] and Dempster [34], which define segment mass distribution and relative CoM locations. For each segment, the segmental CoM was computed by linear interpolation along the proximal–distal axis between joint landmarks. It should be clarified that individual body segments were not analyzed as independent predictors in the classification models. Instead, standardized anthropometric mass distributions were applied to reconstruct the whole-body CoM trajectory from segmental positions. Consequently, each segment contributes to the global CoM proportionally to its relative mass fraction. Segments with larger mass fractions (e.g., trunk and thighs) exert proportionally greater influence on CoM displacement and its derived kinematic and entropy-based features. Therefore, intergroup discrimination reflects coordinated segmental movement patterns manifested at the level of the whole-body CoM rather than isolated segment-level effects. The resulting whole-body CoM trajectory was decomposed into horizontal (x) and vertical (y) components for two-dimensional analysis. All reported kinematic parameters—including displacement range, variability, velocity, acceleration, and MSE—were derived exclusively from the reconstructed whole-body CoM trajectory. Velocity and acceleration were computed as the first and second temporal derivatives of CoM displacement, respectively.

To characterize movement variability and distributional properties, statistical descriptors were computed from the CoM time series. MSE analysis was applied to the vertical CoM trajectory, and the Complexity Index (CI) was defined as the average MSE value across scales 1–6. Higher CI values are generally interpreted as indicating greater temporal complexity and irregularity in CoM fluctuations over time, whereas lower CI values correspond to more regular and predictable trajectories during the 30-s sit-to-stand test [37, 38]. All kinematic and CoM variables were derived from markerless two-dimensional joint trajectories obtained from smartphone videos; no laboratory-grade motion-capture or force-plate system was used [40]. Functional classification (normal vs. weak) was based on age- and sex-specific normative repetition thresholds [31, 33–35]. All extracted features are summarized in Table 1.

Feature selection and model implementation

A comprehensive set of statistical features was initially computed from the CoM time series, including mean, standard deviation, maximum, minimum, range, skewness, kurtosis, and coefficient of variation (CV). To reduce redundancy and mitigate multicollinearity, feature importance and correlation analyses were conducted within the modelling framework. Based on these evaluations, mean, standard deviation, and range were selected as input variables for classification modelling to enhance interpretability and reduce overfitting risk under small-sample conditions.

All data preprocessing and machine learning analyses were conducted using Orange Data Mining software (version 3.36; University of Ljubljana, Slovenia), which operates on Python (compatible with Python 3.10/3.11) and utilizes scikit-learn as its computational backend. Classification models were trained separately for the baseline (June–July 2022) and follow-up (January–February 2023) sessions. For each session, each participant contributed one subject-level feature vector summarizing their 30STS performance and was labelled as Normal or Weak according to age- and sex-specific normative thresholds. Each classification dataset therefore contained 32 independent subject-level observations.

To evaluate cross-subject generalization, repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation was implemented using the built-in cross-validation module in Orange. In each repetition, data were randomly partitioned into five stratified folds; four folds were used for training and one for testing, and this process was rotated across all folds. The procedure was repeated 100 times with different random partitions, and performance metrics were averaged across all folds and repetitions.

Three classification algorithms available in Orange were implemented: (1) logistic regression (LR) with L1 regularization [49]; (2) random forest (RF) with 500 trees and a maximum tree depth of 2 [50] to constrain model complexity under small-sample conditions; and (3) support vector machine (SVM) with a radial basis function kernel [53–55]. These classifiers were selected to represent complementary modelling strategies frequently used in biomedical research: a sparse linear model with interpretability (LR), a non-parametric ensemble capable of capturing non-linear interactions (RF), and a margin-based kernel method appropriate for limited sample sizes (SVM). More complex models (e.g., deep neural networks or gradient boosting) were not considered appropriate given the modest sample size (n = 32).

Model performance was primarily evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) as a threshold-independent discrimination metric [55], supplemented by classification accuracy (CA), F1 score, precision, recall, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), which are commonly recommended for classification assessment in biomedical applications [54].

Statistical analysis

Within-group longitudinal changes between baseline and follow-up were evaluated using paired-sample t-tests, reflecting the repeated-measures design in which the same participants were assessed at two time points. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-sided).

For machine learning evaluation, model performance was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) as the primary metric, supplemented by classification accuracy (CA), F1 score, precision, recall, and MCC. Performance metrics were averaged across stratified five-fold cross-validation and 100 repetitions, as described in the previous section.

Given the modest sample size and the feature-level exploratory analyses, statistical findings are interpreted in conjunction with effect direction, consistency across related metrics, and visual summaries. Future studies with larger cohorts may further validate these findings using more flexible modelling frameworks or non-parametric approaches.

Feature definitions

Stability indices

Postural stability was quantified using variability of CoM trajectories as a surrogate marker of balance control [42, 56–58]. For both horizontal (CoM-x) and vertical (CoM-y) directions, we calculated peak-to-valley amplitude, standard deviation, and path length per unit time. Whole-body CoM was estimated using the segmental method, applying Asian population–specific mass distribution ratios reported by Ae et al. [33], grounded in the classical distribution data of Dempster [34] and de Leva [31]. In this framework, segment lengths were obtained from each participant’s pose-estimation keypoints and combined using fixed mass ratios, such that the reconstructed whole-body CoM trajectories primarily reflect global body movement relative to the base of support rather than raw limb lengths. Moreover, several derived features (e.g., coefficient of variation, skewness, kurtosis, and the multiscale entropy-based Complexity Index) are dimensionless, thereby reducing direct dependence on absolute body size.

Kinematic indices

Velocity was computed as inter-frame displacement divided by time interval, and acceleration as the rate of change of velocity [59]. These parameters reflect movement coordination and initiation/termination control during STS transitions [60, 61]. Features such as acceleration variability have been associated with balance and functional performance in older adults [43, 41, 42].

Discussion

The six-month interval was selected as a pragmatic monitoring timeframe in community-dwelling older adults. Rather than inducing overt functional change, this interval allowed examination of the longitudinal sensitivity of pose-derived CoM features under natural conditions.

The present study examined whether markerless reconstruction of whole-body CoM kinematic patterns during the 30STS test could differentiate between normal and weak older adults and capture longitudinal variation within a stable functional classification. The longitudinal design therefore focused on participants who remained within the same functional category across sessions, enabling evaluation of within-stratum temporal behavior rather than modeling functional transition or clinical progression.

Overall, CoM-derived kinematic and entropy-based features characterized movement control patterns associated with functional status classification.

CoM stability and sway characteristics

The absence of significant longitudinal changes in the Normal group is consistent with the region-of-stability framework proposed by Hof et al. [57], in which stable movement is maintained when CoM motion remains within functional limits. CoM acceleration–derived stability measures have previously been shown to identify sit-to-stand difficulty [62], and accelerometry-based indices reflect balance and coordination characteristics [17, 61].

In contrast, the Weak group exhibited increased extremal CoM displacement and altered distributional properties, suggesting modified movement organization and transient instability. Distributional indices such as skewness and kurtosis have been shown to reflect changes in postural regulation strategies [50, 57, 62, 63]. Importantly, although repetition count declined in the Weak group, several CoM-derived indices primarily describe variability and structural organization of movement rather than repetition frequency alone, indicating qualitative differences in kinematic control patterns.

Global CoM representation

Accordingly, intergroup differences observed in CoM-derived kinematic and entropy-based features likely represent differences in global movement coordination structure. Segments with larger relative mass fractions (e.g., trunk and thighs) exert proportionally greater influence on overall CoM displacement and its derived temporal characteristics. However, discrimination does not arise from any single segment but from the integrated movement pattern expressed at the level of the whole-body CoM.

Importantly, because repetition count quantifies only task quantity, whereas CoM-based features describe spatial variability, temporal organization, and coordination structure, the global CoM representation captures dimensions of movement regulation that are not directly represented by repetition frequency alone [12, 62]. This conceptual distinction supports the independent discriminative validity of CoM-derived features observed in the classification analysis.

Whole-body CoM integrates the mass-weighted contributions of multiple body segments based on standardized anthropometric models [33–35]. Observed intergroup differences therefore reflect coordinated multi-segment movement organization rather than isolated segment-level effects. This global kinematic representation captures aspects of postural regulation that are not directly represented by repetition count alone [12, 62].

Velocity and acceleration features

Velocity and acceleration parameters further characterized movement execution patterns. Muscular power is an important determinant of sit-to-stand performance and functional mobility in older adults [59, 64], and reduced lower-limb power has been associated with altered balance regulation [65, 66].

In the present study, reduced velocity and constrained variability patterns in the Weak group (see Fig. 4) may reflect conservative movement strategies characterized by reduced excursion and fluctuation [67–69]. Acceleration variability indices have previously been linked to balance control and functional performance [41–43], supporting their relevance as indicators of movement regulation. These kinematic characteristics describe temporal and structural aspects of performance that are not fully represented by repetition count alone (Tables S3, S4).

Multiscale sample entropy (MSE)

MSE analysis revealed distinct coordination patterns between groups. MSE is widely interpreted as a multiscale measure of temporal irregularity in motor signals [36–38], where higher values are generally associated with greater temporal complexity, whereas lower values correspond to more regular and constrained patterns [37–39].

In the Normal group, entropy redistribution across joints suggested coordinated modulation rather than uniform decline. Proximal load-bearing joints tended toward relatively greater regularity, whereas distal and mid-limb joints exhibited relatively higher complexity. This pattern is broadly consistent with models of sit-to-stand control in which stabilization and compensatory flexibility are distributed across joints [66, 70–72].

In contrast, the Weak group exhibited reduced distal entropy and relatively greater proximal involvement, aligning with postural control frameworks describing proximal compensation when distal control becomes insufficient [35, 42, 70, 72]. Similar redistribution patterns have been described in aging populations experiencing balance challenges [71, 73, 74].

The Complexity Index derived from vertical CoM trajectories further indicated lower temporal complexity in the Weak group compared with the Normal group (see Fig. 5 and Table S5). Together, these findings suggest that entropy-based metrics characterize interjoint coordination structure and temporal organization of movement beyond repetition count alone [15, 36, 38, 39]. Such coordination-level adaptations are conceptually consistent with center-of-mass velocity–position models proposed for dynamic balance regulation [75], although the present analysis did not explicitly model stability boundaries or dynamic limits of support.

Machine learning classification

Machine learning analysis demonstrated consistent cross-validated discrimination within this cohort between the Normal and Weak groups across both assessment sessions. RF achieved the highest classification performance within this cohort, consistent with its ability to model non-linear feature interactions [50]. LR provided stable and interpretable performance [51], whereas the SVM remained effective but exhibited greater sensitivity to sample variation [51–53].

Repeated stratified fivefold cross-validation (100 repetitions) was implemented to mitigate sampling variability under small-sample conditions. Although performance estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the modest cohort size, the consistent cross-validated discrimination across models indicates that CoM-derived kinematic and entropy-based features contain meaningful subject-level discriminative information.

Importantly, repetition count was not included as an input feature in any classification model. Functional labels (Normal vs. Weak) were defined using age- and sex-specific repetition thresholds, whereas classification performance was derived exclusively from reconstructed whole-body CoM trajectories and their associated kinematic and entropy characteristics. Accordingly, model discrimination reflects information contained in pose-derived movement organization rather than repetition frequency itself.

Taken together, these findings support the hypothesis that CoM-derived kinematic and entropy-based features can achieve subject-level discrimination independent of repetition count alone within this cohort.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size (n = 32) limits statistical precision and generalizability of performance estimates. Although repeated stratified cross-validation was implemented to mitigate sampling bias, external validation using independent cohorts is required to confirm reproducibility and stability of the findings.

Second, functional classification was defined using age- and sex-specific repetition thresholds [11, 12] without incorporation of multidimensional clinical balance scales. Consequently, discrimination was anchored to repetition-based criteria rather than comprehensive clinical assessments. Future studies should integrate standardized balance and functional scales to enhance clinical interpretability and ecological validity.

Third, anthropometric variables such as height and body weight were not systematically recorded, and CoM trajectories were not normalized to individual body dimensions. Residual body-size effects may therefore persist. In addition, no laboratory-grade motion-capture or force-plate system was used for external validation. Although prior studies have reported acceptable agreement between markerless and marker-based systems [32], multimodal validation would strengthen measurement confidence.

Future research should include larger and more diverse cohorts, incorporate anthropometric normalization, integrate standardized clinical scales, and employ multimodal validation approaches to improve generalizability and clinical applicability [14, 15, 76].

Integrated interpretation

The observed interjoint changes are consistent with Winter’s postural control framework [35, 42] and contemporary balance and coordination models [66, 70]. Under minor perturbations, upright posture is primarily stabilized through an ankle strategy. When distal outputs are insufficient, control shifts proximally (hips, trunk, upper limbs), often accompanied by co-contraction and stiffening to preserve stability. In our data, the Weak group exhibited this shift, showing increased proximal (shoulder, elbow) MSE and decreased distal (ankle) MSE, consistent with a shift toward increased proximal involvement and reduced distal variability [71, 72]. By contrast, the Normal group did not display such systematic redistribution, maintaining more balanced interjoint coordination.

Collectively, these findings highlight the value of MSE as a candidate digital biomarker capable of capturing subtle shifts in control strategies that are not detectable through repetition counts alone [15, 36–38]. This approach may contribute to quantitative assessment frameworks relevant to fall-risk–related clinical evaluation.

Taken together, classification performance exceeded chance-level expectation under repeated stratified cross-validation within this dataset, supporting the hypothesis that CoM-derived kinematic and entropy-based features contain subject-level discriminative information beyond repetition count alone. The consistent performance observed across different model types provides preliminary support for considering MSE-derived features as candidate digital biomarkers of movement organization. This quantitative framework aligns with prior research integrating machine learning, postural metrics, and sensor-based technologies for functional assessment and fall-related risk evaluation [67, 68, 77]. Further validation in larger and independent cohorts is necessary to determine reproducibility and broader clinical applicability.

Conclusions

This study examined whether markerless smartphone-based reconstruction of whole-body CoM kinematic patterns during the 30-s sit-to-stand test can characterize movement control differences between normal and weak older adults over a six-month interval. By combining smartphone video acquisition with pose estimation–based kinematic reconstruction, the conventional repetition-based 30STS was extended to a multidimensional assessment of movement organization.

The findings indicate that CoM-derived kinematic and entropy-based features reflect distinct movement patterns associated with functional classification. Classification analyses demonstrated consistent cross-validated discrimination at the subject level based exclusively on CoM-derived features, independent of repetition count as an input variable. These results suggest that pose-derived CoM metrics capture aspects of movement regulation that are not directly represented by repetition number alone.

However, given the modest sample size and single-cohort design, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. The reported classification performance should be considered preliminary estimates of discriminative potential. Further validation in larger and clinically characterized populations is required to assess reproducibility and to clarify the potential contribution of CoM-based digital metrics to functional assessment in older adults.
