 ABSTRACT

Passive thermometry is critically important because most fuels and materials irradiation experiments are
not instrumented, and it is necessary to understand the irradiation temperature to properly interpret any
post-irradiation examination data, including evolving properties and/or microstructures. The standard
passive thermometry approach uses continuous dilatometry to evaluate changes in the instantaneous
coefficient of thermal expansion during post-irradiation thermal annealing. This approach has limitations
in terms of sample size (minimum length requirements) and the maximum irradiation temperature that can
be accurately determined, which is limited by the reduced swelling (and therefore recovery) following
higher temperature irradiation and limitations on the furnaces used with push-rod dilatometers. This work
evaluates two new proposed techniques for post-irradiation evaluation of passive SiC temperature
monitors: differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and Raman spectroscopy. DSC is an extremely
sensitive technique that can be used for any specimen geometry and is capable of higher temperature
operation. Raman spectroscopy is similar in that it is a surface technique capable of examining extremely
small samples (submillimeter), can be used with a heated stage up to 1,500°C (planned for future work),
and is capable of mapping local irradiation temperatures throughout a sample.

Existing SiC samples that were previously irradiated over a wide range of temperatures were cut into
multiple pieces to allow for annealing studies using multiple different techniques: dilatometry, DSC, and
Raman spectroscopy. This approach mitigates the concern that samples analyzed using one technique may
have a slightly different irradiation history than those analyzed using a different technique. Recovery was
clearly observed during annealing using both DSC and dilatometry. In some cases, a direct comparison
could not be made due to some of the DSC runs accidentally including material from multiple specimens
and issues with using an alternative DSC sample holder for the highest temperature annealing studies.
Nevertheless, one trend was clear: the DSC runs resulted in higher irradiation temperatures compared to
those of the dilatometry runs. Part of this could be attributed to the higher temperature ramp rates used
during the DSC runs, which are often preferred to reduce noise in the measurements. By comparison,
dilatometry has previously been shown to produce better data at lower ramp rates. Future work should
further investigate the ideal ramp rate for both techniques to produce consistent results. Additional work
should evaluate the best holder material to use for DSC runs exceeding 1,000°C to provide reliable data
while preventing interactions between SiC and the holder.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Most fuels and materials irradiation experiments are not instrumented due to the time, cost, and
complexity associated with executing instrumented experiments in high-power test reactors. Therefore,
estimations of irradiation temperatures must be inferred passively. Historically, arrays of melt wires have
provided an indication of the maximum temperature during irradiation. However, these arrays provide
only binary indications of whether a specific wire has melted, and they may not provide a good indication
of irradiation temperatures during normal operation if there were any unanticipated transient power
operations.

Passive SiC temperature monitors (TMs) have been investigated dating back to the 1960s [1]. This class
of passive thermometry leverages the predictable recovery of many thermophysical and
thermomechanical properties of SiC—specifically dense, phase-pure beta phase (3C) SiC fabricated via
chemical vapor deposition—upon post-irradiation thermal annealing. The idea is that if those properties
can be monitored during post-irradiation annealing, a rapid recovery in those properties should be evident
as the irradiation temperature is approached. Previous works have demonstrated such recovery while
monitoring lattice parameters [1, 2], dimensional changes [3-7], thermal conductivity [5, 6], and
electrical resistivity [2, 4, 8].

Oak Ridge National Laboratory has adopted continuous dilatometry as the standard approach to
measuring the recovery of radiation-induced swelling during post-irradiation thermal annealing. The
process is automated and has been used to evaluate hundreds (if not thousands) of TMs in the past two
decades. The current dilatometric approach, including data processing and estimation of irradiation
temperatures, is summarized in previous works [9, 10]. Despite the process being well-established with
extensive operational history, dilatometry has some disadvantages. For example, the push-rod
dilatometers use linear variable differential transformers (LVDTs) to continuously measure displacement
during annealing. LVDT measurements typically have uncertainties on the order of micrometers, which
requires the specimen length to be ~1 cm or larger to ensure that the recovery of the radiation-induced
swelling can be accurately measured. Low-dose and/or high-temperature experiments, for which swelling
in SiC is lower, can require longer lengths and suffer from increased noise due to the smaller amount of
swelling recovery, making the use of dilatometry challenging for these conditions. A large TM length can
be problematic for some experiments that have small specimen size and/or large spatial temperature
variations—for example, those involving monolithic nuclear fuel irradiation. In addition to seeing lower
swelling recovery and higher noise during annealing of samples irradiated at higher temperatures, push-
rod dilatometers also have limitations in their maximum operating temperature. For example, the current
instrument at ORNL is limited to a maximum temperature of ~1,200°C. Fuel centerline temperatures for
some reactor applications can be higher, and the temperature during dilatometry needs to be increased
beyond the irradiation temperature to observe significant annealing.

Recent works have demonstrated that other techniques could offer advantages compared to dilatometry
for evaluating passive SiC TMs. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was used to quantify stored
energy release in SiC during post-irradiation annealing following irradiation at temperatures of
237–247°C and 607°C [11]. Although this previous work did not focus specifically on the use of DSC for
passive thermometry, it was clear from the results that the initiation of stored energy release occurred near
the irradiation temperature, suggesting that DSC could be a promising technique for passive thermometry.
DSC is especially attractive because it can be used for any specimen geometry, and the current furnace
available at ORNL allows for testing specimens to temperatures as high as 1,500°C. Similarly, Raman
spectroscopy has been used to establish correlations between the Raman peak shift, radiation-induced
swelling, and irradiation temperature, among other properties such as thermal conductivity [12]. ORNL’s
Raman spectrometer also includes a heated stage that allows for examining specimens during heating up

 2

to 1,500°C. Finally, Raman spectroscopy is attractive because it can be performed on extremely small
samples (submillimeter), and it can be used to map the spatial variations in temperature by scanning
across the surface of a passive TM.

This work provides initial results evaluating dilatometry, DSC, and Raman spectroscopy for passive SiC
thermometry. When possible, measurements were performed using fragments from the exact same
irradiated material to eliminate concerns regarding variations in the irradiation history of materials
evaluated using different techniques. The work leveraged existing SiC samples that were irradiated under
a wide range of temperatures spanning light-water reactor applications (<300°C) to high-temperature gas-
cooled reactors (>900°C). Data processing scripts were created to automate the analysis and calculation of
irradiation temperatures, with initial efforts to establish metrics to quantify data fits. The long-term goal
would be to improve upon these metrics to eventually establish more statistically based uncertainties in
the irradiation temperatures.

 2. METHODOLOGY

2.1 SAMPLES

Previous irradiation experiments focused on materials relevant to fusion energy were performed over a
wide range of temperatures and included passive SiC TMs that were never analyzed. These TMs were
also large enough that they could be sectioned into multiple pieces for performing annealing studies via
dilatometry, DSC, and (in some cases) Raman spectroscopy. All samples were high-purity, dense beta
phase (3C) SiC fabricated by Rohm and Haas (later purchased by Dow Chemical) using chemical vapor
deposition.

Table 1 summarizes all the annealing experiments that were performed across 16 specimens irradiated at
nominal temperatures from 240°C to 1,000°C and dose levels from 2 to 10 dpa. All dilatometry
measurements were performed using ORNL’s standard temperature ramp rate of 1°C/min. The
dilatometry sample lengths were approximately 20 mm. As mentioned previously, DSC measurements
generally exhibit lower noise at higher ramp rates, so a ramp rate of 10°C/min was used for all
experiments performed under this program. In addition, previous samples were annealed at a ramp rate of
3°C and were analyzed under this program to compare how ramp rate affects the estimated irradiation
temperatures. Those previous samples also used a smaller mass, with values as low as 19 mg compared
with the samples that were tested under this program that had values as high as 94 mg. These differences
also allowed for some insights to be provided regarding the effect of sample mass during DSC runs. In
addition to the annealing experiments with dilatometry and DSC, some samples were characterized via
Raman spectroscopy. Those measurements are described in Section 3.3.

2.2 DILATOMETRY

The dilatometry measurements were performed using a NETZSCH DIL 402 CD horizontal dual pushrod
dilatometer. All tests were performed in an inert (helium) atmosphere with a flow rate of 150 mL/min.
The maximum temperature during each annealing run was set to be 300°C higher than the nominal
irradiation temperature. The heating rates are provided in Table 1, and the cooling rate was set to
2.5°C/min in all cases.

2.3 DIFFERENTIAL SCANNING CALORIMETRY

DSC measurements were performed using a NETZSCH DSC 404 F1 thermal analyzer. Because this is a
new technique for determining the expected irradiation temperature, standard testing conditions have yet
to be determined. The tests were performed in an inert (argon) atmosphere with a flow rate of 70 mL/min.
Typically, platinum–rhodium crucibles are used, but it was found that silicon carbide has a reaction with
platinum beginning at 1,000°C. Therefore, alumina crucibles were used for tests annealing two samples
(B14, B15) beyond 1,000°C. For determining the specific heat capacity of a material, empty crucibles
must be run for 3 cycles to create a baseline. Subsequently, a standard material is run—in this case, a
0.5 mm thick sapphire standard. This type of testing was not performed for all samples. It was
hypothesized that running with a standard sample is not necessary if the goal is simply to analyze
deviations in DSC measurements performed during heating vs. cooling. The heating rates are shown in
Table 1. All tests performed with a 10°C/min heating rate used a 20°C/min cooling rate. Similarly, all
tests performed with a 3°C/min heating rate used a 10°C/min cooling rate.

2.4 RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY

Raman spectroscopy was performed using a Renishaw inVia Qontor with a 633 nm wavelength laser and
a 50× lens. Maps of the local (spatially dependent) Raman shift were obtained using an automated high-
speed sample stage with a 1 µm step size. Raman shift data were processed using the software Wire 5.5
and Fityk 1.3.1 to perform the deconvolution of the Raman signals and obtain the peak position of each
band. All Raman peaks were assumed to have a Gaussian shape.

As described in a previous publication [12], the change in the LO Raman peak position of SiC was found
to be well-correlated with the radiation-induced swelling and, therefore, the irradiation temperature
(swelling in SiC is highly temperature-dependent). The swelling temperature dependence is due to the
increased vacancy–interstitial recombination that occurs due to increased defect mobility with increasing
temperature. The Raman peak position (wavenumber) was found to be linearly correlated with irradiation
temperature to an accuracy of ±55°C. This correlation, established for irradiation temperatures between
235 and 750°C and assumed to be valid within the point defect swelling regime (i.e., temperatures less
than ~1,000°C), was used to relate the measured Raman peak positions to an estimated irradiation
temperature.

 3. RESULTS

3.1 DILATOMETRY

Figure 1–Figure 16 include the dilatometry results from all specimens. Each figure shows the
instantaneous coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) measurements (solid symbols) during heating (red)
and cooling (blue), as well as their difference (black). The cooling data were fit to a third-order
polynomial (red dashed line), and the difference data were fit to an asymmetric sigmoid curve (black
dashed line) [9]. The heating data fit was determined by simply subtracting the difference fit from the
cooling fit. Only the data in the gray shaded regions were fit to avoid fitting noisy portions of the data far
from the region of interest. As described in Campbell et al. [9], the median, minimum, and maximum
temperatures are calculated by determining inflection points in the difference curve fit, drawing tangent
lines from the heating curves at those temperatures, and finding where those tangent lines intersect the
cooling curves. The figures show the tangent lines (dashed) and the estimated median, minimum, and
maximum temperatures (star markers). Finally, the goodness-of-fit parameters for the cooling and
difference fits are shown in the top of each figure.

3.3 RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY

Figure 33 shows example Raman spectra and Figure 34 through Figure 36 show the Raman maps of all
the samples that were measured. Figure 33 shows three examples of the Raman spectra of samples
irradiated to 2 dpa at nominal temperatures of 240, 600, and 1,000°C. Three distinctive regions were
observed at 200–600 cm−1, 600–1,000 cm−1 and 1,000–1,900 cm−1, corresponding to the presence of Si
(symmetrical signal), SiC and carbon, respectively. In general, Raman scattering in SiC includes a
transverse optic phonon (TO) at around 790 cm−1 and a longitudinal optic phonon (LO) at around
973 cm−1. However, as defects, other polytypes or amorphization occur, other phonons become optically
allowed. As the amount of defects or amorphization increases, the Raman signal reflects the density of
phonon states in the Brillouin zone, resulting in a broad Raman spectrum shifted to lower frequencies [13,
14]. This broadening is clearly observed in Figure 33a–c, as the nominal irradiation temperature is
reduced from around 1,000°C to 240°C.

Raman spectroscopy imaging was performed for all samples, providing a detailed map of the surface of
the irradiated samples. Figure 34 through Figure 36 show examples of the maps that can be generated
with a spatial resolution of 1 µm, with thousands of measurements performed for each sample. For each
measurement, a single Raman spectrum was obtained, such as those shown in Figure 33. A curve fitting
was performed for each spectrum to obtain the position of the LO band, which could be used to identify
the irradiation temperature. Gaussian curves were selected to fit these bands, following the approach
established previously for interpreting the broadening of the Raman signals for Si and Ga [15]. Because
the defective SiC results in a signal representing the density of phonon states, additional bands can appear
that can complicate the assignment of the LO band, particularly if those bands appear at higher
frequencies as observed in Figure 33c. Gaussian fitting of all bands helps to improve the identification of
the LO band. For samples irradiated at around 240°C, an additional band at around 942.2 cm-1 was
consistently found that provided an accurate down shift of the LO band and ultimately resulted in very
similar irradiation temperatures compared to those determined by dilatometry. The Raman maps in Figure

 22

34 through Figure 36 show the heterogeneity of the samples and the importance of measuring a large data
set to account for microstructural variations.

 4. DISCUSSION

4.1 COMPARISON ACROSS TECHNIQUES

Table 2 summarizes the irradiation temperatures obtained using dilatometry (dil), DSC, and Raman
spectroscopy, as well as goodness-of-fit parameters (R2) for the fits to the cooling (“cool”) and difference
(“diff”) data. There are some clear trends in the data:

• When using higher ramp rates of 10°C/min during DSC (specimens from capsules IMR03, IMR06
 and IMR09, see Table 1), irradiation temperatures are consistently over-predicted compared to those
 obtained via dilatometry. There is some evidence of this in the literature [11].

• When using lower ramp rates of 3°C/min during DSC (specimens from capsules IMR07, IMR08, and
 IMR11, see Table 1), irradiation temperatures are closer to those obtained via dilatometry (e.g., for
 specimens A16, A33, and A36), although the DSC temperatures are more erratic and may not be
 trustworthy.

• The fits to both the cooling and difference data, whether using dilatometry or DSC, generally resulted
 in reasonable values for R2 (>0.95). The fits were less accurate at higher irradiation temperatures,
 which would be expected based on the reduced radiation-induced swelling with increasing irradiation
 temperatures, which results in a lower amount of recovery during annealing. This is most evident in
 the data from the IMR09 specimens.

• The unreliable DSC data from the specimens from capsules IMR07, IMR08, and IMR11 are likely an
 artifact of the low specimen masses (see Table 1), which were approximately three times smaller than
 those used in the specimens from capsules IMR03, IMR06, and IMR09. The smaller mass reduced
 the amount of stored energy release during annealing, which resulted in minimal differences in the
 DSC curves during heating vs. cooling. In some cases, specimens with small masses still produced
 reasonably high values for R2, but the irradiation temperatures varied significantly despite having the
 same nominal value. For example, specimens A33 and C22 both appeared to have decent fits
 (R2>0.97) but resulted in median temperatures of 626 and 473°C, respectively, which is likely non-
 physical for an experiment that was designed for a constant temperature of 600°C.

• In all but two cases, the irradiation temperatures estimated from Raman spectroscopy were within the
 range of those estimated via dilatometry. The uncertainties in the irradiation temperatures using
 Raman spectroscopy are generally higher (compared to those from dilatometry) at lower irradiation
 temperatures and lower at higher irradiation temperatures.

• The median, minimum, and maximum temperatures, as predicted using the approach outlined in
 Section 2.2, are not in a consistent order for all specimens. This result suggests that the previously
 established [9] methods for identifying inflection points and how they correspond to the median,
 minimum and maximum temperatures may need to be revisited.

4.2 ISSUES AT HIGH ANNEALING TEMPERATURES

One of the original goals of this work was to improve the ability to measure higher irradiation
temperatures, beyond 1,000°C, that are relevant to those at the centerline of ceramic nuclear fuels during
reactor operation. This project only had access to four samples that were irradiated at nominally 1,000°C.
Dilatometry was able to resolve slight changes in in the instantaneous CTE of samples B14 and B15 (see
Figure 13 and Figure 14, respectively) when the annealing temperature exceeded 1,000°C or less, but
there was clearly a large amount of noise in the CTE data. This resulted in poor fits, particularly for the
difference data. Nevertheless, reasonable median irradiation temperatures of 929 and 922°C were
determined for samples B14 and B15, respectively. However, it is difficult to trust these temperatures
given the poor quality of the fits from which the temperatures were estimated.

The A18 and A22 samples were irradiated in a different capsule but at the same nominal temperature. The
CTE data for these samples (Figure 15 and Figure 16) are far less noisy (R2>0.99) and indicate clear
recovery starting well below 800°C, with estimated median temperatures of 613 and 752°C for samples
A18 and A22, respectively. Although this may seem encouraging, this clearly indicates that the specimens
did not achieve the desired irradiation temperatures as opposed to dilatometry successfully resolving
recovery closer to 1,000°C. The DSC data from these samples also indicate temperatures in the
600–800°C range. It is possible that the higher dose in the IMR11 capsule resulted in more significant
radiation-induced swelling of the Nb-1Zr specimen holders, which would have reduced the irradiation
temperatures (smaller gas gap for heat transfer). Together, these results highlight the challenges of using
dilatometry for irradiation temperatures approaching 1,000°C.

DSC was attempted on fragments from samples B14 and B15 as shown in Figure 29 and Figure 30. All
other DSC data were acquired using a standard Pt-20Rh sample holder, whereas the B14 and B15 samples
were acquired in an Al2O3 holder. This was done to avoid potential chemical interactions between Pt-
20Rh and SiC at temperatures above 1,000°C. Unfortunately, the Cp data from the SiC samples obtained
with the Al2O3 sample holders do not make sense, even at relatively low temperatures. For example, the
Cp data were negative during cooling of both samples, and the heating curves never dropped below the
cooling curves as would normally be expected during the release of stored energy from the irradiated
samples. Additional tests will need to be run to confirm the origins of the non-physical Cp data and
determine the best sample holder material for DSC runs at temperatures >1,000°C.

 5. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK

This work investigated DSC and Raman spectroscopy as alternative techniques to the conventional push-
rod dilatometer for inferring irradiation temperatures from passive SiC TMs. Existing samples irradiated
over a wide range of temperatures were sectioned into multiple pieces so that the same material (i.e., with
identical irradiation histories) could be evaluated using different techniques. In general, dilatometry was
able to consistently determine the irradiation temperature, as evidenced by the agreement across multiple
samples irradiated under the same or similar conditions as well as the high goodness-of-fit parameters
when fitting the data during cooling and the difference curves between heating and cooling. DSC also
resulted in good fits, but only when the sample mass was large enough (~70–100 mg). In addition, DSC
consistently over-predicted irradiation temperatures when a higher temperature ramp rate of 10°C/min
was used.

The following topics should be pursued based on the results of this work:

• Determine the optimum temperature ramp rates for dilatometry, DSC, and (once annealing data are
 available) Raman spectroscopy

 26

• Determine a minimum sample size for DSC measurements to observe sufficient stored energy release

• Determine the best sample holder material for high-temperature (>1,200°C) DSC runs

• Develop better metrics for relating the onset of recovery (CTE, DSC, Raman shift, etc.) to the
 median, minimum, and maximum irradiation temperatures, potentially relating these to the goodness-
 of-fit parameters

• After establishing more detailed guidance for best practices, initiate a round-robin campaign across
 multiple organizations, ideally utilizing irradiated material from a well-instrumented irradiation
 experiment

• Engage with the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) to develop appropriate
 standards to capture lessons learned from this work and other related works and ultimately provide
 guidance on how best to infer irradiation temperature from passive SiC TMs
