CONTENT AND OUTCOME OF NON-PHARMACOLOGICAL REHABILITATION FOR WOMEN WITH TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY – A SCOPING REVIEW

Kajsa LIDSTRÖM HOLMQVIST, Marie MATÉRNE, Grahame SIMPSON, Jessica MASSEY, Thomas STRANDBERG

Abstract

AbstractObjectiveTo identify and map evidence on the content and outcomes of non-pharmacological rehabilitation for women with traumatic brain injury.DesignScoping review.Subjects/patientsWomen aged 18–65 years with traumatic brain injury of any severity.MethodsThe PubMed, CINAHL and PsycINFO databases were searched. Studies including individuals with nontraumatic brain injuries, text and opinion papers, conference abstracts, study protocols, and grey literature were excluded. Inclusion criteria were: either ≥ 50% of the sample were women, or outcome data distinguished women’s results from the rest of the sample. A data-extraction form was used. Extracted data included specific details concerning the participants, context, study methods, and key findings.Results41 studies with varying designs were included. None of the interventions described had a specific focus on women or women’s needs. Community or outpatient settings dominated. The interventions were grouped into 3 main categories with underlying sub-categories – Alternative and complementary interventions, Training to restore functions, and Skills training to enhance cognitive functioning – with the latter category containing the most studies.ConclusionThe interventions described targeted a wide range of consequences from traumatic brain injury but without a clear gender perspective. Current rehabilitation models need to be developed to also include such a perspective.Key words: brain injuries, female, gender, intervention
This article reviews the literature on rehabilitation interventions for women aged 18–65 years who have sustained a traumatic brain injury. Three databases were searched to find studies describing interventions. Only studies in which at least half the participants were women or the results for women were separated from those for men were included. Forty-one studies are described in the results, categorized based on target area: Alternative and complementary interventions, Training to restore functions, and Skills training to enhance cognitive functioning. The third category contained the most studies. The results show that none of the studies were specifically developed for or adapted to women’s needs. As women experience somewhat different symptoms and consequences in life from traumatic brain injury in comparison with men, this study emphasizes the clinical and research importance of developing and evaluating interventions with a gender perspective.

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of long-term disability and mortality worldwide and has been identified as a significant global health concern (). The severity of TBI can be classified as mild, moderate, or severe, with severe TBI indicating significant consequences in terms of long-term effects on motor, sensory, cognitive, communication, behavioural, and social factors. TBI rehabilitation is recommended to be delivered by a multidisciplinary team with a focus on functional recovery and psychosocial adjustment guided by person-centred goals (–). Approaches include attempts to restore functions (e.g., motor skills; coordination, and balance) or to train individuals to learn to use compensatory techniques such as assistive technology or metacognitive strategies (). Psychosocial adjustment encompasses the process of adapting to changes in identity, emotional well-being, and social roles following injury, with the aim of promoting participation in daily life activities and quality of life ().

In TBI rehabilitation, the influence of both sex and gender has been underappreciated where gender extends beyond biological sex and integrates the social, biological, cultural, and behavioural factors that shape rehabilitation processes and outcomes (). There are important reasons to apply a gendered perspective to neurorehabilitation in the context of current disparities between females and males across impairments, functional/psychosocial outcomes, and rehabilitation treatments (, ). The need to develop intervention strategies to address these disparities for women after acquired brain injury (ABI) has been identified as a global health issue across neurorehabilitation, as identified by both the World Stroke Organization () and the Lancet Neurology Commission in Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) (). In relation to TBI, there may be multiple factors that contribute to these disparities. For instance, sex-specific biological and social factors can affect both TBI rate and recovery (). For example, hormonal disturbances due to TBI in women have been suggested as an explanation for some differences in sex-related outcomes. Moving on from possible biological factors, another difference lies in the epidemiology of TBI, with population-based prevalence estimates showing that men are more than twice as likely to be affected (–). This may be another contributing factor to the paucity of studies conducted on women with TBI, which has led to an incomplete understanding of the impact of TBI in females (, ).

In addition to the variations in incidence rates, gender disparities in the causes of TBI have also been identified, with women being more likely than men to sustain TBI due to intimate partner violence (, ). Furthermore, symptom profiles can also vary by gender, with women reporting higher levels of anxiety, depression, and post-concussion symptoms after TBI. Women have also reported poorer outcomes than men even though the size of the differences differs depending on age and severity of injury with women of child-bearing age with mild TBI showing poorer outcomes (). Women also report high degrees of social isolation, loss of friendships, and difficulties in adjusting to a new sense of self while feeling not understood or trivialized ().

Given this context of gender-based disparities, and the call for intervention strategies to address such disparities, this scoping review aimed to identify and map evidence on the content and outcomes of non-pharmacological rehabilitation for women under 65 years of age with TBI, guided by the following research questions:

METHODS

This scoping review follows the methodological guidelines of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) (, ) and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses – Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (). The analytical process was supported by the Covidence software. The study is registered in Open Science Direct (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/QUY3T), and a study protocol has been published ().

Eligibility criteria were structured by participants, concept, and context, according to the recommendations for scoping reviews by Peters et al. ().

Eligibility criteria: participants

This scoping review included studies in which at least half of the sample were adult women (aged 18–65 years) who had sustained a TBI of any severity, or in which outcome data were reported by gender, distinguishing women’s results from the rest of the sample. Studies with a mixed sample including individuals with non-TBI were excluded.

Eligibility criteria: concept

Content of non-pharmacological rehabilitation interventions. This review considered studies that described the content of all types of non-pharmacological rehabilitation interventions delivered to women, except invasive treatments such as brain stimulation or acupuncture, treatment at emergency units, or interventions targeting secondary diagnoses such as depression.

Outcome of rehabilitation interventions. Relevant outcomes covered both quantitative and qualitative measures, including scores on measures of function or participation, quality of life, coping strategies or cognitive strategies, as well as key themes elicited from qualitative research.

Eligibility criteria: context

Studies in which participants received a rehabilitation intervention in hospital (inpatient and outpatient) or in community-based settings were considered. No geographical restriction was placed on included studies.

Types of sources

Peer-reviewed experimental and quasi-experimental study designs – including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), non-randomized controlled trials, pre–post studies, and interrupted time-series studies – were included, as well as cohort studies, case control studies, cross-sectional studies, and case reports. Qualitative studies with various designs were also included. Text and opinion papers, conference abstracts, grey literature, and study protocols were excluded.

Search strategy

Only studies in English were included, as most research is published in English. Studies published from the year 2000 onwards were included, as studies began more frequently to apply a gender perspective at the turn of the millennium.

The PubMed (PubMed), CINAHL (EBSCO), and PsycINFO (EBSCO) databases were searched using a search strategy developed in collaboration with research librarians, who also conducted all searches. An initial limited pilot search of PubMed was undertaken to ensure that relevant articles were included. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, as well as the index terms describing the articles, were used to develop a full search strategy for the 3 databases. This search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was applied for each included database. The main search was conducted in February 2024, and an updated search was completed in October 2025. Searches for each database are presented in Appendix S1.

Study selection

All identified studies were collated and uploaded into Covidence (), and duplicates were removed. Three independent reviewers (KLH, MM, TS) assessed the titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. One reviewer (KLH) screened all titles and abstracts, while the other 2 reviewers (MM, TS) screened half each. To determine whether most participants in a study were women/female and/or whether the study outcomes were reported separately by sex/gender, it was sometimes necessary to screen the methods and result sections as well. A few disagreements were resolved through joint discussion. Potentially relevant studies were read in full and assessed against the eligibility criteria using the same procedure as in the previous step. In studies selected for data extraction, manual searches of reference lists were conducted to identify additional studies of interest.

Data extraction

Data were extracted by 2 reviewers (KLH, TS) using a data-extraction form () adapted by the reviewers from Peters et al. (). Extracted data included specific details regarding the participants, context, study methods, and key findings relevant to the review questions. The data-extraction method was piloted on 10 articles before further use, and no further modifications of the extraction form were made. The 2 reviewers compared their data extractions and agreed on a joint extraction.

Data analysis and presentation

Information on authors, year, country of origin, setting, aim, type of studies, methods, population/severity of TBI, intervention descriptions, outcome measures, and quantitative and qualitative outcomes are presented in and . The content and outcome of each intervention were analysed and categorized according to intervention type. Many interventions included more than 1 type (e.g., both restorative and skills-directed content) and were categorized based on their primary target area or content. The authors jointly determined the primary focus of each study. Two authors (KLH, TS) performed an initial categorization, which was discussed and refined by all authors until consensus was reached. For case studies, only results applying to female participants are reported here. Studies on participants’ experiences of the rehabilitation content and outcome were very few and were summarized on a manifest level. In addition, such studies reported on experiences from interventions that were evaluated with quantitative outcomes in other included studies. The results from each intervention are therefore reported together, including both participants’ experiences and quantitative outcomes.

RESULTS

All in all, the 2 searches generated 10,633 references. After removing 6,205 duplicates, 4,428 titles/abstracts were screened, leaving 163 articles for full-text review, whereof 14 were from the updated search. Thirty-two articles, of which 9 were from the updated search, met the eligibility criteria, with an additional 9 identified through a manual search, resulting in 41 included articles. The study-selection process and reasons for the exclusion of studies at full text are reported in .

None of the identified studies had a primary purpose of examining rehabilitation interventions focused on the specific needs or challenges faced by women after TBI. In several single-participant case studies, the participant was a woman (n = 7) (), but the choice of a woman as the case was incidental to the primary purpose of the study. There was a trend in more recent studies to include more women and thus meet the eligibility criteria.

Studies from the United States dominated (n = 21) (28–49), followed by studies from Canada (n = 7) (27, 30, 50–54), and Australia (n = 5) (55–59). There were also 2 studies from India (, ), 2 from New Zealand (, ), 2 from the Netherlands (, ), and 1 each from Taiwan () and Turkey (). The design of the studies varied; case studies were most common (n = 12), followed by pre–post studies (n = 8), RCTs (n = 8), feasibility studies (n = 4), single-case experimental designs (SCED) (n = 3), and mixed-method designs (n = 3). The studies were conducted in several service contexts, with community settings (n = 17) and outpatient care (n = 19) being the most common. The target groups for the interventions represented a variety of TBI severities. provides details of the context aims, target groups within TBI, study designs, and methodologies.

The RCTs (n = 8) used 2 forms of control group: waitlist controls (, ) and active controls (, , , , , ). Of the RCTs using active controls, 3 studies used interventions that were considered “treatment as usual” for the target area in question (, , ), whereas 3 used study-specific control interventions (, , ). The study-specific control interventions had the same target area as the experimental intervention but differed partly in content and delivery: Esquenazi et al. () used a different walking support system and method of feedback; Bay et al. () compared their self-developed psychological lifestyle intervention focusing on stress management with a mindfulness intervention; and Raina et al. () compared their fatigue-directed intervention with controls receiving health education.

Content and outcome of interventions

The final categorization consisted of 3 main categories: Alternative and complementary interventions, Training to restore functions, and Skills training to enhance cognitive functioning. These main categories and their underlying sub-categories () are summarized below in narrative form. provides detailed extracted data on the individual interventions, specific outcome measures, and outcomes for each study.

Alternative and complementary interventions

Within this category, the sub-categories of yoga (, , , ) and mindfulness interventions (, ) were identified (see ). When specified, intervention providers were identified as yoga therapists, with a mix of one-to-one interventions (, , ) and group interventions (, , ). All interventions had a manual or predefined structure. The duration of both intervention types varied between 6 and 12 weeks, with 1 to 2 sessions each week.

Yoga. Of 4 yoga interventions, all followed a classic format with breathing exercises, yoga postures, and meditation or relaxation. Two also included a psychoeducational component. Of these 2, 1 was a group-based online intervention directed not only to TBI survivors but also to caregivers and clinicians. Among other topics, the psychoeducation included education on the brain, resilience, positive thinking, and gratitude (). The other intervention for TBI participants had a psychoeducational component aimed at cultivating resilience skills ().

The outcome measures covered a wide range of physical, behavioural, and cognitive functions, such as physical () capabilities, emotional regulation (, , ), and resilience (). Different quality of life questionnaires were also common (, ). Three papers used qualitative interview data alongside quantitative measures (, , ).

Mindfulness. Of the 2 mindfulness interventions, 1 followed a classic mindfulness structure (), while the other followed Kabat-Zinn’s mindfulness-based stress-reduction programme in conjunction with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, which includes group discussions (). Outcome measures evaluated depression (, ), stress (), and quality of life ().

Overall, the results from the yoga and mindfulness interventions suggested positive outcomes on women’s quality of life, with 2 studies reporting statistically significant improvements (, ). Positive outcomes in physical functions were also reported, whereas the results on improvement in psychological wellbeing were inconsistent. Interview data described positive outcomes from yoga interventions regarding women’s physical functions, cognitive functions and motivation to participate in activities in the community.

Training to restore functions

This category has two sub-categories: training to restore physical functions and training to restore cognitive functions.

Training to restore physical functions. Eleven papers focused on the restoration of physical functions (, , , , , , , , , , ), as outlined in . Two concerned the restoration of visual convergence (, ), whereas 9 described interventions to restore various aspects of physical functions important for mobility, such as balance, strength, and coordination (, , , , ); cardiovascular capacity (, ); walking ability (, , ); and motor dual-tasking (). The interventions were mostly provided by physiotherapists. One paper described a multidisciplinary intervention provided by a neuropsychologist, a physiotherapist, and a physician, with a focus on returning to physical activity after concussion ().

Both interventions focusing on visual convergence (, ) were progressive. Outcome measures assessed convergence and accommodation. One study showed decreased diplopia (), and the other showed statistically significant changes in all measures of vergence ().

All interventions targeting various aspects of mobility had a progressive approach, with increasingly challenging exercises. The exercises used included cervical spine, cardiovascular, static balance, dynamic balance (), ocular and vestibular rehabilitation with cervical management, somatosensory motor control and neck strengthening (), treadmill walking with a bodyweight support system or a robotic gait orthosis (), rock climbing (), structured walking (, ), and transport activities in closed and open environments (). Four interventions were delivered individually, whereas the rock-climbing intervention involved both individual and group formats (). A circuit-training programme (, ) addressed common impairments in cognition, balance, endurance, vision, vestibular function, and self-management. Sessions also included education on recovery-related topics (, ).

Four studies (, , , ) evaluated balance and/or gait/walking speed, along with measures of daily functioning or independence. Overall, the results showed improvements in balance and gait/walking speed: large effect sizes regarding symptom reduction were reported by Campbell et al. (), and statistically significant improvement in mobility was reported by Esquinazi et al. (). Two studies evaluating self-rated goal achievement in occupational performance and satisfaction showed clinically important improvements (, ). In the multidisciplinary intervention (), participants described their experiences of the treatment journey after concussion, including perceived active ingredients and the impact of the multidisciplinary approach.

Training to restore cognitive functions. Two case studies reported on interventions aimed at restoring attention () and memory (). Both interventions used a typical restorative approach involving a hierarchical structure with progressively harder exercises with many repetitions, which were delivered individually (, ). The study focusing on attention used manipulation and repetition of auditory and visual stimuli (), whereas the study focusing on memory used paper-and-pencil exercises and various music exercises, among others (). Both case studies utilized neuropsychological tests as outcome measures (, ). In the work by Banerjee et al. (), participants also self-evaluated depression, anger, and mood regulation; sleep disturbance; negative recurrent thoughts; retrograde memory; generalization of functions; and perception of “self”. Both studies showed statistically significant improvements in some or all neuropsychological tests, and Banerjee et al. () reported improvements in most of the participants’ self-evaluations.

Skills training to enhance cognitive functioning

Twenty-two papers described a variety of interventions aimed at enhancing cognitive functioning (, –, , , , , , , , , , , , –, –), as shown in . The papers generally stated a target area for the interventions but were often broad in approach, and some encompassed additional elements. One intervention was person-centred, with the client defining and prioritizing goal activities and the skills training tailored to manage those activities (). The interventions were categorized into sub-categories by main target area, as follows: interventions focusing on metacognitive skills training (n=9); skills training directed towards community participation (n=3); and skills training to build psychological resources (n=10).

All papers described a clear structure for the intervention, either in the form of a manual or through defined stages. Most were delivered face-to-face in individual (, , , , , , , –, , ) or group formats (, , , , , ) or using a combination of both (). A few were delivered online or via video (, ). Among studies that specified the professionals delivering the intervention, the following were listed: psychologist/neuropsychologist (, , , , ), occupational therapists (, , , , , ), and speech therapist ().

Below follows a description of the interventions by target areas.

Metacognitive skills training. Ten interventions focused on metacognitive skills training (, , , , , , , , , ), as indicated in , whether in a general sense (, , , ), directed to a specific activity (, ) or directed to 1 or more predefined executive functions (, ). Four studies employed a broad general approach, with 3 using a multi-context approach () focused on the generalization of metacognitive strategies to different activities and contexts (, , ). The fourth study focused on learning a generic metacognitive strategy (CO-OP) to perform any activity ().

One paper described an intervention aimed at improving social communication skills using metacognitive skills training and goal-based therapy in a mix of group and individual sessions (). Three interventions used group-based metacognitive skills training and cognitive behavioural techniques to improve emotional regulation (, , ). Two included training in structured problem-solving frameworks (, ), and 1 also incorporated training in attention and the use of external aids (). One study described a programme to improve prospective memory skills (), including metacognitive skills training for developing self-awareness of memory deficits, as well as using a cueing system and organizational strategies ().

Outcome measures varied widely between the different studies. Neuropsychological tests, questionnaires or tests regarding awareness, self-regulation, measures of goal achievement, and various measures of quality of life/well-being were commonly used. Due to the large variation in outcome measures, further information concerning the outcome measures of each study is provided in . Two RCT studies showed statistically significant improvements in problem-solving, among other outcomes (, ). In the case studies and pre–post studies, evaluations of functional performance showed positive outcomes (, , , ); this also applied to the majority of the neuropsychological tests used, regardless of study design (, , , , , ). Outcomes regarding reintegration (, ), participation and quality of life () showed no changes.

Skills training directed towards community participation. Three interventions were aimed at enhancing community participation by targeting either specific activities (, ) or engagement in a broader sense (). In a case study, Imhoff et al. () described an intervention in a car-driving simulator with automated feedback. Tate et al. () used a person-centred approach in a feasibility study, with the client being supported to identify and prioritize 3 meaningful activities, upon which the intervention then focused. Sessions included feedback on performance and strategies to “troubleshoot” barriers ().

In an SCED study, Kersey et al. () described a manualized community-based group intervention to improve social participation. The main content involved building a participation plan with strategies for participation and identifying barriers and supports ().

Regarding the driving simulation (), the participants’ speeding duration decreased but returned to the initial duration after feedback was removed. Time spent in an incorrect lateral position decreased. For the intervention supporting meaningful activities (), the results were insufficient to meet Goal-Attainment Scaling (GAS) criteria for a clinically meaningful standard of performance, but reliable changes were reported in leisure, community integration, quality of life, self-esteem, and improved mood. The intervention on social participation () showed clinically meaningful improvements in all measures of participation. provides details of outcomes and outcome measures used.

Skills training to build psychological resources. Nine interventions focused on developing coping skills that targeted areas impacting life situation as a whole (, , , , , , , , ). The target areas were resilience (), advocacy (), psychological flexibility using acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) (, ), fear avoidance (, ), negative behaviours related to anosognosia (), and fatigue management (). Many interventions were manual-based and combined treatment sessions with home assignments. Most had elements of psychoeducation and strategy use; when specified, intervention providers were identified as trained psychologists. The intervention targeting anosognosia () focused on behavioural therapy techniques using strategies, feedback, and reflexion in a collaborative and supporting therapeutic alliance.

The advocacy and fatigue-management interventions were group-based (, ). The ACT intervention (, ) and the fear-avoidance interventions (, ) were adaptations of manual-based interventions originally developed for non-TBI groups.

To evaluate outcomes, a broad range of outcome measures were used, ranging from neuropsychological tests and questionnaires to interviews on participants’ experiences. In summary, the RCT studies showed positive results. The intervention targeting resilience showed a clinically significant improvement in symptoms (), and the intervention targeting advocacy increased advocacy behaviour significantly (). The SCED studies on the fear-avoidance intervention showed an increase in satisfaction with daily life and a decrease in avoidance behaviours (, ). The case study evaluating the anosognosia interventions showed a decrease in targeted behaviours ().

The ACT intervention was found to be feasible to implement in community-based rehabilitation, and participants’ experiences were generally positive (, ). The interventions targeting fatigue management were also found to be feasible for the target group ().

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge this is the first review undertaken to identify and map evidence regarding the content and outcomes of non-pharmacological rehabilitation for women under the age of 65 with TBI. An overall and notable finding is that none of the identified studies specifically addressed women’s needs. Even though earlier research (, , ) had reported gender-based differences after TBI, no studies explicitly focused on women or aimed to examine a specific gender perspective. It was more by chance that some case studies involved a woman, as no rationale for including a woman in these studies was described. Therefore, we can assume that studies focusing on women’s specific rehabilitation needs after TBI are rare and that interventions targeting women need to be developed, studied, and trialled in rehabilitation settings for the future. However, it should be emphasized that women after TBI clearly have many of the same needs and challenges as men after TBI, and women participants were still benefiting from interventions improving cognitive efficacy, mobility, and so forth.

To address gender disparities Lian et al. () state that TBI rehabilitation models need to go beyond injury-related impairments and include clinical assessments “that acknowledge disrupted gender roles, responsibilities, relationships, and identities after TBI” (p. E173). In our results, most of the studies had a focus on injury-related impairments even though we could see a trend toward a more holistic view in some categories. The outcome measures used in the included studies did not reflect a gendered perspective, possibly indicating a lack of gender awareness in existing rehabilitation models.

One way to address the specific needs of women is to apply a gender lens to person-centred approaches in which the woman is involved in the whole process through goal setting, intervention, and evaluation of outcome. In this review, 1 intervention described a person-centred approach with the client defining and prioritizing goal activities that she considered meaningful and important, and the skills training was tailored to manage those activities (). Another 4 studies (–, ) used the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM) () for goal-setting and evaluation of outcome. The COPM is a person-centred interview-based measure and it is promising that it is used. However, it does not ensure that the intervention is person-centred and targets the person’s specific goals, nor does it ensure that the interview includes a gender perspective. In all, 5 interventions out of 41 in this study described a person-centred approach to some extent. This support research stating that person-centredness is not fully implemented in TBI rehabilitation (, ). Besides obstacles described in research (, ), a person-centred approach requires staff with knowledge of gender differences in outcomes after TBI. This is crucial to be able to ask gender-relevant questions during the goal-setting process, as the women themselves cannot be expected to spontaneously identify these. Considering the scarcity of research on understanding gender issues in women with TBI (, ), more knowledge is needed to help rehabilitation staff support gender-differentiated rehabilitation.

In our results, studies published in recent years included more women in their samples than previously. This may indicate an increased and more gender-specific interest in studies of rehabilitation after TBI, which is consistent with other research in this area (). However, to improve rehabilitation for women, clinical research is needed that not only includes women but also separates and compares gender-specific patterns across the lifespan rather than just outcomes (). Such research approaches may lead to improved support and treatment for both women and men after TBI.

Furthermore, compared with other categories in our results more studies have recently been published within the sub-category Skills training to build psychological resources, indicating increased interest in a relatively holistic approach to rehabilitation after TBI. Overall, the main category, Skills training to enhance cognitive functioning, which included the sub-category above, contained more recent studies than the categories that focused on restoration of functions. One implication may be that interventions focusing on managing difficulties in everyday life after TBI – such as through coping mechanisms and managing psychological effects after injury – are gaining increased attention in rehabilitation. Other researchers have reported a similar emphasis (, ). Overall, these results may indicate an increased interest in sex and gender differences and needs in rehabilitation interventions after TBI.

Given the interest in the management of psychological effects after injury, it is worth noting the lack of studies addressing interventions for women’s TBI after intimate partner violence. Earlier research has shown that women are more often affected by TBI due to intimate partner violence than men and that the numbers of affected women are undercounted (). Such women present a complex medical picture, where traumatic experiences with conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) co-vary with the effects of the TBI. Therefore, it is essential to develop and evaluate interventions to address the needs of this sub-group of women.

Earlier research on women’s experiences of living with TBI in a broader sense shows different experiences compared with men (, ) and one of our research questions was about women’s experiences of received rehabilitation. Among the broader group of qualitative investigations into the lived experience of women with TBI, there was little exploration of women’s experiences past treatment. According to guidelines on the evaluation of complex health interventions, studies of participant experiences should be part of evaluating interventions (), such as those used in the area of rehabilitation, and can identify important gaps and areas for improvement. Future research studies with a focus on participant experiences are needed and can contribute to a deepened understanding of the gendered related impacts of rehabilitation interventions.

Methodological considerations

The aim of this study was to describe interventions specifically for women; however, no such studies were found in the initial searches, and most studies had a clear bias where most participants were men. Therefore, we adjusted the eligibility criteria and accepted studies in which women comprised at least half of the sample or in which women’s results could be extracted. This approach is open to discussion, and a counterargument may be that this approach made it difficult to be conclusive about effects regarding women. Our decision was based on the assumption that, if at least half of the participants were women, then both men’s and women’s perspectives would be reflected, and the study would provide information regarding the applicability of the interventions for women.

There are several possible ways to categorize interventions, and this was a challenging task in this study, as the interventions covered a wide range of target areas and content. We chose to use a conventional approach, with a main categorization based on either restorative or compensatory content. This categorization was complicated by the fact that many interventions had elements of both. Other methods of categorization would certainly have been possible, but we consider that the chosen classification provides a useful overview.

The aim of scoping reviews is explorative and does not include a quality appraisal of included studies (). However, it is notable that a large proportion of the included studies in this study were case studies contributing with low evidence; only 8 were RCT studies. This result highlights the need for studies with larger samples and improved designs and does also indicate that research on TBI rehabilitation in general, and regarding women specifically, needs further development.

In conclusion, the studies included in this scoping review covered a wide range of interventions from which women with TBI seem to benefit. However, the results show a lack of interventions developed for or adapted to women’s needs specifically. Further, a focus on injury-related impairments seemed to dominate the content, even though there was a trend toward a more holistic approach in recent studies. The outcome measures used did not clearly reflect a gender perspective and were mostly evaluating on impairment level rather than on activity and participation level. A possible conclusion could be that current rehabilitation models need to be developed to also include a gender perspective. To achieve this, an important clinical implication is that rehabilitation staff of all professions need to have knowledge of sex and gender differences to meet the individual needs of the person and tailor the interventions accordingly. In addition, to support and secure a gender perspective in rehabilitation, outcome measures capturing such perspectives and differences need to be developed. Further, outcomes have to a larger extent to be evaluated on activity and participation level to better capture gender perspectives.
