Dimension-Independent Convergence of Underdamped Langevin Monte Carlo in KL Divergence
Shiyuan Zhang Qiwei Di Xuheng Li Quanquan Gu

Abstract
Underdamped Langevin dynamics (ULD) is a widely-used sampler for Gibbs distributions π ∝ e − V \pi\propto e^{-V} , and is often empirically effective in high dimensions. However, existing non-asymptotic convergence guarantees for discretized ULD typically scale polynomially with the ambient dimension d d , leading to vacuous bounds when d d is large. The main known dimension-free result concerns the randomized midpoint discretization in Wasserstein-2 distance ( 43 ) , while dimension-independent guarantees for ULD discretizations in KL divergence have remained open. We close this gap by proving the first dimension-free KL divergence bounds for discretized ULD. Our analysis refines the KL local error framework ( 1 ) to a dimension-free setting and yields bounds that depend on tr ⁡ ( 𝐇 ) \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{H}) , where 𝐇 \mathbf{H} upper bounds the Hessian of V V , rather than on d d . As a consequence, we obtain improved iteration complexity for underdamped Langevin Monte Carlo relative to overdamped Langevin methods in regimes where tr ⁡ ( 𝐇 ) ≪ d \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{H})\ll d .

The classical
overdamped
Langevin diffusion (OLD)

\displaystyle{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{X}_{t}^{\mathrm{OLD}}=-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t}^{\mathrm{OLD}})+\sqrt{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{t}

(1.1)

converges to
\pi
under mild conditions, and its Euler–Maruyama discretization yields the Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) algorithm.
Motivated by Hamiltonian dynamics, the
underdamped
Langevin diffusion (ULD) augments the state with a momentum variable and evolves on phase space:

\displaystyle{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{P}_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{P}_{t}=-\gamma\bm{P}_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{t},

(1.2)

where
\gamma>0
is the friction parameter.
ULD has invariant distribution
\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\propto\exp\big(-V(\mathbf{x})-\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}/2\big)
, so its
\mathbf{x}
-marginal is the target
\pi(\mathbf{x})
.
Discretizations of ULD (collectively, ULMC) are empirically competitive and can provably improve iteration complexity over overdamped methods in several regimes (see Table
1
).

A key limitation of existing non-asymptotic theory is that many convergence bounds for Langevin discretizations scale polynomially in the ambient dimension
d
.
Such dimension dependence can be pessimistic in high-dimensional applications where the geometry of
V
is effectively low-dimensional (e.g., ridge-separable
(
43
)
).
Recent work has shown that, in some cases, the relevant complexity is governed by spectral quantities of a Hessian upper bound
\mathbf{H}\succeq\nabla^{2}V
, such as
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})
, leading to dimension-free guarantees for specific overdamped and Wasserstein-based underdamped schemes
(
33
;
43
)
.
However,
dimension-independent guarantees for discretized underdamped Langevin in KL divergence
have remained open. Notably, in the strongly log-concave setting, KL convergence is strictly stronger than convergence in Wasserstein distance or total variation, since it implies Wasserstein convergence via Talagrand’s
T_{2}
inequality (see, e.g., Section 1.4 in
16
) and total variation convergence via Pinsker’s inequality.

This paper resolves the above question by establishing the first
dimension-free
KL convergence rates for ULMC discretizations.
Concretely, we show that both standard ULMC and the randomized midpoint discretization (RMD) admit KL iteration complexities depending on
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})
rather than
d
.
Our main contributions are:

•

In the
\alpha
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth setting, we establish non-asymptotic convergence bounds in KL divergence for both standard ULMC and the randomized midpoint discretization. The resulting iteration complexity depends on
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})
rather than explicitly on the ambient dimension
d
. In the strongly convex setting, our KL guarantee further implies convergence in Wasserstein distance via Talagrand’s inequality, and we show that the resulting rate enjoys a strictly better dependence on the condition number
\kappa
than that of
43
.

•

In the general convex setting (
\alpha=0
), prior work does not provide any
dimension-free
convergence rates for Langevin dynamics. In this work, we establish the first dimension-free KL convergence guarantees for ULMC and the randomized midpoint discretization (RMD), with complexity governed by
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})
instead of
d
. Moreover, the dimension-free convergence rate of RMD is
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{3})
, matching the state-of-the-art rate
(
1
)
in this setting.

•

Technically, our improvement stems from two ideas (i) bounding the strong and weak local errors in a manner compatible with
\mathbf{H}
-weighted norms and (ii) controlling the change-of-measure terms without introducing explicit dimension dependence via crude Gaussian moment bounds. These two ingredients allow us to close a strictly tighter error recursion and ultimately yield the first dimension-free KL guarantees for underdamped Langevin discretizations.

Dimension-free Sample Complexity.
The line of work on dimension-free sampling complexity originates from the connection between Langevin dynamics and optimization. The convergence guarantees of first-order optimization methods typically do not depend explicitly on the ambient dimension
d

(
52
)
. In contrast, the convergence rates of Langevin-based sampling algorithms often exhibit an explicit dependence on
d
, stemming from the presence of isotropic Gaussian noise in the sampling dynamics. To bridge this gap,
33
proved a dimension-free convergence rate for overdamped Langevin dynamics in two settings. When
V
is
\alpha
-strongly convex and
L
-Lipschitz, they proved a dimension-free sample complexity of
\Theta(L^{2}/(\alpha^{2}\epsilon^{2}))
in Wasserstein distance. When
V
is
\alpha
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth, it characterized the sample complexity via an upper bound
\mathbf{H}
of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}V
, with sample complexity
\Theta(\kappa^{2}\beta^{-1}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})/\epsilon^{2})
in KL divergence. In particular, when
V
has a ridge separable structure with mild conditions, the sample complexity is independent of the dimension. Under the same setting and notation,
43
analyzed underdamped Langevin sampling with a doubly randomized algorithm and showed that a sample complexity of order
\Theta\big(\kappa[\beta^{-1}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})]^{1/3}\epsilon^{-2/3}\big)
is sufficient in Wasserstein distance. However, the corresponding sample complexity for underdamped Langevin dynamics in the KL divergence remains unexplored.

Shifted Composition.

4
proposed a KL local error framework that reduces the problem of establishing tight convergence bounds for sampling algorithms to the verification of local assumptions. This framework was first developed for overdamped Langevin dynamics in
4
and was later extended to the underdamped setting in
1

2
and
3

66
, where an improved cross-regularity analysis was developed, leading to faster convergence rates for a deterministic double midpoint method under higher-order differentiable assumptions.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the ULMC discretization methods, including the standard ULMC and the randomized midpoint discretization (RMD,

3.1
Underdamped Langevin Monte Carlo

The practical application of underdamped Langevin dynamics (ULD) requires the construction of a discrete-time sampling algorithm, named Underdamped Langevin Monte Carlo (ULMC). It produces a Markov chain
\{\mathbf{X}_{nh}^{\mathtt{Alg}},\mathbf{P}_{nh}^{\mathtt{Alg}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
through the use of an appropriate numerical discretization scheme. In this paper, we focus on two discretization methods, standard ULMC and the randomized midpoint, both of which are motivated by the equivalent integral representation of the ULD (
1.2
) given below:

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{0}+(1-e^{-\gamma t})/\gamma\cdot\bm{P}_{0}+\bm{\xi}_{0,t}^{(1)}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s}){\,\mathrm{d}}s,}

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t}\bm{P}_{0}+\bm{\xi}_{0,t}^{(2)}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s}){\,\mathrm{d}}s,}

(3.1)

where the random processes
\bm{\xi}_{s,t}^{(1)}
and
\bm{\xi}_{s,t}^{(2)}
are given by the Itô integral

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{s,t}^{(1)}\coloneqq\sqrt{2\gamma}\int_{s}^{s+t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(s+t-u)}}{\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{u},\qquad\bm{\xi}_{s,t}^{(2)}\coloneqq\sqrt{2\gamma}\int_{s}^{s+t}e^{-\gamma(s+t-u)}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{u}.

The discretization methods are essentially approximations of the intractable
integral terms
in (
3.1
).

Standard ULMC.

The standard ULMC is arguably the simplest discretization method. With a given step size
h
, ULMC approximates
\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})
in (
1.2
) with
\nabla V(\bm{X}^{\ULMC}_{nh})
for
t\in[nh,(n+1)h)
.
The
integral terms
in (
3.1
) thus have closed-form solutions with
\nabla V(\bm{X}_{nh}^{\ULMC})
being a constant vector. Therefore, the standard ULMC proceeds with the following iterations:

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{(n+1)h}^{\ULMC}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{nh}+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\bm{P}_{nh}^{\ULMC}+\bm{\xi}_{nh,h}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(h-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\Big)\nabla V\big(\bm{X}_{nh}^{\ULMC}\big),

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{(n+1)h}^{\ULMC}

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma h}\bm{P}_{nh}^{\ULMC}+\bm{\xi}_{nh,h}^{(2)}-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\nabla V\big(\bm{X}_{nh}^{\ULMC}\big).

(3.2)

Randomized Midpoint Discretization (RMD).

The randomized midpoint discretization, first proposed in
57
, aims to provide a more accurate estimation of the
integral terms
in (
3.1
) by replacing the integral with the expectation over a randomized stepsize. In this paper, we follow the doubly randomized implementation in
1
. In detail, let
\{(u_{n},v_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. random variables on
[0,1]^{2}
, independent of the Brownian motion, with distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(u_{n}\in A)

\displaystyle=\int_{A\cap[0,1]}\frac{h(1-e^{-\gamma(1-u)h})}{h-(1-e^{-\gamma h})/\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}u,\qquad\mathbb{P}(v_{n}\in A)=\int_{A\cap[0,1]}\frac{h\gamma e^{-\gamma(1-v)h}}{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

(3.3)

The numerical scheme aims to replace the first
integral term
with
[h-(1-e^{-\gamma h})/\gamma]\nabla V(\bm{X}_{(n+u_{n})h})/\gamma
. Observe that the expectation over
u_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big({h-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}}\Big)\mathbb{E}_{u_{n}}[\nabla V(\bm{X}_{(n+u_{n})h})]={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\int_{nh}^{(n+1)h}\frac{e^{-\gamma((n+1)h-s)}}{\gamma}\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s}){\,\mathrm{d}}s.}

Thus, the expectation above is an unbiased estimation of the first
integral term
. A similar property holds for the integral term in the equation of the momentum. However, the “randomized midpoint” vectors
\bm{X}_{(n+u_{n})h}
and
\bm{X}_{(n+v_{n})h}
are unavailable in general. Instead, the numerical scheme approximates them with auxiliary vectors
\widehat{\bm{X}}^{+}_{(n+u_{n})h}
and
\widehat{\bm{X}}^{++}_{(n+v_{n})h}
, respectively, both obtained using standard ULMC starting from
(\bm{X}_{nh}^{\RM},\bm{P}_{nh}^{\RM})
. In summary, the randomized midpoint discretization calculates

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{X}}^{+}_{(n+u_{n})h}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{nh}^{\RM}+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\bm{P}_{nh}^{\RM}+\bm{\xi}_{nh,u_{n}h}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(u_{n}h-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma u_{n}h}}{\gamma}\Big)\nabla V\big(\bm{X}_{nh}^{\RM}\big),

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{X}}^{++}_{(n+v_{n})h}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{nh}^{\RM}+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\bm{P}_{nh}^{\RM}+\bm{\xi}_{nh,v_{n}h}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(v_{n}h-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma v_{n}h}}{\gamma}\Big)\nabla V\big(\bm{X}_{nh}^{\RM}\big),

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{(n+1)h}^{\RM}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{nh}^{\RM}+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\bm{P}_{nh}^{\RM}+\bm{\xi}_{nh,h}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(h-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\Big)\nabla V\big(\widehat{\bm{X}}_{(n+u_{n})h}^{+}\big),

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{(n+1)h}^{\RM}

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma h}\bm{P}_{nh}^{\RM}+\bm{\xi}_{nh,h}^{(2)}-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\nabla V\big(\widehat{\bm{X}}_{(n+v_{n})h}^{++}\big).

(3.4)

We make the following assumptions on the convexity and smoothness of the function

Assumption 3.1
.

The function
V(\cdot)
is twice-differentiable, and
\beta

\displaystyle\alpha\mathbf{I}\preceq\nabla^{2}V\preceq\mathbf{H}\preceq\beta\mathbf{I},

In prior work

\alpha>0

\alpha>0
) and the general convex case (
\alpha=0
).

We focus on the Underdamped Langevin Dynamics (ULD), which evolves in the phase plane
(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})
of the displacement
\bm{X}_{t}
and the momentum
\bm{P}_{t}
.

Error of one-step discretization.

We first introduce the notation for the one-step discretization error, distinguishing between two types: weak and strong errors. This terminology is adopted from the classical theory of weak and strong convergence in numerical analysis (see, e.g., Section 9 in
40
).

Definition 3.2
.

Suppose that the initial conditions of the ULD and the numerical discretization method
\mathtt{Alg}
are
(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{P}_{0})=(\bm{X}_{0}^{\mathtt{Alg}},\bm{P}_{0}^{\mathtt{Alg}})=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
.
The one-step weak error
\mathcal{E}^{w}
and strong error
\mathcal{E}^{s}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\coloneqq h^{-1}\|\mathbb{E}\bm{X}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}}-\mathbb{E}\bm{X}_{h}\|\vee\|\mathbb{E}\bm{P}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}}-\mathbb{E}\bm{P}_{h}\|;

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{s}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\coloneqq h^{-1}\|\bm{X}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}}-\bm{X}_{h}\|_{L_{2}}\vee\|\bm{P}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}}-\bm{P}_{h}\|_{L_{2}}.

Using this definition,
1
proposed a KL local error framework, which characterizes the convergence of discretization methods with their one-step discretization errors, including the weak and strong errors. Furthermore, we need the following one-step cross-regularity condition.

Let
(\mathcal{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the Markov semigroup associated with the underdamped Langevin (
1.2
), and let
\mathcal{P}_{h}
be its time-
h
transition operator. Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}}
denote the Markov transition kernel induced by one step of the numerical integrator with step size
h
. Then
(\bm{X}_{h},\bm{P}_{h})
and
(\bm{X}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}},\bm{P}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}})
satisfy

\displaystyle(\bm{X}_{h},\bm{P}_{h})\sim\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h},\quad(\bm{X}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}},\bm{P}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}})\sim\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}}.

The cross-regularity condition characterizes the divergence of two transition kernels
\mathcal{P}_{h}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{h}
starting from different initial conditions:

Definition 3.3
.

Transition kernels
\mathcal{P}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy the
cross-regularity condition
with function
b(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
if for any initial conditions
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}),(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, the distributions
\delta_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{p}}}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
and
\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}
satisfy

\displaystyle\text{KL}\big(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{h}\|\delta_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{p}}}\mathcal{P}_{h}\big)\lesssim\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}}{\gamma h^{3}}+\frac{\|\mathbf{p}-\bar{\mathbf{p}}\|^{2}}{\gamma h}+b^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}).

Once the one-step errors of discretization methods are obtained, the KL local error framework provides the convergence rate in a plug-and-play way, for which we will provide a comprehensive guideline in Section

3.3
KL local error framework

In this section, we give a detailed description of the KL local error framework proposed in

.
We consider the ULD chain

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{n}|\bm{\psi}_{n-1}\sim\delta_{\bm{\psi}_{n-1}}\mathcal{P}_{h},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,N,

\bm{\psi}_{0}=(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{P}_{0})\sim\nu

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{n}^{\mathtt{Alg}}|\bm{\psi}_{n-1}^{\mathtt{Alg}}\sim\delta_{\bm{\psi}_{n-1}^{\mathtt{Alg}}}\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathtt{Alg}},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,N,

with initial condition
\bm{\psi}_{0}^{\mathtt{Alg}}=(\bm{X}_{0}^{\mathtt{Alg}},\bm{P}_{0}^{\mathtt{Alg}})\sim\mu

At the center of the framework is the

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\eta^{\mathbf{x}},\eta^{\mathbf{p}}}(\bm{\psi},\widehat{\bm{\psi}})=\big(\bm{X},\bm{P}+\eta^{\mathbf{x}}(\bm{X}-\widehat{\bm{X}})+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}(\bm{P}-\widehat{\bm{P}})\big).

Using the shifted operator, with a target process

\bm{\psi}^{\text{aux}}

\bm{\psi}^{{\text{sh}}}
iteratively:

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{{\text{aux}}}_{n}|\bm{\psi}_{n-1}^{{\text{sh}}}\sim\delta_{\bm{\psi}_{n-1}^{{\text{sh}}}}\mathcal{P}_{h},

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{\text{sh}}_{n}={\mathcal{T}}_{\eta^{\mathbf{x}}_{n},\eta_{n}^{\mathbf{p}}}(\bm{\psi}_{n}^{\text{aux}},\bm{\psi}^{\text{target}}_{n})=\big(\bm{X}_{n}^{\text{aux}},\bm{P}_{n}^{\text{aux}}+\eta_{n}^{\mathbf{x}}(\bm{X}_{n}^{\text{target}}-\bm{X}_{n}^{\text{aux}})+\eta_{n}^{\mathbf{p}}(\bm{P}_{n}^{\text{target}}-\bm{P}_{n}^{\text{aux}})\big),

with initial condition
\bm{\psi}_{0}^{\text{sh}}=(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{P}_{0})\sim\nu

To apply the KL local error framework described in Section

Lemma 4.1

Applying similar considerations, we derive the dimension-free version of the cross-regularity assumption for ULMC below.

Lemma 4.2

For

\mathcal{P}

6
Overview of Proof

In this section, we use the analysis of RMD in the strongly convex setting (Theorem
5.2
) to illustrate the proof strategy for our dimension-free results. Let
\pi
be the invariant distribution of ULD (
1.2
). Using Corollary
3.6
with
\nu=\pi
, we can bound the KL divergence in Theorem
5.2
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{KL}\big(\mu(\mathcal{P}^{\mathtt{Alg}})^{N-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\|\pi\big)\lesssim C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle\quad+A_{w}\big(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{w}\big)^{2}+A_{s}\big(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{s}\big)^{2}+\bar{b}^{2}.

(6.1)

Since the Wasserstein term is small when
Nh
is large, we focus on the remaining terms. Substituting Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
4.2
into (
6.1
), we have

\displaystyle A_{w}\big(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{w}\big)^{2}+A_{s}\big(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{s}\big)^{2}+\bar{b}^{2}\lesssim\beta h^{4}\log\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha h}\Big)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle+\beta^{1/2}h^{3}\log\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha h}\Big)\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{i}^{\text{aux}}}\big[\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle+\beta^{3/2}h^{5}\log\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha h}\Big)\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{i}^{\text{aux}}}\big[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\big]\Big].

(6.2)

To continue, we require the following dimension-free change-of-measure lemma, which allows us to control the state-dependent terms in the recursion without introducing any explicit dimension dependence.

Lemma 6.1

(Change-of-measure, dimension-free)
.

Consider a measure
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
-\beta\mathbf{I}\preceq\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x})\preceq\mathbf{H}\preceq\beta\mathbf{I}
. With
\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\propto\exp(-V(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2})
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+\beta\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{p}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+\beta\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi).

Remark 6.2
.

For the inequality regarding
\nabla V(\mathbf{x})
, we apply Donsker-Varadhan’s variational formula to get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]}\lesssim{\beta}\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi)

\displaystyle\qquad+\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\exp\big(\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}/(4\beta)\big)\Big].

A direct analysis of the logarithmic moment generating function on the right-hand side leads to an explicit dependence on the dimension
d
, which is not dimension-free. To overcome this issue, we instead apply a Taylor expansion of the exponential and bound the expectation of each order separately. This refined analysis yields a tighter bound that depends only on
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})
.

For the inequality with regard to the momentum term, note that under the invariant distribution
\pi
, the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{p}
is Gaussian, implying
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}]\simeq d
. This observation is crucial: it demonstrates that if we do not introduce the
\mathbf{H}
-matrix norm in the local error analysis (Lemma
5.1
), the final complexity bound would remain dimension-dependent regardless of remaining analysis. This underscores our modification, using the
\mathbf{H}
-norm in the momentum error analysis.

Using Lemma
E.1
, we can convert the maximum expectation over auxiliary processes before step
n
into a term involving
\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\text{KL}(\nu_{i}^{\text{aux}}\|\pi)
. Thus, (
6.2
) becomes

\displaystyle A_{w}\big(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{w}\big)^{2}+A_{s}\big(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{s}\big)^{2}+\bar{b}^{2}\lesssim\beta h^{4}\log\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha h}\Big)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle\qquad+\beta^{3/2}h^{3}\log\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha h}\Big)\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\text{KL}(\nu_{i}^{\text{aux}}\|\pi)\Big].

(6.3)

Finally, we can apply Theorem
3.5
for any
n\leq N-1
to obtain

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\text{KL}\big(\nu^{\text{aux}}_{n}\|\pi\big)\lesssim C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\pi)+\beta h^{4}\log\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha h}\Big)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle\qquad+\beta^{3/2}h^{3}\log\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha h}\Big)\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\text{KL}(\nu_{n}^{\text{aux}}\|\pi).

Substituting into (
6.3
), we obtain the final KL bound for Theorem
5.2
. We conclude by choosing
h
small enough so that the KL divergence is at most
\epsilon^{2}
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we establish the first dimension-free KL convergence guarantees for discretizations of underdamped Langevin dynamics. Our bounds depend on
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})
, where
\mathbf{H}
is an upper bound on the Hessian
\nabla^{2}V
, rather than on the ambient dimension
d
, yielding improved rates in regimes where
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})\ll d
. We show that both standard ULMC and the randomized midpoint discretization (RMD) enjoy dimension-free KL convergence, and our results cover both the strongly convex and the general convex settings.

Appendix C
Analysis of Cross Regularity Condition

In this section, we are going to prove the dimension-free version of the cross-regularity condition for the standard ULMC, similar to Lemma 4.2 in
1
. We first define the Rényi divergence as follows:

Definition C.1
.

Consider two measures
\mu
and
\nu
,
q>1
,
R_{q}
is the Rényi divergence, defined by:

\displaystyle R_{q}(\mu||\nu)=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\int\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Big)^{q}\mathrm{d}\nu.

In the limit of
q\to 1^{+}
, the Rényi divergence will reduce to KL divergence:

\displaystyle R_{1}(\mu||\nu)=\int\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Big)\mathrm{d}\nu=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Big[\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Big].

Here are the basic useful propositions of Rényi divergence.

Lemma C.2

(Data processing inequality)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
,
and let
T:\Omega\to E
be any measurable map.
Then for any Rényi order
q\in[1,\infty]
,

R_{q}(T_{\#}\mu\,\|\,T_{\#}\nu)\;\leq\;R_{q}(\mu\,\|\,\nu).

Lemma C.3

(Weak triangle inequality for Rényi divergence)
.

For any probability measures
\mu,\nu,\pi
, any Rényi order
q\in[1,\infty)
,
and any relaxation parameter
\lambda\in(0,1)
, it holds that

\displaystyle R_{q}(\mu\,\|\,\pi)\;\leq\;\frac{q-\lambda}{q-1}\,R_{q/\lambda}(\mu\,\|\,\nu)\;+\;R_{(q-\lambda)/(1-\lambda)}(\nu\,\|\,\pi).

(C.1)

In particular, by setting
\lambda=1-\varepsilon
and
q=1+\varepsilon
and letting

\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, Proposition
C.3
yields the classical
bound for the Kullback–Leibler divergence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\pi)\leq 2\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu)+\log\big(1+\chi^{2}(\nu\|\pi)\big).

(C.2)

We then present the Girsanov Theorem:

Theorem C.4

(Girsanov)
.

Let
(\bm{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a standard Brownian motion under the Wiener measure
\mathbb{W}
and let
(\bm{Y}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a progressive process with
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{W}}\int_{0}^{T}\|\bm{Y}_{s}\|^{2}ds<\infty
. Let
\bm{M}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\langle\bm{Y}_{s},{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{s}\rangle
for
t\in[0,T]
and let
[\bm{M},\bm{M}]=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\|\bm{Y}_{s}\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s
denote the quadratic variation. Define the exponential martingale

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{M}):=\exp\Big(\bm{M}-\frac{1}{2}[\bm{M},\bm{M}]\Big).

Assume that
\mathcal{E}(\bm{M})
is a
\mathbb{W}
-martingale and define the measure
\mathbb{Q}
on path space via

\displaystyle\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{Q}}{{\,\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{W}}=\mathcal{E}(\bm{M})_{T}.

Then, under
\mathbb{Q}
,

\displaystyle t\mapsto\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{t}:=\bm{B}_{t}-[\bm{B},\bm{M}]_{t}=\bm{B}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\bm{Y}_{s}ds

is a standard Brownian motion.

Lemma C.5
.

Suppose
(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})
satisfies the following SDE for a function
\mathbf{b}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{P}_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t,\\
{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{P}_{t}=\mathbf{b}(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{t},\end{cases}

Another process
(\bm{X}_{t}^{\prime},\bm{P}_{t}^{\prime})
satisfies the following SDE for a function
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{X}^{\prime}_{t}=\bm{P}^{\prime}_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t,\\
{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{P}^{\prime}_{t}=\mathbf{b}^{\prime}(\bm{X}^{\prime}_{t},\bm{P}^{\prime}_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{t},\end{cases}

Moreover, define

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\coloneqq\mathrm{Law}\big((\bm{X}_{h},\bm{P}_{h})\big),\qquad\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathrm{Law}\big(({\bm{X}}^{\prime}_{h},{\bm{P}}_{h}^{\prime})\big).

The KL divergence between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\text{KL}\big(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\big)\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\bm{X}_{s}^{\prime},\bm{P}_{s}^{\prime})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg]

where
\bm{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
.

Proof of Lemma
C.5
.

Using Theorem
C.4
with
\bm{Y}_{t}=-\Delta(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})/\sqrt{2\gamma}
, then

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{t}=-\int_{0}^{t}\langle\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s}),{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{s}\rangle/\sqrt{2\gamma}

and
[\bm{M},\bm{M}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s/(2\gamma)
. Thus, suppose
\mathbb{Q}_{h}
is a distribution on the path space up to time
h
, which satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{Q}_{h}}{{\,\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{W}_{h}}=\mathcal{E}(\bm{M})_{h}:=\exp\Big(\bm{M}_{h}-\frac{1}{2}[\bm{M},\bm{M}]_{h}\Big).

Then we have under
\mathbb{Q}_{h}
,
{\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{t}:={\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{t}+\Delta(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t/\sqrt{2\gamma}
is a standard Brownian motion. Note that the equation of
(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})
satisfies:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{P}_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t,\\
{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{P}_{t}=\mathbf{b}^{\prime}(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{2\gamma}\big[[\Delta(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})/\sqrt{2\gamma}]{\,\mathrm{d}}t+{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{t}\big].\end{cases}

Thus, under
\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{t}
,
(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})
satisfies the same evolution equation as
(\bm{X}_{t}^{\prime},\bm{P}_{t}^{\prime})
. Using the data-processing inequality (Lemma
C.2
),

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\text{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{h}\|\mathbb{W}_{h})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\bigg[\log\exp\Big(\bm{M}_{h}-\frac{1}{2}[\bm{M},\bm{M}]_{h}\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\bm{M}_{h}-\frac{1}{2}[\bm{M},\bm{M}]_{h}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\bigg[-\int_{0}^{h}\frac{\langle\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s}),{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{s}\rangle}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\bigg[-\int_{0}^{h}\frac{\langle\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s}),{\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{s}\rangle}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg].

Since
{\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion under
\mathbb{Q}
, the first term is equal to 0. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime})\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\bigg[\frac{1}{4\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\bm{X}_{s},\bm{P}_{s})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg].

Moreover, under
\mathbb{Q}
,
(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})
has the same evolution equation as
(\bm{X}_{t}^{\prime},\bm{P}_{t}^{\prime})
, and thus holds the same distribution. We finally derive the inequality:

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\bm{X}_{s}^{\prime},\bm{P}_{s}^{\prime})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg].

∎

Cross-regularity term.

With the local discretization error controlled (cf. Lemma
C.5
and the bounds in Section
B
),
we now bound the cross-regularity term.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

We apply (
C.2
) to decompose the cross-regularity KL divergence into a local discretization term and a kernel-stability term:

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{h}\|\delta_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{p}}}\mathcal{P}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq 2\text{KL}(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{h}\|\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h})+R_{2}(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h}\|\delta_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{p}}}\mathcal{P}_{h})

(C.3)

where
\mathcal{P}_{h}
denotes the time-
h
transition kernel of ULD and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{h}
denotes the one-step ULMC kernel.
We bound the two terms on the right-hand side of (
C.3
) separately.
We first use Theorem
A.2
to bound the term
R_{2}(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h}\|\delta_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{p}}}\mathcal{P}_{h})
:

\displaystyle R_{2}(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h}\|\delta_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{p}}}\mathcal{P}_{h})\lesssim\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}}{h^{3}}+\frac{\|\mathbf{p}-\bar{\mathbf{p}}\|^{2}}{h}\Big).

Secondly, we bound the term
\text{KL}(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\prime}\|\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h})
. In Lemma
C.5
, we take

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\prime}(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{P}_{t})=-\gamma\bm{P}_{t}-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})\qquad\mathbf{b}(\bm{X}_{t}^{\ULMC},\bm{P}_{t}^{\ULMC})=-\gamma\bm{P}_{t}^{\ULMC}-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})

Then we obtain

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\prime}\|\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{P}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{h}\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h}{4\gamma}\sup_{t\in[0,h]}\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(\beta h^{3}\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}+\gamma h^{4}\,\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+\beta^{2}h^{5}\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\Big),

where the second inequality holds due to the Hölder inequality, and the last inequality holds due to Lemma
B.1
.

Proof of Lemma

Note that

\displaystyle\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x}+u(\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x})){\,\mathrm{d}}u\bigg](\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}^{1/2}\cdot\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x}+u(\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x})){\,\mathrm{d}}u\bigg]\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\cdot\mathbf{H}^{1/2}(\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x}),

so using the definition of the operator norm, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{H}^{1/2}\|\cdot\bigg\|\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x}+u(\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x})){\,\mathrm{d}}u\bigg]\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\bigg\|\cdot\|\mathbf{H}^{1/2}(\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\beta}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\cdot\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\|\cdot\|\mathbf{H}^{1/2}(\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x})\|=\sqrt{\beta}\|\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{H}},

where the second inequality holds because
\mathbf{H}\preceq\beta\mathbf{I}
and
\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{z})\preceq\mathbf{H}
for any
\mathbf{z}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})-\nabla V(\bm{x})\|^{2}\big]\leq\beta\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\big].

(D.1)

We then analyze the term
\|\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{H}}
. According to the integral form of ULD in (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{\gamma}\mathbf{p}+\bm{\xi}_{0,t}^{(1)}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s}){\,\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{\gamma}\mathbf{p}+\bm{\xi}_{0,t}^{(1)}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\cdot\nabla V(\mathbf{x})+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}[\nabla V(\mathbf{x})-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s})]{\,\mathrm{d}}s.

Therefore, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bm{X}_{t}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 4\Big(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{\gamma}\Big)^{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}+4\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bm{\xi}_{0,h}^{(1)}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\big]+4\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg)^{2}\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+4\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}[\nabla V(\mathbf{x})-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s})]{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\bigg].

Firstly, since
1-e^{-\gamma t}\leq\gamma t\leq\gamma h
, we have

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{\gamma}\Big)^{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\leq h^{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}.

Secondly, the term
\|\bm{\xi}_{0,h}^{(1)}\|_{\mathbf{H},L_{2}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bm{\xi}_{0,h}^{(1)}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=2\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bm{B}_{s}\bigg\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\gamma\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}\Big)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma h^{3}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H}),

(D.2)

where the second equality holds due to the Itô symmetry, and the first inequality holds because
1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\leq\gamma(t-s)\leq\gamma h

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}}s\leq\int_{0}^{t}(t-s){\,\mathrm{d}}s=\frac{t^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{h^{2}}{2},

(D.3)

where the first inequality holds because
1-e^{-z}\leq z
; we also have
\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\leq\beta\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}
because
\mathbf{H}\preceq\beta\mathbf{I}
. Finally, since

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}[\nabla V(\mathbf{x})-\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s})]{\,\mathrm{d}}s\bigg\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq t\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}}{\gamma}\Big)^{2}\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}s

\mathbf{H}\preceq\beta\mathbf{I}

D.3

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 4\beta h^{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}+8\beta\gamma h^{3}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+\beta^{2}h^{4}\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+4\beta^{2}t\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{s})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\big]{\,\mathrm{d}}s.

Using the Grönwall’s Inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla V(\bm{X}_{t})-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{\beta^{2}t^{4}}\big(\beta h^{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}+\beta\gamma t^{3}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+\beta^{2}t^{4}\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\big)

Therefore, as long as

where

Appendix E
Proof of change-of-measure lemma

Lemma E.1

(Change-of-measure, dimension-free)
.

Consider a measure
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
-\beta\mathbf{I}\preceq\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x})\preceq\mathbf{H}\preceq\beta\mathbf{I}
. With
\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\propto\exp(-V(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2})
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+\beta\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{p}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+\beta\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi).

Proof of Lemma
E.1
.

In the Donsker–Varadhan variational lemma (Lemma
I.3
), by setting
U(\mathbf{x})=\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}/(4\beta)
, we have,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]}{4\beta}\leq\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi)+\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\exp\big(\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}/(4\beta)\big)\Big]\leq\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi)+\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})}{2\beta},

where the second inequality holds due to Lemma
I.4
. Therefore, rearranging terms,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]\leq 2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+4\beta\cdot\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi)

Similarly, for the bound of
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}]
, using the Lemma
I.3
with
U(\mathbf{p})=\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}/(4\beta)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}]}{4\beta}\leq\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi)+\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\exp\big(\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}/(4\beta)\big)\Big]\leq\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi)+\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})}{2\beta},

where the second inequality holds due to Lemma
I.5
. Therefore, rearranging terms,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}]\leq 2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})+4\beta\cdot\text{KL}(\mu\|\pi).

∎

\displaystyle\qquad\bigg(1+\frac{\beta^{2}h}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})}\bigg)\frac{(L_{s,\mathbf{x}}+(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})L_{s,\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}}\bigg\}\lesssim 1-L_{n},

(H.5)

and

\displaystyle(L_{b,\mathbf{x}}+h^{-1}L_{b,\mathbf{p}})^{2}\gamma h^{3}\lesssim 1.

(H.6)

Assume
\beta h^{2}\lesssim 1
. Note that
\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}}\lesssim 1/h
, and

\displaystyle 1-L_{n}

\displaystyle=1-\exp\bigg(-c\int_{nh}^{(n+1)h}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{t}^{\mathbf{p}}){\,\mathrm{d}}t\bigg)

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{nh}^{(n+1)h}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{t}^{\mathbf{p}}){\,\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\simeq(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})h.

For the first term in (
H.5
), we can compute as

\displaystyle\frac{(L_{w,\mathbf{x}}+(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})L_{w,\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}h}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{(L_{w,\mathbf{x}})^{2}}{(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}h}+\frac{(L_{w,\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})h}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\beta^{6}h^{10}}{(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}h}+\frac{\beta^{4}h^{8}}{(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})h}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\beta^{4}h^{7}}{(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})},

where the last inequality holds due to
\gamma\simeq\sqrt{\beta}
and
\beta h^{2}\lesssim 1
. For the second term in (
H.5
), we have

\displaystyle\bigg(1+\frac{\beta^{2}h}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})}\bigg)\frac{(L_{s,\mathbf{x}}+(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})L_{s,\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\frac{(L_{s,\mathbf{x}}+(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})L_{s,\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}}+\frac{\beta^{2}h(L_{s,\mathbf{x}}+(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})L_{s,\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{4}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})},

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\frac{(L_{s,\mathbf{x}})^{2}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}}+(L_{s,\mathbf{p}})^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}h(L_{s,\mathbf{x}})^{2}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{4}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})}+\frac{\beta^{2}h(L_{s,\mathbf{p}})^{2}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\frac{\beta^{4}h^{6}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}}+\beta^{2}h^{4}+\frac{\beta^{6}h^{7}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{4}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})}+\frac{\beta^{4}h^{5}}{(\gamma+\eta^{\mathbf{p}}_{nh})^{2}(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\beta^{2}h^{4}+\frac{\beta^{3}h^{5}}{(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})},

where we use
\beta h^{2}\lesssim 1
and
\gamma\simeq\sqrt{\beta}
.
To show
(
H.5
)
, it suffices that

\displaystyle\max\bigg\{\frac{\beta^{4}h^{7}}{\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}}},\beta^{2}h^{4},\frac{\beta^{3}h^{5}}{\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}}}\bigg\}\lesssim(\omega_{+}+\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})h.

Strongly Convex.
In this case,
\omega\simeq\alpha/\sqrt{\beta}
. We only need

\displaystyle\beta^{3}h^{4}\lesssim\alpha^{2}/\beta,\beta^{2}h^{3}\lesssim\alpha/\sqrt{\beta}.

The solution to this is

\displaystyle h\lesssim\alpha^{1/2}\beta^{-1}=1/(\beta^{1/2}\kappa^{1/2}),h\lesssim 1/(\beta^{7/6}\kappa^{1/3}).

The condition in this case suffices that

\displaystyle h\lesssim 1/(\beta^{7/6}\kappa^{1/2}).

(H.7)

General Convex.
In this case,
\omega=0
. We need

\displaystyle\beta^{2}h^{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}},\beta^{3}h^{4}\lesssim(\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}})^{2}.

Recall the definition of

\displaystyle\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}}=\frac{c_{0}\omega}{\exp(\omega(Nh-nh+Ah))-1}.

It takes the minimum when
n=0
. Thus, we have
\eta_{nh}^{\mathbf{p}}\gtrsim 1/(Nh)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\beta^{2}h^{3}\lesssim\frac{1}{Nh},\;\beta^{3}h^{4}\lesssim\frac{1}{N^{2}h^{2}},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle h\lesssim N^{-1/4}\beta^{-1/2}\ \land\ N^{-1/3}\beta^{-1/2}.

(H.8)

Appendix I
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma I.1

(Talagrand’s
T_{2}
inequality)
.

Let
\pi(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp(-V(\mathbf{x}))
. Suppose
V
is
\alpha
-strongly convex for
\alpha>0
. Then for any distribution
\mu
, the Wasserstein 2-distance can be bounded by the KL divergence, satisfying

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\pi)\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\text{KL}(\mu,\pi).

Lemma I.2

(Stein’s Identity)
.

Let
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function, and
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a differentiable vector field. Let the distribution
\pi
be defined as
\pi\propto\exp(-V)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sim\pi}[\langle\nabla V,\mathbf{F}\rangle]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}].

Lemma I.3

(Donsker-Varadhan’s variational formula)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},P_{0})
be a probability space and
U(x)
be a measurable function. Then for any distribution
P
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[U(x)]+\text{KL}(P||P_{0})\geq-\log\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{0}}\exp(-U(x)),

and the infimum is attained when
P(x)\propto P_{0}(x)\exp(-U(x))
.

Lemma I.4
.

Let
\lambda
be a scalar such that
0<\lambda\leq 1/(4\beta)
. Then the norm of
\nabla V
satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\exp(\lambda\|\nabla V\|^{2})]\leq 2\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H}).

Proof of Lemma
I.4
.

Due to the Taylor expansion,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\exp(\lambda\|\nabla V\|^{2})]
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\exp(\lambda\|\nabla V\|^{2})]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|\nabla V\|^{2k}]}{k!},

(I.1)

so it suffices to bound
M_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|\nabla V\|^{2k}]
. For
k\geq 1
, we call the Stein’s identity (Lemma
I.2
) with
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2k-2}\nabla V(\mathbf{x})
and obtain

\displaystyle M_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[\langle\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2k-2}\nabla V(\mathbf{x}),\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\rangle\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\nabla\cdot\Big(\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2k-2}\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=(2k-2)\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2k-4}\langle\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla V(\mathbf{x}),\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\rangle\Big]}_{I_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2k-2}\cdot\Delta V(\mathbf{x})\Big]}_{I_{2}}.

(I.2)

For the term
I_{1}
, when
k\geq 2
, note that
\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x})\preceq\beta\mathbf{I}
due to the
\beta
-smoothness of
V
, so

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla V(\mathbf{x}),\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\rangle\leq\beta\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}.

Therefore, the upper bound of
I_{1}
is

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq\beta\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2k-2}]=\beta M_{k-1}.

(I.3)

For the term
I_{2}
, since
\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x})\preceq\mathbf{H}
, we have
\Delta V(\mathbf{x})=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{x}))\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})
, so the upper bound of
I_{2}
is

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2k-2}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})\Big]=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})M_{k-1}.

(I.4)

Plugging (
I.3
) and (
I.4
) into (
I.2
), we have

\displaystyle M_{k}\leq[(2k-2)\beta+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})]M_{k-1}.

(I.5)

Since
M_{0}=1
, by recursively using (
I.5
), we have

\displaystyle M_{k}\leq\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}[2j\beta+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})]=(2\beta)^{k}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\Big[j+\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})}{2\beta}\Big].

(I.6)

Plugging (
I.6
) into (
I.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\exp(\lambda\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2})]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\beta\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\Big[j+\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})}{2\beta}\Big].

(I.7)

We make the crucial observation that when
z\in(0,1)
the Taylor expansion of
(1-z)^{-s}
is

\displaystyle(1-z)^{-s}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(j+s).

Setting
z=2\beta\lambda
and
s=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})/(2\beta)
, under the condition
\beta\lambda<1/4
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\exp(\lambda\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2})]\leq(1-2\beta\lambda)^{-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})/2\beta}.

Taking the logarithm on both sides, we have

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\exp(\lambda\|\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\|^{2})]\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H})}{2\beta}\log\big(1/(1-2\beta\lambda)\big)\leq 2\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H}).

where the last inequality holds because
\log(1/(1-z))\leq 2z
for
0<z\leq 1/2
.

∎

Lemma I.5
.

Suppose that
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\sim\pi
, i.e.,
\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},\mathbf{I})
. Then for
\lambda<1/(4\beta)
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2})]\leq 2\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H}).

Proof of Lemma
I.5
.

Let the eigenvalue decomposition of
\mathbf{H}
be

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top},

then
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
can be written as

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}q_{i}^{2},\quad\text{where}\quad q_{i}=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{p}.

Note that
\{q_{i}\}
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables because
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, for
\lambda\leq 1/(4\beta)
, we have

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2})]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\mu_{i}q_{i}^{2})]=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log(1-2\lambda\mu_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}2\lambda\mu_{i}=2\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{H}).

where the second equality holds because
\mathbb{E}[\exp(zq_{i}^{2})]=(1-2z)^{-1/2}
for
z<1/2
, and the inequality holds because
\log(1/(1-z))\leq 2z
for
z\in(0,1/2]
.
∎
