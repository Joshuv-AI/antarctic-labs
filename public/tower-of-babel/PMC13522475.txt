Evaluating hygienic practices in cattle slaughterhouses and beef retail shops in Sidama region, Ethiopia: implications for public health
Authors: Ashenafi Syoum, Tarikua Asrat, Teshager Dubie, Aklilu Asmelash, Gebremedhin Gebrezgabiher, Fanuel Bizuayehu Yihunie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Foodborne diseases are a major global health problem driven by gaps in regulatory enforcement, resources, and food handler education. This study aimed to assess hygienic practices among slaughterhouse and retail shop workers in Hawassa city, Sidama, Ethiopia. A cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2022 to June 2023, involving 190 participants (40 abattoir workers and 150 retail shop workers) using structured questionnaires and direct observation. Significant hygiene lapses were observed, including the absence of hot water baths for cleaning knives, lack of handwashing sinks, and insufficient use of protective gear in abattoirs. In retail shops, risky behaviors such as displaying uncovered meat, lack of cooling facilities, and improper sanitation practices were prevalent. Only 45% of abattoir workers and 21% of retail shop workers demonstrated good meat handling practices. Notably, food safety training (AOR = 5.03, 95% CI = 1.09–23.22, P = 0.038) was significantly associated with better hygiene practices among abattoir workers. Among retail shop workers, both food safety training (AOR = 3.96, 95% CI = 1.30–12.07, P = 0.016) and higher educational level (AOR = 5.42, 95% CI = 1.62–18.11, P = 0.008) were significantly associated with better hygiene practices. The study concludes that inadequate education and limited training are key contributors to poor meat handling practices. It recommends the implementation of comprehensive training programs, ongoing education, and regular monitoring to improve compliance with food safety regulations and reduce the risk of foodborne diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57493-3.

Introduction

Foodborne diseases remain a significant global public health challenge, affecting both developed and developing nations. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), contaminated food contributes to approximately 600 million illnesses, 420,000 deaths, and 33 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) annually worldwide1. These staggering figures underscore the critical need for stringent food safety measures, particularly in meat handling, where improper practices can lead to the transmission of pathogens2. In developing countries, the risk is exacerbated by inadequate infrastructure, limited regulatory enforcement, and low awareness among food handlers3.

Ethiopia boasts the largest livestock population in Africa, with 70 million cattle, making the livestock sector a cornerstone of economy4. It contributes approximately 20% to the national Gross Domestic Product (GDP), 40% to the agricultural GDP, and 20% to foreign exchange earnings5. Despite this, the potential of meat industry remains untapped due to persistent challenges in hygiene and safety standards. Over 300 local abattoirs operate across the country, yet many lack basic sanitary facilities, such as hot water baths, handwashing sinks, and proper disposal systems6. This deficiency not only compromises meat quality but also poses severe health risks to consumers, especially given the cultural preference for raw or undercooked meat dishes like “Kitfo” and “Kurt”2,7.

Meat hygiene is particularly critical at two key points in the supply chain as slaughterhouses and retail shops8–10. Slaughterhouses serve as the first line of defense against contamination, yet studies reveal widespread unhygienic practices, including the use of poor quality water sources, unclean tools and inadequate handwashing3,9,11,12. Retail shops further amplify these risks by displaying meat uncovered, using non-food-grade cutting boards, and lacking refrigeration9,12. Existing studies in cities such as Mekelle and Jigjiga highlight gaps in worker training and education, but regional variations necessitate localized investigations13,14.

While previous research in Sidama region has identified significant public health risks, including a high prevalence of zoonotic pathogens such as Salmonella and extended-spectrum β-lactamase producing Escherichia coli in raw cattle meat, a critical gap remains in understanding the hygiene continuum from production to point-of-sale15. Current evidence highlights that microbial contamination on contact surfaces like knives and chopping boards often exceeds safety limits due to poor handling16, yet most studies have focused narrowly on either the retail environment or specific bacterial strains rather than the integrated chain of slaughterhouses and retail shops. Furthermore, there is a lack of comprehensive data identifying the specific socio-demographic and environmental factors that drive these inadequate hygienic practices in the Hawassa context. Without a systematic assessment that links the hygiene status of slaughterhouses with retail environments and investigates the underlying factors influencing these behaviors, it remains difficult to develop evidence-based interventions to reduce the microbial burden and the subsequent risk of foodborne zoonoses in the region16,17. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) determine the proportion of abattoir workers and beef retail shop workers in Hawassa city demonstrating good hygienic meat handling practices (2) identify sociodemographic factors independently associated with practice level in each setting; and (3) document facility-level hygiene conditions at slaughterhouses and retail shops through direct observation, in order to contextualize self-reported practice data. The findings are intended to inform targeted, evidence-based interventions to improve meat safety, reduce the burden of foodborne illness, and strengthen public health outcomes in rapidly urbanizing areas of Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

This study was conducted in Hawassa, Sidama, Ethiopia. Hawassa is the capital of the Sidama Region in southern Ethiopia. It lies on the shores of Lake Hawassa in the Great Rift Valley, approximately 275 km south of Addis Ababa. The city, with an elevation of 1,708 m above sea level, experiences a tropical climate with an average annual rainfall of approximately 1,200 mm and temperatures ranging from 15 °C to 25 °C (Fig. 1). As of 2023, Hawassa’s population is estimated to be approximately 577,075, making it one of the fastest-growing urban centers in Ethiopia. The city’s economy is driven by industries such as textiles, food processing, and tourism, while agriculture remains a significant sector, with many residents engaged in subsistence farming and livestock rearing16.

Study design and study population

A cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2022 to June 2023 in slaughterhouses and meat retail shops in Hawassa city. Participants included abattoir workers in meat inspection, supervision, slaughtering, cleaning and transportation, and meat handlers with direct contact with meat and meat handling surfaces in retail shops.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Respondents were eligible if they (1) held a valid health certificate issued by the relevant municipal authority, (2) had been working as a meat handler for at least 6 months at the time of data collection, and (3) were aged 18 years or older.

Exclusion criteria

Workers who were unable to communicate adequately to complete the interview, or who were under 18 years of age, were excluded.

Study variables and measurements

Dependent variable

The level of hygienic meat handling practice (categorized as good or poor) was the dependent variable of this study. A score of ≥ 70% on the hygiene practice questionnaire was classified as good practice and a score of < 70% was classified as poor practice.

Independent variables

Sociodemographic Factors: age of meat handlers was categorized as 18–29 and ≥ 30 years, education status was grouped as Illiterate, Primary school, and High school & above, service duration (≤ 5 years and > 5 years), and food safety training (yes/no). Sex (all respondents were male) were constants and therefore excluded from inferential analyses.

Operational definition

Hygienic practices

The practical application of sanitary measures and food handling procedures by meat handlers to ensure the safety of meat.

Good hygienic practice

Participants who correctly performed or reported 70% or more of the recommended hygiene tasks were categorized as having “good” practices14.

Poor hygienic practice

Participants who scored below the 70% threshold were categorized as having “poor” practices14.

Abattoir workers

Individuals directly involved in the slaughtering, flaying, and primary processing of carcasses within the slaughterhouse facility.

Retail meat workers (butchers)

Individuals involved in the portioning, handling, and sale of beef to consumers in retail shops.

Food safety training

Any formal training on meat hygiene or food safety delivered by a recognized provider (government, NGO, or vocational institute) that the respondent reported attending; recorded as Yes if the respondent attended at least one formal session in the past 5 years, No otherwise.

Health certificate

A medical fitness certificate issued by the local health authority permitting the holder to work in food handling; recorded as Yes if the participant presented a valid certificate within the last 6 months.

Data collection

A survey combining structured face to face interviews and direct observation was conducted to assess meat hygiene and handling practices in slaughterhouses and beef retail shops. The structured questionnaire and observational checklist were adapted from a previously validated instrument used in a similar Ethiopian setting20. The questionnaire was originally prepared in English, translated into Amharic and Sidama, and back-translated into English to verify semantic equivalence, and pretested on five respondents from non-study sites prior to full data collection. The questionnaire covered themes including sociodemographic characteristics, observational checklists and structured interview questions (Supplementary material).

Data were collected by two trained data collectors fluent in both Amharic and Sidama, who completed a standardization session before fieldwork to ensure consistent application of the checklist across sites. Data collection followed a sequential two-phase design at each site. In the first phase, interviews were conducted when workers were not yet engaged in active meat handling before the midday trading period at retail shops and before the start of slaughtering operations at the abattoir. In the second phase, direct observation was conducted during peak activity periods between 12:00 PM and 1:00 PM at retail shops and during active slaughtering operations at the abattoir without a specific announcement marking the transition between phases. Workers were not specifically notified that the observation phase had commenced, in order to capture routine practices under real working conditions. All data collection was conducted during a non-fasting period in the Ethiopian Orthodox Christian calendar to ensure observations reflected typical operating conditions.

Data management and analysis

The study’s raw data was entered, coded, organized, and validated using Microsoft Excel 2019 before being imported for analysis in SPSS 23 and STATA 17 (only for Firth’s penalized logistic regression) statistical software. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study participants, with all categorical variables expressed as frequencies and percentages. A multi-collinearity test was carried out to see the correlation between independent variables using co-linearity statistics (variance inflation factor (VIF)) and the mean of VIF was less than 5 among independent variables. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (where p > 0.05 indicated adequate fit). The chi-square (χ2) test (Fisher’s exact test, when expected cell counts were < 5) and a binary logistic regression model was used to test the association between the outcome and the explanatory variables. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the association between hygienic practice level and independent variables among retail shop workers. For the abattoir group, standard maximum likelihood logistic regression was considered inappropriate due to the small sample size (n = 40) and low events-per-variable ratio (EPV = 6, below the recommended minimum of 10), which can produce biased coefficient estimates and unstable confidence intervals. Firth’s penalized logistic regression was therefore applied for the abattoir analysis to minimize small-sample bias and reduce the risk of estimate instability. For both groups, variables with a bivariable P-value of less than 0.25 were eligible for inclusion in the multivariable model, and statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05. The significant association was declared at a p-value less than 0.05 in multivariable analysis. The questionnaire’s section had 18 and 20 questions to assess the practices of slaughterhouse and retail shop workers, respectively, with two possible responses: “yes” and “no”. Each correct practice reported a score of one (1) point. For evaluation, a score ≥ 70% indicated that food handlers practiced ≥ 13 and ≥ 14 out of 18 and 20 hygienic practices in slaughterhouse and retail shop workers, respectively, which are listed in the questions and were considered “good” food hygienic practices. The 70% cutoff was adapted from similar regional studies14.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee of Samara University, College of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Science on 10 November 2022 (SU/IRC/0104/2022). The ethics approval covered all data collection activities conducted in this study, including structured face-to-face interviews and direct observation of workplace practices and facilities. Institutional permission to conduct the study was obtained from the Hawassa municipal abattoir management and the relevant city administration authority prior to data collection. The study participants were informed about the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant, with confidentiality maintained through secure record-keeping and the omission of personal identifiers.

Factors associated with the practice level of the respondents

Chi-square analysis revealed a statistically significant association (P < 0.05) between hygienic practice levels and both education status and food safety training (Table 3). Among abattoir workers, those with high school education or above demonstrated a notably higher proportion of good hygienic practices (75.0%) compared to illiterate workers (42.9%). Similarly, workers who had received food safety training showed markedly better compliance (76.9%) relative to their untrained counterparts (29.6%). However, age and work experience were not significantly associated with the practice level of the respondents (P > 0.05).

Beef handling practices at retail shops

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 150 food handlers working in beef retail shops were surveyed, as summarized in Table 5. All respondents (100%) were male, with the majority (66.7%) aged over 30 years. The majority of the food handlers had no formal education (39.3%) and had completed at least primary school (34.7%). 60% of the food handlers had more than five years of work experience. Only 26 (17.3%) of all the food handlers in the study had received food hygiene training, and all the meat handlers had undergone medical checkups in the last six months. The majority of the respondents at retail shops had no food safety training experience and had poor hygienic practices (86.3%).

Discussion

This study reveals a critical public health gap, the majority of meat handlers in both settings demonstrated poor food safety hygiene practices, with only 45% (95% CI: 32.5–67.2) of abattoir workers and 21% (95% CI: 14.7–26.8) of retail shop workers meeting the threshold for good practice. The lower good hygienic practices (45%) in the present study is consistent with findings of Gutema et al.20 in Bishoftu and Tegegne and Phyo14 in Jijiga, Ethiopia, who reported relatively similar result. Khanal and Poudel21 reported that 52.6% of butchers in Ratnanagar Municipality, Nepal, had good meat safety practices, while Matchawe et al.22 in Cameroon found 37.2%. In contrast, Abdullahi et al.23 in Malaysia reported 77.7% good practices among abattoir workers, likely reflecting stronger regulatory enforcement, higher baseline education, and more developed food safety infrastructure. At the lower end, Miner et al.24 in Nigeria reported only 8.6% good practices, a figure that underscores the spectrum of conditions across low-income settings. These irregularities might be due to variations in sample size, cutoff points, and the sociodemographic conditions of the study subjects. This reflects the situation in many developing countries including Ethiopia, where inadequate worker awareness and insufficient basic infrastructure collectively undermine adherence to food hygiene standards.

Among retail shop workers, only 21% demonstrated good hygienic practices, substantially lower than that observed in the slaughterhouse. This difference may partly reflect the more structured operational environment of the municipal abattoir, where supervisory staff are present and at least some regulatory oversight is applied, compared with the dispersed and largely unregulated retail sector. A similar proportion of good practices was reported by Gebeyehu and Tsegaye25 in Ethiopia (25.7%) and by Ashuro et al.26 in the Gedeo Zone, Ethiopia (25.1%), while Al Banna et al.27 in Bangladesh reported 16.3%, suggesting a consistent pattern of poor retail meat hygiene across diverse low-income settings. However, it was much lower than the findings reported by Yenealem et al.28(66.4%) in Gondar, Teferi29, (51.3%) in North Shewa Zone, Ethiopia, and Bhandari et al.30 in Nepal; these higher figures likely reflect the different scoring cutoffs, inclusion criteria, and study populations employed in those investigations31.

Direct observation of the Hawassa municipal abattoir revealed several critical infrastructure deficiencies, including the absence of entrance disinfection, no hot water baths for knife sterilization, no handwashing sinks at workstations, all slaughter processes conducted at ambient room temperature, and a complete absence of gloves and head covers among workers. These findings reported across geographically distinct Ethiopian cities over more than a decade; Haileselassie et al.13 reported similar gaps in Mekelle municipal abattoir, where the facility was described as below standard with no cooling rooms or modern stunning equipment, and Gutema et al.20 in Bishoftu likewise documented inadequate handwashing facilities and absence of cold chains. This indicates that the problems are systemic, arising from chronic underfunding of municipal infrastructure and insufficient regulatory enforcement, rather than from isolated management failures. These deficiencies violate the requirements of both the Ethiopian Meat Inspection Proclamation No. 274/197032 and the Codex Alimentarius Code of Hygienic Practice for Meat (CXC 58-2005)33, the latter of which mandates handwashing facilities at all workstations, hot water or steam for equipment sterilization, and temperature-controlled post-slaughter processing environments. Specifically, the absence of any cooling or chilling capacity at the abattoir with Hawassa average ambient temperature ranges from 15 °C to 25 °C, that favor the rapid multiplication of major pathogens including Salmonella spp., E. coli O157:H7, and Staphylococcus aureus, posing a significant foodborne disease risk to consumers16.

In retail shops, deficiencies were even more widespread. All retail shops (100%) displayed meat uncovered and without any cooling facility in display cabinets. The majority used non-food-grade plastic or wooden cutting boards (83.3%); 98% had no hot water bath for knife sanitation; all (100%) transported carcasses in vehicles lacking cooling systems; and all shops used borehole water rather than treated municipal water for washing meat and equipment. No workers at any retail shop used gloves. These observations mirror those of Gutema et al.20 in Bishoftu, who similarly identified lack of cold chain, non-food-grade cutting surfaces, and inadequate handwashing as key risk factors in retail shops across Ethiopia. The use of wooden or plastic cutting boards is risky because these porous materials are difficult to sanitize and act as reservoirs for pathogens like Shigella and Staphylococcus aureus species34. The reliance on untreated borehole water is especially concerning, as groundwater sources may carry fecal coliforms and enteric pathogens absent from treated municipal supplies35. Lack of cold chain and non-refrigerated vehicles for transport ensures that any contamination acquired at the abattoir is amplified before reaching the consumer36. Under the Ethiopian Public Health Proclamation No. 200/200037, raw animal products are required to be stored between 1 °C and 4 °C; compliance with this requirement was zero across all retail shops surveyed in the present study.

A failure of personal protective equipment (PPE) use was observed across both the abattoir and retail shops. No abattoir and two retail shop workers wore a mask during operations. Glove use was equally absent in both settings. Gutema et al.20 in Bishoftu similarly reported that no workers in slaughterhouses or retail shops wore gloves. The Codex Alimentarius Code of Hygienic Practice for Meat (CXC 58-2005)33 on personal hygiene for food handlers require that workers in meat processing and retail use appropriate respiratory and hand protection to prevent direct contamination of exposed meat surfaces from droplets, aerosols, and skin contact. The combined absence of mask use and poor handwashing practices is particularly concerning in Ethiopia due to their cultural preference for raw or minimally cooked beef dishes such as “Kitfo” and “Kurt”, where no post-purchase cooking step eliminates pathogens deposited during handling2,7.

No worker in either the abattoir or any retail shop used a chemical sanitizer when washing service utensils, knives, hooks, or cutting boards. This finding represents complete non-compliance with one of the most fundamental requirements of the clean-rinse-sanitize sequence prescribed by international meat hygiene standards. The FAO Good Practices for the Meat Industry38 and the Codex CXC 58-200533 specify that all meat-contact surfaces must be cleaned with detergent, rinsed with potable water, and then sanitized with an approved food-contact disinfectant at appropriate concentrations. Rinsing with water alone removes visible debris but is insufficient for eliminating bacterial pathogens, particularly in the nicks and grooves of cutting boards and knife blades where biofilms can accumulate39. Although 65% of abattoir workers reported washing their hands before and after meat handling, only 69.2% of those workers used soap. In retail shops, the problem was more severe only 16.7% of workers reported washing hands before and after touching meat, and of these, only 15.3% used soap. Handwashing with water alone is recognized as largely ineffective for removing lipid-enveloped viruses and bacterial pathogens from hand surfaces; soap is required to emulsify fats and mechanically dislodge microorganisms40. The Codex General Principles of Food Hygiene (CXC 1-1969, revised 2020)41 and the Ethiopian Public Health Proclamation No. 200/200037 both explicitly require that food handlers wash hands with soap at defined critical points. Handwashing non-compliance in the abattoir was largely infrastructure-driven, as the complete absence of sinks on the slaughter floor made compliance physically impossible regardless of worker motivation. In contrast, in retail shops where 80% of premises had functional handwashing sinks, the same non-compliance among 83.3% of workers reflects a purely behavioral gap, requiring different interventions: capital investment in abattoir infrastructure and behavioral training with supervisory enforcement in retail shops.

In retail shops, 90% of workers collected money while simultaneously handling or selling raw meat without any intervening handwashing. Currency is a well-documented fomite surface carrying diverse microbial communities, such as fecal coliforms and Staphylococcus aureus, reflecting the many handling environments through which money passes42. In a context where baseline handwashing rates are already very low, the introduction of a high-contamination fomite into the meat-handling sequence substantially amplifies the risk of pathogen transfer to retail meat. In the abattoir, almost half of workers reported eating or drinking during their shift in the workplace which is prohibited practice under both the Meat Inspection Proclamation and Codex CXC 1-1969 creating a bidirectional contamination risk between work surfaces and food consumed on site.

In the abattoir, 47.5% of workers reported sinking knives in hot water after each activity, yet direct observation confirmed a complete absence of hot water bath facilities in the slaughter hall. The absence of hot water baths as a physical infrastructure gap also means that the reported use of hot water for knife sterilization was structurally impossible. These responses almost certainly reflect social desirability bias of respondent’s tendency to report idealized rather than actual behaviors when they know that the correct answer is expected. The systematic review by Insfran-Rivarola et al.43 found that effect sizes for self-reported practices are higher than those for directly observed practices, confirming that self-report overestimates compliance in food safety research. Future studies should maximize the proportion of behavioral assessment conducted through direct, unannounced observation at multiple time points to minimize this bias. In retail shops, all workers reported performing routine fly control as standard practice, yet direct observation found that all shops displayed meat openly without any cover in the display cabinet. Effective fly control is practically unachievable over uncovered meat displays, as open surfaces are accessible to flies regardless of other pest control measures. This divergence suggests that workers’ understanding of fly control may refer to occasional reactive measures using manual shooing rather than structural prevention. The other critical and paradoxical finding is that all workers in both settings possessed valid health certificates, yet the majority demonstrated poor hygienic practices. In Ethiopia, health certificates are issued through medical examination rather than demonstrated competency in food hygiene, functioning as administrative documents rather than evidence of food safety knowledge. This gap represents an important policy failure, a regulatory system in which 100% of workers are certified but only 21% retail shops and 45% abattoir workers practice well is not effectively protecting public health.

Food safety training was the significant determinant in both settings, while higher educational attainment was additionally significant among retail shop workers. Workers who had received food safety training were approximately five and four times more likely to demonstrate good practices than those who had not in abattoir and retail shops, respectively. This finding aligns with the results reported by Al Banna et al.27 and Ashuro et al.26, with odds of 8.98 in Bangladesh and 2.2 in the Gedeo Zone, respectively. The reason is that meat handlers gain awareness of proper meat hygiene practices through their training in this area. Additionally, meat handlers in retail shops who completed high school and above had 5.229 times more good practices than illiterate individuals did. These findings are consistent with those from Gedeo Zone, Ashuro et al.26, Nepal, Bhandari et al.30 and North Shewa Zone, Teferi29, with odds ratios of 2.58, 2.8 and 5.5, respectively. These consistent findings across diverse settings suggest that higher educational attainment improves worker’s capacity to read and internalize written food safety guidelines, comprehend the biological mechanisms underlying contamination and respond to corrective feedback from supervisors.

Limitations of this study

Our findings must be interpreted with the following limitations in mind. First, the abattoir sample was small, and although Firth’s penalized regression was used to address this, the wide confidence intervals suggest that abattoir findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger studies. Second, Self-reported data may overestimate compliance due to social desirability bias, as workers tend to report ideal rather than actual behaviors, a concern supported by the contradictions observed between interview responses and direct observation in this study. Third, the direct observations were conducted during a specific period, which might not capture variations in practices during different times of the day, week, or season. Finally, no microbiological sampling of meat or contact surfaces was conducted alongside the behavioral assessment.

Conclusion

This study provides an understanding of the variability of practices related to hygienic meat practices among slaughterhouse and retail shop workers in Hawassa city. This study revealed that majority of the meat handlers had poor food safety hygiene practices in both settings, with only 45% of abattoir workers and 21% of retail shop workers meeting the threshold for good practice. Food safety training was significantly associated with better practices in both settings, while higher educational attainment was additionally associated with good practices among retail shop workers. Additionally, they do not use personal protective equipment when meat is handled and working for a long time at abattoirs, and retail shops negatively affect the food safety practices of workers. Therefore, to improve food safety hygiene practices among meat handlers, targeted and mandatory food safety training programs should be delivered to all meat handlers in both settings; educational opportunities should be strengthened for workers with low literacy; handwashing infrastructure must be installed at workstation level in slaughterhouses; and the provision and use of basic personal protective equipment including gloves and masks should be enforced in both abattoirs and retail shops.
