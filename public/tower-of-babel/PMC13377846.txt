Prospective payment system transformation (2000–2024): temporal trends, payment architecture, and cross-national variation
Authors: Anahita Behzadi, Zohreh Bagherinezhad, Maliheh Ghobadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProspective Payment Systems (PPS) have been widely adopted over the past two decades as instruments for cost containment and efficiency improvement in health systems. However, much of the existing literature focuses on individual payment arrangements or single-country experiences, with limited attention to broader temporal, structural, and regional patterns reported across PPS-related reforms. This study examines patterns reported in the PPS-related literature published between 2000 and 2024, with particular emphasis on temporal trends, payment architecture, and regional variation in reform approaches.MethodsThis study constitutes a secondary narrative analysis of 168 studies included in a prior systematic review. No additional searches were conducted. Using an interpretive analytical framework, the included studies were synthesized across three dimensions: temporal trends reported in the literature, structural characteristics of payment architecture, and regional or country-level variation in implementation and reform patterns.ResultsFrom 8,615 identified records, 168 studies met the inclusion criteria. Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG)-based systems (32.1%) and pay-for-performance arrangements (26.2%) were the most frequently examined configurations, followed by global budget and capitation approaches (17.9%). A substantial increase in publications was observed after 2016, with 63.7% of studies published between 2016 and 2024. Across the reviewed literature, three broad periods of policy and research emphasis were identified: an earlier period focused primarily on cost standardization and inpatient expenditure control (2000–2007); a subsequent period characterized by increasing attention to hybrid arrangements and quality-linked components (2008–2015); and a more recent period emphasizing bundled payments, context-adapted designs, and integration with budgetary constraints (2016–2024). Regional variation revealed heterogeneous implementation and reform patterns across North America, Europe, and Asia.ConclusionsThe findings of this review suggest that PPS-related reforms are more appropriately understood as configurations of payment architecture embedded within broader institutional and governance contexts rather than as isolated payment models. Reported outcomes appear to depend less on model labels and more on design configuration and implementation capacity. Future research and policy analysis may benefit from more systematic reporting of payment architecture and its institutional prerequisites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-026-00799-9.Keywords: Prospective payment systems, Payment architecture, Diagnosis-related groups, Health financing reform, Strategic purchasing, Regional variation

Introduction

The sustained rise in health expenditures over recent decades has placed the financial sustainability of health systems at the forefront of public policy concerns [1]. Empirical evidence indicates that this upward trajectory has been driven by a confluence of factors, including technological innovation, demographic shifts—most notably population ageing—and the expansion in both the scope and intensity of service utilization [2, 3]. At the same time, disentangling the specific contribution of “technology” remains methodologically challenging, as its effects are closely intertwined with income growth, demographic structures, and broader institutional contexts [4].

Within this landscape, Prospective Payment Systems (PPS) have emerged as a central strategy in payment reform. Under PPS arrangements, payment rules and rates are established prior to service delivery. By enhancing cost predictability and embedding efficiency-oriented incentives, such systems are intended to improve the allocation of resources within health systems [5, 6].

PPS, however, does not constitute a single, static framework. Rather, the PPS-related literature encompasses a diverse spectrum of payment arrangements and reimbursement approaches that have been implemented and modified across different institutional and policy contexts over time. Across the literature, these arrangements have been described as ranging from relatively simple predetermined budgets and case-based reimbursement approaches to more standardized classification-based systems, including diagnosis-related group (DRG) payment models [7]. These systems have themselves undergone further refinement, incorporating elements such as risk adjustment, supplementary and settlement rules, integration with complementary instruments, and mechanisms designed to align provider incentives with efficiency and cost-containment objectives [8]. Accordingly, analysis of PPS-related reforms requires attention not only to individual payment mechanisms but also to the broader configuration of payment architecture, including adjustment rules, settlement mechanisms, and complementary incentive structures operating within different health system contexts [9].

Importantly, the trajectory of PPS implementation and modification has not been uniform across countries. Variations in institutional and operational contexts—including health system structure, information and coding capacity, governance arrangements, and prior reform experience—have shaped both the timing and form of PPS-related reforms [9–11]. Consequently, comparative analysis of PPS-related reforms requires consideration of both temporal patterns reported across the literature and regional variation in implementation approaches, reform priorities, and institutional adaptation.

Despite the breadth of scholarship on PPS, a substantial proportion of existing studies remain confined to single-country or single-program evaluations, or concentrate primarily on the outcomes of a specific payment mechanism—such as costs, length of stay, or quality indicators [12, 13]. By contrast, relatively few studies have synthesized how PPS-related reforms have been described across the literature in terms of temporal trends, structural characteristics of payment architecture, and regional variation in implementation and adaptation between 2000 and 2024. The absence of such comparative synthesis limits opportunities to identify recurring reform patterns, compare implementation approaches across settings, and inform context-sensitive policy design.

While our prior systematic review examined the behavioral consequences of PPS reforms, the present analysis shifts the focus toward temporal, structural, and regional patterns reported across the PPS-related literature.

Against this background, the present study examines temporal trends, structural characteristics of payment architecture, and regional patterns reported across the PPS-related literature published between 2000 and 2024. The analysis of providers’ behavioral responses is reported in a separate manuscript and does not form part of the present article.

Methods

Study design

This study presents a secondary analysis of a body of literature previously identified, screened, and finalized through a systematic review [14].The parent review examined the effects of Prospective Payment Systems (PPS) on provider behavior. In contrast, the present study draws on the same dataset to examine and interpret temporal and structural patterns reported across the PPS-related literature published between 2000 and 2024.

The analysis was organized around three broad interpretive dimensions: (1) temporal trends and shifts in policy emphasis reported across the literature; (2) structural characteristics and changes in payment architecture; and (3) regional and country-level variation in the implementation and adaptation of PPS-related reforms.

No additional searches, screening procedures, or eligibility assessments were conducted for this study. All studies retained in the parent systematic review were included in the secondary analysis without modification.

For the purposes of the present secondary analysis, an interpretive analytical framework was developed to organize and synthesize recurring patterns identified across the included studies. Broad analytical categories and extraction principles were refined iteratively during the analytical process to support conceptual consistency across the synthesis.

Because the present article focuses on temporal and structural patterns in PPS-related reforms, detailed analysis of providers’ behavioral responses is reported separately and does not form part of this manuscript.

Data sources and scope

The dataset comprised 168 articles included in the primary systematic review after comprehensive searching, screening, and full-text assessment. The review was conducted across four databases—PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase—and covered the period from 1 January 2000 to 31 December 2024. The complete search strategy, including detailed search strings and keywords, is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Reference management and duplicate removal in the primary review were undertaken using EndNote X9. For the present secondary analysis, a structured extraction database was developed and maintained in Microsoft Excel to organize the extracted information. The study identification and selection process are described in detail in the primary systematic review, as no additional search or screening was conducted for the present analysis. The study identification and selection process of the primary systematic review is presented in Supplementary File 2 (PRISMA flow diagram).

Identification of temporal and structural reform evidence

The unit of analysis in this study consisted of extractable information related to temporal trends, payment design changes, implementation patterns, and structural characteristics of PPS-related reforms. Accordingly, all 168 studies were retained in the analysis, and none were excluded on the basis of topic, content, or methodological quality.

Extractability was determined strictly on the basis of explicit reporting within the study text. No inference was made beyond the authors’ stated findings. The analysis focused on organizing and interpreting reported information rather than evaluating the effectiveness or validity of individual reforms.

Information was considered extractable and classifiable if a study reported at least one of the following: the introduction, implementation, or modification of a PPS-related reform; changes in payment design or architecture, including payment base, settlement mechanisms, adjustment methods, scope of coverage, or integration with complementary payment arrangements; or country- and region-specific experiences in implementing, adapting, or restructuring PPS-related reimbursement systems, together with any reported contextual or institutional factors.

Studies lacking extractable information relevant to the present synthesis were not excluded. Instead, they were recorded in the extraction matrix as containing no extractable temporal or structural reform information, thereby preserving analytical transparency and traceability.

Quality appraisal

Methodological quality assessment was conducted as part of the parent systematic review. Detailed quality assessment results are provided in Supplementary File 3. In the present secondary analysis, quality ratings were used solely as contextual information. No study was excluded based on methodological quality; however, findings from lower-quality studies were interpreted with appropriate caution during synthesis.

Coding framework and data organization

A structured analytical framework was developed to organize and synthesize information reported across the included studies. The framework was refined iteratively during the analytical process and was used solely to support consistent extraction and organization of reported evidence. It was not used for study selection or exclusion.

For each study, the following information was extracted and organized when explicitly reported: bibliographic characteristics (year of publication, country or countries examined, and study design); characteristics of the PPS-related reform or reimbursement arrangement (type or version, level of implementation, and timing of reforms, where specified); components of payment architecture (payment base, settlement mechanisms, adjustment approaches, scope of coverage, and related structural elements); reported reform characteristics and implementation features (including reforms, policy modifications, innovations, and staged implementation processes); and contextual factors related to implementation, governance, or health system organization.

The absence of any extracted element reflected limitations in reporting within the source study and did not result in exclusion of studies or findings from the synthesis. Given the large volume of descriptive and contextual information and its primarily supportive role within the narrative synthesis, the complete extraction matrix is not presented in the manuscript or supplementary materials and was maintained solely as an internal organizational resource.

To improve analytical transparency, a supplementary coding framework detailing the interpretive categories, temporal groupings, and cross-national synthesis patterns underlying the narrative analysis is provided in Supplementary File 4.

Synthesis and analytical strategy

Given the conceptual and reporting heterogeneity across the included studies, quantitative pooling was neither feasible nor methodologically appropriate. Evidence was therefore synthesized narratively. An interpretive thematic approach was used to organize recurring patterns and themes identified across the literature [15].

The analytical process proceeded in three stages. First, two researchers independently reviewed and extracted text segments referring to temporal changes, payment reforms, structural characteristics of reimbursement arrangements, or cross-national variation in implementation patterns. Analytical categories were developed iteratively through repeated comparison across studies and refinement of emerging thematic groupings.

Second, conceptually related findings were organized into broader interpretive themes, resulting in three principal analytical domains: temporal trends in PPS-related reforms; structural patterns in payment architecture; and regional and country-level variation in implementation and adaptation.

Third, the synthesized themes and supporting evidence were integrated into a narrative–analytical framework corresponding to temporal, structural, and regional dimensions of PPS-related reform patterns.

Trustworthiness and analytical rigor

To enhance analytical credibility, the extraction and interpretation of relevant evidence were undertaken independently by two researchers. Differences in interpretation were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary. A record of key extraction decisions, category refinements, and theme consolidation was maintained throughout the analytical process. The final structural typology was refined through iterative review to ensure consistency with the extracted evidence.

Consideration of publication bias

Because the synthesis was narrative and did not involve statistical aggregation, publication bias was assessed qualitatively. This assessment considered the geographical representation of studies, temporal patterns in publication volume, the potential influence of English-language restrictions on evidence coverage, and the possible underrepresentation of specific PPS typologies or levels of care in the published literature.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of included studies

Temporal Trends in Publications of Included Studies (2000–2024)

Across the 168 included studies, the temporal distribution of publications demonstrates a substantial increase in scholarly attention to prospective payment systems and related reimbursement reforms over the study period. During the first interval (2000–2007), 24 studies (14.29%) were published, with the literature primarily concentrating on the early implementation and operational implications of DRG-based systems and Medicare PPS in high-income health systems.

Between 2008 and 2015, the number of publications increased to 37 studies (22.02%), representing a 68% rise relative to the preceding period. During these years, the literature expanded geographically and began to include a broader range of health system contexts, particularly in parts of Asia and Europe.

The final interval (2016–2024) accounted for 107 studies (63.69%), corresponding to a 195% increase compared with the previous period. More than two-thirds of the included evidence was published during these nine years. Collectively, these patterns suggest a marked intensification of academic and policy interest in the role of payment system reform within broader health system transformation efforts.

Types of payment systems examined

In this review, payment mechanisms such as pay-for-performance (P4P) and fee-for-service (FFS) were included only when discussed as components of broader PPS-oriented reimbursement architectures or reform packages rather than as standalone prospective payment systems.

Across the 168 included studies, DRG- and case-based payment systems constituted the most frequently examined category, represented in 54 studies (32.1%). These included various national configurations such as Medicare DRG, SwissDRG, CHS-DRG, K-DRG, and Tw-DRG, as well as related activity- and case-based reimbursement arrangements.

Pay-for-performance (P4P) schemes were the second most frequently discussed category, appearing in 44 studies (26.2%), primarily in combination with broader PPS reforms or quality-linked purchasing strategies. Global budget and global payment arrangements were addressed in 30 studies (17.9%).

Bundled payment arrangements were examined in 20 studies (11.9%), largely in the context of U.S. initiatives such as the Bundled Payments for Care Improvement (BPCI) program and the Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement (CJR) model. Episode-based payment systems were evaluated in 13 studies (7.7%), while explicitly mixed or hybrid payment arrangements were identified in 5 studies (3.0%). Fee-for-service models appeared in 2 studies (1.2%), primarily as comparator mechanisms or as components embedded within broader reimbursement reforms.

Temporal and structural trends in PPS-related literature (2000–2024)

A narrative synthesis of the 168 included studies suggests that the published literature on prospective payment systems (PPS) increasingly reflects a transition from relatively discrete reimbursement mechanisms toward more complex and multi-layered payment architectures associated with strategic purchasing and system-level coordination.

Rather than reconstructing the historical development of PPS reforms directly, the present analysis examines how PPS-related reforms, design features, and implementation priorities have been represented across the empirical literature over time.

Across the reviewed studies, three broad analytical dimensions emerged: (1) temporal shifts in dominant research and policy emphases; (2) structural changes in the configuration of payment architecture; and (3) regional variation in reported pathways of implementation and adaptation.

Periodization and key turning points

Period dominated by efficiency-oriented PPS research (2000–2007): emphasis on cost containment and payment standardization

Between 2000 and 2007, the reviewed literature was largely shaped by concerns related to cost containment and operational efficiency within hospital payment systems. During this period, PPS was predominantly examined as a mechanism for standardizing reimbursement and improving the predictability of inpatient expenditures, particularly in studies evaluating DRG implementation in high-income health systems. Prominent publications focused on economic and operational outcomes, including hospital technical efficiency under PPS reforms [16] and reductions in length of stay following DRG implementation [17].

At the same time, the literature highlighted the extent to which PPS implementation depended on institutional capacity and operational readiness. For example, several studies described barriers to DRG adoption in Japan [18] and discrepancies between actual hospital costs and DRG-based reimbursement as indicators of implementation challenges [19].

Toward the latter part of this period, two developments became increasingly visible in the literature. First, some studies began to explore broader organizational and non-economic implications of PPS reforms [20], although detailed analyses of provider behavioral responses are presented separately and are beyond the scope of the present study. Second, early pay-for-performance (P4P) components and other incentive-based mechanisms started to appear alongside PPS arrangements, suggesting an emerging shift in the literature from isolated payment rules toward more integrated reimbursement strategies [21, 22].

Period of expanding quality integration and hybrid payment designs (2008–2015)

From 2008 to 2015, the reviewed literature moved beyond a predominantly efficiency-oriented perspective and increasingly examined the relationship between cost containment and quality improvement within PPS-related reforms. During this period, PPS was more frequently portrayed in the literature as a strategic purchasing instrument rather than solely a reimbursement mechanism, particularly in studies evaluating hybrid and multi-component payment arrangements.

A notable trend across the published evidence was the growing attention to blended payment structures intended to align financial incentives with quality objectives at the system level. Evaluations of global budget arrangements and mixed reimbursement strategies illustrate this shift in emphasis [23].

At the methodological level, the literature also reflected a gradual strengthening of evaluation approaches. Studies increasingly employed quasi-experimental methods, including propensity score matching [24], difference-in-differences analyses [25], and interrupted time-series designs [26], moving beyond relatively simple before–after comparisons that had characterized much of the earlier literature.

In parallel, the reviewed studies demonstrated greater attention to unintended consequences, implementation governance, and regulatory oversight in the design of PPS-related reforms [27]. However, detailed behavioral and causal analyses of provider responses remain outside the scope of the present article.

Period of expansion, specialization, and contextual adaptation (2016–2024)

The literature published between 2016 and 2024 increasingly reflected three interconnected trends: (a) broader geographical diffusion of PPS implementation and research; (b) increasing specialization of payment architecture for specific clinical domains and population groups; and (c) greater emphasis on system-level outcomes, implementation governance, and equity considerations.

A substantial proportion of studies from this period originated from China and other Asian health systems, alongside a growing body of evidence from low- and middle-income countries. Collectively, these publications suggest that PPS-related reforms were no longer discussed primarily within high-income settings but were increasingly examined across diverse institutional and policy environments [28–33].

At the same time, the reviewed literature demonstrated increasing specialization in payment design. Examples included the TARPSY framework for psychiatric services in Switzerland [34], pediatric-specific reimbursement reforms [35], and procedure-level payment arrangements targeting selected clinical interventions [36]. The literature also described context-adapted and scalable payment approaches, including the Diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) system in China [33], and multi-hospital global budget arrangements aimed at strengthening regional coordination [37].

Across this period, the published evidence further emphasized the importance of implementation context and organizational readiness in shaping PPS performance [38], Equity considerations also became more visible within evaluative frameworks [39], while several studies explored the role of PPS-related reforms in supporting care coordination and more integrated models of service delivery [40, 41].

Consistent with the scope of the present study, detailed analyses of provider behavioral responses and strategic adaptation mechanisms are reported separately [42, 43]. Structural trends and emerging typologies of payment architecture

Aligned with the temporal trajectory described above, the literature indicates that PPS has undergone structural transformation along several principal dimensions:

From relatively discrete payment arrangements to more complex and multi-layered architectures

In the earlier literature, PPS arrangements were primarily described in relation to DRG-based systems and relatively discrete case-based or per capita reimbursement structures. Expected outcomes were commonly framed in operational and financial terms, particularly reductions in length of stay and inpatient expenditures [17].

Over time, the reviewed studies increasingly examined hybrid and multi-component payment arrangements. The Alternative Quality Contract (AQC), for example, was discussed as a blended architecture linking global budgets with explicit quality-related incentives [23, 44, 45]. Bundled and episode-based payment approaches were likewise described as mechanisms that expanded the unit of payment beyond individual services or hospital admissions toward broader episodes of care [46, 47].

In more recent years, the literature has also drawn attention to context-adapted and scalable payment approaches, including the diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) framework in China and multi-hospital global budget arrangements intended to strengthen regional coordination mechanisms [33, 37].

From cost containment toward cost–quality alignment and value-oriented approaches

Beginning in the mid-2000s, the reviewed literature increasingly discussed pay-for-performance (P4P) mechanisms as complementary incentive components intended to align PPS-related reforms with quality objectives [21, 48]. Across many studies, these arrangements were presented not as standalone payment systems, but as additional layers incorporated into broader reimbursement architectures.

During the subsequent period, several evaluations reported that cost containment objectives could, under particular design and governance conditions, be pursued alongside quality improvement initiatives [23].

The literature also reflected growing interest in blended arrangements combining global budgets, performance-based incentives, and value-oriented purchasing strategies [49].

More recent publications further emphasized the role of integrated payment approaches in aligning financial incentives with coordination, quality, and system-level performance goals. Collectively, these studies suggest an increasing shift in the literature from narrowly cost-focused reimbursement mechanisms toward more multidimensional and value-oriented payment arrangements.

From hospital-centered payment systems toward broader care coordination across the continuum

The reviewed literature indicates that early PPS-related reforms were predominantly focused on inpatient and hospital-based reimbursement arrangements. During this period, evaluations primarily examined operational outcomes such as inpatient utilization, hospital expenditures, and length of stay following DRG implementation [17].

Over time, however, the literature increasingly documented the expansion of PPS-related reforms into outpatient and pathway-oriented payment arrangements. Several studies reported shifts in service delivery from inpatient to outpatient settings following DRG-related reforms [50]. while others examined outpatient payment reforms and their implications for service volume and expenditure patterns [51].

More recent publications have increasingly described bundled and episode-based payment approaches as mechanisms intended to strengthen coordination across episodes of care and treatment pathways [24, 40], In parallel, some studies have explored the role of PPS-related reforms in supporting more integrated and structured delivery systems at the organizational and system levels [41].

From general payment frameworks toward domain-specific and specialized designs

The reviewed literature increasingly indicates that specialization has become a prominent feature of more recent PPS-related reforms and reimbursement arrangements. Studies examining the TARPSY framework in psychiatric care [34], along with analyses of inpatient psychiatric payment systems [52], ], illustrate how service areas characterized by distinct clinical and organizational requirements often necessitate more tailored classification and reimbursement structures.

Similarly, publications focusing on pediatric-specific reimbursement reforms [35] and procedure-level payment refinements targeting selected interventions [36] reflect a broader trend toward greater granularity and specialization in payment design.

Regional and country-level variation in reported PPS reform patterns

The reviewed literature suggests that the development, implementation, and evaluation of PPS-related reforms have varied considerably across regions and health system contexts. Across the included studies, these differences were commonly associated with variation in governance structures, institutional capacity, financing arrangements, and policy priorities.

North America: emphasis on contractual innovation and hybrid payment arrangements

In the United States, the literature predominantly focused on hybrid, bundled, and episode-based payment arrangements. Bundled payment initiatives for selected surgical procedures were frequently discussed as examples of efforts to redefine the unit of payment beyond individual services or hospital admissions [46, 47]. Similarly, the Alternative Quality Contract (AQC) was examined in multiple studies as a blended global payment arrangement incorporating explicit quality-related incentive components [23, 44, 45].

In Canada, the reviewed studies more commonly emphasized primary care payment reforms and broader system-level outcomes, including referral patterns and potentially avoidable hospitalizations [50, 53].

Europe: institutional diversity, specialized payment designs, and equity-oriented evaluation

Across the European literature, PPS-related reforms were described within a wide range of institutional and financing contexts, reflecting substantial diversity among health systems. Some studies focused on efficiency-oriented reimbursement reforms and incentive structures within specific service sectors [54], whereas others examined the effects of activity-based payment arrangements on hospital financing patterns and length of stay [26, 55].

The reviewed literature also suggests that European health systems have been prominent settings for the development and evaluation of more specialized reimbursement arrangements, particularly in psychiatric care and other service areas requiring tailored classification frameworks [5, 34].

In addition, several publications increasingly emphasized value-oriented evaluation, distributive considerations, and stakeholder engagement in PPS-related reform design and assessment [39, 49]. Compared with some other regions, the European literature more frequently incorporated equity-related concerns into discussions of PPS implementation and evaluation.

Asia: expansion, scalability, and context-adapted payment approaches

Across the Asian literature—particularly studies conducted in China—PPS-related reforms were frequently discussed in relation to scalability, administrative adaptation, and large-scale implementation. The diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) framework, for example, was described as a locally adapted classification and reimbursement approach designed to support implementation across diverse institutional settings [33]. Several studies also examined outpatient payment reforms and their implications for healthcare expenditure and service utilization patterns [51].

In addition, multi-hospital global budget arrangements were discussed as mechanisms intended to strengthen regional coordination and resource management within broader health system reforms [37]. In Japan, the reviewed literature commonly described gradual adjustments to reimbursement rules and incentive structures within selected service sectors [56, 57]. Studies from South Korea and Taiwan likewise evaluated domain-specific DRG implementation and associated quality outcomes, including the provision of low-value services [32, 58]. Collectively, the Asian literature reflects a strong emphasis on context-sensitive implementation strategies, scalability, and administrative adaptation within PPS-related reforms.

Low- and middle-income countries: gradual adoption and implementation constraints

Across studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries, PPS-related reforms were commonly discussed in the context of gradual implementation, institutional adaptation, and resource constraints. The reviewed literature frequently emphasized the importance of administrative infrastructure, organizational readiness, and financial sustainability in shaping the implementation and performance of reimbursement reforms.

In several African settings, studies examining performance-based financing and capitation arrangements reported both potential improvements and substantial implementation challenges, with outcomes varying across institutional and policy contexts [30, 31]. In Latin America, the literature frequently focused on pay-for-performance initiatives in primary care and the extent to which outcomes appeared sensitive to specific design characteristics and implementation conditions [28, 59]. Similarly, studies from Southeast Asia highlighted capitation-based and performance-linked primary care reforms as important pathways in ongoing payment system restructuring efforts [29].

Taken together, the literature from low- and middle-income settings underscores the importance of contextual adaptation, governance capacity, and implementation readiness in shaping PPS-related reform trajectories and outcomes.

Overall, the reviewed literature suggests a transition from relatively discrete and predominantly hospital-centered reimbursement arrangements toward more complex and multi-layered payment architectures integrating case-, episode-, or population-based payment approaches with budgetary controls, settlement mechanisms, and, in some settings, explicit quality- or value-oriented components [17, 23, 46].

Across the included studies, these patterns were reflected in three broad shifts in research and policy emphasis: an earlier concentration on cost containment and operational efficiency; a subsequent focus on quality integration and more rigorous evaluation approaches; and a more recent emphasis on specialization, scalability, and contextual adaptation across diverse health system environments [24, 33, 34]. The reviewed evidence also indicates substantial regional variation in the ways PPS-related reforms have been discussed and implemented. Studies from North America predominantly focused on contractual innovation and episode-based reimbursement arrangements, whereas the European literature more frequently emphasized equity considerations, specialized payment designs, and value-oriented evaluation frameworks. In contrast, studies from Asia—particularly China—commonly examined the integration of PPS with broader budgetary and settlement mechanisms within large-scale implementation contexts [37, 39, 45].

Taken together, the reviewed studies highlight the importance of administrative infrastructure, coding and classification capacity, risk-adjustment systems, and implementation governance in shaping the design and operational performance of PPS-related reforms. Consistent with the scope of the present study, detailed analyses of provider behavioral responses to these incentive structures are presented separately [38, 42, 60].

Discussion

The findings of this secondary analysis suggest that PPS-related reforms, as represented in the reviewed literature, cannot be adequately understood merely as shifts between payment system labels. Rather than indicating simple movement from one payment type to another, the patterns identified in this review point to a broader reconfiguration of payment architecture and financial governance within health systems. In the earlier literature, PPS was predominantly framed as a mechanism for determining case- or admission-based reimbursement and enhancing cost predictability. Over time, however, the reviewed studies increasingly described PPS-related arrangements as incorporating risk redistribution, cost–quality alignment, and system-level incentive structures [17, 23, 33, 38] In this sense, the literature reviewed here suggests a shift in emphasis from service pricing toward resource-allocation governance, whereby payment arrangements are understood not only as reimbursement tools but also as institutional mechanisms for structuring incentives and directing financial flows within health systems [41, 60, 61].

The temporal patterns identified in this synthesis were organized into three broad periods of research and policy emphasis rather than treated as fixed global policy phases. In the earlier period (2000–2007), the literature primarily examined DRG-based and case-based payment arrangements, with an explicit focus on operational efficiency and the containment of inpatient expenditure. Despite the relative simplicity of these early designs, studies from this period already underscored the importance of data capacity, coding accuracy, and institutional readiness in shaping reported outcomes [16, 17]. Even in these ostensibly straightforward arrangements, implementation architecture appeared to matter. The gradual appearance of pay-for-performance (P4P) mechanisms toward the end of this period was discussed in the literature as an early indication of movement beyond single-dimensional financial incentives toward more multi-objective reimbursement arrangements [21].

During the subsequent period (2008–2015), the reviewed literature increasingly focused on more complex and hybrid payment architectures. Hybrid arrangements—particularly global payments combined with explicit quality components—suggested that, under certain design and governance conditions, cost reduction objectives could be pursued alongside the maintenance or improvement of selected quality indicators [23, 44]. At the same time, the broader use of quasi-experimental designs [25, 26] strengthened the empirical assessment of structural reform effects. Nevertheless, some studies documented unintended consequences and sensitivity of outcomes to design details [27]. These findings suggest that quality within PPS-related frameworks is inherently multidimensional: prioritizing incentivized metrics may channel provider behavior toward those dimensions while leaving non-incentivized aspects of care comparatively less visible [62]. The literature from this period can therefore be interpreted as reflecting an effort to manage the tension between economic efficiency and the multidimensional nature of healthcare quality [63].

In the most recent period (2016–2024), the reviewed studies increasingly emphasized expansion, specialization, and contextual adaptation in PPS-related reforms. Two developments were particularly visible: the extension of the unit of payment from single services or admissions toward episodes or care pathways, and the development of specialized payment designs tailored to the clinical logic of specific fields. Bundled and episode-based payments [40, 46] were commonly described as mechanisms for promoting cross-sectoral coordination and financial integration along the continuum of care. This broader functional framing positioned PPS-related payment architecture as a potential instrument for organizing care pathways, rather than solely as a tool for inpatient cost control. Concurrently, specialized variants in areas such as psychiatry and paediatrics [34, 35] illustrated how heterogeneity in service production may limit the adequacy of uniform payment designs. In some contexts—most notably China—the combination of PPS-related arrangements with explicit budget constraints and multi-layered settlement mechanisms suggests that large-scale reforms have increasingly embedded financial governance within payment architecture. From this perspective, the reviewed literature portrays PPS less as a single technical formula than as a configurable institutional arrangement operating across multiple levels of the health system.

Regional variation further underscores the context-dependence of PPS-related reform patterns. Studies from North America more frequently emphasized contractual innovation and hybrid payment arrangements. European studies, by contrast, often reflected institutional diversity, equity considerations, and specialized payment designs. In parts of Asia—particularly China—the literature placed greater emphasis on scalability and the integration of PPS-related reforms with budgetary controls and settlement mechanisms. Such heterogeneity suggests that reported outcomes are shaped less by the nominal classification of a payment system than by its design features, regulatory arrangements, implementation capacity, and institutional context [64, 65].

Taken together, the patterns identified in this review support conceptualizing PPS-related reforms not as a single payment system, but as a configurable set of design components. By “design basket,” we refer to a modular combination of elements—such as the unit of payment, risk-adjustment mechanisms, budget constraints, quality-linked incentives, and settlement rules—that may be assembled differently across institutional contexts. This perspective enables more meaningful comparison of heterogeneous reforms and offers a basis for developing a shared analytical vocabulary in the evaluation of payment architecture [66]. Integrating this macro-level architectural perspective with analyses of providers’ behavioral responses—addressed in a separate manuscript—may further clarify how institutional payment design interacts with micro-level incentive dynamics.

Study limitations

This study constitutes an analytical narrative synthesis rather than a quantitative meta-analysis. Its objective was not to estimate pooled effect sizes or to compare the magnitude of impact across payment arrangements. Accordingly, the findings relate to temporal, structural, and regional patterns reported across the literature on prospective payment system (PPS)-related reforms, rather than to the relative performance or superiority of any specific payment system.

The geographical distribution of the included studies was uneven, with certain countries and regions more prominently represented in the published literature. As a result, some reform trajectories and implementation experiences may appear more visible within the synthesis than others. In addition, the restriction to English-language publications and the scope of the selected databases may have contributed to the underrepresentation of certain policy experiences, institutional documents, or locally reported reforms.

Finally, the interpretive nature of narrative synthesis inevitably involves a degree of analytical judgement in the organization and interpretation of reported evidence. Although the analytical process was conducted systematically and included independent review and iterative refinement of analytical categories, the findings remain dependent on the scope, reporting quality, and conceptual framing of the included studies.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that prospective payment systems (PPS) are more appropriately understood in the reviewed literature not merely as isolated technical instruments or single payment models, but as broader configurations of payment architecture within the domain of strategic purchasing. Within this architecture, the choice of payment unit—whether case-based, episode-based, or population-based—constitutes only one component. The overall function and reported performance of PPS-related arrangements appear to emerge from their interaction with settlement rules, budgetary constraints, risk-adjustment mechanisms, the scope of covered services, and, in certain contexts, explicit quality- or value-linked elements. The patterns identified across the reviewed literature further suggest that implementation outcomes may depend less on the nominal classification of a payment system than on the coherence of its broader architectural configuration and the institutional capacity available to support implementation.

From this perspective, comparative assessment and interpretation of payment reforms may be more informative when focused on underlying payment architecture and implementation conditions rather than on model labels alone. The cross-national variation identified in this review further underscores that no universal template for PPS exists. Implementation feasibility, data governance capacity, and institutional adaptability appear to function not merely as external contextual conditions, but as integral elements shaping PPS-related reform processes. Finally, the analysis of providers’ behavioural responses to financial incentives—which may help explain the rationale behind certain design adaptations—is presented in a separate manuscript and serves as a complementary perspective to the present analysis.

Implications for research and policy

The findings of this study indicate that categorising payment reforms solely by “type of payment” may be insufficient for meaningful cross-national comparison. Future research may benefit from conceptualizing and reporting PPS-related reforms in terms of payment architecture, with explicit specification of core design components. These include the unit of payment, scope of coverage (inpatient, outpatient, or care pathway), settlement and risk-adjustment rules, linkages to budget ceilings or compensation mechanisms, and the level of implementation (local, regional, or national). In addition, more systematic reporting of institutional context and implementation capacity—particularly data quality and coding standards, monitoring and auditing capabilities, and regulatory arrangements—could improve the precision and comparability of future analyses of payment reform.

From a methodological standpoint, this architectural perspective may also strengthen comparative and quasi-experimental approaches to the study of payment reform. Rather than attributing observed effects to broad “model” categories, researchers may be better positioned to examine which specific components of payment architecture, and under which institutional conditions, are associated with particular outcomes. For policymakers, the findings suggest that PPS-related reform design involves both technical and governance considerations. The reviewed literature indicates that adjustments to payment units or tariff structures, when implemented without sufficiently defined settlement mechanisms, risk-adjustment approaches, or accountability arrangements, may contribute to implementation instability or heterogeneous outcomes across settings. Accordingly, payment reforms may benefit from early consideration of implementation prerequisites, including data infrastructure and coding standards, transparent settlement procedures, regulatory and audit capacity, and coordination across sectors and levels of care delivery.

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that PPS-related reform may be more effectively understood not simply as substitution between payment models, but as reconfiguration of payment architecture within specific institutional and implementation contexts. Such configurations are likely to require alignment with existing administrative and organizational capacity, as well as ongoing monitoring and adaptation throughout implementation processes.
