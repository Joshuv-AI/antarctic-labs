Macrophage‐Dependent Intercellular Crosstalk in Multiphenotypic Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Yangyang Huang, Lingyun Luo, Xiantao Ma, Yajuan Huang, Chen Zhang, Xingjie Hao, Le Zhang, Hongling Zhu, Lei Dai, Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is now recognized as a complex, multiphenotypic metabolic syndrome fueled by inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, rather than a single disease entity. Cardiac macrophages, as the most abundant immune cells in the heart, are emerging as central players in HFpEF pathogenesis by orchestrating intercellular crosstalk and driving maladaptive remodeling. This review delineates the intricate macrophage‐dependent vicious cycle in HFpEF. We first elaborate on the heterogeneity of cardiac macrophages and the diverse clinical and preclinical phenotypes of HFpEF. We then synthesize evidence demonstrating how risk factors, like aging, cardiometabolic disease, and hypertension, induce profound phenotypic switching and functional reprogramming of macrophages across different HFpEF subtypes. Subsequently, we detail how these reprogrammed macrophages, in turn, propagate disease progression by promoting inflammation, fibrosis, and endothelial dysfunction through complex interactions with cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and other cellular components. Targeting specific macrophage subsets and their effector mechanisms presents a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating the burden of this heterogeneous syndrome.

Heart failure (HF) constitutes a major global health burden, with prevalence exceeding 64 million. Among HF subtypes, HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) predominates. 1 Growing recognition reveals HFpEF is not a single disease entity defined by ejection fraction, but rather a complex, heterogeneous metabolic syndrome. 2 This heterogeneity poses significant therapeutic challenges. 3

Inflammation and metabolic dysregulation are universal pathophysiological mechanisms in HFpEF, 4 where macrophages play a crucial role. 5 Multiple studies report phenotypic switching and functional alterations in macrophages across various HFpEF phenotypes in patients and preclinical models, such as hypertensive, 6 aging, 7 and cardiometabolic 8 subtypes. These reprogrammed macrophages further exacerbate HFpEF progression. 9 Targeting specific macrophage populations may represent an effective therapeutic strategy for multiphenotypic HFpEF. 10

Although several excellent reviews have addressed macrophage biology in heart failure, 4 , 5 , 11 recent advances, particularly those leveraging single‐cell technologies and lineage tracing, have substantially reshaped our understanding of cardiac macrophage heterogeneity in HFpEF. 8 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, prior reviews have largely provided broad perspectives on cardiac immunometabolism, whereas a focused synthesis integrating macrophage phenotype‐specific roles and their intercellular crosstalk across distinct clinical and preclinical HFpEF subtypes, incorporating the latest 2025 to 2026 discoveries, remains lacking. This review addresses 3 key aspects: (1) cardiac macrophages, (2) heterogeneity of HFpEF, and (3) macrophage‐driven vicious cycle in HFpEF: we first describe macrophage phenotypic transitions and functional reprogramming in HFpEF, then detail how reprogrammed macrophages, in turn, influence HFpEF progression. MACROPHAGES IN THE HEART

Cardiac macrophages, constituting 6% to 8% of noncardiomyocytes in adult mice, represent the most abundant immune population in the heart 15 and serve as critical mediators of cardiac development, tissue homeostasis, and injury responses. This is based on their dynamic adaptation to microenvironmental cues. 16 In the following section, we will introduce cardiac macrophages from 2 perspectives: classical categorization and developmental origin (Figure 2).

Macrophages in the heart.

Cardiac macrophages constitute 6% to 8% of myocardial cells and are primarily classified into 2 main populations based on origin and function. Resident cardiac macrophages (CCR2−) are characterized by expression of markers, including CD163, LYVE1, FOLR2, F13A1, TIMD4, and CD169. Monocyte‐derived macrophages (CCR2+) originate from circulating monocytes, express CCR2, and accumulate in the heart under stress conditions. Beyond the traditional M1/M2 polarization framework, cardiac macrophages exhibit a broad spectrum of functions essential for both homeostasis and disease: secretion of both proinflammatory (eg, TNF‐α and IL‐6) and anti‐inflammatory (eg, IL‐10 and TGF‐β) cytokines, promotion of fibrosis (via TGF‐β), and regulation of extracellular matrix dynamics and clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria (efferocytosis/heterophagy) from cardiomyocytes to maintain metabolic homeostasis. And there exists cellular crosstalk: modulating fibroblast activity and differentiation into myofibroblasts, facilitating electrical conduction with cardiomyocytes, presenting antigens to T cells, linking innate and adaptive immunity and regulating angiogenesis and vascular integrity. Macrophages are broadly polarized into classically activated (M1) and alternatively activated (M2) subtypes. 17 The M1 subset produces elevated levels of proinflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, interferon‐γ, and interleukin 6, whereas M2 macrophages exhibit immunomodulatory functions through anti‐inflammatory factors, like interleukin 4 and transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β. 18 The heterogeneity and plasticity of macrophages in both homeostatic and injured hearts extend beyond the traditional M1/M2 macrophage polarization paradigm. 11 , 12 Recent advances in single‐cell RNA sequencing have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of this diversity, revealing that cardiac macrophages comprise a spectrum of transcriptionally distinct subsets with specialized functions. 8 , 12 These populations, broadly categorized as tissue‐resident (C‐C chemokine receptor type 2 [CCR2]−) and monocyte‐derived (CCR2+) macrophages, exhibit divergent roles in homeostasis and disease. 12 Beyond inflammatory modulation, macrophages perform a repertoire of functions, including antigen presentation, to orchestrate adaptive immune responses, efferocytosis to clear apoptotic cardiomyocytes and maintain tissue homeostasis, direct modulation of fibroblast activity influencing extracellular matrix remodeling, and even electrical coupling with cardiomyocytes to facilitate cardiac conduction. 5 , 12 In other HF models, monocytes–macrophages also play critical roles in disease pathogenesis. In pressure overload–induced HF (transverse aortic constriction model), monocyte‐derived inflammatory CCRL2+ macrophages drive adverse remodeling, 13 whereas cluster of differentiation (CD) 163+ resident macrophages exert protective effects via interleukin‐10. 19 In ischemic cardiomyopathy, pathologically expanded CD206+ interleukin‐4Rα+ macrophages promote fibrosis through FIZZ1. 20 Human HF studies (Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM)) reveal that protective tissue‐resident macrophages (lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1+, CD163+, and SIGLEC1+) are progressively replaced by inflammatory monocyte‐derived macrophages (C‐C motif chemokine ligand 3+, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+, Krüppel‐like factor 2+ (KLF2+)) expressing interleukin 1B, TNF, and AREG, a key mechanism perpetuating chronic inflammation. 14 Furthermore, interleukin‐1β–mediated crosstalk between CCR2+ macrophages and FAP+POSTN+ fibroblasts drives fibrotic remodeling, 21 whereas cardiac aging induces accumulation of bone marrow–derived Lyve1− macrophages and loss of tissue‐resident Lyve1+ macrophages in vascular niches. 22 Given that inflammation, fibrosis, and microvascular dysfunction are central pathologic features of HFpEF, future studies should further investigate how these specific monocyte–macrophage subsets and their intercellular communication networks contribute to the pathogenesis of distinct HFpEF phenotypes.

Human and murine cardiac macrophages originate from 2 distinct lineages with divergent functional and developmental characteristics. 5 Resident cardiac macrophages, which are derived from embryonic hematopoietic progenitors, maintain tissue homeostasis via oxidative phosphorylation. They are characterized by the expression of surface markers, including CD163, lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1, folate receptor β, F13A, T‐cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain‐containing 4, and CD169, while remaining CCR2 negative. 5 These self‐renewing populations perform multifaceted protective functions by suppressing inflammatory responses, promoting tissue repair, 23 regulating cardiomyocyte regeneration, 24 orchestrating coronary angiogenesis, 25 and fine‐tuning adaptive remodeling processes through modulation of electrical conduction patterns and metabolic adaptations. In contrast, monocyte‐derived macrophages originate from definitive hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow and spleen. 12 They rely on glycolysis‐driven metabolism and are recruited to the heart during injury or stress. Typically expressing CCR2 receptors, 26 monocyte‐derived macrophages accumulate with aging or disease progression and exhibit transcriptional heterogeneity across epigenetically distinct subpopulations, as revealed by single‐cell RNA‐sequencing studies. 14 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Unlike their resident counterparts, these cells demonstrate potent proinflammatory activity through cytokine production and cellular interactions, ultimately promoting pathologic remodeling cascades that drive myocardial dysfunction and accelerate the progression of heart failure. 5

Evidence suggests HFpEF manifests as heterogeneous biological endotypes, each demonstrating unique organ‐level pathophenotypes. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 In addition to the classification systems summarized in Table 1, Shah et al have proposed a clinically oriented framework distinguishing between “predisposition phenotypes” (based on risk factors) and “clinical presentation phenotypes” (based on clinical characteristics), which serves as a practical guide for therapeutic decision‐making in patients with HFpEF. 36 Roh et al later categorized HFpEF into 3 subtypes based on preclinical models: age associated, cardiometabolic, and hypertension associated. 3 Borlaug et al proposed a pathophysiology‐based classification consisting of: (1) hypertensive ventricular stiffening, (2) obesity‐cardiometabolic stress, and (3) microvascular inflammation. 37 Through cluster analysis of 20 clinical studies, Peters et al categorized HFpEF into 3 distinct clinical profiles: (1) older, vascular aging; (2) metabolic, obese; and (3) relatively younger, natriuretic peptide deficiency. 38

All above phenotyping from various perspectives enhances our understanding of the heterogeneous nature of HFpEF and helps guide precision medicine. However, regardless of the classification system, different phenotypes often exhibit varying degrees of overlap and are not mutually exclusive.

MACROPHAGE‐DRIVEN VICIOUS CYCLE IN HFpEF

HFpEF and macrophages engage in reciprocal pathophysiological interactions. Established HFpEF risk factors, including aging, metabolic dysregulation, and pressure overload, induce macrophage phenotypic alterations and functional reprogramming. 11 , 18 , 36 These modified macrophages subsequently drive cardiac structural/functional deterioration through dysregulated inflammatory mediator production and maladaptive crosstalk with other cellular components, thereby establishing a self‐perpetuating vicious cycle. 39 , 40 , 41 Notably, cardiac macrophage alterations may precede and actively drive HFpEF development, serving as the primary driver of this pathologic cycle. 11 In this section, we characterize this vicious cycle. We first summarize the landscape of cardiac macrophage phenotypic switching and functional reprogramming in multiphenotypic HFpEF (Figure 3), and subsequently delineate how reprogrammed cardiac macrophages promote pathologic cardiac remodeling.

Changes in macrophages during HFpEF.

Macrophages exhibit distinct phenotypic and functional alterations across HFpEF subtypes, contributing to disease pathogenesis through inflammatory modulation, metabolic reprogramming, and fibrotic responses. In age‐associated HFpEF, macrophages display reduced MHC‐II expression accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired autophagy, and a skewed cytokine profile featuring elevated IL‐6, CXCL8, MMP‐9, TGF‐β, galectin‐3, and NOX2, alongside decreased lipoxygenase activity. Cardiometabolic HFpEF, driven by obesity and diabetes, promotes glycolytic reprogramming in macrophages, leading to expanded both proinflammatory (CD11b+F4/80+CD86+CCR2+Timd4−) and anti‐inflammatory (CD11b+F4/80+CD206+CCR2−Timd4+) subsets, impaired mitochondrial transfer, and elevated IL‐1β, Plin2, TNF‐α, and IL‐6 with reduced TGF‐β. In hypertension‐associated HFpEF, CCR2+ monocyte infiltration is prominent, causing macrophages with low MHC‐II, elevated CXCR4 (with reduced PPARγ), TREM2/sTREM2 upregulation, suppressed cGAS‐STING signaling, and increased OPN, TGF‐β, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. Age‐Associated HFpEF

Macrophages undergo substantial age‐related alterations, initiating with phenotypic shifts accompanied by secretome remodeling and functional reprogramming.

Hulsmans et al demonstrated that in a physiological aging model of HFpEF, cardiac macrophages undergo expansion, a change primarily driven by an age‐related systemic shift in hematopoiesis. 43 This was characterized by a significant increase in hematopoietic stem cells and myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow and spleen, accompanied by upregulation of cardiac C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) expression. 43 These macrophages also exhibited elevated interleukin‐10 expression. 43 In a separate HFpEF model developed by Kucsera et al using aged mice (24 months old) fed a choline‐deficient diet, an increased count of cardiac CD68+ macrophages was observed, along with heightened Il1b expression. 7 Glezeva et al reported that macrophages stimulated with serum from elderly patients with HFpEF (mean age, 79 years) exhibited significantly elevated expression of M2 markers, CD206 (25%) and interleukin‐10 (20%), whereas the levels of M1 markers, including TNF‐α, interleukin‐1β, and CXCL10, remained unchanged. 44

Cardiometabolic HFpEF

Cardiac macrophages undergo significant reprogramming under cardiometabolic stress. Prattichizzo et al and Wang et al demonstrated that hyperglycemia drives expansion of proinflammatory macrophages and induces senescence via GLUT1‐dependent pathways, 45 whereas Wilkinson et al reported the generation of a C‐X‐C motif chemokine receptor 2–enriched senescence‐associated secretory phenotype. 46

In genetically predisposed models, Büttner et al used the obese ZSF1 rat (a model combining obesity, hypertension, and glucose intolerance) and observed a marked increase in cardiac CD68+ macrophage infiltration, particularly in the left ventricle. 47 Franssen et al, also using the ZSF1 model, found that these macrophages exhibit a proinflammatory phenotype characterized by upregulated NADPH oxidase 2 expression, leading to oxidative stress, albeit with potentially suppressed NF‐κB–driven cytokine production. 48

In diet‐induced models, Liu et al demonstrated that in a high‐fat diet model, cardiac MCP (monocyte chemoattractant protein)‐1 is upregulated, promoting the accumulation of proinflammatory macrophage subsets (CD11b+F4/80+CD86+CCR2+Timd4−) and a decrease in anti‐inflammatory (CD11b+F4/80+CD206+) and resident (CCR2−Timd4+) macrophages. 49 They further showed that these inflammatory macrophages secrete elevated interleukin‐1β, promote mitochondrial reactive oxygen species in cardiomyocytes, and exhibit reduced TGF‐β. 49 Dona et al, using a Western diet in female mice, observed an increase in CD11b+CD80+ macrophages and activation markers (CD86 and Cd83), alongside a proinflammatory gene signature and Plin2 upregulation. 50 King et al and Dick et al reported that in apolipoprotein E knockout mice fed a Western diet, diastolic impairment is associated with monocyte expansion and loss of anti‐inflammatory Timd4+ macrophages. 51 , 52

In multihit models, Li et al used a combination of high‐fat diet and angiotensin II infusion and observed increased cardiac CD68+ and galectin‐3+ macrophage infiltration, alongside upregulation of proinflammatory and profibrotic genes. 53 Zhang et al, in a HFpEF secondary to metabolic syndrome model, found an increase in cardiac CD64+ macrophages, which was associated with a proinflammatory cardiac oxylipin profile (elevated 12‐HETE/18‐HEPE ratio). 54

Sharma et al identified that obesity‐induced adipose tissue hypoxia activates hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α in macrophages, triggering glycolytic reprogramming with interleukin‐1β overproduction. 55 Zhang et al further suggested that this hypoxic environment can upregulate FOS and ALOX5, amplifying senescence‐associated secretory phenotype and profibrotic signaling. 56

Hypertension‐Associated HFpEF

In hypertension‐associated HFpEF, pressure overload and neurohumoral activation are central drivers of cardiac macrophage reprogramming. 43 These models can be broadly categorized as follows. In neurohumoral activation models, such as the SAUNA model (combining salty drinking water, unilateral nephrectomy, and chronic aldosterone infusion), cardiac macrophage expansion is driven by monocyte recruitment and systemic activation of hematopoiesis in the bone marrow and spleen. 43 These macrophages shift toward a profibrotic phenotype marked by elevated interleukin‐10 production, which acts autocrinely to promote osteopontin and TGF‐β secretion, indirectly activating cardiac fibroblasts and stimulating collagen deposition, thereby impairing myocardial relaxation and increasing stiffness. 43 Similarly, in models involving mineralocorticoid receptor activation, macrophages expand and adopt a profibrotic program characterized by increased interleukin‐10 secretion, contributing to fibroblast activation and diastolic dysfunction. 57 In DOCA‐salt hypertensive models, CD68+ macrophages accumulate prominently within fibrotic regions and upregulate TGF‐β1 expression. 58 This process is driven by CD8+ T‐cell–mediated cytotoxicity enhancing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and subsequent phagocytosis by macrophages. Stressed cardiomyocytes release MCP‐1 via the STING‐dependent pathway, promoting macrophage recruitment to the heart. 58

In pressure overload and angiotensin II models, transverse aortic constriction induces significant expansion of cardiac CCR2+ monocyte‐derived macrophages, a process driven by cardiac induction of CC chemokines leading to increased circulating Ly6ChiCCR2+ monocytes and their subsequent myocardial infiltration. 59 These CCR2+ macrophages exhibit a proinflammatory phenotype and contribute to adverse remodeling by secreting proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin‐1β and TNF‐α, activating T cells in mediastinal lymph nodes, and promoting fibroblast activation and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. 59 In pressure‐overload models relevant to HFpEF, recruited and activated macrophages secrete a range of fibrogenic mediators, including cytokines (eg, TNF‐α, interleukin‐1β, and interleukin‐6), growth factors (eg, TGF‐β), and matricellular proteins, which collectively enhance extracellular matrix deposition and myocardial stiffness. 57

In multihit models that combine hypertensive stimuli with other metabolic or stress factors, distinct macrophage alterations are observed. The combinatory model of high‐fat diet and N‐nitro‐L‐arginine methyl ester induces cardiac inflammation accompanied by an increase in cardiac macrophages exhibiting enhanced inducible nitric oxide synthase expression, indicating a proinflammatory M1‐like phenotype. 42 In the “3‐hit” HFpEF mouse model combining cardiomyocyte‐specific CaVβ2a overexpression, high‐fat diet, and N‐nitro‐L‐arginine methyl ester, significant alterations in cardiac macrophages occur, particularly a marked expansion of profibrotic M₂‐like macrophages (CD206+CD68+). 60 These macrophages are associated with enhanced TGF‐β mRNA expression and increased phosphorylation of Smad2/3, indicating activation of the TGF‐β signaling pathway. 60 In a hypertensive and obese mouse model induced by combined high‐fat diet and N‐nitro‐L‐arginine methyl ester administration, cardiac resident macrophages undergo substantial changes characterized by accumulation of monocyte‐derived CCR2+ macrophages. 10 This expansion is associated with a proinflammatory and profibrotic shift, marked by elevated secreted phosphoprotein 1 (Spp1) expression in CCR2+ macrophages. Conversely, transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation reverses these changes by reducing CCR2+ macrophage numbers and suppressing Spp1 expression, while simultaneously inducing a reparative phenotype in T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma family+ and major histocompatibility complex 2+ macrophages through upregulation of Igf1. 10

Reprogrammed Macrophage in HFpEF Pathogenesis

As stated above and summarized in Table 3, the phenotypic and functional reprogramming of cardiac macrophages is not merely a passive consequence of HFpEF; rather, it actively instigates and amplifies the pathologic remodeling that defines the syndrome. Following their alteration by HFpEF risk factors, these reprogrammed macrophages engage in maladaptive crosstalk with other cardiac cells, thereby executing a diverse array of pathogenic programs. The specific mechanisms through which they contribute to cardiac dysfunction, however, vary significantly across the different phenotypic subtypes of HFpEF, as detailed below and summarized in Figure 4.

Age‐Associated HFpEF

Inflammation and fibrosis constitute 2 pivotal mechanisms through which macrophages contribute to heart failure pathogenesis.

Systemic inflammation, aging, and HFpEF demonstrate strong interconnections. 61 Parabiosis experiments reveal that young hearts exposed to aged blood acquire HFpEF‐like features, whereas aged hearts regain functional capacity when exposed to young blood. 62 Trial et al demonstrated that targeted inhibition of TNF‐α+ proinflammatory macrophages in an aging female‐induced HFpEF model improved diastolic function by reducing cardiac inflammatory infiltration and collagen deposition. 63 Using a choline‐deficient diet‐induced aged mouse model, Kucsera et al revealed that CD68+ macrophages drive a proinflammatory cardiac environment via interleukin‐1β and C‐C motif chemokine ligand 2 secretion, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of age‐related HFpEF. 7

In conclusion, inflammation and fibrosis constitute 2 pivotal mechanisms through which macrophages contribute to age‐associated HFpEF pathogenesis.

Cardiometabolic HFpEF

In cardiometabolic HFpEF, macrophages serve as central orchestrators of diastolic dysfunction through 3 interconnected pathogenic pathways: metabolic reprogramming, inflammatory amplification, and fibrotic activation.

Metabolic reprogramming of macrophages constitutes the initial trigger. Obesity‐associated adipose tissue hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α in macrophages, driving a glycolytic shift with suppressed oxidative phosphorylation and succinate accumulation. 55 This metabolic switch promotes interleukin‐1β overproduction via the NLR family pyrin domain‐containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, establishing a proinflammatory milieu. 55 Concurrently, lipid overload activates resident cardiac macrophages, which adopt proinflammatory phenotypes and recruit circulating leukocytes through MCP‐1/CCR2 signaling. 49 The metabolic stress extends to hematopoietic niches, where Vcam1 upregulation remodels bone marrow microenvironments to favor monocyte‐derived macrophage production, 9 perpetuating cardiac infiltration.

Inflammatory amplification sustains and propagates myocardial dysfunction. Epicardial adipose tissue–derived adipokines polarize recruited macrophages toward a glycolytic, proinflammatory state characterized by interleukin‐6, TNF‐α, and sustained interleukin‐1β secretion. 64 , 65 Interleukin‐1β emerges as a critical effector: it amplifies mitochondrial reactive oxygen species in adjacent cardiomyocytes, directly impairing relaxation and promoting arrhythmogenicity. 66 Importantly, therapeutic depletion of proinflammatory macrophages or interleukin‐1β/mitochondrial reactive oxygen species inhibition rescues diastolic dysfunction without correcting systemic metabolism, 49 establishing that inflammation, rather than metabolic derangement per se, drives cardiac dysfunction at this stage. Clinical evidence supports this paradigm: CD64+ macrophage abundance correlates positively with diastolic dysfunction severity and inversely with microvascular density in patients with metabolic syndrome–associated HFpEF. 54

Fibrotic activation represents the final common pathway to myocardial stiffening. Macrophage‐derived TGF‐β, interleukin‐6, and MCP‐1 promote fibroblast‐to‐myofibroblast transition and excessive extracellular matrix deposition. 67 Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α and TGF‐β cooperatively upregulate matrix metalloproteinase‐2/9 and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase‐1, disrupting matrix homeostasis. 68 This fibrotic response extends hypoxia from adipose tissue to the myocardium by reducing capillary density, 69 creating a self‐amplifying loop that perpetuates macrophage recruitment and activation. During resolution phases, M2 macrophages secrete TGF‐β1, which can paradoxically increase stiffness if inflammation persists. 67

These 3 pathways converge to establish a vicious cycle: metabolic stress drives macrophage reprogramming, inflammatory mediators sustain activation, and fibrotic remodeling cements structural dysfunction.

Hypertension‐Associated HFpEF

In hypertension‐associated HFpEF, monocyte‐derived macrophages orchestrate adverse cardiac remodeling through 3 interconnected pathogenic pathways: monocyte recruitment and accumulation, macrophage‐fibroblast crosstalk, and T‐cell–mediated amplification of fibrosis.

Monocyte recruitment and accumulation represent the initiating event. Pressure overload and neurohumoral activation induce cardiac expression of CC chemokines, promoting infiltration of circulating Ly6ChiCCR2+ monocytes and their differentiation into proinflammatory macrophages. 59 Lineage tracing studies demonstrate that CCR2+ monocyte‐derived macrophages expand markedly in hypertensive hearts, whereas tissue‐resident CCR2− macrophages are progressively diluted. 59 The pathogenic significance of this recruitment is underscored by loss‐of‐function studies: macrophage depletion or CCR2 blockade prevents hypertension‐induced end‐organ damage and diastolic dysfunction, 70 establishing a causal role for recruited macrophages. In the SAUNA model (salt, unilateral nephrectomy, and aldosterone), cardiac macrophage expansion is driven by systemic activation of hematopoiesis in bone marrow and spleen, linking neurohumoral stimulation to myeloid cell production. 43

Macrophage‐fibroblast crosstalk drives perivascular and interstitial fibrosis. Recruited macrophages secrete a repertoire of fibrogenic mediators that activate cardiac fibroblasts. Spp1 (osteopontin) emerges as a key effector: Spp1+ macrophages accumulate in fibrotic niches and promote myofibroblast differentiation through paracrine signaling. 71 C‐X‐C motif chemokine receptor 4+ macrophages contribute to fibroblast‐to‐myofibroblast transition via CXCL3, 57 whereas TGF‐β1 secretion initiates collagen production 72 and establishes autoregulatory loops that sustain its own expression. 73 interleukin‐1β from macrophages enhances fibrotic responses and directly impairs diastolic function. 74

T‐cell–mediated amplification sustains and propagates the fibrotic response. Monocytes recruit T cells via CXCL9/10/11–C‐X‐C motif chemokine receptor 3 signaling, 75 creating a positive feedback loop. Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells amplify macrophage TGF‐β1 production through crosstalk with stressed cardiomyocytes: cardiomyocyte apoptosis triggers efferocytosis by macrophages, which, in turn, upregulate TGF‐β1 in a CD36‐dependent manner. 58 , 76 CCR2 blockade attenuates T‐cell activation and early left ventricular hypertrophy, 59 highlighting the interdependence of myeloid and lymphoid compartments.

These pathways converge to establish a self‐perpetuating cycle of extracellular matrix remodeling and diastolic dysfunction. Diffuse collagen accumulation increases ventricular stiffness, impairs relaxation, 41 and promotes arrhythmogenicity. 77 Fibrosis emerges as a major determinant of morbidity and mortality in hypertensive HFpEF. 78 Importantly, the pathogenic loop can be interrupted: transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation reduces CCR2+ macrophage numbers, suppresses Spp1 expression, and induces a reparative phenotype in T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma family+ and major histocompatibility complex 2+ macrophages through Igf1 upregulation, 10 suggesting that targeting macrophage recruitment and polarization may break the inflammation‐fibrosis cycle.

THERAPEUTIC IMPLICATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The Current Mainstream Treatment Methods

Contemporary guideline‐directed medical therapies for HFpEF have demonstrated significant clinical benefits, with emerging evidence suggesting their effects may be partly mediated through immunometabolic modulation. Sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (namely, empagliflozin and dapagliflozin) have robustly reduced the composite risk of cardiovascular death or hospitalization for HF in patients with HFpEF across the ejection fraction spectrum, as evidenced by the Empagliflozin Outcome Trial in Patients With Chronic Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction (EMPEROR)‐Preserved 79 and Dapagliflozin Evaluation to Improve the LIVEs of Patients With PReserved Ejection Fraction Heart Failure (DELIVER) 80 trials. Beyond their hemodynamic and metabolic effects, sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors exert direct anti‐inflammatory actions relevant to HFpEF pathogenesis. A recent Mendelian randomization study provides genetic evidence supporting the anti‐inflammatory effects of Sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibition, demonstrating that the chemokine CXCL10 mediates ≈17.85% of the protective effect against HF. 81 Similarly, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, specifically semaglutide, have shown marked efficacy in the distinct obese phenotype of HFpEF. The STEP‐HFpEF trials demonstrated substantial improvements in symptoms, physical limitations, and exercise function, alongside profound weight loss and reduced systemic inflammation, as measured by hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein). 82 Although not designed to target immune cells directly, these therapies are associated with improvements in inflammatory burden.

Interventions on Macrophages

Interleukin‐1 Antagonists

Preclinically, Liu et al demonstrated that interleukin‐1β mediates high‐fat diet–induced HFpEF, and its inhibition rescued diastolic dysfunction in mouse models. 49 This mechanistic insight is powerfully translated by the Canakinumab Anti‐inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) trial, which provided critical proof of concept that inhibiting the innate immune system via interleukin‐1β blockade with canakinumab reduces recurrent cardiovascular events in patients with prior myocardial infarction. 83 However, the increased risk of fatal infections observed with canakinumab in this trial underscores the critical need for careful patient selection and monitoring when implementing such potent immunomodulatory strategies. 84

NLRP3 Inflammasome Inhibitors

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a critical intracellular complex in macrophages that cleaves pro‐interleukin‐1β into its active, secreted form, making it an upstream target of interleukin‐1β. In the context of HFpEF, preclinical evidence supports this approach, as Pinar et al have reviewed the potential of targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome to treat cardiovascular fibrosis, a key pathologic feature in HFpEF. 85 The oral agent colchicine, an oral NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor with broad anti‐inflammatory effects, has demonstrated efficacy in reducing cardiovascular events in postinfarction (COLCOT 86 ) and chronic coronary disease (LoDoCo2 87 ). These clinical successes highlight the potential of targeting the inflammasome. However, the specific role of colchicine or other more specific NLRP3 inhibitors in HFpEF awaits formal investigation in dedicated clinical trials.

Chemokine Inhibitors

In hypertension‐associated HFpEF, where CCR2+ monocyte‐derived macrophages are key drivers of perivascular fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction, 59 antagonists of chemokine receptors, such as CCR2 or CCR5, offer a precise intervention. Preclinically, Patel et al demonstrated that blocking CCR2+ monocyte infiltration attenuates T‐cell activation, reduces macrophage accumulation, and preserves diastolic function in pressure‐overload models. 59 Chan et al similarly showed that a CCR2 antagonist, INCB3344 (a CCR2 antagonist), could reverse vascular macrophage accumulation in a hypertensive mouse model. 88 INCB3344 is a potent and selective small‐molecule antagonist of the mouse CCR2 receptor, representing 1 of the first rodent‐active CCR2 antagonists available for preclinical pharmacologic studies. 89 The clinical translation of this approach is under investigation, as targeting the CCR2/5 axis represents a promising strategy to specifically limit the influx of proinflammatory and profibrotic macrophages in selected HFpEF phenotypes without causing broad immunosuppression. 90

LIMITATIONS OF CURRENT EVIDENCE

Despite growing evidence implicating macrophages in HFpEF pathogenesis, most findings to date remain associative rather than causal. Most studies have identified phenotypic alterations in macrophage subsets without establishing whether these changes actively drive disease or merely reflect underlying pathology. Preclinical models, although informative, imperfectly recapitulate the complexity of human HFpEF, and the lack of phenotype‐stratified mechanistic studies limits our ability to link specific macrophage populations to distinct clinical subtypes.

Furthermore, the remarkable plasticity of macrophages poses challenges for therapeutic targeting, as subset‐specific functions may shift across disease stages and tissue contexts. Future investigations using lineage‐specific depletion, single‐cell resolution tracking, and cell‐state–targeted interventions are urgently needed to establish direct causal relationships between macrophage activity and HFpEF pathogenesis. This study revealed that immune cell abundance in the HFpEF myocardium is comparable to that of nonfailing controls, questioning the direct role of local myocardial inflammation as a universal driver of the syndrome. 91 This finding underscores the need to reevaluate the source and nature of the inflammatory signals: whether they are systemic, derived from noncardiomyocyte niches like the epicardial adipose tissue, or transient and thus missed in end‐stage human samples. These complexities highlight that future investigations using lineage‐specific depletion, single‐cell and spatial resolution tracking in human tissues, and cell‐state–targeted interventions are urgently needed to establish direct causal relationships between specific macrophage activities and HFpEF pathogenesis.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this review underscores the pivotal role of macrophage‐driven intercellular crosstalk in the pathogenesis of multiphenotypic HFpEF. It is evident that a self‐perpetuating vicious cycle exists: systemic risk factors (eg, aging, metabolic dysfunction, and hypertension) trigger the recruitment and reprogramming of cardiac macrophages, which, in turn,+ become primary effectors of myocardial remodeling through the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, promotion of fibrosis, and disruption of metabolic homeostasis. The heterogeneity of HFpEF is mirrored by the distinct phenotypic alterations and pathogenic mechanisms exhibited by macrophages in each subtype, necessitating a nuanced understanding of these context‐dependent roles.

Moving forward, breaking this macrophage‐centered vicious cycle holds immense therapeutic promise. Future research should focus on elucidating precise molecular targets within specific macrophage subpopulations and their signaling pathways. Developing strategies to modulate macrophage polarization, inhibit their proinflammatory and profibrotic secretome, or enhance their reparative functions could pave the way for novel, phenotype‐specific treatments. Ultimately, shifting the focus from a purely cardiocentric view to one that targets immune‐metabolic crosstalk may be the key to effectively managing the complex and growing burden of HFpEF.
