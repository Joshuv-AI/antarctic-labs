Joint Identification of Linear Dynamics and Noise Covariance via Distributional Estimation Journal: Systems & Control Letters
Yang Hu, Na Li

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a novel framework for the joint identification of system dynamics and noise covariance in linear systems, under general noise distributions beyond Gaussian. Specifically, we would like to simultaneously estimate the dynamical matrix A A and the noise covariance matrix Σ \varSigma using state transition data. The formulation builds upon a novel parameterization of the state-transition distribution, which enables more effective use of distributional “shape” information for improved identification accuracy. We introduce two practical estimators, namely the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) and the score-matching estimator (SME), to solve the joint dynamics-covariance identification problem, and provide rigorous analysis of their statistical properties and sample complexity. Simulation results show that the proposed estimators outperform the ordinary least squares (OLS) baseline.

Introduction

System identification—the problem of estimating system dynamics using state transition data—has been a long-standing research field with broad applications, ranging from control and robotics to econometrics and neuroscience. In the past, classical theoretical results in this field mainly focused on the consistency (i.e., asymptotic convergence) of estimators, including ordinary least-squares (OLS) estimators
[
15
]
and maximum likelihood estimators (MLE)
[
16
]
. The more recent advances in learning theory have brought significant progresses in establishing finite-time convergence guarantees for these estimators
[
26
,
23
,
21
,
12
]
. These developments have, in turn, laid foundations for finite-time regret analyses in downstream control synthesis tasks
[
5
,
17
,
6
,
25
]
.

However, these finite-time analyses of linear system identification have primarily focused on estimating the nominal system dynamics (e.g., the dynamic matrix
A
for autonomous linear systems), typically under strong simplifying assumptions on the noise model like isotropic Gaussian or sub-Gaussian noise. Such assumptions do facilitate tractable analysis and favorable sample complexity guarantees, though at the risk of over-simplifying real-world systems by ignoring the crucial “shape” information hidden in the noise distribution, such as its tail behavior and higher-order moments. For instance, the success of OLS-based estimators in identifying nominal dynamics is known to implicitly rely on the light-tailed nature of the noise, which may not hold in practice and could lead to performance degradation.

Beyond the question of whether such noise assumptions are realistic or not, another important yet often overlooked issue is the identification of the noise covariance structure itself. Even under the classical assumption of light-tailed Gaussian noise, estimating the noise covariance remains a meaningful and practically relevant task. Such information plays a pivotal role in a variety of downstream applications, including distributionally robust control
[
33
,
29
]
, risk-sensitive control
[
13
,
9
]
, and adaptive Kalman filtering
[
10
,
14
]
, where knowledge of the noise covariance is typically assumed a priori. Therefore, there is a clear need to move beyond merely identifying the nominal dynamics and to develop efficient methods for jointly identifying both the system dynamics and the underlying noise structure.

These observations have recently spurred growing interest in noise modeling and identification. Following the pioneering work
[
19
]
, multiple covariance identification methods have been designed in the past decade, including auto-covariance least-squares (ALS) methods
[
20
,
8
]
and MLE methods
[
2
,
32
]
. However, these methods only focus on the noise covariance identification and generally assume known system dynamics. In brief, while established methods exist for estimating system dynamics or noise covariance separately, principled approaches for the joint identification of both are still lacking.

One intuitive approach to bridge the above gap between system identification and noise covariance identification is to resort to a two-stage scheme—first identifying the nominal dynamics, and then recovering the noise covariance using the identified dynamics. However, as discussed later in this paper, such a tandem approach is technically equivalent to assuming Gaussian noise, thereby failing to capture the richer “shape” information presented in more general noise models. These challenges motivate the following research question:

Is it possible to perform joint identification of dynamics

and noise covariance for generic noise distribution?

Here by “joint identification” we explicitly mean to rule out the tandem approach stated above. Instead, we would like to reparameterize the problem to encapsulate both the dynamics and the covariance into a uniform set of parameters, hence enabling algorithms that effectively and efficiently estimate the parameters for the joint identification. As we will see later, an appropriate generic parameterization scheme is critical to this task.

Contributions.
In this paper, we propose to model noise distribution as a unit-covariance base distribution scaled by the covariance matrix, such that the state transition distribution belongs to a density family parameterized by the dynamical matrix
A
and the scale matrix
\varSigma
. Such reparameterization is favored in that it is general enough to include a wide range of known noise distributions, and that it directly encodes
A
and
\varSigma
in the parameters to reformulate the joint identification task as a parameter estimation problem. Based on such reparameterization, we propose two novel estimators, i.e. the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) and the score-matching estimator (SME), that outperform the naive ordinary least-squares (OLS) estimator via theoretical arguments and empirical evidence.

The contribution of this paper is two-fold.

1.

We establish the connection between state transition in linear systems subject to generic stochastic noise and
\phi
-density families, and highlight an important sub-collection of elliptical families that greatly facilitates computation. As far as we know, this is the first paper to study the problem of joint identification of linear dynamics and noise covariance (the “
joint identification
” problem) and propose an appropriate model to reformulate it as a parameter estimation problem.

2.

We propose two novel estimators, namely MLE and SME, to solve the joint identification problem, along with a characterization of their key properties and a preliminary sample complexity analysis. The performance of the estimators is compared against the naive OLS baseline and justified by empirical evaluation results.

Problem Formulation and Preliminaries

In this section, we first formulate our system setting and the joint identification problem, and then provide some necessary preliminaries on the candidate distribution families.

2.1
Problem Formulation

System Setting.
Consider a linear time-invariant (LTI) system

x^{\prime}=A_{\star}x+\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}w,

(1)

where
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the current and next
state
of the system, respectively;
A_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the
dynamical
matrix, and
\varSigma_{\star}\in\mathcal{S}_{\succ\bm{0}}^{d}
is the
scale
matrix that regulates the covariance of the effective noise input
\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}w
, both of which are unknown and to be estimated;
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an i.i.d.
unit noise
with zero mean (
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[w}}\right]=\bm{0}
) and unit covariance (
\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[w}}\right]=I
). We emphasize that, as discussed in the introduction, we would like to handle
general
noise distributions, rather than being confined to any specific choices like Gaussian noise. Specifically, we assume a known
base density

\phi(\cdot)
of the unit noise
w
, which is allowed to take
any
form, as long as it satisfies the following regularity assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Base density)
.

The base density
\phi
is positive and twice continuously differentiable, and induces a finite first-order moment. Further, denote the gradient and Hessian of
\log\phi(w)
by
\bdxscr{g}(w)
and
\mathcal{H}(w)
, respectively; i.e.,

\displaystyle\bdxscr{g}(w)

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{w}\log\phi(w)=\frac{\nabla_{w}\phi(w)}{\phi(w)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(w)

\displaystyle:=\nabla^{2}_{w}\log\phi(w)=\nabla_{w}\bdxscr{g}(w).

As will be seen later, the parameter estimators generally involve log-densities and their derivatives, and the assumption is hence made to facilitate the design of such estimators.

The Joint Identification Problem.
On a high level, the goal of the proposed
joint identification
problem is to identify the dynamical and scale matrices
(A,\varSigma)
, using state transition data
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})\mid i\in[N]\}
. Note that, in general, we don’t make assumptions on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, which is allowed to contain both single- and multiple-trajectory data.

A natural and concrete way to solve this joint identification problem is to convert it into a parameter estimation problem, which leads to various well-known estimators including the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) and the score-matching estimator (SME) proposed below. Specifically, for any loss function
\ell(x,x^{\prime};A,\varSigma)
penalizing “less likely” transitions from
x
to
x^{\prime}
under parameters
(A,\varSigma)
, the corresponding estimator is

(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma}):=\arg\min_{A,\varSigma}\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i};A,\varSigma).

(2)

The high-level goal of the remaining paper is to construct proper losses
\ell
and characterize the corresponding estimators in terms of statistical properties including sample complexity.

The Elliptical Family.
Despite the generality of
\phi
-density families, it could be analytically and computationally hard to handle the log-densities and their derivatives in closed forms. To further facilitate the design and analysis of algorithms, we consider an important special type of
\phi

Definition 1

(Elliptical density)
.

A density
\phi
is called

if there exists a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi(w)=\psi(w^{\top}w)
.

Elliptical base densities are favorable since the derivatives of their log-densities can be written in simple closed forms.

Lemma 1
.

For an elliptical base density
\phi(w)=\psi(w^{\top}w)
, the gradient
\bdxscr{g}(w)
and Hessian
\mathcal{H}(w)
of
\log\phi(w)
are given by

\displaystyle\bdxscr{g}(w)

\displaystyle=\rho(w^{\top}w)w,

(5a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(w)

\displaystyle=\rho(w^{\top}w)I+\tau(w^{\top}w)ww^{\top},

(5b)

where the functions
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\tau:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are specified by

\displaystyle\rho(z)

\displaystyle:=2\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\big[\log\psi(z)\big]=\frac{2\psi^{\prime}(z)}{\psi(z)},

\displaystyle\tau(z)

\displaystyle:=4\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}\big[\log\psi(z)\big]=\frac{4\big(\psi^{\prime\prime}(z)\psi(z)-\psi^{\prime}(z)^{2}\big)}{\psi^{2}(z)}.

Proof.

By direct calculation. See
A

∎

3
Joint Identification

In this section, we provide our solutions to the joint identification problem formulated above. We start by a motivating section that illustrates the issue with naive ordinary least-squares (OLS) estimators. Then we proceed to introduce two well-known estimators that solve the problem, namely the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) and the score-matching estimator (SME), along with their characterizations and convergence properties in our setting. Finally, we present a sample complexity guarantee for the proposed estimators.

3.1
The Naive OLS estimator and its limitations

At first glance, one may naturally wonder how this problem could potentially be different from the standard system identification problem that only identifies the
A
matrix (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
). Indeed, it is intuitive to come up with the following estimator
(\hat{A}_{\mathrm{ols}},\hat{\varSigma}_{\mathrm{ols}})

(6)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\hat{A}_{\mathrm{ols}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1},\\

(7)

However, a major concern of the above two-step procedure is that it completely ignores the information encoded in the
\phi
-density family of candidate distributions. To briefly illustrate this point, we consider the 1-dimensional special case
2

\displaystyle\bm{0}

The above characterization appears to be complicated for a generic

Corollary 5

(MLE characterization, elliptical family)
.

For an elliptical family
\{f_{A,\varSigma}\}
with base density
\phi(w)=\psi(w^{\top}w)

1

satisfies the following system of equations:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\hat{A}_{\mathrm{mle}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1},\\

(10)

where

are dataset-specific coefficients determined by the base density, where
\rho(z)=\frac{2\psi^{\prime}(z)}{\psi(z)}
(see
Lemma

1

Proof.

Plugging
Lemma

1
into
Lemma

∎

The above corollary clearly reveals the connection of MLE and the OLS estimator for elliptical families. On the one hand, MLE is in general different from the OLS estimator when
\rho_{i}

\rho_{i}\equiv-1
is constant and is thus canceled out). On the other hand, MLE for elliptical families take a similar form as OLS estimators, which can be viewed a reweighted version of the OLS formula. Indeed, since
|\rho(z)|\to 0
as

3.3
The Score-Matching Estimator (SME)

In this section, we consider another type of estimators, i.e. the score-matching estimators (SME) first introduced in

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{\prime})\sim d^{\mathcal{D}}}\Bigl[\big\|\nabla_{x^{\prime}}\log f_{A,\varSigma}(x^{\prime}|x)\big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt{}+2\tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{x^{\prime}}^{2}\log f_{A,\varSigma}(x^{\prime}|x)}}\right)\Bigr].

Proof.

See Appendix A of

3.4
Unified Estimators for Gaussian Noise

As briefly discussed above, it is intuitive to expect that the MLE and SME estimators coincide with the naive OLS estimator for the Gaussian family, which is indeed the case and can be easily verified as a sanity check in the following lemma.

Lemma 9

(Unified Gaussian estimators)
.

For the Gaussian family (i.e., one with base density
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\big(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\big)
), under the regularity assumption that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\succ\bm{0}
, the MLE and SME estimators both coincide with the naive OLS estimator, i.e.

(\hat{A}_{\mathrm{mle}},\hat{\varSigma}_{\mathrm{mle}})=(\hat{A}_{\mathrm{sme}},\hat{\varSigma}_{\mathrm{sme}})=(\hat{A}_{\mathrm{ols}},\hat{\varSigma}_{\mathrm{ols}}).

Proof.

Note that
\rho(z)\equiv-1
and
\tau(z)\equiv 0
, and the results follows from
Corollary

5
and
Lemma

8
immediately.
∎

The above lemma also serves as a piece of supporting evidence that the MLE and SME estimators are supposed to outperform the naive OLS estimator by leveraging the shape information implicitly encoded in the
\phi
-density family. On the contrary, since Gaussian distribution is the max-entropy distribution of prescribed mean and covariance, the estimators naturally degenerate into the naive OLS estimators due to a lack of additional structural information.

3.5
Numerical Implementations

In this section, we briefly discuss how to numerically compute the proposed OLS, MLE and SME estimators efficiently.

OLS.
The OLS estimator can be computed either using the closed-form formula in (
7
), or by solving the optimization in (
6
) using a generic gradient-based solver, where the latter may be preferred for large datasets to avoid numerical issues.

MLE and SME.
In general, the MLE and SME estimators should be computed by solving the optimization formulated in (
9
) and (
11
), respectively, using a generic gradient-based solver.

Alternatively, since MLE and SME for elliptical families are known to be reweighted OLS estimators in the form

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\hat{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})x^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1},\\
\hat{\varSigma}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa_{i}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})\big(x^{\prime}_{i}-\hat{A}x_{i}\big)\big(x^{\prime}_{i}-\hat{A}x_{i}\big)^{\top},\end{array}}}\right.

with weight functions
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
and
\kappa_{i}(\cdot)
determined by data
(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})
, we may apply the following iterative algorithm that produces a sequence
\{(\hat{A}_{k},\hat{\varSigma}_{k})\mid k=1,2,\ldots\}
, where

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\hat{A}_{k+1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\hat{A}_{k},\hat{\varSigma}_{k})x^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\hat{A}_{k},\hat{\varSigma}_{k})x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1},\\
\hat{\varSigma}_{k+1}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa_{i}(\hat{A}_{k},\hat{\varSigma}_{k})\big(x^{\prime}_{i}-\hat{A}x_{i}\big)\big(x^{\prime}_{i}-\hat{A}x_{i}\big)^{\top}.\end{array}}}\right.

It can be verified that the above iterative algorithm converges to the optimum for certain estimators and certain distributions (for example, MLE for the multivariate Student-t distribution, as shown in
Section

4
below), but a general convergence guarantee is hard to establish. Curious readers are referred to Chapter 13 of
[
3
]
for a set of sufficient conditions that guarantee uniqueness and convergence of the iterative algorithm in the case without dynamics (i.e., the location parameter is simply
\mu
instead of
Ax_{i}
). We leave the detailed analysis of this algorithm as future work.

3.6
Single-trajectory Sample Complexity

In this section, we present a single-trajectory sample complexity analysis for the proposed MLE and SME estimators in the case where the system is stable and the noise is sub-Gaussian.

Assumption 3

(Single-trajectory data)
.

\mathcal{D}
contains transitions collected from a single trajectory; i.e., we have

subject to the dynamics
x_{i}=A_{\star}x_{i-1}+\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}w_{i-1}

Assumption 4

(Sub-Gaussian noise)
.

The noise

is i.i.d. sub-Gaussian with

\sigma^{2}
(see
Definition

2

Assumption 5

(Stability)

The system is stable in the sense that
\rho(A_{\star})<1
, and that the Gramian

is bounded by
\varGamma
. Consequently,
\|x_{i}\|\leq C_{x}
,
\forall i\in[N]
.

Assumption 6

(Bounded
\varTheta
)
.

The hypothesis space
\varTheta
is bounded such that
\|A\|\leq C_{A}
,
\forall A\in\varTheta_{A}
and
\|\varSigma^{-1/2}\|\leq C_{\varSigma}

Theorem 10

(Sample Complexity)
.

1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
and
6

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d\log(d/\delta)}{N}},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\varSigma}-\varSigma_{\star}}}\right\|

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we study sample-efficient joint identification of linear dynamics and noise covariance for generic noise distributions via a novel parameterization of the state-transition distribution. Building upon both theoretical and experimental evidence, we highlight the necessity to leverage the “shape” information of distributions that is implicitly encoded in the
\phi
-density families, and propose two practical estimators (i.e., MLE and SME) to solve the joint identification problem, along with characterization of their key properties and a sample complexity guarantee. Simulation results confirms the outperformance of the proposed estimators against the naive OLS baseline at higher computational costs, showing a trade-off between sample complexity and computational efficiency. Future work includes discovering better estimators to account for a wider range of distribution families and designing more efficient algorithms to compute the estimators.

Appendix B
Sample Complexity Analysis

In this section, we present our sample complexity analysis in a generic way. Specifically, consider any
unbiased
expected loss
\mathcal{L}(A,\varSigma)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell\big(\varSigma^{-1/2}(x^{\prime}-Ax),\varSigma\big)}}\right]
, i.e.,

(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})=\arg\min_{A,\varSigma}\mathcal{L}(A,\varSigma).

The corresponding estimator for
(A,\varSigma)
is constructed by minimizing its empirical version, namely

\displaystyle(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})

\displaystyle:=\arg\min_{A,\varSigma}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\varSigma),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\varSigma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\big(\varSigma^{-1/2}(x^{\prime}_{i}-Ax_{i}),\varSigma\big).

In this section, we make the following assumption on
\ell
.

Assumption 7

(Well-conditioned losses)
.

\ell(w,\varSigma)
is
L
-smooth in
w
, and
\mu
-strongly convex in both
w
and
\varSigma
;
\ell(0,\varSigma)=0
,
\forall\varSigma\in\varTheta_{\varSigma}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell(w,\varSigma)}}\right]\leq C_{\ell}
.

In addition, prompted by
Corollary

5
and
Lemma

8
, we also assume that the estimator
\hat{A}
can be expressed in a reweighted OLS form, as summarized in the following assumption.

Assumption 8

(Reweighted OLS form)
.

There exist weights
\lambda_{i}\in(\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+})
such that
\hat{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1}
.

We point out that assuming bounded instance-specific weights
\lambda_{i}
’s should not be deemed as restrictive in the following sense. In practice, we usually have an initial guess of
(A,\varSigma)
, especially when we have some prior knowledge of the system. When the hypothesis space
\varTheta
is selected as a sufficiently neighborhood around
(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})
, it can be shown that such bounds
\lambda_{+}>\lambda_{-}>0
exist with high probability, owing to the continuity of the solution in
A
and
\varSigma
.

The following lemma specifies the sample complexity bound for estimating
A
using data collected from a single trajectory.

Lemma 11
.

Under Assumptions
3
,
5
,
7
and
8
, given any
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, there exists an
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
where
N_{0}=\tilde{\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\big(\|\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}\|\sigma\big)^{2}d\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}}\right)
, such that for any
N>N_{0}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{A}-A_{\star}}}\right\|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d\log(d/\delta)}{N}}.

Proof.

It is straight-forward to bound the error as follows:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{A}-A_{\star}}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1}-A_{\star}}}\right\|

(13a)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(x^{\prime}_{i}-A_{\star}x_{i})x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1}}}\right\|

(13b)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1}}}\right\|

(13c)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1}}}\right\|

(13d)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1/2}}}\right\|\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1/2}}}\right\|

(13e)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}}\cdot\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{-1/2}}}\right\|}{\sigma_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)^{1/2}},

(13f)

where in (
13c
) we introduce the scaled noise
\tilde{w}_{i}:=\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}w_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}-A_{\star}x_{i}
, which is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\big(\|\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}\|\sigma\big)^{2}
by
Lemma

15
; in (
13d
) we use the facts that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\succcurlyeq\lambda_{-}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}
when
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{-}
(
\forall i\in[N]
), and that
\|AB\|\geq\|AC\|
for any
A
when
B\succcurlyeq C
are both symmetric; in (
13e
) we apply the sub-multiplicativity of matrix norms.

Now it only suffices to bound the numerator and denominator in (
13f
). For the numerator, using
Lemma

16
, we know that with probability at least
1-\delta
, with sufficiently large
N\geq N_{0}
, where
N_{0}=\tilde{\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\big(\|\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}\|\sigma\big)^{2}d\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}}\right)
,
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\succcurlyeq\frac{\big(\|\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}\|\sigma\big)^{2}}{4}NI,

and thus

\sigma_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}}}\right)\geq\frac{\big(\|\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}\|\sigma\big)^{2}}{4}N.

Meanwhile, the denominator is bounded by

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{A}-A_{\star}}}\right\|\lesssim\frac{2\lambda_{+}}{\|\varSigma_{\star}^{1/2}\|\sigma\lambda_{-}}\sqrt{\frac{d\log(d/\delta)}{N}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Under the premises of
Lemma

11
, and Assumptions
4
,
5
and
6
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big|\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\varSigma)-\mathcal{L}(A,\varSigma)\big|\lesssim\frac{d\log(dN/\delta)\log(1/\delta)}{N}.

Proof.

We start by decomposing the error into three terms

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\varSigma)-\mathcal{L}(A,\varSigma)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\varSigma)-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma)-\mathcal{L}(A_{\star},\varSigma)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\mathcal{L}(A_{\star},\varSigma)-\mathcal{L}(A,\varSigma).

For the first term, with probability at least
1-\delta/4
, we have

\displaystyle\big|\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\varSigma)-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma)\big|

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell\big(\varSigma^{-1/2}(x^{\prime}_{i}-Ax_{i}),\varSigma\big)-\ell\big(\varSigma^{-1/2}(x^{\prime}_{i}-A_{\star}x_{i}),\varSigma\big)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L\big\|\varSigma^{1/2}(A-A_{\star})x_{i}\big\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}L\|\varSigma^{1/2}\|^{2}\|A-A_{\star}\|^{2}\|x_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}LC_{x}^{2}C_{\varSigma}^{2}\|A-A_{\star}\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{d\log(d/\delta)}{N},

where we plug in
6
,
7
and
Lemma

11
. Similarly, for the third term, with probability at least
1-\delta/4
,

\big|\mathcal{L}(A,\varSigma)-\mathcal{L}(A_{\star},\varSigma)\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}LC_{x}^{2}C_{\varSigma}^{2}\|A-A_{\star}\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{d\log(d/\delta)}{N}.

For the second term, note that with the true
A_{\star}
,
\varSigma_{\star}^{-1/2}(x^{\prime}_{i}-A_{\star}x_{i})=w_{i}
are i.i.d. samples from the base distribution of zero mean. Therefore, using

14

\|w_{i}\|\leq\sqrt{2c\sigma^{2}\log\frac{8N}{\delta}},

with probability at least

. Consequently,

\displaystyle\big|\ell(w_{i},\varSigma)-\mathbb{E}_{w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell(w,\varSigma)}}\right]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\ell(w_{i},\varSigma)-\ell(0,\varSigma)\big|+\big|\mathbb{E}_{w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell(w,\varSigma)}}\right]\big|

\displaystyle\leq Lc\sigma^{2}\log\frac{8N}{\delta}+C_{\ell}

\displaystyle\big|\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma)-\mathcal{L}(A_{\star},\varSigma)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|\ell(w_{i},\varSigma)-\mathbb{E}_{w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell(w,\varSigma)}}\right]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2Lc\sigma^{2}}{N}\log\frac{8N}{\delta}\log\frac{8}{\delta}+\frac{2C_{\ell}}{N}\log\frac{8}{\delta}

with probability at least
1-\delta/4
. The proof is now completed by applying union bound to the inequalities above.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Under the premises of

, for any

\big\|\hat{\varSigma}-\varSigma_{\star}\big\|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d\log(dN/\delta)\log(1/\delta)}{N}}.

Note that, by definition of

\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})\geq\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star}),\quad\mathcal{L}(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})\geq\mathcal{L}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma}).

Therefore, we shall apply
Lemma

\displaystyle 0\leq{}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})+\mathcal{L}(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})-\mathcal{L}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\big|\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})-\mathcal{L}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})\big|+\big|\mathcal{L}(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})\big|

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{A},\hat{\varSigma})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(A_{\star},\varSigma_{\star})

\big\|\hat{\varSigma}-\varSigma_{\star}\big\|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d\log(dN/\delta)\log(1/\delta)}{N}}.

Sub-Gaussian Random Variable.
Sub-Gaussian random variables, a notion that intuitively captures distributions with tails “lighter” than Gaussian, shall be defined as follows.

Definition 2

(Sub-Gaussian)
.

A random vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be sub-Gaussian with
variance proxy

\sigma^{2}
, if

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\big(v^{\top}(x-\mu)t\big)}}\right]\leq\exp\big(\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2\big),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\forall v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},

where
\mu:=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x}}\right]
is the expectation of
x
.

As its name suggests, sub-Gaussian random variables have tails “lighter” than Gaussian, as summarized below.

Lemma 14
.

Given a sub-Gaussian random vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
, we have

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|x-\mu\|\geq t}}\right]\leq 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2c\sigma^{2}}}}\right)

for some universal constant
c
, where
\mu:=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x}}\right]
.

The following lemma guarantees that the linear transform of a sub-Gaussian random variable is still sub-Gaussian.

Lemma 15
.

Given a sub-Gaussian random vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
, for any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
Ax
is also a sub-Gaussian random vector, with variance proxy
\big(\|A\|\sigma\big)^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu:=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x}}\right]
. Then for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\big(v^{\top}(Ax-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Ax}}\right])t\big)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\big((v^{\top}A(x-\mu)t\big)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{A^{\top}v}{\|A^{\top}v\|}}}\right)^{\top}(x-\mu)\cdot(\|A^{\top}v\|t)}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big(\sigma^{2}(\|A^{\top}v\|t)^{2}/2\big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big((\|A\|\sigma)^{2}t^{2}/2\big),

where in the last inequality we use the fact that
\|A^{\top}v\|\leq\|A\|
.
∎
