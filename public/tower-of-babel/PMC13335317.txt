Senescence modulated cardiac repair with pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes
Authors: Jian Zhao, Shin Kadota, Hajime Ichimura, Xiao Yang, Hotaka Kusabuka, Hideki Kobayashi, Yui Kitaori, Hidekazu Takahashi, Zouhour Guedi Omar, Ada Caruso, Yuki Tanaka, Naoko Shiba, Yuji Shiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) offer great promise for treating heart failure. However, their clinical application remains limited owing to poor cell engraftment, highlighting the need for scalable production methods that preserve therapeutic efficacy. Although in vitro expansion using glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) inhibitors can substantially increase hiPSC-CM yield, the therapeutic potential of these expanded cells is yet to be fully characterized.MethodshiPSCs were differentiated into non-expanded cardiomyocytes (N-CMs) via a conventional monolayer protocol or were further replated and cultured with CHIR99021 to generate expanded cardiomyocytes (E-CMs). Both E-CMs and N-CMs were transplanted into a rat myocardial infarction model to evaluate engraftment efficiency. To prevent senescence in E-CMs, cells were treated with the PAPD5 inhibitor BCH001 (E-CM-B) or DMSO as a control (E-CM-D) and then transplanted in the same manner. In vitro, cellular characteristics, including senescence and maturation, were evaluated by adhesion assays, immunocytochemistry, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis, and mitochondrial assays. In vivo, cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography, engraftment was monitored via bioluminescent imaging, and the properties of engrafted cells were examined through histological and immunohistochemical analyses.ResultsAlthough GSK3β inhibitor–mediated expansion increased hiPSC-CM yield, it induced telomere shortening, elevated DNA damage, and upregulation of senescence-associated markers, ultimately compromising engraftment and functional recovery after transplantation. Treatment with BCH001 during expansion attenuated senescence markers and promoted mitochondrial maturation. Transplantation of these pharmacologically rescued hiPSC-CMs significantly improved outcomes in infarcted hearts, including increased graft size, enhanced left ventricular function, reduced fibrosis, and promoted in vivo maturation.ConclusionsThis study identifies cellular senescence as a major barrier to the therapeutic efficacy of expanded hiPSC-CMs. Transient pharmacological intervention with a PAPD5 inhibitor during in vitro expansion effectively restores hiPSC-CMs, preserving their regenerative potential. These findings establish a scalable, clinically relevant strategy to overcome current limitations in hiPSC-CM therapy, paving the way for safer and more effective cell-based treatments for heart failure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05062-w.

Background

Regenerative medicine using pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (PSC-CMs), encompassing both induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived and embryonic stem cell (ESC)-derived cardiomyocytes, is emerging as a next-generation therapy for heart failure, with clinical trials now underway globally [1, 2]. Previous studies have demonstrated that transplanted PSC-CMs can engraft within the host myocardium, establish electrical coupling, and subsequently improve cardiac function [3–5]. Despite these successes, post-transplantation engraftment efficiency remains low, with more than 90% of transplanted cells failing to engraft, limiting broader clinical application [6, 7].

To address this limitation, strategies such as the overexpression of CCND2 or miR-590-3p, which promote the proliferation of PSC-CMs, have been reported to increase graft size and enhance engraftment efficiency [8, 9]. However, these approaches introduce considerable safety concerns. Uncontrolled proliferation carries the risk of tumorigenesis from the graft, while the immature state of the cardiomyocytes may elevate the risk of arrhythmias [10, 11]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for approaches not only to expand the graft size but also to facilitate the maturation of PSC-CMs, a crucial factor for mitigating these risks [12]. Furthermore, the production of the large quantities of PSC-CMs required for therapy is both costly and labor-intensive, largely due to the reliance on repeated differentiation from undifferentiated PSCs.

A promising strategy to overcome these obstacles is the in vitro expansion of PSC-CMs prior to transplantation. This approach could substantially increase the available cell supply while simultaneously reducing the economic and logistical burdens of preparation, thereby greatly improving the scalability of PSC-based therapies for heart failure. In 2020, Buikema et al. reported a protocol for expanding PSC-CMs by combining cell passaging with a GSK3β inhibitor to stimulate proliferation [13]. This technique marked a significant advancement in scalable cell production [14–16]. Nevertheless, no study has yet determined whether these expanded PSC-CMs retain their engraftment capacity and, more critically, their therapeutic efficacy following transplantation into damaged myocardium, especially considering the risk that cellular senescence may develop during the expansion process [17, 18]. Cellular senescence has been recognized as a critical barrier to tissue regeneration and is increasingly studied not only in the context of organismal aging but also as a direct contributor to cardiac dysfunction at the cellular level [19–22].

In this study, we therefore investigated whether expanded PSC-CMs can engraft as effectively as those produced by conventional methods in a rat model of myocardial infarction and assess their applicability in cardiac regenerative medicine. We also examined the influence of cellular senescence, a potential consequence of expansion, on post-transplant engraftment. Finally, we explored the potential of telomere extension agents to improve both the engraftment efficiency and the functional maturation of these expanded cells.

Methods

Cell culture and cardiac differentiation

The 253G1 human iPSCs (hiPSCs) (RIKEN BioResource Center) used in this study were genetically modified using the CRISPR-Cas9 technique to insert the Venus-Akaluc sequence into the AAVS1 locus, as previously reported [23]. The Venus-Akaluc sequence was obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Center (pcDNA3 Venus-Akaluc; RDB15781) [24]. Cardiac differentiation was induced based on previously reported protocols [23, 25], with minor modifications (see Fig. 1a). Undifferentiated hiPSCs were cultured as a monolayer in Essential 8 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) -coated plates. For differentiation, 1 μM CHIR99021 was added to the culture. On the following day (Day 0), the medium was replaced with a cardiac differentiation medium consisting of RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27 minus insulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Activin A (100 ng/mL; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was added to the medium on Day 0, followed by 5 ng/mL BMP4 (R&D Systems) and 1 μM CHIR99021 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) on Day 1, and 1 μM XAV939 (Sigma-Aldrich) on Day 3. On Day 5, the medium was changed to cardiac differentiation medium without any additional supplements. On Day 7, the medium was switched to RPMI 1640 with B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific). From Day 9 to Day 11, CM purification was conducted using glucose- and glutamine-free DMEM medium (Gibco, A14430-01) supplemented with 4 mM lactate [26, 27]. On Day 11, the media was changed to RPMI 1640 with B27 supplement. Generation of expanded cardiomyocytes (E-CMs)

On Day 12 of differentiation, cells were dissociated with Trypsin–EDTA at 37 °C for 15 min and then replated onto Matrigel-coated dishes at a density of 8 × 104 cells/cm2 in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B27, 2 μM CHIR99021, 5 μM Y-27632, and 10% KnockOut Serum Replacement (Thermo Fisher Scientific). From Day 13 onward, the medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27 and containing 2 μM CHIR99021 and was refreshed every two days. On Day 18, CHIR99021 was replaced with 2 μM Wnt-C59 (S7037, Selleck chemicals, Houston, TX, USA). For generating E-CM-B or E-CM-D, cells were treated with either 10 μM BCH001 (S8977, Selleck chemicals) or 0.1% DMSO, respectively, from Day 12 to Day 20 (see Fig. 4a). N-CM-B or E-CM-R were similarly generated by treating N-CMs with 10 μM BCH001 or E-CMs with 1 μM RG7834 (T12719, TargetMol Chemicals, Boston, MA, USA) from Day 12 to Day 20, respectively.

Heat shock and cryopreservation

On Day 19, both non-expanded and expanded CMs, with or without additional treatment with BCH001 or DMSO, were subjected to heat shock at 43 °C for 30 min to enhance cell survival after transplantation as reported previously [28, 29]. After the heat shock, the medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27, 100 ng/mL insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), 200 nM cyclosporin A, and 5 μM Y-27632. On Day 20, the cells were dissociated using Trypsin–EDTA at 37 °C for 15 min, resuspended in CELLBANKER 1 Plus reagent (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan, CB023), and transferred into cryovials. The cryovials were placed in a controlled-rate freezing container (CoolCell, Corning) and stored at − 80 °C. On the following day, they were transferred to a − 150 °C freezer for long-term storage. Cell viability of hiPSC-CMs at the time of harvest, as assessed by Trypan Blue staining.

Animal experiments

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All animal procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the Animal Experimentation Guidelines of Shinshu University, Japanese national regulations, and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval Nos. 021059 and 024053). Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Group sizes were determined based on power analyses using variance estimates from previous studies. The experimental subjects were male athymic F344/NJcl-rnu/rnu rats (9–12 weeks old; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at Shinshu University under controlled environmental conditions with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water throughout the study. The groups were as follows: N-CM (n = 10), E-CM (n = 9), E-CM-B (n = 9), and E-CM-D (n = 9). On Day 28 after transplantation, rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal administration of mixed anesthesia, consisting of a combination of medetomidine, midazolam, and butorphanol (MMB) at five times the induction dose, followed by injection of potassium chloride into the inferior vena cava.

Myocardial infarction (MI) model

Surgical procedures were performed on a warming pad under MMB, administered via subcutaneous injection. The MMB solution was prepared using 0.15 mg/kg medetomidine hydrochloride (Dolben, Kyoritsu Seiyaku, Tokyo, Japan), 2.0 mg/kg midazolam (Dormicum, Maruishi Seiyaku, Osaka, Japan), and 2.5 mg/kg butorphanol tartrate (Vetorphale, Meiji Seika Pharma, Tokyo, Japan). It was diluted with saline (Otsuka Pharma, Tokyo, Japan) to a final volume of 0.5 mL per 100 g of body weight. During the operation, anesthesia was maintained with 2.5% sevoflurane (Mylan, Canonsburg, PA, USA) delivered via mechanical ventilation. A 1.5–2 cm incision was made in the fourth left intercostal space, followed by dissection of the pectoral and intercostal muscles to expose the heart. The mid-left anterior descending artery (LAD) was ligated using a 6–0 silk (Natsume Seisakusho, Tokyo, Japan) suture, and successful infarction was confirmed by the appearance of pallor in the apex of the heart. The thoracic cavity, muscle layer, and skin were closed in layers with 4–0 silk (Natsume Seisakusho). Meloxicam (4 mg/mL, 0.2 mg/kg; Metacam, Boehringer Ingelheim, Tokyo, Japan) diluted in saline was administered subcutaneously for postoperative analgesia, followed by an intramuscular injection of atipamezole hydrochloride (Atipame, Kyoritsu Seiyaku) at a dose of 0.15 mg/kg body weight to reverse anesthesia. The animal was extubated once spontaneous respiration was restored.

Cell transplantation

Seven days after MI induction, hiPSC-CMs were injected into the infarcted area via re-thoracotomy. For transplantation, 2 × 107 cells were suspended in 70 μL of a Matrigel-containing pro-survival cocktail and directly injected into the anterior wall and apex of the heart using a 29G needle. The pro-survival cocktail contained 50% growth factor-reduced Matrigel, 100 µM ZVAD [benzyloxycarbonyl-Val-Ala-Asp(O-methyl)-fluoromethyl ketone, Calbiochem, San Diego, CA, USA], 50 nM Bcl-XL BH4 (cell-permeant TAT peptide, Calbiochem), 200 nM cyclosporine A (Novartis), 100 ng/mL IGF1 (PeproTech), and 50 µM pinacidil (Sigma-Aldrich).

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed 2 days before transplantation (pre-transplant) and on Day 28 post-transplantation. After anesthetizing the animals, a 30 MHz probe (MX400; Primetech, Tokyo, Japan) was used with a transthoracic echocardiography system (Vevo2100; FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto, Ontario, Canada) to measure the left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), end-systolic diameter (LVESD), and heart rate. The left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) was calculated using the following formula: LVFS (%) = 100 × [(LVEDD − LVESD)/LVEDD]. All measurements were performed over three consecutive heartbeats, and the average value was calculated.

Bioluminescence imaging (BLI)

The luminescent substrate AkaLumine-HCl (TokeOni, Sigma-Aldrich, 808,350) was diluted to 10 mM in saline and stored at − 80 °C. On Day 4 and Day 28, after transplantation of hiPSC-CMs, the rats were anesthetized, and TokeOni (20 nmol/g) was injected intraperitoneally. After 20 min, luminescence images of the rats were captured with a 2-min exposure time using an 8 × 8 pixel composite image. BLI was performed using the NightOWL II LB983 (Berthold, Bad Wildbad, Germany) imaging system, and the data were analyzed using Indigo2 software. The threshold was set based on the data obtained on Day 28.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Rat hearts were harvested, washed with PBS, sliced at 2 mm thickness, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then paraffin-embedded, and sections were prepared at a thickness of 4 µm. All sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) and Picrosirius Red staining to identify the scar tissue area.

For immunohistochemistry, antigen retrieval was performed by heating in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or Tris–EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) for 10 min. Blocking was carried out for 1 h at 15–25 °C, using 1.5% normal goat or donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, and the specific secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The list of antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

For chromogenic staining, HRP-polymer secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) were used, followed by visualization with diaminobenzidine (DAB substrate kit, SK-4100, Vector) and counterstaining with Hematoxylin. Images were captured using the NanoZoomer 2.0-RS (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan) and analyzed with NDP.view2.lnk (Hamamatsu Photonics) software.

For immunofluorescence staining, counterstaining with 4ʹ,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was performed after secondary antibody incubation. Images were captured using a BZ-X710 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan), and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. Engrafted hiPSC-CMs were identified with green fluorescent protein (GFP), βMHC, or human-specific antibodies, including nucleolin. GFP-positive areas were measured for the quantification of graft size. Only grafts with sufficient area for clear visualization and quantitative analysis were included in the immunostaining analyses.

Western blotting analysis

Frozen hiPSC-CMs were thawed and seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well onto Matrigel-coated 6-well plates (Corning). After three days of culture, the hiPSC-CMs were lysed using M-PER mammalian cell lysis reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting cell lysates were homogenized using a 26G needle and syringe. Protein samples (20–30 μg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE on Any kD Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked for 1 h with TBST (0.1% Tween-20) containing 5% non-fat dry milk, and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Detection was performed using Western Blot Hyper HRP Substrate (Takara Bio), and signals were visualized with the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Flow cytometry analysis

hiPSC-CMs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained for 1 h at room temperature with either Alexa Fluor 647-labeled mouse anti-cTnT antibody (BD Biosciences, Clone 13–11, 1:100) or mouse immunoglobulin G1 kappa isotype control (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA; clone MG1-45; 1:100), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:200). Fluorescence signals were detected using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and data analysis was performed using FACS DIVA software and FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Immunocytochemistry

Cryopreserved hiPSC-CMs were thawed and seeded at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well onto Matrigel-coated 8-well chamber slides (Corning, NY, USA). After three days of culture, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton-X 100 at room temperature for 10 min. Blocking was performed using normal goat or donkey serum for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/ml). Fluorescence images were acquired using a BZ-X710 microscope, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. To identify multinucleated cells, wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) and DAPI staining were used.

Senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining

Frozen hiPSC-CMs were thawed and seeded onto a 6-well plate coated with Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. After 3 days of culture, SA-β-Gal staining was performed using the Cellular Senescence Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, KAA002) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For each sample, 12 random low-magnification (10×) fields were selected, and the number of positive cells was calculated as a percentage of the total number of cells.

Quantitative fluorescence in situ hybridization (Q-FISH)

After thawing, the cryopreserved hiPSC-CMs were seeded onto an 8-well chamber slide coated with Matrigel at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well. After 3 days of culture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton-X for 10 min at room temperature, followed by another wash with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were treated with Proteinase K at 37 °C for 5 min. After the treatment, dehydration was performed by sequentially incubating in 70%, 90%, and 100% ethanol for 5 min each.

The TelC-Cy5 PNA probe (F1003; PNA Bio, Newbury Park, CA, USA) was diluted to 500 nM in a hybridization buffer (50% formamide, 10 mM Tris, 0.5% blocking reagent [Roche 11096176001]). The slides were pre-treated with pre-hybridization buffer (50% formamide in 2 × SSC). The probe mixture was heated at 85 °C for 5 min to denature the probe. The pre-hybridization buffer was then removed from the slides, the denatured probe mixture was added, and the slides were covered with a coverslip. All subsequent steps were performed in the dark.

The slides were then incubated at room temperature for 2 h, followed by two washes with washing buffer (1.4 ml formamide, 0.02 ml 1 M Tris, 0.58 ml water, pH 7.5) for 15 min each, and two washes with PBST for 5 min each. Next, the slides were blocked with 1.5% normal goat serum for 30 min. The slides were then incubated with a mouse anti-cTnT antibody (clone 13–11) for 1 h, followed by an Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. After staining, dehydration was performed again with 70%, 90%, and 100% ethanol for 5 min each. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min, and the slides were mounted with a cover slip. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Keyence BZ-X710 microscope with a 100 × objective. Images were collected under identical acquisition settings across all experimental groups (including exposure time, illumination intensity, and gain) and saved as 16-bit TIFF files.

Telomere FISH signals were quantified using the Telometer software (http://demarzolab.pathology.jhmi.edu/telometer/index.html). The analyses were performed using the 100 × (telomere) preset, with predefined minimum and maximum spot sizes (10 and 350 pixels2, respectively), rolling ball size (5 pixels), and despeckle ratio (0.5). Background normalization and rolling-ball subtraction were applied as implemented in Telometer, followed by spot detection using a fixed threshold value of 10. Nuclear regions of interest (ROIs) were manually defined on DAPI images, excluding edge, overlapping, or abnormal nuclei. All images were analyzed using identical preset and threshold settings.

Telomere length measurement by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Relative telomere length (RTL) was measured based on the published protocol [30]. Genomic DNA was extracted from cells using the Tissue and Cell DNA Extraction Kit (Takara Bio, U0952B). Telomere length was determined using the following primers: telomere primer (A), 5′-CGGTTTGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTTTGGGTTTTTGGGTTTTTTT-3′; telomere primer (B), 5′-GGCTTGCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCT-3′. PCR was initiated with denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 25 s and 56 °C for 1 min. The human β-globin gene was used as a single-copy reference, with the following primers: hbg1, 5′-GCTTCTGACACAACTGTGTTCACTAGC-3′; hbg2, 5′-CACCAACTTCATCCACGTTCACC-3′. PCR was performed with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 25 s and 58 °C for 1 min. Each 10 µl reaction contained 5 µl SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A66732), 1 µl of each primer, 10 ng genomic DNA, and nuclease-free water. Amplification was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and samples were run in triplicate. RTL was expressed as the telomere-to–single-copy gene (T/S) ratio, calculated as 2−ΔΔCt.

Cell adhesion assay

Cryopreserved hiPSC-CMs were thawed and seeded onto 96-well glass-bottom plates (Matsunami Glass, Osaka, Japan) coated with Matrigel, iMatrix (111, 221, 332, 411, 511; Nippi, Tokyo, Japan), Vitronectin, or fibronectin at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. Twelve hours after seeding, images were captured using a BZ-X710 microscope, and the number of adherent cells was analyzed using ImageJ software.

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cells were detected using the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) method.

In vitro experiments: hiPSC-CMs were seeded onto 96-well glass-bottom plates (GP96000; Matsunami Glass, Osaka, Japan) coated with Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. After incubating for 12 h, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and then treated with a blocking solution containing 0.25% Triton-X for 30 min at room temperature. Immunostaining for cTnT was performed, followed by TUNEL staining. Images were captured and analyzed using the Operetta high-throughput imaging system and Harmony software (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

In vivo experiments: Paraffin-embedded cardiac tissue sections were treated with Proteinase K at 37 °C, followed by antigen retrieval. The sections were then incubated with TUNEL solution for 60 min in a humidified chamber at 37 °C in the dark. Immunofluorescence staining for cTnT was subsequently performed. Images were acquired using a BZ-X710 microscope, and cell counting and analysis were performed using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing analysis (RNA-seq)

RNA was extracted from frozen cells using the NucleoSpin RNA Kit (Takara Bio), and sequencing analysis was performed by Takara Bio Inc. cDNA was synthesized using the SMART-Seq mRNA Kit (Takara Bio). Library preparation was carried out using the Nextera DNA Library Preparation Kit, followed by sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). RNA-seq reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) [31]. All samples were mapped to the hg38 human genome using STAR (v2.7.11b) [32], and a gene count matrix was generated using featureCounts (v2.0.6) [33]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, and a plot was generated using the PCAtools package (https://github.com/kevinblighe/PCAtools) with transcripts per million (TPM) normalization. Differential expression analysis was then performed using the DESeq2 package [34]. Finally, GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package [35].

Seahorse mitochondrial stress test

The energy metabolism of hiPSC-CMs was assessed using the Seahorse XF Cell Mitochondrial Stress Test kit (103010-100, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). hiPSC-CMs were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well on Matrigel-coated 8-well XFp plates and cultured for 7 days. At 24 h post-seeding (Day 1), the medium was replaced with RPMI-B27 without Y-27632, and the cells were subsequently maintained for an additional 6 days, with medium changes every other day. On Day 7, the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using the Agilent Seahorse XFp Analyzer following the manufacturer's instructions, with six technical replicates per group. Prior to the assay, the sensor cartridge was calibrated for 24 h under CO₂-free conditions. Baseline OCR was recorded first, followed by sequential injections of mitochondrial electron transport chain inhibitors at the specified time points: 2 μM oligomycin, 3 μM carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 0.5 μM rotenone and antimycin A. OCR values were normalized to cell viability.

Telomeric repeat amplification protocol (TRAP) assay

Telomerase activity was assessed using a TRAP assay. In brief, 6000–100,000 cells were pelleted at 3000 g for 5 min, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. The cell pellets were lysed in ice-cold NP-40 lysis buffer (1000 cells/μL; ~ 60 μL) for 30 min on ice. A telomerase-positive hiPSCs lysate dilution series was prepared as a standard curve, and a heat-inactivated lysate (85 °C for 10 min) served as a negative control. TRAP-PCR was performed by adding 2 µL of the lysate to a 48 µL master mix containing 10 × TRAP buffer, dNTPs, TRAP primer mix, TS primer, BSA, and Taq polymerase. The mix was then cycled at 25 °C for 30 min, 95 °C for 5 min, and 29 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, followed by 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products (20 µL) were separated on 10% polyacrylamide gels in 0.5 × TBE (using 19:1 Acryl:Bis acrylamide in Tris–borate-EDTA [TBE]buffer) at 4 °C for 3 h (protected from light). The gel was fixed using a fixative, then scanned using the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

3′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE)

The 3′ RACE was performed with minor modifications to the previously described protocol [36, 37]. Total RNA (1–2 µg) was ligated to a 5′–adenylated/3′–blocked adapter (Universal miRNA Cloning Linker, New England Biolabs, S1315S) using T4 RNA Ligase 2, truncated KQ (New England Biolabs, M0373S), in the presence of 25% PEG 8000 and an RNase inhibitor (20 µL total; 25 °C, 16–24 h). The ligation products were purified using RNA Clean & Concentrator columns (Zymo Research, R1015) with on-column DNase I treatment and eluted in 11 µL of nuclease-free water. cDNA was synthesized using a universal RT primer (5 pmol) and SuperScript IV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the following conditions: primer annealing at 65 °C for 5 min, reverse transcription at 55 °C for 10 min, and enzyme inactivation at 80 °C for 10 min.

PCR amplification was performed using PrimeSTAR GXL polymerase (Takara Bio). A first-round PCR was carried out using the telomerase RNA component (TERC)_F1 forward primer and the universal RT reverse primer. Subsequently, a second-round nested PCR was performed using the TERC_F2 forward primer and the same universal RT reverse primer, with the first-round PCR products as templates. The final PCR products were analyzed by 3% agarose gel electrophoresis to visualize mature and extended TERC species. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transcript copy numbers were normalized to 18 s, and the samples were run in triplicate. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired two-tailed t-test, one-way, or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's multiple-comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant when the adjusted P value was below the predetermined threshold: ****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05; NS, P > 0.05. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). SPSS (version 30, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.4.0, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) were used for statistical analyses.

Limited engraftment capacity of expanded cardiomyocytes

Cardiomyocytes were differentiated from hiPSCs using monolayer culture methods, as described in our previous reports [23, 25]. All hiPSCs utilized in this study were genetically modified to integrate Venus-Akaluc [24], a reporter that enables both in vivo high-intensity bioluminescence imaging and direct immunostaining with anti-GFP antibodies [23]. We prepared two distinct cell populations for comparison: non-expanded cardiomyocytes (N-CMs), which were continuously cultured without passaging, and expanded cardiomyocytes (E-CMs), which were passaged on Day 12 and subsequently cultured with the GSK-3β inhibitor CHIR99021 until Day 18, followed by the Wnt inhibitor Wnt-C59 for one day, based on the original protocol for hiPSC-CM expansion [13]. Both populations were heat-shocked on Day 19 and cryopreserved on Day 20 of differentiation (Fig. 1a), following procedures previously established [3, 23, 25, 29]. Compared to N-CMs, E-CMs formed a more uniform monolayer upon replating (Fig. 1b), yielded an approximately 3.0-fold increase in cell number (Fig. 1c, n = 9 differentiation batch), exhibited a comparable proportion of cardiac troponin T (cTnT)-positive cells (Fig. 1d), demonstrated superior cell viability at the time of collection (Fig. 1e), and the percentage of multinucleated cells was 2.1-fold higher (Fig. 1f, g). Furthermore, the mean cell size of E-CMs is 2.5-fold larger than that of N-CMs (Fig. 1h), and mature sarcomere protein MLC2v was upregulated in E-CMs (Fig. 1i,j), and maturation-related genes, such as ATP2A2, TNNI3, CD36, and FABP3 were upregulated in E-CMs by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 1). These initial findings demonstrate that passaging combined with CHIR99021 treatment enhances the yield and partially promotes the maturation of hiPSC-CMs in vitro.

To assess therapeutic efficacy, N-CMs or E-CMs were transplanted into the hearts of athymic rats following myocardial infarction at a dose of 2 × 107 cells per animal. Echocardiographic analysis revealed significantly greater functional recovery in the N-CM-treated group compared to the E-CM-treated group (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Despite the transplantation of an equivalent number of cells, bioluminescence imaging (BLI) analysis showed a trend toward lower engraftment of E-CMs at later stages (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). Notably, when the threshold defined for late-stage measurements was applied to early-stage BLI of E-CMs, the signal values were above background but near the lower limit of detection, appearing extremely low yet not completely absent (Supplementary Fig. 2g). Consistent with the late-stage BLI results, histological analyses demonstrated a 72% reduction in the mean graft-to-left ventricle (LV) area percentage in E-CM–derived grafts compared with N-CMs (Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Fig. 3a). Furthermore, E-CM grafts exhibited a 58% reduction in the proportion of proliferating CMs, a 3.3-fold increase in the number of apoptotic cells, and a 1.3-fold higher proportion of mature cells within the graft tissue (Fig. 2e–j, Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). However, given the increased apoptosis, it remains unclear whether mature cells are preferentially retained or whether cellular stress inhibits proliferation and survival, resulting in a relative enrichment of mature cells. While the density of endothelium within the grafts was comparable between the two groups, the total scar area was 1.5-fold larger in recipients of E-CMs compared with N-CMs (Supplementary Fig. 3d–g). Recent studies suggest that proliferative stimuli or cellular stress, including excessive cell division, can activate senescence-associated pathways even in CMs [21, 22, 39]. Gene expression analysis revealed enhanced maturation following BCH001 treatment

Finally, to elucidate the molecular basis underlying the observed phenotypic differences, we conducted comprehensive gene expression analyses comparing the N-CM, E-CM, and E-CM-B groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the three groups formed distinct clusters, indicating divergent global transcriptomic profiles and substantially different molecular states (Fig. 6a). To investigate the broader effects of PAPD5 inhibition, we treated cells with another PAPD5 inhibitor, RG7834, during expansion (E-CM-R) in the same manner as with BCH001 and included N-CMs treated with BCH001 (N-CM-B) in the RNA-seq analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10). N-CM-B resembled E-CM-B and E-CM-R more closely than untreated N-CMs, suggesting that PAPD5 inhibition mitigates senescence and promotes CM maturation. Furthermore, SA-β-Gal staining confirmed that senescence was not increased in N-CM-B or E-CM-R (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). We also performed qRT-PCR for maturation- and senescence-related genes, which showed trends consistent with RNA-seq results (Supplementary Fig. 11c–h). These findings indicate that PAPD5 inhibition delays senescence and promotes CM maturation.

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the challenges and opportunities of scalable cardiomyocyte production for cardiac repair, establishing three key findings. First, we reveal that while PSC-CMs can be expanded, this process paradoxically leads to reduced graft survival post-transplantation. Second, we identify a mechanistic basis for this failure: expanded PSC-CMs exhibit features of cellular senescence, including decreased proliferative capacity and elevated senescence markers, alongside signs of maturation. Third, and most importantly, we demonstrate that the addition of a telomere-elongating agent, BCH001, during expansion not only attenuates senescence but also enhances engraftment and further promotes a mature cardiomyocyte phenotype.

For regenerative therapies to become clinically mainstream, overcoming the prohibitive cost of producing the estimated one billion cardiomyocytes required per patient remains a formidable challenge [45]. In this context, expanding PSC-CMs with GSK-3β inhibitors, such as CHIR99021, is a highly attractive strategy due to its operational simplicity and scalability [13]. Indeed, our application of this method yielded an approximately threefold increase in cell numbers from the same starting population. However, our data critically demonstrated that these E-CMs acquired a senescent phenotype, which significantly impaired their engraftment and therapeutic potential following transplantation.

The senescent phenotype we observed offers a compelling explanation for the poor engraftment of E-CMs. Cellular senescence is intricately linked to both cell proliferation and differentiation [46, 47]. While it can exert beneficial effects in specific contexts like development and heart regeneration [48, 49], our study demonstrates its detrimental impact in the context of cell expansion for therapy. Here, senescence was associated with a cascade of negative outcomes, including diminished proliferative capacity, telomere shortening, compromised cell adhesion, and ultimately, poor graft survival, suggesting that excessive senescence during the expansion phase fundamentally hinders the therapeutic utility of PSC-CMs.

To overcome this expansion-induced barrier, we investigated whether modulating cellular senescence could restore the function of E-CMs. Among various anti-senescence compounds, we focused on the PAPD5 inhibitor BCH001, which has been previously shown to promote telomere elongation in undifferentiated hiPSCs derived from patients with dyskeratosis congenita [37]. Remarkably, incorporating BCH001 into the expansion protocol not only mitigated the senescent phenotype but also enhanced the functional properties of the CMs. Further supporting this mechanism, we found that the expression levels of FOXC1 and FOXC2—transcription factors upregulated when telomerase is inhibited [50]—were reduced in our E-CM-B group. Along with our telomere length and TERC 3′-end analyses, these findings are in line with a model in which inhibiting PAPD5 promotes telomere maintenance and alleviates senescence-associated deficits arising from the expansion of hiPSC-CMs.

These findings carry significant clinical implications, as it is well established that telomere shortening is a hallmark of patients with heart failure [51, 52]. Previous studies have shown that telomere elongation via TERT gene overexpression can ameliorate cardiac dysfunction in preclinical models of myocardial injury [40, 53]. Conversely, PSC-CMs with experimentally shortened telomeres exhibit profound mitochondrial and contractile deficits [50]. In the present study, treating E-CMs with a PAPD5 inhibitor elongated telomeres, mitigated senescence, and enhanced cell survival after transplantation. These results, therefore, position PAPD5 inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy not only for enhancing cell-based therapies but also, perhaps, for directly treating heart failure, a possibility that warrants dedicated future investigation.

A particularly intriguing outcome of our study was that BCH001 treatment also led to increased expression of genes associated with CM maturation. PSC-CMs are generally considered functionally immature, resembling fetal CMs [54]. While this immature state confers proliferative potential, the optimal degree of maturation for transplantation remains unclear. We previously demonstrated that prolonged culture for two months enhanced the angiogenic potential of PSC-CMs and contributed to increased graft size following transplantation [23], while others have reported that transplanting more mature cardiomyocytes reduces arrhythmia risk [12]. Collectively, these findings suggest that guiding CMs toward a more mature state is beneficial for improving graft integration, therapeutic efficacy, and safety.

The present study, however, reveals a critical caveat: promoting CM expansion and maturation does not guarantee enhanced therapeutic benefit and, in fact, can impair engraftment. Notably, BCH001 treatment mitigated this decline, suggesting that the therapeutic advantage of maturation is highly dependent on the biological pathways through which it is achieved. Our findings identify proliferation-induced cellular senescence as a detrimental condition that compromises graft formation despite some maturation markers increasing. Thus, the most significant contribution of this work is the demonstration that BCH001 treatment can uncouple beneficial maturation from detrimental, proliferation-induced senescence. Elucidating the precise mechanisms governing the interplay between senescence and maturation is a crucial next step for optimizing PSC-CMs for regenerative medicine.

Although our study observed a strong correlation between enhanced graft retention and improved cardiac function, the direct functional effects of the E-CM-B remain to be fully elucidated. Interestingly, a recent study in rats reported improvements in cardiac function following transplantation of immotile PSC-CMs, suggesting that the presence of the graft itself, rather than the functional capacity of the CMs, may contribute to functional recovery [55]. Moreover, given that the rapid heart rate of rats (> 400 beats per minute) may hinder synchronous contraction with hiPSC-CMs [45], it is likely that, in this rat-based study, the direct force-generating contribution of hiPSC-CMs is less dominant than their indirect effects in improving cardiac function.

There are several limitations to this study. First, only a single iPSC line was used, as the use of multiple lines was technically challenging due to the complexity of in vivo transplantation experiments. Second, we did not examine the potential off-target effects or clinical translatability of the PAPD5 inhibitor BCH001. In particular, its suitability for GMP manufacturing and the safety profile of transient exposure were not assessed in this study. These issues are beyond the scope of the present work and warrant further investigation. Third, the practical scalability and cost-effectiveness of BCH001 treatment were not assessed. Although BCH001 treatment resulted in an approximately threefold expansion, it remains unclear whether this level of improvement justifies the additional complexity and cost in a translational or manufacturing context. Fourth, the animal model employed also has inherent limitations. In particular, the substantially faster intrinsic heart rate of the rat host may mask the beating of the engrafted human cells, making it difficult to accurately assess arrhythmia risk. Although electrical coupling between the host and graft was observed in previous studies [56–59], this represents a distinct issue and does not directly address arrhythmogenic risk, which was not examined in the present study. Therefore, future studies in large animal models will be necessary to evaluate whether enhanced cellular maturation influences arrhythmogenic risk.

Conclusion

While scalable in vitro expansion of PSC-CMs is essential for the clinical translation of cardiac regenerative medicine, our findings reveal that conventional expansion methods can induce a detrimental senescent phenotype that compromises subsequent engraftment. This study provides a novel and effective solution to this critical barrier. By strategically incorporating a PAPD5 inhibitor during the expansion phase, we successfully mitigated cellular senescence, which not only restored but also enhanced engraftment efficiency. Crucially, this approach also promoted cardiomyocyte maturation, directly addressing a key safety concern associated with the immaturity of transplanted cells. This work, therefore, establishes a new paradigm for generating therapeutically potent cardiomyocytes, offering a potential strategy to develop safer and more effective cell-based therapies for heart failure.
