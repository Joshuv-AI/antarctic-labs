Multi-omics analysis of genetic drivers linking aortic stenosis and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction in heart failure
Authors: Zeeshan Ahmed, Prithvi Govindareddy, Jayden Mathew, Naveena Yanamala, Partho P Sengupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic stenosis (AS) and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) often coexist in heart failure (HF), but the mechanisms linking them remain unclear. While AS increases afterload and promotes myocardial stiffening, emerging AI-based evidence suggests LVDD can precede the development of AS or progress simultaneously, indicating shared upstream mechanobiological and inflammatory drivers. This study explores the genetic contributors connecting AS and LVDD to identify early molecular markers and convergent pathways in HF.MethodWe analyzed Whole Genome Sequence (WGS) and RNA-seq data of the HF patients, generated using their Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) samples. Overall bioinformatics analysis was divided into two modules, 1) gene variant and annotation analysis, and 2) gene expression and enrichment analysis. We utilized our peer review published and open source WGS and RNA-seq pipelines to process Next-Generation Sequence (NGS) data. Furthermore, we performed bioinformatics and statistical analysis to identify genetic variations, expressions, regulation, enrichments, and disease annotations.ResultsWe identified genetic markers uniquely associated with AS, LVDD, and shared between them. Furthermore, we report genes with significant expression, and functional variations, and discuss their relationship with other cardiovascular diseases (e.g. Vascular and Cardiac Stiffness, Aortic Dissection, Left Atrial Enlargement, Left Ventricular Hypertrophy, Outflow Tract Obstructive Defects, Non-Compaction Coronary Artery Disease, Arrhythmia, Congestive Heart Failure, and Hypertrophic, Dilated, and Ischemic Cardiomyopathy) and non-cardiovascular diseases (non-CVDs) (e.g. Type 1 Diabetes, Diabetic Nephropathy, Skeletal Anomalies, Rheumatoid Arthritis, Atypical Femoral Fractures, Chronic Kidney Disease, Dehydrated Hereditary Stomatocytosis, Schizophrenia, Varicose Veins, High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema, Periodontitis, and Respiratory Disorder) including multiple cancer types (e.g. Breast, Lung, Colorectal, Pancreatic, Hypopharyngeal, Acute Lymphoblastic, and Oral Squamous Cell Carcinomas) and rare genetic disorders (e.g. Hypophosphatasia, Multiple Sclerosis, Campomelic Dysplasia, Lymphatic Malformation). We validated our results through state of science literature, gene-disease annotation databases, and electronic health records.ConclusionsAS and LVDD share both clinical and genomic associations, with overlapping genetic drivers that are enriched in pathways related to inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and vascular stress responses. This work supports the potential of blood-based multi-omics profiling to uncover early, systemic molecular signals of cardiac dysfunction and lays the groundwork for future tissue-specific studies to guide precision diagnosis, risk stratification, and targeted therapeutics in HF.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13040-026-00564-y.Keywords: Aortic stenosis, Left ventricular, Diastolic dysfunction, Heart failure, Genetic markers

Introduction

Heart Failure (HF) is among the most significant causes of morbidity and mortality around the globe [1, 2]. Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for nearly half of all HF cases and has traditionally been attributed to myocardial stiffness and impaired diastolic relaxation [3]. Aortic stenosis (AS), long viewed as a valvular disease that causes left ventricular (LV) dysfunction through pressure overload, is now increasingly recognized as being present even in early HFpEF. Large registries, such as the ESC Heart Failure Long-Term Registry, report that approximately 10% of HF patients have aortic valve disease, with AS particularly prevalent in the HFpEF subgroup [4]. Clinical studies further show that mild AS and aortic sclerosis often coexist with subclinical left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD), suggesting an early and underappreciated connection between these conditions [5]. Furthermore, patients with AS, even after transcatheter valve replacement, show ongoing myocardial stiffening, inflammation, and extracellular-matrix expansion, which influences progression of HFpEF [6].

Recent advances in AI-based predictive models have further challenged the conventional downstream model of AS-induced dysfunction; deep learning algorithms trained on echocardiography and ECG data demonstrate that diastolic dysfunction scores can predict future AS progression, even in patients with anatomically normal valves [7–10]. These findings support the idea that diastolic dysfunction may have a shared pathophysiology with AS. To explain this paradox, we hypothesize that AS and LVDD arise in parallel from a common mechanoinflammatory environment marked by arterial stiffness, elevated afterload, and disrupted ventriculo–valvular–vascular coupling. This shared biomechanical and inflammatory dynamics provides a unifying framework for understanding the co-evolution of AS and LVDD, particularly in HFpEF.

Despite growing clinical and experimental evidence supporting this model, the underlying genetic architecture that may drive this interconnected pathology remains poorly understood. Evidence from the Framingham Heart Study suggests that cardiovascular disease (CVD) has a complex multifactorial etiology including a genetic component [11]. Multi-omics data, including high-quality sequenced DNA and RNA of transcribed genes, can inform us of a HF patient’s inherent genetic makeup with the most comprehensive view of the genome [12]. DNA-based gene variant detection, when combined with RNA-seq-driven gene expression analysis has the potential to reveal novel genetic markers and stratify HF patient populations based on their disease risk [13, 14].

We conducted a multi-omics investigation using whole genome sequencing (WGS) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from HF patients. This approach allowed us to interrogate both inherited genetic variants and active transcriptional changes, offering a broad view of systemic pathways relevant to cardiac remodeling. PBMCs, although peripheral, reflect immune and inflammatory activity, which plays a central role in both myocardial and valvular pathology. Our analysis integrated variant annotation, gene expression profiling, enrichment analysis, and disease annotation to identify distinct and overlapping genetic signatures associated with AS and LVDD. These findings provide new insight into the molecular mechanisms linking valvular and myocardial dysfunction and support the development of precision diagnostics and targeted therapies for patients at risk of HFpEF.

Methodology

In this study, we analyzed Whole Genome Sequence (WGS, n = 96) and RNA-seq (n = 61, with matched WGS samples) data of the HF and Cardiomyopathy patients, generated using their PBMCs samples. We utilized Illumina NovaSeq6000 S4 (2×150bp) for sequencing with 30X coverage using Illumina compatible library (e.g., Nextera DNA Flex; TruSeq Stranded mRNA). The WGS cohort was based on 35 female and 60 male cases aged between 29 to 94 years, and one unknown case. However, RNA-seq cohort included 21 female and 40 male cases aged between 45 to 92 years. Further details about gender, age, ethnicity, and sequence data are provided in the Supplementary Material #1. The RNA-seq dataset was compared against healthy control samples (n = 11) without any reported HF diagnosis. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects. All human samples were used in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and all experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Rutgers University. All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institution and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. We utilized our peer review published, and open source WGS and RNA-seq pipelines to process Next-Generation Sequence (NGS) data. Furthermore, we performed downstream bioinformatics and statistical analysis to identify target genetic variations, expressions, regulations, enrichments, pathways, and disease annotations. Overall data analysis was divided into two modules, 1) gene variant and annotation, and 2) gene expression and enrichment.

Gene variant and annotation

We processed WGS data (n = 96) using JWES [15], which mainly applies Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA) for mapping sequence data against the reference human genome (hg38) [16], and Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) for variant discovery and annotation [17]. The outcome of JWES is a Variant Call Format (VCF) file, which we utilized for further downstream analysis, including variant-level quality filtering, classification of coding variations, and identification of functionally relevant alleles associated with AS, LVDD, and both [18]. We utilized Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) to predict the effect of variants [19], calculated functional prediction scores using database of Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (dbSNP) [20], drawn gene-variant-disease relationship with ClinVar [21], and interpreted variant using population allele frequencies from genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) [22]. We performed functional variant burden analysis and based on the alterations predicted to produce substantial disruption in protein structure and function, we categorized variants into three main categories i.e., high, moderate, and low impact. Variant impact categories were defined in accordance with the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) consequence classification framework [19]. High impact variants comprise predicted loss-of-function alterations, including stop-gained, frameshift, splice acceptor, and splice donor variants, expected to cause substantial disruption of protein structure and function. Moderate impact variants encompass missense substitutions and in-frame insertions or deletions predicted to alter protein properties without complete abrogation of function. Low impact variants include synonymous substitutions and other changes unlikely to affect protein function. Modifier variants represent intronic, intergenic, and other non-coding alterations with uncertain or minimal functional consequences. The term “functional variants” throughout this study refers to variants predicted to produce biologically relevant alterations in gene or protein function, encompassing high and moderate impact variants as classified by VEP, as well as protein-altering variants further supported by pathogenicity prediction scores from SIFT [20] and PolyPhen-2, both integrated within the dbNSFP annotation database [20], and cross-referenced against ClinVar disease-variant associations [21]. The descriptor “highly pathogenic” applied to specific BIRC5 and MYBPC3 variants reflects ClinVar pathogenicity classifications [21], while “highly damaging” applied to the ITGB1 variant reflects combined SIFT and PolyPhen-2 deleteriousness scores, both standard frameworks in clinical genomics variant interpretation. Furthermore, we utilized annotation databases including Gene Ontology (GO) [23], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [24], Reactome Knowledgebase (RK) [25], and Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) [26] to identify and validate pathogenic variants with potential biological relevance to AS, LVDD, and both. We visualized results, most importantly by generating network diagrams annotating gene-variant and gene-variant-disease relationships.

Gene expression and enrichment

We processed RNA-seq data (n = 61) using GVViZ pipeline [27], which mainly applies HISAT (hierarchical indexing for spliced alignment of transcripts) [28] with Bowtie2 [29] to align the sequences against reference hg38, and RSEM (RNA by Expectation Maximization) for the quantification and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by aligning reads to reference de novo transcriptome assemblies [30]. The major outcome of this pipeline includes quality metrics, isoforms, and gene expressions. Next, we extracted and classified genes relevant to AS, LVDD and both, based on their expression values to perform gene expression, enrichment and annotation analysis. We performed pathway and disease enrichment analysis to identify biological processes and molecular interaction networks overrepresented within the stratified gene sets. Functional enrichment was conducted using the KEGG database to associate cellular functions with stratified gene sets. The enrichment analysis included calculation of False Discovery Rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.05 to control Type I errors and maintain rigorous multiple-testing corrections. DEGs were identified based on these FDR values and an absolute log2 fold change threshold of >1.5 and were then utilized for pathway and disease enrichment analysis. Enrichment was restricted to pathways with a minimum of two and a maximum of 5,000 genes to eliminate overly broad or narrow biological categories, ensuring results remained relevant to cardiovascular physiology, and redundant terms and overlapping ontologies were filtered to provide a concise, non-redundant functional summary of the biological findings. The top twenty significantly enriched pathways from each dataset were identified and projected to visualize gene enrichment signatures. These visualizations characterized the enrichment ratio, gene count, and statistical significance, highlighting the primary molecular mechanisms and biological themes associated with each HF phenotype.

Results

We identified through extensive literature review and cross-referencing with established disease–gene and pathway databases, nine genes known to be associated with AS (BMP2, TGFB3, ALPL, ITGA5, RUNX2, SPP1, PIEZO1, AREG, SOX9), nine genes reported for their role in LVDD (MYBPC3, COL3A1, TGFB2, SOD2, BIRC5, ANKRD1, MYH9, NDRG1, GDF15), and twenty seven genes shared between both AS and LVDD (TGFB1, NOS3, IL6, AGT, AGTR1, MPO, NOTCH1, ACE, COL1A1, LGALS3, MMP9, TIMP1, TNF, TNNI3, TNNT2, RHOA, ITGB1, PTK2, FN1, ROCK1, FOXO1, CRP, MMP2, AMOTL2, NPPB, YAP1, TTN). The gene selection was based on prior experimental and clinical evidence of disease association, including differential expression, functional involvement in key pathophysiological processes (e.g., calcification, fibrosis, inflammation, and myocardial remodelling), and support from curated resources such as DisGeNET [31], and pathway/ontology annotations including GO [23], KEGG [24], and Reactome [25]. Functional characterization further suggested that these genes were also enriched in biological processes central to cardiovascular disease, including extracellular matrix organization, inflammatory signalling, and cardiac muscle contraction, supporting their relevance to AS and LVDD. Further, we report genes with significant expression regulation, functional variations, and their relationship with AS, LVDD, and other CVDs and non-CVDs including multiple cancer types and rare genetic disorders (Tables 1 and 2).

Gene variant profiling and annotation

The WGS data analysis, uncovered functional variants from genes associated with AS, LVDD, and may have convergent role in AS and LVDD. Furthermore, we investigated their relationships with other CVDs. To characterize the WGS dataset, we performed a comprehensive descriptive variant analysis across all 96 samples. A total of 3,469,545 to 4,400,530 single nucleotide variants (SNVs) were identified per sample, with a mean of 3,632,878 SNVs in male samples (n = 60) and 3,655,876 SNVs in female samples (n = 35). Across the 45 target genes associated with AS, LVDD, and their shared biology, a total of 16,136 unique variant-gene pairs were identified. Functional annotation of target gene variants revealed that the majority were classified as modifier impact (mean 2,713 per sample, ~93%), reflecting intronic and non-coding alterations, followed by low impact variants (mean 75 per sample, ~2.6%), predominantly synonymous substitutions. Moderate impact variants, including missense substitutions, were identified at a mean of 46 per sample (~1.6%), while high impact variants, comprising predicted loss-of-function alterations such as stop-gained and splice-disrupting changes, were identified in a subset of samples (7 of 96 samples, mean 0.1 per sample).

Across the cohort, the most frequently observed variant consequences in target genes included intronic variants (n = 205,084), downstream gene variants (n = 25,735), upstream gene variants (n = 20,212), synonymous variants (n = 4,906), and missense variants (n = 4,313), with three stop-gained variants identified across the cohort. Comparison of variant profiles between male and female samples revealed no statistically significant differences in SNV counts (Mann-Whitney U = 964.0, p = 0.507), moderate impact variants (U = 948.5, p = 0.434), or high impact variants (U = 1022.5, p = 0.832) within target genes, suggesting that the genomic landscape of AS- and LVDD-associated loci is broadly consistent across sexes. At the genome-wide level, a marginal but statistically significant difference in total SNV burden was observed between male (mean = 3,632,878) and female (mean = 3,655,876) samples (U = 695.0, p = 0.006), though the biological significance of this difference is likely limited given the small effect size. Variant frequency analysis within the target gene cohort identified 6,534 singleton variants (observed in a single sample), 3,231 low-frequency variants (cohort allele frequency 0.01–0.05), 3,848 common variants (AF 0.05–0.50), and 2,523 high-frequency variants (AF > 0.50).

Cross-referencing with gnomAD genome frequencies revealed that the majority of variants in target genes are classified as common in the general population (gnomADg AF > 0.05, n = 46,404), while 2,888 variants were ultra-rare (gnomADg AF < 0.001) and 1,047 were rare (gnomADg AF 0.001–0.01), highlighting a subset of potentially disease-relevant low-frequency alleles. Recurrence analysis identified variants present across a very high proportion of samples (≥20 samples) in genes including TTN, ALPL, AGT, and TNNT2, consistent with common population-level polymorphisms in these large and highly variable genes. In contrast, disease-relevant variants with moderate recurrence (present in 5–19 samples) were identified across multiple AS- and LVDD-associated genes including MYBPC3, NOTCH1, ACE, and ROCK1, reflecting a combination of population-enriched and potentially disease-contributing alleles.

Gene expression and enrichment

Integrating In-house developed and published bioinformatics pipelines with systematic expression stratification and pathway enrichment, we ensured the precise identification of transcriptionally active genes that drive the biological convergence between AS and LVDD phenotypes leading to HF. Transcriptomic profiling across the case group (IDs 1058–1118) and the control group (IDs 648–659) was performed in two steps. First, gene expression analysis of all genes known to be associated with AS, LVDD, and have a strong overlapping between both AS and LVDD (Figs. 1B, and 2A, B, and C). This was conducted to identify specific upregulation or downregulation patterns, especially regarding significant DEGs that were identified using quantified expression values. Second, gene enrichment analysis of all genes known to be associated with AS, LVDD, and strongly intersect between AS and LVDD (Fig. 2A, B, and C).

AS-associated gene expression analysis

AS-specific gene expression identified heatmap-based expression for all nine markers with major association to vascular mineralization and cardiac remodeling (Fig. 2A). Differential expression analysis identified ALPL, ITGA5, and PIEZO1 as significant DEGs (FDR < 0.05, log2 fold change > 1.5), exhibiting a distinct variating downregulation pattern in the case cohort compared to controls (Fig. 2A). ALPL was the highly expressed gene in this subset, with significantly high intensity in the control group. Gene expression profiling showed variable intensity in ALPL expression in case samples (Fig. 2A). CVD found coronary calcification [34] and mineralization-related disorders [35]. ITGA5 also exhibited a high-intensity profile that was very strong in the control group in comparison to specific elderly case subjects. Expression analysis indicated high ITGA5 expression across the control group (Fig. 2A). CVD associations involved cell-matrix adhesion [36] and structural remodeling [37]. Similarly, PIEZO1 displayed high intensity in controls and a slightly lower distribution in case subjects (Fig. 2A). CVD links are limited to mechanotransduction [38, 39] and pressure regulation [40, 41].

RUNX2 showed a lower expression in controls, which was absent across most of the case group with some subjects having little or no expression at all. This marker has a crucial role in vascular calcification [42], fibrosis, and arterial stiffness [43] causing inflammation linked to matrix remodeling in CVD progression [44]. The remaining AS markers, SPP1 and TGFβ3 did not show any significant variation in expression as it was too low to compare while BMP2, SOX9, and AREG had no expression across the comparison groups. The expression profiling and highest fold enrichment analysis of AS genes revealed Hippo, PI3K-AKT, and TGF-β as statistically significant signaling pathways which play a vital role in the interconnected networks controlling cell growth, survival, and proliferation of cardiomyocytes, differentiation, and inflammation. Crosstalk of these pathways regulates heart development and repair, while their dysregulation contributes to HF, hypertrophy, and vascular disease, making them key therapeutic targets.

LVDD-associated gene expression analysis

LVDD-specific gene expression identified heatmap-based expression for eight markers with major characterized by dominant structural and stress-response markers (Fig. 2B). Differential expression analysis identified MYH9 and NDRG1 as significant DEGs (FDR < 0.05, log2 fold change > 1.5), characterized by a marked downregulation in the case cohort compared to the healthy control baseline (Fig. 2B). MYBPC3 and BIRC5 showed a moderately lower expression. MYH9 expressed solid high intensity in control subjects, while expression was significantly variable in case group (Fig. 2B). CVD associations include hypertension [45] and cardiac remodeling [46]. NDRG1 also exhibited consistently high intensity in the control group, while showing considerably variable and lower intensity in the case group (Fig. 2B). Its CVD associations involve cellular stress [47] response in myocardial tissues. MYBPC3 showed moderately low expression in controls in comparison to LVDD cases where the expression dropped further (Fig. 2B). CVD associations include HF and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [48]. BIRC5 also displayed faintly low expression in the case cohort, slightly more visible in the control group (Fig. 2B). It is known to be involved in cell survival signaling in HF [49]. Very low expression was observed in TGFB2, GDF15, COL3A1, and ANKRD across both groups (Fig. 2B). These genes are associated with syndromic aortic aneurysm, metabolic survival proteins, and cardiac ankyrin repeat protein activity [50–53]. The expression profiling and highest fold enrichment analysis of LVDD genes revealed Hippo signaling, the AGE-RAGE pathway and cytoskeletal dynamics as the statistically significant pathways. The AGE-RAGE signaling significantly contributes to cardiomyopathy and CVD triggering inflammation, and damaging cytoskeletal integrity, particularly altering actin can lead to cell damage, fibrosis, hypertrophy, and impaired heart function. This disrupts cardiomyocyte structure and function with cytoskeletal changes playing a crucial role in cellular stiffness and contractility, promoting conditions like HF.

Gene-expression analysis of genes shared between AS and LVDD

The gene expression analysis of the subset of genes shared between LVDD and AS reflected interrelated role and shared molecular biology functions in the diseases (Fig. 2C). Based on the established criteria (FDR < 0.05, log2 fold change > 1.5), TGFB1, TIMP1, and RHOA were identified as significant downregulated DEGs in the case cohort compared to controls (Fig. 2C). They are critically involved in cardiovascular fibrosis and remodeling [54–57]. MMP9 and ITGB1 also showed considerably high expression with distinctly lower expression in the case group. They are primarily involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, inflammation, and cellular migration [58–61]. NOTCH1, ROCK1, and FOXO1 had low expressions in the case group while little or no expression was observed in the control group. They are linked to chronic hypoxic cardiomyocyte conditions [62–64]. LGALS3 showed consistently high in both cases and control group. Although it is a robust and active biomarker of chronic inflammation and HF regardless of the specific phenotype it did not any considerable variation between the comparison groups. MPO, TNF, ACE, NOS3, and FAK showed insignificant expression across the comparison groups. Statistical analysis revealed AGE-RAGE, cardiomyopathy, stress in atherosclerosis, and TGFB signaling as significant with respect to shared genes. The AGE–RAGE axis plays a key role in cardiovascular disease by promoting oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis and myocardial injury. In cardiomyopathy, chronic metabolic and inflammatory stress activates dysfunctional signaling pathways that impair cardiac structure and function. Cellular stress responses, including oxidative and mechanical stress, further contribute to vascular inflammation and plaque progression in atherosclerosis. Additionally, dysregulated TGF-β signaling is central to cardiac remodeling and fibrosis, influencing vascular stiffness, myocardial hypertrophy, and disease progression across multiple forms of CVD.

To examine the relationship between genomic variation and transcriptional dysregulation, we performed an integrative analysis of WGS variant burden and RNA-seq expression findings within the curated 45 target gene set. Differential expression analysis identified eight significant DEGs across the three gene groups, including ALPL, ITGA5, and PIEZO1 for AS; MYH9 and NDRG1 for LVDD; and TGFB1, TIMP1, and RHOA for shared genes, all meeting the threshold of FDR < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change greater than 1.5, and all exhibiting downregulation in the case cohort relative to controls. Variant burden analysis within these eight DEGs revealed a mean of 447 variant observations per gene across the 96-sample WGS cohort, compared to a mean of 339 per gene among the remaining 37 non-DEG target genes, representing a 32% higher variant burden in transcriptionally dysregulated genes. Notably, PIEZO1, MYH9, and ALPL showed the highest combined variant and expression burden, suggesting that accumulated genomic variation in these loci may contribute to their transcriptional dysregulation in HF. It is important to note that this integrative analysis was conducted within the scope of the curated target gene set, as the RNA-seq analysis in this study was focused on these disease-relevant genes. Broader transcriptome-wide integration of WGS variant enrichment with genome-wide differential expression profiles represents an important direction for future investigation and would further strengthen the multi-omics framework proposed in this study.

Discussion

Molecular drivers of AS mineralization

Expression profiling of AS genes identified Hippo, PI3K–AKT, and TGF-β signaling as significantly enriched pathways [65], highlighting their roles in regulating cardiomyocyte growth, survival, differentiation, and inflammation. Crosstalk among these pathways governs cardiac development and repair, while their dysregulation promotes hypertrophy, vascular disease, and HF. Enrichment of these genes was dominated by mechanotransduction and structural remodeling, including Hippo signaling, PI3K–Akt signaling, ECM–receptor interaction, and focal adhesion, underscoring the role of mechanical stress in valvular and early cardiac remodeling. Reduced expression of cell–matrix adhesion and mechanosensory genes in cases suggests impaired cellular responses to pressure overload and progressive disruption of the valvular interstitium. AS progression reflects a transition of valvular cells toward an osteoblast-like phenotype [66]. The low expression of AS genes likely drives the pro-calcific microenvironment and accelerates valvular stiffening as mechanical signaling becomes increasingly dysregulated [67].

Genomic landscape of LVDD pathogenesis

LVDD genes showed enrichment in Hippo signaling, AGE–RAGE signaling, and cytoskeletal dynamics as dominant pathways [68]. AGE–RAGE activation contributes to inflammation, oxidative stress, and cytoskeletal damage, promoting fibrosis, hypertrophy, and impaired myocardial relaxation [69]. Alterations in actin and sarcomeric components directly affect cardiomyocyte stiffness and contractility, central features of diastolic dysfunction [70]. LVDD-specific genes showed the highest enrichment in cytoskeletal components with significant overrepresentation of hypertrophic, dilated, and diabetic cardiomyopathy pathways, reinforcing the role of structural and metabolic stress [71]. The reduced expression of these genes in cases indicates loss of transcriptional stability in pathways essential for myocardial integrity and mechanical load management. Downregulation of key regulators of contraction and relaxation likely impairs calcium sensitivity, contributing to delayed relaxation and disease progression.

Shared molecular frameworks for myocardial and valvular remodeling

Shared gene analysis revealed significant enrichment of AGE–RAGE signaling, cardiomyopathy pathways, stress-related atherosclerosis mechanisms, and TGF-β signaling, defining a common molecular response to chronic pressure overload. The AGE–RAGE axis promotes inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and matrix remodeling. It alters the actin cytoskeleton modifying the structural integrity of the heart muscles. While dysregulated TGF-β signaling drives fibrosis, myocardial hypertrophy, and tissue stiffening across cardiovascular phenotypes. Gene enrichment further highlighted hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, TGF-β signaling, and the renin–angiotensin system, linking systemic stressors such as hypertension to local myocardial and valvular remodeling. Cardiac remodeling potentially is directed by how the heart adapts its structure to maintain function under different types of stress. High-expression functional analysis emphasized actin cytoskeleton regulation, leukocyte transendothelial migration, and proteoglycan-related pathways, reflecting active structural reorganization and inflammatory signaling.

Across both AS and LVDD, shared genes converge on inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) turnover as core drivers of disease. While ECM-regulatory genes showed stable expression in controls, their heterogeneous and reduced expression in cases suggests a dysregulated “fibrotic flux,” marked by imbalance between matrix synthesis and degradation. Collectively, these findings indicate that AS and LVDD are linked by a unified transcriptomic program of chronic inflammation, impaired mechanosensing, and maladaptive tissue remodeling rather than representing isolated disease processes. Biomarkers with strong associations reported for AS have also been linked to multiple non-CVDs. These include chronic disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis [72], multiple sclerosis [73], type 1 diabetes mellitus [74], chronic kidney disease [75], early-onset inflammatory bowel disease [76], systemic lupus erythematosus [77], ankylosing spondylitis [78], schizophrenia [79], ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, and atypical femoral fractures [80]. Several rare diseases have also been associated with these genes, including skeletal anomalies [81] (congenital/genetic forms), Loeys–Dietz syndrome type 5, hypophosphatasia, lymphatic malformation 6, and campomelic dysplasia.

Many non-CVD conditions were found to be associated with the targeted genes. For LVDD-associated genes, linked non-CVD conditions include chronic diseases such as type 1 diabetes mellitus [82], diabetic nephropathy [83], and pelvic organ prolapse; rare diseases including vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome [84], Loeys–Dietz syndrome type 4 [85], MYH9-related disorder, and Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 4D; as well as several cancers, including lung cancer [86], breast cancer [87], pancreatic cancer [88], and adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma [89]. Genes shared between AS and LVDD have also been linked to multiple chronic diseases, cancers, and rare diseases [90]. Detailed classification is provided in Table 2 (Fig. 3A and C). In the gene expression analysis, the distribution of age at the time of HF diagnosis across genders indicated a slighter earlier diagnosis in males (45 years and older) compared with females (51 years and older). Similarly, age distribution based on variant profiling revealed an earlier onset of the disease in males (29 years and older) than in females (51 years and older).

Molecular biomarkers of a shared mechanoinflammatory mechanism in AS and LVDD

The molecular findings from this study provide direct evidence linking specific identified biomarkers to the central hypothesis that AS and LVDD co-evolve from a shared mechanoinflammatory environment characterized by arterial stiffness, elevated afterload, and disrupted ventriculo–valvular–vascular coupling. Among the 27 shared genes, TGFB1, RHOA, and ROCK1 most directly reflect the mechanosensory and fibrotic components of such a framework. TGFB1 was among the most highly expressed genes in control subjects, with considerably lower expression observed in HF cases, and with two functional variants (rs1800470, rs1800472) associated with ischemic heart disease and coronary heart disease respectively, consistent with the dysregulated TGF-β signaling identified here as a potential driver of fibrosis, myocardial hypertrophy, and tissue stiffening. RHOA similarly showed very high expression in controls with considerably lower expression in cases, and its two functional variants (rs2878298, rs11716445) associated with vascular stiffness, supporting its role within the vascular stiffness component of the shared disease environment. ROCK1 on the other hand showed low expression in cases with little or no expression in controls and had a high-impact functional variant associated with atherosclerosis and vascular stiffness, further reflecting the pressure-mediated cytoskeletal remodeling that is enriched in the shared pathway analysis.

The ECM remodeling signature identified among the shared genes between AS and LVDD provides additional molecular grounding for the disrupted ventriculo–valvular–vascular coupling component of the hypothesis. MMP9 and ITGB1 showed considerably high expression in controls with distinctly lower expression in cases, while TIMP1 showed very high expression in controls with considerably lower expression in HF cases. Together, these expression profiles reflect the dysregulated “fibrotic flux” described in this study, characterized by an imbalance between matrix synthesis and degradation. Functional variants in MMP9 (rs3918242, rs1805088, rs8125581) were linked to ischemic stroke and abdominal aortic aneurysm, TIMP1 variants (chrX:g.47585341C > T, chrX:g.47585480 G > C, chrX:g.47585524 G > A, chrX:g.47586579C > T) were associated with hypertension, and ITGB1 carried a highly damaging variant linked to cardiac remodeling and cardiac stiffness, consistent with the pressure overload and vascular stiffness mechanisms described in the central hypothesis.

The inflammatory component of the mechanoinflammatory hypothesis is also supported by several shared genes. NOTCH1, which had the highest diversity of functional variants in this study, was associated with bicuspid aortopathy, aortic dissection, and left ventricular outflow tract defects, supporting its role in ventriculo–valvular structural regulation. IL6, TNF, CRP, and MPO collectively define an inflammatory gene signature linking systemic immune activation to both valvular calcification and myocardial fibrosis (Table 1). LGALS3 showed consistently high expression across both case and control groups with no considerable variation between them, consistent with its characterization in this study as a robust biomarker of chronic inflammation and HF irrespective of specific phenotype, suggesting it reflects a constitutively active rather than disease-stage-specific inflammatory signal. TTN, which had the largest number of functional variants (n = 276, across 91 participants), was associated with a broad spectrum of cardiovascular phenotypes—including cardiomyopathies, arrhythmias, and ventricular hypertrophy—supporting its role as a sarcomeric stress-response integrator linking valvular and myocardial dysfunction.

Finally, the pathway enrichment findings across all three gene subsets provide the strongest transcriptomic support for the shared upstream regulation proposed in the central hypothesis. Hippo signaling was significantly enriched in both the AS-specific and LVDD-specific gene sets. AGE–RAGE signaling was significantly enriched in both the LVDD-specific and shared gene sets. TGF-β signaling was significantly enriched in both the AS-specific and shared gene sets. The co-enrichment of these pathways across both AS and LVDD gene sets, rather than their segregation into disease-specific signatures, is consistent with the hypothesis that the two conditions share common upstream mechanobiological and inflammatory drivers, and represents the most direct pathway-level evidence from this study supporting the proposed parallel co-evolution model of AS and LVDD in HF.

Conclusion

To investigate the genetic basis of the interconnected pathology of AS and LVDD, we performed a multi-omics blood-based analysis of HF patients. Integration of genetic variants and transcriptional profiles with pathway and disease enrichment analyses revealed distinct and overlapping molecular signatures associated with AS and LVDD. Notably, the rate of shared genetic markers was higher than that observed for AS- or LVDD-specific markers, supporting a strong biological relationship between the two conditions. Importantly, the biomarkers directly supporting the mechanoinflammatory co-evolution hypothesis—including TGFB1, ROCK1, and RHOA (mechanosensory-fibrotic axis); MMP9, TIMP1, COL1A1, and ITGB1 (ECM remodeling and vascular coupling disruption); NOTCH1 (ventriculo–valvular structural coupling); IL6, TNF, CRP, MPO, and LGALS3 (chronic inflammatory milieu); and TTN (sarcomeric stress response integrator)—collectively define a shared upstream molecular program of impaired mechanosensing, chronic inflammation, and dysregulated ECM remodeling. The convergent enrichment of AGE–RAGE, TGF-β, and Hippo signaling pathways across both AS- and LVDD-associated gene sets further confirms that these conditions share a common mechanobiological regulatory program rather than representing sequentially or causally linked isolated disease processes. These findings, across age and gender, validated through state-of-the-science literature, gene–disease annotation databases, and electronic health records, highlight the power of blood-based multi-omics profiling to capture early detection markers linking valvular and myocardial dysfunction. As a minimally invasive and clinically scalable approach, peripheral blood analysis provides an important foundation for the development of precision diagnostics and targeted therapies for patients at risk of HFpEF. While tissue-specific next-generation sequencing, may further refine cell-level mechanisms, blood-based genomic signatures offer a practical and translational framework for early risk stratification and personalized disease management.

Limitations of our study include, no further experiments were performed to further validate our results (e.g., sequencing to confirm WGS variants, RT-qPCR to verify RNA-seq expression patterns). Furthermore, we had limited access to the clinical records, but available details are provided in the Supplementary Materials. In the future, we recommend utilizing heart tissue sequencing along with PBMCs for more direct sequencing and localized information on the molecular state of cardiomyocytes and structural remodeling. In addition, it could be valuable to test associations that link individual variants to AS/LVDD and other CVD status.
