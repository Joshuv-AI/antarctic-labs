Oscillating solutions to the mean-field Langevin descent-ascent flow
Jean-Christophe Mourrat, Loucas Pillaud-Vivien

Abstract
. We present a counterexample to the statement of convergence of the mean-field Langevin descent-ascent flow on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . We consider payoff functions that are shaped as a double well in each coordinate, and for which the deterministic dynamics admits a limit cycle. When the coupling between the two coordinates is sufficiently strong and the entropic regularization sufficiently small, we show that the mean-field dynamics remains close to this cyclic behavior, and in particular, does not converge.



1.
Introduction

A natural procedure for finding a saddle point of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is to iteratively take small steps in the direction of
(-\nabla_{x}f,\nabla_{y}f)
. In the limit of infinitesimally small steps, we obtain a continuous dynamics called the gradient descent-ascent flow. It is well known that this gradient descent-ascent flow can fail to converge, even for simple functions such as
f(x,y)=xy
, for which the flow circles around the origin. A natural idea to remedy this is to add entropic regularization, which amounts to optimizing over probability measures rather than points, and to study the resulting mean-field Langevin descent-ascent (MFL-DA) flow.
Given
\varepsilon>0
and a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the MFL-DA flow is given by

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\varepsilon\,\partial_{x}^{2}\mu_{t}+\partial_{x}\left(\mu_{t}\,\partial_{x}\int f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(y)\right),}\\
\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\nu_{t}=\varepsilon\,\partial_{y}^{2}\nu_{t}-\partial_{y}\left(\nu_{t}\,\partial_{y}\int f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\right).}\end{cases}

This is the Wasserstein gradient descent-ascent flow for the functional

\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)=\iint f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\nu+\varepsilon\,H(\mu)-\varepsilon\,H(\nu),

where
H(\mu)=\int\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu
is the (negative) differential entropy. The laws
(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})
are the marginal laws of the processes
(X_{t},Y_{t})
solving

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\left(-\int\partial_{x}f(X_{t},y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(y)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},}\\
\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\left(\int\partial_{y}f(x,Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}B^{\prime}_{t},}\end{cases}

where
B_{t},B^{\prime}_{t}
are independent standard Brownian motions.

This framework was introduced and studied in
[
1
]
, where it is shown that if the flow converges, then the limit must be the unique entropy-regularized mixed Nash equilibrium. Whether convergence actually holds was posed as an open problem in
[
13
]
. Part of the motivation for this question is that the entropic regularization endows the problem with strong structural properties: for instance, under mild assumptions, the partial equilibrium measures satisfy uniform log-Sobolev inequalities, which suggests that the regularization might tame the cycling behavior inherent to the deterministic dynamics.
Several partial results support this intuition. Local convergence has been established independently in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
. Global convergence is known to hold under two-timescale modifications of the flow
[
6
,
7
]
; see also
[
5
]
for other modified dynamics. However, in a companion paper
[
8
]
, one of us showed that in the finite-dimensional setting, gradient descent-ascent can fail to converge even under a two-sided Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition — the natural finite-dimensional analog of the uniform log-Sobolev assumption
[
9
]
. The question of global convergence in the mean-field setting, however, remained open.

The point of this paper is to show that the answer is negative: there exist functions for which the MFL-DA flow stays in a compact region and yet does not converge. More precisely, we consider functions indexed by
\alpha\geqslant 1
of the form: for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(1.3)

f_{\alpha}(x,y):=\alpha xy-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{x^{4}}{12}+\frac{y^{2}}{2}-\frac{y^{4}}{12}\,.

For this choice of the function
f
, the well-posedness of the system (
1.1
) follows from
[
3
,
4
]
. As will be confirmed below (see Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
), the quartic large-scale behavior of
f
ensures the confinement of the flow.
Computing the partial derivatives:
\partial_{x}f_{\alpha}(x,y)=\alpha y-x+x^{3}/3,\,\partial_{y}f_{\alpha}(x,y)=\alpha x+y-y^{3}/3
and writing
m_{t}:=\int x\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
and
n_{t}:=\int y\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(y)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]
for the means, the SDE system (
1.2
) becomes

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\left(-\alpha n_{t}+X_{t}-X_{t}^{3}/3\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},}\\
\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\left(\alpha m_{t}+Y_{t}-Y_{t}^{3}/3\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}B^{\prime}_{t}\,.}\end{cases}

For this choice, the deterministic dynamics (
\varepsilon=0
, Dirac initial condition) admits a limit cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
that circles around the origin and is contained in the annulus

(1.5)

\mathcal{A}:=\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ :\ 3\leqslant m^{2}+n^{2}\leqslant 6\},

see Proposition
2.1
. When the coupling parameter
\alpha
is sufficiently large and the entropic regularization
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, we show that the stochastic mean-field dynamics remains close to this limit cycle for all times, and in particular does not converge.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\delta\in(0,1]
,
\alpha<+\infty
sufficiently large, and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, the following holds. If
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is deterministic and belongs to
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
, then for every
t_{0}\geqslant 0
, there exists a parametrization
(\overline{m}_{t},\overline{n}_{t})_{t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]}
of the curve
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
such that for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]
, we have

\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}-\overline{m}_{t}|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[|Y_{t}-\overline{n}_{t}|^{2}]\leqslant\delta.

In this circumstance, the means
(\mathbb{E}[X_{t}],\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}])
therefore circle around the origin for all times while remaining away from it; in particular, the measures
(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})
do not converge as
t
tends to infinity.

We have stated Theorem
1.1
with the assumption that
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is deterministic and belongs to
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
. This is only for the sake of clarity of exposition. The more general Theorem
3.3
below allows for random initial conditions, provided that they are sufficiently concentrated and with a mean sufficiently close to
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
. Theorem
3.3
assumes a deterministic upper bound on
X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}
, but this itself can be weakened, as the proof will hopefully make clear.

The proof proceeds in two main steps. The first is a careful analysis of the deterministic dynamics, carried out in Section
2
, for which we establish the existence of a unique limit cycle via the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem and construct a Lyapunov function certifying its exponential stability (Propositions
2.1
and
2.4
). The second step, in Section
3
, is an inductive argument (Theorem
3.3
) showing that the stochastic fluctuations introduced by the noise remain small over each period of the cycle, so that they cannot accumulate and drive the system away from the periodic orbit. Finally, experiments illustrating the result are presented in Section
4
.

2.
Analysis of the deterministic dynamics

In this section, we focus on the analysis of the deterministic dynamics, where we set
\varepsilon=0
and we impose the initial measures
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
to be Dirac masses. In this case, the evolution (
1.4
) becomes deterministic and reduces to the differential system

(2.1)

\partial_{t}m=-\alpha n+m-\frac{m^{3}}{3},\hskip 20.00003pt\partial_{t}n=\alpha m+n-\frac{n^{3}}{3}.

We let
F_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the vector field driving this system of equations,

(2.2)

F_{\alpha}(m,n):=\left(-\alpha n+m-\frac{m^{3}}{3},\alpha m+n-\frac{n^{3}}{3}\right).

We recall that the annulus
\mathcal{A}
is defined in (
1.5
).

Proposition 2.1

(limit cycle)
.

The system (
2.1
) admits a unique non-constant periodic orbit, which we denote by
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
. This orbit circles around the origin and is contained in
\mathcal{A}
. It is a limit cycle of every trajectory of the system (
2.1
) except the one that stays put at the origin. The smallest period
T_{\alpha}
of the motion along this cycle satisfies

(2.3)

\frac{4\pi}{2\alpha+1}\leqslant T_{\alpha}\leqslant\frac{4\pi}{2\alpha-1}.

Proof.

We set
r:=\sqrt{m^{2}+n^{2}}
. For
(m,n)
solving (
2.1
), we can write

(2.4)

\partial_{t}(r^{2})=2(m\,\partial_{t}{m}+n\,\partial_{t}{n})=2r^{2}-\frac{2}{3}(m^{4}+n^{4}).

Since
\frac{r^{4}}{2}\leqslant m^{4}+n^{4}\leqslant r^{4}
, we have

2r^{2}\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{3}\right)\leqslant\partial_{t}(r^{2})\leqslant r^{2}\left(2-\frac{r^{2}}{3}\right).

Hence, either
m(0)=n(0)=0
and the trajectory stays put at the origin, or
\liminf_{t\to+\infty}r^{2}\geqslant 3
and
\limsup_{t\to+\infty}r^{2}\leqslant 6
. Since the trajectory either stays put at the origin or never visits it, we can define an angular variable
\theta
such that
m=r\cos\theta
and
n=r\sin\theta
, and we have

(2.5)

\partial_{t}\theta=\frac{m\partial_{t}n-n\partial_{t}m}{r^{2}}=\alpha+\frac{1}{3r^{2}}mn(m^{2}-n^{2})=\alpha+\frac{r^{2}}{12}\sin(4\theta),

where we used
mn=\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}\sin(2\theta)
and
m^{2}-n^{2}=r^{2}\cos(2\theta)
to derive the last identity. Since
\alpha\geqslant 1
and
r^{2}\leqslant 6
in the annulus
\mathcal{A}
, we see that
\partial_{t}\theta
never vanishes in
\mathcal{A}
, and in particular the vector field
F_{\alpha}
never vanishes in
\mathcal{A}
. (We can also note that the system (
2.1
) turns anticlockwise in
\mathcal{A}
.) Using this, the previous observation on the liminf and limsup of
r^{2}
, and the Poincaré-Bendixson theorem
[
10
, Theorem 3.7.1]
, we deduce that the system (
2.1
) admits a limit cycle in
\mathcal{A}
. The divergence of the vector field
F_{\alpha}
is
\nabla\cdot F_{\alpha}(m,n)=2-r^{2}
, which is strictly negative everywhere in
\mathcal{A}
. It thus follows from
[
10
, Theorem 3.4.2]
that every limit cycle in
\mathcal{A}
is stable. If there were two distinct cycles in
\mathcal{A}
, then there would need to be an unstable cycle in-between (since the Poincaré return map
[
10
, Section 3.4]
cannot have two stable fixed points without having an unstable fixed point in-between), which we ruled out. Hence there is exactly one limit cycle for the system (
2.1
), which we denote by
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
. The fact that every trajectory except the one staying put at the origin is attracted to
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
is again a consequence of the Poincaré-Bendixson theorem
[
10
, Theorem 3.7.1]
. Recalling that
\partial_{t}\theta
is bounded away from zero, we see that
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
circles around the origin. It remains to see (
2.3
). Let
(m_{0},n_{0})
be a point on
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
, let
(m_{t},n_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the solution to (
2.1
), with
(\theta_{t},r_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
the associated polar coordinates, and let
T_{\alpha}>0
be the minimal period of this motion (recall that
F_{\alpha}
never vanishes along
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
). By the Jordan curve theorem, the set
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
contains exactly two connected components. On the unbounded component, the winding number of
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
is null. It jumps by
\pm 1
unit upon crossing
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
, and the winding number of
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
at the origin is
(\theta_{T_{\alpha}}-\theta_{0})/(2\pi)
. By (
2.5
) and the fact that
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, we have that
|\partial_{t}\theta-\alpha|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, and since
\alpha\geqslant 1
, the winding number at the origin is
1
and

T_{\alpha}\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)\leqslant{\theta_{T_{\alpha}}-\theta_{0}}=2\pi\leqslant T_{\alpha}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right).

This yields (
2.3
) and thus completes the proof.
∎

A key aspect we will exploit later is that
T_{\alpha}
can be made arbitrarily small by sending
\alpha
to infinity, while at the same time keeping the rough location of the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
unchanged, in the sense that it always stays within the fixed annulus
\mathcal{A}
. Although we will not need this, we mention that one can in fact describe the asymptotic shape of
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
more precisely in the limit of large
\alpha
. Indeed, notice from (
2.3
) that
\partial_{t}(r^{2})
remains bounded uniformly over
\alpha\geqslant 1
and
(m,n)\in\mathcal{A}
. Hence, as
\alpha
tends to infinity, the radius
r
along the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
cannot change by more than
C/\alpha
(since
T_{\alpha}\leqslant 4\pi/\alpha
). In other words, the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
asymptotically becomes a circle as
\alpha
tends to infinity. Moreover, we have that

0=\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}\partial_{t}(r_{t}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}\left(2r_{t}^{2}-\frac{2r^{4}}{3}(\cos^{4}\theta_{t}+\sin^{4}\theta_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since for large
\alpha
, the radius
r
is close to a constant
R_{\alpha}
and
\partial_{t}\theta
is close to the constant
\alpha
, and using also that
\cos^{4}
and
\sin^{4}
average to
\frac{3}{8}
, we get from the previous display that
R_{\alpha}^{2}\simeq R_{\alpha}^{4}/4
, that is,
R_{\alpha}\simeq 2
. In other words, the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
tends to the circle of radius
2
centered at the origin as
\alpha
tends to infinity.

Using symmetry, we record information on the first and second moments of
m
and
n
along the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 2.2

(square average)
.

Consider the system (
2.1
) initialized at a point in the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
, and recall that we denote by
T_{\alpha}
the minimal period of the resulting trajectory. We have

(2.6)

\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}m_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}n_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t=0

and

(2.7)

\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}m_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}n_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\geqslant\frac{3}{2}.

Proof.

We first observe that the function
t\mapsto(-n_{t},m_{t})
is also a solution to (
2.1
). By uniqueness of the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
, we deduce (
2.6
) and the equality in (
2.7
). Since the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
is contained in the annulus
\mathcal{A}
, we have
m^{2}+n^{2}\geqslant 3
everywhere in
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
, and this completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

As per our previous discussion on the fact that
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
tends to the circle of radius
2
as
\alpha
tends to infinity, one could show that
\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}m_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t
and
\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{T_{\alpha}}n_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t
tend to
2
as
\alpha
tends to infinity.

We can strengthen the statement of stability of the cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
as follows. Recall the notation for the vector field
F_{\alpha}
in (
2.2
).

Proposition 2.4

(Lyapunov function)
.

Let
g(z):=\dist^{2}(z,\mathscr{C}_{\alpha})
denote the squared distance between
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the limit cycle
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
. There exist
\delta,c>0
such that for every
\alpha
sufficiently large and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

g(z)\leqslant\delta\hskip 10.00002pt\implies\hskip 10.00002pt(F_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla g)(z)\leqslant-cg(z).

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
is a smooth simple closed curve, we can find a neighborhood
U
of
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
in which the nearest-point projection

\pi:U\to\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
is well-defined and smooth, so that for every
z\in U
, we have

g(z)=\inf_{z^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}}|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}=|z-\pi(z)|^{2}.

The optimality condition yields that
z-\pi(z)
is orthogonal to the tangent to
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
at
\pi(z)
, which is oriented along
F_{\alpha}(\pi(z))
, that is, we have

(2.8)

(z-\pi(z))\cdot F_{\alpha}(\pi(z))=0.

By the envelope theorem
[
2
, Theorem 2.21]
, we also see that

(2.9)

\nabla g(z)=2\left(z-\pi(z)\right),

and thus

F_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla g(z)=2\left(z-\pi(z)\right)\cdot F_{\alpha}(z).

Using (
2.8
), we can rewrite this as

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla g(z)

\displaystyle=2\left(z-\pi(z)\right)\cdot\left(F_{\alpha}(z)-F_{\alpha}(\pi(z))\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(z-\pi(z)\right)\cdot\nabla F_{\alpha}(\pi(z))\left(z-\pi(z)\right)+O\left(|z-\pi(z)|^{3}\right),

where
\nabla F_{\alpha}(u)
denotes the Jacobian of
F_{\alpha}
at
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and where we used that the second derivatives of
F_{\alpha}
do not depend on
\alpha
. Denoting
\nu(z):=\frac{z-\pi(z)}{|z-\pi(z)|}
for a unit normal to
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
at
\pi(z)
, we can rewrite this as

(2.10)

F_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla g(z)=g(z)\left[2\,\nu(z)\cdot\nabla F_{\alpha}(\pi(z))\nu(z)+O\left(\sqrt{g(z)}\right)\right].

Recalling that
\nu(z)
is orthogonal to
F_{\alpha}(\pi(z))=(F_{\alpha,1}(\pi(z)),F_{\alpha,2}(\pi(z)))
, and that the choice of orientation of
\nu(z)
is immaterial, we can take
\nu(z)=(F_{\alpha,2}(\pi(z)),-F_{\alpha,1}(\pi(z)))/|F_{\alpha}|
(recall from Proposition
2.1
that
F_{\alpha}
only vanishes at the origin). In view of (
2.10
), the proof of Proposition
2.4
will be complete provided that we can show that, for
\alpha
sufficiently large,

(2.11)

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{A}}\begin{pmatrix}F_{\alpha,2}(z)\\
-F_{\alpha,1}(z)\end{pmatrix}\cdot\nabla F_{\alpha}(z)\begin{pmatrix}F_{\alpha,2}(z)\\
-F_{\alpha,1}(z)\end{pmatrix}<0.

We have

\nabla F_{\alpha}(m,n)=\begin{pmatrix}1-m^{2}&-\alpha\\
\alpha&1-n^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Noting that the statement (
2.11
) only concerns the symmetric part of
\nabla F_{\alpha}
, we can rewrite it as

(2.12)

\inf\left\{(m^{2}-1)F_{\alpha,2}(m,n)^{2}+(n^{2}-1)F_{\alpha,1}(m,n)^{2}\ :\ m^{2}+n^{2}\in[3,6]\right\}>0.

We now proceed to prove (
2.12
).
We have

(2.13)

(m^{2}-1)F_{\alpha,2}(m,n)^{2}+(n^{2}-1)F_{\alpha,1}(m,n)^{2}\\
=\alpha^{2}(m^{4}+n^{4}-m^{2}-n^{2})+R_{\alpha}(m,n),

where

R_{\alpha}(m,n):=\frac{4\alpha}{3}mn(m^{2}-n^{2})+(m^{2}-1)\left(n-\frac{n^{3}}{3}\right)^{2}+(n^{2}-1)\left(m-\frac{m^{3}}{3}\right)^{2}.

We can find a constant
C<+\infty
such that for every
(m,n)
with
m^{2}+n^{2}\leqslant 6
, we have
|R_{\alpha}(m,n)|\leqslant C\alpha
.
We now bound the leading-order term from below. Since
m^{4}+n^{4}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(m^{2}+n^{2})^{2}
, we have that

m^{4}+n^{4}-m^{2}-n^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}r^{4}-r^{2}=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}(r^{2}-2).

Since
r^{2}\geqslant 3
in the annulus
\mathcal{A}
, this quantity is bounded from below by
3/2
. Returning to (
2.13
), we thus obtain that for every
(m,n)\in\mathcal{A}
,

(m^{2}-1)F_{\alpha,2}(m,n)^{2}+(n^{2}-1)F_{\alpha,1}(m,n)^{2}\geqslant\frac{3\alpha^{2}}{2}-C\alpha.

For
\alpha
sufficiently large, the right-hand side is strictly positive. This completes the proof of (
2.12
), and therefore of the proposition.
∎

3.
Mean-field dynamics

We now come back to the study of the mean-field SDE in (
1.4
), with the notation
m_{t}=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
and
n_{t}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]
; in other words we now consider the case of
\varepsilon>0
. An important ingredient of our analysis, extending the fact that the deterministic dynamics studied in the previous section remains in a fixed bounded set uniformly over
\alpha\geqslant 1
, is that
(X_{t},Y_{t})
satisfy moment bounds that are uniform in
\alpha
. We start with estimates on the second moments.

Proposition 3.1

(Second moment bound)
.

For every
\alpha\geqslant 0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
t\geqslant 0
, we have

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}+Y_{t}^{2}]\leqslant\max\left(8,\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}]\right).

In particular, if
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}]\leqslant 8
, then
|m_{t}|^{2}+|n_{t}|^{2}\leqslant 8
for every
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

By Itô’s formula, we have

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]=-\alpha m_{t}n_{t}+\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{4}]}{3}+\varepsilon,

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}]=\alpha m_{t}n_{t}+\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}]-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{4}]}{3}+\varepsilon.

Denoting
\varphi(t):=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}]
, and using that

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{4}]+\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{4}]\geqslant\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]^{2}+\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}]^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\varphi(t)^{2},

we obtain that

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\varphi(t)\leqslant\varphi(t)-\frac{1}{6}\varphi(t)^{2}+2\varepsilon.

The quantity on the right side is negative whenever
\varphi(t)\geqslant 8
, so (
3.1
) follows by a comparison argument. The last part of the proposition statement is obtained using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

We define

(3.2)

\widetilde{X}_{t}:=X_{t}-m_{t},\hskip 20.00003pt\widetilde{Y}_{t}:=Y_{t}-n_{t}.

Noting that

(3.3)

\partial_{t}m_{t}=-\alpha n_{t}+m_{t}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{3}]}{3},\hskip 20.00003pt\partial_{t}n_{t}=\alpha m_{t}+n_{t}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{3}]}{3},

we obtain that

(3.4)

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{X}_{t}=\left[(1-m_{t}^{2})\widetilde{X}_{t}-m_{t}\left(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\right)-\frac{1}{3}\left(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}]\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},

and similarly for
\widetilde{Y}
.

Proposition 3.2

(Moment bounds)
.

For every even integer
k\geqslant 2
and provided that
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{k}+Y_{0}^{k}]
is finite, there exists a constant
C_{k}
depending only on
k
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{k}+Y_{0}^{k}]
such that for every
\alpha\geqslant 0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, and
t\geqslant 0
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{k}+\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{k}]\leqslant C_{k}.

Proof.

By Itô’s formula, we have for every integer
k\geqslant 2
that

(3.5)

\frac{1}{k}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{k}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{k-1}\left((1-m_{t}^{2})\widetilde{X}_{t}-m_{t}\left(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\right)-\frac{1}{3}\left(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}]\right)\right)\right]\\
+\varepsilon(k-1)\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{k-2}].

We start with the analysis of the case
k=4
. Noting that
\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1/2}{2}+\frac{1/2}{6}
, we have by Hölder’s inequality that

(3.6)

\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{X}_{t}|^{3}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]^{\frac{3}{4}}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{6}]^{\frac{1}{4}}.

By Proposition
3.1
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]\leqslant C,\hskip 20.00003pt|m_{t}|^{2}+|n_{t}|^{2}\leqslant C,

where the constant
C<+\infty
is allowed to depend on an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}]
. We thus find that, possibly after enlarging the constant
C
,

\frac{1}{4}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{4}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{4}]+C\left(\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{X}_{t}|^{5}]+\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{X}_{t}|^{3}]+\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{6}]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\varepsilon\right)-\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{6}].

For some constant
C^{\prime}<+\infty
, we have for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
that
C|x|^{5}\leqslant\frac{x^{6}}{9}+C^{\prime}
and
C\sqrt{|x|}\leqslant\frac{|x|}{9}+C^{\prime}
, hence

\frac{1}{4}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{4}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{4}]+C\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{X}_{t}|^{3}]-\frac{1}{9}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{6}]+2CC^{\prime}+C\varepsilon.

Denoting
\varphi(t):=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{4}]
, using Jensen’s inequality and the assumption
\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, and reabsorbing everything into a larger constant
C<+\infty
, we have that

\frac{1}{4}\partial_{t}\varphi(t)\leqslant\varphi(t)+C\varphi(t)^{\frac{3}{4}}-\frac{1}{9}\varphi(t)^{\frac{3}{2}}+C.

The right-hand side is negative whenever
\varphi(t)
is sufficiently large, so we obtain the result for the fourth moment of
\widetilde{X}_{t}
.

Now that
|\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}]|
is controlled, a similar argument allows one to bound the moments of
\widetilde{X}_{t}
order
k
for every even integer
k
from (
3.5
). The argument for controlling the moments of
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
is identical.
∎

For convenience, we use the notation
p_{t}:=(m_{t},n_{t})
. We also recall the notation
T_{\alpha}
from Proposition
2.1
for the minimal period of the deterministic dynamics (
2.1
) along the cycle. Our main result Theorem
1.1
is a direct consequence of the following inductive structure.

Theorem 3.3

(Induction)
.

There exists a constant
C<+\infty
such that the following holds for every
k>\frac{3}{2}
,
\alpha<+\infty
sufficiently large,
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, initialization
X_{0},Y_{0}
with
X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}\leqslant 8
, and
t_{0}\geqslant 0
. If

(3.7)

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k},\hskip 10.00002pt\dist(p_{t_{0}},\mathscr{C}_{\alpha})\leqslant\alpha^{1-k},

then the same inequalities are valid at time
t_{0}+T_{\alpha}
, that is,

(3.8)

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k},\hskip 10.00002pt\dist(p_{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}},\mathscr{C}_{\alpha})\leqslant\alpha^{1-k},

and moreover, letting
\overline{p}_{t_{0}}
denote the point on
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
that is closest to
p_{t_{0}}
and
(\overline{p}_{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
denote the solution to the deterministic system in (
2.1
), we have for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]
that

(3.9)

|p_{t}-\overline{p}_{t}|\leqslant C\alpha^{1-k}.

Proof.

We fix
k>\frac{3}{2}
. Under our assumption that the initial condition must satisfy
X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}\leqslant 8
, we can bound all moments of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
uniformly over
\alpha\geqslant 0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, and
t\geqslant 0
. In particular, we have
|p_{t}|^{2}=|m_{t}|^{2}+|n_{t}|^{2}\leqslant 8
for all
t\geqslant 0
. From now on, we assume that (
3.7
) holds.

Step 1.
In this step, we show that for
\alpha
sufficiently large and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we have for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]
that
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant 2\alpha^{-k}
and
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant 2\alpha^{-k}
. Using that
|m_{t}|^{2}\leqslant 8
and the Itô identity (
3.5
), we have that

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]+3\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{X}_{t}|^{3}]+\varepsilon.

Since by Proposition
3.2
all moments of
\widetilde{X}_{t}
are bounded, we can appeal to Hölder’s inequality to find a constant
C<+\infty
such that
\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{X}_{t}|^{3}]\leqslant C\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]^{1-\frac{1}{2k}}
, so that

(3.10)

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]+C\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]^{1-\frac{1}{2k}}+\varepsilon.

Denoting
T:=\sup\left\{t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]\ :\ \mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant 2\alpha^{-k}\right\}
, using that
T_{\alpha}\leqslant 4\pi/\alpha
and redefining
C<+\infty
as necessary, we have for every
t\in[t_{0},T]
that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k}+C\alpha^{-1}\left(\alpha^{-k}+\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-k}+\varepsilon\right).

For
\alpha
sufficiently large and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we can make sure that the right-hand side above is smaller than
2\alpha^{-k}
, and therefore that
T=t_{0}+T_{\alpha}
as desired. The argument for
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{2}]
is identical.

Step 2.
In this step, we show that provided that
\alpha
is sufficiently large, we have for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]
that

(3.11)

\dist(p_{t},\mathscr{C}_{\alpha})\leqslant\alpha^{1-k}.

Recall the notation
F_{\alpha}
in (
2.2
), and the identity (
3.3
) which we can rewrite as

(3.12)

\partial_{t}p_{t}=F_{\alpha}(p_{t})+R_{t},

where we have set

\displaystyle R_{t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{3}(m_{t}^{3}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{3}],n_{t}^{3}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{3}])

\displaystyle=\left(-m_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]-\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}],-n_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{2}]-\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{3}]\right).

Recalling that
\widetilde{X}_{t}
has bounded moments of every order, and using the result of the previous step, we can argue as in the sentence above (
3.10
) to find a constant
C<+\infty
such that for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]
,

(3.13)

|R_{t}|\leqslant C\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-k}.

Recall that we write
g(z)=\dist^{2}(z,\mathscr{C}_{\alpha})
. We have

\partial_{t}(g(p_{t}))=(F_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla g)(p_{t})+R_{t}\cdot\nabla g(p_{t}).

Let
\delta,c>0
be as given by Proposition
2.4
, and let
T:=\sup\{t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]\ :\ g(p_{t})\leqslant\delta\}
. For every
t\in[t_{0},T]
, we can appeal to this proposition to bound the first term in the previous display by
-cg(p_{t})
.
Using that
|\nabla g(z)|=2\sqrt{g(z)}
, the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities, we have

|R_{t}\cdot\nabla g(p_{t})|\leqslant 2\sqrt{g(p_{t})}|R_{t}|\leqslant\frac{c}{2}g(p_{t})+\frac{2|R_{t}|^{2}}{c}.

Combining these and enlarging the constant
C<+\infty
, we obtain that for every
t\in[t_{0},T]
,

\partial_{t}(g(p_{t}))\leqslant-\frac{c}{2}g(p_{t})+C\alpha^{1-2k}.

Integrating this (and updating
C
) yields that for every
t\leqslant T
,

g(p_{t})\leqslant e^{-\frac{c}{2}t}\alpha^{2-2k}+C\alpha^{1-2k}(1-e^{-\frac{c}{2}t}).

By taking
\alpha\geqslant C
, we ensure that the right-hand side of the inequality above remains below
\alpha^{2-2k}
. In particular, since
k>1
, we have
T=t_{0}+T_{\alpha}
and the announced inequality (
3.11
) is proved.

Step 3.
For
\alpha
sufficiently large, the point in
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}
that is closest to
p_{t_{0}}
is well-defined; we denote it by
\overline{p}_{t_{0}}=(\overline{m}_{t_{0}},\overline{n}_{t_{0}})
, and let
\overline{p}_{t}=(\overline{m}_{t},\overline{n}_{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
denote the solution to the system (
2.1
) with this initialization. In this step, we show that for a sufficiently large constant
C<+\infty
, the inequality (
3.9
) is valid. We denote the
L^{\infty}
norm of
\nabla F_{\alpha}
on the ball of radius
\sqrt{8}
by
L_{\alpha}
. Recalling that
(p_{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
and
(\overline{p}_{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
both take values in this ball, as well as (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), we have for almost every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]
that

\partial_{t}\left(|p_{t}-\overline{p}_{t}|\right)\leqslant L_{\alpha}|p_{t}-\overline{p}_{t}|+C\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-k},

with

|p_{0}-\overline{p}_{0}|\leqslant\alpha^{1-k}.

Integrating this, we find that for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{\alpha}]
,

|p_{t}-\overline{p}_{t}|\leqslant e^{L_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}}\left(\alpha^{1-k}+C\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-k}\right).

The conclusion follows by noting that
L_{\alpha}\leqslant C\alpha
while
T_{\alpha}\leqslant 4\pi/\alpha
.

Step 4.
We now show that
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k}
and
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k}
. As preparation for this, we deduce from the previous step and Proposition
2.2
that

(3.14)

\left|\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}m_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leqslant C\alpha^{1-k},\hskip 20.00003pt\frac{1}{T_{\alpha}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}m_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\geqslant\frac{5}{4}.

Using (
3.5
) with
k=2
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]=(1-m_{t}^{2})\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]-m_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}]-\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{4}]+\varepsilon.

We recall from (
3.10
) that
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]\leqslant C\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-k}+\varepsilon
, and a small modification of the argument leading to this bound, starting from the previous display, allows one to bound the absolute value of
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]
by the same expression. For
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, using again that
T_{\alpha}\leqslant 4\pi/\alpha
, we thus have that

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}(1-m_{t}^{2})\left(\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{2}]\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\alpha^{-\frac{3}{2}-k}.

Using (
3.5
) with
k=3
and similar arguments as those leading to the bound on
|\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{2}]|
, one can show that
|\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}]|\leqslant C\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-k}
, and therefore

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}m_{t}\left(\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{3}]-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t}^{3}]\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\alpha^{-\frac{3}{2}-k}.

Combining the last three displays (and using again that we take
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and adjusting
C
), we obtain that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}^{2}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{2}]\left(1+2\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}(1-m_{t}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\\
-2\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{3}]\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}m_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+C\alpha^{-\frac{3}{2}-k}.

Using again that
|\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{3}]|\leqslant C\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-k}
and (
3.14
) yields that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}+T_{\alpha}}^{2}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{2}]\left(1-\frac{T_{\alpha}}{2}\right)+C\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}-2k}+C\alpha^{-\frac{3}{2}-k}.

Recalling that
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{2}]\leqslant\alpha^{-k}
, that
T_{\alpha}\geqslant 2\pi/\alpha
, and that
k>\frac{3}{2}
, we obtain the desired result by taking
\alpha
sufficiently large.
∎

4.
Numerical experiments

In this section, we display some numerical experiments showing the behavior described in the main theorems. For this we compute a particle approximation of the minimax SDE that we study, that is, we take particles
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
and solve

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{t}^{j}+X^{i}_{t}-\frac{(X^{i}_{t})^{3}}{3}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\ \ \frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{t}^{j}+Y^{i}_{t}-\frac{(Y^{i}_{t})^{3}}{3}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}B^{\prime}_{t}\,,

via a standard Euler-Maruyama discretization scheme. In the following we took the parameters
N=500
,
\alpha=1.5
and i.i.d. initializations taken as
X_{0}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(-0.2,0.25)
and
Y_{0}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0.4,0.25)
. We simulate until
T=20
to observe a steady behavior. We show this for two different diffusion coefficients:
\varepsilon=0.25
and
\varepsilon=0.5
. In the first case the system appears to stabilize on a periodic motion, whereas in the second case, the system seems to converge to a stationary measure which should be, up to particle approximation, the one depicted in
[
13
,
11
]
.

We show in Figure
1
the empirical mean and variance of the particles as a function of time
t
. We observe that in the first case, for which
\varepsilon=0.25
, these quantities stabilize to seemingly periodic functions, whereas in the second case, they converge to constants.
