The QAOA on the ring of disagrees
Kunal Marwaha

Abstract
We study the performance of symmetric local algorithms finding large cuts on the cycle graph. Such algorithms that cannot see the whole graph at depth p p cut at most a 2 ​ p + 1 2 ​ p + 2 \frac{2p+1}{2p+2} fraction of edges in expectation. We prove that the QAOA achieves this value, a long-standing conjecture of [ 4 ] . Curiously we do this without finding the optimal parameters. Instead we show it is equivalent to find an optimal pair of Laurent polynomials of degree at most 2 ​ p − 1 2p-1 . This is made possible by recasting the QAOA on one qubit in the language of quantum signal processing.

Introduction

Let
C_{n}

n\geq 3

C_{n}
has size either
n

n-1
depending on whether

In these algorithms only the local neighborhood around an edge is used to decide whether or not an edge is cut.
Consider any such algorithm that observes local neighborhoods up to depth

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
[

is a symmetric local algorithm.
Here the problem of finding large cuts of

has been called

For this problem
[

conjectured that the depth-
p
QAOA finds cuts of size

While this conjecture is supported by significant numerical evidence

1.1
Statement

Define the
cost
and
mixer
Hamiltonians

\displaystyle H_{C}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}(I-Z_{i}Z_{i+1})\,,

\displaystyle H_{M}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\,,

where indices are modulo
n
. The depth-
p
QAOA state, given
\vec{\beta}:=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p}),\vec{\gamma}:=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p})
, is

\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle:=e^{-i\beta_{p}H_{M}}e^{-i\gamma_{p}H_{C}}\dots e^{-i\beta_{1}H_{M}}e^{-i\gamma_{1}H_{C}}\lvert+\rangle^{\otimes n}\,.

Here we prove the following exact formulae:

Theorem 1

(The QAOA cannot see the whole graph)
.

When
n\geq 2p+2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=\frac{2p+1}{2p+2}\,.

Theorem 2

(The QAOA sees the whole graph)
.

When
n<2p+2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=\begin{cases}1\quad&n\textnormal{ is even}\\
\frac{n-1}{n}\quad&n\textnormal{ is odd}\,.\end{cases}

1.2
Proof outline

The proof of
Theorem

1
proceeds in three steps.

First, we use the free fermionic picture proven in
[
9
]
. This shows that running the QAOA on
C_{2k}
is equivalent to simultaneously running the QAOA on
k
one-qubit systems. The angles are the same up to a factor of two and sign. Each one-qubit system uses a cost operator
X\cos\theta+Z\sin\theta
, where system
j
sets
\theta_{j}:=\pi\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2k}
.

Second, we analyze the depth-
p
QAOA on one qubit with cost operator
X\cos\theta+Z\sin\theta
.
We prove an
expressibility theorem
which shows how the performance of the QAOA can be expressed as a function of
\theta
. With larger values of
p
more complicated functions can be expressed. We show that choosing the angles
\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}
is equivalent to choosing a pair of Laurent polynomials of
z:=e^{i\theta/2}
whose degree is
\leq 2p-1
. The performance of the QAOA is a fixed function of this pair of polynomials. Now we can look for optimal polynomial pairs instead of optimal angles.

Third, we suppose the QAOA cannot see the whole graph. This happens when
p<k
. On one qubit, the inner product of the final QAOA state with the
+1
-eigenstate of the cost operator has the form
\sum_{j=-p}^{p+1}c_{2j-1}z^{2j-1}
. The performance ratio of the QAOA simplifies to

1-\sum_{j=-p}^{p+1}|c_{2j-1}|^{2}\,.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz, the sum is no smaller than
\frac{1}{2p+2}
. This bound is saturated by making all coefficients equal. We find a polynomial pair that achieves this condition. Curiously the optimal angles are not easy to write down.

So far we only have looked at cycles with even length. But the performance of the QAOA only depends on the local neighborhood, which is a path graph of
2p+2
vertices. As a consequence this argument extends to cycles with odd length.

The proof of
Theorem

2
depends on whether
n
is even or odd.

When
n
is even we follow the proof of
Theorem

1
where
n=2k
.
Then
n<2p+2
when
p\geq k
.
We find a polynomial pair where the QAOA on a one-qubit system has error proportional to
\cos^{2}(p\theta)\cos^{2}(\theta/2)
. Suppose
p=k
. Then for every one-qubit system
j
,
\cos^{2}(p\theta_{j})=\cos^{2}(j\pi-\frac{\pi}{2})=0
. So the QAOA perfectly optimizes each one-qubit system. Then the QAOA perfectly optimizes
C_{2k}
at
p=k
, and thus at any larger
p
. In this case it is easy to write down the optimal angles.

When
n
is odd there is also a free fermionic picture proven in
[
9
]
with slightly different angles. If
n=2k+1
there are
k
nontrivial free fermionic systems. As in the even case, we use an
expressibility theorem
to look for a good pair of Laurent polynomials of
z:=e^{i\theta/2}
whose degree is
\leq 2p-1
. When
p=k
we show the existence of a polynomial pair that optimizes every free fermionic system. Then the QAOA also optimizes
C_{2k+1}
at any larger
p
. In contrast to the even case we did not find a simple closed form for the optimal angles.

Claim 3

Consider the one-qubit Hamiltonian

3
Expressibility of QAOA on one qubit

Consider the QAOA on one qubit with mixer Hamiltonian
X
, and cost Hamiltonian

h_{c}(\theta)=X\cos\theta+Z\sin\theta\,.

Let
\lvert\theta\rangle
and
\lvert\theta_{\perp}\rangle
be the
+1
- and
-1
-eigenstates of
h_{c}(\theta)
. The performance of the QAOA here is

\langle\chi(\theta)\rvert h_{c}(\theta)\lvert\chi(\theta)\rangle=|\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle|^{2}-|\langle\theta_{\perp}|\chi(\theta)\rangle|^{2}=2|\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle|^{2}-1\,.

We show how this value can be expressed as a function of
\theta
. We use a technique from the theory of quantum signal processing. This requires a bit of setup.

Definition 4
.

A Laurent polynomial is a polynomial in both
z
and
z^{-1}
, i.e.
f(z)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{j}z^{j}
where finitely many
c_{j}
are nonzero. Its degree is the maximum
r
where either
c_{r}
or
c_{-r}
is nonzero.

We use the notation
\mathbb{R}[z,z^{-1}]
for Laurent polynomials with real coefficients. In general
z
can be on the complex plane. We also use the following notation:

f^{\#}(z):=f(z^{-1})

When
f\in\mathbb{R}[z,z^{-1}]
and
|z|=1
,
f^{\#}(z)=f(z^{*})=\left(f(z)\right)^{*}
.

For each integer
r\geq 0
, we define a special set of
pairs
of Laurent polynomials
(A,B)
:

Definition 5
.

For each integer
r\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{S}_{r}
be

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{r}:=\{(A,B):{}

\displaystyle A,B\in\mathbb{R}[z,z^{-1}],

(1)

\displaystyle\deg A,\deg B\leq r,

\displaystyle\forall m\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1,z^{m-r}A(z)\textnormal{ and }z^{m-r}B(z)\textnormal{ have no constant term},

\displaystyle A(z)A^{\#}(z)+B(z)B^{\#}(z)=1\}.

The third condition of
\mathcal{S}_{r}
specifies that the only nonzero coefficients of
A
and of
B
are
c_{j}
where
j
has the same parity as
r
.

The performance of the QAOA on one qubit is characterized by a polynomial pair in
\mathcal{S}_{r}
:

Theorem 6

(Expressibility theorem)
.

Fix
p\geq 1
. The depth-
p
QAOA on one qubit, with cost function
h_{c}(\theta)
and angles
\vec{\tilde{\gamma}}=(\tilde{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}_{p}),\vec{\tilde{\beta}}=(\tilde{\beta}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\beta}_{p})
, has performance

\displaystyle\langle\chi(\theta)\rvert h_{c}(\theta)\lvert\chi(\theta)\rangle

\displaystyle=-1+\frac{1}{2}\left|z^{-2}A(z)+z^{2}A^{\#}(z)-iB(z)-iB^{\#}(z)\right|^{2}\,,\qquad z=e^{i\theta/2}\,,

for some pair of polynomials
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
. Conversely, every
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
corresponds to angles
\vec{\tilde{\gamma}}=(\tilde{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}_{p}),\vec{\tilde{\beta}}=(\tilde{\beta}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\beta}_{p})
where the depth-
p
QAOA achieves the above performance.

We will use the rest of this section to prove
Theorem

6
.

3.1
Rewriting the QAOA unitary

We start by rewriting the QAOA to look more like quantum signal processing. For any Pauli
P
and angle
\phi
, let

R_{P}(\phi):=e^{-i\phi P}\,.

Claim 7
.

The unitary associated with the depth-
p
QAOA on one qubit, with cost function
h_{c}(\theta)
and angles
\vec{\tilde{\gamma}}=(\tilde{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}_{p}),\vec{\tilde{\beta}}=(\tilde{\beta}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\beta}_{p})
, can be written as

\displaystyle R_{X}(\phi_{2p})R_{Y}(\theta/2)R_{X}(\phi_{2p-1})R_{Y}(\theta/2)\dots R_{X}(\phi_{1})R_{Y}(\theta/2)\,,

where
\phi_{2j}=\tilde{\beta}_{j}+\frac{\pi}{2}
,
\phi_{2j-1}=\tilde{\gamma}_{j}-\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Proof.

We study one layer of the QAOA unitary:

e^{-i\tilde{\beta}X}e^{-i\tilde{\gamma}h_{c}(\theta)}=R_{X}(\tilde{\beta})e^{-i\tilde{\gamma}h_{c}(\theta)}

We rewrite the rotation around
h_{c}(\theta)
as

\displaystyle h_{c}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\theta&\cos\theta\\
\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\
-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}=R_{Y}(-\theta/2)XR_{Y}(\theta/2)

\displaystyle e^{-i\tilde{\gamma}h_{c}(\theta)}=R_{Y}(-\theta/2)R_{X}(\tilde{\gamma})R_{Y}(\theta/2)

Then one layer of the QAOA unitary has the form

R_{X}(\tilde{\beta})R_{Y}(-\theta/2)R_{X}(\tilde{\gamma})R_{Y}(\theta/2)\,.

We rewrite

R_{Y}(-\theta/2)=(-iX)R_{Y}(\theta/2)(+iX)=R_{X}(\pi/2)R_{Y}(\theta/2)R_{X}(-\pi/2)\,.

Then one layer of the QAOA unitary has the form

R_{X}(\tilde{\beta})\Big(R_{X}(\pi/2)R_{Y}(\theta/2)R_{X}(-\pi/2)\Big)R_{X}(\tilde{\gamma})R_{Y}(\theta/2)=R_{X}(\tilde{\beta}+\pi/2)R_{Y}(\theta/2)R_{X}(\tilde{\gamma}-\pi/2)R_{Y}(\theta/2)\,.

The claim follows after expanding each layer of the QAOA unitary in this form.
∎

3.2
QAOA unitary from Laurent polynomials

We show how the matrix elements of the QAOA unitary are related to Laurent polynomials. Let

z=e^{i\theta/2}\,.

We write
R_{X}(\phi)
and
R_{Y}(\theta/2)
in the
Y
-basis (explicitly, mapping
X
to
Y
,
Y
to
Z
, and
Z
to
X
):

\displaystyle C(\phi)

\displaystyle:=R_{X}(\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\phi)&-\sin(\phi)\\
\sin(\phi)&\cos(\phi)\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle:=R_{Y}(\theta/2)=\begin{pmatrix}z^{-1}&0\\
0&z\end{pmatrix}\,.

We start by considering matrices of the form
C(\phi_{r})DC(\phi_{r-1})\dots DC(\phi_{0})
. When
r=2p-1
, this matches a depth-
p
QAOA unitary without the last
D
unitary.

In this
Y
-basis, we show how the matrix entries are described by real Laurent polynomials in
\mathcal{S}_{r}
. This comes directly from the study of quantum signal processing
[
6
,
5
]
. We state the claims here and defer the proofs to
Appendix

B
:

Claim 8
.

For every
r\geq 0
,

\left\{C(\phi_{r})DC(\phi_{r-1})D\cdots DC(\phi_{0})\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}:\phi_{0},\dots,\phi_{r}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A(z)\\
B(z)\end{pmatrix}:(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{r}\right\}\,.

Corollary 9
.

For every
p\geq 1
, and in the
Y
-basis,

\bigg\{C(\phi_{2p})DC(\phi_{2p-1})D\cdots DC(\phi_{1})D:\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{2p}\in\mathbb{R}\bigg\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}z^{-1}A(z)&-zB^{\#}(z)\\
z^{-1}B(z)&zA^{\#}(z)\end{pmatrix}:(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}\right\}\,.

3.3
Putting it together

We now have the tools to prove the expressibility theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

6
.

Fixing
\vec{\tilde{\gamma}},\vec{\tilde{\beta}}
is equivalent to fixing
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{2p}
to some real values. We study

\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle=\langle\theta\rvert C(\phi_{2p})DC(\phi_{2p-1})D\cdots DC(\phi_{1})D\lvert+\rangle\,.

Recall that
h_{c}(\theta)=R_{Y}(-\theta/2)XR_{Y}(\theta/2)
. So
\lvert\theta\rangle=R_{Y}(-\theta/2)\lvert+\rangle
, and so

\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle=\langle+\rvert DC(\phi_{2p})DC(\phi_{2p-1})D\cdots DC(\phi_{1})D\lvert+\rangle\,.

By
Corollary

9
, the matrix below can be equivalently written with some pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
:

DC(\phi_{2p})DC(\phi_{2p-1})D\cdots DC(\phi_{1})D=\begin{pmatrix}z^{-1}&0\\
0&z\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z^{-1}A(z)&-zB^{\#}(z)\\
z^{-1}B(z)&zA^{\#}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}z^{-2}A(z)&-B^{\#}(z)\\
B(z)&z^{2}A^{\#}(z)\end{pmatrix}

Writing
\lvert+\rangle
in the
Y
-basis, we have
\lvert+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
i\end{pmatrix}
. For this pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
,

\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{-2}A(z)+z^{2}A^{\#}(z)-iB(z)-iB^{\#}(z)\right)\,,

Then the performance of the depth-
p
QAOA has the form

\displaystyle\langle\chi(\theta)\rvert h_{c}(\theta)\lvert\chi(\theta)\rangle=2|\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle|^{2}-1=-1+\frac{1}{2}\left|z^{-2}A(z)+z^{2}A^{\#}(z)-iB(z)-iB^{\#}(z)\right|^{2}\,.

The converse direction holds because
Corollary

9
is an equivalence.
∎

Theorem

6
means that for the QAOA on the ring of disagrees, we can search over all angles
\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}
, or equivalently over all Laurent pairs
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
.

Corollary 10
.

Suppose
n=2k
. For
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=\max_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}}\left[1-\frac{1}{4k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left|z_{j}^{-2}A(z_{j})+z_{j}^{2}A^{\#}(z_{j})-iB(z_{j})-iB^{\#}(z_{j})\right|^{2}\right]\,,

where

z_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}/2}=\exp[i\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2k}]\,.

Proof.

Using
3
, we have

\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=k-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle\chi(\theta_{j})\rvert h_{c}(\theta_{j})\lvert\chi(\theta_{j})\rangle\,,\qquad\theta_{j}=\pi\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2k}\,.

The corollary follows after using
Theorem

6
to expand each
\langle\chi(\theta_{j})\rvert h_{c}(\theta_{j})\lvert\chi(\theta_{j})\rangle
as a Laurent polynomial in
z_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}/2}
.
∎

Given a set of angles
\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}
, one can construct the Laurent pair
(A,B)
using
8
. But the reverse direction is also possible: given the Laurent pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
, there are efficient algorithms to compute the angles
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{2p}
(e.g.
[
2
,
10
]
), and thus the angles
\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}
.

4
When the QAOA cannot see the whole graph

Let
F
be the Laurent polynomial in
z
that represents the amplitude
\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle
:

F(z):=\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{-2}A(z)+z^{2}A^{\#}(z)-iB(z)-iB^{\#}(z)\right)\,.

(2)

Then the performance ratio of the QAOA on
C_{2k}
is always of the form

1-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|F(e^{i\theta_{j}/2)}|^{2}\,.

In the depth-
p
QAOA,
F
is a Laurent polynomial of degree at most
2p+1
. Moreover,
F^{\#}=F
. So

F(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}c_{2p+1-2m}\cdot z^{2p+1-2m}=\sum_{m=0}^{p}c_{2m+1}(z^{2m+1}+z^{-(2m+1)})\,.

When the QAOA cannot see the whole graph, the performance ratio simplifies. First, consider
|F|^{2}
:

\displaystyle|F(e^{i\theta_{j}/2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|2\sum_{m=0}^{p}c_{2m+1}\cos((2m+1)\cdot\theta_{j}/2)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=4\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{p}c_{2m+1}c_{2m^{\prime}+1}^{*}\cos((2m+1)\cdot\theta_{j}/2)\cos((2m^{\prime}+1)\cdot\theta_{j}/2)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{p}\frac{1}{2}c_{2m+1}c_{2m^{\prime}+1}^{*}\left(\cos(\theta_{j}\cdot(m-m^{\prime}))+\cos(\theta_{j}\cdot(m+m^{\prime}+1))\right)\,.

The angles
\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k}\}
are equally spaced at
\pi/k
increments around the half circle. As a result, many
\cos
terms average to zero:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\cos(\theta_{j}\cdot c)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\cos(\frac{2j-1}{2k}\cdot c\pi)=\mathbbm{1}_{c=0\text{ mod }2k}\cdot k\cos(\frac{c\pi}{2k})

We track the nonzero terms when the QAOA cannot see the whole graph, i.e.
p<k
:

•

For the first term, this only occurs when
(m-m^{\prime})=0\mod 2k
; this can only occur at
m=m^{\prime}
.

•

For the second term, this only occurs when
(m+m^{\prime}+1)=0\mod 2k
. This never occurs when
p<k
, so this term contributes zero.

Altogether, when
p<k
, the performance ratio of the depth-
p
QAOA takes the form

1-2\sum_{m=0}^{p}|c_{2m+1}|^{2}=1-\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}|c_{2p+1-2m}|^{2}\,.

4.1
Upper bound

We first use Cauchy-Schwarz to prove an upper bound on the QAOA’s performance. There are other proofs of this fact (e.g.
[
7
]
), but our proof will help us identify the lower bound.

Claim 11
.

Suppose
n=2k
. For any
k>p\geq 0
,

\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle\leq\frac{2p+1}{2p+2}\,.

Proof.

The claim is trivial at
p=0
, since

\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=\frac{1}{2k}\langle+\rvert^{\otimes 2k}H_{C}\lvert+\rangle^{\otimes 2k}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

We now study
p\geq 1
. On one qubit, the mixer Hamiltonian at
\theta=0
matches the cost Hamiltonian, and so the QAOA layers are rotations about the
X
-axis. Since the initial state is
\lvert+\rangle
, the state remains
\lvert+\rangle
up to a global phase. Then the squared amplitude
|\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle|^{2}=|\langle+|+\rangle|^{2}=1
. Since

|F(z)|^{2}=|\langle\theta|\chi(\theta)\rangle|^{2}\,,

we have

1=|F(e^{i\cdot 0/2})|^{2}=\left|\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}c_{2p+1-2m}\right|^{2}\leq(2p+2)\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}|c_{2p+1-2m}|^{2}\,,

by Cauchy-Schwarz. Then

\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}|c_{2p+1-2m}|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2p+2}\,.

So the performance ratio of the depth-
p
QAOA is

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}|c_{2p+1-2m}|^{2}\leq 1-\frac{1}{2p+2}\,.

∎

4.2
Lower bound

We now show that the QAOA can meet this upper bound. The only inequality in
11
is Cauchy-Schwarz, which is saturated when
F
has coefficients of equal value.

Claim 12
.

Suppose
n=2k
. For any
k>p\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle\geq\frac{2p+1}{2p+2}\,.

Proof.

The claim is trivial at
p=0
, since

\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=\frac{1}{2k}\langle+\rvert^{\otimes 2k}H_{C}\lvert+\rangle^{\otimes 2k}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

We now study
p\geq 1
. Consider the polynomial

F_{p}(z)=\frac{1}{2p+2}\sum_{m=0}^{p}(z^{2m+1}+z^{-(2m+1)})=\frac{1}{2p+2}\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}z^{2p+1-2m}\,.

If
F=F_{p}
, the performance ratio of the depth-
p
QAOA is

1-\sum_{m=0}^{2p+1}|c_{2p+1-2m}|^{2}=1-\frac{2p+2}{(2p+2)^{2}}=\frac{2p+1}{2p+2}\,.

In our convention
z=e^{i\theta/2}
,
F_{p}
has a simpler form:

F_{p}(z)=\frac{1}{2p+2}\frac{z^{2p+2}-z^{-2p-2}}{z-z^{-1}}=\frac{1}{2p+2}\frac{\sin((p+1)\theta)}{\sin(\theta/2)}=\frac{\cos(\theta/2)}{p+1}U_{p}(\cos\theta)\,,

where
U_{p}
is the
p^{\text{th}}
Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind.
U_{p}
has real coefficients, and satisfies

\displaystyle U_{p}(1)=p+1\,,

\displaystyle U_{p}(1)>0\,,

\displaystyle U_{p}(y)\geq U_{p}(1)\text{ if }y>1\,,

\displaystyle|U_{p}(y)|\leq U_{p}(1)\text{ if }{-1}\leq y\leq 1\,.

Then there exists a polynomial pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
that sets
F
equal to
F_{p}
. The proof is somewhat technical; it is deferred to
21
in
Appendix

C
.
∎

4.3
System size independence

So far we have only looked at cycles with even length (
n=2k
). We now handle the case when
n
is odd. Recall that the QAOA is a local algorithm. This is because of a lightcone argument. The chance any edge is cut is a function only of its local neighborhood. When the QAOA cannot see the whole graph the local neighborhood around each edge is always the same regardless of
n
.

Fact 13

(
[
4
]
)
.

For any choice of
\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}
, the value

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\cdot\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle

is fixed for all
n\geq 2p+2
.

13
implies that Claims
11
and
12
apply to cycles with odd length. This proves
Theorem

1
.

5
When the QAOA sees the whole graph

Theorem

2
follows by
14
(when
n
is even) and
18
(when
n
is odd). We prove each case.

5.1
When
n
is even

During the preparation of this document we found that
[
8
]
also proved
14
.

Claim 14
.

Suppose
n=2k
. For any
p\geq k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=1\,.

Proof.

Fix
p
. Consider the Laurent polynomials

A_{p}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{3-2p}+z^{1-2p}\right)\,,\qquad B_{p}(z)=\frac{1}{2}(z-z^{-1})\,.\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:A-B-sparse-cos}

(3)

We verify that
(A_{p},B_{p})\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
. They have real coefficients, powers of
z
with odd parity, and degree at most
2p-1
. It remains to check the normalization:

A_{p}A_{p}^{\#}=\frac{1}{4}\left(2+z^{2}+z^{-2}\right)\,,\qquad B_{p}B_{p}^{\#}=-B_{p}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left(2-z^{2}-z^{-2}\right)\,.

Then
A_{p}A_{p}^{\#}+B_{p}B_{p}^{\#}=1
.

By
Corollary

10
, the performance ratio of the QAOA on
C_{2k}
is at least

\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle\geq 1-\frac{1}{4k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left|z_{j}^{-2}A_{p}(z_{j})+z_{j}^{2}A_{p}^{\#}(z_{j})-iB_{p}(z_{j})-iB_{p}^{\#}(z_{j})\right|^{2}\,,

where
z_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}/2}=\exp[i\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2k}]
. The below expression simplifies when
z=e^{i\theta/2}
:

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{-2}A_{p}(z)+z^{2}A_{p}^{\#}(z)-iB_{p}(z)-iB_{p}^{\#}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(z^{1-2p}+z^{-1-2p}+z^{2p-1}+z^{2p+1}-2iB_{p}(z)+2iB_{p}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(z+z^{-1})(z^{2p}+z^{-2p})

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta/2)\cos(p\theta)\,.

So the performance ratio of the QAOA is at least

1-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\cos^{2}(\theta_{j}/2)\cos^{2}(p\theta_{j})=1-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\cos^{2}(\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2k})\cos^{2}(p\pi\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2k})\,.

Suppose
k=p
. Then for all
1\leq j\leq k
,

\cos^{2}(p\pi\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2k})=\cos^{2}(j\pi-\frac{\pi}{2})=0\,.

So the performance ratio is exactly
1
. When
p\geq k
, we can use the associated angles for the first
k
layers of the QAOA, and set the remaining angles to
0
.
∎

Since in expectation the QAOA finds cuts of maximum size there can be no fluctuation above this value. So the QAOA always finds a cut of maximum size.
In this case, there is a simple choice of angles to optimize
C_{2k}
.

Claim 15
.

For any
p=k\geq 2
, the QAOA finds a perfect cut of
C_{2k}
with angles

\displaystyle\phi_{j}=\begin{cases}0&1\leq j\leq p-1\\
\pi/4&j=p\\
-\pi/4&j=p+1\\
0&p+2\leq j\leq 2p\end{cases}

where

(-\gamma_{1},2\beta_{1},\dots,-\gamma_{p},2\beta_{p})=(\tilde{\gamma}_{1},\tilde{\beta}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}_{p},\tilde{\beta}_{p})=(\phi_{1}+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi_{2}-\frac{\pi}{2},\dots,\phi_{2p-1}+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi_{2p}-\frac{\pi}{2})\,.

Proof.

Using
8
, we decompose the Laurent pair chosen in
14
. Note that

\begin{pmatrix}z^{1-p}&0\\
0&z^{p-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(z+z^{-1})/2&(z-z^{-1})/2\\
(z-z^{-1})/2&(z+z^{-1})/2\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z^{1-p}&0\\
0&z^{p-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z^{3-2p}+z^{1-2p}\\
z-z^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{p}(z)\\
B_{p}(z)\end{pmatrix}\,,

Moreover,

C(-\pi/4)DC(\pi/4)=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\
-1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z^{-1}&0\\
0&z\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&-1/\sqrt{2}\\
1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z+z^{-1}&z-z^{-1}\\
z-z^{-1}&z+z^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

This is generated by
\phi_{j}=0
for
1\leq j\leq p-1
,
\phi_{p}=\pi/4
,
\phi_{p+1}=-\pi/4
, and
\phi_{j}=0
for
p+2\leq j\leq 2p

So, the QAOA with these angles (up to a factor of

in
\beta
and sign in

when
p=k

∎

When
p>k

starting with the angles in

for any

(i.e. beyond depth

When

Claim 16

(
[

)
.

Consider the one-qubit Hamiltonian

h_{c}(\theta)=X\cos\theta+Z\sin\theta

and one-qubit QAOA state

\lvert\chi(\theta)\rangle:=e^{-i\tilde{\beta}_{p}X}e^{-i\tilde{\gamma_{p}}h_{c}(\theta)}\dots e^{-i\tilde{\beta}_{1}X}e^{-i\tilde{\gamma_{1}}h_{c}(\theta)}\lvert+\rangle\,,

where

\vec{\tilde{\gamma}}:=(\tilde{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}_{p})=(-\gamma_{1},\dots,-\gamma_{p})
and
\vec{\tilde{\beta}}:=(\tilde{\beta}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\beta}_{p})=(2\beta_{1},\dots,2\beta_{p})

For

\displaystyle\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=k-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle\chi(\theta_{j})\rvert h_{c}(\theta_{j})\lvert\chi(\theta_{j})\rangle\,,\qquad\theta_{j}=\pi\cdot\frac{2j}{2k+1}\,.

Note that the angles

are slightly different.
16
allows us to use

6
, which gives the following consequence:

Corollary 17
.

Suppose

. For
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=\max_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}}\left[\frac{n-1}{n}-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left|z_{j}^{-2}A(z_{j})+z_{j}^{2}A^{\#}(z_{j})-iB(z_{j})-iB^{\#}(z_{j})\right|^{2}\right]\,,

where

z_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}/2}=\exp[i\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{2j}{2k+1}]\,.

We now prove that the QAOA always finds a cut of maximum size on the odd cycle.

Claim 18
.

Suppose
n=2k+1
. For any
p\geq k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k+1}\cdot\max_{\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=\frac{2k}{2k+1}=\frac{n-1}{n}\,.

Proof.

Since the maximum cut size is
n-1
, we just need to prove a lower bound.

Suppose the claim is true when
p=k
. Then when
p\geq k
, we can use the associated angles for the first
k
layers of the QAOA, and set the remaining angles to
0
. So we focus on the case
p=k
.

Invoking
Corollary

17
, the claim is true if

F(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{-2}A(z)+z^{2}A^{\#}(z)-iB(z)-iB^{\#}(z)\right)=0

for every
z_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}/2}=\exp[i\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{2j}{2p+1}]
.

Consider the function

F_{p}(z)=\frac{1}{4p+2}(z+z^{-1})\sum_{m=-p}^{p}z^{2m}\,.

For each
z_{j}
, the sum can be rewritten as

\sum_{m=-p}^{p}z_{j}^{2m}=\frac{z_{j}^{2p+1}-z_{j}^{-(2p+1)}}{z_{j}-z_{j}^{-1}}=\frac{\sin((2p+1)\theta_{j}/2)}{\sin(\theta_{j}/2)}=\frac{\sin(j\pi)}{\sin(j\pi/(2p+1))}=0\,.

So
F_{p}(z_{j})=0
for each
z_{j}
. The claim is proven if there exists a Laurent polynomial pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
that sets
F=F_{p}
.

In our convention
z=e^{i\theta/2}
,
F_{p}
has a simpler form:

\displaystyle F_{p}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4p+2}(z+z^{-1})\cdot\frac{z^{2p+2}+z^{2p}-z^{-2p}-z^{-(2p+2)}}{z^{2}-z^{-2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(\theta/2)}{2p+1}\cdot\frac{\sin((p+1)\theta)+\sin(p\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(\theta/2)}{2p+1}\left(U_{p}(\cos\theta)+U_{p-1}(\cos\theta)\right)\,,

where
U_{p}
is the
{p}^{\text{th}}
Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind.

Note that the polynomial
V(y)=U_{p}(y)+U_{p-1}(y)
has real coefficients, and satisfies

\displaystyle V(1)=2p+1\,,

\displaystyle V(1)>0\,,

\displaystyle V(y)\geq V(1)\text{ if }y>1\,,

\displaystyle|V(y)|\leq V(1)\text{ if }{-1}\leq y\leq 1\,.

In fact the last three conditions are preserved under positive linear combinations, so they are satisfied by positive sums of Chebyshev polynomials. By
21
, there exists a polynomial pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
that sets
F
equal to
F_{p}
.
∎

6
Discussion

We have shown that the QAOA finds cuts with largest possible expected size among symmetric local algorithms on the ring of disagrees.
It is surprising that we do not know any
classical
algorithm in this family matching the performance of the QAOA.
Our results also apply when the edges of the ring are an assortment of agree and disagree, since the performance of the QAOA does not depend on the sign of any edge (e.g.
[
7
, Appendix A]
).

Although we proved the existence of optimal angles, we do not know a closed form for these angles. They must be related to the roots of Chebyshev polynomials.

We are not aware of prior work studying the QAOA in the language of quantum signal processing.
Another quantum algorithm which is optimized with a good choice of Laurent polynomials is
[
3
]
.

During the preparation of this document we became aware of another group (Uri Kol, Maor Ben Shahar, Kfir Sulimany, Dirk Englund) independently proving
Theorem

1
using the Lean proof assistant. Unlike this work, such a proof is written in computer code, and can be formally verified, once the definitions and theorem statements are properly checked.

This work was completed with the help of contemporary artificial intelligence systems. It is exciting to see what new research can be done by humans equipped with this emerging technology.

Proof of reduction to one-qubit systems (

For convenience, we rederive the result in

First, let
\tilde{H}_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{2k}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}
.
All indices are modulo
2k
.
Then

\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert H_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle=k-\frac{1}{2}\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert\tilde{H}_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle\,.

We wish to minimize
\langle\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rvert\tilde{H}_{C}\lvert\vec{\gamma},\vec{\beta}\rangle

\displaystyle S_{j}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(Y_{j}\pm iZ_{j}\right)

\displaystyle a_{j}

\displaystyle=S_{j}^{-}\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j-1}(-X_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle a_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=S_{j}^{+}\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j-1}(-X_{j^{\prime}})

We verify the canonical anticommutation relations. For
v>u
, we have

\displaystyle a_{v}a_{u}

\displaystyle=S_{v}^{-}\prod_{j=1}^{v-1}(-X_{j})\cdot S_{u}^{-}\prod_{j=1}^{u-1}(-X_{j})=-S_{v}^{-}S_{u}^{-}\prod_{j=u}^{v-1}(-X_{j})\,,

\displaystyle a_{u}a_{v}

\displaystyle=S_{u}^{-}\prod_{j=1}^{u-1}(-X_{j})\cdot S_{v}^{-}\prod_{j=1}^{v-1}(-X_{j})=S_{u}^{-}S_{v}^{-}\prod_{j=u}^{v-1}(-X_{j})=-a_{v}a_{u}\,,

so
\{a_{u},a_{v}\}=0
. Similarly,
\{a_{u}^{\dagger},a_{v}^{\dagger}\}=0
and
\{a_{u},a_{v}^{\dagger}\}=\{a_{v}^{\dagger},a_{u}\}=0
.

When
u=v
, we have

\displaystyle a_{u}^{2}

\displaystyle=(S_{u}^{-})^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(Y_{u}-iZ_{u})(Y_{u}-iZ_{u})=\frac{1}{4}\left(Y_{u}^{2}-iZ_{u}Y_{u}-iY_{u}Z_{u}-Z_{u}^{2}\right)=0\,,

\displaystyle a_{u}a_{u}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=S_{u}^{-}S_{u}^{+}=\frac{1}{4}(Y_{u}-iZ_{u})(Y_{u}+iZ_{u})=\frac{1}{4}\left(Y_{u}^{2}-iZ_{u}Y_{u}+iY_{u}Z_{u}+Z_{u}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(I-X_{u}\right)\,.

so
\{a_{u},a_{u}\}=0
. Similarly,
\{a_{u}^{\dagger},a_{u}^{\dagger}\}=0
. However,
\{a_{u},a_{u}^{\dagger}\}=\frac{1}{2}\left(I-X_{u}+I+X_{u}\right)=I
.

In this picture,

H_{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{2k}(2a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}-1)

For
u<2k

(a_{u}+a_{u}^{\dagger})(a_{u+1}-a_{u+1}^{\dagger})=Y_{u}(-iZ_{u+1})(-X_{u})=Z_{u}Z_{u+1}\,.

For
u=2k

(a_{2k}+a_{2k}^{\dagger})(a_{1}-a_{1}^{\dagger})=-(-iZ_{1})Y_{2k}\prod_{i=1}^{2k-1}(-X_{i})=-Z_{1}Z_{2k}\prod_{i=1}^{2k}(-X_{i})\,.

Altogether, this means

\tilde{H}_{C}=-(a_{2k}+a_{2k}^{\dagger})(a_{1}-a_{1}^{\dagger})P_{X}+\sum_{u=1}^{2k-1}(a_{u}+a_{u}^{\dagger})(a_{u+1}-a_{u+1}^{\dagger})

We then add a phase factor to unify the expression. Let
b_{j}:=a_{j}e^{-i\pi\cdot j/(2k)}

(e^{i\pi\cdot j/(2k)}b_{j}+e^{-i\pi\cdot j/(2k)}b_{j}^{\dagger})(e^{i\pi\cdot(j+1)/(2k)}b_{j+1}-e^{-i\pi\cdot(j+1)/(2k)}b_{j+1}^{\dagger})=e^{-i\pi/2k}\left(e^{i\pi(j+1)/k}b_{j}b_{j+1}-b_{j}b^{\dagger}_{j+1}\right)+\text{h.c.}

Similarly,

-(a_{2k}+a_{2k}^{\dagger})(a_{1}-a_{1}^{\dagger})=(b_{2k}+b_{2k}^{\dagger})(e^{i\pi/2k}b_{1}-e^{-i\pi/2k}b_{1}^{\dagger})=e^{-i\pi/2k}\left(e^{i\pi/k}b_{2k}b_{1}-b_{2k}b_{1}^{\dagger}\right)+\text{h.c.}

Moreover, the QAOA stays within the
P_{X}=1
subspace, as we start in
\lvert+\rangle^{\otimes 2k}
, and
P_{X}
commutes with
H_{M}
and
H_{C}
. So within this subspace,

\displaystyle H_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2k}(2b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}-1)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{C}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\pi/2k}\sum_{j=1}^{2k}\left(e^{i\pi(j+1)/k}b_{j}b_{j+1}-b_{j}b^{\dagger}_{j+1}\right)+e^{i\pi/2k}\sum_{j=1}^{2k}\left(b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j+1}-e^{-i\pi(j+1)/k}b_{j}^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}_{j+1}\right)\,.

We now apply a Fourier transform. Let
c_{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}\sum_{j=1}^{2k}e^{i\omega\cdot jm}b_{j}
, where
\omega=2\pi/(2k)
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{2k}e^{-i\omega m}c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k}\sum_{m=1}^{2k}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{2k}e^{i\omega(-1-j+j^{\prime})m}b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2k}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{2k}2k\cdot\delta_{j^{\prime}=1+j}b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=1}^{2k}b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j+1}\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{2k}e^{i\omega m}c_{m}c_{1-m}

\displaystyle H_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2k}(2c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}-1)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{C}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\omega/2}\sum_{m=1}^{2k}e^{i\omega m}c_{m}(c_{1-m}-c_{m}^{\dagger})+e^{i\omega/2}\sum_{m=1}^{2k}e^{-i\omega m}c_{m}^{\dagger}(c_{m}-c_{1-m}^{\dagger}).

We group the cost Hamiltonian. Let
\theta_{m}=(m-1/2)\omega

\theta_{1-m}=-\theta_{m}

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{2k}e^{i\theta_{m}}\left(c_{m}c_{1-m}-c_{m}c_{m}^{\dagger}\right)+e^{-i\theta_{m}}\left(c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{m}-c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{1-m}^{\dagger}\right)

Since
\{c_{m},c_{1-m}\}=0

e^{i\theta_{m}}c_{m}c_{1-m}+e^{i\theta_{1-m}}c_{1-m}c_{1-(1-m)}=2i\sin(\theta_{m})c_{m}c_{1-m}

\{c_{m}^{\dagger},c_{1-m}^{\dagger}\}

\cos(\theta_{m})=\cos(\theta_{1-m})

e^{i\theta_{m}}+e^{i\theta_{1-m}}=2\cos(\theta_{m})

\tilde{H}_{C}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}2i\sin(\theta_{m})(c_{m}c_{1-m}+c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{1-m}^{\dagger})+2\cos(\theta_{m})(c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{m}+c_{1-m}^{\dagger}c_{1-m}-1)\,.

We start in the maximum eigenstate of

c_{j}

\lvert 11\rangle\langle 11\rvert-\lvert 00\rangle\langle 00\rvert

\lvert 00\rangle
and
\lvert 11\rangle

X_{m}:=c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{m}+c_{1-m}^{\dagger}c_{1-m}-1

Z_{m}=i(c_{m}c_{1-m}+c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{1-m}^{\dagger})
. Then

\displaystyle H_{M}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=1}^{k}X_{m}

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{C}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=1}^{k}\cos(\theta_{m})X_{m}+\sin(\theta_{m})Z_{m}\quad

\displaystyle\theta_{m}=\pi\cdot(2m-1)/(2k)\,.

In this picture, the initial state is
\lvert+\rangle^{\otimes k}

\tilde{H}_{C}
and

are

\displaystyle h_{c}(\theta)

Each application of

\phi_{0}

The coefficient of

a_{r}a_{-r}+b_{r}b_{-r}\,.

Since
AA^{\#}+BB^{\#}=1

\phi

B^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}:=C(-\phi)\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix},

. Assume

0

\phi

Appendix C
Proof of existence of optimal Laurent polynomials

To start, we require the following statement:

Lemma 20
.

Consider a polynomial
V(y)
with real coefficients that satisfies

\displaystyle V(1)>0\,,

\displaystyle V(y)\geq V(1)\text{ if }y>1\,,

\displaystyle|V(y)|\leq V(1)\text{ if }{-1}\leq y\leq 1\,.

Then the polynomial

f(y):=-1+(y/4+1)\frac{V^{2}(y/2+1)}{V^{2}(1)}

has a simple root at
0
, and all other roots are complex conjugate pairs (i.e. not on the real line).

Proof.

At
y=0
, we have

f(0)=-1+\frac{V^{2}(1)}{V^{2}(1)}=0
.
We then consider

\frac{f(y)}{y}=-\frac{1}{y}+(1/y+1/4)\frac{V^{2}(y/2+1)}{V^{2}(1)}\,.

We show
\frac{f(y)}{y}
has no roots on the real line. Then it will have only complex roots; since all coefficients are real, these complex roots come in conjugate pairs. We split into four cases:

•

When
y<-4
, both terms are positive, so
f(y)/y>0
.

•

When
-4\leq y<0
, we have
|y/2+1|\leq 1
, and so
|V(y/2+1)|\leq|V(1)|
. This implies
f(y)/y\geq-1/y+(1/y+1/4)=1/4>0
.

•

When
y=0
,
f(y)/y
has a removable singularity, which is at least
1/4
by continuity.

•

When
y>0
, we have
V(y/2+1)\geq V(1)
. This implies
f(y)/y\geq 1/4
. ∎

Claim 21
.

Fix
p\geq 1
. Consider a degree-
p
polynomial
V(y)
with real coefficients that satisfies

\displaystyle V(1)>0\,,

\displaystyle V(y)\geq V(1)\text{ if }y>1\,,

\displaystyle|V(y)|\leq V(1)\text{ if }{-1}\leq y\leq 1\,.

Then there exists a polynomial pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
that satisfies
B^{\#}=-B
and

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{-2}A(z)+z^{2}A^{\#}(z)-iB(z)-iB^{\#}(z)\right)=(z+z^{-1})\frac{V(z^{2}/2+z^{-2}/2)}{2V(1)}\,.

Proof.

By
Lemma

20
, the polynomial

f(y):=-1+(y/4+1)\frac{V^{2}(y/2+1)}{V^{2}(1)}\,.

has roots of the form
\{0,a_{1},a_{1}^{*},\dots,a_{p},a_{p}^{*}\}
.
Let
c
be the leading coefficient of
\frac{V(y/2)}{2V(1)}
.
We decompose

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=yq(y)q^{*}(y)\,,

\displaystyle q(y)

\displaystyle:=-c\prod_{m=1}^{p}\left(y-a_{m}\right)=w(y)+ib(y)\,,

\displaystyle q^{*}(y)

\displaystyle:=-c\prod_{m=1}^{p}\left(y-a_{m}^{*}\right)=w(y)-ib(y)\,,

where

\displaystyle A(z):=z^{2}(z-z^{-1})\cdot w\left((z-z^{-1})^{2}\right)+z^{2}(z+z^{-1})\frac{V(z^{2}/2+z^{-2}/2)}{2V(1)}\,,

\displaystyle B(z):=(z-z^{-1})\cdot b\left((z-z^{-1})^{2}\right)\,.

We verify that
(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{2p-1}
.
All coefficients are real, and all powers of

have odd parity. We defer checking the degree to the end. Here we check the normalization. Note that

is

times a function of

, so

. Next,

has two parts: when exchanging

Altogether,

\displaystyle A^{\#}A+B^{\#}B

\displaystyle=-(z-z^{-1})^{2}\Big(w^{2}\left((z-z^{-1})^{2}\right)+b^{2}\left((z-z^{-1})^{2}\right)\Big)+(z+z^{-1})^{2}\frac{V^{2}(z^{2}/2+z^{-2}/2)}{4V^{2}(1)}

\displaystyle=-y(w(y)+ib(y))(w(y)-ib(y))+(y+4)\frac{V^{2}(y/2+1)}{4V^{2}(1)}

\displaystyle=(y/4+1)\frac{V^{2}(y/2+1)}{V^{2}(1)}-f(y)\,,

where

.

We now inspect the degree of

and
B

has (ordinary) degree

. Meanwhile,

\leq p
