Cryptic transcription in aging
Authors: Di Wu, Binh P Nguyen, Elena Zholdybayeva, Ju Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCryptic transcription is an aberrant form of transcription that initiates from non-canonical, non-promoter regions within the gene. In mammals, a repressive chromatin state effectively inhibits cryptic transcription, preserving transcriptional fidelity. Aging-induced loss of trimethylated lysine 36 on histone H3 reduces DNA methylation, and accumulation of promoter-like histone modifications renders gene bodies permissive to spurious initiation by RNA polymerase II. This allows previously repressed intragenic regions to acquire promoter-like chromatin features. Cryptic transcription produces aberrant RNAs that interfere with normal gene expression, leading to truncated proteins or nonfunctional mRNAs. The aberrant RNA and protein products impair stem cell self-renewal and differentiation, thereby contributing to the development of aging and disease. This review summarizes the mechanisms underlying age-related cryptic transcription, focusing on the function of H3K36me3, DNA methylation, H3K4me3, H3K27ac, and transcription factors. In addition, we discuss the effects of cryptic transcription on aging stem cells and aged tissues.Keywords: aging, chromatin structure, cryptic transcription, epigenetic, histone modification

Introduction

Aging is characterized by a progressive decline in organismal function driven by the accumulation of senescent cells and altered behaviors of non-senescent cells. Aging is associated with a gradual loss of tissue function and an increased risk of disease.1 The defining features of aging encompass genomic instability, telomere shortening, epigenetic changes, impaired proteostasis, defective macroautophagy, disrupted nutrient sensing, mitochondrial impairment, cellular senescence, depletion of stem cells, modified intercellular communication, persistent inflammation, and microbial imbalance.2 Among these hallmarks, epigenetic alterations promote age-associated chromatin opening.3 One of the major functions of the chromatin structure is the maintenance of the integrity and stability of the eukaryotic genome.4

Increased cryptic transcription is a conserved feature of aging in yeast and worms, driven by dysregulation of chromatin structure. Cryptic transcription is a type of transcriptional dysregulation characterized by aberrant initiation of transcription from non-promoter regions within gene bodies.5 During aging, the level of H3K36me3 is reduced within gene bodies, particularly at age-associated cryptic transcription start sites (cTSSes), which impairs KDM5B and DNMT3B recruitment, resulting in intragenic H3K4me3 accumulation and DNA hypomethylation, thereby facilitating cryptic transcription initiation. The upregulation of activator protein 1 (AP-1) family transcription factors (TFs) is associated with p300/CBP, resulting in H3K27ac deposition. Accordingly, increased cryptic transcription is increasingly recognized as a feature of mammalian aging.

This article aims to systematically review the molecular regulatory mechanisms of cryptic transcription during aging. It focuses on how dysregulated epigenetic modifications, particularly H3K36me3 and DNA methylation, drive abnormal cryptic transcription, and it explores the potential therapeutic value of targeting cryptic transcription to delay aging, enhance stem cell function, and prevent or treat age-related diseases.

Cryptic transcription as a conserved feature of aging

As early as 2003, intragenic cryptic transcription with Pol II aberrantly initiating transcription from promoter-like sequences within the gene body was identified in yeast.6 McCauley et al. developed a method to detect cryptic transcription by examining exon-based expression levels within transcripts. Using this approach, they demonstrated that cryptic transcription is elevated with age in aged murine hematopoietic stem cells (mHSCs) and culture-expanded human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs).7 Traditional RNA-seq fails to distinguish cTSSes formation from other 5′ end-generating events. In addition, RNA fragmentation provides more even coverage across the gene body but remains relatively depleted at the 5′ and 3′ ends, thus compromising the precise mapping of transcript boundaries.8 To overcome this limitation, researchers have employed DECAP-seq to sequence transcript 5′ ends to detect cTSSes in hMSCs. Data analysis revealed that cTSSes appear in both young and old samples, but age-increased cTSSes are more prevalent in the old.7 These results support the conclusion that cryptic transcription increases with age. Researchers employed precision run-on RNA-seq methodologies (PRO-seq and PRO-cap) in the IMR90 human lung fibroblast model to map cTSSes. The analyses of proliferating and senescent IMR90 cells identified that senescent cells harbor unique cryptic TSSs within gene bodies compared with proliferating cells.9 The analysis also detected bidirectional transcription typical of enhancers at cryptic TSSs specific to proliferating cells, with bidirectional transcription reduced at senescent-specific cryptic TSSs. This shift in transcriptional pattern demonstrates that during senescence, poised intragenic enhancers undergo an enhancer-to-promoter conversion at senescent cTSSes, thereby activating cryptic transcription (Figure 1).

Aging-induced cryptic transcription

Enhancers within genes that are stable in the youth will exhibit a promoter-like chromatin state during aging, initiating abnormal cryptic transcription and producing new cryptic transcripts.

Experiments have revealed that increased cryptic transcription is a hallmark of aging across mammalian tissues. ChRO-cap analysis of frozen liver tissue from mice aged 2–4 months and 22–25 months identified more unique cTSSes in aged mice, with the majority of cryptic transcription observed in female mice.9 In addition, analysis of 25 publicly available aging and senescence RNA-seq datasets from a range of tissues revealed that in 21 of them, cryptic transcription is elevated with age.7 However, elevated cryptic transcription was not observed in Rett and Werner syndromes, premature aging models driven by distinct molecular defects.10,11 We speculate that the mechanisms underlying cryptic transcription in normal aging may differ from these in certain premature aging pathologies.

Neural stem cells (NSCs) typically remain in a resting state and become active under certain conditions.12 McCauley et al. analyzed RNA-seq data from quiescent neural stem cells (qNSCs) and activated neural stem cells (aNSCs) in the subventricular region of old and young mice.7 The analysis revealed that the exon-based TPM ratio increased with age in both male and female aNSCs, but the change was not observed in qNSCs. These findings indicate that age-associated cryptic transcription occurs in NSCs, but only in aNSCs. During aging, aNSCs from female mice exhibit a gradual increase in cryptic transcription throughout the transcript, whereas male cells show an elevation restricted to the last exon. This suggests that cryptic transcription associated with aging exhibits sex-specific features in murine aNSCs. Sex-specific differences in age-related cryptic transcription are observed in human aging, though with a potentially divergent pattern across species. Reanalysis of a published human dermal fibroblast dataset revealed age-associated cryptic transcription exclusively in males.13 This observation contrasts with the female predominance observed in aged mouse livers and aNSCs. The sex-specific cryptic transcription may serve as a molecular signature for assessing sex differences in aging, analogous to the reported sex-specific effects of calorie restriction.14 Sex differences in adult neurogenesis have been reported in rodents under basal conditions, after stress, and after learning.15 Investigators raised the possibility that alterations in cTSSe usage between sexes may represent a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying sex differences in neurogenesis during aging. However, considering the discrepancies between species and tissues, sex differences in cryptic transcription remain to be fully explored. DNA methylation at GAGE10 declines with age in females, whereas it remains stable in males.16 DNA hypomethylation within gene bodies can promote transcription initiation at cTSSes.17 Progressive DNA hypomethylation at GAGE10 during female aging may reflect sex-specific epigenetic drift or altered X chromosome inactivation maintenance. Given that DNA hypomethylation within gene bodies can promote cryptic transcription initiation, this female-specific epigenetic change may create a locally permissive environment for aberrant transcription. However, whether GAGE10 harbors functional cryptic TSSes that become activated in female aging tissues, and whether such activation contributes to cellular dysfunction, remains to be experimentally validated.

In yeast, RNA-seq analyses reveal that genes exhibiting age-associated cryptic transcription are typically longer in length and transcribed at lower frequencies relative to randomly chosen gene sets.18,19 However, Lickwar et al. showed that cryptic transcription repressed by the Set2/Rpd3S pathway is not influenced by gene length or transcription frequency.20 This conclusion was drawn from the set2Δ yeast rather than from senescent cells. Investigation of cryptic transcript upregulation in aged worms revealed that genes exhibiting greater accumulation of cryptic transcripts were typically longer than a matched set of randomly selected background genes from the worm genome.18 In mHSCs and hMSCs, transcripts exhibiting increased cryptic transcription with age are longer than the average.7 Whether this length bias in aging represents a true biological feature or methodological detection bias remains to be determined. The analysis of genes with cTSSes demonstrated that genes with cTSSes typically have fewer and longer introns, shorter exons, shorter 5′ UTRs, and shorter 3′ UTRs.9 Researchers speculated that the vast excess of intronic sequence in genes with cTSSes results in prolonged RNA polymerase II residence time and the aberrant activation of cTSSes.21 The general features of genes harboring cTSSes remain consistent across proliferating and senescent states, indicating that these structural characteristics predispose genes to cryptic initiation independent of cellular context.

Comparative analysis of chromatin marks at cTSSes and canonical TSSes was performed, demonstrating both similarities and differences in chromatin composition.9 Both canonical and cryptic TSSs were depleted of H3K36me3, despite the high H3K36me3 levels typically present in the gene bodies in which cryptic TSSs are located. Cryptic TSSs have H3K4me1 restricted to the −2 and +2 nucleosomes, whereas H3K4me1 is depleted across the −2 to +2 region at canonical promoters. Age-increased cTSSes exhibit decreased H3K36me3 and increased H3K4me1, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac, accompanied by reduced DNA methylation and a more open chromatin state. Canonical TSSs have histone variants H3.3 and H2A.Z within one nucleosome on either side of the TSS.22 Cryptic TSSes are marked by a centrally positioned nucleosome that contains both H2A.Z and H3.3 histone variants. In senescence, cryptic TSSs gain well-positioned H2A.Z while losing H3.3 occupancy, with H2A.Z enrichment reminiscent of that at canonical TSSs. Most histone modifications and variants are enriched at the −1 and +1 nucleosomes flanking canonical transcription start sites. These differential chromatin features, including H3K4me1 distribution and H2A.Z/H3.3 positioning, suggest that nucleosomes at cryptic TSSes are less precisely positioned than those at canonical TSSes.

Epigenetic mechanisms driving cryptic transcription

Epigenetic mechanisms are alterations in genomic regulation that do not alter the DNA sequence, including DNA methylation, DNA demethylation, chromatin remodeling, and histone post-translational modifications.23 In mammals, cryptic transcription is normally repressed by a repressive chromatin state. During aging, the chromatin becomes more open due to reduced H3K36me3, loss of CpG methylation, and increased H3K4me3, which is permissive for RNA polymerase II entry and promotes cryptic intragenic transcription initiation.5 Cryptic transcription results from a failure to maintain ordered chromatin structure over gene bodies.24 This failure leads to the exposure of promoter-like sequences within genes. RNA polymerase II initiates inappropriate transcription from internal sites and produces cryptic transcripts.

H3K36me3 deposition facilitates recruitment of KDM5B to intragenic regions to erase intragenic H3K4me3.25 H3K36me3 guides the recruitment of DNMT3B to actively transcribed gene bodies to establish de novo DNA methylation.26 Aging-associated H3K36me3 loss leads to reduced DNA methylation and elevated intragenic H3K4me3, which together permit cryptic promoter activation and transcription initiation.27 Cryptic TSSes in senescence acquire a promoter-like chromatin state, characterized by elevated H3K4me3, reduced H3K4me1, increased H3K27ac, enriched p300/CBP, and well-positioned H2A.Z. As a result, repressive chromatin at cryptic promoters is compromised, allowing RNA polymerase II to initiate transcription from intragenic positions within gene bodies (Figure 2).

Epigenetic mechanism of cryptic transcription during aging

The gene bodies of actively transcribed genes are maintained in a relatively closed chromatin environment. In young cells, H3K36me3 across gene bodies, co-transcriptionally conferred by SETD2, serves as a scaffold to recruit chromatin modifiers KDM5B and DNMT3B. KDM5B removes intragenic H3K4me3, DNMT3B deposits de novo CpG methylation intragenically, together establishing a repressive chromatin state that suppresses cryptic transcription. During aging, declining H3K36me3 levels impair the recruitment of these factors, leading to H3K4me3 accumulation and DNA hypomethylation. Concurrently, AP-1 recruits P300/CBP to increase H3K27ac and further relax chromatin. These epigenetic changes create a permissive chromatin state that enables RNA polymerase II to initiate cryptic transcription from within the gene body.

H3K36me3 methylation is predominantly mediated by SETD2.28 The SET domain is the catalytic domain that mediates H3K36 trimethylation, flanked by the AWS domain at the N-terminus and the PS domain at the C-terminus in the primary sequence.29 RNA-seq analysis of SetD2-silenced mouse embryonic stem cells showed increased spurious transcripts and DECAP-seq revealed elevated intragenic transcription start sites.17 Previous studies have shown that deletion of SET2 shortens the replicative life span of yeast.18 Setd2 deficiency impairs HSC self-renewal and causes malignant transformation.30 Researchers reduced SETD2 expression in hMSCs and found that hMSC self-renewal is decreased.7 The finding that SETD2 deficiency impairs hMSC self-renewal is consistent with increased cryptic transcription limiting lifespan in yeast. On the basis that SETD2 catalyzes H3K36me3 and suppresses cryptic transcription, researchers speculated that elevated cryptic transcription potentially impairs self-renewal.

H3K36me1 and H3K36me2 decrease from 5′ to 3′ in gene bodies and are replaced by H3K36me3 toward the 3′ end.31 H3K36me2 is broadly distributed in broad domains covering both genic and intergenic regions, whereas H3K36me3 is predominantly associated with transcriptionally active gene bodies.32,33 H3K36me1/2 are produced by multiple enzymes, including NSD1/2/3 and potentially ASH1L, whereas H3K36me3 is predominantly deposited by SETD2.34 Consequently, SETD2 deficiency results in the loss of H3K36me3 and a concomitant increase in H3K36me1/2 levels.31 H3K36me2 restricts the expansion of H3K27me3 into H3K27me2 domains, maintaining the proper demarcation between H3K27me2 and H3K27me3 chromatin domains.35 In yeast, Set2-mediated H3K36 methylation represses cryptic transcription by recruiting Rpd3S,36 and suppresses histone exchange via H3K36me3-mediated inhibition of histone chaperone binding.37 ChIP-seq analysis of young and old hMSCs reveals that H3K36me3 is depleted within gene bodies with age, especially around age-increased cTSSes.7 H3K36me3 deposition at gene bodies requires SETD2 engagement with nucleosomes during active transcription elongation.38 Disruption of Setd2 in mESCs resulted in a reduction of H3K36me3 levels without affecting other histone modifications.39 SETD2-mediated H3K36me3 enhances FACT recruitment and modulates nucleosome dynamics, thereby repressing cryptic transcription.40 The reduction of H3K36me3 leads to a decrease in the recruitment of FACT complexes, resulting in reduced nucleosome occupancy on active genes. Therefore, failure to reassemble nucleosomes after Pol II transit leads to chromatin depletion and increased cryptic transcription in mammals.

H3K4me3 is enriched surrounding transcription start sites of active genes and positively correlates with gene expression levels.41 KDM5B removes intragenic H3K4me3 to repress cryptic intragenic transcription, thereby preventing spurious transcription initiation.25 The ratio of H3K4me1 to H3K4me3 at cTSSes determines whether a regulatory region functions as an enhancer or a promoter. Active promoters are marked by high H3K4me3 and depletion of H3K4me1 at the TSS, whereas enhancers are characterized by strong H3K4me1 enrichment and lack of H3K4me3.42 In senescence, an increased H3K4me3/H3K4me1 ratio at cTSSes reflects an enhancer-to-promoter conversion, accompanied by H2A.Z deposition with concomitant H3.3 loss and reduced DNA methylation specifically at these cryptic sites, features that correlate with spurious transcription initiation from intragenic sites.9

DNMT3B-dependent intragenic DNA methylation restricts aberrant transcriptional events within the gene body. DNMT3B is targeted to gene bodies via recognition of H3K36me3, resulting in intragenic CpG methylation.26 Cryptic TSSes associated with aging typically exhibit reduced DNA methylation, leading to further chromatin opening. Loss of Dnmt3b in mESCs leads to intragenic DNA hypomethylation and increased spurious transcription initiation.17

The study performed unsupervised motif analysis using HOMER to investigate TF binding sites at unique cTSSes, which revealed significant enrichment of AP-1 family TF-binding motifs that exhibited extremely high significance in senescent cells.9 BATF is an AP-1 family TF that functions as a global regulator of class switching through its dual requirements in TFH cell development and B cell isotype switching.43 However, BATF is not expressed in IMR90 cells. Researchers overexpressed BATF in proliferating IMR90 cells and performed PRO-cap to reveal that BATF overexpression generated new cryptic transcription sites primarily within genes.9 Separately, replicative lifespan curves and EdU incorporation assays revealed that BATF overexpression reduced cell growth and inhibited DNA synthesis. These results suggest that AP1 factors can promote cryptic transcription and impair cellular fitness. However, it remains unclear whether the growth defect is directly caused by cryptic transcription. During cellular senescence, upregulated AP-1 transcription factors bind as pioneer factors to enhancer elements, bookmarking a prospective enhancer landscape that foreshadows and drives the senescence transcriptional program.44 During cellular senescence, poised intragenic enhancers undergo activation toward a promoter-like chromatin configuration, marked by elevated H3K4me3, recruitment of the acetyltransferases p300 and CBP, and subsequent H3K27ac deposition. In mammals, CBP/p300 is the principal histone acetyltransferase responsible for catalyzing H3K27ac.45,46 H3K27ac is enriched at active enhancers, where it functions as a critical epigenetic signal for enhancer activity, enhancer RNA production, and transcription of enhancer-regulated genes.47 Overall, these results suggest that AP-1 factors, which can be activated during the integrated stress response,48 may drive cryptic transcription and thereby contribute to impaired cell health.

Biological consequences of cryptic transcription

Cryptic transcription produces aberrant RNA molecules that may generate non-functional or harmful proteins and disrupt cellular functions.49 The phenomenon was initially discovered in yeast and subsequently observed in mammalian aging. In yeast, Set2-mediated H3K36 methylation suppresses cryptic transcription by recruiting the Rpd3S histone deacetylase complex and the ISW1b chromatin-remodeling complex to maintain a repressive chromatin state in the wake of elongating RNA polymerase II. Under nutrient stress, Set2 loss triggers cryptic transcription within gene bodies, generating antisense RNAs that extend into host gene promoters and interfere with sense transcription.50 Loss of H3K36me3 within gene bodies decreases lifespan in C. elegans.51 In cellular senescence models, genes with cryptic transcription are enriched for pathways related to signaling, cell communication, cell adhesion, and cell development/differentiation pathways.9 The protein products of cryptic transcription-prone genes function in signal transduction and cell adhesion pathways that contribute to SASP and cancer, underscoring the importance of maintaining transcriptional fidelity. The SASP enables senescent cells to secrete inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and other signaling molecules, contributing to age-related tissue dysfunction and promoting tumorigenesis.52,53 The mRNAs that result from cryptic transcription are less complex and simply lack some portion of the 5′ end of the full-length transcript, which can include the 5′ UTR and often a part of the coding sequence, depending on the location of the cryptic promoter.27 Collectively, these structural changes ultimately impact the functional integrity of the protein produced. Ribosome profiling analysis of Dnmt3b-deficient mESCs demonstrated a decrease in ribosomes from 5′ UTRs and ribosome accumulation within transcripts, especially in introns.17 This pattern suggests that cryptic transcripts are potentially translated into aberrant proteins. As cryptic transcription increases, more cryptic transcripts may associate with ribosomes, potentially inhibiting the translation of full-length transcripts and contributing to the age-associated loss of proteostasis.5,54

Cryptic transcripts are subject to one of two fates (Figure 3). The RNA exosome degrades a large proportion of cryptic transcripts.55 Some cryptic transcripts are stable, capped, polyadenylated molecules, which are delivered to the ribosome to produce aberrant proteins.17 The peptides synthesized from cryptic transcripts can likewise undergo different pathways.5 Some aberrant peptides may fold properly into active proteins and go on to function in the cell. However, such proteins often lack regulatory or enzymatic domains, or the cryptic transcripts may encode entirely different peptides if a frameshift occurred. Misfolded aberrant peptides can engage the chaperone system, thereby titrating away factors essential for the folding of endogenous proteins. Finally, still other aberrant peptides generated from cryptic transcripts may undergo proteasomal degradation. Sen et al. proposed that age-related spurious transcription promotes a noisy transcriptome and degradation of coherent transcriptional networks and further speculated that cryptic transcripts may represent one source of such transcriptional noise.9

Consequences of cryptic transcription

Cryptic transcripts are processed via two divergent pathways. They can be degraded by the RNA exosome. Or they are processed as endogenous mRNAs, exported from the nucleus, and translated by the ribosome. The resulting peptides may fold correctly into active proteins and go on to function in the cell. Such proteins may lack regulatory or enzymatic domains, thus forming dysfunctional proteins and possibly interfering with cellular function. If the cryptic transcript caused a frameshift, such proteins would encode an entirely different peptide. The peptides may misfold, engaging the chaperone system and possibly titrating factors needed for the proper folding of endogenous proteins. Finally, the aberrant peptides generated from cryptic transcripts may be degraded by the proteasome, increasing the burden on the proteasome and autophagy systems.

Transcriptional interference (TI) is one proposed mechanism by which cryptic transcription disrupts normal gene transcription. TI occurs when cryptic transcripts extend through and overlap with endogenous transcription start sites, thereby inhibiting transcription from the overlapping TSS.50 Cryptic unstable transcripts (CUTs) constitute a poorly characterized class of non-coding RNAs in budding yeast.56 Although CUTs have historically been dismissed as by-products of transcriptional noise, mounting evidence indicates that these unstable transcripts actively participate in modulating gene expression programs. CUTs regulate gene expression through mechanisms including attenuation, TI, and alternative transcription start site choice.57 SRG1 is an upstream intergenic transcript of SER3, transcribed from its own promoter and TSS.58 Transcription of SRG1 mediates repression of SER3 through TI.59 Transcription of SRG1 leads to Spt6p- and Spt16p-mediated nucleosome assembly over the SER3 promoter region, subsequently impairing binding of transcription activators and the TATA-binding protein.60 In mammals, definitive proof is still accumulating, and indirect evidence supports its occurrence. H3K36me3 deposition by SETD5 on active gene bodies serves as an epigenetic mark that promotes transcription elongation and preserves the fidelity of co-transcriptional processes, including splicing. In Setd5 haploinsufficient murine NSCs, RNA polymerase II exhibits a reduced elongation rate and impaired processivity specifically on SETD5 target genes, resulting in a delayed transcription wavefront along gene bodies.61 Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that intragenic cryptic transcription can interfere with the elongation of endogenous, full-length transcripts in mammalian cells through mechanisms analogous to TI described in yeast. Further studies are required to establish a direct causal link between cryptic transcription and mammalian aging. Increased cryptic transcription contributes to a noisy transcriptome and degradation of coherent transcriptional networks, representing a loss of transcriptional fidelity during aging.

Aberrant transcription within gene bodies has been associated with the emergence of cancer-promoting transcripts. A systematic analysis of RNA-seq data from 14 cancer types within The Cancer Genome Atlas demonstrated that tumor samples show a higher prevalence of cryptic antisense polyadenylated transcripts (CAPTs) than normal tissues.62 CAPTs represent polyadenylated antisense transcripts that predominantly arise from intronic regions of protein-coding genes. Correlation analysis between CAPT expression level and DNA methylation level of associated CpG sites showed that a majority of CAPT-to-CpG site links exhibit a negative correlation across cancer types.62 DNA methylation levels at the TSS regions of certain expressed CAPTs decrease in tumors with CAPT expression. Reduced DNA methylation at CAPT TSS regions may contribute to the activation of cryptic antisense transcription. The activation of CAPTs is regulated by chromatin modulation and related TFs. LRRK1-CAPT is identified as an oncogenic lncRNA in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). Silencing of LRRK1-CAPT via CRISPR interference or siRNA significantly reduced proliferation, colony formation, and migration. Overexpression of LRRK1-CAPT increased proliferation and colony formation. LRRK1-CAPT represents a cancer-specific therapeutic target, broad-spectrum epigenetic drugs paradoxically induce CAPT activation. DNMT inhibitors (DNMTis) have been used to treat myelodysplastic syndromes and acute myeloid leukemia.63 HDACis such as vorinostat and romidepsin have been used to treat refractory cutaneous and peripheral T cell lymphoma.64 DNMTi and HDACi trigger de novo transcription of CAPTs and upregulate the oncogenic lncRNA LRRK1-CAPT across multiple cell lines.62 Consequently, the identification of CAPTs not only provides novel insights into the complexity of cancer transcriptomes but also suggests the potential value of targeted, locus-specific epigenetic interventions to complement broad epigenetic modulation strategies.

Conclusions

Cryptic transcription is a feature of cellular senescence and tissue aging driven by a permissive chromatin state. During aging, epigenetic changes, including reduced H3K36me3, local DNA hypomethylation, and increased chromatin accessibility, facilitate the conversion of intragenic enhancers into promoters. This restructured chromatin structure, along with the activity of transcription factors like AP1, enables cryptic transcription initiation from non-canonical sites within gene bodies. The age-associated increase in cryptic transcription undermines transcriptional fidelity, promoting a noisy transcriptome and degrading transcriptional networks. Experimental alteration of spurious transcription has profound consequences for cellular health, primarily affecting intracellular signal transduction pathways. Chromatin-mediated loss of transcriptional fidelity leads to aberrant transcription initiation inside genes, producing spurious transcripts during cellular senescence and tissue aging. Age-related cryptic transcription selectively targets genes involved in signal transduction, cell communication, and cell adhesion pathways that contribute to SASP. This selective targeting indicates that the loss of transcriptional fidelity is a functionally consequential feature of cellular senescence and tissue aging. This breakdown of transcriptional regulation is hypothesized to be a contributing factor to the aging process. This review systematically summarizes evidence for the widespread occurrence of cryptic transcription and its epigenetic regulatory mechanisms in mammalian aging, extending the decline in transcriptional fidelity previously observed in yeast and worms to higher organisms.

The investigation of age-associated cryptic transcription in mammals is in its early stages, leaving several essential mechanistic and therapeutic questions unanswered. Recent studies have established that cryptic transcription increases during mammalian cellular senescence and tissue aging, driven by characteristic chromatin alterations including reduced H3K36me3, loss of DNA methylation, and gain of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac at intragenic sites. These changes promote enhancer-to-promoter conversion and transcriptional noise across diverse stem cell compartments. Collectively, cryptic transcription emerges as a conserved hallmark of mammalian aging, extending findings from yeast and nematodes to human and murine systems. This discovery provides a theoretical foundation for understanding aging mechanisms and developing anti-aging strategies. By linking chromatin dysregulation to aberrant transcription initiation, this work offers a novel framework for developing targeted interventions against age-related functional decline. Furthermore, the functional consequences of cryptic transcripts, including the production of aberrant peptides and disruption of normal gene expression, warrant further investigation to elucidate their causal roles in cellular dysfunction and organismal aging. Sex-specific mechanisms may modulate cryptic transcription during aging, though the directionality of this sex bias appears to differ between mice and humans, and further validation in additional human tissues is warranted.

The comprehensive characterization of cryptic transcripts will require methodological innovations that integrate nascent transcription mapping with mature RNA analysis. PRO-seq and PRO-cap map nascent transcription but fail to assess transcript stability or steady-state levels, limiting functional interpretation of detected cryptic species. Conversely, RNA-seq provides steady-state quantification but suffers from 5′/3′ coverage bias. Long-read sequencing addresses this limitation by capturing full-length transcript structures, including splicing patterns and isoform composition. Future integration of PRO-cap with long-read sequencing potentially facilitates systematic dissection of the biological functions of cryptic transcripts in aging, enabling precise mapping of their full-length structures and functional contributions to cellular senescence and age-related pathologies.

Therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the pathways driving age-related cryptic transcription may mitigate structural and functional tissue deterioration associated with aging. The dCas9-HDAC3 induces H3K27 deacetylation at the target loci, but the dCas9-HDAC3 effect on gene expression is bidirectional and context-dependent.65 SETD2-dCas9 increases H3K36me3 levels site-specifically at target sites, with robust de novo deposition observed at sites lacking endogenous H3K36me3.66 Future studies are needed to characterize the deposition of H3K36me3 by SETD2-dCas9 at additional genomic loci, including cTSSes. In summary, cryptic transcription represents a conserved feature of aging and senescence with significant implications for cellular homeostasis. While epigenetic editing tools such as dCas9-HDAC3 and SETD2-dCas9 offer promising avenues for locus-specific modulation of histone modifications, broad-spectrum epigenetic drugs, including DNMT inhibitors and HDAC inhibitors, may inadvertently activate oncogenic CAPTs. Therefore, future therapeutic strategies should prioritize targeted, locus-specific epigenetic interventions to precisely suppress deleterious cryptic transcription while preserving essential gene expression programs.
