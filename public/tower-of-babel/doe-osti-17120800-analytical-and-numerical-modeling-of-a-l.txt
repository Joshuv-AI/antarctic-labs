 Abstract
 This document presents a time-domain and frequency-domain analysis of a two-wire transmission
 line terminated by a lumped inductive load. In the time domain, the transient behavior is simulated
 using the Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) method with a staggered leapfrog discretiza-
 tion of the Telegrapher’s equations. To ensure numerical stability at the boundary and prevent
 voltage divergence, an implicit inductive load update scheme is developed and implemented. In
 the frequency domain, analytical formulations for the phasor voltage and current distributions are
 derived using hyperbolic functions. The input impedance and natural resonance frequencies are
 obtained from these phasor representations of voltage and current. Physical parameters based on
 a parallel copper wire geometry in an ideal air dielectric are established for the system. Finally,
 the framework is verified by simulating a broadband Gaussian pulse source up to 600 MHz. The
 numerical FDTD simulation results are compared against the analytical models for characteristic
 impedance, input impedance, and load impedance spectral responses.

Example: Gaussian Pulse Source
To demonstrate the application of the two-wire transmission line framework, a Gaussian pulse in the
time-domain is implemented for the source voltage:

The duration of the Gaussian pulse is set to τ = 0.25 × 10−9 s, and the time delay is established at

Voltage and Current Distribution along the Transmission Line
The steady-state voltage and current distribution along the transmission line at the upper frequency
limit of f = 600 MHz are illustrated in Figure 4. At the inductive load (x = ℓ), the voltage and current
are expected to be 90◦ out of phase, due to the fundamental relation:

In phasor notation, the imaginary unit j introduces a +90◦ phase rotation in the complex plane,
meaning the voltage must lead the current by 90◦ . This phase relation is clearly observed at x = ℓ in
Figure 4, where the voltage phase is ϕ = 0◦ while the current lags by ϕ = 270◦ = −90◦ .
 If the load impedance ZL = jωL is much smaller than the characteristic impedance Zc of the
transmission line, such that ZL << Zc , the inductive load approximates a short-circuit boundary
condition, forcing the voltage at the load to zero. This behavior is demonstrated in Figure 4. A small
inductance of L = 25 nH shows the voltage approaching zero at x = ℓ while current peaks.
 On the other hand, if the load impedance is much larger than the characteristic impedance of
the line ZL >> Zc , the inductive load approximates an open-circuit boundary condition. Under this
condition, the line current is expected to drop to zero at the load. This behavior is demonstrated in
Figure 5. A large inductance of L = 25 GH shows current dropping to zero at x = ℓ while the voltage
reaches a maximum.

 • Step 473 (t = 1.50 ns): Peak Pulse at Left Boundary
 The peak of the Gaussian pulse enters the line at x = 0 m. At this stage, the voltage (5 V) and

 • Step 1000 (t = 3.17 ns): Peak of Forward Propagation at Mid-Point

 • Step 1526 (t = 4.84 ns): Peak Reaches the Inductive Load at Right Boundary

 current to drop toward zero.
 • Step 2052 (t = 6.50 ns): Peak Returns to Mid-Point Post-Reflection
 The reflected pulse peak returns to the mid-point x = 0.5 m traveling in the −x-direction.

 mismatch reflects the pulse, driving the voltage negative and drawing a negative current spike.
