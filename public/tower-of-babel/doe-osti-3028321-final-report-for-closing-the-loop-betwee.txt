 Final Report for Closing the Loop Between In Situ Stress Complexity and
 EGS Fracture Complexity
DOE Award Number:
 Award Recipient Organization: University of Utah (DOE Project DE-EE0007080)
 Subrecipient Organization: Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (Award Number: 2-2446)
Principal Investigator: Dr. Kayla A. Kroll

Characterizing in situ stress is essential in Enhanced Geothermal System (EGS) for both risk
assessment and operation design. Standard stress measurement techniques such as diagnostic
fracture injection tests (DFITs) require creating hydraulic fractures and apply analytical methods
such as the G-function analysis to estimate the stress. These classical approaches rely on several
assumptions, including but not limited to: (i) the induced fracture is planar and persistent, and (ii)
the fracture plane is normal to the minimum horizontal stress. However, these assumptions may
not hold in EGS environments like Utah FORGE, where thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM)
coupling, wellbore deviation, and rock heterogeneity can alter the local stress condition and
consequently lead to fracture complexities in both near-well and far-field regions.

To address the research gap, this project aims to integrate experimental block fracturing tests and
high-fidelity numerical simulations to advance the understanding of the connection between in situ
stress, wellbore orientation, and hydraulic fracturing patterns in crystalline rocks under EGS
conditions. More specifically, a suite of hydraulic fracturing simulations have been conducted
using LLNL’s GEOS simulator, individually focusing on THM DFIT modeling, near-well
hydraulic fracture nucleation and propagation, and large-scale hydraulic fracturing. In parallel, the
experimental study at the University of Pittsburgh (U. Pitt.) systematically investigates how factors
such as wellbore configurations, thermal treatments, and applied principal stresses influence
fracture patterns. Experimental observations and property measurements on FORGE rocks or
analogous samples are further used for model calibration and validation.

At the conclusion of the project, we have achieved following accomplishments:
• Selected minifrac tests or DFITs have been analyzed and modeled, with detailed evaluation of
 pressure-dependent permeability, thermal stresses, and poromechanics coupling.
• A new phase-field formulation has been developed to model near-well fracture nucleation and
 propagation and further applied to both experimental setup and field conditions to examine
 how fracture patterns are connected with wellbore configuration and applied stresses.
• Large-scale hydraulic fracturing simulations have been performed for FORGE Stage 3
 stimulation to study the effects of stress roughness/heterogeneity on the fracture geometry and
 to calibrate an apparent toughness anisotropy based on microseismic data.
• Block fracturing experiments have been conducted to study how fracture geometry and
 pressure signature behave with various settings, e.g., temperature, stress. Also, they provided
 key data for model validation and guidance of future fracture-based stress characterization.

Keywords: Enhanced Geothermal Systems, Hydraulic Fracturing, Near-well Fracture Complexity, Stress
Roughness, True-triaxial Block Fracturing

1 Executive Summary
This report documents and summarizes the research including both numerical modeling and
laboratory experiment carried out to investigate the relationship between the in situ stress condition
and EGS hydraulic fracture complexity associated with Utah FORGE.

1.1 Background
In situ stress plays a central role in both operational design and risk assessment for EGS
development, particularly for hydraulic stimulation. For example, the EGS hydraulic fracture
produced in hot crystalline granites commonly exhibits greater vertical extent than those in
unconventional oil and gas reservoirs, where the intrinsic fabrics of sedimentary rocks can lead to
stress alteration across layers and hinder vertical propagation. In addition, in situ stress strongly
influence fracture initiation and propagation near the wellbore, especially when the wellbore is
deviated, which is common in EGS operations. Therefore, a clear understanding of how in situ
stress is connected with hydraulic fracture characteristics is essential for the interpretation and
optimization of EGS stimulations.

At the Utah FORGE site, multiple minifrac tests or DFITs, including those in well 58-32 and well
16(B)78-32, have been conducted to estimate the in situ stress magnitude and orientation.
Hydraulic stimulation programs have also been performed in the deviated sections of both injector
(16(A)78-32) and producer (16(B)78-32). Collectively, these fracturing activities provide valuable
datasets that support numerical simulations and laboratory experiments, enabling validation of
physics solvers, evaluation of existing stress estimation techniques, and characterization of
potential rock fabrics or heterogeneities within the formation.

1.2 Objectives
The primary objective of this project is to integrate experimental true-triaxial block fracturing tests
at various temperatures and high-fidelity numerical simulations to systematically study the
connection between in situ stress condition and the hydraulic fracture complexity. The ultimate
goal is to provide Utah FORGE and future EGS applications with high-fidelity experiment and
modeling data as well as scientific understanding that improve stress characterizations. For this
purpose, four interrelated technical tasks are defined for this project.

Task 1 – Apply the high-fidelity THM solver that incorporates fracturing capabilities to model
traditional minifrac tests and DFITs at the Utah FORGE site to infer in situ stress

Task 2 – Apply a high-resolution phase-field model that can capture fracture nucleation, initiation,
and propagation in the near-wellbore region to characterize the observed fracture complexity with
different in situ stress fields and wellbore configurations.

 5

Task 3 – Build and calibrate a field-scale hydraulic fracturing model that incorporates stress
“roughness” inferred from geophysical logs and apparent anisotropy in rock toughness to infer the
far-field stress. The modeled fracture propagation trajectory is compared with the microseismic
catalog recorded during stimulations.

Task 4 – Conduct true-triaxial blocking fracturing tests under controlled conditions to provide
parameters to, and calibrate and validates the numerical models using FORGE granites or
analogous samples.

1.3 Key findings
Key findings are summarized below for each technical task and for the overall project:
• Simulation results of the minifrac test conducted in well 16(B)78-32 indicate potential
 pressure-dependent behavior in rock permeability, suggesting that natural fractures play an
 important role in fluid leakoff. Additionally, the comparison of the simulated and field pressure
 response, including G-function curves, shows that the fracture may remain open at the end of
 the cycle, and consequently, 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 can be over-estimated. To avoid this, future minifrac tests
 and DFITs should require sufficiently long shut-in period to ensure fracture closure.
• THM simulation results of DFITs suggest that by simply applying classic G-function analysis
 or the fracture compliance method without accounting for the thermal stress or poroelastic
 coupling can underestimate 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 .
• Parametric studies using the novel phase-field model developed in this project have
 systematically investigated the connection between near-well fracture patterns (e.g., fracture
 shape, alignment, and nucleation azimuth) and the applied stress conditions (magnitude and
 orientation), wellbore orientation and deviation. Curved and inclined fractures are more likely
 to occur under the reverse stress condition for the vertical well, whereas for the horizontal well,
 they only take place under the normal and strike-slip conditions. Also, the curvature and
 inclination of fractures increase with a lower 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 and a larger deviation angle.
• The baseline phase-field model that incorporates the real geologic and wellbore conditions at
 the FORGE site has also been constructed. Modeling results with various in situ stresses show
 the fracture geometry becomes non-planar and secondary fractures occur when the horizontal
 stress difference decreases at the presence of rock heterogeneity.
• Comparing large-scale modeling results and field observation inferred from microseismic
 catalog for vertical and horizontal growth of the Stage 3 hydraulic fracture, we confirm the
 effects of stress roughness on resulting fracture geometry and obtain a calibrated apparent
 toughness anisotropy of ∆𝐾𝐼𝑐 ≈ 0.5 MPa ∙ m1/2.
• In block fracturing experiments, the thermal induced stress due to pre-cooling circulation
 significantly affects breakdown pressure and fracture geometry. Experiments show reduction
 in breakdown pressure and promote transitions from longitudinal to transverse or mixed
 fracture patterns due to thermo-elastic stress effects, particularly for horizontal wells.
 Meanwhile, such thermal stress and rock heterogeneity generate multi-scale fracture
 complexity. Cooling induces microfractures at the grain scale, which act as seed fractures and
 contribute to more complex, multi-directional fracture propagation at the macroscale.
• Fracture closure behavior is strongly influenced by surface roughness and asperity interactions.
 The observed asperity resistance leads to systematic overestimation of minimum stress in G-
 function analysis, indicating that closure reflects progressive contact of rough fracture surfaces
 rather than true stress conditions.

 6

Overall, the results of laboratory experiments and numerical modeling collectively show that stress
interpretation is strongly affected by coupled processes, such as thermal stress, poroelasticity,
stress and rock heterogeneity, and fracture surface roughness, which can lead to complex fractures
geometries like nonplanar shape, delayed or progressive closure, and apparent toughness
anisotropy.

These insights directly inform future stress characterization in EGS by demonstrating that
conventional methods (e.g., G-function analysis) can misestimate stress if these effects are
neglected, and by providing validated modeling and experimental frameworks to correct for them.
Incorporating these mechanisms enables more accurate inference of in situ stress, better
interpretation of field diagnostics, and improved design of stimulation strategies in crystalline
geothermal reservoirs.

2 Introduction
This section provides the introduction to the report, outlining the purpose, scope of work, and the
overall structure.

2.1 Purpose of the report
The primary purpose of the report is to document, integrate, and interpret the numerical modeling
and laboratory experimental efforts that have been performed to investigate how hydraulic fracture
complexities across scales are connected with in situ stress conditions at the Utah FORGE site.
More specifically, the report summarizes key findings individually obtained from (i) THM
modeling of fracture injection tests, (ii) phase-field simulations of near-well fracture patterns, (iii)
large-scale hydraulic fracture simulations with stress roughness, and (iv) true-triaxial blocking
fracture experiments. Collectively, these efforts produce valuable datasets, guidance for numerical
and experimental model design, and a suite of hydraulic fracturing solvers in GEOS. Finally, the
report consolidates the lessons learnt from each task, identifies the limitations of the current study,
and proposed potential future enhancements, with the ultimate goal of informing and improving
stress characterization improvement in crystalline EGS reservoirs.

2.2 Scope of work
The project comprises four technical tasks designed to validate the existing stress estimate
techniques and investigate the connection between the hydraulic fracture patterns and in situ stress
under EGS-related conditions through an integrated experimental and numerical framework. The
specific tasks include:

Task 1 – THM modeling of DFITs and minifrac tests conducted in FORGE wells, including
detailed analyses on the effects of fracture closure parameter, pressure-dependent matrix
permeability, cooling-induced thermal stress, and poroelastic coupling.

Task 2 – High-resolution phase-field simulations of fracture nucleation, initiation, and propagation
in the near-wellbore region to characterize the observed fracture complexity with different in situ
stress fields and wellbore configurations.

Task 3 – Field-scale modeling of fracture growth associated with the FORGE hydraulic
stimulation, with the primary focus on comparing modeled fracture propagation trajectory against
microseismic catalog and examining the effects of stress “roughness” inferred from geophysical
logs as well as apparent anisotropy in rock toughness.

Task 4 – True-triaxial block fracturing experiments under controlled conditions to provide
parameters to, and calibrate and validates the numerical models using FORGE granites or
analogous samples.

The remainder of report is organized as follows:

3 THM Modeling of DFITs/Minifrac Tests
This section presents the background motivation, methodology, and key findings for the numerical
modeling of DFITs or minifrac tests conducted at the Utah FORGE wells. The specific injection
test studied is the minifrac test 2 (MF2) on the vertical section of well 16(B)78-32, which was
carried out in 2023. A number of influential factors including pressure-dependent rock
permeability, cooling-indcued thermal stress, and poroelastic coupling are examined in the
numerical investigation.

3.1 Background
DFITs and minifrac tests are a common type of the injection test performed in subsurface energy
systems. These tests provide important information such as fracture closure pressure and
instantaneous shut-in pressure (ISIP), from which in situ stress conditions can be inferred. During
a typical DFIT, the well-head pressure is measured across two stages: (1) a controlled injection
stage to create a hydraulic fracture, and (2) a shut-in stage where injection is stopped and the
hydraulic fracture gradually closes as fluid inside leaks into surrounding rock matrix. The pressure
data are then used in various analyses including G-function tests (Economides & Nolte, 1989) to
recover the stress conditions.

Several minifrac tests and DFITs have been completed at different wells at the Utah FORGE site.
In 2017 and 2019, a series of injection tests were performed in a pilot well 58-32 at the Utah-
FORGE site to estimate the in situ stress. These injection activities were conducted in three
separate zones, isolated with packers and bridge plugs during tests. A detailed description of the
site geology, well 58-32, and all injection cycles can be found in Xing et al. (2020). Later in 2023,
another round of minifrac tests was conducted at seven different depths in the uncased open
borehole of well 16(B)78-32. To identify the induced cracks, geophysics logs were collected prior
and after the tests. Among these tests, only MF2 at depth of 5495 feet has visible induced hydraulic
fractures from the log data. More detailed description of this minifrac test program can be referred
to in Kelley et al. (2024).

Here, we primarily study the MF2 in well 16(B)78-32 due to availability of geophysics log and
confidence that the induced hydraulic fractures have been formed. The modeling parameters are
calibrated to match the pressure measurement in the injection and shut-in stages in Cycle 1 of MF2.
During the history-matching process, it is found the pressure-dependent rock permeability is
necessary to better capture the pressure curve at shut-in. The calibrated model then serves as the
baseline, from which we further investigate the individual influence of the thermal stress and
poroelastic coupling.

3.2 Methodology and Model Setup
This section summarizes the formulations and solution algorithms for modeling the DFITs or
minifrac tests in GEOS. The non-linear hydraulic aperture model of interest, which is the Barton–
Bandis model is presented and incorporated into the model to better capture the fracture closure
response.

3.2.5 Solution Algorithms
To enhance numerical convergence and facilitate parameter calibration, the DFIT modeling
workflow is divided into two stages. The first stage includes the simulation of fluid injection and
fracture propagation until shut-in begins. Subsequently, in the second stage, the modeled hydraulic
fracture geometry and pressure results are processed and imported to a separate model to simulate
fluid leak-off and fracture closure during the shut-in period during which fracture propagation is
deactivated. Material parameters, in situ stresses, and hydro-static pressure are kept consistent in
both phases to ensure a coherent and accurate representation of the hydraulic fracture’s behavior
throughout the simulation process.

Next we study the effects of poroelastic coupling on the fracture closure behavior during the shut-
in stage. Here, we consider the Biot coefficient equals to unity, corresponding to the case with
incompressible solid grain. Figure 3-7 compares shut-in responses with and without poroelastic

pressure in the surrounding formation. This pore-pressure buildup causes poroelastic dilation of
the rock matrix, which generates additional compressive stress acting on the fracture walls and
promotes earlier fracture closure.

3.3.4 Implications of Findings for Utah FORGE and EGS Technologies
Based on the key findings of the THM modeling presented above, we discuss and summarize their
implications for the FORGE project and future EGS development.

First, the calibration practice of the numerical model against field observations indicates that
injection tests at FORGE may be strongly influenced by fluid dissipation into pre-existing natural
fractures, thereby causing the leakoff process very sensitive to the pressure in the rock matrix.
Also, as described earlier, the case with a lower 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 than that reported by Battelle (Kelley et al.,
2024) exhibits a better match to the field data. It is mainly because the previous estimate in the
report was made assuming that fracture closure has occured before the test completed. However,
our modeling indicates that there is possibility that the fracture remains open even at the end of
shut-in. Therefore, the 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 may be overestimated if fracture closure has yet happened. Also
notably, this finding implicates that a sufficient long shut-in period is necessary to ensure complete
fracture closure for accurate DFIT interpretation.

Additionally, based on the calibrated models, we conduct the numerical studies individually
involving cooling-induced thermal stress and poroelastic coupling. The simulations with and
without thermal effects indicate that simply using fracture closure pressure without considering
possible thermal stress in the DFIT analysis can underestimate 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 , and such discrepancy can
be magnified as the fracture size and initial aperture increase. More importantly, because the
presence of cooling-induced thermal stress can delay the fracture closure, the field DFIT testing
should be conducted with sufficient shut-in time particularly in EGS where the thermal effects play
a dominant role. As for the simulations studying poroelastic effects, the results suggest that directly
approximating 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 with the fracture closure pressure while ignoring poroelastic effects can lead
to overestimation.

Last but not least, results from both numerical studies indicate that the compliance method
provides a more robust and reliable means of identifying fracture closure compared to the
conventional G-function approach. However, as discussed above, directly using the inferred
closure pressure as a proxy for 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 can be problematic when thermal and poroelastic effects are
not explicitly considered. Despite that, if thermo-poroelastic effects can be appropriately
quantified, either through analytical corrections or fully coupled numerical modeling, the
compliance method remains an optimal tool for determining fracture closure pressure so as to
estimate the in situ stress.

3.3.5 Limitations/Future improvements
Although a number of simplifications have been applied in the current numerical model, the
simulations still yield reasonable results and provide meaningful insights into the processes. With
that said, the numerical model itself still requires further improvements, and the current separated,
two-stage modeling workflow is not ideal for fully capturing the coupled dynamics of injection
and shut-in. Future work will therefore focus on addressing the following limitations.
• Wellbore storage and wellbore compressibility are not currently incorporated into the hydraulic
 fracturing model. These effects can strongly influence the initial pressure rise during injection,
 control fluid leakoff behavior during shut-in, and have a significant impact on pressure
 evolution during flowback tests when fluid is pumped back from the formation into the

 21

 wellbore. Incorporating wellbore storage and compressibility will be a primary focus of future
 model development.
• Near-wellbore fracture tortuosity and associated fluid loss are not considered in the present
 study. These effects are particularly important for deviated and horizontal wells, where
 complex fracture geometry is easier to form. For the specific minifrac test examined here, the
 treatment interval is located in a vertical section of the wellbore where the near-wellbore effects
 may be minimal. Nevertheless, near-wellbore fluid and energy losses should be incorporated
 in future studies to improve prediction.
• The current two-stage modeling approach does not allow all state variables to be continuously
 tracked throughout the entire injection and shut-in process. Developing a single-run model or
 a more streamlined workflow that can simulate the full injection–shut-in cycle in a unified
 manner would improve consistency, reduce uncertainty introduced by stage transitions, and
 enable more rigorous interpretation of pressure and fracture evolution.

4 Phase-field Modeling of Near-well Fracture Patterns
This section provides the background motivation, methodology, and key results for the modeling
of near-well fracture patterns using a novel phase-field formulation for hydraulic fracture
nucleation and propagation. The study focuses on a systematic numerical investigation based on a
representative, laboratory block fracturing setup with various combinations of applied principal
stresses and wellbore configuration. At the end, simulations of real field tests are also presented
for qualitative comparison between the field observations and numerical prediction.

4.1 Background
Prior studies have evidenced that nucleation and propagation of hydraulic fractures in the near-
wellbore region are influenced by a number of factors. Notable examples include the in-situ stress
condition and wellbore configuration. In fact, a non-preferred in-situ stress regime relative to the
wellbore trajectory may give rise to the reorientation of hydraulic fractures nucleated from the
wellbore, and further lead to near-wellbore fracture tortuosity. These near-wellbore complexities
may be magnified for the Utah FORGE project and Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) in
general where the injection and production wells are most likely horizontal or even inclined.

Near-wellbore fracture complexities are often observable through image logs, which offer valuable
insights into in situ stress characteristics. However, leveraging this information requires a high-
fidelity model capable of capturing the interplay among various factors influencing fracture
behavior. Compared with traditional discrete fracture approaches, the phase field method presents
significant advantages for modeling the complex geometries of hydraulic fractures in the near-
wellbore region. In this research project, a novel phase field formulation was developed, enabling
the modeling of hydraulic fracture nucleation and propagation in the near-well region (Fei et al.,
2023). Meanwhile, as part of this research effort, laboratory experiments were conducted to
investigate how factors such as stress regimes, wellbore orientation, and thermal cooling influence
fracture nucleation patterns. Details of laboratory experiments are presented in Section 6.

In this work, the recently proposed phase field method is employed to conduct a comprehensive
set of numerical experiments aimed at exploring the intricate relationships among near-wellbore
fracture nucleation complexity, in situ stress conditions, and wellbore deviation. The model is
designed to complement the experimental work. Accordingly, the numerical experiments are
tailored to represent the experimental setup, with rock properties calibrated based on a simple
experiment chosen as the base case. In addition to this combined numerical and experimental study,
the phase-field model is also applied to reproduce the near-well fracture pattern observed from the
field image log after minifrac tests at Utah FORGE well 16(B)78-32.

4.2 Methodology
In this subsection, we provide a brief description of the phase-field formulation employed in this
work. The full version of the formulation and more details of mathematical derivations can be
referred to Fei et al. (2023). Subsequently, we design a series of numerical experiments to
investigate the effects of the in-situ stress condition and wellbore deviation on the near-well
fracture pattern.

4.3.2 Summary of Numerical Experiment Results
With the calibrated parameters, we proceed to the main part of the numerical study that is to
simulate the near-well hydraulic fracture under varying stress conditions and well deviations. We
analyze the simulation results by examining the individual impact of the well deviation and the
stress condition. To elucidate their effects on the near-well fracturing behavior, we primarily focus
on three key features of the nucleated fractures: (i) the fracture geometry (planar or curved), (ii)
the fracture alignment relative to the well (longitudinal or inclined), and (iii) the fracture nucleation
location on the well circumference. These features are emphasized because they can be extracted
from image log data, allowing us to directly apply the insights gained from these simulations to
the field study. Here, we summarize the key findings, and readers can refer to the prior report (Fei
et al., 2025) for detailed discussions.

On the effect of wellbore deviation on fracture shape and inclination: We first study how the
wellbore deviation affects the near-well hydraulic fracture geometry. As detailed in the report (Fei
et al., 2025), the cases with no well deviation consistently exhibit planar hydraulic fractures that
are longitudinal to the well, regardless of the stress orientation or magnitude. When the wellbore
begins to deviate, the near-well fracture may exhibit a curved S-shape and incline relative to the
wellbore axis, depending on the specific stress regime and wellbore orientation (vertical versus
horizontal). For the vertical well, the fractures remain planar and aligned with the wellbore axis

 27

when it is rotated about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 for the vertical well. However, when the wellbore is rotated about
𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 or both horizontal stress axes for all stress regimes, the resulting fracture becomes curved,
forming an S-shape, and inclines relative to the wellbore axis, as demonstrated in Figure 4-5.
Generally, the degree of fracture inclination increases with the wellbore deviation angle, regardless
of the orientation or magnitude of the stress field. The curvature of the fracture increases with the
wellbore deviation angle, particularly under the normal stress condition for the vertical well. For
the horizontal well, the trend becomes differently. More specifically, the near-well fracture may
exhibit a curved S-shape and incline relative to the well axis, only when (i) rotation about either
𝜎𝑣 or both 𝜎𝑣 and 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 at normal stress regime, or (ii) rotation about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 only or both stress
axes at strike-slip regime. In the reverse stress regime, all results show planar fractures, irrespective
of the rotation axis/axes. All these observations highlight that the effects of wellbore deviation are
clearly dependent on the stress regime and the main wellbore orientation. Also notably, across all
stress regimes, we notice that the fracture inclination generally increases with the deviation angle
for both horizontal and vertical wells, suggesting that the stress state near the well gets more altered
as the deviation angle grows.

Figure 4-5 Extracted phase-field fractures of representative examples for the vertical well case under varying well deviations.

On the effect of wellbore deviation on fracture nucleation: The well deviation also influences the
fracture nucleation location. Taking the vertical well as an example, Figure 4-6 shows the location
of fracture nucleation on the well circumference for a specific set of examples under the reverse
stress condition with 𝜎𝑣 = 3 MPa. As can be seen, for cases with no well deviation, fractures
nucleate at the “north” and “south” poles of the circular well, where tensile stress typically
concentrates. However, when the wellbore is rotated about both horizontal axes, the fracture
nucleation location shifts counterclockwise due to altered stress distribution in the near-well
region. A more quantitative analysis is presented in the previous report (Fei et al., 2025) where the

 28

fracture azimuth is defined to measure the fracture nucleation location. The key observation is,
across all stress orientations and magnitudes, only the cases with well rotation about both stress
axes exhibit a nonzero fracture azimuth. This observation aligns with the analytical solution
presented in Zoback (2007), where the fracture position consistently occurs near the wellbore wall
closest to 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 , regardless of the vertical well’s deviation about either horizontal stress axis.
However, when the well is deviated about both horizontal axes, the fracture position begins to
shift. The same conclusion can be drawn for the horizontal well case, that the shift of fracture
nucleation location only happens when the wellbore deviates about both stress axes, but the
specific direction of the shift depends on the stress regime applied. Specifically, the fracture
nucleation point begins to shift counterclockwise from the “north” and “south” poles under the
normal stress condition, while it shifts toward the “west” and “east” poles of the well circle when
the stress regime transitions to reverse and strike-slip conditions.

On the effect of stress condition on fracture shape and inclination: For brevity, we show only the
cases of (i) the vertical well rotated about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 or both horizontal stress axes, and (ii) the
horizontal well rotated about 𝜎𝑣 , 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 , or both 𝜎𝑣 and 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 axes. For the vertical well with
fixed stress magnitudes, fracture inclination is maximized under reverse stress conditions, while it
is minimized under strike-slip conditions. For the horizontal well, the effects of the wellbore
deviation and the stress orientation are closely linked. When the wellbore deviation about 𝜎𝑣 only,
the cases under the reverse or strike-slip condition consistently produce longitudinal and planar
fractures, whereas those under the normal stress condition lead to inclined and curved fractures.
Conversely, when the wellbore deviation about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 only, the normal stress condition results in
longitudinal and planar fractures instead, while the fractures formed in the strike-slip cases become

 29

inclined and curved. Regarding the stress magnitude, a universal effect for both vertical and
horizontal wells can be observed that an increasing stress magnitude lowers the fracture inclination
and curvature, making the results more stress-controlled.

On the effect of stress condition on fracture nucleation: The stress orientation also influences the
azimuth of the fracture nucleation location when the wellbore is rotated about two stress axes. For
the vertical well, the azimuth deviates the most under the reverse stress condition, while as the
stress regime transitions to normal and strike-slip conditions, the azimuth decreases and
approaches zero, indicating that fracture nucleation becomes increasingly governed by the in situ
stress conditions. For the horizontal well, the fracture nucleation in the reverse stress regime
consistently shows less deviation from its original position in the no-deviation case compared to
the other two stress regimes. Next, we analyze the impact of the stress magnitude. For both vertical
and horizontal wells, a higher magnitude of 𝜎𝑚𝑖𝑛 generally leads to near-well fractures that are
more planar and longitudinal to the well. This observation indicates that near-well hydraulic
fracturing processes are more dictated by the in situ stress when the stress magnitude is higher.
There are a few exceptions. When the vertical well is under normal stress condition and the
horizontal well is under reverse stress condition, the influence of the stress magnitude on the
fracture azimuth is unclear. Therefore, to study the behavior of near-well fractures in greater detail,
we need to consider the interplay between the stress orientation and the ratio of the principal
stresses.

4.3.4 Implications of Findings for Utah FORGE and EGS Technologies
The systematic numerical study presented in Section 4.3.2 show clear evidence that the near-well
fracture patterns are influenced by both wellbore deviation and in situ stress conditions. One
important goal of this research is to leverage the insights and knowledge gained from this
numerical study to enhance existing methods for stress estimation in the Utah FORGE reservoir.
This means that if the wellbore deviation is known in advance, it is possible to deduce the in situ
stress conditions from the observed near-well fracture patterns. Generally, we can determine the
wellbore deviation based on the principal stress directions, which can be identified through
standard fracture injection tests on a non-deviated vertical well, whose near-well fractures are
usually regular and normal to 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 . With the wellbore deviation known a priori, we can apply the
insights gained from the above numerical investigation to infer in situ stress conditions when
relevant fracture features are available from image log data. For clarity, we divide the discussions
into three parts, individually focusing on (i) the fracture geometry (planar or curved), (ii) fracture
alignment relative to the well (longitudinal or inclined), and (iii) the fracture nucleation azimuth
on the well circumference.

Fracture geometry: We begin by discussing how the fracture geometry observed from the image
log can be utilized to infer the in situ stress condition. In general, our numerical results demonstrate
that the near-well fracture geometry can be either planar or curved (S-shaped), depending on the
specific wellbore deviation and in situ stress condition applied. Generally, the vertical well tends
to exhibit curved fractures when the wellbore deviates about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 or both horizontal stress axes
under the reverse or normal stress condition, particularly when the deviation angle is high and/or
𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 is low. Regarding the horizontal well, curved fractures are more likely to develop in the
normal and strike-slip stress regimes when the wellbore is deviated. Also, when the deviated
wellbore is rotated only about 𝜎𝑣 , we can infer that the stress regime is probably in a normal
condition if the curved fracture is observed. On the other hand, the occurrence of curved fractures
indicates a strike-slip condition when the horizontal well is deviated solely about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 . Although
the analysis currently provides only qualitative information, we believe it has the potential to aid
engineers and researchers in stress characterization based on fracture geometry observed from

 32

image logs. A more quantitative study can be performed by measuring the curvature of the near-
well fracture and correlating it with the deviation angle and stress magnitude. This would offer a
more robust tool for in situ stress analysis.

Fracture alignment to the wellbore axis: We switch our focus to the fracture alignment to the well
and explore its potential for inferring in situ stress states. The simulation results reveal two primary
types of fracture alignment: longitudinal to the well and inclined to the well. For a vertical well,
inclined fractures are observed when the wellbore is deviated about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 or both horizontal stress
axes under the normal or reverse stress condition. Inclined fractures may also form in a strike-slip
regime, but this typically requires a high wellbore deviation angle and/or low 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 . As for the
horizontal well, the presence of inclined fractures in a deviated well indicates a normal stress
condition if the rotation is about 𝜎𝑣 , while it points to a strike-slip condition if the rotation is about
𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 . However, under a reverse stress regime, inclined fractures may also appear when the
deviation angle is high and/or 𝜎𝑣 is low. Thus, additional information is necessary to refine stress
interpretation when inclined fractures are observed in a horizontal well.

Fracture nucleation azimuth: Finally, we examine the fracture nucleation azimuth and discuss how
this feature can help identify the in situ stress condition. As discussed in Section 4.3.2, the fracture
nucleation azimuth is highly dependent of the well deviation and the stress orientation.
Specifically, only the examples with well deviation about both stress axes transverse to the
wellbore exhibit a shift in the fracture nucleation point on the wellbore circumference. On the other
hand, the near-well hydraulic fracture typically nucleates at a location on the well wall with 90
from the axis of the smaller principal stress traversing the wellbore. This observation can be useful
for confirming whether 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 and 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 are indeed the principal stresses. Normally, it is assumed
that the vertical stress is a principal stress, thus the horizontal stress direction can be determined
by conducting a fracture injection test on a vertical well. However, certain natural or induced
activities, such as fault slip, may perturb the in situ stress state. To verify whether the inferred
horizontal stress direction is truly a principal stress, a deviated vertical well can be drilled with
rotation about one of the inferred horizontal stress axes by a certain angle (such as well 16(A)78-
32 for FORGE). Based on our numerical study, the fracture nucleation point should remain
unchanged if the well is only deviated about one principal stress axis. If a shift in the fracture
nucleation location is observed, it then suggests that the inferred horizontal stress direction may
not be the true principal stress direction. The same concept can be applied to horizontal wells.
However, it is noted that such method for horizontal wells is only valid in the normal and reverse
stress regimes, as the fracture nucleation point may shift significantly in a strike-slip regime, even
when the well is rotated solely around the 𝜎𝑣 axis. Also, the fracture nucleation point can be
sensitive to the near-well heterogeneities or local damage as demonstrated in the field test
simulation in Section 4.3.3. For example, fractures may nucleate at a local weak zone on the
wellbore wall instead of at the expected location where the tensile stress reaches maximum.
Therefore, further comprehensive studies are necessary to ensure a reliable use of this fracture
nucleation azimuth for interpreting the in situ stress condition.

Subsequently, we explore the potential application of this knowledge to characterize in situ stress
conditions at the Utah FORGE site. To provide context, we describe relevant injection activities
conducted at the FORGE site. In 2017 and 2019, a series of injection tests were conducted on the
vertical monitoring well 58-32, with the primary purpose to measure the in situ stress and rock

 33

properties. Specifically, the stress was inferred by interpreting the closure pressure from various
injection scenarios. However, this interpretation may be influenced by near-well features, and the
lack of associated log data to characterize near-well fracture patterns adds uncertainty. This
motivates the study of the impact of near-well fracture complexity and underscores the importance
of this work. Later in 2023, seven minifrac injection tests were conducted on the production well
16(B)78-32, all of which are located at the vertical or nearly vertical section of the open-hole
wellbore Fortunately, image log data are available before and after injections tests, providing
crucial information about the induced near-well fractures. For example, Figure 4-7 already shows
the post-injection log data from Minifrac Test 2 (MF2) at a depth of approximately 5495 ft MD,
where induced fractures are clearly visible. As can be seen, the induced fractures are generally
longitudinal to the wellbore, with slight inclinations. According to the findings of this study, such
inclined fractures suggest that the wellbore may not be perfectly aligned with the principal stress
directions. This is most likely due to slight deviations in the wellbore trajectory, as 16(B)78-32
gradually transitions into a deviated orientation along its depth, with its orientation rotating mainly
about 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 . If this is the case, our numerical study suggests that the in situ stress regime is
probably normal or reverse. In addition, the image log indicates that the induced fractures are
predominantly thin and straight. This observation suggests a strike-slip condition or the normal

strikes north-northeast at 20–30, which is consistent with conclusions made in (Kelley et al., 2024)
In summary, our preliminary conclusion is that the in situ stress regime at the depth of MF2 is most
likely normal, with 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑎𝑥 striking north-northeast at 20–30. Also notably, this application of the
knowledge gained from our numerical study further demonstrates the practical value and
significance of this work.

4.3.5 Limitations/Future Improvements
To further enhance this work and broaden the application of the proposed phase-field formulations,
we suggest the following future improvements:
• The current study now is restricted to hydraulically induced fractures, so the conclusions may
 not apply to other types of fractures or damage, such as drill-induced fractures or borehole
 breakouts. However, the phase-field formulation itself is not restricted to hydraulic fracturing
 modeling. Additional systematic studies are necessary to investigate different types of near-
 well failures for stress characterization.
• Thermal effects have not yet been fully integrated into the phase-field formulation, even though
 they play a crucial role in EGS. For now, we only tested a one-way coupling approach that is
 to run the fully coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical simulation first to only model a pre-cooling
 stage in the experiment and incorporate the corresponding mechanical response to the
 fracturing stage where the phase-field model is activated. A future study should consider
 including full thermal effects in the phase-field simulation.
• In the presented numerical experiment, we fix the width of the injection zone to one thirds of
 the domain height, whereas, in real injection scenarios, the perforation zone is typically
 minimal relative to the reservoir scale. As a result, potential boundary effects may influence
 both the experimental and numerical investigations. Additional numerical simulations and
 experimental tests have been conducted on radially-notched wellbores with varying injection

 34

 zone widths. Both simulation and experiment indicate that a planar, penny-shaped fracture is
 more likely to develop from the initial notch when the injection zone size shrinks.
• The field test simulation with rock heterogeneities has indicated potential formation of rock
 bridges, which could eventually result in non-persistency of hydraulic fractures. This non-
 persistency may influence the crack compliance during closure, further affecting the stress
 estimation based on the conventional DFIT analysis. Future research should more
 systematically investigate how the rock bridges and non-persistent cracks are created under
 different scenarios, so as to inform the DFIT analysis and modeling.

5 Large-field Hydraulic Fracture Simulations with Stress Roughness
This section summarizes the background motivation, methodology, and key results for the large-
scale hydraulic fracture simulations that accounts for stress roughness and apparent toughness
anisotropy.

5.1 Background
In most geologic formations, the vertical gradient of minimum horizontal stress (𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 ) is greater
than the hydrostatic gradient, which should, in theory, drive hydraulic fractures to grow upward.
However, field observations rarely show pronounced upward fracture propagation predicted by
models based on a smoothly varying 𝜎ℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 field. In sedimentary formations, layering can hinder
fracture propagation across layers, partially explaining the limited fracture height growth.
Crystalline rocks lack such layering but still contain structural fabric that produces inherently
“rough” in situ stress fields. This stress roughness superimposed on the overall stress gradient can
produce apparent toughness anisotropy, making the vertical toughness appear much larger than
the horizontal toughness. Ignoring such stress roughness or resulting toughness anisotropy can
therefore lead to inaccurate predictions of hydraulic fracture geometry and growth rates,
particularly at larger scales.

Potential evidence of such toughness anisotropy is seen in the Stage 3 stimulation in well 16(A)78-
32 at Utah FORGE site (McClure, 2023). Despite an estimated stress gradient far exceeding the
hydrostatic gradient, which should favor substantial upward growth, the microseismic catalog
instead indicates fractures that are nearly circular. To better understand the growth rate of the
fracture, Figure 5-1 presents the distance-time plot for all microseismic events associated with
Stage 3. The plot separates the horizontal and vertical distances of each event to identify
differences in growth rates between the two directions. The vertical distance envelope exhibits a
slightly steeper gradient than the horizontal distance envelope, indicating a marginally faster
fracture propagation rate in the vertical direction. Interestingly, this difference is smaller than what
would be expected for a smooth stress gradient consistent with the field estimations at Utah
FORGE. Numerical simulations incorporating spatial stress variation and toughness anisotropy are
thus essential for evaluating the influence of stress roughness and for quantifying the underlying
rock fabric.

5.2.2 Model Assumptions and Validation
To simplify the model and due to some missing components in the modeling tool, several
assumptions were introduced:
• The wellbore itself is not included in the simulation.
• The perforation cluster is represented by a single planar fracture. This approach is in line with
 previous work in the literature (Fu et al., 2020).
• Near-wellbore losses, which are expected to be significant given the measured field pressures
 that would otherwise imply unphysical toughness values, are not explicitly modeled.
• The potential deterioration of the crosslinked gel viscosity after injection is not accounted for
 in the model.
• No well model is employed and injection is represented by point source with a specified mass
 flux.
• The presence of proppant is neglected.
Despite these simplifying assumptions, the model effectively serves the purpose of assessing the
impact of stress roughness on fracture propagation.

The baseline model incorporating the apparent toughness anisotropy has been validated in the
previous technical report (Cusini et al., 2025). Specifically, simulation results have demonstrated
that our hydraulic fracturing model, when incorporating specific apparent toughness anisotropy,
can equivalently reproduce the far-field fracture geometry induced by a rough stress profile. This
validation supports that stress roughness features can be effectively characterized through
parameter calibration of apparent toughness anisotropy using our hydraulic fracturing solver.

5.3 Results and Discussions
This section provides the simulations results of three modeling scenarios. The first case considers
a smooth stress gradient, with the apparent toughness anisotropy calibrated through history
matching with field fracture propagation data inferred from MEQ events. In the second case, the
stress profile is extracted from sonic log data to evaluate the expected anisotropy, and the model
is rerun incorporating a layered upscaled stress profile in z direction as shown in Figure 5-3.
Finally, the third scenario involves a model with 3D heterogeneous stress fields generated from a
geostatistical model based on the sonic log data (Figure 5-4). All these three simulations allow us
to compare the calibrated toughness anisotropy with the anisotropy predicted from the stress
profile and assess its consistency with observed fracture growth.

5.3.1 Simulation I: Calibration of Apparent Toughness Anisotropy
We start with the simulation case for calibrating potential apparent toughness anisotropy, while
keep a uniform stress gradient. Figure 5-4(a) shows the fracture growth over time, comparing the
simulation results using calibrated parameters KIc = 0.55 MPam1/2 with those inferred from MEQ
data. The modeled fracture propagation closely matches the field observations in both horizontal
and vertical directions. With the adopted stress gradient and toughness anisotropy, the simulation
also captures the discrepancy between horizontal and vertical growth.

To further highlight the effects of toughness anisotropy, we repeat the simulation with isotropic
rock toughness (i.e., KIc, v = KIc, h) keeping all other parameters unchanged. Figure 5-4(b)
presents the results for the isotropic case compared to the fracture growth inferred from MEQ data.
While the horizontal growth remains consistent with the field results, the vertical growth in the
isotropic case is excessively rapid, confirming that a certain degree of toughness anisotropy is
necessary to match field observations.

Notably, the simulated fracture propagation for both cases nearly ceases t = 120 minutes. This
behavior is expected, as the injection rate gradually reduces to zero after this point, halting further
fracture propagation in the simulation. However, field results inferred from MEQ data suggest
continued fracture growth even after injection stops. A possible explanation is that microseismic
events might occur with a delay relative to the actual fracture propagation, leading to an

 40

underestimation of fracture growth when inferred solely from MEQ data. For the purposes of this
study, we continue to use the MEQ data to infer fracture growth while acknowledging its potential
limitations.

5.3.4 Implications of Findings for Utah FORGE and EGS Technologies
Two main implications of findings as summarized below can be obtained through this numerical
investigation.

First, we compare the calibrated apparent toughness anisotropy presented in Section 5.3.1 with the
anisotropy that one would expect given the stress profile measure from the image logs. To this end,
we apply the homogenization method proposed by Dontsov and Suarez-Rivera (2021) to estimate
an “analytical” apparent toughness anisotropy based on the field stress profile shown by the gray
line in Figure 5-3(a). Detailed calculation has been described in the previous technical report
(Cusini et al., 2025), and thus omitted here for brevity. The resulting “analytical” apparent
toughness anisotropy is approximately KIc = 0.27 MPam1/2, which is comparable to the calibrated
value of KIc = 0.55 MPam1/2. This overall agreement suggests that the calibrated toughness
anisotropy may indeed be the effect of a non-smooth stress. Also notably, according to Fu et al.
(2019), the apparent toughness anisotropy is positively correlated with the magnitude of stress
variation and a characteristic length that defines the wavelength of the roughness. This
characteristic length is also closely associated with the rock fabric features. Notably, the toughness
anisotropy obtained from both the hydraulic fracturing simulation and the analytical
homogenization technique exhibits a relatively small magnitude. This observation suggests that
either the stress roughness magnitude or the characteristic length (or both) is relatively small.
Based on the stress profile shown in Figure 5-3(a), the stress variation can be roughly estimated at
2 MPa, which corresponds to a characteristic length of approximately 10-70 mm for the rock
fabric. Conclusively, this modeling task provides Utah FORGE and future EGS development with
a workflow that consists of large-scale hydraulic fracturing simulation for calibration of apparent

 43

toughness anisotropy, the computation of the analytical toughness anisotropy based on the field
stress profile, and the inference of the characteristic length for rock fabrics.

Second, the simulation results of the case with a 3D heterogenous stress field clearly indicates that
the in situ stress field at the depth of the Utah FORGE stimulation exhibit certain degree of
anisotropy. This finding further provides strong evidence that the formation of crystalline rocks
could also be anisotropic in fabrics, although not as pervasive as in sedimentary rocks. Specific
reasons to explain such apparent anisotropic feature will be explore in future research. Also
notably, the much decelerated fracture propagation speed under isotropic heterogeneity implies
that the strong variations of rock properties or in situ stress distribution in all three directions may
act as additional resistance to fracture growth or lead to an apparent additional toughness. This is
consistent with the apparent toughness anisotropy in previous literature that the vertical stress
roughness could hinder the vertical fracture growth. If such stress roughness appears in all
direction, we therefore may anticipate a global reduction in fracture propagation speed.

5.3.5 Limitations/Future Improvements
The current study still has a number limitations as listed below, and future work can be applied to
address them:
• The current study now is restricted to Stage 3 stimulation. The primary reasons are: (i)
 stimulation from a single cluster, making the model setup easier than other multi-cluster stages;
 (ii) its microseismic catalog is available and clearly indicate the development of an
 approximately planar hydraulic fracture. However, the current workflow combining numerical
 modeling and analytical calculation should be employed to study other stimulation stages in
 the future to provide more information.
• Thermal effects have not yet been integrated into the current simulation, even though they play
 a crucial role in EGS. The cooling-induced thermal stress due to fluid injection may provide
 additional driving force for fracture opening and propagation. Although the thermal effect may
 not influence the qualitative behavior or final conclusion, future investigation is still needed to
 consider the thermal component in the model.
• Preexisting natural fractures are neglected here in the model. The presence of natural fractures
 could lead to alteration of local in situ stress field and/or additional pathways for injection
 fluid, therefore possibly affecting the final fracture growth and microseismic response. Major
 natural fractures identified from well log data and other techniques should be included in future
 work.

6.1 Methodology
6.1.1 True-triaxial High-temperature Hydraulic Fracturing Block Test Setup
The experimental setup can handle testing temperatures of up to 200 °C under true triaxial loading
conditions. The design aims to simulate three different stress regions (normal fault, reverse fault,
and strike-slip fault) using a hydraulic piston-actuated loading cell, as depicted in Figure 6-1(a).
To ensure an even distribution of stress on the testing sample, aluminum loading plates of at least
3-inch thickness are inserted between each side of the sample and the tilt saddle on the actuator,
Figure 6-1(b). The wellbore direction and loading stress direction are shown in Figure 6-1(c). To
further mitigate friction and prevent the shear boundary conditions, Teflon sheets are placed
between each face of the sample and the aluminum loading plate, as shown in Figure 6-1.

Figure 6-1 (a) 200 Ton Poly-axial Load Frame; (b) Loading cell inside the load frame with testing samples and loading plates; (c)
the orientations of the loading stress and the borehole; (d) Teflon sheets placed between the testing samples and loading plates.

As illustrated in Figure 6-2, the injection system employs two O-rings to create a seal in the
injection area. When the targeted load is applied, the force exerted on the O-rings results in lateral
deformation, blocking the borehole's cross-sectional area and isolating the central injection area.
This enables a build-up of fluid pressure in this region when the outlet port is closed and the
injection port is pressurized by the fluid from the syringe pump. Furthermore, to maximize cooling
efficiency, we have implemented a new design: instead of using perforation holes in the center, we
utilize a solid center piece with perforation holes located near the O-rings. This design forces the
cooling fluid to circulate through the annulus between the injection tubing’s outer surface and the
borehole wall's inner surface, maximizing the contact area and increasing cooling efficiency.
Multiple operational parameters, including circulation rate, injection fluid viscosity, and injection
rate, can be adjusted, enabling us to target different dissipative mechanisms for the hydraulic
fracturing propagation, such as a toughness-dominated (fracture surface creation) regime and a
viscosity-dominated (viscous fluid flow) regime. The wide range of parametric space paves the
way for a systematic investigation into their respective impacts on fracture patterns and pressure
records.

 45

Figure 6-2 A novel borehole injection system has been developed to maximize cooling efficiency by utilizing two O-rings to create
a seal and direct the cooling fluid through the annulus between the borehole wall and tubing wall.

As shown in Figure 6-3, improved and more precise temperature measurements were achieved by
isolating the thermocouple tips from the circulating fluids, effectively reducing potential noise in
the temperature data caused by environmental factors. Using a newly designed set of injection
tools, repeated circulation tests were conducted to measure changes in borehole wall temperature.

Figure 6-3 Illustration of the enhanced wellbore temperature measurement setup.

Before the experiment, the dry testing block and all loading plates are heated in an oven. To prevent
unnecessary thermally induced damage before the experiment, the oven temperature is increased
in steps of 50 °C every six hours until it reaches the target temperature of 200 °C. The testing block
is then left in the oven for at least two days to achieve isothermal conditions at the targeted
temperature. Heating the loading plates to the same target temperature is essential as they are in
direct contact with the testing sample during the experiment, and we aim to avoid creating a large
thermal gradient at the boundary and minimize heat loss during the experiment.

To start the experiments, the injection system with a thermocouple is installed into the testing
sample. Then the actuator in the wellbore direction (C-D direction in Figure 6-1(c)) is loaded with
a stress of 3.44 MPa. This causes the two O-rings to become compressed and the central
pressurization area is isolated. The outlet port is then be closed and the borehole pressure is
incrementally increased to 0.68 MPa and maintained for 10 minutes to evaluate the seal integrity.
Subsequently, the stress on each direction is incrementally increased by a step of 3.44 MPa until
the targeted value is reached. This approach is employed to minimize deviatoric stress during the
loading phase and to prevent any unnecessary damage. The borehole pressure is continuously

 46

monitored and kept at 0.68 MPa during this loading stage. Any drops in borehole pressure may
indicate the occurrence of induced damage during the loading phase. After attaining the designated
in-situ stresses, room temperature water circulation initiates and continues for a duration of 20
minutes. The temperature within the borehole is continuously monitored and recorded, as
illustrated in Figure 6-4 for example. After the circulation process concludes, the injection fluid,
which is red-colored dye glycerin, is pumped at a fixed flow rate of 3 ml/min using a syringe pump
until the sample breaks down, reaching the peak borehole pressure.

6.1.2 Post-test analysis and characterization
We have innovatively designed and implemented protocols for analyzing post-peak pressure
records. These protocols aim to enhance our ability to understand and infer in-situ stress under
complex conditions. In Figure 6-5, the methodology involves maintaining the injection rate after
reaching the breakdown pressure to observe post-peak pressure behaviors, which is referred to as
the shut-in phase. The shut-in phase commences when the borehole pressure stabilizes at a constant
value. After the shut-in phase, a reopening test is performed by injecting the same fluid at the same
flow rate into the borehole to observe pressure responses. Similarly, when the pressure reaches the
peak in the reopening test, another shut-in phase is implemented. The procedure then moves to the
step-rate pressure test, during which the borehole pressure increases in increments of 0.68 MPa
(100 psi). Each step commences only after the pressure level has stabilized at a constant flow rate.
At each stage, the pressure level and corresponding flow rate are recorded and plotted for analysis.
Following the step-rate phase, another shut-in period occurs, after which the wellbore pressure
decreases to ambient level. With these completed, the entire test procedure is considered finished.

Figure 6-5. Full wellbore pressure record in a complete test cycle.

6.2 Results and Discussions
In this section, we present key experimental observations and detailed analyses of results. The full
testing dataset for each scenario is provided in a supplementary document as referred in Appendix,
including full pressure record, G-function analysis, and post-test fracture characterization.

6.2.1 Thermal Stress on Breakdown Pressure
Our experimental data has demonstrated that thermal stimulation by cold-fluid injection into hot
rock can measurably reduce the breakdown pressure. As shown in Figure 6-9, comparison between
the non-thermal treatment group (left) and the thermal treatment group (right), conducted under
the same wellbore orientation and in-situ stress conditions, reveals an approximately 15%
reduction in breakdown pressure attributable to thermally induced stress.

 51

Figure 6-9 Breakdown pressure comparison between the non-thermal treatment group (left) and the thermal treatment group (right)
under the same wellbore direction and in-situ stress conditions.

The theoretical maximum thermal stress for a constrained body under plane strain is given by:
                               σ 𝑇 = α 𝑇 Δ𝑇𝐸/(1 − ν),                                           (24)
where αT is the coefficient of thermal expansion, E is Young’s modulus, ν is Poisson’s ratio, and
ΔT is the temperature differential. For Charcoal granite with typical properties (αT ≈ 7–8 × 10–6
/°C, E ≈ 55–65 GPa, ν ≈ 0.2–0.25) and a temperature differential of at least ΔT = 150°C from
experimental measurement, this yields at least σT ≈ 80 MPa. At the borehole wall, the classical
Kirsch solution combined with the thermal contribution gives the effective tangential stress:
                                σθ = 3σℎ − σ𝐻 − σ 𝑇 ,                                           (25)
where σh and σH are the minimum and maximum in-situ stresses. Fracture initiation occurs when
σθ reaches the tensile strength σt. Given typical laboratory-scale effective stress conditions, a
thermal stress of 80 MPa far exceeds the quantity (3σh – σH - σt) that are applied at the testing,
implying that thermal loading alone should be sufficient to initiate tensile fracture at the borehole
wall.

breakdown pressure is only a fraction of the theoretical thermal stress magnitude. This apparent
discrepancy may be resolved by reconsidering the problem through the framework of fracture

During the thermal stimulation process, the convective heat transfer coefficient at the fluid–rock
interface is high (particularly under forced-flow conditions given the fluid are flowing through the
annulus between the injection tubing and wellbore wall), so the wellbore wall temperature drops
rapidly toward the injection fluid temperature. However, the thermal front penetrates the rock
much more slowly than the convective cooling of the surface, as conduction into the rock mass is
governed by the diffusive timescale. This produces a steep but spatially shallow thermal gradient
near the wall. Although this gradient generates high theoretical thermal stresses at the surface,
these stresses are concentrated within a thin layer whose extent, particularly for short to moderate
injection durations, is comparable to or smaller than the characteristic material length lm. This thin
thermally stressed zone is therefore insufficient to fully engage the fracture process zone and
generate the critical stress intensity required for macroscopic crack propagation.

Moreover, in our experimental configuration, no external heating source is applied to maintain the
temperature of the rock block during the injection period. As a consequence, the entire testing
block progressively loses heat to the surroundings throughout the injection process. This means
that the far-field rock temperature is also decreasing over time, which in turn reduces the effective
temperature differential ΔT between the cooled wellbore wall and the surrounding rock mass. The
actual thermal gradient driving the near-wellbore stress field is therefore lower than the idealized
theoretical condition, which assumes a constant far-field temperature. This further diminishes both
the magnitude and the spatial extent of the thermally induced stress field relative to the theoretical
prediction, providing an additional reason for why the observed reduction in breakdown pressure
is substantially less than what the plane-strain thermal stress formula would suggest.

constant closure pressure confirms that Cridge is a reproducible rock property that does not degrade
with repeated loading and the asperities re-engage at the same stress threshold in every cycle in
very short time. The changing G-value, in contrast, reflects the evolving fracture geometry
(progressive extension, increased leak-off surface area, and altered compliance) between
successive injection cycles. In other words, the G-function is actually simultaneously recording
two independent phenomena: a stress-independent rock property (Cridge) and a stress- and
geometry-dependent fracture state (system compliance).

The proposed ARM also provides an explanation for the systematic discrepancy between the
compliance method (Pcc) and the holistic method (Pch). Rather than representing uncertainty or
error in either method, the two picks are measuring different stages of the same progressive closure
process. The compliance method identifies the first significant stiffness change, corresponding to
the engagement of the tallest ridges and most prominent asperities. The holistic method captures a
later stage of closure, where a larger population of contacts has engaged and the system stiffness
has changed more substantially. The systematic gap between the two picks is itself a measure of
the closure range created by the rough fracture surface—the pressure interval over which the
fracture transitions from open to mechanically closed through progressive asperity engagement.

Based on the discussions above, the ARM model can be understood as when fluid pressure declines
during shut-in, these rough fracture surfaces do not close as smooth planes, which make
simultaneous contact. Instead, closure proceeds as a progressive mechanical transition: the tallest
asperities and most prominent mineral ridges make contact first, at pressures significantly above
the far-field minimum stress, and the load is gradually transferred from fluid pressure to an
increasingly dense network of grain-scale contact points. The G-function detects closure as the
point where the pressure-decline signature transitions from fracture-dominated (compliant)
behavior to formation-dominated (stiff) behavior. Thus. in crystalline rock, this stiffness transition
occurs when a sufficient number of asperities have engaged to significantly alter the system
compliance—an event that takes place at a pressure above σhmin because the ridge contacts
themselves carry load. The G-function therefore measures the composite stiffness of the fracture-
plus-asperity system, not the far-field stress directly.

Based on the understanding above, we propose that the G-function closure pressure in crystalline
rock is related to the true minimum horizontal stress by:
 𝑝closure (𝐺 − function) = σℎ𝑚𝑖𝑛 + 𝐶ridge (28)
where Cridge is the asperity resistance, a stress-independent quantity governed by the rock’s grain-
scale mechanical properties. We further propose that Cridge can be expressed as a fraction of the
intact tensile strength:
                                       𝐶ridge = ησ𝑡 ,                                          (29)
where η is the asperity retention factor (dimensionless) and σt is the indirect tensile strength
obtained from standard Brazilian disc tests. Our experimental data indicate that η ranges from 0 to
1 and that its value depends on the fracture geometry and fracture length. It is also governed by
intrinsic grain-scale properties, including grain size distribution, mineral hardness, crystal
cleavage orientations, and grain boundary strength.

Thus, when a hydraulic fracture propagates through a crystalline rock, it does not produce a
perfectly smooth plane. The fracture path navigates through and around mineral grains of varying
strength and orientation, leaving intact bridges, interlocking contacts, and stepped offsets at grain
boundaries. These features act as natural proppants with a load-bearing capacity that is a fraction
of the intact rock’s tensile strength. The G-function reads the stiffness transition caused by these
engaging contacts, not the far-field stress itself. The parameter η captures this retained strength
fraction and can, in principle, be estimated from a standard Brazilian disc test on core from the
same formation.

6.2.3 Thermal Stress as a Mechanism for Promoting Transverse Fracture Initiation and Reducing
 Near-Wellbore Tortuosity
The experimental results presented in Table 6-1 and Figure 6-13 reveal a systematic and repeatable
pattern: without thermal stress, hydraulic fractures initiated from the wellbore are exclusively
longitudinal across all tested stress states and well orientations. In contrast, the introduction of
thermal stress (ΔT ≈ 175 °C) triggers a transition toward transverse or mixed fracture geometries.
This transition can be understood through the framework of near-wellbore stress competition
between the tangential stress component σθθ and the axial stress component σzz. The critical
physical mechanism is that the thermo-elastic stress induced by wellbore cooling is isotropic which
reduces all stress components equally at a given radial distance from the wellbore (Y. Lu et al.,
2024). When cold fluid contacts the hot wellbore wall, the resulting thermal contraction generates
a tensile thermo-elastic stress σΔT = αd|ΔT|, where αd is the drained thermo-elastic effective stress
coefficient. Although only a fraction of the theoretical maximum is mobilized due to finite thermal
penetration depth, the effective thermal stress is sufficient to fundamentally alter the stress
competition at the wellbore wall.

Figure 6-13 Classification of fracture categories in vertical and horizontal wells under thermal and no-thermal treatments.

For a horizontal wellbore drilled along the minimum in-situ stress direction, the Kirsch solution
shows that the tangential stress σθθ at the wellbore wall is amplified by the stress concentration
effect and is substantially higher than the axial stress σzz, which is approximately equal to σh.
Because the thermo-elastic stress is isotropic, it subtracts equally from both components. Since σzz
starts at a much lower compressive level than σθθ, the same isotropic thermal reduction drives σzz
into tension well before σθθ reaches the tensile failure threshold. Once σzz becomes tensile, the
energy required for transverse fracture initiation drops dramatically, and the transverse geometry
becomes energetically favorable. These experimental observations are in strong agreement with
the numerical predictions of G. Lu et al. (2024), who showed that for a horizontal well drilled
along the minimum stress direction, cooling of sufficient magnitude and duration can shift the
preferred fracture initiation from longitudinal to transverse. Our results provide the first systematic

experimental validation of this numerically predicted geometry transition under true triaxial stress
conditions representative of EGS environments.

single fracture is formed. The multiple fracture sets from the thermal case — identified as Fracture

presence of two distinct fracture orientations at the wellbore wall provides direct microscale
evidence that the thermal stress has activated failure in multiple directions simultaneously,
consistent with the isotropic nature of the thermo-elastic stress field. These thermally induced
microfractures serve as "seed fractures" that pre-condition the rock for multi-directional failure
during subsequent hydraulic pressurization. And we can also clearly observe the branching

The implications of these conjugate microfractures are threefold. First, they provide a physical
mechanism for the mixed fracture geometry observed at the macroscale: when the wellbore wall
contains pre-existing microfractures in both the longitudinal and transverse orientations, hydraulic
pressure can propagate fractures along either or both paths simultaneously, producing the
combined geometry. Second, the conjugate pattern indicates that the thermal stress acts not only
through the continuum-scale thermo-elastic stress modification modeled by G. Lu et al. (2024),
but also through discrete microstructural damage at the grain scale. The thermal gradient between
the cold injection fluid and the hot rock generates differential thermal contraction that exploits
grain boundaries and pre-existing micro-defects, creating a network of seed fractures that operates
alongside the continuum thermo-elastic stress to lower the effective resistance to fracture initiation.
Third, the rock bridges observed between fracture segments in both thermal and non-thermal cases
have implications for the interpretation of shut-in and closure pressure data, as the segmented
fracture morphology departs from the continuous, uniformly closing fracture assumed in classical
DFIT analysis.

6.2.4 Implications of Findings for Utah FORGE and EGS Technologies
The experimental findings presented here have direct implications for the planning and execution
of hydraulic stimulation and in-situ stress testing in EGS.

First, the wellbore cooling can be used as a fracture geometry control tool. Our results demonstrate
that by managing the cooling circulation time and the temperature difference between the injected
fluid and the reservoir rock, operators can promote transverse fracture initiation from horizontal
wells drilled along the minimum stress direction. This is particularly effective when the minimum
stress is low relative to the intermediate and maximum stresses which is a condition commonly
encountered in EGS reservoirs at depth. At the same time, the thermal stress can be used as
reduction of near-wellbore tortuosity. For horizontal wells where longitudinal initiation followed
by far-field reorientation creates tortuous fracture paths, controlled cooling prior to injection can
bypass this problem by promoting direct transverse initiation. This has the potential to reduce
injection pressures, improve proppant placement, and enhance circulation efficiency.

Second, cooling circulation time is also a controllable lever in addition to circulation temperature.
In the field, operators often focus on the temperature difference ΔT when planning cooling
operations. The analysis shows that ΔT alone is insufficient to predict the thermal effect, but what
matters is whether the thermal front has penetrated beyond l m. Based on thermal diffusion, the
penetration depth grows with the square root of time. At Utah FORGE, where reservoir
temperatures exceed 200°C and wellbore radii are ~0.1 m, the thermal penetration needs to reach
tens of centimeters to fully engage the fracture process zone. This means that short-duration
cooling (minutes) will produce only modest reduction of breakdown pressure, while extended
circulation (hours to days) will progressively increase the effective thermal stress by extending the
thermally stressed zone well beyond l m. The relationship between cooling time and breakdown
pressure reduction is therefore nonlinear, and there is a threshold duration below which thermal
effects are marginal, and above which they become substantial.

Third, the asperity resistance effects that we observed in the experiments will cause the current G-
function interpretation inaccurate. Because the G-function identifies closure when grain-scale
ridges and asperities first engage—at a pressure above the true minimum horizontal stress—the

 59

apparent closure occurs earlier in the pressure decline than the true mechanical closure. The G-
function therefore registers a smaller closure G-value than would be obtained if the fracture
surfaces were smooth and closure occurred precisely at the far-field stress. The consequence is that
the computed fluid efficiency is systematically lower than the true efficiency. However, in reality,
the fracture is still partially open and retaining fluid at the point where the G-function declares it
closed; the remaining decline from apparent closure to true mechanical closure represents a period
during which the fracture holds fluid on its asperity contacts, but this retained volume may be
misattributed to leak-off. Second, it will result in a systematically overestimated leak-off
coefficient. This overestimation carries directly into formation permeability estimates, which are
often back-calculated from the leak-off behavior during after-closure analysis. An inflated leak-off
coefficient implies a higher apparent permeability, which may lead to erroneous conclusions about
the formation’s flow characteristics.

Lastly, our experiment data will have implications on the multi-scale fracture characterization. The
combination of macroscale fracture mapping on the 6-inch cube surfaces and 2-inch over-cores
with microscale SEM observations provides a comprehensive picture of how thermal stress
operates across scales. The continuum-scale thermo-elastic stress governs the macroscale
geometry transition, while the grain-scale thermal damage creates seed fractures that precondition
the rock for multi-directional failure. Both mechanisms act synergistically to promote transverse
initiation and should be considered in coupled THM models of EGS stimulation.

7 Conclusions and Recommendations
7.1 Summary of Conclusions
This project integrated THM modeling of DFITs, phase-field simulations of near-well fracture
behavior, large-scale hydraulic fracture modeling, and true-triaxial laboratory experiments to
investigate how in situ stress governs fracture complexity in EGS systems. Across all tasks, the
results consistently demonstrate that fracture behavior is controlled by a combination of stress
state, thermal and poroelastic effects, rock heterogeneity and fabric patterns, and wellbore
configuration.

From Task 1, THM modeling of DFITs showed that thermal stress and poroelastic coupling
significantly influence pressure decline and fracture closure behavior, leading to potential
misinterpretation of closure pressure when using conventional methods. Task 2 established that
near-well fracture geometry, orientation, and nucleation are highly sensitive to both stress regime
and wellbore deviation, with non-planar and inclined fractures emerging under realistic EGS
conditions. Task 3 demonstrated that stress roughness and heterogeneity can produce apparent
toughness anisotropy and strongly influence large-scale fracture growth, providing an explanation
for discrepancies between classical models and field observations. Task 4 experiments confirmed
that thermal cooling reduces breakdown pressure, promotes transverse and complex fracture
geometries, and that fracture surface roughness introduces asperity-controlled closure behavior
that biases stress estimates with the conventional G-function analysis.

Collectively, these findings show that conventional assumptions, such as planar fractures, smooth
fracture surfaces, and direct equivalence between closure pressure and minimum stress, can be
invalid in crystalline EGS environments with non-vertical well configurations. Instead, fracture
evolution and pressure response reflect coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical processes and multi-
scale rock fabric effects. Importantly, this work provides a clear path forward for stress
characterization in EGS by demonstrating that future interpretations must explicitly account for
processes that are typically neglected in conventional approaches, including thermal stress,
poroelastic coupling, stress heterogeneity, and fracture surface roughness, in order to obtain more
reliable estimates of in situ stress for EGS reservoirs.

7.2 Recommendations for Future Work
Future work should focus on improving existing modeling frameworks to more accurately capture
the full injection–shut-in cycle. In particular, incorporating wellbore effects, near-well tortuosity,
and continuous state evolution will enhance predictive capability and reduce uncertainty in model
calibration.

Additional research is needed to systematically quantify the impact of fracture surface roughness
and asperity resistance on stress interpretation, including development of modified DFIT analysis
methods that explicitly account for these effects. Laboratory measurements and field-scale
validation should be combined to establish practical correction factors or new diagnostic
approaches. Numerical modeling can also be applied by extending the existing capability to
account for the presence of rock bridges across the fracture plane, enabling parametric study to
systematically explore the influence of rock asperity on G-function curves.

 61

Finally, the role of thermal stimulation using circulation pre-cooling should be explored in greater
detail, including optimization of cooling strategies (temperature and duration) to control fracture
initiation, reduce near-wellbore tortuosity, and enhance stimulation efficiency. These efforts will
support the development of more reliable and physics-based workflows for stress characterization
and stimulation design in future EGS operations.
