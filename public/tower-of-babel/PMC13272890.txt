Awareness, Knowledge, and Perceived Feasibility of World Health Organization Labour Care Guide Among Midwives in Enugu State, Nigeria
Authors: Ukah Cynthia Chinonso, Majani Edward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLabour‐related complications remain a major contributor to maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality, especially in low‐resource settings. To improve labour monitoring and outcomes, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the Labour Care Guide (LCG) in 2020 to replace the traditional partograph. However, its adoption and understanding among midwives, who are frontline providers of maternity care, remain uncertain.AimsTo assess the knowledge and perceived feasibility of the WHO Labour Care Guide among midwives in Enugu State, Nigeria.Materials and MethodsAnalytical cross‐sectional study was conducted among 43 midwives working in public health facilities in Enugu. Data were collected using a structured, self‐administered questionnaire and analysed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data, and associations were tested using Fisher's exact tests, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsAwareness of the LCG was reported by 30 midwives (69.8%). Among them, 16 (37.2%) had poor knowledge, and only 10 (33.3%) perceived it as highly feasible for routine use. Twenty‐nine (96.7%) midwives incorrectly believed that the LCG includes alert/action lines and key barriers to its implementation included perceived complexity, time consumption, insufficient training, and staff shortages. Years of experience were not significantly associated with knowledge of LCG (p = 0.169).ConclusionDespite a relatively high level of awareness, substantial knowledge gaps and negative perceptions of its feasibility persist among midwives regarding the LCG. The tool is not currently in routine use in Enugu. There is a need for intensified training efforts, inclusion of the LCG in pre‐service midwifery curricula, and supportive policy measures to promote its effective implementation.

Introduction

Maternal mortality remains unacceptably high in low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries (LLMICs), representing one of the most pressing global health challenges of our time. In 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that over 90% of all maternal deaths occurred in LLMICs, with more than 700 women dying each day from preventable pregnancy‐ and childbirth‐related causes [1]. Although significant progress has been made to combat this by expanding access to skilled maternity care, many countries are still not on track to achieve the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target of reducing the global maternal mortality ratio to fewer than 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030 [2]. This persistent gap between increased access and suboptimal outcomes is largely attributed to the poor quality of care provided during pregnancy, labour, and the postpartum period.

In sub‐Saharan Africa, these challenges are particularly acute. In Africa, for example, only 74% of births were attended by skilled health personnel in 2024 (WHO, 2024), and many health facilities continue to face challenges such as understaffing, inadequate infrastructure, insufficient supervision, and limited access to essential medicines and equipment [2]. Nigeria, as Africa's most populous country, exemplifies these systemic challenges with a maternal mortality ratio that remains among the world's highest. To tackle these, global health organisations have promoted evidence‐based approaches to intrapartum care that emphasise quality, safety, and positive childbirth experiences. The WHO's intrapartum care model, for instance, has demonstrated effectiveness in enhancing childbirth experiences, increasing maternal satisfaction with the quality of obstetric care, and reducing fear associated with labour and childbirth [3].

Recognising the need for comprehensive quality improvement, the WHO also championed woman‐centred, evidence‐based intrapartum care as a core strategy for improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. In 2018, the organisation introduced a comprehensive intrapartum care model that emphasises several key principles: respectful maternity care, emotional support during childbirth, effective communication, pain management, continuity of care, and regular monitoring during childbirth. This model also focuses on enhancing the skills and competency of skilled birth attendants while improving the physical environment during childbirth [4].

Building on these principles, the WHO Labour Care Guide (LCG), also known as the new generation partograph, was launched in 2020 as an updated labour monitoring tool designed to enhance clinical decision‐making while promoting dignified, individualised care. Unlike the traditional partograph, which primarily tracks cervical dilation and labour duration, the LCG incorporates broader dimensions of quality, including maternal comfort, pain relief, and woman‐provider communication. The LCG is intended to be used for all pregnant women, regardless of their risk status or place of delivery [5], and this further aligns with the vision of a positive childbirth experience.

However, the reality of implementing these evidence‐based tools in low‐resource settings presents significant challenges. Despite these global efforts to promote evidence‐based intrapartum care, the traditional partograph, which is a labour monitoring chart introduced by the WHO to help detect complications early, is still poorly utilised in many low‐resource settings. A 2019 audit of partograph use in Uganda revealed incomplete documentation in over 60% of reviewed cases, with healthcare providers attributing the gaps to ward congestion, equipment shortages, and inadequate supervision [6]. Similarly, a recent cross‐sectional study among midwives in Anambra State, Nigeria, found that only 30% of midwives reported consistent partograph use, despite a 78% awareness rate, and just 60% could correctly identify its clinical purpose [7]. These findings suggest a persistent gap between global recommendations and local implementation.

Given these documented challenges with the traditional partograph, questions arise about healthcare workers' readiness for the more comprehensive LCG. Also, despite its potential to improve intrapartum care, the Labour Care Guide is still in the early stages of global rollout, and little is known about the level of knowledge or readiness for its implementation in many low‐resource countries. To date, only one mixed‐method study has explored knowledge, perceptions, and impediments of the WHO Labour Care Guide among doctors and nurses in Enugu State, Nigeria [8]. However, the study included both doctors and nurses across multiple healthcare tiers, limiting the depth of understanding specific to frontline maternity care providers such as midwives, who are primarily responsible for intrapartum care delivery.

In contrast, the present study addresses these limitations by focusing exclusively on midwives and drawing data from three distinct facility types (a teaching hospital, a general hospital, and a missionary hospital). This broader and more contextually diverse sample offers a more representative understanding of midwives' knowledge and the perceived feasibility of LCG implementation in real‐world settings. This study, therefore, aims to assess the level of awareness, knowledge, and perceived feasibility of implementing the WHO Labour Care Guide among midwives in Enugu State, with the goal of informing future training and implementation strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This is analytical cross‐sectional study conducted in three hospitals located in two local government areas (LGAs) of Enugu State, Nigeria. This design was appropriate for assessing the level of knowledge and perceived feasibility of implementing the WHO Labour Care Guide (LCG) among midwives at a single point in time. It allowed for the collection of quantitative data from a defined population to examine associations between variables such as years of experience and knowledge level. Data collection was carried out in August 2024.

Study Setting

This study was conducted in the selected healthcare facilities in Enugu State. Enugu State is one of the five states in southeastern Nigeria and shares boundaries with Anambra, Ebonyi, Abia, Benue, and Kogi States. It covers a land area of approximately 7161 km² and has a projected population of 3,267,837 people, comprising 1,596,042 males and 1,671,795 females (National Population Commission, 2023). Enugu State has a three‐tier healthcare system comprising primary, secondary, and tertiary facilities. Primary healthcare is delivered through health centres and primary health clinics managed by local governments. Secondary care is provided by general hospitals operated by the state government, while tertiary care is offered by specialist and teaching hospitals. This study was specifically focused on three health facilities within Enugu State: a tertiary‐level teaching hospital, a secondary‐level general hospital, and a missionary hospital, selected to represent a range of healthcare delivery levels and ownership types.

The University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH) is a tertiary health facility located at Nkanu West Local Government Area of Enugu State, Nigeria, along Port Harcourt expressway, about 25 km from Enugu metropolis. It receives obstetric and gynaecological referrals from primary, secondary, private‐missionary, and private nonmissionary hospitals. The annual delivery rate is currently 870. UNTH Ituku‐Ozalla, Enugu, has a total of 45 consultant obstetricians–gynaecologists, 60 resident doctors, and 10 nurses who work in the OGBYN unit. Labour is routinely managed by midwives and resident doctors using partograph for all eligible parturients.

Enugu State has 28 General hospitals (GHs) which are located in the 17 local government areas that made up the state. Poly General Hospital (PGH) is secondary‐level facility located at Asata IV, Enugu North Local Government, Enugu State, Nigeria, and has an average of three medical doctors and 10 nurses each. The hospital has a functional antenatal and labour ward units, led by consultant obstetricians or a principal medical officer and midwives. They report an average of 100–140 deliveries per month. For many years, partograph has been used for labour monitoring in the centre.

Mother of Christ Specialist Hospital is a private missionary hospital located at Ogbete West, Enugu North Local Government, beside Holy Ghost Cathedral, Achara, Enugu, Nigeria. It is a specialist hospital and has dedicated wards for different medical specialities such as maternity, paediatrics, surgery, and internal medicine. It has an average of two medical officers and 12 midwives who work in the obstetric care unit of the hospital. It has an average delivery rate of 134 per month.

Study Participants

We included all midwives working in the labour wards of three selected hospitals in Enugu State: University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH), Mother of Christ Specialist Hospital, and Poly General Hospital, Asata. These hospitals were chosen randomly to ensure representation across different levels of healthcare delivery and types of facility ownership in Enugu State. A total of 60 midwives were eligible during the study period, 21 from UNTH, 21 from Mother of Christ, and 18 from Poly General. We selected the three hospitals using simple random sampling from among facilities in Enugu State that conduct regular deliveries and operate 24‐h labour wards. To ensure full participation, we adopted a total population approach, distributing questionnaires to all eligible and consenting midwives available during the data collection period.

Sampling Method and Procedure

Sample Size

The sample size for this study included all midwives who were eligible to participate in the selected facilities and met the inclusion criteria during data collection.

Sampling Procedure

The sampling procedure involved two stages:

Stage 1: Selection of Institutions: A random sampling technique was used to select three health institutions from the state to ensure an unbiased selection of facilities for the study.

Stage 2: Selection of Respondents: Within each selected facility, participants were recruited through convenience sampling based on their availability and willingness to participate at the time of data collection. They were approached consecutively during their work shifts and only midwives who had worked in the labour ward for at least 1 month and were directly involved in managing labour were included in the study.

We used a pretested, semi‐structured, self‐administered questionnaire to collect data. The questionnaire was developed based on WHO Labour Care Guide materials and existing literature, and it consisted of three sections: sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge of the Labour Care Guide, and perceived feasibility of its implementation. We ensured face validity by consulting two senior midwifery educators and one obstetrician with expertise in LCG use. The tool was pretested with six midwives from a non‐study facility, and we revised it based on their feedback to improve clarity and ease of understanding without losing the intended content.

Variables

Knowledge was assessed using a structured questionnaire containing 8 Likert scale‐type questions related to the WHO Labour Care Guide. Each correct response was scored as 1, while incorrect or ‘I don't know’ responses were scored as 0. The total knowledge score for each respondent was calculated and used to categorise knowledge levels as follows: respondents who scored more than 50% were categorised as having good knowledge, while those who scored 50% or less were categorised as having poor knowledge. Perceived feasibility was assessed using a set of 8 Likert‐scale questions rated on a 4‐point scale: Strongly Disagree = 0, Disagree = 1, Agree = 2, and Strongly Agree = 3. The mean feasibility score for each respondent was computed. A mean score of ≥ 2.5 was interpreted as high feasibility, while a mean score < 2.5 indicated low feasibility.

Data Management and Analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analysed using Microsoft Excel and IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Both descriptive and inferential statistics were applied. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, and associations between them were tested using Fisher's exact test. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 at a 95% confidence level. Results are presented in tables and figures.

Ethical Consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC) with Ref number: NHREC/05/01/2008B‐FWA00002458‐1RB00002323. Participants' confidentiality was ensured, and informed consent was sought before data collection.

Discussion

Labour‐related complications remain a major contributor to maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality, particularly in low‐resource settings. To improve labour outcomes, tools such as the traditional partograph and the more recently introduced WHO Labour Care Guide (LCG) have been recommended for use by skilled birth attendants.

The current study revealed that although 69.8% of the midwives surveyed were aware of the LCG, a considerable proportion, 37.2%, demonstrated poor overall knowledge of its use in labour management. This finding aligns with earlier study by Nnagbo et al. (2024), that highlighted persistent knowledge gaps regarding the LCG among maternity care providers in sub‐Saharan Africa [8]. Without adequate understanding of labour monitoring tools and with Nigeria ranking third highest for maternal mortality rate globally (Annals of Global Health, 2025; Healthy Newborn Network, 2024), the country's healthcare system may struggle to meet the targets outlined in the National Strategic Health Development Plan II (2018–2022), which prioritises skilled attendance at birth and improved quality of intrapartum care (Federal Ministry of Health, 2019).

Of note, many respondents in this study were able to correctly identify basic components of the LCG, such as monitoring foetal heart rate and maternal vital signs, and acknowledged its emphasis on individualised care. However, majority of the midwives incorrectly believed that the LCG included alert and action lines. This misconception is widespread and likely due to the shift in design between the two tools. Unlike the partograph, the LCG does not offer pre‐marked thresholds; rather, it requires the provider to actively assess for deviations from normal, interpret clinical significance, and make individualised decisions. Yadav et al.'s (2022) analysis of the LCG specifically stated that it is more difficult to quickly scan and interpret it compared with the partograph, as one must ‘think upon and search for deviations from normal’ rather than rely on visual cue lines (e.g., alert/action lines) to prompt decision‐making (Yadav et al., 2022). This cognitive demand may contribute to confusion and reluctance to adopt the tool among staff with limited exposure to structured LCG training.

In Nigeria, where critical staff shortages and workforce challenges are well‐documented in primary health care maternity centres (Nwagha et al., 2019), this increased cognitive demand presents a substantial implementation barrier. Research has shown that many primary health care facilities lack adequate numbers of skilled midwives, with non‐midwifery trained personnel often attending to pregnant women and women in labour (Nwagha et al., 2019). Tool adoption in safe motherhood must not only be addressed but also the workforce conditions that enable its effective utilisation.

Furthermore, the absence of these traditional reference points may be especially problematic in settings, where midwives may lack the skill or confidence to assess more subjective parameters such as caput, moulding, and foetal position. These skills, which are essential for the effective use of the LCG, are not always emphasised in routine practice. Unlike the WHO Safe Childbirth Checklist, which has been incorporated into midwifery school curricula in some countries, the LCG has not yet been widely integrated into pre‐service education. Nigeria's Nursing and Midwifery Council, which regulates all cadres of nurses and midwives (NMCN, 2023), ought to consider mandating LCG training within both the national midwifery curriculum and as part of the required continuing professional development for license renewal. The current study also found that years of clinical experience were not significantly associated with knowledge of the LCG among the midwives. Whether respondents had less than 1 year of experience or more than 10 years, there was no statistically significant difference in knowledge level (p = 0.169). This indicates that familiarity with the LCG may not be inherently linked to the duration of professional practice, but rather to targeted exposure through training opportunities and institutional support. This interpretation is further supported by the findings from the study that among those who were knowledgeable about the LCG, the majority had accessed information through workshops. This suggests that such targeted educational efforts remain the most effective mode of disseminating knowledge about the LCG and aligns with findings from Adjie et al. [9], who reported that while the LCG is perceived as more detailed and easier to complete, midwives are still adjusting to its new structure and therefore require frequent training. These training sessions not only correct such misunderstandings but also build provider confidence and familiarity with the new approach to labour monitoring.

The findings of our study also point to important concerns regarding perceived feasibility. Although the LCG is designed to promote woman‐centred, respectful care, only 33.3% of the midwives who were aware of it perceived it as highly feasible for routine use. Key barriers identified by the participants included the belief that the tool was more difficult to interpret than the previous partograph, that it consumed more time, and that existing workload pressures would make its use impractical. These perceptions reflect implementation challenges within Nigeria's under‐resourced health system especially in facilities where basic supplies and adequate staffing are frequently inadequate. Introducing a tool that requires more time and cognitive effort, without addressing underlying system strengthening, risks minimal adoption without meaningful practice change. While some of these concerns may stem from unfamiliarity rather than actual experience, since the midwives may not have used the LCG in practice, the validity of these perceptions may also be questionable. Although midwives in other regions who have had an actual experience with the LCG agreed that more time, information, and effective communication were needed to complete it [9, 10].

Key barriers to LCG use identified in the study are knowledge gap and perceived low feasibility, indicating the need for ongoing training. These challenges, coupled with concerns about workload and staff shortages, point to the importance of supportive supervision and hands‐on workshops to improve confidence, correct misunderstandings, and promote routine use of the tool in labour monitoring.

Limitations

While our study provides targeted insights by focusing exclusively on midwives, the primary deliverers of intrapartum care who would be the main users of the LCG in practice and captures diverse perspectives across three different facility types in multiple local government areas, several limitations should be acknowledged. The findings are subject to limitations such as social desirability bias, where respondents may provide answers they perceive as professionally expected, and potential self‐report bias inherent in questionnaire‐based assessments. The relatively small sample size and restriction of our study to a single state may limit the generalisability of the findings. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are recommended to confirm these results. Additionally, since the study focused on self‐reported knowledge and perceptions rather than observed practice, actual clinical behaviours could not be assessed. These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Nonetheless, the insights provided open avenues for future implementation research to explore practical challenges and enablers associated with the adoption of the WHO Labour Care Guide in real‐world settings.

Conclusions

This study revealed that while awareness of the WHO Labour Care Guide (LCG) among midwives in Enugu was relatively high, significant gaps remain in knowledge and perception. More than one in three respondents demonstrated poor knowledge of the tool, and nearly half held negative perceptions regarding its feasibility for routine use. The LCG is not currently in active use in the study settings used, and key barriers identified include insufficient staffing, lack of structured training, low familiarity with the tool's design, and concerns about time consumption in high‐pressure labour wards. To address these challenges, there is an urgent need to integrate LCG‐focused training into both in‐service professional development programs and pre‐service midwifery education curricula. Sensitisation workshops and mentorship programs should be implemented to enhance familiarity with the tool and demonstrate its clinical value. Additionally, institutional policies should support task‐shifting strategies and staffing reinforcements to reduce workload pressure, making routine LCG use more feasible.
