3D-ReGen: A Unified 3D Geometry Regeneration Framework
Geon Yeong Park, Roman Shapovalov, Rakesh Ranjan, Jong Chul Ye, Andrea Vedaldi, Thu Nguyen-Phuoc

Abstract
We consider the problem of regenerating 3D objects from 2D images and initial 3D shapes. Most 3D generators operate in a one-shot fashion, converting text or images to a 3D object with limited controllability. We introduce instead 3D-ReGen, a 3D re generator that is conditioned on an initial 3D shape. This conceptually simple formulation allows us to support numerous useful tasks, including 3D enhancement, reconstruction, and editing. 3D-ReGen uses a new conditioning mechanism based on VecSet, which allows the regenerator to update or improve the input geometry with consistent fine-grained details. 3D-ReGen learns a widely applicable regeneration prior from off-the-shelf 3D datasets via self-supervised pretext tasks and augmentations, without additional annotations. We evaluate both the geometric consistency and fine-grained quality of 3D-ReGen, achieving state-of-the-art performance in controllable 3D generation across several tasks.

Introduction

While 3D content creation is a complex process that requires significant expertise and effort, generative AI has the potential to radically transform it.
Similar to how language models have revolutionized coding, AI can accelerate 3D artist workflows and open 3D content creation to a much wider audience.
However, whereas coding is concerned primarily with a single modality, namely text, 3D content creation is a complex task that involves multiple tasks and modalities.

For example, 3D artists often start with a 2D concept art that captures the 3D content that must be created.
While the concept art can be captured by an image, the artist is often given additional specifications of a different nature.
An example workflow may have the artist sketch the 3D shape using a 3D sculpting tool, followed by iterative refinement or editing.
In other cases, the artist starts from a noisy scan (3D point cloud) of a real-life object, obtained using photogrammetry, and reconstructs that into a clean, faithful 3D shape that can be used in production.
All these examples require the 3D generation process to be controlled by 3D signals of various types (sculpts, scans, block-outs) in addition to 2D signals (concept art).

Recent advances in 3D generation
[
78
,
65
,
81
,
56
]
have focused on an image-based workflow, where a 3D object
{\bm{x}}^{H}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
(a surface) is generated from a 2D image
I\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
.
1
1

1

This is true for text-based generation as well, as the text prompt is converted to a 2D image via a text-to-image model.

Hence, generation amounts to drawing a sample
{\bm{x}}^{H}
from the distribution
p({\bm{x}}^{H}\mid I)
.
However, in the more elaborate workflows discussed above, it is necessary to control the generation process by an additional signal
{\bm{y}}^{L}
, like a sculpt, a block-out, or a 3D scan.
While the nature and interpretation of these signals are widely different, there are some important similarities too.
First, all such 3D signals can be represented as 3D shapes
{\bm{y}}^{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Secondly, these 3D shapes
{\bm{y}}^{L}
contain
significantly less (or partially distorted) information
than the target shape
{\bm{x}}^{H}
.
For example,
\mathbf{y}^{L}
may be (i) a coarse
but
geometrically aligned proxy (e.g., voxel grid or bounding box) or (ii) a degraded and potentially misaligned geometry (e.g., a noisy scan or a distorted intermediate generation).
Hence, the common goal is to map a
L
ow-information 3D shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
to a
H
igh-information 3D shape
{\bm{x}}^{H}
.
In this paper, we thus ask whether, despite their different nature, all these tasks can be solved by a
single
architecture and learning formulation.

We call this common problem 3D shape
re
generation
.
Formally, the goal of 3D regeneration is to draw a 3D shape
{\bm{x}}^{H}
from a distribution
p({\bm{x}}^{H}\mid{\bm{y}}^{L},I)
where
I
is a 2D image and
{\bm{y}}^{L}
is an auxiliary 3D shape that may contain significantly less information than
{\bm{x}}^{H}
. Two key challenges arise in training general 3D regenerators: (a) limited understanding of task-agnostic
architectural
design, and (b) the scarcity of paired 3D training
datasets
.

Architecture.

Prior work has addressed specific instances of regeneration problems (e.g., editing
[
25
]
, enhancement
[
11
]
) using tailored task-specific architectures, whereas we seek a
unified architecture
that can be applied to all types of 3D downstream regeneration tasks.
To design such a general-purpose architecture, we start from VecSet
[
78
]
, a powerful and highly flexible 3D latent space representation.
VecSet is based on a pair of encoder and decoder functions
{\bm{z}}=\mathcal{E}({\bm{x}})
and
{\bm{x}}=\mathcal{D}({\bm{z}})
, where
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times d}
is a set of
N
latent vectors of dimension
d
.
In prior works, this latent space has been used to train latent conditional diffusion models, usually in the form of a Diffusion Transformer (DiT)
[
44
]
implementing a denoising function
{\bm{z}}^{\prime}=\Phi({\bm{z}},I)
, where
{\bm{z}}
is a noisy latent and
I
is a conditioning image.
As we show in
Sections

3
and
2
, we can adapt this architecture to solve the 3D regeneration problem by conditioning the diffusion process on the input coarse shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
, also encoded in the same latent space
{\bm{z}}^{L}=\mathcal{E}({\bm{y}}^{L})
.
Rather than injecting 3D controls via a separate cross-attention pathway
[
81
,
19
]
, we encode both the conditioning shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
and the target shape
{\bm{x}}^{H}
using the same VecSet encoder and concatenate their tokens as joint input to the DiT. This design minimally modifies the pretrained generator, allowing the 3D condition to be processed by the same self-attention mechanism used during pretraining. While cross-attention is effective for sparse and simple controls (e.g., bounding boxes), in general regeneration scenarios involving dense or noisy inputs (e.g., corrupted scans), we show that our simple VecSet-level concatenation strategy empirically outperforms cross-attention and other conditioning mechanism alternatives.

Dataset.

While recent efforts have explored 3D conditioning in generative models (e.g., CLAY
[
81
]
,
[
19
]
), their applicability to broader downstream tasks (e.g., enhancement, editing) remains unclear, partly due to the lack of scalable task-specific training
dataset
.
Specifically, these methods are largely restricted to conditional generation given clean, geometrically accurate controls aligned with the target shape (e.g., bounding boxes).
Thus, it remains unexplored whether a single architecture can robustly handle the wider spectrum of 3D inputs
{\bm{y}}^{L}
with severe degradations or mismatched geometry (e.g., noisy scans, lossy-compressed assets, or distorted outputs from low-quality generators), limiting the range of inverse problems such priors can address. Although large-scale 3D base datasets are now generally available
[
8
]
, these datasets lack the annotations required to learn 3D regeneration models (namely pairs
({\bm{x}}^{H},{\bm{y}}^{L})
).
Another contribution of this paper is to introduce protocols that can automatically convert a generic 3D dataset into training data for 3D regeneration, where
{\bm{y}}^{L}
is a degraded version of
{\bm{x}}^{H}
, obtained via data augmentation, without additional expensive annotations.

To show the effectiveness of our approach, we experiment in particular with three different tasks:
(
a
) Enhancement: Given a very low-resolution or even corrupted 3D component of a large 3D scene, we upgrade its quality;
(
b
) Editing: Given an initial 3D shape, we allow the model to change significant parts of it;
(
c
) Reconstruction: Given an initial 3D scan reconstructed from a small number of views using photogrammetry, we recover a full and geometrically faithful 3D object.
Importantly, all these tasks are solved using the same exact model architecture.
Our experiments show that 3D-ReGen achieves state-of-the-art results even compared to models that are designed specifically for each task.

Related Work

Advances in 3D generative models.

The increasing availability of large-scale 3D datasets

Some methods generate 3D assets using only 2D supervision from multi-view rendered images

In contrast, methods with direct 3D supervision

24

86
,
87

can explicitly generate SDFs, from which 3D meshes can be cleanly extracted with arbitrary topology.

Most 3D generators take text or image prompts as input, which are intuitive but inherently under-constrained.
To improve controllability, recent works introduce explicit 3D-aware controls into native 3D generators, such as voxels, bounding boxes, or other structured primitives (e.g., CLAY
[

]
, PoseMaster
[

, Hunyuan3D-Omni

).

3D generative priors for downstream tasks.

Despite advances in 3D
generation
, a broader question remains: how can 3D generative priors provide a unified framework across multiple

?
In 2D, foundation generative models increasingly support editing, reconstruction, and other tasks within a single architecture
[
64
,
77
,
23
,
55

.
In contrast, 3D downstream applications often rely on task-specific architectures or training-free adaptations

A principled design for unified 3D task generalization and scalable construction of paired training data remains underexplored.
In this work, we take an initial step by addressing both architectural and data aspects, with the goal of encouraging progress toward unified, generalizable, and versatile 3D regeneration priors.

Let

denote the surface of a 3D object that we wish to generate, where the suffix stands for
H

In
3D regeneration

{\bm{x}}^{H}
from a probability distribution
p({\bm{x}}^{H}\mid{\bm{y}}^{L},I)

3.1
Model architecture

3D-ReGen builds upon a pre-trained latent diffusion model, operating in a compact latent space of 3D shapes
[
30
,
27
,
85
,
65
,
31
]
, for which we use VecSet
[
78
]
.

3D Generation using VecSet.

VecSet’s
[
78
]
encoder is a transformer neural network that maps a (centered) 3D shape
{\bm{x}}\subset[-1,1]^{3}
to
K

D
-dimensional latent vectors

{\bm{z}}={\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{P}};K)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}.

It does so by sampling a point cloud

{\mathcal{P}}=\{(p_{i},n_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}

from the shape, where
p_{i}\in{\bm{x}}
is a surface point and
n_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
its corresponding normal.
Then, it further subsamples
K\ll N
points from
{\mathcal{P}}
using Farthest Point Sampling (FPS), uses them to initialize the tokens, and then uses cross-attention to pool information from all points
{\mathcal{P}}
into the tokens.
The decoder
{\mathcal{D}}
, another transformer, evaluates the Signed Distance Function (SDF)
{\mathcal{D}}(q\mid{\bm{z}})\in\mathbb{R}
of the 3D shape
{\bm{x}}
at any query location
q\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
by attending to the latent tokens
{\bm{z}}
.

Given this latent space, VecSet models the conditional distribution
p({\bm{z}}\mid I)
over latent codes instead of the distribution
p({\bm{x}}\mid I)
over 3D shapes.
Following
[
27
,
30
]
, we do so by using a Diffusion Transformer (DiT)
[
44
]
, which learns a denoiser neural network
{\bm{z}}_{t-1}=\Phi({\bm{z}}_{t}\mid I,t)
that takes as input a noised version
{\bm{z}}_{t}
of the latent code
{\bm{z}}
and partially denoises it to
{\bm{z}}_{t-1}
, conditioned on the image
I
and step
t
.
The image
I
is tokenized using a pre-trained image encoder (DINOv2
[
43
]
) and integrated into the DiT through cross-attention layers.

From Generation to Regeneration.

We now generalize the model above to sample the distribution

p({\bm{x}}^{H}\mid{\bm{y}}^{L},I).

As noted in
Section

1
, while the meaning of the conditioning signal
{\bm{y}}^{L}
differs depending on the task, they can all be written as 3D shapes
{\bm{y}}^{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Hence, we propose to use the same VecSet representation for
{\bm{y}}^{L}
as well, and to learn the conditional distribution

p({\bm{z}}^{H}\mid{\bm{z}}^{L},I)

where
{\bm{z}}^{H}
and
{\bm{z}}^{L}
are the latent codes of
{\bm{x}}^{H}
and
{\bm{y}}^{L}
, respectively.

Utilizing the same latent space for both the conditioning and generated signals
{\bm{z}}^{L}
and
{\bm{z}}^{H}
opens up a very simple yet effective design for the DiT

{\bm{z}}_{t-1}^{H}=\Phi({\bm{z}}_{t}^{H}\mid{\bm{z}}^{L},I,t);

namely, we can simply
concatenate
the conditioning tokens
{\bm{z}}^{L}
to the noisy tokens
{\bm{z}}^{H}_{t}
and pass them together as input to the DiT.
In the experiments, we show that this is more effective than alternative designs where other encodings are used, including designs that use cross-attention to pass the conditioning tokens rather than concatenation.

Fewer tokens for coarser shapes.

A useful property of VecSet, which we do not believe was noted or used before, is that the number of latent tokens
K
can be adjusted according to the complexity of the encoded shape
{\bm{x}}
.
In particular, because the conditioning shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
contains little information, it can be encoded using far fewer tokens
{\bm{z}}^{L}={\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{P}}^{L};C)
than the generated shape
{\bm{x}}^{H}
, where
C\ll K
.
Due to the quadratic complexity of self-attention, this significantly improves the model’s efficiency.
Note that sparse voxel grids
[
65
]
, also popular in 3D generation, do not have this property as a high voxel resolution is still required to encode accurately even a simple shape.

Token pre-processing.

The authors of
[
6
]
recently noted that concatenating two VecSets approximates merging the two underlying 3D shapes, which differs from the regenerative effect we aim to achieve.
We thus pre-process the conditioning tokens
{\bm{z}}^{L}
before concatenation so that the model can better distinguish them from the noisy latent tokens
{\bm{z}}^{H}
.
We do so by applying an MLP with zero-initialized weights, similar to ControlNet
[
82
]
.
Zero-initialization avoids “shocking” the model when new control tokens
{\bm{z}}^{L}
are introduced.
We also find it useful to add positional embeddings to
{\bm{z}}^{L}
to help the model identify their spatial locations.
Formally, we can write our diffusion step as

{\bm{z}}_{t-1}^{H}=\Phi\Big({\bm{z}}_{t}^{H}\oplus\text{MLP}\big({\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{P}}^{L};C)+\text{PE}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{L})\big)\Bigm|I,t\Big),

where
\oplus
denotes concatenation along the token dimension.
Note that only
{\bm{z}}^{H}_{t}
is denoised whereas
{\bm{z}}^{L}
remains fixed as a condition.

Training Data.

In order to train the model to capture the conditional distribution

p({\bm{z}}^{H}\mid{\bm{z}}^{L},I)
,
we require a dataset of triplets

\{({\bm{z}}_{i}^{H},{\bm{z}}_{i}^{L},I_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

comprising the encodings of high- and low-quality 3D shapes
{\bm{x}}^{H}
and
{\bm{x}}^{L}
along with the corresponding (optional) guidance images
I_{i}
.
The triplets must be specific to the different tasks one wishes to solve (e.g., enhancement, reconstruction, editing).
3D training data is already scarce, and procuring paired data for each task is much harder still.
Hence, we develop instead data engineering pipelines that construct such triplets automatically, starting from a collection of 3D objects
{\bm{x}}^{H}
.
We detail this process for each target application in
Section

3.2
.

3.2
Applications

We introduce several example applications of 3D-ReGen to show that the same model architecture and 3D dataset (
Section

3.3
) can be used for multiple purposes.

Base dataset.

All our applications are trained from the
same
base 3D dataset
{\mathcal{D}}
as we only change how the data is automatically augmented in pre-processing.
The dataset comprises approximately 1M collection of artist-authored 3D objects very similar in spirit to Objaverse
[
9
]
.

Compositional Shape Enhancement.

In
shape enhancement
, we map a low-quality 3D shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
to a high-quality one
{\bm{x}}^{H}
.
We apply this idea to a
compositional
setting
[
36
,
4
,
5
,
74
,
57
]
, where a large 3D object is first decomposed and then upgraded one component at a time.
We are motivated by the fact that 3D generators operate at a finite resolution, and even high resolution ones like Sparc3D
[
32
]
fail to generate sufficient details for large 3D objects such as whole scenes in one go.

Data Construction.

Please see the Appendix for an illustrative overview.
To simulate the compositional (re)generation process, we start by arranging random ground-truth (and thus high-quality) 3D objects
{\bm{x}}^{H}\in{\mathcal{D}}
in simple
k\times k
grids.
These pseudo-compositions are then normalized (as a whole) in the
[-1,1]^{3}
cube and encoded using VecSet into tokens.
Inspired by SDEdit
[
41
]
, given a pre-trained DiT, we add noise to these tokens at level
t^{*}<T
and denoise them back to
t=0
.
The 3D scene is then decoded from the denoised tokens and the degraded 3D shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
of each object is extracted from the resulting geometry.
Increasing
k
means that more objects are represented by the same number of tokens, controlling how much information is removed per-object, which also determines the final number of tokens
{\bm{z}}^{L}
we use to represent
{\bm{y}}^{L}
.

{\bm{z}}^{H}
is instead obtained by encoding the original object
{\bm{x}}^{H}
with the full token count.

Generality.

While we simulate the degradation process due to generating multiple objects together, in practice, as we show in the Appendix, our technique results in pairs
({\bm{z}}^{H},{\bm{z}}^{L})
that are representative of degradations common in other applications too, more so than, say, applying generic 3D smoothing filters
[
47
,
15
,
52
]
to
{\bm{x}}^{H}
.
As a result, the resulting model can also be potentially applied to a wide range of degradations, including low-quality 3D assets (e.g., from old games) and models affected by lossy 3D compression.

Guided Image-to-3D.

In
guided image-to-3D
we map an initial sparse but accurate 3D point cloud
{\bm{y}}^{L}
reconstructed from one or more images
\{I_{1},\dots,I_{L}\}
into a full 3D object
{\bm{x}}^{H}
faithful to the input data.
We are motivated by the fact that current image-to-3D generators produce high-quality and detailed 3D shapes that are however not necessarily faithful to the input image, and/or cannot account for more than one image as input.
On the other hand, large 3D reconstruction neural networks like VGGT
[
58
]
and many others
[
61
,
22
,
33
]
reconstruct the visible geometry as faithfully as possible from several views, resulting in 3D reconstructions
{\bm{y}}^{L}
that are accurate, but are incomplete due to occlusion, lack details, and can be noisy.

Data Construction.

Given a ground-truth 3D object
{\bm{x}}^{H}
, we render 1–4 random views
\{I_{1},\dots,I_{L}\}
and pass them to VGGT to obtain a sparse reconstruction
{\bm{y}}^{L}
.
For rendering, camera poses are sampled uniformly in azimuth, focal length, and with elevations in
[-10^{\circ},30^{\circ}]
.
In order to apply
{\mathcal{E}}
to encode
{\bm{y}}^{L}
, we also require the normals for each surface point, which VGGT does not provide, so we estimate them using covariance analysis
[
42
]
.
Because the scale and pose of
{\bm{y}}^{L}
is arbitrary, we align it to
{\bm{x}}^{H}
.
The conditioning image
I
is randomly chosen from the rendered views and the triplet
({\bm{z}}^{H},{\bm{z}}^{L},I)
is added to the training dataset.

3D Shape Editing.

In
3D shape editing
we map an initial 3D shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
into a modified shape
{\bm{x}}^{H}
that reproduces
{\bm{y}}^{L}
except for a specific 3D region
M
which is edited to match a guide image
I
. The 3D masks can be as simple as a box or ellipsoid that fully contains the region of geometry we want to edit.
Following
[
13
,
26
]
, the image
I
is obtained automatically by (1) rendering a view of the original object
{\bm{y}}^{L}
, (2) masking the 2D region corresponding to the 3D edit
M
, and (3) using a text-to-2D model
[
23
]
to inpaint the masked detail based on textual instructions.

Data Construction.

To generate training data for this model we need to simulate the editing process.
The target 3D object
{\bm{x}}^{H}
is sampled as usual from the base dataset
{\mathcal{D}}
, the image
I
is a render of this object, and
{\bm{z}}^{H}
is the encoding of
{\bm{x}}^{H}
.
Then, it only remains to show how to construct the shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
(and corresponding code
{\bm{z}}^{L}
) that represents the
source
of the edit.

To this end, given
{\bm{x}}^{H}
, we first randomly sample an edit region
M
as a 3D mask. The 3D masks can be as simple as a box or cylinder containing the geometry we want to edit.
Then we remove the original points within
M
and the corresponding normals from
{\mathcal{P}}^{H}
to form
{\mathcal{P}}^{L}
.
We obtain
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}={\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{P}}^{L};C-k)
and use
C-k<C
tokens instead of all
C
tokens.
The remaining
k
edit tokens are used to tell the model where the edit should be applied and are initialized as learnable mask positional embeddings
{\bm{z}}^{M}
in latent space.
The final encoding for the degraded shape
{\bm{y}}^{L}
is the concatenation
{\bm{z}}^{L}=\tilde{{\bm{z}}}\oplus{\bm{z}}^{M}
of
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}
with
k
learnable mask tokens
{\bm{z}}^{M}
.
We thus obtain the final training triplet
({\bm{z}}^{H},{\bm{z}}^{L},I)
.

The number of edit tokens
k
represents the editing capacity.
It is adaptively set as
k=\max(C*r,k_{\min})
, where
r
is the ratio of masked to total source points.
Capping
k
with
k_{\min}\in[0,C)
guarantees minimal editing capacity.
This adaptive allocation balances identity preservation with editing flexibility, enabling larger masks to produce greater geometric changes.

3.3
Implementation Details

Our base model is an in-house VecSet-based 3D generator, which is trained on the base dataset
{\mathcal{D}}
and its multi-view renderings, in a similar manner to prior works
[
27
,
30
,
85
,
65
]
.
This results in initial weights for both the VAE and the DiT.
We also apply the augmentation strategies described in
Section

3.2
to the base dataset to obtain the training datasets for each application.

The
enhancement
DiT is trained by combining the augmentations for the enhancement models as well as the editing one.
Following the literature
[
56
,
18
,
79
]
, we train the DiT by progressively increasing the number of tokens
K=512,1024,2048
for 49K, 112K, and 390K steps.
To train the
reconstruction
DiT, we fine-tune the enhancement model on the VGGT outputs for 25K steps.
To train the
Shape editing
DiT we used the masked training set and
K{=}2048
high-quality tokens.
Training converges in just 25K steps.
For every model, we used
C=512

low-quality
conditioning tokens.

For additional robustness, we further augment the conditioning 3D shape by adding Gaussian blobs imitating floaters and by perturbing point normals, and we augment the image conditioning via random crops, color jitter, random backgrounds, and blur.
We adopt the
v
-prediction
[

Conditioning Mechanism.

[

In

Diffusion Parameterization.

We further compare our diffusion formulation with a rectified flow baseline
[
35
,
38
]
, where the model directly maps coarse latents
{\bm{z}}^{L}
to the ground-truth latent distribution without explicit Gaussian noise. This alternative choice is inspired by DetailGen3D
[
10
]
.

Table

4
shows that our diffusion parameterization achieves better reconstruction on the validation set.

Applicability of existing generation frameworks to
re
generation.

We further test whether existing (controllable) 3D
generation
frameworks are readily generalizable to
regeneration
tasks. First, we apply TRELLIS to enhancement and reconstruction by respectively encoding degraded geometry or VGGT predictions into sparse structures in its first stage, followed by SLAT generation in the second stage. As shown in
Fig.

9
, TRELLIS fails to recover fine geometry, as it is trained on

which does not generalize to broken, incomplete inputs. Similarly, although CLAY
[
81
]
is not publicly available, we evaluate Hunyuan3D-Omni
[
19
]
, a closely related
concurrent
framework, on reconstruction tasks and observe similar performance degradation (see Appendix). These results suggest that existing
generation
frameworks do not generalize to regeneration tasks with degraded/incomplete geometry without fine-tuning, motivating dedicated regeneration formulations and data.

Overall, using official baselines (when available) and controlled reimplementations under a fixed backbone and dataset, we show that our improvements stem from both dataset design
and
a generalizable architecture.

5
Conclusion

We have introduced 3D-ReGen, a versatile 3D regenerator that unifies diverse 3D-to-3D tasks under a single framework.
Future work includes extending the framework to dynamics
[
76
]
and incorporating richer control signals, such as skeletons for rigged assets. Regarding limitations, while 3D-ReGen demonstrates strong controllability, exploring alternative 3D latent representations (e.g., SLAT on active voxels
[
67
]
) may further improve generalizability.

Appendix 0.A
Training Details

Architecture.

As described in the main paper, 3D-ReGen is built on a frozen pre-trained 3D VAE whose backbone is a 24-layer Transformer.
This VAE is trained by randomly sampling the latent token count
C\in\{512,1024,2048\}
.
For the DiT denoiser, the 3D condition is provided as VecSet latents, augmented with a zero-initialized learnable positional embedding.
The full model contains approximately 2.8B parameters.
Each Transformer block uses a hidden dimension of 2048 with 16 attention heads, 24 layers, 11 skip connections, and an image embedding dimension of 1536.
The input point cloud resolution is fixed to
N=32{,}768
points for both high-quality and low-quality shapes.

Training.

We use the AdamW
[
40
]
optimizer with a learning rate of
10^{-4}
for
C=512
and
2\times 10^{-5}
for
C\in\{1024,2048\}
, with a warm-up from
10^{-6}
during the first epoch.
Training follows the scaled linear time schedule of Stable Diffusion
[
45
,
34
]
with
\beta_{\text{start}}=0.00085
and
\beta_{\text{end}}=0.0015
.

Remark on the necessity of an enhancement model.

In 3D enhancement, preserving consistency with the original measurements is crucial since any mismatch in rotation or scale between the coarse input and the enhanced output can break downstream applications. For example, if a model deviates from the original 6-DoF pose of the coarse part, it may not fit properly into the original composite object.
Single-image 3D generative models often fail to maintain this pose consistency because they lack explicit 3D conditioning or any other form of shape constraints.
Moreover, the ambiguous nature of generation conditioned on a single view means that these models may miss important detail or hallucinate structures that do not match the true geometry, especially in occluded regions. Relevant examples are provided in the supplementary web page.

Appendix 0.E
More Related Works

3D Downstream Tasks.

Going beyond 3D generation, we present 3D-ReGen as a unified framework for 3D geometry regeneration, built upon an image-to-3D generative model that directly produces SDF representations.
Leveraging a versatile control space and self-supervision with base 3D datasets, our approach enables a controllable 3D generation pipeline supporting diverse downstream tasks, including 3D enhancement, shape reconstruction, and editing. This application enables us to leverage key advantages of 3D diffusion models in downstream tasks: (1) clean and coherent surface extraction via SDFs, (2) a 3D-native latent space, and (3) strong generative capacity.
