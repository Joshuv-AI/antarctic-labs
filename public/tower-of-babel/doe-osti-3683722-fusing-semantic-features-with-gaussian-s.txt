ABSTRACT: Reconstructing 3D surfaces from electro-optical satellite imagery is an
important capability for generating high-quality digital elevation models at scale.
Recently, Gaussian splatting has emerged as a state-of-the-art technique for 3D
reconstruction from satellite imagery. However, Gaussian splatting is optimized solely
on RGB imagery, making it susceptible to errors when dealing with the radiometric
inconsistencies and textureless regions common in satellite images. To address this, we
propose a method for fusing Gaussian splatting with vision foundation models that is
specifically tailored to satellite imagery. While recent work has explored fusing Gaussian
splatting and vision foundation models, it has been studied only on terrestrial datasets,
which, unlike multi-date satellite imagery, contain more constrained illumination at
smaller scene scales. To account for these challenges, we introduce a method for
computing multiscale satellite image embeddings along with a per-image feature
alignment module. Benchmarked on the IARPA 2019 Challenge Dataset, our method
reduces mean reconstruction error from 1.65 m to 1.57 m—a 5.2% relative
improvement over previous methods. These results demonstrate that vision foundation
models can enhance the geometric accuracy of satellite-based 3D reconstruction.

 1. Introduction
 Three-dimensional (3D) surface reconstruction from fortuitous multiview high-
 resolution satellite electro-optical (EO) imagery is a technique of increasing interest for
 Earth observation tasks, including crop monitoring [1], disaster response [2], and more. Tra-
 ditional surface reconstruction methods typically leverage stereo image pairs and measure
 image-to-image pixel disparities to triangulate points in 3D [3–5]. More recently, differ-
 entiable rendering techniques such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [6] and Gaussian
 splatting [7] have been used to formulate a global multiview optimization and achieve
 state-of-the-art (SOTA) surface reconstructions. A central challenge for both traditional and
 state-of-the-art methods is maintaining robust 3D estimation in the presence of image-to-
 image variations, such as changes in illumination, and textureless regions, both of which
 hinder consistent 3D reconstruction.
 Semantic (object- and concept-level) cues can be more robust than RGB values to
 changing lighting, seasons, and noise, motivating the longstanding interest in how such in-
 formation can benefit 3D reconstruction. For instance, a building’s pixel values may change
 under illumination, but its semantic properties, such as its identity and relative position in
 the scene, remain largely unchanged. Recognizing this potential, recent 3D reconstruction
 techniques propose fusing differentiable rendering, which typically uses RGB images only,
 with semantic information from computer vision foundation models [8–10]. Foundation
 models are large neural networks trained to produce feature embeddings that capture
 semantic content and serve as strong priors for numerous downstream classification and
 segmentation tasks [11–14], including satellite imagery [14]. Foundation model features
 correlate with semantic content [12], as shown in Figure 1.

 A promising recent example of these fusion approaches, Feature3DGS by Zhou et al.,
 proposes combining foundation models with Gaussian splatting by minimizing a joint RGB
 image and feature loss [10]. They show that incorporating the foundation model features
 improves reconstruction accuracy and enables new downstream workflows, such as 3D
 semantic segmentation.

 Techniques like Feature3DGS are encouraging, but have been studied using close-
 range, ‘terrestrial’ imagery (imagery captured near the Earth’s surface by people, cars,
 drones, etc.), and have not been applied to satellite imagery. Satellite imagery introduces
 significant distribution shifts relative to terrestrial imagery, including near-nadir viewpoints,
 solar angle and seasonality effects, and distinct sensor models. Our work addresses this gap
 by fusing semantic content from foundation models with Gaussian splatting for satellite
 surface reconstruction. Our contributions include
 • The first application of semantic Gaussian splatting to overhead multi-date satellite
 reconstruction.
 • A per-image feature alignment module (FiLM) for multi-date/multi-illumination
 overhead imagery, enabling consistent feature supervision across views.
 • A framework for evaluating computer vision foundation models on a satellite image
 segmentation task, which serves as a performance predictor for fusion with Gaus-
 sian splatting.

 1.1. Background and Related Work
 This section begins by discussing related work on foundation models. Next, we
 discuss traditional surface reconstruction techniques including Gaussian splatting-based
 techniques. We then provide an overview of previous works that investigate fusing seman-
 tic information and 3D reconstruction.

 1.2. Computer Vision Foundation Models
 Foundation models are neural networks trained on large-scale datasets that, after
 training, form a basis for being finetuned on downstream tasks [15,16]. The initial training,
 termed ‘pretraining’, is often self-supervised, meaning the dataset does not require labels.
 In computer vision, a common self-supervised task is masked image prediction, which
 tasks the model with generating randomly masked image patches [12,13,17]. Another task
 is predicting representations that are invariant to random crops, rotations, and scale aug-
 mentations [12,13]. Pretrained foundation models, such as DINOv2 [12] and DINOv3 [13],
 have demonstrated excellent performances on a variety of downstream classification tasks
 including autonomous driving [18], medical image processing [19], and other applica-
 tions [20]. Foundation models can also be trained with supervised learning. The segment
 anything model (SAM) [11], for instance, is trained on millions of wide-scale image datasets
 with ground truth segmentation maps. SAM produces representations that are useful for
 downstream segmentation and classification tasks in targeted domains, including satellite
 imagery [21]. Foundation models generally outperform simpler models like convolutional
 neural networks (CNNs) or vision transformers (ViTs) alone due to the scalability and
 generalization of foundation models across applications and tasks.
 Our experiments focus on the ViT-based foundation models DINOv2, DINOv3, SAM,
 and SAM2.1 as these models were the SOTA at the time of this project. Future efforts could
 consider even newer models such as SAM3 [22].

 1.4. Surface Reconstruction from Satellite Images
 Reconstructing surfaces from multiple overlapping satellite images is typically framed
 as the fusion of several image-pair (i.e., stereo) problems [5,23]. Each stereo problem tri-
 angulates 3D points by computing pixel-to-pixel disparities across images. Semi-global
 matching [24] and multi-global matching [4] are traditional techniques for robustly esti-
 mating surfaces from pixel disparities. While these techniques offer robust performances,
 they are sensitive to occlusions, shadows, and other non-linearities that are not explic-
 itly modeled by pixel-to-pixel matching algorithms [25,26]. More recently, differentiable
 rendering techniques, including NeRFs [6,26,27] and Gaussian splatting, achieve SOTA
 reconstruction accuracy by solving a true global multiview optimization and modeling the
 aforementioned effects like occlusions and shadows.

 1.5. Gaussian Splatting for Surface Reconstruction
 Gaussian splatting is a SOTA technique for multiview reconstruction that was origi-
 nally developed for new view synthesis [28]; the task of rendering novel scene views from
 a collection of given input views. Gaussian splatting parameterizes a scene as a collection
 of 3D (or 2D [29]) Gaussians. Each Gaussian is defined by several learnable parameters
 including a mean, rotation, translation, color and opacity. These parameters are optimized
 by minimizing a photometric loss between rendered (i.e., ‘splatted’) Gaussians and the
 given input images. After optimization, the scene may be rendered from novel views.
 Since its inception, variants of Gaussian splatting have been proposed to support
 surface reconstruction [29], including Aira et al.’s Earth Observation Gaussian Splatting
 (EOGS) [7], the satellite-specific variant leveraged in this work. EOGS proposes approximat-
 ing satellite sensor models with an affine projection, a reasonable local approximation [30]
 that is compatible with typical Gaussian splatting rasterization kernels. Additionally, EOGS
 implements explicit shadow modeling via a differentiable implementation of the shadow
 mapping technique proposed by the seminal work of [31].
 Our work leverages an implementation of EOGS that we modified by (a) replacing 3D
 Gaussians with 2D Gaussians, which are known to better approximate scene surfaces [29],
 (b) adding functionality for duplicating and splitting Gaussians [28], (c) adding terms to
 include the feature channels, which enables fusion with the foundation model embeddings.

 1.6. Fusing Semantic Content and Surface Reconstruction
 Several studies investigate fusing semantic segmentation and reconstruction to im-
 prove geometric accuracy. Hane et al. propose an energy formulation for joint surface
 reconstruction and classification [32]. Kundu et al. propose a conditional random field
 for joint estimation of a voxel’s occupancy and semantic category [33]. More recently,
 Feature3DGS [10] fused foundation model embeddings with Gaussian splatting by adding
 a feature channel to Gaussians, which is optimized by a feature loss that accompanies the
 traditional photometric loss. As a result, the reconstructed 3D scene is a field that defines
 both the density and a feature vector each point. This strategy implicitly tasks the optimiza-
 tion with jointly solving for the 3D reconstruction and segmentation. Our work is most
 similar to Feature3DGS—we use the same loss function and feature rendering technique.
 However, our method tailors the optimization for satellite imagery by leveraging EOGS
 shadow mapping techniques and introducing a feature alignment module.

 2.5. Computing Details
 Given the high dimensionality of some of the embeddings (see “Embedding Di-
 mensions” in Table 1) and the fusion of multiple resolutions, some of the precomputed
 embeddings were extremely large, up to ~10 GB per image for the largest models. We
 thus computed and saved the embeddings to disk for each model and image. All model
 embeddings were downsampled to 256 dimensions using PCA prior due to GPU memory
 limitations. PCA was computed individually per image. We experimented with comput-
 ing PCA across the dataset, but found that it generally reduced reconstruction accuracy
 compared to per-image PCA. In an online or operational environment where images are
 asynchronously processed as they become available, it is advantageous to complete PCA
 on a per-image basis, rather than having to compute PCA over all the images jointly for
 every new image.
 We performed foundation model inference on a mixture of NVIDIA A100, H100, and
 H200 GPUs, hosted on Sandia’s Common Engineering Environment. Gaussian splatting
 experiments were performed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Generally, the time to generate
 embeddings from each model scales with their parameterization: larger models are slower.
 However, in this study, the embeddings only needed to be generated once per model
 per image. Once the embeddings were saved, all the reconstruction methods could be
 trained and tested from the same offline files. Additionally, for all but the largest 7B
 DINOv3 models, multiple instances of each foundation model could be run in parallel on
 a single GPU, generating embeddings for multiple images simultaneously. In an online
 or operational setting, embeddings could be generated within a processing pipeline with
 sufficient compute.

 We use the Adam optimizer to optimize all learnable parameters. Initial Gaussian
 positions and scales are initialized by interpolating a sparse point cloud generated from
 Marí et al.’s bundle adjustment method [35]. The FiLM parameters and upsampling
 MLP are initialized randomly at the start of training. All learnable parameters are jointly
 optimized for 3000 iterations with a batch size of 1. Training without features (i.e., the
 EOGS method) takes ~2 min. Training with features takes ~15 min. In alignment with the
 EOGS paper, we do not start shadow mapping until iteration 1000 to allow Gaussians to fit
 the initial geometry.

 3. Results
 This section describes our experimental results. Our experiments fall into two cate-
 gories: (1) Evaluating the off-the-shelf performance of computer vision foundation models
 for the semantic segmentation of satellite imagery. (2) Measuring the impact of fusing
 semantic information with Gaussian splatting by comparing reconstruction accuracy using
 only color images versus using color and feature images. In practice, we use the results
 from the first experiment as a principled way to down-select which models to carry forward
 into the fusion experiments—we find that models which perform well on the semantic
 segmentation task tend to benefit 3D reconstruction.

 3.1. Foundation Model Evaluation
 We evaluate how well foundation model embeddings capture semantic structure in
 satellite imagery by measuring class clustering and separability. For each model, we extract
 feature embeddings, divide them into 16 × 16 patches, and assign each patch a ground
 truth class (ground, building, vegetation, noise, and water) based on majority LiDAR
 segmentation (see Appendix A. Segmentation). The dataset is split into train and test sets,
 with N-fold cross-validation (Figure 6).

 3.2. Reconstruction Results
 We now discuss the results from fusing foundation model features with Gaussian
 splatting, using DINOv2 base, DINOv3 large lvd1618m, and SAM huge. Color (RGB) and
 PCA-reduced features for all scenes and models are visualized in Figure 9. As a result of
 adding feature channels to the Gaussians, each point in our 3D reconstructed point clouds
 contain both color and a feature vector, allowing us to visualize feature point clouds for
 each reconstruction in addition to the traditional RGB cloud. We observe that the visualized
 features generally cluster by scene content; for example, buildings and vegetation tend
 to be distinct colors. We also observe that features are largely invariant to color values.
 For instance, building colors tend to vary in the RGB plots but remain relatively fixed in
 the DINOv3 feature plots (e.g., Scene 2 for DINOv2 and DINOv3), suggesting that the
 features encode higher-level semantic content rather than raw pixel intensities. As a result,
 these features provide complementary information that the reconstruction can leverage
 alongside traditional color imagery. We provide a qualitative example of zero-shot 3D
 segmentation in Appendix A.2.
 Consistent with this interpretation, incorporating feature images improves the ge-
 ometric accuracy of the reconstructions. Table 4 summarizes the reconstruction mean
 squared error (MSE) for models fused with Gaussian splatting, where all feature-based

 reconstruction accuracy for all models on Scenes 1 and 2. In contrast, its effect on Scene 3

 4. Conclusions
 We present evidence that off-the-shelf foundation models can improve the recon-
 struction of overhead scenes from multi-date, multiview satellite imagery, particularly in
 untextured image regions. Our experiments suggest that these features complement RGB
 information by providing per-pixel spatial encodings, as illustrated in Figure 11. Notably,
 this is especially beneficial in large, flat regions of a scene, such as the buildings in Scene 3,
 where image matching based on RGB alone is the most ambiguous. Conversely, we find
 that highly textured scenes, such as Scene 2, do not benefit significantly from feature fusion,
 making the additional computational cost unjustified. Future work could explore a priori
 methods for determining where such features are likely to be most useful.
 We also demonstrated that a foundation model’s performance in an a priori AKNN
 classification task can be indicative of later gains in Gaussian splatting reconstruction,
 offering a relatively simple heuristic for down-selecting foundation models. Of the models
 we tested, we find that larger DINO models perform better on both AKNN classification
 and reconstruction tasks, and that DINOv3 lvd1689m performed the best in both tasks. We
 find that adding FiLM module to the foundation model embeddings to account for image-
 to-image feature variations significantly improves reconstruction results. Additionally, we
 demonstrate that reconstructed feature point clouds support zero-shot segmentation tasks
 (Figure A1 in the Appendix A), such as filtering buildings and vegetation.
 Our work has limitations. Incorporating features significantly increases the required
 computational cost. Runtime could potentially be reduced by lowering the dimensional-
 ity of the Gaussian features and relying more heavily on the MLP upsampling module;
 however, as discussed by Zhou et al. [10], this comes at the cost of reduced reconstruction
 quality. Second, the foundation model features lack a principled interpretation. In par-
 ticular, it remains unclear exactly what information they capture. Although we showed
 that they encode both class-specific properties (e.g., road versus vegetation) and spatial
 information, the extent to which each is represented, and what additional information may
 also be encoded, remains unknown. Future work could further investigate the types of
 information these features capture and how they might be leveraged to improve reconstruc-
 tion accuracy. We hope this work proves useful for future investigations into leveraging
 semantic content for 3D reconstruction from satellite imagery.

 Appendix A. Segmentation
 Appendix A.1. Backprojection Method
 Our AKNN experiments leverage ground truth segmentation maps for each image
 in the dataset, but segmentation maps were only provided for the nadir point-of-view
 (POV). We created per-image segmentation maps by reprojecting nadir maps into the view
 images’ POV. In particular, we reproject the nadir segmentation maps into each image by
 (1) constructing a mesh from the LiDAR points, (2) intersecting backprojected image rays
 with the mesh, (3) sampling the nadir segmentation at each intersection point. Figure A1
 provides an example of this reprojection for an image in our dataset. This allows us to
 measure the segmentation performance of our models on each image in the dataset.

 Figure A1. We reproject the nadir-view segmentation map (left) from each satellite image’s panchro-
 matic view image (middle) to use as ground truth (right) for the segmentation task. Colors in the
 segmentation maps correspond to different ground truth classes.
