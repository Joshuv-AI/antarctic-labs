Fine particulate matter and tobacco product exposure exacerbates metabolic syndrome-related colon cancer via regulating oxidative stress and tumor-associated macrophage interactions
Authors: Shenghua Zhang, Dongfang Meng, Haibo Zhang, Fei Pan, Yongsheng Gao, Jijin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) is closely associated with prolonged exposure to fine particulate matter (PM 2.5) and tobacco carcinogens, 4-methylnitrosamino-l-3-pyridyl-butanone (NNK). However, due to a lack of understanding of its intricate pathological processes, there is currently no approved clinical medication or successful treatment plan. Also, tobacco carcinogens and PM 2.5 are implicated in the progression of CRC by the onset of a metabolic disorder. Nonetheless, direct evidence connecting the metabolic disorder to NNK/PM 2.5-mediated CRC development has yet to be developed.MethodsWe investigated CRC cells treated with NNK/PM 2.5 to investigate the function of metabolic alterations in CRC (NNK/PM 2.5)-induced tumor growth. The results were verified using human CRC cancer samples, cell-derived xenografts, and online survival analysis. To clarify the mechanism of regulation of changes in metabolism, metabolic tracking, western blotting, massarray assay, real-time PCR, and reporter assays were conducted. SPSS version 20.0 or GraphPad Prism version 10.0 were used for all statistical analyses.ResultsOur research indicates that NNK/PM 2.5, elicit characteristics of metabolic syndrome, notably hyperglycemia, decrease in reactive oxygen species (ROS) contents, and a substantial upregulation of anti-oxidative stress-related gene expression, thereby facilitating the progression of colon cancer in a murine model. By increasing the expression of glucose transporter 3 (GLUT3) and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), NNK/PM 2.5 increases the uptake of glucose in tumor-associated macrophages. This leads to the increased expression of insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2), which in turn paracrinely hyper-activates the insulin receptor (IR) in colorectal cancer cells, facilitating its nuclear import. Through IR/NPM1 mediation, nuclear IR and nucleophosmin (NPM1) interact to activate the CD274 promoter and produce programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1). Inhibiting glycolysis, diminishing macrophages, or obstructing PD-L1 impedes NNK/PM 2.5-induced colorectal cancer growth. Analysis of patient samples and public databases reveals elevated expression of tumoral PD-L1 and phosphorylated insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor/insulin receptor (pIGF-1R/IR), as well as elevated levels of IGF2 and GLUT1 in tumor-associated macrophages, suggesting possible negative prognostic biomarkers for patients with colorectal cancer.ConclusionsOur results provided new insights into the mechanisms of intestinal cancer progresses by paracrine IR-PD-L1 signaling, which is aided by the deregulation of oxidative stress and macrophage communication brought on by smoking carcinogen-induced the metabolic syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40170-026-00442-5.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the predominant tumor of the digestive tract and poses a significant hazard to human health [1]. As a type of malignant tumor with a prevalent cause of tumor-related mortality, it impacts millions globally. An estimated 200,000 new cases of intestinal cancer are reported in China annually, accounting for 71–90% of all malignant tumors, according to the China Cancer Registry’s annual report [2, 3]. Despite the fact that the widespread use of early diagnostics has reduced the incidence and death rates, patients with regional or distant metastases have a poor prognosis due to the limited therapeutic efficacy [4]. The risk factors for CRC include a variety of lifestyle, genetic, and health-related factors. Among these factors, age, family history, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), obesity, dietary habits, genetic disorders, and physical inactivity is considered a significant high-risk factor for the onset and advancement of colorectal cancer [4, 5]. Recent data indicate that long-term polluted air, especially particulate matter 2.5 and cigarette smoking exposure are connected with an elevated chance of getting colon cancer and may also facilitate the advancement of the disease [6, 7]. Tobacco products has been shown to have several harmful effects that can promote the development and progression of colon cancer [7, 8]. Nonetheless, the causal relationship between smoking and colorectal cancer, along with the associated molecular pathways, remains little investigated. Prominent carcinogens included NNK and PM 2.5, have been demonstrated to facilitate multiple cancer forms, including colon, lung, liver, and stomach cancer [9]. They achieve this via altering the genes and epigenetic components of tumor suppressor and proto-oncogene genes, as well as by triggering oncogenic signaling pathways that ultimately promote tumor growth in certain cells. However, it is still unclear whether precise mechanisms lead to carcinogens-mediated CRC progression.

The advancement of cancer is an elaborate process that is controlled by the complex communication between cancer cells and the surrounding tissue in the tumor micro-environment (TMiE), as well as the elements in the tumor macroenvironment (TMaE) that are influenced by the host [10]. Tumor initiation and progression are facilitated by a variety of environmental factors that affect stromal cells in the TMiE and TMaE, such as dietary impacts and biological hazards [10, 11]. Carcinogens have been linked to the onset of metabolic syndrome, often termed insulin resistance syndrome [12, 13]. This syndrome includes at least three medical conditions: raised levels of glucose in the blood, increased serum triglyceride concentrations, decreased high-density lipoprotein levels, hypertension, and central obesity [14, 15]. The genesis and progression of several cancer types, including colorectal cancer, are linked to the metabolic disorder’s constituents, especially hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia [6, 16]. Changes in glucose and lipid metabolism have an impact on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), an important kind of stromal cell in the tumor microenvironment (TMiE) [17, 18]. Tumor-associated macrophages have a major impact on the development of cancer by interfering with anti-tumor immune responses and secreting growth factors, inflammatory cytokines, and proteases [17]. Therefore, it is theorized that increased levels of glucose and lipids in individuals with metabolic syndrome may impact the immune-modulating role of TAMs, potentially leading to the advancement of colorectal cancer. Nevertheless, the specific impacts and fundamental mechanisms of metabolic syndrome induced by carcinogens on the evolution of CRC are still mainly unexplored. Tumor cells and nearby stromal cells create immunological checkpoint proteins to prevent the host’s immune system from fighting the tumor [19]. The interaction between the PD-1 and its ligand, PD-L1, is the main factor affecting T-cell activity in TMiE [20]. PD-L1 facilitates the progression of cancer by promoting the development of immune evasion and cancer stem-like cell (CSC) characteristics [21, 22]. The transcription of PD-L1 is influenced by numerous cytokines and growth factors, such as IGF2, interleukins (ILs), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and epidermal growth factor (EGF). Recent research has shown that nucleophosmin (NPM1) activates the transcription of PD-L1 [23]. Its carcinogenic potential and the mechanism by which it contributes to the development of colorectal cancer are, nevertheless, poorly understood.

In addition to determining possible molecular and cellular targets to prevent the progression of carcinogens-mediated carcinoma of the colon, our current study seeks to clarify the mechanisms that connect carcinogens-induced metabolic syndrome to the development of colorectal cancer. This study shows that altered glucose metabolism in TAMs and hyperglycemia brought on by carcinogens work together to accelerate the development of CRC. This is achieved by inducing anti-oxidative stress in colorectal cancer cells and activating the IR-PD-L1 signaling cascade.

Materials and methods

Antibodies, reagents and PM 2.5 sample preparation

The subsequent antibodies were employed for western blotting, immunohistochemistry (IHC), or immunofluorescence (IF) assays: anti-GLUT1 (Cat#: abs171708, 1:1200 for immunoblotting; 1:220 for IF), anti-GLUT3 (Cat#: abs158839, 1:1000 for western blotting; 1:200 for IF), Na+/K+-ATPase (Cat#: abs172973, 1:1000 for western blotting), phospho-IGF-1R (Cat#: abs172369, 1:1200 for immunoblotting), IGF-1R (Cat#: abs119799, 1:1200 for immunoblotting), insulin receptor (Cat#: abs119646, 1:1000 for western blotting), insulin receptor related protein (Cat#: abs158895, 1:1200 for immunoblotting), IGF2 (Cat#: abs115963, 1:1000 for western blotting), NPM1 (Cat#: abs173435, Cat#: abs106184, 1:1200 for immunoblotting), PD-L1 (Cat#: abs171654, 1:1000 for immunoblotting), anti-KI67 (Cat#: abs145260, 1:120 for IHC) and anti-Nrf2 (Cat#: abs130481, 1:1200 for immunoblotting; 1:120 for IF) were purchased from Absin (Shanghai, China). Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) provided the HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (#A0208, 1:8000), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG(H + L) (#A0216, 1:8000), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (#A0423, 1:200), and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG(H + L) (#A0473, 1:200). The study made use of the following chemical inhibitors: TargetMol Chemicals Inc. (Shanghai, China) provided NNK (#T20533). We bought mecamylamine (#HY-B1395A) and propranolol (#HY-B0573B) from MedChemExpress (MCE). We purchased chloroquine (CQ, #HY-17589 A), an autophagy inhibitor, from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA). Sigma-Aldrich provided the compounds 2-NBDG (catalog number 72987), and 2DG (catalog number D6134). Unless otherwise noted, the small molecule inhibitors NNK were soluble in DMSO (#HY-Y0320, MedChemExpress).

The protocol of PM 2.5 sampling preparation and processing methods was performed in accordance with our previous reports [24]. In brief, quartz filter (8 cm ×10 cm, 2500QAT-UP, Pallflex Products, Putnam, CT, USA) was used to consecutively and weekly harvest PM 2.5 in Beijing and Xi’an, China (January-June 2016–2018) at a flow rate of 166 L/min. The collected PM 2.5 particle filters were stored in -80 °C. The particle mass was subjected to anhydrous alcohol and 1% HNO3 solution, and then dissolved in double distilled water. Next, the mass was sonicated for 48 h and concentrated by vacuum freeze-drying. The freeze-dried extraction was resuspended in double distilled water and centrifuged at 5000 rpm to kick the water-soluble matter off. Finally, the water-insoluble portion was suspended in phosphate buffer (GIBCO Corporation, Gaithers-burg, MD, USA) for the following experiments.

Cells and culture

Human colorectal cancer cell lines (HCT116, Caco-2, SW480, SNU-C2A, and HT-29) were acquired from the ATCC. The cultivated CRC cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin-streptomycin. Dr. Yuanyuan Li from the College of Health Industries at Chongqing University of Education graciously provided the THP-1 cells. THP-1 cells were grown in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. The cells were sustained at an approximate temperature of 37 °C in an atmosphere with humidity containing 5% carbon dioxide. The concentrations of glucose in the DMEM and RPMI 1640 solution were 4.5 g/L (25 mM) and 2 g/L (11 mM), respectively. We added a vehicle (DMSO), NNK/PM 2.5, or other inhibitors to the medium containing 2 g/L glucose (referred to as the Gstd condition) unless otherwise noted. All cell lines were verified using short tandem repeat (STR) testing in addition to standard mycoplasma contamination evaluations. In 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the tibias and femurs. The remaining bone marrow cells were cultivated in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), antibiotics, and 10% L929 conditioned media to promote differentiation after the red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using a 1× RBC lysis buffer (catalog number abs9101, Absin). For ten days during the maturation phase, BMDMs were stimulated twice daily with NNK (1 µM) and PM 2.5 (5 µg/mL).

Cell proliferation assay

A density of 5000 cells per well was used to seed the cells in 96-well plates. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, cell viability was evaluated using a CCK-8 Kit (#CK04, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan). Following treatment, cells were exposed to 10 µL/well of CCK-8 working solution for an hour. A MD SpectraMax M5 Microplate Reader (MD, USA) was used to quantify the cells’ absorbance at 450 nm (OD450). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the EdU test was performed using the Meilun EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (#MA0424-1, Meilunbio) to evaluate the proliferation potential of cancer cells.

Transwell analysis

For migratory assessment, 5 × 104 cells were seeded into Transwell plates (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark) with 250 µL of serum-free fresh media. The complete media was introduced into the lower chamber. Following treatments, the migrating cells were fixed with Zenker-formol fixative after 24 h, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and enumerated using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan). The upper chamber was initially coated with Matrigel (Corning, New York, USA), after which 1 × 105 cancer cells were incubated in the upper chamber and cultured with 200 µL of serum-free medium, while 700 µL of full culture medium was added to the lower chamber. After receiving treatment for 24 h, the invasive cancer cells were frozen, stained, and counted under a Leica microscope.

Real time-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

After being inoculated onto 6-well plates, the CRC cells were cultured for a full day. Cells were washed with PBS after treatments. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, RNA was then extracted using TRNzol Universal solution (#DP424, TIANGEN, China). Then, using the Hieff® qPCR TaqMan Probe Master Mix (#11205ES, Yeasen, China), 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed to create cDNA. The Hifair® III One Step RT-qPCR SYBR Green Kit (#11143ES, Yeasen, China) was then used to run RT-qPCR on the ABI7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The ΔΔCT approach was used to evaluate the relative expression levels of each targeted gene. GAPDH served as an internal regulator.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay

The cancer cells were seeded into six-well plates and grown until they achieved 80% confluence. After the treatments, the production of ROS in the entire cell population was determined by 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (10 µM) labeling (DCFH-DA, #HY-D0940, MedChemExpress). To detect mitochondrial ROS, the MitoSOX Red (#HY-D1055, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was employed as directed by the manufacturer. After every treatment, cells cultured in 6-well plates were stained with 1 µM of MitoSOX Red solution for 30 min at 37 °C. After a final PBS wash, the cells were seen under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Corporation, Japan) to assess the production of total and mitochondrial ROS. Image-Pro Plus6.0 from Media Cybernetics was used to analyze the mean fluorescence intensity.

Lipid peroxidation and biochemical parameters analysis

C11-BODIPY 581/591 probe (#HY-D1301, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China,) was used to evaluate the lipid peroxidation of cancer cells. Following a one-hour incubation period at 37 °C with 50 µM working solution, the cells were meticulously washed three times with PBS. Ultimately, using a fluorescence microscope, cells were seen and photographed. It was possible to quantify the relative lipid peroxidation level. The levels of glutathione (GSH) in the cell lysates were measured using a total glutathione cell-based detection kit (#600360-96, Amyjet Scientific, China) in compliance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Total glutathione levels were determined using the OD value at 412 nm. Malondialdehyde (MDA) was detected in cancer cells using a Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (#RK05818, Abconal, Wuhan, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Ultimately, a microplate reader was used to measure the MDA levels at 532 nm. The iron concentrations were determined by following the manufacturer’s instructions and utilizing an Iron Assay Kit (#ab83366, Abcam, USA).

Western blot assay

Protein samples were extracted from cells using the RIPA buffer (#G2002, Servicebio) enhanced with a combination of phosphatase and protease inhibitors (#P1048, Beyotime). After centrifuging the extracts for 20 min at 12,000 × g, the supernatant was collected. Equal amounts of extracted proteins were then separated using 10%-12% SDS-PAGE. After the separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, they were blocked for one and a half hours at room temperature using Tris-buffered saline (TBS) and 5% skim milk with 0.1% Tween-20 (#GC204002, Servicebio). Primary antibodies were then added, and the membranes were kept at 4 °C for the entire night. The next day, we performed five TBST membrane washes. Subsequently, the membranes were treated for two hours at room temperature with matching secondary antibodies. Ultimately, an ECL chemiluminescent detection system (#P0018FM, Beyotime) was used to visualize the protein bands on the membrane, and ImageJ (version 1.53, NIH) was used for analysis.

Circulating lipid contents and blood pressure assay

After a 4-hour or 12-hour fast, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of either 0.55 U/kg BW insulin or 2.25 g/kg BW glucose (12–14 h). These injections were administered for the purpose of testing glucose or insulin tolerance, respectively. By drawing blood from the tip of the tail and using a Roche blood glucose test strip, the animal’s blood sugar levels were measured. A blood glucose meter was used to assess the fasting and basal glucose levels after an overnight period during which no food was consumed. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, a veterinary hematology analyzer (Fujifilm) was used to measure the blood’s triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein levels. The BP-1000 Blood Pressure Analysis System was used to test the blood pressure of mice utilizing the noninvasive tail-cuff method.

Plasma insulin contents assay

The mouse insulin ValukineTM ELISA Kit (Ultrasensitive) (#VAL630, Valukine Bio-Techne, China) was used to measure the plasma insulin levels in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Body metabolism and body composition assay

We evaluated variations in the respiratory exchange ratio and energy expenditure using PhenoMaster-TSE Systems. The mice were individually acclimated and given unrestricted access to food and water in a home cage. For three days, the mice were kept in their enclosures. In order to generate regression plots, hourly plots of RER and EE, bar graphs of RER (light and dark phases), and statistical analysis results, average data were acquired using the CalR application (https://calrapp.org/). These data were analyzed using both the mass effect and the group effect. Bone mineral content (BMC), bone mass density (BMD), lean mass, and fat mass were measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) using the Inalyzer DEXA system (Medikors, Seongnam-si, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). During the body scan, isoflurane was used to knock the mice out.

18 F-FDG-labled glucose uptake assay

We employed hybrid imaging using a nanoScan PET/MRI system and assessed the amount of glucose absorbed in the mice’s colon tissues. To reduce movement distortions before imaging, the mice were sedated and kept at 1%-1.5% isoflurane. They were kept at a constant 33–34 °C internal body temperature with a Multicell heating system. For static PET imaging, an average of 9.5 ± 0.8 MBq of FDG in 0.2 ml was employed after intravenous administration via the tail vein. T1-weighted MR images were obtained using a three-dimensional gradient echo sequence with the following settings: repeated repetition time (TR) of 25 ms, effective echo time (TE_eff) of 3.4 ms, field of view (FOV) of 64 mm, and 128 × 128 matrix size. With all corrections enabled, the Tera-Tomo 3D engine was used to rebuild the PET images in full detector mode. With eight rounds of strong regularization, the reconstruction procedure produced voxels with a size of 0.5 mm. Using the InterView Fusion software (version 3.03), a three-dimensional volume of interest (VOI) for organs and tumors was constructed using the anatomical outlines from MR imaging. The SUVs were computed for each VOI, with spherical shapes created for the sites of muscles and tumors and VOIs set at a constant diameter of 2 mm. The data was quantified using the standard uptake value ratio (SUVR), which is calculated by dividing the SUV of the volume of VOI in the colon tumor location by the SUV of the VOIs in the muscle region.

Animal experiments design

The protocol used in the animal tests was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute, Shandong First Medical University, and Shandong Academy of Medical Sciences in Jinan, Shandong PR, China. We bought C57BL/6 mice from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing) and FVB/N mice from Shulaibao Biotechnology (Wuhan). The mice were cared for in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. The mice were kept in a controlled environment devoid of any pollutants that can cause disease. They were maintained in a light-dark cycle with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness at a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C. Food and drink were also freely available to them. Mice were randomly grouped for every trial carried out on live animals. Bias-free monitoring was used to track the development of metastatic tumors as well as the growth of tumors. There was no statistical determination of the sample size for animal trials.

For the animal experiment, the NNK and PM 2.5 were dissolved in DMSO and subsequently diluted in corn oil. The mice were given a treatment volume of 100 µL and a final dose of 10 µg/mL of PM 2.5 and 3 µmol of NNK. PBS was used to dissolve 2DG. A final dose of 500 mg/kg body weight (BW) was given, and each mouse received a treatment volume of 100–150 µL depending on their BW. The neutral liposome solution (final dose: 0.7 mg per mouse; treatment volume: 100 µL per animal) was used to dissolve the clodronate liposome. To obtain a final dose of 100 µg per mouse, the anti-PD-L1 antibody was dissolved in saline and administered in a treatment volume of 100 µL per mouse. Male mice of the FVB/N and C57BL/6 strains, aged 8 weeks, were administered either a vehicle consisting of 5% DMSO in corn oil or NNK/PM 2.5 through via oral ingestion two times a week for a maximum of 20 weeks in order to monitor the progression of tumors and changes in metabolism produced by NNK/PM 2.5. Male C57BL/6 mice, eight weeks old, were administered NNK/PM 2.5 or Veh twice a week for 17 weeks in the subcutaneous tumor models. Subsequently, 100 µL of saline containing 30% Matrigel was used to inject 1 × 106 cells of HCT116-Luc mice under their skin. Following that, these mice were treated with either Veh or NNK/PM 2.5 for 4 additional weeks. About a week after detectable tumors with a minimum size of 50 mm3 formed, this treatment was started. We followed the ethical recommendation that subcutaneous tumors must not have a diameter larger than 15 to 17 mm or be smaller than 10% of the mouse’s body weight, or less than 1,900 mm3.

To create orthotopic tumor models via intracecal injection, vaporized isoflurane was first used to anesthetize the mice. Following this, a surgical incision was created on the wall of the upper abdomen to facilitate the removal of the cecum. Next, a solution containing 50 µl of PBS and either 1 × 106 transfected or wild-type HCT116 cells was carefully inserted between the several layers of the cecal wall’s muscularis and mucosa. After the injection, the abdominal cavity was sealed using autoclips and surgical sutures, and the cecum was replaced. Subcutaneous tumor models were created by co-injecting BMDMs and HCT116 cells into the right flank of 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice. 1 × 106 HCT116 cells and 2 × 105 BMDMs suspended in 200 µL of PBS with 25% Matrigel were injected into each animal. BMDMs and their sublines were exposed to either the vehicle or a mixture of 1 µM NNK and 5 µg/mL PM 2.5 for ten days prior to the co-treatment. To determine the tumor volume, the short and long diameters of the tumors were measured with a calliper. The formula (long diameter×short diameter2)/2 can be used to get the tumor volume. Every two days, the tumor’s volume was measured, and its dimensions were taken with the least amount of bias possible. Tumor burden was calculated using the formula tumor burden = N×V, which involved multiplying the average number of tumours (N) by the average volume of tumours (V). To summarize, measurements were taken of the number and size of tumors in five uniformly spaced sections of each colon to determine the total number of tumors and their overall impact. One month following the injection of cells, 10% of the mice’s body weight, a tumor size greater than 1,900 mm3, or the animals’ descent into a condition of moribundity were the reasons for their death.

Histopathology assay

For H&E staining, all tissue was preserved for 24 h at 4 °C in zenker-formol fixative. After that, the tissues underwent sectioning, embedding, immersion, and dehydration. After xylene was used to deparaffinize the Sect. (5 μm thick), gradient alcohol hydration was performed. After that, slides were stained for five to ten minutes using a H&E staining solution (#ab245880, Abcam, China). Deionized water was used to rinse away any excess stain, and then eosin staining was applied for 30 to 2 min. For the IHC test, tumor slices (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized in a series of xylene and rehydrated using a series of decreasing ethanol concentrations. They were then heated in a 0.01 M sodium citrate solution for antigen retrieval and treated in 3% H2O2 for 25 min for endogenous peroxidase blocking. The tumor sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (#10711454001, Roche) for 45 min, and then incubated with corresponding antibodies (1:300) throughout the entire night at 4 °C. The following day, the tumor slices were cleaned and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for an hour at room temperature. Ultimately, the sample sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and stained using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen kit (#11718096001, Roche). Finally, a light microscope was used to detect all positive staining from immunohistochemistry and histological inspection.

Immunofluorescent (IF) assay

Following medication administration, the cells were triple-washed in PBS, fixed for 15 min in zenker-formol fixative, and blocked for 45 min with 5% goat serum (#12352203, Merck) containing 0.5% Triton X100 (#85112, Thermo Scientific™). After that, the cells were treated for an entire night at 4 °C with a 1:200 dilution of the anti-4-HNE (4-hydroxynonenal) antibody. The cells were then washed with PBS and treated for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark with the Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) secondary antibody, which was labeled with Alexa Fluor 488. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (#1155, BioFroxx) following PBS washing. Ultimately, a fluorescent microscope was used to see and photograph the cell samples. Tumor tissues were prepared by blocking frozen 5-µm-thick slices of tumor tissue in 5% goat serum (#12352203, Merck) with 0.5% Triton X100 (#BO016, BIOISCO) in saline for 45 min. After that, the primary antibodies were incubated for a full night at 4 °C. After being washed with PBS, the slices were incubated with Alexa Fluor-labeled secondary antibodies for an hour at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI solution (#LM80001S, LMAI Bio) for ten minutes at room temperature. Images were immediately captured with a Japanese Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

Primary cells culture and isolation of colon-derived macrophages

After the tumor-filled colons of C57BL/6 mice were surgically removed, the colons were then sliced into tiny pieces using surgical scissors. This was done in order to differentiate orthotopic transplant tumors from CRC cells. The mouse tumor dissociation kit was used to further fragment the pieces of colon tumor into separate cells in accordance with the manufacturer’s suggested methods. Individual cells were either prepared for RNA extraction or cultured in RPMI 1640 media with 10% FBS and antibiotics for additional research. The remaining non-tumor portions of the colon were separated into individual cells using the mouse colon dissociation kit (#130-095-927, Miltenyi), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Red blood cells (RBCs) were destroyed by exposing cell pellets to a solution called RBC lysis buffer. A 1:50 dilution of TruStain fcXTM (#101320, BioLegend) in FACS buffer (PBS containing 1% BSA, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.05% sodium azide) was applied to the separated cells after they had been placed on ice for 15 min. The cells were then labeled for 30 min at a low temperature using allophycocyanin (APC)/cyanine7 (Cy7)-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 (#103116, BioLegend, at a ratio of 1:100) and phycoerythrin (PE)/Cy7-conjugated anti-mouse F4/80 (cat. no. 123114, BioLegend, at a ratio of 1:100) antibodies. Following two washes with FACS buffer, the cells were sorted using a FACS flow cytometer. Individual cells were either prepped for RNA or conditioned media (CM) in order to perform IGF2 ELISA.

Energy metabolism measurements

Using a Seahorse XF Extracellular Flux Analyser (Seahorse Bioscience) and the manufacturer’s instructions, the rates of extracellular acidification (ECAR) and mitochondrial oxygen consumption (OCR) in cells were measured.

Colon samples metabolomics

There were only slight changes made to the instrumental analysis and sample preparation protocols from a prior investigation. A pre-cooled Biospec BioPulverizer was used to smash the colon tissue. Upon reaching a final 25 mg/mL concentration, the tissue metabolite was extracted over the course of 24 h at a temperature of -80 °C using 80% methyl alcohol. After centrifuging at 17,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was examined using a Vanquish UPLC system connected to a Thermo Fisher Scientific Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer. The column used in the experiment was a 20 × 4.6 mm SeQuant Zic-pHILIC guard column made by MilliporeSigma in Burlington, Massachusetts, USA. Mobile phase A was composed of 10 millimolars of ammonium carbonate and 0.05% ammonium hydroxide. The concentration of acetonitrile in mobile phase B was 100%. The temperature of the column was 30 °C. The gradient condition was that between 0 and 13 min, the concentration of mobile phase B decreased from 80% to 20%. The mobile phase B concentration stays at 20% for the next 13 to 14 min. There was a negative number in the ESI mode. The mass spectrometer was set up with a scan range of 60 to 910 m/z. The mass resolution was set at 70,000 and the AGC objective was set at 1 × 106, Five microliters (µL) of the sample were added. The EL-Maven program was utilized to identify the metabolite peaks by means of a comparison with an internal, standard-based metabolite library. After normalizing the median value of the total metabolite peak area, additional statistical analysis was conducted.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

Promega (Addgene Cat#: 107003) was the source of the pGL3-promoter vector. Using the jetPRIME® transfection reagent, the cells were uniformly divided into 24 wells and then exposed to 0.6 µg of vectors per well. Next, to standardize the transfection efficiency, 10 nanogrammes of pRL-Tk vectors per well were co-transfected. After transfection for 12 h, serum-free media was added to the medium and left for another 12 h. For a duration of 24 h, cells containing foreign genetic material were subjected to either IGF2 or conditioned media derived from macrophages. The Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (#E1910; Promega) was then used to measure the activity of luciferase, an enzyme that was utilized as a marker, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bioinformatics analysis

We obtained datasets from the The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA). From these datasets, we manually extracted gene expression levels and clinical data. Examination of STRING-related protein interactions A network analysis was performed utilizing 55 genes that exhibited substantial overexpression in the NPM1(high) group (Fc ≥ 2; and P < 0.05) in order to identify the NPM1-related network. Prognosis-associated genes were defined as those that appeared in at least two prognostic gene profiles for both colon and rectal cancer. Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves were used to analyze the survival rates of colon cancer patients. For the purpose of performing survival analysis, the GLUT1(high) and GLUT1(low) groups as well as the GLUT3(high) and GLUT3(low) groups were determined using the median value of the data. A log-rank test was used to determine the data’ statistical significance. The GSEA user guide’s instructions were followed when using the GSEA program (version 4.3.2) to conduct GSEA.

Statistical analysis

The mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three or more independent experiments was used to show the values. To investigate the differences between two groups, the Student’s t-test was utilized. Tukey’s post-hoc test was used after a one-way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups. GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, USA) was used for statistical analysis. A difference was considered statistically significant if the P value was less than 0.05.

NNK/PM 2.5 exposure promotes dysregulated metabolism function and potential colon cancer progression

To determine if metabolic syndrome occurs during carcinogen-induced colon tumorigenesis, we examined FVB mice given NNK/PM 2.5 for 28 weeks, following a protocol for colorectal cancer development (Fig. 1A). Fasting plasma glucose levels of 100 mg/dL or higher, triglyceride levels of 150 mg/dL or higher, decreased HDL cholesterol, elevated blood pressure, and central adiposity are usually required to diagnose metabolic disorder. Analysis of blood samples taken at regular intervals during NNK/PM 2.5 exposure showed temporal variations in HDL, TG, and glucose levels (Fig. 1B, C). NNK/PM 2.5 exposure reduced HDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, and glucose to metabolic syndrome levels (Fig. 1B, C). As seen in Figs. 1D and E, prolonged NNK/PM 2.5 exposure lowered glucose tolerance and raised insulin resistance. However, the blood insulin levels did not significantly change (Fig. 1F). As revealed in Figs. 1G and H, NNK/PM 2.5-exposed mice had lower BW and SBP than controls.

We also examined if the metabolic disorders observed in NNK/PM 2.5-exposed FVB mice resulted from extended exposure to NNK/PM 2.5. To examine this, we assessed the effects of NNK/PM 2.5 administration on mice possessing a C57BL/6 genetic lineage. The metabolic syndrome generated by NNK/PM 2.5 and the resulting alterations in SBP and BW, as shown in Fig. 1A-H in FVB mice, were also detected in C57BL/6 mice after being exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 for 20 weeks, as depicted in Fig. 1I-P. As expected, C57BL/6 mice that were exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 for a duration of 20 weeks had an elevated and impaired metabolic progression. Thus, prolonged exposure to NNK/PM 2.5 leads to the development of metabolic syndrome in rodents. Subsequently, we examined whether the increases in blood glucose or TG levels caused by NNK/PM 2.5 exposure contribute to the advancement of CRC. We utilized a precise colon cancer animal model by administering HCT116 cells subcutaneously in C57BL/6 mice. The mice who developed metabolic syndrome as a result of exposure to NNK/PM 2.5 for 20 weeks were injected with luciferase-labeled CRC cells (HCT116-Luc) under the skin and subsequently treated to either a control substance or NNK/PM 2.5 for 8 weeks (Fig. 1Q). Our study revealed a significant increase in the growth of HCT116-Luc tumors (HCT116-Luc-sc) that were implanted subcutaneously in mice exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 compared to mice treated with DMSO (Fig. 1R). The data indicate that metabolic changes generated by NNK/PM 2.5, specifically hyperglycemia, may worsen the course of CRC. Additionally, due to the extremely rapid metabolism of NNK in the human body after smoking (mainly converted into NNAL by carbonyl reductase), there is almost no original NNK in the plasma. Currently, the mainstream methods do not routinely detect free NNK in the blood of smokers. After NNK entered the circulation, the main metabolites were NNAL and glucuronide conjugates (total NNAL). Therefore, as indicated in Supplementary Tables 1 & 2, we further measured the content of NNAL in the plasma of clinical patients. Pearson correlation analysis showed that plasma total NNAL, as a stable blood biomarker of NNK tobacco carcinogen exposure, had no significant correlation with age (r = 0.086, P = 0.472). Plasma NNAL exhibited strong positive correlations with fasting blood glucose, serum triglyceride, CRP, lymph node metastasis rate and advanced TNM stage (all P < 0.001), and a strong negative correlation with HDL-C (r = − 0.712, P < 0.001). Notably, NNAL showed very strong positive correlations with TAMs GLUT1, TAMs IGF2, tumor pIGF-1R/IR and PD-L1 expression (r ranged from 0.817 to 0.856, all P < 0.001). These results further confirm that blood NNAL level is closely associated with metabolic disturbance, systemic inflammation, macrophage glucose metabolic reprogramming, IGF2 paracrine activation and PD-L1-mediated immune escape in colorectal cancer patients, supporting that NNAL can serve as a reliable peripheral blood surrogate biomarker for evaluating NNK exposure and CRC progression risk.

NNK/PM 2.5 facilitate CRC progression in response to glucose exposure

Our next goal was to distinguish NNK/PM 2.5-induced tumor activity from metabolic syndrome, which can lead to oncogenic signal transductions and immune function alterations that can affect cancer formation. NNK/PM 2.5 was given to orthotopic HCT116-Luc cell tumor (HCT116-Luc-Ortho) mice for six weeks. Mouse exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 were given a high-carbohydrate or high-fat diet to raise their blood glucose or triglycerides (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Mice fed a high-carbohydrate or high-fat diet for eight weeks had elevated blood glucose and TG levels (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). Similar values were found in NNK/PM 2.5-induced metabolic syndrome animals (Fig. 1J, K). Long-term high-fat diets may increase insulin production, causing diabetes and hepatic insulin resistance. Diabetes risk increases with a diet high in carbohydrates, which make up over 70% of calories. Mice fed a high-fat or carbohydrate diet for eight weeks have reduced glucose metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Due to metabolic changes, long-term HCD or HFD consumption may elevate glucose or cholesterol levels. We next performed metabolic phenotyping of mice treated with HCD or HFD therapy without NNK/PM 2.5 to assess how the diets affected metabolic status regulation. Our research showed that mice on an HFD had no changes in food intake but significantly increased body weight (BW) and inguinal and epididymal white adipose tissue (iWAT and eWAT) compared to NCD mice. DEXA study showed that mice fed a high-fat diet had less lean tissue and more fat tissue than mice fed a normal chow diet (NCD) (Supplementary Fig. 1I). HCD-fed mice had similar diet consumption, body weight, and iWAT and eWAT masses to NCD-fed mice (Supplementary Fig. 1F-H). The results of the DEXA analysis showed that mice fed a high-fat diet had smaller amounts of lean tissue and higher amounts of fat tissue compared to mice fed a normal chow diet (Supplementary Fig. 1I). BMD and BMC were similar in mice fed an HCD or HFD compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 1J). We employed a complete animal monitoring system to measure RER and EE to evaluate energy regulation. In the light phase, mice fed a conventional chow diet, high-carbohydrate diet, or high-fat diet exhibited RER values of 0.77 ± 0.06, 0.82 ± 0.09, and 0.71 ± 0.04, respectively. During the dark phase, the RER values were 0.84 ± 0.07, 0.88 ± 0.03, and 0.73 ± 0.04 in order (Supplementary Fig. 1K, L). The results show that carbohydrates are the predominant energy source during the dark period, whereas lipids and carbs create energy during the light phase. It is also expected that HFD-fed mice will use fat as an energy source, while HCD-fed animals will use carbohydrates. We also found that animals fed an HCD or HFD had similar glucose clearance, insulin sensitivity, insulin production, and fasting blood glucose levels to mice fed an NCD (Supplementary Fig. 1M-P). Thus, mice fed a high-carbohydrate or high-fat diet may have higher blood glucose or TG without metabolic changes due to excessive nutrient ingestion.

We tracked primary and metastatic orthotopic HCT116-Luc tumors (HCT116-Luc-Ortho) in mice using bioluminescence imaging (BLI). We found that HCD feeding and NNK/PM 2.5 exposure increased tumor growth in distant and major organs. Therefore, NNK/PM 2.5 exposure and increased glucose consumption may induce colorectal cancer.

NNK/PM 2.5-treated tumor-associated macrophages promotes CRC progression in response to glucose exposure

We studied colon tissues from four groups: HCT116-Luc-Ortho-DMSO/HCD, HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD, HCT116-Luc-Ortho-DMSO/HFD, and HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HFD to determine how high glucose levels affect NNK/PM 2.5-induced colorectal cancer progression. These approaches were used to assess the tumor activity of CRC cells from initial HCT116-Luc-Ortho cultures (Fig. 3A). HCT116-Luc-Ortho-DMSO/HCD CRC cells formed colonies and spheres better than HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD cells. Despite this, the two groups moved similarly (Fig. 3B). The restricted dilution assay demonstrated that HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD CRC cells were more tumorigenic than those from DMSO/HCD (Fig. 3C). We then examined how NNK/PM 2.5 exposure affected colorectal cancer cells. Two CRC cell lines, HCT116 and SW480, were treated with DMSO or NNK/PM 2.5 for 8 weeks. The investigation observed no differences in these cells’ colony, sphere, and migration abilities (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Thus, NNK/PM 2.5 exposure may promote colorectal cancer via affecting stromal cells rather than CRC cells. HCT116-Luc-Ortho cells were immunofluorescently examined for CD45 + leukocytes and F4/80 + macrophages. This study examined stromal cells that may produce NNK/PM 2.5-induced CRC in mice given an HCD. The HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD group had more CD45 + leukocytes, including F4/80 + macrophages, than the DMSO/HCD group (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 3B). Arg1 + M2-type macrophages (Arg1+/F4/80+) were substantially more abundant in the HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD group than in the HCT116-Luc-Ortho-DMSO/HCD group (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 3B). We evaluated fluorescence-activated cell sorting-separated colorectal cancer cells and macrophages from tumors. Compared to control macrophages from HCT116-Luc-Ortho-DMSO/HCD, colon macrophages from HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD upregulated genes encoding M2 markers and anti-inflammatory mediators (Fig. 3E). We also found that mouse bone marrow-derived monocytes (BMDMs) and THP-1 human monocytes exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 had higher levels of M2-specific markers CD206 and Arg1, but not iNOS, compared to their controls exposed to DMSO for the same duration (Supplementary Fig. 3C). The HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HFD group did not show NNK/PM 2.5-induced alterations (Fig. 3D). Thus, NNK/PM 2.5-induced macrophage infiltration and M2-like pro-tumoral features may aid colorectal cancer (CRC) development, especially in glucose-rich environments.

We found that NNK/PM 2.5 exposure in glucose-rich environments affects macrophage pro-tumoral activities. We tested THP-1 and BMDMs with and without glucose administration to see how NNK/PM 2.5 affected them. THP-1 and BMDMs were grown in glucose-varying mediums. Standard culture media for THP-1 and BMDMs contains 2 g/L glucose (11 mM, Gstd), which matches high blood glucose levels in NNK/PM 2.5-induced metabolic syndrome in FVB and C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 1). THP-1s and BMDMs had limited viability in glucose-free culture medium for only 7 days, so they were grown in media supplemented with glucose at 0.8 g/L (4.4 mM, Glow) or 10 g/L (55 mM, Ghigh), mimicking suboptimal or diabetic blood glucose levels. We obtained conditioned media (CM) from THP-1 cells exposed to DMSO or NNK/PM 2.5 under Glow, Gstd, or Ghigh conditions and gave it to HCT116 cells. The ability of HCT116 cells to produce colonies and spheres was considerably better when exposed to THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/Ghigh, PM 2.5, or PM 2.5/Glow than when incubated with DMSO, or Glow (Fig. 3F). BMDM/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/Ghigh enhanced CRC cell tumor activity compared to other BMDM-derived conditioned media (Supplementary Fig. 4A). The tumor activity of SW480, HCT116, and HT-29 cells were significantly reduced when grown with conditioned media from THP-1 or BMDM cells previously exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 and 2DG. Compared to THP-1/CM/NNK/PM 2.5 or BMDM/CM/NNK/PM 2.5, this reduction was found (Fig. 3G, Supplementary Fig. 4B). We tested animals with HCT116-Luc-Ortho tumors. These mice were given DMSO or NNK/PM 2.5 with HCD. Clodronate, a liposomal macrophage-depleting drug, was also used. This experiment examined how these chemicals affected mice (Fig. 3H). Clodronate injection inhibited tumor growth and spread in the HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD group, according to BLI (Fig. 3I) and histology (Fig. 3J). Compared to DMSO/HCD, the group treated with clodronate, HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD, showed a significantly higher overall survival rate (Fig. 3K). Since high glucose levels promote tumor growth and colorectal cancer progression, we investigated how excessive glucose affects CRC cell oxidative stress. Under high glucose conditions, NNK/PM 2.5-treated tumor-associated macrophages increased tumor growth and reduced ROS levels while increasing anti-oxidative stress gene expression in the THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5 and BMDM/CM-NNK/PM 2.5 groups (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). We then evaluated if the exposure to NNK/PM 2.5 affected the way that TAMs metabolized glucose. The glycolysis and TCA cycle metabolites in the colons of NNK/PM 2.5-exposed animals did not differ significantly from those of control mice, according to an LC/MS-based global metabolomics investigation of colon tissues from FVB mice (Fig. 4E, F). However, macrophages from mice exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 had significantly higher rates of oxygen consumption (OCR) and extracellular acidification (ECAR) than macrophages from control animals when glycolytic and oxidative fluxes were studied using Seahorse technology (Fig. 4G). We also found that THP-1 cells directly exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 produced higher 2-NBDG, OCR, ECAR, and ATP (Supplementary Fig. 7A-C). NNK/PM 2.5 did not affect HCT116 and SW480 cells’ metabolic characteristics, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 7D-F. Results suggest that NNK/PM 2.5 stimulates colon cell glucose uptake, especially TAMs. To determine how NNK/PM 2.5 enhances glucose use in TAMs, we examined the effects of NNK and PM 2.5 on macrophage GLUT expression. After three days of PM 2.5 treatment, THP-1 cells exhibited increased GLUT1 and GLUT3 protein expression, even at doses as low as 1.25 µg/mL. At 5 µg/mL concentration, the greatest increase was seen (Fig. 4H). However, GLUT1 and GLUT3 expression levels did not alter significantly after three days of exposure to NNK (1 µM) (Fig. 4I). The expression of SLC2A1 and SLC2A3 in PM 2.5-exposed THP-1 cells increased with time (Fig. 4J). However, THP-1 cells treated with NNK (1 µM) did not show any such increases. After 12 h of PM 2.5 exposure, but not NNK, THP-1 membranes had significantly increased GLUT1 and GLUT3 protein levels (Fig. 4K). Compared to single exposure, NNK/PM 2.5 increased MEM fraction glucose transporter levels and 2-NBDG absorption (Fig. 4L and M). PM 2.5 binds to the AhR, while NNK binds to the nAChR and β-adrenergic receptor. Pretreatment of THP-1 cells with β-AR (propranolol; P), nAChR (mecamylamine; M), or AhR (SR1; S) inhibitors efficiently reduced glucose uptake and GLUT1 and GLUT3 expression produced by NNK/PM 2.5 in WCL and MEM fractions (Fig. 4N, O). This suggests that the NNK/nAchR and PM 2.5/AhR signaling pathways work together to increase macrophage glucose uptake. GLUT1 and GLUT3 protein expression and migration increase tumor development.

NNK/PM 2.5 promote colon cancer progression through paracrine activation of insulin receptor in CRC cells

We investigated how NNK/PM 2.5-enhanced glucose absorption affects macrophage tumor-promoting activity to determine how growth factors and cytokines affect cancer-stroma communication. THP-1 cells treated to DMSO or NNK/PM 2.5 under varied glucose settings (Glow, Gstd, or Ghigh) and HCT116 cells exposed to their conditioned medium were employed. The activation patterns of receptor tyrosine kinases linked with cancer cell activities were examined in HCT116 cells. THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/Ghigh caused the highest IGF-1R/IR phosphorylation in HCT116 cells compared to THP-1/CM-DMSO, THP-1/CM-DMSO/Ghigh, THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/Glow, or THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5 (Fig. 5A). HCT116 cells exposed to THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/Ghigh, PM 2.5, or PM 2.5/Glow had higher IGF-1R/IR phosphorylation than cells exposed to DMSO, or Glow (Fig. 5B). We evaluated colorectal cancer cells cultivated with conditioned media from bone marrow-derived macrophages exposed to DMSO or NNK under Glow, Gstd, or Ghigh conditions. CRC cells grown with BMDM-conditioned medium performed similarly (Supplementary Fig. 8A). In HCT116 cells treated to THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/Ghigh, immunoprecipitation analysis showed that IR phosphorylation was higher than IGF-1R phosphorylation (Fig. 5C). These data suggest that NNK/PM 2.5-exposed macrophages and glucose supply activate IR in colorectal cancer cells. Next, we checked NNK/PM 2.5-exposed macrophages for insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor/insulin receptor ligands. In THP-1 cells treated with NNK/PM 2.5, qPCR showed a substantial increase in IGF2 mRNA expression (Fig. 5D). Immunoblotting and immunofluorescence showed that NNK/PM 2.5-treated THP-1 cells and BMDMs had elevated IGF2 protein (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 8B, C). The NNK/PM 2.5-induced transcriptional increase of IGF2 expression was greater in Ghigh than Gstd or Glow. However, 2DG reduced the effects (Fig. 5D, E). Combined medium from THP-1 cells exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 and an IGF2-neutralizing antibody failed to phosphorylate IGF-1R/IR in grown cancer cells (Fig. 5F, G). IGF-2 from NNK/PM 2.5-stimulated macrophages stimulates IGF-1R/IR in colorectal cancer cells via a paracrine mechanism. In mice with HCT116-Luc-sc tumors, IGF2 transcripts, IGF-1R/IR phosphorylation, and IGF2-expressing macrophages increased significantly. These studies were done on mice that developed metabolic syndrome after chronic NNK/PM 2.5 exposure. We examined HCT116-Luc-sc tumors in mice with NNK/PM 2.5-induced metabolic syndrome to determine if TAMs transport IGF2 to CRC cells. According to the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay on HCT116-Luc-sc-derived cells’ conditioned media, NNK/PM 2.5-exposed tumors’ CD45+/F4/80 + macrophages produced more insulin-like growth factor 2 than DMSO-exposed tumors. Non-immune cells (CD45-/F4/80-) and F4/80-immune cells (CD45+/F4/80-) produced similar amounts of IGF2 to their control cells. NNK/PM 2.5-induced glucose metabolism in macrophages likely caused IGF2 transcriptional production, which triggered the IGF-1R/IR pathway in CRC cells via paracrine signaling (Fig. 5H).

We employed CRISPR/Cas9 to produce THP-1 cells and BMDMs with altered or intact IGF2 expression to investigate the role of paracrine IGF2 signaling in colorectal cancer cell carcinogenesis. THP-1 cells and their IGF2-deficient sublines were treated with DMSO or NNK/PM 2.5 to make conditioned medium. HCT116 cells treated with THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/sgRNACon formed colonies and spheres better (Fig. 5I). However, HCT116 cells treated with THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/sgRNAIGF2 had no statistically significant difference in tumorigenicity compared to those treated with DMSO. HCT116 cells were then subjected to DMSO- or NNK/PM 2.5-treated THP-1 CM. With IR-specific shRNA, HCT116 cells’ IR expression was either abolished or left unchanged. IR-specific shRNA did not boost HCT116 cells’ ability to form colonies and spheres in response to THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5, unlike control shRNA (Fig. 5J). We then went ahead and conducted tests on animals. In the first trial, BMDM/sgRNACon or BMDM/sgRNAIGF2 that had been treated with DMSO or NNK/PM 2.5 were put into mice along with CRC cells. Compared to animals co-injected with HCT116 + BMDM/sgRNACon-DMSO, HCT116 + BMDM/sgRNAIGF2-NNK/PM 2.5, or HCT116 + BMDM/sgRNAIGF2-DMSO, the tumor growth in mice co-injected with HCT116 + BMDM/sgRNACon-NNK/PM 2.5 was much larger (Fig. 5K). In the supplementary testing protocol, BMDMs treated with DMSO or NNK/PM 2.5 were co-injected with either HCT116/sgRNACon or HCT116/sgRNAIR. The tumor growth was much larger in mice co-injected with HCT116/sgRNACon+BMDM-NNK/PM 2.5 than in mice co-injected with HCT116/sgRNACon+BMDM-DMSO, as indicated by the data shown in Fig. 5L. Nevertheless, animals co-injected with HCT116/sgRNAIR+BMDM-NNK/PM 2.5 and mice co-injected with HCT116/sgRNAIR+BMDM-DMSO did not significantly differ in tumor growth. Furthermore, using the same set of experiments, we found that in HCT116 and SW480 cells, the application of sgRNAIGF2 significantly increased ROS levels while concurrently lowering the expression of genes linked to anti-oxidative stress, including HO-1, NQO1, GCLC, and GCLM (Supplementary Fig. 9A). On the other hand, the suppression of IGF2 or IR by corresponding sgRNA consistently raised ROS levels while lowering the expression of genes linked to the protection against oxidative stress (Supplementary Fig. 9B, C). The findings emphasize the role that macrophage-produced IGF2 plays in the development of CRC that is brought on by NNK/PM 2.5.

IGF2 promotes IR nuclear translocation and induces IR-NPM1 binding and upregulation of anti-oxidative stress

In CRC cells, macrophage-induced IGF2 appears to promote tumor growth via distinct mechanisms than normal IGF-1R/IR signaling. IR forms complexes with RNA polymerase II and transcription regulators in the nucleus. Immunoblotting and nuclear protein intensity measurements showed that THP-1 cells subjected to NNK/PM 2.5 increased nuclear pIGF-1R/IR in HCT116 cells after 0.5 h (Fig. 6A, B). However, THP-1 cells exposed to NNK/PM 2.5 with an IGF2-neutralizing antibody did not increase nuclear pIGF-1R/IR levels in the CM. We then examined how fake IGF2 affected colorectal cancer cells. Nuclear pIGF-1R/IR levels in HCT116 cells increased considerably after 0.5 h of IGF2 therapy. IGF2 shifted IGF-1R/IR from the cell surface to the cytosol and nucleus. Streptavidin pulldown was used to analyze biotinylated cell surface proteins in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6C). During examination, IGF2-treated HCT116 cells’ chromatin fractions showed nuclear pIGF-1R/IR (Fig. 6D). The activation, migration into the nucleus, and binding to chromatin of IGF-1R/IR by IGF2 appears plausible. The nuclear localization sequence (NLS) may be present, but additional proteins like KPNA2 and KPNB1 are needed to translocate IR into the nucleus. Importin-mediated protein nuclear import involves KPNA2, an adaptor protein. IGF2 caused IR, KPNB1, and KPNA2 to form a compound in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6E). IGF2 increased nuclear pIGF-1R/IR levels, while repressing KPNA2 and KPNB1 with siRNA greatly attenuated this rise (Fig. 6F). IR and IGF2 play important roles in CRC development, hence we combined IP-MS IR-interacting proteins with proteomics upregulated proteins. Because of this integration, we observed that NPM1 interacts with and is controlled by IR. This was confirmed using HT-29, Caco-2, SNU-C2A, HCT116, and other CRC cell lines (Fig. 6G). IGF2 from macrophages may increase colorectal cancer (CRC) aggressiveness by cooperating with nuclear IGF-1R-IR. Previous research has showed that NPM1 and NCL are primarily in nucleoli. It travels between nucleoli, nucleoplasm, and cytoplasm. Our idea also suggests that NPM1 activates PD-L1, an IR-associated nuclear protein that regulates CRC transcription. A publicly accessible TCGA dataset showed that NPM1 expression levels are linked to six prognostic markers (KIAA0101, CDKN3, TPX2, MCM2, RRM2, PRC1, and FGFR2). The indications have prognostic relevance in colon and rectal cancer. IGF2 activated a complex of nuclear IR, NPM1, RNA polymerase II (Pol II), and core histones in a co-immunoprecipitation investigation (Fig. 6H, Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). Both FVB and C57BL/6 mice’s first tumors indicated a link between IR and NPM1. After prolonged NNK/PM 2.5 exposure, animals’ blood glucose levels increased, indicating metabolic syndrome (Supplementary Fig. 10C). Mammalian expression vectors with the IR binding domain of NPM1 were generated using FLAG-tagged variants of FL, OD, ΔOD, HBD, or NBD of NPM (Fig. 6I). A study of IGF2-stimulated CRC cells using an anti-FLAG antibody found a correlation between IR and the FL, ΔOD, and HBD NPM1 mutants (Fig. 6J). However, HBD-deficient truncation mutants could not connect with IR. Using shRNA transfection, we created cells that suppressed NPM1 to confirm its role in IR interaction. Next, we reintroduced NPM1 or a mutant version (ΔHBD) (Fig. 6K). IR-NPM1 and IGF2 interacted less in FL-transfected cells compared to NPM1 and ΔHBD-transfected cells. These results imply NPM1 interacts with nuclear IR via the HBD.

We examined how the IR/NPM1 complex stimulates CRC cell aggression via NNK/PM 2.5-primed macrophage activation. THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5 or THP-1/CM-DMSO shRNA transfection permanently reduced NPM1 expression in HCT116 cells. THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5 reduced sphere and colony development in two subclones relative to the control group (Fig. 6L). IGF2-induced IR-NPM1 interaction upregulates PD-L1 expression to promotes CRC progression

To identify the potential biological targets of the IR-NPM1 interaction, we also looked into how IGF2 affected the expression of genes associated with NPM1. The transcriptional increase in PD-L1 expression brought on by IGF2 was significantly reduced in CRC cells (HCT116 or SW480) that were stably transfected with shRNA that selectively targeted NPM1, IR, or KPNB1, as seen in Figs. 7A and B. THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5 increased PD-L1 expression in normal CRC cells, but not in cells with NPM1 or IR deletion. (Fig. 7C). pIGF-1R/IR and PD-L1 expression in CRC cells were not upregulated by conditional media (CM) derived from THP-1 cells treated with NNK/PM 2.5 in the presence of an IGF2-neutralizing antibody (THP-1/CM-NNK/PM 2.5/IGF2Ab) (Fig. 7D). After prolonged exposure to NNK/PM 2.5, PD-L1 expression was increased in both the primary tumors of mice and the HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD animals. Additionally, this exposure significantly raised blood glucose levels, which is consistent with metabolic syndrome (Fig. 2). The information suggests that the nuclear IR-NPM1 complex, which is aided by macrophage-produced IGF2, causes CRC cells to express more PD-L1. There are three potential sites on the PD-L1 promoter where NPM1 can attach. NPM1 mainly interacts with the 469–690 bp region of the PD-L1 promoter through its nucleic acid binding domain (NBD), which has a nuclear localization sequence (NLS) in its HBD. The IGF2 improved the interaction between nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) and insulin receptor (IR) with the P3 region in two different types of colon cancer cells (Fig. 7E). When comparing NPM1-ΔHBD cells to NPM1-FL cells, there was a significant decrease in both the activation of the CD274 promoter and IGF2 binding to the P3 area (Fig. 7F, G). Furthermore, as seen in Fig. 7H, there was a decrease in the expression of pIGF-1R/IR and PD-L1 produced by IGF2 in SW480/NPM1-ΔHBD cells relative to SW480/NPM1-FL cells. Furthermore, IGF2 generated by macrophages promotes nuclear IR-NPM1 complex formation via the NPM-HBD domain, resulting in the transcription-dependent production of PD-L1. We assessed the functional importance of PD-L1 expression mediated by macrophages in the progression of colorectal cancer induced by NNK/PM 2.5. Under HCD circumstances, NNK/PM 2.5 exposure was administered to mice harboring HCT116-Luc-Ortho, either in isolation or in combination with a PD-L1-neutralizing antibody (Fig. 7I). Injection of PD-L1Ab substantially inhibited the growth and spread of HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD tumors, as shown by BLI-based analysis and histology-based tumor quantification (Fig. 7J-L). Additionally, the group treated with PD-L1Ab had a considerably higher survival rate than the group treated with HCT116-Luc-Ortho-NNK/PM 2.5/HCD, as illustrated in Fig. 7M. The findings demonstrate that the development of colorectal cancer brought on by NNK/PM 2.5 depends on PD-L1 expression, which is increased by IGF2 generated by macrophages.

Patients with lymph node metastases were more likely to have higher levels of GLUT1 or IGF2 expression in CD68 + macrophages, as well as pIGF-1R/IR and PD-L1 expression in tumors, than patients without lymph node metastases (Fig. 8D). Next, the association between the expression of these markers and other clinicopathological features was assessed. CD68 + macrophages expressed higher amounts of GLUT1 and IGF2 in advanced stage or high grade colorectal cancer tumors than in early stage or low grade malignancies (Fig. 8E). Additionally, according to a study of the TCGA dataset, individuals with colorectal cancer who expressed high levels of GLUT1 or GLUT3 had a shorter lifespan and a lower disease-free survival than those who expressed low levels of these proteins (Fig. 8F). Based on our findings, tumor-associated PD-L1 expression and tumor-associated macrophage GLUT1-IGF2 expression could be used as biomarkers to predict the progression of colorectal cancer and as targets for therapeutic interventions during CRC treatment.

Discussion

Many cancers, including colorectal cancer, are associated to metabolic syndrome. NNK from tobacco and PM 2.5 from air pollution caused colorectal cancer [27, 28]. However, it was unclear if PM 2.5 or NNK may alter CRC cell metabolism and what role metabolic regulatory expression plays in colorectal carcinogenesis. We demonstrated the importance of NNK/PM 2.5 in metabolic syndrome progression, notably hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia. NNK/PM 2.5 increases GLUT1 and GLUT3 expression and membrane localization, upregulating genes involved in TMiE macrophage infiltration and TAM glucose absorption. NNK/PM 2.5-primed macrophages produce IGF2, activating and nuclear translocating IR in CRC cells. Histones, Pol II, NPM1, and nuclear IR form a complex on the CD274 promoter that promotes PD-L1 expression, giving CRC cells immune-evasive features that enable rapid growth and metastasis [21, 29]. Disrupting the intercellular IR-NPM1 signaling pathway via genetic deletion of IGF2, IR, or NPM, pharmacological inhibition of macrophages or glycolysis, or targeting the PD-L1 axis reduced NNK/PM 2.5-induced CRC growth. Our findings shows that hyperglycemia and other NNK/PM 2.5-induced metabolic abnormalities in TMaE are used to create a tumor niche in TMiE, attracting and training macrophages to increase CRC cell PD-L1 expression and accelerate CRC progression.

It is widely believed that some components of metabolic syndrome serve as additional risk factors for the onset and progression of CRC [30, 31]; however, the role and mechanism of the metabolic syndrome’s characteristics in CRC advancement generated by colorectal cancers have remained largely unclear. Our findings validated the role of hyperglycemia induced by NNK/PM 2.5 in the progression of colorectal cancer. In cancer cells, glycolysis is typically hyperactive to provide adequate energy for fast proliferation. Increased glucose concentrations result in the transcriptional, translational, and post-translational regulation of many carcinogenic pathways and DNA alterations. Our research indicates that NNK/PM 2.5 exposure enhances glucose uptake by macrophages via a two-step mechanism: initially, PM 2.5/AhR-mediated transcriptional upregulation of GLUT1 and GLUT3, followed by NNK/AChR-induced transporter membrane translocation, leading to the upregulation of genes associated with pro-tumoral activities and tumor infiltration. Thus, NNK/PM 2.5 exposure likely facilitates CRC progression by inducing hyperglycemia in TMaE, which predisposes macrophages in TMiE to adopt pro-tumoral phenotypes, although the potential influence of additional mechanisms cannot be entirely dismissed. Tissue injury is caused by proinflammatory mediators and inducible nitric oxide synthase generated by M1 macrophages [32, 33]. M2 macrophages express Arg1, CD206, and scavenger receptors, produce various anti-inflammatory mediators, and suppress the immune system while also contributing to angiogenesis, tumorigenesis, and tissue remodeling or repair [34, 35]. The significance of glucose metabolism in macrophage immunomodulation is well established. The M2 macrophages exhibit elevated mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates, whereas M1 macrophages predominantly rely on glycolysis. Indeed, M2 macrophages are universally recognized as tumor-associated macrophages that significantly consume glucose. TAMs are associated with a specific pathogenic condition and exhibit M1 and M2 phenotypes dependent on TMiE. Using a single cell-based method, TAM subtypes such as resident-tissue macrophage-like, proliferating, lipid-associated, immune regulatory, inflammatory cytokine-enriched, pro-angiogenic, and interferon-primed were further defined. As a result, despite their similarities, TAMs are more than merely an M2 macrophage subgroup. Our results show that NNK/PM 2.5-primed macrophages undergo metabolic reprogramming to develop M2-type phenotypes and increased glucose consumption, resulting in immunosuppressive TMiE. How macrophages educated by metabolic preprogramming mediated by NNK/PM 2.5 impart aggressive features on CRC cells is another crucial question. NNK/PM 2.5-primed macrophages transcriptionally upregulate IGF2 through enhanced glycolysis, which in turn paracrinely activates IR signaling in CRC cells, according to kinase profiling findings. IGF1, IGF2, and its receptors, as well as IGF-binding proteins, make up the IGF axis. Although IGFs often have comparable physiological and cellular roles in tumor progression, growth, development, and metabolism, they play different roles in controlling glucose metabolism in monocytes. Monocytes engage in pro-inflammatory and aerobic glycolysis as a result of IGF1-mediated IGF-1R activation. On the other hand, mature macrophages are preprogrammed by IGF2 to have anti-inflammatory properties. One interesting discovery of our work is that IGF2 generated from macrophages can promote the aggressiveness of CRC cells by upregulating the transcription of PD-L1. IGF-1R/IR signaling is conventionally understood to involve cell surface ligand binding, autophosphorylation, docking of cytosolic proteins, and biological output [36, 37]. Notably, PD-L1 expression was induced by macrophage-derived IGF2 via KPNB1- and KPNA2-facilitated nuclear localization of IR. Consequently, we hypothesized that IR interaction with the PD-L1 promoter would be enhanced by a nuclear partner. This concept is corroborated by our discovery that IGF2, through NPM1, facilitates the creation of complexes including IR, histones, and Pol II. NPM1 interacts with other transcription factors, such NF-κB or c-Myc, to bind to the PD-L1 promoter and facilitate transcription. Thus, NPM1 might support PD-L1 production by acting as IR’s transcriptional co-activator. In our investigation, HFD had no discernible effect on the advancement of NNK/PM 2.5-induced CRC. The genetic heritage of the mouse model and experimental factors like HFD feeding duration could be to blame for this disparity. Our results clarify the role that diabetes mellitus and hyperglycemia play as significant risk factors for the onset and spread of certain malignancies as well as the impact that smoking status has on the effectiveness of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy. Our mechanistic understanding of the metabolic issues produced by NNK/PM 2.5 in TMaE and TMiE indicates that biomarkers for prognosis and targets for therapy for those suffering from CRC may be identified within the GLUT1, GLUT3, and IR-NPM1 pathway components linked to macrophages.

However, the present study has several inherent limitations. First, all mechanistic and in vivo observations were derived from mouse models with relatively short-term NNK/PM 2.5 intervention and fixed genetic backgrounds, which cannot fully recapitulate the long-term, low-dose exposure characteristics of human populations as well as the complex clinical comorbidities of metabolic syndrome such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Second, this work mainly focused on tumor-associated macrophages in the tumor microenvironment; the potential contributions of other stromal cell types including cancer-associated fibroblasts and endothelial cells to NNK/PM 2.5-mediated metabolic crosstalk and colorectal cancer progression were not explored. Third, the clinical validation was performed based on a small-sized tissue microarray cohort, and further verification in larger multicenter patient cohorts is required to confirm the prognostic value of macrophage GLUT1/IGF2 and tumoral IR/PD-L1 biomarkers. Fourth, although glycolysis inhibition, macrophage depletion, and PD-L1 blockade exerted obvious anti-tumor effects in mouse models, the pharmacokinetic properties, potential toxicity, and combined efficacy of these interventions with conventional colorectal cancer therapies remain to be clarified. Finally, the conclusions regarding receptor signaling rely partially on pharmacological inhibitors, and potential off-target effects cannot be completely ruled out; subsequent genetic intervention models are needed to further verify the specificity of the NNK/nAChR-PM 2.5/AhR-GLUT1/GLUT3 and IR-NPM1-PD-L1 regulatory axes.

Conclusion

Carcinogens may significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of colorectal cancer by enhancing interactions between the tumor and adjacent tissue through systemic and local glucose metabolic disturbances mediated by NNK/PM 2.5 (Fig. 9). Due to the intricacy of the molecular and cellular networks in TMiE and TMaE, the theoretical pathways established from CRC tumors may not be applicable to the development of CRC induced by CCs. Additional critical insights may be derived from research examining the correlation between colon macrophage GLUT1, GLUT3, and IGF2 activity, colon tumor proliferation and metastasis, as well as the metabolic and smoking profiles of colorectal cancer patients.
