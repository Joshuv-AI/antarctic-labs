 ABSTRACT

A new facility, the removable beryllium-target rod rabbit holder facility (RB*-TRRH), was designed and
fielded in the large removable beryllium (RB*) position in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). This
facility supports three shortened target rod rabbit holders (sTRRHs), which allow for the irradiation of six
axially stacked rabbit capsules per sTRRH. This will allow for the simultaneous irradiation of 18 rabbit
capsules in the RB* position and will provide an alternative location for low-cost rabbit capsules to be
irradiated in HFIR. The first capsules inserted into this facility are 18 perforated capsules that contain
representative steel specimens of interest to the calandria of Canada deuterium uranium (CANDU)
reactors as part of a lifetime extension program with Canadian Nuclear Laboratories (CNL).

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 THE HIGH FLUX ISOTOPE REACTOR

The High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) is an 85 MWth, beryllium-reflected, light-water-cooled and
-moderated flux trap (FT)-style reactor that burns through its highly enriched uranium fuel element every
cycle. During a HFIR cycle, the control plates are slowly withdrawn to maintain 85 MWth. The cycle ends
when 85 MWth can no longer be maintained or the control plates have reached their maximum withdrawn
position. A typical HFIR cycle lasts 24–25 days and occurs ~ 7 times per year. The large removable
beryllium (RB*) position, which sits just outside the control plates, is generally the second highest
neutron flux position in the reactor. Additionally, the RB* position offers a larger experiment diameter
than most other experiment positions within the reactor.

HFIR supports many missions, however the. The core missions are neutron scattering (beam lines),
materials irradiations, and isotope production. In the FT, materials irradiations and isotope production
frequently compete for space; however, the RB* position has historically been underutilized, likely due to
the costs associated with designing RB* experiments and the lower neutron flux. The development of an
FT capsule style facility for the RB* position is intended to drive down the costs associated with utilizing
the RB* region and further increase the utilization of HFIR.

1.2 CALANDRIA AND SPECIMENS

The first capsules for the RB* facility being developed are steel specimens to support a CANDU lifetime
extension program, specifically looking at accelerated irradiation of weldment steel from the calandria.
The specimens consist largely of round Charpy toughness (RCT), tensile (ten), four-point bend (4PB),
and rod specimens. The specimens were fabricated by Canadian Nuclear Laboratories (CNL) out of 304
steel with 308 steel weld filler. These specimens are to be irradiated in the RB* position utilizing the new
facility, which allows them to reach representative displacements per atom (dpa) and helium generation
(Heappm) at ~ 60°C specimen temperature, mirroring the expected damage to transmutation ratio and
temperature conditions in the calandria of CANDU reactors.

In the initial experiment, flux monitors in the form of sealed vanadium capsules with dose wires inside
were included in the capsules to mitigate uncertainties in neutron fluence. These will be evaluated with
gamma spectroscopy in the post-irradiation examination and can be used in conjunction with STAYSL to
estimate the neutron flux spectra. The flux wires consist of Nb, Cu, Ti, Fe, and Co, which target different
energies of the neutron spectrum depending on activation threshold energies. Specifics of the masses and
locations of the flux monitors are included in a sponsor report on the first irradiation[5] .

Another consideration for RB* is the displacement of the beryllium plugs for the insertion of
experiments, which reduces the effectiveness of the RB* region in reflecting neutrons. As a result, all
experiments in the reflector have a negative impact on the reactivity of the reactor and the potential to
reduce cycle length. Figure 5 shows the expected cycle reduction from a series of SHIFT calculations for
replacing the beryllium plug material with aluminum (Al), stainless steel (SST), molybdenum (Mo),
aluminum with a 1 mm gadolinium liner (Gd), and a MiniFuel (MF) experiment. The HFIR experiment
guide (EGF-6.1) places a limit of a 1.3 day cycle length reduction on cycle length without requiring
administrative approval.

Reactivity values of materials and operational history (shown in Figure 6) were considered when selecting
the facility material. At the time of the decision, cycle 500 had just completed, which contained WIRE-21
[7], a heavily instrumented RB* experiment from cycle 498 to 500. The operational history indicated a
less than ½ day cycle length reduction for WIRE-21, which has a larger negative impact on RB*
reactivity than the facility for this experiment. Additionally, cycle 500 experienced two reactor scrams
prior to the operation of the cycle, which are believed to have contributed to its shortened length. As a
result, the removable beryllium target rod rabbit holder (RB*-TRRH) facility was fabricated out of Al-
6061 T6 with 316 SST pins holding the three sections due to the low neutron cross section, high thermal
conductivity, low heat generation rate, coolant compatibility, and low toxicity. Though beryllium was
considered, there was no perceived benefit because the standard deviation of cycle length from cycle 484–
500 was 0.44 days, including cycles that carried an RB* experiment of greater reactivity worth.
Additionally, HFIR operations expressed concern with the hazard and toxicity associated operating a
beryllium facility. The estimated end of cycle worth of the aluminum experiment was ~ −30 cents,
resulting in less than 0.5 days of cycle length reduction.

2.2 EXPERIMENT FACILITY

Historically, the RB* position has been underutilized, driven largely by costs associated with designing
experiments with the facility, which have traditionally been one-off type experiments. As a result, an
RB*-TRRH facility was designed to accommodate the same style capsules or “rabbits” traditionally
inserted in the FT with the goal of lowering costs and increasing the utilization of the RB* facility.
Toward this goal, a trefoil facility was designed to hold three target rod rabbit holders (TRRHs) that
would contain irradiation capsules in the same configuration of the FT.

The RB* facility (RB-TRRH) consists of three aluminum sections: a bottom pin section, a middle section,
and a top section, each with a different trefoil shape to accommodate three sTRRHs. The sTRRHs are
constrained in a similar fashion as in the FT, in which the top hexagonal structure of the sTRRH is held in
place radially by the RB-TRRH (see Figure 8a) and at the bottom the sTRRH pins slot into individual
holes (see Figure 8b). RB-TRRH rests on the bottom of the RB* liner and accommodates three sTRRHs.
Coolant flow enters the facility from the top, leaving the sTRRH fully constrained in the RB-TRRH.

The three sTRRHs inside the RB-TRRH can each hold six standard rabbit capsules, either perforated or
sealed. Additionally, the capsules can be axially offset by up to a 40 mm, with an aluminum spacer to
adjust the axial position of the experiment capsules. This allows experiments to take advantage of the
more stable neutron flux above the centerline of the reactor (Figure 2). The sTRRHs are shortened for two
primary reasons: The pins of the full length TRRHs, which can accommodate 7–8 rabbits, are secured
below the reactor core, and the full length TRRHs extend above the reflector. The RB-TRRH resting on
the bottom of the RB* liner results in the sTRRH being positioned axially higher in the reactor than the
traditional TRRH; consequently, the seventh capsule in the sTRRH would extend above the reactor core
and experience a significantly reduced neutron flux. Furthermore, the RB-TRRH facility is designed to fit
entirely inside the RB* liner, which reduces uncertainty with flow characteristics above the RB* position
and causes the height of the sTRRH to be reduced.

2.3 THERMAL DESIGN

Before insertion into the reactor, the flow performance of any facility must be demonstrated to ensure that
the experiment has no adverse impacts on reactor operation. This is formally documented in detail in
C-HFIR-2024_006, Rev. 0. For this report, the coolant characteristics and how they would affect
experiment temperature are reported. A water test loop (shown in Figure 11) capable of emulating
HFIR-relevant flow rates and pressure drops was used to test the fully assembled facility, including test
capsules. The flow test section is documented in GN-2023-053 and attached in APPENDIX A. The pump
speed is controlled by a variable-frequency controller. An Azbil MTG18A electromagnetic flow meter
was used to measure flow rate, and two Omegadyne DPG409-100DWU pressure transducers were used to
measure pressure drop across the test section. The contribution of height to the pressure differential is
considered negligible under these flow conditions.

The convection coefficient is affected by exact tolerances of the experiments and the facility as well as
coolant temperature; however, none of these changes result in a hfilm that could dramatically alter the
specimen temperature model. The goal of the experiment was to keep the steel specimens below 80°C. As
a result, perforated capsules were used to allow specimens to directly interact with the HFIR coolant. The
sample temperatures were modeled in a steady-state thermal model using ANSYS 23R1. Material heat
generation rates were pulled from previous RB* experiments that utilized MCNP to calculate the energy
deposition in Al and SST in this experiment position. These values were determined to be 7.27 W/g for
Al and 13.2 W/g for SST. The axial flux profile was ignored for the temperature model because the
heating decreases as experiments move axially away from the HFIR centerline. As a result, capsules off
the centerline can be expected to be a few degrees cooler. A convective boundary was applied to all
surfaces within the capsule expected to be in direct contact with HFIR coolant. The specimens in the
capsule consisted of three RCT specimens stacked on top of four 4PB specimens, which have four tensile
specimens stacked around. The results of this model are shown below in Figure 13.

The average specimen temperature of the model was 65 ± 7 SD °C. The highest temperature in the
specimens is in the center of the RCT specimens. There will likely be some coolant movement between
the specimens, which will further decrease the temperature in a real experiment.

 3. CONCLUSION

A trefoil RB*-TRRH facility was developed, which has now been inserted into HFIR. The facility and
sTRRHs were fabricated out of aluminum and tested in a water flow loop outside the reactor. These data
were used in calculations to determine the safety and limitations of facility insertion into the reactor.
Currently, 18 perforated capsules containing SST specimens of relevance to CANDU reactor operation
are being irradiated in the facility. Ultimately, this facility will allow increased utilization of HFIR for
materials irradiation experiments.
