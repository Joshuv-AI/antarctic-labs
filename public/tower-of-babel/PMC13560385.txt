Insulin pump therapy for T1D: a real-world analysis of a novel two-in-one insulin pump in Chinese adults with type 1 diabetes
Authors: Yan Lü, Ziyi Li, Yang Yang, Xiaofen Lian, Shaoqing Shi, Fan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo assess the real-world use, glycemic outcomes, and patient experience of a new two-in-one insulin pump (with switchable tubeless patch-on and tubed portable forms, available with standard or short cannulas) in Chinese adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D). Methods: This study was based on a multicenter survey in China. By the end of December 2025, 47 T1D patients had been treated with the Ari two-in-one insulin pump for at least 3 months. The questionnaire covered blood glucose management, quality of life (QoL), product experience, and patients’ future expectations. All data were self-reported and analyzed using descriptive statistics. Results: The mean HbA1c level in the month before the survey was 7.2 ± 1.4%. The sensor-derived glucose metrics: time in range (TIR, 3.9–10.0 mmol/L) was 74.0 ± 14.1%, time above range (TAR, > 10.0 mmol/L) was 17.9 ± 4.9%, and time below range (TBR, < 3.9 mmol/L) was 8.1 ± 2.8%. All of the mentioned values were within clinical targets except that TBR did not reach the clinical goal for hypoglycemia. Compared with the period before pump therapy, 55.3% and 53.2% of patients reported a reduction in the frequency of hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic episodes, respectively. Regarding QoL, 48.9% of the patients reported feeling confident in their treatment and freedom in their daily activities, 78.7% reported better wearing comfort, and 76.6% experienced effective improvement in their overall QoL. In terms of product experience, the distribution of the form of usage of the pump was: 15 cases (31.9%) used the portable (tubed) form, 14 cases (29.8%) used the patch (tubeless) form, and 18 cases (38.3%) had experience with both forms.Among the latter group, satisfaction levels were highest for device safety alerts, operating system, operational stability, and overall design. Regarding future technologies, over half of the patients were not sufficiently aware of hybrid closed-loop pumps. Their core functional demands consisted of two types of intelligent modules: (1) automatic insulin delivery adjustment, and (2) integration with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). Conclusion: This first cross-sectional patient survey in China showed that the new two-in-one insulin pump was associated with good glycemic control (TIR 74%) and improved QoL. This highly switchable design is innovative as it directly addresses the major issue of skin health in long-term pump therapy and offers great flexibility. Future development should progress toward an automated closed-loop system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02219-8.

Introduction

Data from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) report that about 537 million adults worldwide have diabetes, and the number is expected to rise to 783 million by 2045 [1]. China has the largest number of diabetes patients in the world, with over 140 million affected [2]. For people with T1D, who have little or no endogenous insulin production, lifelong exogenous insulin therapy is essential. Currently, multiple daily injections (MDI) and continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII, also known as insulin pump therapy) are the two intensive treatment regimens [3]. CSII more closely mimics physiological insulin secretion and can provide individualized and fine-tuned dosing. Many studies have shown its superiority compared to MDI in improving glycemic control and QoL, especially for T1D patients [4]. However, traditional insulin pumps still suffer from problems such as high upfront and consumable costs, wearing inconvenience, and complicated operation [5–6].

Furthermore, single-form insulin pumps, particularly those of the conventional tubed type, also have limitations, such as limited site rotation, skin irritation, and a heavy long-term skin care burden. All of these problems can affect the stability of insulin absorption and can lead to reduced patient adherence over time [7–8].

In April 2024, the Ari insulin pump received market approval in China to address the clinical issues described above. The product is indicated for treating T1D in children, adolescents, and adults [9]. The Ari insulin pump introduces a world-first, switchable two-in-one design that allows transition between a patch-on (tubeless) and a portable (tubed) form, along with a balanced indwelling cannula to improve wearing comfort. Before launch, the developer completed a series of internal clinical feasibility assessments and registration trials, thereby establishing preliminary evidence for infusion precision, safety, and short-term glycemic control [10]. Among these, the pivotal registration study (NCT06741943) has been registered and made public on a clinical trial platform, whereas early internal exploratory data remain undisclosed due to the manufacturer’s confidentiality requirements [11]. Given the limited time since market entry, it remains to be systematically explored how this device, with its innovative “one pump, two forms” design, is adopted in real-world settings and what its overall value is.

Thus, this cross-sectional questionnaire survey was designed to provide a scientific assessment of this novel insulin pump, and particularly its two-in-one functionality, from the patient’s perspective, in order to obtain evidence-based support for the clinical promotion and future optimization of this device in Chinese T1D management.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and general information

In this study, we used a cross-sectional survey design to investigate Chinese T1D patients using the Ari insulin pump. The respondents were from 12 provinces or autonomous regions in China. In total, we collected 47 valid questionnaires, including 22 males and 25 females. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 75 years, with a mean of 48.21 ± 7.29 years. The duration of T1D ranged from 2 to 35 years, with a mean of 19.85 ± 2.13 years. Regarding insulin pump use duration: 22 patients (46.8%) had used it for 3–6 months, 18 (38.3%) for 6–12 months, 3 (6.4%) for 1–3 years, and 4 (8.5%) for more than 3 years.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age between 18 and 75 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of T1D with disease duration ≥ 1 year; (3) current use of the Ari insulin pump for ≥ 3 months without any allergic reactions to the device; (4) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation.

Exclusion criteria: (1) type 2 diabetes or other types of diabetes; (2) lack of indication for insulin pump therapy; (3) use of the Ari insulin pump for less than 3 months; (4) presence of severe systemic comorbidities; (5) use of medications that may have a significant effect on blood glucose; (6) mental illness or communication disorders.

All participants signed informed consent forms. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the lead participating hospital (Approval No. 2025-024-01).

Study methods

The multicenter cross-sectional survey was conducted from December 1 to 31, 2025. We distributed the online questionnaire through the Endocrinology Departments of five hospitals (Lianyungang Municipal Oriental Hospital, Peking University Shenzhen Hospital, Maoming People’s Hospital, Huizhou Municipal Central Hospital, and The First Huizhou Hospital Affiliated to Guangdong Medical University), as well as through the communities and the official platform of the Ari Diabetes Foundation. Each questionnaire took approximately 9 min to complete on average, and the effective response rate was 91.28%.

The device under investigation was the Ari two-in-one CSII system. It allows free switching between a patch (tubeless) and a portable (tubed) device (Figs. 1 and 2) [9]. The device measures 51 mm × 40 mm × 11 mm and weighs approximately 26 g, with a 3 mL insulin reservoir holding a maximum of 300 insulin units. There are two infusion line lengths (15 cm and 60 cm), along with balanced indwelling cannulas (26G × 6 mm or 26G × 9 mm) [9].

This CSII system supports refined, scenario-adaptive infusion, namely, up to 48 programmable basal rate segments over 24 h to mimic, e.g., physiological insulin secretion; an option for a temporary basal rate for exercise, illness, menstruation, and fasting; both conventional prandial bolus and extended dual-wave bolus mode for postprandial control and mixed-meal glucose fluctuation; and a bolus calculator that considers, e.g., real-time glucose, target range, carbohydrate ratio, insulin sensitivity factor, and active insulin on board in order to reduce manual dosing errors [9].

In this paper, for the sake of consistency, all CGM-derived metrics are called “sensor glucose.” The raw CGM data were not collected, nor were they fully analyzed using a professional platform. All sensor glucose indicators and events were self-reported by patients via the questionnaire. The reported HbA1c from each study center was checked against the paper lab reports, without a verification protocol or NGSP criteria.

The self-administered questionnaire consisted of 26 items covering five domains: demographics, glucose management, QoL, product experience, and future expectations. There were no questions referring to the model of CGM device or time of use of such a device; therefore, such data were not collected. The questionnaire was not tested for reliability or validity. The results are intended only for internal descriptive analysis. They have no statistical generalizability and reflect only the subjective experiences of the patients.

Patient-reported feedback

Glycemic management indicators

(1) HbA1c level measured within one month prior to the survey; (2) Sensor-derived metrics: TIR (3.9–10.0 mmol/L), TBR (< 3.9 mmol/L), TAR (> 10.0 mmol/L) [12]; (3) Self-reported change in frequency of low (< 3.9 mmol/L) and high (> 13.9 mmol/L) glucose events compared with the pre-pump period [13].

Quality of life assessment

Treatment-related confidence, freedom in daily activities, wearing comfort, and improvement of the overall QoL were assessed [14].

Product experience feedback

(1) Distribution of users among three categories: tubed (portable), tubeless (patch), or both. (2) Satisfaction ratings with regard to safety alerts, operating system, operational stability, and overall design. Satisfaction should be reported separately for each user group [5].

Future technology expectations

(1) Awareness level regarding hybrid closed-loop (artificial pancreas) systems [15]; (2) Most desired features (e.g., automated infusion adjustment, seamless CGM integration, scenario-adaptive tuning) [16].

Results

Glycemic management indicators

No baseline HbA1c data were collected before Ari pump initiation. The mean HbA1c in the month prior to the survey was 7.2 ± 1.4%. All 47 patients used CGM routinely; 21 also performed fingertip testing. Sensor glucose reporting was jointly guided by CGM trends and fingertip values.

In Table 1, the sensor-derived metrics were as follows: TIR (3.9–10.0 mmol/L): 74.0 ± 14.1% (above the 70% target); TBR (< 3.9 mmol/L): 8.1 ± 2.8% (above the < 4% goal); TAR (> 10.0 mmol/L): 17.9 ± 4.9% (below the < 25% criterion). Compared with the pre-pump period, 55.3% and 53.2% of patients reported that they had experienced obviously fewer hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic events, respectively (Table 2). Because there were no items in the questionnaire concerning hypoglycemia prevention or emergency intervention, we did not analyze this aspect.

Pump usage among the 47 patients: portable (tubed) only – 15 (31.9%); patch (tubeless) only – 14 (29.8%); both forms – 18 (38.3%) (Table 4).

Satisfaction ratings (subjective) were collected in four areas: (1) safety alerts, (2) operating system, (3) operational stability, and (4) overall design. Satisfaction rates for the portable group were 66.7%, 80.0%, 80.0%, and 80.0%; for the patch group: 85.7%, 92.9%, 92.9%, and 92.9%; for the both-forms group: 88.9%, 100.0%, 94.4%, and 94.4%). Overall acceptance of the Ari pump was high. Only the portable group had one dissatisfied rating per dimension.

Future technology expectations

For the question on the understanding of hybrid closed-loop pumps, over half (53.2%) of participants reported little to no understanding of hybrid closed-loop pumps – either “heard of but not understood” or “completely not understood” (Table 5). For the question on the desired features among the listed options, “automatic insulin infusion adjustment” and “seamless CGM connection” were the most desired (36 responses each), followed by “scenario-adaptive adjustment” (27 responses) (Table 6). These are the results reflecting patients’ subjective expectations rather than an objective needs assessment.

Discussion

Managing T1D is centered on mimicking physiological insulin delivery via exogenous routes in order to manage long-term glycemic stability and to delay complications [17]. In comparison to MDI, CSII allows for finer, more adaptive insulin infusion - improving HbA1c and smoothing glycemic swings - and therefore it is a mainstay of current T1D therapy [18]. Insulin pump technology is still evolving: from conventional tubed devices and basic patch pumps toward intelligent closed-loop systems, with the aim of continuously improving infusion precision, wearing comfort, and user adherence [19].

The Ari two-in-one pump evaluated here is switchable, i.e., it has portable (tubed) and patch (tubeless) forms combined with a balanced indwelling cannula. The idea is to cope with the main limitations of single-form pumps: the wear constraints, the limited site rotation, and the heavy long-term skin care demands [9]. This is a cross-sectional study based on real-world patients. It gives a first hint of the clinical value of the device. Here are the observations.

Switchable design meets diverse needs. The switchable design of the Ari pump is a dual-form design that permits flexible changes of the pump form according to lifestyle, clothing, or skin status, and allows infusion sites to rest periodically, thus alleviating the problem of prolonged wear on the skin and supporting stable insulin absorption [10]. Over one-third of participants had used both pump forms. This means that patients have a genuine demand for scenario-based configuration switching. Those who had used both forms gave the highest ratings for device operation, device stability, and overall device design. This novel dual-structure accordingly improved patient wear experience.

Lightweight build and smart features boost adherence. As also reported by Ze Q, Castle JR et al. [20–21], the fact that the pump is light and portable lessens the foreign-body sensation and activity limitations, and it can be worn in a more discreet and comfortable way. The multi-device intelligent control makes dose adjustment and data viewing easy, which helps the patients with self-management. It can also be used for remote monitoring by family members or clinicians, another advantage reported by Consoli A et al. [22]. All these features work to lighten the psychological burden and the operational complexity of long-term pump use. Almost half of our participants said that they felt more confident in their treatment and had more freedom in daily life, demonstrating that this pump exerted a positive impact on both QoL and adherence [shows4].

Favorable sensor-based glucose control. After use of the pump, patients reached TIR targets and had much less hyperglycemia; over half felt that they had fewer hypo- and hyperglycemic episodes. The pump has fine-tuned multi-segment basal rates, multi-mode bolus options, and a smart dose calculator, which can be used for many different diets and situations. Besides better adherence, all these elements together led to better overall glycemic control [5].TBR was still high in this cohort, indicating an opportunity for better hypoglycemia management.

Patient-stated future directions. Awareness of hybrid closed-loop systems is still low, but patient demands strongly focus on the most seamless CGM linkage and automated insulin delivery, consistent with the trend of smart diabetes management [23]. The hardware and connectivity already present in the Ari pump provide a solid base for developing closed-loop algorithms and smart upgrades. In addition, lower device costs, better insurance coverage, and a stronger personal ecosystem for patient-clinician collaboration will be needed for broader adoption [24].

Limitations. This cross-sectional survey had several limitations. First, limitations in the interpretation of glycemic outcomes. The duration of device use varied considerably; the mean HbA1c of 7.2% only reflected a cross-sectional snapshot, not a standard endpoint. There was no baseline HbA1c, therefore no pre-post comparison. All glucose data were self-reported; only the HbA1c values were checked against lab reports, but without a uniform standard or NGSP criteria, which introduced reporting bias. Second, no objective monitoring. No raw CGM data or professional platform analyses were collected; no device model or duration of use was recorded; the reporting protocol was not uniform and was not traceable. Third, the questionnaire did not include any items on prevention or intervention for hypoglycemia, so the high TBR remained unexplained. Fourth, design. This custom survey did not undergo any reliability or validity testing, nor did it have any qualitative support. The sample was small and the subgroup distributions were not representative of the whole, which did not allow generalization. Fifth, self-report bias. All metrics were from the patients’ subjective reports, were not supported by any objective device data or clinician assessment, and could be influenced by patients’ psychological habits; they were not equivalent to objective clinical efficacy.

Conclusion. The Ari insulin pump - with its novel two-in-one switchable structure, lightweight portable design, and smart interactive features - improved wear experience and treatment adherence in T1D patients, achieved good overall glucose control, and demonstrated strong clinical utility. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design, small sample, reliance on subjective data, and missing baseline measurements, these results represent only initial real-world performance. Future large-scale, standardized, prospective controlled studies with uniform intervention period, baseline and objective CGM data, and long-term follow-up are needed to demonstrate glycemic stability, skin safety, and cost-effectiveness, and to provide a higher level of evidence to support the clinical adoption and intelligent evolution of the device.
