Polyamines are i-motif disruptors
Authors: Auroni Deep, Himanshu Shekhar, Rozaleen Dash, Shilpi Minocha, Vivekanandan Perumal, Saran Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyamines are vital polycations involved in diverse cellular processes and nucleic acid interactions. However, a precise molecular mechanism for their gene regulatory roles, particularly through specific DNA secondary structures, is unclear. We report that the biogenic polyamines spermine, spermidine, and putrescine selectively destabilize DNA i-motif structures. In silico docking predicted that polyamines exhibit a strong affinity for i-motifs over other DNA forms. Biophysical analyses, including circular dichroism, surface plasmon resonance, and thermal melting, confirmed polyamine-induced disruption of both telomeric (hTeloC) and promoter region i-motifs (e.g., HIF-1A, BCL2, VEGF-A), while leaving G4s and the corresponding duplex DNA largely unaffected. Crucially, immunofluorescence studies using an i-motif-recognizing antibody provided the first in cellulo evidence of reduced intranuclear i-motif formation following polyamine treatment. Transcriptomic profiling further demonstrated that putrescine treatment preferentially alters the expression of genes enriched with putative i-motif sequences in their promoter regions. Our findings establish a novel regulatory axis in which polyamines act as endogenous, structure-specific destabilizers of DNA i-motifs, directly affecting gene expression. This work provides a mechanistic insight into transcriptional control through DNA secondary structures and suggests new therapeutic strategies targeting polyamine-i-motif interactions.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, i-motifs, polyamines, G-quadruplexes, gene regulation, DNA secondary structures, putrescine, spermine, spermidine

Introduction

Polyamines are small, positively charged aliphatic molecules central to mammalian physiology. The mammalian polyamine system comprises putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, synthesized from the precursor ornithine (Figures S1A–S1C). Polyamine binding to nucleic acids and proteins regulates the key molecular systems of DNA replication, transcription, translation, and cell proliferation.1,2 Their charge-dependent binding to the grooves of canonical B-form DNA has been well documented, with intracellular concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 30 mM, depending on multiple patho-physiological factors.3,4,5,6 The intracellular polyamine distribution is reported to be dynamic, with spermine enriched in nuclei and putrescine elevated in the cytoplasm during DNA synthesis, while overall levels are observed to be higher in cancer cells compared with normal cells.6 This dynamic distribution underscores their multifaceted involvement in molecular mechanisms of DNA replication, RNA transcription, and protein synthesis as well as in various patho-physiological states. Polyamines have been frequently documented to be central to aging and inflammation, with spermidine reported as an anti-aging biogenic compound, while being commercially used as a supplement.7,8

Recent studies have revealed that polyamines can recognize and localize to specific genomic regions,9 potentially regulating gene expression through direct interaction with nucleic acid motifs. This led us to speculate about their role in modulating genomic morphology. Non-canonical DNA secondary structures, such as G-quadruplexes (G4s) and i-motifs (iMs), have emerged as important regulators of genome function, and several reports state their relevance in cellular and molecular pathways, both at the level of deoxyribonucleic acids and ribonucleic acids.10,11 G4s form in guanine-rich sequences, whereas iMs arise from cytosine-rich tracts. G4s and iMs originate from guanine-rich putative G4 sequence (PGS) and cytosine-rich putative iM sequence (PIS) regions, respectively. Both intrinsic sequence features and extrinsic factors like pH, molecular crowding, and protein interactions govern their formation, disruption, and stability.12,13,14,15 Unlike well-characterized G4s, iMs have only recently been validated in living cells using an iM-specific ScFv.14 Their formation is pH sensitive—stabilized under acidic conditions and destabilized near physiological pH—and fluctuates in a cell cycle-dependent manner.16,17 Further, the prevalence of these structures in the promoter regions of genes has led to speculations pertaining to the use of these structures for ligand-based transcriptional perturbations.18,19

While polyamines are well established for their global influence on cellular processes, including DNA replication and transcription, the precise molecular mechanisms governing their transcriptional control remain poorly defined.1,15 Their regulation has often been attributed to broad electrostatic effects on chromatin or indirect modulation of transcription factors. We hypothesized a more specific, structure-targeted mechanism: the direct modulation of iM DNA by polyamines as a functional switch for gene expression. The primary step to arrive at this hypothesis was to establish the structural modulation of iMs by polyamines. Previous predictions often relied on limited structural modeling, lacking comprehensive experimental validation.3 Our study provides the first comprehensive experimental characterization to bridge this gap, integrating molecular docking with biophysical techniques, in cellulo validation, and an unbiased transcriptomic analysis.

We systematically investigated the polyamine-iM axis in mammalian cells, examining how putrescine, spermidine, and spermine affect iM folding and stability under physiological conditions. Using in silico, biophysical, and molecular approaches, we probed the mechanistic basis of polyamine-mediated modulation and its impact on transcriptional regulation. Our study focused on well-characterized genomic iMs, namely, the promoter iMs (HIF-1A, BCL2, and VEGF-A) and the telomeric hTeloC iM.11,15,20,21,22,23 These insights will advance our understanding of the epigenetic and structural complexity of the genome and may reveal novel avenues for therapeutic intervention targeting the polyamine-iM axis to induce altered gene expression as corrective measures.

Results

Molecular docking reveals high affinity and selective binding of polyamines to iMs

To assess the binding specificity of polyamines for distinct DNA structures, we performed molecular docking using HADDOCK 2.4. Structural coordinates for single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), G4s, iMs, and duplex DNA were sourced from the Protein Data Bank, to represent the major DNA conformations present in vivo, encompassing distinct topologies and electrostatic environments. For polyamines, the structures were generated from their SMILES notation (Figures S1A–S1G). Docking analyses yielded unique interaction profiles for each complex (Figures 1A and S1H). An analysis of the top three clusters demonstrated that polyamines had significantly greater binding affinities for iMs and G4s as opposed to canonical B-form DNA (Figure 1B). Notably, polyamine-iM complexes, with each one of the three polyamines, exhibited the most negative HADDOCK scores and low cross-validation root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), indicative of most favorable interactions and binding conformations (Figures 1C and 1D). Buried surface areas were comparable overall, though spermine and spermidine formed larger interaction interfaces, reflecting their size-dependent interaction (Figure 1E). The favorable energies for iM-polyamine complexes stem from the electrostatic attraction between negatively charged DNA and polycationic ligands, while G4 affinities likely arise from their planar stacking.

Among polyamines, spermine consistently exhibits the greatest stabilization, highlighting the importance of size and multivalency in polyamine-DNA interactions.

Molecular dynamics simulations reveal polyamine-induced disruption of iM structural integrity

To mechanistically resolve how polycations interact with the iM at the atomic level, we performed 200-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the 8AYG iM structure in explicit solvent under both acidic (pH 5.0) and neutral (pH 7.0) conditions in the presence of putrescine (+2), spermidine (+3), and spermine (+4), using the AMBER force field. Structural stability was assessed via Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) and radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bond occupancy was tracked across the trajectory, per-residue flexibility was quantified via Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), and conformational sampling was visualized through principal-component analysis (PCA) of the MD ensemble, collectively establishing that polycation-induced destabilization scales with charge density and is amplified at neutral pH where the iM is inherently less stable. At pH 5.0, spermine induced the largest increase in RMSD and radius of gyration relative to the untreated control, indicating greater structural perturbation and reduced compactness of the iM (Figures 2A and 2B). Polyamine addition also modified the hydrogen bonding network and increased residue-level fluctuations across several regions of the structure (Figures 2C and 2D). Similar trends were observed at neutral pH, where spermine and putrescine promoted greater conformational variability compared with the control, as reflected by RMSD, Rg, hydrogen bond, and RMSF analyses (Figures 2E–2H). Representative structures extracted from the trajectories demonstrated marked polyamine-induced distortions of the folded iM architecture, particularly in the presence of spermine (Figure 2I). PCA further showed that polyamine-bound systems sampled broader conformational space than the untreated iM, consistent with enhanced structural flexibility and destabilization (Figure 2J). Together, these findings support a model in which polyamines disrupt iM structural integrity and promote conformational rearrangement.

Polyamines bind iMs with high affinity and greater uniformity

Molecular docking identified spermine and putrescine as the two polyamines with the most favorable and structurally selective interaction profiles at the iM fold across all DNA targets screened, motivating their prioritization for experimental SPR validation (Figures 1A–1E). Spermidine, by contrast, showed intermediate scores across all metrics and lacked a preferential structural signature and was, therefore, deprioritized for experimental follow-up. To biophysically investigate the influence of polyamines on DNA secondary structures, we performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements. These experiments utilized well-established genomic iM sequences (hTeloC, HIF-1A, BCL2, and VEGF-A) and G4 sequences, alongside control duplex DNA constructs11,15,20,21,22,23 (Table S1).

Both putrescine and spermine demonstrated measurable iM affinity by SPR, spanning a low-nanomolar to sub-micromolar range (KD∼26.5 to 572 nM across all eight iM measurements) (Figure 3A). Kinetic dissection revealed that they achieve comparable equilibrium affinities through mechanistically distinct routes: putrescine displayed faster association rates (KA up to 4.25 × 106 M−1s−1 at hTelo), while spermine compensated with substantially slower dissociation (KD as low as 0.002 s−1 at hTelo), indicating that its higher charge density stabilizes the bound complex primarily by retarding off-rate kinetics (Figures 3B and 3C; Table S3). The iMs also exhibited the lowest inter-locus variability of the three structure types, with a log10 standard deviation of 0.4681 compared with 1.3485 for G4 and 1.0041 for dsDNA, a 2.9-fold and 2.1-fold improvement in binding consistency, respectively, as visualized in the KD uniformity heatmaps (Figure 3D and 3E). For putrescine, iM affinity exceeded G4 affinity by at least one order of magnitude at HIF1A (KD(GQ)/KD(iM) = 33-fold; Δlog10 = +1.52) and was consistently tighter or comparable across BCL2 and VEGFA loci (Table S3). Spermine similarly engaged iM structures within the 27–300 nM range across BCL2, hTelo, and VEGFA, with the pronounced G4 preference at HIF1A (KD∼2.93 nM; log10KD∼ = 0.47) representing a sequence-context-specific exception rather than a general structural preference (Table S3). A Kruskal-Wallis test found no significant difference in median affinity between structural classes (H = 1.09, p = 0.58), reinforcing that the key discriminating feature is binding consistency rather than mean affinity (Figure 3D). Taken together, these SPR data provide direct kinetic and equilibrium-level evidence that both putrescine and spermine preferentially and consistently engage iM structures forming at selected sequences, supporting a model in which direct polyamine-iM interaction underlies an array of patho-physiological phenotypes.

Polyamines selectively destabilize DNA iM structures

To investigate the influence of polyamines on DNA secondary structures, we conducted circular dichroism (CD) and thermal melting analyses. Polyamine treatment consistently disrupted the iM CD signature, characterized by a positive peak at 280–290 nm and a minima near 260 nm24 (Figures 4A–4D). Spermidine and spermine exhibited the strongest disruptive effects on characteristic iM CD peaks, leading to complete loss of iM peaks and negating the necessity for thermal melting studies. While an equimolar concentration of putrescine alone did not completely disrupt the iM CD peaks, thermal melting analysis revealed measurable destabilization, with even the weakest effect resulting in a Tm reduction by 2.16°C, demonstrated by VEGFA iM treated with putrescine (Figures 4E–4H; Table 1). This indicated reduced iM stability and promoted unfolding in the presence of polyamines. In control experiments, the CD spectra of three promoter dsDNA constructs displayed hybrid signals due to their high G-C content, but showed no significant polyamine-induced alterations, supporting charge- and structure-specific polyamine binding to iMs (Figure S2). In contrast, polyamine treatment largely preserved G4 CD signatures and notably, increased the Tm of human telomeric G4s (Figures 1I–1L, S3, and S4; Tables 2 and S4). The observed selective destabilization of iMs, but not G4s, by polyamines, despite their formation on complementary DNA strands, represents a significant finding with implications for iM-specific genomic modulation and gene regulation.

Polyamines destabilize iMs in a concentration-dependent manner

The structural integrity of iMs was found to be highly sensitive to polyamine concentration. To assess polyamine effects on iMs and the differential impact of the three polyamines, we incubated each sequence with increasing polyamine concentrations (125 μM to levels fully disrupting the iM CD signal; Figures S5A–S5D). Spermine caused complete disruption at the lowest concentrations, followed by spermidine and putrescine respectively, indicating that the disruptive ability scales with size and charge. A more direct analysis focused on the negative ellipticity minima near 260 nm, a marker of iM integrity, by plotting ellipticity against concentration (Figure S6). The gradual, non-sigmoidal response supports a non-cooperative interaction model, where each polyamine independently destabilizes iM structures. Polyamines were found to attenuate iM structural integrity in a dose-dependent manner. These concentration-dependent, structure-specific effects suggest a mechanism for polyamine-mediated regulation of iM stability, wherein genomic formation of iM varies depending on the intracellular polyamine concentration.

Polyamine treatment reduces intranuclear iMs in human cell lines

To directly assess the impact of polyamine uptake on intranuclear iM folding, we employed iM-specific antibody (iMab)-based immunofluorescence. In U87MG human glioblastoma cells, 5 mM polyamine treatment led to a marked decrease in iMab fluorescence intensity, indicative of reduced iM formation. The trend was consistent for each one of the three mammalian polyamines (Figures 5A and 5B). These findings were further validated in Huh7 human hepatocellular carcinoma cells, where polyamine treatment similarly resulted in a significant reduction of intranuclear iM loci (Figures 5C and 5D). A consistent reduction was also observed in a non-cancerous HEK293T cell line (Figure S7). Our in cellulo observations thus support a model in which polyamines directly disrupt intranuclear iM structures, confirming the physiological relevance of our biophysical findings.

Putrescine-induced gene expression remodeling correlates with promoter iM content

To investigate the global impact of polyamines on gene expression, we performed transcriptomic analysis on U87MG cells treated with 5 mM putrescine for 24 h. Putrescine was selected for this analysis due to its lower cytotoxicity compared with spermidine and spermine (Figure S8). While spermine and spermidine demonstrated superior potency in disrupting iM structures in vitro, a consequence of their higher cationic charge and multivalency, their greater cytotoxicity precluded their use in cellular studies. Putrescine, the biosynthetic precursor to both, offered a critical advantage in the cellular context: its favorable viability profile permitted sustained treatment, enabling transcriptomic analysis free from cytotoxic confound. Putrescine thus serves as the physiologically tractable agent for studying the downstream gene-regulatory consequences of iM disruption by polyamines in living cells.1,25 The 5 mM putrescine concentration was chosen based on CD spectroscopy and MTT assays, as it preserved cell viability, while inducing significant destabilization to complete disruption of iMs (Figures S5, S6, and S8). PCA of the transcriptomic profiles distinctly separated the treated and control groups (Figure 6A), demonstrating significant gene expression remodeling following putrescine exposure. The top 1,000 genes from both differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and non-DEGs were selected for PIS analysis in their promoter regions (Figure 6B). DEGs were further classified into up- and downregulated categories, and promoter region sequence analysis highlighted a substantial correlation: the density of PISs was significantly higher in the highly regulated genes compared with those with minimal changes (Figure 6C).

Our data show that polyamines preferentially regulate PIS-rich genes. Genes with significant expression changes had nearly double the promoter PIS density compared with those with minimal changes (Figure 6D), and a greater proportion contained promoter PISs (Figure 6E). These findings emphasize the role of promoter PISs as a key regulatory feature associated with prominent gene expression changes. Last, qPCR validation indicated that putrescine selectively enhanced the expression of certain genes containing promoter iMs, such as HIF1A, BCl2, and VEGFA, while genes lacking a promoter PIS, like CA12, showed no significant changes (Figure 6E), reinforcing the influence of polyamines on transcription regulation through PIS sites. However, it is also to be noted that no significant differences were observed between the up- and downregulated gene groups regarding PIS density (Figures S9A and S9B), indicating that promoter PISs can lead to a bidirectional alteration in gene expression.

Discussion

This study uncovers a novel regulatory mechanism by which biogenic polyamines, namely, putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, selectively destabilize DNA iM structures, thereby modulating gene expression through direct physico-chemical interactions. Employing an integrated strategy encompassing molecular docking, biophysical characterization, cellular imaging, and transcriptomic analyses, we demonstrate that polyamines interact preferentially with iM conformations, altering their structural stability and influencing the genomic landscape.

Our in silico docking results predict robust and structure-specific binding of polyamines to iMs, exceeding their affinity for other DNA forms. MD simulation trajectories also revealed that polyamines induced pronounced conformational destabilization and structural collapse of the iM, a trend corroborated by SPR, which showed markedly greater uniformity in binding of polyamines to iM structures compared with their corresponding G4 and dsDNA. These predictions are validated by CD and thermal melting analyses, which confirm the selective and potent disruption of iM structures by polyamines. These findings support the growing evidence that iMs, long hypothesized to inhabit functional regions of the genome, act as dynamic regulatory elements in living cells. Notably, previous studies, including those by Zeraati et al., have established the in vivo existence of iMs in the nuclei of human cells, highlighting their physiological relevance.14,20,26

Recent studies have also shown the importance of iMs in various biological mechanisms.11,27,28 Our transcriptomic analysis further complements this hypothesis, as we found that genes exhibiting significant changes in expression upon putrescine treatment had a higher density of the promoter region PISs. Consistent with their established role as molecular switches, these structures may be modulated by small molecules such as polyamines to remodel gene expression.26 Our results align with a growing body of evidence suggesting a crucial regulatory role for iMs, which can act as dynamic switches within the genome.15,26 Their dynamic behavior allows iMs to respond to changes in the cellular environment, such as fluctuations in pH or metabolite concentrations, leading to corresponding changes in gene expression.

Our work thus establishes polyamines as previously unrecognized, structure-specific chemical modulators of iM DNA, showing contrasting effects compared with the minor stabilization reported by Molnar et al.3 This discrepancy possibly arises from the different methodological approach, wherein we utilized multiple biologically relevant genomic sequences and employed direct structure-specific biophysical assays revealing a stark decrease in thermal stability. In addition, our study encompasses the concentration-dependent nature of polyamine activity on iM structures such that the concentration is increased gradually until complete disruption of the iM peak is observed. Furthermore, our findings offer a preliminary link to hypothesize the impact of polyamine-induced alterations of genomic structure on gene expression. Despite the significance of these discoveries, limitations remain. Further genome-wide validations and detailed investigations, including reporter assays and chromatin immunoprecipitation, are necessary to clarify the biological scope and establish the causative relationship between polyamine-iM interactions and altered gene expression. Taken together, our study illuminates new dimensions of transcriptional control, further opening promising directions for therapeutic intervention by targeting these unique genomic switches and their physico-chemical interactions.

Material and methods

Computational modeling: Molecular docking

To investigate the interactions between polyamines and DNA structures, in silico molecular docking was conducted using HADDOCK 2.4 software. The structures of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), G4s, iMs, and duplex DNA were sourced from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Ligand structures for mammalian polyamines were generated in Python based on their SMILES representations. The nucleic acid models were identified using the following PDB codes: 2HY9 for G4, 8AYG for iM, 1D28 for duplex DNA (dsDNA), and one strand of 1BNA for ssDNA.27,29,30,31 For docking simulations, active residues in iMs and G4s were designated as cytosine (C) and guanine (G) tracts, respectively. The entire sequence of canonical B-form nucleic acid was considered interactive. Active ligand residues were defined based on solvent accessibility (greater than 15% relative solvent accessibility [RSA]) and proximity to surface neighbors (within ≤6.5 Å and ≥40% RSA), while buried residues were excluded.

Docking parameters included generating 1,000 rigid-body structures with five minimization trials, followed by 200 semi-flexible refinement structures and 200 explicit solvent refinements. OPLS (Optimized Potentials for Liquid Simulations) non-bonded parameters were used with water as the solvent, alongside center-of-mass and surface-contact restraints with a force constant of 1.0. Electrostatic energy was modeled using a constant dielectric (ε = 78.0). The final models were ranked by the HADDOCK score, with additional water analysis conducted post-solvent refinement. Clustering analysis was performed based on RMSD with a 1.5-Å cutoff and a minimum cluster size of four. Doxorubicin bound to dsDNA served as the positive control for both HADDOCK molecular docking and ViewDock analysis. HADDOCK scores for the largest cluster with the lowest RMSD value were plotted using GraphPad Prism, and statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t tests. Finally, the docked structures were analyzed with the ViewDock tool in Chimera to determine the HADDOCK-based binding energies.

Molecular dynamics simulation

MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2023.3 to investigate the interactions of three naturally occurring polyamines, spermine (+4), spermidine (+3), and putrescine (+2) at pH 5.0 and 7.0, resulting in eight 200-ns systems with controls. Protonation states were consistent with the pH dependence of cytosine (protonated at pH 5.0, neutral at pH 7.0) and the ligands were fully protonated. Systems were parameterized with AMBER99sb-ILDN for DNA and GAFF2 for ligands, solvated with TIP3P water, and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. Following energy minimization and equilibration under NVT/NPT (Number of particles, Volume, Temperature/Number of particles, Pressure, Temperature), production runs were performed under periodic boundary conditions with with long-range electrostatic interactions treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) electrostatics. The trajectories were analyzed for RMSD, radius of gyration, RMSF, hydrogen bonding, and PCA, which showed charge-dependent effects on iM stability. Spermine was found to promote more stable conformations compared with the more dynamic behavior observed with spermidine.

Surface plasmon resonance

Binding interactions between polyamines and oligonucleotides were studied using a Biacore X100 instrument (Cytiva) equipped with a CM5 sensor chip. Putrescine, spermidine, and spermine were immobilized on the chip surface by amine coupling. Samples were injected at concentrations ranging from 1.25 to 20 μM in 50 mM sodium cacodylate buffer containing 100 mM KCl (pH 5.0) at a flow rate of 30 μL/min. The association and dissociation times were 180 and 300 s, respectively. The sensor surface was regenerated using 10 mM glycine-HCl (pH 1.5). Kinetic constants were calculated from the sensorgrams using the 1:1 fit model using BIA Evaluation 2.0.1 (Cytiva) software.

Biophysical characterization: Circular dichroism spectroscopy

CD spectroscopy was employed to evaluate the effects of polyamines on DNA secondary structure. Samples were prepared containing 10 μM oligonucleotides (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT) in a buffer of 10 mM sodium cacodylate and 100 mM KCl at pH 5.0. This acidic pH was chosen to study the stability of iMs, allowing for the assessment of polyamines’ destabilizing effects while iMs were in a state of maximal stability. For the preparation of duplex DNA (dsDNA), equimolar volumes of PISs and PGSs were mixed, and the buffer pH was adjusted to physiological levels (pH 7.2). Samples were then heated to 95°C for 5 min and slowly cooled to facilitate the formation and annealing of secondary structures. Polyamines were added after the samples cooled to 37°C. CD spectra were recorded using a JASCO 815 spectrophotometer equipped with a Peltier temperature controller and a 1-mm quartz cell. Measurements of ellipticity were taken from 320 to 220 nm, incorporating baseline corrections for buffer contributions. Three accumulations were performed for each sample, and the final results were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

Thermodynamic analysis: Thermal melting studies

Thermal melting analyses were performed to evaluate the thermal stability of DNA structures in the presence of polyamines. Samples were prepared as described for CD spectroscopy. Readings were taken on a JASCO815 spectrophotometer with a Peltier temperature controller and a quartz cell of 1 mm optical path length.

Thermal melting curves were generated from 20°C to 95°C, with ellipticity values plotted against temperature using GraphPad Prism. A Boltzmann equation was used to fit the experimental data via non-linear regression and to calculate the melting temperature (Tm) for each sample.

Cell culture and cytotoxicity assay

U87MG (human glioblastoma) and Huh7 (human hepatocellular carcinoma) cell lines were cultured in DMEM (HiMedia) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To identify the optimal polyamine concentration for cell-based studies, a cytotoxicity assay was conducted. Huh7 (10,000 cells/well) and U87MG (15,000 cells/well) cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with varying concentrations of putrescine, spermidine, and spermine (0–30 mM) for 24 h. Cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay with a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. After a 1-h incubation, formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a reference reading at 630 nm using a Cytation Plate Reader.

Visualization of iMs: Immunofluorescence microscopy

Immunofluorescence assays were conducted to visualize the impact of polyamines on intracellular iM structures. U87MG and Huh7 cells were seeded onto coverslips in 35-mm2 cell culture dishes and incubated for 24 h at 37°C under 5% CO2. HEK293T cells were used for non-cancer cell line control. Cells were pre-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 min, followed by washing with 1X PBS. Cells were then fixed with 2% PFA in PBS for 1 h at 4°C, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% PBST (Triton X-100) for 30–45 min at 4°C. Cells were treated with 5 mM spermine, spermidine, and putrescine for 1 h at room temperature, with PBS used as a control. After washing with PBS, cells were blocked with 3% BSA in 0.5% PBST for 2 h at room temperature.

Cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with iMab (Absolute Antibody, AB01462-30.135 Anti-iM DNA) primary antibody (1:100 dilution in 3% BSA). Following PBS washes, cells were incubated with secondary rabbit anti-FLAG antibody (CST DYDDDK Tag Rabbit mAB) (1:500 dilution) for 4 h at room temperature, followed by anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 (1:500 dilution) for 2 h. Cells were treated with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 10 min, washed with PBS, and mounted onto glass slides using mounting media. Images were acquired using a Radical RTC7-NXF epifluorescence microscope, and fluorescence intensities within the nucleus were quantified using ImageJ software. The variances were analyzed using a Student’s two-tailed t test.

Transcriptome-wide analysis: RNA sequencing and bioinformatics

For differential gene expression analysis, whole-transcriptome sequencing was conducted using an Illumina platform. U87MG cells were treated with 5 mM putrescine for 24 h, and transcriptomic samples, treated and untreated, were collected in TRIzol and sent in triplicate to Unipath Speciality Laboratories. 5 mM putrescine was selected based on the results obtained from studying concentration-dependent iM dynamics and cell cytotoxicity assay. RNA quality was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis, Qubit fluorometer, and NanoDrop absorbance measurements. Raw FASTQ files were assessed for quality using FastQC (v.0.11.8). Adapters and low-quality reads were trimmed using Fastp (v.0.23.4) with default parameters. Alignment was performed using STAR (v.2.7.9a) and HISAT2 (v.2.2.3) with the GRCh38.p.14 human genome assembly, and an R2 value > 0.99 validated alignment consistency, leading to downstream analysis using HISAT2 results. Transcripts were quantified with featureCounts (v.2.0.3), and DESeq2 (v.1.42.0) was used for normalization and differential expression analysis. Wald tests compared putrescine-treated samples with untreated controls, identifying DEGs based on |log2Fold Change| ≥0.5 and α < 0.05.

To identify PISs in promoter regions of genes, a python-based analysis was performed. We retrieved gene symbols from the dataset and obtained promoter sequences using the Ensembl REST API. For each gene, promoter regions were defined based on the transcription start site (TSS) location i.e., −1,000 to +100 of the TSS, providing us with a uniform promoter length of 1,101 bases. A regular expression-based pattern search was applied to detect cytosine-rich sequences (C{3,5}[ATGC]{1,7}C{3,5}[ATGC]{1,7}C{3,5}[ATGC]{1,7}C{3,5}) within the extracted sequences. The number of PISs, locations, and promoter lengths were recorded, and results were compiled in a CSV file for further analysis (Tables S5 and S6). This regular expression pattern was selected because iM structures require at least four polycytidine tracks separated by loops of ≤10 nucleotides, with experimental evidence showing that cytosine tracts of 3–5 bases represent the minimum length required for iM folding at near-physiological conditions.24 Genes with detected PIS motifs were counted, providing insights into the potential regulatory role of these structures. From the CSV files obtained, PIS densities in the promoter region were calculated as [(Number of promoter region PIS)/(Length of promoter region)]∗1,000.

The results and the variances between the datasets were further analyzed using a Mann-Whitney U test owing to their non-Gaussian distribution.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis: Quantitative PCR

To assess the effect of polyamines on gene expression, RNA was extracted from U87MG cells exposed to 5 mM putrescine for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol-chloroform method and quantified using an Implen N60 NanoPhotometer. RNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized using a Thermo cDNA Synthesis Kit, and RT-qPCR was performed using TAKARA Sybr Mix on a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System. Transcript fold changes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with 18S rRNA serving as the housekeeping gene. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. The variances between transcript levels of the genes were measured using a Student’s t test.
