Multimodal atlas of single neuron metabolic electrophysiological coupling uncovers circadian rewiring
Authors: Man Yuan, Siyuan Ge, Wenwei Qian, Chenjian Miao, Wei Liang, Qi Chen, Hongying Zhu, Wei Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeuronal metabolism fundamentally modulates synaptic activity, yet how single-cell metabolic architecture aligns with electrophysiological diversity remains elusive. By integrating patch-clamp electrophysiology with single-neuron mass spectrometry (SNMS), we resolve metabolomic heterogeneity across suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) neurons, revealing six metabolic states with distinct synaptic dynamics, from lipid-enriched SCN1 exhibiting high-frequency presynaptic activity to quiescent SCN6. Pathway analysis linked metabolic state-specific signatures to sulfur metabolism, glutathione regulation, and citrate cycle dynamics. Machine learning and correlation networks mapped metabolites to functional parameters: histidine, carnitine, and creatinine regulated neuronal activity, validated by intracellular metabolite delivery experiments. Strikingly, light-dark cycles dynamically reconfigured most of the metabolite-postsynaptic current correlations, including light-induced taurine coupling to synaptic transmission. This multimodal platform establishes cellular metabolism as a tunable factor associated with neuronal heterogeneity and circadian plasticity, suggesting potential therapeutic avenues for circadian disorders.Subject areas: cellular physiology, neuroscience, metabolomics

Introduction

The mammalian brain comprises billions of neurons,1 each integrating thousands of synaptic inputs2 to form functional networks. Synaptic activity, reflecting neuronal physiology, can be quantified through postsynaptic currents (PSCs) recorded via whole-cell patch clamp.3 Key parameters include rise time (Tr), decay time (Td), amplitude (Iamp), area under the curve (Iauc), and event frequency (Freq), collectively defining synaptic integration dynamics.

Crucially, synaptic efficacy hinges on postsynaptic components (e.g., ion channels and dendritic spines) whose function is metabolically regulated. Diverse metabolites modulate ion channel activity. For instance, lipids and fatty acids exert bidirectional effects, inhibiting ATP-sensitive K+ (KATP) channels4 while activating two-pore domain K+ (K2P) channels5; cholesterol alters membrane microenvironments to regulate transient receptor potential (TRP) channels6,7; phosphoinositides gate TRP mucolipin channels8; polyunsaturated fatty acids (e.g., DHA and EPA) suppress voltage-gated K+ and Na+ currents.9,10,11 Such metabolite-channel interactions position cellular metabolism as a fundamental modulator of synaptic signaling.

While neuronal heterogeneity in morphology and electrophysiology is established across cortical, hippocampal, and hypothalamic regions,12,13,14 single-cell transcriptomics (e.g., Patch-seq15) further revealed molecular diversity.16,17,18,19 However, the metabolomic basis of electrophysiological heterogeneity remains unexplored, primarily due to technical limitations in concurrent metabolomic-electrophysiological profiling at single-cell resolution. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the central circadian pacemaker, exemplifies functional diversity. Its subregions contain neurons with distinct neurochemical phenotypes20 and GABAergic synaptic dynamics,21 while metabolic variations correlate with circadian phases.22,23,24 This makes the SCN an ideal model for probing metabolism-electrophysiology relationships.

The development of single-cell mass spectrometry technology, from early capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry coupling analyses25,26 to later spatially resolved MALDI mass spectrometry imaging,27 laid the groundwork for investigating metabolic heterogeneity at the cellular level. Advances in instrument performance in recent years have enabled the acquisition of more comprehensive quantitative metabolomic data from individual mammalian neurons. Furthermore, methods that combined patch-clamp electrophysiological recordings with subsequent mass spectrometry analysis were also reported.28,29,30,31 In this study, building on these prior developments, we established and optimized an experimental protocol by integrating induced nanoESI/MS technology with whole-cell patch-clamp recording techniques.32,33

This approach uncovers metabolically defined states and establishes correlative links between metabolites and electrophysiological parameters. It reveals light-dark cycle-dependent reconfiguration of these relationships, providing a foundational framework for the metabolic regulation of neuronal diversity and circadian plasticity.

Results

Metabolic heterogeneity in SCN neurons revealed by single-neuron mass spectrometry

To investigate the relationship between neuronal activity and metabolism at the level of individual cells, we have introduced a cutting-edge platform known as single-neuron mass spectrometry (SNMS).32 By integrating patch clamp recording with mass spectrometry techniques, this approach empowers us to acquire pairwise information of electrophysiological characteristics and metabolomic profiles of neurons within brain slices (Figure 1A).

973 neurons were randomly collected from the SCN of the mouse hypothalamus, ranging from zeitgeber time (ZT) 0 to ZT10 (where ZT0 is defined as lights-on, the onset of the light period). This dataset served as the basis for defining metabolic states via unsupervised clustering and building the primary metabolic-electrophysiological association models. Within the m/z range of 70–750, 948 ion signals were detected at a Freq exceeding 20% in all neurons, with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) greater than 3. A comprehensive identification process led to the recognition of 350 metabolites based upon the human metabolic database (HMDB) and Reference Set of Metabolite Names (RefMet). These metabolites primarily encompassed amino acids such as GABA, glutamine, glutamate, histidine, arginine, and aspartic acid; energy compounds such as creatine, creatinine, and adenosine diphosphate (ADP); lipids including phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and PG; as well as amino acid derivatives such as spermidine, taurine, and hypotaurine. For each neuron, PSCs over a 1-min period were simultaneously recorded, and five features were calculated to represent the electrophysiological activity: Iamp, Freq, Tr, Td, and Iauc (Figure 1A).

To investigate the relationship between neuronal activity and the cellular metabolome at the single-neuron level, we paired electrophysiological data, encompassing Iamp, Freq, Tr, Td, and Iauc of PSCs, with the metabolomic profiles of the same individual neurons. The metabolomics data underwent preprocessing, which included peak alignment, noise filtering, and other procedures, resulting in a numeric matrix that encompassed all neurons and their corresponding mass spectral features. Similarly, the electrophysiological data were preprocessed through baseline alignment and background subtraction, yielding another numeric matrix that contained all the electrophysiological features of single neurons. These two matrices, representing single-cell metabolome and electrophysiology, were intertwined. Subsequently, we leveraged the metabolomic profiles of individual neurons for unsupervised clustering, facilitating a thorough comparison of the distinct electrophysiological features exhibited by each cluster. Correlation analysis was then conducted on both datasets, aligning them based on the metabolomic and electrophysiological features of every single neuron (Figure S1).

Principal component analysis (PCA) (Figure S2A) and hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA)34,35 (Figure S2B) revealed no discernible batch effects. The SCN neurons were clustered into six metabolic states (SCN1-6) based on their metabolome using Seurat36,37 in an unsupervised manner (Figure 1B). To assess whether the metabolic state clustering was influenced by sampling time, we analyzed the distribution of collection times across the six metabolic states in the primary dataset (n = 973 neurons). Neurons from all time points were represented across all clusters, with no significant enrichment of any specific ZT within a given state (Figure S3). These results confirm that the metabolic state clustering is independent of sampling time. Subsequently, we applied a support vector machine (SVM) algorithm to evaluate the performance of the unsupervised clustering (Figure S4A). The accuracy of the SVM model was 0.81, and the AUC for each cluster was above 0.96 (Figures S4B and S4C). To elucidate the metabolomic characteristics unique to each neuronal metabolic state, a comparative analysis was performed to ascertain the relative abundance of metabolites across the metabolic states. This was achieved through Student’s t test and fold change (FC) calculations for differential analysis. Metabolites with an FC > 1.5 and an adjusted p value <0.05 (Bonferroni correction) were selected as characteristic metabolomic markers38 (Figures 1C and S5).

Notably, the SCN1 cluster exhibited a substantial enrichment of lipids, such as PG, diradylglycerol (DG), phosphatidylserine (PS), and phosphorylcholine (PC) (Figures 1C and S6). The SCN2 cluster was abundant in organic acids and organic nitrogen compounds, including carnitine, creatine, and creatinine (Figures 1C and S6). The SCN3 cluster was distinguished by the presence of mevalonic acid, niacinamide, and pantothenic acid (Figures 1C and S6), which are pivotal in energy metabolism and redox balance.39,40,41 The SCN4 cluster featured amino acids and organic acids such as glutamine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, taurine, and phosphocreatine (Figures 1C and S6). Glucosamine, succinyladenosine, and 2-hydroxyadipic acid were enriched in the SCN5 cluster (Figures 1C and S6). Lastly, the SCN6 cluster demonstrated a significant presence of organic acids, including lactic acid, succinic acid, and glutaconic acid, along with amino acids such as methionine, lysine, and hypotaurine (Figures 1C and S6). Furthermore, lipids such as triacylglycerol (TG) and PA were also observed to be abundant within the SCN6 cluster (Figure 1C).

SCN neurons classified by metabolomics correspond to distinct electrophysiological phenotypes

We next analyzed the metabolic pathways enriched in each subpopulation of SCN neurons using metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA)42 based on KEGG (Figure 2A). The “sulfur metabolism” pathway, associated with the biosynthesis of the amino acids and vitamins such as cysteine, methionine, cystathionine, and thiamine,43 was specifically enriched in the SCN1 cluster. The “taurine and hypotaurine metabolism” pathway was specifically enriched in the SCN3 cluster. This pathway has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in neurotransmission across various brain regions, such as the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, cerebellum, brain stem, and hypothalamus,44,45 thereby highlighting its significance in neural communication and physiological regulation. The metabolic pathways specific to the cluster SCN6 included energy metabolism, such as the “citrate cycle” pathway, which provided metabolites that could chelate Ca2+, Mg2+, and Zn2+, functioning as an endogenous regulator of glutamate receptors.46 “Glutathione metabolism” was particularly enriched in the neurons of SCN4 and SCN6. The metabolites within this pathway have the potential to modulate the architecture and functionality of a variety of ion channels, including but not limited to KATP channels and Kir4-Kir5 channels.47 Additionally, the pathway for “arginine biosynthesis” is also prominently featured in the neurons of SCN4 and SCN6, and this pathway has been reported to directly or indirectly engage in significant biological phenomena such as vasodilation, calcium ion release, adenosine triphosphate regeneration, neurotransmission, and cell proliferation.48

Intriguingly, our electrophysiological profiling revealed a functional mosaicism across SCN metabolic subpopulations (Figure 2B). Neuronal cluster SCN1 emerged as particularly noteworthy, demonstrating both the highest spontaneous release Freq and quantal content (Iauc) among all groups - findings that vividly illustrate its exceptionally active presynaptic terminals (Figure 2B). The postsynaptic landscape showed equally compelling contrasts: SCN3 neurons possessed remarkably amplified current Iamp, while their SCN4 counterparts exhibited the most attenuated responses, suggestive of fundamental differences in either ion channel density or operational efficiency (Figure 2B). Dynamic channel kinetics further distinguished these populations, with SCN5 neurons displaying the briefest Tr value (indicative of rapid channel activation49), whereas SCN2 cells paradoxically combined prolonged Tr (sluggish activation) with remarkably compressed Td (rapid deactivation) alongside minimal quantal release (Figure 2B). Interestingly, the SCN6 subpopulation presented an electrophysiological paradox - exhibiting both the lowest firing Freq and most lethargic signal decay kinetics - characteristics aligning with a functionally quiescent neuronal phenotype (Figure 2B). In addition, the distribution analysis using kernel density estimation50 revealed marked variations in electrophysiological features across six subpopulations, indicating distinct synaptic activity patterns (Figures 2C–2G). These results demonstrate substantial heterogeneity in SCN neuronal subpopulations, suggesting a metabolome-electrophysiology link at the single-cell level.

Machine learning predicts electrophysiology from metabolomic profiles

We established a predictive modeling framework to further verify the relationship between cellular metabolic states and electrophysiological characteristics (Figure 3A). The single-neuron metabolomic dataset was stratified into training (70%) and validation (30%) cohorts using randomized partitioning, preserving population heterogeneity in both subsets. Model development incorporated Bayesian hyperparameter optimization with 5-fold cross-validation to prevent overfitting while maximizing predictive accuracy.

Regression models based on the single-neuron metabolome predict electrophysiological features

A 5-fold cross-validation was applied to the training set “A,” splitting it into subsets “A1-A5.” In each fold, 4 subsets were used for training (light blue), and the remaining subset served as the validation set (green) to compute performance metrics. The final model performance was derived by averaging validation results across all folds. The optimal model was then tested on the test set “B,” with performance evaluated via a prediction-measured plot and correlation coefficient calculation.

The finalized model demonstrated robust performance across all five electrophysiological parameters when evaluated against the independent validation set (Figures 3B–3F). Predicted values showed statistically significant correlations with experimental measurements. This systematic validation confirms that intrinsic metabolic signatures contain sufficient information to reconstruct key functional properties of neurons, establishing a quantitative link between biochemical composition and electrophysiological phenotype at single-cell resolution.

Metabolome-electrophysiology correlations in SCN neurons

The significant heterogeneity observed among SCN neurons suggests diverse functional roles and activity levels within this population.51,52 Building upon prior findings that categorized SCN neurons into six distinct metabolic states based on their metabolomic profiles, each possessing unique electrophysiological signatures, this raised the question of whether characteristic metabolites for each metabolic state correlate with its specific electrophysiological properties. To address this, we extracted the electrophysiological and metabolomic features from each metabolic state and calculated their correlations using Pearson’s r, aiming to identify integrated, network-wide correlations between the metabolome and electrophysiology across these distinct metabolic states. Metabolites demonstrating a correlation coefficient (r) greater than 0.8 with a statistical significance (p value) less than 0.05 were deemed associated with electrophysiological activities.

Our correlation analysis revealed intricate and diverse associations between electrophysiological parameters and metabolic signatures across the six metabolic states. For example, the Iamp showed predominantly inhibitory associations, exhibiting negative correlations with intracellular levels of 85 metabolites, including key regulators such as creatine, methionine, taurine, and adenosine monophosphate (AMP), while displaying only 2 positive correlations (Figure 4A). Tr presented a similarly skewed profile, correlating positively with just one metabolite but negatively with 43 others, such as glucosamine, lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) (Figure 4B). Td demonstrated a more balanced interaction profile, associating positively with 16 metabolites (e.g., alanine, spermidine, N6, N6, and N6-trimethyl-L-lysine) and negatively with 15 (including asparagine, histidine, arginine, tyrosine, tryptophan) (Figure 4C). Iauc exhibited extensive bidirectional modulation, showing 33 positive correlations with metabolites such as glyceric acid 1,3-biphosphate and diacylglyceryl-3-O-carboxyhydroxymethylcholine (DGCC), counterbalanced by 31 negative correlates such as creatinine, niacinamide, mevalonic acid, carnitine, glycerophosphocholine, and O-phosphotyrosine (Figure 4D). Freq displayed a strong positive bias, linking to 24 metabolites positively (including urea, ST, and serine) compared to only 3 negative associations (Figure 4E). Notably, several electrophysiology-linked metabolites identified in this analysis, including taurine, carnitine, and serine, known regulators of neuronal activity, ion channel function, and membrane potential, align with prior findings.53,54,55

Through systematic classification of network-associated metabolites, we identified distinct correlational profiles between metabolite categories and specific electrophysiological parameters (Figures 5B and 5C). For example, Tr exhibited negative correlations with carbohydrate-related compounds (including “carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates”), and oxidized lysophosphatidylglycerols (Ox-LPG). Iamp demonstrated significant negative associations with nitrogen-containing biomolecules (“amino acids, peptides, and analogues”). Notably, Iauc manifested broad-spectrum positive regulation across the metabolome while maintaining specific negative correlation with amino acid derivatives. Dual regulatory mechanisms emerged in Td dynamics, where both inhibitory and enhancing effects were principally mediated by amino acid-related metabolites. In contrast to this multifaceted regulation pattern, Freq displayed exclusive negative correlation with carbohydrate metabolites (“carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates”), coupled with predominant positive regulation by the amino acid category. Of particular significance, “amino acids, peptides, and analogues”emerged as the metabolite class most frequently associated with electrophysiological parameters in this network, suggesting their potential central position within the electrophysiological association network.

The metabolic states of SCN neurons exhibited a remarkable diversity in intracellular metabolomics, which displayed a strong correlation with distinct electrophysiological characteristics. A pertinent inquiry arises as to whether the pivotal metabolites associated with the heterogeneity of electrophysiological features in SCN neurons originate from specific metabolic pathways. We therefore dissect the metabolic processes associated with each electrophysiological characteristic of SCN neurons using MSEA42 based on KEGG. Initially, we scrutinized the metabolites that exhibited a negative correlation with electrophysiological features and unearthed a plethora of metabolic pathways (with p < 0.05 in MSEA) linked to diverse biological processes (Figure 5D). For instance, the Iamp was related to the pathways related to redox balance such as “glutathione metabolism,” which influences the architecture and operational capabilities of ion channels, including KATP channels and Kir4-Kir5 channels, via the process of S-glutathionylation.47 “The pentose phosphate pathway,” which affects the activity of voltage-gated K+ (Kv) channels by influencing NAD and NADH,57 was related with Tr. In addition, although much less, the pathways (with p < 0.05 in MSEA) related to metabolites in positive correlation with Freq, Iauc, and Td were also dissected (Figure 5E). “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis” was related to Iauc, which is in line with the pivotal role of glycolysis in maintaining the normal function of neurons by maintaining ATP levels at synapses.58 “Selenocompound metabolism” and “beta-alanine metabolism” were in relation to Td. Beta-alanine increases the membrane conductance of neurons by activating chloride ion channels.59 Freq was positively correlated to “arginine biosynthesis,” which is involved in key processes such as neurotransmission, vasodilation, ATP regeneration, calcium ion release, and cell proliferation.48

Key metabolites modulate SCN neuron electrophysiology

Due to the strong interdependence between metabolism and electrophysiological characteristics in SCN neurons, we have designed a series of experiments to explore their causal relationship. A viable strategy involves elevating the intracellular concentration of specific metabolites in SCN neurons via patch-clamp pipettes. Upon membrane rupture, the metabolites within the pipette can diffuse into the neuron, while we simultaneously record PSCs.

Histidine, carnitine, and creatinine were selected as pivotal metabolites to examine their regulatory impact on electrophysiological characteristics in SCN neurons for the following reasons: 1) The three metabolites exhibited significant correlations with specific electrophysiological parameters. Specifically, histidine displayed a negative correlation with Td, carnitine showed a negative correlation with both Iauc and Iamp, and creatinine demonstrated a negative correlation with Iauc (Figure 5A); 2) These metabolites were identified as potential indicators of the metabolic states of SCN neurons. For example, carnitine and creatinine served as metabolic markers for the SCN2 metabolic state, while histidine exhibited the lowest level in the SCN4 metabolic state (Figure 1C); 3) Neuronal metabolic states enriched with these specific metabolites showcased distinctive electrophysiological characteristics. For instance, among all six metabolic states, the SCN2 metabolic state exhibited the lowest Td and Iauc, and the SCN4 metabolic state exhibited the lowest Iamp and the highest Tr (Figure 2A).

In SCN neurons, Td peaked following the intracellular application of a low histidine concentration (0.25 ppm/1.61 μM) and progressively declined as histidine levels increased (1 ppm/6.45 μM; 2 ppm/12.89 μM) (Figures 6A and 6B). This pattern mirrored the inverse relationship between histidine and Td observed in Figures 4C and 5A. Histidine delivery also influenced other parameters, including Iamp, Freq, and Tr, but not Iauc (Figures 6C–6F).

Increasing carnitine concentrations decreased both Iauc and Iamp in SCN neurons (Figures S7A–S7C), consistent with the negative correlations observed between carnitine and both Iamp and Iauc (Figures 4A, 4D, and 5A). Conversely, carnitine exerted no effect on the other parameters examined (Figures S7D–S7F).

The highest Iauc occurred at a low creatinine concentration and progressively diminished with increasing creatinine levels (Figures S8A and S8B), aligning with the negative correlation between creatinine and Iauc shown in Figures 4D and 5A. Furthermore, Iamp and Td were also modulated by creatinine concentration (Figures S8C and S8D), whereas Freq and Tr remained unaffected by changes in creatinine (Figures S8E and S8F). Collectively, these results confirm the direct regulatory roles of these three metabolites on neuronal activities within SCN neurons.

Light-dark cycle rewires metabolic-electrophysiological coupling

Since the SCN is a key regulator of circadian rhythms, we then investigated how light-dark cycle phases influence the correlation between neuronal metabolites and electrophysiological properties. To enable a controlled and reliable comparison between light and dark phases, an additional cohort of 227 neurons was collected from the same batch of animals. This dedicated dataset comprised 107 neurons sampled during the light phase (day group) and 120 neurons sampled during the dark phase (night group), and was used exclusively for differential analyses of day/night metabolic and electrophysiological profiles. PCA (Figures S9A and S10A) and HCA (Figures S9B and S10B) confirmed minimal batch effects across sample batches.

Comparison of metabolite intensities between the night and day groups revealed 89 differentially abundant metabolites: 37 upregulated during the day and 52 upregulated at night. This diurnal variation reveals distinct neuronal metabolic states, with metabolite levels differing across the light-dark cycle, suggesting potential variations in metabolic regulation. Night-upregulated metabolites included creatine, glutamine, and carnitine (Figure 7A). Elevated carnitine levels potentially reflect increased neuronal demand for fatty acid oxidation and energy metabolism during the night.60 Day-upregulated metabolites included creatinine and O-phosphotyrosine (Figure 7A), with increased creatinine possibly linked to light-phase energy balance and creatine metabolism.61

Electrophysiological analysis of PSCs showed significant differences between groups. The Night group exhibited a significantly increased Iauc compared to the day group (Figure 7B). Td and Iamp also trended higher at night, though not significantly, while Tr and Freq showed no significant differences (Figure 7B).

To validate the stability of these states across the light-dark cycle, we applied a classifier trained on the primary metabolic dataset. Specifically, an SVM model was trained on the metabolic profiles of the 973 neurons (Figure S4) and subsequently used to classify an independent cohort of 107 Day and 120 Night neurons. The classifier readily identified all metabolic states in both the day and night groups. Comparing PSC electrophysiological properties of these states at night (Figure 7C) with their counterparts during the preceding light phase (Figure 2B) revealed both shared and distinct characteristics. For instance, SCN4 neurons consistently showed the lowest Iamp in both phases. SCN1 neurons exhibited the largest Iauc in both phases, and SCN6 neurons had the lowest Freq in both phases. However, SCN2 neurons displayed low Iauc during the light phase but high Iauc at night. Conversely, SCN6 neurons showed high Td during the light phase but low Td at night. These findings suggest the heterogeneous regulation of electrophysiological-metabolomic coupling in SCN neurons by the light-dark cycle.

Correlation analysis between metabolites and PSC features in the night group (Figure 7D), compared to light-phase data (Figure 5A), identified light-dark cycle-dependent associations. During the light phase, GABA, asparagine, spermine, taurine, and creatine negatively correlated with Iamp, but these correlations were absent at night (Figures 5A and 7D). Creatinine, carnitine, and O-phosphotyrosine negatively correlated with Iauc during the light phase, but not at night (Figures 5A and 7D). Glyceric acid 1,3-bisphosphate positively correlated with Iauc during the light phase, a relationship not observed at night (Figures 5A and 7D). Conversely, at night, ATP negatively correlated with Iamp, lysine negatively correlated with Tr, and beta-glycerophosphoric acid negatively correlated with Td, whereas these correlations were absent during the light phase (Figures 5A and 7D).

Several metabolites correlated with different electrophysiological features depending on the light-dark cycle (Figures 5A and 7D). For example, glutamine negatively correlated with Iamp during the light phase but positively correlated with Freq at night (Figures 5A and 7D). Histidine, arginine, and tyrosine negatively correlated with Td during the light phase but positively correlated with Tr at night (Figures 5A and 7D). Tryptophan negatively correlated with Td during the light phase but positively correlated with Iauc at night (Figures 5A and 7D). This indicates that the association patterns between specific metabolites and synaptic activity metrics are reconfigured across the light-dark cycle.

We hypothesize that metabolites both differentially expressed and correlated with electrophysiological features may critically underlie the observed differences between Night and Day groups (Figures 5A, 7A, and 7D). Thirty-five metabolites differed between groups and correlated with electrophysiological features during the light phase (Figures 5A and 7A). Nineteen metabolites differed between groups and correlated with electrophysiological features at night (Figures 7A and 7D). For instance, carnitine (upregulated at night) negatively correlated with Iamp and Iauc during the light phase but showed no correlation at night (Figures 7A and 7D). Creatinine (decreased at night) negatively correlated with Iauc during the light phase but showed no correlation at night. Glutamine (increased at night) negatively correlated with Iamp during the light phase and positively correlated with Freq at night. These metabolites represent potential key factors associated with the distinct electrophysiological characteristics of SCN neurons under day and night conditions.

Discussion

The inability to resolve metabolomic and electrophysiological features in single neurons has obscured the connections between cellular metabolism and neuronal activity. By integrating SNMS with patch-clamp electrophysiology, we reveal a metabolomic architecture associated with electrophysiological diversity in SCN neurons. We identify six metabolomically distinct states, establish correlative and functional metabolite-electrophysiology relationships, and demonstrate light-dark cycle-dependent reconfiguration of these interactions. This work provides a foundational framework for metabolic processes related to neuronal heterogeneity.

SCN neurons exhibited pronounced metabolomic stratification, segregating into six metabolic states defined by discrete metabolite enrichments. These metabolic states align with specialized functional roles. For instance, SCN1 (lipid-enriched: PG, DG, PC) showed the highest synaptic vesicle release Freq and quantal content (Iauc), consistent with heightened presynaptic activity. PG, a cardiolipin precursor critical for mitochondrial integrity,62 may sustain this hyperactivity by bolstering energy production. SCN2 (organic acids-enriched: carnitine, creatine) displayed minimal Iauc and rapid current decay (Td), indicative of metabolic constraints on synaptic efficacy. SCN3 (redox cofactors-enriched: mevalonate, niacinamide) exhibited amplified current Iamp, suggesting enhanced postsynaptic receptor density or conductance. SCN6 (organic acids-enriched: methionine, lysine) demonstrated low Freq and prolonged Td, reflecting a quiescent state of neuronal activity. This metabolomic taxonomy may complement transcriptomic classifications. Future multi-omics studies may clarify whether metabolomic states map onto established transcriptomic frameworks.

In this study, we employed a machine learning framework to investigate the relationship between single-neuron metabolomic profiles and electrophysiological characteristics. By integrating Bayesian hyperparameter optimization with 5-fold cross-validation, the predictive model we developed successfully forecasted five key electrophysiological features of neurons with statistically significant correlations. However, it is noteworthy that when we attempted to construct the inverse model, using electrophysiological features to predict metabolite levels, the predictive accuracy was markedly reduced. This asymmetry in unidirectional prediction likely stems from the high dimensionality of the metabolomic data relative to the comparatively limited number of electrophysiological features. Future studies incorporating a broader array of electrophysiological measurements may enable a more comprehensive characterization of the bidirectional relationship between these two modalities.

We have discovered a diverse range of metabolites that exhibit correlation with electrophysiological features in SCN neurons. Glyceric acid 1,3-bisphosphate (1,3-BPG), a glycolytic intermediate positively correlated with Iauc, its conversion via phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) generates ATP63,64,65—the primary energy source for synaptic vesicle exocytosis and neurotransmitter release. This ATP production supports intracellular calcium signaling,66,67 thereby maintaining calcium homeostasis and facilitating neurotransmission. Conversely, taurine’s negative correlation with Iamp aligns with its role in mitigating calcium overload68 and oxidative stress.69,70

Beyond correlative relationships, causal experiments confirmed that metabolites directly tune electrophysiological parameters. For example, carnitine (negatively correlated with Iamp and Iauc) suppressed both parameters. Carnitine exists in cells in the forms of free carnitine and acylcarnitines.60 Carnitine promotes ATP production by transporting long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria for β-oxidation.60 Carnitine can alter the affinity between glutamate and metabotropic glutamate receptors to prevent glutamate-induced neurotoxicity.71 By inhibiting protein kinase C and regulating the activity of NADPH oxidase, carnitine reduces the production of superoxide anions, thus lowering oxidative stress.72 Long-chain acylcarnitines, such as palmitoylcarnitine, can interact with the cell membrane to affect membrane fluidity and the activity of membrane enzymes and transport proteins.60 Additionally, long-chain acylcarnitines activate protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B to reduce the phosphorylation of insulin receptors and protein kinase B.73 Acetylcarnitine has various effects such as reducing oxidative stress, stabilizing membrane structure, regulating protein and gene expression, and enhancing cholinergic neurotransmission.60 Therefore, carnitine/acylcarnitines may regulate neuronal activity through the regulation of energy metabolism, the regulation of glutamate receptors, and the regulation of protein kinase C activity, the maintenance of membrane stability. Histidine (anti-correlated with Td) shortened decay kinetics, potentially through histamine biosynthesis.74 Creatinine (anti-correlated with Iauc), derived from creatine and phosphocreatine metabolism,61 exerts an influence on energy balance and creatine metabolism. It is plausible that creatinine affects neuronal activities through modulating energy metabolism. While our data establish strong correlative and concentration-dependent relationships, definitive proof of the underlying molecular pathways will require future targeted pharmacological and genetic experiments. Such mechanistic validation is essential to determine whether these metabolites directly modulate ion channel function or act indirectly through intracellular signaling cascades.

The light-dark cycle profoundly reshaped both the metabolomic landscape and its functional linkages to electrophysiology in SCN neurons. We observed coordinated diurnal oscillations in 89 metabolites, including night-elevated carnitine and glutamine, which are key mediators of energy metabolism and fatty acid oxidation, alongside day-enriched creatinine, indicative of distinct energy buffering mechanisms across circadian phases. This metabolic reprogramming was paralleled by electrophysiological divergence, most notably a significant increase in synaptic quantal content (Iauc) during the night phase, reflecting heightened synaptic vesicle release or postsynaptic sensitivity aligned with the animal’s active period.

Critically, the light-dark cycle induced a dynamic reorganization of correlations between metabolites and electrophysiological parameters. For example, glutamine showed a negative correlation with PSC Iamp during the day but switched to a positive correlation with event Freq at night. Similarly, histidine and arginine exhibited weakened associations with current decay kinetics (Td), while demonstrating new correlations with Tr. This rewiring of correlation networks suggests that the association between metabolic state and synaptic function may be light-dark cycle-dependent. Furthermore, certain specific relationships, such as the nighttime-specific negative correlation between ATP and Iamp, imply that the coupling between energy metabolism and synaptic function may adjust across the light-dark cycle. Taken together, metabolites that show both diurnal changes in abundance and light-dark cycle-dependent electrophysiological correlations, including carnitine (elevated at night and negatively correlated with Iamp and Iauc during the day), represent potential key molecules associated with circadian synaptic plasticity.

Limitations of the study

While this study focused on the core region of the SCN, deliberately selected due to its role as the primary recipient of retinal innervation and its direct relevance to our investigation of light-influenced metabolic and electrophysiological states, we recognize that this focus on a single subregion represents a limitation. The SCN is anatomically and functionally heterogeneous, with distinct core and shell regions exhibiting differences in afferent connectivity, neuropeptide expression, and circadian oscillatory properties. Future investigations incorporating targeted recordings from both the core and shell regions will be essential to determine whether the metabolic-electrophysiological relationships identified in the present study exhibit spatial heterogeneity within the nucleus.

We acknowledge that the current study primarily focused on network-level correlations between the metabolome and electrophysiology across the entire SCN neuronal population. While this approach successfully identified overarching diurnal patterns that characterize the SCN’s functional state as a synchronized circadian pacemaker, it does not fully capture potential metabolic state-specific dynamics. A more granular understanding of how individual metabolic states fluctuate across the day-night cycle and correspond to distinct electrophysiological outputs will require larger sample sizes within each metabolic state. Such data will be critical to definitively resolve subtype-specific diurnal dynamics in metabolism and electrophysiology.

The current study was designed to compare metabolic and electrophysiological profiles between light and dark phases, focusing on day-night differences. Accordingly, our dataset for circadian comparison comprises samples collected at these two phases. While this approach effectively captures diurnal contrasts, it is important to note that our analysis characterizes diurnal differences rather than continuous circadian rhythms. Formal circadian profiling, including rhythm period detection and curve fitting using established algorithms such as JTK_CYCLE, requires time-series data collected across multiple time points spanning at least a full circadian cycle. Such high-temporal-resolution sampling was beyond the scope of the present study but represents a critical direction for future investigations. Studies with denser sampling across the 24-h cycle will be essential to resolve the precise circadian dynamics of the single-neuron metabolome and its relationship to electrophysiological rhythms.

This study aimed to link SCN neuron metabolic state to synaptic activity measured by PSCs. While we identified metabolic states correlating with distinct PSC signatures, we did not classify neurons by evoked firing patterns (defining peptidergic subtypes such as VIP/AVP neurons) or map their spatial coordinates within SCN subregions, a limitation we acknowledge. Integrating metabolic state, peptidergic identity, spatial location, and firing properties is an exciting future direction. Mapping our metabolic states to established peptidergic subtypes will require subsequent studies using fluorescent labeling or post-hoc immunohistochemistry to identify peptide expression in the same neurons characterized by our SNMS platform. Additionally, we acknowledge that examining the correlation between action potentials and the metabolome is also important, as it can reflect the relationship between metabolism and cellular activity. Therefore, this aspect should be explored in future work.

Experimental model and study participant details

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Science and Technology of China (Ethical Approval Number: USTCACUC26100124003). Male C57BL/6J mice (4–5 weeks old, Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology) were group-housed (4–6 per cage) under a 12-h light:12-h dark cycle (23°C, ad libitum food/water) for 2–3 weeks prior to experiments.

Method details

Acute brain slice preparation

Mice were euthanized via rapid decapitation. Brains were dissected and immersed in ice-cold, oxygenated (95% O2/5% CO2) cutting solution containing (in mM): 30 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 194 sucrose, 10 D-glucose, 4.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, and 1 MgCl2 (pH 7.4, 300–310 mOsm/kg). Coronal hypothalamic sections (270 μm) were prepared using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica). Slices recovered for 30 min at 32°C in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF: 124 mM NaCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM D-glucose, 4.5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 1 mM MgCl2; pH 7.4, 300–310 mOsm/kg), then held at 23°C–25°C until recording.

Combined electrophysiology and single-neuron mass spectrometry (SNMS)

SCN neuron identification and validation

To ensure the precise identity of patched neurons, we implemented a 2-fold validation strategy combining rigorous anatomical localization with single-cell metabolomic profiling. First, for anatomical identification, we strictly followed the standard mouse brain stereotaxic atlas to target the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) under microscopy, using unambiguous landmarks: (a) location directly dorsal to the optic chiasm; (b) a bilaterally symmetrical, compact cellular structure forming the floor of the third ventricle; and (c) clearly delineated boundaries under transmitted light. Patch-clamp recordings were performed exclusively within this defined region. Second, we leveraged the known neurochemical properties of SCN neurons, specifically that over 95% are GABAergic with high intracellular GABA concentrations. In our single-neuron mass spectrometry data, GABA was consistently detected with high signal-to-noise. Therefore, we established an objective quality control criterion: only neurons in which GABA was detected with a signal-to-noise ratio greater than 3 in their single-cell metabolomic profile were included in the final analysis dataset.

Electrophysiological recordings

Slices were transferred to a recording chamber perfused with oxygenated ACSF (23°C–25°C). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from the core region of suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) neurons were performed using borosilicate pipettes (3–5 MΩ) filled with 185 mM NH4HCO3/80 mM NH4Cl. Signals were amplified (MultiClamp 700B), digitized (Digidata 1550B), and acquired via pCLAMP (Molecular Devices). Neurons were voltage-clamped at −70 mV. Only cells with tight seals (>1 GΩ) and uncompromised membranes were analyzed.

Cytoplasmic aspiration for SNMS

After 1-min recordings of postsynaptic currents (PSCs), gentle negative pressure was applied to aspirate cytoplasmic contents into the pipette. Pipettes were promptly withdrawn and coupled to an Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Mass spectrometry parameters

Electrospray ionization was performed in positive ion mode with 4-kV alternating current (500 Hz). Spray tips were positioned 5 mm from the MS orifice. Settings: capillary temperature, 275°C; S-lens RF, 50%; resolution, 70,000; maximum injection time, 10 ms; micro-scans, 1. Data acquisition used Thermo Scientific Tune software (v2.9.0.2926).

Metabolite identification and quantification

Raw MS files (.raw) were converted to.mzML format (ProteoWizard MSConvert). Peak detection, alignment, and filtering were performed using XCMS (R package). Ions with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) > 3 and detection frequency >20% across neurons were retained. Missing values were imputed via sequential k-nearest neighbors (kNN). Intensity was normalized to total ion current (TIC). Metabolites were identified by matching m/z values (±15 ppm) to Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and RefMet databases. MS/MS spectra validated histidine, carnitine, and creatinine identities.

With the exception of carnitine, creatine, and histidine, which were confirmed by comparison with authentic standards using tandem mass spectrometry (Figure S11), all other metabolites reported in this study were putatively identified. Putative identifications were based on exact mass matches (mass error <15 ppm) against values from metabolite databases (HMDB and RefMet). These putative assignments require future confirmation by MS/MS spectral matching with chemical standards.

Neuronal subtyping and cluster validation

Sampling strategy for metabolic state definition

To define metabolic states and construct the primary metabolic-electrophysiological association models, neurons were randomly sampled from the SCN across a broad time window spanning zeitgeber time (ZT) 0 to ZT10 (where ZT0 is defined as lights-on, the onset of the light period). This dataset (n = 973 neurons) was subsequently used for unsupervised clustering analysis.

Unsupervised clustering

Z scored metabolomic data underwent principal component analysis (PCA). The top 100 principal components were used for unsupervised clustering via shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization (Seurat R package) to assign cluster labels to each neuron. Subsequently, t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) was applied to visualize the cluster structure in two-dimensional space. Metabolites with FC > 1.5 and adjusted p value < 0.05 (Bonferroni correction) were selected as characteristic metabolites for each cluster. Neurons and their characteristic metabolites were then sorted according to cluster labels, and a heatmap was generated to visualize the intensity patterns of these metabolites across all metabolic states.

Analysis of sampling time distribution

To assess whether sampling time influenced metabolic state classification, we analyzed the distribution of sampling times across the identified states in the primary dataset (n = 973 neurons). The zeitgeber time of each neuron was recorded during collection, and the number and percentage of cells from each time point were calculated for each metabolic cluster (Figure S3).

Machine learning modeling

Regression for electrophysiology prediction

The metabolomic profiles were used to predict electrophysiological features via Bayesian-optimized regression (MATLAB). Models were trained on randomly sampled subsets comprising 70%, 50%, and 45% of the total neuronal dataset, with the remaining data reserved as the held-out test set. Predictive performance was assessed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients (r) and corresponding p-values between the predicted and measured electrophysiological values for each training/test split ratio (Table S1).

Classification of neuronal metabolic states

Support vector machines (SVM) classifier (Python) was trained (5-fold cross-validation). Performance was assessed via ROC (receiver operating characteristic curve), AUC (area under curve) and accuracy.

Metabolite set enrichment analysis

Characteristic metabolites for each cluster were imported into MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), and the “Enrichment Analysis” was selected for analysis using the “KEGG” database. Enriched pathways for each cluster were visualized using dot plots.

Visualization of electrophysiological features

The probability density of electrophysiological parameters for each cluster was calculated, and the distribution of electrophysiological data was visualized. Subsequently, the mean electrophysiological parameters of neurons within each cluster were determined, followed by Z score normalization. The differences in electrophysiological parameters among the groups were then illustrated using a heatmap.

Metabolite-electrophysiology correlation networks

Metabolic state-averaged metabolite intensities and electrophysiological parameters were correlated (Pearson’s r). Significant associations (p < 0.05) were visualized as networks (Cytoscape v3.9.1).

To establish a concentration-response relationship between key metabolites and electrophysiological activity, we directly controlled the intracellular concentration of selected metabolite by incorporating them into the patch-clamp pipette solution. For each metabolite, we prepared pipette solutions with low, medium, and high concentrations, with the medium concentration approximating the endogenous level measured in our single-neuron metabolomic data. Sampling during light and dark phases

Day/night group sampling

An additional cohort of 227 neurons was collected from the same batch of animals specifically for day/night comparison analysis. This cohort included 107 neurons sampled during the light phase (Day group) and 120 neurons sampled during the dark phase (Night group), and was used exclusively for the differential analyses. Neurons from Night-group slices were classified using the SVM model.

Differential analysis

Metabolite intensities were standardized within batches. Day-Night differences were tested via two-sided t-tests (FDR <0.05, |fold change| > 1.2). Electrophysiological parameters were compared by t test.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R (v4.1.0), Python (v3.8), or MATLAB (R2022a). Batch effects in the metabolic data were assessed using principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA). Classification performance of the SVM model was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC) and accuracy. For each of the six metabolic states, differential analysis was conducted using Student’s t test and fold change (FC) calculations. Characteristic metabolites for each metabolic state were defined as those with an FC > 1.5 and a Bonferroni-adjusted p value < 0.05. Metabolite set enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst, with p values calculated using Fisher’s exact test based on the hypergeometric distribution (Over-Representation Analysis) for the input list of metabolites. Comparisons of electrophysiological features among the six metabolic states were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with P-value adjustment for multiple comparisons. To prevent overfitting while maximizing predictive accuracy, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization was incorporated into predictive model development, using 5-fold cross-validation. Prediction performance was evaluated by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients and corresponding p values between predicted and actual values. Metabolites with a correlation coefficient (r) > 0.8 and p < 0.05 were considered associated with electrophysiological activities. Correlation networks between metabolites and electrophysiological features were visualized using Cytoscape software. In the metabolite delivery experiment, all electrophysiological features were normalized to the mean baseline value obtained from the same mouse under control conditions (without metabolite application). Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD for multiple comparisons. For day/night comparison analysis, metabolite intensities were first standardized within each batch. Day-night differences were analyed using two-sided t-tests with FDR <0.05 and |fold change| > 1.2. Multiple-testing corrections were performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (false discovery rate, FDR) method. Electrophysiological features were compared using t-tests.
