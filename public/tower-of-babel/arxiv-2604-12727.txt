Abstract
. We determine sufficient criteria for the differential smoothness of ambiskew polynomial rings defined and studied by D. A. Jordan in several papers [ 25 , 31 , 33 , 35 ] .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
16E45, 16S30, 16S32, 16S36, 16S38, 58B34

1.
Introduction

Following Almulhem and Brzeziński
[
2
, Section 1]
, “Determining which classes of non-commutative algebras correspond to smooth non-commutative varieties or manifolds is one of the outstanding problems of non-commutative geometry”. With this aim, Brzeziński and Sitarz
[
16
]
argued that a method to construct a suitable graded differential algebra - a differential structure - should determine
smoothness
of a non-commutative variety: this kind of smoothness is referred to as
differential
. The idea behind the
differential smoothness
of algebras is rooted in the observation that a classical smooth orientable manifold, in addition to de Rham complex of differential forms, admits also the complex of
integral forms
isomorphic to the de Rham complex
[
41
, Section 4.5]
. Since its introduction, Brzeziński and several authors (e.g.
[
11
]
–
[
16
]
and
[
36
,
37
,
44
,
48
,
49
]
) have characterized the differential smoothness of different families of noncommutative algebras (see Section
2.1
for more details).

On the other hand, in a series of papers started in
[
30
]
, Jordan introduced a class of
certain iterated skew polynomial rings
(in the Ore’s sense
[
43
]
)
K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta]
on two indeterminates
x,y
over a commutative ring
K
which include the universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and several quantum groups. Two years later, in
[
32
]
he extended this class of rings by considering an extra parameter
\rho\in\Bbbk\ \backslash\ \{0\}
and includes the quantized Weyl algebra, the universal enveloping algebra of the Dispin Lie superalgebra, and an algebra introduced by Woronowicz
[
55
]
. Related with that, in his paper
[
33
]
he defined the
ambiskew polynomial rings
which are closely related to the
generalized Weyl algebras
defined and studied by Bavula
[
3
,
5
]
, and the
down-up algebras
defined by Benkart and Roby
[
7
,
8
]
.

Recently, in
[
47
]
–
[
53
]
the authors have considered the question on the differential smoothness of different families of noncommutative algebras. Our purpose in this paper is to investigate this smoothness in the setting of the ambiskew polynomial rings. Just as Almulhem and Brzeziński said
[
2
, Section 1]
, “Despite some recent progress in uncovering functorial ways of checking differential smoothness
[
15
]
one needs to study algebras on case by case basis and construct suitable differential and integral complexes”. We keep this observation in mind and present all the details of the differential smoothness of the algebras of interest. In this sense, this paper is a natural continuation of the study carried out by the authors for families of noncommutative polynomial algebras on three generators (for more details, see
[
44
]
and
[
50
]
–
[
53
]
), and contributes to the research on the non-commutative geometry of different types of algebras.

Organization of the article.
In Section
2
we consider the preliminaries on differential smoothness of algebras and ambiskew polynomial rings, in order to set up notation and render this paper self-contained. Section
3
contains the original results of the paper: Theorems
3.5
and
3.10
. In Section
4
we present some noncommutative ring families that can be expressed as ambiskew polynomial rings, in order to discuss the applicability of the results obtained in the previous section. Finally, Section
5
presents some ideas for an immediate future work that continues the study carried out here.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers including zero. The word ring means an associative ring with identity not necessarily commutative.
Z(R)
denotes the center of the ring
R
. All vector spaces and algebras (always associative and with unit) are over a fixed field
\Bbbk
.
K
is used to denote a commutative
\Bbbk
-algebra.
\Bbbk^{\times}
denotes the non-zero elements of
\Bbbk
. As usual, the symbols
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
denote the fields of real and complex numbers, respectively. For a
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
,
{\rm Aut}_{\Bbbk}(R)
is the set of
\Bbbk
-automorphisms of
R
.

2.1.
Differential smoothness of algebras

See Brzeziński et al.
[
16
, Section 2]
or
[
9
,
11
,
14
]
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
16
, Section 2.1]
)
.

(i)

A
differential graded algebra
is a non-negatively graded algebra
\Omega
with the product denoted by
\wedge
together with a degree-one linear map
d:\Omega^{\bullet}\to\Omega^{\bullet+1}
that satisfies the graded Leibniz’s rule and is such that
d\circ d=0
.

(ii)

A differential graded algebra
(\Omega,d)
is a
calculus over an algebra

A
if

\Omega^{0}A=A\quad{\rm and}\quad\Omega^{n}A=A\ dA\wedge dA\wedge\dotsb\wedge dA\quad(dA\ {\rm appears}\ n-{\rm times}),

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
(this last is called the
density condition
). We write
(\Omega A,d)
with
\Omega A=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega^{n}A
. By using the Leibniz’s rule, it follows that

\Omega^{n}A=dA\wedge dA\wedge\dotsb\wedge dA\ A.

A differential calculus
\Omega A
is called
connected
if
{\rm ker}(d\mid_{\Omega^{0}A})=\Bbbk
.

(iii)

A calculus
(\Omega A,d)
is said to have
dimension

n
if
\Omega^{n}A\neq 0
and
\Omega^{m}A=0
for all
m>n
. An
n
-dimensional calculus
\Omega A

admits a volume form
if
\Omega^{n}A
is isomorphic to
A
as a left and right
A
-module.

The existence of a right
A
-module isomorphism means that there is a free generator, say
\omega
, of
\Omega^{n}A
(as a right
A
-module), i.e.
\omega\in\Omega^{n}A
, such that all elements of
\Omega^{n}A
can be uniquely expressed as
\omega a
with
a\in A
. If
\omega
is also a free generator of
\Omega^{n}A
as a left
A
-module, it is said to be a
volume form
on
\Omega A
.

The right
A
-module isomorphism
\Omega^{n}A\to A
corresponding to a volume form
\omega
is denoted by
\pi_{\omega}
, i.e.

(2.1)

\pi_{\omega}(\omega a)=a,\quad{\rm for\ all}\ a\in A.

Using that
\Omega^{n}A
is also isomorphic to
A
as a left
A
-module, any free generator
\omega
induces an algebra endomorphism
\nu_{\omega}
of
A
by the formula

(2.2)

a\omega=\omega\nu_{\omega}(a).

Note that if
\omega
is a volume form, then
\nu_{\omega}
is an algebra automorphism.

Next, we recall the key ingredients of the
integral calculus
on
A
as dual to its differential calculus.

Let
(\Omega A,d)
be a differential calculus on
A
. The space of
n
-forms
\Omega^{n}A
is an
A
-bimodule. Consider
\mathcal{I}_{n}A
the right dual of
\Omega^{n}A
, the space of all right
A
-linear maps
\Omega^{n}A\rightarrow A
, that is,
\mathcal{I}_{n}A:={\rm Hom}_{A}(\Omega^{n}(A),A)
. Notice that each of the
\mathcal{I}_{n}A
is an
A
-bimodule with the actions given by

\displaystyle(a\cdot\phi\cdot b)(\omega)=a\phi(b\omega),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{n}A,\ \omega\in\Omega^{n}A\ {\rm and}\ a,b\in A.

The direct sum of all the
\mathcal{I}_{n}A
, that is,
\mathcal{I}A=\bigoplus_{n}\mathcal{I}_{n}A
, is a right
\Omega A
-module with action given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle(\phi\cdot\omega)(\omega^{\prime})=\phi(\omega\wedge\omega^{\prime}),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{n+m}A,\ \omega\in\Omega^{n}A\ {\rm and}\ \omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{m}A.

Definition 2.2

(
[
9
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

A
divergence
(also called
hom-connection
) on
A
is a linear map
\nabla:\mathcal{I}_{1}A\to A
such that

(2.4)

\nabla(\phi\cdot a)=\nabla(\phi)a+\phi(da),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{1}A\ {\rm and}\ a\in A.

Note that a divergence can be extended to the whole of
\mathcal{I}A
,

\nabla_{n}:\mathcal{I}_{n+1}A\to\mathcal{I}_{n}A,

by considering

(2.5)

\nabla_{n}(\phi)(\omega)=\nabla(\phi\cdot\omega)+(-1)^{n+1}\phi(d\omega),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{n+1}(A)\ {\rm and}\ \omega\in\Omega^{n}A.

By putting together (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we get the Leibniz’s rule

(2.6)

\nabla_{n}(\phi\cdot\omega)=\nabla_{m+n}(\phi)\cdot\omega+(-1)^{m+n}\phi\cdot d\omega,

for all elements
\phi\in\mathcal{I}_{m+n+1}A
and
\omega\in\Omega^{m}A

[
9
, Lemma 3.2]
. In the case
n=0
, if
{\rm Hom}_{A}(A,M)
is canonically identified with
M
, then
\nabla_{0}
reduces to the classical Leibniz’s rule.

Definition 2.3

(
[
9
, Definition 3.4]
)
.

The right
A
-module map

F=\nabla_{0}\circ\nabla_{1}:{\rm Hom}_{A}(\Omega^{2}A,M)\to M

is called a
curvature
of a hom-connection
(M,\nabla_{0})
.
(M,\nabla_{0})
is said to be
flat
if its curvature is the zero map, that is, if
\nabla\circ\nabla_{1}=0
. This condition implies that
\nabla_{n}\circ\nabla_{n+1}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

\mathcal{I}A
together with the
\nabla_{n}
form a chain complex called the
complex of integral forms
over
A
. The cokernel map of
\nabla
, that is,
\Lambda:A\to{\rm Coker}\nabla=A/{\rm Im}\nabla
is said to be the
integral on
A
associated to

\mathcal{I}A
.

Given a left
A
-module
X
with action
a\cdot x
for all
a\in A,\ x\in X
, and an algebra automorphism
\nu
of
A
, the notation
{}^{\nu}X
stands for
X
with the
A
-module structure twisted by
\nu
, i.e. with the
A
-action
a\otimes x\mapsto\nu(a)\cdot x
.

The following definition of an
integrable differential calculus
seeks to portray a version of Hodge star isomorphisms between the complex of differential forms of a differentiable manifold and a complex of dual modules of it

An
n

(\Omega A,d)

(\Omega A,d)

of

The
n
-form
\omega:=\Theta_{n}^{-1}(1)\in\Omega^{n}A
is called an

.

The algebra of complex matrices
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with the
n
-dimensional calculus generated by derivations presented by Dubois-Violette et al.
[
21
,
22
]
, the quantum group
SU_{q}(2)
with the three-dimensional left covariant calculus developed by Woronowicz
[
55
]
and the quantum standard sphere with the restriction of the above calculus, are examples of algebras admitting integrable calculi.

The following proposition shows that the integrability of a differential calculus can be defined without explicit reference to integral forms. This allows us to guarantee the integrability by considering the existence of finitely generator elements that allow to determine left and right components of any homogeneous element of
\Omega(A)
.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
(\Omega A,d)
be an
n
-dimensional differential calculus over an algebra
A
. The following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

(\Omega A,d)
is an integrable differential calculus.

(2)

There exists an algebra automorphism
\nu
of
A
and
A
-bimodule isomorphisms

\Theta_{k}:\Omega^{k}A\rightarrow\ ^{\nu}\mathcal{I}_{n-k}A,\quad k=0,\ldots,n,

such that for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{k}A
and
\omega^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega^{m}A
, we have that

\displaystyle\Theta_{k+m}(\omega^{\prime}\wedge\omega^{\prime\prime})=(-1)^{(n-1)m}\Theta_{k}(\omega^{\prime})\cdot\omega^{\prime\prime}.

(3)

There exists an algebra automorphism
\nu
of
A
and an
A
-bimodule map
\vartheta:\Omega^{n}A\rightarrow\ ^{\nu}A
such that all left multiplication maps

\displaystyle\ell_{\vartheta}^{k}:\Omega^{k}A

\displaystyle\ \rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{n-k}A,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\vartheta\cdot\omega^{\prime},\quad k=0,1,\dotsc,n,

where the actions
\cdot
are defined by
(
2.3
)
, are bijective.

(4)

(\Omega A,d)
has a volume form
\omega
such that all left multiplication maps

\displaystyle\ell_{\pi_{\omega}}^{k}:\Omega^{k}A

\displaystyle\ \rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{n-k}A,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\pi_{\omega}\cdot\omega^{\prime},\quad k=0,1,\dotsc,n-1,

where
\pi_{\omega}
is defined by
(
2.1
)
, are bijective.

A volume form
\omega\in\Omega^{n}A
is an
integrating form
if and only if it satisfies condition
(4)
of Proposition
2.5

[
16
, Remark 2.3]
.

The most interesting cases of differential calculi are those where
\Omega^{k}A
are finitely generated and projective right or left (or both)
A
-modules
[
10
]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

(1)

[
16
, Lemma 2.6]
Consider
(\Omega A,d)
an integrable and
n
-dimensional calculus over
A
with integrating form
\omega
. Then
\Omega^{k}A
is a finitely generated projective right
A
-module if there exist a finite number of forms
\omega_{i}\in\Omega^{k}A
and
\overline{\omega}_{i}\in\Omega^{n-k}A
such that, for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{k}A
, we have that

\omega^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\omega_{i}\pi_{\omega}(\overline{\omega}_{i}\wedge\omega^{\prime}).

(2)

[
16
, Lemma 2.7]
Let
(\Omega A,d)
be an
n
-dimensional calculus over
A
admitting a volume form
\omega
. Assume that for all

, there exists a finite number of forms
\omega_{i}^{k},\overline{\omega}_{i}^{k}\in\Omega^{k}(A)
such that for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{k}A
, we have that

\omega^{\prime}=\displaystyle\sum_{i}\omega_{i}^{k}\pi_{\omega}(\overline{\omega}_{i}^{n-k}\wedge\omega^{\prime})=\displaystyle\sum_{i}\nu_{\omega}^{-1}(\pi_{\omega}(\omega^{\prime}\wedge\omega_{i}^{n-k}))\overline{\omega}_{i}^{k},

where
\pi_{\omega}
and
\nu_{\omega}
are defined by
(

)
and

)
, respectively. Then
\omega
is an integral form and all the

are finitely generated and projective as left and right
A
-modules.

In order to relate the dimension of an integrable calculus
(\Omega A,d)
with the “size” of the underlying affine algebra
A
, a suitable notion of dimension is needed

16
, p. 421]
: its Gelfand-Kirillov dimension
\text{GKdim}(A)
introduced by Gelfand and Kirillov

Definition 2.7

(
[
16
, Definition 2.4]
)
.

An affine algebra
A
with integer Gelfand-Kirillov dimension
n
is said to be
differentially smooth
if it admits an
n
-dimensional connected integrable differential calculus
(\Omega A,d)
.

From Definition
2.7
it follows that a differentially smooth algebra comes equipped with a well-behaved differential structure and with the precise concept of integration
[
15
, p. 2414]
.

Several examples of noncommutative algebras have been shown to be differentially smooth: quantum spheres, noncommutative torus, the coordinate algebras of the quantum group
SU_{q}(2)
, the noncommutative pillow algebra, the quantum polynomial algebras, Hopf algebra domains, families of Ore extensions, some 3-dimensional skew polynomial algebras, diffusion algebras in three generators, and noncommutative coordinate algebras of deformations of examples of classical orbifolds (e.g.
[
11
]
-
[
16
]
,
[
21
,
22
,
36
,
37
,
44
]
). Of course, there are examples of algebras that are not differentially smooth. Consider the commutative algebra
A=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/\langle xy\rangle
. A proof by contradiction shows that for this algebra there are no one-dimensional connected integrable calculi over
A
, so it cannot be differentially smooth
[
16
, Example 2.5]
.

2.2.
Ambiskew polynomial rings

Definition 2.8

(
[
32
, Section 1.1]
)
.

Let
K
be a finitely generated commutative algebra over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
, let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\Bbbk}(K)
and consider
u\in K
and
\rho\in\Bbbk\ \backslash\ \{0\}
. Form the skew polynomial ring of automorphism type
K[x;\sigma]
and extend
\sigma
to
K[x;\sigma]
by setting

\sigma(x)=\rho^{-1}x\quad{\rm whence}\quad\sigma^{-1}(x)=\rho x.

There is a
\sigma^{-1}
-derivation
\delta
of
K[x;\sigma]
such that
\delta(K)=0
and
\delta(x)=u-\rho\sigma(u)

[
20
, p. 41]
(see also
[
29
, Section 2.8]
). Consider the skew polynomial ring

(2.7)

\displaystyle K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta],

with defining relations given by

(2.8)

\displaystyle xk=

\displaystyle\,\sigma(k)x,

(2.9)

\displaystyle yk=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{-1}(k)y,\quad{\rm and}

(2.10)

\displaystyle yx=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{-1}(x)y+\delta(x)=\rho xy+u-\rho\sigma(u).

Let us see that the iterated skew polynomial ring considered in Definition
2.8
can be written as an ambiskew polynomial ring over
K
.

By definition, an
ambiskew polynomial ring

A
over a
\Bbbk
–algebra
R
is an algebra
A(R,\alpha,v,\rho)
generated by
R
and two elements
x,y
subject to the relations

(2.11)

\displaystyle xr

\displaystyle=\alpha(r)x,

(2.12)

\displaystyle yr

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1}(r)y,\quad{\rm for\ all}\,\,r\in R,\,{\rm and}

(2.13)

\displaystyle yx

\displaystyle=\rho xy+v,

where
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\Bbbk}(R),v\in R
and
\rho\in\Bbbk^{\times}
. Let us take
R=K
and
\alpha=\sigma
. Then the first defining relation of
A
gives
xk=\sigma(k)x
which agrees with relation (
2.8
). The second defining relation of
A
gives
yk=\sigma^{-1}(k)y
, which agrees with relation (
2.9
).

Now, from the definition of the Ore extension
K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta]
we have that

yx=\sigma^{-1}(x)y+\delta(x).

Since
\sigma(x)=\rho^{-1}x
, we obtain
\sigma^{-1}(x)=\rho x
, and so
yx=\rho xy+\delta(x)
. By hypothesis,
\delta(x)=u-\rho\sigma(u)
. Setting
v=u-\rho\sigma(u)\in K
we obtain that
yx=\rho xy+v
, which is relation (
2.10
) in the definition of an ambiskew polynomial ring. Hence the iterated skew polynomial ring given in (
2.7
) is an ambiskew polynomial ring over
K
.

In Section
4
we present in some detail some families of algebras that are closely related to ambiskew polynomial algebras.

3.
Differential and integral calculus of ambiskew polynomial rings

This section contains the original results of the paper: Theorems
3.5
and
3.10
. We start with some preliminary facts.

Definition 3.1

(
[
1
, Chapter 11]
)
.

Let
K
be a commutative ring. The
Krull dimension
of
K
, denoted by
\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)
, is the supremum of the lengths
n
of chains of prime ideals

\mathfrak{p}_{0}\subsetneq\mathfrak{p}_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak{p}_{n}

in the prime spectrum of
K
. If no such finite supremum exists, then one writes
\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)=\infty
.

If
K
is a finitely generated commutative
\Bbbk
-algebra, then the Gelfand-Kirillov dimension of
K
coincides with its Krull dimension, that is,
\mathrm{GKdim}(K)=\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)

[
39
, Theorem 4.5]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
K
be a finitely generated commutative
\Bbbk
-algebra, where
\Bbbk
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. Assume that
\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)=0
and that
K
is reduced. Then the following assertions hold:

(1)

K
is a finite-dimensional
\Bbbk
-vector space.

(2)

There exists an integer
m\geq 1
such that
K\cong\Bbbk^{m}
.

(3)

Every
\Bbbk
-derivation
d:K\to K
is zero.

Proof.

Since
K
is a finitely generated
\Bbbk
-algebra, it is Noetherian. Hence the assumption
\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)=0
implies that
K
is Artinian by
[
1
, Theorem 8.5]
. On the other hand, by Noether normalization there exists a finite injective
\Bbbk
-algebra homomorphism
\Bbbk[y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}]\hookrightarrow K
with
r=\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)

[
24
, Theorem 13.3]
,
[
42
, Corollary 14.3]
. Since
\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)=0
, we get that
r=0
, and therefore
K
is integral, hence finite, over
\Bbbk
. In particular,
\dim_{\Bbbk}K<\infty
. Since
K
is Artinian, it is a finite direct product of Artinian local rings by
[
1
, Theorem 8.7]
. Thus,
K\cong\prod_{i=1}^{m}K_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
for suitable maximal ideals
\mathfrak{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{m}
. Because
K
is reduced, each local factor
K_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is a reduced Artinian local ring. Let
\mathfrak{n}_{i}
denote the maximal ideal of
K_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
. Since the nilradical of an Artinian ring is nilpotent by
[
1
, Proposition 8.4]
, and since in an Artinian local ring the nilradical coincides with the unique prime ideal, namely
\mathfrak{n}_{i}
, it follows that
\mathfrak{n}_{i}
is nilpotent. As
K_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is reduced, we must have
\mathfrak{n}_{i}=0
. Hence each
K_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is a field.

Now each residue field
K/\mathfrak{m}_{i}
is a finitely generated
\Bbbk
-algebra which is a field. Therefore it is algebraic over
\Bbbk
by Noether normalization; see again
[
42
, Corollary 14.3]
. Since
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed, each such algebraic extension is trivial, and so
K/\mathfrak{m}_{i}\cong\Bbbk
. Therefore
K_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}\cong\Bbbk
for all
i
, and consequently
K\cong\Bbbk^{m}
.

Finally, let
d:K\to K
be a
\Bbbk
-derivation. Under the identification
K\cong\Bbbk^{m}
, let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
be the standard pairwise orthogonal idempotents, so that

e_{i}^{2}=e_{i},\quad e_{i}e_{j}=0\ (i\neq j)\quad{\rm and}\quad 1=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{m}.

Since
d
is a derivation,

d(e_{i})=d(e_{i}^{2})=d(e_{i})e_{i}+e_{i}d(e_{i})=2e_{i}d(e_{i}).

Multiplying by
e_{i}
gives
e_{i}d(e_{i})=2e_{i}d(e_{i})
, and so
e_{i}d(e_{i})=0
. In this way,
d(e_{i})=2e_{i}d(e_{i})=0
. Now every element of
K\cong\Bbbk^{m}
has the form

\lambda_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}e_{m}\text{ with }\lambda_{i}\in\Bbbk,

and since
d
is
\Bbbk
-linear and vanishes on
\Bbbk
, we obtain

d(\lambda_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}e_{m})=\lambda_{1}d(e_{1})+\cdots+\lambda_{m}d(e_{m})=0.

This shows that
d=0
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The reducedness assumption in Proposition
3.2
is essential. Indeed, if
K=\Bbbk[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2})
, then
\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)=0
, but the map
d:K\to K
determined by
d(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
is a non-zero
\Bbbk
-derivation.

We introduce the following notion that is principal for the key results of the paper.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
K
be a commutative
\Bbbk
-algebra, let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\Bbbk}(K)
, and consider
\rho\in\Bbbk\setminus\{0\}
. We say that an element
u\in K
satisfies the
(\sigma,\rho)
-balance condition
whenever

\rho^{2}\sigma^{2}(u)-2\rho\sigma(u)+u=0.

Any such element will be called
(\sigma,\rho)
-balanced
.

The following is the first result of the paper.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
A=K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta]
over a finitely generated commutative algebra
K
, with
u\in K
and
\rho\in\Bbbk\setminus\{0\}
. If
u
is
(\sigma,\rho)
-balanced and
\mathcal{K}\text{dim}(K)=0
, then
A
is differentially smooth.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
, we have
d\mid_{K}=0
. Now, consider the following automorphisms:

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1}(k),

\displaystyle\ \nu_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle\ \nu_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\rho y,

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(k)

\displaystyle=\sigma(k),

\displaystyle\ \nu_{y}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-1}x,

\displaystyle\ \nu_{y}(y)

\displaystyle=y.

The map
\nu_{x}
can be extended to an algebra homomorphism of
A
if and only if the definitions of
\nu_{x}(k_{i})
,
\nu_{x}(x)
and
\nu_{x}(y)
respect the relations defining the algebra, i.e.

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(x)\nu_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=\nu_{x}(\sigma(k))\nu_{x}(x),

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(y)\nu_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=\nu_{x}(\sigma^{-1}(k))\nu_{x}(y),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(y)\nu_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho\nu_{x}(x)\nu_{x}(y)+\nu_{x}(u)-\rho\nu_{x}(\sigma(u)).

Since
u
is
(\sigma,\rho)
-balanced, the previous equations hold.

Similarly, the map
\nu_{y}
can be extended to an algebra homomorphism of
A
if and only if

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(x)\nu_{y}(k)

\displaystyle=\nu_{y}(\sigma(k))\nu_{y}(x),

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(y)\nu_{y}(k)

\displaystyle=\nu_{y}(\sigma^{-1}(k))\nu_{y}(y),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(y)\nu_{y}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho\nu_{y}(x)\nu_{x}(y)+\nu_{y}(u)-\rho\nu_{y}(\sigma(u)).

Once again, this is true because of the condition of
u
. Furthermore, these automorphisms mutually commute since their restrictions to
K
are given by
\sigma
and its inverse, respectively, and on the generators
x
and
y
they act by scalar multiplication.

Let
\Omega^{1}A
be the free right
A
-module with basis
dx,dy
, endowed with the left

A
-module structure

adx=dx\nu_{x}(a)\quad{\rm and}\quad ady=dy\nu_{y}(a),\quad{\rm for}\,\,a\in A.

The idea is to extend the correspondences
x\mapsto dx
and
y\mapsto dy
to a map
d:A\to\Omega^{1}A
that satisfies the Leibniz’s rule. This is possible if it is compatible with the nontrivial relations (
2.8
), (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), i.e. if the equalities

\displaystyle dxk

\displaystyle\ =\sigma(k)dx,

\displaystyle dyk

\displaystyle\ =\sigma^{-1}(k)dy,\ {\rm and}

\displaystyle dyx+ydx

\displaystyle\ =\rho dxy+\rho xdy

hold. Note that
d(a)=0
for
a\in K
.

Define
\Bbbk
-linear maps
\partial_{x},\partial_{y}:A\rightarrow A
such that

\displaystyle d(a)=dx\partial_{x}(a)+dy\partial_{y}(a),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ a\in A.

Since
dx
and
dy
are free generators of the right
A
-module
\Omega^{1}A
, these maps are well-defined. Note that
d(a)=0
if and only if
\partial_{x}(a)=\partial_{y}(a)=0
. By using
adx=dx\nu_{x}(a)
and
ady=dy\nu_{y}(a)
and the definitions of the maps
\nu_{x}
and
\nu_{y}
, we get that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(x^{k}y^{l})=

\displaystyle\ kx^{k-1}y^{l}\quad{\rm and}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}(x^{k}y^{l})=

\displaystyle\ l\rho^{-k}x^{k}y^{l-1}.

Thus
d(a)=0
if and only if
a
is a scalar multiple of the identity. This shows that
(\Omega A,d)
is connected where
\Omega A=\Omega^{0}A\oplus\Omega^{1}A
.

Since
\Omega^{2}A=\omega A\cong A
as a right and left
A
-module, with
\omega=dx\wedge dy
, where
\nu_{\omega}=\nu_{x}\circ\nu_{y}
, we have that
\omega
is a volume form of
A
. From Proposition
2.6
(2) we get that
\omega
is an integral form by setting

\omega_{1}^{1}=dx,\quad\omega_{2}^{1}=dy,\quad\bar{\omega}_{1}^{1}=-\rho^{-1}dy\quad{\rm and}\quad\bar{\omega}_{2}^{1}=dx.

By Proposition
2.6
(2), we consider the expression
\omega^{\prime}:=dx\alpha+dy\beta
with
\alpha,\beta\in K
, to obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\omega_{i}^{1}\pi_{\omega}(\bar{\omega}_{i}^{1}\wedge\omega^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\ dx\pi_{\omega}(-\alpha\rho^{-1}dy\wedge dx)+dy\pi_{\omega}(\beta dx\wedge dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ dx\alpha+dy\beta=\omega^{\prime}.

As we saw above, all elements of
\Omega^{1}A
can be generated by
\omega_{i}^{1}
and
\bar{\omega}_{i}^{1}
for
i=1,2
, so that Proposition
2.6
(2) guarantees that
\omega
is an integral form. Then
\mathrm{GKdim}(K)=0
, which implies that
\mathrm{GKdim}(A)=2
.

Finally, Proposition
2.5
shows that
(\Omega A,d)
is an integrable differential calculus of degree
2
, whence
A
is differentially smooth.
∎

Example 3.6
.

Assume that
\Bbbk
contains a primitive third root of unity
\omega
. Let

K=\Bbbk e_{0}\oplus\Bbbk e_{1}\oplus\Bbbk e_{2}\cong\Bbbk^{3},

where
e_{i}e_{j}=\delta_{ij}e_{i}
and
e_{0}+e_{1}+e_{2}=1
. Define
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\Bbbk}(K)
by

\sigma(e_{0})=e_{1},\quad\sigma(e_{1})=e_{2},\quad\sigma(e_{2})=e_{0},

and set
\rho=\omega
and
\quad u=e_{0}+\omega e_{1}+\omega^{2}e_{2}
. Then

\sigma(u)=e_{1}+\omega e_{2}+\omega^{2}e_{0}=\omega^{-1}u,

and so
\sigma^{2}(u)=\omega^{-2}u
. Hence

\rho^{2}\sigma^{2}(u)-2\rho\sigma(u)+u=\omega^{2}(\omega^{-2}u)-2\omega(\omega^{-1}u)+u=u-2u+u=0,

that is,
u
is
(\sigma,\rho)
-balanced.

Since
K\cong\Bbbk^{3}
, we have that
\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dim}(K)=0
.
K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta]
is differentially smooth by Theorem
3.5
. In this case, the defining relations are given by

xe_{i}=e_{i+1}x,\quad ye_{i}=e_{i-1}y,\quad{\rm for}\ i=0,1,2,

and
yx=\rho xy+u-\rho\sigma(u)=\omega xy
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
A
be an ambiskew polynomial ring
A(R,\alpha,v,\rho)
in two indeterminates over a
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
, with
v\in R
and
\rho\in\Bbbk\setminus\{0\}
. If
v-\rho\alpha(v)=0
and
\text{GKdim}(R)=0
, then
A
is differentially smooth.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of Proposition
3.5
; instead of the
(\alpha,\rho)
-balanced condition, assume that
v-\rho\alpha(v)=0
.
∎

Example 3.8
.

The statement of Corollary

recovers classical examples of differentially smooth algebras
[

(i)

The

\Bbbk[x,y]
can be viewed as the ambiskew polynomial ring

. Then

and
\mathrm{GKdim}(R)=\mathrm{GKdim}(\Bbbk)=0
, whence
\Bbbk[x,y]

(ii)

The

(iii)

The quantum plane
\mathcal{O}_{q}(\Bbbk^{2})=\Bbbk\{x,y\}/\langle xy-qyx\rangle

. This implies that

Example 3.9
.

Let
R=\Bbbk^{m}=\Bbbk e_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Bbbk e_{m-1}
, where
e_{i}e_{j}=\delta_{ij}e_{i}
and
1=e_{0}+\cdots+e_{m-1}
. Fix a primitive
m
-th root of unity
\omega\in\Bbbk^{\times}
, and define
\alpha(e_{i})=e_{i+1},\ i\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
together with
\rho=\omega
and
v=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\omega^{i}e_{i}
. It follows that

\alpha(v)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\omega^{i}e_{i+1}=\omega^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\omega^{i}e_{i}=\omega^{-1}v,

whence
v-\rho\alpha(v)=v-\omega(\omega^{-1}v)=0
.
Consider the ambiskew polynomial ring
A=A(R,\alpha,v,\rho)
generated by
R
and two indeterminates
x,y
subject to the relations

xe_{i}=e_{i+1}x,\quad ye_{i}=e_{i-1}y\quad{\rm and}\quad yx=\omega xy+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\omega^{i}e_{i}.

Since

is finite-dimensional over
\Bbbk
, we have that
\mathrm{GKdim}(R)=0
, and so
A(R,\alpha,v,\rho)
is differentially smooth.

Theorem
3.10
is the second result of the paper.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
A=K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta]
be an iterated skew polynomial ring in two indeterminates
x,y
over the finitely generated commutative
\Bbbk
-algebra
K=\Bbbk[k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}]
. Assume that

u=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\beta_{j}k_{1}^{\alpha_{1j}}\cdots k_{n}^{\alpha_{nj}},

where
\beta_{j}\in\Bbbk
and
\alpha_{1j},\ldots,\alpha_{nj}\in\mathbb{N}
for all
0\leq j\leq m
. Suppose further that:

(1)

u
is
(\sigma,\rho)
-balanced;

(2)

u-\rho\sigma(u)\in\Bbbk
;

(3)

\sigma(k_{i})=a_{i}k_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, with
a_{i}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
;

(4)

\rho\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\alpha_{ij}}=1,\ 0\leq j\leq m
;

(5)

\mathrm{GKdim}(K)=n
.

Then
A
is differentially smooth.

Proof.

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, define maps on the generators of
A
by

\displaystyle\nu_{k_{i}}(k_{j})

\displaystyle=k_{j},

\displaystyle\nu_{k_{i}}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{i}x,

\displaystyle\nu_{k_{i}}(y)

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{-1}y,

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(k_{i})

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{-1}k_{i},

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\rho y,

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(k_{i})

\displaystyle=a_{i}k_{i},

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-1}x,

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(y)

\displaystyle=y,

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

We first check that these maps extend to algebra automorphisms of
A
.

For
\nu_{k_{i}}
, it suffices to verify that the defining relations of
A
are preserved. Since
\nu_{k_{i}}
fixes
K
pointwise, we have that

\displaystyle\nu_{k_{i}}(x)\nu_{k_{i}}(k)

\displaystyle=a_{i}xk=a_{i}\sigma(k)x=\nu_{k_{i}}(\sigma(k))\nu_{k_{i}}(x),

\displaystyle\nu_{k_{i}}(y)\nu_{k_{i}}(k)

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{-1}yk=a_{i}^{-1}\sigma^{-1}(k)y=\nu_{k_{i}}(\sigma^{-1}(k))\nu_{k_{i}}(y),

for all
k\in K
, and

\displaystyle\nu_{k_{i}}(y)\nu_{k_{i}}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{-1}ya_{i}x=yx

\displaystyle=\rho xy+u-\rho\sigma(u)

\displaystyle=\rho\nu_{k_{i}}(x)\nu_{k_{i}}(y)+\nu_{k_{i}}(u)-\rho\nu_{k_{i}}(\sigma(u)).

Thus each
\nu_{k_{i}}
extends to an automorphism of
A
.

Next, consider
\nu_{x}
. Since
\nu_{x}|_{K}=\sigma^{-1}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(x)\nu_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=x\sigma^{-1}(k)=\nu_{x}(\sigma(k))\nu_{x}(x),

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(y)\nu_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=\rho y\sigma^{-1}(k)=\rho ky=\nu_{x}(\sigma^{-1}(k))\nu_{x}(y),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ k\in K.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\nu_{x}(y)\nu_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho yx

\displaystyle=\rho(\rho xy+u-\rho\sigma(u))

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}xy+\rho u-\rho^{2}\sigma(u),

whereas

\displaystyle\rho\nu_{x}(x)\nu_{x}(y)+\nu_{x}(u)-\rho\nu_{x}(\sigma(u))

\displaystyle=\rho x(\rho y)+\sigma^{-1}(u)-\rho u

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}xy+\sigma^{-1}(u)-\rho u.

These two expressions agree precisely because
u
is
(\sigma,\rho)
-balanced, that is,

2\rho u-\rho^{2}\sigma(u)-\sigma^{-1}(u)=0.

Hence
\nu_{x}
extends to an automorphism of
A
.

A similar computation shows that
\nu_{y}
also extends to an automorphism. Indeed, since
\nu_{y}|_{K}=\sigma
, we get that

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(x)\nu_{y}(k)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-1}x\sigma(k)=\nu_{y}(\sigma(k))\nu_{y}(x),

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(y)\nu_{y}(k)

\displaystyle=y\sigma(k)=\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(k))y=ky=\nu_{y}(\sigma^{-1}(k))\nu_{y}(y),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ k\in K,

and

\displaystyle\nu_{y}(y)\nu_{y}(x)

\displaystyle=y(\rho^{-1}x)=\rho^{-1}yx

\displaystyle=xy+\rho^{-1}u-\sigma(u),

while

\displaystyle\rho\nu_{y}(x)\nu_{y}(y)+\nu_{y}(u)-\rho\nu_{y}(\sigma(u))

\displaystyle=\rho(\rho^{-1}x)y+\sigma(u)-\rho\sigma^{2}(u)

\displaystyle=xy+\sigma(u)-\rho\sigma^{2}(u).

Again, equality follows from the
(\sigma,\rho)
-balance condition.

Since all these maps act diagonally on the generators, it is immediate that
\nu_{k_{1}},\ldots,\nu_{k_{n}},\nu_{x},\nu_{y}
commute pairwise.

Because
\mathrm{GKdim}(K)=n
, the standard growth formula for iterated skew polynomial rings yields
\mathrm{GKdim}(A)=n+2
. We now construct an
(n+2)
-dimensional integrable differential calculus on
A
.

Let
\Omega^{1}A
be the free right
A
-module with basis
dk_{1},\ldots,dk_{n},dx,dy
.

Define a left
A
-module structure on
\Omega^{1}A
by

(3.2)

adk_{i}=dk_{i}\nu_{k_{i}}(a),\quad adx=dx\nu_{x}(a),\quad ady=dy\nu_{y}(a),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ a\in A\ {\rm and}\ 1\leq i\leq n.

Accordingly, the defining commutation rules in
\Omega^{1}A
are given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle k_{i}dk_{j}

\displaystyle=dk_{j}k_{i},

\displaystyle k_{i}dx

\displaystyle=dxa_{i}^{-1}k_{i},

\displaystyle k_{i}dy

\displaystyle=dya_{i}k_{i},

\displaystyle xdk_{i}

\displaystyle=dk_{i}a_{i}x,

\displaystyle ydk_{i}

\displaystyle=dk_{i}a_{i}^{-1}y,

\displaystyle xdx

\displaystyle=dxx,

(3.4)

\displaystyle xdy

\displaystyle=\rho^{-1}dyx,

\displaystyle ydx

\displaystyle=\rho dxy,

\displaystyle ydy

\displaystyle=dyy,

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Now, we extend the assignments

k_{i}\mapsto dk_{i},\quad x\mapsto dx,\quad y\mapsto dy

to a
\Bbbk
-linear map
d:A\to\Omega^{1}A
satisfying the Leibniz’s rule. We must verify that
d
is compatible with the defining relations of
A
.

From relation (
2.8
), we get that

\displaystyle d(xk_{i})

\displaystyle=dxk_{i}+xdk_{i}=dxk_{i}+dk_{i}a_{i}x,

\displaystyle d(\sigma(k_{i})x)

\displaystyle=d(a_{i}k_{i}x)=a_{i}dk_{i}x+a_{i}k_{i}dx

\displaystyle=a_{i}dk_{i}x+dxk_{i},

and these two expressions coincide.

Similarly, relation (
2.9
) implies that

\displaystyle d(yk_{i})

\displaystyle=dyk_{i}+ydk_{i}=dyk_{i}+dk_{i}a_{i}^{-1}y,

\displaystyle d(\sigma^{-1}(k_{i})y)

\displaystyle=d(a_{i}^{-1}k_{i}y)=a_{i}^{-1}dk_{i}y+a_{i}^{-1}k_{i}dy

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{-1}dk_{i}y+dyk_{i},

so the second defining relation is also preserved.

Finally, applying
d
to relation (
2.10
) yields

\displaystyle d(yx)=dyx+ydx=dyx+\rho dxy,

while

\displaystyle d(\rho xy+u-\rho\sigma(u))

\displaystyle=\rho dxy+\rho xdy+d(u-\rho\sigma(u))

\displaystyle=\rho dxy+dyx+d(u-\rho\sigma(u)),

because
xdy=\rho^{-1}dyx
. Since
u-\rho\sigma(u)\in\Bbbk
, we have that
d(u-\rho\sigma(u))=0
. Then
d(yx)=d(\rho xy+u-\rho\sigma(u))
, which shows that
d
is well defined.

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, define
\Bbbk
-linear maps
\partial_{k_{i}},\partial_{x},\partial_{y}:A\to A
by requiring that

d(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}dk_{i}\partial_{k_{i}}(a)+dx\partial_{x}(a)+dy\partial_{y}(a),\quad{\rm for}\ a\in A.

Since
dk_{1},\ldots,dk_{n},dx,dy
form a free right
A
-basis of
\Omega^{1}A
, these maps are well defined. As it can be seen, the PBW monomials satisfy the relations given by

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{i}}(k_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots k_{n}^{t_{n}}x^{l_{1}}y^{l_{2}})

\displaystyle=t_{i}k_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots k_{i}^{t_{i}-1}\cdots k_{n}^{t_{n}}x^{l_{1}}y^{l_{2}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(k_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots k_{n}^{t_{n}}x^{l_{1}}y^{l_{2}})

\displaystyle=l_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{-t_{i}}k_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots k_{n}^{t_{n}}x^{l_{1}-1}y^{l_{2}},

\displaystyle\partial_{y}(k_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots k_{n}^{t_{n}}x^{l_{1}}y^{l_{2}})

\displaystyle=l_{2}\rho^{-l_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{t_{i}}k_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots k_{n}^{t_{n}}x^{l_{1}}y^{l_{2}-1}.

In this way,
d(a)=0
if and only if
a\in\Bbbk
. Therefore, the differential calculus

\Omega A=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{n+2}\Omega^{j}A

is connected.

We now pass to higher-degree forms. The universal extension of
d
, subject to relations
(
3.3
) and (
3.4
), yields for
2\leq l\leq n+1
:

(3.6)

\displaystyle dk_{q(1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge

\displaystyle\ dk_{q(l)}=(-1)^{\sharp}dk_{p(1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge dk_{p(l)},

\displaystyle dk_{q(1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge

\displaystyle\ dk_{q(s)}\wedge dx\wedge dk_{q(s+1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge dk_{q(l-1)}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\ =(-1)^{l-s-2}\prod_{r=s+1}^{l-1}a_{r}^{-1}\bigwedge_{t=1}^{l-1}dk_{q(t)}\wedge dx,\quad 1\leq s\leq l-2,

\displaystyle dk_{q(1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge

\displaystyle\ dk_{q(s)}\wedge dy\wedge dk_{q(s+1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge dk_{q(l-1)}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\ =(-1)^{l-s-2}\prod_{r=s+1}^{l-1}a_{r}\bigwedge_{t=1}^{l-1}dk_{q(t)}\wedge dy,\quad 1\leq s\leq l-2,

\displaystyle dk_{q(1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge

\displaystyle\ dk_{q(s)}\wedge dy\wedge dx\wedge dk_{q(s+1)}\wedge\dotsb\wedge dk_{q(l-2)}

(3.9)

\displaystyle\ =-\rho^{-1}\prod_{r=s+1}^{l-2}a_{r}^{-1}\bigwedge_{t=1}^{l-2}dk_{q(t)}\wedge dx\wedge dy,\quad 1\leq s\leq l-2,

where

q:\{1,\ldots,l\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}

is injective,

p:\{1,\ldots,l\}\to\operatorname{Im}(q)

is increasing, and
\sharp
denotes the number of transpositions needed to transform
q
into
p
.

Since the automorphisms
\nu_{k_{1}},\ldots,\nu_{k_{n}},\nu_{x},\nu_{y}
commute, no additional relations arise. In particular,

\displaystyle\Omega^{n+1}A=

\displaystyle\ \left(\bigoplus_{r=1}^{n}dk_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dk_{r}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dk_{n}\wedge dx\wedge dy\right)A

\displaystyle\ \oplus(dk_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dk_{n}\wedge dx)A\oplus(dk_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dk_{n}\wedge dy)A.

Set

\omega=dk_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dk_{n}\wedge dx\wedge dy.

Then
\Omega^{n+2}A=\omega A\cong A
as both a right and a left
A
-module, and

\nu_{\omega}=\nu_{k_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\nu_{k_{n}}\circ\nu_{x}\circ\nu_{y}.

Thus,
\omega
is a volume form on
A
.

For notational convenience, set

z_{i}=k_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq n,\quad{\rm and}\ z_{n+1}=x,\ z_{n+2}=y,

and define constants given by

c_{i,j}=1\text{ for }1\leq i,j\leq n,\quad c_{i,n+1}=a_{i},\quad c_{i,n+2}=a_{i}^{-1}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq n.

By Proposition
2.6
(2), the calculus is integrable once one exhibits suitable dual bases. For
1\leq j\leq n+2
, define

\displaystyle\omega_{i}^{j}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{k=1}^{j}dz_{p_{i,j}(k)},\ 1\leq i\leq\binom{n+2}{j},

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}_{i}^{n+2-j}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sharp_{i,j}}\prod_{(r,s)\in P_{i,j}}c_{r,s}^{-1}\bigwedge_{k=j+1}^{n+2}dz_{\bar{p}_{i,j}(k)},\ 1\leq i\leq\binom{n+2}{j},

where

p_{i,j}:\{1,\ldots,j\}\to\{1,\ldots,n+2\}

and

\bar{p}_{i,j}:\{j+1,\ldots,n+2\}\to(\operatorname{Im}(p_{i,j}))^{c}

are increasing injective maps,
\sharp_{i,j}
is the number of transpositions needed to transform

\big(\bar{p}_{i,j}(j+1),\ldots,\bar{p}_{i,j}(n+2),p_{i,j}(1),\ldots,p_{i,j}(j)\big)

into
(1,\ldots,n+2)
, and

P_{i,j}=\{(s,t)\in\{1,\ldots,j\}\times\{j+1,\ldots,n+2\}\mid p_{i,j}(s)<\bar{p}_{i,j}(t)\}.

If
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{j}A
, then it can be written uniquely as

\omega^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{\binom{n+2}{j}}\left(\bigwedge_{k=1}^{j}dz_{p_{i,j}(k)}\right)\alpha_{i},\ \alpha_{i}\in A.

This implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{\binom{n+2}{j}}\omega_{i}^{j}\pi_{\omega}\left(\bar{\omega}_{i}^{n+2-j}\wedge\omega^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\binom{n+2}{j}}\left(\bigwedge_{k=1}^{j}dz_{p_{i,j}(k)}\right)\alpha_{i}=\omega^{\prime}.

Thus, the hypotheses of Proposition
2.6
(2) are satisfied,
\omega
is an integrating volume form, and therefore
\Omega A
is a connected integrable differential calculus of dimension
n+2=\mathrm{GKdim}(A)
. This means that
A
is differentially smooth.
∎

With the aim of studying different kinds of noncommutative rings appearing in

-
skew derivation on

Two years later, he and Letzter
[
29

investigated the prime ideals in such rings. In Section 14 of their work, they defined the following class of noncommutative rings.

Let
T
be a Noetherian algebra over a field
\Bbbk

\tau
be a
\Bbbk
-algebra automorphism of

\tau

\tau

may be defined as the
\Bbbk
-algebra generated by

with the relations

(4.1)

\displaystyle yx=

\displaystyle\,\beta xy+d,

(4.2)

\displaystyle ax=

\displaystyle\,x\tau(a),\quad{\rm and}

(4.3)

\displaystyle ya=

\displaystyle\,\tau(a)y,\quad{\rm for\ all}\,\,a\in T.

Since the
\tau
-derivations
\tau^{-1}\delta\tau
and

.

\tau

and

Let

\Bbbk

\displaystyle vx=

\displaystyle\,q^{-2}xv,

\displaystyle\,vu

4.4.
Generalized Weyl algebras

Ambiskew polynomial rings are closely related to the generalized Weyl algebras defined and studied by Bavula
[
3
,
5
]
.

Given an automorphism
\sigma
and a central element
a
of a ring
B
, the
generalized Weyl algebra

B(\sigma,a)
is the ring extension of
B
generated by
X^{-}
and
X^{+}
subject to the relations

(4.4)

X^{-}X^{+}=a\quad{\rm and}\quad X^{+}X^{-}=\sigma(a),

and, for all
b\in B
,

(4.5)

X^{+}b=\sigma(b)X^{+}\quad{\rm and}\quad X^{-}\sigma(b)=bX^{-}.

If
c=\sigma(a)-pa
for some element
a\in B
, Jordan and Wells
[
34
]
say that the four-tuple
(B,\sigma,c,p)
is
conformal
. In this case, if
z:=yx-\sigma(a)=p(xy-a)
, then

(4.6)

\displaystyle yz=

\displaystyle\,py(xy-a)=p(yxy-\sigma(a)y)=pzy,

(4.7)

\displaystyle zx=

\displaystyle\,p(xy-a)x=p(xyx-x\sigma(a))=pxz,

and, for all
b\in B
,

(4.8)

zb=p(x\sigma(b)y-ab)=bz.

This means that
z
is a normal element of
B
called the
Casimir element
of
B
. This induces a
\Bbbk
-automorphism
\zeta
of
B
such that for all
b\in B
,

\zeta(b)=b,\quad\zeta(y)=p^{-1}y\quad{\rm and}\quad\zeta(x)=px.

Of course, if
p=1
then
z
is central.

Note that if we consider
\omega:=yx=pxy+c
, then
b\omega=\omega b
for all
b\in B
, and in the conformal case, we get
\omega=z+\sigma(a)
and
B[z]=B[\omega]
.

We can extend
\sigma
to a
\Bbbk
-automorphism - also denoted
\sigma
- of
B[\omega]
by setting
\sigma(\omega)=p\omega+\sigma(c)
and
\sigma^{-1}(\omega)=p^{-1}(\omega-c)
. In this way,

y\omega=\sigma(\omega)y\quad{\rm and}\quad x\omega=\sigma^{-1}(\omega)x.

In the conformal case, we have that
\sigma(z)=pz
.

The following result establishes the relation between ambiskew polynomial rings and generalized Weyl algebras.

Proposition 4.4

(
[
4
, Lemma 1.2]
,
[
34
, Corollary 2.6]
)
.

The ambiskew polynomial ring
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
is isomorphic to the generalized Weyl algebra
B[\omega](\sigma,\omega)
, where
\sigma
is extended to
B[\omega]
by setting
\sigma(\omega)=p\omega+\sigma(c)
. In the conformal case,
R(B,\sigma,c,p)\cong B[z](\sigma,z+\sigma(a))
, where
\sigma(z)=pz
.

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
4.4
, one obtains an analogue of Theorem
3.10
for generalized Weyl algebras.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
W=B[\omega](\sigma,\omega)
be the generalized Weyl algebra associated, via Proposition
4.4
, to the ambiskew polynomial ring
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
, where
B
is a finitely generated commutative
\Bbbk
-algebra generated by
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
. Assume that there exists an element

u=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\beta_{j}b_{1}^{\alpha_{1j}}\cdots b_{n}^{\alpha_{nj}}\in B,

with
\beta_{j}\in\Bbbk
and
\alpha_{1j},\dots,\alpha_{nj}\in\mathbb{N}
for all
0\leq j\leq m
, such that

c=u-p\sigma(u).

Suppose further that:

(1)

u
is
(\sigma,p)
-balanced,

(2)

c=u-p\sigma(u)\in\Bbbk
;

(3)

\sigma(b_{i})=a_{i}b_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, with
a_{i}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
;

(4)

\displaystyle p\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\alpha_{ij}}=1,\text{ for }0\leq j\leq m
;

(5)

\text{GKdim}(B)=n
.

Then
W
is differentially smooth.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.4
, the generalized Weyl algebra
W=B[\omega](\sigma,\omega)
is isomorphic to the ambiskew polynomial ring
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
. Since
c=u-p\sigma(u)
, the latter can be written as the iterated skew polynomial ring

B[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta],

where
\delta(B)=0
and
\delta(x)=u-p\sigma(u)
. Therefore, all the hypotheses of Theorem
3.10
are satisfied with
K=B
and
\rho=p
. Hence
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
is differentially smooth.
∎

4.5.
Down-up algebras

The
down-up algebras

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, with
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
(these algebras were originally defined over
\mathbb{C}
but can be defined over an arbitrary field) were introduced and investigated by Benkart and Roby
[
7
,
8
]
as generalizations of algebras by a pair of operators: the “down”  and “up”  acting on the vector space
\mathbb{C}P
for certain partially ordered sets
P
. Kirkman et al.
[
38
]
proved that a sufficient and necessary condition to assert that a down-up algebra
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
be Noetherian is that
\beta\neq 0
. Benkart
[
7
]
proved that for
\lambda\neq 0
,
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong A(\alpha,\beta,\lambda\gamma)
, which means that will be no loss of generality in assuming that
\gamma=1
.

Since Jordan
[
33
, Theorem 3.1]
proved that down-up algebras can be expressed as ambiskew polynomial rings, next we discuss the applicability of the results obtained in the previous section to these algebras. We consider the notation and the four cases described by Jordan
[
33
, Section 3]
.

For arbitrary
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
, the
down-up algebra

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra generated by the indeterminates
d
and
u
subject to the relations

(4.9)

\displaystyle d^{2}u=

\displaystyle\,\alpha dud+\beta ud^{2}+\gamma d,

(4.10)

\displaystyle du^{2}=

\displaystyle\,\alpha udu+\beta u^{2}d+\gamma u.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
\beta\neq 0
and let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be the roots, necessarily non-zero, of the equation

(4.11)

\beta X^{2}+\alpha X-1=0

and let
H
be the subgroup
\langle\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\rangle
of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. It is well-known that the defining automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{C}[t,z]
will always be of one of the following three types

\displaystyle z\mapsto\lambda z,\quad

\displaystyle t\mapsto\mu t\quad

\displaystyle(\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times})

\displaystyle z\mapsto\lambda z,

\displaystyle t\mapsto t+\nu\quad

\displaystyle(\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times})

\displaystyle z\mapsto\lambda z+\sum_{\lambda=\mu^{i}}\eta_{i}t^{i},\quad

\displaystyle t\mapsto\mu t\quad

\displaystyle(\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\,\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{C}).

These are known as
triangular
automorphisms
[
40
]
and every triangular automorphism is conjugate to an automorphism of one of the three listed types
[
54
, Proposition 1]
.

The next result is one of the most important proved by Jordan
[
33
]
.

Proposition 4.6

(
[
33
, Theorem 3.1]
)
.

Let
R
be a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra. Then
R
is isomorphic to a down-up algebra
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
with
\beta\neq 0
if and only if
R
is isomorphic to an ambiskew polynomial ring
R(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p)
for some
\mathbb{C}
-automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{C}[t]
, some
p\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, and some monic
c\in\mathbb{C}[t]
with
{\rm deg}(c)=1
.

The idea of the proof of Proposition
4.6
is to show that a down-up algebra
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is an ambiskew polynomial ring over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[t]
with either
\sigma(t)=\lambda t
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, or
\sigma(t)=t+\tau
for some
\tau\in\mathbb{C}
. For the completeness of the paper, let us recall the four cases of down-up algebras depending on the nature of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, as it was made by Jordan
[
33
, Section 3]
.

•

Case 1
. Assume that
\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}
and that
\mu_{i}\neq 1
for
i=1,2
. In other words,
\alpha^{2}+4\beta\neq 0
and
\alpha+\beta\neq 1
.

Let
B=\mathbb{C}[t]
and let
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}[t])
given by
\sigma(t)=\mu_{2}^{-1}t
. Consider the elements

\rho=\mu_{1}^{-1}\quad{\rm and}\quad c=-\mu_{1}^{-1}\left(t+\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\gamma}{1-\mu_{2}}\right).

From
[
23
, Proposition 1]
we know that
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
is generated by the indeterminates
x,y
and
t
subject to three relations

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mu_{2}yt=

\displaystyle\,ty,

(4.13)

\displaystyle xt=

\displaystyle\,\mu_{2}tx,\quad{\rm and}

(4.14)

\displaystyle xy-\mu_{1}yx=

\displaystyle\,t+\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\gamma}{1-\mu_{2}}.

Jordan asserted that by (
4.14
) the generator
t
is redundant, so using (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) it follows that
x
and
y
satisfy the down-up relations (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) with
y=d
and
x=u
; conversely, to check that if two generators
x,y
satisfy (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), and (
4.14
) is used to define
t
, then (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) hold. These facts imply that
A(\sigma,\beta,\gamma)=R\left(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p\right)
.

Note that
(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p)
is conformal with

a=\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}t+\frac{\gamma\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}{(1-\mu_{1})(1-\mu_{2})},

and hence

z=yx-\frac{1}{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}t-\frac{\gamma\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}{(1-\mu_{1})(1-\mu_{2})}

is a Casimir element. By Proposition
4.4
,

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\Bbbk[t,z](\sigma,z+\sigma(a))\quad{\rm where}\,\,\sigma(t)=\mu_{2}^{-1}t\ \ {\rm and}\ \ \sigma(z)=\mu_{1}^{-1}z.

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
has also a second presentation as an ambiskew polynomial ring given by

R(\mathbb{C}[z],\sigma,c,\mu_{2}),\quad{\rm where}\,\,\sigma(z)=\mu_{1}^{-1}z\ \ {\rm and}\ \ c=z+\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\gamma}{1-\mu_{1}}.

•

Case 2
. Suppose that
\mu_{1}=1\neq-\frac{1}{\beta}=\mu_{2}
. Equivalently,
\alpha+\beta=1
and
\alpha\neq 2
.

Let
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}[t])
with
\sigma(t)=-\beta t
. Consider

p=1\quad{\rm and}\quad c=-t+\frac{\gamma}{\beta+1}.

Then
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
is generated by
x,y
and
t
subject to the relations

(4.15)

\displaystyle yt=

\displaystyle\ -\beta ty,

(4.16)

\displaystyle-\beta xt=

\displaystyle\ tx,\quad{\rm and}

(4.17)

\displaystyle xy-yx=

\displaystyle\ t-\frac{\gamma}{\beta+1}.

As in the
Case A
, these are the same with
\mu_{1}=1
and
\mu_{2}=-\frac{1}{\beta}
, which implies that
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=R(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p)
. To decide if
(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p)
is conformal we consider
\gamma=0
.

Suppose that
\gamma=0
. Then
(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p)
is conformal with
a=\frac{1}{1+\beta}t
, the Casimir element given by

z=yx+\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}t=xy-\frac{1}{1+\beta}t

and
yx+\beta xy=(1+\beta)z
. Again, by Proposition
4.4
,

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\mathbb{C}[t,z](\sigma,z+\sigma(a))\quad{\rm with}\,\,\sigma(t)=-\beta t\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\sigma(z)=z.

As it can be seen,
\sigma
is triangular of type (a) and
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
can be expressed as an ambiskew polynomial ring
R(\mathbb{C}[z],\sigma,c,p)
, where

(4.18)

\sigma(z)=z,\,\,p=-\beta\quad{\rm and}\quad c=(1+\beta)z.

In the case
\gamma\neq 0
(we may suppose that
\gamma=1
), for
a\in\mathbb{C}[t]
, the element
\sigma(a)-a
has zero constant term, which means that
(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p)
is not conformal. If
f=(\beta+1)\omega+\beta t-1
, then
\mathbb{C}[t,\omega]=\mathbb{C}[t,f]
and
\sigma(f)=f+1
, due to Proposition
4.4
,

(4.19)

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\mathbb{C}[t,f](\sigma,\omega)\quad{\rm with}\,\,\sigma(t)=-\beta t\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\sigma(f)=f+1.

In this case,
\sigma
is triangular of type (b). An alternative presentation of
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
as an ambiskew polynomial ring can be obtained in the following way. Let

h=\beta^{-1}(f+(\beta+1^{-1}))=\beta^{-1}\left((\beta+1)yx+\beta t-\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right)

From expression (
4.17
) for
t
, it follows that
h=xy+\beta^{-1}yx
, and so
\sigma(h)=h+\beta^{-1}
, whence
h,x
and
y
satisfy the relations

(4.20)

\displaystyle hy-yh=

\displaystyle\,-\beta^{-1}y,

(4.21)

\displaystyle hx-xh=

\displaystyle\ \beta^{-1}x,\quad{\rm and}

(4.22)

\displaystyle yx+\beta xy=

\displaystyle\ \beta h,

and

(4.23)

\displaystyle A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=R(\mathbb{C}[h],\sigma,\beta h,-\beta)\quad{\rm where}\,\,\sigma(h)=h+\beta^{-1}.

With this presentation, the four-tuple
(\mathbb{C}[h],\sigma,\beta h,-\beta)
is conformal with

a=\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}h-\frac{1}{(1+\beta)^{2}}

and Casimir element given by

z=yx-\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}h+\frac{1}{\beta(1+\beta)^{2}}.

This presentation is similar to the presentation of
A(1-\beta,\beta,1)
as an iterated skew polynomial ring formulated by Carvalho and Musson
[
18
]
.

•

Case 3
. Now, we assume that
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}\neq 1
and write
\mu=\mu_{1}
. Then
\alpha^{2}+4\beta=0,\,\mu=\frac{2}{\alpha},\,\alpha\neq 2
and
\mu^{2}=-\frac{1}{\beta}
.

Let
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}[t])
defined by
\sigma(t)=\mu^{-1}t
, Consider

p=\mu^{-1}\quad{\rm and}\quad c=-\mu^{-1}\left(t+\frac{\mu^{2}\gamma}{1-\mu}\right).

The defining equations for
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
are

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mu yt=

\displaystyle\ ty,

(4.25)

\displaystyle xt=

\displaystyle\ \mu tx,\quad{\rm and}

(4.26)

\displaystyle xy-\mu yx=

\displaystyle\ t+\frac{\mu^{2}\gamma}{1-\mu}.

If we compare with
Case A
, then we get that
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\mu
, so that
R(B,\sigma,c,p)=A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.

Since for
a\in\mathbb{C}[t]
,
\sigma(a)-pa
has zero coefficient of
t
,
(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,,c,p)
is not conformal. By Proposition
4.4
,

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\mathbb{C}[t,\omega](\sigma,\omega)\ {\rm with}\ \ \sigma(t)=\mu^{-1}t\ \ {\rm and}\ \ \sigma(\omega)=\mu^{-1}\left(\omega-\mu^{-1}t-\frac{\mu^{2}\gamma}{1-\mu}\right).

With the aim of showing that
\sigma
is conjugate to a triangular automorphism of type (c), let
h=\omega-\frac{\mu^{2}\gamma}{(1-\mu)^{2}}
. Then
\mathbb{C}[t,\omega]=\mathbb{C}[t,h]
and

(4.27)

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\mathbb{C}[t,h](\sigma,\omega)\quad{\rm where}\,\,\sigma(t)=\mu^{-1}t\ \ {\rm and}\ \ \sigma(h)=\mu^{-1}(h-\mu^{-1}t).

•

Case 4
. Finally, consider
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=1
. Then
\alpha=2
and
\beta=-1
. Let
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}[t])
with
\sigma(t)=t-\gamma
. Consider
p=1
and
c=-t
. Then
R(B,\sigma,c,p)
is generated by
x,y
and
t
subject to the relations

(4.28)

\displaystyle yt=

\displaystyle\ ty-\gamma y,

(4.29)

\displaystyle xt=

\displaystyle\ tx+\gamma x,\quad{\rm and}

(4.30)

\displaystyle xy-yx=

\displaystyle\ t.

Using a similar reasoning to the presented in
Case A
, it can be seen that
R(B,\sigma,c,p)=A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. If
\gamma=1
, then three equations are the same that those obtained by setting
\beta=-1
and
t=h
in (
4.20
), (
4.21
) and (
4.22
).

Suppose that
\gamma\neq 0
(we may assume that
\gamma=1
). After some computations, we get that
A(2,-1,1)\cong U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
.
(B,\sigma,c,p)
is conformal with

a=\frac{1}{2}(t^{2}+t)\ \ {\rm and\ Casimir\ element}\ \ z=yx-\frac{1}{2}(t^{2}-t).

Once again, from Proposition
4.4
it follows that

(4.31)

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\Bbbk[t,z](\sigma,z+\sigma(a))\quad{\rm with}\,\,\sigma(t)=t-1\ \ {\rm and}\ \ \sigma(z)=z.

In this way,
\sigma
is triangular of type (b) with
\lambda=1
.

Note that if
\gamma=0
,
(\mathbb{C}[t],\sigma,c,p)
is not conformal because
\sigma(a)-pa=0
for all
a\in\mathbb{C}[t]
. Once again, Proposition
4.4
implies that

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\Bbbk[t,\omega](\sigma,\omega)\quad{\rm with}\quad\sigma(t)=t\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\sigma(\omega)=\omega-t.

Note that
\sigma
is triangular of type (c) with
\lambda=\mu=1
, and
A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is isomorphic to the universal enveloping algebra of the Heisenberg Lie algebra.

In the following, we show in detail that Theorem
3.10
cannot be applied to any of the four previous cases.

•

Case 1
. Both presentations have diagonal automorphism on the polynomial base, namely
\sigma(t)=\mu_{2}^{-1}t
and
\sigma(z)=\mu_{1}^{-1}z
, so condition (3) of Theorem
3.10
is satisfied. However, in both realizations the corresponding element
u-\rho\sigma(u)
is not an element of the field
\Bbbk
:

c=-\mu_{1}^{-1}\left(t+\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\gamma}{1-\mu_{2}}\right)\notin\Bbbk\quad\text{and}\quad c=z+\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\gamma}{1-\mu_{1}}\notin\Bbbk.

Hence, condition (2) fails.

•

Case 2
. When
\gamma=0
the presentation over
\mathbb{C}[z]
has
\sigma(z)=z
, condition (3) holds but
c=(1+\beta)z\notin\Bbbk
, and therefore condition (2) fails. When
\gamma\neq 0
, the alternative presentation

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=R(\mathbb{C}[h],\sigma,\beta h,-\beta)\quad{\rm with}\quad\sigma(h)=h+\beta^{-1},

shows that the automorphism is no longer diagonal on the polynomial generator, so condition (3) fails; moreover,
c=\beta h\notin\Bbbk
.

•

Case 3
. The presentation over
\mathbb{C}[t]
has diagonal automorphism
\sigma(t)=\mu^{-1}t
, but

c=-\mu^{-1}\left(t+\frac{\mu^{2}\gamma}{1-\mu}\right)\notin\Bbbk,

so condition (2) fails. On the other hand, the conjugated presentation

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\mathbb{C}[t,h](\sigma,\omega)\quad{\rm with}\quad\sigma(t)=\mu^{-1}t,\quad\sigma(h)=\mu^{-1}(h-\mu^{-1}t),

shows that the automorphism is triangular rather than diagonal, so condition (3) is no longer satisfied in that realization.

•

Case 4
. If
\gamma\neq 0
then already in the presentation over
\mathbb{C}[t]
one has
\sigma(t)=t-\gamma
, which is translational, whence condition (3) fails. If
\gamma=0
, then
\sigma(t)=t
but
c=-t\notin\Bbbk
, so condition (2) fails. Moreover, the alternative presentation

A(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\cong\Bbbk[t,\omega](\sigma,\omega)\quad{\rm with}\quad\sigma(t)=t,\quad\sigma(\omega)=\omega-t,

again falls outside the diagonal setting required in condition (3).

Therefore, the down-up algebras lie just beyond the scope of Theorem
3.10
.

5.
Future work

Generalized down-up algebras
(
GDU algebras
for short) were introduced by Cassidy and Shelton
[
19
]
as a generalization of the down-up algebras considered in Section
4.5
.

Following
[
19
, Section 2]
, let
\Bbbk
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. Fix scalars
r,s,\gamma\in\Bbbk
and a polynomial
f\in\Bbbk[x]
. A
generalized down-up algebra

L:=L(f,r,s,\gamma)
is a unital associative
\Bbbk
-algebra generated by
d,u
and
h
, subject to relations

(5.1)

\displaystyle dh-rhd+\gamma d=

\displaystyle\,0,

(5.2)

\displaystyle hu-ruh+\gamma u=

\displaystyle\,0,\quad{\rm and}

(5.3)

\displaystyle du-sud+f(h)=

\displaystyle\,0.

When
f
has degree one, all down-up algebras in
[
7
,
8
]
are retrieved. Cassidy and Shelton
[
19
]
showed that
L
has Gelfand-Kirillov dimension three and it is Noetherian if and only if it is a domain, or equivalently,
rs\neq 0

Almulhem and Brzeziński
[
2
, Section 1]
asserted on the GDU algebras that “It is natural to ask whether they are differentially smooth. In this note we do not attempt to answer this question, but rather provide the first paving stones for a path that might lead to an answer by constructing a class of skew derivations.” We have searched the literature and apparently no work has been done that studies the differential smoothness of these algebras. Due to the relationships between generalized Weyl algebras and GDU algebras, and the study of the automorphisms and the derivations of these two families of algebras carried out in
[
13
,
17
,
19
,
38
]
, we consider that an immediate task is to address this investigation, so that we can formulate sufficient conditions that guarantee the smoothness of GDU algebras, and as a consequence, we can obtain some conclusions that were pending at the end of Section
4.5
when we show the limitations of Theorem
3.10
.
