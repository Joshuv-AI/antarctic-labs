EEG blink and gaze control using random forest classification for accessible assistive robotic navigation in real world conditions
Authors: Mouad Nechchad, Badr Elkari, Imane El Midaoui, Shadia Ait El Cadi, Zineb M’Hifed, Loubna Ourabah, Abebaw Degu Workneh, Yassine Chaibi, Shaik Mohammad Irshad, Z M S El-Barbary, Mourad Yessef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an EEG-based Brain–Computer Interface for intuitive robotic navigation driven by ocular activity. A real multi-subject dataset collected from 15 participants was used to extract blink- and gaze related EEG features for five control commands. Eight supervised classifiers were evaluated under stratified 5-fold cross-validation: Random Forest, multilayer perceptron, support vector machine, k-nearest neighbors, RUSBoost, Naive Bayes, decision tree, and linear discriminant analysis. Among them, Random Forest achieved the best overall performance, reaching 98.74 ± 1.19% accuracy, 0.9874 Macro-F1, and 0.9993 macro-AUC, demonstrating excellent robustness and class separability across the five ocular classes. The decoded commands were transmitted wirelessly to an embedded Raspberry Pi platform, where they were converted into safe motor actions for wheelchair type robot navigation. Real world experiments in indoor environments confirmed stable motion control, reliable command execution, and successful obstacle avoidance without physical interaction from the user. These findings support the feasibility of a low-cost, portable, and non-invasive BCI solution based on realistic multi-subject EEG data for assistive mobility applications. Although the full control loop operated online, the end-to-end system latency was not quantitatively benchmarked in the present study and remains an important limitation to be addressed in future work. Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) has emerged as a pivotal technology in brain-computer interface (BCI) systems, offering non-invasive access to brain activity for various applications in assistive technology, healthcare, and human-computer interaction (HCI)1–3. Historically, EEG has been extensively used in clinical settings to diagnose neurological conditions such as epilepsy4–6, sleep disorders7,8, and brain injuries9–12. More recently, advancements in biosignal processing and machine learning have expanded its application to control external devices, leveraging the brain’s electrical activity for intuitive communication and interaction. In the context of assistive technologies, several studies have explored the use of EEG signals for mobility solutions, particularly for individuals with motor impairments13,14. Early BCI systems primarily focused on motor imagery-based control, where users imagined limb movements to generate distinguishable EEG patterns15. However, these systems often required extensive training and posed challenges in terms of accuracy, real-time performance, and usability in daily life. Furthermore, motor imagery-based systems can be mentally taxing and may not offer the same level of ease and comfort as more intuitive control methods. These limitations have led to a growing interest in alternative BCI control mechanisms. One such alternative is eye-movement-based EEG control, which has gained increasing attention due to its natural and intuitive interaction paradigm16–18. Eye movements, such as looking left or right and blinking, can be easily controlled and do not require intensive mental focus. Research has demonstrated that EEG signals generated from ocular activities can effectively be harnessed to control assistive devices. These findings have opened new possibilities for the development of more user-friendly and accessible BCI systems, particularly for individuals with severe motor impairments. Despite these advances, the use of eye-movement-based EEG for controlling assistive devices is still relatively underexplored, particularly in real-world applications.

While machine learning classifiers have been integrated into BCI systems to improve signal decoding, challenges remain in achieving consistent real-time control with high accuracy19. Moreover, most existing systems rely on small datasets or limited experimental conditions, making them less robust and generalized to diverse users and environments20. This gap in research calls for the development of more comprehensive and validated EEG datasets, along with innovative methods for real-time signal classification that can be used across different assistive technologies.

Parallel advancements in human-computer interaction (HCI) and virtual reality (VR) have also utilized EEG signals to create adaptive user interfaces, facilitating hands-free control of digital devices and immersive environments21. In healthcare, EEG continues to play a crucial role in diagnosing eye movement disorders and monitoring vestibular function, contributing to personalized rehabilitation strategies. However, the integration of these advancements into assistive devices, such as wheelchairs, remains limited. The objective of this study is to develop and evaluate an EEG-based control system that enables paralyzed individuals to navigate a wheelchair using intuitive eye movements. By capturing and processing EEG signals corresponding to specific ocular actions (look left, look right, left wink, right wink, and double blink), the study aims to create a responsive and user-friendly brain-computer interface. This system integrates advanced signal processing and machine learning techniques to accurately classify eye movement patterns, contributing to the advancement of assistive robotics and expanding the applications of EEG in human-computer interaction and medical diagnostics. To provide a broader and more rigorous comparative assessment, this study evaluates eight classification models under a unified stratified 5-fold cross-validation protocol. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews recent advances in EEG-based brain-computer interfaces and EEG-driven robotic control, and positions our contribution with respect to existing classification and control strategies. The Methodology section then details the proposed eye-movement BCI framework, including EEG acquisition with BITalino, preprocessing, dataset construction from real experiments with 15 participants, data augmentation, feature extraction, the eight evaluated classification models (Random Forest, MLP, SVM, k-NN, RUSBoost, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and LDA), the evaluation protocol based on stratified 5-fold cross-validation, the command-generation scheme, and the embedded implementation on the differential-drive robot. The Results and Discussion section presents the offline classification performance on the real EEG dataset, compares the eight evaluated models using multiple metrics, and reports the real-time navigation experiments in three increasingly complex indoor scenarios. Finally, the Conclusion summarizes the main findings, discusses current limitations, and outlines perspectives for future work on advanced decoding, sensor fusion, and large-scale validation.

Related works

EEG control refers to the use of brain signals, captured through an EEG device, to interact with and control external systems such as computers, robots, or other devices22–25. EEG measures the electrical activity of the brain using sensors placed on the scalp. In26, the authors introduced advanced classification techniques, notably Particle Swarm Optimization and Radial Basis Function Network (PSO-RBFN), fast Fuzzy Support Vector Classifier (FFSVC), and Improved Fast Fuzzy Support Vector Classifier (IFFSVC), for EEG signal classification in real-time BCI applications. Their approach effectively addressed the low signal-to-noise ratio by combining Particle Swarm Optimization with Radial Basis Function Networks. However, the study’s reliance on standard datasets limits its generalizability to real-world applications, and scalability concerns for more complex systems were not fully explored. Islam et al. proposed using SVM, k-NN, and ANN classifiers for EEG-based brain control machines focused on directional commands27. The system achieved high accuracy, notably 95.21% with SVM. Yet, the framework lacked real-time testing and user adaptability analysis, raising concerns about its practical deployment. In28, Al-Saegh et al. addressed data scarcity in EEG classification with the CutCat augmentation method, improving CNN-based classification for motor imagery tasks. Another work was presented in in29. A real-time EEG classification method using SCA-optimized filtering and LightGBM classifiers has been developed. The system achieved high accuracy but was heavily dependent on the optimization algorithm’s performance. Additionally, the method’s robustness across diverse datasets was not extensively validated. Siuly and Li developed an Optimum Allocation-Principal Component Analysis (OA-PCA) feature extraction method coupled with Least Squares Support Vector Machine (LS-SVM) for epileptic EEG classification, achieving 100% accuracy30. Despite its effectiveness, the model’s complexity and reliance on PCA may hinder scalability for real-time applications. In31, authors introduced Local Neighbor Descriptive Pattern (LNDP) and One-Dimensional Local Gradient Pattern (1D-LGP) feature extraction for epileptic EEG classification, yielding impressive accuracy. However, their approach’s computational complexity and limited generalization to non-epileptic EEG datasets present notable weaknesses. Li et al. proposed a Convolutional Neural Network-Long Short-Term Memory (CNN-LSTM) feature fusion network for motor imagery EEG classification, effectively capturing spatial and temporal features32. Nonetheless, the model’s reliance on large datasets and potential overfitting are critical limitations. Other works aim to control robotic platforms using EEG Signals. In33, an SVM-based BCI for robotic arm control, using PCA and FFT for feature extraction has been established. Although achieving 85.45% accuracy, the system’s limited subject pool and reliance on the EMOTIV EPOC headset reduce its robustness. A low-frequency EEG decoding for robotic arm control, utilizing PLS-based models was implemented in34. While innovative, the study’s exclusive focus on delta band frequencies might overlook critical higher-frequency information essential for more precise control. A good review of advancements in cognitive neuroscience and robotics has been conducted, focusing on brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and brain-robot interfaces (BRIs). Their work highlighted the integration of EEG signal processing and deep learning for robot control35. Lastly, in36, Ban et al. developed a multifunctional robot control system using a multimodal brain-machine interface combining Steady-State Visual Evoked Potential (SSVEP), Electrooculography (EOG), and gyroscope signals. The proposed ACB-Net model achieved high accuracy but may suffer from increased computational complexity and potential user fatigue due to the visual stimulation required for SSVEP. These studies collectively demonstrate the rapid advancements in EEG signal processing and its applications in brain-computer interfaces and robotics. Despite impressive progress, challenges such as computational complexity, scalability, and generalization across diverse datasets remain significant barriers to practical deployment. In addition to the recent EEG-BCI methods reviewed above, the present revised study broadens its own experimental comparison by evaluating eight supervised classifiers under a unified stratified 5-fold cross-validation protocol. This extended benchmark includes Random Forest, MLP, SVM, k-NN, RUSBoost, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and LDA, and was adopted to provide a broader and more methodologically consistent comparison in the specific context of compact ocular EEG-based robotic control. Methodology

The methodology proposed in this study relies on the use of micro-ocular movements to control a mobile robotic platform or an electric wheelchair. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the process begins with the detection of EEG signals generated during specific eye movements (lateral gaze, single blink, or double blink). These brain signals, recorded through electrodes placed on the frontal region, are transmitted to a control unit (represented here by a Raspberry Pi board). The control unit is responsible for signal processing, extracting relevant features, and classifying them in order to identify the type of eye movement performed. Finally, the corresponding commands are translated into navigation instructions for the robotic platform like wheelchair. This approach establishes a complete loop from the user’s intention (eye movement) to the physical execution of the command (robot movement), thus providing a natural and non-invasive interaction between the human and the assistive device.

Eye-controlled robotic navigation system architecture. schematic illustration created by the authors.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, The process begins with the acquisition of EEG signals, recorded during specific eye movements using frontal electrodes. To ensure signal clarity, a preprocessing stage is applied, including filtering and segmentation, which reduces noise and isolates the portions of the signals relevant to ocular activity. Next, a training dataset is created by labeling the preprocessed EEG segments according to the type of eye movement performed (e.g., left gaze, right gaze, single blink, double blink). This step establishes the foundation for supervised learning by associating raw brain activity with corresponding movement intentions.

Proposed BCI processing pipeline.

From this dataset, a set of discriminative features (mean, maximum, standard deviation, entropy, skewness, etc.) is extracted to capture the essential statistical and temporal properties of the signals. These features are then provided as input to machine learning (ML) classifiers and deep neural network (DNN) models, which are trained to recognize patterns associated with each ocular movement. Once classification is achieved, the system translates the recognized movement into a control command. Finally, these commands are executed on the robotic platform, completing the loop from user intention to robotic action.

Participants

Fifteen healthy adult volunteers participated in the EEG data acquisition sessions. None reported neurological disorders, visual impairments, or other conditions that could affect the execution of the ocular tasks. All participants were recruited from the Euro-Mediterranean University of Fes and provided written informed consent after receiving a clear explanation of the study objectives and experimental procedures.

All experiments were conducted individually in a controlled environment within the Robotics and Cobotics Laboratory of the School of Digital Engineering and Artificial Intelligence (EIDIA). To ensure consistency across sessions, lighting conditions, seating posture, and surrounding noise were standardized during data acquisition.

For each of the five ocular movements, every participant performed the task at least five times. After signal quality verification, the three most reliable trials per participant and per movement were retained for dataset construction. This resulted in 45 original EEG signals per movement (15 participants × 3 retained trials), corresponding to 225 original signals across the five classes.

To improve dataset robustness and maintain class balance for model training, a controlled signal-level data augmentation strategy was subsequently applied to the retained signals. The final dataset structure is detailed in the following subsection. Overall, the present study should be interpreted as a multi-subject proof-of-concept experiment, designed to evaluate the feasibility and robustness of the proposed EEG-based ocular control framework under standardized real acquisition conditions.

EEG signal acquisition

EEG data were acquired using the BITalino® Evolution biosignal acquisition system, equipped with three Ag/AgCl electrodes strategically positioned according to the international 10–20 system as shown in Fig. 3. Two electrodes were placed at the FP1 (left frontopolar) and FP2 (right frontopolar) positions of the international 10–20 system to capture eye-related EEG activity, while a third electrode was positioned as the reference. The signals were recorded at a sampling frequency of 1 kHz, with amplitudes ranging from 0 to 1200 mV.

EEG electrode placement and BITalino acquisition module.

The experiment involved 15 volunteers, in a controlled environment to minimize noise and artifacts. Each participant was guided through a sequence of five distinct ocular tasks: looking left, looking right, left-eye wink, right-eye wink, and double blink. These tasks were mapped directly to corresponding robotic commands: turn left, turn right, move forward, move backward, and stop, respectively. Real-time monitoring during acquisition ensured the consistency and integrity of the recordings, providing a high-quality dataset for further processing and classification.

Preprocessing

The raw EEG signals collected during the experiments underwent a dedicated preprocessing stage to improve their quality and reliability. Because EEG recordings are inherently sensitive to various sources of noise and artifacts, such as muscle activity, power-line interference, and minor head movements, a Hamming-window-based filtering step was applied during preprocessing. This operation reduced high-frequency noise and improved the visibility of the signal patterns associated with ocular movements, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Preprocessing and segmentation of the five ocular EEG tasks.

After filtering, the signals were segmented into temporal windows, each corresponding to one of the five ocular tasks performed during the experiment: M1 (looking left), M2 (looking right), M3 (left-eye wink), M4 (right-eye wink), and M5 (double blink). To avoid overlap between movements, controlled waiting intervals were inserted between consecutive tasks. These are denoted as W12, W23, W34, and W45, representing the resting periods between successive movements (for example, W12 is the waiting time between M1 and M2).

This structure ensured that each EEG segment captured only the intentional activity associated with a specific eye movement, resulting in a set of clean and structured signals ready for the subsequent stages of feature extraction and classification.

Dataset construction and data augmentation

Based on the retained EEG trials described above, the dataset was organized into five classes corresponding to the five ocular tasks. To improve the diversity and robustness of the training data while preserving class balance, a signal-level data augmentation strategy was applied to the original EEG signals. As can be seen in Fig. 5, three lightweight and commonly used transformations were adopted, namely amplitude scaling, Gaussian noise injection, and temporal shifting, as these techniques are widely used to introduce realistic variability in EEG signals while preserving the underlying class-related patterns39–41. The augmentation procedure was repeated twice, and at each repetition the transformation parameters were slightly varied, including different scaling factors, noise levels, and temporal shift values, in order to generate realistic but non-identical samples that better reflect natural inter trial variability and acquisition differences. After augmentation, the dataset contained 270 samples per class, resulting in a balanced dataset of 1350 samples in total. Each sample was then represented by a 7 dimensional feature vector extracted from the corresponding EEG segment. It should be noted that the final dataset size includes both the retained original EEG signals and the augmented samples generated from them. The final dataset size reported in this study includes both the retained original EEG signals collected from participants and the augmented samples generated from them.

Each segmented EEG sample was labeled according to the corresponding ocular task performed by the participant : left gaze, right gaze, left-eye wink, right-eye wink, or double blink. The resulting dataset was structured for supervised multi-class classification, with balanced representation across the five classes. After feature extraction, each sample was represented by a 7-dimensional feature vector and associated with its corresponding class label, forming the final input matrix used for model training and evaluation.

RUSBoost

RUSBoost

RUSBoost combines random undersampling with boosting in order to improve classification robustness, especially in settings where local decision instability may affect performance. In this study, it was included as an additional ensemble-based baseline to complement the Random Forest classifier and to broaden the comparative analysis under the same evaluation framework.

Naive Bayes (NB)

Naive Bayes (NB)

Naive Bayes is a probabilistic classifier based on Bayes’ theorem under the conditional independence assumption between features. Despite its simplicity, it is often used as a lightweight baseline in biomedical signal classification due to its low computational cost and straightforward interpretability.

Decision Tree (DT)

Decision Tree (DT)

The Decision Tree classifier performs hierarchical partitioning of the feature space using sequential decision rules. It was included in this study as an interpretable baseline model, allowing direct comparison between single-tree decisions and ensemble-based approaches such as Random Forest and RUSBoost.

Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)

Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)

LDA is a classical statistical classifier that seeks linear combinations of features maximizing class separability. It has been widely used in EEG pattern recognition because of its simplicity, efficiency, and good performance in low-dimensional feature spaces.

Evaluation protocol. The classification performance was evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation. In each fold, approximately 80% of the samples were used for training and 20% for testing, while preserving the class distribution across folds. Feature standardization was performed using the training partition only, and the same transformation parameters were then applied to the corresponding test partition. Final performance was reported as the mean ± standard deviation across the five folds.

Command generation

Once the classifier predicted the ocular movement class, the output probabilities were mapped to a discrete control command for the robotic platform. For instance, a detected left gaze (M1) is immediately translated into a left-turn command, while a double blink (M5) triggers a stop action, ensuring safety during operation. The classifier was coupled to the robot controller through a two-stage decision process. First, the predicted ocular class was mapped to a high-level navigation command. Second, this command was converted by the embedded control unit into low-level motor-control instructions for the differential-drive platform.

To implement this mapping in real time, the control unit continuously monitors the classifier outputs, applies the decision rule, and generates the corresponding motor control instructions. These low-level commands are then transmitted to the motor driver circuitry of the mobile robot, guaranteeing seamless translation from brain activity to physical action.

Ethical approval and informed consent

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The EEG experiments were reviewed and authorized by the direction of the School of Digital Engineering and Artificial Intelligence (EIDIA) at the Euro-Mediterranean University of Fes (UEMF), which ensured that the study respected ethical standards related to participant safety, confidentiality, and data protection. All participants received a clear explanation of the study objectives and procedures. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to EEG data collection.

The Raspberry Pi handles the incoming data stream and performs on-device signal processing, including filtering, feature extraction, and real-time classification to infer the intended ocular movement. While eight classification models were compared offline under the same evaluation protocol, Random Forest was selected for deployment on the robotic platform because it achieved the best overall performance and robustness. Once the user’s intention is identified, the predicted class is translated into a safe and discrete PWM motor command, enabling reliable navigation in close proximity to humans.

This embedded implementation ensures real-time, onboard decision-making without external computation, which is essential for practical human-robot interaction scenarios.

Results and discussion

This section presents the experimental results obtained from the classification of five ocular EEG tasks using eight supervised models, namely RF, MLP, SVM, k-NN, RUSBoost, NB, DT, and LDA. The evaluation was conducted using stratified 5-fold cross-validation, and the reported accuracy values therefore correspond to the mean ± standard deviation across the five folds. Multiple metrics were considered, including Accuracy, Macro-F1, and the macro and micro areas under the curve, namely macro-AUC and micro-AUC, in order to provide a robust and fair assessment of performance across the five command classes. In addition, confusion matrices, Receiver Operating Characteristic curves (ROC), precision-recall curves, and actual-versus-predicted plots were used to further analyze the discriminative ability and robustness of each classifier.

Table 3 summarizes the overall classification performance of the eight evaluated models. RF achieved the best overall results, with an accuracy of 98.74 ± 1.19%, a Macro-F1 of 0.9874, and a macro-AUC of 0.9993, confirming its excellent ability to discriminate between the five ocular EEG classes. k-NN and DT also delivered strong performance, with accuracies of 93.78 ± 1.54% and 92.44 ± 2.44%, respectively, together with high Macro-F1 values, indicating robust multi-class decoding capability. MLP and RUSBoost showed intermediate performance, both around 81% accuracy, whereas SVM remained lower at 75.63 ± 2.98%, suggesting higher sensitivity to EEG variability in the considered feature space. The weakest results were obtained with LDA and especially NB, which reached 66.96 ± 1.37% and 59.48 ± 2.90% accuracy, respectively. Overall, these results indicate that ensemble-based and neighborhood-based models are the most suitable strategies in the present ocular EEG classification framework, with RF providing the best compromise between accuracy, robustness, and separability for subsequent real-time robotic deployment.

To evaluate the real-time capability of the proposed EEG-based Human–Robot Interface, a set of indoor navigation experiments was conducted using the developed mobile assistive robot. The aim of these trials was to verify whether the strong offline performance obtained by the best-performing classifier selected from the eight-model comparative analysis could be reliably transferred to live motion control in realistic conditions. For this purpose, the robot was tested in different environments of increasing complexity, starting from a standard corridor path and gradually introducing visual distractors and static obstacles to simulate more challenging navigation conditions.

During all experiments, the robot received continuous ocular-based control commands (move forward, turn left, turn right) extracted from EEG signals and classified in real time using the trained Random Forest model. No physical interaction, remote joystick, or predefined trajectory was provided to the robot, ensuring full user intention–driven motion. This experimental protocol allowed us to assess the robustness, responsiveness, and safety of the proposed BCI system when deployed in real environments, ultimately demonstrating its potential for future assistive mobility applications.

The user initially maintained a neutral gaze, which was consistently classified as the “Move Forward” command (M3), allowing the robot to advance along the central axis of the corridor (Frames 1–13). This prolonged straight motion with no lateral drift demonstrates the high temporal stability of the classifier and the robust actuation response of the mobile platform when continuous EEG segments are mapped to the same command.

In this study, the term real-time refers to online operation of the full control loop, including EEG acquisition, preprocessing, classification, wireless communication, and robot actuation without offline post-processing. However, the end-to-end system latency was not quantitatively benchmarked in the present version, which should be considered a limitation of the study.

Conclusion

In this work, a real-time EEG-based navigation system was developed and validated using a realistic dataset generated through controlled experiments with a human participants. The results demonstrate that blink- and gaze-related ocular activities contain sufficiently discriminative information for reliable robotic control. Among the evaluated machine-learning models, the Random Forest classifier achieved the best performance, offering high robustness to EEG signal variability and ensuring accurate command generation. The successful execution of three experimental navigation scenarios, including interaction with obstacles, confirms the practical suitability of the proposed framework for assistive mobility in human robot interaction contexts.

This study contributes a low-cost, portable, and non-invasive solution toward empowering individuals with motor impairments by enabling intuitive neural control of assistive platforms. However, some limitations remain, including the restricted number of ocular commands, sensitivity to muscle artifacts, and the need for user-specific calibration. A first limitation of this study lies in the moderate size of the original acquisition cohort, which involved 15 participants. Although the final dataset was substantially enriched through controlled signal-level augmentation, the number of real human-acquired EEG recordings remains limited. Therefore, the present work should be interpreted as a multi-subject proof-of-concept study rather than a large-scale validation study. Another limitation is that the end-to-end latency of the system was not quantitatively measured.

Future investigations should include a larger and more diverse participant population to further assess the generalizability and robustness of the proposed framework in broader real-world assistive scenarios. Future developments will also focus on expanding the range of controllable actions through more advanced neural decoding, improving robustness using adaptive filtering and deep-learning-based models, incorporating additional sensors to support shared autonomy and safety, and validating the system across a broader and more diverse population to ensure real-world usability. Lastly, the perspective will include a detailed timing analysis of acquisition, processing, communication, and actuation delays.
