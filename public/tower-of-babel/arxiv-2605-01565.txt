Vertex connectivity of the nonzero nonunit core of the comaximal graph of ℤ n \displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n} .
Bilal Ahmad Rather

Abstract
This article settles Problem 7.2 posed by [Banerjee, Special Matrices (2022)] for the induced subgraph G 2 \displaystyle G_{2} of the comaximal graph Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) \displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}) when n \displaystyle n is squarefree. Let n = p 1 p 2 ⋯ p m \displaystyle n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m} with distinct primes p 1 < ⋯ < p m \displaystyle p_{1}<\cdots<p_{m} , and let G 2 \displaystyle G_{2} be the graph on the nonzero nonunit residue classes modulo n \displaystyle n . We use Chinese remainder representation of ℤ n \displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n} , and encodes each vertex by the set of vanishing coordinates. This converts G 2 \displaystyle G_{2} into a weighted blow-up of a disjointness graph on nonempty proper subsets of { 1 , … , m } \displaystyle\{1,\dots,m\} . Within this model, we derive exact class sizes, explicit degree formulas, the minimum-degree layer, and a short-path criterion. The main theorem proves the connectivity of G 2 \displaystyle G_{2} as κ ⁡ ( G 2 ) = ∏ i = 1 m − 1 ( p i − 1 ) = ϕ ⁡ ( n ) p m − 1 \displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1)=\tfrac{\phi(n)}{p_{m}-1} . Consequently, earlier upper bound is sharp, G 2 \displaystyle G_{2} is maximally connected, and its edge connectivity agrees with its minimum degree. We also obtain distance formulas, diameter and radius information, and a linear-time algorithm once the prime factorization is known.

Introduction

Graphs built from algebraic objects frequently expose structural information that is hard to read directly from the original algebra. Among the best known examples are zero-divisor graphs, unit graphs, annihilating-ideal graphs, and comaximal graphs. In the present setting, the relevant object is the comaximal graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(R)
of a commutative ring
\displaystyle R
with identity, introduced by Sharma and Bhatwadekar
[
18
]
. Its vertices are the elements of
\displaystyle R
, and two distinct elements are adjacent exactly when they generate the whole ring as an ideal. Even this basic definition already mixes algebra and graph theory in a natural way, ideal generation becomes adjacency, maximal ideals manifest themselves as obstruction sets, and ring decomposition often turns into graph decomposition. The finite residue ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is especially attractive because it is simultaneously elementary, canonical, and rich enough to display nontrivial spectral and connectivity phenomena.

The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
has been investigated from several viewpoints. Structural aspects of comaximal graphs for general commutative rings were studied by Maimani, Salimi, Sattari, and Yassemi
[
8
]
, by Moconja and Petrović
[
10
]
, and by Samei
[
17
]
. These works clarified how algebraic decompositions of the ring govern graph-theoretic properties such as connectedness, domination, and local neighborhoods. On the graph-theoretic side, the study of connectivity and Laplacian invariants belongs to the broader program of understanding how robust a network is under vertex failures, for that background we refer to West
[
19
]
. The spectral language initiated by Fiedler
[
7
]
and surveyed by Merris
[
9
]
, Mohar
[
11
]
, Brouwer and Haemers
[
3
]
, and de Abreu
[
5
]
is especially relevant because algebraic connectivity and vertex connectivity often interact in subtle ways. Other algebraic invariants are given in
[
13
,
15
,
16
,
12
,
14
]
.

A decisive step for
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
was made in
[
2
,
1
]
, they analyzed the Laplacian spectrum and several structural features of the graph. The induced subgraph on the nonzero nonunit elements,

G_{2}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})\big[\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}:\ x\neq 0,\ \gcd(x,n)\neq 1\}\big],

appears as the genuinely nontrivial core of the full comaximal graph. Indeed, units are universal vertices, while the zero element has a highly controlled neighborhood. An equitable partition of
\displaystyle G_{2}
can be obtained based on proper divisor
\displaystyle d_{i}
. Its structure is described through divisor classes (see remark
2.4
), several spectral statements are proved, and an upper bound if established for the vertex connectivity of
\displaystyle G_{2}
when
\displaystyle n
is squarefree
[
2
,
1
]
. The paper
[
2
]
then ended with a natural open problem: determine the exact value of
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
for

n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m},\qquad 2\leq p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{m},

where the
\displaystyle p_{i}
are prime. This is Problem 7.2 in
[
2
]
.

The importance of this question is both technical and conceptual. Technically, vertex connectivity measures the smallest number of vertices whose deletion disconnects the graph, and hence it is the most basic fault-tolerance parameter. In the arithmetic setting of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
, a minimum vertex cut identifies the thinnest layer through which the nontrivial comaximal interactions must pass. Conceptually, an exact formula for
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
reveals which prime factors control the fragile directions of the graph. At first sight one might expect the answer to depend on the whole factorization of
\displaystyle n
in a complicated way, because the adjacency relation on
\displaystyle G_{2}
is defined globally through comaximality. One of the main messages of the present paper is that, after the right change of viewpoint, the answer becomes remarkably clean.

Our method is based on the Chinese remainder theorem. When
\displaystyle n
is squarefree, the ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is isomorphic to the direct product
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{F}_{p_{m}}
, and every residue class can be written as a coordinate vector. For vertices of
\displaystyle G_{2}
, at least one coordinate is zero and at least one coordinate is nonzero. We therefore encode a vertex by its zero-set, namely the subset of coordinates on which the vector vanishes. This transforms the ring-theoretic adjacency relation into an elementary set-theoretic one: two vertices are adjacent if and only if their zero-sets are disjoint. In other words,
\displaystyle G_{2}
becomes a weighted blow-up of a disjointness graph on the nonempty proper subsets of
\displaystyle[m]=\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Once this model is available, the exact connectivity problem can be attacked by purely combinatorial means.

The first outcome of this approach is a transparent decomposition of the vertex set into layers
\displaystyle X_{S}
, indexed by the nonempty proper subsets
\displaystyle S\subsetneq[m]
. Each layer
\displaystyle X_{S}
consists of all vertices whose zero-set is
\displaystyle S
, and its cardinality is given explicitly by

\displaystyle|X_{S}|=\prod_{i\notin S}(p_{i}-1).

The second outcome is an exact degree formula. For a vertex in
\displaystyle X_{S}
, the degree depends only on
\displaystyle S
, and the minimum degree occurs precisely on the layer whose zero-set omits the largest prime index. The third and decisive step is to show that the layer
\displaystyle X_{{m}}
is a separator of size
\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1)
and that removing fewer vertices can never disconnect the graph. This proves the exact formula

\kappa(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1)=\frac{\phi(n)}{p_{m}-1}.

Thus the upper bound from
[
2
]
is sharp, and the graph
\displaystyle G_{2}
turns out to be maximally connected in the classical sense that
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})=\delta(G_{2})
.

Beyond the open problem itself, the support-set model yields several additional consequences. We obtain a complete distance formula between two vertices in terms of their zero-sets, and this leads to a short proof that the diameter is
\displaystyle 3
whenever
\displaystyle m\geq 3
. We also identify the central layers of the graph and provide a simple algorithm for computing
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
once the distinct prime factors of
\displaystyle n
are known. The algorithmic viewpoint is useful, as it separates two stages of the computation, prime factorization, which is external to the graph problem, and the graph-theoretic evaluation of the connectivity, which is linear in the number of prime factors. In this way the paper not only answers the open problem but also clarifies the mechanism behind the answer.

From a literature perspective, the main novelty of the present work lies in replacing the divisor-based description of
[
2
,
1
]
by a support-set geometry tailored to the squarefree case. That reformulation seems to be absent from the existing comaximal-graph literature. It reveals that
\displaystyle G_{2}
behaves like a nonuniform Kneser-type disjointness graph, with weights inherited from the residue sizes of the coordinate fields. This change of model simplifies the proofs, isolates the extremal cut, and naturally suggests further questions for nonsquarefree moduli, edge connectivity, and the classification of all minimum separators.

[
2
]
). Section

\displaystyle G_{2}
is a weighted blow-up of a disjointness graph. Section
4
derives the neighborhood and degree formulas, identifies the unique minimum-degree layer, and prepares the connectivity argument. Section
5
contains the exact solution to Problem 7.2 o f
[
2
]
, proves that the bound is sharp, and deduces maximal connectivity. Section
6
studies metric consequences of the model, including an exact distance formula, the diameter, and short routing statements. Section
7
gives an explicit algorithm, establishes its correctness and complexity, and collects numerical comparisons and computation tables. Finally, Section
8
summarizes the contribution, discusses limitations, and records directions for further research.

2
Preliminaries and notation

Throughout the paper, all graphs are finite, simple, and undirected. For a graph
\displaystyle G
, the vertex set is denoted by
\displaystyle V(G)
, the neighborhood of a vertex
\displaystyle v
by
\displaystyle N_{G}(v)
, the degree of
\displaystyle v
by
\displaystyle\deg_{G}(v)
, the minimum degree by
\displaystyle\delta(G)
, the vertex connectivity by
\displaystyle\kappa(G)
, and the edge connectivity by
\displaystyle\lambda(G)
.
Two vertices
\displaystyle u\neq v
are called
false twins
, if they have exactly the same open neighborhood and are
not
adjacent, that is,
\displaystyle N(u)=N(v)
and
\displaystyle uv\notin E(G).
We fix a squarefree modulus

\displaystyle n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m},
with
\displaystyle 2\leq p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{m},

where
\displaystyle m\geq 2
and the
\displaystyle p_{i}
are distinct primes. We write
\displaystyle[m]=\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Let
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
be the comaximal graph of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, and let
\displaystyle G_{2}
be the induced subgraph on the nonzero nonunit elements.
For a subset
\displaystyle S\subseteq[m]
, we write
\displaystyle d_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}p_{i},
with the convention that the empty product equals
\displaystyle 1
. For a vertex
\displaystyle x\in G_{2}
, its zero-set will later be denoted by
\displaystyle Z(x)\subseteq[m]
.
For a squarefree modulus
\displaystyle n
, the support classes in
\displaystyle G_{2}
will be indexed by nonempty proper subsets of
\displaystyle[m]
.

The following known facts form the background of our arguments. The next theorem is the arithmetic form of the Chinese remainder theorem used to encode vertices by coordinate zero patterns.

Theorem 2.1

(Chinese remainder theorem, Chap. 7,
[
6
]
)
.

There is a ring isomorphism

\mathbb{Z}_{n}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p_{1}}\times\mathbb{F}_{p_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{F}_{p_{m}}.

Under this identification, each element of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
may be viewed as an
\displaystyle m
-tuple
\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
with
\displaystyle x_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}}
.

The following criterion translates comaximality in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
into a gcd condition, (Banerjee, eq. (2)
[
2
]
, see also
[
1
]
). For
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the vertices
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent in
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
if and only if

\gcd\big(\gcd(x,n),\gcd(y,n)\big)=1\qquad\text{or}\qquad\gcd(x,y,n)=1.

(1)

The next inequality is the standard connectivity chain that will allow us to identify edge connectivity once vertex connectivity is known.

Proposition 2.2

(Whitney inequalities, Theorem 4.2.10,
[
19
]
)
.

For every connected graph
\displaystyle G
,

\kappa(G)\leq\lambda(G)\leq\delta(G).

The following statement records the best available bound from the earlier literature and is the starting point of the present paper.

Proposition 2.3

(Banerjee, Theorem 6.3,
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
be squarefree with distinct primes in increasing order. Then

\kappa(G_{2})\leq\phi(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m-1})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

The next observation connects the ring-theoretic notation from
[
2
,
1
]
with the graph studied here.

Remark 2.4
.

In
[
2
,
1
]
, a graph denoted by
\displaystyle G_{2}
, is described through the divisor classes

A_{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}:\ \gcd(x,n)=d\},

where
\displaystyle d
runs over the proper divisors of
\displaystyle n
. The present paper keeps the notation
\displaystyle G_{2}
for the same induced subgraph but reindexes the classes by subsets of
\displaystyle[m]
, which is more efficient in the squarefree case.

The existing literature gives a strong structural and spectral description of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
and, in particular, an upper bound for
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
in the squarefree case
[
2
]
. What was missing is an exact formula for
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
, a proof of sharpness of the bound
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})\leq\phi(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m-1})
, and a combinatorial explanation of why the extremal separator is tied to the largest prime factor. Likewise, no explicit distance formula for
\displaystyle G_{2}
seems to have been isolated in the squarefree setting. The remainder of the paper fills these gaps.

3
A weighted disjointness model for
\displaystyle G_{2}

The results of this section recast
\displaystyle G_{2}
as a weighted disjointness graph. This viewpoint is not explicit in
[
2
]
and is the combinatorial engine behind all later connectivity and distance proofs.

Using Theorem
2.1
, we identify
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{F}_{p_{m}}
.
For
\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, define the zero-set

Z(x)=\{i\in[m]:x_{i}=0\}.

For every nonempty proper subset
\displaystyle S\subsetneq[m]
, define

X_{S}=\{x\in V(G_{2}):Z(x)=S\}.

The next proposition identifies exactly which zero-sets occur in the graph
\displaystyle G_{2}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

A vector
\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
belongs to
\displaystyle V(G_{2})
if and only if
\displaystyle Z(x)
is a nonempty proper subset of
\displaystyle[m]
.

Proof.

A vertex of
\displaystyle G_{2}
is by definition nonzero and nonunit. In the product ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{F}_{p_{m}}
, an element is a unit exactly when all coordinates are nonzero, and it is zero exactly when all coordinates are zero. Therefore,
\displaystyle x
is nonzero and nonunit if and only if at least one coordinate vanishes and at least one coordinate is nonzero, which is equivalent to saying that
\displaystyle Z(x)
is nonempty and proper.
∎

The next proposition gives the exact size of each support class.

Proposition 3.2
.

For every nonempty proper subset
\displaystyle S\subsetneq[m]
,

|X_{S}|=\prod_{i\notin S}(p_{i}-1).

Proof.

A vector lies in
\displaystyle X_{S}
exactly when its coordinates indexed by
\displaystyle S
are zero and its coordinates indexed by
\displaystyle[m]\setminus S
are nonzero. The coordinates in
\displaystyle S
are forced, while for every
\displaystyle i\notin S
there are exactly
\displaystyle p_{i}-1
nonzero choices in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}}
. Multiplying the independent choices, we obtain

|X_{S}|=\prod_{i\notin S}(p_{i}-1).

∎

The next theorem converts ring-theoretic adjacency into set-theoretic disjointness.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
and
\displaystyle y\in X_{T}
, where
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle T
are nonempty proper subsets of
\displaystyle[m]
. Then
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent in
\displaystyle G_{2}
if and only if
\displaystyle S\cap T=\emptyset
.

Proof.

By Equation
1
,
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent in
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
if and only if

\gcd\big(\gcd(x,n),\gcd(y,n)\big)=1.

Since
\displaystyle n
is squarefree and
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
, we have
\displaystyle\gcd(x,n)=d_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}p_{i}
. Likewise,
\displaystyle\gcd(y,n)=d_{T}=\prod_{i\in T}p_{i}
. Thus, we have

\gcd\big(\gcd(x,n),\gcd(y,n)\big)=\gcd(d_{S},d_{T})=\prod_{i\in S\cap T}p_{i}.

This equals
\displaystyle 1
precisely when
\displaystyle S\cap T=\emptyset
. Since
\displaystyle G_{2}
is an induced subgraph of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
, and the same criterion describes adjacency inside
\displaystyle G_{2}
.
∎

The following corollary identifies the global structure of
\displaystyle G_{2}
as a weighted blow-up.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{m}
be the graph whose vertex set is

\mathscr{P}^{\ast}([m])=\{S\subseteq[m]:\ \emptyset\neq S\subsetneq[m]\},

where two subsets are adjacent exactly when they are disjoint. Then
\displaystyle G_{2}
is the blow-up of
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{m}
obtained by replacing each vertex
\displaystyle S
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{m}
with the independent set
\displaystyle X_{S}
of size
\displaystyle\prod_{i\notin S}(p_{i}-1)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
, the vertex set of
\displaystyle G_{2}
is the disjoint union of the classes
\displaystyle X_{S}
over all nonempty proper
\displaystyle S\subsetneq[m]
. By Theorem
3.3
, there are no edges inside a class
\displaystyle X_{S}
, and between two distinct classes
\displaystyle X_{S}
and
\displaystyle X_{T}
either all possible edges appear or none appear, according as
\displaystyle S\cap T=\emptyset
or not. This is exactly the definition of the stated blow-up of
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{m}
.
∎

The next corollary records the order of the graph and serves as a consistency check for the partition.

Corollary 3.5
.

The graph
\displaystyle G_{2}
has
\displaystyle|V(G_{2})|=n-1-\phi(n)
vertices.

Proof.

A vertex of
\displaystyle G_{2}
is precisely a residue class modulo
\displaystyle n
that is neither zero nor a unit. There are
\displaystyle n
residue classes in total, exactly one of them is zero, and exactly
\displaystyle\phi(n)
of them are units. Hence, we obtain

|V(G_{2})|=n-1-\phi(n).

∎

\displaystyle A_{d}
is already sufficient for spectral calculations

4
Degree profile and local structure

This section derives explicit local formulas that are missing in the existing literature. In particular, we identify the unique minimum-degree layer and isolate the exact neighborhood that later becomes a minimum vertex cut.

For
\displaystyle S\subseteq[m]
, we write
\displaystyle S^{c}=[m]\setminus S
. The next proposition identifies the entire neighborhood of a vertex from its zero-set alone.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
, then

N(x)=\bigsqcup_{\emptyset\neq T\subseteq S^{c}}X_{T}.

In particular, the neighborhood depends only on
\displaystyle S
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, a vertex
\displaystyle y\in X_{T}
is adjacent to
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
if and only if
\displaystyle T\cap S=\emptyset
. Since every
\displaystyle T
indexing a vertex of
\displaystyle G_{2}
is nonempty and proper, the condition
\displaystyle T\cap S=\emptyset
is equivalent to
\displaystyle\emptyset\neq T\subseteq S^{c}
. The union is disjoint, since the classes
\displaystyle X_{T}
are disjoint.
∎

The next theorem gives an exact degree formula in terms of the support set.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
, then

\deg(x)=\sum_{\emptyset\neq T\subseteq S^{c}}\prod_{i\notin T}(p_{i}-1)

and, equivalently,

\deg(x)=\prod_{i\in S}(p_{i}-1)\left(\prod_{j\in S^{c}}p_{j}-\prod_{j\in S^{c}}(p_{j}-1)\right).

Proof.

The first identity follows immediately from Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
3.2
, since the degree is the sum of the sizes of all neighboring classes. For the second identity, we count the neighbors directly in the coordinate model. Since
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
, a vector
\displaystyle y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
is adjacent to
\displaystyle x
if and only if the following two conditions hold: (i) for every
\displaystyle i\in S
, the coordinate
\displaystyle y_{i}
is nonzero, and (ii) on the complementary set
\displaystyle S^{c}
, the vector
\displaystyle(y_{j})_{j\in S^{c}}
is not entirely nonzero, as
\displaystyle y
must remain a nonunit. There are
\displaystyle\prod_{i\in S}(p_{i}-1)
ways to choose the coordinates indexed by
\displaystyle S
. On the coordinates indexed by
\displaystyle S^{c}
, there are
\displaystyle\prod_{j\in S^{c}}p_{j}
arbitrary choices and
\displaystyle\prod_{j\in S^{c}}(p_{j}-1)
forbidden choices in which all coordinates are nonzero. Multiplying these independent choices, we obtain

\deg(x)=\prod_{i\in S}(p_{i}-1)\left(\prod_{j\in S^{c}}p_{j}-\prod_{j\in S^{c}}(p_{j}-1)\right).

∎

The following corollary shows that each layer consists of pairwise nonadjacent vertices with identical open neighborhoods.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
\displaystyle x,y\in X_{S}
, then
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are nonadjacent and
\displaystyle N(x)=N(y)
. Thus, each class
\displaystyle X_{S}
is a false-twin class.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle S\cap S=S\neq\emptyset
, Theorem
3.3
shows that
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are not adjacent. The equality of neighborhoods is exactly the second statement in Proposition
4.1
.
∎

The next theorem locates the minimum degree exactly.

Theorem 4.4
.

The minimum degree of
\displaystyle G_{2}
is

\delta(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1),

and this value is attained precisely by the vertices in the class

X_{[m]\setminus\{m\}}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
, and and let
\displaystyle J=S^{c}
. Then by Proposition
4.1
, we have

N(x)=\bigsqcup_{\emptyset\neq T\subseteq J}X_{T}.

Hence,
\displaystyle N(x)
contains each singleton class
\displaystyle X_{\{j\}}
with
\displaystyle j\in J
. Therefore, we obtain

\deg(x)\geq\sum_{j\in J}|X_{{j}}|.

Now,
\displaystyle|X_{\{j\}}|=\prod_{i\neq j}(p_{i}-1).
As
\displaystyle p_{1}<\cdots<p_{m}
, the smallest among these singleton-class sizes is

|X_{\{m\}}|=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

If
\displaystyle|J|\geq 2
, then
\displaystyle\deg(x)
contains at least two disjoint singleton layers in its neighborhood, so we have

\deg(x)\geq|X_{\{j_{1}\}}|+|X_{\{j_{2}\}}|>|X_{\{m\}}|

for any distinct
\displaystyle j_{1},j_{2}\in J

\displaystyle|J|=1

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle\{j\}

\displaystyle N(x)=X_{\{j\}},
and therefore, we have

\deg(x)=|X_{\{j\}}|=\prod_{i\neq j}(p_{i}-1).

This is minimized exactly when
\displaystyle j=m
, and hence

\delta(G_{2})=|X_{\{m\}}|=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1),

with equality holds precisely on the class
\displaystyle X_{[m]\setminus\{m\}}
.
∎

Corollary 4.5

For every vertex
\displaystyle x\in X_{[m]\setminus\{m\}}

\displaystyle N(x)=X_{\{m\}},

Proof.

\displaystyle S=[m]\setminus\{m\}
of Proposition

\displaystyle S^{c}=\{m\}
and the only nonempty subset of
\displaystyle S^{c}
is
\displaystyle\{m\}
.
∎

5
Exact solution of Problem 7.2

This section contains the main contribution of the paper, the exact value of
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
. Theorem
5.5
sharpens Proposition
2.3
from an upper bound to an equality and proves, in addition, that
\displaystyle G_{2}
is maximally connected.

The next lemma recovers Banerjee’s separator in the support-set language and shows that it really disconnects the graph.

Lemma 5.1
.

The class
\displaystyle X_{\{m\}}
is a vertex cut of
\displaystyle G_{2}
, and consequently,

\kappa(G_{2})\leq|X_{\{m\}}|=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

Proof.

For any vertex
\displaystyle x\in X_{[m]\setminus\{m\}}
, Corollary
4.5
, gives

\displaystyle N(x)=X_{\{m\}}.

Therefore, after deleting the entire class
\displaystyle X_{\{m\}}
, every vertex of
\displaystyle X_{[m]\setminus\{m\}}
becomes isolated. Hence,
\displaystyle G_{2}-X_{\{m\}}
is disconnected, so
\displaystyle X_{\{m\}}
is a vertex cut. The cardinality formula follows from Proposition
3.2
.
∎

The next lemma shows that any smaller deletion leaves at least one vertex in the critical class
\displaystyle X_{\{m\}}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle W\subseteq V(G_{2})
with

|W|<\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

Then there exists a vertex

\displaystyle u\in X_{\{m\}}\setminus W.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
, we have

|X_{\{m\}}|=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

Since
\displaystyle|W|<|X_{\{m\}}|
, the set
\displaystyle W
cannot contain all vertices of
\displaystyle X_{\{m\}}
. Hence some
\displaystyle u\in X_{\{m\}}\setminus W
survives.
∎

The next lemma shows that every surviving vertex whose zero-set avoids
\displaystyle m
is directly linked to the surviving anchor
\displaystyle u
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\displaystyle u\in X_{\{m\}}
, and let
\displaystyle v\in X_{S}
be any vertex with
\displaystyle m\notin S
. Then
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
are adjacent.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle m\notin S
, we have
\displaystyle S\cap\{m\}=\emptyset
. Theorem
3.3
now implies that
\displaystyle v
is adjacent to every vertex of
\displaystyle X_{\{m\}}
, in particular to
\displaystyle u
.
∎

The next lemma is the key robustness step, every surviving vertex whose zero-set contains
\displaystyle m
still reaches the anchor through one surviving neighbor.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\displaystyle W\subseteq V(G_{2})
with
\displaystyle|W|<\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1),
and choose
\displaystyle u\in X_{\{m\}}\setminus W
as in Lemma
5.2
. If
\displaystyle v\in V(G_{2})\setminus W
and
\displaystyle m\in Z(v)
, then
\displaystyle v
lies in the same connected component as
\displaystyle u
in
\displaystyle G_{2}-W
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle v
survives, it belongs to some class
\displaystyle X_{S}
with
\displaystyle m\in S
. So, by Theorem
4.4
, we have

\deg(v)\geq\delta(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

As
\displaystyle|W|
is strictly smaller than this number, the deleted set
\displaystyle W
cannot contain all neighbors of
\displaystyle v
. Hence, there exists a neighbor
\displaystyle w\in N(v)\setminus W.
Since
\displaystyle v
and
\displaystyle w
are adjacent, Theorem
3.3
gives

\displaystyle Z(v)\cap Z(w)=\emptyset.
As
\displaystyle m\in Z(v)
, it follows that
\displaystyle m\notin Z(w)
. Therefore, Lemma
5.3
implies that
\displaystyle w
is adjacent to
\displaystyle u
. Hence,
\displaystyle v-w-u
is a path in
\displaystyle G_{2}-W
, and
\displaystyle v
lies in the same component as
\displaystyle u
.
∎

The next theorem solves Problem 7.2 of
[
2
]
completely.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\displaystyle n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
with distinct primes in increasing order. Then

\kappa(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1)=\frac{\phi(n)}{p_{m}-1}.

Proof.

Lemma
5.1
gives the upper bound

\kappa(G_{2})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

For the reverse inequality, let
\displaystyle W\subseteq V(G_{2})
satisfy

|W|<\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

By Lemma
5.2
, there exists a surviving vertex
\displaystyle u\in X_{{m}}\setminus W
. Now take any surviving vertex
\displaystyle v\in V(G_{2})\setminus W
. If
\displaystyle m\notin Z(v)
, then by Lemma
5.3
,
\displaystyle v
is adjacent to
\displaystyle u
. If
\displaystyle m\in Z(v)
, then by Lemma
5.4
,
\displaystyle v
is connected to
\displaystyle u
in
\displaystyle G_{2}-W
. Thus, every surviving vertex lies in the same connected component as
\displaystyle u
, so
\displaystyle G_{2}-W
is connected. Therefore, no deletion of fewer than
\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1)
vertices disconnects
\displaystyle G_{2}
. Combining the upper and lower bounds gives

\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

Since
\displaystyle\phi(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(p_{i}-1)
, the equivalent form
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})=\frac{\phi(n)}{p_{m}-1}
follows immediately.
∎

The next corollary shows that the graph is maximally connected.

Corollary 5.6
.

The graph
\displaystyle G_{2}
satisfies

\kappa(G_{2})=\delta(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

Proof.

Theorem
5.5
gives the value of
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
, and Theorem
4.4
gives the same value for
\displaystyle\delta(G_{2})
.
∎

The next corollary identifies the edge connectivity as well.

Corollary 5.7
.

The edge connectivity of
\displaystyle G_{2}

\lambda(G_{2})=\kappa(G_{2})=\delta(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1).

By Proposition

\kappa(G_{2})\leq\lambda(G_{2})\leq\delta(G_{2}).

Corollary

6
Metric consequences and short routing

The support-set model also yields precise metric information. To the best of our knowledge, the exact distance formula below has not been stated for
\displaystyle G_{2}
in the squarefree case. From now on, unless explicitly stated otherwise, we assume
\displaystyle m\geq 3
whenever metric statements involving diameter
\displaystyle 3
or the center of the graph are discussed.

The next theorem determines the distance between two vertices entirely from their zero-sets.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
and
\displaystyle y\in X_{T}
, where
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle T
are nonempty proper subsets of
\displaystyle[m]
. Then

d_{G_{2}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }S\cap T=\emptyset,\\[5.69054pt]
2,&\text{if }S\cap T\neq\emptyset\text{ and }S\cup T\neq[m],\\[5.69054pt]
3,&\text{if }S\cap T\neq\emptyset\text{ and }S\cup T=[m].\end{cases}

Proof.

If
\displaystyle S\cap T=\emptyset
, then
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent by Theorem
3.3
, so the distance is
\displaystyle 1
.
Assume now that
\displaystyle S\cap T\neq\emptyset
. Then
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are not adjacent. If
\displaystyle S\cup T\neq[m]
, choose
\displaystyle k\in[m]\setminus(S\cup T)
. Since
\displaystyle m\geq 3
is not needed in this case, by Proposition
3.2
, the singleton class
\displaystyle X_{{k}}
is nonempty. Any vertex
\displaystyle z\in X_{{k}}
satisfies
\displaystyle{k}\cap S=\emptyset
and
\displaystyle{k}\cap T=\emptyset
, so by Theorem
3.3
,
\displaystyle x\sim z
and
\displaystyle z\sim y
. Hence
\displaystyle d(x,y)\leq 2
. As
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are not adjacent, so
\displaystyle d(x,y)=2
.

Finally, assume that
\displaystyle S\cap T\neq\emptyset
and
\displaystyle S\cup T=[m]
. We first show that
\displaystyle d(x,y)\neq 2
. If there were a common neighbor
\displaystyle z\in X_{U}
, then
\displaystyle U
would have to be disjoint from both
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle T
. Hence

\displaystyle U\subseteq[m]\setminus(S\cup T)=\emptyset,
contradicting the fact that
\displaystyle U
is nonempty. Thus
\displaystyle d(x,y)\geq 3
.

To show that
\displaystyle d(x,y)\leq 3
, note that
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle T
are proper subsets whose union is all of
\displaystyle[m]
. Therefore, both differences
\displaystyle T\setminus S
and
\displaystyle S\setminus T
are nonempty. Choose
\displaystyle a\in T\setminus S
and
\displaystyle b\in S\setminus T
. Take any
\displaystyle u\in X_{{a}}
and
\displaystyle v\in X_{{b}}
. Then
\displaystyle{a}\cap S=\emptyset
, so
\displaystyle x\sim u
. Also
\displaystyle{a}\cap{b}=\emptyset
, so
\displaystyle u\sim v
, and finally
\displaystyle{b}\cap T=\emptyset
, so
\displaystyle v\sim y
. Thus

\displaystyle x-u-v-y
is a path of length
\displaystyle 3
, and therefore
\displaystyle d(x,y)=3
.
∎

The next corollary gives the diameter of the graph.

Corollary 6.2
.

If
\displaystyle m=2
, then
\displaystyle G_{2}
is complete bipartite and
\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(G_{2})=2
. If
\displaystyle m\geq 3
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(G_{2})=3.

Proof.

When
\displaystyle m=2
, the only nonempty proper subsets of
\displaystyle[2]
are
\displaystyle{1}
and
\displaystyle{2}
, so
\displaystyle G_{2}
is the complete bipartite graph with bipartition
\displaystyle X_{{1}}\cup X_{{2}}
, and its diameter is
\displaystyle 2
.

For
\displaystyle m\geq 3
, Theorem
6.1
shows that every pair of vertices is at distance at most
\displaystyle 3
, so
\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(G_{2})\leq 3
. To see that the value
\displaystyle 3
occurs, choose

\displaystyle S=[m]\setminus\{1\},
and
\displaystyle T=[m]\setminus\{m\}.
Then
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle T
are nonempty proper subsets,
\displaystyle S\cap T\neq\emptyset
, and
\displaystyle S\cup T=[m]
. By Theorem
6.1
, any
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
and
\displaystyle y\in X_{T}
satisfy
\displaystyle d(x,y)=3
. Hence,
\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(G_{2})=3
.
∎

The next theorem identifies the center of the graph for
\displaystyle m\geq 3
.

Theorem 6.3
.

For
\displaystyle m\geq 3
, the radius of
\displaystyle G_{2}
is
\displaystyle 2
, and its center is exactly

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}X_{{i}}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x\in X_{{i}}
for some
\displaystyle i\in[m]
. For any vertex
\displaystyle y\in X_{T}
, if
\displaystyle i\notin T
, then
\displaystyle{i}\cap T=\emptyset
, so
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent by Theorem
3.3
. If
\displaystyle i\in T
, then
\displaystyle{i}\cap T\neq\emptyset
. As
\displaystyle T
is proper and
\displaystyle m\geq 3
, we cannot have
\displaystyle{i}\cup T=[m]
, indeed, that would force
\displaystyle T=[m]
. Therefore the second case of Theorem
6.1
applies, and
\displaystyle d(x,y)=2
. Hence every vertex in a singleton class has eccentricity at most
\displaystyle 2
.

Such a vertex is not adjacent to another vertex in the same class by Corollary
4.3
, so its eccentricity is exactly
\displaystyle 2
. Thus every vertex in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is central and the radius is at most
\displaystyle 2
. Since the graph is not complete, the radius cannot be
\displaystyle 1
, so the radius is exactly
\displaystyle 2
.

Now let
\displaystyle x\in X_{S}
with
\displaystyle|S|\geq 2
. Choose
\displaystyle a\in S
and define

T=[m]\setminus\{a\}.

Because
\displaystyle|S|\geq 2
, the set
\displaystyle S\cap T=S\setminus\{a\}
is nonempty, and clearly
\displaystyle S\cup T=[m]
. By Theorem
6.1
, every
\displaystyle y\in X_{T}
satisfies
\displaystyle d(x,y)=3
. Hence the eccentricity of
\displaystyle x
is at least
\displaystyle 3
, so
\displaystyle x
is not central. Therefore the center is exactly
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
.
∎

The next corollary gives a uniform routing bound after arbitrary deletions below the connectivity threshold.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\displaystyle W\subseteq V(G_{2})
satisfy

\displaystyle G_{2}-W

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle X_{{m}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(G_{2}-W)\leq 4.

Connectivity follows from Theorem
5.5
. By Lemma

\displaystyle u\in X_{{m}}\setminus W

5.3
and

\displaystyle 2
from
\displaystyle u
in
\displaystyle G_{2}-W
. Therefore, any two surviving vertices are at distance at most
\displaystyle 4
from one another.
∎

∎

The next corollary describes how the connectivity changes when a new largest prime factor is appended.

Corollary 7.5
.

Let
\displaystyle n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
with
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, and let
\displaystyle q>p_{m}
be a prime. Then

\kappa\big(G_{2}(nq)\big)=(p_{m}-1)\kappa\big(G_{2}(n)\big).

Proof.

Applying Theorem
5.5
to
\displaystyle nq
gives the required identity

\kappa\big(G_{2}(nq)\big)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(p_{i}-1)=(p_{m}-1)\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1)=(p_{m}-1)\kappa\big(G_{2}(n)\big).

∎

The earlier literature provides structural and spectral decompositions of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
, but not a direct computation rule for
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
. Theorem
7.3
shows that once the squarefree factorization is available, the exact connectivity can be read off with linear complexity and no graph construction at all.

8
Conclusion and future work

We have given a complete solution to Problem 7.2 of Banerjee
[
2
]
. For the squarefree modulus
\displaystyle n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m},
with
\displaystyle 2\leq p_{1}<\cdots<p_{m},
the induced subgraph
\displaystyle G_{2}
of the comaximal graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
on the nonzero nonunits satisfies

\kappa(G_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(p_{i}-1)=\frac{\phi(n)}{p_{m}-1}.

The proof is based on a support-set model obtained from the Chinese remainder theorem. In this model, vertices are indexed by nonempty proper subsets of
\displaystyle[m]
, adjacency becomes set disjointness, and
\displaystyle G_{2}
is a weighted blow-up of a disjointness graph. This viewpoint also yields explicit class sizes, degree formulas, the unique minimum-degree layer, a transparent minimum separator, maximal connectivity, the exact edge connectivity, and a metric description including the diameter and center.

The main limitation of the paper is that the argument is tailored to the squarefree case. Once repeated prime powers are allowed, coordinates no longer reduce to simple zero versus nonzero choices, and the disjointness model must be replaced by a more delicate multilevel description. That nonsquarefree situation seems genuinely harder and cannot be treated by a superficial modification of the present proof details of results. A second limitation is that we determined the exact value of the connectivity but did not classify all minimum vertex cuts. SO, we leave the following two problems

Problem 8.1
.

Classify all minimum vertex cuts of
\displaystyle G_{2}
when
\displaystyle n=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
is squarefree. Is every minimum separator forced, up to graph automorphism, to arise from the class
\displaystyle X_{{m}}
?

Problem 8.2
.

Extend Theorem
5.5
to general moduli
\displaystyle n
with repeated prime powers. In particular, determine
\displaystyle\kappa(G_{2})
for
\displaystyle n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
with at least one
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}>1
.
