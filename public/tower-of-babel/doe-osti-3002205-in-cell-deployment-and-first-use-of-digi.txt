 ABSTRACT

Digital image correlation (DIC) is a noncontact, optical method increasingly used across industries and
research environments for acquiring multidimensional strain data. At Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s
(ORNL’s) Severe Accident Test Station (SATS), DIC has been extensively applied to study the
thermomechanical response of nuclear fuel claddings, yielding fundamental insights into material
behavior. However, these efforts have focused exclusively on unirradiated materials and relied on the
SATS out-of-cell infrastructure. Efforts over the past two years have been made to extend these
capabilities to ORNL’s Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory SATS system within the hot-cells to
enable testing of irradiated fuel cladding materials. Integrating DIC into the hot-cell SATS infrastructure
presents unique challenges, including enabling remote operation of optical equipment and adapting
auxiliary hot-cell systems for DIC implementation. This report discusses design, stand-up testing, and
application of DIC to an irradiated nuclear fuel cladding segment. A DIC testing rig was successfully
built and validated out-of-cell through extensive surrogate tests and calibrations. The rig and specialized
DIC furnace were installed in the hot-cell, and a DIC test was successfully conducted. Results from that
test correspond to expectations for Zr-based alloys and showed similar uncertainties compared to out-of-
cell tests.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The Severe Accident Test Station (SATS) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory has been used over the past
decade to examine various cladding materials for their responses to accident transients, most commonly
assessing material response during loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA) events [1-7]. The SATS uses a four-
lamp infrared furnace to heat a tube sample, typically at 5°C/s [8] with an internal tube overpressure. The
standard data coming from these tests are burst temperature and pressure together with caliper-based
measurements of the tube diameter, burst width, and burst length. Although these data are invaluable for
providing a basis for comparison to historic, seminal datasets, they lack the resolution necessary to
capture the deformation process from the initial state to burst, which is an essential aspect of accurate
modeling and validation efforts. A custom infrared heating furnace with a viewport to the sample was
designed and demonstrated as a method to perform visual inspection of the cladding during LOCA
testing. Digital image correlation (DIC) was chosen as a primary tool for nondestructive evaluation during
testing. The methodology for in-situ DIC strain mapping was developed out-of-cell over the past few
years and has resulted in both 2D and 3D strain mapping capabilities for transient LOCA testing and
isothermal creep testing of fuel cladding [5, 9, 10].

Integrating DIC into the SATS out-of-cell infrastructure has been highly successful, leading to model gap
assessments that yielded direct improvements to BISON simulations of LOCA tests [11], which are
critical for the licensing and safety of current and novel nuclear fuel and cladding [12]. To date, DIC has
only been available for SATS testing of unirradiated materials. Although effective for establishing
baseline understanding and models, these efforts omit critical variables related to irradiation and corrosion
effects encountered during cladding service.

To investigate these phenomena from a thermomechanical perspective, extending DIC capabilities to hot-
cell environments represents a natural progression of the work conducted over the past five years.
Integrating DIC into the hot-cell system enables a high-fidelity mechanistic evaluation of irradiated
claddings, including high-burnup pins or rods from lead test assemblies of accident-tolerant fuel
claddings, such as Cr-coated Zr alloys, supporting efforts critical to licensure. Implementing DIC into the
hot-cell SATS required considerable design efforts, from ensuring remote operation of optical equipment
to modifying hot-cell infrastructure. This work reports on the extension of DIC to in-cell testing on

 1

irradiated fuel and cladding, including the design, out-of-cell validation, in-cell installation, and
preliminary results of the first test on irradiated fuel.

 2. DIC TEST RIG: DESIGN AND OUT-OF-CELL TESTING

2.1 DESIGN OF THE IN-CELL TESTING RIG

DIC collection on unirradiated samples during a transient has undergone significant iteration over past
five years since its initial deployment [9, 10]. The collection system has improved in efficiency and
rigidity and reduced the time required to set up, calibrate, and run a test. However, bringing the system
into a hot-cell environment required further improvements. The in-cell rig was required to (1)
simultaneously host both 2D and 3D (stereo) DIC, (2) permit calibration with minimal or no movement of
the sample, (3) maintain a minimal footprint within the hot-cell, (4) ensure all lighting, camera, and lens
components are easily replaceable within the hot-cells, and (5) enable full exploration of all stereo angles
and working distances presently possible outside the hot-cell.

The out-of-cell DIC system, which was not so tightly constrained, did not meet these requirements. The
ability to simultaneously host both 2D and 3D DIC is valuable because of the expected difficulty of
calibration within the hot-cell and the need to ensure adequate alignment, focus, and lighting for two
separate perspectives. Although stereo-DIC captures surface strain in 3D and is inherently more accurate,
2D DIC can be performed with greater speed and without the challenges of camera and lighting
positioning that stereo-DIC involves. To accomplish these goals together, the DIC rig was designed
around a Velmex UniSlide stage. The stage was configured with adapters to host coupled lateral and
rotational positioning stages, to which mounting platforms were attached. Each platform carries studs that
guide and position a camera carriage, which can be secured with two hex nuts. Upon camera failure, the
hex nuts can be removed and the camera lifted off. Figure 1 provides images of the assembly. The
Velmex slide was secured to a lab jack, permitting height adjustment of the camera centerline as needed
within the cell using a single screw.

This frame was fitted with four Manfrotto adjustable arms to permit up to four LED lights to be mounted
on the system to illuminate the sample. LEDs are also not expected to survive for more than a few weeks
or months in the hot-cell environment. To permit maximum flexibility in the hot-cell, the base of each
LED was secured to a neodymium magnet. As procured, each Manfrotto arm was terminated by a
threaded magnetic steel piece. The LED could thus be easily attached or removed from the system as
required for in-cell replacement of failed lights.

Finally, the calibration process was simplified compared to the out-of-cell process. In the standard
process, the camera assembly would be set up and focused precisely on the sample. Then, the operators
would remove the sample, open the furnace, and place the calibration grid in the position of the sample to
run the calibration. After calibration, the operators would replace the sample and shut the furnace.
Throughout this process, the operators would adjust the lighting, but the cameras would be left in place.
For the in-cell operations, this process was inverted. Movement of the sample and delicate glass tube for
DIC was considered hazardous and time-consuming in the hot-cell environment. Thus, the lab jack was
secured to a mobile frame so that the camera system could be focused on the rod. The whole system could
then be slid away from the furnace without changing the stereo angle or focusing conditions of the stereo
cameras. This setup provided room to calibrate without moving the sample and with ideally little to no
change in lighting conditions. After calibration, the camera system could physically slide back into place,
minor adjustments to lighting or rig positioning could be made while maintaining the stereo angle and
camera focus, and the test could be conducted.

2.2 CAMERA SYSTEM AND DIC ANALYSIS

For data collection, three monochromatic FLIR Grasshopper3 5 MP cameras were used—two for stereo
collection and one for 2D collection. The stereo-DIC setup utilizes 50 mm focal-length lenses from
Edmund Optics, oriented at an approximate stereo angle of 15°. A TCLWD020-275-23 Opto Engineering
telecentric lens with a working distance of 275 mm and a depth of field of 10.4 mm collected data for the
2D DIC. This type of lens maintains a constant magnification if the specimen remains within the focal
window, which is critical for accurately capturing out-of-plane deformation. Conventional lenses can
introduce magnification bias when the specimen expands toward the camera, potentially distorting the
results. In contrast, the telecentric lens ensures that the magnification of 3D deformation onto the 2D
image sensor remains constant throughout the deformation process, effectively eliminating any error from
out-of-plane motion. Moreover, the choice of these lenses permitted the simultaneous collection of 2D
and 3D DIC under the physical constraints of the in-cell DIC rig.

Images from both stereo and 2D DIC systems were processed using VIC-3D and VIC-2D software from
Correlated Solutions Inc., respectively. Although separate programs, they utilize the same underlying DIC
algorithm. During the stereo analysis, a subset size of 47 pixels was employed. The subset size for the 2D
DIC system was increased to 71 pixels to account for differences in magnification between the two
setups. To ensure consistency, strain fields were defined over the same region for both 2D and stereo-DIC
analyses. This was ensured by comparing pixel locations near common features. These considerations
enabled the capture of identical pattern features, enhancing the fidelity of the comparative analysis.

2.3 LIMITATIONS IN OPTICAL PATHWAY STABILITY FOR HOT-CELL DEPLOYMENT

The well-established out-of-cell SATS DIC configuration benefits from various enhancements that are
challenging to implement in the hot-cell deployment. A significant source of error in SATS DIC data at
high temperatures arises from distortions in the optical pathways due to heat waves and fluid flow (steam,
argon, air, etc.). These disturbances occur primarily from (1) the stochastic fluid flow moving upward
through the quartz reaction tube, which is unavoidable in steam environments, and (2) the hot air flow
from within the furnace to the exterior via the unimpeded viewport. The mixing of furnace air with

 3

ambient laboratory air creates turbulence that disrupts the optical environment more substantially than
steam flow alone. To stop air flow and mixing, the out-of-cell SATS configuration typically employs an
optical-grade quartz window flush against the viewport. However, this window remains hotter than
ambient temperatures and can produce some heat waves from its outer surface. An air knife is usually
employed to create air flow parallel to the quartz window surface, promoting mixing and maintaining a
stable external temperature. These enhancements have significantly reduced inherent error, allowing
SATS to reliably measure thermal expansion with accuracy comparable to that of conventional
dilatometry.

These improvements are not guaranteed for hot-cell deployment and long-term use. Installing the air knife
proved impractical in the hot-cell, and although a window was introduced into the hot-cell furnace, it has
a larger gap than that of typical out-of-cell setups. Additionally, its fragility complicates replacement in
the event of breakage. Therefore, validation testing was performed without these augmentations to assess
system performance under conditions representative of the highest level of noise.

2.4 OUT-OF-CELL DEMONSTRATION AND PRELIMINARY 2D VS. STEREO-DIC
 COMPARISON

Verification burst testing was conducted out-of-cell to demonstrate design feasibility within the
operational constraints of hot-cell operation, including proximity to the furnace and limited adjustment
capabilities. Alongside the deformation data collected from testing, a specialized calibration target
optimized for the hot-cell system was developed. Although not shown, extensive validation of the
calibration target was performed, ensuring its accuracy and reliability to work within the geometric
constraints of the hot-cell and camera system. The validation process involved comparisons with
established, commercially produced targets used in the out-of-cell system and conventionally used
metrics tying the calibration to real space. This custom target was confirmed to meet the specific demands
of high-fidelity stereo-DIC and will not be a limiting factor in data quality. The discussion of results from
burst testing is based on this calibration qualification.

Transient burst testing was conducted at a heating rate of 5°C/s and an internal pressure of 8.3 MPa on
both Cr-coated and bare Zircaloy-4 specimens in stress-relief annealed conditions. Figure 2 presents the
DIC strain maps from the final images before rupture for the Cr-coated specimen. Figure 2a shows the
telecentric image, and Figure 2b shows an image from one of the stereo-DIC cameras. The lines running
along the hoop direction illustrate examples of line scans, which will be discussed subsequently to better
understand the discrepancies between 2D and stereo-DIC. Given the well-established capability of stereo-
DIC to provide accurate 3D surface reconstructions, the stereo-DIC data will serve as the baseline for
measuring the 2D data. This comparison involves assessing the level of error that may arise when stereo-
DIC is unavailable due to various complications associated with hot-cell operations, particularly when the
fail-safe 2D DIC option is the only alternative.

The average strain fields for both the 2D and stereo-DIC datasets, which were synchronized based on
features, were calculated across the entire regions of interest (ROIs; entire field in Figure 2). These
averages were then plotted as a function of temperature. The results are shown in Figure 3a for the bare
Zircaloy-4 specimen and in Figure 3b for the Cr-coated specimen. Averaging over these regions
facilitates a straightforward comparison of the general behavior captured by the 2D DIC against the
accurate stereo-DIC reference frame. Overall, the measurements of average hoop strain correlate well,
although the stereo-DIC tends to report lower axial strain values beyond the initial phase of the test. The
noise levels in both datasets are qualitatively similar. However, the hoop strain obtained from stereo-DIC
is consistently lower in magnitude.

A qualitative comparison of the maps presented in Figure 2 indicates some discrepancies between the
strain fields recovered from 2D and stereo-DIC. To better understand local deviations between the two
techniques, exploratory line scans were conducted along the hoop direction (example in Figure 2) for both
the bare Zircaloy-4 and Cr-coated specimens. These line scans were derived from the strain fields
captured just before rupture. In both tests, the specimen ruptured slightly out of view on the left side of
the cladding relative to the camera perspective. Whereas the 2D DIC profile reflects the hoop strain
projected onto a 2D sensor, the hoop strain captured from stereo-DIC reflects a line scan along the actual
cladding surface in the hoop direction. Therefore, to account for the differences in measurement
approaches, normalized profiles were used for a more accurate comparison between the two techniques,
considering that the distance along the profile may vary due to these differences.

Figure 4a and Figure 4b present the resultant normalized line scans for the uncoated and Cr-coated
Zircaloy-4 claddings, respectively. For both the bare reference and the Cr-coated specimens, it is evident
that 2D DIC generally overestimates hoop strain in higher strain regions, particularly those off the central
axis near the balloon region. Notably, this discrepancy is more pronounced for the uncoated reference
specimen, which exhibited approximately 50% greater strain measured by 2D DIC compared to the
stereo-DIC data. In contrast, the strain difference between the two techniques for the Cr-coated specimen
was about 10%. Examining the Zircaloy-4 specimen from the central region (0.5 normalized distance) to
the end of the line scan opposite the rupture (1 normalized distance) revealed considerable overlap
between the line scans. Similarly, the Cr-coated specimen displayed a high degree of congruence between
the 2D and stereo-DIC curves, particularly at the central point.

 6

 Figure 4. Line scans from regions near the balloon to the opposite side of the strain field away from the
 rupture for (a) the uncoated Zircaloy-4 and (b) the Cr-coated Zircaloy-4.

The observations from the line scans highlight the fundamental differences between 2D and stereo-DIC in
accurately capturing the ballooning process, which is necessary if the latter is unavailable in hot-cell
testing. Using raw 2D DIC data requires caution, particularly when considering the entire strain field, and
relying on analysis near the central axis of the cladding is recommended. Data collected away from the
centerline of the tube are inherently subject to bias, as depicted in Figure 4. In contrast, 2D DIC excels at
capturing the average behavior across the strain field and is highly consistent with stereo-DIC results
(Figure 3). However, because the rupture occurred out of view in both tests, expanding the analysis region
to encompass more of the balloon may exacerbate any discrepancies and require further investigation.

These demonstration tests revealed that although 2D DIC can consistently capture average strain behavior
with stereo-DIC, its susceptibility to biases makes it less reliable for accurate full characterization of
ballooning processes. Therefore, successfully implementing stereo-DIC in hot-cell testing is the priority,
and 2D DIC can serve as a supplementary option. A more detailed investigation of these biases is
warranted and will be addressed in future work.

 3. IN-CELL LOCA TEST WITH DIC

3.1 TEST PREPARATION

A fuel rod segment, named 47I-C05C1, was prepared for testing in the in-cell SATS. This segment has a
calculated burnup of approximately 33 MWd/MTU and was 216 mm long. The oxide was cleaned off the
exterior of the cladding on the last inch of both ends to permit proper sealing with Swagelok fittings. Fuel
from the top 25 mm of the sample was then removed by insertion into a 25 mm–deep bath of hot nitric
acid. Defueling permits good gas communication into the sample for pressurization of the sample.
Approximately the first 35 mm of the sample corresponded to a spacer grid position. The sample was then
secured to a standard SATS test train such that the center of the sample was in the center of the DIC
furnace after installation.

The top end of the sample was connected to the top end of the test train, and the bottom was sealed with a
Swagelok cap. This permitted pressurization of the sample from the defueled end. Two type-S
thermocouples were secured to the sample with a wrapped platinum wire positioned approximately
25 mm above the center of the sample. Finally, high-temperature ceramic paint was applied to

 7

approximately half of the sample and centered around the sample center for DIC patterning. A white base
coat was applied using spray paint. The sample was then speckle coated with a black spray paint, which
was applied by lightly spraying the sample at a distance of about 3 feet. The sample was then loaded onto
the SATS test chamber. During this process, the thermocouples were dislodged from the platinum wire,
requiring the sample to be removed, the thermocouples resecured, and paint reapplied. The second
loading was successful, but one of the thermocouples was dislodged from the platinum. The secured
thermocouple was used as a control, and the second thermocouple monitored the tube temperature.

Finally, the DIC rig was installed in the hot-cell. Two LED lights were installed on the rig and positioned
while observing the images from the cameras to maximize image quality. The 2D camera was focused on
the sample by physically adjusting the position of the rig and the fine-positioning stage. The stereo
cameras were then adjusted and focused to the correct position at a stereo angle of approximately 12°.
Despite the use of centering rings to help keep the sample in the center, ideal viewing of the sample by
the stereo cameras was not possible due to a slight center misalignment of the sample within the tube. The
sample was still reasonably centered, but its edges were not optimally viewed by one of the cameras.
Following initial setup, the rig was slid backward, and the stereo cameras were calibrated. The DIC rig
was repositioned for collection, and steam flow was initiated.

3.2 LOCA TEST AND ANALYSIS

The SATS test used flowing steam and started at room temperature with an initial ramp to 300°C. The
sample was pressurized to 8.18 MPa and held at 300°C for 10 min. At the end of the hold, a 5°C/s ramp
rate increased the sample temperature until burst. Following burst, the sample immediately depressurized
through the burst. Throughout the test, both the stereo-DIC cameras and the 2D DIC cameras collected
data at 4 frames per second. Figure 5 shows pictures of the test during the 5°C/s ramp while DIC data
were collected.

 Figure 5. Images of the furnace with the DIC rig and data acquisition equipment collecting data.

Figure 6 shows the temperature and pressure history during the 5°C/s heating rate LOCA test, and Figure
7 shows a photograph of the sample after the test. The time range shown represents the regime in which
the DIC analysis was performed. The control thermocouple was in contact with the cladding, while the
limit thermocouple recorded the steam temperature at the same axial location as that of the control
thermocouple. The cladding rupture temperature and pressure were 860°C and 8.18 MPa, respectively.
Rupture occurred approximately 50 mm above the cladding centerline, and this location was outside the
viewport of the camera system. Therefore, the analysis presented does not represent the cladding balloon
and burst behavior but rather showcases the cladding deformation in a regime of lower magnitude and
more uniform strains.

Figure 11 shows example images of the stereo camera hoop-strain mapping. A smaller region of the
cladding was successfully tracked due to the limited visibility of the cladding from the left stereo camera
(Figure 8). Uniform strains were mapped throughout the LOCA test, and some localized strain anomalies
became present in the final seconds before rupture. The ROI chosen for 2D DIC analysis was also used
for the 3D DIC analysis. Both 2D and 3D strain data from the ROI are directly compared in Figure 12.
The hoop direction (exx), axial direction (eyy), and in-plane shear (exy) strains were plotted as averages

slightly higher hoop strain immediately before the rupture event. Axial strains slightly increased
throughout the LOCA test. The lack of a large negative change in eyy indicates the presence of a small
balloon size because large balloons are concurrent with a cladding length shrinkage to accommodate
large, localized hoop strains.

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

This report presents the first use of DIC for examining nuclear fuel cladding undergoing a standard
LOCA-simulant test in the in-cell SATS. The DIC rig used for in-cell examination differed from that of
out-of-cell DIC testing because of the unique environment of the hot-cells. The in-cell rig was designed
and validated for both 2D and 3D (stereo) DIC measurements. Predeployment DIC rig validation testing
showed that both 2D and 3D DIC yield valid results at relatively low strains (<6%–8%), after which 2D
DIC begins to significantly overestimate the strains. Thus, 2D DIC can be used in the hot-cells, but
caution should be exercised in interpreting the data at higher strains and in regions of nonuniform
deformation.

A SATS LOCA test was then performed on an irradiated fuel segment at a pressure of 8.18 MPa and a
ramp rate of 5°C/s to burst. Both 2D and 3D DIC were used, collecting images at a rate of 4 frames per
second. The sample burst approximately 50 mm above the center of the furnace, where the cameras
collected the DIC data. Thus, the in-situ data do not directly correspond to the burst but instead captured a
low strain uniform deformation regime. Both 2D and 3D DIC analyses showed similar trends for the low-
strain region of the cladding that was visible during the LOCA test. The continued analysis will involve
assessing temperature-dependent deformation and further investigating the fundamental differences
between 2D and 3D DIC techniques for transient testing.
