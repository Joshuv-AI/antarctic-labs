Social Distancing Equilibria in Games under Conventional SI Dynamics
Connor D. Olson, Timothy C. Reluga

Abstract
The mathematical characterization of social-distancing games in classical epidemic theory remains an important question, for their applications to both infectious-disease theory and memetic theory. We consider a special case of the dynamic finite-duration SI social-distancing game where payoffs are accounted using Markov decision theory with zero-discounting, while distancing is constrained by threshold-linear running-costs, and the running-cost of perfect-distancing is finite. In this special case, we are able construct strategic equilibria satisfying the Nash best-response condition explicitly by integration. Our constructions are obtained using a new change of variables which simplifies the geometry and analysis. As it turns out, there are no singular solutions, and a time-dependent bang-bang strategy consisting of a wait-and-see phase followed by a lock-down phase is always the unique strategic equilibrium. We also show that in a restricted strategy space the bang-bang Nash equilibrium is an ESS, and that the optimal public policy exactly corresponds with the equilibrium strategy.

1
Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic brought significant death and disruption to the lives of
many people globally during 2020 and 2021. While the world waited for the
development of what was hoped to be a pandemic-ending vaccine,
social-distancing was a universally-accessible behavioral intervention
promoted by many health agencies. In principle, this was individual people
quarantining themselves on their own volition to avoid getting sick and
contributing to the epidemic. While health agencies desire their subjects to
follow their recommendations when applicable, the effectiveness of these
interventions in democratic states falls upon the willingness of people to
maintain them.

One approach to quantifying interventions like social distancing is to
analyze each individual’s behavior in the context of the decisions of
others around them. To do this, we can appeal to classical game theory as a
model individual behavior. The social distancing game (SDG) is the most
prominent form of a continuous time (or differential) epidemic game. This type
of game represents contact-patterns as a strategy and considers the
consequences of each individual seeking to optimize their own well-being over
the course of an epidemic. One early manifestation of this game was developed by

21
, while a discrete time analogue was put-forward in

4
in the parlance of a public avoidance game.

Since this time, extensive work has been done on social distancing games, especially since 2020.
Recent works in this vein include

(
18
;
10
;
26
;
5
;
13
;
3
;
16
;
17
;
27
;
2
;
1
;
25
;
24
)
.
These authors take many distinct approaches to studying social distancing,
from exploring the effects of different individual decision processes to modeling the impact of limited health care
resources on how individuals behave.
One key question which has not be well-addressed in the literature is whether the rational behaviors captured by these
models are unique and under what conditions uniqueness fails.
One serious attempt at addressing this question in the SIR SDG was made by
22
, who focused on
the infinite-time horizon case but did not give a complete formal proof.
One limitation of this work is the method of analysis is not easily generalizable to even a slightly
more complicated game, so the general question of uniqueness in epidemic games is still unresolved.

The recent paper by
24
also presents a uniqueness result for the SIR social
distancing game in the infinite-horizon case by demonstrating that the
Nash equilibrium behavior only depends on the current number of infected and the final number
of susceptibles, which also gives analytic expressions for the epidemic trajectory. This model deviates from
Reluga’s by accounting for the costs of infection and intervention in a mathematically convenient way.
Accounting for costs in such a manner, potentially over modeling the
costs in a realistic fashion, is a common theme in epidemic game theory and potentially impedes the development
of general theory which can further advance our understanding of the systems these games are meant to model.
Just like we should strive to utilize epidemic models which accurately capture the disease dynamics in a real human population,
we should model costs in a fashion that reflect the way costs are incurred by individuals enduring an epidemic.

The goal of this paper is to present the initial steps toward a complete uniqueness proof of the Nash equilibrium behavior
in the SI variant of the social distancing game. Like
22
, we work in the framework developed by

19
, with the use of artificially cost-convexity.
The scenario we imagine is an epidemic in which infection is
permanent and only transmitted through peer-to-peer interactions, with a
fixed cost of infection. Pathogens which roughly fit these assumptions are the
herpes simplex virus, Hepatitis B, or HIV.
While we develop our theory in the context of infectious diseases, it should
be noted that the SI model also has important applications
in the spread of ideas and information, and our results are equally important
there. This was originally pointed out by
23
,
and re-emerged in modern memetic theory
(
14
;
9
)
.

This paper proceeds in the following fashion. First, we construct the SI
social-distancing game. Using two assumptions about what the optimal behavior
should be, we reduce the strategy space to two-phase
off-on strategies. We leverage this simplicity to prove the two-phase off-on Nash equilibrium
is an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)
(
15
)
in the restricted strategy space.
We are also able to demonstrate the surprising result that the Nash behavior corresponds
with the socially optimal behavior, which reflects the discrete time result from
4
. We then introduce the
maximum-principle analysis of the game, and use it to establish that two-phase
off-on strategies are the unique strategic equilibria of the SI
social-distancing game. This demonstrates the long-held belief that the SI
social-distancing game has unique Nash equilibrium.

2
SI Distancing Game

We study the simplest epidemic with SI dynamics. Consider a population of
fixed size
N
where people may become infected by a disease, and once
infected, they stay infected forever. Every individual is hypothesized to be
either susceptible or infected – there is no immunity or recovery. Let
t
be
the date,
S(t)
be the measure of the susceptible portion at
t
, while
I(t)

is the measure of the infected population that is infected at
t
. Let infection
occur by direct contact according to the law of mass-action with rate
\overline{\sigma}\beta
,
where
\beta
is the baseline transmission rate and
\overline{\sigma}
is the
relative overall reduction in transmission due to social-distancing (
\overline{\sigma}\in[0,1]
).
Then the rates of change in the susceptible and infected are given as

\displaystyle\dot{S}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\sigma}\beta SI,

(1a)

\displaystyle\dot{I}

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma}\beta SI.

(1b)

Our epidemic starts at time
t=0
with initial condition

\displaystyle S(0)

\displaystyle=N-I_{0},\,I(0)=I_{0}.

(1c)

Epidemic games hypothesize that there are trade-offs between the costs of
infection and the costs of prevention, and that individuals will choose
behaviors that balance these trade-offs. Suppose the cost of getting infected
at date
t
is
C_{i}(t)
, while a susceptible individual spends at rate
c_{s}(t)

to prevent infection. We define a relative infection risk for an individual

\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
as a function of their spending rate,
such that
\sigma(0)=1
and
\sigma
is decreasing. When the population is
spending at average per-capita rate
\overline{c}_{s}
, then the relative overall transmission
reduction
\overline{\sigma}=\sigma(\overline{c}_{s})
. We assume the population is sufficiently large
that the average spending rate is approximately independent of any one individual’s choice
of spending rate.

The disutility to an individual is the sum of the cost of infection and the
accrued cost from social distancing. When an individual has perfect
information about their own state, they will stop expensive prevention behaviors as soon
as they are infected.
If costs are exponentially discounted at rate
h
, then an individual
becoming infected at time
t
would accumulate a disutility

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{-h\tau}c_{s}(\tau)d\tau+C_{i}(t)e^{-ht}.

(2)

If a person makes it to the end of the game without being infected,
they will accumulate a disutility

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{f}}e^{-h\tau}c_{s}(\tau)d\tau.

(3)

But each of these possible outcomes is uncertain.
If
p_{s}(t)
is the probability of still being susceptible at time
t

and
p_{i}(t)
is the probability of being infected at time
t
,
then the expected disutility
D
from
t=0
to
t=t_{f}
is

\displaystyle D=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-h\tau}c_{s}(\tau)d\tau+C_{i}(t)e^{-ht}\right)\dot{p}_{i}(t)dt+p_{s}(t_{f})\int_{0}^{t_{f}}e^{-ht}c_{s}(t)dt

(4)

Under System (
1
),
p_{i}=1-p_{s}
and the hazard rate for this susceptible
individual to become infected is

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{s}/p_{s}=\sigma(c_{s}(t))\beta I

(5)

with
p_{s}(0)=1
. Using integration-by-parts, one can then show

D=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}e^{-ht}[c_{s}(t)+\sigma(c_{s}(t))\beta I(t)C_{i}(t)]p_{s}(t)\,dt.

(6)

The disutility
D
depends on the individual’s social-distancing investments
c_{s}(t)
,
and the course of the epidemic
I(t)
, which depends
on the average social-distancing costs
\overline{c}_{s}(t)
. Thus,

D(c_{s},\overline{c}_{s})
as defined by Eq. (
6
) under Eqs. (
5
) and
(
1
) defines a set of dynamic population games parameterized by the
values of
t_{f}
,
\sigma
,
C_{i}
,
h
,
\beta
, and
N
.

We will not consider this full family, and instead restrict ourselves
to an important special case where there is no discounting (
h=0
),
the cost-of infection
C_{i}
is constant over time, and social distancing
has the form of a threshold-linear response

\displaystyle\sigma(z)=\max(0,1-mz)=(1-mz)^{+},

(7)

where
m
is the efficiency of spending. Eq. (
7
) implies
a rolling cost of
1/m
is enough to surely prevent infection.
Eq. (
7
)’s choice of
\sigma
may seem arbitrary and
overly-restrictive, but its (weak) convexity is enough to satisfy the economic
principle of diminishing returns (that investments become less efficient as
they grow because you do the easy things first), and allows us to exploit
linear-programming principles that lead to an analytically integrable system.

By a judicious choice of units for population, time, and money, we will
lose no generality in selecting
C_{i}=\beta=N=1
. Thus, costs will
be measured relative to the cost of infection,
S
and
I
will represent
fractions of the total population, and in the absence of intervention, half
the population is infected in
\ln(1/I_{0}-1)\approx-2.3\log_{10}I_{0}
.
(So if initially
0.1\%
of the population is infected, then half the
population will be infected in about 7 time steps and only
0.1
%
of the population will be uninfected after 14 time steps.)
We further simplify our notation, taking
p(t):=p_{s}(t)

and
c:=c_{s}/C_{i}
.
And so finally, our dynamic SI social-distancing population game (SISDG) has the form

\displaystyle D(c,\overline{c})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\left[c+(1-mc)^{+}I\right]p\,dt.

(8a)

\displaystyle p(0)=1,\quad\dot{p}

\displaystyle=-(1-mc)^{+}Ip,

(8b)

\displaystyle I(0)=I_{0},\quad\dot{I}

\displaystyle=(1-m\overline{c})^{+}I(1-I),

(8c)

parameterized by the game-duration
t_{f}
, the efficiency-of-distancing
m
,
and the initial infected fraction
I_{0}
.

3
Preliminary analysis

Before proceeding, let us provide some context for our analysis by first
commenting on some special limiting/boundary cases of System
8
’s
SISDG. First, observe that prevalence is increasing and bounded
(
\dot{I}\geq 0
,
I_{0}\leq I(t)<1
),
and strictly-increasing away from the extremes.
The probability of susceptibility at time
t
,

p(t)\geq\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}I(t;I_{0})dt\right)\geq\exp(-t)>0
. Because
rolling costs are never negative (
c\geq 0
and
c+(1-mc)^{+}I\geq\min(1/m,I_{0})>0
),
the epidemic will always be expected to impose an
unavoidable price on each individual (
D(c,\overline{c})\geq\min(1/m,I_{0})(1-e^{-t_{f}})>0
), no matter their actions.
On the other hand, a person
need never do worse than assured infection in the SISDG.
(since
D(0,\overline{c})\leq 1-e^{-t_{f}}<1
).
If the rolling cost of
prevention exactly offsets the expected rolling cost of infection (
m=1
),
social distancing provides no benefits. For all less-efficient cases (
m<1
), the
upper-bound on
I(t)
implies rolling-costs of social distancing outweigh the
expected rolling-costs of the infection, and that social-distancing is always
irrational.

We are interested in finding strategic equilibria of this population game and
characterizing their properties. We would like to find strategies that
improve on alternatives and are stable against perturbation. Said differently,
we would like to find a strategy
c^{*}
that satisfies the Nash condition

\displaystyle c^{*}\in\argmin_{c}D(c,c^{*})

(9)

and the invasion condition

\displaystyle c^{*}\in\argmin_{\overline{c}}\quad D(\overline{c},\overline{c})-D(c^{*},\overline{c}).

(10)

Any strategy that satisfies both conditions is called an ESS
(evolutionarily-stable strategy). In practice, the invasion condition rarely
holds globally and is difficult to establish when it does. While we are unable to
prove this condition over the general strategy space, and thus focus on proving uniqueness of those
strategic equilibria which satisfy the Nash condition, we do obtain good insight by first restricting
the available strategies.

4
Delay strategies

A natural first approach to the study of the SI social-distancing game
defined by System
8
is to try various strategies numerically.
With the benefits of hindsight, this is best done in a restricted
strategy-space.
Because we are modeling a permanent state-change, the risk-of-infection for a
susceptible individual is monotonically increasing.
The threshold-linear form of
\sigma
in Eq. (
7
) suggests
there may be a tipping point, before which the risk is too small relative
to the cost of distancing, and after which it is efficient to distance
maximally (a bang-bang form).
Let us define a new coordinate variable
x\in[0,t_{f}]
to specify
the duration of social-distancing at the end of the game, with
x=0

implying no social distancing and
x=t_{f}
implying social-distancing
for the full duration of the game.
A strategy can be represented in terms of the duration
x
as
a two-phase piecewise-constant function

\xi(t;x)=\operatorname{H}(t-t_{f}+x)/m
,
where
\operatorname{H}(t)
is a Heaviside step function that is
0
when
t

is negative and
1
when
t
is positive.
We only consider positive-duration games (
t_{f}>0
), such that
each duration
x
uniquely defines a strategy, and for any pair of delays

(x,\overline{x})\in[0,t_{f}]\times[0,t_{f}]
, we integrate System (
8
) to
explicitly construct the restricted disutility

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x,\overline{x}):=D(\xi(t;x),\xi(t;\overline{x}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{f}-x}Ip\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{f}-x}^{t_{f}}p/m\,\mathrm{d}t

(11a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1-e^{x-t_{f}}\left(1-x/m\right)\quad\text{if $I_{0}=1$,}\\
1-p(x,\overline{x})\left(1-x/m\right)\quad\text{if $I_{0}<1$,}\end{cases}

(11b)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad p(x,\overline{x})=

\displaystyle\frac{1-I(s)}{1-I_{0}}\exp\left(-I(s)(\overline{x}-x)^{+}\right),

(11c)

\displaystyle s=

\displaystyle t_{f}-\max(x,\overline{x}),

(11d)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad I(u)=

\displaystyle I_{0}/\left(I_{0}+(1-I_{0})e^{-u}\right).

(11e)

These closed-forms for
\mathcal{D}(x,\overline{x})
allow us to extract insights to the game-play of
the delay-strategy SI social-distancing game (DSISDG) that are inaccessible
in the general cases of the SISDG.

4.2
Interpretation

Further analysis is needed to interpret the implications of

x^{*}(m,I_{0},t_{f})
from Eq. (
13
). We construct the
emblematic restricted disutility

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\overline{x})=\mathcal{D}(\overline{x},\overline{x})=1-\frac{(1-\overline{x}/m)}{1-I_{0}+\exp(t_{f}-\overline{x})}

(16)

which tells us the typical accrued costs to an initially-susceptible
individual playing the population-strategy
\overline{x}
. The relative
restricted disutility

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}(x,\overline{x})=\mathcal{D}(x,\overline{x})/\mathcal{E}(\overline{x})

(17)

tells us how badly off a strategy
x
performs relative to the
population strategy
\overline{x}
. For an example, see
Figure
1
.

Our first conceptual result is that the
DSISDG (
11
) never suffers from free-riding –
the strategy that optimized the populations welfare also
optimizes the individual’s well-being in the sense that

\displaystyle x^{*}=\argmin_{\overline{x}\in[0,t_{f}]}\mathcal{E}(\overline{x}).

(18)

At equilibrium, the emblematic disutility has the convenient form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x^{*})=\begin{cases}(1-I(t_{f}))/(1-I_{0}),&\text{if $x^{*}=0$},\\
(1+x^{*}-mI_{0})/m(1-I_{0}),&\text{if $x^{*}\in(0,t_{f})$},\\
t_{f}/m,&\text{if $x^{*}=t_{f}$}.\end{cases}

(19)

To see how much social-distancing would actually help (see Fig.
2

5
), we define
the epidemic burden on the
population as the sum of the accrued costs of infection and
prevention over the whole population,

\displaystyle B(\overline{x})

\displaystyle=I_{0}+(1-I_{0})\mathcal{E}(\overline{x})

(20)

\displaystyle=I(t_{f}-\overline{x})+\frac{\overline{x}}{m}\left(1-I(t_{f}-\overline{x})\right)

(21)

and the equilibrium improvement-over-indifference

\displaystyle\Delta B^{*}=B(0)-B(x^{*})

(22)

tells us how much equilibrium social-distancing reduces the burden of
the epidemic relative to the case where no preventative actions are
taken (
\overline{x}=0
).
Figure
4

I_{0}=10^{-4}

5
General Analysis

To generalize our analysis, we will allow individuals to adopt piecewise-continuous strategies
c:[0,t_{f}]\to[0,\infty)
.
In this setting, Eq
9
becomes

c^{*}\in\argmin_{c\,\in\,[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow[0,\infty)}D(c,c^{*}).

(25)

Our present goal is to establish that each game described by
System
8
has just one Nash equilibrium.

A additional condition we require is that
c^{*}
is subgame-perfect
(
12
)
. In
practice, this condition simplifies the process of determining a best-response,
and implies that all individuals are acting in their own
best-interest at all times between the start and end of the game.

To analyze System
8
for a more general strategy space,
we adopt the standard approach of fixing the population behavior
\overline{c}
and determining the
best response
c_{B}(\overline{c})
using a Hamiltonian approach,
specifically through the application of Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle
(
7
)
.

We define the Hamiltonian as

H=-[c+(1-mc)^{+}I]p-(1-mc)^{+}IpV+k(1-m\overline{c})^{+}I(1-I).

(26)

Applying the maximum principle, we obtain the present time personal state adjoint equation

\dot{V}=(1-mc)^{+}I(V+1)+c.

(27)

with terminal condition
V(t_{f})=0
. Because we treat the population behavior
\overline{c}
as fixed, we can safely ignore
k
and its corresponding equation. This is further justified by the following:
V(t)
can be interpreted as the present time cost of being susceptible
(
6
)
, and applying integration by parts to Eq
8a
, we obtain

D(c,\overline{c})=-V(0).

(28)

Therefore, the initial cost of being susceptible at time 0 is equivalent (up to the sign) to the disutility of playing
c
against
\overline{c}
. This relation will be key to our analysis, as it will allow us to connect the boundary value problem obtained through the optimization process to an initial value problem that we can construct solutions for.

To determine
c_{B}(\overline{c})
, we utilize an argument typically deployed in dynamic programming.
In order to minimize the disutility,
Eq
28
states that we need to minimize the magnitude of
-V(0)
. Starting at
V(t_{f})=0
,
c_{B}(\overline{c})
is
constructed in reverse time to lead to the minimal increase in
-V(t)
at each
t\in[0,t_{f}]
. Therefore, we must have

c_{B}(\overline{c})\in\argmin\{\sigma(c)I(V+1)+c\}.

(29)

Constructing the best response in this fashion removes explicit dependence upon
\overline{c}
, only depending on the population behavior through the state
I
.
This dependency of
c^{*}
on only the state variables
I
and
V
implies that both the personal and population
strategies are specified at each point in time through Eq
29
.
This naturally constructs a Nash equilibrium because the two strategies match and the personal strategy is by construction a
best response. Also, because this strategy is optimal at each time point by construction, it is also subgame perfect.

Taking
c^{*}
to be the Nash strategy which satisfies Eq
29
for each
(I,V)
, we obtain the
following Filippov System
(
11
)
which defines
c^{*}
.

\displaystyle\dot{I}

\displaystyle=\sigma(c^{*})I(1-I),

(30a)

\displaystyle\dot{V}

\displaystyle=\sigma(c^{*})I(V+1)+c^{*}

(30b)

\displaystyle c^{*}(I,V)

\displaystyle\in\begin{cases}\{0\}&\text{if}\;I\big(V+1\big)<\frac{1}{m},\\
[0,\frac{1}{m}]&\text{if }\;I\big(V+1\big)=\frac{1}{m},\\
\{\frac{1}{m}\}&\text{else},\\
\end{cases}

with boundary conditions

Lemma 3

For

Fix
\Phi_{0}<\min{(I_{0},\frac{1}{m})}

7
Discussion

Here we have presented the first formal proof of the uniqueness of Nash equilibria in the finite time social distancing game as
studied by
21
;
22
for an SI epidemic. These equilibria strategies are two-phase off-on strategies, which reflect the linearity of
(1-mc)^{+}
and the monotonicity of the risk of infection.
The method of analysis we have deployed to establish uniqueness in this simplified case will be applied analyze to the positive discounting case
(h>0)
in a forthcoming work, and our ultimate goal is to characterize the Nash equilibria structure for the SI social distancing game for any admissible convex
\sigma
.

Our proof focused on the perfect intervention case because of its mathematical simplicity, but one cannot expect that social distancing interventions are perfect in many cases. While slightly more difficult, we expect that within this approach one can prove uniqueness for imperfect interventions, where
\sigma(c)=\max{(1-mc,b)}
for
b\in[0,1)
.
In lieu of a proof, we observe that the only difference is that

\dot{I}>0
when
\Phi>\frac{1}{m}
, which should not affect the conclusions but may make the equations more difficult
to analyze. Our as-yet-unpublished results with
h>0
do suggest that this conjecture could be provable within this framework.

The second aspect of our analysis was investigating the efficacy of the Nash equilibria strategies, which we did by restricting
the strategy space to only on-off strategies. A key insight from this analysis is social distancing in this game is only
efficacious for epidemics which go on for some duration but a vaccine arrives before too long, dependent on the efficiency of
the intervention. We also demonstrated that the DSISDG does not have a free-riding effect, which corroborates a conclusion
from
4
.

Because of our ability to explicitly construct the DSISDG equations, we were able to prove that the Nash equilibrium in the
restricted strategy space is a global ESS (Appendix-
10
, Theorem
10
). While we would like to extend this
characterization to the general strategy space, even a two-dimensional strategy space can make this analysis very
difficult, as seen by
20
. We are unaware of any successful efforts to codify the concept of an ESS for an
infinite-dimensional strategy space or to develop the tools to characterize an ESS over such a space. This system may provide
the perfect mixture of complexity and simplicity to undertake such an effort.

Our results here are strongly dependent on the monotonicity of risk in the SI epidemic dynamic. They will certainly
not generalize in full to SIR theory, where risk goes up and down.
Any attempt to analyze even the SIR variant, like in
(
22
)
, quickly encounters difficulties that we avoided.
An interesting insight is all of our general results should hold if we replace
I(t)
with any monotonically increasing function

0<f(t)\leq 1
. In this situation, we lack the ability to construct explicit equations but can establish the requisite monotonicity.

Minima of Special Functions

For our proof of the ESS conditions on the restricted strategy space, we encounter two functions for which
the Nash strategy is a global minimum to each, and this allows us to establish the necessary inequalities. Here,
we present proofs of both facts.

Lemma 8
.

Let
C>0
, then if there exists
x\in(0,t_{f})
with
C=\frac{1}{m-x}
,
x
is a global minimizer of

\exp(Cx)\Big(\frac{x}{m}-1\Big)

(45)

Proof.

The first derivative condition gives

0=\exp(Cx)\Big(C\Big(\frac{x}{m}-1\Big)+\frac{1}{m}\Big),

(46)

which is satisfied when
\frac{x}{m}-1=-\frac{1}{mC}
. As there is a single critical point, if it is a minimum it
is necessarily global.

To establish this as a minimum, we look at the second derivative given by

\exp{(Cx)}\Big(C^{2}\Big(\frac{x}{m}-1\Big)+\frac{2C}{m}\Big),

(47)

which is positive at the critical point, and therefore that point is a minimum.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
I(t)
be as given in Eq.
11e
. Suppose there exists an
x\in(0,t_{f})
with
I(t_{f}-x)=\frac{1}{m-x}
, then
x
is a global minimum of

(1-I(t_{f}-x))\Big(\frac{x}{m}-1\Big).

(48)

Proof.

The critical point condition is

0=(1-I(t_{f}-x))\Big(\frac{1}{m}+I(t_{f}-x)\Big(\frac{x}{m}-1\Big)\Big),

(49)

which is satisfied when
x
satisfies
I(t_{f}-x)=\frac{1}{m-x}
.

The second derivative evaluated at the
x
satisfying the condition is

\frac{(1-I(t_{f}-x)I(t_{f}-x))}{1-I_{0}}\Big(1-\frac{x}{m}\Big)>0.

(50)

because
\frac{1}{m-x}=I<1
, which implies that
\frac{x}{m}<1
.
∎

Differentiability of restricted disutility

Continuity of
\mathcal{D}
in both variables follows because
p(x,\overline{x})
given in Eq
11c
is continuous in both variables resulting from the continuity of
both
\max(x,\overline{x})
and
(x-\overline{x})^{+}
. To demonstrate differentiability of Eq
11b
, it suffices to show that
p(x,\overline{x})

is differentiable. To demonstrate this, we split into the cases
x<\overline{x}
and
x>\overline{x}
, and then demonstrate equality of these
when
x=\overline{x}
.

When
x<\overline{x}
,

\frac{d}{dx}p(x,\overline{x})=\frac{I(t_{f}-\overline{x})\Big(1-I(t_{f}-\overline{x})\Big)}{1-I_{0}}\exp{\Big(-I(t_{f}-\overline{x})(\overline{x}-x)\Big)},

(51a)

\frac{d}{d\overline{x}}p(x,\overline{x})=\frac{I(t_{f}-\overline{x})\Big(1-I(t_{f}-\overline{x})\Big)(\overline{x}-x)}{1-I_{0}}\exp{\Big(-I(t_{f}-\overline{x})(\overline{x}-x)\Big)}.

(51b)

When
x>\overline{x}
,

\frac{d}{dx}p(x,\overline{x})=\frac{I(t_{f}-x)\Big(1-I(t_{f}-x)\Big)}{1-I_{0}},

(52a)

\frac{d}{d\overline{x}}p(x,\overline{x})=0.

(52b)

Taking the one-sided limits as
x\to\overline{x}
gives us

\frac{d}{dx}|_{x=\overline{x}}p(x,\overline{x})=\frac{I(t_{f}-x)\Big(1-I(t_{f}-x)\Big)}{1-I_{0}}.

(52c)

Similarly, taking
\overline{x}\to x
, we have

\frac{d}{d\overline{x}}|_{\overline{x}=x}p(x,\overline{x})=0.

(52d)

These demonstrate that
\mathcal{D}(x,\overline{x})
is continuously differentiable on
(0,t_{f})\times(0,t_{f})
and continuous up to the endpoints.

10
ESS Proof

In this appendix we prove that the Nash equilibrium for the DSISDG
11
is a global Nash equilibrium and in fact an ESS
(
15
)
. To do this, we lean upon a relation establish through the local optimality condition,
which is

\frac{d}{dx}|_{x=\overline{x}}\mathcal{D}(x,\overline{x})=0.

(53)

This is equivalent to
x
satisfying

0=\frac{1-I(t_{f}-x)}{m(1-I_{0})}\Big(I(t_{f}-x)(m-x)-1\Big),

(54)

giving the condition of local optimality

I(t_{f}-x)=\frac{1}{m-x}.

(55)

An immediate intermediate result is that this game has no free-riding, so the local social optimum is equivalent to the local best-response. The derivative condition for the social optimum is

0=\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{D}(x,x)=\frac{1-I(t_{f}-x)}{m(1-I_{0})}\Big(I(t_{f}-x)(m-x)-1\Big),

(56)

exactly the same as the horizontal derivative giving the local best-response.

To establish that the local Nash equilibrium given by
x^{*}
satisfying the local condition Eq
55
is an ESS,
we prove that it is a global Nash equilibrium with invasion potential. We establish these through the two inequalities given
in the following theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Let
x^{*}\in(0,t_{f})
satisfy Eq
55
. Then for all
y\in[0,t_{f}]
,
y\not=x^{*}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x^{*},x^{*})

\displaystyle<\mathcal{D}(y,x^{*}),

(57a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x^{*},y)

\displaystyle<\mathcal{D}(y,y).

(57b)

Eq
57a
is the Nash equilibrium condition and Eq
57b
is the invasion condition. Together these
establish
x^{*}
as an ESS.

Proof.

To establish Eq
57a
, we analyze

\mathcal{D}(y,x^{*})-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},x^{*})=p(y,x^{*})\Big(\frac{y}{m}-1\Big)-p(x^{*},x^{*})\Big(\frac{x^{*}}{m}-1\Big).

(58)

To establish the desired inequality, we first presume
y<x^{*}
. In this case,

\mathcal{D}(y,x^{*})-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},x^{*})=\frac{1-I(t_{f}-x^{*})}{1-I_{0}}\exp{(-I(t_{f}-x^{*})x^{*})}\Big(\exp{(I(t_{f}-x^{*})y)}\Big(\frac{y}{m}-1\Big)-\exp{(I(t_{f}-x^{*})x^{*})}\Big(\frac{x^{*}}{m}-1\Big)\Big).

(59)

Taking
C=I(t_{f}-x^{*})
and applying Lemma
8
, we see that
x^{*}
is the global minimizer, so the previous difference is positive. Therefore,

\mathcal{D}(y,x^{*})-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},x^{*})>0.

(60)

In the second case of
x^{*}<y
, we have

\mathcal{D}(y,x^{*})-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},x^{*})=\frac{1-I(t_{f}-y)}{1-I_{0}}\Big(\frac{y}{m}-1\Big)-\frac{1-I(t_{f}-x^{*})}{1-I_{0}}\Big(\frac{x^{*}}{m}-1\Big).

(61)

Applying Lemma
9
,
x^{*}
is the global minimum, so we have

\mathcal{D}(y,x^{*})-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},x^{*})>0,

(62)

which establishes the Nash equilibrium condition.

To prove the invasion condition Eq
57b
, we similarly analyze

\mathcal{D}(y,y)-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},y)=p(y,y)\Big(\frac{y}{m}-1\Big)-p(x^{*},y)\Big(\frac{x^{*}}{m}-1\Big).

(63)

Again, we separate this into cases. If
x^{*}<y
,

\mathcal{D}(y,y)-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},y)=\frac{1-I(t_{f}-y)}{1-I_{0}}\exp{(-I(t_{f}-y)y)}\Big(\exp{(I(t_{f}-y)y)}\Big(\frac{y}{m}-1\Big)-\exp{(I(t_{f}-y)x^{*})}\Big(\frac{x^{*}}{m}-1\Big)\Big).

(64)

Applying Lemma
8
with
C=(I(t_{f}-y)
demonstrates that
x^{*}
is the global minimum, and therefore

\mathcal{D}(y,y)-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},y)>0.

(65)

Lastly, when
x^{*}>y
,

\mathcal{D}(y,y)-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},y)=\frac{1-I(t_{f}-y)}{1-I_{0}}\Big(\frac{y}{m}-1\Big)-\frac{1-I(t_{f}-x^{*})}{1-I_{0}}\Big(\frac{x^{*}}{m}-1\Big).

(66)

Applying Lemma
9
, we obtain

\mathcal{D}(y,y)-\mathcal{D}(x^{*},y)>0,

(67)

which establishes the invasion condition.
∎
