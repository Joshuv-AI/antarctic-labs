Intracoronary thrombus in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction: Practical management strategies for the interventional cardiologist
Authors: Debabrata Dash, Umanshi Dash, Rohit Mody
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intracoronary thrombus is a hallmark of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and remains a major challenge during primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI). Large thrombus burden is associated with distal embolization, microvascular obstruction, no-reflow, stent thrombosis, and adverse clinical outcomes. Optimal management requires an individualized approach integrating pharmacological and mechanical strategies. This review summarizes the pathophysiology, angiographic assessment, and clinical implications of intracoronary thrombus in STEMI. Contemporary pharmacological therapies, including dual antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors, and intracoronary thrombolytics, are discussed alongside mechanical approaches such as aspiration thrombectomy, mechanical thrombectomy devices, direct stenting, and deferred stenting. Current evidence from major clinical trials and guideline recommendations is reviewed to clarify the selective role of these therapies in modern practice. A practical stepwise management algorithm is proposed to assist interventional cardiologists in tailoring therapy according to thrombus burden, coronary flow, and lesion characteristics. Emerging technologies and future directions aimed at improving thrombus management and microvascular reperfusion are also highlighted. This manuscript is a narrative review of intracoronary thrombus management in acute coronary syndrome, particularly STEMI during PPCI. A structured literature search was performed in PubMed and Embase, along with major guideline documents and cardiovascular society recommendations. Studies published from 2000 to 2026 were included using keywords such as “STEMI,” “intracoronary thrombus,” “thrombectomy,” and “no-reflow.” Eligible evidence included randomized controlled trials, observational studies, meta-analyses, and guidelines, while chronic coronary syndromes and chronic total occlusion-related thrombus were excluded. No formal quality assessment or statistical synthesis was performed, and findings were synthesized qualitatively for clinical relevance.

Introduction

Occlusion of the infarct-related artery (IRA), either partial or complete, due to intracoronary thrombus represents the defining pathological feature of patients presenting with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). In contemporary clinical practice, a large thrombus burden (LTB) is observed in up to 90% of STEMI presentations and creates important technical and prognostic challenges during primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI).1 A LTB is associated with an increased risk of distal embolization, no-reflow phenomenon, stent thrombosis, and adverse long-term cardiovascular outcomes.2 Therefore, selecting an appropriate management strategy is essential for achieving optimal results during primary PCI, typically requiring a combination of pharmacological therapy and mechanical thrombus-modifying techniques. This review synthesizes current evidence and provides practical guidance for interventional cardiologists. This review specifically focuses on intracoronary thrombus management in the acute coronary syndrome setting, particularly STEMI during PPCI, and does not address chronic organized thrombus encountered in stable coronary disease or chronic total occlusions.

It is important to recognize that the evidence base supporting intracoronary thrombus management is highly heterogeneous, comprising randomized controlled trials, small single-center studies, retrospective registries, and feasibility analyses. Many studies differ substantially in thrombus definition, adjunct pharmacotherapy, procedural strategy, and endpoint selection. Furthermore, several commonly cited investigations are underpowered for clinical endpoints and rely predominantly on surrogate angiographic or procedural outcomes. Therefore, interpretation of the evidence requires careful distinction between hypothesis-generating data and interventions supported by robust randomized trials with demonstrated clinical benefit.

Pathobiology of coronary thrombus and clinical implications

STEMI results from acute coronary occlusion following plaque rupture or erosion leading to platelet activation, thrombin generation, and fibrin-rich clot formation. The thrombus can be platelet-rich (“white”) or fibrin-rich (“red”) depending on the stage of evolution. LTB reflects ongoing thrombogenesis and impaired endogenous fibrinolysis. Mechanical disruption during PCI may dislodge thrombus fragments, causing distal embolization and microvascular obstruction (MVO). These mechanisms contribute to no-reflow and infarct expansion. Thrombus burden also correlates with inflammatory activity, hypercoagulability, and delayed presentation. The presence of LTB is associated with worse outcomes including mortality and stent thrombosis.

Despite its widespread use, interpretation of the TIMI thrombus scale may vary among operators. For practical clinical use, the grading system is often simplified into two categories: low thrombus burden (TIMI grades 1–3) and LBT(TIMI grades 4–5).5 However, when the infarct-related artery is totally occluded (TIMI grade 5), the actual extent of thrombus cannot be reliably determined. Once patients with a patent infarct-related artery are excluded, TIMI grade 5 alone has not consistently remained an independent predictor of distal embolization.4 When a grade 5 thrombus is initially identified, restoration of antegrade coronary flow is recommended, typically achieved by guidewire passage or advancement of an uninflated balloon across the lesion. After flow is re-established, the thrombus burden can be reassessed and reclassified within TIMI grades 1–4. In this refined framework, grades 1–3 indicate relatively small thrombus burden, whereas grade 4 corresponds to a LTB. Using this approach, TIMI grade 4 thrombus has been linked with a higher incidence of stent thrombosis and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) during long-term follow-up, including outcomes at two years.5

Chiung-Jen Yip and colleagues described a set of angiographic characteristics that indicate active or extensive intracoronary thrombosis.6 They identified six angiographic findings (Table 2) that suggest a substantial thrombus burden: (1) an abrupt vessel cutoff pattern at the site of occlusion, (2) visible accumulation of thrombotic material proximal to the obstruction, (3) a reference diameter of the IRA greater than 4.0 mm, (4) partial rather than complete occlusion with angiographic evidence of thrombus whose maximal length exceeds three times the reference vessel diameter, (5) the presence of a floating or mobile thrombus proximal to the culprit lesion, and (6) persistent contrast dye stagnation distal to the occlusion. Together, these angiographic markers help clinicians recognize and estimate a LBT, which is particularly relevant during PPCI. Identifying these features allows operators to adapt procedural strategy, including the use of adjunctive pharmacological therapy or mechanical thrombus-modifying techniques. Clinical prognosis is influenced not only by the quantity of intracoronary thrombus but also by its biological composition. Thrombi that are rich in erythrocytes and fibrin (“red thrombus”) are generally more resistant to therapeutic interventions and have been associated with less favorable outcomes, including a higher incidence of 30-day MACE and mortality, compared with platelet-dominant (“white”) thrombi.7

In addition, thrombi encountered in patients presenting late after symptom onset often become more organized and may respond poorly to commonly used coronary devices such as angioplasty balloons and aspiration catheters. The structural properties of the fibrin network within the clot further influence treatment response. Thrombi composed of thicker fibrin strands tend to be more amenable to both pharmacologic and mechanical disruption. In contrast, clots characterized by a dense mesh of thin fibrin fibres are relatively resistant to thrombolytic therapy as well as catheter-based mechanical approaches.8,9 These differences in thrombus architecture contribute to variability in procedural success and clinical outcomes during.

Management strategies

During PPCI, the extent thrombus is closely linked to both procedural success and subsequent clinical outcomes. Management of LTB requires a structured and multifaceted strategy because intracoronary thrombosis is a complex and dynamic process. Therapeutic approaches generally combine pharmacologic therapy, aimed at inhibiting platelet aggregation and the coagulation cascade, with mechanical techniques designed to restore coronary blood flow. In selected cases, thrombus removal strategies may also be considered to reduce clot burden. In theory, thrombus extraction can limit distal embolization, thereby decreasing the risk of MVO and improving microvascular reperfusion. In patients presenting with STEMI, current guidelines recommend PPCI as the preferred reperfusion strategy, as it reliably restores epicardial flow and is associated with lower rates of early and long-term adverse events, including MACE, compared with fibrinolytic therapy.10 Nevertheless, lesions characterized by a LTB remain technically challenging during PPCI. To overcome these difficulties and minimize thrombotic load, a range of pharmacological and device-based strategies are often employed during the procedure.

Pharmacological strategies

Antiplatelet therapy

Early and timely therapeutic intervention is essential to counter the self-propagating process of intracoronary thrombus formation. Prompt initiation of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) plays a key role in limiting thrombus progression and has been shown to reduce thrombus burden while improving clinical outcomes in patients presenting with STEMI. Aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor reduce platelet aggregation and thrombus propagation. Potent agents such as prasugrel and ticagrelor achieve faster onset and greater thrombus reduction compared with clopidogrel.11 In patients undergoing PCI, intravenous P2Y12 inhibitors, such as cangrelor, may be considered in selected situations—for example, in patients who are intubated, have not received prior oral P2Y12 therapy, or are unable to take oral medications. The use of intravenous platelet inhibition in such circumstances helps ensure rapid and effective platelet blockade, thereby reducing the risks associated with LTB during STEMI management.

Anticoagulation

Anticoagulation is a critical component of PPCI in patients with STEMI. Anticoagulant therapy prevents ongoing thrombin generation and limits further thrombus propagation during the procedure. Unfractionated heparin (UFH) remains the most commonly used agent because of its rapid onset, predictable effect, and ease of monitoring. Alternatives such as bivalirudin, a direct thrombin inhibitor, may be used in selected patients, particularly when bleeding risk is high. Adequate anticoagulation reduces the risk of procedural thrombosis, catheter-related clot formation, and distal embolization. Current guidelines recommend administering anticoagulation at the time of PPCI and maintaining therapeutic levels throughout the procedure.12

Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors

Several studies have previously demonstrated potential benefits of GPI in patients with STEMI, including improved survival and enhanced reperfusion. However, many of these observations were reported in the era preceding the routine use of DAPT, more potent P2Y12 inhibitors, thrombus aspiration techniques, and modern drug-eluting stents. As contemporary pharmacologic and device-based strategies have evolved, the incremental benefit of routine GPI administration during primary PCI has become less clear.12 Trials evaluating pre-hospital administration of GPI have not shown meaningful clinical advantages and therefore this approach is not currently recommended.13 The 2023 ESC ACS guidelines14 and the 2025 ACC/AHA ACS guidelines15 do not support routine use of GPI during PPCI. Their use is primarily reserved for bailout situations, including large residual thrombus burden, slow-flow/no-reflow, distal embolization, or other thrombotic complications encountered during PCI.

Administration of GPI via the intracoronary route has been proposed to achieve higher local drug concentrations at the thrombus site, potentially enhancing thrombus dissolution. While several small studies and meta-analyses have suggested possible benefits, these findings were not confirmed in larger randomized controlled trials (RCTs).16,17

Intracoronary thrombolysis

Intracoronary thrombolysis (ICT) with low-dose fibrinolytic agents, such as alteplase, tenecteplase, or urokinase, allows direct delivery of the drug at the thrombus site, potentially enhancing thrombus dissolution while limiting systemic exposure. This targeted approach may facilitate restoration of epicardial flow, improve microvascular perfusion, and reduce distal embolization during PPCI.12 Several observational studies and small clinical trials have suggested that intracoronary thrombolysis can improve TIMI flow grade, myocardial blush grade, and ST-segment resolution in patients with a LTB.18,19

The RESTORE-MI trial (Restoring Microcirculatory Perfusion in STEMI) is currently evaluating intracoronary tenecteplase as an adjunctive therapy to improve microvascular reperfusion in STEMI patients with LTB.20 This study is expected to provide more robust and definitive data regarding both the efficacy and safety of targeted ICT in contemporary STEMI management.

Given the limited number of patients examined and the heterogenicity in study methods, further research is needed to determine the exact benefits of ICT. However, preliminary results have been encouraging, and it is promising that there was no indication of major bleeding risk, even though most patients in these studies did not receive potent P2Y12 inhibitors. In conclusion, low-dose intracoronary thrombolytics could be a potential option for a LTB, particularly if other more conventional treatments have proven ineffective.9

Mechanical strategies

While numerous pharmacologic and device-based strategies have been evaluated for thrombus modification, the overall quality of evidence is variable. Several studies are limited by small sample sizes, non-randomized design, and selection bias toward operators or centers with higher procedural expertise. In addition, many trials use surrogate endpoints such as TIMI flow grade or myocardial blush grade, which do not always correlate with long-term clinical outcomes such as mortality or reinfarction.

Manual aspiration thrombectomy

The Thrombus Aspiration during Percutaneous Coronary Intervention in Acute Myocardial Infarction Study (TAPAS) was a relatively large single-centre trial that randomised 1071 patients with STEMI to manual thrombus aspiration followed by PCI or to conventional PCI alone. The study demonstrated improved myocardial reperfusion with manual thrombus aspiration, reflected by higher myocardial blush grade and more complete ST-segment resolution.21 In addition, cardiac and all-cause mortality at 1 year were significantly lower in the aspiration group.22

In contrast, two large multicentre trials—Thrombus Aspiration in ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction in Scandinavia (TASTE)23 and ThrOmbecTomy with PCI versus PCI Alone (TOTAL)24—failed to demonstrate a clinical benefit of routine thrombus aspiration. The TASTE trial showed no reduction in all-cause mortality at either 30 days or 1 year, and the rates of recurrent myocardial infarction and stent thrombosis were similar between treatment strategies. Likewise, the TOTAL trial did not show improvement in the primary composite endpoint of cardiovascular death, recurrent myocardial infarction, cardiogenic shock, or New York Heart Association Functional Classification class IV heart failure at 180 days. Importantly, the TOTAL trial raised safety concerns by demonstrating a higher incidence of stroke within 30 days among patients undergoing thrombus aspiration. Based on these findings, contemporary guidelines recommend against routine thrombus aspiration prior to PPCI.25,26 Nevertheless, the absence of benefit with routine use does not necessarily preclude selective application. Instead, the available evidence supports a more judicious approach, particularly in patients with a LTB. In addition, procedural techniques should be optimized to minimize the potential risk of embolic stroke.

Notably, the major RCTs evaluating thrombus aspiration enrolled all-comer STEMI populations and did not specifically require a high thrombus burden for inclusion, leaving uncertainty regarding potential benefits in patients with LTB.23,24 Subgroup analyses from both TASTE and TOTAL similarly failed to demonstrate benefit in patients with LTB defined by TIMI Thrombus Grade criteria (grade 4–5 in TASTE and grade 3–5 in TOTAL).23,27,28 However, these analyses have several limitations. In the TASTE trial, the LTB subgroup represented only about 31% of the overall cohort, limiting statistical power.22,28 In TOTAL, although approximately 84% of participants were categorized as having LTB, thrombus grading was performed before guidewire crossing; notably, 72% were classified as grade 5 thrombus, representing total occlusion. This methodology limited accurate assessment of the true thrombus burden after restoration of flow.28,29

Given these limitations, selective use of thrombus aspiration in patients with confirmed LTB may still be reasonable, particularly after guidewire or balloon crossing clarifies the underlying thrombotic load in cases of complete occlusion on initial angiography. Although the absolute risk remains low, the TOTAL trial and subsequent meta-analyses have raised concern regarding a potential association between thrombus aspiration and stroke. 24,30, 31, 32Some analyses suggest that the increase in stroke events within 48 h may be procedure-related.33 There was also a numerical increase in strokes occurring between 90 and 180 days, including haemorrhagic events; however, these later events could not be clearly attributed to the procedure itself.33 Importantly, the TASTE trial and several meta-analyses did not demonstrate a significant increase in stroke risk.29,34,35

Further insights come from analyses by Yoshinobu Sotomi and colleagues, which suggest that stroke risk following thrombus aspiration may reflect institutional practice patterns rather than patient-specific factors.36 Their findings indicate that the excess stroke risk is largely confined to the periprocedural period (within 7 days of PCI) and does not persist thereafter. These observations imply that careful procedural technique and appropriate periprocedural management may mitigate this risk. From a technical standpoint, strategies such as optimal guide catheter engagement, maintaining aspiration during catheter withdrawal, adequate guide flushing and back-bleeding, and avoiding excessive guide manipulation may help reduce systemic embolization. In addition, registry data indicate that hospitals with higher procedural volumes of thrombus aspiration do not demonstrate a greater incidence of stroke compared with lower-volume centers.37

Future technological innovations should therefore focus on reducing systemic embolization and improving aspiration efficiency. Although additional RCTs in patients with confirmed LTB would help clarify the role of thrombus aspiration, such studies would require very large sample sizes to detect meaningful differences in hard clinical outcomes.9

In nutshell, major trials including TAPAS, TASTE and TOTAL, demonstrated the evolution from early support for routine thrombus aspiration to a more selective strategy in STEMI. Mechanical thrombectomy

Mechanical thrombectomy has been less extensively investigated than manual thrombus aspiration. The AngioJet Rheolytic Thrombectomy in Patients Undergoing Primary Angioplasty for Acute Myocardial Infarction (AiMI) Trial enrolled 480 patients undergoing PPCI and randomized them to routine adjunctive AngioJet rheolytic thrombectomy (Boston Scientific, USA) or PCI alone. In contrast, the JETSTENT Trial evaluated 501 patients with LTB (TIMI thrombus grade 3–5) and compared AngioJet rheolytic thrombectomy before direct infarct-artery stenting with direct stenting alone. Although rheolytic thrombectomy did not significantly improve infarct size or angiographic perfusion parameters, it was associated with lower MACE at 6 months and 1 year.39 Based on the currently available evidence, major guideline do not recommend routine use of rheolytic thrombectomy.25,26

Another emerging mechanical thrombectomy approach is the Indigo CAT RX Aspiration System developed by Penumbra Inc. (Alameda, CA, USA). This system provides continuous aspiration through a larger aspiration port and lumen compared with conventional manual aspiration catheters and operates with a dedicated aspiration pump. Although its use in coronary thrombosis has increased, clinical evidence remains limited. The ROPUST Study, a single-center retrospective analysis of 123 patients treated with the Penumbra system, reported restoration of TIMI 3 flow in 90.2% of cases, compared with 87% of patients presenting with baseline TIMI 0–1 flow.40 Similarly, the CHEETAH Study—a prospective, single-arm, multicenter study including 400 patients with TIMI thrombus grade 4 or 5—demonstrated the feasibility and safety of sustained mechanical thrombectomy. The primary composite endpoint (cardiovascular death, recurrent myocardial infarction, cardiogenic shock, or NYHA class IV heart failure) occurred at a low rate, and a final myocardial blush grade (MBG) 3 was achieved in 99.75% of patients. Importantly, neither study reported device-related serious adverse events.41

The Aspiration, Vasodilators, Imaging, and Stenting (AVIS) protocol evaluated sustained mechanical aspiration thrombectomy using the Indigo™ CAT RX system in 105 ACS patients with LTB undergoing PCI. Most patients initially had TIMI 0 flow, TIMI grade 5 thrombus, and poor MBG. Following thrombectomy, there was significant improvement in TIMI flow, thrombus burden, and MBG. Mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) improved from 38.4% to 44.6% at 6 months. Three deaths occurred within 30 days, none device-related. The study concluded that sustained mechanical aspiration thrombectomy is safe and effective, while the AVIS protocol may optimize myocardial perfusion and procedural success in high-thrombus ACS.42

Penumbra's mechanical aspiration system delivers continuous, controlled suction, enabling more effective removal of LTB compared with manual devices that rely on intermittent aspiration. This mechanism reduces the risk of incomplete thrombus extraction and distal embolization. Its larger-lumen catheters and stronger suction are specifically designed for high thrombus loads, making it advantageous when manual aspiration is ineffective. Unlike operator-dependent manual techniques, the Penumbra system standardizes thrombus removal, thereby improving procedural consistency and reproducibility.43

An alternative strategy involves the nitinol stent-based self-expanding retrieval device Solitaire Stent Retriever (Medtronic, USA), which is widely used in the treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke. Preliminary experience in small patient cohorts suggests that this device can effectively reduce intracoronary thrombus burden and improve TIMI flow in patients with LTB who have not responded to conventional therapies.44

The RETRIEVE AMI study is the first RCT assessing the feasibility and safety of stent retriever thrombectomy (SRT) with the Solitaire X device in STEMI patients with LBT. Baseline thrombus volume was similar across SRT, manual aspiration, and no modification groups. Both strategies reduced thrombus effectively, proportional to the initial burden. No device-related arterial injury was observed, and in-hospital and 6-month major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events rates were comparable. SRT retrieved larger thrombus volumes than manual aspiration. The study also demonstrated discordance between angiographic and optical coherence tomography (OCT) -derived thrombus assessment, highlighting the role of OCT in identifying high thrombus burden and evaluating device performance. These findings support tailored thrombus-modification strategies and encourage future studies integrating intravascular imaging for personalized management (Table 4).45

More recently, Spirito and colleagues reported the first-in-human experience with the EnVast Stent Retrieval Catheter (formerly the NeVa device).46 In a cohort of 61 patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction and LTB, the device demonstrated an excellent safety profile (Table 4). Only one patient experienced side-branch embolization, which was successfully managed with vacuum-assisted aspiration. Approximately 23% of patients developed transient coronary spasm, but no coronary dissections or perforations were observed. Device performance before stenting was encouraging, with fewer than 10% of patients having final TIMI flow <3 and approximately one quarter showing MBG <2.46

The CHEETAH and ROPUST studies, evaluating the Penumbra aspiration system, are primarily feasibility and observational analyses. Although these studies demonstrated high rates of procedural success and favorable angiographic outcomes, their single-arm or retrospective design, lack of control groups, and limited sample sizes preclude definitive conclusions regarding clinical efficacy. Similarly, the RETRIEVE AMI trial represents an important proof-of-concept randomized study of stent retriever thrombectomy; however, it was not powered to detect differences in hard clinical endpoints. The observed equivalence in MACE should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of clinical equivalence or superiority. Consequently, routine use of mechanical thrombectomy cannot currently be recommended (Table 5). Direct stenting

During PPCI in patients with a LTB, distal embolization is a common occurrence and may result in distal epicardial vessel occlusion or microvascular obstruction, ultimately leading to adverse clinical outcomes. Direct stenting has been proposed as a strategy to reduce thrombus manipulation by trapping the thrombus against the vessel wall, thereby potentially limiting distal embolization. Despite this theoretical advantage, direct stenting has notable limitations. These include possible underestimation of true vessel diameter, difficulty in delivering the stent across tight or calcified lesions, risk of incomplete stent expansion, and late malapposition—all of which may predispose to restenosis or stent thrombosis.

Clinical evidence remains mixed. A meta-analysis of five small randomized trials (n = 754) demonstrated improved ST-segment resolution and lower in-hospital cardiovascular mortality with direct stenting, without a significant impact on target lesion revascularization.47 Conversely, a large propensity-matched study involving 10,944 patients from major thrombus aspiration trials showed that, although direct stenting was more frequently performed in patients undergoing thrombectomy, it did not improve myocardial perfusion or clinical outcomes at 30 days or one year.48 Reassuringly, no increased risk of stent thrombosis or target vessel revascularization (TVR) was observed. In view of these findings, the role of direct stenting remains uncertain, and its use should be individualized based on lesion morphology and procedural feasibility. It is indicated in soft thrombus or lesions with minimal calcification and less vessel tortuosity. While thrombus aspiration may aid in facilitating direct stenting in selected cases, it should not be undertaken solely for this purpose.

Deferred stenting

Deferred stenting has been proposed as a strategy to limit distal embolization and reduce the risk of no-reflow during PPCI. The concept involves postponing stent implantation after initial reperfusion to allow partial thrombus dissolution and recovery of the microcirculation.

Early evidence from the DEFER-STEMI trial, which enrolled 101 selected STEMI patients with restored TIMI 3 flow but high risk of no-reflow, suggested potential benefits. Stent implantation deferred by approximately 9 h was associated with lower rates of no-/slow-reflow, fewer procedural thrombotic complications, and improved myocardial salvage at 6 months.49 However, larger and more contemporary studies have not confirmed these advantages. The DANAMI 3-DEFER trial, including 1215 patients randomized irrespective of risk profile, used a longer deferral interval (median ∼3 days) and showed no improvement in clinical outcomes compared with immediate stenting.50 Similarly, smaller randomized studies such as MIMI51 and INNOVATION52 did not demonstrate consistent reductions in infarct size or microvascular obstruction, although a subgroup benefit in anterior myocardial infarction was noted in INNOVATION. Both DEFER-STEMI and DANAMI 3-DEFER also reported a small but relevant risk of re-occlusion prior to the staged procedure (Table 5).49,50

The PRIMACY (Primary Reperfusion Secondary Stenting) trial investigated whether deferring stent implantation after initial reperfusion could improve outcomes by allowing thrombus resolution and reducing distal embolization. Although deferred stenting was associated with favorable procedural characteristics, the trial failed to demonstrate significant reductions in microvascular obstruction, infarct size, or major adverse cardiovascular events compared with immediate stenting. These findings did not support the routine use of deferred stenting during PPCI.53 Qiao et al pooled data from nine studies involving patients with STEMI undergoing PPCI, many of whom had LBT. Compared with immediate stenting, deferred stenting was associated with improved LVEF and a lower risk of no-/slow-reflow. However, it did not significantly reduce mortality, reinfarction, TVR, or MACE. These findings suggest that while deferred stenting may improve myocardial reperfusion, it has not demonstrated a clear benefit in hard clinical outcomes (Table 5)54. Another metanalysis evaluated four randomized trials involving 1570 STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI, many with substantial thrombus burden. Deferred stenting was associated with lower rates of no-/slow-reflow and improved myocardial blush grade, suggesting better microvascular perfusion. However, these angiographic benefits did not translate into significant reductions in mortality, reinfarction, target-vessel revascularization, or major adverse cardiovascular events. The findings indicate that while deferred stenting may improve procedural outcomes, evidence supporting a meaningful clinical benefit remains insufficient (Table 5).55

Contemporary guideline recommendations do not support routine deferred stenting in patients undergoing PPCI. The 2023 ESC ACS Guidelines assign a Class III (No Benefit), Level of Evidence A recommendation against routine deferred stenting after restoration of coronary flow, based on the lack of demonstrated clinical benefit in RCTs.14 Similarly, the 2025 ACC/AHA ACS Guidelines do not recommend routine deferred stenting, citing the failure of major randomized studies to show improvements in hard clinical outcomes.15 However, both guidelines acknowledge that a selective deferred stenting strategy may be considered in highly selected patients with persistent large thrombus burden despite restoration of TIMI 3 flow, where concerns regarding distal embolization, no-reflow, or stent malapposition remain.

Adjunctive strategies

In addition to established pharmacological and mechanical approaches, several adjunctive techniques have been employed in selected patients with large thrombus burden or no-reflow during primary PCI, although robust validation remains limited. These include thrombus pulsation/thrombo-pulsing techniques and the use of intracoronary vasodilators such as nicorandil, adenosine, nitroprusside, verapamil, diltiazem, and epinephrine to improve microvascular perfusion and mitigate no-reflow. Recent evidence from a contemporary network meta-analysis suggested that intracoronary verapamil and epinephrine may improve final TIMI 3 flow, while emerging studies with nicorandil continue to demonstrate potential benefits in reducing microvascular dysfunction and no-reflow after primary PCI.55, 56 However, current evidence remains insufficient to support routine use, and these strategies should be individualized according to procedural and hemodynamic circumstances.

Conclusion

Intracoronary thrombus remains a major procedural challenge during PPCI, necessitating a systematic yet flexible management strategy. The initial step involves careful assessment of thrombus burden using angiographic features and the TIMI thrombus grading system, which helps guide therapeutic decision-making. Management should be according to thrombus burden, lesion morphology, coronary flow, and clinical context. In patients with a large thrombus burden, selective use of mechanical thrombus-reduction strategies, including aspiration thrombectomy or other thrombectomy techniques, may be considered. Pharmacological therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, comprising DAPT with potent P2Y12 inhibitors and appropriate anticoagulation, while intravenous or intracoronary GPI may be reserved for bailout situations such as large residual thrombus, slow-flow/no-reflow, or distal embolization. ICT may represent a therapeutic option in carefully selected patients with persistent thrombus despite conventional measures. Stent implantation strategy is equally important; direct stenting may minimize thrombus disruption in suitable lesions, whereas deferred stenting may be considered in highly selected patients with substantial residual thrombus following restoration of adequate coronary flow (TIMI ≥2, preferably TIMI 3). Importantly, much of the available evidence supporting these strategies is derived from heterogeneous studies with limited statistical power, and therefore many contemporary approaches should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than guideline-directing.
