The effect of excess body weight on short-term outcomes following minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: J. G. Chen, Y. Zhao, T. S. Koh, M. I. B. I. Lim, J. H. Tan, I. Seow-En
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe converging trends of a global increase in the incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) and the prevalence of obesity suggest that surgery for CRC in patients with excess body weight (EBW) will become an increasingly common clinical entity. We aimed to evaluate the effect of EBW, defined as being overweight or obese, on short-term perioperative outcomes following minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery (MIRCS).Materials and methodsThe review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched for relevant studies. Only comparative studies comparing MIRCS in patients with vs without EBW were included. Studies evaluating open rectal cancer surgery were excluded. This review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024591252).ResultsTwenty-six retrospective cohort studies involving 7172 patients were analysed. Patients with EBW had greater rates of open conversion (risk ratio (RR) 1.64, 95% CI 1.19–2.26), longer operative durations (mean difference (MD) 20.81 min, 95% CI 13.31–28.30), increased postoperative morbidity (RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.24), and higher readmission rates (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.04–1.66) compared to patients without EBW undergoing MIRCS. No significant differences were found for length of hospital stay, intraoperative blood loss, surgical site infection, anastomotic leakage, gastrointestinal recovery, mortality, circumferential resection margin positivity, or lymph node harvest.ConclusionOur review indicates a moderate increase in technical complexity and perioperative risk among patients with EBW undergoing MIRCS. These findings underscore the need for careful preoperative risk stratification and optimisation in this higher-risk patient cohort.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10151-026-03358-w.

Key Summary Points?

Evidence on the impact of excess body weight in minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery remains limited. This meta-analysis is the first to compare short-term outcomes between patients with EBW and patients without EBW undergoing MIRCS across laparoscopic and robotic platforms, incorporating both BMI-based and imaging-derived measures of adiposity to better reflect operative complexity.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide, with incidence rates having nearly doubled since 1990 [1]. Over the same period, the global prevalence of obesity has risen sharply, with most regions reporting sustained increases from 1990 to 2022 [2]. Obesity is a well-established risk factor for CRC. In a 2021 meta-analysis of 84 studies including over 52 million individuals, Zhang et al. reported a 30% higher risk of CRC among obese individuals and a 14% incremental risk of CRC for each 5 kg/m2 increase in body mass index (BMI) [3]. These converging epidemiological trends suggest that CRC in patients with obesity will become an increasingly common clinical entity, underscoring the need to better understand its implications for surgical management and patient outcomes.

Surgery for rectal cancer differs from that for colon cancer because of the need for rectal mobilisation along the mesorectal fascia. This procedure can be technically demanding because of the restricted operating space deep within the confines of the bony pelvis, with the risk of rectal perforation or injury to other critical structures in close proximity. Operative challenges are further amplified in the presence of a bulky or locally advanced tumour, a narrow male pelvis, as well as prior neoadjuvant radiation.

Obesity has been consistently associated with increased postoperative morbidity following rectal cancer surgery [4, 5]. Excess visceral adiposity and a thickened mesorectum further reduce pelvic working space and can result in altered dissection planes or suboptimal operative visualisation. Moreover, obesity has been recognised as an independent risk factor for anastomotic leakage and surgical site infections after rectal cancer resection [4, 6, 7], possibly reflecting the interplay between increased technical difficulty and obesity-related metabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, which may adversely affect tissue perfusion and wound healing.

Minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery (MIRCS) offers tissue magnification and enhanced visualisation, enabling precise dissection within the narrow pelvic space [8]. Compared with open surgery, laparoscopic or robotic-assisted rectal resections are associated with decreased blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and faster postoperative gastrointestinal recovery, albeit at the expense of longer operative times [9, 10]. A 2022 meta-analysis by Kong et al., including 12 randomised controlled trials with 3709 patients, demonstrated significantly improved overall survival following laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery compared with open surgery [11]. MIRCS may therefore confer both short-term perioperative and long-term oncological advantages, contributing to its increasing worldwide adoption. Nonetheless, MIRCS is technically demanding and associated with a substantial learning curve, particularly in anatomically challenging or high-risk patient populations [8].

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we evaluate the effect of excess body weight (EBW) on short-term perioperative outcomes following MIRCS, focusing specifically on technical complexity, postoperative morbidity, and early recovery. EBW was conceptualised as an umbrella construct encompassing both BMI-based definitions of overweight and obesity and imaging-derived measures of visceral or mesorectal adiposity, recognising that central adiposity may more directly reflect operative difficulty during rectal cancer surgery. This approach distinguishes the present analysis from prior meta-analyses that relied exclusively on BMI.

Methods

Literature search

This systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [12]. The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (PROSPERO Registration Number CRD42024591252; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024591252). Two reviewers (JGC, YZ) conducted independent searches on electronic databases PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library to identify eligible studies. The databases were searched from their respective dates of inception to 31 May 2025. Grey literature, conference abstracts and pre-prints were excluded. The full electronic search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Eligibility criteria

Comparative studies comparing outcomes of patients with EBW and patients without EBW undergoing MIRCS were included. Studies defining EBW using imaging-based thresholds for visceral, mesorectal, or perirenal fat area were included alongside BMI-based definitions, as these measures more directly reflect central adiposity and operative difficulty.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) non-comparative design; (2) inclusion of colon cancer without separate reporting of rectal cancer outcomes; (3) inclusion of open rectal cancer surgery; (4) non-human studies; (5) inclusion of patients aged < 18 years; or (6) publication in a non-English language.

Selection of studies and data collection

Records retrieved from the electronic databases were downloaded and imported into PICO Portal, an online systematic review management and data extraction platform [13]. Following deduplication, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers, after which full-text screening was performed based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies in study selection or data extraction were resolved by consensus discussion between reviewers.

The following data were extracted from eligible studies: (1) study characteristics (author, study period, country); (2) participant characteristics (age, BMI, sex); (3) intraoperative outcomes (operative duration, estimated blood loss, conversion to open surgery, circumferential resection margin positivity, lymph node harvest); and (4) postoperative outcomes (length of hospital stay, surgical site infection (SSI), anastomotic leakage, time to first flatus, time to resumption of oral intake, overall postoperative morbidity, short-term postoperative mortality, and readmission). Where outcomes were reported as medians with ranges or interquartile ranges, values were converted to means and standard deviations using established methods described by Wan et al. [14]. Where necessary, group-level means and standard deviations were combined using formulas recommended in the Cochrane Handbook [15].

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were conversion to open surgery and postoperative length of hospital stay. Secondary outcomes included operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, overall postoperative morbidity, anastomotic leakage, surgical site infection, readmission, short-term postoperative mortality, circumferential resection margin (CRM) positivity, and lymph node harvest. Overall postoperative morbidity was defined as the occurrence of any recorded postoperative complication as reported by individual studies. Standardised stratification by Clavien–Dindo grade was not consistently feasible owing to heterogeneous reporting, and outcome definitions (including morbidity, gastrointestinal recovery, and anastomotic leakage) were analysed as reported by the original studies.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) software (Cochrane Collaboration). Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for dichotomous outcomes using the Mantel–Haenszel random-effects model [16], accounting for between-study variability. Continuous outcomes were analysed using mean differences (MDs) with corresponding 95% CIs. Prespecified subgroup analyses for the primary outcomes were performed in patients with obesity, defined according to the World Health Organization criterion of BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [17].

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Risk of bias and study quality were independently assessed by two reviewers (JGC, YZ) using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies [18]. Studies were considered representative of the exposed cohort if participants were drawn from national or regional datasets. Adequate follow-up was defined as a minimum of 28 days after hospital discharge; studies that did not report follow-up duration or adequacy were rated as not stated (NS) for that domain. Overall study quality was categorised as high (NOS score 7–9), moderate (score 5–6), or low (score < 5). Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s test. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic, with heterogeneity interpreted according to conventional thresholds (I2 0–40%, low; 30–60%, moderate; 50–90%, substantial; 75–100%, considerable).

EBW, encompassing overweight and obesity, was operationalised heterogeneously across studies. Eleven studies [19–26, 28, 33, 35] defined EBW as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, two studies [37, 38] used BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2, and seven studies [27, 29–32, 34, 36] used BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2.

Several studies used imaging-derived adiposity metrics. Magnetic resonance imaging-quantified mesorectal fat area cut-offs were > 20 cm2 (Felsenreich et al. [39]), > 19.7 cm2 (Shioi et al. [40]), and > 26 cm2 (Yamaoka et al. [41]). Computed tomography-quantified visceral fat thresholds included > 5.36 L (Baastrup et al. [42]) and > 130 cm2 (Shiomi et al. [43]). Levic et al. [44] used perirenal fat area (> 24 cm2) as a surrogate measure of visceral obesity.

Ten studies [20, 21, 23–25, 29, 37, 39–41] included robotic rectal cancer surgery, while 14 studies [19, 22, 26–28, 30–36, 42, 44] analysed laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery. Two studies, Zhao et al. [38] and Shiomi et al. [43], evaluated both robotic and laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery, with the data for the laparoscopic and robotic subgroups presented separately. These studies were stratified into robotic surgery (RS) and laparoscopic surgery (LS) subgroups when displayed in the forest plots.

Risk of bias of included studies

On the basis of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, all studies were rated as having moderate or high methodological quality (score ≥ 5 of 9), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1; however, the observational nature of the included studies introduces inherent risks of residual confounding and selection bias. Common limitations included retrospective study design, heterogeneous definitions of EBW, limited adjustment for confounding variables in some cohorts, and variable or short follow-up durations for postoperative outcomes.

Funnel plots demonstrated no evidence of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. 2), although potential outcome-specific bias cannot be excluded.

Conversion to open surgery

Sixteen studies [19–21, 23–25, 28, 29, 32, 33, 35, 36, 38, 42–44], including 5329 patients, reported conversion to open surgery. EBW was associated with a significantly higher risk of conversion compared with patients without EBW undergoing MIRCS (RR 1.64, 95% CI 1.19–2.26; I2 = 26%).

Several studies attributed higher conversion rates in patients with EBW to impaired pelvic exposure and increased technical difficulty related to excess visceral and mesorectal adiposity, including multilayering of bowel loops, thicker adipose tissue, and restricted instrument manoeuvrability within the narrow pelvis [19, 42].

Paradoxically, Levic et al. [44] reported that EBW, defined by an increased perirenal fat area (PFA), was associated with lower conversion rates to open surgery, as well as comparable perioperative morbidity. The authors attributed these discrepancies to heterogeneity in methods of assessing PFA and variability in surgical approaches across prior studies, but also questioned the utility of PFA as a surrogate marker for visceral obesity and a predictor of outcomes after laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery.

Postoperative length of stay

Pooled analysis of 22 studies [20, 21, 23–33, 35–39, 41–44], including 6528 patients, demonstrated no significant difference in postoperative length of stay (LOS) between patients with EBW and patients without EBW (MD 0.41 days, 95% CI − 0.01 to 0.83; I2 = 80%). Given the substantial heterogeneity, LOS findings should be interpreted cautiously and likely reflect variability in discharge practices, perioperative pathways, and institutional protocols across studies.

Surgical duration

Twenty-one studies [20, 21, 23–27, 29–39, 41, 43, 44] encompassing 6093 patients evaluated operative duration for MIRCS. Patients with EBW had significantly longer operative durations than patients without EBW (MD 20.81 min, 95% CI 13.31–28.30; I2 = 85%). Nineteen of 21 studies reported longer operative times in EBW cohorts.

Intraoperative blood loss

Analysis of 17 studies [20, 23–27, 30–32, 34–36, 38, 41–44] reporting this outcome across 4610 patients found no significant differences in intraoperative blood loss (MD 4.86 ml, 95% CI − 2.51, 12.24; I2 = 86%).

Overall postoperative morbidity

Pooled analysis of 6444 patients across 21 studies [19–21, 23–36, 38, 39, 42, 44] demonstrated a higher rate of postoperative complications among patients with EBW following MIRCS (RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03, 1.24; I2 = 6%). Six out of the 21 studies documented 30-day complications [20, 21, 25, 26, 28, 42] while others did not specify the duration during which the complications occurred [19, 23, 24, 27, 29–36, 38, 39, 44].

Duchalais et al. [21] reported a 50% higher overall postoperative complication rate in patients with EBW undergoing MIRCS compared with their non-EBW counterparts. This excess morbidity was driven predominantly by postoperative ileus, which accounted for close to half of all complications. Bege et al. [19] observed that the increased postoperative morbidity among patients with EBW was largely comprised of minor Clavien–Dindo grade I events [45]. For complications of Clavien–Dindo grade II and above, no significant differences were detected between the EBW and non-EBW cohorts [19].

Surgical site infections (SSI)

Meta-analysis of 17 studies [19–23, 25–28, 30–37] reporting the incidence of SSIs, comprising 3620 patients, found no significant difference among the rates of postoperative SSIs in patients with EBW versus patients without EBW (RR 1.44, 95% CI 0.99, 2.09; I2 = 0%).

Anastomotic leakage

Pooled analysis of 4961 patients across 20 studies [20–27, 30–38, 40, 42, 44] showed no significant difference in rates of postoperative anastomotic leak among patients with EBW (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.91, 1.39; I2 = 0%).

Postoperative gastrointestinal recovery

Postoperative gastrointestinal recovery metrics appear comparable between patients with EBW and patients without EBW undergoing MIRCS. Pooled analysis of five studies [20, 24, 26, 32, 37] with 819 patients found no significant difference in time to first flatus (MD 0.19 days; 95% CI − 0.11, 0.49; I2 = 28%). Likewise, pooled analysis of four studies [26, 30, 32, 37] with 738 patients reported no significant difference in time to resumption of oral intake (MD − 0.18 days; 95% CI − 0.55, 0.20; I2 = 0%). These findings were consistent across all contributing cohorts for both the endpoints of time to flatus and commencement of oral intake. Nonetheless, reporting of gastrointestinal recovery endpoints was inconsistent across studies. For example, Duchalais et al. [21] documented a substantial incidence of postoperative ileus among patients with EBW but did not provide standardised measures of gastrointestinal recovery such as time to flatus or tolerance of oral intake.

Readmissions

Six studies [21, 23, 25, 39, 42, 44] with 1785 patients reported readmission rates following MIRCS. Pooled analysis demonstrated that patients with EBW had a higher readmission rate compared to patients without EBW (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.04, 1.66; I2 = 0%). However, Hellan et al. [23] was the only individual study showing a significantly higher readmission rate among patients with EBW, at almost double that of patients without EBW (18.3% vs 9.4%, p = 0.016).

Short-term postoperative mortality

Ten studies [19, 23, 26, 28, 32, 33, 35, 39, 42, 44] reported postoperative mortality rates among 2393 patients following MIRCS. Pooled analysis found no significant difference in mortality between patients with EBW and patients without EBW (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.50, 1.73; I2 = 0%). Three studies documented 30-day mortality [23, 26, 44], one recorded 90-day mortality [42], and others did not specify the duration during which mortality was recorded [19, 28, 32, 33, 35, 39].

Circumferential resection margin positivity

Pooled analysis of 4675 patients from 11 studies [20, 21, 23–26, 29, 32, 36, 38, 39] showed no significant differences in rates of positive circumferential resection margin between patients with EBW and patients without EBW (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.80, 1.52; I2 = 0%).

Lymph node harvest

Pooled analysis of 5873 patients from 19 studies [20, 21, 23–27, 29, 30, 32, 33, 35, 36, 38, 39, 41–44] demonstrated no significant differences in the number of retrieved lymph nodes between patients with EBW and patients without EBW (MD − 0.28, 95% CI − 1.02, 0.46; I2 = 78%).

Conversion to open surgery

Pooled analysis of nine studies [19–21, 23–25, 28, 33, 35], including 2169 patients, showed a significantly increased rate of conversion to open surgery in patients with obesity compared with patients without obesity (RR 1.84, 95% CI 1.22–2.76; I2 = 23%).

Length of hospital stay

Pooled analysis of nine studies [20, 21, 23–26, 28, 33, 35], including 2315 patients, showed no significant difference in postoperative length of stay observed (MD 0.38 days, 95% CI − 0.21 to 0.97; I2 = 50%).

Subgroup analyses of secondary outcomes were broadly consistent with the primary EBW analysis. However, surgical site infection rates were higher among patients with obesity (RR 2.07, 95% CI 1.20–3.57; I2 = 24%), and intraoperative blood loss was greater (MD 30.10 mL, 95% CI 9.30–50.91; I2 = 50%).

Discussion

Obesity has emerged as a major global health challenge approaching pandemic proportions, with its prevalence having more than tripled worldwide over the past 5 decades [46, 47]. Recent analyses from the Global Burden of Disease Study indicate that this rise spans all age groups, regardless of ethnic or socioeconomic strata [48]. Among adults, the World Health Organization (WHO) defines “excess body weight” as overweight or obesity by BMI, i.e. BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 [49]. However, BMI has limitations as a proxy for adiposity, with waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio being used instead as a measure of abdominal or central obesity [50]. EBW may also be proxied by CT-quantified visceral fat area, with cut-offs and thresholds varying by cohort [6, 51].

Operative conversion is a surrogate measure of intraoperative technical difficulty. A 2016 meta-analysis by Qiu et al., including 14 489 patients across 10 studies, evaluated the outcomes rectal cancer surgery in patients with obesity versus patients without obesity [4]. However, few individual studies evaluated exclusively MIRCS, with only four studies reporting conversion rate; pooled analysis of these showed a significantly higher conversion rate among patients with obesity [4]. A 2017 meta-analysis of 43 studies including a mix of both laparoscopic colon and rectal surgery also demonstrated a higher incidence of conversion to open surgery with higher BMI [52].

Our meta-analysis reveals significantly higher operative conversion, postoperative morbidity and readmission rates, as well as a longer operative duration, among patients with EBW undergoing MIRCS. While the observed increase in operative time of approximately 20 min on average is modest and unlikely to be clinically consequential for individual patients, it likely reflects greater technical complexity and cumulative resource utilisation at the health-system level, particularly in high-volume centres.

Postoperative length of stay, operative blood loss, gastrointestinal recovery, rates of SSI, anastomotic leak, and mortality, as well as circumferential resection margin positivity rates and lymph node yield were comparable to patients without EBW. Subgroup analyses of patients with obesity, using a BMI cut-off of ≥ 30 kg/m2 showed consistent results with patients with EBW with regard to the primary outcomes of conversion and hospital length of stay.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed for several continuous secondary outcomes, including operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative length of stay, and lymph node harvest. This variability likely reflects differences in surgical platform (robotic versus laparoscopic), case mix, definitions of EBW, and perioperative protocols across institutions. In contrast, heterogeneity was low or absent for most dichotomous outcomes, including conversion to open surgery, postoperative morbidity, anastomotic leakage, surgical site infection, and mortality, supporting the robustness of these pooled estimates.

Taken together, these findings suggest that EBW is associated with a moderate, rather than prohibitive, increase in technical difficulty and perioperative risk in MIRCS. Although relative risks for conversion and readmission were higher among patients with EBW, the absolute differences across most outcomes were modest, and serious adverse events, including anastomotic leakage and mortality, were not increased. From a practical standpoint, these results are most relevant for preoperative counselling, operative planning, and resource allocation, rather than as a basis for excluding patients with EBW from minimally invasive approaches.

Excess intraabdominal adiposity promotes ‘layering’ of small bowel loops within the pelvis, whereby a heavy mesentery causes bowel loops to stack over the operative field, limiting exposure during MIRCS. A bulky mesorectum in patients with EBW also reduces effective working space in the fixed bony pelvis and may obscure operative planes. Moreover, dissection of a fatty mesentery produces more smoke and oozing, further impairing visibility. In addition, EBW limits patient tolerance of a steep Trendelenburg position and higher pneumoperitoneal pressures, which are often applied to mitigate these intraoperative challenges.

A 2025 meta-analysis by Zhang, including seven studies with 1334 patients undergoing robotic-assisted rectal cancer surgery, showed no statistical difference in conversion rate, length of hospital stays, and overall complications between patients with obesity and patients without obesity [53]. This may reflect a potential mitigating effect of the robotic platform on obesity-related technical challenges; however, available evidence remains limited and does not demonstrate elimination of obesity-associated operative risk. Long-term oncologic outcomes, including disease-free and overall survival in patients with EBW undergoing MIRCS, remain insufficiently studied and warrant dedicated prospective investigation.

Notably, although postoperative morbidity and readmission rates were significantly higher among patients with EBW, mortality, anastomotic leak, and surgical site infection rates were comparable to those in patients without EBW. This suggests that complications occurring in patients with EBW were predominantly minor (e.g. postoperative ileus) or non-technical in nature, potentially reflecting a higher burden of underlying comorbid conditions rather than technical failure. Consistent with this, surgeons in the included studies adopted additional intraoperative strategies to mitigate the technical challenges associated with greater adiposity, including conversion to open surgery to improve exposure and tissue visualisation.

Our review is constrained by the use of BMI as an imprecise surrogate for EBW and by heterogeneity in the assessment of visceral/abdominal adiposity, namely, non-standardised measurement methods and variable cut-points. These heterogeneous definitions reflect the current literature and may introduce measurement variability, although pooled effect directions remained consistent across studies. Asian populations have also been found to have increased visceral fat storage at the same BMI values compared to other ethnicities, with greater health issues at lower BMI levels [54–57].

Notwithstanding the heterogeneity in the operationalisation of EBW and the observational design of the included cohorts, the inclusion of studies employing diverse definitions across multiple surgical units and geographic regions likely enhances the robustness of our findings and strengthens their external validity and generalisability. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to compare outcomes between patients with EBW and patients without EBW undergoing MIRCS across both laparoscopic and robot-assisted approaches. In addition, laparoscopic and robotic approaches were analysed together under the umbrella of MIRCS. While this reflects contemporary clinical practice, the robotic platform may partially mitigate obesity-related technical challenges. As such, platform-specific effects may have been diluted in pooled analyses, and the available data were insufficient to permit a definitive comparison between laparoscopic and robotic approaches in patients with EBW.

Future studies should prioritise prospective registries with standardised body-composition metrics and harmonised definitions of postoperative morbidity to better delineate risk stratification strategies in patients with EBW undergoing MIRCS.

Conclusion

Our review indicates moderately increased technical complexity and a modestly less favourable perioperative risk profile among patients with EBW undergoing MIRCS. While EBW was associated with higher rates of conversion, postoperative morbidity, and readmission, major adverse outcomes, including anastomotic leakage and mortality, were not increased. These findings underscore the need for careful preoperative risk stratification, optimisation, and judicious operative planning when performing MIRCS in patients with EBW.
