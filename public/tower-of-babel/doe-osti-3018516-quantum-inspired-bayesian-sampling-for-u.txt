Abstract:

With increasing simulation and measurement data, machine learning and artificial intelligence
have been widely used in computational decision-making of complex engineering systems. The
resulting tools, such as uncertainty quantification solvers, reinforcement learning, and physics-
informed machine learning, have achieved great success in critical DOE tasks such as material
discovery and design, energy system modeling and control, and numerical weather and climate
prediction. A core topic in scientific machine learning and artificial intelligence is Bayesian
inference: given an observed data set, people want to estimate the posterior distribution of a
(possibly large) number of hidden parameters.

Due to the flexibility and weak assumptions, Bayesian sampling has been the mainstream Bayesian
inference solvers despite the rapid progress of approximate Bayesian inference. Classical Bayesian
sampling methods such as Markov-chain Monte Carlo suffer from a low-acceptance rate due to
the random walk nature, therefore state-of-the-art techniques use Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and its
variants to efficiently draw posterior samples in a high dimension. The key idea of Hamiltonian
Monte Carlo and its variants is to simulate the Hamiltonian dynamics of a classical particle with a
fixed mass, and their performance significantly degrades when the posterior distribution is highly
spiky or has multiple modes.

Leveraging the idea of quantum physics, this project has investigated new theory, algorithms and
applications of Bayesian inference (especially Bayesian sampling). The main results include: (1)
novel quantum-inspired Bayesian sampling methods that can lead to better accuracy for
challenging multi-modal or spiky distributions, (2) more scalable machine learning framework
leveraging tensor-compressed Bayesian inference, and (3) Bayesian and sampling approaches for
verifying the robustness of continuous and binary neural networks.

 Main results

In this DOE sponsored project, we have published research results around three main directions:
(1) fundamental theory and algorithms of novel Bayesian sampling, (2) scalable Bayesian
inference based on low-rank tensor approximation, (3) neural network robustness verification via
Bayesian and sampling methods. The main results of each direction are summarized below.

1. Fundamental Theory and Algorithms.

In this direction, we have mainly asked two questions: (1) how can we improve the accuracy of
Bayesian sampling when drawing samples from challenging multi-modal and spiky distributions?
(2) how can we shrink the number of samples and use a minimal number of samples to characterize
the targeted distributions?

1.1 Quantum-inspired Bayesian Sampling

Figure 1: (a)-(c) traditional HMC sampling results with different mass parameters. (d)-(f) our QHMC
sampling method with a stochastic and varying mass parameter.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) is an efficient Bayesian sampling method that can make distant
proposals in the parameter space by simulating a Hamiltonian dynamical system. Despite its
popularity in machine learning and data science, HMC is inefficient to sample from spiky and
multimodal distributions. Motivated by the energy-time uncertainty relation from quantum
mechanics, we have proposed a Quantum-Inspired Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm (QHMC)

in [1]. This algorithm allows a particle to have a random mass matrix with a probability distribution
rather than a fixed mass. We have further proved the convergence property of QHMC and further
show why such a random mass can improve the performance when we sample a broad class of
distributions. In order to handle the big training data sets in large-scale machine learning, we have
further developed a stochastic gradient version of QHMC using Nosé-Hoover thermostat called
QSGNHT, and we also provided theoretical justifications about its steady-state distributions.

 In the experiments, we have demonstrated the effectiveness of QHMC and QSGNHT on
synthetic examples, bridge regression, image denoising and neural network pruning. The proposed
QHMC and QSGNHT can indeed achieve much more stable and accurate sampling results on the
test cases. Fig. 1 has shown one demonstrating example with very spiky distributions. For
traditional HMC sampling methods, one has to carefully tune the mass parameters, and it is very
challenging to capture the whole distribution. Instead, with our QHMC method, the algorithm can
make the mass parameters random and adaptive, enabling accurate sampling from the distribution
function.

1.2. Online Thinning of Bayesian Samplers

 Figure 2: Our online thinning approach for MCMC-type Bayesian samplers.

A fundamental challenge in Bayesian inference is efficient representation of a target distribution.
Many nonparametric approaches do so by sampling a large number of points using variants of
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). In [2], we have proposed an MCMC variant that retains only
those posterior samples which exceed a kernelized Stein discrepancy (KSD) threshold, which we
call KSD thinning. As shown in Fig. 2, this method can identify the bad samples on the fly, and
remove them to reduce the sample size without influencing the quality of sample distributions.

 We have also established the convergence and complexity trade-offs for KSD thinning as
a function of the KSD threshold parameter, sample size, and other problem parameters. We have
also provided experimental comparisons against other online nonparametric Bayesian methods

that generate low-complexity posterior representations. We have observed superior
consistency/complexity trade-offs across a range of settings including MCMC sampling on two
Bayesian inference problems from the biological sciences, and 10 × inference speedup and storage
reduction for Bayesian neural networks with no loss of accuracy and no increase in training time.

2. Scalable Bayesian Machine Learning via Low-Rank Tensor Approximation.

A major challenge in many machine learning tasks is that the model expressive power depends on
model size. Meanwhile, as the model size increases, it is increasingly difficult to quantify model
uncertainties. To address this challenge, we have utilized low-rank tensor approximation to reduce
the cost of large-scale Bayesian machine learning, for both sampling-based and variaitonal
inference solvers.

2.1 Sampling-Based Low-Rank Bayesian Solvers.

In [3], we first proposed a unified Bayesian framework for low-rank tensor learning that addresses
three central challenges: automatic rank determination, scalability to large datasets, and
uncertainty quantification. Instead of fixing tensor ranks and optimizing parameters
deterministically, our method placed structured shrinkage priors over tensor factors and rank-
controlling hyper-parameters so that unnecessary components are automatically driven toward
zero during training. The framework is highly general and applies to multiple tensor formats—
including CP, Tucker, and tensor-train decompositions—and to diverse tasks such as tensor
completion, regression, and tensorized neural networks for classification. To perform inference
efficiently in large-scale settings, we have developed stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(SGHMC) and Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) solvers, enabling approximate
posterior sampling without requiring restrictive mean-field assumptions.

 Experimentally, the method has demonstrated strong performance in both tensor
completion and tensorized neural network tasks. On synthetic and MRI tensor completion
problems, our method more accurately recovers the true tensor rank and achieves lower
reconstruction error than prior mean-field Bayesian approaches, which often underestimate rank.
In neural network experiments on Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10, the Bayesian tensorized
models achieve substantial compression ratios—up to roughly 100× or more—while maintaining
competitive or improved predictive performance compared to maximum-a-posteriori training.
Importantly, the Bayesian formulation improves predictive log-likelihood, indicating better
uncertainty calibration. Overall, rank-adaptive tensor compression and uncertainty-aware learning
was achieved simultaneously within a scalable, general-purpose Bayesian framework.

2.2. Variational Inference-Based Low-Rank Tensor Machine Learning

While the above tensor-based Bayesian sampling can greatly reduce the computational cost of
uncertainty quantification for machine learning models, it still cannot handle large-size neural
network models with billions of model parameters. To address this challenge, in [4] we have
proposed a stochastic variational inference framework for rank-adaptive, end-to-end tensorized
training. By coupling low-rank tensor approximation with a customized stochastic variational

inference scheme featuring an analytical/numerical hybrid update, we have overcome the
instability and scalability challenges that arise in nonlinear tensorized networks.

 Table 1: Compressed training results on various neural network benchmarks.

 As shown in Table 1, extensive experiments demonstrate that our method achieves
orders-of-magnitude parameter reduction while maintaining, and in some cases improving,
predictive accuracy. On benchmarks ranging from hundreds of thousands to billions of
parameters, our framework consistently reduces both training and inference variables. Notably,
on a large-scale deep learning recommendation model with 4.25 billion parameters, our method
automatically reduces the model to approximately 1.6 × 10⁵ parameters—a 26,000× reduction—
while preserving nearly the same accuracy. Compared with fixed-rank tensorized training and
post-training compression, our rank-adaptive Bayesian approach achieves superior compression–
accuracy tradeoffs and avoids repeated retraining. Beyond compactness, the Bayesian
formulation also provides uncertainty quantification, positioning this framework as a practical
and principled solution for energy-efficient and resource-constrained neural network training.

3. Bayesian and Sampling Solvers for Neural Network Verification.

Deep neural networks are vulnerable to small input perturbations that can lead to incorrect or
dangerous predictions. This vulnerability is especially problematic in safety-critical applications,
including autonomous driving, healthcare, and finance. Consequently, robustness verification—
certifying correctness under bounded input perturbations—is essential for trustworthy AI systems.
In this project, leveraging the developed Bayesian and sampling solvers, we have developed new
tools for verifying the robustness of both continuous and binary neural networks.

3.1. Bayesian Inference with Certifiable Adversarial Robustness
In [5], we developed a principled Bayesian framework for adversarially robust training of Bayesian
neural networks (BNNs) with formal certification guarantees. Rather than approximating worst-
case perturbations via gradient-based attacks, we reformulated adversarial robustness directly at
the level of the likelihood. We introduced a robust likelihood that marginalizes the softmax output
over worst-case perturbations within an ε-ball around each input, thereby embedding adversarial

robustness into Bayesian posterior inference itself. Because exact computation of worst-case
perturbations is intractable, we derived certified lower bounds using Interval Bound Propagation
(IBP), yielding a tractable and differentiable objective. Crucially, this modification integrates
naturally with common approximate inference methods—including HMC, Bayes by Backprop,
SWAG, NoisyAdam, and VOGN—allowing robust training without abandoning standard
Bayesian machinery. The resulting procedure preserves Bayesian coherence while explicitly
encouraging the posterior to concentrate on robust regions of parameter space.

 We have shown that our method enables the first non-trivial certified robustness guarantees
for BNNs on MNIST, FashionMNIST, and CIFAR-10. Training with the IBP-based robust
likelihood produces substantial certified robust accuracy—up to 75% on MNIST (ε = 0.1), 73%
on FashionMNIST (ε = 0.1), and 50% on CIFAR-10 (ε = 1/255)—whereas standard training or
PGD-based adversarial training yields essentially zero certified guarantees. Moreover, the certified
safe radius approximately doubles compared to conventional training, confirming that the posterior
is pushed toward more robust solutions. Beyond robustness, we have also observed improved
uncertainty calibration, particularly on out-of-distribution data, suggesting that incorporating
adversarial invariances into the likelihood enhances epistemic behavior.

3.2. Quantum-Inspired Approach for Verifying Binary Neural Networks

In resource-constrained or edge environments, the computational and memory costs of full-
precision DNNs can be prohibitive. Binary neural networks (BNNs), which restrict weights and
activations to binary values, provide reduced hardware complexity, smaller memory footprints,
and energy-efficient bitwise operations. Although BNNs enable efficient deployment on FPGAs,
ASICs, and microcontrollers, they remain vulnerable to adversarial and structured perturbations.
Robustness verification of BNNs is therefore crucial for reliable edge deployment and for
understanding the limitations of aggressively quantized models. Existing approaches primarily
rely on classical exact solvers such as SAT, MILP, and SMT tools, but the resulting combinatorial
optimization problems are intrinsically expensive on CPUs and GPUs, often exhibiting
exponential worst-case complexity.

 Figure 3. Proposed quantum-inspired framework for BNN verification.

 Recent advances in quantum-inspired computing hardware have enabled practical systems
such as digital annealers, Ising machines, and free-energy solvers. In [6], we proposed an Ising
and quantum-inspired approach for robustness verification of BNN as shown in Fig. 3. We show
that robustness verification of a generic C-class BNN can be reformulated as a Quadratic
Unconstrained Boolean Optimization (QUBO) instance. Unlike MILP- or SAT-based encodings
for classical solvers, our formulation directly matches the native input format of Ising and
annealing-style hardware, enabling a unified interface with quantum annealing, gate-based
quantum algorithms (e.g., QAOA), and digital or analog Ising machines.

Table 2. Comparison of hardware executions and classical baselines for BNN robustness verification on a
representative QUBO instance. Gurobi is included as an industry-standard mixed-integer/QUBO optimizer,
while Simulated Annealing (SA) serves as the annealing annealing-style baseline. For each solver we report
the number of constraints satisfied by the best returned sample and the total runtime. *Best sample among
all samples obtained from the quantum annealer.

 We have verified the robustness of a ten-class BNN trained on MNIST using simulated
annealing and free energy machine (FEM). We have further demonstrated the deployment of this
framework on practical D-wave quantum annealer and commercial digital annealer. The results in
Table 2 demonstrated that the proposed QUBO mapping is practically solvable for nontrivial
BNNs and extensible to emerging quantum-inspired hardware.
