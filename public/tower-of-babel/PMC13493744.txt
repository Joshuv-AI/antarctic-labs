Pediatric Melanoma: Emerging Therapies and Ongoing Clinical Trials
Authors: Pablo Balado-Simó, Marc Hernández-Santacana, Miguel Mansilla-Polo, Cristina Carrera, Susana Puig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric melanoma is a rare but clinically distinct malignancy, accounting for 1–4% of all melanoma cases. It encompasses unique subtypes—Spitzoid melanoma, melanoma arising in congenital melanocytic nevi, and conventional melanoma—each with specific biological behavior and therapeutic implications. While surgical excision remains the primary treatment, systemic therapies are often adapted from adult protocols and used off-label, with limited pediatric-specific data on efficacy, dosing, and safety. This narrative review summarizes current treatment approaches, including the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies, and highlights outcomes from available prospective and retrospective studies. Furthermore, it reviews ongoing clinical trials investigating novel strategies such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, natural killer (NK) cell infusions, Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitors, and cancer vaccines. Despite emerging interest, challenges persist due to the rarity of the disease, lack of long-term toxicity data, and limited biomarker-driven stratification. Expanding pediatric-focused clinical research, integrating molecular profiling, and addressing developmental and psychosocial needs are essential for advancing care. Ongoing trials may help pave the way for safer and more effective therapies tailored to this vulnerable population.Keywords: Pediatric melanoma, CAYA melanoma, Immunotherapy, Targeted therapy, Clinical trials

Introduction

Pediatric melanoma is a rare but increasingly recognized malignancy, accounting for approximately 1–4% of all melanoma cases and 1% of all pediatric malignancies [1, 2]. This percentage is greater in adolescents, where melanoma causes 7.1% of all malignancies in subjects aged 15–19 years [3]. Pediatric melanoma exhibits distinct histologic subtypes, which include three subtypes: Spitz melanoma, melanoma arising in congenital melanocytic nevi (CMN), and conventional melanoma [1, 4]. Each of them has unique biological behaviors and therapeutic implications [4], as well as different dermoscopic characteristics [5].

Diagnostic delays are common due to atypical clinical presentations and a low index of suspicion in children. Pediatric melanomas often present without the classic features—amelanotic, bleeding/bumps, color uniformity, de novo, evolution (ABCDE)—in adults, so alternative algorithms have been proposed to help with detection: the modified ABCDE and color, uniformity, and pop-up (CUP) [6]. Also, Spitzoid lesions often mimic benign Spitz nevi and Spitz melanoma, as these entities are difficult to distinguish without molecular analysis [4]. As a result, those rare cases of real melanomas are diagnosed at advanced stages, impacting prognosis and therapeutic decision-making.

The rarity of pediatric melanoma has major therapeutic implications. Very few systemic therapies have been formally evaluated in children, and pediatric-specific approvals, dosing data, and long-term safety information remain limited. Consequently, treatment decisions for high-risk or advanced disease are frequently based on adult melanoma evidence, small pediatric series, compassionate-use experiences, or off-label use of immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies. Treatment guidelines for pediatric melanoma are largely extrapolated from adult studies, particularly for high-risk and metastatic cases [7, 8]. The cornerstone of therapy remains surgical resection (which is curative in the majority of cases) [8]; however, adjuvant and systemic therapies are often used off-label in pediatric settings with limited data on pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and toxicity.

While recent advances in immunotherapy and precision oncology have revolutionized melanoma management in adults, translating these breakthroughs to pediatric patients remains challenging. The scarcity of dedicated clinical trials, the rarity of the disease, and concerns over long-term toxicities in growing children have slowed therapeutic innovation. However, some studies are exploring the role of molecular diagnostics and registries in improving classification and guiding therapy [9].

This narrative review aims to summarize the current treatment landscape of pediatric melanoma, highlight ongoing clinical trials investigating emerging therapies, and discuss future directions that could pave the way for more personalized, effective, and safer interventions in this vulnerable population.

In this review, we focus on children and adolescents and, when relevant, on the broader children, adolescents and young adults (CAYA) population, given the rarity of melanoma in younger age groups and the frequent inclusion of adolescents and young adults in available cohorts and clinical trials. For clarity, we refer to “pediatric” as < 18 years and to “CAYA” as patients ≤ 21 years; when evidence comes from trials, including older AYA patients (e.g., up to 30 years), this is explicitly stated.

Methods

The objective of this narrative review was to identify, summarize, and critically evaluate the clinical evidence supporting current and emerging therapies for pediatric melanoma, including both approved and off-label treatments, as well as ongoing clinical trials. This review was conducted following the recommendations of the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) [10]. The search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar, and ClinicalTrials.gov from 1 January 2010 to 1 August 2025. The following search terms and their combinations were used: “pediatric melanoma,” “childhood melanoma,” “adolescent melanoma,” “cutaneous melanoma,” “immunotherapy,” “checkpoint inhibitors,” “nivolumab,” “pembrolizumab,” “ipilimumab,” “targeted therapy,” “BRAF inhibitor,” “MEK inhibitor,” “dabrafenib,” “trametinib,” “CAR-T cells,” “B7-H3,” “NK cells,” “Wnt pathway,” “Tegavivint,” “clinical trial,” and “emerging treatment.”

No geographic restrictions were applied. Only full-text peer-reviewed articles published in English were considered. The inclusion criteria were:

Primary inclusion: randomized controlled trials (RCTs), Phase 1–3 clinical trials and prospective clinical studies in CAYA populations (≤ 21 years).

Primary inclusion: randomized controlled trials (RCTs), Phase 1–3 clinical trials and prospective clinical studies in CAYA populations (≤ 21 years).

Secondary inclusion: retrospective studies with > 5 patients, case series, and case reports when prospective data were unavailable.

Secondary inclusion: retrospective studies with > 5 patients, case series, and case reports when prospective data were unavailable.

Exclusion: animal/in vitro studies, conference abstracts without full text and editorials.

Exclusion: animal/in vitro studies, conference abstracts without full text and editorials.

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (P.B.S. and M.H.S.). Full texts meeting inclusion criteria were retrieved, and disagreements were resolved by discussion.

For each included study, the following data were extracted: study design, patient population, sample size, tumor characteristics, therapeutic intervention (type, mechanism, dosage), comparator(s), main outcomes (response rates, progression-free survival, toxicity), follow-up duration and reported adverse events (AEs).

For ongoing clinical trials, we reviewed entries from ClinicalTrials.gov, filtering by condition (“melanoma”), age group (child, adolescent, CAYA), and status (“recruiting,” “active,” or “completed”). Relevant trials were included if they enrolled patients ≤ 21 years of age and tested immunotherapies, targeted therapies, or cellular therapies.

Given the rarity of pediatric melanoma and the heterogeneity of study designs, patient populations, age ranges, treatment settings, interventions, and outcome measures, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, findings were synthesized narratively by grouping studies according to treatment class, clinical setting, age group, disease stage, and level of evidence. When available, histologic subtype, molecular alterations, response outcomes, survival endpoints, and adverse events were also considered. Comparative tables were used to summarize clinical patterns, limitations of the available evidence, and future research directions.

Results

Current Treatment Landscape

Surgical excision with appropriate margins remains the standard first-line treatment for localized pediatric melanoma [8]. The indications for sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) in children are extrapolated from adult guidelines, typically applied for tumors > 0.8 mm in thickness or those with ulceration [8], the T1b stage according to American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition [11].

In resected high-risk stages (IIB–IIIC), adjuvant systemic therapies such as anti-PD-1 agents (pembrolizumab in KEYNOTE-716 trial [12] and nivolumab in CheckMate 76K [13]) have shown significant survival benefit in adults and have included a small number of adolescent patients (≥ 12 years). In contrast, for unresectable or metastatic disease (stage III–IV), systemic therapies are frequently employed, though most are used off-label. Among the included studies, the most frequently reported systemic agents were immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly pembrolizumab and nivolumab, and targeted therapies for BRAF-mutated melanoma, such as dabrafenib and trametinib.

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors (ICIs)

Overall, evidence for immune checkpoint inhibitors is strongest in adolescents and in the adjuvant setting, whereas data in younger children and in rare molecular subtypes remain sparse. Several retrospective series, early-phase trials, and isolated case reports have evaluated the use of ICIs in pediatric melanoma [14–21, 25–38]. A recent systematic review included 64 patients treated with PD-1 inhibitors, highlighting variable outcomes and a limited safety database [39].

In 2025, inside the MELCAYA EU project (https://www.melcaya.eu), Mandalà et al. [14] reported 99 pediatric patients who had received systemic therapy, including 81 treated with anti-PD-1 agents. The median age was 14 years (range 2–18), with 37 patients aged ≤ 12 years. Overall, 38 patients received adjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy, achieving 3-year PFS and OS rates of 70.6% and 81.1%, respectively. Two patients were treated with neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy; both achieved a pathologic complete response and remained disease-free. A total of 56 patients were treated for advanced disease: 43 with first-line anti-PD-1-based therapy, 12 with second-line, and 5 with third-line regimens. Among those receiving first-line anti-PD-1 monotherapy, the ORR was 25% and the 3-year OS was 34%, reflecting outcomes in a heavily pretreated advanced-disease cohort. Reported toxicities were consistent with previous findings in adult melanoma.

A retrospective cohort study from three Chinese centers included nine patients (mean age 11.3 years) with stage IIB–IV melanoma who received adjuvant pembrolizumab within 3 months of surgery [15]. The 1-year recurrence free survival (RFS) was 88.9%, with only one recurrence (11%) reported. Median RFS was not reached at the time of the analysis. AEs were mostly mild.

The Phase 1–2 KEYNOTE 051 trial [16] enrolled 154 pediatric patients with various advanced solid tumors, including 8 with melanoma (5 were PD-L1-negative). No objective responses were observed in the melanoma subgroup. Across the entire cohort, the overall response rate (ORR) was 9%, mainly in lymphoma cases. Grade 3–5 AEs occurred in 45% of participants. Importantly, the study was primarily designed to assess safety and dosing and although objective response was assessed for each tumor type, the very small number of patients with melanoma limits the interpretation of efficacy in this subgroup.

In addition to cohort data, case reports describe favorable outcomes with pembrolizumab in adolescents [25]. One case of a patient with xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) and advanced melanoma achieved prolonged disease stabilization after six cycles of pembrolizumab [26].

Regarding nivolumab, in a retrospective series from Poland [17], eight adolescents with melanoma were treated with nivolumab or pembrolizumab. Complete responses were observed in six of eight patients, with estimated 3-year progression-free survival (PFS) of 78%. Grade ≥ 2 AEs occurred in three cases. A US-based series by Bowden et al. [18] included six CAYA patients (ages 2–20 years) treated with adjuvant nivolumab. All remained alive and recurrence-free after a median follow-up of 3.4 years. Mild immune-related AEs were seen in two patients; no severe AEs occurred. In the ADVL1412 trial [19], one adolescent with melanoma received nivolumab as part of a broader early-phase safety study. No objective response was observed, although the trial was not designed to assess melanoma-specific efficacy.

Additionally, several case reports support the potential activity of nivolumab in pediatric melanoma [27–29]. One adolescent with mucosal melanoma remained disease-free after resection and adjuvant nivolumab [30] and an infant with congenital metastatic melanoma achieved partial response with nivolumab [31].

Although no formal trial has evaluated the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab in pediatric melanoma, several case reports describe its off-label use. These include a 2-year-old patient reported by Mitre et al. (2021) [32] and an adolescent in Aljundi et al. (2022) [33]. In some cases, combination therapy was administered after failure of PD-1 blockade alone. While safety data in these anecdotal reports appear manageable, efficacy outcomes are heterogeneous, and long-term follow-up is scarce.

Ipilimumab monotherapy has been studied in two prospective trials. In a Phase 1 trial including 33 children with relapsed or refractory solid tumors (12 of whom had melanoma) [21], ipilimumab was administered at escalating doses (1, 3, 5, or 10 mg/kg). No complete or partial responses were observed in the melanoma subgroup, although seven patients had stable disease for ≥ 6 weeks. Toxicities increased with dose. Geoerger et al. [20] published a Phase 2 multicenter trial which enrolled 12 adolescents (aged 12–18 years) with unresectable stage III or IV melanoma. Patients received either 3 mg/kg (n = 4) or 10 mg/kg (n = 8) every 3 weeks for four doses. The 1-year overall survival was 75% in the 3 mg/kg group and 62.5% in the 10 mg/kg group. Partial responses or stable disease were observed in a limited number of patients and toxicity was notably higher at the 10 mg/kg dose. Also, several case reports and small series describe ipilimumab use in monotherapy [34–37]. It has also been used in combination with pembrolizumab [38], as well as with nivolumab as we stated before.

Targeted Therapy: BRAF/MEK Inhibitors

Dabrafenib and trametinib were administered under compassionate access in a retrospective Italian series of six pediatric patients with BRAF V600E/K-mutant melanoma [22]. Four patients received adjuvant therapy and remained disease-free at a median follow-up of 22 months. Two patients had metastatic disease; one achieved a complete response lasting 19.3 months, while the other progressed and died. Treatment-related AEs included elevated creatine kinase, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) reduction, diarrhea, and rash. In a Phase 1 dose-escalation trial [23], six adolescents (12–17 years) with unresectable BRAFV600-mutant melanoma received vemurafenib (720 or 960 mg BID). No dose-limiting toxicities were observed, but no objective responses occurred. Median PFS was 4.4 months and overall survival (OS) 8.1 months. The study was terminated early due to poor accrual.

Interferon-α2b

High-dose interferon-α2b has been evaluated in two pediatric cohorts. A US prospective study treated 15 children with resected stage III melanoma using adult-based dosing [24]. After a median of 19.8 months, 12 of 15 patients remained recurrence-free. Toxicities were frequent, including grade 3–4 neutropenia, liver enzyme elevation, and fatigue. A separate Chinese cohort (n = 15) [15] reported similar treatment with median RFS of 38.7 months. Ten patients relapsed, and five discontinued treatment early due to toxicity. Grade ≥ 3 AEs were observed in 13%, with pyrexia, rash and neutropenia being the most frequent events.

ICIs

A European prospective multicenter study, included in MELCAYA (https://www.melcaya.eu) project, will start recruiting patients aged 12–30 years to assess response and safety of anti-PD-1 therapy, with or without ipilimumab, in early and advanced melanoma, analyzing reproductive adverse events collecting samples to analyze anti-Mullerian hormone. Separately, a long-term Dutch registry (NCT04196452) is following patients under 18 years treated with ipilimumab to evaluate safety and survival outcomes. A completed real-world observational study (NCT06163170) evaluated nivolumab ± ipilimumab in pediatric patients with resected or metastatic melanoma, contributing real-world data on safety and effectiveness.

Cell-Based Therapies

CAR-T cell platforms targeting B7-H3 are currently being tested in pediatric solid tumors, including melanoma. A Phase 1 study (NCT04897321) is assessing the safety and MTD of B7-H3-directed CAR-T cells in patients ≤ 21 years with relapsed/refractory tumors. Another Phase 1 trial (NCT04483778) is evaluating B7-H3 CAR-T cells alone or in combination with CD19 CAR and pembrolizumab in children and young adults up to 26 years. Separately, a long-term follow-up protocol (NCT06508775) includes pediatric patients previously treated with MB-CART19.1, MB-CART20.1, or MB-CART2019.1 to monitor relapse and long-term transgene persistence. A completed study (NCT02107963) explored anti-GD2 CAR-T cells in children and young adults aged 1–35 years with GD2+ solid tumors, including melanoma.

Natural killer (NK)-cell-based therapies are also being investigated. A Phase 1 trial (NCT03420963) is recruiting patients aged 1–40 years to evaluate the safety and dosing of expanded cord blood NK cell infusion following chemotherapy in relapsed/refractory melanoma and other pediatric solid tumors.

Targeted Therapies

The β-catenin pathway inhibitor tegavivint is under investigation in a Phase 1/2 study (NCT04851119) in patients aged 12 months–30 years. The study includes melanoma within its indication and is evaluating safety, maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and early activity signals.

Vaccine-Based Approaches

A Phase 1 trial (NCT00258687) assessed the safety and feasibility of an autologous GM-CSF-secreting tumor cell vaccine (GVAX) in pediatric and adult patients, including a melanoma cohort under 18 years. Another completed trial (NCT00020267, not shown in table) evaluated a melanoma antigen gene (MAGE)-12 peptide vaccine in adolescents and adults with MAGE-12+ tumors, including melanoma, though not exclusive to the pediatric population.

Discussion

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on systemic therapies and ongoing clinical trials for pediatric melanoma, highlighting gaps and opportunities for therapeutic advancement. Pediatric melanoma remains rare and biologically heterogeneous, and critical limitations impede evidence-based treatment decisions. Most systemic therapies are extrapolated from adult protocols and used off-label, often without robust pediatric pharmacokinetic or safety data, creating uncertainty regarding optimal dosing, age-related pharmacodynamics, and long-term effects [1, 7, 40].

Efficacy interpretation is further complicated by heterogeneity in age, disease stage, treatment setting, and melanoma subtype. Many studies combine CAYA, with some trials extending to patients over 30 years. Therefore, outcomes from CAYA cohorts should not be assumed representative of strictly pediatric populations, particularly children under 12 years. Patients treated in early-phase, retrospective, or compassionate-use settings often have advanced or biologically aggressive disease, partially explaining lower response rates compared with adults [14].

Biological differences among pediatric subtypes are notable. Conventional melanomas in adolescents may resemble adult ultraviolet (UV)-associated melanoma, whereas Spitz melanoma and melanoma arising in CMN have distinct drivers, tumor mutational burdens, kinase fusions, NRAS-driven biology, or developmental origins. These variations may affect tumor immunogenicity, immune microenvironment composition, and sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Future studies should report outcomes by age group, histologic and molecular subtype, driver alteration, tumor mutational burden, PD-L1 status, treatment setting, and disease stage [4].

In the MELCAYA cohort, outcomes differed substantially according to treatment setting. Adjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy was associated with favorable survival outcomes, whereas anti-PD-1 monotherapy in advanced disease showed more modest activity, with a 3-year OS of 34% [14]. These poorer outcomes should not be interpreted solely as age-related inferiority, because they may reflect advanced disease burden, referral bias, prior treatment exposure, small sample size, and biological heterogeneity. Nevertheless, they underscore that adult efficacy estimates cannot be directly extrapolated to all pediatric melanoma subtypes.

Additionally, there is an urgent need for biomarkers to guide therapy in pediatric melanoma [4]. Infrequent and lethal central nervous system (CNS) primary melanoma can be a specific subtype of melanoma developed during infancy in the setting of patients with large/giant congenital melanocytic nevi [41]. These melanomas usually harbor NRAS Q61 mutations and are resistant to systemic therapies [42]. Currently, only a handful of molecular markers (e.g., BRAFV600 mutations) inform treatment selection, and others, such as PD-L1 expression in immunotherapy responses, remain underexplored or unvalidated in this age group [2, 9]. However, available clinical evidence remains limited and insufficiently stratified by histologic or molecular subtype, precluding subtype-specific therapeutic recommendations and underscoring the need for molecularly driven pediatric trials.

The scarcity of long-term safety data further complicates management decisions. Children may experience unique toxicities or late effects, including endocrine disruption, impaired development and fertility or secondary malignancies. These risks are particularly relevant when considering immunotherapies or targeted therapies with known cumulative toxicities [43].

In parallel, psychosocial support considerations are crucial yet often underreported. Pediatric and adolescent patients face developmental and emotional challenges that may be exacerbated by aggressive treatments and prolonged follow-up, including disruptions in schooling, social relationships, and age-appropriate autonomy. Treatment-related sequelae such as visible scarring, disfiguring surgery, alopecia, chronic fatigue, or functional limitations may negatively affect body image, self-esteem, and social integration, particularly during adolescence [44]. These factors can also influence adherence to prolonged systemic therapies and surveillance protocols, underscoring the need for integrated, family-centered supportive care [45, 46].

Moreover, structured transition pathways from pediatric to adult melanoma services are essential to ensure continuity of care, long-term toxicity monitoring and sustained psychosocial support as patients age, yet these aspects remain insufficiently addressed in most clinical studies [47].

Several ongoing clinical trials are investigating innovative approaches that may help bridge these gaps. Retrospective and prospective studies are evaluating the safety and efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in pediatric melanoma, with early signals of clinical benefit in selected patients [16]. Trials such as the MELCAYA project and Dutch ipilimumab registry aim to generate prospective safety and outcome data that could help refine future use.

Cellular therapies are also being explored, particularly CAR-T-cell platforms targeting B7-H3 and GD2 [48]. While still in early development, these strategies may offer new therapeutic avenues for refractory or high-risk cases. Natural killer (NK) cell infusions [49] and long-term follow-up of patients treated with CAR-T may further inform safety and durability.

Targeted agents such as tegavivint, represent a mechanism-driven approach, particularly relevant for tumors with dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling [50, 51]. Vaccine-based immunotherapies, such as GVAX or peptide vaccines against melanoma-associated antigens (e.g., MAGE-12), remain experimental but reflect a broader trend toward personalized immunotherapy [52].

Moving forward, several challenges are to be faced in this field (Table 3). The expansion of international pediatric trial networks will be essential to overcome the rarity of the disease and to conduct adequately powered studies [8, 9]. Precision medicine approaches, including routine molecular profiling, can enhance patient stratification and trial eligibility [53]. Combinatorial therapies that integrate immunotherapy with cell-based or targeted agents may boost efficacy, particularly in advanced-stage disease. Protocols should be tailored to the pediatric population, with dosing, toxicity monitoring and long-term follow-up designed around developmental considerations [54]. Lastly, a more consistent integration of psychosocial support within clinical research frameworks is necessary to address the broader impact of melanoma diagnosis and treatment in children [55].

Limitations

This narrative review is subject to limitations inherent to rare disease research, including small sample sizes, retrospective biases, heterogeneous study designs, incomplete long-term follow-up, and inconsistent reporting of toxicity. A major limitation of the available evidence is that many studies combine biologically distinct melanoma subtypes and broad age groups, often without age-stratified or molecularly stratified outcomes. Therefore, conclusions regarding children younger than 12 years and rare subtypes such as CMN-associated melanoma or Spitz melanoma should be considered hypothesis-generating. In addition, several ongoing trials include adolescents and young adults up to 30 years or older, limiting the direct applicability of their results to strictly pediatric populations.

Conclusions

Pediatric melanoma presents unique clinical, histologic, and therapeutic challenges that differentiate it from adult melanoma. While surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment, data supporting systemic therapies in children are limited and largely extrapolated from adult populations. Recent advances in immunotherapy and targeted treatments show promise, but their pediatric application requires further validation through dedicated trials. The ongoing development of cell-based therapies, molecularly targeted agents and registries represents an important step toward personalized and evidence-based management. To improve outcomes, future research must prioritize pediatric-specific study designs, long-term safety monitoring and the integration of precision medicine and supportive care tailored to young patients.
