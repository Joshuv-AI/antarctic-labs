ABSTRACT
Key Points•HMA-VEN is well tolerated in patients with AML/MDS who relapsed after alloHCT.•HMA-VEN is effective for relapsed AML/MDS after alloHCT, even in patients who have failed prior VEN-based regimens.

Introduction

Outcomes of patients who relapse with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) after allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant (alloHCT) remain poor. In addition to the potential exposure to multiple lines of therapy in relapsed/refractory (R/R) MDS or AML, complications particular to alloHCT, such as graft-versus-host disease, impaired graft function, and transplant-associated infections, further impact outcomes. As a result, choosing a salvage therapy for post-alloHCT relapse is challenging. Unfortunately, there are limited retrospective studies and no large-scale prospective studies in this setting to guide decision-making. Hypomethylating agents (HMA) are frequently used as monotherapy and in combination with donor-lymphocyte infusion (DLI) for post-alloHCT relapse. HMA not only have antileukemia activity but also increase the graft-versus-leukemia effect without exacerbating graft-versus-host disease.1

In older patients and patients with comorbidities, an HMA in combination with venetoclax (HMA-VEN) has become the standard of care for frontline therapy of AML.2 HMA-VEN is also an effective salvage regimen for R/R AML and MDS.3, 4, 5 However, data on the efficacy of HMA-VEN for treatment of relapse of AML and MDS after alloHCT remain limited to small retrospective studies.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Moreover, the determinants of response and the toxicity of the regimen in the post-alloHCT setting remain unknown. In this study, we report on the outcomes for patients with AML or MDS relapsing after alloHCT, who received HMA-VEN. We also performed multivariable analysis to identify factors associated with response and survival after treatment with the regimen.

Methods

Patients and eligibility

This study was approved by the City of Hope National Medical Center Institutional Review Board and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We retrospectively identified patients who had relapsed AML or MDS following an alloHCT between August 2016 and December 2023. Patients were included in the study if they were treated with at least 1 cycle of an HMA-VEN. Selection of HMA-VEN dosing was at the discretion of the treating physician.

End points and definitions

The study end points included complete response (CR), CR with incomplete hematological recovery (CRi), and morphologic leukemia-free state (MLFS). AML response definitions were based on European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 classifications, and MDS response definitions were based on the International Working Group 2023 response criteria for higher-risk MDSs.13,14 In patients with CR or CRi, minimal residual disease negativity was also assessed with multiparametric flow cytometry (sensitivity, 0.01%). The survival end point included overall survival (OS), defined as the time from initiation of HMA-VEN treatment to the date of death from any cause. Additionally, the benefit stratification from HMA-VEN therapy was defined by the genetic risk classification for adults with AML receiving less-intensive therapies: the ELN 2024 recommendations.15 Toxicity from HMA-VEN was graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient demographics, disease characteristics, transplant-related variables, pre-HCT HMA-VEN exposure, treatment responses, and toxicities were summarized with descriptive statistics. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with treatment response, defined as achieving a CR, CRi, or an MLFS with salvage HMA-VEN therapy. The following covariates were evaluated in these analyses: prior HMA-VEN failure, time from HCT to relapse, cytogenetic risk, ELN HMA-VEN risk classification, and best response to prior HMA-VEN. All variables with P < .10 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic model.

OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were then used to identify prognostic factors for OS, evaluating the same set of covariates as above; variables with P < .10 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable Cox model.

A landmark analysis at day 60 after HMA-VEN administration was performed to compare OS between patients who achieved CR, CRi, or MLFS after HMA-VEN salvage therapy and those who did not respond. Patients who died before day 60 after HMA-VEN administration or had not reached day 60 by the time of last follow-up were excluded from this landmark analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and 2-sided P values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Patient characteristics

Treatment and response

The median number of HMA-VEN cycles received was 2 (range, 1-21). A total of 64 (77%) patients received decitabine, 14 (17%) patients received azacitidine, and 5 (6%) patients received both. Of the patients who received both HMA, all patients were switched for no response or loss of response to HMA, but switching agents did not improve response. Apart from 1 (5%) patient who had a 5-day course, 95% of the patients received azacitidine 75 mg/m2 for 7 days. Decitabine was dosed at 20 mg/m2 for all patients. Of the patients who received decitabine, 43 (62%) received a 5-day course, 25 (36%) received a 10-day course, and 1 (1%) received a 4-day course. VEN was dosed at 400 mg daily for patients without CYP3A4 inhibitors, 200 mg for patients with moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors, and 70 mg to 100 mg for patients with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. The median duration for VEN administration was 21 days (range, 4 days to continuous) for all patients. For 50% of the patients (n = 7) diagnosed with MDS, VEN was administered for a duration of 14 days based on the VERONA trial.16 Fifty-four (65%) patients received >1 cycle of HMA-VEN. Of these patients, 27 (50%) required dose reductions for cycle 2 of HMA-VEN because of cytopenia. For the second cycle, HMA duration alone was reduced in 7 (26%) patients, VEN duration alone was reduced in 14 (52%) patients, and both HMA and VEN duration were reduced in 6 (22%) patients.

CR/CRi was achieved in 40 (48%) patients, and MLFS was achieved by 2 (2%) patients. Patients who did not achieve CR/CRi or MLFS either had no response or disease progression. Of the patients with CR/CRi, 58% (n = 23) achieved minimal residual disease negativity. The median time from start of treatment to initial best response was 63.5 days (range, 21-452). The median response duration is 6.5 months (range, 1.0-58.3). In the univariable logistic regression analysis, none of the evaluated covariates, including prior HMA-VEN failure, time from HCT to relapse, cytogenetic risk, ELN HMA-VEN risk classification, or best response to prior HMA-VEN, were significantly associated with achieving a treatment response (CR, CRi, or MLFS) at the 0.10 significance level (supplemental Table 1). Therefore, a multivariable logistic regression model was not performed.

Survival outcomes

At 6 months and 12 months, the OS for all patients was 55% (95% confidence interval [CI], 44-65) and 37% (95% CI, 26-47), respectively (Figure 1A). Median OS for the entire cohort was 7.6 months (range, 4.4-10.3). When comparing patients with prior VEN failure vs those who did not, no significant differences in OS were observed (Figure 1B). At 6 months, OS was 42% (95% CI, 26-58) for patients with prior VEN failure vs 54% (95% CI, 25-76) for those without. As shown in Figure 1C, time to relapse after alloHCT significantly affected OS. Patients who relapsed ≥180 days after HCT had significantly better OS compared to patients who relapsed <180 days after HCT. In the ≥180 days cohort, 6-month OS was 64% (95% CI, 48-76) and 12-month OS was 45% (95% CI, 30-59), whereas the <180 days cohort had a 6-month OS of 46% (95% CI, 30-61) and 12-month OS of 27% (95% CI, 14-41; P = .02).

When stratifying patients by the 2024 ELN HMA-VEN benefit-risk groups (low, intermediate, and high), a significant difference in OS was observed across the 3 subgroups (P < .01; Figure 1D). At 6 months, OS was 63% (95% CI, 43-78) for the high-benefit group, 63% (95% CI, 35-81) for the intermediate-benefit group, and 36% (95% CI, 17-56) for the low-benefit group. At 12 months, OS was 56% (95% CI, 37-72), 31% (95% CI, 11-54), and 9.1% (95% CI, 0.9-29.9), respectively.

Relapse at <180 days after HCT (hazard ratio [HR], 1.79; P = .02) and patients from the low-benefit group (HR, 2.88; P = .01) had worse OS. Toxicity

The most common adverse events were grade 3 and 4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, which both occurred in 99% (n = 82) of the patients. All patients who experienced neutropenia had severe neutropenia (grade 3, 2.4% [n = 2]; and grade 4, 97.6% [n = 80]), and 20 (24%) patients had febrile neutropenia. Additionally, most patients who experienced thrombocytopenia (n = 78) had grade 4 thrombocytopenia. Within 30 days of receiving their last cycle of HMA-VEN, 3 patients (3.6%) died from an infectious complication related to therapy. Two of these patients died from septic shock secondary to bacteremia, and 1 died from bacterial pneumonia.

DLI and second transplant

After salvage with HMA-VEN, 7 (8%) patients received subsequent DLI only. For these patients, their median OS from the first DLI infusion was 2.2 months. Additionally, 20 (24%) patients were able to be bridged to a second alloHCT. Starting from the time of their second alloHCT, the median OS is 15.1 months. In a landmark analysis on day 60 after HMA-VEN, 68 patients were included, and the median follow-up time was 9.3 months (range, 2.1-70.3). Responders to HMA-VEN had much better survival than nonresponders, with 6-month OS of 89.9% vs 35.7% and 12-month OS of 60.8% vs 21.4% (P < .001), respectively.

Discussion

HMA-VEN has become the standard treatment for patients with newly diagnosed AML who are not eligible for intensive chemotherapy.2 This regimen is also effective for the treatment of R/R AML and MDS,3, 4, 5 but the data for HMA-VEN to treat relapsed AML and MDS after alloHCT are limited. Our results show that HMA-VEN is an effective salvage therapy for both relapsed AML and MDS after alloHCT.

Because of the heterogeneity of the retrospective studies that explored the efficacy and safety of HMA-VEN for post-alloHCT AML and MDS relapse, there is variation in the results. In 2 meta-analyses of these studies by Du et al and Shahzad et al, the pooled CR/CRi rates were 32% and 39%, respectively.6,8 Additionally, the pooled 6-month OS and 12-month OS reported by these meta-analyses were 42% and 51% and 23% and 29%, respectively.6,8 Our CR/CRi rate of 48%, 6-month OS of 55%, and 12-month OS of 37% were higher than the pooled results provided by these meta-analyses but are within the range of the referenced individual studies.11,17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 The most reported toxicities in these studies were neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Both meta-analyses by Du et al8 and Shahzad et al6 reported a high incidence of grade 3 or 4 neutropenia (88% and 73%, respectively) and thrombocytopenia (81% and 71%, respectively). Grade 3 or 4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia occurred in 99% of our patients, but only 3.6% (n = 3) of the patients died from an infectious complication within 30 days of treatment with HMA-VEN. The high incidence of cytopenia may be expected in this patient population because of their exposure to multiple lines of therapy.

To strengthen the evidence for the use of HMA-VEN for the treatment of relapsed myeloid disease after alloHCT, our study defines new factors associated with the outcomes. Although other studies have explored HMA-VEN efficacy in patients with prior exposure to HMA-VEN or other VEN-based regimens, none have described the response of HMA-VEN for salvage after VEN failure. This is an important distinction because VEN failure has negative prognostic implications for patients diagnosed with AML or MDS. More than half of the patients in this study had a history of treatment with a VEN-based regimen before relapse after alloHCT, and 28% of these patients experienced treatment failure with their VEN-based regimen. Our results showed that there was no difference in treatment response or in survival among patients who had experienced treatment failure with a VEN-based regimen. Hence, prior exposure or failure of a VEN-based regimen may not be a consideration for its use after HCT relapse, but this observation requires confirmation in larger studies. Additionally, AML or MDS that relapsed within 180 days of transplant was correlated with lower OS. Although our multivariable analysis did not show that relapse within 180 days was significantly associated with worse OS, other studies have found that earlier relapse is significantly associated with worse survival outcomes.7,17,20

Another factor that our study highlights is the use of genetic risk stratification by the 2024 ELN HMA-VEN criteria, which could help determine which patients would benefit from using HMA-VEN as salvage therapy.15 As predicted, patients in the high-benefit group had the best 6- and 12-month OS, whereas patients in the low-benefit group had the worst 6- and 12-month OS after HMA-VEN salvage. Our multivariable analysis showed the high-benefit group had significantly better OS compared to the low-benefit group. Although the ELN 2024 risk stratification occurs at the time of initial diagnosis, our study shows it remains a useful tool in selecting candidates for HMA-VEN salvage for post-alloHCT relapse. Moreover, the dosing, frequency, and duration of HMA-VEN were determined by the treating physician; therefore, we could not determine the optimal dose to treat relapsed AML or MDS after alloHCT.

Our results also show that about a quarter of responders can undergo consolidation with alloHCT. However, it’s unclear if subsequent alloHCT or DLI for patients achieving remission after post-alloHCT relapse is beneficial.26 In alignment with our results, Murdock et al showed that achieving CR after post-alloHCT relapse was associated with improved survival, but their results showed no benefit in relapse-free survival, relapse, or non-relapse mortality (NRM) after giving these patients DLI or a secondary alloHCT.26 Further investigation is needed to determine whether maintenance with HMA-VEN would be an effective strategy and which subset of these patients may benefit from consolidation with cellular therapy.

In conclusion, HMA-VEN remains the most practical and well-tolerated regimen for treatment of relapse of AML/MDS after alloHCT, and we show its efficacy even in patients who were previously treated with a VEN-based regimen. The response rates and leukemia-free survival are clinically meaningful and may also allow a subset of these patients to undergo a second alloHCT with curative intent.
