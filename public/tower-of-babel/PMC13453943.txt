Lymphatic Drainage of Cerebrospinal Fluid Using Lymph Node Seeker 64Cu‐Labeled Gram‐Negative Bacterial Extracellular Vesicles With Positron Emission Tomography (PET)
Authors: Kyuwan Kim, Changjin Lee, Hayoung Shin, Ran Ji Yoo, Yoori Choi, Chanwoo Lee, Insoo Kim, Jaewook Lee, Minseok Suh, Yun‐Sang Lee, Yong Song Gho, Dong Soo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is drained into the systemic lymphatics via paravertebral lymph nodes. Superficial and deep cervical lymph nodes collect CSF in mice, but the exact and quantified routes are unknown. Recently, we simultaneously visualized cervical, sacral and iliac lymph nodes via serial imaging on the intrathecal [64Cu]Cu‐albumin positron emission tomography. Paravertebral lymph nodes might act as sentinels to monitor the CSF, brain, and spinal cord. We used 64Cu‐labeled Escherichia coli extracellular vesicles, outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), as lymph node seekers for intrathecal administration and quantified the differential amounts of various paravertebral lymph nodes along the axis of the brain and spinal cord in mice. The quantified results revealed 77.3% in superficial and deep cervical lymph nodes, 11.4% in abdominal/pelvic lymph nodes and 11.3% in sacral lymph nodes. Click‐labeled [64Cu]Cu‐OMVs were drained to reach and stop at the lymph nodes on serial quantification. The cervical lymph nodes drained most of the OMV‐laden CSF, which is proportional to the surface areas of the brain (70%) and spinal cord in mice. We propose that all paravertebral lymph nodes monitor the segmental regions of the brain and spinal cord as immediate sentinel lymph nodes against the central nervous system.

Introduction

Brain waste drains via cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) into systemic circulation through lymphatics. Initial evidence that CSF drains into the systemic circulation via the lymphatic system was demonstrated in large animal models (Bradbury and Cole 1980; Boulton et al. 1998), and with advances in detection technologies, this phenomenon was also confirmed in small animals (Mathieu et al. 2013; Louveau et al. 2015). Studies using transgenic mouse models revealed that meningeal lymphatic vessels are functionally linked to CSF‐mediated immune regulation, waste clearance, and metabolic diseases (Aspelund et al. 2015). Moreover, aging was shown to alter CSF flow dynamics (Ma et al. 2017; Sarker et al. 2023), which may be influenced by lymphatic vessel contractility (Kwon et al. 2017). These alterations in CSF flow can modulate immune cell activity around the CSF compartments (Jacob et al. 2019), suggesting a potential link to various neurological disorders (Patel et al. 2019).

Paravertebral lymph nodes are known to be initial collectors of brain‐derived fluids, including small molecules, macromolecules, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and even cells. Previously unknown pathways from CSF to meningeal lymphatics were elucidated by discovering dural lymphatics in humans and mice (Louveau et al. 2015). Subsequent studies identified defects or gaps within arachnoid barrier cell layers (Kutomi and Takeda 2020; Møllgård et al. 2023; Smyth et al. 2024). The earliest report, using classical histopathology in pigs, described these as cranial arachnoid granulation like dural gaps (CAG‐DGs) penetrating into sagittal sinuses (Kutomi and Takeda 2020). Identifying routes from CSF to dural lymphatics drove investigations of defects in arachnoid barrier cells containing claudin‐11. Møllgård et al. (2023) found discontinuities near dural veins in mice, corroborated by Smyth et al. (2024), who termed these openings “arachnoid cuff exits.” These exits surround bridging veins connecting brain tissue to dural venous sinuses. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast injected intrathecally in humans accumulates adjacent to these bridging veins, suggesting outward drainage of CSF without reverse inflow from dura to subarachnoid space.

CSF lymphatic drainage has been visualized using a wide range of contrast materials. Quantum dot–based fluorescence imaging (Mathieu et al. 2013, Louveau et al. 2015), near‐infrared dyes such as 40 kDa poly(ethylene) glycol–conjugated IRDye680 (Ma et al. 2017), and radiolabeled albumin using Cu‐64 (Sarker et al. 2023) demonstrated lymphatic flow with various resolution and retention characteristics. Additionally, intrathecal gadobutrol‐enhanced MRI provided high‐resolution structural imaging of CSF drainage (Eide et al. 2018). However, the exact passages outside the central nervous system (CNS) remained unclear. Discoveries such as sacral lymph nodes indicated diverse drainage routes following intrathecal and intra‐cisterna magna injections (Ma et al. 2017; Kwon et al. 2017). Recently, the delineation of drainage via cribriform plates to cervical lymph nodes through the nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus improved understanding of drainage paths beyond dura (Yoon et al. 2024). Still, quantitative proportions and main outlets for CSF drainage remain uncertain. Additionally, drainage behaviors of diverse solutes ranging from electrolytes, small molecules, macromolecules, and EVs are largely unknown.

Previously, we demonstrated that radiolabeled albumin persists within lymph nodes after intrathecal injection for mice (Sarker et al. 2023, 2022). Due to the limited volume and production rate of CSF in mice, intra‐cisterna magna injection is technically challenging and difficult to reproduce. Therefore, we adopted an intrathecal approach, which required fine‐tuning of both injection volume and rate to avoid retention at the injection site or excessive spread. However, radiolabeled albumin posed an additional challenge, as it tends to efflux from lymph nodes after arrival (Sarker et al. 2023). The problem may have been solved by using the ratio of current lymph node activity over the tentative input function or adopting 24‐h delayed imaging.

In this study, we used Escherichia coli‐derived EVs, that is, outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) (Kim et al. 2017; Won et al. 2023), to quantify the lymphatic drainage. OMVs provide advantages over albumin, being larger particulate materials more readily retained in lymph nodes. This allows us to focus on key lymphatic structures—superficial and deep cervical, gastric, pancreaticoduodenal, lumbar aortic, medial iliac, and sacral lymph nodes—that are likely to serve as initial collectors of CSF‐derived contents along cranial and spinal drainage pathways. We labeled OMVs via click chemistry, directly conjugating dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO)‐tagged OMVs to [64Cu]Cu‐chelator‐N3, thus eliminating separation steps post‐labeling (Lee et al. 2018; Park et al. 2019). Micro positron emission tomography (microPET) enabled repeated quantitative whole‐body scans without background interference (Ko et al. 2016; Son et al. 2020). We chose PET imaging because it offers sensitivity and quantitative accuracy needed to follow radiolabeled EVs—nanoparticles (20∼100 nm)—in CSF at picomolar doses, without perturbing fluid dynamics. MRI requires millimolar Gd‐based agents and reports only bulk diffusion, while computed tomography lacks soft‐tissue contrast at low particle loads; neither methods can noninvasively resolve EV drainage kinetics or provide absolute tracer quantification in deep CNS regions. By leveraging the distinct properties of EVs and the sensitivity of PET imaging, our study provides a robust platform for visualizing and quantifying CSF drainage pathways.

Results

OMV Labeling With 64Cu

OMVs were isolated from Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) ΔmsbB as previously reported (Won et al. 2023). The average diameter of the isolated OMVs was 20.3 ± 1.1 nm, consistent with the previous report (Suppl. Figure S1A). SDS‐PAGE demonstrated the protein composition of OMVs different from whole‐cell lysates (Suppl. Figure S1B). Notably, an outer membrane protein OmpA was enriched in OMVs, whereas a cytosolic protein FtsZ was scarcely detected, as previously reported (Won et al. 2023) (Suppl. Figure S1C).

Radio‐labeling of OMVs. (A) Schematic of radio‐labeling on OMVs. Alkyne‐tethered OMVs were conjugated with [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 via click chemistry. (B) Labeling efficiency and quality of radio‐labeled OMVs were analyzed using instant thin‐layer chromatography.

Purified OMVs were radio‐labeled to trace their in vivo biodistribution across different administration routes. The surfaces of the vesicles were covalently coupled with DBCO molecules to the amine residues of vesicular membrane proteins exposed on the extracellular surface. The DBCO‐labeled OMVs were subsequently coupled with chelated 64Cu radionuclide‐NOTA‐N3 complexes using alkyne‐azide click chemistry (Figure 1A). The radio‐labeling efficiency was determined using radio instant thin layer chromatography‐silica gel (iTLC‐SG) with 0.1 M citric acid as the mobile phase after every radio‐labeling procedure. The retention factor (R f) of 64Cu was 0.9–1.0, and that of [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 was 0.5–0.6. R f of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs was 0.1–0.2, and the radio‐labeling efficiency of these vesicles was 99% (Figure 1B). Thin‐layer chromatographic analysis confirmed the preferential chelation of 6 4Cu with NOTA‐N3 and spatial localization of radioactivity on OMVs, which implies the efficient coupling process of alkyne‐azide click chemistry and the effective removal of excess free DBCO molecules via diafiltration after the coupling with DBCO on the surface of OMVs. We observed that [64Cu]Cu‐OMVs exhibited comparable stability in both human serum and phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4, maintaining approximately 99% stability up to 48 h (Suppl. Figure S2).

After undergoing labeling with DBCO and Cu, OMVs were assessed to determine whether their characteristics were altered before and after diafiltration to remove unbound DBCO. Non‐radioactive Cu was used for characterization of OMVs. The size distribution was not significantly altered: OMVs, 20.3 ± 1.1 nm; DBCO‐labeled OMVs, 22.8 ± 2.2 nm; and Cu‐labeled OMVs, 21.9 ± 2.0 nm (Suppl. Figure S3A). There were also no significant differences in the yields (both in total protein amounts and total particle numbers) before and after the labeling process (Suppl. Figure S3B, S3C). SDS‐PAGE revealed comparable protein composition profiles across all OMV samples (Suppl. Figure S3D). Immunoblotting assays further confirmed consistent levels of OmpA when the equal amounts of protein were loaded. The cytosolic protein FtsZ was not detected in any of the OMV samples (Suppl. Figure S3E). Furthermore, murine macrophages (RAW264.7) were treated with non‐labeled OMVs, DBCO‐labeled OMVs, or Cu‐labeled OMVs to assess the potential impact of DBCO‐ and Cu‐ labeling on OMV activity. All OMVs mediated comparable, dose‐dependent release of TNF‐α and IL‐6, indicating that the labeling procedure did not affect vesicle‐associated activity (Suppl. Figure S3F).

64Cu‐labeled OMVs in the Gastrointestinal Tract After Oral Administration

After oral administration, 64Cu‐labeled OMVs were traced in the gastrointestinal tract (Figure 2; Suppl. Movie 1). From the esophagus to the rectum, the 64Cu radioactivity remained in or around the luminal boundary and did not cross the mucosa‐epithelial barrier in mice. Beyond the expected half‐life of gastric emptying, 64Cu radioactivity remained longer. Considering that the mice were fed ad libitum between imaging sessions (especially at 2, 4, and 8 h and the next day), the 64Cu radioactivity was late in excretion from the gastric lumen. These results suggest that the 64Cu radioactivity remained stable with the OMVs in the gastrointestinal milieu, although the labeled 64Cu could be released or recycled in the gastric lumen until 24 or 48 h after oral administration.

Differences in the Distribution of 64Cu‐Labeled OMVs After Subcutaneous and Intramuscular Administration

After subcutaneous administration to the right forelimb and right hindlimb, subcutaneous collection activity was maintained around the administration sites until 48 h (Figure 3A; Suppl. Movie 2A). A very slow flow directed the radioactivity to a dermal flow (or backflow) with scattered localization near and far from the administration site. Since we did not separate free [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 from 64Cu‐labeled OMVs, the radioactivity of this rare impurity ([64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3) was released via the bladder. Until 8 h [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 was slowly released, which indicates that the lymph flow from the subcutaneous areas after the administration of [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 took much longer than that after the intravenous administration (Figure 5A). Notably, only subcutaneous administration via the right hindlimb resulted in signals from the thoracic duct outlet to the subclavian vein. After entering systemic blood circulation, hepatic excretion may result in signals in the intestine and even in the rectum at 8 h. The remaining subcutaneous radioactivity at the sites of administration suggested that the radioactivity in situ was integrated within professional phagocytes (e.g., residential macrophages) or possibly non‐professional phagocytes such as stromal and parenchyma cells under the skin.

Intramuscular administration disclosed a slightly different distribution from that of subcutaneous administration (Figure 3B; Suppl. Movie 2B). The retention of radioactivity at the administration site was identical to that after subcutaneous administration. The radioactivity appeared in the bladder after an hour but rapidly disappeared afterwards (no activity after 2 h), which suggests that interstitial [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 was removed within 2 h. This initial return of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs to the systemic blood circulation enabled the 64Cu‐labeled OMVs to be excreted into the intestine, resulting in a faint intestinal radioactivity signal. During this normal drainage and excretion of [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 and 64Cu‐labeled OMVs, the radioactivity was retained in the lymph node, probably due to the capture of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs by sentinel lymph nodes. The nearest and brightest lymph node was definitely the regional sentinel, and the second or third might be sequential or simultaneous drainage from the first lymph node.

Capture of Distributed 64Cu‐labeled OMVs Along the Communicating Lymphatic Channels After Intraperitoneal Administration

After intraperitoneal administration, 64Cu‐labeled OMVs remained in the peritoneum until 48 h, and the impurities [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3, which were drained from the peritoneum to the systemic blood circulation, were excreted via the kidneys and bladder within 2 h (Figure 4; Suppl. Movie 3). Time‐series images showed broad expansion of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs throughout the peritoneal cavity, consistent with dissemination along the peritoneal surfaces. From the beginning, 64Cu‐labeled OMVs drained from the peritoneum to the pleural cavity via diaphragmatic lacunae and stomata which connects the pleural cavity and peritoneum. They were captured by scattered lymph nodes around the pleural cavity and mediastinum that reached the cervical lymph nodes. The radioactivity of these scattered lymph nodes did not change on qualitative readout, which was confirmed by quantification.

Capture of Circulating 64Cu‐labeled OMVs by the Spleen and Liver After Intravenous Administration

After intravenous administration, the phagocytic system of the spleen and liver took up almost all 64Cu‐labeled OMVs; however, the impurities of [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 were immediately excreted via the kidneys and bladder, and the liver appeared to degrade the 64Cu‐labeled OMVs (Figure 5A, B; Suppl. Movie 4). The degradation products of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs were traced with sequential PET imaging, and we identified two methods of excretion. The first method involves the use of [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA or [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA peptides, which are soluble and excreted via the kidneys and bladder; this process continues until the next day. With this excretion of soluble 64Cu‐labeled metabolites, another group of metabolites associated with cholic acids were excreted into the intestines (1–8‐h images) and the rectum (8‐h image). After 24 h, radioactivity was continuously maintained by the phagocytic system of the spleen and the liver.

Biodistribution of 64Cu‐Labeled OMVs After Intrathecal Administration

We focused on three points regarding intrathecal administration. First, we focused on the volume, rate, and route of administration. In accordance with our previous reports (Patel et al. 2019; Sarker et al. 2022), we administered the sample in a 6‐µL volume at a rate of 700 nL/min via the L5/6 disc space. Second, to adjust the administration volume and desired radioactivity, we pre‐labeled NOTA‐N3 with 64Cu and labeled them with DBCO‐OMVs using click reaction. and did not perform a separation procedure to remove the unlabeled [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3. Labeling efficiencies of 95% or higher was confirmed by instant liquid radio chromatography. Third, we investigated possible drainage, circulation, recirculation, metabolism and excretion or no absorption of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs and their impurities. Notably, [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 and rare hepatic degradation are important. We used intrathecal administration. This mimicked oral administration, as we administered the radiotracer into the seemingly closed compartment, very slow drainage from the CSF space and un‐perturbing administration of the radiotracer made the situation physiologic and apparently in a closed compartment.

Immediately after the intrathecal administration, radioactivity was detected around the spinal cord; after an hour, it reached the basal cistern and dorsal parts of the cistern; at the paravertebral lymph nodes from the bilateral lower (deep) cervical, gastric/pancreaticoduodenal, lumbar aortic, medial iliac, and bilateral sacral levels (Figure 6A, B; Suppl. Movies 5, 6, 7). The radioactivity of the eight lymph nodes and bilateral superficial cervical (also known as mandibular) lymph nodes, that is, 10 lymph nodes in total, appeared in the 1‐h images and was sustained throughout the 2–24‐h images (Figure 6C). In terms of lymph node uptake, the mouse shown was representative of the intrathecally injected group. The other mice exhibited a similar pattern, with a slightly delayed appearance of the upper abdominal lymph nodes, a delayed peak in cervical lymph node uptake (larger radioactivity in the 2 h image than in an hour image), and the absence of contralateral sacral lymph node signals. Notably, the bilateral and intense uptake in both the deep and superficial cervical lymph nodes was consistently observed. The consistency of deep and superficial cervical uptake, both bilateral and bright, was prominent.

The bodily systemic faint uptake showed a similar pattern. Initially, there was no radioactivity in the bladder, which implies that the impurity [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA did not appear in the bladder after an hour in all mice. In all three mice, radioactivity slightly appeared in the liver and subsequently in the intestine. It also faintly but continuously appeared in the bladder. This finding was considered to show the drainage of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs after remaining/saturating in paravertebral/superficial cervical lymph nodes. 64Cu‐labeled OMVs drained via the lymphatic channels, entered the systemic circulation and reached the liver to be slowly metabolized and excreted via the kidneys and hepatobiliary tract. In a mouse, the radioactivity was quite prominent in the liver and intestine, including the rectum, at 8 h, which was reminiscent of the oral administration case. This in vivo distribution of OMVs within the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was more clearly visualized following an incidentally leaked intrathecal administration, providing supportive evidence for the connection between the lymphatic and systemic circulatory systems (Suppl. Movie 8).

The quantified results revealed a segmental distribution of drainage: upper (superficial and deep cervical 29.3% and 48.0%, respectively), 77.3%; middle (pancreaticoduodenal, gastric, lumbar aortic, and medial iliac), 11.4%; caudal (sacral), 11.3%. The appearance time or buildup and sustained radioactivity imply that all initial lymph nodes were the first stop of the drained 64Cu‐labeled OMVs. Considering the behavior of the lymphatic‐lymph node flow after a subcutaneous/intramuscular administration, the capture/keeping mechanism of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs enabled the visualization of the first stop from the CSF of the brain and spinal cord.

Discussion

In this in vivo imaging experiment, we investigated the location and function of paravertebral lymph nodes to monitor the brain and spinal cord in mice. After intrathecal administration of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs around the lower lumbar CSF space without leakage, ten lymph nodes were identified as the first stops. This finding was obtained using two characteristics of PET imaging: (1) 64Cu‐labeled OMVs could be caught by the lymph nodes that met for the first time within the lymphatic vessels; (2) 64Cu‐labeled OMVs administered in a compartment will release radioactive emission, which can be imaged with ultimate contrast (infinite contrast) in cases of oral, subcutaneous, intramuscular, and most importantly intrathecal routes but not intravenous route. We believe that the CSF is drained within this amount (fraction) to the lymph nodes of the cervical level, lower thoraco‐abdominal level, and lumbo‐sacral level.

The advantages of radiolabeled EVs were first reported by us using luminal [99mTc]Tc‐HMPAO labeling of EV‐mimetic nanovesicles (ENVs) (Hwang et al. 2015; Choi and Lee 2016). The following trials focused on the lipid membrane labeling including our own failing to follow the long‐term fates of EVs or ENVs in vivo (Hwang et al. 2015; Choi and Lee 2016; Lai et al. 2014, 2015; Gangadaran et al. 2018). Non‐covalent bond labeling has been known to cause problem in tracing the biodistribution of EVs or ENVs in its integrity. Among them, fluorescent dye was the best known as the detached dye permanently stays in the body and show its own distribution (Lai et al. 2014, 2015; Gangadaran et al. 2018). Brain visualization was the most cumbersome in aspect of this approach. Iodine or other mimetics ([99mTc]TcO4−) was also employed for thyroid visualization (Gangadaran et al. 2018; Hong et al. 2021; Hwang et al. 2021). Luminal [99mTc]Tc‐HMPAO labeling of EVs or ENVs also posed challenges for image interpretation. Since the luminal metabolite of [99mTc]Tc‐HMPAO is hydrophilic, once the EV/ENV is leaked, it follows the hepatobiliary excretion pathway through the liver and intestines (Hwang et al. 2015). This hepatobiliary excretion pathway of hydrophilic metabolite of [99mTc]Tc‐HMPAO was identical to the excretion pathway of the metabolized 64Cu‐labeled OMVs in this investigation. The difference was that the metabolized products of the OMV/ENV membranes tightly held the [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐peptide fragments and selected their route to excrete via hepatobiliary or renal pathways. We adopted the click reaction of alkyne‐azide and prelabeled [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 (Park et al. 2019). The reaction was almost complete with a small amount of unlabeled [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3, which made the after‐labeling separation procedure unnecessary. On the other hand, the small amount of unlabeled [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 yielded the chances to understand and interpret the sequential biodistribution and differences of originally unlabeled [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 and the metabolized [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 with or without peptide fragments.

Several studies suggested that the biodistribution of EVs can vary under certain conditions, potentially due to intrinsic changes in the parent cells, such as those associated with aging or disease (Goldberg 2021; Duggan et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2025). This phenomenon not only underscores a critical feature of EV biology that warrants rigorous scientific investigation, but also highlights an unresolved question regarding the mechanisms governing these distributional alterations. In this study, we propose that radioisotope‐based in vivo tracking offers a highly stable, non‐invasive, and biologically compatible imaging strategy that is fundamentally distinct from conventional methods. This approach enables the observation of EV behavior in vivo without disrupting systemic physiology. Our findings with OMVs, together with previously reported observations for ENVs (Kim et al. 2024), support this concept, as distinct biodistribution patterns were observed between OMVs and ENVs following comparable administration routes. These differences are readily visualized in the present OMVs data (Figures 4 and 6A,B) and in the previously reported ENVs data (Kim et al. 2024), further emphasizing the sensitivity and utility of our imaging platform.

Previous studies have shown that OMVs effectively induce long‐term anti‐tumor responses (Kim et al. 2017) and later mass‐produced OMVs in the following report to be used as stimulant for CD8+ T cell in combination cancer immunotherapy (Won et al. 2023). Intra‐tumoral routes were favored as booster and the whereabouts of OMVs raised the curiosity. We examined this question in the present investigation and found that most of the subcutaneously administrated 64Cu‐labeled OMVs stayed within injection sites and small amount revealed the lymphatic outflow from muscles of subcutaneous spaces (Figure 3A; Suppl. Movie 2A). We observed that the clearance patterns of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs varied depending on the route of administration, likely due to the differences in lymphatic drainage pathways for each route. Subcutaneous injections enabled broader diffusion through the superficial lymphatic system and caused drainage into nearby and distant lymph nodes, whereas intramuscular injections showed more localized absorption with faster entry into the bloodstream due to the rich vascularization of muscle tissue. More diffuse but significant drainage to the blood vessels via thoracic and right lymphatic duct (Hu et al. 2024) were followed by the hepato‐biliary (gall bladder) excretion to the intestines. The drained lymphatics of the intramuscular site carried 64Cu‐labeled OMVs to the first (sentinel) lymph node and secondary ones. This lymph‐node uptake characteristics was expected and proven in this comparison study of the fates of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs using various routes. The imaging finding of one route reciprocally and directedly supported another. The lymph node uptake characteristics of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs were finally contrasted with the absence of this characteristics of a mammalian (human) mesenchymal stem cell‐derived 64Cu‐labeled ENVs (Kim et al. 2024); this is another example that every group of EVs are different in their behavior in vivo (Kang et al. 2021; Iannotta et al. 2024; Hagey et al. 2023). Thus, we speculate that 64Cu‐labeled OMVs could not be used for drug delivery via oral route. This speculation contradicts the report on the systemic uptake of orally administrated DiD‐labeled OMVs (Jones et al. 2020), which might have been due to fluorescent dye released from OMVs (Lai et al. 2015; Gangadaran et al. 2018; Song et al. 2022). We suspect that the intestinal luminal space is full of OMVs and EVs derived from various microbiome and host itself and added trace amount of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs were released via esophago‐gastro‐intestinal‐colic‐rectal peristalsis (Figure 2; Suppl. Movie 1). Two items must be clarified: gastric radioactivity remained for an extended period, though evacuated at last 2 days after, considering minutes of gastric liquid emptying and tens of minutes of gastric solid emptying.

Intrathecal administration was prominent in taking advantage of nuclear imaging, that is, click reaction‐labeled OMVs PET, compartmental injection and follow‐up for tracing the fate at the lymph node stopping OMVs in vivo. Here, we visualized and quantified the CSF‐lymphatic drainage in mice. The clearance of CSF radioactivity of OMVs was slower than albumin similarly labeled with click reaction (Sarker et al. 2023) (Supp. Figure S4). The lymph nodes as the first stops were deep cervical lymph nodes (bilateral), pancreaticoduodenal and gastric (one for each), lower lumbar aortic (one at the midline), medial inguinal (one at midline) and sacral (bilateral) lymph nodes (Van den Broeck et al. 2006; Abu‐Hijleh et al. 1995; Shinohara 1997). There were eight lymph nodes in total and the superficial cervical lymph nodes (bilateral) were also involved. Individual variation could be explained by the amount of total administered volume of intrathecal 64Cu‐labeled OMVs and their fraction of the CSF of each mouse. The rate was exactly controlled, and the total amount was identical (Sarker et al. 2023, 2022), but when we scrutinized the early images, the time of arrival of radioactivity to the basal cistern and the paravertebral lymph nodes differed mouse to mouse. Another individual variation was the unilateral non‐visualization of the sacral lymph node and relatively varying intensity of the abdominal lymph nodes. In contrast, superficial and deep cervical lymph nodes showed consistent symmetric radioactivity and ratio of radioactivity of both lymph nodes over the radioactivity of CSF space.

We present our findings in the context of the well‐established connections among the brain, spinal cord, and lymphatic and circulatory systems of the body. The skull and vertebrae have bone marrow, whose marrow sinus veins are connected with the dura sinus veins via bridging veins (Cai et al. 2019). Dura veins are connected with the brain parenchyma via other bridging veins (Smyth et al. 2024). The arachnoid barrier cell layer is tightly closed with claudin‐11, except for structured gaps formerly referred to as CAG‐DGs in pigs (Kutomi and Takeda 2020), which were later found in mice as defects in the arachnoid barrier cell sheets between dura vein and subarachnoid space (Møllgård et al. 2023). This gap/defect was again reported in both mice and humans and is called the arachnoid cuff exit (Smyth et al. 2024). We now know that the subarachnoid space CSF is not entirely separated from the dura extracellular matrix, and using these gaps/defects/exits of fluid, small molecules, macromolecules, and particulate materials such as EVs can move out of the brain and spinal cord. Routinely and/or if needed, skull/vertebrae bone marrow cells (mostly immune) can commute to and from the brain/spinal cord parenchyma and subarachnoid CSF spaces (Brioschi et al. 2021; Cugurra et al. 2021).

Fluorescent dyes labeled with various sized materials (Louveau et al. 2015; Aspelund et al. 2015), radio‐labeled inulin/proteins (Sarker et al. 2022; Iliff et al. 2012; Kress et al. 2014), and MRI contrast dyes (Iliff et al. 2013; Albayram et al. 2022) were successfully used to visualize or quantify the drainage of CSF, probably via the lymphatic system. The investigators dedicated a lot of effort to understand the condition and obstacles or perturbation effects upon these normal CSF‐lymphatic drainage systems. Here, we used a lymph node seeker, 64Cu‐labeled OMVs (Figure 6A, B; Suppl. Movies 5, 6, 7), and an established radiochemistry and animal PET system to identify the first stop of CSF‐lymphatic drainage in mice. The 64Cu‐labeled OMVs were intrathecally administered, circulated throughout the cisterns and peri‐spinal cord CSF spaces, and casually drained to the regional lymphatics via adjacent lymphatics (Sarker et al. 2023, 2022). 64Cu‐labeled OMVs in lymphatic vessels that emanate from inside the arachnoid barrier cell layer around the brain and spinal cord are destined to be captured by the lymph nodes; they remain there indicating that they originated from CSF.

Based on these observations concerning the situation of the lymph nodes and central nervous system, we propose that the brain and spinal cord release waste via the CSF to the lymphatic system, which benefits the gaps/exits/defects of arachnoid barrier cells. Draining lymphatics, which were visualized using fluorescent dye‐labeled ovalbumin injected into the intrathecal spaces and subsequently found in the epidural lymphatics, gathered together with regional lymph nodes via valves and/or lymphatic vascular contraction and hydrostatic pressure differences. Due to an unknown mechanism, the lymph nodes only captured the OMVs but not other substances. Drained lymphatic was recently clarified to move via nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus and easily reach the superficial or deep cervical lymph nodes (Yoon et al. 2024). The perineural sheaths of cranial nerves have been proposed as routes for CSF to move through systemic lymphatic channels outside the brain/spinal cord (Ma et al. 2019; Proulx 2021). However, the cribriform plates with olfactory nerves have been reported to be the main routes with many submucosal plexus forms in the vast nasopharyngeal area (Yoon et al. 2024), whereas the dorsal dura meningeal lymphatics also drain CSF waste.

We suggest that the CSF‐lymphatic drainage follows the general rule of interstitial space‐lymphatics interaction, i.e., “demand is accompanied by drainage”. The demand follows the supply and surface area of the dura of the brain and spinal cord. The waste supplied from the brain and spinal cord demands removal from the CSF, which is necessary for the surface to pass through. Despite the absence of the measured dural surface area, we estimated the surface area of the brain and spinal cord using their reported weights (Roderick et al. 1973; Badea et al. 2007). The weights of the male C57BL/6 brain and spinal cord were 488 and 132 mg, respectively, whose surface areas were converted to 62 and 26 mm3, respectively. The surface area of the brain was 70% of the total brain and spinal cord area, which was similar to the 77% of uptake among the administered 64Cu‐labeled OMVs at the cervical lymph nodes. We propose that the cervical lymph nodes receive drainage not only from the cervical spinal cord but also from the brain. In addition to the demand, the local condition of the lymph nodes themselves, which is related to the position, and probable latent inflammation may have affected the drainage proportion. The abdominal and pelvic lymph nodes varied the most (Sarker et al. 2022; Kress et al. 2014) (Suppl. Table S1), and the sacral lymph nodes showed definitive asymmetry (Figure 6A; Suppl. Table S1; Suppl. Movies 5, 6, 7). This large variation, including sometimes non‐visualization of one side, reflects the physiological diversity of the CSF‐lymphatic drainage of each individual mouse. Individual variation should be expected in any mouse; otherwise, normal states of CSF‐lymphatic drainage are presumed to vary.

The mechanism by which OMVs affect the lymph nodes remains to be determined, and the variation in the lymph node‐seeking ability of other bacterial or prokaryotic OMVs or EVs is also unknown. Mammalian or other human cell‐derived EVs or ENVs of various sizes are of particular interest. We are certain that the highly purified and preserved EVs, ENVs, and OMVs should be labeled with chelator pre‐bound 64Cu or other relevant radionuclides. No separation procedures are preferred to preserve the specificity of labeling (Bq/µg) and not to damage the EVs, ENVs, or OMVs. Serial imaging is recommended, or at least delayed imaging is mandatory. Since membrane binding is very tight and robust in every route of administration, we recommend that investigators interpret the linear rectum radioactivity in oral administration, sub‐skin faint but diffuse radioactivity in subcutaneous administration, and gallbladder radioactivity in the hepatobiliary excretion and differential initial renal/bladder radioactivity or delayed/sustaining bladder activity after the hepatic metabolism. The most important factor was the degree of lymph node radioactivity. Our radiolabeling strategy for OMVs enabled the observation of physiological CSF dynamics without artificially inducing or disrupting natural flow (Suppl. Figure S5). This approach allowed us to identify lymph node seekers in proximity to the CSF pathways. Based on this interpretation, we propose that the radioactivity observed in lymph nodes following intrathecal injection reflects the first physiological stop or drainage point within the CSF outflow route, rather than a result of nonspecific diffusion or systemic redistribution. This imaging method can now be used to evaluate the effect of any disease‐modifying treatment in mice to determine whether these treatments improve the CSF‐lymphatic drainage. If these works are reproduced, the CSF‐lymphatic drainage can also be easily evaluated after drainage modulation and/or stimulation (Hablitz and Nedergaard 2024; Jiang‐Xie et al. 2024; Murdock et al. 2024). It remains to be determined whether the lymph node retention and CSF–lymphatic drainage behavior observed in this study is unique to OMVs or represents a more general property of EVs. Further studies will be essential not only for distinguishing OMV‐specific behavior from broader EV‐associated transport mechanisms, but also for defining how the lymph node–seeking property of OMVs is influenced by age (Da Mesquita et al. 2018). In particular, additional validation using mammalian (human) EVs or ENVs under closely matched experimental conditions will be required to determine whether the present findings are OMV‐specific or represent a more general vesicle‐associated phenomenon.

Methods

Mice

Seven‐ to 8‐week‐old C57BL/6 male mice were used for imaging. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Seoul National University (SNU211019‐1 and SNU‐230802‐3). All methods were performed according to relevant IACUC guidelines and regulations of Seoul National University and ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Preparation of OMVs

We used purified OMVs as previously reported (Won et al. 2023). Briefly, the E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔmsbB mutant (SL Bigen Co. Ltd., Korea) was cultured in particle‐free vegetable lysogeny broth at 30°C for 16 h. The cells were removed via centrifugation at 10,000 × g, and the cell‐free culture medium was subsequently filtered through a 0.2‐µm filter. The clarified conditioned medium was concentrated using a tangential flow filtration system with a 100‐kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO). OMVs in the concentrated medium were enriched via metal‐based precipitation. OMVs in the concentrated medium were precipitated by adding metal ions, which induced vesicle aggregation through charge neutralization and metal ion‐mediated bridging between negatively charged membrane components, particularly phosphate groups. The resulting OMV aggregates were collected by centrifugation at 13,000 × g, resolubilized in a buffer containing EDTA, and subsequently purified by size‐exclusion chromatography using a Sephacryl S‐300 column (17059901, Cytiva, USA). The final preparation was filtered through a 0.1 µm pore‐sized membrane and stored at ‐80°C until use.

Dynamic Light Scattering Analysis

The size distribution of OMVs was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instrument Ltd. UK). OMVs and DBCO‐ and DBCO‐Cu‐labeled OMVs (5 µg/mL) were subjected to the following parameters: laser wavelength = 633 nm; number of measurements = 5; measurement duration = 30 s; chamber temperature 25°C.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis

Total particle concentrations of OMVs and their labeled derivatives (DBCO‐ and Cu‐DBCO‐labeled OMVs) were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis with Nanosight LM10‐HS system using NTA software version 2.3 (Malvern Instrument Ltd., Malvern, UK) as previously reported (Won et al. 2023). Briefly, HEPES‐buffered saline (20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl) was used to dilute OMVs and their labeled derivatives and analyzed with the following parameters: volume: 0.5 mL; laser wavelength = 405 nm; camera level = 12; number of measurement = 3; measurement duration = 30 s; viscosity = 0.89 cP; chamber temperature = 25°C; detection threshold = 5; blur = auto; minimum track length = 10; minimum expected size = auto; range of detection = (1.0–2.5) × 109 particles/mL.

Protein Quantification

Total protein concentration of OMVs and their labeled derivatives (DBCO‐ and Cu‐DBCO‐labeled OMVs) were measured using the Bradford protein assay (5000006, Bio‐Rad, USA). Bovine serum albumin (P0914, Sigma Aldrich, USA) was used as the standard, and absorbance was recorded at 595 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX, Agilent, USA).

SDS‐PAGE and Immunoblotting Analysis

To assess the protein profile of E. coli BL21 (DE3) ΔmsbB WCL and OMVs, 2 µg of each sample was loaded onto 12% resolving gels. To analyze OMVs after labeling, the same amount of protein (2 µg/well) from OMVs, DBCO‐labeled OMVs, and Cu‐DBCO‐labeled OMVs were loaded under identical conditions. All gels were subjected to electrophoresis and stained with SimplyBlue SafeStain (LC6060, Invitrogen, USA), a Coomassie Brilliant Blue based stain, following the manufacturer's protocol.

Immunoblotting analysis of WCL and OMVs was performed by separating WCL (10 µg) and OMVs (1 µg) on 12% resolving gels, followed by transfer to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with rabbit anti‐OmpA (lab‐made; 1:50,000 dilution) or rabbit anti‐FtsZ (AS10‐715, Agrisera, Sweden; 1:2,000 dilution). After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated mouse anti‐rabbit IgG (sc‐2357, Santacruz Biotechnology, USA; 1:10,000 dilution). Bands were visualized using SuperSignal ELISA Femto Substrate (37074, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Labeled OMVs (DBCO‐labeled OMVs and Cu‐DBCO‐labeled OMVs) were analyzed with the same protocol.

In Vitro Cytokine Assay

Mouse macrophages (RAW264.7) were seeded in 96‐well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and treated with OMVs in a dose‐dependent manner for 24 h. Following treatment, cell‐free conditioned media were collected by serial centrifugations and stored at −80°C before the assay. The concentrations of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α and IL‐6) were determined using DuoSet ELISA kits (DY410 and DY406, R&D systems, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Click Chemistry Labeling of 64Cu to OMVs and the Radio‐labeling Efficiency

OMVs were radio‐labeled with 64Cu using an alkyne‐azide click chemistry‐based method (Figure 1). Two hundred micrograms (200 µg) of OMVs were incubated with 200 µg of DBCO‐NHS ester (FC‐6113, FutureChem, Korea) (Suppl. Figure S6A) in 1 mL of PBS, pH 7.4 containing 9.5% sucrose (PBSS) for 6 h at 25°C. The unbound DBCO was removed via diafiltration using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter devices with a 100‐kDa MWCO (UFC510096, Merck, USA), centrifuge at 4,550 × g for 5 min at 4°C.

The HCl solution of 64Cu was heated at 70°C until every solution that contained 64Cu was evaporated by N2 gas for 30 min. After the 64Cu solution had evaporated, the pH level was adjusted to 5 by adding 1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0). Eighteen nmol of NOTA‐N3 (FC‐6095, FutureChem, Korea) (Suppl. Figure S6B) was added to 64Cu, which was titrated to pH 5.0 and reacted at 80°C for 10 min. Ten nmol of [64Cu]Cu‐NOTA‐N3 were respectively added to the previously fully reacted DBCO‐labeled OMVs, followed by incubation at 4°C for 16 h. The radio‐labeling efficiency was determined using instant thin‐layer chromatography‐silica gel (iTLC‐SG, SGI0001, Agilent, USA) with 0.1 M citric acid as the mobile phase.

Stability Test for 64Cu‐Labeled OMVs in Human Serum

Fifty µL of [6 4Cu]Cu‐OMVs (200 µg in 1 mL of PBSS, pH 7.4) were mixed separately with 0.22 µm‐filtered human serum and PBS, pH 7.4 at a 1:4 volume ratio, and incubated at 25°C for 0, 4, 8, 24, and 48 h. The radiochemical purity under human serum and PBS, pH 7.4 conditions were analyzed via iTLC‐SG using 0.1 M citric acid as a mobile phase.

Imaging of 64Cu‐Labeled OMVs: Oral, Subcutaneous, Intramuscular, Intraperitoneal and Intravenous Administration

Imaging of 6 4Cu‐labeled OMVs was performed using GENISYS4 (SOFIE Biosciences, USA) and SimPET (Brightonix Imaging, Korea). All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Seoul National University Hospital.

Seven‐ to 8‐week‐old mice were imaged using GENISYS4 were scanned for 3 min, whereas those imaged using SimPET were scanned for 10 min via static acquisition after oral, subcutaneous (right forelimb and right hindlimb), intramuscular, intraperitoneal, and intravenous administrations (n = 3 per each administration group; Suppl. Figure S7) of adequate 64Cu radioactivity that was suitable for administration routes (Suppl. Table S2). During the imaging studies, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane containing 3 % O2. Images were reconstructed using the internal algorithm of the GENISYS4 software or SimPET software.

Imaging of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs: Intrathecal Administration

Imaging of 64Cu‐labeled OMVs was performed using GENISYS4 (SOFIE Biosciences). This animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Seoul National University Hospital. Seven‐week‐old mice were scanned for 5 min via static scanning after an intrathecal administration of 0.97 MBq 64Cu to three mice (Suppl. Figure S7; Suppl. Table S2). During the imaging study, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane containing 3% O2. All images were reconstructed using the internal algorithm of the GENISYS4 software.

To quantify the segmental distribution of 64Cu‐labeled OMV drainage from the CSF to various lymph nodes, we first measured the percentage of injected dose (%ID) that remained in the CSF at multiple time points (0, 1, 4, 8, and 24 h) across three subjects. The decay of 64Cu in the CSF was modeled using an exponential function. Assuming that the 64Cu‐labeled OMV input to each lymph node was proportional to the CSF output, we normalized the %ID uptake in the caudal, middle, and upper (deep and superficial) lymph nodes to the CSF output. Time‒activity curves were generated for each segment of the lymph nodes, and the cumulative activity was used to calculate the percentage of total drainage from the CSF to each segment of the lymph nodes. The upper segment was subdivided into deep and superficial lymph node segments.
