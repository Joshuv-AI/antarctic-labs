Main text

Wind power is a crucial component in the transition to carbon-neutral energy systems.1 By the end of 2025, global wind capacity exceeded 1,346 GW, representing the highest growth since 2020.2 To harness more energy, turbine capacities and efficiencies have steadily increased (Figure 1A). However, larger turbines introduce significant challenges, including increased structural demands and the efficiency constraint known as the Betz limit. First proposed by Albert Betz in 1919, this limit states that a wind turbine can capture at most 59.3% of the kinetic energy in wind. Due to land-use limitations for onshore wind deployment, attention has increasingly shifted toward far-offshore resources. However, the highly concentrated offshore wind supply chain results in prolonged construction timelines and elevated costs, particularly in Europe and the US.

High-altitude wind energy: Elevating wind energy from planar to spatial perspectives

Since wind speed generally increases with altitude, the atmosphere contains abundant exploitable wind energy resources. Consequently, high-altitude wind energy (HAWE) has emerged as a promising solution. As early as 1985, Loyd demonstrated that tethered wing-based HAWE systems could achieve power outputs up to five times higher than conventional wind turbines. HAWE systems typically operate at altitudes of 300–15,000 m and consist of an airborne device connected to a ground station via a tether. In terms of energy conversion, HAWE can be classified into two canonical configurations, namely, ground- and fly-generator systems. The former is predominantly represented by kite-based systems, which generate electricity by driving ground-based generators through tether tension. In contrast, the latter employs buoyant platforms, such as helium-filled aerostats, to lift wind turbine rotors to high altitudes for operation.

Transformative advantages in capacity and efficiency

HAWE systems offer significant advantages in both capacity and efficiency. Since wind power density is proportional to the cube of wind speed, higher-altitude winds enable substantially greater energy generation. Based on the fifth-generation ECMWF reanalysis dataset, wind resources in East Asia are increasingly dominated by the subtropical jet stream at higher altitudes, as topographic and frictional influences diminish. At altitudes of approximately 10 km, the climatological mean of maximum wind speeds approaches 40 m/s. On average, wind speed increases by ∼1.7–3 m/s for every 1 km rise in altitude. The upper-level jet stream is most pronounced over eastern and central China, the Korean Peninsula, Japan, the Yellow Sea, the East China Sea, and the Sea of Japan, exhibiting strong west-to-east zonal meandering. In these regions, wind power density is particularly high, averaging up to 29,881.8 W/m2.

Furthermore, the subtropical jet stream is primarily driven by latitudinal temperature gradients, and its intensity exhibits marked seasonal variability, resulting in significant seasonal differences in high-altitude wind power density. Wind resources are most abundant in winter, when the jet stream is stronger and more extensive, with an average wind power density of 9,434.99 W/m2. In contrast, summer exhibits the weakest wind resources, with an average wind power density of 1,947.1 W/m2. Statistical analysis indicates that for every 1 km increase in altitude, the mean wind power density rises by ∼123.2–866.8 W/m2. At an altitude of around 10 km, wind resources are on average 35.2 times greater than those near the ground (Figure 1B).

Specifically, the efficiency enhancement mechanism is achieved by the duct geometry, which creates a significant negative pressure zone at the exit. This induces axial suction, thereby augmenting the mass flow rate through the rotor plane compared to a conventional unconstrained rotor.4 While the Cp can exceed the classical Betz limit, this does not violate fundamental fluid mechanics. The Betz limit is theoretically derived for an open-disk rotor in an unconstrained flow field, whereas the diffuser-augmented HAWE system redefines the flow boundary conditions by acting as a flow concentrator. When the turbine is placed within this narrowest section, the system is referred to as a diffuser-augmented wind turbine.

At present, HAWE systems are primarily deployed in remote or infrastructure-constrained regions. Their large-scale deployment depends on economic competitiveness, commonly evaluated through the levelized cost of energy (LCOE). A 2021 report by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory indicated that HAWE currently exhibits relatively high LCOE due to its low technology maturity and immature supply chains.5 However, the Airborne Wind White Paper published by Airborne Wind Europe projects that, with sufficient investment, HAWE’s LCOE could decline below that of other renewable and fossil sources by 2035–2040, reaching about 40% lower than conventional wind by 2050.

Perspectives on HAWE systems for energy decarbonization

HAWE systems provide a novel pathway for energy decarbonization. This section highlights the differences between HAWE and conventional wind turbines in power forecasting and operational optimization, and discusses key requirements for long-term planning, short-term scheduling, and real-time control within the ground-airspace system.

Compared with conventional wind power forecasting, research on HAWE forecasting remains limited and mainly focuses on pumping- or drag-mode systems operating at only 100–200 m using mechanistic models. Models developed for near-surface turbines often degrade under high-altitude conditions or are not directly applicable. This work examines HAWE forecasting from two perspectives. First, in operating mode, conventional turbines have fixed locations and hub heights, whereas HAWE devices can adjust operating altitude to exploit stronger and more stable winds. Second, in data usage, conventional forecasting relies mainly on surface observations, whereas HAWE requires broader sources, including radar products, commercial aircraft reports, multi-level reanalysis, and vertically resolved numerical weather prediction.6 Therefore, HAWE forecasting must explicitly represent altitude-dependent wind variability, aerodynamic characteristics, and system configurations, making the problem more complex.

In maximum power tracking, altitude selection requires balancing additional energy capture against the energy consumption and wear associated with climbing and descending. Two approaches are commonly adopted. The first improves measurement coverage to reduce wind uncertainty, although dense sensor deployment is often impractical.7 The second quantifies uncertainty using historical data and incorporates it into model predictive control for altitude scheduling. Recent studies increasingly employ a two-stage framework, combining uncertainty prediction (e.g., Gaussian process regression) with Bayesian optimization to determine optimal operating altitude,8 while model predictive control enforces operational constraints and enables online adaptation.9

Within ground-airspace energy systems,10 research has mainly focused on ground-generator systems, whereas fly-generator systems can deliver more stable power by avoiding the energy-recovery phases. Future development should address three timescales. At the planning level, HAWE’s high capacity factor and capacity credit require reassessment of long-duration storage needs and multi-energy coordination, including hydrogen production and compressed CO2 storage. At the short-term operational level, coordinated dispatch with HAWE and conventional generators can enhance peak shaving and frequency regulation, while optimized converter and grid-connection designs can reduce voltage fluctuations. At the real-time control level, grid-forming control strategies, including virtual synchronous generators and fast-response storage, together with dedicated inverter standards, are required to ensure safe and secure operation.

Role of HAWE in supporting the low-altitude economy

At the intersection of the low-altitude economy and energy decarbonization, HAWE systems are emerging as versatile aerial infrastructure. Beyond energy generation, helium-based HAWE platforms can support low-altitude transport, airborne charging, traffic monitoring, and communication services. Equipped with modular sensing and relay payloads, they can also provide integrated power, communication, and surveillance functions in remote regions where conventional infrastructure coverage remains limited, such as the Tibetan Plateau and offshore islands.

The deployment of HAWE systems also faces challenges in airspace management, environmental impact, and operational safety. Operating altitudes must avoid civil aviation routes and coordinate with controlled low-altitude airspace, potentially under a combined approval-registration framework. Environmental concerns include risks to migratory birds and possible interference with flight paths. Safety risks include tether failure, entanglement, and aerostat escape. For safety assurance, a dual shutdown mechanism can be applied under abnormal conditions: first, disconnect the tether from the envelope, then de-energize the power cable and energy system. Tether motion control can reduce entanglement or knotting risk, while emergency deflation via vent valves or tear panels can reduce secondary accident risks.

Finally, HAWE’s feasibility is constrained by key technical bottlenecks. Material strength and system power-to-weight ratio determine altitude and payload capacity. In addition, the long-term durability of tethers and aerostat envelopes under extreme environmental conditions—such as strong ultraviolet radiation, thunderstorms, icing, and severe wind shear—critically affects life cycle cost and operational reliability. Overcoming these barriers is essential for large-scale deployment.
