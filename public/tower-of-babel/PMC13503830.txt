Integrated in silico and in vitro assessment of Azadirachta indica leaf extract against multi-drug resistant Citrobacter koseri and Staphylococcus saprophyticus
Authors: Md. Nazmussakib Shuvo, Tawsif Al Arian, Md. Mujahid Hossain, Shamal Kumer Sil, Salequl Islam, Md. Niamul Shahadat, Md. Hafizur Rahman, Nuhu Alam, Aparna Shil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Azadirachta indica (Neem) is well-known for its therapeutic potential against bacteria, while Staphylococcus saprophyticus and Citrobacter koseri are significant public health threats due to their potential to cause skin infections. This study aims to identify phytocompounds from the methanolic extract of A. indica leaves and evaluate their efficacy to inhibit those bacteria through in vitro and computational approaches. The bacteria were isolated from boil-derived pus, and the extract’s growth-inhibitory effect was examined by the disc diffusion method. Additionally, GC-MS identified phytochemicals remaining in the extract. The pharmacokinetic and ADMET properties of those phytochemicals were assessed via PKCSM and SwissADME servers. Screened phytocompounds were docked with functional proteins using AutoDock Vina and GLIDE. Following that, molecular dynamics simulation in GROMACS, post-simulation, and DFT analysis were conducted. Five of the sixteen phytocompounds with favorable pharmacokinetics and toxicity profiles were docked with Peptidoglycan D, D-transpeptidase MrdA of Citrobacter koseri, and D-alanine--D-alanine ligase of Staphylococcus saprophyticus. Among them, 2-(Acetoxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene, and Cyclopropane-1-carboxamide, 2-butyl-N-(5,6,7,8-tetrahydro-7,7-dimethyl-5-oxoquinazolin-2-yl)- demonstrated higher binding affinity, greater stability, and smaller energy gap in docking, simulation, and DFT analysis, respectively. This study highlights the two compounds as promising lead phytocompounds to inhibit those bacteria. Further in vivo investigations are essential to validate their therapeutic effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51906-z.

Introduction

Staphylococcus saprophyticus, a coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) species, is a part of the normal skin flora. It is primarily a commensal organism, but it can work as an opportunistic pathogen in infections. The infections caused by the organism are less common than those caused by Staphylococcus aureus, but should not be considered as mere infections. It is responsible for the infection in the skin and soft tissues of elderly and immunocompromised individuals1,2. On the other hand, like S. saprophyticus, several soft tissue and skin infections, including abscesses, bullous erysipelas, and folliculitis, can be caused by C. koseri. It may be found in healthy individuals. However, it is usually detected and isolated from the cellulitis lesions of the elderly, neonates, and immunocompromised patients3,4. This pathogen has the ability to sabotage the microbiome on the skin and also exhibits resistance to multiple drugs to complicate the treatment strategy5. To combat the situation, the drug can be produced using a conventional or computational approach6. The conventional approach takes ten to fifteen years to develop a drug, and the success rate is only 13%7–9. Comparatively, the in silico approach is less costly and less time-consuming than trying every stone and throwing them in the dark6. That’s why screening and testing the phytocompounds to speculate their potential as a drug and to narrow down the further testing materials using an in silico approach enhances the success of a drug development project. Drugs can be made from chemically synthesized molecules or natural compounds derived from plants. Chemical-based medication design screens synthetic molecules, while phytocompound-based design screens plant-derived compounds10,11.

Since ancient times, every component of the plant has been utilized in various forms of traditional medication and home remedies for the treatment of a wide range of human illnesses12,13. The majority of its occurrences are on the Indian subcontinent, which includes Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, Nepal, the Maldives, and Sri Lanka14. In this subcontinent, the tree is known as ‘Arishtha’ in Sanskrit, which translates as ‘a reliever of sickness’, which is why it is also called ‘Sarbaroganibarini’. Villagers continue to use the tree as a village pharmacy15. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), plant species having compounds with medicinal use are referred to as medicinal plants16,17. Globally, there have been a lot of studies on medicinal plants. Such plants offer an exciting source of starting materials for the development of innovative drugs with desired qualities like excellent efficiency, minimal side effects, and economic viability18. Based on surveys carried out by the WHO, over 80% of people utilize medicinal plants globally to address an extensive variety of medical conditions19. As unbelievable as it may sound, fossil fuel-related records provide evidence that people have been employing plants for medicinal purposes for around 60,000 years20. Numerous studies have demonstrated that plant-based constituents, i.e., phytocompounds, have a broad spectrum of biological activity, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antifungal, antimalarial, anticancer, and antioxidant properties21. For several thousand years, conventional medical practices like Ayurveda have used A. indica, also referred to as neem, an evergreen plant belonging to the Meliaceae family that is native to the Indian subcontinent22. Many diverse phytochemicals with distinct biological and medicinal properties can be extracted from numerous components of neem, including leaves, bark, seeds, flowers, fruits, and roots23–27. It has historically been used to treat diseases of the skin, infections, inflammatory disorders, fever, and dental disorders. It functions effectively for several epidermal disorders, including dermatitis, pimples, and eczema28. Because of its qualities as an antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antiparasitic, analgesic, wound-healing, and antimicrobial substance that not only improves healthcare but also addresses numerous cosmetic issues, it is currently the most well-established and popular skin care application. A. indica is said to be effective against infected wounds, septic burns, and a range of skin diseases29.

The study presented here was conducted to quantitatively analyze the phytochemicals of the crude methanolic extract obtained from the leaves of A. indica by GC-MS and evaluate their absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity using a computational method. Moreover, the efficacy of the plant extract is assessed on the isolated and identified organisms from the pus samples, and the effectiveness of the phytochemicals against the essential protein of C. koseri and S. saprophyticus by the binding affinity and stability using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation, respectively. The research will expand our understanding of the potential phytocompounds available in neem leaves and their significance in drug development approaches by analyzing the DFT of the lead compounds to ensure their pharmacokinetic properties against these organisms.

Materials and methods

In vitro methods

Collection of clinical samples

Three pus samples were collected from three different patients carrying skin furuncles (upon verbal permission) who attended Jahangirnagar University (JU) Medical Centre, Savar, Dhaka, for treatment. The use of informed verbal consent obtained from all patients, without written documentation, was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethical committee. A trained nurse collected pus samples in sterile transport medium following aseptic techniques30. The samples were transported immediately through a cold chain to the one health laboratory at the Microbiology Department, JU, for further microbiological investigations.

Isolation and identification of the bacterial colony

The samples were inoculated for enrichment in diluted tryptic soy broth (TSB) for four hours and then streaked on tryptic soy agar (TSA) and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. TSA can isolate and cultivate fastidious and non-fastidious germs from clinical specimens and other materials31. In accordance with Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology, the selected isolates were subjected to standard biochemical tests and were preserved in trypticase soy broth (TSB) containing 20% glycerol at -30°C32. To verify the species of the selected colonies, 16S rDNA amplification was conducted via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using a previously published method33. A QIA quick PCR purified kit (Qiagen Hilden, Germany) was used to purify the amplified products following the manufacturer’s protocol. The purified products were sent to Macrogen Inc., South Korea, for amplicon sequencing according to the Sanger sequencing method, and the nucleotide sequences of the samples were compared with the basic local alignment tool (BLAST) (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to confirm the species of the bacteria32,34. The MEGA 10.1.7 software (https://www.megasoftware.net/) was used to align and construct a dendrogram with the sequences of other organisms, which presented the greatest degree of similarity35. Following the identification of the isolates, the efficacy of the plant extract and antibiotics was assessed.

Extract preparation

Fresh leaves of A. indica were collected from the botanical garden of Jahangirnagar University. The leaves were subsequently washed with clean water and left to dry on a clean sheet for one week at room temperature, after which the dried leaves were powdered with a grinder. A Soxhlet apparatus was employed to retrieve extracts from the powdery form of the leaves. Ethanol was used as a solvent, and the extraction procedure continued for approximately 12 hours in the device36. The crude plant extract present at the bottom of the flask was subsequently obtained by evaporating ethanol under reduced pressure at 45°C, using a rotary evaporator6. The crude extract served two purposes: verifying the effectiveness against the isolates in comparison to prevailing antibiotics and screening of phytocompounds through GC-MS.

Assessment of the antibiotic susceptibility of the isolates

To determine the antibiotic sensitivity pattern, in vitro testing via the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method was employed37. Twelve antibiotics (Oxoid, UK) from eleven antimicrobial classes were tested, including amikacin (30 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), cefepime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), ertapenem (10 µg), trimethoprim (5 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), tigecycline (15 µg), azithromycin (15 µg), and tetracycline (30 µg), which were employed against both organisms. In addition, nalidixic acid (30 µg) was used for C. koseri, and vancomycin (30 µg) was utilized for S. saprophyticus. These classes of antibiotics are used because they are central for therapy and for defining multidrug resistance in Enterobacterales and other nosocomial pathogens38,39.

Assessment of the efficacy of the plant extract against the isolates

The disc-diffusion technique was employed to determine the effectiveness of the plant extracts against the isolates. Each plate contained four discs of plant extract. Two of them were impregnated with 100 and 200 µl of plant extract. The antibiotic discs (ertapenem and ampicillin) were treated as positive controls, and the other disc containing the solvent of the extract was considered a negative control6,40.

Phytocompounds screening gas chromatography-mass spectrometry

The extract was quantitatively and qualitatively characterized by the GC-MS technique41. The experiment was conducted at the Bangladesh Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (BCSIR) Laboratories in Dhaka, Bangladesh, using a Shimadzu 2010 Plus gas chromatograph fitted with a QP2020 Shimadzu mass detector. The column used was SH-Rxi-5Sil MS, which had dimensions of 0.25 m (DF), 0.25 mm (ID), and 30 m (L). The device was initially set to 80°C and maintained at that temperature for 2 minutes. Next, the oven temperature was raised to 280°C and held there for five minutes. The temperature of the injection port was maintained at 220°C, while the rate of the flow of helium was controlled at 1.72 ml/min. The voltage of ionization was 70 eV. The samples were injected in split-less mode. The range of the mass spectral scan was set to 45–350 (m/z). The temperature of the ion source was kept at 280°C, whereas the temperature of the interface was maintained at 230°C. The MS commenced at 7.4 minutes and ended at 50 minutes, including a solvent cut time of 7.4 minutes. The identification of the peaks was accomplished by the use of a computer program that matched the mass spectra with the library of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST 08, NIST 08s, and NIST 14), which also verified the component names, molecular weights, and structures. To enhance reliability, tentative assignments were cross-checked using retention indices, and only those compounds with NIST match scores exceeding 85% were retained in the results42,43. Eventually, we verified the efficacy of the phytochemicals against these organisms via computational approaches.

Computational methods

Prediction of the drug-like properties of the phytochemicals

The physicochemical and structural conditions of drug-like molecules are known as drug-likeness properties44. The drug-likeness properties of the 33 phytocompounds retrieved from GC-MS were determined by the SWISS ADME web server (http://www.swissadme.ch/)45,46. The canonical SMILES of the phytochemicals were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and used in the server. To evaluate the results, this server uses the five sets of rules proposed by Lipinski, Muegge, Veber, Ghose, and Egan47–51. The ADMET properties of those phytochemicals were determined.

Prediction of the ADMET properties of the phytochemicals

ADMET analysis looks at different physiological aspects of compounds, such as how they are absorbed, distributed, metabolized, excreted, and toxic. To predict these properties of the phytochemicals, a web server called pkCSM (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/prediction) works well52. There were a number of parameters that were determined by the server. These parameters included human intestinal absorption, blood-brain barrier, Caco-2 permeability, excretion, bioavailability, CYP1A2 inhibitor, CYP2D6 substrate, CYP2C3A4 inhibitor, CYP2C19 inhibitor, P gp-I and gp-II inhibitor, and drug-drug interactions. Moreover, it also determines the toxicity of the substance, which includes AMES toxicity, hERG inhibitors, hepatotoxicity, and the maximum dosage that could be tolerated. The canonical SMILES of the compounds were used to evaluate the ADMET properties, and the selected phytocompounds were subjected to the docking stage with the functional proteins of the bacteria.

Molecular docking with AutoDock Vina

Selection and preparation of the target protein

The peptidoglycan D,D-transpeptidase MrdA (Uniprot ID: A0A078LGK8) (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/A0A078LGK8/entry) was selected from C. koseri, and D-alanine--D-alanine ligase (Uniprot ID: Q49Z31) (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/Q49Z31/entry) was chosen from S. saprophyticus on the basis of the function of the protein. Peptidoglycan D, D-transpeptidase MrdA accelerates the formation of the cell wall of C. koseri53. On the other hand, D-alanine--D-alanine ligase is involved in the biosynthesis of the cell wall of S. saprophyticus54. The structure of the protein was retrieved from the AlphaFold server (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). Model confidence was assessed using per-residue predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores, which range from 0 to 100. Structures with pLDDT > 70 in predicted active sites were prioritized to minimize uncertainty55. prepared, and minimized for molecular docking with the help of the SwissPdb viewer (https://spdbv.unil.ch/disclaim.html#)56. The active sites were determined by Biovia Discovery Studio and CASTp (computer atlas of surface topography of proteins) (http://sts.bioe.uic.edu/castp/index.html?2cpk)57,58.

Preparation of the ligands for docking

The sdf files of the selected phytocompounds and antibiotics were downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)59. Ertapenem and ampicillin were selected as control antibiotics for C. koseri and S. saprophyticus, respectively. The sdf format of the ligands was converted into the pdbqt format, and minimization was performed with PyRx software (https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/downloads/)60. Following this procedure, the ligands were ready for docking.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking of a ligand with a protein is anticipated as a ligand’s preferred binding modes with a protein having a known three-dimensional structure61. AutoDock Vina was used for molecular docking62. A grid box was constructed to determine the binding site of the protein in the force field in silico. For peptidoglycan D,D-transpeptidase MrdA, the center of the grid box was X= -1.5319, Y= -0.0229, Z= 8.1983, and the size of the grid box was X= 78.8133, Y= 64.2907, Z= 161.3522 Å. For peptidoglycan D,D-transpeptidase MrdA, the center and the size of the grid box for another protein, D-alanine--D-alanine ligase, were X= 1.366, Y= -0.1771, Z= 0.7875, and the size of the grid box was X= 58.71635, Y= 51.64139, Z= 65.0999 Å. The grid box covered all the binding sites of the target protein. The command prompt was used to run the program, and nine poses were produced. The pose that belongs to the highest negative affinity score was selected. The output file with all the information regarding the binding affinity and stability was determined by molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The docking was performed three times, and the highest average value was considered for further evaluation. Additionally, the interactions were visualized with PyMol and Biovia Discovery Studio63,64.

Molecular docking with GLIDE

Grid-based ligand docking with energetics (GLIDE) (https://www.schrodinger.com/platform/products/glide/) is a widely used molecular docking program developed by Schrödinger. The LigPrep application was employed for the processing of the lead compounds9. The procedure entailed the introduction of hydrogen atoms, elimination of ionic compounds, and assimilation of ions within a pH range of 7 ± 2.0. The protein preparation module was utilized to construct the 3D structures of native as well as mutant protein variants. This involved the completion of absent side chains, incorporation of cap termini, generation of disulfide bonds, addition of hydrogen molecules, and the construction of bond ordering65. The process of minimizing energy was executed utilizing the OPLS3e force field66.

A grid box was constructed using the known binding sites of the proteins. The receptor grid generation module defaults for the van der Waals perimeter factor of scaling (1.0) and charging threshold (0.25) were used to determine the grid size. The ligand docking program and the GLIDE XP parameters were used to carry out GLIDE docking. Prior to the analysis of the medicinal compounds, a scaling factor of 0.80 was used to adjust the van der Waals radius, and a score threshold of 0.15 was employed as a standard criterion56.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rm{Docking score = avdW + bCoul + Hbond + Metal + Lipo + BuryP + RotB + Site}$$\end{document} The present context employs the variables ‘a’ and ‘b’ to represent the coefficient standards of van der Waals (vdW) and Coulomb (Coul) energies, respectively. In addition, the term ‘vdW’ denotes the van der Waals energy, whereas ‘Coul’ is indicative of the Coulomb energy. This orthogonal dual-docking approach (AutoDock Vina for initial high-throughput screening followed by GLIDE-XP for high-precision rescoring and consensus validation) reduces false positives and improves pose reliability, particularly for understudied targets. Subsequent 100-ns MD simulations assessed binding stability under physiological conditions, complemented by post-MD analyses (FEL, Rg, SASA, DCCM) to probe conformational ensembles and dynamical coupling. MM/GBSA provided physics-based relative binding free energies and decomposition of interactions, while DFT evaluated intrinsic chemical reactivity (HOMO-LUMO gaps, global descriptors) of the top leads. This stepwise, multi-evidence pipeline follows established integrated in silico workflows in recent phytocompound inhibitor discovery studies targeting resistant bacterial pathogens68–70.

MD simulation of the protein-ligand complex

Protein and protein-ligand complex microscopic stability is assessed using molecular dynamics, an advanced automated simulation method. The structure, function, fluctuation, interaction, and behavior are demonstrated to do this. The behavior of the six complexes was investigated using GROMACS version 2023.1 (https://manual.gromacs.org/documentation/2023.1/index.html) for MD calculations64. CHARMM general force field parameterized protein content. The SwissParam server (https://www.swissparam.ch/) implements ligand topologies71. The structures were vacuum minimized 2500 times using steepest descent to address steric issues. The SPC water model solvated the structure. After the addition of Na+ and Cl- ions, the gmx genion instrument balanced the system. This was done to ensure system electrical neutrality. After minimization, MD simulations went into production, NVT, and NPT. Two phases balanced the systems. First, a 100 picosecond NVT equilibration was performed to maintain the particle number, volume, and temperature. The procedure aimed to raise the system temperature to 300 kelvin. The second stage involved a precise 100 picosecond NPT equilibration to achieve temperature, pressure, and particle number homogeneity. It was essential to maintain system density and pressure. The protein group’s location was limited by bond limitations on all bonds during simulations. The system entropy reduced because NVT and NPT restricted water molecules around the protein, relaxing them. The Parrinello-Rahman barostat method and v-rescale thermostat were utilized for molecular dynamics72. The thermostat and barostat were adjusted for 100 picoseconds. To constrain covalent bonding, the Linear Constraint Solver application was used. Chemical bond interactions were handled using the sophisticated (particle-mesh Ewald) or PME approach. Every system has a 100-ns production run after equilibrium.

Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)

The RMSF values can be used to determine the local flexibility based on residue displacement during the MD simulation. The value is useful for characterizing local changes along the protein chain. The RMSF for the residue is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${RMSF}_{I}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}{\sum}_{I=1}^{T}{\left({r}_{i}{\prime}\left(t\right)\right)-{r}_{i}\left({t}_{ref}\right)}^{2}>}$$\end{document}

The RMSF is determined over trajectory time (T). The reference time is t ref. The position of residue i is ri, and its atoms are r’ after superposition on the reference. N- and C-terminal protein tails vary more than other regions74.

Radius of gyration (Rg)

When mass weighting is considered, the radius of gyration (RG) is generally defined as the root mean square distance of an atomic group from its shared center of mass. The compactness of a molecular structure is indicated by the Rg, which reflects the proximity of the atoms to the center of mass. This analysis is crucial for assessing the system’s overall stability and folding characteristics over time74.

Solvent accessible surface area (SASA)

The solvent accessible surface area (SASA) was calculated using the gmx SASA module of GROMACS 2023.1 over the course of a 100 ns molecular dynamics trajectory. The analysis was performed on the full complex, and the SASA values were recorded at regular intervals to monitor changes in surface exposure. The default probe radius (0.14 nm) and solvent parameters were applied. This analysis helped assess the folding stability and surface fluctuations of the protein–ligand complex during simulation75.

Hydrogen bonds

Intramolecular hydrogen bonds were analyzed using the gmx hbond tool in GROMACS 2025.1. The analysis focused on hydrogen bonds formed within the protein throughout a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation. Default criteria for the hydrogen bond distance (≤ 0.35 nm) and angle (≥ 120°) were used. Hydrogen bond counts were computed for each frame of the trajectory to evaluate structural stability and integrity over time76.

Free energy landscape (FEL)

FEL analysis was conducted to evaluate the conformational energetics and stability of the complex77. The trajectory file from 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations was used as input for principal component analysis (PCA), from which the first two eigenvectors (PC1 and PC2) were selected as reaction coordinates. The free energy of each conformation was calculated using the Boltzmann relation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta G= {-k}_{B}Tlnln P$$\end{document}where ΔG is the free energy, kB is the Boltzmann constant (1.380649 × 10⁻23 J/K), T is the temperature (298.15 K), and lnP is the normalized probability distribution of the conformations across the PC1–PC2 space78. The 3D FEL was visualized using matplotlib to map local energy minima and transition states on a rugged conformational landscape. This approach allows the identification of dominant low-energy basins and metastable states, which are widely adopted in protein dynamics studies79.

Post-simulation evaluation

MMGBSA binding free energy

To estimate the binding free energy of the ligand to the protein, Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) calculations were performed using the gmx_MMPBSA tool (v1.6.3) (https://valdes-tresanco-ms.github.io/gmx_MMPBSA/v1.6.3/), which integrates GROMACS with AmberTools to enable end-state free energy analysis80. The approach decomposes the binding free energy (ΔG bind) into molecular mechanical energy (van der Waals and electrostatic interactions) and solvation energies (polar and nonpolar contributions), based on the equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {G}_{bind}= \Delta {E}_{vdW}+ \Delta {E}_{ele}+ \Delta {G}_{polar}+ \Delta {G}_{polar}$$\end{document}

Entropy contributions were neglected, as is commonly done in comparative binding affinity assessments81.

A total of 1001 snapshots were extracted at equal intervals from the 100 ns production trajectory (MD_center.xtc) of the protein-ligand complex. The calculations were conducted on three separate systems: the complex, the receptor (protein alone), and the ligand. The final ΔG bind was computed as:

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM)

To explore residue-level correlated motions within the protein-ligand complexes, dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis was performed using the C-alpha atoms of the protein from the 100 ns MD trajectories. The MD trajectories were processed with MD analysis in Python, where displacement vectors from the mean positions were computed for each residue over time. The normalized correlation coefficients between residue pairs were calculated according to the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rm{Cij= (\triangle ri.\triangle rj)(\triangle ri^2).(\triangle rj^2)}$$\end{document} The values of Cij range from +1 (fully correlated motion) to −1 (fully anticorrelated motion), with 0 indicating no correlation. The resulting matrices were visualized using Seaborn heatmaps, where red indicates positive correlation and blue indicates anticorrelation. This approach provides insight into the allosteric communication and long-range interactions modulated by ligand binding, as established in previous studies83,84.

Density functional theory (DFT) of the lead molecules

Gaussian 09 (https://computing.ch.cam.ac.uk/software/gaussian-09) was used to optimize the lead compound. It is a powerful tool for optimizing and calculating the electron structure and geometry of the compound85. The calculation method was DFT/B3LYP, basis set 6-31G86,87. The outcomes were illustrated and evaluated through GaussView 05 (https://www.strath.ac.uk/professionalservices/is/software/gaussview509/)88. Computational analysis

Six phytocompounds demonstrated the drug-likeness properties

Among the sixteen phytochemicals, ‘drug-likeness’ analysis through SwissAdme filtered six compounds that abided by at least four of the five rules: Lipinski’s, Egan’s, Veber’s, Muegge’s, and Ghose’s rules, enabling them to be considered for drug development studies (Table 4).

Six phytocompounds had ADMET properties

Five out of six phytocompounds did not have any toxicity, including AMES toxicity, hepatotoxicity, Tetrahymena pyriformis toxicity, and Minnow toxicity (Table 5). However, N-[5-(3,4-Dimethoxy-benzyl)-[1,3,4]thiadiazol-2-yl]-3-fluoro-benzamide and pentasiloxane, dodecamethyl- showed hepatotoxicity and AMES toxicity, respectively. Discussion

Plant extracts have the ability to boost the immunity of the skin and possess antimicrobial properties against diverse skin pathogens, indicating their potential to manage various skin disorders97. Notably, A. indica stands out among these plants for exhibiting significant anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects against a broad spectrum of skin-infecting pathogens, particularly bacteria resistant to several drugs98,99. Although phytocompound-based drugs present substantial potential, their formulation and utilization in Bangladesh remain constricted100. Therefore, ongoing research has focused on evaluating the efficacy of the leaf extract of A. indica against organisms isolated from pus samples of skin infections. Additionally, the phytocompounds of the extract were identified through GC-MS analysis and investigation of the pharmacokinetics, ADMET properties, and DFT analysis of the lead compounds to assess their potential in combating the pathogenicity of the organisms. For our study, three pus samples were collected from three patients who were suffering from skin infections. Severe localized inflammation, which is frequently accompanied by pus development from several pyogenic bacteria, is an example of a purulent wound infection. These infections can prolong hospital stays, impede the healing of wounds, and increase overall costs and mortality101. Two gram-negative and a gram-positive organism were isolated from these three samples. Moreover, all of the samples were catalase positive and coagulase negative, and two samples could hydrolyze starch. Additionally, the genome sequencing data obtained via BLAST and dendrograms confirmed samples 1 and 2 as C. koseri and sample 3 as S. saprophyticus. Furthermore, antibiotic profiling of the bacteria against 12 commercial antibiotics revealed resistance of C. koseri and S. saprophyticus to 6 and 5 antibiotics, respectively. Therefore, the selection of antibiotics for use as controls against these organisms was justified; ertapenem had the greatest inhibitory effect (36.1 mm) on C. koseri, and ampicillin mostly inhibited S. saprophyticus (33.1 mm). Assessing the antibiotic profile of the isolates was important not only for establishing the antibiogram but also for determining the control for the study. Although the isolates were multidrug resistant, they were sensitive to the crude methanolic extract of A. indica. The 200 µl extract resulted in 16- and 14-mm inhibition zones for C. koseri and S. saprophyticus, respectively (Table 2). Therefore, the in vitro findings clearly reveal the relationships of these organisms, and although they are resistant to several commercial antibiotics, they are sensitive to the phytocompounds in the crude extract, indicating the capacity of the phytocompounds to be used to inhibit these organisms.

The phytocompounds in the crude extract were analyzed via GC‒MS. Overall, 21 compounds were detected via GC‒MS analysis, and 5 of these compounds were related to volatile methyl siloxane (Table 3)96. Among these phytocompounds, 6 demonstrated drug-likeness properties on the basis of consensus criteria agreed upon by the majority of the prominent scientists (Table 4). Five of these compounds exhibited superior performance in ADME property analysis, including water solubility, Caco-2 permeability, intestinal absorption (human), and interaction with drug transporters and metabolizing enzymes such as P-gp, CYP2D6 (substrate), and CYP1A2, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4 (inhibitors). Importantly, none of these compounds exhibited any signs of toxicity (Table 5), indicating the essential criteria—toxicity and pharmacokinetic assessment for viable drug candidate determination. Evaluating ADMET parameters early in the drug discovery process is critical, as compounds with poor pharmacokinetics or high toxicity can undermine the expensive phases of drug development102. The implementation of computer-aided drug discovery is one of the most efficient strategies for screening databases and uncovering novel compounds that are effective against multidrug-resistant organisms103. For this reason, in silico or computer-aided drug design can be a game-changer, acting as an indispensable tool in contemporary drug discovery. It enables scientists to swiftly evaluate and refine potential lead compounds, significantly reducing both time and resource consumption in the development process and ultimately accelerating the delivery of safe and effective drugs to the market104.

Moreover, unveiling novel pharmacological targets is another fundamental phase in the drug development process. By scrutinizing the structural and functional roles of essential proteins, along with the identification of potential therapeutic targets, designing drugs that specifically target these proteins becomes feasible105. The peptidoglycan D,D-transpeptidase MrdA from C. koseri and D-alanine--D-alanine ligase from S. saprophyticus were chosen because of their contributions to the cell wall formation of these organisms53,54. Therefore, inhibiting these proteins ultimately results in the destruction of the organism. Once the target protein and phytocompound filtration were performed, the interaction of the targeted protein with the lead compounds was assessed via molecular docking.

In the drug development process, molecular docking is a widely recognized structure-based computational technique frequently employed in drug discovery106. The 5 phytocompounds were docked with the target proteins. Both proteins had relatively high binding affinities for Cyclopropane-1-carboxamide, 2-butyl-N-(5,6,7,8-tetrahydro-7,7-dimethyl-5-oxoquinazolin-2-yl)- (-9 and -8 kcal/mol). 2-(Acetoxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene had the second-highest affinity score (-8.1 and -7.9 kcal/mol). The compounds produced similar, sometimes better, results than did the control drugs, ertapenem and ampicillin (-9.1 and -6.2 kcal/mol, respectively) (Table 6). The docking was conducted by AutoDockVina, and the stability of the docked complexes was evaluated through MD simulation107. Further docking with GLIDE-XP identified cyclopropanecarboxamide as the top scorer for both enzymes (−7.92 and −7.53), which is consistent with its ability to engage mixed hydrophobic, charged, and hydrogen-bond contacts at known active-site residues. Ampicillin and the biphenylene derivative follow closely, whereas ertapenem is the weakest binding partner to peptidoglycan D,D-transpeptidase MrdA (−5.92) (Table 7). Therefore, the significance of these phytocompounds in controlling these infectious bacteria was justified by both AutoDock Vina and Glide outcomes.

Docking results were also interpreted with consideration of AlphaFold confidence. High pLDDT (>80) in the binding pockets of both proteins supported reliable ligand poses, with binding affinities for top compounds aligning well with interactions at conserved residues. While AlphaFold predictions enabled modeling of these understudied targets (no experimental structures available in PDB), structural confidence metrics are crucial for interpreting results. High pLDDT scores in catalytic cores minimized binding-site uncertainty, yielding stable docked complexes and MD trajectories (e.g., RMSD < 0.5 nm for lead compounds). However, lower-confidence regions (pLDDT 50-70) in loops could propagate uncertainty, potentially affecting induced-fit effects in MD or overpredicting binding stability for ligands interacting with flexible residues. This is mitigated by our use of ensemble docking (via AutoDock Vina and GLIDE) and 100-ns MD simulations, which captured conformational sampling108. In addition, the results were highest in the wet-lab settings (Table 2). Due to the absence of ligand-bound crystal structures for the exact target proteins from C. koseri and S. saprophyticus, classical redocking-based validation of the docking protocol was not possible. Instead, docking reliability was supported by benchmarking against the commercial antibiotics (ertapenem for MrdA and ampicillin for D-alanine--D-alanine ligase) as positive controls, parameters validated in our prior work on related bacteria6. These rank orders set initial expectations but, as usual, do not fully determine behavior in dynamics or free-energy reweighting.

In our study, a multilayered computational strategy was used, which progressed from consensus docking to MD stability assessment to free-energy ranking (MM/GBSA) to electronic reactivity validation (DFT). Similar integrated pipelines have been successfully applied in recent studies identifying natural-product inhibitors against multidrug-resistant bacterial targets. These approaches, like ours, prioritize mechanistic insight and lead to confidence prior to experimental progression68–70. Building on these docking and stability results, analysis of the Peptidoglycan D,D-transpeptidase MrdA-ligand complexes in 100-ns MD simulations revealed clear differences in binding behavior. The RMSD traces show that 2-(acetoxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene (a1), cyclopropanecarboxamide N-((2Z)-3,7-dimethyl-2,6-octadienyl)- (a2), remained below ~0.5 nm, suggesting compact, minimally perturbed binding modes, whereas ertapenem (e1) experienced large excursions (>1.0 nm) before partial stabilization—evidence of significant conformational adaptation or suboptimal fit. The RMSF profiles agreed: ampicillin (c1) generally reduced local flexibility, whereas e1 induced pronounced fluctuations in loop-rich regions. The Rg and SASA trends corroborate these findings; the e1 complex displays broader Rg fluctuations and greater solvent exposure, which is consistent with looser packing, whereas a1/a2/c1 maintains tighter, less solvent-exposed states. Additionally, FEL surfaces have smoother, shallower basins for d1–a1 and p1–a1 points to restricted conformational ensembles and efficient energy funnels, whereas rugged landscapes for d1–a2 and especially p1–e1 indicate multiple metastable states separated by higher barriers, again aligning with elevated RMSD and SASA for e1.

MM/GBSA identifies p1–a2 as the most favorable complex (ΔG ≈ −8.78 kcal/mol), driven by strong van der Waals plus supportive electrostatics (GGAS ≈ −15.50), offset by moderate solvation cost. p1–e1 is instructive: Despite exceptionally favorable gas-phase interactions (GGAS ≈ −53.20) and abundant hydrogen bonds, its very large polar desolvation penalty (EGB ≈ +69.0; GSOLV ≈ +67.33) erodes the net affinity (ΔG ≈ −6.59). This underscores a key principle: plentiful H-bonds or strong Coulombic terms can be illusory if they require desolvating highly polar groups without compensating for burial, and they may coincide with global rearrangements (as seen in RMSD/Rg). For d1, a1 ultimately outperforms a2 by MM/GBSA (−6.22 vs −2.61), an apparent inversion relative to docking that likely reflects a better overall balance of dispersion and solvation for a1 in the simulated environment and differences in induced fit captured by MD. Furthermore, DCCM differentiates ligands by their dynamical imprint: a1 preserves coherent, positively correlated domain motions (a hallmark of stable allosteric coupling), a2 induces a mix of correlated/anti-correlated blocks consistent with localized flexibility, and e1 elicits extended anti-correlation, suggesting perturbed long-range communication pathways.

DFT analysis is crucial for determining the effectiveness of lead compounds against the target protein. The energy gap between the HOMO and the LUMO serves as an indicator of a compound’s chemical reactivity109. In the density of states (DOS) spectrum, the green and red colors denote the negative and positive phases, respectively. The red color represents the positive phase, whereas the green color represents the negative phase. This phenomenon is clearly described in the density of states (DOS) spectra of molecules, which characterize the energy levels per unit energy (Figure 2). For 2-(acetyloxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene and cyclopropane-1-carboxamide, 2-butyl-N-(5,6,7,8-tetrahydro-7,7-dimethyl-5-oxoquinazolin-2-yl)- molecules, the energy discrepancies between the HOMO and LUMO orbitals are 0.14 eV and 0.17 eV, respectively. Conversely, the energy gaps of the control drugs were 0.18 eV and 0.15 eV for ampicillin and ertapenem, respectively. Because the energy gap is small, it is easier for electrons to move between molecules, which makes them more reactive and softer110.

Moreover, the equations outlined in the methods section establish a relationship between the global chemical reactivity descriptors, including the electron affinity (A), ionization potential (I), chemical hardness (η), chemical softness (β), electronic chemical potential (μ), and global electrophilicity index (ω). In addition, the HOMO is directly correlated with the ionization potential (I = -EHOMO), whereas the LUMO is connected to the electron affinity (A = -ELUMO)111,112. Chemical hardness (η) and softness (σ) are qualities that are indicative of the capacity to transmit charges inside molecules. When the electronic chemical potential (μ) is low, both molecules (-0.15 eV and -0.16 eV) may be able to donate electrons. The electrophilicity indices, 0.15 eV for 2-(acetoxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene and 0.15 eV for cyclopropane-1-carboxamide and 2-butyl-N-(5,6,7,8-tetrahydro-7,7-dimethyl-5-oxoquinazolin-2-yl), along with the electronic chemical potential, indicate the existence of a substantial electrophile in the molecules. While these descriptors identified 2-(acetoxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene and Cyclopropane-1-carboxamide, 2-butyl-N-(5,6,7,8-tetrahydro-7,7-dimethyl-5-oxoquinazolin-2-yl)- as promising lead candidates, a limitation of the present work is the absence of formal replicate-based statistical analysis, explicit uncertainty estimation, and systematic robustness/sensitivity testing for the molecular dynamics simulations and MM/GBSA free energy calculations. Due to resource constraints, these analyses were performed using single standardized trajectories, a practice that remains common in many published hypothesis-generation and virtual screening studies in reputable journals. In contrast, docking results (AutoDock Vina performed in three independent replicates and GLIDE-XP in multiple runs) and DFT calculations showed high reproducibility with identical or virtually identical outcomes across executions. Future lead-optimization efforts or studies requiring absolute quantitative precision should incorporate multi-replicate MD runs, block averaging, or bootstrapping for uncertainty quantification, and targeted sensitivity analyses to further strengthen confidence in binding free energy estimates. Interestingly, the previous GC‒MS analysis confirmed the presence of these phytocompounds in the crude plant extracts.

Recent studies have similarly reported antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of A. indica leaf extracts against bacteria, with individual phytochemicals identified as key antibacterial agents through combined in vitro and in silico approaches113,114. However, our identification of 2-(acetoxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene and cyclopropane-1-carboxamide, 2-butyl-N-(5,6,7,8-tetrahydro-7,7-dimethyl-5-oxoquinazolin-2-yl)- as stable, high‑affinity binders to essential proteins of S. saprophyticus and C. koseri extends these findings by implicating new neem‑derived scaffolds with promising activity against clinically relevant, potentially drug‑resistant pathogens. While the disc-diffusion assay confirms the crude extract’s ability to inhibit growth of the target pathogens, it remains a qualitative phenotypic screen and does not provide direct evidence of engagement with the computationally predicted targets. The docking, MD, MM/GBSA, and DFT results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating predictions that require future experimental validation through target-specific enzyme inhibition assays, biophysical binding studies, or genetic approaches. Such mechanistic confirmation is a recognized next step in advancing phytocompound-based leads from multi-component extracts toward therapeutic development.

Conclusions

Taken together, the results of the present study underscore the therapeutic potential of Azadirachta indica leaf phytocompounds against the clinically relevant pathogens Staphylococcus saprophyticus and Citrobacter koseri. By combining in vitro growth inhibition assays with comprehensive computational analyses, we identified two lead molecules, 2-(acetoxymethyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)biphenylene and cyclopropane-1-carboxamide, 2-butyl-N-(5,6,7,8-tetrahydro-7,7-dimethyl-5-oxoquinazolin-2-yl)-, which demonstrated strong binding affinity, structural stability, and favorable electronic characteristics toward key bacterial proteins essential for survival. Findings from this study, therefore, provide mechanistic insights into the antimicrobial potential of neem-derived phytochemicals while also emphasizing their promise as prospective lead phytocompounds for tackling drug-resistant bacterial infections. However, further in vivo investigations and clinical validation are necessary to establish their efficacy and safety, ultimately facilitating their translation into therapeutic applications.
