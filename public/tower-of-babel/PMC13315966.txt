Functional SLC29A3/ENT3 drives autophagic clearance of intracellular viral particles
Authors: Arnav Joshi, Ross C. Larue, Rajgopal Govindarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Loss of SLC29A3/ENT3, an endolysosomal nucleoside transporter, increases host susceptibility to HIV-1 infection and viral integration.•Functional SLC29A3 is required for the efficient clearance of intracellular viral proteins, such as capsid protein p24.•Loss of SLC29A3 leads to the persistent accumulation of HIV-1 particles within endolysosomes.•SLC29A3-mediated viral clearance is driven by the activation of the host autophagic machinery.

Introduction

HIV-1 is one of the most prevalent infectious diseases with about 40.8 million people living with HIV (PLWH) by the end of 2024 (Global HIV & AIDS Statistics — Fact Sheet | UNAIDS, n.d.). The antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimens have dramatically reduced the HIV-1 associated mortality and made the disease chronically manageable. Despite their efficacy, anti-viral agents involved in ART often result in treatment limiting toxicities that impact overall treatment adherence. Furthermore, disruption of therapy often results in rebound viremia within 2–4 weeks, even with long term exposure to ART undermining overall outcomes of the therapy (Mdluli et al., 2022a; Vanhamel et al., n.d.-a). One of the major reasons for rebound viremia is the presence of provirus reservoirs in the CD4+ T-cells, especially Central memory CD4 T cells (TCM) (Vanhamel et al., n.d.-b). These latent reservoirs develop well before the virus can be detected within the serum and therefore demand continuous drug exposure to manage the viral load (Vanhamel et al., n.d.-c). For instance, a clinical study reports that despite 2.6–3.7 years of ART treatment, time to viral rebound (HIV-1 RNA ≥1000 copies/mL) without intervention ranged between 13 and 112 days (Mdluli et al., 2022b). Since complete sterilization of HIV latent reservoir is difficult to achieve (Chun and Fauci, 1999), surrogate approaches for periodic clearance of intracellular viral proteins are necessary. Hence, complementary approaches directed at sequestering latent viruses are important in addition to replication cessation.

Autophagy is a conserved homeostatic mechanism responsible for degradation of cellular components as well as xenobiotics (Parzych and Klionsky, 2014). The role of autophagy in context of HIV-1 is widely debated. While certain viral antigens stimulate autophagy in certain cells (CD8+ T-cells), HIV-1 proteins (such as Nef) inhibit autophagy processes in certain cells (dendritic cells, macrophages) (Dinkins et al., 2015; Killian, 2012; Lamsira et al., 2025). However, studies have shown that following fusion and internalization, autophagic targeting of the virus provides resistance against infection by reducing viral load during early stages (Cabrera-Rodríguez et al., 2021a; Cloherty et al., 2021; Viret et al., 2018). This is particularly true for viral particles that enter through endocytosis, where it is generally believed that this results in trapped and non-infectious viral particles sequestered within the endosomes (Miyauchi et al., 2009a). Pharmacological activation of autophagy, by agents such as rapamycin, has shown to work synergistically with anti-viral agents (Campbell and Spector, 2013), indicating autophagic degradation of viral particles as a potential strategy for minimizing viral load. However, immunosuppressive responses from prolonged exposure of autophagy activators limits overall utility of this approach (Yang et al., 2015). We have earlier identified the role of lysosomal equilibrative nucleoside transporter 3 (SLC29A3/ENT3) in governing autophagy processes by regulating lysosomal adenosine export, modulating the AMPK-mTOR-ULK signaling axis (Bissa et al., 2026; Nair et al., 2019). While it has been reported that SLC29A3 facilitates the release of viral genome of viruses including SARS-CoV-2, the role of SLC29A3-governed autophagy has not been well understood in context of HIV-1 infection and lifecycle (Hsieh et al., 2023). As a portion of enveloped- HIV-1 can localize within the endolysosomes during infection (Khan et al., 2022a), which is closely associated with SLC29A3-regulated autophagy, understanding the impact of functional SLC29A3 on the viral lifecycle is essential. Through our study, we show that the presence of functional SLC29A3 is beneficial for accelerating the clearance of intracellular viral particles and reducing overall viral burden.

Results

Generation of a tractable human cell system for studying SLC29A3-HIV-1 dynamics

To study the transporter-virus dynamics, we utilized lab-derived non-replicating strains of both non-HIV enveloped vesicular stomatitis virus G (VSV-G) pseudotyped HIV-1 viral virions and clinically relevant HIV enveloped HIV-1 viral particles (Clade B and Clade C) (Williams et al., 2023a). Clade B is a dominant strain in the western world and accounts for 2.7–14.3% of global infections and Clade C is a globally prevalent strain, accounting for 19.5–26.5% global infections predominantly in African and Asian countries (Williams et al., 2023b). VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles were generated by assembling viral envelop (pMD2.G), gag/pol (psPAX2), pHIV-ZsGreen, and Vpr-INmNeon Green (INmNG) (Francis and Melikyan, 2018) proteins in WT HEK293-T cells (Fig. 1A). In our system, pHIV-ZsGreen is derived from the pSIN (where SIN stands for Self-Inactivating) lentiviral vector with a reporter gene that encodes fluorescent protein ZsGreen, enabling identification of transduced cells with a stable integration event. Vpr-INmNeon Green vector produces mNeon Green-tagged HIV-1 integrase fused to viral Vpr, which allows incorporation of tagged integrase into the virion and subsequently enables microscopic visualization of viral intracellular particles. To establish infection in a tractable human cell system, we first generated SLC29A3-KD HEK293-T subclones by lentiviral transduction of short hairpin RNAs targeting SLC29A3 exonic regions (Nair et al., 2019) (Fig. 1B). Since HIV-1 viral particles bind to the cellular receptor CD4 and either CCR5 or CXCR4 chemokine coreceptors allowing for fusion with the host cell plasma membrane (Alkhatib et al., 1996; Dalgleish et al., 1984), we generated CD4+ SLC29A3-KD HEK293-T clones by lentiviral transduction of CD4 (Fig. 1C). HEK293-T cells have a baseline expression of CCR5 (Qi et al., 2016) and CXCR4 (Uhlén et al., 2015), sufficient for successful infection of lab derived HIV-1 strains (Reil et al., 1994). The expression of CD4 and CXCR4 was validated through immunofluorescence imaging where CD4 staining was only seen in CD4+ SLC29A3-KD cells while CXCR4 staining was observed in both SLC29A3-KD and CD4+ SLC29A3-KD cells (Fig. 1D). Importantly, it is known that pseudotyped HIV-1 viral particles can escape the endolysosomes, resulting in > 20-fold higher infectivity than HIV enveloped HIV-1 viral particles (Aiken, 1997). The difference arises from the co-receptor interaction of the viral particles; VSV-G interacts with ubiquitous LDL receptors to undergo large conformational changes exposing the fusion loop of VSV-G with endocytic membranes, as opposed to HIV-Env viruses that require CD4 and CCR5/CXCR4 interactions (Finkelshtein et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2009). Next, to avoid clonal differences in experiments, we exogenously replenished SLC29A3 in CD4+ SLC29A3-KD HEK293-T cells by transient transfection of SLC29A3 plasmid to generate CD4+ SLC29A3-OE HEK293-T subclones (Fig. 1E). No significant difference was observed in the expression of CD4 and CXCR4 with the reintroduction of SLC29A3 (Fig. 1D). These systems enable a fair comparison of the role of SLC29A3 in viral infection.

Loss of SLC29A3 increases susceptibility to HIV-1 infections

To study the infection dynamics, we infected HEK293-T WT, SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE CD4+ clones with increasing concentrations of viral particles, calculated from p24 concentration of virus stock. Viral titers were optimized between minimum detectable signal and cell death in WT HEK293-T cells. Each viral genome contained ZsGreen (a basic green fluorescent protein) as the reporter gene, which served as a surrogate marker for integration efficiency. The extent of infection was assessed two days post-infection by quantifying host ZsGreen signal. Fluorescent microscopy imaging of the transduced subclones suggested increased incidence of infection in HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD as compared to WT and SLC29A3-OE conditions. In case of HIV-enveloped viral particles - Clade B and Clade C, the difference was more appreciable with the higher titer (51 ng/µl) (Fig. 2A). Infection with VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 viral particles showed dose-dependent increase in infectivity with increasing viral titers (4.25–51 ng/µl). SLC29A3 deficient cells have greater host genome integration of viral genes

To confirm that the GFP signal arises from integration of the reporter genome into host cells, we performed PCR analysis of isolated total cellular genomic DNA at various time points (2h-6d) post infection for ZsGreen integration products. Greater expression of ZsGreen was observed in host genome of HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD cells as compared HEK293-T SLC29A3-OE cells following infection with Clade B and Clade C viral particles (Fig. 3A). Densitometric analysis suggests that at day 6, SLC29A3-KD cells had 16% significantly greater ZsGreen integration than the SLC29A3-OE cells following Clade B infection. Similar observation was made with VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles, where the SLC29A3-KD cells had 18% significantly greater integration at the terminal time point (day 6), compared to SLC29A3-OE cells (Fig. 3B). While the residual non-integrated 2-LTR circles degrade rapidly over the course of 48 h, the PCR products detected at later time points (such as 4d and 6d) are a result of genomic integration of ZsGreen. To experimentally confirm that the detected ZsGreen was deduced from the host genome, and not residual non-integrated 2-LTR circles, we performed a control experiment in presence of an allosteric integration inhibitor - BIB-II, which blocks integration via disruption of integrase oligomerization and interaction with cellular co-factor LEDGF/p75, led to minimal PCR products being detected61. We observed a faint PCR signal that was perhaps attributed to the residual viral genomes or 2-LTR circles of defective integration reactions, both of which were degraded over time in SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE conditions (Fig. 3B).

We corroborated this observation by quantifying ZsGreen integration by qPCR analysis of genomic DNA from Clade B and C transduced cells. We observed highest difference in ZsGreen expression (2-(∆Ct), relative to GAPDH) between -KD and -OE at 6d time point – 0.19 ± 0.09 (KD) and 0.004 ± 0.003 (OE) for Clade B, and 0.009 ± 0.001 (KD) and 0.003 ± 0.0006 (OE) for Clade C (Fig. 3C). Similar difference was observed with VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 viral particles at 6 d - 0.02 ± 0.01 (KD) and 0.008 ± 0.005 (OE) (Fig. 3D). 2-LTR circles are an indirect measure of nuclear import where they dramatically increase if entry is altered as well as they have been shown to increase substantially when integrase is inhibited by inhibitors (Friedrich et al., 2010). Therefore, we quantified the levels of 2-LTR circles and observed peak signal between 6 h – 1d which was reduced at later timepoints (Fig. 3D). No significant difference was observed between -KD and -OE subclones in the levels of these amplified products suggesting that SLC29A3 not playing a role in the integrase catalytic activity or degradation of viral genomic products. Instead, these results indicate that the differences we observe are because of a decrease in the amounts of integration complexes and not a specific effect on nuclear import or catalytic activity.

SLC29A3 drives cytosolic clearance of HIV-1 particles

As discussed earlier, autophagic clearance of virus (virophagy) is a means of resistance to infection. To understand whether the reduced infection incidences were associated with SLC29A3-mediated virophagy, intracellular viral capsid protein p24 levels were assessed. We immunoblotted the total cell lysate of Clade B and C as well as VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles transduced CD4+ SLC29A3-KD and CD4+ SLC29A3-OE cells for p24 capsid protein. Since the viral strains used for this study are non-replicating, quantification of p24 over time periods will inform about fate of viral particles from the initial input and not replication, in SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE conditions.

Immunoblot analysis showed the highest viral burden at 2 h and 6 h infection with Clade B and C infection in CD4+ HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD cells (Fig. 4A). The p24 was retained for a prolonged period; 2 days for Clade B and 4 days for Clade B until appreciable clearance was observed (Fig. 4A). On the other hand, SLC29A3-OE cells showed lower infectivity and faster clearance starting at day 1 after infection for both the viral particles (Fig. 4A). Similar observation was made for VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles which retained p24 capsids until day 4 while clearance was seen starting day 1 in SLC29A3-OE cells (Fig. 4B). We confirmed the immunoblotting observations through a p24-targeted ELISA and observed significantly higher viral load at 2 h post infection in SLC29A3-KD cells as compared to SLC29A3-OE cells following Clade B (44% difference) and Clade C (51% difference) infection (Fig. 4C). Similar results were observed in VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped virion transduced cells, where SLC29A3-KD cells had significantly higher p24 (57% greater) than SLC29A3-OE cells 2 h after infection. The difference was maintained at about 40% across 12 h – 2d (Fig. 4D). Such occurrence was also observed in a human T-cells, which showed reduced p24 levels in SLC29A3-OE conditions 1 day following infection with 200 ng/µl of VSV-G enveloped viral particles (50.4% reduction), Clade B (18% reduction), and Clade C (31% reduction) (Fig. 4E). These cells naturally expresses CD4, allowing us to test infectivity of all three viral particles without overexpression of the receptor. To confirm that SLC29A3-mediated autophagy drives the clearance of viral particles, SLC29A3 was re-expressed in CD4+ HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD cells in a dose-dependent manner by increasing the expression plasmid concentration from 0.5 to 10 µM during transfection. The cells were subsequently transduced with equivalent titers of Clade B, Clade C, or VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 viral particles. Immuno-blotting analysis revealed that increasing SLC29A3 expression progressively reduced intracellular p24 levels, while simultaneously decreasing the autophagy substrate SQSTM1 (Fig. 4F). These findings suggest that enhanced SLC29A3-governed autophagy promotes efficient clearance of viral particles. Persistent endolysosomal virus accumulation occurs with loss of SLC29A3

Endolysosomes are the site of autophagic degradation and an important component in the SLC29A3-regulated autophagy processes. Therefore, immunofluorescence microscopy was performed to understand the fate of viral particles following endocytosis. For imaging of accumulation of encapsulated viral particles and/or capsid cores during infection, we employed a co-transfection with WT and mNeonGreen fused integrases which remained trapped within the capsid core until uncoating. Assessing the phenomenon at the endolysosomal level, we observed colocalization of engulfed viral particles or viral cores (visualized through integrase-conjugated mNeonGreen) with endolysosomal marker LAMP-I/II (Fig. 5Ai). At 0 h after infection, faint neon-green signal on the cell membrane was attributed to clusters of VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles interacting with the membrane (SLC29A3-KD 0.24 ± 0.03 Mean GFP intensity/DAPI; SLC29A3-OE 0.25 ± 0.006) (Fig. 5Aii). Our observations suggest greater endolysosomal accumulation by 12 h in SLC29A3-KD cells as compared to SLC29A3-OE cells (SLC29A3-KD 0.67 ± 0.09 Mean intensity/DAPI; SLC29A3-OE 0.5 ± 0.07) (Fig. 5Aii). While less number of viral cores are retained by 24 h in the SLC29A3-OE conditions, the capsid cores appear to persist within the SLC29A3-KD clones within the same time frame (SLC29A3-KD 0.58 ± 0.03 Mean GFP intensity/DAPI; SLC29A3-OE 0.42 ± 0.03) (Fig. 5Aii). Autophagy modulation regulates intracellular viral load

Further to confirm that SLC29A3-goverened autophagy resulted in faster clearance of viral particles, we performed infection experiments in the presence of autophagy modulators – rapamycin (RAPA) and chloroquine (CQ). RAPA is an autophagy inducer that inhibits mTORC-1 and can rescue the inadequate MTOR inhibition observed in SLC29A3-KD cells (Bissa et al., 2026; Nair et al., 2019). CQ is an autophagy inhibitor that can negate the SLC29A3 pH-sensitive transport activity (Rahman et al., 2017) by raising lysosomal pH (Jacquin et al., 2017), abolishing the SLC29A3 effect as the transporter requires acidic environment for activity.

Following infection with VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles under RAPA or CQ co-exposure, we immunoblotted whole cell lysates for capsid protein p24 to assess the viral burden. We observed that the differences earlier observed between SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE cells were abolished (Fig. 6A). RAPA treated cells showed lower p24 burden, except for the initial time points, suggesting intracellular clearance of viral particles even in SLC29A3-KD conditions (Fig. 6A). On the other hand, CQ co-treatment resulted in higher p24 across the two subclones and interestingly, showed high accumulation of matrix protein - p17 (Fig. 6A). We confirmed these results by p24-targeted ELISA at day 2 post infection, where we observed reduction of p24 with RAPA (36% reduction) and increase with CQ (437% increase) in SLC29A3-KD cells (Fig. 6B). Similar trend was observed in SLC29A3-OE conditions with a modest decrease with RAPA (11% reduction) and increase with CQ (177% increase) with respect to untreated conditions (Fig. 6B). We further probed for early time periods (2h-1d) and observed reduction in viral load starting 6 h of initial infection and RAPA exposure (Fig. 6Ci). Conversely, p24 was retained for longer period with only sharp reduction at 1d in SLC29A3-OE conditions (Fig. 6Cii). Discussion

Host autophagic responses are often stimulated with retrovirus infections as a protection mechanism facilitating viral clearance (Choi et al., 2018a; Zhu et al., 2020). However, HIV-1 infection can interfere with autophagy in associated immune cells (Cabrera-Rodríguez et al., 2021b; Sagnier et al., 2014). HIV-1 has evolved mechanisms to interact with core autophagy machinery (Beclin1, TFEB) and autophagosome maturation, thereby facilitating viral persistence (Choi et al., 2018b). Inhibition of virophagy may prevent the degradation of latent viral reservoirs and, when combined with treatment interruptions, can result in uncontrolled viral replication. Augmentation of SLC29A3-governed autophagy signaling can therefore lead to favorable outcomes in curbing viral load. Our results indicate faster clearance of viral particles, which thereby results in reduced viral gene integration in host genome and lower overall infectivity, in SLC29A3-OE HEK293-T cells. Viral particles are retained within endolysosomes of SLC29A3-KD HEK293-T cells for extended periods which may increase chances of infection. Further, pharmacological modulation of autophagy by rapamycin reduced p24 levels in both SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE conditions while CQ led to persistent and higher p24 levels when comparing with untreated VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped virion transduced clones (Fig. 6).

The use of autophagy inducers has been extensively studied in the context of curbing viral load. Studies have elicited the mechanisms of autophagy curbing latent viral load such as HIV-1 Nef blocking autophagy (Castro-Gonzalez et al., n.d.). Such occurencescan potentially be rescued by autophagy-stimulating interventions like trehalose (Rusmini et al., 2019). Further, clinical studies have suggested that RAPA treatment benefits patients by controlling HIV-1 levels (Donia et al., 2010) as well as improving secondary pathologies like Kaposi sarcoma (Di Benedetto et al., 2010). This highlights the safety of RAPA in HIV patient settings. Clinical modulation of virophagy can emerge as a potential avenue for alternative treatment strategies. While autophagy induction with RAPA to suppress autophagy has been under study for long time, its clinical application is undermined due to immunosuppressive properties (Baccarani et al., 2010; Mu et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2002). Our studies suggest an important role for SLC29A3 in HIV-1 lifecycle than previously understood.

While in the present study we evaluate the role of SLC29A3 in early-stages of HIV-1 lifecycle, cells infected with replication competent HIV-1 would continuously produce viral proteins that are packaged within late endosomes (Post et al., 2025). Endolysosomal disruption by CQ has shown to increase the release of HIV-1 (Khan et al., 2022b). Considering the critical role of SLC29A3 in regulating endolysosomal homeostasis, it is equally important to study whether the effects stem from general endolysosomal dysfunction or selective virophagy. Furthermore, as discussed earlier, HIV-1 proteins like Nef can affect autophagy functions. Therefore, future studies with infectious replication strains of HIV-1 are necessary to assess the broader impact of autophagy and the role of SLC29A3 in late-stages of viral lifecycle. Differences exist between the mechanisms of endosomal escape by different strains of viruses (such as VSV-G enveloped viral particles and Clade B/C) due to their interactions with ubiquitous endolysomal receptors. This causes VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles to show higher infectivity. Hence, to reduce the observational bias, further experiments need to be performed with clinic-derived replicating strains of HIV-1 in SLC29A3 modulated humanized animal models or with isolated human primary immune cells to better understand the role of ENT3. The effect of SLC29A3 on initial entry versus post-entry clearance also needs to be studied further. HIV-1 viral particles localize within endosomes following fusion (Miyauchi et al., 2009b). In our experiments, differences observed at the early time points suggested an additional potential role for SLC29A3 in viral entry. Further studies investigating the involvement of SLC29A3 in the viral entry process, including the use of fusion inhibitors such as Enfuvirtide, may provide additional mechanistic insight.

In summary, to facilitate chronic suppression of viruses, in addition to autophagy enhancing agents, development of activity potentiators of SLC29A3 can be a rational approach. While there have been identified some agents such as interferons to augment SLC29A3 expression (Hsieh et al., 2023), an effort to determine clinically viable drug candidates for long term clinical application thereby is necessary.

Material and methods

Cell culture

HEK293-T (CRL-3216) cells were procured from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HEK293-T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD clones were generated through shRNA mediated gene knock-down as done previously (Nair et al., 2019). SLC29A3 was exogenously replenished in the HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD cells by transient transfection of SLC29A3 expression vector (pcDNA3.1-CMV-SLC29A3-YFP) or (pcDNA3.1-CMV-SLC29A3-HA). Cell pools for HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD or SLC29A3-OE were subsequently used for the experiments. Human T-cells, isolated from PBMC, were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 2 mM glucose, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 10 mM HEPES and used within 3 days of thawing from stock vials.

Expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNeasy Mini Kit (#74,106; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following manufacturer’s protocols. One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed using RevertAid RT Reverse Transcription kit (#K1691; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Quantitative PCR was performed using QuantStudio™ 7 Flex (#4485,701; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Following primers were designed and procured from Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, VA: SLC29A3 – F: GGGCATATAAACTCCGAAACTGC R: GGAGGAAGTATCCACCTTCACC, CD4 – F: GTACAGCTTCCCAGAAGAAGAG R: CAGCTTGGATGGACCTTTAGT, and GAPDH - F: TGGCCTTCCGTGTTCCTAC R: GAGTTGCTGTTGAAGTCGCA. Fold change (2-(∆∆Ct)) was calculated by normalizing the gene of interest relative to GAPDH and plotted.

Virus production

HIV-enveloped and VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 viral particles were produced through previously described methods (Hoyte et al., 2017; Koneru et al., 2019). HEK293-T cells were transfected with the following vectors – 5 µg pMD2.G (Addgene plasmid #12,259), 5 µg psPAX2 (Addgene plasmid #12,260), and 10 µg pHIV-Zsgreen (Addgene plasmid #18,121) (Welm et al., 2008), and 2.5 µg Vpr-INmNeon Green (INmNG) (Francis and Melikyan, 2018) using calcium phosphate transfection kit (631,312, Takara Bio Inc) for generating VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles. HEK293-T cells were additionally transfected with either 2.5 µg pHIV-1Env-Fab or 2.5 µg pVax1 to generate Clade B and Clade C enveloped viral particles respectively. Media was replaced 16 h post transfection and virus containing media was harvested after 2–3 days. Virus was concentrated using centrifuge filters, cutoff 10 kDa (UFC901008, Millipore; Burlington, MA) and titer was estimated using p24 ELISA (0801,200, ZeptoMetrix; Buffalo, NY).

HIV infection screen

HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 5 x 104 cells/well. Cells were transduced with incremental volumes of VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles viral particles from 10 µL, 25 µL, 50 µL, 100 µL, and 250 µL and enveloped (Clade B and Clade C) viral particles at 25 µL (8.5 ng/µL) and 250 µL (51 ng/µL) in serum-free media and Polybrene (0.8 mg/ml). Media was replaced 2 h post-infection and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 days. Cells were visualized for ZsGreen fluroscence using ZOE Fluorescent Cell Imager (1450,031, Bio-Rad Hercules, CA). Cells were thereafter trypsinized and fixed with 300 µL of 4% formaldehyde. GFP+ were counted using Cytek® Northern Lights™ flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences; Fremont, CA) in the B1-A channel (ZsGreen : Ex λ 496 nm, Em λ 506 nm). Data analysis was performed using Floreada.

ZsGreen PCR analysis

Whole cell (genomic and non-genomic) DNA from CD4+ HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles and enveloped (Clade B and Clade C) virion transduced cells was isolated using DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (69,504 Qiagen; Hilden, Germany) at 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 1 d, 2 d, 4 d and 6 d Similarly, another set was included with VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped virion transduced cells treated with allosteric integrase inhibitor 10 µM BIB-II. ZsGreen (F: TGTACTGGGTCTCTCTGGTTAG R: GGGAAACGCCTGGTATCTTTAT) was PCR amplified along with GAPDH (F: TGGCCTTCCGTGTTCCTAC R: GAGTTGCTGTTGAAGTCGCA) as loading control and separated using agarose gel electrophoresis on a 2.5% agarose gel with 0.01% Ethidium bromide and Invitrogen™ 1 Kb Plus DNA Ladder (10–787–018, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA). Gels were visualized on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad; Hercules, CA). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ. Background signal was normalized by measuring regions equivalent in size to the target band immediately above and below each band, and the corrected values were used for band intensity quantification. For qPCR analysis, 100 ng of extracted DNA was amplified with ZsGreen (F: TGTACTGGGTCTCTCTGGTTAG R: GGGAAACGCCTGGTATCTTTAT) and 2-LTR (F: CCAGAGAGACCCAGTACAAGCA R: GGCTAACTAGGGAACCCACTGC) using QuantStudio™ 7 Flex (#4485,701; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Gene expression normalized with GAPDH (2-(∆Ct)) was calculated and plotted.

p24 ELISA

Whole cell lysates of HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles and enveloped (Clade B and Clade C) virion transduced cells were prepared with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease, phosphatase inhibitors, and PMSF. Equal volumes (20 µL) of lysates were utilized for the p24 ELISA (0801,200, ZeptoMetrix; Buffalo, NY) performed as per manufacturer’s instructions. Quantified p24 was normalized with total cellular protein content quantified with Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) and plotted onto bar graph using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA).

Immunoblotting analysis

HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE cells were transduced with either type of virion, volume equivalent to 200 ng/µl followed by 1.5 h of glucose deprivation. The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor (PIA32955, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA), phosphatase inhibitor (PIA32957, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) and 1 mM PMSF. Cells were lysed by intermittent vortexing under ice followed by a clarifying spin (13000xg for 10 min at 4 °C). Supernatant was isolated and quantified for total protein with Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA). 30 µg of total cell lysate along with PageRuler™ Plus Prestained Protein Ladder (26,619, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) was separated by SDS-PAGE on an 12% polyacrylamide gel using Mini-PROTEAN® electrophoresis system (#1658001FC, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Separated proteins were transferred onto methanol activated PVDF membranes through wet transfer in Owl™ VEP-2 Mini Tank Electroblotting System (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) following manufacturer’s protocol. Non-specific sites on protein bearing PVDF blots were blocked by incubating with 5% BSA solution in 1x TBST for 1.5 h at room temperature. Western blotting analysis was performed using Anti-HIV1 p55 + p24 + p17 antibody (ab63917; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) with β-actin (13E5) Rabbit mAb (4970; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) as the loading control, incubated overnight followed by washing with 1x TBST and incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies. Proteins were visualized through chemiluminescence using Clarity and Clarity Max ECL Western Blotting Substrates (1705,061, Bio-Rad; Hercules, CA) on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad; Hercules, CA). Blots were quantified for densitometry analysis using ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012) and plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA).

Immunofluorescence microscopy

For mNeon-Green based virion imaging, confluent HEK293-T SLC29A3-KD and SLC29A3-OE cells grown on coverslips were transduced with 250 ng of VSV-G enveloped HIV-1 pseudotyped viral particles, Clade B or Clade C viral particles for 2 h in serum free media and polybrene (0.8 mg/ml). Coverslips were fixed and blocked with BSA at the timepoints – 0 h, 2 h, 12 h, and 24 h as described earlier. Incubation with LAMP-I (D2D11) (9091; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) primary antibody was carried out in PBS solution containing 0.02% Tween-20 and 5% bovine serum albumin for 2 h at room temperature. Slides were washed thrice with PBS containing 0.02% Tween-20 for 10 min each. Slides were incubated with secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgGs) conjugated with AlexaFluor 594 for 1 h at room temperature and then washed as before. Images were acquired using a Nikon AXR Confocal microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s T-test (when comparing 2 groups) and ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test (when comparing >2 groups). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001. Statistical analysis methods for individual figures detailed in figure legends.
