Right amygdala ablation reduces maladaptive negative interpretation bias and symptoms in a patient with post-traumatic stress disorder
Authors: Tao Xie, Sanne J H van Rooij, Sai Sun, Noah K Bryson, Phillip Demarest, Haeorum Park, Luigi Maccotta, Shuo Wang, Peter Brunner, Jon T Willie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNegative interpretation bias – the tendency to perceive ambiguous stimuli as threatening – is a core cognitive distortion underlying post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). The amygdala, a key structure in fear processing, is implicated in PTSD-related hypervigilance and heightened fear perception, yet direct causal evidence linking amygdala function to negative interpretation bias remains limited. Here, we present a unique prospective case of a male patient with refractory epilepsy and comorbid chronic PTSD, who underwent stereoelectroencephalography-guided radiofrequency ablation (SEEG-guided RFA) targeting the right amygdala. Before ablation, the patient exhibited heightened negative interpretation bias, judging ambiguous faces as more fearful compared to control groups of epilepsy patients without PTSD. Intracranial SEEG recordings revealed that this cognitive bias was associated with increased late-phase event-related potential (L-ERP) differentiation between fearful and happy faces in the right amygdala. Immediately following focal right amygdala ablation, negative interpretation bias and PTSD symptom severity were significantly reduced, while seizure frequency remained unchanged. Notably, reductions in L-ERP amplitude after ablation closely tracked decreases in fear perception, highlighting L-ERP as a potential biomarker of negative interpretation bias and therapeutic response. Our findings provide causal evidence from an n-of-1 study that the right amygdala supports maladaptive negative interpretation bias in a patient with PTSD and that right amygdala ablation can contribute to the relief of PTSD symptomatology.Subject terms: Post-traumatic stress disorder, Neural circuits

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating psychiatric condition characterized by re-experiencing, avoidance, negative emotions and thoughts, and hyperarousal following exposure to severe trauma1–3. A core cognitive mechanism underlying PTSD symptom severity is negative interpretation bias—the tendency to perceive ambiguous stimuli as threatening4,5. This cognitive distortion leads individuals to perceive their environment as more dangerous than it is, contributing to persistent fear responses and difficulties in emotional regulation. Empirical evidence indicates that PTSD symptom severity is associated with negative interpretation bias6,7, and modifying this bias may help alleviate PTSD symptoms8.

A growing body of evidence has linked negative interpretation bias to dysfunction within the amygdala4,5,9—a key structure involved in fear regulation and expression10, and a central hub for PTSD-related hypervigilance and heightened fear perception11–15. Neuroimaging studies consistently demonstrate heightened amygdala responses to fear-related stimuli in individuals with PTSD16, and exaggerated amygdala responses to neutral stimuli have also been observed and correlate with PTSD symptom severity17,18. This over-responsivity suggests that the amygdala plays a critical role in assigning threat value to ambiguous stimuli, directly contributing to negative interpretation bias. However, these findings are primarily correlational, leaving the causal relationship between amygdala dysfunction and negative interpretation bias unresolved.

Focal amygdala lesions provide a critical opportunity to establish a causal link between amygdala function and negative interpretation bias. Indeed, chronic focal disruption of amygdala activity alters fear perception and cognitive biases19–22. Studies in non-PTSD individuals have shown that amygdala damage preferentially reduces sensitivity to fearful and other negative emotional expressions, while processing of positive emotions such as happiness remains largely intact23–28. In a landmark study, Adolphs and colleagues demonstrated that patients with chronic bilateral amygdala lesions showed markedly lower recognition accuracy for fearful faces, while their perception of other emotions, including happiness, remained normal23. This valence-specific blunting of responses to fear-related stimuli suggests a specific role of the amygdala in negative emotional processing29–33. These observations raise the possibility that amygdala lesions could relieve negative interpretation bias and reduce PTSD symptoms by disrupting maladaptive fear evaluation processes. Notably, our prior work demonstrated that right amygdala and hippocampus ablation in the context of temporal lobe epilepsy was associated with PTSD symptom improvement; and at the neural level, pre- to post-ablation functional neuroimaging during a task contrasting fearful and neutral faces revealed reduced hyperactivation of the intact left amygdala and bilateral dorsal anterior cingulate cortex—neural changes paralleling those observed with PTSD symptom amelioration34. However, it remained unclear whether these benefits stemmed from direct amygdala disruption solely or from secondary effects of contemporaneous hippocampal ablation and seizure reduction35–37.

Stereoelectroencephalography-guided radiofrequency ablation (SEEG-guided RFA) is a minimally invasive neurosurgical technique that creates focal thermocoagulative lesions for treating epilepsy38–41. Specifically, the ablation volumes depend on electrode geometry and power-duration parameters. Tissue surrounding the electrode contacts is heated above a critical thermal threshold (≈ 58–60 °C)42, producing coagulative necrosis and irreversible loss of local neuronal activity within a focal zone typically 3–7 mm in diameter43–46. Sublethal temperature elevations in surrounding tissue yield a circumferential transition zone of edema and transient functional disruption. Brain regions outside the transition region are spared direct injury but may exhibit indirect network-level effects or functional reorganization. While combined ablation of the amygdala, hippocampus, and entorhinal cortex is standard clinical practice for treating medial temporal lobe epilepsy, partial ablation (such as focal amygdala ablation) in seizure-related networks for therapeutic and neurocognitive benefit is also clinically well-accepted43–46. Importantly, SEEG-guided RFA can be performed at the bedside in awake patients, allowing for concurrent intracranial EEG recording, neurological examination, and cognitive assessment throughout the course of the procedure47. Patients can likewise engage in behavioral tasks before and immediately after ablation, providing a unique opportunity to assess ablation-related behavioral and neural changes in near real-time. In this study, we present a rare case of an index patient with both refractory epilepsy and comorbid chronic PTSD who underwent focal right amygdala ablation as the first of two staged procedures for seizure control (Fig. S1a), providing a unique opportunity to dissociate amygdala-related affective changes from hippocampus-related seizure networks.

To track how amygdala ablation modulates fear processing, it is critical to identify neural signatures that reflect these changes. One such signature is the late positive potential (LPP), a centro-parietal event-related potential (ERP) component that emerges around 300 ms after stimulus onset and reflects sustained attention to emotionally salient stimuli48,49. The LPP has been extensively studied in PTSD and trauma exposure, with evidence suggesting that its modulation varies depending on PTSD symptom severity, the valence of emotional stimuli (e.g., fear-related vs. neutral images), and cognitive regulation processes50–56. However, while LPP is well-established as a cortically derived index of emotional processing, it remains unclear whether similar neural dynamics are observed subcortically and directly within the amygdala—particularly in the context of negative interpretation bias. Moreover, whether disrupting amygdala function alters these neural signatures remains unknown. By evaluating intracranial LPP-related neural activity before and just after amygdala ablation, this study provides a unique opportunity to evaluate how interpretation bias dynamically unfolds within amygdala-related neural networks.

We used a facial emotion discrimination task, a well-established paradigm for quantifying interpretation bias, while recording neural changes. In this task, participants make a binary judgment (fearful vs. happy) for facial expressions that are equally distributed along a continuum from happy to fearful, providing a sensitive measure of how individuals perceive ambiguous emotional stimuli57–60. Given that viewing fearful faces hyperactivates the amygdala in subjects with PTSD16 and that amygdala damage has been associated with reduced sensitivity to fearful expressions23–28, we expect this paradigm to potentially provide a direct measure of amygdala-dependent facial emotional interpretation bias. And prior research, including our own, has demonstrated altered responses to ambiguous facial expressions in neuropsychiatric conditions such as anxiety and depression61. Specifically, the index patient completed the facial emotion discrimination task before and shortly after ablation, enabling us to examine the neural correlates of negative interpretation bias and the immediate effects of right amygdala ablation on both fear-related behavioral and neural responses. We additionally delivered high-frequency electrical stimulation to functionally map amygdala-related emotional processing prior to the ablation.

By combining prospective clinical-behavioral assessments, intracranial electrophysiology, direct electrical stimulation, and focal ablation, this prospective n-of-1 study provides unique causal evidence that the right amygdala can drive maladaptive negative interpretation bias and PTSD symptomology, yielding a mechanistic framework for future amygdala-targeted ablation and neuromodulation interventions.

Results

The index patient was a 41-year-old male with intractable temporal lobe epilepsy undergoing intracranial SEEG monitoring for definitive seizure localization prior to surgical treatment (Fig. S1). He developed severe PTSD following civilian trauma during adolescence, with a pre-ablation score of 50 (severe) on the PCL-5 (PTSD Checklist for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-Fifth Edition). He performed a facial emotion discrimination task before (252 trials across three blocks) and after ablation (336 trials across four blocks). In this task, the patient was asked to view a series of morphed facial expressions and judge each face as either happy or fearful (Figs. 1a and S4). As a control, we recruited a cohort of 16 intractable epilepsy patients (7 males, 37 ± 11 years) undergoing intracranial SEEG monitoring at the same hospital (Fig. S2). None of these control patients had a PTSD diagnosis, nor did they screen positive for PTSD symptoms on a standard screening questionnaire (the Primary Care PTSD screen for DSM-5; PC-PTSD-5). The control group completed an average of 263 ± 74 trials of the same facial emotion discrimination task. The index patient’s amygdala electrode implantation (Figs. S2 and S3) included 3 SEEG contacts in the left amygdala (all from the lateral nucleus) and 7 contacts in the right amygdala (3 from the lateral nucleus). The control group included 67 contacts (35 from the lateral nucleus) across 14 patients in the left amygdala, and 53 contacts (28 from the lateral nucleus) across 11 patients in the right amygdala (Fig. S2). Intracranial SEEG and standard 10–20 montage scalp EEG were recorded simultaneously for all participants.

Behavioral and neural evidence of negative interpretation bias in the index patient with PTSD

Behaviorally, the index patient exhibited a pronounced negative interpretation bias compared to the control group (Fig. 1c). Specifically, the index patient judged the most ambiguous faces (at the 50% fear intensity level) as fearful at a significantly higher rate than the control group (p = 0.03), and showed a near-significant trend toward heightened fear judgments for faces with moderate fearful expressions (60% level). By contrast, the index patient’s judgments of happy faces (below 50% level) did not differ significantly from the control group, indicating that the negative interpretation bias was specific to fear-related stimuli rather than reflective of a generalized impairment. Across the control group, male and female patients exhibited similar responses (Fig. S4c), demonstrating consistency across the sexes. These findings support the notion that the index patient’s negative interpretation bias represents a specific tendency to interpret ambiguous stimuli as threatening—a cognitive distortion characteristic of PTSD.

We next examined the neural correlates of this negative interpretation bias, focusing on the LPP—a centro-parietal scalp EEG component typically observed with latency greater than 300 ms post-stimulus onset that reflects sustained processing of emotionally salient stimuli and presumed to reflect activity of medial frontal-parietal and cingulate cortices. Consistent with prior findings56, the index patient exhibited significantly higher LPPs (350–650 ms post-stimulus onset) for fearful compared to happy faces at the centro-parietal scalp sites (Cz and Pz), while this difference between fearful-happy faces was absent in the control group (Fig. 1d).

Given the amygdala’s established role in fear processing and its reciprocal connectivity with prefrontal and cingulate cortices12, we hypothesized that neural activity in amygdalae would also reflect this differential fearful-happy processing. Specifically, we examined bilateral amygdalar responses and those of other sampled intracranial regions within the same time window, referring to them as late-phase ERPs (L-ERPs) to align with the LPP period. Consistent with LPP differentiation, the index patient exhibited significantly higher L-ERPs for fearful faces compared to happy faces in the right lateral amygdala (t(214) = 2.8, p = 0.006; Fig. 1e-top-left). Such differences were not observed in the medially adjacent basal nucleus or laterally adjacent white matter (Fig. S5), confirming that the fearful-happy L-ERP differentiation was most specific to the lateral nucleus of the right amygdala. Furthermore, this differentiation was absent in the left amygdala (t(214) = 0.6, p = 0.57; Fig. 1e, bottom-left), suggesting hemispheric asymmetry.

We likewise examined L-ERPs in the control group and found no significant L-ERP differentiation in either the right lateral amygdala (t(20) = 0.5, p = 0.64) or the left lateral amygdala (t(26) = 0.8, p = 0.41; Fig. 1e-right), indicating that the index patient’s right amygdala response pattern was not a common feature observed across the control group. To account for individual variability, we compared the index patient’s fearful-happy L-ERP differentiation to the full distribution of differentiation values from the control group. The index patient’s right lateral amygdala showed stronger differentiation than the 90th percentile of the control group distribution, whereas contacts in the left lateral amygdala showed weaker differentiation (Fig. 1f), further reinforcing the specificity of enhanced fearful-happy L-ERP differentiation in the right amygdala.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the negative interpretation bias in the index patient was closely linked to enhanced fearful-happy L-ERP differentiation in the right lateral amygdala, suggesting a prominent role of the right amygdala in maladaptive threat interpretation in PTSD.

Functional mapping of the right amygdala in the index patient

During reduction of antiseizure medications in the index patient, multiple spontaneous right hippocampus-onset epileptic seizures were recorded with rapid involvement of the ipsilateral amygdala and entorhinal cortex (Fig. S6). Following resumption of antiseizure medications, we performed high-frequency direct electrical stimulation (functional mapping) to assess subjective sensory and emotional responses across right amygdala nuclei in the index patient (Fig. 2a). Thresholds for sensory awareness varied across nuclei, with sensations first reported at stimulation intensities of 1.5 mA for basal nucleus, 2.0 mA for basolateral nucleus, and 2.0 mA for lateral nucleus (Fig. 2b). Stimulation of the basal and basolateral nuclei induced subjective arousal and cephalic chill/thrill sensations, while stimulation of the lateral nucleus evoked similar chill/thrill sensations in the shoulder and chest (Fig. 2c). While subjective, these findings highlight potential nucleus-specific functional heterogeneity within the right amygdala10,62,63.

Notably, across all these nuclei, the patient consistently reported euphoric emotional experiences (Fig. 2c), consistent with prior studies demonstrating that transient amygdala stimulation can shift emotional experiences toward positive valence64. These nucleus-specific stimulation effects, particularly the consistent positive valence evoked across sites, further highlight the right amygdala’s involvement in emotional valence processing in the index patient. While electrical stimulation of the amygdala is known to induce autonomic physiology (thrill/piloerection)65, the valence of a subjective experience may be impacted by anticipation of safety or threat. Whether the valence-related semiology induced by amygdala stimulation arises from network functional coupling (tuning hypothesis) or from uncoupling (release hypothesis) remains to be investigated66–68.

By contrast, stimulation of the left lateral amygdala up to 2.5 mA failed to induce any subjective responses (Fig. S7). Only at a higher threshold of 3.0 mA, did the patient report a pleasant autobiographical memory ("playing with childhood friends”), likely reflecting nonspecific activation of a broader memory network. The relative absence of affective responses from left amygdala stimulation, compared to low-threshold consistently positive experiences evoked from the right amygdala, suggests lateralized functional specialization or asymmetric network tuning/release, at least in the index patient.

Taken together, the stimulation results further support a critical role for the right amygdala in mediating affective salience. When viewed alongside the patient’s pre-ablation behavioral and neural patterns (Fig. 1c–f), these findings provide convergent evidence for a lateralized amygdala contribution to maladaptive threat processing.

Confirmation of right amygdala ablation in the index patient

First-stage right amygdala SEEG-guided RFA was performed two months prior to a subsequent second-stage epilepsy treatment targeting the remaining medial temporal lobe seizure network via laser interstitial thermal therapy of the right hippocampus and entorhinal cortex (Fig. S1). The amygdala ablation was performed primarily for seizure control, with post-ablation improvement in PTSD symptoms prospectively observed as a secondary outcome. During the amygdala ablation procedure, radiofrequency thermocoagulation was performed through the same SEEG contacts used for epilepsy monitoring, ensuring precise targeting and focal ablation43–46. We targeted the basal and lateral nuclei of the right amygdala while the patient was awake and undergoing concurrent intracranial EEG signal recording and neurological examination (Figs. 3 and S3).

Subsequent post-ablation MRI confirmed that the necrotic core and associated transition region with edema were highly focal and centered on the right amygdala, with limited extension into adjacent anterior peri-amygdalar regions (Figs. 3, S8, and S9). The necrotic core volume measured 1.09 cm3, and the right amygdala volume measured 1.63 cm3. Accordingly, 47% of the right amygdala volume was ablated, and 71% of the total necrotic core overlapped with the right amygdala. The remaining 29% of the necrotic core primarily involved adjacent anterior peri-amygdalar regions, with only 0.08% of the right hippocampus identified as overlapping with the necrotic core. Volumetric overlap between the necrotic core and amygdala yielded an Intersection-over-Union (IoU) of 0.396 and a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.568, indicating focal and anatomically constrained targeting. While 0.08% of the right hippocampus was identified as overlapping with the necrotic core, a small posterior displacement of the hippocampal head was also observed post-ablation (Fig. S8c), suggesting that the actual ablated portion of the hippocampus was even less than measured and that the hippocampus should be considered >99.9% intact after initial amygalotomy.

Representative SEEG traces recorded during the facial emotion discrimination task from both ablated and non-ablated channels are shown in Fig. 3c. Neural activity in the ablated channels was markedly reduced following ablation, while no noticeable changes were observed in the non-ablated channels. Power spectral density analysis further confirmed a significant reduction in neural activity at the ablated channels after the procedure (Fig. 3d). Specifically, the power spectral density (Fig. 3e) in the ablated channels was significantly lower compared to channels in the left amygdala (t(6) = −5.1, p = 2.2 × 10−3) and all other non-ablated channels outside the right amygdala (t(153) = −13.4, p = 1.2 × 10−27). These findings confirm both target engagement and effective disruption of neural activity in the right amygdala.

Right amygdala ablation reduces negative interpretation bias in the index patient: behavioral evidence

Before ablation, the index patient exhibited a clear PTSD-related negative interpretation bias at the behavioral level, which was closely linked to differential L-ERP in the right amygdala during processing of fearful relative to happy faces (Fig. 1). This pre-ablation pattern led us to hypothesize that ablation of the right amygdala would causally reduce the patient’s negative interpretation bias and modulate the corresponding L-ERP signatures.

Following ablation, the index patient exhibited a significant reduction in negative interpretation bias (Fig. 4a). The tendency to judge ambiguous faces as fearful significantly decreased (t(5) = 4.3, p = 0.008), along with reduced fearful judgments of fearful faces (t(5) = 5.6, p = 0.003; Fig. 4e-left). In contrast, judgments of happy faces were unchanged (t(5) = 1.6, p = 0.17; Fig. 4e-left), indicating that the reduction was specific to fear-related stimuli rather than reflecting a generalized shift in response. To ensure that the behavioral effects were not driven by the unequal trial counts before (three blocks) and after ablation (four blocks), we examined block-level performance (Fig. S4h). Across all post-ablation blocks, we observed a consistent reduction in fearful judgments for ambiguous and fearful faces (50–100% levels), while responses to happy faces (30–40% levels) remained comparable to pre-ablation performance, demonstrating that the overall pre-post shift was robust and not attributable to differences in trial count.

These behavioral changes were accompanied by alterations in reaction time (RT) and confidence ratings during emotion judgments. Across all faces, RT and confidence values increased significantly post-ablation (RT: from 0.7 to 1.1 s, t(565) = 5.4, p = 1.3 × 10−7; confidence: from 0.2 to 0.4, t(565) = 4.1, p = 4.8 × 10−5; Fig. S4d), suggesting greater deliberation and reduced certainty in emotion categorization. Notably, before ablation, the patient exhibited a fear-dominant perceptual bias, showing greater confidence when categorizing fearful faces compared to happy faces (t(212) = 2.1, p = 0.04; Fig. S4e). This asymmetry disappeared after ablation (t(269) = 0.41, p = 0.68; Fig. S4e), suggesting a reduction in the perceptual weighting of fear-related stimuli. Additionally, the structured RT and confidence distribution observed in both the control group and the index patient pre-ablation — where less ambiguous faces were processed faster and with higher confidence — was disrupted post-ablation, resulting in a flattened response pattern (Fig. S4f). This post-ablation behavioral shift suggests altered decision-making dynamics for emotion categorization, aligning with prior evidence of amygdala involvement in emotion-based decision-making57–59.

To further compare these behavioral changes with the normative range, we examined the index patient’s post-ablation performance relative to the control group (Fig. S4g). The post-ablation psychometric curve revealed subtle but distinct changes in emotion categorization: fearful judgments decreased for fearful faces but slightly increased for happy faces, indicating opposite changes across the emotional continuum. This pattern suggests that amygdala ablation not only reduced negative interpretation bias but also altered the overall calibration of affective evaluation. Specifically, the post-ablation values fell within the control range at intermediate fear levels (30–60%), whereas the index patient rated clearly fearful faces (70–100% level) less fearfully and clearly happy faces (0% level) more fearfully than controls—a pattern that may reflect a complex alteration in affective evaluation. We note as a limitation that between-group effects were only formally assessed in control patients without ablation, as post-ablation data were available exclusively for the index patient. A similar trend was observed in the within-subject comparison, where highly happy faces (0% level) were possibly judged more fearful post-ablation (p = 0.05), while more fearful faces (50–70% level) elicited significantly fewer fearful judgments (Fig. 4a).

Right amygdala ablation reduces negative interpretation bias in the index patient: neural evidence

We next examined the neural correlates underlying these behavioral changes. Before ablation, differences in L-ERPs during fearful-happy processing were observed across multiple sampled right-hemisphere regions, including the middle, inferior, and superior temporal gyri, fusiform gyrus, amygdala, and parahippocampal gyrus (Fig. 4b-top). After ablation, this differentiation vanished across the sampled right-hemisphere (Fig. 4b-bottom), consistent with the functional loss of the right amygdala and its causal role in wider fear processing networks. Notably, a new differential pattern of L-ERPs emerged focally in the left lateral amygdala during post-ablation fear-happy judgements, with distinct L-ERPs observed for fearful compared to happy faces (Fig. 4b-bottom). These acute changes in L-ERP differentiation following right amygdala ablation suggest dynamic reorganization of neural processes across distributed emotion-related brain networks involved in perceiving emotional facial expressions.

Given the emergence or release of fearful-happy differentiation in the left amygdala post-ablation, we further examined how L-ERPs changed in the left amygdala following right amygdala ablation. Before ablation, no significant fearful-happy L-ERP differentiation was observed in the left lateral amygdala (Fig. 4c, top-left). Following ablation, a new pattern emerged, with fearful faces eliciting significantly higher L-ERPs compared to happy faces (t(286) = 2.0, p = 0.05; Fig. 4c, top-right). As expected, no such differentiation was observed in the ablated right amygdala (t(286) = 0.0, p = 0.99; Fig. 4c-bottom), consistent with successful targeted functional disruption (Fig. 3). Direct comparison of pre- and post-ablation responses in the left lateral amygdala (Fig. 4d) revealed a significant reduction in L-ERP amplitudes for ambiguous faces (t(82) = 2.4, p = 0.02) and a near-significant trend toward reduction for fearful faces (t(250) = 2.0, p = 0.051), while no significant changes were observed for happy faces (t(250) = 0.4, p = 0.71). Importantly, the alignment between reduced L-ERP amplitudes (Fig. 4e-right) and the reduction in fear perception after ablation (Fig. 4e-left) highlights L-ERPs as a potential neural marker of negative interpretation bias, providing a promising biomarker of changes in maladaptive threat processing in PTSD.

The collapse of fearful-happy L-ERP differentiation across the right-hemisphere, combined with the significant shift away from judging ambiguous faces as fearful following right amygdala ablation, together underscore the central role of the right amygdala in driving negative interpretation bias in the index patient. These findings also highlight the potential utility of L-ERPs as a neural biomarker that tracks changes in fear perception, providing an objective measure of negative interpretation bias and its modulation following amygdala intervention.

Post-ablation PTSD symptom outcomes in the index patient

Consistent with the neural-behavioral changes observed during emotional facial expression processing and subjective effects of functional amygdala stimulation, the patient’s explicit PTSD symptoms showed rapid clinical improvement following right amygdala ablation alone. PTSD symptoms (Fig. 5a-right), measured by the PCL-5, decreased substantially from severe (score = 50) to mild (score = 21) over two months post-ablation, falling below the clinical threshold for active PTSD69. Based on this reduction, the index patient would be classified as a treatment responder, no longer meeting the clinical criteria for PTSD69. This clinical improvement was reflected across all four DSM-5 symptom clusters70: Cluster B (intrusion), Cluster C (avoidance of trauma reminders), Cluster D (negative alterations in cognition and mood), and Cluster E (alterations in arousal and reactivity), with each showing a post-ablation reduction in symptom severity (Figs. 5a-right, and S10). These reductions were consistent with the patient’s subjective reports of improved emotional regulation and reduced fear-driven responses. In addition, anxiety symptoms showed clinical improvement, as reflected by decreased Beck Anxiety Index (BAI) score from 15 to 10 (Fig. 5b-left); while depressive symptoms also improved, with Beck Depression Index-II (BDI-II) score decreasing from 17 to 11 (Fig. 5b-right).

Importantly, right amygdalotomy alone did not alleviate seizure burden, which remained unchanged at approximately 10 seizures per month in the three months before and two months after ablation (Fig. 5c), indicating that the reduction in PTSD symptoms was not secondary to improved seizure control. This is consistent with the presence of interictal discharge spikes in representative right amygdala SEEG recordings both before and shortly after ablation (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, following subsequent laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) to ablate the right hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, the patient’s seizure burden significantly decreased—to 0.33 seizures per month in the first six months and 0.83 seizures per month in the second six months after the procedure—indicating that clinically meaningful seizure control was only achieved after more extensive medial temporal lobe intervention.

Notably, while seizure frequency was initially unchanged by right amygdalotomy in the index patient, the patient did report subjective reduction in tachycardia and seizure aura intensity (Fig. 5d), suggesting selective impairment or awareness of autonomic seizure semiology. This potential physiological change aligned with clinical improvements in the alterations in arousal and reactivity domain (Cluster E) of PTSD symptoms, where the cluster score decreased from 15 to 8 (Fig. 5a-right). Specifically, two items previously rated as “extremely” and one as “quite a bit” before amygdala ablation were reduced to “a little bit” and “moderately” at two months post-ablation (Fig. S10).

Discussion

This study provides prospective, n-of-1, within-subject causal evidence that the right amygdala contributes to negative interpretation bias and PTSD symptomatology. In a rare index patient with PTSD undergoing continuous intracranial SEEG recordings and focal right amygdala ablation, we show that disrupting right amygdala activity reduced negative interpretation bias and fear perception, accompanied by significant improvements in PTSD symptoms. Notably, reduced L-ERP differentiation in a distributed brain network closely tracked behavioral changes and predicted PTSD outcome. These findings highlight L-ERP as a potential biomarker of negative interpretation bias that predicts therapeutic response.

Before ablation, the index patient exhibited heightened negative interpretation bias (Fig. 1), judging ambiguous faces as fearful significantly more often than controls—a pattern linked to PTSD severity4,6–8. Immediately following right amygdalotomy (Fig. 3), this bias was significantly reduced (Fig. 4), and PTSD symptoms improved dramatically and persistently (Fig. 5), providing highly suggestive evidence that the patient’s right amygdala contributed to fear perception and negative interpretation bias related to PTSD symptoms. Importantly, judgments of happy faces remained largely unchanged, confirming that the effect was more specific to fear processing than to general emotional perception. This is consistent with prior lesion studies in non-PTSD individuals showing that chronic amygdala damage selectively disrupts the processing of fearful, but not happy, faces24,26–28. Although our primary analyses revealed reduced negative interpretation bias (Fig. 4d, e), the psychometric curves across individual emotion levels revealed a more complex effect: fearful judgments modestly increased for clearly happy faces and decreased for highly fearful faces (Figs. 4a and S4g, h). This bidirectional pattern suggests that amygdala ablation may blunt calibration of affective evaluation more broadly, not solely fear bias. These results should be interpreted cautiously, and larger cohorts will be required to determine their generalizability.

Prior functional imaging studies suggest distinct roles of the left and right amygdala in emotional regulation71–79. Specifically, in the context of fear processing, we recently proposed that the right amygdala primarily mediates sensory-driven fear responses, including autonomic arousal, while the left amygdala is more engaged in cognitive fear regulation and safety learning79. Supporting this, the index patient experienced PTSD symptom relief, particularly in the Cluster E domain of hyperarousal and reactivity (Figs. 5 and S10), as well as diminished perception of tachycardia and seizure auras over two months following right amygdalotomy (Fig. 5d). Notably, our prior work identified greater pretreatment amygdala activation as a predictor of nonresponse to trauma-focused therapy80, and focal ablation involving the right amygdala led to sustained PTSD symptom reduction for up to 12 months post-ablation34. Together, these findings support the idea that right amygdalotomy—or other focal means of amygdala functional blockade—may reduce excessive threat sensitivity, permitting a more adaptive state of safety learning, possibly via released left amygdala function79.

Neural activity provided further insight into left-right amygdala interactions during emotional processing. While a distinct L-ERP correlate of heightened fear assessment was evident in the right amygdala, this was abolished by right amygdalotomy, causing compensatory emergence or release of an analogous L-ERP from left amygdala (Fig. 4c). Our observations are consistent with prior studies indicating that the two amygdalae do not function independently but interact dynamically to regulate emotional perception81,82. Our results are also broadly consistent with prior studies that suggest that the right amygdala may be preferentially involved in rapid, automatic threat detection, whereas the left amygdala is more involved in contextual or elaborative processing of emotional stimuli75,83. Specifically, the emergence of fearful-happy differentiation in the left amygdala post-ablation could indicate a shift toward slower or more deliberative processing in the absence of right-amygdala input. This interpretation is supported by the observed increases in RTs and reduced confidence in emotion judgments post-ablation (Fig. S4d–f). Together, these observations may be consistent with a compensatory contribution of the left amygdala following right-sided disruption. The precise mechanisms and functional implications of such left amygdala engagement remain uncertain and will require further investigation.

Our findings suggest important clinical implications for amygdala-targeted therapies, including permanent ablation or reversible neuromodulation. Focal ablation of a dysfunctional right amygdala may directly address the underlying pathophysiology of PTSD37. Prior studies indicate that brain damage involving the amygdala is associated with reduced PTSD prevalence in trauma-exposed individuals, supporting the amygdala’s role in PTSD development and maintenance84. We previously reported that unilateral right amygdala-hippocampus ablation for epilepsy can alleviate PTSD symptoms, though the potentially distinct effects of amygdala and hippocampal ablation could not be disambiguated34,36. The current study extends prior work by isolating the effects of right amygdala ablation alone, thereby providing a therapeutic proof of concept for focal right amygdalotomy in PTSD. Beyond ablation, prior studies have explored neuromodulatory interventions targeting the amygdala, including direct electrical stimulation85–88 and transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS)89–92. Additionally, interventions that modulate amygdala activity through indirect pathways have shown promise80. However, these approaches still face challenges. For instance, a recent study found that tFUS targeting the amygdala acutely increased psychophysiological arousal and negative emotional reactivity89, highlighting the potential counter-therapeutic complexity of amygdala neuromodulation. Our findings may suggest that hemispheric laterality and particular stimulation conditions may be critical to designing optimally precise and effective neuromodulation strategies for refractory PTSD.

This study also highlights the unique potential of SEEG-guided RFA as both therapeutic and research tools. SEEG-guided RFA is a safe and well-tolerated neurosurgical procedure used typically in the setting of epilepsy43–46. The lesion size is highly predictable and limited, the procedure carries less risk than open surgery, and it can be performed at the bedside without sedation40. The relative risk of neurological deficit depends on the anatomical target and ablation parameters, but can be minimized by gradually titrating radiofrequency power and continuously monitoring clinical and electrophysiological parameters in the awake patient. Indeed, our patient exhibited no undesirable or unexpected neurological deficits following amygdala ablation, and postoperative imaging revealed only the expected focal ablation with limited evolving perilesional edema at one day (Fig. S9b) and two months post-procedure (Fig. S9d). Because SEEG-guided RFA may be performed during continuous neural recording and behavioral and neurological assessments, it provides spatially- and temporally-precise within-subject causal inference and serves an underappreciated role in human systems neuroscience research. Although this approach is increasingly implemented in specialized epilepsy centers45, its broader use in cognitive and affective neuroscience remains limited. A recent study by Gula et al. demonstrated that SEEG-guided RFA alters large-scale brain connectivity using cortico-cortical evoked potentials, but behavioral effects were not assessed47. In contrast, our study uniquely combines within-subject intracranial EEG, brain stimulation, awake neurosurgical ablation, rapid social-emotional psychometric assessment, and relevant clinical outcomes to innovatively demonstrate the causal role of human amygdala in negative interpretation bias and PTSD maintenance.

The longitudinal changes following isolated amygdala ablation in the index patient are not well defined. In a prior report of amygdalohippocampotomy using laser ablation, PTSD symptom severity (Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5, CAPS-5) decreased by 31% and 68% at six months and one year post-ablation34. The current index patient’s reduction in PTSD symptoms—assessed two months after right amygdala ablation—may similarly reflect durable modulation of fear-related circuitry, though ongoing network plasticity could lead to evolving emotional responses over time. While longitudinal monitoring is required to determine the persistence and stability of these improvements, extended follow-up was not possible in our case because the patient underwent right hippocampal-entorhinal laser ablation two months later as a second-stage epilepsy treatment (Fig. S1). Anecdotally, however, the index patient maintained persistent subjective benefits with respect to PTSD symptoms at one year following ablations.

Our study design combined a group-level comparison of the index patient (versus non-PTSD controls) with an n-of-1 (within-subject) design for interventions. The index patient’s PTSD phenotype differed statistically from the behavior and neurophysiology of the control group, while the patient served as his own control in assessing the meaningful effects of stimulation and ablation. N-of-1 designs are commonly applied in chronic conditions in which outcomes are subjective and rely on patient-reported measures93,94. Ideally, n-of-1 studies involve sequential exposure to different treatment conditions or placebo, often with intervening washout periods, enabling intra-individual comparisons while mitigating inter-patient heterogeneity inherent to conventional clinical trial designs. While controlled n-of-1 trials can be randomized or blinded95, the surgical nature of the present study precluded these features. As a result, potential order effects related to repeated task participation—including learning, fatigue, expectancy, discomfort, or other non-specific effects—cannot be fully excluded, particularly given the acute post-ablation testing window. In addition, early postoperative periods can be accompanied by broader psychological changes, including mood improvement, anxiety relief, or perceived therapeutic benefit, all of which could potentially influence facial emotional evaluation. Likewise, we did not control for potential order effects arising from direct electrical brain stimulation performed several hours prior to amygdala ablation. However, our stimulation was brief and not administered during task performance, and previously reported effects of focal direct electrical stimulation are generally transient (on the order of seconds)96 and state-dependent97. Accordingly, our electrical stimulation is unlikely to have influenced subsequent post-ablation behavior or neurophysiology. Notably, stimulation-related effects (euphoria) and ablation-related effects (reduced fear perception but preserved happiness processing and PTSD symptom improvement) were internally consistent across measures (Fig. 4).

While our findings exemplify the causal role of the human amygdala in negative interpretation bias and PTSD maintenance, important limitations apply. First, although we used the PCL-5, one of the most widely used self-report measures of PTSD and a validated screening and longitudinal assessment tool69, self-report instruments are vulnerable to response bias and may not capture the full range or severity of clinical change that may have been detected by a structured clinician-administered interview (CAPS-5)98. Second, decisions regarding patient medications were made solely on clinical grounds and were not experimentally controlled, and unexpected effects of comorbid epilepsy and antiseizure medications upon neural and cognitive processing cannot be fully controlled or excluded. Importantly, while antiseizure medications were briefly reduced during early hospitalization to capture seizures, they were fully resumed prior to all experimental procedures and continued postoperatively. All in-hospital seizures occurred exclusively during the period of medication reduction. After antiseizure medications were fully resumed, no seizures were observed during behavioral testing, functional electrical stimulation, or SEEG-guided amygdala ablation (Fig. S1b). Furthermore, the patient received no other neuromodulatory or psychotherapeutic interventions during the study. Third, although volumetric analyses demonstrated that the ablation was highly focal and centered within the right amygdala, with only 0.08% of the hippocampus possibly involved (Fig. S8), we cannot fully exclude the possibility that tissue adjacent to the amygdala—including adjacent anterior peri-amygdalar areas and regions affected by edema—may also have been functionally impacted. Finally, as an n-of-1 study, the generalizability of these findings remains inherently limited. The effects observed here may not extrapolate to all individuals with PTSD, epilepsy, or comorbid presentations, and larger samples will be required to determine the consistency. While the present findings highlight the potential therapeutic impact of focal amygdala interventions, future studies involving larger cohorts and longer-term follow-up are needed to systematically evaluate efficacy, mechanisms, and the long-term risks and benefits of such approaches.

Using cognitive-behavioral characterization, intracranial recordings, functional electrical stimulation, and focal amygdala ablation, we demonstrate that disrupting right amygdala activity in an index patient with PTSD reduced negative interpretation bias, altered neural responses to emotional facial perception, and dramatically improved PTSD symptoms. Notably, right amygdalotomy-induced reductions in this cognitive bias were closely associated with changes in L-ERPs across distributed regions involved in emotional facial perception, highlighting L-ERP as a potential biomarker for tracking interpretation bias and monitoring therapeutic response. These findings provide prospective, n-of-1, within-subject causal evidence that the right amygdala drives maladaptive fear interpretation and that its ablation can contribute to symptom relief in PTSD.

Methods

The index patient

We studied a unique index case of a 41-year-old ambidextrous male (Fig. S2) with chronic PTSD (Fig. S10) and medically refractory medial temporal lobe epilepsy (Fig. S6). His PTSD stemmed from multiple trauma exposures, including physical and sexual abuse during adolescence. Cognitive assessment using the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence–Second Edition (WASI-II) revealed a Full-Scale IQ standard score of 106 (66th percentile), indicating normal intellectual ability. To localize the seizure focus, stereoelectroencephalographic (SEEG) electrodes were implanted in the right frontal and bilateral temporal lobes (Fig. S3a–d). The SEEG electrodes consisted of platinum/iridium contacts with a diameter of 0.8 mm, spaced 3.5 mm apart (PMT; contact length 2 mm, insulation length 1.5 mm). Seven SEEG contacts were implanted in the right amygdala, and three SEEG contacts were implanted in the left amygdala, enabling detailed neural recordings from both hemispheres. Disposable Ag/AgCl electrodes were placed on the scalp using conductive paste, following the international 10–20 system for electrode placement (Fig. S3e). Scalp electroencephalography (EEG) was recorded from the following sites: frontal (F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8), central (C3, Cz, C4), temporal (T7, T8), parietal (P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8), and occipital (O1, Oz, O2). Local field potential neural activities in SEEG and EEG were simultaneously recorded and amplified at 2000 Hz using a Nihon Kohden JE-120 amplifier (Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan). Task control and synchronized acquisition of neural signals were managed with the BCI2000 general-purpose brain-computer interface software99. Pre-ablation magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used to produce three-dimensional brain models with FreeSurfer (version 7.2.0). The anatomical locations of the SEEG contacts were determined by co-registering the post-ablation computed tomography scan with the pre-ablation MRI using SPM12 software, and this co-registration was refined using the Versatile Electrode Localization Framework tool100.

The index patient participated in three key procedures during his hospitalization period (Fig. S1b): functional electrical stimulation of the amygdala, amygdala ablation, and the facial emotion discrimination task. Specifically, following SEEG electrode implantation and admission to the epilepsy monitoring unit, informed research consent was obtained on Day 1. On Day 3, the index patient completed 252 trials (three blocks) of the facial emotion discrimination task. On Day 5, functional electrical stimulation of the right amygdala (50 Hz) was performed at 11:00 AM for approximately one hour. Additional surgical consent was obtained, and SEEG-guided right amygdala ablation began at 1:40 PM and was completed within 30 min. After a 20-min recovery period, the index patient completed 336 trials (four blocks) of the facial emotion discrimination task. The pre-ablation facial emotion discrimination task was limited to three blocks due to constraints of clinical scheduling, whereas a fourth block could be completed during the post-ablation session. Furthermore, the patient completed the PTSD Checklist for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-Fifth Edition (PCL-5), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) before hospitalization and two months after the amygdala ablation. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Washington University in St. Louis, and the index patient provided informed consent to participate.

Surgical management and seizure course in the index patient

The index patient underwent a two-stage surgical treatment for seizure control (Fig. S1a). In the first stage, SEEG-guided radiofrequency ablation was performed using the implanted electrodes to target the right amygdala (Fig. S9a–c), as described above. Because seizure burden remained unchanged following this first-stage procedure (Fig. 5c), the patient subsequently underwent a second-stage intervention. Specifically, two months following right amygdala ablation, the laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) was performed to ablate the right hippocampus and entorhinal cortex (Fig. S9d).

Patient-reported outpatient seizure frequencies were recorded across four periods: the three months before amygdala ablation, two months after amygdala ablation, the first six months post-LITT, and the second six months post-LITT (Fig. S1a). Antiseizure medications were briefly reduced during early hospitalization to facilitate seizure capture and were fully resumed on the morning of Day 3, prior to all experimental procedures, and continued postoperatively (Fig. S1b). The patient did not receive any additional neuromodulatory or psychotherapeutic interventions during the study. In-hospital seizures occurred exclusively during the period of antiseizure medication reduction, specifically on Day 2 at 1:19 AM, 2:39 AM, 3:21 AM, 5:05 AM, 5:51 AM, 7:38 AM, 9:00 AM, 9:51 AM, and 6:51 PM, and on Day 3 at 3:55 AM (red dots in Fig. S1b). The final in-hospital seizure occurred more than 7 h prior to the initiation of behavioral testing (approximately 11:00 AM on Day 3), and all seizures occurred prior to full resumption of antiseizure medications. Notably, no seizures occurred during all the behavioral testing, functional electrical stimulation, or the SEEG-guided amygdala ablation procedure.

SEEG-guided amygdala ablation protocol in the index patient

Following intracranial seizure localization and electrical stimulation mapping, a multidisciplinary epilepsy case conference involving the neurological, neurosurgical, and neuropsychology teams was held to determine therapeutic options. SEEG-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) was performed in the basal and lateral nuclei of the right amygdala (Figs. S3 and S9) at the bedside while the index patient was awake and undergoing concurrent SEEG signal recording. RFA is a well-established technique that creates thermocoagulative lesions to treat various pathologies, including epilepsy43–46. Radiofrequency energy (G4 RF Generator, Boston Scientific Neuromodulation Corporation, USA) was delivered through the same individual SEEG contacts used for recording neural activity. A large-surface-area grounding pad placed on the patient’s skin served as a return path, and electrode impedance was verified to be within the 300–400 Ohm range. Monopolar thermocoagulation was delivered using a 480 kHz carrier frequency, beginning at 1 W and incrementally increasing by 1 W every 60 s. This process continued until reaching a threshold of 3–4 W, at which point vaporization occurred at the electrode-tissue interface, as indicated by the onset of recording artifacts in intracranial SEEG and surging impedance detected by the generator. Once this threshold was reached, power delivery was discontinued. The procedure was then repeated for each target contact. Throughout the procedure, neurological function and pain status were continuously assessed. The presence or absence of epileptiform discharges was evaluated at each stage. A total of five SEEG contacts spanning the basal and lateral amygdala were successfully ablated without evidence of adverse events (Fig. S3).

Volumetric quantification of ablation and target overlap in the index patient

Post-ablation volumetric analyses were performed to quantify the spatial correspondence between the RFA intervention region and medial temporal lobe regions of interest (ROIs), including the amygdala and hippocampus. Specifically, post-ablation T1-weighted MRI volumes were co-registered to pre-ablation T1-weighted MRI volumes using SPM12 and resampled to 1 mm3 isotropic resolution before mask generation and volumetric analysis. The target ROI regions were defined from FreeSurfer recon-all parcellations of the pre-ablation T1-weighted MRI. The hippocampal and amygdalar ROIs were extracted from these parcellations, and their surfaces were reconstructed using a marching-cubes algorithm to generate three-dimensional meshes. The RFA intervention regions were defined from the post-ablation MRI and manually annotated under the supervision of a fellowship-trained neurosurgeon (JTW) to delineate the necrotic core and surrounding transition region. The necrotic core was identified by T1-weighted hyperintensities, whereas the transition region was identified by adjacent hypointense regions consistent with perilesional edema. Surface meshes of the ablation regions were reconstructed in the native RAS coordinate space and voxelized onto the same spatial grid as the pre-ablation hippocampal and amygdalar ROIs to ensure anatomical alignment. Two RFA intervention regions were analyzed: the necrotic core and the combined ablation zone (i.e., necrotic core plus surrounding transition region). For each RFA intervention region, a surface boundary was extracted, and a signed-distance function (SDF) mask was computed from the surface to assign voxels within the enclosed boundary to the corresponding region. The total volume of each region was then calculated as the product of voxel size (1 mm3) and the number of voxels contained within the SDF-defined mask.

Voxel-wise Boolean operations were performed to quantify spatial correspondence between the target ROIs (i.e., amygdala and hippocampus) and RFA intervention regions. The intersection volume (VI) between the target ROI region (Vroi) and the RFA intervention region (Vabl) was computed using a logical AND operation. Accordingly, the percentage of target ROI affected by ablation (Proi) quantified the fraction of the target region encompassed by the RFA intervention region, and the percentage of total ablation volume within the target ROI (Pabl) represented the proportion of the overall RFA intervention localized within the target region. These metrics characterized both the degree of target engagement and the specificity of the ablation: Proi=VIVroi; Pabl=VIVabl.

Two standard spatial similarity indices were also computed to evaluate volumetric overlap: the IoU and the Dice similarity coefficient (Dice). IoU measured the proportion of shared to total combined volume, whereas Dice provided a normalized measure of spatial overlap. Both indices were expressed as unitless values ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect correspondence): IoU=VIVroi+Vabl−VI; Dice=2VIVroi+Vabl.

Facial emotion discrimination task protocol

The facial emotion discrimination task was selected to quantify the negative interpretation bias through perceptual judgments of ambiguous emotional faces—a process strongly linked to amygdala function57. This paradigm allows granular, parametric manipulation of emotional intensity (0–100% fear intensity level) while maintaining precise control over visual and temporal parameters, making it well-suited for intracranial electrophysiological recording. The task has been validated across healthy and clinical populations, including depression and anxiety57–60, ensuring direct comparability to prior literature and neural benchmarks.

The facial emotion discrimination task was performed before and after amygdala ablation to evaluate ablation-induced changes in the index patient’s emotional facial perception. The participant was positioned in front of a flat-screen monitor (27-inch diagonal size) at a viewing distance of 60 cm to express varying levels of fear intensity, ranging from happy to fearful expressions. Face stimuli consisted of four individuals (two females and two males) originally derived from the STOIC database101 and obtained from the openly available facial expression dataset released by ref. 59, which is distributed under the CC-BY 4.0 license (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). Specifically, each face was assigned a “fear intensity level” from 0% (fully happy) to 100% (fully fearful), with intermediate morphed faces ranging from 30 to 70% in steps of 10% (Fig. S4a). To generate the morphed face continua between fearful and happy expressions, pixel values and locations were interpolated between the fearful and happy faces using a piece-wise cubic-spline transformation over a Delaunay tessellation of manually selected control points57,58. To ensure uniformity across stimuli, the luminance and contrast of the images were normalized using the SHINE toolbox102.

In each trial (Fig. S4b), a single face was displayed for 1 s, followed by a prompt asking the participant to identify the facial emotion (fearful or happy) by pressing the corresponding keyboard arrow key (left or right). A 500 ms blank screen followed the response, after which the participant rated the confidence in the judgment using the left, down, and right arrow keys. A three-point scale was used to represent confidence: 0 (“very sure”), 1 (“somewhat sure”), and 2 (“unsure”). Trials were separated by a randomly jittered intertrial interval (ITI) of 1000–2000 ms. The participant was instructed to respond quickly, but no time limit was imposed. No feedback was provided during the task. Each face was presented three times in a pseudo-random order within each block, resulting in 84 trials per block. If the participant pressed a key more than once during either the emotional judgment or confidence rating periods, the trial was defined as invalid. Invalid trials were excluded from behavioral analysis but retained for electrophysiological analysis. The index patient completed three blocks (total trials: 252; invalid trials: 2) before ablation and four blocks (total trials: 336; invalid trials: 19) after ablation. An eye tracker (120 times/second; Tobii Pro Fusion, Tobii, Sweden) was used to verify that the subject maintained gaze on the center of the screen throughout the experiment. Neural activities were recorded from SEEG and EEG electrodes, and the behavioral responses were analyzed to assess the effects of amygdala ablation on emotional facial perception processing.

We compared the index patient’s behavioral and neural effect on the facial emotion discrimination task with a cohort of 16 intractable temporal lobe epilepsy patients (7 males; mean age 37 ± 11 years) undergoing intracranial SEEG monitoring at the same hospital (Fig. S2). All control patients had a Full-Scale IQ ≥ 70 and were free of comorbid PTSD or other trauma-related disorders, as confirmed by self-reports, directed clinical interview, and use of the Primary Care PTSD screen for DSM-5 (PC-PTSD-5, https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/assessment/screens/pc-ptsd.asp)103. The control group was selected to match the index patient in terms of epilepsy focus (frontal-temporal lobe involvement), electrode implantation coverage, and cognitive capacity. The control group included 67 contacts (35 from the lateral nucleus) across 14 patients in the left amygdala, and 53 contacts (28 from the lateral nucleus) across 11 patients in the right amygdala. Whole-brain SEEG coverage (200 ± 31 contacts per patient, range 146–264) included both hemispheres, providing comparable sampling of temporal and limbic regions across subjects. The control group completed an average of 262 trials (range 84–336) of the same facial emotion discrimination task. Intracranial SEEG and scalp EEG were recorded simultaneously for all patients. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Washington University in St. Louis, and all patients in the control group provided informed consent to participation.

Electrophysiology in emotional processing

SEEG and EEG signals during the facial emotion discrimination task were processed using EEGLAB (version 2022.0)104 and custom code developed in MATLAB (2024a, The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Before performing analyses, signals recorded from all SEEG and EEG contacts were visually inspected, and those exhibiting epileptic or artifactual activity were excluded. Spatially distributed noise common to all SEEG contacts was removed using a common average reference (CAR) filter. The same CAR filter was applied to EEG signals to ensure consistency in noise reduction across recording modalities. The signals were then filtered using a digital zero-phase shift band-pass filter of 0.5–30 Hz (pop_eegfiltnew(), EEGLAB). We focused on the time range of LPPs, a neural marker beginning approximately 300 ms after stimulus onset, which is sensitive to emotionally salient stimuli48,49. Specifically, continuous EEG and SEEG signals were epoched into 1.3-s segments, spanning −500 to 800 ms relative to stimulus onset, with the pre-stimulus interval (−500 to 0 ms) serving as the baseline. The epoch duration was limited to 800 ms post-stimulus onset, as previous studies suggest that LPP effects terminate by approximately 800 ms58. For each trial, signals were baseline-corrected by subtracting the average activity during the baseline period. To enable equal comparisons between pre- and post-ablation conditions, signals were further normalized by dividing by the standard deviation of all baseline data within their respective pre- or post-ablation datasets. Due to technical issues, EEG signals could not be analyzed after ablation. Since LPPs typically reflect responses in scalp EEG, the corresponding neural responses observed in intracranial SEEG (e.g., in the amygdala) during the same time window were referred to as late-phase ERPs (L-ERPs).

Amygdala functional stimulation protocol of the index patient

Electrical stimulation (50 Hz, biphasic, pulse width 250 μs; CereStim neurostimulator, Blackrock) was delivered to pairs of adjacent SEEG contacts (bipolar) located within the basal and lateral amygdala of the index patient. Each stimulation block consisted of 10 stimulation and 10 sham stimulation trials, presented in a pseudo-randomized order. Within each block, the stimulation was delivered at a constant intensity for all 10 stimulation trials, starting at 0.5 mA and increasing by 0.5 mA for each subsequent block. During each trial, stimulation was applied for 1 s, followed by a 3–5 s intertrial interval, during which the index patient was asked to indicate whether he felt any sensations or other subjective experiences by pressing the left or right arrow key. At the end of each block, the index patient was debriefed to describe any sensations experienced during stimulation. Stimulation for each pair of contacts was terminated once the index patient could accurately identify at least 70 percent of stimulation trials by reported feeling a sensation, or alternatively, if stimulation induced epileptiform afterdischarges. This procedure was performed on four pairs of SEEG contacts: the right basal amygdala, right basolateral amygdala, right lateral amygdala, and left lateral amygdala (Fig. S7). Dose-response relationships were determined. Task control, stimulation delivery, and synchronized acquisition of neural signals were managed with the BCI2000 general-purpose brain-computer interface software99.

Psychiatric symptom scoring and categorization in the index patient

The PCL-5 is a 20-item self-rated scale that assesses symptoms across the four DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-Fifth Edition) clusters. These clusters including (Fig. S10): intrusions (Cluster B: items 1–5), avoidance of trauma reminders (Cluster C: items 6–7), negative alterations in cognitions and mood (Cluster D: items 8–14), and alterations in arousal and reactivity (Cluster E: items 15–20)70. The PCL-5 score ranges for categorical levels of PTSD symptoms are as follows: 0–10 = minimal, 11–20 = mild, 21–40 = moderate, 41–60 = severe, and 61–80 = very severe105. Based on clinical recommendations and validation studies, a PCL-5 score of 31–33 or higher is often used as the threshold for probable PTSD diagnosis69. The BAI scores were categorized into the following levels of anxiety symptoms: 0–7 = minimal, 8–15 = mild, 16–25 = moderate, and 26–63 = severe106. The BDI-II scores were categorized into the following levels of depressive symptoms: 0–13 = minimal, 14–19 = mild, 20–28 = moderate, and 29–63 = severe107.
