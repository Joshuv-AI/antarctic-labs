Quantitative comparison of heat flow, guarded-heater and AC Harman methods for thermoelectric module efficiency Preprint: APS/123-QED
K. Okawa, Y. Amagai, N. Sakamoto, N.-H. Kaneko

Abstract
The evaluation of thermoelectric conversion efficiency remains challenging owing to the lack of internationally standardized measurement protocols. Commonly used techniques—including the heat flow, guarded heater, and AC Harman methods—differ fundamentally in their operating principles and sensitivity to heat losses. In this study, we benchmark three module-level efficiency measurement techniques—the heat-flow, guarded heater, and AC Harman methods—using commercial Bi 2 ​ Te 3 \rm Bi_{2}Te_{3} -based modules with different substrates materials. The conversion efficiencies obtained using the heat flow and guarded heater methods showed good agreement within experimental uncertainty for temperature differences up to 70 K 70\text{\,}\mathrm{K} . In contrast, the AC Harman method underestimated the conversion efficiency by approximately 30 % 30\text{\,}\% . Through systematic measurements on modules with different substrates and detailed finite element simulations, this underestimation was attributed to boundary-condition effects and radiative heat dissipation, which significantly reduce the effective temperature difference developed across the module in the Harman configuration. These results highlight the limitations of the AC Harman method for quantitative conversion-efficiency evaluation under non-ideal thermal environments and emphasize the necessity of accounting for radiative and substrate-related heat losses. Nevertheless, with appropriate modeling and correction, strategies, the AC Harman method remains a viable tool for rapid performance screening. Our results provide a quantitative benchmark of major measurement techniques and contribute to clarify best practices for module-level thermoelectric metrology and guide method selection, fully supporting future efforts toward methodological standardization.

II.3
AC Harman method

The AC Harman method is a valuable tool for estimating the
ZT
, enabling rapid assessment of module-level performance (Fig. 1(d)). The experimental setup used for the AC Harman method, as shown in Fig. 2(b), employed a heater block placed beneath the module to control its temperature. Silicone grease was applied between the module and the heater block to ensure good thermal contact. Unlike the other two methods, one side of the module was exposed to the environment, resulting in a temperature difference caused by heat loss due to thermal radiation (
Q_{\rm rad}
).

In previous studies, we evaluated BiTe-based thermoelectric materials using a comparable approach and found that the
(R_{\rm DC}-R_{\rm AC})/R_{\rm AC}
value measured at ambient pressure was underestimated by up to
40\text{\,}\%
compared with measurements performed under high-vacuum conditions, owing to increased convective heat loss
29
. This behavior is attributed to the transition from the viscous flow regime to the molecular flow regime, which increases the number of molecules contributing to heat conduction. In the present apparatus, when operated under vacuum conditions with pressures
P\leq${10}^{-1}\text{\,}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}$
, the effect of convective heat loss can be neglected.

For the time-domain voltage measurements, AC or DC currents with identical root-mean-square values were applied to the module using an AC/DC current source (Keithley 6221), and the resulting output voltage was measured with a nanovoltmeter (Keithley 2182A). For the frequency-domain measurements, an LCR meter (HIOKI IM3590 impedance analyzer) operated in analyzer mode was used to perform frequency-sweep measurements, with the integration time set to one cycle corresponding to a single period of the applied signal.

IV.2
Numerical simulation

To clarify the origin of this discrepancy, we examine the factors contributing to the difference in conversion efficiency evaluated using the AC Harman method. A key distinction lies in the boundary conditions employed in the measurements. In the AC Harman method, one side of the module is effectively open: heater on the high-temperature side is positioned beneath the module, while the opposite side is exposed to a vacuum, resulting in an uncontrolled temperature on the low-temperatures side. In addition, radiative heat transfer from surfaces not in contact with the heater block induces multidirectional heat flow, thereby reducing the effective temperature difference within the module.

This highlights careful boundary-condition control is essential for precise conversion-efficiency measurements.

. Although the AC Harman method is widely used for material evaluation and its reliability has been extensively discussed, the present study demonstrates that substrate properties and radiative transfer can introduce significant discrepancies in module-level conversion-efficiency estimates.

V
Conclusion

A comparative study was conducted to evaluate three measurement methods for characterizing thermoelectric modules using a commercial
\rm Bi_{2}Te_{3}
-based modules with different substrates. An experimental apparatus was developed to estimate the thermoelectric conversion efficiency using these methods. From a standardization viewpoint, our findings offer several important insights.

First, the heat flow method (HFM) and the guarded heater method (GHM) yielded close conversion efficiency values within comparable uncertainty ranges when measuring temperature gradients between the hot and cold sides of the modules. Second, the AC Harman method was effective for evaluating small temperature differences; however, an analytical approach was required to accurately assess conversion efficiency at larger temperature differences, which presents a challenge for module-level evaluation under such conditions. This result indicates the necessary of correcting for radiative heat loss in the AC Harman method, which becomes a dominant source of error at high temperatures, as well as accounting for heat loss through the substrates. Importantly, our methodology using identical modules, systematic boundary condition control, and coupled numerical-experimental validation, contributes to the development of practical measurement protocols, selection guidelines for appropriate techniques, and design principles for reference modules, thereby accelerating the progress toward internationally thermoelectric module metrology.
