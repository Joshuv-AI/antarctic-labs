Assessing the impact of indication for labor induction on delivery outcomes: a five-year cohort study
Authors: Heidi Kruit, Mika Gissler, Leena Rahkonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs the rate of induction of labor (IOL) is steadily increasing with the increasing rates of maternal age and obesity, having consequences on perinatal morbidity, health economics, and maternal childbirth experience, evidence for decision making should be available related to the indications. This study investigates the association of IOL indications with the delivery outcomes and provides clinicians with valuable information to weigh the benefits and potential risks associated with IOL.Materials and methodsThis retrospective five-year cohort study was conducted in Helsinki University Hospital between 2017 and 2021. A total of 16 377 pregnant women undergoing IOL ≥ 37 gestational weeks with a live singleton fetus in cephalic presentation were included. The primary outcome measures were the rates of cesarean delivery (CD) and composite adverse neonatal outcome (including one or more of the following: perinatal death, neonatal intensive care admission, umbilical artery blood pH value ≤ 7.05, base excess value <-12, and 5-minute Apgar score 0–6).ResultsThe study population consisted of 16 377 women. The most common indications for IOL were post-term pregnancy (31.3%) and pre-labor rupture of membranes (PROM) (26.3%). Overall, the CD rate was 20.3%, and the incidence of composite adverse neonatal outcome was 12.7%. Delivery outcomes varied according to the indication for induction. The highest CD rate of 27.9% was observed among women induced for hypertensive disorders (adjusted OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.4–1.8), whereas inductions for maternal exhaustion were associated with the lowest CD rate. Composite adverse neonatal outcomes were most frequent (19.1%, n = 179) following induction for fetal indications and least frequent (7.4%, n = 42) among women induced because of fear of childbirth. In analyses, performed separately for each IOL indication, nulliparity, unfavorable cervix, maternal age ≥ 35 years, maternal height < 164 cm, and pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 30 remained independent risk-factors for CD.ConclusionsThe indication for IOL is associated with both CD and adverse neonatal outcomes. While indication-specific differences should be considered when counseling women and planning induction, maternal characteristics and parity remain important determinants of CD risk. These findings may support individualized clinical decision-making regarding IOL.Keywords: Induction of labor, Indication for labor induction, Cesarean delivery, Adverse neonatal outcome, Induction failure, Maternal characteristics, Parity

Introduction

The incidence of induction of labor (IOL) is increasing globally, being currently approximately 30% in the developed countries [1–3]. The IOL rates may be explained by the evolving induction indications due to elevating rates of advanced maternal age, obesity, and pregnancy complications and by the development of diagnostics and pregnancy care practices. The induction indications range from evidence-based indications such as post-term pregnancy and pre-labor rupture of membranes (PROM) to relative or even conflictive indications, such as non-diabetic fetal macrosomia [4, 5]. Although labor is mostly induced for reducing maternal or neonatal morbidity, elective inductions for non-medical reasons or on maternal request have also increased [6, 7]. The indication and decision on IOL should be carefully weighed against the potential risks and benefits of continuing the pregnancy.

The increasing incidence of IOL has established an abiding debate on the effect of induction on the rates of cesarean delivery (CD) and adverse perinatal outcomes. An abundance of research has focused on risk factors contributing to CD likelihood, but little attention has been given to the relationship between the IOL indication and delivery outcome. In particular, large cohort studies comparing maternal and neonatal outcomes across different induction indications within the same population are scarce. A few retrospective studies focusing on the indication of IOL with relatively small sample sizes have addressed this question. Parkes et al. showed in their retrospective cohort study on 796 women that the indication for IOL impacts the risk of CD in nulliparas, specifically in IOL for fetal indications [8]. Similarly, in Kumari’s et al. study on 409 women, mode of delivery was affected by indication of induction, with IOL for suspected macrosomia and hypertensive disorders in pregnancy having the highest rates of CD [5]. On the contrary, in Gerli’s et al. retrospective cohort of 324 women, no single indication of IOL, but instead advanced maternal age was associated with the risk for CD [9].

Given the variety of indications and evidence supporting them, a connection between the induction indication and risk for adverse delivery outcomes, such as CD and NICU, may be assumed to exist. As the decision on IOL has consequences on maternal morbidity [10], health economics [11] and maternal childbirth experience [12], evidence for decision making should be available related to the indications.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the adjusted association of indication for IOL and delivery outcomes, including CD and composite adverse neonatal outcome in a large retrospective cohort. By comparing outcomes across a broad range of induction indications within the same population, this study addresses an important gap in the existing literature and provides information that may support clinical counselling and decision-making regarding IOL.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Helsinki University Hospital, from 1.1.2017 to 31.1.2021. Helsinki University Hospital is a large tertiary academic center with 65 947 deliveries during the study period, with the mean labor induction rate of 28% and CD rate of 17% during the study period. Elective inductions or CD with no medical indication are not performed. We included all pregnant women who underwent IOL at or beyond 37 weeks of gestational age with a live singleton fetus in cephalic presentation. Women with multiple gestation, preterm delivery, intrauterine fetal demise, and breech presentation were excluded from the study. Formation of the study population is presented in Fig. 1. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (IRB), Committee for Obstetrics and Gynecology, Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District (nr. HUS/3172/2018 and HUS/54/2019). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, written informed consent was waived by the IRB according to national legislation (Medical Research Act 488/1999, Ch.2a (23.4.2004/295), Sect. 5 and 10a). All methods were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Flow chart of the study population during 2017–2021, n = 16,377

The primary outcome measures of the study were the rates of CD and composite adverse neonatal outcome following each indication for IOL. The composite adverse neonatal outcome included one or more of the following: perinatal death, neonatal intensive care (NICU) admission, umbilical artery blood pH value ≤ 7.05, base excess value <-12, and 5-minute Apgar score 0–6. The secondary outcome measures included post-partum hemorrhage defined as blood loss ≥ 1000 ml, indication for CD, shoulder dystocia (defined as a need for additional obstetric maneuvers to deliver the fetus after the head was delivered and gentle traction had failed), grade III-IV perineal tear, severe maternal complication, birthweight ≥ 4500 g, large for gestational age (LGA) defined as birthweight ≥ + 2 standard deviations (SD), and small for gestational age (SGA) defined as birthweight ≤ -2 SDs, 5-minute Apgar score, umbilical artery blood gas values, and NICU admission. Severe maternal complications included relaparotomy or surgical deep wound revision, uterine rupture, postpartum hysterectomy, pulmonary embolism, septicemia, and intensive care admission. The data for baseline characteristics and outcomes were collected from the hospital’s electronic health record database.

The indications for labor induction were categorized as post-term pregnancy (gestational age ≥ 41 + 0 weeks), PROM (with no onset of spontaneous contractions within 24 h, and in case of Group B streptococcus (GBS) colonization within 12 h), hypertensive disease of pregnancy (including pregestational and gestational hypertension and preeclampsia), diabetes (including pregestational diabetes or gestational diabetes), fetal growth restriction (FGR), non-diabetic fetal macrosomia, cholestasis of pregnancy, fear of childbirth (defined as labor induction with a referral from maternity clinic due to fear or concern over childbirth and ICD-10 diagnosis O99.80), oligohydramnios, maternal exhaustion (including physical and psychological exhaustion of pregnancy) and psychosocial indications, other maternal reason, and other fetal reason. The category “other maternal reason” included previous rapid delivery (less than two hours from the start of contractions), previous stillbirth, previous fetal macrosomia or shoulder dystocia, previous sphincter injury, antepartum hemorrhage, maternal pregestational or gestational medical condition (excluding diabetes and hypertensive diseases), substance abuse, and other maternal risk factor such as advanced age > 40 years, ovum donation pregnancy, and morbid obesity. The category of “other fetal reason” included decreased fetal movements or non-reassuring cardiotocogram (CTG) or bioprofile, infection, unstable presentation, blood type immunization, cardiac arrythmia and anomaly. The “other maternal reason” and “other fetal reason” indications were grouped because the individual conditions were relatively uncommon and separate analyses would have resulted in small subgroup sizes and limited statistical reliability. In cases with multiple recorded indications for induction of labor, the primary indication was defined as the leading clinical reason for induction as documented in the medical record at the time of decision-making.

In case of an unfavorable cervix, defined as Bishop score < 6, IOL was started with cervical ripening by oral 50 mcg misoprostol administered every 4 h or a single 75 ml balloon catheter (Rüsch two-way Foley Couvelaire tip catheter size 22 Ch, Teleflex Medical, Athlone, Ireland) retained for a maximum of 24 h, or a combination of both. When Bishop score ≥ 6 was reached, IOL was continued by artificial rupturing of the membranes in case of intact amniotic membranes, and oxytocin in the absence of spontaneous regular contractions. The induction method was chosen according to cervical status and the joint preference of the patient and the attending obstetrician. Balloon catheter was the preferred method for women with a history of previous CD, whereas in cases of PROM both balloon catheter and oral misoprostol were used during the study period. Failed induction was diagnosed after 12 h of oxytocin administration with ruptured membranes and no cervical progress [13] Labor arrest was defined as cervical dilation of 6 cm or more, membrane rupture and no progress despite of 4 h or more of adequate contractions [13]. Continuous cardiotocography was routinely used during labor and oxytocin augmentation was started if labor did not progress or contractions were deemed inadequate.

Categorical variables were compared by the chi-square test and analyzed for odd ratios (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Nulliparous and multiparous women were analyzed separately for the primary outcome measures, as the different CD rates according to parity following labor induction are well-established [14]. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using multiple logistic regression, performed separately for each induction indication with the indication of interest as the exposure variable and all other indications combined served as the reference group. The models were adjusted for parity, unfavorable cervix (defined as Bishop score < 6), maternal age ≥ 35 years, maternal height < 164 cm, and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. These variables were selected as they are established risk factors for induction failure and CD [15–18]. Furthermore, the cut-off 164 cm for maternal height was set at the average height of Finnish women, based on our previous findings linking shorter stature with increased CD risk [14, 19]. Separate logistic regression models were used to allow clearer interpretation of indication-specific associations given the clinical heterogeneity of induction indications and differences in baseline risk profiles across indication groups. The potential impact of small induction indication groups on the logistic regression models was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors, which were all within acceptable limits, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding the smallest groups. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed by using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The study population consisted of 16 377 women who underwent IOL (Fig. 1). The characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the women was 32.3 (5.2 SD) years, the mean BMI 25.8 (5.5 SD) kg/m2 and the mean gestational age 40.4 (1.4 SD) weeks. The proportion of women with post-term pregnancy was 41.0% (n = 6707 at the time of induction. Of these, post-term pregnancy was recorded as the indication for induction in 5098 cases, while the remaining women at ≥ 41 weeks underwent IOL due to other obstetric indications.

Severe maternal complication occurred in 82 (0.5%) women and was more common following unplanned CD than vaginal delivery: 72.0% [n = 59] vs. 28.0% [n = 23] (p < 0.001, OR 10.2 95% CI 6.3–16.6). Of the women who had severe complication, 18.2% (n = 15) had a history of previous CD.

The respective CD rates according to indication of IOL are presented in Table 3. In the total study population, the highest CD rate of 27.9% occurred following IOL in hypertensive disease of pregnancy (adjusted OR 1.6 95% CI 1.4–1.8), while the lowest CD of 10.0% rate occurred following induction for maternal exhaustion. Composite adverse neonatal outcome rate was the lowest (7.4%, n = 42) in inductions for fear of childbirth, and the most frequent (19.1%, n = 179) following induction for fetal reasons. When excluding the other fetal reason category including all anomalies, the composite adverse neonatal outcome occurred most often following IOL for FGR in the total study population (15.9%, n = 77) (Table 3).

Birthweight ≥ 4500 g occurred in 2.9% [n = 465] of all neonates, with the CD rate of 28.2%. Among those, whose induction indication was diabetes (n = 1866) or non-diabetic fetal macrosomia (n = 374), birthweight ≥ 4500 g occurred in 3.4% (n = 64) and 12.4% (n = 46), with the CD rates of 28.1% (n = 18) and 17.4% (n = 8), respectively.

LGA occurred in 3.2% [n = 526] of all neonates, with the CD rate of 26.6%. Among those, whose induction indication was diabetes (n = 1866) or non-diabetic fetal macrosomia (n = 374), LGA occurred in 7.0% (n = 131) and 20.1% (n = 75), with the CD rates of 28.2% (n = 37) and 16.0% (n = 12), respectively.

SGA occurred in 3.4% [n = 561] of all neonates, with the CD rate of 23.9%. Among those, whose induction indication was FGR (n = 483) or oligohydramnios (n = 253), SGA occurred in 38.1% (n = 184) and 9.1% (n = 23), with the CD rates of 14.3% (n = 69) and 22.9% (n = 58), respectively.

Nulliparous women had higher risk for CD compared to multiparas (OR 3.5 95% CI 3.2–3.8). The rate of CD was higher among the women with unfavorable cervix and need of cervical ripening compared to women with favorable cervix at the start of IOL (20.7% vs. 14.7%; p < 0.001, OR 1.6 95% CI 1.5–1.8). Women with advanced maternal age ≥ 35 or ≥ 40 years more frequently had CD compared to women < 35 (21.7% [n = 1218] vs. 19.6% [n = 2111]; p = 0.001, OR 1.1 95% CI 1.1–1.2) and women < 40 years (26.7% [n = 344] vs. 19.2% [n = 2985]; p < 0.001, OR 1.5 95% CI 1.3–1.7) respectively. CD also occurred more often in obese women with BMI ≥ 30 compared to women with BMI < 30 (24.8% [n = 805] vs. 19.2% [n = 2243]; p < 0.001, OR 1.4 95% CI 1.3–1.5), and in shorter women with height < 164 cm compared with women ≥ 164 cm (25.6% [n = 1660] vs. 16.7% [n = 1609]; p < 0.005, OR 1.7 95% CI 1.6–1.9).

In logistic regression analyses, performed separately for each induction indication, nulliparity, unfavorable cervix, maternal age ≥ 35 years, maternal height < 164 cm, and pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 remained independently associated with CD (data not shown). For composite adverse neonatal outcome, nulliparity, maternal height < 164 cm, and pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 remained independently associated across each induction indication (data not shown). Post-term pregnancy, diabetes, and non-diabetic macrosomia as indications for labor induction were independent risk factors for CD, while PROM, FGR, maternal exhaustion and psychosocial reasons presented a low risk for CD (Table 5). Induction indications diabetes and maternal and fetal reasons were independently associated with composite adverse neonatal outcome (Table 5).

Discussion

Our retrospective five-year cohort study, conducted at a tertiary academic hospital and encompassing 16 377 cases of labor induction, demonstrates that post-term pregnancy (≥ 41 gestational weeks), diabetes and non-diabetic macrosomia as indications for labor induction are associated with increased odds of CD, while psychosocial reasons and maternal exhaustion are associated with a lower odds of CD. Approximately 20% of induced labors end in CD, also in the current study. Notably, nulliparous women had a markedly higher CD rate of 30% compared to 10% in multiparous women, regardless of the indication for induction. This disparity highlights the pivotal role of parity in induction success and the mode of delivery. The highest CD rate occurred in cases of induction for hypertensive diseases of pregnancy (one in three women), while the lowest rate was observed following inductions for maternal exhaustion and psychosocial reasons (one in ten women). Similar result on hypertensive disorders being associated with a high risk for CD following induction has also been previously demonstrated [5]. In addition to confirming previous findings, our large cohort provides further evidence that induction indication and parity are associated with cesarean delivery risk in a clinical practice setting.

The most common induction indications included post-term pregnancy, PROM, diabetes, and hypertensive disease, collectively accounting for 75% of all inductions. The increasing rates of IOL have sparked concerns regarding the validity of induction indications, but our study shows that fewer than one in ten inductions were performed for relative indications, such as non-diabetic fetal macrosomia, maternal exhaustion, and fear of childbirth. Moreover, recent research suggests that even elective indications in uncomplicated pregnancies may improve outcomes without raising the CD rate [20]. A substantial body of evidence has shown that IOL does not result in an increased CD rate [14, 20–24]. However, with the rising rates of advanced maternal age, obesity, and complicated pregnancies leading to a constant increase in labor inductions, the number of CD procedures has also eventually risen, as we have not been able to reduce the rate of unsuccessful inductions leading to CD. This trend results in increased healthcare resource utilization and costs, underscoring the need for standardized induction protocols and the development of methods to minimize unsuccessful inductions.

Post-term pregnancy has been associated with increased rates of operative delivery and maternal and neonatal adverse outcomes [23, 25–28] Our study also found, in line with previous research, that labor induction for post-term pregnancy was associated with a high rate of CD being one in four women [9, 23, 25–28]. However, we did not observe a significant increase in composite adverse neonatal outcomes in cases of post-term pregnancy induction compared to other indications, although recent research have demonstrated that labor induction before 42 gestational weeks [29] and even before 41 + 0 weeks of gestation offers potential benefits in reducing the incidence of stillbirth and associated complications, without an increase in the rates of CD or healthcare costs [27, 30].

PROM, which occurs in 8 to 19% of pregnancies, is a significant cause of maternal and neonatal morbidity, especially with delayed onset of labor, and elevates the likelihood of CD [31, 32]. However, in our study PROM was not associated with an increased risk of CD, and neonatal morbidity remained comparable to other indications. PROM represents a distinct clinical pathway, as labor is induced in the presence of ruptured membranes and the clinical decision-making is often driven by prevention of maternal and neonatal infection rather than by the indications typically underlying induction with intact membranes. Therefore, outcomes following PROM are not directly comparable with inductions in women with intact membranes. While in some countries PROM cases are not included in reported induction rates, in Finland they are routinely registered as inductions of labor. As PROM accounts for roughly one quarter of all inductions, excluding these cases would not have reflected actual clinical practice in our setting. These findings are consistent with previous studies and further support the distinct clinical nature of PROM-related inductions.

In our dataset, a significant proportion of primiparous women who underwent IOL for diabetes and non-diabetic macrosomia had induction failure, 40.8% and 42.9%, respectively, and delivered by CD. Similar results of increased risk of CD in women with suspected macrosomia were presented in the meta-analysis of non-randomized trials by Sanchez- Ramos et al. [33]. Among those induced due to non-diabetic macrosomia, one in ten infants had a birth weight of ≥ 4500 g, and one in five were LGA, highlighting the absence of a sensitive diagnostic tool for estimating fetal weight late in pregnancy. Remarkably, over 80% of these larger infants were delivered vaginally. These findings underline the importance of individually tailored care based on careful evaluation of known risk factors, clear counseling and shared decision-making and when considering labor induction. Pregnancies with LGA fetus are linked to elevated maternal and perinatal health risks, such as CD, postpartum hemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, and birth trauma. To mitigate these complications, IOL has been proposed as a potential intervention. Nevertheless, despite the presence of compelling evidence supporting labor induction for suspected macrosomia/LGA, current guidelines do not advocate for the routine IOL in this patient group [34, 35].

Hypertension and pre-eclampsia, high-priority indications for IOL, are associated with improved outcomes and should be recommended for women with mild hypertension beyond 37 weeks of gestation, as supported by the HYPITAT study [30]. Continuing the pregnancy does not confer benefits once pre-eclampsia is diagnosed, and majority of women with pre-eclampsia who undergo IOL achieve vaginal birth, even at an early gestational age [16]. Although in our study hypertensive disease was associated with the highest CD rate among all the IOL indications, 70% of the women with hypertensive disease had a successful induction and vaginal delivery, which is in line with a previous large cohort study and supports IOL in these women [36].

Interestingly, induction for FGR resulted in successful vaginal delivery in over 85% of cases in our study. While FGR has traditionally been considered a risk factor for adverse intrapartum outcomes, the current evidence, as well as our results, suggests high probability of vaginal delivery and that induction versus expectant management does not result in a significant difference in perinatal outcomes encouraging IOL in cases of FGR [37–39].

Psychosocial reasons, such as maternal exhaustion and fear of childbirth, were associated with a low risk for CD and composite adverse neonatal outcome following IOL compared to other indications. This may be attributed to the fact that most of these women were multiparous. IOL in psychosocial indications may be beneficial in many physical and mental health related aspects, including the mother’s wellbeing and interaction with the newborn post-partum. Therefore, when considering labor induction for psychosocial reasons, individualized counseling and careful assessment of maternal circumstances, potential benefits, clinical safety, and resource implications are warranted. Our findings suggest that, in appropriately selected women, particularly multiparas, induction for psychosocial indications can be associated with favorable delivery outcomes.

Oligohydramnios and fetal reasons resulted in relatively high CD rates, also in multiparous women in our study. Similarly, in the study by Parkes et al., IOL for fetal indications was found to increase the risk of CD [8]. The increased risk for CD may be attributed to fetal distress but also the tendency in clinical practice to hesitate on the decision to proceed with IOL or to opt for CD more readily in such cases.

Advanced maternal age and obesity are increasing globally, including in Finland, where in 2022, 26.7% of pregnancies occurred in women ≥ 35 years and 5.7% in women ≥ 40 years. With one in five pregnant women now having BMI ≥ 30, gestational diabetes has also risen to 25%. Maternal age, nulliparity, obesity, and low Bishop score are established risk factors for induction failure and CD, which was confirmed in our study across all indications for labor induction. The increased risk of cesarean delivery associated with post-term pregnancy and diabetes may be explained by several interrelated clinical factors. Post-term pregnancies are more likely to be associated with decreased cervical readiness and increased fetal size, which may reduce the likelihood of successful vaginal delivery and increase the probability of induction failure. In diabetic pregnancies, fetal macrosomia, altered placental function, and associated maternal comorbidities may be associated with labor dystocia and a higher likelihood of operative delivery. In addition, the timing of induction relative to cervical status and progression of pregnancy-related metabolic changes may further influence delivery outcomes in these groups.

These findings may be used to support pre-induction counseling by providing patients with indication-specific estimates of cesarean delivery and neonatal outcomes, allowing more concrete discussion of expected delivery outcomes before induction. In nulliparous women and in inductions for post-term pregnancy, diabetes, and fetal macrosomia, counseling may emphasize a higher likelihood of cesarean delivery and the potential need for closer intrapartum surveillance. In contrast, in multiparous women and in psychosocial indications, counseling may focus on the generally favorable prognosis and lower odds for CD. These results may also help clinicians identify higher-risk inductions that may benefit from earlier senior involvement and more proactive labor management.

The strengths of our study lie in its substantial study population and the comprehensive dataset from detailed medical registry. Our study population consisted of medically and clinically indicated inductions of labor, and elective inductions without medical indication were not included. Therefore, the findings are most directly generalizable to settings with similar clinical practice patterns. Nevertheless, the study has several limitations. Its single-center, retrospective design may limit the generalizability of the findings, as local clinical protocols, institutional practices, and patient selection criteria may influence both the indication for IOL and subsequent delivery outcomes. NICU admission constituted the largest component of the composite neonatal outcome and may be influenced by institutional admission thresholds and local clinical practices in addition to true neonatal morbidity, which may have introduced variability in the composite outcome across indications. Some cases included multiple indications for IOL, which may introduce overlap and variability in classification and outcomes. In addition, the categories “other maternal reason” and “other fetal reason” included several clinically heterogeneous conditions. These categories were created to avoid multiple small subgroups with limited statistical power; however, the included conditions may differ substantially in their underlying risk profiles. Another limitation of this study is that certain potentially relevant variables, such as previous CD, fetal sex, and birthweight category, were not included in the adjusted models. In particular, some variables may represent post-exposure factors, and their inclusion could introduce overadjustment bias. Therefore, the findings related to these categories should be interpreted with caution. The choice of induction method was based on cervical status and shared decision-making between clinicians and patients, which may have introduced selection bias. Both clinician judgment and patient preference may therefore have influenced the selection of induction methods and potentially the observed outcomes. The study also exhibited variability in induction methods employed across cases. While all methods used are evidence-based and widely accepted in clinical practice, this heterogeneity may introduce residual confounding, as method selection can vary according to clinical indication and institutional practice. This should be considered when interpreting the association between induction indication and delivery outcomes. As multiple secondary outcomes were analyzed without adjustment for multiplicity, there is a potential risk of type I error, and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Severe maternal complications were rare and therefore not analyzed in detail by induction indication; these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, the proportion of missing data was low and is reported in the respective tables; however, complete-case analysis may still introduce a potential risk of selection bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the indication for labor induction plays a role in determining the risks of CD and adverse neonatal outcomes during the IOL process. Indications such as post-term pregnancy, diabetes, and non-diabetic fetal macrosomia were associated with an increased risk of cesarean delivery, whereas psychosocial indications and maternal exhaustion were associated with a lower risk. Composite adverse neonatal outcomes were most frequent in inductions for fetal reasons and diabetes-related indications. Parity had a strong modifying effect on outcomes, with substantially higher cesarean delivery rates in nulliparous women compared with multiparous women across all induction indications. These findings highlight the importance of considering both induction indication and parity when counseling patients and planning induction of labor.
