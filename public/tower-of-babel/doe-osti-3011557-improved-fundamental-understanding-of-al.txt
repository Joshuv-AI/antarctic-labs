 ABSTRACT

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)’s Hanford Site in Washington State houses 177 underground
storage tanks containing millions of gallons of nuclear and chemical waste with high aluminum content.
Aluminum (Al) salt reactions with strong bases produce aluminum hydroxides, like boehmite (γ-AlOOH),
which are main components of insoluble nuclear waste sludge. Understanding the morphology,
crystallinity, and dissolution behavior of boehmite under waste tank conditions is necessary for improving
waste management and mitigation strategies. The ORNL team uses in situ multimodal analysis strategy to
study Al chemistry of simulated tank waste using microfluidic reactors and advanced chemical imaging
and mass spectrometry (MS) techniques. Because the SALVI device is vacuum compatible and
transferrable among different platforms, we can use it in scanning electron microscopy (SEM), vacuum
ultraviolet single photon mass spectrometry (VUV SPI-MS), and time-of-flight secondary ion mass
spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) to study the chemical speciation and colloid stability in liquids in this project.
Additionally, ex situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and x-ray diffraction (XRD) spectroscopy
can be used to verify particle phase to further the understanding of the Al-bearing phases. This report gives
a summary of the technical progress of the ORNL tasks. A plan for year 2 performance is recommended in
the summary.

 8

 1. INTRODUCTION

 A diverse set of Al phases have been identified in Hanford waste sludge, including gibbsite [α-
Al(OH)3], boehmite [γ-AlOOH], amorphous aluminum hydroxide, dawsonite [NaAl(OH)2CO3], nitrate-
cancrinite [Na8(AlSiO4)6(NO3)2•nH2O], aluminosilicates, and soluble aluminate [NaAl(OH)4].1-4 The three
predominant Al-bearing solid phases are sodium aluminate [NaAl(OH)4], gibbsite [α-Al(OH)3], and
boehmite [γ-AlOOH], followed by unknown phases, zeolite and a unknown Cr phase.5 The variation in
particle size is several orders of magnitude. While many particles are in the nanometer (nm) to micrometer
(µm) range, the largest gibbsite agglomerates observed are up to 7 cm.6, 7 The particle size distribution and
the morphological variability of Al-bearing solid phases directly control the rheological behavior of waste
sludge. Limited studies have been carried out on the solubility and dissolution/precipitation kinetics of
gibbsite and boehmite after the adsorption of Cr (III) species.8 However, no such studies have been
conducted Al-silicates and dawsonite.
 In this project, we aim to address the following issues: (1) Why are the measured Al solubilities in the
waste highly variable and generally higher than those predicted from simple chemical systems? (2) How
can we improve the existing thermodynamic model (e.g., using machine learning) to account for the
complex interactions of chemical species in waste sludge? (3) What are the effects of the coexisting
chemical components on Al phase dissolution and transformation? (4) Specifically, what controls gibbsite
to boehmite transformation or vice versa? (5) What controls the large variabilities observed in the efficiency
of aluminum removal among different tanks? (6) How does the stratification of waste sludge (e.g., the
formation of saltcake) affect Al dissolution and removal? And (7) how can we formulate new washing and
leaching solution recipes to accelerate Al dissolution and removal? Our preliminary work indicates that
chemical species such as borate and CO2 may increase Al solubility and enhance boehmite to gibbsite
transformation, respectively. The issue of whether these species can promote Al dissolution and removal in
waste pretreatment will also be addressed.
 Dr Yu’s team at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory performs Tasks 1 and 2 in this program and the
results serve the Research Theme A–Insight into in situ formation of Al-bearing phases in simulated tank
waste. Our unique technical solutions will use the system for analysis at the liquid vacuum interface
(SALVI) microreactor, in combination with other analytical techniques, to investigate the in situ formation
of Al-bearing phases in simulated HLW sludge environments. In the first year of performance, several tasks
were performed, including the following,
 • Task 1.1a: In situ liquid vacuum ultraviolet single photon mass spectrometry (VUV SPI-MS)
 analysis of chemical composition,
 • Task 1.1b: In situ liquid time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) analysis of
 chemical composition,
 • Task 1.2: Ex situ multimodal imaging and analysis of Al-bearing phases
 • Task 2.1: In situ scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging of particles in slurry conditions
 This annual report provides technical updates of each of these subtasks in the first year of performance
at ORNL. Results from each subtask is described in their own chapter. A summary and next-step work plan
is suggested for the second year’s performance.

 2. TASK 1.1A: IN SITU & EX SITU CHARACTERIZATION OF AL-BEARING PHASES
 FORMED IN SIMULATED TANK WASTE SOLUTIONS

 Our goal in Task 1 is to perform detailed characterization of Al-bearing phases in simulated tank waste
solutions using ex-situ and in-situ mass spectrometry of liquid samples. Two unique MS techniques, ToF-
SIMS and VUV SPI-MS were employed primarily. The Al-bearing solids of interest include gibbsite,
boehmite, aluminosilicates, dawsonite, nitrate-cancrinite, and soluble sodium aluminate. We used in situ
VUV SPI-MS and in-situ liquid SIMS to characterize simulated tank waste solutions and determine the
dependence of Al chemical speciation on ionic strength in the alkaline pH range. This task can provide
critical experimental information about aqueous speciation and the related solubility-controlling mineral
phases for the development of the proposed thermodynamic model in Task 5.

2.1 TASK 1.1A. In situ VUV SPI-MS analysis of chemical composition

 A paper was published based on results reported in this chapter.9 Synchrotron-based single-photon
ionization mass spectrometry (SPI-MS) using vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) photons, or VUV SPI-MS, is a
powerful technique used to investigate properties of chemical systems10. VUV SPI-MS is a “soft” method
of ionization, allowing complex system detection with less fragments compared to other ionization methods
such as electron impact or charged particles 11. The “system for analysis at the liquid–vacuum interface
(SALVI)”, a vacuum compatible microreactor can be integrate with VUV SPI-MS allowing high-vacuum
analysis of biological and environmental chemistry at the liquid interface 10, 12. Tiny apertures in the SALVI
all for slow evaporation with no temperature or composition change and proton beam ionization of the
surface13 14.

2.1.1 Materials and methods

2.1.1.1 SALVI fabrication

 The SALVI device is a portable and vacuum compatible microfluidic reactor.14-16 The rectangular
microchannel is 200 μm (width) × 300 μm (depth) in a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) block. This channel
is covered by a 100 nm thick SiN membrane (Norcada, 1.5 × 1.5 mm2 in area) on a supporting 200 μm thick
silicon frame of 7.5×7.5 mm2 in area to form the detection area. Figure 1 depicts the schematic of the
SALVI device setup coupled with VUV SPI-MS at the Advanced Light Source (ALS) beamline 9.0.2.
After microfluidics fabrication using soft lithography, two micropores (2 µm in diameter and 100 µm apart,
Figure 1b) were milled on the center of the channel using the scanning electron microscopy-focused ion
beam (SEM-FIB, ThermoFisher Helio Dual Beam Quanta) to allow evaporation of molecules in vacuum
and provide ions for ionization in high vacuum.10, 13 The chamber vacuum was kept on the order of 10-7
Torr during analysis. More details of SALVI integration to VUV SPI-MS are described in our recent
studies.10, 13

2.1.1.2 Boehmite sample preparation

 The boehmite stock solution of 1 mg/mL was prepared using 10 mg of AOH60 powder (AOH60
powder, Apyral, particle size 0.4 to 1.6 μm with D50 1 µm) and deionized water (DI water) with resistance
of 18.2 MΩ (Barnstead water purification system, Nanopure diamond). The stock solution was then
ultrasonicated for 5 min. The 10 μg/mL boehmite solution was made by diluting 1 mL boehmite stock
solution into 99 mL DI water. Afterwards, a series of samples with different pH conditions of the 10μg/mL
boehmite (including pH 1, 3, 7, 9, and 13) were prepared. The pH of boehmite solution was adjusted by
adding a suitable amount of hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) prior to VUV SPI-MS
analysis, and the solution pH can be regarded as constant over time. The solution pH was determined using
a pH meter (Fisherbrand™ accumet™ AP110 Portable pH Meter Kit). In order to reduce possible

 10

interference of PDMS signals in mass spectral analysis, each device was kept baking at 70 °C in a vacuum
oven (ThermoFisher, Fisherbrand™ Isotemp™ Model 281A Vacuum Oven) overnight prior to usage. The
prepared boehmite solutions were then injected into the microchannel and sealed with PEEK fittings
(Agilent Technologies) for VUV SPI-MS analysis.

2.1.1.3 VUV SPI-MS liquid analysis

 The SALVI device was attached to the bottom electrode plate of the microchannel plate (MCP) after
the pH adjusted boehmite solutions injected into the microchannel (Figure 1a). The VUV SPI-MS analysis
was carried out by a three-meter VUV monochromator at the 9.0.2 Chemical Dynamics Beamline at the
ALS of the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.18 The VUV photon flux was adjusted to about 5 mm
above the SiN membrane by optimizing the height of the mass spectrometer before data collection.10, 13, 17
After ~20 minutes pump down process, the chamber was maintained at the desirable vacuum 10-7 Torr
during sample analysis. The molecules evaporated at a rate of about 108 cm−2⋅s−1 (calculated by a custom
code) from the two holes on the SiN membrane were ionized by the bright and tunable synchrotron VUV
photon light (Figure 1a).10, 14 Meanwhile, surface tension at the aqueous surface can hold the liquid in the
micrometer-sized aperture along the microchannel and keep the vacuum stable. Recently, gamma-
irradiation was found to facilitate the aggregation of boehmite particles in solution 19. Nevertheless, as a
“soft” ionization method, the VUV irradiation will not likely change the structure of evaporated molecules
from the microchannel. Thus, the mass spectral results can provide more insights into the pH effect on
boehmite dissolution in liquids. To optimize signals, the MCP voltage was tuned for detection of ions of

 11

interest. Ions were collected and detected by the ToF mass spectrometer. SPI-MS measurements were
acquired for photon energies ranging from 8.0 to 12.6 eV for all boehmite samples. The energy step was
0.1 eV to obtain the PIE curve of each mass by integrating the peak intensities at each photon energy.

2.1.1.4 Data analysis

 Mass calibration of the VUV SPI-MS was performed by using the known chemical compounds like
water, oxygen, or rare gases.13 During data acquisition, a continuous wave ion beam was converted to a
pulsed one to obtain more photons.11 The resulting mass resolution was not uniform over the mass range
because higher relative energy is acquired by small molecules, which leads to narrower peaks. On the
contrary, lower relative energy acquired by larger molecules can produce wider peaks. Thus, unit mass at
the peak center was used for peak identification. The peak centers are slightly different at various photon
energies for a particular species. However, the peak identifications based on the unit mass are consistent
among different PIEs. For example, the ions detected at 12.5 eV, 12.0 eV, and 11.5 eV were consistent.
All mass spectra were plotted and integrated in Origin® 2018.
 In this study, the AEs of key solvated boehmite ions were all smaller than 11.5 eV according to mass
spectral analysis as shown in Figure 2. However, identification of ions may be more difficult at lower IEs
because the characteristic peaks have lower ion counts. Therefore, mass spectra at 12.5 eV were chosen
for peak identification after evaluation of all spectra. The AE was determined to be the first point on the
PIE curve where ion counts began to increase from the background signal. There was a slight difference in
the total counts of different aqueous solutions of boehmite particles because of random surface topography
and instrument efficiency.

 As discussed above, the mass of ions could not be absolutely determined because unit mass is used for
peak identification. However, peak separation is not a problem since peak positions can be identified to two

 12

decimal places of accuracy. To improve the accuracy of identification, the key reactants/products were
identified according to the following criteria. First, the magnitude of peaks was higher than their
neighboring peaks and at least three times higher than the base lines. Second, product peaks reported in
previous papers relevant to boehmite hydrogen and/or hydroxide behavior were included and searched in
the spectra to verify their observations. Furthermore, three approaches were adopted to improve the
likelihood and reliability of peak identification. First, peaks except interference, such as system residuals
from previous experiments and PDMS fragments, that have higher intensities than adjacent peaks were
selected as alternative key products, reactants, or fragments. To minimize the interference from PDMS
peaks in peak identification, all possible PDMS peaks (shown in Table S1) are smoothed (counts divided
by 10) in the spectra. Second, the identities of peaks were proposed based on existing knowledge of
boehmite solvation products.20-22 Thirdly, peak identification was based the AEs and PIE curves comparison
with the reported values of chemicals coupled together with molecular mass determined from the spectral
analysis. As to the observed species where AEs and/or structures that have not been found in previous
studies, the new makeup and structure was deduced by considering the spectrum, PIE curve, and the most
likely structures based on knowledge of the boehmite dissolution and known literatures (as shown in Table
1).23, 24

2.1.2.2 The pH effects on boehmite dissolution

 The normalized intensities were used to compare key observed ions from the VUV SPI-MS spectra.
The pH dependence of representative ions on boehmite dissolution was depicted in Figure 4. It is interesting
to note that boehmite solvation products are detected with different intensities under different pH
conditions. The new findings suggest that the dissolution of boehmite and solvation of particles in solvent
actually produce more complex solvated ions and cluster ions with water, presenting a new understanding
compared to the existing simpler picture of the presence of molecular form of boehmite solvated in water.
Specifically, the peaks of smaller aluminum oxides and hydroxides, for example m/z+ 43 AlO+, m/z+ 60
AlOOH+, and m/z+ 78 Al(OH)3+, have highest intensities in the acidic environment at pH 3. Also, alkalis
conditions (i.e., pH 9, pH 13) can stimulate the formation of aluminum oxides and hydroxides, like AlOOH+
and Al(OH)3+ (Figure 5a). In contrast, these ions show lower normalized counts in the neutral solution at
pH 7. Aluminum hydroxides with water clusters, including m/z+ 97 Al(OH)2+…2H2O and m/z+ 106
(AlOH)2+…H2O revealed different features among the studied pH solutions. For example, m/z+ 97
(Al(OH)2+…2H2O), its abundance in the acidic solution at pH 3 was slightly larger than in the strong
alkaline solution at pH 13. As to m/z+ 106 (AlOH)2+…H2O, the higher intensities in alkaline solutions at
pH 9 and 13. Also, the acidic condition of pH 3 can promote its formation.

 Compared with aluminum oxides, aluminum hydroxides, and small cluster ions, the formation of larger

and alkaline conditions. Additional product identification of boehmite were summarized in Table 1. Our
results show that, in addition to the molecular form of boehmite, a variety of aluminum oxides, hydroxides,
and cluster ions incorporating water molecules coexist in the solvent cage. Varying pH could influence the
formation of hydrogen bonds, which will change the structure of cluster ions. Their relative abundances
also vary depending on the pH condition, suggesting the hydronium ions and hydroxide ions actively
participate in the dissolution process at the molecular level. Specifically, boehmite tends to form 1D
nanorods or nanofibers in acidic conditions; whereas 2D and 3D nanostructures are detected in alkaline
conditions 21. In terms of composition, nanofibers are mainly made up by smaller aluminum oxides
elements (i.e., AlO and AlO2 as shown in Figure 3a, 3e, and 3f at acidic conditions, such as pH 1 and pH

3D nanotubes contain more aluminum hydroxide units, like Al(OH) and Al(OH)2, as shown in Figure 3c,
3d, and 3g.

2.1.2.3   Conclusion

 In this study, we performed in-situ VUV SPI-MS analysis of dissolved boehmite particles in different
pH conditions in high vacuum. Liquid analysis in VUV SPI-MS was made possible by using the vacuum
compatible SALVI microreactor. The dissolution pathways of boehmite at the solid – liquid, particularly in
liquid water are more complex than previously conceived. Specifically, known dissolved products of
boehmite, such as Alok+ and AlOOH+, are confirmed by their AEs determined from the PIE curves in
addition to mass spectral analysis. Moreover, the AE values of newly found dissolution products have been
reported, and their molecular structures reported with mass spectral analysis for the first time. Different
types of dissolved ions were observed, and they show distinct intensities at different pH conditions. These
new findings show that the solvation of boehmite depends on pH; and pH could indicate the change of the
water microenvironment and affect the hydrogen bounding in aqueous solutions. Our results imply that
future studies on boehmite in liquid, such as temperature and ionic strength dependence, is highly important
to understand how boehmite dissolution could be impacted in the microenvironment in the existence of
cluster ions in waste streams. We provide new insights into the pH dependence effect of boehmite, such
fundamental understanding is important to design nuclear waste processing and environmental mitigation
solutions. In future studies, quantum chemical calculations are desirable to determine the products more
precisely by comparing the experimentally obtained AEs of ions with theoretical predications.

 This subtask will investigate the solid–liquid (s–l) interface of the dissolution and solvation of boehmite
in different pH conditions using advanced in situ liquid ToF-SIMS technique developed in Dr. Yu’s

3.1 SALVI fabrication

 As discussed in prior sections, the SALVI device preparation is a multistep process involving the
construction of a portable, vacuum compatible microfluidic reactor. Mainly, soft lithography is used. The
SU-8 molding and plasma activation of PDMS and the SiN surface bonding in a cleanroom. This process
creates a microchannel 200 µm × 150μm enclosed in a PDMS block. The channel is covered by a 100 nm
thick SiN membrane (Norcada, Edmonton, AB, Canada, 1.5 × 1.5 mm2 in area) supported by a 200 µm
thick silicon frame with dimensions 7.5 × 7.5 mm2 to form the detection area.14-16

3.2 Sample preparation

 Boehmite (APYRL AOH 60, AlOOH, Nabaltec, Schwandorf, Germany), Gibbsite (R6011, γ-Al(OH)3,
Noah Technologies, San Antonio, Texas, USA) and in-house synthesized nano-boehmite particles
suspended into DI water then injected into SALVI devices for analysis. Liquid samples were prepared using
DI water in the concentration of 10 µg/mL before getting injected into the microchannel. The liquid mixture
was vortexed for 1 min and sonicated for 5min prior to analysis.

3.3 Optimization of in situ liquid ToF-SIMS

 Optimization of in situ liquid ToF-SIMS has been conducted using the ToF-SIMS V spectrometer
(IONTOF, GmbH, Münster, Germany) at the CNMS facility. To determine suitable primary beam and
acquisition settings, approximately 1 mL of DI water was filled into the microchannel of the SALVI device.
The device was then installed on a top mount sample holder and transferred into the load lock chamber of
the ToF-SIMS to pump down to reach vacuum at about 10-6 Torr. The device was then pushed to the main
chamber for in situ analysis. The vacuum was maintained on the order of 10-6 Torr during data acquisition.
 The base setting of the liquid SIMS is to use the Liquid Metal Ion Gun (LMIG) in fast imaging mode,
where highest spatial resolution is possible. The Bi3+ primary ion beam rastered on the SiN membrane,
which was used to protect the liquid within the microchannel. The primary ion beam was set in the inter-
locked milling mode with an energy of 30 kV, beam emission current of 0.8µA and cycle time of 100µs.14-
16
 The probing area was set to be 2 µm in diameter circle on the SiN membrane. The beam raster pattern
was in the random mode with a spatial resolution of 128 by 128 pixels. At least 200 data points were
collected after the SiN membrane was punched through. The flood gun was set as 300 V during all
acquisitions.29 Each sample was analyzed in five different spots in the positive ion mode and the negative
ion mode, respectively, to obtain replicate data.

Figure 6. Schematics of in situ liquid ToF-SIMS optimization using three different modes.

 The optimization of liquid SIMS was focused on primary ion beam settings, such as pulse width, single
pulse width, pulse bunching, and charge compensation (Figure 6). We utilized three different operation
modes, namely, the multiplex pulse mode, single pulse buncher mode, and single pulse switching mode.
The multiplex pulse mode contains three different primary beam pulse widths within the primary beam
raster mode, that is, 50 ns, 100 ns and 200 ns. These three different pulses were applied sequentially,
allowing repeatedly rastering of the primary ion beam on the surface during a scan. The 200 ns pulse widths
were selected for SiN membrane milling, while 50 ns and 100 ns pulse beams were utilized for data
acquisition. Since this mode utilized three different pulse widths sequentially, it took more than 1000 s to
punch through 2 µm diameter aperture on the SiN membrane.
 The single pulse with buncher mode contains very short ion pulse with increased energy through the
bunching system of the time-of-flight (ToF) analyzer. The milling of the SiN membrane was done by using
the 200 ns pulse width beam, which took ~ 500 − 600s for the membrane punch-through. After the punch-
through, the pulse width was set to 70 ns with a buncher voltage up to 2200 V and charge compensation to
− 40V. The bunching system allows the reduction of the pulse width of pulses from the range of several
tens of ns to the sub ns. As a result, the energy of the primary ion beam is increased, which reduces the
lateral resolution in high current bunched modes to a few micrometers.
 The single pulse switching mode used the 200 ns pulse width during milling and switched to the 70 ns
pulse width after the punch-through for data acquisition. It took about 500 – 600 s for the SiN punch-
through used the 200ns pulse width. After the punch through, the pulse width was manually adjusted to 70
ns to acquire at least 200 data point for each sample run.
 The acquired ToF-SIMS data was processed using the SurfaceLab 7.0 (IONTOF) software. Each raw
data file was reconstructed based on the depth profiles (see Figure 7). Reconstructed data was calibrated
using peaks m/z+ 12 C+, m/z+ 18 H2O+, m/z+ 91 (H2O)5H+, m/z+ 127 (H2O)7H+, m/z+ 42 AlO+, m/z+ 208
Al3O2HF5+ in the positive mode and m/z− 15 O−, m/z− 35 (H2O)OH−, m/z− 71 (H2O)3OH−, m/z− 42 AlO2−,
and m/z− 160 Al3O5−, respectively.

 SYNTHESIS AND CHARACTERIZATION OF NANOBOEHMITE

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL PROCEEDURE

 The synthesis of boehmite nanoparticles was conducted using a hydrothermal synthesis technique
adopted from reports by Zhang et al.21, 32 Prior to synthesis, a 1 M NaOH solution was prepared by
dissolving NaOH pellets (ACS reagent, ≥97%, MilliporeSigma) in deionized (DI) water. Similarly, a 0.25
M Al(NO₃)₃ solution was prepared by dissolving Al(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (ACS reagent, ≥98%, MilliporeSigma) in
DI water. DI water required for synthesis was filtered with a Milli-Q water purification system (Merck
KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), and pH measurements were recorded using a Thermo Scientific Orion Star
A215 digital pH probe. As a precautionary measure, the aluminum nitrate solution was stored in a glass
bottle covered with aluminum foil to prevent light-induced degradation.
 The sodium hydroxide solution was used to titrate a 78 mL of aluminum nitrate solution until the pH
reached 10. The resulting solution was allowed to stir while being covered for one hour (hr.). Al(OH)3 gel-
like precipitates formed were collected from the solution via centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 minutes.
Excess salts were removed by washing the gel precipitates three times with DI water. The precipitates were
resuspended in deionized water, and a diluted 1 M sodium hydroxide solution (pH 12) was used to titrate
the suspension to pH 10. The suspension was then loaded into two 45 mL Teflon lined stainless steel
autoclaves. Both autoclaves were sealed before being secured in a rotary oven set to 200 °C and 10 rpm for
48 hrs. After heating, the autoclaves were placed in a 1-gal utility bucket filled with room-temperature
water for cooling overnight. The solids recovered from the vessels were dried in an electric oven at 90 °C
for 72 hrs. before being transferred into storage vials. Synthesis conducted with a total of 0.53 g of
aluminum yields roughly 7.60 g of boehmite solids. Figure 12 displays an overview of experimental
procedures.

representative of boehmite.

 5. TASK 2.1: IN SITU SEM IMAGING OF PARTICLES IN SLURRY CONDITIONS

 Task 2 will provide critical information about particle size distribution and aggregation that can be used
to constrain a constitutive relationship for the rheological properties of waste slurry. Such information will
be needed for modeling particle settling, consolidation and salt crust movement in waste pretreatment.
Particle size and aggregation affect the dissolution of boehmite, which is assumed to be the rate-limiting
step in the aluminum dissolution process.6 Particle size and aggregation also control the rheological
properties of waste slurry in waste processing.34 Boehmite has been a good model system to understand the
dispersity and rheological properties of the waste slurry that depends on surface and solution chemistry,
although its platelet-like shape imposes a challenge for in situ measurements and modeling. In situ liquid
SEM offers the advantage of imaging particles in the liquid medium directly, providing more insights on
systems by observing particle dispersity compared to the conventional SEM analysis of static particle shape,
size, and morphology in a dry vacuum environment. We performed in situ liquid SEM and image analysis
to study hard crust formation, particle aggregation, and settling behavior of Al-bearing phases under the
conditions that closely match Hanford Tank Waste using the SALVI device.

5.1 SALVI fabrication

 SALVI is a microfluidic reactor device with vacuum compatibility.14, 15 The fabrication process of
SALVI is entirely conducted in a cleanroom environment. The microfluidic channel structure was
fabricated with SU-8 molding and PDMS prototyping. This process creates a microchannel of 200µm wide
× 150μm depth × 5 mm long enclosed in a PDMS block. The PDMS piece was cut out from the SU-8 mold
and plasma bonded to the 100 nm thick silicon nitride (SiN) membrane (Norcada, Edmonton, AB, Canada,
1.5 × 1.5 mm2 in area). Two ports were used as inlet and outlet of the microfluidic channel (Supelco,
Bellefonte, PA, USA, 15 cm long, 1.58 mm O.D. × 0.5 mm I.D.). A set of PEEK union is used for sealing
up the tubing which makes it a closed loop system. The fabricated SALVI device was graphite coated for
15 nm thick to reduce surface charging during the in situ liquid SEM analysis.34

5.2 Sample preparation

 We utilized commercial boehmite (AlOH60 Nabaltec, Schwandorf, Germany), commercial synthetic
boehmite (ACTILOX 200SM, Nabaltec, Schwandorf, Germany), and in-house synthesized nano-boehmite
(see section 4). Boehmite particles were suspended into DI water with a concentration of 10 µg/mL. The
liquid mixtures were vortexed for 1min and sonicated for 5min before loading into a SALVI device.

5.3 Instrument information

 In situ SEM imaging of boehmite particles in DI water was performed using plasma focused ion beam
(PFIB) - SEM (Thermos Scientific Helios 5 Hydra UX Dual Beam Multiple Ion Plasma FIB/SEM, USA)
at ORNL CNMS. A series of apertures of 1 µm in diameter were milled using the focused ion beam and
they are spread 100 µm apart each other. The Xe Ion beam energy was 30 kV, and the current was 1 nA
with 900 ms dwell time. The inner diameter of the aperture was roughly 1 µm diameter after milling. The
working distance was 4.1 mm. The stage was 52° tilted to accommodate the PFIB angle. After milling,
secondary electron (SE) images were taken in the aperture area using following settings: 5 kV electron
beam energy, 50,000 times magnification, and 0.1 nA beam current.

 The optimization of in situ liquid SEM was performed using commercial boehmite AOH60 and 200SM.
Approximately 30 SE images of apertures with particles were captured to identify particles and their size
distributions for AOH60 and 200SM, respectively. Figure 25 shows images of apertures of the boehmite

cause of the slight wetting of the aperture perimeters, which can be resolved by precisely controlling sample
injection using a pump in the future. The liquid volume underneath the aperture may circulate with higher
electron beam energy (10kV), this can be reduced by using lower beam energies (i.e., 1 − 5 kV).

 6. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

In the first year of performance of Tasks 1 and 2 of the EM project at ORNL, several subtasks have been
performed, including the following,
 • Task 1.1a: In situ liquid VUV SPI-MS analysis of chemical composition,
 • Task 1.1b: In situ liquid ToF-SIMS analysis of chemical composition,
 • Task 1.2: Ex situ multimodal imaging and analysis of Al-bearing phases
 • Task 2.1: In situ SEM imaging of particles in slurry conditions
 A manuscript based on in situ analysis of VUV SPI-MS was published in Task 1.1a. This paper offers
new results of the pH dependent effect of boehmite and provide more insights into a more detailed solvation
mechanism at the s-l interface. By comparing the key products under different pH conditions, fundamental
understanding of boehmite dissolution is revealed to assist the engineering design of waste processing and
storage solutions.
 In Task 1.1b, we performed optimization of in situ liquid ToF-SIMS using DI water by comparing three
modes of primary ion beam settings. Using the optimal conditions, boehmite and gibbsite dissolved in DI
water at natural pH were analyzed to validate molecular imaging capabilities. More systematic experiments
will be performed by varying pH and ionic strength conditions simulating those in the tank wastes in the
second year of performance. A technical note reporting in situ liquid SIMS optimization is being prepared.
 In Tasks 1.2, we performed optimization of in situ liquid SEM using a new PFIB-SEM instrument.
Commercial boehmite particles dissolved in DI water were used. The optimal conditions for aperture
milling using PFIB and subsequent SE imaging were conducted by adjusting beam energy and dwelling
conditions. Using optimal conditions, in situ liquid SEM imaging of three different boehmite particles,
which have different particle sizes, were conducted. Preliminary results show particle size measurements
are feasible. Similar to Task 1, a series of systematic experiments will be performed by varying pH and
ionic strength conditions simulating those in the tank wastes in the second year of performance, providing
both particle size and chemical composition information.
 In Tasks 2.1, hydrothermal synthesis of aluminum hydroxide nanoparticles was successfully conducted
to produce nano-sized boehmite particles. Multimodal ex situ characterization of the nanoparticles was
performed through a combination of SEM, TEM, ToF-SIMS, and XRD. SEM and TEM image analysis
verified that the as prepared particles are largely 20 – 90 nm. Understanding the morphology, crystallinity,
and dissolution behavior of boehmite under waste tank conditions is necessary for improving waste
management and mitigation strategies. In the second year, we plan to synthesize gibbsite and other relevant
aluminum-containing salts in the nanoscale, which will present a more holistic view of particles present in
the waste form.
 Additionally, more analysis utilizing machine learning, specifically supervised learning, will be
performed on the data acquired from all tasks in year 2.
