How to cultivate equity-oriented health care at an organizational level: lessons from a process evaluation of EQUIP emergency
Authors: Colleen Varcoe, Annette J. Browne, Vicky Bungay, Nadine Wathen, Erin Barker, David Byres, Cheyanne Stones, Shayna Dolan, Allie Slemon, Agnes Black, Caitlyn Cater, Scott Comber, Victoria Smye, Elder Roberta Price
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWidening social, economic, and health inequities intensify the need for equity strategies in health care if population health is to be improved. EQUIP Health Care, a program of research and knowledge mobilization aimed at supporting health care organizations to promote equity, has been developing and testing organizational-level interventions. This process evaluation of the implementation of EQUIP Emergency, a collaboration among staff and leaders at three diverse Emergency Departments, community and Indigenous leaders, and researchers, provides direction to organizations wishing to promote health and health care equity.MethodsThis process evaluation, grounded in the Active Implementation Frameworks, is based on analysis of qualitative comments from a staff survey (n = 313 comments), qualitative interviews with staff, leaders, and researchers (n = 43), and observational data captured as fieldnotes (n = 784 entries). A thematic analysis identified four themes pertaining to the process and implications of engaging with EQUIP Emergency.ResultsDespite a rhetorical and practical commitment to equity at all levels, other priorities, including ensuring staffing adequate to provide care, orienting new staff, and responding to escalating crises such as the unregulated drug poisoning emergency, violence in Emergency Departments, and later in the study, COVID-19, overshadowed efforts toward equity. The material (e.g. staffing) and non-material (e.g. skills) resources available to support and aligned with equity goals shaped the capacity of staff, leaders, and the organizations to enact equity goals. Our analysis led to 10 suggestions for leaders at all levels of health care, including clinical, executive, and policy leaders, for systematically cultivating equity in health care service settings.ConclusionsTo promote equity, health care organizations must approach equity as foundational, not as an “add-on,” link equity to key organizational issues, engage leaders from all relevant employment groups at the outset and throughout, engage and compensate staff at all levels and from all roles, and counter siloed bureaucracies (systemic structural misalignments) within their systems. Time for leaders to comprehensively plan should be augmented with analysis of local patient and staff data that can be shared with direct care providers to set the stage for implementing equity strategies and evaluating progress as an embedded way of doing business.Trial registrationClinical Trials.gov #NCT03369678 (registration date November 18, 2017)

Background

Against the backdrop of widening social inequities, the Canadian health care system is under immense pressure evident in long waits for care and staff turnover and burnout [1, 2]. Endemic anti-Indigenous racism, crises within the health care system, including significant shortages and stresses in the health care workforce exacerbated by COVID-19 and the unregulated drug poisoning emergency (previously referred to as the toxic drug supply crisis) [3], have profoundly greater impacts on people who are structurally disadvantaged by geography, poverty, racism, discrimination related to gender, mental health, substance use and ability, and other forms of stigma and discrimination.

Emergency Departments (EDs), which, in the context of a long-standing shortage of primary health care providers in Canada [4] are often the only option for people seeking health care, serve as key sites where these health inequities manifest, and as a site of opportunity to mitigate such inequities [5–7]. However, EDs have been plagued by staffing shortages, overcrowding, and long wait times for decades [8–10], conditions which are worsening. Moreover, ED staff report significant barriers to promoting equity in this context; barriers include low levels of knowledge about appropriate and responsive care that considers the complexity of patients’ intersecting inequities and the structural disadvantages shaping these experiences, health system policies, and oversights in administrative leadership support [6, 11–13].

Our research studying an organizational intervention to promote equity in primary care (EQUIP Primary Health Care- EQUIP PHC) showed that when people experience care that they perceive as culturally safe, trauma- and violence-informed and using a harm reduction lens, they feel more confident and comfortable with the care team and the care they receive, and in turn are better able to manage their own health, with subsequent improvements in health outcomes [14]. We learned that organizations and staff could be supported to implement equity oriented health care (EOHC) tailored to their settings, that interprofessional conversations were more impactful for staff than education (which was necessary but not sufficient), that disruptions to usual care approaches were inevitable [15, 16], and that organizational, funding and system-level factors actively constrain how care can be delivered [17]. However, throughout, research participants (patients, leaders, and care providers) repeatedly pointed to the critical role of EDs in fostering equity across the primary care continuum, the challenges to doing so, and the negative impact those challenges had on patients, staff, the primary care sector, and the health care system as a whole.

Research and interventions to systematically enhance the capacity of EDs to mitigate the harms of persistent health and healthcare inequities are in the early stages of development [18–20]. For example, those aiming to improve health care for Indigenous peoples, who in Canada are the least well served by health and social services, are rare [20]. In a recent systematic review, MacLean et al. found across settings that among the few interventions, fewer focused at the organizational level. They argue that while interventions aimed at the interpersonal level may improve individual patient experiences, multilevel interventions have potential for greater impact and more sustainable change. Thus, we designed, implemented, and evaluated EQUIP Emergency (EQUIP ED), a complex organizational-level intervention to promote equity-oriented health care in ED contexts with the aim of reducing discrimination and improving care experiences for patients and staff. Analyses of the impact of the EQUIP ED intervention are reported elsewhere [21, 22]. In this paper we report a process evaluation of the implementation of EQUIP ED and offer recommendations regarding how to build on the lessons learned in other EDs and contexts. Our purpose is to provide suggestions to leaders at all levels of health care, including clinical, executive, and policy leaders, for systematically cultivating equity in health care service settings.

Methods

A process evaluation is considered an essential part of designing and testing complex interventions [23, 24], and typically aims to foster understanding about “what works,” by describing whether the intervention was delivered as intended and the quantity of intervention implemented (in this case, the extent to which each ED participated), how it was implemented, and the reach of the intervention. Moore et al. [23] assert that such analysis lays the groundwork for further adaptations, for identifying what is required to replicate the intervention in other contexts, and for understanding the impact of an intervention and the factors influencing that impact. Importantly, they note issues to be considered in a process evaluation “include training and support, communication and management structures, and how these structures interact with implementers’ attitudes and circumstances to shape the intervention” [23, p.2]. Thus, we undertook a process evaluation of EQUIP ED to assess how the intervention was implemented and to what extent, and the features of the environment and processes that influenced implementation, with the goal of identifying recommendations for future equity-oriented interventions.

Theoretical grounding

The study was guided by core tenets of complexity theory, especially emergence, non-linearity and sensitivity to initial conditions (‘butterfly effect’), which emphasize that complex systems, far from being in equilibrium states, are continually in states of flux whereby even small changes can precipitate sudden, often unexpected turns into new paths of development [25–27]. Following Walby [25], we closely attend to intersectionality and social (and health) inequities, showing how these interact in non-linear and context-dependent ways and emphasizing multiple, overlapping social systems that are only semi-autonomous as they interact in complex ways. Walby, as do Greenhalgh and Papoutsi [26], offer complexity theory as a way to understand power in the context of social change. Complexity theory is particularly useful in answering questions about organizations and systems such as, ‘why is it difficult to initiate or sustain change despite using various interventions’, or ‘why do particular outcomes occur within different systems’? [28]. Specific to this study, organizations such as EDs are considered complex adaptive systems (CASs), operating within a series of sub-systems that contribute to how patient care is organized and carried out [13, 29–31]. CASs are characterized as non-linear, unpredictable, and self-organizing, making change initiatives such as new interventions challenging to implement [29]. Attending to each ED as a CAS required an intervention that could be flexible and responsive to their unique sub-systems (i.e., staff, organizational policies, resources, pressures, and priorities), necessitating tailoring to the unique context within these settings [29]. The study was also guided by critical theoretical approaches to health equity and intersectionality. Health equity is understood as “the absence of avoidable, unfair, or remediable differences among groups of people” [32]. Promoting health equity requires providing greater support to those most disadvantaged in terms of the structural and social determinants of inequity and health. Intersectionality refers to the understanding that people experience inequities at the confluence of intersecting structural conditions, including systemic racism, poverty, ableism, gender-based discrimination, and other unfair disadvantages [33, 34].

Study design

Through a series of studies [14, 15, 35–38] EQUIP Health Care has designed and tested interventions to support organizations to promote equity in health care in Canada. We identified three key dimensions of equity-oriented health care (EOHC) and 10 strategies to operationalize those key dimensions (Fig. 1) to attend to: a) structural inequities and structural violence; b) multiple, intersecting forms of racism, discrimination, and stigma, and; c) the frequent mismatches between dominant approaches to service delivery and the needs of people most affected by inequities. The three key dimensions are: a) trauma- and violence-informed care (TVIC): recognizing and limiting the effects of trauma and violence, including structural violence, on peoples’ lives, health and care experiences; b) culturally-safe care/anti-racism approaches: reducing power imbalances, systemic racism, and discrimination, and their effects; and c) harm reduction/substance use health: preventing harms from substance use stigma, and in the process, promoting opportunities for wellbeing in the context of substance use.Fig. 1Key dimensions of equity-oriented health care and strategies for system Improvement

EQUIP ED was undertaken in British Columbia (BC), Canada by ED staff and leaders, Indigenous leaders, and researchers in three diverse hospitals in three different regional health authorities1. As described elsewhere [39], our protocol explicitly built on EQUIP PHC and was tailored to the specific contexts of EDs; we studied how EDs could improve care to those least well served, including Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples. Given the magnitude of inequities experienced by Indigenous peoples in Canada, our research program has been conducted in collaboration with Indigenous peoples and organizations serving them. Consequently, EQUIP ED was conducted under the guidance of a steering committee comprised of Indigenous academics, leaders in health care and Elders within a tripartite leadership model of researchers, health care providers and Indigenous leaders. The lead research team was comprised of academics with disciplinary backgrounds in nursing, implementation science, public health and finance, a health care executive leader, a clinical research leader and an Indigenous Elder.

Our prior research in the primary care sector [15–17, 35, 37] identified the core components (elements that cannot be reduced or eliminated) of the EQUIP intervention as flexible [27], but as including a) attention to the key dimensions of EOHC (as described above), b) engagement of all stakeholders, including all levels of staff and those seeking care (patients or clients), and c) focusing care improvements on those most disadvantaged and least well-served. We conceptualized the intervention as being operationalized through two approaches: staff orientation to the key dimensions and organizational integration and tailoring. As shown in Fig. 2 [39], our intervention theory proposed that staff would operationalize key dimensions using the 10 strategies in ways tailored to their settings, and posited that these approaches would lead to greater capacity for equity-oriented competence at the provider and policy level, and subsequently improved patient and staff experiences.Fig. 2Intervention theory for EQUIP ED

Intervention theory for EQUIP ED

Through collaboration among Indigenous leaders, ED staff and leaders, and researchers, EQUIP ED intended to improve the quality of care and care experiences for both patients and staff. Building on the lessons learned from EQUIP PHC we revised and tailored the approach in six ways: a) we used a tripartite leadership model of clinical leaders, Indigenous and community leaders, and researchers, b) we drew on the concept of Front Line Ownership (FLO) [52] to frame direct care staff as leaders of the intervention work, inviting staff at each site to form Working Groups (WGs); FLO is meant to support authentic commitment to organizational change, and assumes that those providing direct care, who are therefore closest to the process, are most well suited to identify and implement change, c) rather than providing mandatory educational activities, including integration-discussion sessions for all staff on the key dimensions of EOHC, as was the case in EQUIP PHC, we provided access to online educational modules, but left engagement up to individual staff, d) we more explicitly emphasized a strengths-based approach, e) we aimed to have a shorter time frame over which to carry out intervention activities (six months) in an effort to support a consistent focus on promoting EOHC, and f) we emphasized the importance of the sites engaging people coming for care (especially those with lived and living experience of inequities) in equity-related service enhancements. To support the approach, we developed a Workbook [53] to guide WGs that included an orientation to the intervention, guidance on how to get organized, assessment activities, guidance on strategy design and evaluation tools and offered skilled “Equity Coaches” to guide them. The research team approached Executive Leaders at each of the three sites and invited them to help plan the study. These Executive Leaders recruited other key leaders in their organizations and committed funding to support participation by direct care staff.

Data collection

Data were collected before, after, and throughout the intervention from December 2017 – December 2020. Intervention activities began in February 2019 with initial meetings of WGs comprised of diverse staff at SPH and SMH. In the months prior, simultaneous with pre-intervention outcome data collection, researchers met with leaders at executive and clinical levels, and staff at each site to explain the study and invite the participation of direct service providers in a WG. An initial catalyst period extended until September 2019, during which time the research team provided each WGs with administrative support in the form of a site-specific research coordinator and a catalyst grant of $10,000. The catalyst grant supported activities identified as priorities by each WG, and served as a heuristic. Intervention activities at all three sites were carried out beyond the end of the research, including into 2024.

As shown in Table 2, data included qualitative comments from staff surveys (n = 313 comments from 393 completed surveys), qualitative interviews with investigators and research staff, site leads, and staff, including WG Members (n = 43), and field notes recorded during data collection with patients, the process of data collection, staff WG meetings, and research team meetings (n = 784 entries). Qualitative interviews were conducted pre-, mid- and post-intervention. They were conducted at a time and place preferred by the participants, with virtual options available, were of 30 minutes to two hours duration, and were recorded and transcribed. Data analysis

Analysis of data related to the implementation process was ongoing throughout the intervention. A thematic analysis of the qualitative interviews, fieldnotes and staff survey comments was conducted using NVivo to organize and code the data. The data were read repeatedly to highlight process-related dynamics specific to each site as well as cross-cutting patterns. The various sets of data provided multiple angles from which to deepen our understanding of the complex process of implementing EQUIP ED. The research team identified preliminary coding categories, which were refined; subsequently, the analysis shifted to a more conceptual representation of the themes pertaining to the process and implications of engaging with EQUIP ED. Finally, we reviewed these themes in relation to the elements of the Active Implementation Frameworks (AIFs), a praxis-oriented, action-based implementation science model developed for complex interventions [54–57]. AIFs include five frameworks: stages of implementation, implementation drivers, implementation teams, usable intervention criteria, and improvement cycles, the first four of which were applicable in this study. AIFs posit that interventions are introduced into systems in four overlapping stages that may repeat over time:

Stage 1: Exploration and Adaptation, involves assessing existing organizational values, norms, culture, climate, resources, and other factors that might influence implementing an innovation in practice.

Stage 2: Installation, encompasses creating and/or acquiring the resources needed to shift practice into alignment with the new intervention’s principles.

Stage 3: Initial Implementation involves practicing the new intervention and integrating its principles.

Stage 4 is Full Implementation.

This conceptualization of the stages of implementation provided a useful way of framing the analytical insights with regard to the process of engagement with EQUIP ED. We used AIFs as a lens through which to view the themes reflected in the data, and to structure our description of the process of implementing EQUIP ED, the inherent complexities and related insights culminating in our recommendations. The identified themes describe how the intervention was implemented and to what extent the contextual features and processes influenced such implementation.

Results

While, as described above, the research team built on their experience and lessons learned with the intervention in primary care contexts to design the intervention, once engagement by ED staff began, each site followed a distinctive path, shaped by its unique character and context, with little or no subsequent influence by the research team.

All three sites engaged in Stage 1 and both SPH and SMH engaged in Stages 2 and 3 to some extent, with none of the sites engaging in full implementation. Despite some ongoing attention to specific key dimensions (e.g., cultural safety training programs, harm reduction initiatives), leaders at all levels expressed the need for greater attention to equity-oriented aims as a priority for their ED and pointed to organizational initiatives and formal statements as evidence of commitment. However, the complexity of and competing demands at each site and the material (e.g., staffing) and non-material (e.g., culture) resources available shaped the nature and extent of implementation through: a) the organizational capacity to prioritize equity and competing priorities, and b) the drivers of the implementation, which in turn determined c) the capacity of the implementation teams and d) the alignment of the intervention with the evolving priorities of the organizations. In what follows, we describe these four themes, identifying the factors influencing the quantity of intervention implemented, how it was implemented, and the reach of the intervention, with a view to identifying recommendations for future EOHC interventions.

In the face of competing demands, the material and non-material resources available shaped the organizational capacity to further equity

Each site was at a different starting point with respect to resources and related features such as its unique internal and external challenges and pressures, reputation, and self-image. SPH initiated and completed the most intervention activities in large part due to its resources and philosophical orientation. In the words of an SPH research lead “[EQUIP] went well because we weren’t so unique and new; the organization was already doing a lot of … thinking about equity” (Researcher Focus Group, April 2021). As the closest hospital to the geographical area known as Vancouver’s Downtown Eastside where intersecting systemic inequities contribute to poverty, homelessness, violence, and ongoing racism and colonization and its effects for Indigenous peoples, SPH has longstanding history of social justice advocacy and serving patients impacted by structural and systemic inequities [41, 42]. SPH is well known for specializing in care for people disproportionately affected by poverty, homelessness, and living with HIV, mental health issues, and/or heavy substance use and is playing a critical role in the ongoing unregulated drug poisoning emergency, caring for patients experiencing overdoses at rates almost 10 times higher than any other hospital in the same region [43, 58, 59]. Thus, SPH had alignment with, resources for, and experience in promoting equity.

SMH was able to mount some intervention activities, also supported by its size, resources and existing attention to inequities. Although historically under-funded, infusions of resources over the previous decade, including the 2013 opening of a new ED five times larger than the former one, set the stage for Executive Leaders to see EQUIP as timely. SMH serves a high proportion of new immigrants and refugees, with 20% of newcomers arriving from India in 2021 [60] and 2.16% people in Surrey self-identify as Indigenous, making it the largest urban Indigenous population in BC [45]. Consequently, SMH has resources and personnel devoted to fostering equity for these groups, including a large Indigenous Health Team, and Indigenous Health Liaisons focused on providing cultural support and advocacy [61]. As with SPH, there was philosophical alignment at the organizational level, experience with efforts to promote equity and resources to support staff engagement.

UHNBC was unable to form a WG and although no intervention activities were undertaken within the study timeframe, this site provided the greatest insight as to what is required to mount an organizational-level equity-oriented intervention. As a regional hospital serving Prince George, a medium-sized city, as well as a large region across rural and remote northern BC, it “serves a population of 288,000, spread out over almost two-thirds of British Columbia, Canada, an area larger than France.” [62]. While all three sites faced overcrowding, this was especially so for the UHNBC ED, which was frequently occupied with admitted patients waiting for beds elsewhere, reflecting problems for rural EDs that continue to escalate [63]. Ongoing nursing staff shortages made it challenging for leaders and staff to meet mandates for accreditation and recruit and orient new staff. Although the Northern Health region has the highest proportion of Indigenous peoples across any health authority in BC [49], at the time of the study, UHNBC had few equity initiatives and few Indigenous-specific services; for example, at the study start, only one part-time Aboriginal2 Liaison Worker was employed to support Indigenous patients for the entire hospital (Field Notes, January 2018). Uptake of EQUIP ED was rendered near impossible by these constrained circumstances.

Stage 1: Exploration and Adaptation began with conversations between researchers and Executive Leaders at each site. In all cases, these conversations built on preexisting relationships between the research leads and leadership team members (ranging from Managers to Directors to Vice-Presidents). At all sites, executive and clinical leaders emphasized their institutions’ philosophical commitment to equity and social justice as values, pointing to statements of commitment to equity for Indigenous peoples expressed in formal organizational statements, in part aligning with the release of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada report [64] in the year prior to the initial planning of EQUIP ED. However, an unrelated study analyzing mission, vision, and value statements in these and other institutions showed that health equity was not prioritized in the institutional statements by any of the three health authorities [65]. Executive Leaders voiced the importance of urgent attention to equity and committed to action. For example, after a brief presentation and discussion of EQUIP, one Director declared “There is nothing more important than this” (Field Notes, September 26, 2016). Commitment was evident in numerous ways – with SPH and SMH immediately committing to significant ‘in kind’ funding in the form of paying for staff participation, leaders agreeing to join the research team as investigators, and recruiting clinical-level leaders to engage – all, in the words of one leader, demonstrating they had “skin in the game” (Field Notes, October 2016). However, even at the proposal stage, as the research team met with leaders at each site, the scarcity of resources for UHNBC was evident, in that, in light of significant staffing and leadership shortages, the organization was not able to commit material resources at the same level (e.g., paid time for direct service providers to participate). Despite multiple efforts by clinical leaders to support time and space for interested staff to participate during work time, a WG at UHNBC was not formed and no adaptations were proposed during the implementation period. Indeed, underscoring the diverse factors beyond commitment required for change, equity-related work at UHNBC gained momentum two years after data collection was complete. As other initiatives and resources increasingly aligned, several linkages and connections were strengthened within the ED, the hospital, and the Northern Health Authority. For example, after data collection ended, the Prince George Medical Staff Physician Association provided funding to support two physicians to lead cultural safety and humility work at the hospital and ED levels. One physician was a member of the EQUIP ED team from study inception and the other met with EQUIP ED team members to identify how the results might shape next steps.

Adaptation began from the outset for SPH and SMH. For example, during proposal development, one Executive Leader made the tripartite leadership model a condition of their hospital’s participation. An Executive Leader at the other site recommended that we “focus on the 80%,” rather than the minority of staff likely to be resistant to ideas related to EOHC, which gave rise to the idea of voluntary WGs. Clinical managers advised calling those who participated in WGs simply “members,” rather than “champions” or “mentors,” which might be read as conveying unreasonable expectations.

Importantly, across all sites, adaptations (such as the ones above) to shape the intervention to each site were based only on executive (e.g., Directors, Vice Presidents) or managerial (e.g., Clinical Managers) input until WGs were formed. Through a series of meetings between researchers and leads at each site, assessment of values, norms, culture, climate, and resources occurred at the level of the Executive Leads and managers. However, given the decision to use FLO to privilege direct care providers as leaders of the intervention, this assessment was used minimally to adapt the intervention, awaiting input from direct care providers. These adaptations were minor and focused on how to integrate FLO as a process and engage direct care providers. For example, in each setting the research team was advised to use “usual” channels of communication to reach staff, such as the 10-minute staff “huddles” at shift change and a weekly bulletin at SMH. The involvement of direct care providers began with pre-intervention data collection when research team members explained the study and its aims related to EOHC during staff meetings or as they encountered staff individually while collecting survey data from patients. Opportunities for direct care providers to provide input to shape equity-oriented unit-specific activities arose only after the WGs were formed. At that point, diverse commitments to and understandings of equity emerged, and the impact of resources available to further equity became more acutely evident. Primarily, direct care providers across all three sites were concerned with their workloads, and being able to meet even the most basic patient needs. The majority of comments on staff surveys (209 of 313 comments on baseline staff surveys) were concerned with workload, patient-to-staff ratios, staffing shortages, and related stress, issues that were reiterated in interviews with WG members as the following interview excerpt shows:It’s always the workload, the business just around the way that the department runs, that sometimes people don’t even have the time to stop and think. Like trauma-informed care for example, everyone believes that that is the right thing to do, but how do you incorporate something that takes more time, and sometimes time is not something that is just right there available for you. (Interview, January 2019)

The resources influencing the capacity for EOHC included the obvious issue of inadequate staffing, and the less obvious issues of expertise, organizational culture, and administrative support. UHNBC was stymied from participating beyond Stage 1 in large part because there were simply too few staff to do so, with staff necessarily focused on keeping the ED functioning. Also, the Executive Leader changed early in the study and was not replaced, and the ED manager had their responsibilities expanded to an additional unit, and eventually left the ED. There were few other equity-oriented initiatives within the organization at that time, and no funding or administrative support for leaders or potential WG members. One possible related initiative, that of the Aboriginal Liaison, as noted, consisted of a single part-time staff member responsible for supporting many Indigenous patients from across the region. At the time of EQUIP ED, in the face of responsibility for responding to increasingly acute and high patient volumes, there were no formal or facilitated mechanisms for staff involvement in decision-making, and, other than physicians, few staff were involved in research or quality improvement initiatives. At SMH, with a much larger staff and a greater economy of scale, there were fewer staffing issues and more initiatives related to EOHC – for example, there was a harm reduction project underway simultaneous with EQUIP ED, and a full-time Indigenous Liaison was assigned to the ED. An experienced Emergency clinical nurse educator from the ED was assigned to support WG members to meet. Further, there was a well-established ED Quality Council linked to systems throughout the organization through which staff were paid to contribute to quality initiatives. Some staff were involved in research as paid work, or through their own graduate education. Patient complaints initiated by Indigenous peoples, commonly regarding experiences of racism, were routed directly to the ED Indigenous Liaison for action (Field Notes, WG Meeting, March 2019).

The WG at SMH had an initial attendance of 13 ED staff comprised of nurses, clinical nurse educators, nursing assistants, and social workers. Others, including the Indigenous Liaison and a security person, joined over time, invited by the WG members. They met as a group with the researchers in 13 formal WG meetings throughout the intervention period, including a meeting to introduce the “Equity Coach” who was available to them. WG members also met in small groups on their own to plan their equity-oriented approaches.

SPH was resourced similarly to SMH with additional unique features of relevance for implementing the intervention. At the time of the study, SPH ED had close to 0% staffing vacancies. There were several dedicated nurse educators and numerous programs of research operating in the department, ranging from randomized control trials for medical interventions to staff-led projects to optimize care within the ED. Numerous equity initiatives were underway, most of which attended to substance use. Such initiatives included, for example, a “Rapid Access Addiction Clinic” where patients could access Addictions Medicine Specialists to assist with withdrawal prevention, receive harm reduction supplies and education, and initiate opiate agonist treatment. SPH also had dedicated hospital-wide programming aimed at building staff capacity to provide safe and appropriate care through an Urban Health and Indigenous Wellness Program. The administrative leadership at SPH provided significant support for staff engagement with EQUIP ED, including assigning administrative support personnel to help facilitate staff participation and an ED nurse educator to help facilitate the WG. Additionally, the SPH Director of Research and Knowledge Translation was an integral member of the EQUIP ED team, providing on-the-ground resources and capacity building for staff engagement and liaising with Executive Leadership to sustain support for the project.

The SPH WG had an initial attendance of 11 ED staff, comprised of nurses, physicians, and social workers. Similar to SMH, they met 9 times in a large group with the research team, in small groups to plan and implement their identified strategies, and with staff from other departments (e.g., Housekeeping, Security, Infection Control) to support such implementation.

In sum, the capacity of the organizations to prioritize equity was in proportion to the material and non-material resources devoted directly and indirectly to equity, including, but not limited to this project. These dynamics not only shaped the possibilities for operationalizing equity but also determined the nature of the implementation drivers at each site.

The level of resources and priority of equity relative to other pressures shaped the competency, organizational, and leadership drivers

Our analysis was informed by the idea that “active implementation is a ‘making it happen’ approach that is mission driven and does not stop until the goal of using and sustaining usable innovations in practice is reached” [54, p. 70]. Fixsen and Blase argue that implementation drivers, including Competency Drivers (building the new knowledge and skills required to support the new ways of practicing), Organizational Drivers (changes in the organization to accommodate the new ways of practicing), and Leadership Drivers (adaptive leadership to solve problems related to interests that compete with the intervention goals, and technical leadership to ensure the intervention is integrated into daily operations), are associated with “consistent innovation use and achievement of intended benefits” [54, p. 71]

The EQUIP ED team had conceptualized the new knowledge and skills required to promote EOHC (and to implement EQUIP ED) primarily as equity competencies – that is, understanding health and healthcare equity, being able to identify inequities in the ED, having the skills to use a structural lens to identify the upstream causes of inequities, and designing related solutions. However, the competency drivers were underdeveloped for two key reasons. First, as noted, the EQUIP ED research team had shifted away from making equity-related education “mandatory,” in part because of feedback from EQUIP PHC that doing so was not necessarily helpful for all (e.g., staff who self-identified as having high levels of knowledge about anti-stigmatizing approaches to care), and in part because of the daunting task and cost of providing and tracking education for large numbers of staff (up to 800 at one site). Consequently, while some WG members at both sites accessed the online education provided, this was not systematically tracked, and the vast majority of staff did not, with the result that there was no shared foundational understanding of equity, or opportunity to engage in discussions regarding how equity-oriented concepts and processes could be initiated or integrated, conversations shown to be critical to implementation of equity-oriented actions in EQUIP PHC [15, 16, 38]. Different understandings of and capacities to enact health equity were evident; for example, SPH WG members did not engage with patients regarding what would enhance patient safety within the ED, despite WG members stated emphasis on creating a space where patients felt welcome and included in their care decisions. Similarly, at SMH, WG discussions initially focused on quality assurance projects such as increasing staff efficiency in a busy care zone (Field Notes, March 2019) with no evident connection to equity.

As importantly, the competencies required were also organizational and administrative. The WGs, who were the de facto implementation teams, were comprised of direct service staff for whom convening meetings, setting agendas, communicating among various parties, and initiating action throughout the institutional bureaucracy were not familiar. For example, one WG member described the challenges faced as fairly basic: “It’s a communication piece too, like I don’t think [the WG members] know how to get a hold of each other.” While the research team offered “Equity Coaches” to support the WG with processes or content related to implementing EOHC and these coaches reached out to WG members occasionally, neither the SPH WG nor SMH WG members engaged with their Coach beyond an introductory meeting, at least in part because the coaches were not “known” to members and WG members were not experienced in working with consultants.

Although both SPH and SMH WGs had support with the above tasks, the EQUIP ED research team had clearly set an intention that the WG members would lead, creating uncertainty regarding roles and responsibilities. This played out in both sites where WGs initiated actions. For example, SPH WG members identified that providing water (by installing water dispensers) would be helpful to patients during long waits, especially those who could not afford or were unable to mobilize to a kiosk to purchase water and did not have a support person. Such an initiative would have saved staff time and frustration responding to repeated patient requests for water. However, despite months of effort to navigate the layers of approval specific to occupational health and safety guidelines within the institution, the WG abandoned the effort. Referring to this initiative, one WG member said “There was a lot of disconnect on what was allowed to be done, what was being done.” At SMH, almost two years were required to install TV monitors in ED waiting areas (one of the WG equity strategies) and obtain approval for content to be displayed.

Leadership drivers were similarly under-developed in this intervention. We found it useful to distinguish between the “adaptive leadership” required for solving “difficult problems that arise when legitimate and competing interests collide” and technical leadership which is needed for managing the daily operations of the organization [54, p.72]. Because the WGs were comprised of members without technical leadership skills and roles, and because WGs were conceptualized as operating independent of their appointed leaders, they also did not have access to adaptive leadership. For example, there was no venue or authority to resolve conflicting opinions outside the WG about access to water creating medical concerns. Further, WG members described significant gaps created by relying on “natural” leadership within the WG.There’s always natural leader-type people in groups, and it seemed like automatically that a couple of people were just taking those roles on, and I felt like they were directing how things went, but they didn’t necessarily have the expertise to do that. Not from a leadership skills standpoint at all, but just from change management and the whole equity thing.(Interview, June 2019)

AIFs specify that organizational drivers rely on administrators who can create the environment within which an innovation can thrive, remove barriers to change, and shift surrounding systems to support the change [54]. However, in the case of EQUIP ED, setting up WGs as the implementation team without clear involvement by and ongoing, regular communication with administrators meant that the intended equity-enhancing innovations were attempted without the concomitant bureaucratic supports or system changes required.

The composition and timing of engagement of the implementation teams shaped their capacity for influencing change

In the case of EQUIP ED, the decision to use FLO, and to privilege direct service staff as ‘the’ implementation team created multiple contradictions among the ways EDs and hospitals operate, the roles and skills of direct care staff, and the intervention approach. Fixsen and Blase explain that the teams of people who do the work of implementation are “not short-term entities … [rather they] become a standard part of how organizations and systems conduct their work” [54, p.67]. However, because participation in WGs was voluntary, and WGs comprised of direct service providers, these teams were not set up to be “a standard part” of the organizations. Further, while Executive Leaders with responsibility for nursing and some allied health workers committed to supporting staff to participate, including paying their time, those in management roles of other groups of workers were not asked to commit. For example, as security services are provided at SMH and SPH by external contractors not under the direct supervisory jurisdictions of hospital executives, there were no mechanisms for approving participation of security personnel (although one security manager was fully supportive) and no arrangements for compensation. Similarly, there was no physician commitment requested, nor provided by physician leadership at SPH or SMH; consequently, individual physicians decided whether to participate, and no resources arranged for such participation. Given how physicians are compensated in these settings, physicians had to forgo income while participating. At SMH, despite invitations from Executive Leaders and the research team, there was no involvement by physicians, other than two who responded to the staff survey; ED physicians largely showed disinterest with some questioning whether Indigenous people were represented in their patient population and expressing surprise at the research team’s emphasis on Indigenous-specific racism (Field Notes, September and October, 2018). None participated in the WG. At SPH, physicians were initially engaged with the project, attending information sessions and initial WG meetings, and collaborating to establish WG priorities. However, over time, physician participation in the WG waned and nurses sustained all project activities. Remaining WG members posited that the project was incompatible with physician scheduling and how their ED work was organized, noting that their physician colleagues kept in touch about the project and supported the activities, but were not involved in what they termed “the day-to-day work of the WG.” (Field Notes June 2019)

As noted, physician leadership was central at UHNBC, but action towards equity-oriented interventions was initiated two years after the end of data collection. Once funds were available to support the physicians’ loss of income for such involvement, a Cultural Safety and Humility group was formed to focus on the ED; the group included Indigenous Elders, the Indigenous Community Engagement and Education Lead, patients, physicians, researchers, and leaders of some community agencies. This group leveraged the findings from EQUIP ED and resources from the Indigenous Health team to design and implement strategies including welcoming artwork, informative pamphlets and information on TV screens in the ED, and eventual employment of Aboriginal Liaison workers specifically for the ED.

Overall, the timing, conceptualization and composition of the WGs was not optimal to the intervention goals. WGs were formed as soon as pre-intervention data collection was complete, with the consequence that local data was not available to inform their work. When baseline patient and staff survey data from each site were presented to the WGs, they provided focus for action. For example, at SMH, WG members were surprised that although, as expected, most staff reported finding most days in the past 12 months “quite” or “extremely” stressful, most also reported having “enough time” to do their work in response to the statement “I am given enough time to do what is expected of me in my job,” prompting the WG to ask the EQUIP ED research team for a literature review on sources of staff stress in ED to inform their thinking about equity-oriented strategies. However, these data were presented after strategies had been planned and as WG momentum waned. Full analyses of baseline data were not completed until after WGs had disbanded. WGs were conceptualized as leading implementation, but did not have access to their leadership in relation to the work, despite leaders expressing willingness to “be at the table” (Field Notes, February 2019) and over time were almost entirely composed of nurses, rather than all work groups.

In sum, the implementation teams of direct service providers did not receive the support needed to develop the organizational and administrative skills to implement EQUIP ED and did not have access to either adaptive or technical leadership. They were strictly voluntary, and in the case of physicians such volunteerism required loss of income. Consequently, the implementation teams were not embedded within the organization or positioned to become part of a new way of working. Further, in retrospect, the provision of local data was not effectively timed and the six-month time frame was insufficient for analysis and the design of strategies.

Organizational capacity and implementation drivers shaped the alignment of the intervention with the priorities of the organizations

Over the process of implementing the intervention, it became increasingly clear how the priorities of the organization overwhelmed the intention of EQUIP ED as an intervention, calling into question the extent to which the intervention met the criteria suggested by AIFs for a useable intervention. The four criteria include having a) a clear description of the intervention so that an organization can assess whether it aligns with its values and principles, b) clearly identified components, c) operational definitions of each feature describing how it should be implemented and, d) practical fidelity (engaging with the intended aspects of intervention) [66]. EQUIP had been well described for primary care and was evaluated at the executive level, at least on the surface, as being philosophically aligned with each ED’s overall aims. The components of the intervention were well defined, with operational definitions of each feature, except that, in alignment with the study purpose, the ‘how’ was unknown for EDs, and consequently, the components were not implemented, and practical fidelity was never approached or assessed.

Given that the goal of the study was to identify what would work to use EQUIP to promote EOHC in EDs, the ‘how’ was not well developed to align with the practical realities of how EDs work. For example, WG members at SPH noted that participation in the WG was, for many, the first time they were involved in a leadership role in their ED and that they were unsure about how to lead the WG activities. They said that having guidance on how to run a meeting, generate group consensus, and advance activities within the larger bureaucracy would have been helpful. Without this, they were unsure of their roles and freedom to move forward. Consequently, at all three sites, there was a sense that staff wanted more direction from either the EQUIP ED research team or their appointed leaders in terms of focus and how to proceed. Issues of concern to staff were not necessarily easy for WG members to link to equity, as one research lead described:They were having so many issues with overcrowding and [patient] flow and that it was the competing priority piece and they couldn’t link those two things … [For equity] you have to have that appeal and that’s why you have to frame it and look at things so that hey, overcapacity is your problem; here is how equity can help … Or, oh, staff morale is a problem; here is how equity can help … So, I think that’s a really important thing that people have to be able to do, is show why and show how equity could make a difference to whatever the current problem is …(Researcher Focus Group, April 2021)

Action also was enhanced when WG concerns aligned with others in the organization. For example, at SPH, the interests of the WG aligned with those of the Indigenous Wellness team, resulting in an effective collaboration to transform the ED waiting area to be welcoming of Indigenous peoples. Further, after the study was completed, SPH ED leaders grappling with violence in the ED used EQUIP ED as an equity-oriented approach to conduct focus groups with staff (ED physicians, nurses, social workers) and design approaches to improve safety for both patients and staff beyond the usual strategies of adding more security equipment and personnel.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths and limitations of a process evaluation are the factors that influence the extent to which the process of implementation can be evaluated, not the factors influencing effectiveness. The greatest strengths of this study included the diversity (size, geographic location, philosophical orientation and population served) among the three EDs (albeit, tempered by the fact that they are all located within one Canadian province), the diversity and wealth of data collected, the enthusiasm and dedication of the WG members, and the level of commitment to equity among the Executive Leaders at all three sites. However, while pre-existing relationships between those leaders and the researchers facilitated the study partnership, they may have truncated leaders’ critical evaluation of the suitability of the intervention and timing. The limitations further included data collection and intervention curtailment by COVID-19 public health orders. Another limitation was that because the project was initiated by researchers based in university settings, and not internally from within the EDs, ownership of the project remained with the research team. While leadership fully endorsed the project, the concerns and issues driving motivation toward EOHC of those within the organizations could have been identified and capitalized upon at the outset and their ownership cultivated.

Within these strengths and limitations, the use of AIFs optimized our ability to derive useful lessons toward redesign of the intervention. The opportunity to work with three diverse EDs using the tripartite model provided unparalleled learning regarding how to implement equity at an organizational level. While AIFs supported analysis within each organization, the data pointed to factors from wider health care and social settings as key facilitators and barriers to cultivating EOHC.

Discussion

This analysis shows that other priorities overshadowed the commitment to equity. Work foundational to keeping the EDs functioning, including ensuring staffing adequate to provide care, and respond to escalating crises such as the unregulated drug poisoning emergency, violence in Emergency Departments, and later in the study, COVID-19, took precedence. The material and non-material resources available to support and aligned with equity goals shaped the capacity of staff, leaders, and the organizations to enact equity goals. Positioning direct care providers as leaders was not effective because they did not have experience navigating bureaucratic systems nor access to leadership. Our analysis suggests new ideas regarding how the intervention should be implemented, who should be involved, and when, useful for informing future implementation research.

Although conceptualized as an organizational-level equity-oriented intervention, EQUIP ED was operationalized primarily through the voluntary efforts of individual service providers. However, contextual factors – social and organizational structures, cultures, policies, and practices – rather than the practices of individual service providers, shape the possibilities for equity. As Anawati et al. [67] argue, mechanisms must be in place to connect policies to the actualities of direct service provision, hence engaging all workgroups, including physicians and formal leaders is required. The key dimensions of EOHC identified by EQUIP were being addressed in the EDs as separate undertakings, usually in separate departments (e.g., cultural safety education, Indigenous liaisons, harm reduction programs); these efforts were not linked to one another, conceptualized as contiguous, or infused throughout the organizations, and in most cases were not well resourced or positioned to effectuate long term change. For example, in their policy analysis concerned with harm reduction in the ED setting, Jiao and colleagues [13] illustrated that organizational policies reinforce hierarchies of power by mandating harm reduction as a specialized, medical intervention. Coupled with the limited availability of specialized services during daytime hours, harm reduction as a principle of EOHC is diluted, thereby limiting how staff can provide comprehensive care for people who use unregulated substances [12, 13]. Other contextual factors specific to organizational culture are equally challenging to equity-oriented interventions. EDs often operate through policy and practice to perpetuate stigmatizing discourses that position people experiencing structural disadvantage as undeserving of care or not meeting the mandate of emergency services (e.g., acute physiologic crises) [68, 69]. And, as was evident in our EQUIP PHC research findings, opportunities to work collaboratively with colleagues and adequate education and training are essential to EOHC [16], yet limited by how EDs are organized and operate [13]. Although we recognized the influence of organizational context at the outset, EQUIP ED was not sufficiently aimed at the level of the organization and offered few opportunities for interprofessional collaboration. Further, because we had questioned the utility and feasibility of mandatory education and learned the relatively greater importance of interprofessional dialogue [16], we made education optional, with the consequence that there was no opportunity to a) track the extent to which staff were oriented to the key dimensions of EOHC (fidelity to the core component), and b) establish a common understanding of EOHC among those involved. Finally, multiple contextual factors, including usual ways of operating (e.g., colonial, bureaucratic, hierarchical) mitigate against interprofessional collaboration and engagement with those with lived and living experiences of inequities. However, the equity-related work at both UHNBC and SPH that was done after the EQUIP study, efforts that arose directly from service providers, succeeded in facilitating such collaboration.

“Who” implements equity interventions and “how” needs to be comprehensive, inclusive of diverse (organizational, administrative, and equity) competencies with alignment between who is involved and what they are responsible for doing. In this study, staff from all roles were highly motivated to contribute, but the lack of alignment (e.g., direct care providers trying to navigate unfamiliar bureaucracies; leaders invited to observe, rather than initiate change) was not effective. To address these lessons, based on analysis of the factors influencing the varied levels of intervention, we offer ten recommendations for cultivating the conditions to support uptake of equity interventions in EDs.

Conclusion

The experience of intentionally supporting three Emergency Departments (EDs) to strengthen efforts and effectiveness toward more equity-oriented health care offers lessons about the unpredictability of implementing complex interventions into complex, “real world” systems. It offers lessons regarding the roles of research and practice, of leaders at different levels, of direct care providers and patients. It underscores the importance of tailoring to unique contexts, timing, relationships, and alignment across many dimensions. This process evaluation demonstrates that equity cannot be advanced significantly by individuals alone, nor by either top-down or bottom-up approaches in isolation. Organizational capacity to deliver on their stated equity priorities requires meaningful commitment at all levels – including through extra-organizational bodies such as those funding health care systems and setting priorities, and those setting accreditation standards and professional standards.

Future research is warranted to examine what effective commitment to equity from leadership looks like, and whether there are elements of commitment that are more related to successful outcomes. Beyond stated commitments at the level of mission statements, or in strategic plans and terms of reference, what is the role of leadership in operationalizing and actualizing equity-oriented initiatives and efforts? How can publicly stated commitments, often transmitted across whole organizations, be tied to resource allocations and accountability? How can leaders catalyze thinking about equity-oriented actions or interventions in ways that transcend the boundaries of dominant, primarily colonial organizational structures and practices, for example, by strategically and meaningfully including representation from people or groups most impacted by inequities? These are priority areas to explore as researchers, healthcare leaders, and those involved in direct care continue to expand the range of strategies required to fulfill commitments to the quintuple aim with regard to advancing health equity [74].
