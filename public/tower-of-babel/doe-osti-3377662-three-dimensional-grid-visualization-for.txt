Executive Summary
The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), in collaboration with the Dubai Electricity and
Water Authority (DEWA) and Infra-X, undertook a project to advance energy visualization and
analysis. The aim of this project is to enhance analytical and three-dimensional visualization
capabilities for distribution network planning and renewable energy integration processes. As the
modern grid continues to evolve with large-scale solar photovoltaic deployment and emerging
distributed energy resources, the ability to effectively analyze, visualize, and communicate
complex grid behaviors has become increasingly critical.

This project focuses on developing empirical use cases based on real distribution feeder data and
engineering workflows, ensuring the outcomes are directly aligned with operational
environment. Through time-series power flow simulations and nodal hosting capacity analysis,
the study quantifies the impacts of high PV penetration on voltage and thermal limits within
DEWA’s representative 11-kV feeders. These analyses identify specific nodes and conditions in
which challenges with distributed energy resources integration arise.

Furthermore, a battery energy storage system (BESS) optimization algorithm was applied to
determine the optimal size and placement of storage systems that can mitigate network
constraints and enhance hosting capacity. The comparative results between base-case and BESS-
augmented scenarios clearly demonstrate improvements in network stability and load
management efficiency. In parallel, the NLR team developed an immersive three-dimensional
visualization framework, enabling interactive exploration of grid simulations using commodity
head-mounted display systems. This framework transforms conventional two-dimensional
simulation data into spatially intuitive visual environments—allowing engineers to analyze
feeder conditions, photovoltaic hosting potential, and BESS effects in real time.

This report represents the first foundational phase in establishing a visualization-driven
analytical ecosystem. It provides a methodological foundation for data integration, visualization
architecture, and simulation-based decision support, paving the way for large-scale adoption of
immersive visualization across DEWA’s Smart Grid Initiative, R&D activities, and future
network resilience studies.

1 Introduction
In a data-rich engineering environment, critical decision-making for stakeholders (e.g., electric utilities)
depends on appropriately managing, analyzing, and visualizing large and complex datasets. One prime
example of such large and complex datasets comes from power delivery infrastructure and its planning
and operation. Introduction of distributed resources such as solar photovoltaics (PV), battery energy
storage systems (BESS), and other distributed energy resources (DERs) are making these datasets even
more complex and intricate to manage. The large-scale three-dimensional (3D) immersive visualization
capabilities can assist engineers, planners, and researchers with richer representation of data, showcasing
depth and additional dimensions that traditional two-dimensional (2D) visuals cannot (Gruchalla and
Brunhart-Lupo 2019). Challenges also lie with futuristic scenario analysis, which is a projection of how
the grid operation could evolve if particular decisions are made and certain assumptions about grid
assets are valid (Molnar et al. 2024). The work performed in this collaborative effort was designed to
observe how immersive visualization with commodity hardware can be a useful tool to enhance power
systems analysis workflows. On multiple occasions, power systems researchers and engineers have
discovered features in their data using the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ (NLR’s) large-scale
immersive visualization that they had not seen in prior investigations on traditional display options
(Brunhart-Lupo, N. et al. 2020). However, the applicability of various visualization modalities is
dependent on the datasets and the specific analyses of those data. This study reports findings from a
collaborative project between Dubai Electricity and Water Authority (DEWA) and NLR that can
provide data-backed insights into strategic decision-making for utilities by:

 1. Pushing new research boundaries in power systems engineering for integrating renewable energy
 and other technologies in the high- and/or low-voltage system,
 2. Designing detailed use cases that showcase value of data visualization for power utilities, and,
 3. Identifying efficient data transfer, architecture, and fundamental requirements for what qualifies
 as minimal data visualization capabilities.
The primary objectives of this collaborative research project between DEWA and NLR are to:

 1. Advance power system visualization by developing and demonstrating immersive 3D
 visualization tools tailored for DEWA’s distribution network, enabling richer interpretation of
 complex grid datasets beyond traditional 2D displays
 2. Support DER integration studies by performing hosting capacity analysis and time series power
 flow simulations for selected DEWA feeders to assess the impacts of high PV and battery energy
 storage (BESS) penetration. Hosting capacity refers to a process used by utilities, regulators and
 developers to determine the available grid capacity for new DERs without requiring expensive
 and time-consuming studies or grid upgrades (Nagarajan and Zakai 2022).
 3. Improve decision-making workflows by providing DEWA engineers and planners with data-
 backed insights into optimal DER siting, sizing, and control strategies, supported by interactive
 visualization.
This research project’s end result is an advancement of data visualization technology for the global utility
industry. Future scenarios that include advanced techniques for grid modernization under the grid
planning, operations, and resilience paradigm need detailed analyses with interactive visualization
capabilities. This report elaborates on the research contributions around two thrust areas:

 1

 1. Scalable planning activities on distributed resource siting, sizing, and controls and their impacts
 on grid operations, and,
 2. 3D visualization application tailored to a commodity head-mounted display (HMD) that
 considers data-driven planning paradigms and can be applicable across other service areas in
 DEWA territory and even other electric utilities.
The rest of the report is organized to provide a comprehensive picture surrounding these two thrust areas.

2 Visualization Background
Visualizing power flow involves a specialized form of multivariate network visualization, where both the
connectivity of a system and the diverse set of attributes tied to its nodes and links must be represented
concurrently. This is a particularly complex area of visualization research, as it demands a unified
depiction of both the network structure and multiple variables (Nobre et al. 2019). In power grid
applications, the spatial configuration—or more generally, the graph topology—dominates the visual
space. As a result, encodings for other critical variables (such as voltage, frequency, phase angle, and
power) are constrained to secondary visual channels such as color, line thickness or texture, and glyph-
based symbols. This constraint poses challenges in discerning how variables such as voltage and power
relate across the network topology, comparing phase differences, or detecting localized phenomena
within subnetworks. One common solution in the visualization community is the use of multiple
coordinated views (Roberts 2007), a technique that integrates multiple synchronized visual interfaces to
support the exploration of complex datasets. This approach has been applied effectively to the analysis
of power system data (Arunkumar et al. 2023; Gruchalla, Novachech, and Bloom 2016), enabling more
granular and flexible interpretations of multivariate patterns. In recent literature, Goodwin et al. (2016)
applied multivariate techniques to study energy geodemographics.

Though less frequently seen, 3D techniques have also been proposed for representing power system
information. These typically expand on 2D schematics by incorporating 3D elements—such as extruded
bar charts to represent attribute magnitude (Overbye and Weber 2000; Wiegmann et al. 2006), 3D
component models (Xu et al. 2006), or volumetric and surface-based displays (Milano 2009; Sanchez-
Hidalgo and Cano 2018). Virtual reality has emerged as another tool set, particularly in the contexts of
power system training (Breen and Scott 1995; Tanaka et al. 2017) and operational control (Cardoso et al.
2013). These virtual reality environments simulate digital twins of electrical infrastructure, allowing
users to interact with virtual systems in a lifelike yet safe environment. In contrast to these earlier efforts,
our current work is intentionally designed to enable immersive analytic interaction with multivariate
power flow data, merging spatial context with variable-rich visual feedback to enhance user insight and
decision-making.

This project builds upon a substantial body of immersive analytics research and development conducted
at NLR, where previous efforts have explored and demonstrated the value of immersive environments
for interpreting complex, multivariate energy data. Earlier work laid the groundwork for visualizing
distribution-level power flow through a custom immersive application designed to reveal interactions
across multiple variables (Palmintier et al. 2016; Gruchalla and Brunhart-Lupo 2019; Gruchalla et al.
2025). That foundation was further extended to city-scale analyses, where immersive tools enabled
exploration of building energy simulations and their aggregate load impacts across urban environments
(Macumber et al. 2016). At a national scale, prior research developed a collaborative immersive
platform for comparing multivariate energy characteristics across U.S. cities (Brunhart-Lupo et al. 2020).
Other prior research efforts involved immersive analytics efforts supporting the collaborative
visualization of campus microgrid operations (Gould et al. 2021) and scenario analysis for electrical load
planning using ensemble techniques (Bush et al. 2017; Bugbee et al. 2019).

This project extends this work to relatively low-cost extended-reality HMDs.

4 Feeder Model Development
The project team selected four subnetworks to focus on and represented the rest of the
subnetworks as composite loads provided by the DEWA team. The selected subnetworks are
divided into multiple sections according to their switch placements and configurations. Each of
the parallel lines connecting the buses is assumed to be transposed for phase balancing. Load
shapes were attached to the distribution loads by engineering judgment to correlate the bus
loadings we received to the load size. The four 11-kV load profiles were assigned across
subnetworks using informed assumptions to maintain consistency with overall feeder loading
characteristics. The load profiles of PV systems and battery were labeled clearly; hence, they
were attached with no ambiguity. The power distribution feeders for futuristic analysis in
DEWA’s service territory given the OpenDSS model of the network is shown in Figure 2.

4.1 Overview of Selected Distribution Feeders
The development of the distribution feeder model for power flow analyses involves creating a
detailed simulation of the power distribution network as shown in Figure 3. This schematic
includes key components such as a 132/33-kV transformer, connecting to four feeders (e.g.,
subnetwork-1, subnetwork-3, subnetwork-5, and subnetwork-10). The model also incorporates
distributed generation sources, including a 2.4-MW battery energy storage system and a 13-MW

 6

solar plant. The distribution feeders operate at 11 kV and are supplied through 33/11-kV
transformers, while DER assets are connected at the 33-kV level via dedicated transformers. The
PV and BESS systems, specifically, are modeled as low-voltage resources (0.26 kV)
interconnected to the 33-kV network through step-up transformers.

The model was developed based on the comprehensive Open-Source Distribution System
Simulator (OpenDSS) files as inputs and then abstracting subnetworks that were not the focus of
the simulation as distribution loads (Dugan R. 2020). OpenDSS is a comprehensive electrical
system simulation tool for electric utility distribution systems developed by EPRI in 1997. This
tool has found numerous applications for distribution planning and analyses for decades and is
popularly used in research areas on emerging grid technologies. In this work, OpenDSS was used
for its easy interface features with Python, which empowers open-source tool development.

The primary goal of this model development process is to accurately represent the physical and
operational characteristics of DEWA’s distribution network. This process involved collecting
extensive data on the network topology, including the layout of substations, transformers,
distribution lines, switches, and load points. Additionally, historical and real-time load data for
each subnetwork and distribution loads are gathered at a 30-minute resolution over 1 month to
understand demand patterns, and technical specifications of all equipment are obtained to ensure
accurate modeling. This baseline modeling process had a few underlying assumptions:

performance under various operating conditions. This developed model and accompanying analysis
enabled development of various DER and load growth scenarios and performance of grid impact studies
to plan for future DER expansions and demand growth.

4.2 DEWA Model Calibration
We encountered several challenges in successfully modeling the base case. The initial OpenDSS model
lacked switch configurations, resulting in loops through the sub-transmission networks and parallel
lines. Even after including the missing switch configurations, the loops created by parallel lines persisted.
To address this, the Newton-Raphson power flow solver in OpenDSS was used instead of the default
backward/forward sweep method. Figure 4 presents the voltage profile at the primary MV level) for the
final time interval of the month (based on 30-minute resolution data) for the baseline DEWA model
shown in Figure 3.

For the time-series power flow analysis, time-series profile data were required for distribution loads as
well as PV systems. However, the sample load dataset did not correspond to the modeled loads. To
resolve this discrepancy, we per-unitized the data by using the median plus one standard deviation value
as the base value and randomly assigning the resulting per-unit load shapes. (Figure 4 shows the voltage
profile across the circuit and Figure 5 shows the power profiles from base case time-series for 30 days at
30 minutes’ resolution). For the PV systems, we treated the two provided profiles as representative and
per-unitized them using the nominal rating of each PV system as the base value. This approach led to a
misalignment between the battery profile and solar power generation profiles when modeling battery
operation, introducing an offset in which the battery continued discharging to the grid while the PV
systems were generating power and charged when the PV systems were offline. To rectify this, we
removed the offset to synchronize the battery charging cycle with solar power production. Additionally,
PV systems were modeled using inverter-based representations with Volt-Var and Volt-Watt control
strategies to capture their impact on voltage regulation and power flow under varying operating
conditions.

During model calibration, the network was validated and refined to ensure stable and consistent
performance under time-series power flow simulations, including checks on phase balance, network
connectivity, and numerical convergence.

Last, the model was reviewed and refined to ensure consistency in network topology, connectivity, and
alignment of generation and load representations, supporting stable and reliable simulation results.

5 Grid Impact Analysis Using Traditional Power Grid
  Analysis Tools
The impact of DER integration on DEWA’s OpenDSS model was analyzed using open-source tools
developed by NLR, such as EMeRGE and DISCO.

The EMeRGE (Emerging technologies Management and Risk evaluation on distribution Grids
Evolution) tool is designed to simplify analysis of complex emerging technologies to enhance
distribution network visibility. The power quality risk metrics based on critical timestamps are
considered under EMeRGE, which can help utilities make decisions about solar PV integration planning
for distribution networks. This work leverages risk-informed hosting capacity outputs from EMeRGE
and computes the sizing ranges for PV deployment scenarios. Originally developed to define grid-risks
by assessing the magnitude of power quality violations, the number of customers impacted, and the
duration of violations; EMeRGE originated from a custom approach to distribution network analysis for
another utility to gauge their state’s power sector policies and electricity sector planning (McKenna et al.
2021). In some cases PV can help reduce power quality issues, whereas in others can cause little to no
change. If the system has existing undervoltages issues PVs can push up the voltage profile, and on the
other hand, if a utility faces overvoltages, PVs could exacerbate the voltage profile. Depending on the
baseline, there can exist a specific level of PV capacity at which the overall risk to the utility is reduced
compared to the scenario without PV, providing a net benefit (as seen in Figure 6). This capacity level
represents a threshold where the risk posed by PV is equivalent to the baseline risk. Beyond this
threshold, any additional PV capacity would result in a net increase in risk, which may require mitigation
strategies.

 Figure 6. Thematic graphic of risk level versus increasing PV hosting capacity

5.1 Nodal Hosting Capacity Analysis
In this analysis, we focused on the subnetworks operating at 11-kV to evaluate their capacity for PV
penetration. Specifically, we computed the nodal hosting capacity for the 117 subnetwork nodes within
the system using EMeRGE. Nodal hosting capacity refers to the maximum amount of distributed energy
resources, such as PV systems, that a specific node in the network can accommodate without violating
operational constraints, such as voltage limits, thermal ratings, and protection coordination. The key
statistical results of the analysis are summarized in the following table.

The hosting capacity values across the 117 nodes are relatively consistent, with a narrow spread between
the minimum (8.09 MW) and maximum (9.59 MW). This indicates that the system exhibits uniform
hosting potential across most sub-networks, suggesting a fairly balanced network design without major
localized bottlenecks. The median value of 8.74 MW being closer to the maximum than the minimum
implies that most nodes can accommodate PV levels above 8.5 MW, with only a few outliers limiting
the lower bound. These lower-capacity nodes (around 8.1 MW) are likely influenced by local constraints
such as thermal limits on feeders, voltage rise sensitivity, or downstream load imbalances.

The relatively high maximum hosting capacity (9.59 MW) highlights that certain nodes can
accommodate nearly 18% more PV than the most constrained nodes, offering opportunities for targeted
PV siting at these locations.

However, the small overall range (nearly 1.5 MW across all nodes) suggests that system-wide upgrades
(e.g., voltage regulation, protection coordination) would be required to significantly increase hosting
capacity beyond this band, rather than localized reinforcement alone.

In summary, the results show a system with generally strong and uniform PV hosting capability, where
incremental improvements to address a few constraint-driven nodes could raise the minimum and make
capacity more equitable across the network. In the following sections, we describe how we use this input
to design several test cases.

5.2 DER Modeling and Integration
We utilized another in-house tool, DISCO, to generate PV deployment scenarios targeting the 11-kV
sub-network nodes (Abraham and Ghosh 2024). The maximum power capacity of each PV system was
constrained to 8 MW, as this value closely aligns with the minimum nodal hosting capacity determined
in the previous step.

DISCO strategically places PV systems at varying distances from the substation—categorized as close,
far, and random—and generates 10 random samples, each containing PV penetration scenarios ranging
from 5% to 200% in 5% increments. The additional PV systems were modeled under different inverter
control modes, including volt-var control and unity power factor operation. The existing model already
includes two PV systems, resulting in an initial penetration level of 29%. Consequently, DISCO
generated a total of 1,020 PV deployment scenarios.

Following the scenario generation, we employed DISCO to perform time-series simulations for each of
the 1,020 cases. From these simulations, we extracted key summary metrics, including voltage and
thermal violations, out of the different modes to systematically rank the deployment scenarios.

 11

Simulation outputs were post-processed to ensure consistent and accurate representation of net power
injections at each node for subsequent analysis and visualization.

We identified this by comparing PV monitor values with their supposed host bus values and observing
near-perfect cancellation, leaving only small residuals at the bus. To fix this, we post-processed the
monitor outputs: for each bus, we summed the negative of all attached element terminal powers and
explicitly added PV/storage exports at their defined bus. This reconstruction yields the correct net bus
injection (generation – load ± line flows), ensuring the time-series results now accurately reflect power
at each node.

5.3 Grid Impact Analysis with Energy Storage
We selected the 10 cases exhibiting the most severe voltage violations and an additional 10 cases with
the highest thermal violations for mitigation. It is important to note that the cases with the worst voltage
violations also exhibited line loading violations.

For each identified case, a BESS was sized accordingly to mitigate the violations. The real power
capacity of the battery was determined to alleviate excessive currents, while the reactive power capacity
was selected to correct voltage deviations at the affected node. The battery was designed to operate for a
duration of 6 hours, consistent with the discharge period of the existing battery in the system. To
maximize its effectiveness, the battery was placed at the most overloaded bus within the network. The
algorithm for BESS optimization is presented here.

5.3.1 Algorithm for BESS Placement and Sizing Optimization
With increased PV penetration, the grid is expected to face voltage and/or thermal violations. There are
some non-wires alternatives such as energy storage placement that can help reduce the extent of these
violations. A BESS sizing algorithm was used to address these violations. The goal of this algorithm is
to determine the optimal placement and sizing (in terms of kW and kWh) of BESS on an electric
distribution network to mitigate (1) thermal violations—where line loadings exceed rated capacity and
(2) voltage violations—where node voltages fall outside acceptable limits. (For the remainder of this
report, we assume American National Standards Institute [ANSI]) range as an example of acceptable
brackets, which is defined between 0.95 and 1.05 p.u.) The algorithm seeks to identify locations and
capacities of BESS units that can address these issues while minimizing system cost or resource use.

The algorithm requires the following inputs:

 • Simulation results identifying thermal and voltage violations,
 • Rated line capacities and nodal voltage values,
 • Feeder topology, including bus and line configurations,
 • System and line impedance data,
 • Desired BESS operational duration (e.g., number of hours needed for peak support), and
 • BESS cost/priority weights, if cost optimization is included.
The process flow to find an optimally sized BESS consists of four steps:

 1. Identification of critical scenarios
 Analyze simulation outputs to identify critical scenarios that trigger either thermal or voltage
 violations:

 12

 o Thermal violation: If line loading exceeds 100% of its thermal rating.
 o Voltage violation: If node voltage exceeds 1.05 p.u. or falls below 0.95 p.u..
 Each violation scenario is flagged and documented with scenario metadata (e.g., scenario ID,
 loading percentage, maximum/minimum voltage observed).
 2. Candidate node selection
 For each identified violation:
 o Select nodes in proximity to overloaded lines (for thermal violations).
 o Select nodes exhibiting the most extreme voltage deviations (for voltage violations).
 These nodes are considered candidate locations for BESS placement, under the assumption that
 strategic local injections of power or reactive support can mitigate violations.
 3. Power capacity estimation
 Estimate the minimum power capacity (in kW) of a BESS required to address the violation:
 o For thermal violations: The battery should reduce the line current to keep the loading
 below the thermal limit (e.g., 100% of line rating).
 o For voltage violations: The battery should inject or absorb reactive power to bring voltages
 within the prescribed limits.
 4. Energy capacity estimation
 Calculate the total duration for which the battery will operate (e.g., hours of peak loading or
 voltage support).
The following formulas were used to calculate the kilowatts (kW, power capacity) and kilowatt-hours
(kWh, energy capacity) of a battery for mitigating thermal or voltage violations:

 • Thermal violation: The battery should reduce the line current to keep the loading below the
 thermal limit (e.g., 100% of line rating).
 Power (kW) = Reduction in current × line voltage ×√3 for a three-phase system

 • Voltage violation: The battery should inject or absorb reactive power (kVAR) to bring voltages
 within the limits.

 Reactive power (kVAR) = Voltage deviation × system impedance (approximation)

 • Energy requirement: Calculate the total duration for which the battery will operate (e.g., hours
 of peak loading or voltage support).

 Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) × required duration (hours)

5.4 A Use Case
The selected use case is based on a detailed OpenDSS representation of a DEWA 11-kV distribution
feeder supplied through a 132/33kV substation and composed of multiple interconnected subnetworks.
The feeder includes both mid- and far-end sections with long radial branches, making it sensitive to
reverse power flow and voltage rise under high distributed generation scenarios. Following baseline
model calibration and nodal hosting capacity analysis, photovoltaic (PV) systems were incrementally
added using the DISCO framework. PV deployments were capped at individual node capacities
consistent with hosting capacity results and placed at varying electrical distances from the substation. As
PV penetration increased beyond the baseline level of approximately 29%, voltage and thermal
conditions remained largely within acceptable limits until penetration levels exceeded approximately
150%. At higher penetrations, particularly in random siting scenarios, widespread overvoltage
conditions and line loading violations emerged, driven by concentrated PV installations on electrically
weak downstream branches.

The violation case illustrates the interaction between high penetrations of distributed PV and the feeder’s
hosting capacity, and how targeted storage deployment can mitigate the resulting stress. The PV units
are distributed across the mid- and far-end sections of the feeder, while the BESS is sited close to the
area of concentrated PV generation.

The use case highlighted here represents one of the most severe violation scenarios identified, with total
PV penetration reaching approximately 189%. In this scenario, multiple feeder extremities exceeded the
ANSI upper voltage limit of 1.05 p.u., and reverse power flow resulted in elevated current levels along
critical feeder segments. To mitigate these impacts, a battery energy storage system (BESS) was
strategically placed at the most overloaded bus identified through the violation screening process. The
BESS was designed to provide both real and reactive power support over a six-hour duration, enabling it
to absorb excess PV generation during peak export periods while simultaneously providing voltage
regulation. When enabled, the BESS significantly flattened the voltage profile, reduced upstream power
flows, and alleviated thermal stress on heavily loaded lines, as demonstrated in the after-mitigation
results. This use case illustrates how targeted, data-driven storage placement can enable substantially
higher PV penetration levels while maintaining compliance with operational voltage and thermal
constraints.

advanced analytical tools to evaluate their impact. Traditional 2D visualizations, although effective for

that enable more comprehensive and intuitive analysis of energy deployment scenarios. These tools

6.1 Role of 3D Visualization in Modern Grid Studies
The visualization of power system data has traditionally relied on 2D representations, such as
geographic information system (GIS)-based mapping, line diagrams, and heatmaps. Although these
techniques can be effective, they often present challenges in distribution system studies resulting from
bus layouts’ complex, geographically dense nature, and tree-like topologies. These limitations can lead to
misinterpretations of system behaviors, particularly in scenarios where spatial relationships and
interdependencies are crucial for decision-making (Gruchalla, Molnar, and Johnson 2023).

Recent research has demonstrated that 3D visualization techniques can offer significant perceptual
benefits when dealing with complex, multivariate datasets. The added depth and spatial representation
enable better differentiation of overlapping elements and facilitate a more intuitive understanding of
system structures and dynamics (Gruchalla, Raghupathi, and Brunhart-Lupo 2021). This aligns with
broader advancements in immersive analytics, an emerging field that integrates immersive technologies
with data analytics and visualization techniques to enhance the exploration and interpretation of
complex datasets.

We have successfully applied immersive analytics at NLR across multiple scientific and engineering
domains. For example, we have used our large-scale immersive environment to support computational
fluid dynamics analysis (Brunhart-Lupo and Gruchalla 2023), materials design, and power flow analysis
(Gruchalla and Brunhart-Lupo 2019). One notable application has been the study of inverter and
advanced distribution management system control strategies, particularly their effects on voltage
regulation across distribution feeders (Palmintier et al. 2016). Furthermore, immersive analysis can
integrate into broader analytical workflows, enhancing collaboration by linking traditional statistical
interfaces, and simulation tools user interfaces with interactive 3D visualizations in immersive
environments. By bridging these modalities, teams of analysts can rapidly explore high-dimensional
datasets, leveraging the strengths of each platform—precision and computation from statistical tools,
flexibility from general-purpose programming environments, and intuitive spatial reasoning from 3D
visualization (Brunhart-Lupo et al. 2020).

Building on this foundation, our joint project with DEWA extends NLR’s immersive analytics
capabilities from high-end, large-scale immersive installations to state-of-the-art, commercially
available HMDs. By leveraging Apple Vision Pro, we provide an economical and accessible solution for
3D grid visualization (as seen in Figure 9; this representation is realized through the Apple Vision Pro
HMD). This transition democratizes access to advanced visualization tools, enabling planners,
engineers, and stakeholders to engage with complex power system data in a more interactive and
insightful manner.

3D visualization transforms grid impact analysis by enabling stakeholders to simultaneously assess
multiple variables in a unified spatial context. Unlike traditional 2D plots, where multiple graphs are
needed to depict different aspects of grid performance, a 3D representation allows users to explore
multivariate relationships within a single immersive environment. By leveraging depth, spatial
orientation, and interactive controls, planners and researchers can better compare deployment scenarios,
identify potential siting issues for energy storage, and evaluate the grid stability (Bugbee et al. 2019).
For example, consider the 189% use case presented in Section 5.4. The traditional voltage profile plots
in Figure 7 and Figure 8 provide an abstracted representation of the system’s voltages with little context.
In contrast, Figure 10 and Figure 11 show voltage, real and reactive power (with their flow directions),
and the generation sources in the geographical context.

6.3 System Architecture
The underlying architecture of the 3D visualization framework is designed to be modular, extensible,
and collaborative, supporting efficient data handling and real-time synchronized interactions across
platforms. At the start of this system is the transformation of simulation outputs1 into a compact and
standardized format using the NLR-derived open-source PowerSystemsData specification (Brunhart-
Lupo and Gruchalla 2025). This format not only ensures efficient data loading and storage but also
facilitates interoperability across different analytical tools and visualization platforms.

Once the simulation data are encoded in this file format, it is ingested by the NoodlesGrid application,
which serves as the central visualization engine. NoodlesGrid runs on a dedicated server and constructs a
dynamic 3D representation of the grid model from the input data. This visualization is then transmitted
via NOODLES, an NLR-developed protocol for synchronizing visual content across distributed
environments (Brunhart-Lupo 2024). Designed for flexibility, NOODLES allows multiple clients to
connect simultaneously and interact with a shared visualization space, regardless of their hardware or
platform.

In this implementation, one such client is a Vision Pro application that renders the 3D grid environment
in immersive mixed reality. Users equipped with Apple Vision Pro headsets can explore, manipulate,
and analyze the grid by body-relative judgments, motion, and interaction. Importantly, because the
visualization state is centrally managed and synchronized via NOODLES, multiple users (including

6.4 Visualization Design and Features
The visualization is designed to represent both the physical and electrical characteristics of the power
grid in an intuitive spatial layout. At the base of the scene lies a flat plane displaying a world map with
the geographic location of the analyzed distribution network, grounding the visualization in a
recognizable and concrete local context. Overlaid on this map is a 2D schematic of the grid topology,
with lines that denote the physical layout of conductors.

This 2D representation then extends into the third dimension by mapping height to line variables:
proportionally to either to its p.u. voltage value or percentage line loading. The lines are colored by
phase or voltage at nominal level. To support interpretation, a 3D scale is placed within the scene,
offering a clear reference for height-to-value scaling. To enable the identification of voltage violations,
the visualization includes horizontal ANSI voltage limit planes rendered within the 3D space. These
planes represent the upper and lower acceptable bounds of voltage, as defined by standard ANSI limits.
When portions of the grid, such as phase conductors, rise above or drop below these safety thresholds,
the corresponding lines visually “pierce” the planes. This direct spatial violation makes it immediately
apparent where and when voltage excursions occur, enhancing the user’s ability to assess grid stability
and power quality at a glance. This approach transforms what is traditionally a tabular or color-coded
warning into a spatial, physicalized cue, aligning with the immersive nature of visualization.

Line thickness encodes power metrics: “width” corresponds to real power (watts), whereas vertical
thickness—or “height” of the line in cross-section—represents reactive power (vars). Power flow
direction is indicated by a chevron series that lies along the lines.

Transformer locations are marked with vertical columns, where the step level of voltage regulators is
visually apparent. Generators, including PV systems and battery storage, are placed within the 3D space
according to their operating voltage and scaled by their power output. Distinct graphical elements
differentiate between generation sources.

In the rear of the scene, a time series performance chart offers a high-level view of the grid’s behavior
over time. A time indicator lets users scrub or animate through the dataset, updating the 3D visualization
accordingly. Crucially, users can probe or query individual elements in the scene (such as a line or a
transformer) to bring up detailed time series charts that display localized values of voltage, power, and
other key metrics. This combination of spatial immersion, real-time interaction, and multivariate
representation provides a rich analytical environment for modern grid studies.

7 Conclusion and Future Works
The added spatial and perceptual dimensions offered by immersive visualization have enabled a
reimagining of how complex power systems data—such as those from DEWA feeders—can be
understood and analyzed. Quasi-static time-series simulations of electric power distribution networks
involve numerous interdependent, time-varying variables distributed across space. Even experienced
engineers face challenges in fully interpreting these interactions when constrained to conventional two-
dimensional geographic visualizations. Immersive analytics is used to address this by extending
traditional four-dimensional visual encodings—such as network topology with line variables mapped to
color and width—into a higher-dimensional space. In this environment, voltage, real power, and reactive
power can be explored simultaneously within the network topology, offering a more comprehensive and
intuitive grasp of system behavior over time.

Engineers from NLR and DEWA applied this immersive tool to validate OpenDSS power flow models
for DEWA’s distribution feeders. The application revealed previously hidden multivariate relationships
and provided utility engineers with deeper insight into the dynamics of the power flow simulations. By
integrating immersive visualization into their workflow, engineers were able to detect and diagnose
modeling issues in the network topology and control logic—issues that had eluded detection using
standard 2D analysis techniques. The immersive environment did not replace traditional analysis but
complemented it, acting as a powerful diagnostic aid that significantly enhanced model verification and
situational awareness.

Some key findings from this research work are summarized below:

 • The baseline model needed some modifications to resolve the loops created by parallel lines—the
 Newton power flow solver was employed in OpenDSS to circumvent the backward sweeping
 approach.
 • Early on the feeder models showed errors in the modeled topology—these errors were detected in
 3D models in which the project team could evaluate the size and direction of the flows. These
 errors were corrected by flipping the “to” and “from” grid nodes in the model.
 • The 3D visualization demo revealed that the power flow directions for a few lines were off. For
 example, a line connected to a PV generator showed the power flow direction was toward the
 generator node, whereas it is expected to be flowing “out” of the generator node. After much
 deliberation, this was revealed to be a monitor/reporting issue with the OpenDSS model. This
 debugging process was another example of how 3D visualization can be helpful to figure out
 possible issues in planning domains, which would be otherwise harder to detect with traditional
 2D graphics.
Although these applications illustrate the qualitative benefits of immersive visualization for power
systems engineering, quantitative assessments of their operational and economic value remain an open
research area. Future work should focus on empirical studies that measure the impact of visualization on
decision-making and system performance.

This includes evaluating potential capital cost savings from earlier error detection, or improvements in
reliability indices such as CML (customer minutes lost) and SAIDI (System Average Interruption
Duration Index). Establishing these metrics would provide stronger evidence base for the broader
adoption of advanced visualization tools in renewable energy integration and grid modernization efforts.
