 Geothermal Closed Loop System
 • Closed loop systems are designed to be
 completely sealed off (hydraulically) from the
 surrounding soil and rock formation(s) and rely
 only on conductive heat transfer in the
 subsurface.
 • A typical closed loop system consists of heat pump
 unit, circulation pump, and the looping piping
 installed in wells, which allow heat exchange with
 the soil/rock.
 • Typically, 5 to 6 inches in diameter, drilled with 20
 feet spacing, and 500 feet deep.
 • Loops inside the wells consist of 1- to 1.25-inch
 diameter high-density polyethylene piping and are
 grouted in place with thermally enhanced grout.
 • Each 500-ft well (or loop) can be approximated to
 provide 2.5 to 3.3 tons refrigeration (30 to 40 MBH)
Source: https://www.nyc.gov/assets/ddc/downloads/Sustainable/GeothermalHeatPumpManual.pdf

 Overview of Geothermal Heat Pump Costs

• GHP-Coefficient of Performance (COP): 3–4
 ◦ More efficient compared to air-source heat pumps (ASHP) (COP 1.75).
• Potentially can provide high savings on energy costs.
• GHPs are eligible for a 30% federal tax credit through 2034.
• GHP systems have an average 20+-year life expectancy for the heat pump
  itself and 25 to 50 years for the underground infrastructure.
 ◦ Maintenance costs are minimal once in the ground.

 Summary
• Sizing the system depends on heating and cooling loads and the ground
  thermal properties.
• The loads depend on size of the building, type of construction, use of the
  building, duration of the heating and cooling seasons, and climate.
• Type of construction energy needs depends on various factors, such as
  insulation, climate, and windows. It is best to consult with a heating,
  ventilating, air-conditioning (HVAC) professional to perform a proper load
  calculation for accuracy.
• Thermal conductivity of the soil directly impacts the size of the bore field
  needed and the drilling cost.
• A thermal conductivity test requires completion of one or more boreholes.

 Installation Best Practices
• Well construction
 ◦ Drilling fluids should be potable water or municipal supplied.
 ◦ Drilling mud/bentonite forms a filter preventing drilling fluid from seeping into aquifer
 formation.
 ◦ Surface runoff—Rehabilitate the drilling site by planting grass cover to prevent washing off
 the soil by surface runoff.
 ◦ Grout and seal well top to prevent surface runoff.
• Well completion
 • Grouting to be completed in a manner that prevents contaminants to the aquifer.
 • Systems pipes follow high-density polyethylene PE345434C or PE355434C with a UV stabilizer
 of C, D, or E, as specified in the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) D-3350 w.

 Potential Impact of Working Fluid Leakage on the
 Environment
• Working fluid circulating within the closed loop typically consists of commercial
  antifreeze mixtures (e.g., propylene glycol) and anti-corrosion agents, diluted
  with water at concentrations of 20%–25%.​
• Although propylene glycol itself is not considered toxic, if leaked into the
  environment, it can serve as an organic nutrient that stimulates microbial
  metabolism, potentially resulting in O2 consumption and subsequent decrease in
  redox potential of the system (Bucci et al. 2018).​
• Continuous leakage of propylene glycol can deplete oxygen levels, disrupt
  microbial communities, and affect groundwater quality, potentially harming
  aquatic ecosystems, plant life, and drinking water sources. Although
  biodegradable, prolonged leakage can lead to cumulative environmental impacts,
  highlighting the need for proper monitoring, nontoxic fluids, and effective
  sealing techniques to minimize these risks.

                                                                     National Laboratory of the Rockies | 34

 Potential Impact of Grouting on the Environment

• Grouting (typically cement and bentonite-based mixtures) is a process for
  sealing the boreholes and ensuring efficient heat transfer.​
• However, defects and voids in the grout can increase the hydraulic
  conductivity, potentially leading to inter-aquifer flux that can affect
  groundwater quality (Bucci et al. 2018, https://doi.org/10.1007/s12665-018-
  7375-9). Leaking grout materials, such as alkalis or heavy metals, may also
  contaminate surrounding soil and groundwater, disrupting local chemistry.​
• To minimize these risks, it is crucial to use high-quality grouting materials,
  apply them correctly to ensure effective sealing, and regularly monitor the
  system’s integrity to prevent leakage and maintain environmental protection.

 Additional Takeaways
• 16,000 gallons of #2 fuel oil is used annually at the Shelter Island school—indicates there may be a
  heating inefficiency within the school that will make any potential geothermal systems less impactful.
 ◦ According to this 2018 CBECS survey, average fuel oil energy intensity for schools in the Northeast is
 around 130.5 gallons/thousand square feet. For the Shelter Island school at 77,000 square feet, this
 would equate to roughly 10,049 gallons. The 16,000 gallons is higher than the estimated fuel oil
 usage for the initially modeled prototype school that was 3x larger.
• The system is a closed loop, where the working fluid is circulated throughout the loop to exchange
  thermal energy (gain or reject) rather than being injected into subsurface or mixed with groundwater.
  The system is designed to be thermally balanced with the building load. For example, warm water is
  circulated from the school to the closed loop during summer to produce cooling, resulting in an
  increase in the subsurface temperature during summer (and vice versa in winter). Over the localized
  area of Fiske Field, the ground temperature will vary throughout the year based on these inputs. The
  initial regional groundwater temperature is factored into the initial modeling; however, once the system
  starts running, the localized groundwater temperature will swing up and down. This process is more
  dependent on the annual load balance, instead of the initial groundwater temperature of 53°F.
