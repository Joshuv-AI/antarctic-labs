Suicidal behavior and Ethiopian students: a systematic review and meta-analysis study
Authors: Girmaw Medfu Takelle, Setegn Fentahun, Girum Nakie, Gidey Rtbey, Fantahun Andualem, Gebresilassie Tadesse, Mamaru Melkam, Redait Awoke, Mulualem Kelebie, Techilo Tinsea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicidal behavior encompasses a spectrum that progresses from suicidal ideation to suicidal attempts. In Ethiopia, although existing research shows considerable variation in the reported prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempts, along with methodological weaknesses and inconsistent findings, there has been no comprehensive systematic review or meta-analysis study to date examining the prevalence and associated factors among Ethiopian students.MethodsA search of published articles was made by the following databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, HINARI, Web of Science, PsycINFO, EMBASE, Psychiatry Online, World Health Organization (WHO) reports, and other gray literature was conducted by searching Google, Google Scholar, and other internet search engines. Using the preset search methods, 12 publications for suicidal Ideation and 10 for suicidal attempt were included based on the eligibility criteria. The data were retrieved in Microsoft Excel spreadsheet format and exported to Stata version 14 for analysis. The statistical heterogeneity was assessed by the I2 test. Due to the presence of high heterogeneity among included studies, a random-effect meta-analysis model was used. Egger’s weighted regression test and funnel plots are used to check the presence of publication bias.ResultThe pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempt among students in Ethiopia was found to be 19.3% (95% CI: 16.02, 22.5) and 8.99% (95% CI: 6.57, 11.43), respectively. Poor social support (ideation: AOR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.86, 3.16; attempt: AOR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.50, 3.36), depression (ideation: AOR = 2.85, 95% CI: 2.00, 4.05; attempt: AOR = 4.04, 95% CI: 2.10, 7.80), family history of suicide (ideation: AOR = 3.49, 95% CI: 2.66, 4.58; attempt: AOR = 4.13, 95% CI: 2.17, 7.83), and disappointment with academic performances (ideation: AOR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.79, 3.92; attempt: AOR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.79, 3.92) were factors significantly associated. However, being female (AOR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.29, 4.14) and anxiety disorders (AOR = 3.38, 95% CI: 1.38, 8.28) were significantly associated with suicidal ideation only.ConclusionThis study revealed that, the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempt among students in Ethiopia is relatively high. The study also identified that poor social support, depression, family history of suicide and disappointment with their school performance were the factors significantly associated with both suicidal ideation and attempt. However, being female and anxiety disorders were significantly associated with suicidal ideation alone. Also, special attention should be given to students with a family history of suicide, depression, anxiety, poor social support, disappointment with their school performance, and for female students, to prevent suicidal behaviors.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08320-6.

Introduction

Suicidal behavior encompasses a spectrum that progresses from suicidal ideation characterized by self-reported thoughts of ending one’s life to suicidal attempts, which involve non-fatal self-injury with the intent to die, and finally to death by suicide [1, 2]. Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts are important components of the spectrum of suicidal behavior and strong predictors of completed suicide [3].

Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts are interrelated but distinct components along the continuum of suicidal behavior. Suicidal ideation is defined as thoughts, wishes, or plans regarding self-inflicted death, whereas suicide attempts are defined as self-injurious behaviors undertaken with at least with an intent to die [4]. Although suicidal ideation is a strong predictor of subsequent suicide attempts, however, not all individuals experiencing suicidal ideation progress to attempting suicide [5, 6].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), nearly one million people die by suicide each year, accounting for about 1.48% of all global deaths [7]. Globally, one person dies by suicide every 40 s, placing suicide among the top 20 leading causes of death across all age groups [8]. It is also among the top two and three causes of mortality for males and females aged 15–29 and 15–44 years, respectively [9–13].

Adolescence and early adulthood constitute a critical developmental stage marked by increased vulnerability to suicidal behaviors [1, 14, 15]. Suicidal behavior among students represents a major global public health concern, as suicide ranks as the second leading cause of death among adolescents and young people aged 10–24 years [1, 15–17]. This stage is characterized by substantial biological, cognitive, and psychosocial transitions, including ongoing brain development that influences emotional regulation and impulse control, heightened responsiveness to peer pressure, academic pressures, identity formation processes, and growing expectations for independence [15, 18].

Suicide rates in Africa range from 2.3% to 6.0% [13, 19, 20]. Sub-Saharan Africa has the second-highest rate of suicide globally at 32 per 100,000 people [9]. Worldwide, suicide rates have risen by approximately 60% in the last 50 years [21]. Evidences also indicates that 85.7% of individuals who attempt suicide had previously experienced serious suicidal ideation [22, 23].

In certain Countries, suicide is the leading cause of death among students, accounting for approximately 19% of mortality in this group [24–27]. Students may often experience significant life changes, acquire new skills, and struggle with challenges that may ultimately result in suicide [15, 28–30].

Students who have suicidal ideation and attempts can have far-reaching and complex effects on themselves, their educational institutions, and society at large [31, 32]. Some of the impacts of suicidal ideation and attempts among students could impair their academic performance, social relationships, and self-esteem issues [32, 33]. Suicidal thoughts and attempts are frequently a sign of underlying mental health conditions such as trauma, anxiety, or depression [15, 33, 34]. These circumstances can seriously harm a student’s well-being, social interactions, and academic achievement [31].

The developmental transition from childhood to adolescence and early adulthood is marked by changes in biology, cognition, responsibility, and psychosocial functioning [17]. These changes are often accompanied by a strong desire to try new things, a low-risk perception, academic strain or demand, and a strong need for independence and self-identity, all of which increase the risk of suicidal ideation and attempts in adolescents and young adults [35, 36].

Although most studies on suicidal ideation and attempts among students have been conducted in high-income countries, an increasing body of research is now examining this public health concern in low- and middle-income countries [37, 38].

Globally, the prevalence of suicidal ideation ranges from 11.2% to 31.9% among high school students [39, 40], 1.3% to 32.7% among college students [33, 41], and 1.8% to 53.6% [42, 43] university students.

A previous study using data from the Global School-Based Health Survey (GSHS) across 46 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) reported pooled prevalence of 14.5% for suicidal ideation and 12.7% for suicide attempts [40, 44]. Similarly, a study conducted among high school students found a pooled prevalence of suicide attempts of 17.2% [45].

A study conducted in sub-Saharan African countries reported that the prevalence of suicidal ideation ranged from 11.2% to 31.9% [39]. Other similar large-scale study with a participant of 635,000 medical students found pooled lifetime prevalence of 22.3% for suicidal ideation and 3.2% for suicidal attempts [46].

Studies conducted among college students found that 21.6% of the sample reported suicidal ideation [47] and, in another study, approximately 49% of college students reported a lifetime history of suicidal ideation and attempts [48]. It is estimated that each year, for individuals ages 18–29 years old, 2.9 million people have suicidal thoughts, and an estimated 477,000 attempt suicide [48, 49].

Although, no pooled prevalence studies have been conducted among students in Ethiopia and evidence from individual studies indicates that the prevalence of suicidal ideation was 7% in Tanzania [50], 6.2% in Seychelles [51], 31.3% in Zambia [52], 23.3% in Benin [53], 21.6% in Uganda [54], 20% in Nigeria [55], 51.5% in Botswana [56], and ranged from 8.2% to 23.7% in Ethiopia [57, 58].

As previous studies showed that, the major risk factors for completed suicide include suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts [10, 59, 60]. Suicidal ideation during a lifetime was associated with a 33% chance of ever creating a plan and a 30% chance of attempting suicide. The likelihood of completing suicide was 55% for those with a lifetime history of suicidal ideation and planning [59].

Based on existing evidence, lifetime prevalence estimates of suicidal ideation in Ethiopia range from 8.2% to 58.3%, while reported rates of suicide attempts vary between 4.2% and 16.2% [24, 40, 42, 57, 58, 61–66]. Psychiatric disorders, substance use, hopelessness, poor social support, dissatisfaction with academic performance, female sex, a family history of suicide, recent family conflict, and a non-supportive family environment were factors significantly associated with suicidal ideation and attempts [33, 67–70].

Compared with other regions, Ethiopia has a limited number of studies examining the prevalence and determinants of suicidal ideation and suicidal attempts among students. Moreover, existing studies are characterized by methodological limitations and substantial inconsistency in their findings. To date, no systematic review or meta-analysis has been conducted in Ethiopia focusing on this population. Therefore, the present study aims to systematically review available Ethiopian studies on suicidal ideation and suicidal attempts among students, synthesize evidence on (1) the prevalence of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts and (2) their associated determinants, and provide recommendations to guide future research.

Methods and materials

Study design

Meta-analysis and systematic review were employed according to Preferred Reporting Items for a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA-P 2020) standard [71].

Searching strategies

The detailed protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis study was registered with PROSPERO under reference ID CRD42024530525. This study aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation, attempts, and the associated factors among Ethiopian students. A comprehensive search for published articles conducted across several databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, HINARI, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Psychiatry Online. In addition, reports from the World Health Organization (WHO) and other gray literature were reviewed through searches conducted using Google, Google Scholar, and other internet search engines. To include further relevant studies, we also reviewed the reference lists of eligible articles. The search employed a combination of terms related to the prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempts, along with associated factors, using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), keywords, and free-text terms connected by Boolean operators. The search query included terms such as (“prevalence” OR “magnitude” OR “epidemiology” OR “proportion” OR “incident”) AND (“suicidal ideation” OR “suicide” OR “suicidal thought” OR “suicidal intent” OR “suicidal behavior” OR “suicidal attempt” OR “killing oneself” OR “suicidal plan”) AND (“associated factors” OR “risk factors” OR “determinants” OR “predictors” OR “burden” OR “correlate”) AND (“students” OR “adolescents” OR “young adulthoods” OR “university students” OR “college students” OR “high school students” OR “secondary school students”) AND (“Ethiopia”). Keywords and free-text terms were also used as needed. Data were extracted from the included articles, and any missing information was requested from the corresponding authors through email.

All studies were assessed for eligibility using predefined criteria before being included in the meta-analysis. The literature search for included articles was conducted from May 20 to June 20, 2025.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they: (i) assessed the prevalence and/or associated factors of suicidal ideation or attempts among students in Ethiopia; (ii) used a community- or institution-based cross-sectional study design; and (iii) were published in English language.

Exclusion criteria

Studies involving animals, editorial letters, previously reviewed articles, study protocols, and studies that reported on suicidal ideation and/or attempts only in adolescents and/or young adults all excluded. Articles without an abstract or complete text were excluded from the review, if they could not provide the required information. Articles published in languages other than English, as well as studies carried out outside Ethiopia, were also excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (GR and SF) independently extracted all the necessary data from the articles using a standardized data extraction format. The data extraction format included the following items: the first author’s name, publication year, region of the study, study population, study design, number of participants, and a screening tool used to assess suicidal ideation and/or attempts, prevalence of suicidal ideation and/or attempt and response rate were extracted. The 95% confidence intervals for prevalence of suicidal ideation and/or attempt and related factors, also extracted. Data from all studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria extracted and tabulated.

After an initial screening of the titles and abstracts, the full texts reviewed in detail. Disagreements regarding the inclusion and interpretation of data were resolved through discussion with the corresponding author and the other reviewers.

Study selection and quality assessment

The methodological quality of the studies included in this review was assessed with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool for observational studies [72]. This nine-item instrument evaluates essential domains including study design, comparability, and statistical methods. To ensure consistent application, two independent reviewers performed the appraisals. Inter-rater reliability was measured using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, and any initial disagreements were resolved through discussion. In cases where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (GM) made the final decision. Each study was then scored and classified as high (≥ 8/9), moderate [5–7], or low (≤ 4) quality. Only those studies rated as moderate or high quality were included for the final review and meta-analysis. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of meta-analytic results.

Outcome of interest

The outcome of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to determine the pooled prevalence and associated factors of suicidal ideation and attempts among students in Ethiopia.

Measurements of suicidal ideation and attempt

This systematic review and meta-analysis had four main objectives: to determine the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and suicidal attempts among students in Ethiopia, and to assess the pooled effects of factors associated with both outcomes. The pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempts was calculated using STATA version 14.0. For the associated factors, the pooled effects were determined using odds ratios (ORs), with the logarithm and standard error of the OR for each study generated in STATA. One article used “Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSS)” [65], one article used “Suicidal Behavior Questionnaire-Revised; (SBQ-R)” [24], two studies used “semi-structured questionnaire” [63, 73], and all the rest articles that included in this study were used “Composite international diagnostic interview (CIDI)” to assess suicidal ideation and attempt among students.

Definitions of suicide ideation and suicide attempts

Suicidal ideation refers to thoughts, wishes or plans of ending one’s own life, regardless of whether the person has a specific intent or engages in related behaviors [74]. Suicide attempts involve deliberate self-harm carried out with the goal of causing death, though the act does not result in fatal outcome [75]. For the purposes of this review, suicidal ideation and suicide attempts were examined across 12-month and lifetime.

Data analysis

We conducted a comprehensive review and meta-analysis of the relevant literature’s stated prevalence and risk factors for suicidal ideation and attempts. The pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempts was calculated by using a random-effects model and are presented as percentages with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed in the included studies by using the I2 statistics [76]. In the included studies, the heterogeneity values were greater than 95%. A subgroup analysis was conducted by categorizing the study regions into three groups (Amhara, Oromia, and others). Furthermore, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on academic level (university students, high school, and secondary school students), mean age of the study population, sample size and assessment tools that the primary studies used. Egger’s test, the funnel plot and trim and fill analysis were used to assess publication bias. All analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software, Version 14. By using a random effect model, the risk factors for suicidal ideation and attempts were determined.

Result

Using the preset search strategies, the database search engines yielded 2,197 articles for both suicidal ideation and attempts. From these 1,582 articles removed due to duplication. After screening the remaining studies, 527 articles were excluded following title and abstract review because they were not relevant to the outcomes of interest. While 43 additional articles were excluded after full-text assessment due to insufficient or irrelevant information. Ultimately, 12 studies met the eligibility criteria for suicidal ideation (Fig. 1), and 10 of those met the criteria for suicidal attempts (Fig. 2). Consequently, 12 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis for suicidal ideation, and 10 studies for suicidal attempts.

Characteristics of included studies

Twelve articles with a total of 9,089 participants for suicidal ideation and 7,930 participants for suicidal attempts were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis study. Of the included articles, ten assessed suicidal attempts, whereas twelve evaluated suicidal ideation. The studies were conducted in five distinct regions of Ethiopia. For suicidal ideation, the regional distribution was Amhara (n = 4), Oromia (n = 5), Tigray (n = 1), Harari (n = 1), and Addis Ababa (n = 1); for suicide attempts, it was Amhara (n = 3), Oromia (n = 5), Harari (n = 1), and Addis Ababa (n = 1). In terms of study settings, five were conducted in high schools and seven among university students. All studies reported employing systematic random sampling for primary data collection. The mean age of participants across studies ranged from 16.9 to 22.71 years. For assessment, eight studies used the CIDI; the other four used a non-standard questionnaire (n = 2), the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (n = 1), and the Suicidal Behavior Questionnaire-Revised (n = 1) (Table 1).

The pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation among students in Ethiopia

In this systematic review and meta-analysis study, twelve studies that assessed the prevalence of suicidal ideation were included. The minimum and maximum prevalence of suicidal ideation from the included articles were 8.2% [58] and 34.0% [24] respectively. Based on the random effect model, the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation is 19.3%, (95% CI: 16.02, 22.5). The I2 result showed higher heterogeneity (I2 = 94.1, P = 0.000) (Fig. 3). Univariate meta-regression was employed to investigate factors associated with heterogeneity, such as region, year of study, study setting, mean age of participants and sample size of the primary studies.

Heterogeneity and publication bias for suicidal ideation

Based on the inverse variance heterogeneity test (I²), a high degree of statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies on suicidal ideation (I² = 94.1%, p < 0.001). To assess the presence of publication bias among the included articles, three methods were employed. First, visual inspection of a funnel plot revealed an asymmetric distribution, suggesting the possible presence of publication bias (Fig. 4). Second, to provide a more objective assessment, Egger’s test was conducted. The result (p = 0.089) indicated no statistically significant publication bias, as the p-value exceeded 0.05 (Fig. 5). Although the funnel plot indicated possible asymmetry, Egger’s regression test did not show statistically significant publication bias (p > 0.05). This may be attributable to the small number of included studies, which limits the statistical power of regression-based methods to detect publication bias [77].

Subgroup analysis for suicidal ideation

Since the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was influenced by substantial heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis has been employed based on the study region, study participants, year of study, mean age of participants, sample size and assessment tools of the included studies. Based on study region, from the included 12 studies, four studies were conducted from the Amhara region [40, 42, 61, 63] and five studies from Oromia region [24, 57, 62, 65, 66] and three studies were conducted out of these regions (Harari, Tigray and Addis Ababa) [58, 64, 73]. The pooled prevalence was found to be 18.17% (95%CI: 14.73, 21.62) in Amhara, 27.35% (95% CI: 16.01, 38.69) in Oromia and 12.89% (95% CI: 8.43, 17.36) in other regions. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was higher among College and University students 23.33% (95% CI: 13.82, 32.84) as compared with secondary and high school students 17.33% (95% CI: 14.27, 20.39). Moreover, the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was higher in studies conducted in 2019 and after [24, 40, 57, 63, 64, 66], 23.33% (95% CI: 13.82, 32.84) than in studies conducted before 2019 [58, 61–63, 65, 73] 18.02% (95% CI: 13.65, 22.40). Subgroup analysis by mean age revealed almost similar pooled prevalence estimates: 19.03% (95% CI: 15.11, 22.96) for participants with a mean age of 18 years or older, and 19.50% (95% CI: 14.01–24.99) for those with a mean age below 18 years. Regarding assessment tools, the highest pooled prevalence was observed in studies using non-standardized tools [16, 73], at 27.00% (95% CI:13.48, 40.52), followed by those employing the BSS or SBQ-R [24, 65], at 18.72% (95% CI: 15.93, 21.51) and studies using the CIDI [40, 57, 58, 62, 66, 78–80], at 17.65% (95% CI:13.86, 21.43). Factors associated with suicidal ideation among students in Ethiopia

Various factors were associated with suicidal ideation in students throughout the 12 primary studies that were included. However, we took into account the factors that have been identified in multiple studies. Based on this, it was found that factors such as having a lack of social support, female gender, having a family history of suicide, depression, anxiety, and getting disappointed in one’s performance in school were strongly associated with suicidal ideation. These factors have been reported by two or more articles in the extracted studies. The meta-analysis quantified these associated factors, showing that students with poor social support had more than double the odds of suicidal ideation (AOR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.86, 3.16) as compared with students having strong support. Similarly, female students were more than twice as likely to experience suicidal ideation as their male counterparts do (AOR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.29, 4.14). The strongest associated factor was a family history of suicide, which increased the odds nearly, three-and-a-half times (AOR = 3.49, 95% CI: 2.66, 4.58). Both anxiety and depression were also substantial associated factors, with odds ratios of 2.85 (95% CI: 2.00, 4.05) and 3.38 (95% CI: 1.38, 8.28), respectively. Finally, students who reported disappointment with their academic performance had 2.46 times higher odds of suicidal ideation (95% CI: 1.75, 3.45), (Fig. 11).

Discussion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study done in Ethiopia to assess the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and suicidal attempts among students. In this study, 12 studies on suicidal ideation and 10 studies on suicide attempts were included, comprising a total of 9,089 participants for suicidal ideation and 7,930 participants for suicide attempts. These studies were analyzed to determine the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and their associated factors among Ethiopian students.

Suicidal ideation

The finding of this systematic review and meta-analysis study revealed that the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and suicidal attempts were found to be 19.3%, (95% CI: 16.02, 22.5). The finding of this study was in line with a study conducted among African medical students with a pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was 18.7% [81]. Also, the result was consistent with a systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted globally; the prevalence was ranged 14.3% to 22.6% [82] and study among college students, prevalence of suicidal ideation was 22.3% [83].

The result of this systematic review and meta-analysis was relatively lower than studies conducted among young University students in Brazil 27% [84]. This variation likely stems from differences in educational systems, family dynamics, and cultural attitudes toward suicide [85, 86]. In Ethiopia specifically, while strong traditional support exists for bereaved families, help-seeking behavior remains constrained by cultural taboos, social shame, and fear of criticism [87, 88].

However, the findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis were higher than studies conducted among Brazilian undergraduate students and globally among medical students, the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was 9.10% and 11.1% respectively [89, 90]. These discrepancies may be attributable to variations in the underlying factors influencing suicide risk across populations, including socioeconomic conditions, academic pressure, mental health stigma, social isolation, and lifestyle factors [85, 91, 92].

This study identified several factors significantly associated with suicidal ideation among students: poor social support, female gender, family history of suicide, anxiety, depression, and disappointment with school performance. Students reporting poor social support were 2.4 times more likely to experience suicidal ideation than those with strong support networks. This finding aligns with research conducted among young university students in Brazil, across Africa among HIV/AIDS patients, and across 46 low- and middle-income countries [84, 93–95]. This association could be due to lack of emotional support from their families, peers or friends are more likely to experience feeling of loneliness, despair and isolation, which can contribute to suicidal ideation [96, 97]. Additionally, social support can help individuals deal with difficult circumstances and the difficulties brought on by psychopathology, which may reduce the risk of suicide.

The likelihood of having suicidal ideation among female students was 2.3 times higher than among male students. This result was supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted in Africa among medical students, studies conducted in Ethiopia among the general population and among HIV/AIDS patients, and globally conducted among college students [59, 81, 95]. The possible reasons could be due to a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors [98, 99]. Hormonal fluctuations, particularly during puberty, and the menstrual cycle may influence mood regulation and increase vulnerability to suicidal ideation among females [98]. Certain mental health conditions associated with suicidal ideation, such as depression, anxiety, and eating disorders are common among females and this could be the reason for the association [100, 101]. Another justification for this result could be that women would probably suffer emotional harm since it is not socially acceptable for them to openly defend their rights. Not only that, but they could also discouraged from learning and become demotivated in their education [62, 101, 102].

Students who had a family history of suicide were more than 3.4 times more likely to have suicidal ideation as compared with students who had not such a history. The finding of this result was supported by studies conducted in Ethiopia among HIV/AIDS patients, and globally conducted studies among the general population [95, 103]. The possible explanations for this association might be a genetic predisposition, environmental influence, shared risk factors, modeling, and social learning could be the contributory factors [104–106].

The pooled likelihood of having suicidal ideation was nearly three (2.85) times more likely among students with anxiety disorders than students who hadn’t such sufferings. This result was supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted globally and a population-based longitudinal study conducted in the Netherlands [107, 108]. The distressing symptoms of anxiety, such as persistent fear, worry, and physical symptoms like trembling and palpitations, can contribute to feelings of desperation and hopelessness, which are associated factors for suicidal behavior [109–111]. Also, students with anxiety disorders suffer from daily feelings of panic or stress and might contemplate suicide as a way to end these recurring feelings.

The finding of this systematic review and meta-analysis study showed that depression had significant associations with suicidal ideation. The risk of having suicidal ideation among students with depression was 3.3 times higher than students without depression. The finding of this study was supported by previous globally conducted studies among medical students [112], Brazil among young university students [84], a systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted in Africa [94], in Ethiopia among HIV/AIDS patients [95] and a study conducted in Africa among medical students [81]. The possible justification for this association could be, the presence of depressive symptoms such as persistent hopelessness, sadness, and worthlessness can contribute to feelings of desperation and despair increasing vulnerability to suicidal ideation [33, 112, 113].

Disappointments with school performance were significantly associated with suicidal ideation in this systematic review and meta-analysis study. The pooled odds of having suicidal ideation among students with disappointments with their school performance was 2.4 times higher than students without such experiences. This result is also supported by studies conducted in Sweden with 10,081 individuals aged from 18 to 29 [34] and studies conducted among high school students in Mexico [114]. The possible explanations for this association could be, that, adolescents and young adults may feel that their lives are worthless and turn to suicide as a means of escape when they are unable to live up to the expectations of their parents and society for positive outcomes [115, 116].

Suicidal attempts

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that the pooled prevalence of suicidal attempts among students in Ethiopia was 8.99% (95% CI: 6.57, 11.43).This finding aligns with a study of African medical students, which reported a pooled prevalence of suicide attempts at 6.59% [81], Iran medical students 8% [117] and Ethiopian high school adolescents 10% [1]. It is also consistent with a global systematic review and meta-analysis, which found a prevalence range of 4.6% to 15.8% [82]. A scoping review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses found that the prevalence of suicide attempts among medical students ranged from 1.64% to 8%, which aligns with the findings of the this systematic review and meta-analysis.

However, the pooled prevalence of suicidal attempts in this study was higher as compared to global prevalence among college students (3.2%), estimates from the Ethiopian general population (4.0%), and a European community-based meta-analysis (6.0%) [11, 87, 118]. The relatively higher prevalence of suicide attempts found in this review may reflect the combined effects of financial hardship, poor mental health service utilization, academic pressure, and lack of proper psychological care among students in low- and middle-income settings such as Ethiopia [119, 120].

But also, the result of this study was relatively lower than a study conducted the Global School-Based Health Survey (GSHS) across 46 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)12.7% [44]. The possible reasons for this discrepancy might be, due to differences in study populations, sociocultural and economic contexts, mental health service accessibility, methodological variations, and the relatively greater psychosocial and academic burdens experienced among Ethiopian students [27, 121].

This systematic review and meta-analysis study revealed that poor social support, depression, family history of suicide, and disappointment with school performance were the factors that were significantly associated with suicidal attempts.

The pooled likelihood of having history of suicidal attempt among students with poor social support was 2.4 times higher than students who had strong social support. This finding was supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted in Africa among medical students and studies conducted in Ethiopia among general population and among HIV/AIDS patients, and globally conducted among college students [59, 81, 95]. One possible explanation for this could be that social support fosters a sense of belonging, which lowers the likelihood of suicide attempts. On the other hand, students who experience loneliness may feel powerless or helplessness and view death as a means of escape [40].

The risk of suicidal attempt among students with depression was four times higher as compared with students who had no depression. This result was supported by globally conducted study among medical students [112], Brazil among young university students [84], a systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted in Africa [94], in Ethiopia among HIV/AIDS patients [95] and a study conducted in Africa among medical students [81]. The reason behind this may be that the direct effects of depression, which raise the probability of suicide thoughts by making people feel worthless and hopeless [40, 122].

This systematic review and meta-analysis study revealed that, students who had family history of suicide has more than four (4.13) time high risk of suicidal attempt as compared with students who hadn’t such history. This association was supported by previous studies [95, 103]. The possible reasons could be similar that explained in the suicidal ideation section. Other reasons might be that mental health problems, such as depression, are inherent, which may increase the risk of suicidal ideation on students [123].

The risk of suicidal attempt was 2.6 times higher among students who had disappointments with their school performance. This might be, due to when they are unable to live up to the expectations of society and their parents for successful outcomes, adolescents and young adults may feel as though their lives are meaningless and resort to suicide as a way out [124, 125].

Strength and limitation of the study

This review has several methodological strengths. First, the use of a predefined search strategy across the specified databases helped minimize assessor bias. Second, two reviewers independently extracted the data and assessed the quality of the included studies, which further reduced the potential for bias. Third, the application of sensitivity, subgroup and meta-regression analyses to explore sources of heterogeneity added analytical rigor.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The synthesis is based exclusively on observational cross-sectional studies, which, while appropriate for estimating prevalence, do not allow for causal inferences regarding the relationships between the identified factors, suicidal ideation, and suicidal attempts. The restriction to English-language articles may have introduced selection bias by omitting relevant studies published in other languages. Additionally, the pooled prevalence estimates may be subject to overestimation bias due to the higher likelihood of publication for studies reporting significant or positive findings. Finally, substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies and should be taken into account when interpreting the pooled estimates. In addition, we fully acknowledge that studies assessed lifetime prevalence particularly susceptible to recall bias, as participants may forget or inaccurately recall past suicidal thoughts and also, use of non-standard assessment tools for primary studies might affect the effect size.

Conclusion and recommendations

According to this systematic review and meta-analysis study, the pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempts among students in Ethiopia is relatively high. The study also identified that poor social support, depression, family history of suicide, and disappointment with their school performance were the factors significantly associated with both suicidal ideation and attempt. However being female and anxiety disorders were significantly associated with suicidal ideation alone. Based on the findings of this study we recommended that students should be screened and treated for suicidal ideation and attempts. Also, special attention should be given to students with a family history of suicide, depression, anxiety, poor social support, disappointed students with their school performance, and for female students, to prevent suicidal behaviors.
