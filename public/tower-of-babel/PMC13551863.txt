Integrated analysis of IGHV rearrangements and cytogenetic abnormalities in a large chronic lymphocytic leukemia cohort (CLL-POL1)
Authors: Paulina Własiuk, Monika Szelest, Magdalena Paziewska, Agnieszka Karczmarczyk, Sylwia Czekalska, Magdalena Zawada, Malwina Hut, Oskar Przybyszewski, Michał Soin, Marzena Wojtaszewska, Monika Pępek, Agnieszka Kwak, Małgorzata Pokrywka, Piotr Wójcik, Aleksandra Leszczyńska, Justyna Bukowicz, Wiktoria Szyca, Ewelina Nowak-Ozimek, Artur Kowalik, Katarzyna Borg, Hanna Makuch-Łasica, Magdalena Wojtas, Iwona Solarska, Emilia Jaskuła, Anna Sobczyńska-Konefał, Klaudia Paruzel, Agnieszka Chudy, Ewa Wawrzyniak, Tomasz Stokłosa, Krzysztof Giannopoulos

ABSTRACT
The somatic hypermutation (SHM) status of immunoglobulin heavy-chain variable region gene (IGHV) is a well-established prognostic factor in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). We analyzed clonotypic IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ rearrangements in 4,251 newly diagnosed CLL patients in Poland (CLL-POL1), representing the largest national cohort to date. In total, 4,328 productive rearrangements were evaluated, of which 93% represented single clonal rearrangements. Additionally, double productive clonal rearrangements were identified in 76 cases (1.8%), of which 27 (35.5%) displayed discordant IGHV SHM status. Stereotyped B-cell receptor subsets were assigned to 10.6% of sequences, predominantly enriched in unmutated CLL (U-CLL) compared to mutated CLL (M-CLL) (72.8% vs. 22%; p < 0.001). High-risk subsets (#1, #2, #5, #6, #59, #99) accounted for over a half of all stereotyped cases and were enriched in patients with TP53 mutation. Most stereotyped subsets showed preferential or exclusive usage of specific IGHV genes. Integration of cytogenetic data revealed a strong enrichment of del(17p), del(11q), and trisomy 12 within U-CLL, whereas M-CLL was characterized by higher frequency of isolated del(13q). In summary, this study highlights distinct IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ pairing preferences and the heterogenous distribution of stereotyped subsets, revealing biologically meaningful associations with TP53 mutation and cytogenetic profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16382-8.

Introduction

Long-term observation emphasizes the key role of the B cell receptor immunoglobulin (BCR IG) in pathogenesis of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) [1–3]. Indeed, BCR IG-mediated antigen stimulation promotes leukemic cell proliferation and survival [3]. Comprehensive analyses of immunogenetic data collected from CLL patients indicate that the clonotypic BCR IG undergoes selection through recognition of specific (auto)antigens, highlighting its active role in shaping the leukemic clone [4]. This supports the concept that CLL evolution is functionally regulated and dynamic, rather than the results of random, stochastic processes. The clinical relevance of BCR signaling is further underscored by the success of therapeutic agents targeting downstream effectors of this pathway, such as ibrutinib, which demonstrate significant therapeutic efficacy even in relapsed or refractory disease [5, 6].

Importantly, CLL is characterized by a restricted IG gene repertoire within the clonotypic BCR, as well as by distinct status of somatic hypermutation (SHM) in rearranged IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ genes. Of note, IGHV SHM status is a well-established and clinically relevant prognostic marker in CLL, allowing stratification of patients into biologically and clinically distinct subgroups [1]. Nevertheless, stereotyped BCRs, present in ~ 40% of CLL cases, exhibit quasi-identical variable heavy complementarity determining region 3 (VH CDR3) and biased IGHV gene usage, indicating the presence of distinct immunogenetic groups with common pathogenic mechanisms and clinical implications [2]. Notably, evidence suggests that CLL patients with similar SHM status but classified into different stereotyped subsets subgroups might demonstrate different, a subset-specific biological features and clinical outcome [2, 7].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to comprehensively characterize the IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ gene rearrangements and SHM status in a large cohort of 4,257 CLL patients from multiple centers across Poland. By integrating immunogenetic profiling with data on BCR stereotypy, TP53 mutation status, and CLL-specific cytogenetic alterations, such as del(17p), del(11q), trisomy 12, and del(13q), we sought to provide new insights into the immunogenetic architecture of CLL.

Materials and methods

Patient group

The study included 4,251 newly diagnosed and previously untreated CLL patients from 11 collaborating institutes across Poland who underwent IGHV gene SHM status assessment between November 2022 and May 2025. Detailed information regarding the contribution of each Laboratory and methods used is presented in Supplementary Table S1. All cases were diagnosed according to the International Workshop on Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia criteria [8]. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical characteristics including gender, age, TP53 mutation status and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) results for del(17p), del(11q), trisomy 12, and del(13q) were collected for correlation analysis. Both clinical and genetic data evaluated in this study were obtained as part of routine diagnostic assessment for CLL patients.

Sequence analysis of IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ genes using Sanger sequencing

Amplification of IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ gene rearrangements was performed with the use of leader primers following ERIC guidelines [9]. In problematic cases, framework region (FR1) primers from BIOMED-2 protocol [10] were used to amplify IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ sequences. Following heteroduplex analysis, PCR products were excised from agarose gel, purified, and subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing. In cases with suspected multiple IGH rearrangements, additional analyses were performed to resolve individual clonotypes. Specifically, when distinct PCR products were visible following amplification with leader primers, bands were excised separately and sequenced according to the ERIC protocol. If direct sequencing revealed overlapping chromatograms, additional BIOMED-2 (FR1) family-specific PCR assays were performed, followed by targeted amplification of the original DNA sample using the corresponding VH leader primer and JH-FR1 primer to obtain the complete IGHV sequence.

Sequence analysis of IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ genes using NGS

IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ libraries were prepared using either TAG-CLL protocol [11] or commercial NGS assays, including the LymphoTrack IGHV Leader Somatic Hypermutation Assay Panel (Invivoscribe, San Diego, CA, USA) and Oncomine BCR IGHV Leader-J Assay (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). PCR products were sequenced on a MiSeq Sequencing System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) or Ion Torrent (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) platforms. Paired reads were directly analysed with MIXCR v4.2 software (TAG-CLL) or dedicated manufacturer’s software (LymphoTrack and Oncomine). In selected cases (n = 31), complementary NGS analysis was performed when Sanger sequencing did not allow unambiguous identification of productive IGHV rearrangements. This included cases with complex rearrangement patterns, such as the presence of more than one productive IGHV rearrangement that could not be reliably resolved using Sanger sequencing. The additional NGS analysis enabled accurate assignment of IGHV rearrangements and assessment of SHM status.

IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ rearrangements annotation and statistical analysis

Sequence data were annotated with the IMGT/V-QUEST tool [12]. Subset analysis was performed using ARRest/AssignSubsets software [13]. Descriptive analysis of categorical variables was performed using absolute and relative frequencies. Associations between variables were evaluated using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v. 4.4.3). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A productive clonal IGHV rearrangement was successfully detected in all analyzed cases. Most patients (3,954; 93%) harbored a single productive rearrangement, whereas multiple clonal rearrangements were observed in 297 cases (7%), including 221 samples with one productive and one unproductive rearrangement, 75 samples with double productive rearrangements, and one sample with three distinct productive IGH rearrangements.

Among cases with a single productive rearrangement, 2,104 (53.2%) were classified as U-CLL (GI ≥ 98%) and 1,850 (46.8%) as M-CLL (GI < 98%). A total of 230 cases (12.4% of the M-CLL group) exhibited a borderline GI value of 97.00-97.99% and were analyzed as a separate category (Supplementary Table S2). Of the U-CLL cases, 1566 (74.4%) displayed 100% GI.

Double positive rearrangements with discordant IGHV gene SHM status were found in 27 cases (0.6%).

IGH gene repertoires

Analysis of IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ gene rearrangements revealed clear preferences in IGHD and IGHJ gene pairing for several IGHV segments. A particularly notable preference was observed for IGHV1-3, which most frequently rearranged with IGHD6-19 (42 cases, 46.2%). Similarly to previously published data [14], rearrangements involving the IGHV1-69 and IGHV1-2 showed a marked tendency to pair with IGHD3-3 (201 cases, 34.1%) and IGDH6-19 (44 cases, 23.4%), respectively. Furthermore, IGHV3-11 was commonly associated with both IGHD3-3 (34 cases, 27.2%) and IGHD3-10 (23 cases, 18.4%), while IGHV3-48 showed recurrent usage of IGHD3-3 (32 cases, 18.9%).

Distinct IGHV and IGHJ combinations were also found (Fig. 2A).

For instance, IGHV1-69 exhibited a strong association with IGHJ6, which accounted for 356 cases (60.4%), while IGHV3-21 gene showed even stronger bias toward IGHJ6, observed in 114 cases (66.3%). Moreover, combinations involving IGHV1-2 frequently utilized IGHJ4 (89 cases, 47.3%) and IGHJ6 (65 cases, 34.6%). A prominent preference for IGHJ4 was also observed in rearrangements with IGHV3-23 (145 cases, 63.6%) and IGHV3-30 (99 cases, 47.4%).

Conversely, IGHV4-34 and IGHV3-7 displayed more heterogeneous pattern of both IGHD and IGHJ gene usage, without a single dominant partner, suggesting less constrained recombination or broader antigen-driven selection.

To investigate whether cohort size influences the detection of stereotyped subsets, data from centers reporting variable numbers of samples were analyzed. Indeed, a strong positive correlation was observed between the number of samples per laboratory and the number of detected subsets (Spearman’s rho = 0.95, p < 0.0001). Moreover, linear regression confirmed that larger cohorts tended to yield a higher number of identified subsets (R2 = 0.85, p < 0.0001).

Among the identified subsets, subset #1 was the most prevalent (108/449, 24%), followed by subset #2 (92/449, 20.5%), and subset #6 (35/449, 7.8%). Stereotyped B-cell receptor subsets were significantly more frequent in U-CLL (327/449, 72.8%) compared to M-CLL (99/449, 22%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). Notably, aggressive subsets, including #1, #2, #3, #5, #6, #7H, #8, #31, #59, and #99, were predominantly associated with U-CLL (284/347, 81.8%, p < 0.0001).

However, in the group of patients with assigned subsets, subset #2 was significantly more prevalent among M-CLL cases compared to U-CLL cases (40.4% vs. 9.8%, < 0.001). Of note, 8.6% (21/244) of cases with borderline SHM status were classified into stereotyped subsets, with subset #2 accounting for 90.5% (19/21) of these assignments. All samples assigned to subset #4 (n = 25) were identified as M-CLL, with one showing borderline SHM status.

Most stereotyped subsets (14/19, 73.7%) in our cohort showed predominant or exclusive usage of a single IGHV gene (Fig. 2C), consistent with their established immunogenetic characteristics. Several subsets displayed uniform SHM status, including subsets #3, #5, #6, #7H, #8, #12, #28A, #59, #99, and #202 (U-CLL) as well as subsets #14, #77, and #201 (M-CLL).

IGH rearrangements in the context of TP53 mutation

The majority of samples examined had no mutations within TP53 (87.1%, 821/942). Nevertheless, the frequency of TP53 mutations was significantly higher in U-CLL compared to M-CLL (p = 0.0044). Among samples harboring TP53 mutations, the most commonly utilized IGHV gene was IGHV3 (37.2%, 45/121), followed by IGHV1 (30.6%, 37/121), of which 22 cases (59.4%) involved IGHV1-69 gene. The IGHV2 and IGHV5 gene usage was observed less frequently (2.5% and 4.9%, respectively) and none of TP53-mutated cases expressed IGHV6 or IGHV7 genes.

Overall, TP53 mutations were found in 3.3% (15/449) of samples with stereotyped BCR sequences. Among them, 13 (86.7%) belonged to stereotyped subsets previously associated with aggressive clinical behavior (e.g. subsets #1, #2, #5, #6, #99). There was a tendency toward a higher proportion of TP53-mutated cases in two subsets sharing a high degree of immunogenetic similarity – subsets #1 and #99 – compared to other subsets (p = 0.059). When comparing subsets #1 and #99 to unassigned cases, a significant association was detected (p = 0.032), suggesting an enrichment of TP53 mutations within these aggressive subsets.

IGH rearrangements and cytogenetic aberrations

When considering cytogenetic aberrations, the prevalences of del(17p), del(11q), and trisomy 12 were significantly higher in U-CLL compared to M-CLL. Detailed analysis showed that del(17p) was detected in 13.3% (110/827) of U-CLL cases compared to 6.9% (40/579) of M-CLL cases (p < 0.001). Similarly, del(11q) was significantly more common in U-CLL (25.8%, 184/712) than in M-CLL (4.1%, 21/513) (p < 0.0001). Cases with trisomy 12 also presented a higher frequency in U-CLL (19%, 107/563) than in M-CLL (9.9%, 42/425) (p < 0.0001). On the other hand, an uneven distribution of isolated del(13q) was observed between M-CLL and U-CLL subgroups with isolated del(13q) occurring more frequently in M-CLL cases (71.9%, 240/334) compared to U-CLL cases (55.6%, 150/270) (p < 0.001). With regard to stereotyped subset analysis, there was a tendency toward a higher prevalence of isolated del(13q) in subset #2 cases (82.4%, 14/17) compared to cases assigned to other stereotyped subsets (58.5%, 31/53) (p = 0.074).

VH gene repertoire

Consistent with previous reports [2], IGHV1-69 represented the most frequent gene in our cohort (Fig. 4).

Notably, an enrichment of IGHV4-34 was observed, along with differences in the relative contribution of less frequent IGHV genes compared to the published dataset [2]. Nevertheless, the association of stereotyped BCR with a restricted set of genes aligned with the abovementioned report [2].

Despite overall consistency in IGHV gene usage across laboratories, significant differences were detected for 21 genes (Supplementary Figure S1A). To better understand these observations, we assessed whether the differences in IGHV gene usage were associated with the applied amplification protocols or sequencing technologies. Notably, neither significant differences were found when comparing NGS with Sanger sequencing approach, nor between different NGS protocols (TAG CLL, LymphoTrack, Oncomine).

Interestingly, significant differences were detected for 17 V-genes among laboratories employing NGS. Twelve of these genes overlapped with the set of genes that showed significant inter-laboratory differences irrespective of sequencing technology, suggesting a reproducible effect. Five genes (IGHV2-5, IGHV5-10, IGHV3-43, IGHV3-20, IGHV1-58) were identified as significant only in NGS-specific analysis and not in the analysis including all laboratories, indicating that these discrepancies likely reflect differences in the patient cohorts rather than methodological biases (Supplementary Figure S1B). In line with this observation, a similar comparison among laboratories using Sanger sequencing revealed 21 significantly different V-genes, among which three (IGHV1-3, IGHV2-5, and IGHV4-30-4) were unique to the Sanger-specific analysis and were not identified as significant in the overall inter-laboratory comparison (Supplementary Figure S1C).

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the immunogenetic architecture and cytogenetic aberrations of CLL in a large, population-based cohort. By analyzing over 4,300 clonal rearrangements and integrating immunogenetic profiling with molecular and cytogenetic features, we identified distinct patterns of V-D-J gene pairing, and subset-specific enrichment of TP53 mutations. Our findings demonstrate that the immunogenetic and cytogenetic landscape of CLL is closely linked to the subset identity, with aggressive stereotyped subsets showing a significant enrichment for high-risk genetic features, including TP53 mutation and del(17p). These results suggest that subset-specific BCR features may contribute to the clonal composition of the disease, with unmutated IGHV genes being more frequent in samples with poor prognostic features, and aggressive subsets more commonly identified in TP53-mutated cases.

The proportion of CLL cases assigned to major stereotyped subsets in the present study (10.6%) is consistent with previously reported large-scale data [2], where 19 major subsets accounting for 13.5% of samples were identified. This finding supports the reproducibility of subset assignment across different cohorts and methodologies. While the ERIC-recommended ARResT/AssignSubsets tool used in diagnostic laboratories enables identification of 19 well-characterized major subsets, the study by Agathangelidis et al. [2] expanded the analysis to include 29 major subsets. The number of identified stereotyped subsets increased with cohort size, consistent with previous reports, which indicated that larger cohorts increase the likelihood of identifying rare subsets [15–17]. In line with previously reported data [2, 14, 18], stereotyped BCR IG rearrangements in the analyzed cohort were predominantly enriched in U-CLL cases and most of the assigned cases were classified into high-risk subsets.

Several major stereotyped subsets, including #1, #12, #59, and #77, were characterized by the preferential usage of two or more phylogenetically related IGHV genes and a uniform SHM status, suggesting functional similarities of the clonotypic BCRs. These findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating that subset assignment often reflects structural convergence of the BCR, particularly within the VH CDR3 region [2, 19, 20].

This indicates that subset assignment is primarily driven by shared conformational characteristics of the BCR, rather than strict IGHV gene identity. Such structural convergence likely reflects antigen-driven selection acting on conserved molecular motif, underscoring the functional relevance of BCR architecture as a determinant of clonal behavior in CLL. Indeed, Polychronidou et al. [19] reported that subset #1 cases – despite utilizing distinct but phylogenetically related IGHV genes – exhibited strikingly similar VH CDR3 structures. This concept is further supported by recent structural study demonstrating that homotypic BCR-BCR interactions, mediated by subgroup-specific epitopes, can initiate cell-autonomous signaling in leukemic B cells [20]. Interestingly, the strength and duration of these interactions appear to correlate with clinical behavior, with stronger and more stable contacts prevailing in indolent cases, and weaker, transient interactions observed in aggressive disease [20]. Overall, structural convergence of the BCR, shaped by antigenic selection, provides a compelling mechanistic framework linking receptor architecture with functional properties and clinical heterogeneity in CLL.

In line with previously published data [18], we found a significant enrichment of TP53 mutations within subsets #1 and #99, thus confirming the link between TP53 alterations and adverse disease biology in specific BCR stereotyped contexts. To date, distinct subset-specific mutational patterns have been described. For instance, subsets #1, #6, #8, and #59 exhibit high frequencies of NOTCH1 mutations (22–34%) often accompanied by trisomy 12, while subsets #2 and #3 show pronounced enrichment for SF3B1 mutations (~ 45%) but rare NOTCH1 alterations [18, 21–23].

Interestingly, the majority of subset #2 cases in our cohort exhibited mutated IGHV, and 17 of 19 borderline IGHV cases were also assigned to this subset, indicating underlying biological heterogeneity. The observed predominance of #2 in patients with isolated del(13q), together with previous reports of recurrent SF3B1 mutations in this subset [18, 21, 22], supports the hypothesis that distinct immunogenetic backgrounds may cooperate with specific genetic alterations to drive CLL pathogenesis through convergent mechanisms. Together, these observations point to a complex interplay between immunogenetic features and genetic alterations in subset #2. Although isolated del(13q) is typically associated with indolent disease, its co-occurrence with SF3B1 mutations in subset #2 may redefine its prognostic implications, reflecting a shift toward more aggressive clinical behavior. Recent study by Hägerstrand et al. [24] has shown that SF3B1 mutations in subset #2 drive specific alternative splicing events affecting the ncBAF chromatin remodeling complex, including a novel BRD9 isoform, with altered protein interactome, potentially reshaping the epigenetic landscape and transcriptional program of leukemic cells [24]. Together with aberrant activation of mTORC1 and MYC pathways observed in murine models harboring both Sf3b1-K700E and del(13q) [25], these findings suggest that dysregulated splicing in the context of a stereotyped BCR background and del(13q) may promote CLL progression through cooperative mechanisms.

Conclusions

The main strength of our study lies in the comprehensive, large-scale evaluation of IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ gene rearrangement patterns and BCR stereotypy in a real-world one of the largest CLL cohort to date. Despite the incomplete cytogenetic and TP53 mutation data, the observed associations highlight the potential of integrated immunogenetic profiling to reveal biologically and clinically relevant subgroups in CLL. Prospective studies with uniform molecular testing and clinical follow-up will be essential to validate these findings and to assess their prognostic significance in the context of current therapeutic strategies [26].
