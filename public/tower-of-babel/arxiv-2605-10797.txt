Muown: Row-Norm Control for Muon Optimization
Kai Lion 1 Florian Hübler 1,2 Bingcong Li 1 Antonio Orvieto 3 Niao He 1 1 Department of Computer Science, ETH Zurich, Switzerland 2 Department of Mathematics, School of Computation, Information and Technology; Technical University of Munich, Germany 3 ELLIS Institute Tübingen, MPI-IS, Tübingen AI Center, Germany {kai.lion,florian.huebler,bingcong.li,niao.he}@inf.ethz.ch antonio@tue.ellis.eu

Abstract
Muon has emerged as a strong competitor to AdamW for language model pre-training, yet its behavior at scale is sensitive to weight decay. Recent work has observed that, for Muon without decoupled weight decay, the spectral norm of weight matrices drifts upward over training. Through a decomposition of the spectral norm into a row-magnitude factor and a row-coherence factor, we identify the former as the empirical driver of this drift under Muon, while the latter remains well-behaved along the trajectory. Motivated by this diagnosis, we introduce Muown, a drop-in replacement for Muon that treats the row-magnitude vector as an explicit optimizer variable, updating it under the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} geometry induced by the decomposition, while applying Muon unchanged to the remaining direction component. We prove that Muown attains the optimal non-convex rates in both deterministic and stochastic regimes under a dual norm aligned with the underlying geometries and with a stochastic noise coefficient that empirically remains below that of Muon throughout training. Across GPT-style pre-training on FineWeb-Edu with model sizes from 124M up to 2.7B parameters, Muown improves perplexity over Muon, SOAP, AdamW, and Lion. It also widens the plateau of near-optimal learning rates across model scales, reduces sensitivity to weight decay, and avoids the spectral norm drift at negligible step-time overhead when appropriately sharded.

Introduction

Moving beyond the diagonal conditioning inherent to Adam and its variants
(
26
;
31
)
, an emerging class of optimizers is engineered to respect the matrix-shaped structure of the linear layers of the Transformer architecture. Notable examples include Shampoo
(
19
)
, SOAP
(
46
)
, Scion
(
38
)
and Muon
(
23
)
. In particular, Muon has been motivated through the perspective of steepest spectral descent
(
13
)
, which computes the update direction as the solution to the optimization problem
\arg\min_{\Delta\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\left\langle\mathbf{G},\Delta\mathbf{W}\right\rangle\text{ s.t. }\left\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}\leq 1
where
\mathbf{G}
is the matrix-shaped gradient of the loss with respect to
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
is the spectral (Schatten-
\infty
) norm.

Despite the encouraging empirical success of Muon, it has been observed that, at scale, its formulation can suffer from numerical overflows in bfloat16
(
30
)
and exploding attention logits
(
25
)
. At the same time, the spectral norm of weight matrices has been documented to grow consistently throughout training under Muon without weight decay
(
38
, Fig. 9)
, violating the
spectral condition
for stable feature learning
(
49
)
, which prescribes
\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}=\Theta(\sqrt{m/n})
and
\left\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}=\Theta(\sqrt{m/n})
. This drift away from the stable feature-learning regime could potentially be linked to the pathologies mentioned above.

A standard remedy is decoupled weight decay
(
14
;
15
;
38
)
, which keeps
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
in check by shrinking the weight matrix uniformly at every step. This intervention is non-targeted, scaling the entire spectrum uniformly, irrespective of which directions are responsible for spectral-norm growth, and has been observed to slow convergence in the early phase of training
(
30
, Fig. 2)
,
(
38
, Fig. 8)
. We introduce a complementary perspective: rather than constraining the spectrum as a whole, we identify systematic row-norm growth as the empirical mechanism driving the spectral-norm drift (Fig.
1(a)
,
1(b)
) and control it by reparameterizing the weight so that the row scales become explicitly trainable. To remain agnostic to the model architecture, this reparameterization is realized implicitly inside the optimizer, leaving the forward pass unchanged. The resulting procedure achieves consistent gains in perplexity across all tested model scales and learning rates, at a minimal step-time overhead compared to Muon.

1.1
Contribution

•

We decompose the spectral norm into a row-magnitude factor and a row-coherence factor (Proposition
1
), which singles out the row magnitude as the component whose evolution tracks the spectral norm under Muon. Building on this perspective, we propose Muown, a drop-in replacement for Muon that promotes the row magnitudes to an explicit optimizer variable, while Muon is applied unchanged to the directional component.

•

Muown retains the optimal non-convex rates of Muon in both deterministic and stochastic settings (Theorems
1
,
2
), but established in the mixed dual norm matching the two update geometries. The result supports the empirical improvements of Muown through an improved noise term.

•

On FineWeb-Edu pre-training from 124M to 2.7B parameters, Muown consistently improves perplexity over Muon, SOAP, AdamW, and Lion, maintaining a consistent perplexity advantage over the best-tuned Muon baseline. Further, it broadens the plateau of near-optimal learning rates across model widths (Fig.
5
), and alleviates the need for weight-decay tuning (Fig.
4(b)
). The method adds only minor step-time overhead once sharded (Table
3
), matching Muon’s runtime in practical scenarios.

Related Work

Shaping Gradient Spectra.
Pre-conditioning is a central tool in optimization for machine learning
(
11
)
: while early approaches focus on efficient use of the Hessian or the Fisher Information
(
33
)
, contemporary methods directly operate on gradients, taking their matrix structure into account. An early notable work in gradient-based structure-aware optimizers is Shampoo
(
19
)
. Rather than applying pre-conditioning on flattened, structureless parameter vectors, Shampoo employs two matrix-shaped pre-conditioners. An idealized variant of Shampoo can be cast as performing steepest spectral descent under the spectral norm, effectively setting the singular values of the parameter update matrix to unity
(
7
)
. SOAP
(
46
)
optimizes singular values adaptively with Adam. Most prominently, Muon
(
23
;
45
)
shapes the spectrum through orthogonalization of the momentum state, approximating the normalized steepest descent direction
(
12
)
under a spectral norm constraint
(
13
)
.

Operator Norm Perspective.
This viewpoint is formalized by the operator-norm framework of metrized deep learning
(
28
;
8
)
, where each layer is equipped with a geometry derived from its input-output semantics, and gradients, treated as dual vectors, are transported back to the primal weight space by a layerwise duality map
(
35
;
5
)
. For MLP layers under a rescaled spectral norm, this map recovers Muon’s orthogonalization
(
7
;
23
)
. Both sides of the spectral condition then live in this layerwise operator norm: the size prescriptions on
\mathbf{W}
and
\Delta\mathbf{W}
, as well as the steepest-descent rule that produces compatible updates. Steepest descent, however, only bounds
\left\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
per step, leaving the control of
\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
along the trajectory as a separate problem.

Spectral Control.
Existing mechanisms for keeping
\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
bounded during training fall into three broad families.
Decoupled weight decay
shrinks
\mathbf{W}
uniformly at every step.
14
cast Muon with decoupled weight decay as a member of the Lion-
\mathcal{K}
family
(
15
)
whose trajectories converge to KKT points of a spectral-norm-constrained problem, with related Frank–Wolfe interpretations developed in
(
41
;
38
)
.
Spectral reparameterizations
hardwire the singular spectrum of
\mathbf{W}
, e.g. as orthogonal under Parseval/Stiefel parameterizations
(
17
)
.
Spectral-norm caps
act on the top singular value via soft penalties
(
50
)
, post-hoc normalization
(
34
)
, or odd-polynomial clipping
(
37
)
. All three families act on the singular spectrum of
\mathbf{W}
as an aggregate quantity, leaving open which component of
\mathbf{W}

empirically
drives spectral-norm growth under Muon.

Our work answers this open question at the parameterization level. We identify the maximal row magnitude
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
as the empirical driver of
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
drift under Muon (Section
3.1
) and reparameterize
\mathbf{W}
so that this driver is promoted to an explicit optimizer variable, updated under its natural
\ell_{\infty}
geometry. Spectral-norm control thus emerges from the construction of the parameterization rather than from an aggregate constraint on
\mathbf{W}
, avoiding both the indiscriminate shrinkage of decoupled weight decay and the orthogonality prior of spectral reparameterizations.

3
Method

Notation.

Let
\mathbf{W}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
denote the current iterate of a weight matrix of a linear layer with
m
and
n
output and input neurons, respectively, and
\mathbf{G}_{t}=\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
its corresponding gradient at iteration
t
with respect to a scalar loss
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
. Division by vectors is elementwise. We write
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for the vector of diagonal entries of a square matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
, and
\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{a})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
for the diagonal matrix with diagonal given by
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. For
p\in[1,\infty]
,
\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|_{p}
denotes the
\ell_{p}
-norm of a vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{S_{p}}
the Schatten-
p
norm of a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, i.e. the
\ell_{p}
-norm of its singular values
\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{\min(m,n)}(\mathbf{A})\geq 0
. In particular
\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
is the spectral norm and
\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{S_{1}}
the nuclear norm. The canonical (Frobenius) inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is
\langle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\rangle:=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{B})
with induced norm
\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}
, and
\langle a,b\rangle=a^{\top}b
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We write
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{M})
for the largest eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{M}
. With slight abuse of notation, we denote the row-norm vector
\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}=(\left\|\mathbf{A}_{1,:}\right\|_{2},\dots,\left\|\mathbf{A}_{m,:}\right\|_{2})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we define the projection which removes the per-row radial component with respect to
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
as
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{A}):=\mathbf{A}-\mathrm{Diag}\!\big(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\big)\,\mathbf{X}.
In other words, for each row
\mathbf{a}_{i}
,
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{A})
subtracts
\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

3.1
Motivation

Row Norm Drift.
The constrained optimization and metrized-deep-learning arguments in Section
2
single out
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
as the quantity that governs stability of feature propagation,
yet steepest spectral descent only bounds
\left\|\Delta\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
per step. Before prescribing a separate control mechanism for
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
itself, we ask a more basic question:
which component of
\mathbf{W}_{t}
is responsible for the observed spectral-norm drift under Muon?
The following decomposition isolates two candidate mechanisms.

Proposition 1

(Spectral Norm Decomposition into Row Magnitude and Coherence)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and suppose each row of
\mathbf{W}_{t}
is nonzero. Define the row-magnitude vector
\mathbf{g}_{t}:=\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and the row-normalized matrix
\mathbf{D}_{t}:=\mathrm{Diag}\!\left(1/\mathbf{g}_{t}\right)\mathbf{W}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

so that
\mathbf{W}_{t}=\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\mathbf{D}_{t}.

Let
\mathbf{C}_{t}:=\mathbf{D}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{t}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

be the Gram matrix of the unit-norm rows, and define
\mathbf{p}_{t}:=|\mathbf{g}_{t}|/\left\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\in[0,1]^{m}
with
\mathbf{P}_{t}:=\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{p}_{t}).

Then the spectral norm of
\mathbf{W}_{t}
admits the decomposition

\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\lambda_{\max}\!\bigl(\mathbf{P}_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathbf{P}_{t}\bigr).

In particular, the spectral norm splits into a row-scale term
\left\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
and a normalized alignment term
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{P}_{t})
.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
B.1
. We remark that the two factors are
disjoint
by design: any positive uniform row-rescaling of

\mathbf{W}_{t}
moves only
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
, while any directional change to the rows
moves only
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{P}_{t})
. The alignment factor
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{P}_{t})\geq 1
measures coherence of the unit-norm neurons in
\mathbf{D}_{t}
, weighted by the normalized row-scale profile
\mathbf{p}_{t}
. It collapses to
1
if and only if the (non-negligible) rows are orthonormal, in which case
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}=\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
and the layer’s operator norm is determined entirely by its maximal row scale.

Row Norm Control.
Across the attention and MLP linear layers of a 500M model trained with Muon, we find that
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
and
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
move in lock-step throughout training (Fig.
1(a)
,
1(b)
; Fig.
10
,
11
), whereas
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{P}_{t})
only exhibits a brief transient rise early in training, remaining bounded thereafter (Fig.
1(c)
,
1(d)
; Fig.
12
). A simple causal intervention confirms that the row scales drive the spectral norm drift: freezing
\mathbf{g}_{t}=\mathbf{g}_{1}
after initialization, a minimal intervention that touches only the row-scale factor, eliminates the systematic growth of
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
(Figs.
1
,
10
).

With
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
identified as the empirical driver of
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
under Muon, we respond by promoting it from a byproduct of the
\mathbf{W}
-update to a trainable variable, optimized under its own geometry. Existing spectral-control schemes instead intervene at coarser granularities of Proposition
1
. Trainable row magnitudes, by contrast, act only on the identified driver
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
, without the orthogonality prior of reparameterizations and without the indiscriminate shrinkage of weight decay.

3.2
Muown

To turn
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
into a controllable variable, we reparameterize each linear layer as
\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})=\Diag(\mathbf{g}/\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}})\mathbf{R}
with
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, i.e. weight normalization
(
40
)
, under which
\mathbf{g}
coincides exactly with the row-magnitude vector of Proposition
1
. To remain agnostic to the model implementation, we realize the reparameterization
implicitly inside the optimizer
:
\mathbf{g}
is held as an additional optimizer state,
\mathbf{R}
is reconstructed from the stored effective weight
\mathbf{W}
on each step, and the forward pass is left unchanged. The resulting procedure (Algorithm
1
, Fig.
2
) is therefore a drop-in replacement for Muon, which decomposes each layer into a direction
\mathbf{R}
, already metrized by the spectral norm, and a diagonal per-neuron gain
\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g})
.

It remains to choose a geometry for
\mathbf{g}
. Composability in the modular-duality framework
(
8
, Def. 6)
requires that the output space of
\mathbf{R}
and the input space of
\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g})
carry the same norm, so the natural metric on
\mathbf{g}
is the induced operator norm
\left\|\mathrm{Diag}(\Delta\mathbf{g})\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
. Since the operator norm of a diagonal matrix equals the largest entry in absolute value, this yields
\left\|\mathrm{Diag}(\Delta\mathbf{g})\right\|_{S_{\infty}}=\|\Delta\mathbf{g}\|_{\infty}
, i.e., the
\ell_{\infty}
geometry on
\mathbf{g}
. This is independently confirmed by Proposition
1
, which identifies
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
as the row-scale factor of
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
.

Steepest descent under
\ell_{\infty}
is SignSGD
(
9
)
, with the entrywise sign as the duality map
(
8
, Ex. 2)
. Its update
\Delta\mathbf{g}=-\eta\,\operatorname{sgn}(\nabla_{\mathbf{g}})
satisfies
\|\Delta\mathbf{g}\|_{\infty}=\eta
by construction, so the row-scale contribution
\left\|\mathrm{Diag}(\Delta\mathbf{g})\right\|_{S_{\infty}}=\|\Delta\mathbf{g}\|_{\infty}
to
\left\|\Delta\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}
is bounded at every step. In practice, SignSGD is the stateless analogue of Adam with both EMAs disabled
(
4
;
47
)
, and AdamW has been shown to converge to KKT points of an
\ell_{\infty}
-constrained problem
(
47
)
. We therefore adopt Adam as the magnitude optimizer in Algorithm
1
, as it inherits the
\ell_{\infty}
geometry while adding momentum-based smoothing against stochastic-gradient noise.

We initialize
\mathbf{R}_{1}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}_{1}
and cached row norms and magnitudes as
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{g}_{1}\leftarrow\left\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}
, so that
\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{1})=\mathbf{W}_{1}
, making Muown start from exactly the same point as Muon under any non-zero initialization scheme. All remaining optimizer states are zero. Following
30
, we scale the
\mathbf{R}
-step by
0.2\sqrt{\max(m,n)}
to match the RMS norm of a typical Adam update, so that a single learning rate
\eta_{t}
drives both the direction and magnitude updates and no separate learning rate for
\mathbf{g}
is introduced. The resulting algorithm can be found in Algorithm
1
.

Setup and Geometry.
Working in the re-parameterized variables
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{\text{row}>0}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{\text{row}>0}
denotes the set of
m\times n
matrices with strictly positive row norms, we consider
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{\text{row}>0}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}):=\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{W}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right)\right)=\mathcal{L}\!\left(\Diag\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}}\right)\,\mathbf{R}\right)
. Since the
\mathbf{g}
- and
\mathbf{R}
-updates take unit-sized steps in the
\ell_{\infty}
- and spectral norms respectively, we equip the joint variable with the product max-norm
\left\|(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})\right\|=\max\{\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}\}
and the canonical inner product
\langle(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}),(\mathbf{g}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}^{\prime})\rangle:=\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\rangle
. The corresponding dual norm is
\left\|(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})\right\|_{*}=\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{S_{1}}
, combining the
\ell_{1}
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the Schatten-
1
(nuclear) norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Each summand is the dual of the norm in which the corresponding update is bounded, so the product norm simply lifts the modular-duality viewpoint of Section
2
to the joint variable
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})
.

Stochastic Convergence.
We consider the objective
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W};\xi)]
with access only to stochastic gradients
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W},\xi)
, and use the re-parametrized gradient oracles
\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t},\xi_{t})=\mathrm{diag}(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}),\xi_{t})\mathbf{D}_{t}^{\top})
and
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t},\xi_{t})=\Diag\left(\frac{\mathbf{g}_{t}}{\left\|\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}}\right)\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{D}_{t}}(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}),\xi_{t}))
, where
\mathbf{D}_{t}=\Diag(1/\left\|\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}})\mathbf{R}_{t}
is the row-normalization of
\mathbf{R}_{t}
.

1
). Denote
\Delta_{1}\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\star}
and the norm equivalence constants
\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}:=\max_{\mathbf{g}}\nicefrac{{\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{1}}}{{\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{2}}},\zeta_{\mathbf{R}}:=\max_{\mathbf{R}}\nicefrac{{\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{S_{1}}}}{{\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}}}
. Then the iterates of Algorithm
2
with

\eta_{t}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{1}(1-\beta_{1})}{LT}}\text{ and }\beta_{1}=1-\min\left\{1,\max\left\{T^{-\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}},\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{1}L}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}T}}\right\}\right\},

where
\hat{\sigma}:=\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\sigma_{\mathbf{g}}+\zeta_{\mathbf{R}}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}
, satisfy

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{*}\right]\leq 6\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{1}L}{T}}+8\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}L\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{T}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}+\frac{4\hat{\sigma}}{T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}}

which matches the optimal
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}L\sigma^{2}}{T}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}\right)
non-convex rate up to constants.

The guarantee is stated for the dual norm of the re-parameterized gradient, and the dominant term matches the optimal stochastic non-convex rate up to constants
(
3
)
, mirroring the rate established for Muon by
(
43

. Moreover, since
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}\geq\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{S_{1}}\geq\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\ell_{2}
/Frobenius counterpart. We caution that the bound concerns
\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. Although
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})
pointwise,
\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
and
\nabla\mathcal{L}
need not be directly comparable. The only algorithm-dependent factor is the noise coefficient
\hat{\sigma}=\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\sigma_{\mathbf{g}}+\zeta_{\mathbf{R}}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}
, where the norm-equivalence constants
\zeta_{\mathbf{g}},\zeta_{\mathbf{R}}
arise, as in recent Muon analyses
(
43

\ell_{1}/\text{Schatten-}1
from
\ell_{2}/\text{Frobenius}
variance bounds. In comparison, the corresponding factor for Muon is
\zeta_{\mathbf{W}}\sigma_{\mathbf{W}}
, hence our theory predicts an improvement of Muown over Muon whenever
\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\sigma_{\mathbf{g}}+\zeta_{\mathbf{R}}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}<\zeta_{\mathbf{W}}\sigma_{\mathbf{W}}

1.6\times

-parameterization as a candidate mechanism for explaining the observed perplexity gains.

In the proof, we first use our choice of geometry to split the descent Lemma into independent
\mathbf{g}
- and
\mathbf{R}

We show that the spectral-norm drift observed under Muon is not an irreducible property of the optimizer. Instead, the decomposition of Proposition
1
isolates the maximal row magnitude
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\infty}
as its empirical driver, while the row-coherence factor remains stable along the trajectory. We propose Muown, which acts on this driver at the parameterization level, lifting
\mathbf{g}
to an optimizer variable updated under the
\ell_{\infty}
geometry singled out by modular duality and leaving Muon unchanged on the direction. We provide evidence that this intervention can improve perplexity, widen the plateau of near-optimal learning rates across model widths, and alleviate the need to tune weight decay. These gains come at a memory cost linear in the number of rows and negligible step-time overhead once the optimizer is sharded across ranks.

Limitations and Future Work.
The decomposition
\mathbf{W}=\Diag(\mathbf{g}/\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}})\mathbf{R}
is undefined when a row of
\mathbf{W}
is exactly zero at initialization, making the direction unidentifiable. Architectures that depend on zero-initialized weight matrices, such as zero-initialized residual output projections or some LoRA adapters, therefore require either a small non-zero initialization or an opt-out from the reparameterization for the affected layers. Our empirical study is also confined to dense GPT-style architectures and pre-training horizons up to 53B tokens. The behavior on convolutional, mixture-of-experts, and substantially longer-horizon regimes remains open. A first natural direction for future work is to vary the geometry imposed on
\mathbf{g}
. Replacing
\ell_{\infty}
with sparsity-inducing
\ell_{1}
-updates would transform magnitude control into structured row-wise sparsity, offering an optimizer-level route to neuron pruning and therefore potentially more efficient inference.

A.3
Comparison with NorMuon

Figure 9:

Both NorMuon
[
29
]
and Muown augment plain Muon with a per-row mechanism on the rows of the 2D weight matrices, but they intervene at different points in the update. Plain Muon orthogonalizes the momentum of
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}
via Newton–Schulz and applies it directly to the parameter without a row-wise state. NorMuon instead applies a post-processing step to the update, maintaining a per-row EMA of the mean-squared entries of the orthogonalized update, dividing the update element-wise by its square root, and renormalizing back to the original Frobenius norm. In contrast, rather than redistributing mass of the update Muown changes the parameterization to control the row-norms of the weight. In essence, both methods equip every row with a scalar quantity (a second-moment statistic for NorMuon and a learned magnitude for Muown). Nevertheless, these row variables serve different purposes. B.1
Proposition
1

Proof.

Since each row of
\mathbf{W}_{t}
is nonzero, the vector
\mathbf{g}_{t}=\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}
is well-defined and has strictly positive entries. Hence

\mathbf{D}_{t}=\mathrm{Diag}\!\left(1/\mathbf{g}_{t}\right)\mathbf{W}_{t}

is well-defined, and for each row
i
,

\mathbf{d}_{t,i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t,i}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t,i}\right\|_{2}},

so
\left\|\mathbf{d}_{t,i}\right\|_{2}=1
. Therefore,

\mathbf{W}_{t}=\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\mathbf{D}_{t}.

Using this factorization, we compute

\mathbf{W}_{t}\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\top}=\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\mathbf{D}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{t}^{\top}\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})=\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t}).

By definition of the spectral norm,

\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{W}_{t}\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\top}),

and therefore

\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}=\lambda_{\max}\!\bigl(\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\bigr).

It remains to factor out the maximal row magnitude. Since
\mathbf{g}_{t}=\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}
, its entries are nonnegative, so
|\mathbf{g}_{t}|=\mathbf{g}_{t}
. By the definition of
\mathbf{p}_{t}
,

\mathbf{g}_{t}=\left\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\,\mathbf{p}_{t},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})=\left\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\,\mathbf{P}_{t}.

Substituting this into the previous identity yields

\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})=\left\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\mathbf{P}_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathbf{P}_{t}.

Since scaling a matrix by a positive scalar scales all eigenvalues by the same scalar, we obtain

\lambda_{\max}\!\bigl(\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\bigr)=\left\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\lambda_{\max}\!\bigl(\mathbf{P}_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathbf{P}_{t}\bigr).

Recalling that the LHS of the equation above is equal to
\left\|\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}
proves the claim.

In this section we will provide the convergence analysis of Algorithm
2
. Remember that

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{\text{row}>0}\to\mathbb{R},\ \,\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}):=\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{W}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right)\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\Diag\left(\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}}\right)\,\mathbf{R}\right).

Theorem 2

(Deterministic Convergence of Muown)
.

Assume
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is
L
-smooth along the trajectory and lower bounded by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\star}
. Denote
\Delta_{1}\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\star}
and assume the deterministic setting, i.e., access to the true gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})
.
Then, with
\beta_{1}=0
and
\eta=\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{LT}}
, the iterates of Algorithm
2
satisfy

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})\right\|_{*}\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{L\Delta_{1}}{T}}.

This matches the optimal
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{L\Delta_{1}/T}\right)
non-convex rate (up to constants).

We will derive this result in steps. Straightforward calculations show that the gradient of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
with respect to the canonical inner product is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{W}\right)\mathbf{D}^{\top}\right),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\Diag\left(\mathbf{g}/\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}}\right)\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{W}\right)-\Diag\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right)\right)\mathbf{D}\right).

where
\mathbf{D}=\Diag(1/\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{\mathrm{row}})\mathbf{R}
is the row-normalization of
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{W}=\Diag\left(\mathbf{g}\right)\mathbf{D}
is the original parameterization. In particular we note that, after re-parameterization, Muown updates
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{g}
with standard, albeit different, first-order updates. In particular Algorithm
2
corresponds exactly to Muown when replacing Adam with its proxy SignSGD. In a first step we show that, under a smoothness assumption on
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, the mixed update of Algorithm
2
can be analyzed separately and combined without issues.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbf{g},\Delta\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{\text{row}>0},\Delta\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\gamma,\eta>0
. Furthermore define
\mathbf{g}^{+}:=\mathbf{g}+\gamma\Delta\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}^{+}:=\mathbf{R}+\eta\Delta\mathbf{R}
and assume
\mathbf{R}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{\text{row}>0}
and that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is
L
-smooth between
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})
and
(\mathbf{g}^{+},\mathbf{R}^{+})
. Then

\displaystyle\ \widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}^{+},\mathbf{R}^{+}\right)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{\gamma\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right),\Delta\mathbf{g}\rangle+\frac{L\gamma^{2}}{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}_{\text{only depends on $\Delta\mathbf{g}$}}+\underbrace{\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right),\Delta\mathbf{R}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{R}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}}_{\text{only depends on $\Delta\mathbf{R}$}}.

Proof.

By the
L
-smoothness of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
we have

\displaystyle\ \widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}^{+},\mathbf{R}^{+}\right)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \left\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right),(\mathbf{g}^{+},\mathbf{R}^{+})-(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{L}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{g}^{+},\mathbf{R}^{+})-(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \gamma\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right),\Delta\mathbf{g}\rangle+\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right),\Delta\mathbf{R}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{L}{2}\max\{\gamma^{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\infty}^{2},\eta^{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{R}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \gamma\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right),\Delta\mathbf{g}\rangle+\frac{L\gamma^{2}}{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}+\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R}\right),\Delta\mathbf{R}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{R}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2},

where we used our choice of inner product and norm in the equality.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
t\in[T]
. We apply Lemma
1
with

\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}:=-\operatorname{sgn}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}:=\operatorname{argmin}_{\left\|\mathbf{O}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}\leq 1}\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\mathbf{O}\rangle.

to get

\displaystyle\ \widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t+1},\mathbf{R}_{t+1}\right)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{\gamma\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}\rangle+\frac{L\gamma^{2}}{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}_{\text{only depends on $\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}$}}+\underbrace{\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}}_{\text{only depends on $\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}$}}

For the
\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}
-term note that
\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}\rangle=-\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{1}
and
\left\|\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, thus

\gamma\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}\rangle+\frac{L\gamma^{2}}{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{g}_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq-\gamma\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{1}+\frac{L\gamma^{2}}{2}.

For the
\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}
-term we have
\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=-\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{S_{1}}
, where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{S_{1}}
is the Schatten-1 (trace) norm and
\left\|\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}\leq 1
, thus

\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{R}_{t}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}^{2}\leq-\eta\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{S_{1}}+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}.

Combining these bounds gives

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t+1},\mathbf{R}_{t+1}\right)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\leq-\gamma\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{1}-\eta\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{S_{1}}+\frac{L}{2}(\eta^{2}+\gamma^{2}).

Summing this inequality from
t=1
to
T
and using
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{T+1},\mathbf{R}_{T+1}\right)\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\star}
gives

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\gamma\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{1}+\eta\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{S_{1}}\right)\leq\Delta_{1}+\frac{L}{2}(\eta^{2}+\gamma^{2})T.

Define
R_{T}:=\Delta_{1}+\frac{L}{2}(\eta^{2}+\gamma^{2})T
. Since both terms in the sum are nonnegative, we can drop one term at a time to obtain

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{R_{T}}{\gamma T},\qquad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{S_{1}}\leq\frac{R_{T}}{\eta T}.

By our choice of product norm
\left\|(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{R})\right\|=\max\{\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\mathbf{R}\right\|_{S_{\infty}}\}
, the corresponding dual norm satisfies

\left\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{*}=\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{1}+\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{S_{1}}.

Combining the two bounds yields

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)\right\|_{*}\leq\frac{R_{T}}{\gamma T}+\frac{R_{T}}{\eta T}.

∎

B.3
Stochastic Convergence (Theorem

For the stochastic convergence result, we will use the following standard bounded variance assumption on the stochastic gradients along the trajectory of Algorithm

Assumption 1
.

The stochastic gradients along the trajectory are unbiased, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t},\xi_{t})]=\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t},\xi_{t})]=\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}),

and have bounded variance

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t},\xi_{t})-\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})\right\|_{1}^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{\mathbf{g}}^{2},\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t},\xi_{t})-\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})\right\|_{S_{1}}^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}^{2}.

Now we are ready to prove the Theorem.

Proof.

As in the deterministic case, we apply Lemma
1
to get

(1)

We start by bounding the first term on the right-hand side through the classical decomposition of the inner product
[
18
, see e.g.]
to get

\displaystyle\ \left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),-\text{sgn}\left(\mathbf{m}_{t}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)-(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{m}_{t},-\text{sgn}\left(\mathbf{m}_{t}\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{m}_{t},-\text{sgn}\left(\mathbf{m}_{t}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)-(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|_{1}\left\|-\text{sgn}\left(\mathbf{m}_{t}\right)\right\|_{\infty}-(1-\beta_{1})\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)-(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|_{1}-(1-\beta_{1})\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 2\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)-(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|_{1}-\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})\right\|_{1},

\bm{\mu}_{t}:=\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})-(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{m}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t}:=\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t},\xi_{t})-\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|\bm{\mu}_{t}\right\|_{1}\leq&\ \beta_{1}^{t-1}\left\|\bm{\mu}_{1}\right\|_{1}+(1-\beta_{1})\left\|\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{\tau-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{t-\tau+1}\right\|_{1}\\

\leq&\ \beta_{1}^{t-1}\left\|\bm{\mu}_{1}\right\|_{1}+(1-\beta_{1})\left\|\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{\tau-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{t-\tau+1}\right\|_{1}+\gamma L\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{\tau}\\
\leq&\ \beta_{1}^{t-1}\left\|\bm{\mu}_{1}\right\|_{1}+(1-\beta_{1})\left\|\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{\tau-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{t-\tau+1}\right\|_{1}+\frac{\gamma L}{1-\beta_{1}}\end{split}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{\tau-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{t-\tau+1}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{\tau-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{t-\tau+1}\right\|_{2}\right]\leq\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{\tau-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{t-\tau+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\sqrt{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{2(\tau-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bm{\epsilon}_{t-\tau+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]}\leq\frac{\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\sigma_{\mathbf{g}}}{\sqrt{1-\beta_{1}^{2}}},

in the last step. Plugging into (

\displaystyle\ \frac{\sigma_{\mathbf{g}}}{1-\beta_{1}}+\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}\sigma_{\mathbf{g}}\sqrt{1-\beta_{1}}T+\frac{\gamma L}{1-\beta_{1}}T,

where we used our choice of
\mathbf{m}_{1}

The effective rank of a matrix is defined as
\mathrm{erank}(\bm{\sigma})=\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^{\min(m,n)}p_{i}\log p_{i})
where
p_{i}=\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{i}}{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{1}}
and
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{\min(m,n)}
contains the singular values of the gradient matrix. We normalize the effective rank by dividing it with
\min(m,n)
. For Muon, we show the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{W}

Gradient Noise (Fig.
7
).
We base the gradient noise analysis on our 124M weight decay ablation setup and consider the configuration with the best final validation loss. This results in Muon (
\eta=4\times 10^{-3},\lambda=0.1
) and Muown without weight decay (
\eta=6\times 10^{-3},\lambda=0
) run. For each of these runs we save the model and optimizer states after
t=1900,3800,5700,7600
and the final
T=9538
iterations. For every checkpoint, we first compute the true gradient
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{W}_{t}\right)
for Muon, and
\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)
for Muown checkpoints over the whole
5
B token training dataset
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{T}\}
. In a second pass we then calculate, for each weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{t}^{(i)}
, resp.
\mathbf{g}_{t}^{(i)},\mathbf{R}_{t}^{(i)}
,

\sigma_{\mathbf{W}_{t}^{(i)}}^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\tau=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{W}_{t}\right)-\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{W}_{t},\xi_{\tau}\right)\right\|_{S_{1}}^{2},

and similarly
\sigma_{\mathbf{R}_{t}^{(i)}}
and
\sigma_{\mathbf{g}_{t}^{(i)}}
. The values of
\zeta_{\mathbf{W}},\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}
and
\zeta_{\mathbf{R}}
are known analytically to be
\zeta_{\mathbf{W}}=\zeta_{\mathbf{R}}=\sqrt{\min\{m,n\}}
for
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\zeta_{\mathbf{g}}=\sqrt{m}
for
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We compute the noise term separately for each weight matrix, plot the median across weights as a bold line, and show the interquartile range as a shaded region. We find that the noise term of Muown remains consistently below that of Muon, with the gap widening over the course of training.
