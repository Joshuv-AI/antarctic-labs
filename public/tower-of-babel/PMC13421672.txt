Timing the strain: skeletal muscle clock as regulator of physical activity
Authors: Ippei Kawano, Gad Asher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal muscle harbors cell-autonomous circadian clock that is implicated in muscle as well as systemic metabolic and physiological functions. Emerging evidence highlights how the muscle clock integrates time-signals to regulate physical activity. Physical activity imposes recurrent and temporally structured physiological and metabolic perturbations. Here we discuss the literature and propose that the muscle clock is well-positioned to execute anticipatory regulation by engaging metabolic programs in advance of predictable muscle demands.

Introduction

For animals, movement is essential for obtaining resources and avoiding threats, but it is also demanding, imposing major energetic and chemical strain on various organs in general, and specifically on skeletal muscle. During vigorous physical activity, ATP turnover in skeletal muscle can increase by orders of magnitude1, depleting local oxygen (O₂) while accumulating acid and carbon dioxide (CO₂)2–4. These alterations trigger compensatory mechanisms aimed at restoring the local physiological and metabolic environment.

Traditionally, such compensatory mechanisms have been interpreted through the framework of homeostasis, defined as the maintenance of a stable internal environment5,6. Within this paradigm, regulation is classically understood through negative feedback: deviations of physiological/metabolic variables from their set points are sensed, and effector systems are engaged to counteract the perturbation. In skeletal muscle, for example, ATP depletion activates AMPK7, whereas low oxygen activates hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs)8, which initiate adaptive programs that improve energy supply. This reactive framework has been widely used for explaining acute molecular responses to physical activity.

However, mechanisms that restore parameters after a single bout of physical activity are not necessarily optimal for repeated bouts. When similar perturbations recur periodically, proactive actions, namely preparing the system in advance, minimize both the delay and amplitude of error-correction9. This is particularly relevant when the required adaptations take time to engage, as enzymes, substrates, and mitochondrial capacity may not be available in sufficient abundance at the time of need. Anticipatory regulation can also support temporal segregation of mutually incompatible processes, such as substrate utilization versus storage, confining each to a specific time window10. In skeletal muscle, catabolic pathways should be engaged during the active phase to support contractile activity, while anabolic processes should be engaged during the inactive phase to support recovery and remodeling.

This logic resembles a familiar problem in control theory: systems exposed to recurrent, structured disturbances can benefit from incorporating an internal model of the disturbance pattern rather than solely relying on error correction11. Applied to physiology, this suggests that anticipatory regulation, in which adaptive programs are engaged before metabolic strain arises, may complement reactive feedback-based control in maintaining homeostasis9.

The circadian clock is an ideal candidate for such an anticipatory regulation mechanism in general and specifically in skeletal muscle (Fig. 1). Most mammalian cells, including skeletal muscle fibers, contain a cell-autonomous transcription-translation feedback loop (TTFL) that directly regulates expression of genes, including those involved in energy metabolism12,13. Here, we review the function of the skeletal muscle clock and evaluate whether it satisfies the formal requirements for an anticipatory regulatory system for physical activity. We further expand on the signals through which physical activity phase-shifts the muscle clock, and propose that local gas-related disturbances, specifically hypoxia, hypercapnia, and acidosis, are strong candidates for shifting muscle clock phase in proportion to metabolic demand. Understanding skeletal muscle circadian biology may illuminate how exercise mechanistically achieves its metabolic benefits. A comprehensive account of circadian exercise physiology is beyond the scope of this review, and readers are referred to other comprehensive reviews on the topic12,15–18.

An overview on the skeletal muscle circadian clock

The mammalian circadian system

The circadian clock, whose period approximately (circa-) matches the day-night cycle (-diem), relies on a transcriptional negative-feedback loop interlinked to auxiliary regulatory loops19,20. The positive “activator” arm consists of the heterodimeric basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) PAS proteins BMAL1 and CLOCK, which bind E-box elements to activate transcription of the negative “repressor” arm genes, Per1/2 and Cry1/2. PER and CRY proteins accumulate and translocate to the nucleus, where they inhibit BMAL1/CLOCK, thereby repressing their own transcription. BMAL1/CLOCK also drives an auxiliary loop, involving RORα/β and REV-ERBα/β, which compete at ROR elements (RORE) in the Bmal1 promoter, with RORs acting as activators and REV-ERBs as repressors, providing additional control over rhythm amplitude and phase. The mammalian circadian system is hierarchically organized, with the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus functioning as the central pacemaker. SCN exhibits a robust, self-sustained circadian oscillation that persists even without external time cues. Nonetheless, light information obtained through melanopsin retinal ganglion cells can synchronize (“entrain”) the SCN to the external time. Remarkably, most mammalian cells possess the above-mentioned TTFL-based circadian clockwork and can cell-autonomously generate rhythmicity even without SCN. However, in peripheral tissues, these rhythms become desynchronized and markedly diminish in SCN-lesioned animals, highlighting the role of the SCN in integration and propagation of time-signals to other tissues and cells throughout the body24,25. SCN synchronizes peripheral clocks through sleep-wake cycles, feed-fast rhythms, core body temperature rhythms, autonomic outputs, and humoral factors such as glucocorticoids. At the cellular level, serum has long been known to reset peripheral clocks, although the specific intracellular pathways mediating this response remain incompletely defined. Recently, a high-throughput single-cell analysis identified steroid hormone signaling pathways, including sex-hormone receptors, as key mediators of serum-induced clock resetting, with Cry2 playing a critical role in this process26.

The skeletal muscle circadian clock

If time-of-day information alone were sufficient to coordinate skeletal muscle physiology, phase cues imposed by the SCN and relayed through systemic signals such as endocrine, autonomic, and body temperature rhythms might, in principle, suffice. Such systemic coordination likely contributes substantially to muscle timing. However, skeletal muscle possesses its own cell-autonomous clock that does not require SCN, raising the question of its functional necessity. The possible answer to this spans the following continuum: (i) the local clock is functionally redundant with SCN-driven systemic cues; (ii) it is necessary but primarily as a local transcriptional regulator rather than a timekeeping system; or (iii) it functions as an independent timekeeping system that complements SCN-driven time signals. Each of these possibilities makes distinct predictions and they are not mutually exclusive.

At one extreme, the skeletal muscle clock may be largely functionally redundant with systemic timing signals. Under this model, loss of the intrinsic skeletal muscle clock would result in negligible physiological disruption provided that SCN-driven systemic rhythms remain intact. As discussed in the next section, genetic loss-of-function models of core clock components in skeletal muscle generally are not supportive of this view.

The alternative possibility is that the molecular clock functions primarily as a local transcription regulator, independent of its function as a timekeeping device. Core clock components coordinate tissue-specific gene expression programs through regulation of key tissue-specific transcription factors, for example MYOD1 in skeletal muscle27, HNF4A in liver28, and PDX1 in pancreatic β cells29. In this regard, genetic loss-of-function experimental models of clock genes abolish not only circadian rhythmicity but also their transcriptional functions, making it difficult to attribute observed phenotypes specifically to loss of rhythmicity per se. Accordingly, at least part of the phenotypes associated with muscle clock disruption likely reflect loss of transcriptional regulation rather than loss of timekeeping. This consideration, however, does not explain why such transcriptional programs would be organized in a cell-autonomous circadian manner, given that systemic signals, such as glucocorticoid rhythms, can, in principle, impose rhythmic gene expression without requiring an intrinsic oscillator.

This leads to the third possibility, in which the skeletal muscle clock serves an independent local timekeeping role that complements the SCN-driven systemic signals. Notably, the inputs to the skeletal muscle clock differ from those sensed by the SCN. For example, physical activity can phase-shift the muscle clock without affecting the SCN clock14, indicating that muscle clocks can be entrained by tissue-specific cues independent of hierarchical SCN imposition. This suggests that the intrinsic muscle clock can integrate information about the local muscle environment that systemic signals do not fully capture, and that its impairment would compromise aspects of function that systemic time signals alone cannot support. As reviewed in the next section, this possibility aligns best with current experimental findings.

At the organismal level, the presence of partially autonomous rhythmicity in peripheral tissues begs for a mode of coordination across different organs. Upon physical activity, fuel allocation is expected to be more efficient when increased substrate utilization in one tissue is matched by appropriately timed mobilization or production in other tissues. In this view, SCN-derived systemic time signals may primarily serve to synchronize tissue phases, while local clocks enable tissue-specific functional optimization. Disruption of either level would therefore be expected to impair systemic metabolic coordination in general and specifically upon physical activiy30.

Structural and contractile phenotypes in skeletal muscle clock-deficient mice

The current understanding of the skeletal muscle clock is largely derived from genetic loss-of-function models. Over time, model specificity has progressed from whole-body knockouts to skeletal muscle-specific and inducible skeletal muscle-specific knockouts, and comparisons across these models have been informative for delineating muscle-intrinsic clock function. Early whole-body clock mutant models, including ClockΔ19, Bmal1−/−, and Rev-erbα−/− animals, exhibited reduced muscle mitochondrial content and respiratory capacity, disrupted myofilament organization, and decreased contractile force27,31. In contrast, skeletal muscle-specific Bmal1−/− or Rev-erbα/β −/− models showed reduced contractile force without changes in mitochondrial content, and at least the former, no overt ultrastructural abnormalities32,33. The contractile deficit is, therefore, common to both global and muscle-specific loss of clock, whereas the absence of mitochondrial and ultrastructural phenotypes in muscle-specific clock deletion points to non-myofiber clocks contributing to normal muscle structure.

Inducible skeletal muscle-specific Bmal1−/− animals were initially reported to have both normal histology and contractile force32, but extended duration of knockout induction revealed reduced specific muscle tension and fibrosis34,35, suggesting that muscle Bmal1 does play a certain role in muscle structure, although the extent to which this reflects its role as a core clock component remains unclear.

The identity of the non-myofiber tissues whose clocks support muscle structure remains unknown. One potential candidate is connective tissue, which plays a critical role in skeletal muscle development by forming a spatial pattern upon muscle formation and through guidance of myoblast migration via secretion of chemical signals36. Notably, collagen VI deficiency produces phenotypes akin to those observed in whole-body clock mutants, including altered ultrastructure, mitochondrial defects, and reduced contractile strength37. This raises the possibility that disruption of clock function in connective tissue cells, including fibroblasts, might affect muscle structural integrity through impairment of extracellular matrix composition.

The expanding repertoire of loss-of-function models highlights the intricate interaction between myofiber and non-myofiber clocks. Nonetheless, the most overt disruptions observed in whole-body mutants likely reflect contributions from clocks outside myofibers, whereas the contribution of the muscle clock itself appears more context-dependent and mechanistically unresolved.

Metabolic alterations in skeletal muscle clock-deficient mice

In contrast to structural phenotypes, the metabolic function of skeletal muscle remains dependent on the intrinsic clock even after development. Skeletal muscle is the primary site of postprandial glucose disposal and a major target of insulin action38. Its insulin sensitivity exhibits circadian variation, with higher responsiveness during the active phase39,40. Both basal insulin sensitivity and its rhythmicity are disrupted in skeletal muscle-specific Bmal1−/− models: insulin-stimulated glucose uptake is markedly reduced in constitutive knockouts and nearly abolished in inducible knockouts, accompanied by decreased GLUT4 abundance32. Glucose oxidation at the onset of the active phase (ZT12) is reduced to levels comparable to the fasted state32, indicating a failure to mount the normal diurnal increase in glucose utilization. Lipid metabolism is also perturbed in a phase-dependent manner. In control animals, muscle triglyceride levels rise prior to the active phase, whereas this anticipatory increase is absent in Bmal1−/− muscle, resulting in persistently low triglyceride levels41. In inducible skeletal muscle-specific Bmal1−/− mice, glycolytic genes are downregulated while fatty acid oxidation genes are upregulated, suggesting the muscle clock normally gates an anticipatory shift toward carbohydrate utilization at the onset of the active phase42. These findings argue against functional redundancy between skeletal muscle and systemic clocks. Circadian clock as mechanism for anticipatory regulation of physical activity

The preceding sections establish that the skeletal muscle clock is a cell-autonomous timekeeping system directly coupled to metabolic effector programs. To evaluate whether it functions as an anticipatory system for physical activity, we define the minimal requirements for such regulation and assess whether the observed biology satisfies them. A system can implement anticipatory regulation of a homeostasis-disrupting event if: (i) the event is temporally predictable; (ii) the system has an internal representation of the expected time of the event; (iii) this representation is coupled to effector systems; and finally (iv) the system can adjust its phase in response to realized event, thereby maintaining alignment with the new temporal pattern of the event. These criteria are general and extend beyond circadian biology, as anticipatory regulation is a core principle of physiological organization more broadly43. In the following section, we consider how these requirements could be implemented in a biological system with a circadian clock. Requirements (ii) and (iii) are addressed by the literature reviewed above; below we address requirements (i) and (iv).

Physical activity is highly temporally structured

For an event to be temporally predictable in a manner that anticipatory regulation would be beneficial, it must be concentrated within a bounded and predictable time window of the day. Furthermore, the energetic cost of maintaining an anticipatory response to the event should not exceed that of an acute response mounted without prior readiness. When an event is instead spread across a broad time window, or randomly occurs, constitutive readiness becomes the more efficient strategy, and an anticipatory response confers no advantage. Conversely, the more tightly phase-restricted the event, the more precisely preparation can be timed, and the lower the energetic cost of sustaining readiness, rendering an anticipatory regulation more advantageous. The threshold window width at which timed preparation becomes advantageous is difficult to specify a priori, as it is based on a tradeoff of the above considerations. Both theoretical modeling and empirical observations of daily physical activity in natural settings are likely needed to determine whether physical activity is sufficiently concentrated within a time window to favor anticipatory regulation.

In mammals, physical activity is usually concentrated within a defined circadian phase (night in nocturnal species and day in diurnal species) and substantially reduced during the opposite phase. Within this active phase, activity is often further structured by ecological constraints. In nocturnal rodents, activity commonly increases at the onset of the dark phase44, while predation risk can suppress activity during brighter moonlit periods (selenophobia)45. When food availability is temporally restricted, mice and other rodents concentrate activity to the time preceding expected food availability, known as food anticipatory activity46. Pre-industrial Hadza hunter-gatherer adults average ~135–174 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, with midday reduction consistent with heat avoidance47–49. These findings suggest that in natural settings, physical activity, particularly the more intense components that are most disruptive to homeostasis, is ecologically confined to a narrow window of several hours within a day, shaped primarily by environmental factors such as food availability, predation, and temperature, with strong circadian patterns, indicating a circadian timekeeping system could be useful in predicting the regularity of these events.

Skeletal muscle clock is aligned to activity itself

The benefit of anticipatory regulation depends on alignment between the expected and realized temporal patterns of the events, as misalignment would itself increase homeostatic deviation and corrective burden. This is particularly relevant given that activity timing can shift substantially in response to environmental changes. A mechanism that senses activity-associated physiological perturbations and couples them to adjustments of the internal timekeeping system is therefore required. Consistently, the skeletal muscle clock is phase shifted by activity14, specifically by muscle contraction, and the direction and magnitude of this shift depend on time of day. In mice, treadmill running at ZT5, namely 5 h after lights on, advances the phase by ~100 min, while at ZT11, i.e., 1 h before lights off, delays the phase by ~62 min, and at ZT17, has no effect on the phase50. This suggests that the skeletal muscle clock receives activity-associated inputs and adjusts its phase accordingly, allowing it to remain aligned with the temporal pattern of activity.

Together, we conclude that the skeletal muscle clock can implement anticipatory regulation of physical activity since: (i) physical activity, particularly its more intense component, exhibits strong circadian patterns of recurrence; (ii) the clock provides an internal representation of the external 24-h cycle; (iii) the muscle clock components can directly regulate gene expression relevant for physical activity; and finally (iv) the muscle clock can daytime-dependently adjust its phase in response to activity.

Requirement (iv) is mechanistically the least resolved. It suggests the existence of muscle contraction-coupled signals that can convey information about metabolic strain to the core clock machinery, yet the identity of these signals is largely unestablished. The remainder of this review examines candidate signals and proposes a testable framework centered on local gas-related disturbances.

Systemic activity-derived time-signals for the skeletal muscle clock

Muscle contraction generates both systemic and local signals that entrain the clock18,51. First and foremost, physical activity affects circulating hormones such as cortisol and raises core body temperature15,52,53. Cortisol can act through the glucocorticoid receptor to reset the muscle clock52. Temperature is considered a universal entraining cue for peripheral clocks, operating through the Heat Shock Factor 1 (HSF1) pathway52. HSF1 physically interacts with the BMAL1/CLOCK heterodimer to reset Per2 rhythms54, providing a plausible molecular mechanism by which physical activity-induced body temperature changes may entrain skeletal muscle clocks. Moreover, because these systemic signals reach most peripheral clocks indiscriminately, they are well-suited to synchronize clocks across different organs and potentially to re-align them with the muscle clock, thereby limiting inter-tissue desynchrony30.

It is noteworthy that studies on the liver-muscle clock communication reported that the core muscle clock operates largely independently of the liver clock, albeit a subset of rhythmic transcripts is affected by loss of the liver clock55–57.

In addition, skeletal muscle releases myokines (IL-6, irisin, Meteorin-like, cathepsin B, etc.)58 and extracellular vesicles59 into circulation. Muscle contraction also increases circulating lactate, which can itself serve as a signaling molecule in autocrine-, paracrine-, and endocrine-like manners60.

Local signals that link muscle contraction to the skeletal muscle clock

Locally, contraction perturbs intracellular ions, ATP and redox state, and the local gas environment, which can shift the muscle clock as well15. Below, we focus on local signals as candidate inputs to the skeletal muscle clock.

If the skeletal muscle-intrinsic clock plays an important role in implementing an anticipatory metabolic program, this raises the question of why such a program should be localized within muscle rather than coordinated by the systemic clock. One plausible explanation is that key entrainment signals required for this program are most directly accessible within skeletal muscle itself, rather than being relayed systemically. Signals fulfilling this role would be expected to both reflect local physiological/metabolic state and exert tissue-specific effects on clock phase. Physical activity is widely recognized as a potent phase-shifting cue for the skeletal muscle clock14,18. However, the mechanisms by which muscular activity entrains the clock remain incompletely defined. Although systemic factors likely contribute, skeletal muscle is well-positioned to directly sense its own energetic strain during contraction.

Muscle contraction can generate two broad categories of signals: those related to the changes in mechanical forces upon contraction, and those reflecting energetic strain and increased metabolic demand. Calcium, the best-supported candidate entrainment signal in skeletal muscle61, mediates excitation-contraction coupling and therefore belongs primarily to the first category. However, signals encoding contractile occurrence alone may be insufficient for implementing an anticipatory metabolic program. To assess whether the current level of anticipatory preparation is adequate, and whether future programs require adjustment, signals reflecting eminent metabolic failure, i.e., those in the second category, may be more informative. AMPK and SIRT1 are candidates within this domain, although direct evidence for their roles as entrainment signals in the context of skeletal muscle contraction remains limited. Their broader relationship with the circadian clock has been reviewed elsewhere62–64.

Contraction-induced anabolic signaling may represent an additional local input to the molecular clock. Muscle contraction, particularly from high-resistance exercise, activates mTOR signaling65,66, which can impact clock period and amplitude: at least in hepatocytes and adipocytes, mTOR inhibition lengthens period and dampens amplitude, while mTOR activation shortens period and augments amplitude, raising the possibility that contraction-induced mTOR signaling may modulate the muscle clock67. Mechanical force itself can also engage signaling pathways that impact the clock. For example, YAP/TAZ is upregulated by mechanical overload in skeletal muscle68, and at least in fibroblasts, YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation disrupts Rev-erbα oscillations and alters some clock component expression via YAP/TAZ binding to the transcriptional effector TEAD68.

Local gas-related disturbances, particularly changes in intramuscular pO2, are strong candidates within this second category for two reasons. First, their magnitude at a given absolute workload varies substantially with the oxidative capacity of the muscle. Physically active individuals exhibit higher pO2 values at rest and a slower decline during activity compared to sedentary individuals69. Second, at least for pO2, the oxygen-sensing HIF pathway is known to directly interact with core clock components in skeletal muscles, as discussed below. Beyond local hypoxia, changes in pCO2 and pH represent emerging metabolic signals that could influence clock rhythmicity, although their relevance in contracting muscles remains uncharted.

Skeletal muscle oxygen tension as a molecular link between activity and skeletal muscle clock

Skeletal muscle undergoes substantial perturbations in its local gas environment with physical activity. During moderate intensity activity (i.e., ~50% maximal oxygen consumption, a.k.a. VO2 max), intramuscular pO2 can fall from ~30 mmHg at rest70,71 down to ~3 mmHg15,72,73. Given that skeletal muscle HIF-1α is expected to accumulate at ~8 mmHg74, moderate intensity is likely sufficient to engage the HIF pathway.

The HIF pathway is a central regulator of hypoxia adaptation. Upon low oxygen conditions, the normally short-lived HIF-α protein is stabilized, heterodimerizes with HIF-1β, and binds to hypoxia-responsive elements (HREs) to activate adaptive genes. Skeletal muscle expresses HIF-1α and HIF-2α, with HIF-1α being more extensively studied in the context of physical activity75. Genetic deficiency of skeletal muscle HIF-1α promotes a more oxidative metabolic phenotype and enhances endurance capacity, albeit at the cost of increased muscle damage during repeated activity76, suggesting a role for HIF-1α in coordinating substrate use under energetic stress.

Recent evidence indicates a functional interaction between the HIF pathway and the circadian clock. First, rhythmic oxygen levels through HIF-1α were reported to synchronize the circadian clock77. Second, as HIFs are transcription factors, much of hypoxia adaptation is mediated by gene expression, as demonstrated by the robust transcriptomic changes seen across different organs upon hypoxia78,79. Interestingly, ~20% of hepatic, ~14% of renal, and ~7% of pulmonary transcriptomic response to hypoxia are time-dependent78. This time-dependency is preserved even under constant dark, where there is no external time-setting light cue, but is lost in clock-deficient Per1/2−/− mice, indicating dependence on the endogenous clock78. The overall magnitude of hepatic hypoxia response is also substantially attenuated by clock deficiency78,79. These findings suggest that the circadian clock responds to and modulates the timing and magnitude of hypoxia-responsive gene expression programs80.

This functional crosstalk may arise, at least in part, from direct interactions between HIF-1α and BMAL1. Both proteins belong to the structurally related basic helix-loop-helix PER-ARNT-SIM (bHLH–PAS) family. While the canonical binding partner of HIF-1α is HIF-1β and similarly for BMAL1 it is CLOCK, BMAL1 can also form non-canonical heterodimers with HIF-1α or HIF-2α under hypoxia, at least in vitro81–84. Genetic deficiency of Bmal1 reduces hypoxia-induced HIF-1α accumulation and target gene expression in hepatocytes79, suggesting that BMAL1 is required for HIF-1α stabilization under hypoxia, and similarly in myotubes, Bmal1 deficiency reduces the expression of HIF target genes85. In vivo, activity induces stronger skeletal muscle HIF-1α accumulation during the active phase (ZT15) compared to the rest phase (ZT3)51, akin to HIF-1α accumulation in liver upon hypoxia79 and both HIF-1α and clock target genes show stronger induction at these times (ZT12, 18)85. These findings suggest that hypoxia responsiveness in skeletal muscle is modulated by the molecular clock. Furthermore, at least part of the metabolic coordination supported by the muscle clock may be mediated by HIF-1α: accordingly, skeletal muscle-specific BMAL1 deficiency exacerbates high-fat diet-induced glucose intolerance, and this can be rescued by genetic HIF-1α stabilization86. Taken together, these findings suggest HIF-1α participates in bidirectional crosstalk between activity-induced local hypoxia and the skeletal muscle clock.

This interaction may be understood in the context of anticipatory regulation. Given that physical activity is concentrated within a temporally bounded window, the occurrence of activity-induced muscle hypoxia is expected to be similarly time-structured. Consistent with this, tissue oxygenation exhibits daily rhythms in vivo that follow activity patterns87, and physiological-range oxygen oscillations are sufficient to entrain peripheral clocks via HIF-1α77. In this context, muscle hypoxia may serve dual roles: first as an acute signal coordinating energy metabolism towards reduced oxygen dependence; and second as a clock entrainment signal. For the first function, activity-induced HIF-1α heterodimerizes with HIF-1β to bind HRE and induce glycolytic genes. BMAL1 may support this transcriptional activity, both by facilitating HIF-1α accumulation79 and by forming a transcriptionally competent complex with it85. For the second function, increased HIF-1α may complex with BMAL1 and regulate E-box-dependent clock gene expression, providing a plausible mechanism by which hypoxia shifts the skeletal muscle clock85. In this framework, the magnitude of clock response could be coupled to the magnitude of HIF signaling, potentially providing a more accurate reflection of metabolic demand than absolute contractile activity alone. However, the O2 threshold that shifts the skeletal muscle clock remains an open question, and thus far evidence for oxygen as a time-signal derives primarily from studies using cultured fibroblasts and hepatocytes77.

In turn, the clock may regulate the sensitivity of skeletal muscle to hypoxia. Evidence from the liver at least directionally supports this claim. Hypoxia-induced hepatic HIF-1α accumulation is itself BMAL1-dependent, with stronger accumulation occurring during the active phase79. Whether the same time-of-day-dependent accumulation applies to skeletal muscle remains to be directly tested, but if so, BMAL1 may facilitate HIF-1α accumulation during the active phase when local hypoxia is more expected, whereas accumulation may be more modest during the inactive phase. This differential HIF-1α sensitivity could be functionally advantageous. One possible explanation is that restricting HIF-1α sensitivity could improve signal-to-noise, as hypoxia arising from sporadic or atypical bouts of activity during the inactive phase may have reduced impact on anticipatory regulation.

The mechanisms that confer time-of-day-dependent HIF-1α activation remain poorly characterized. Among other it could arise because of circadian variations in cellular factors that regulate HIF-1α stability, including mitochondrial reactive oxygen species88, prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) cofactor availability, and levels of PHD-inhibiting metabolites such as fumarate and succinate.

Gas-related disturbances other than oxygen as inputs to the skeletal muscle clock

The preceding section framed HIF-1α as a key mechanistic link between physical activity-induced skeletal muscle hypoxia and the skeletal muscle clock. However, HIF-1α stability is not determined by oxygen availability alone. Both acidosis and hypercapnia, which can co-occur with hypoxia in skeletal muscle during intense activity, suppress hypoxia-induced HIF-1α accumulation, at least in vitro89–91. Notably, acidosis and hypercapnia may not be strictly coupled to hypoxia in exercising skeletal muscle. Measurements of intracellular pO2 via myoglobin desaturation using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) indicate that pO2 falls from rest (~34 mmHg) to a plateau (~3–5 mmHg) at moderate intensity as low as 30% of maximal work rate, and does not decrease further with increasing intensity71–73. In contrast, intramuscular pH, measured by 31P-MRS, declines progressively with increasing power output from ~7.06-7.11 at rest to ~6.87–6.95 at high intensity, with no evidence of plateau2,92. Direct simultaneous measurement of intramuscular pO2, pH, and pCO2, using a fiber optic sensor inserted into contracting forearm muscle, further confirms this dissociation: while intramuscular pO2 approached 0 mmHg at 30% maximum voluntary contraction, pH continued to decline and pCO2 continued to rise with higher workloads93. Consistently, venous pCO2 measurement can temporarily double (from ~46 to 48 mmHg at rest to ~100 mmHg) under intense activity3,4. These findings demonstrate that intramuscular pO2, pH, and pCO2 change differently across the activity intensity spectrum. Since HIF-1α stability is jointly shaped by all three variables, its net level may reflect the integrated local gas-metabolic environment rather than pO2 alone. Specifically, moderate-intensity activity may primarily lower local pO2 and engage HIF-1α signaling, while high-intensity activity may additionally drive muscle acidosis and hypercapnia, suppressing HIF-1α signaling89–91. Future studies should further clarify whether intensity-specific local gas changes differentially impact the muscle clock.

Beyond the effect on HIF-1α stability, pH and pCO2 may impact the clock through independent pathways, as shown by several in vitro studies. Low pH suppresses clock oscillation, independently of hypoxia or HIF-1α, through mTORC1 inhibition driven by acid-induced peripheral redistribution of lysosomes away from their activator RHEB, thereby reducing translation of core clock proteins89. On the other hand, alterations in CO2 concentration are sufficient to affect several core clock gene expression and to shift circadian phase87. In NIH3T3 cell line, shifting the cells from 5% CO2 to 1% without changing oxygen led to ~5 h phase advancement, and a pulse of 10 h shift to 1% led to ~8 h phase advancement87. These findings provide plausibility that pH and CO2 may serve as mutually independent inputs to the skeletal muscle clock. Future studies should clarify whether these pathways mechanistically connect physical activity to the skeletal muscle clock in vivo.

Conclusions and future directions

The metabolic benefits of physical activity are widely recognized, yet the mechanism is only partially understood. Decades of work have characterized how individual bouts of activity perturb muscle homeostatic variables to engage reactive sensors, including AMPK, PHDs, and SIRTs, that initiate training adaptation15,94. How these transient signals are converted to sustained metabolic states, however, remains elusive95. Epigenetic changes likely constitute a part of the answer95, but are unlikely the sole mechanism. An emerging body of evidence indicates that the time of day at which physical activity is performed shapes both its acute physiological response and exercise capacity51,96, pointing to a layer of temporal regulation distinct from reactive feedback correction and from epigenetic memory.

The skeletal muscle clock is ideally positioned to address this gap. It exhibits autonomous 24-h phase and can be updated by activity itself to align muscle metabolism with the temporal pattern of activity. Within the framework developed in this review, activity-induced local gas-related perturbations (hypoxia, hypercapnia, and acidosis) are candidate signals through which muscle contraction shifts the clock in proportion to local metabolic strain, with HIF-1α positioned as a key mechanism. Local gas alterations, in this view, are not merely collateral consequences of activity to be corrected, but informational signals that encode adaptive demand.

Among these signals, hypercapnia and acidosis have rarely been conceptualized as bona fide metabolic signals in the context of muscle clock regulation. This framing is not well justified, as neither is a strict correlate of hypoxia, and each imposes distinct physicochemical constraints on cellular function, and both have been shown in vitro to modulate clock function independently87,89. Whether they can entrain the muscle clock in vivo, and how their signals are integrated with HIF-1α across different intensities of activity, are testable open questions.

The framework presented here does not account for the heterogeneity of skeletal muscle as an organ. Intramuscular pO2 is not uniform: fiber-type differences in oxygen consumption, diffusivity, and myoglobin concentration, as well as potential mismatches between motor unit recruitment and capillary perfusion units, likely produce spatially heterogeneous pO297,98. Whether the threshold engaging HIF-1α and the molecular clock are correspondingly fiber-type-dependent remains unknown. Future studies should address these complexities.

As above-mentioned, growing evidence suggests that the timing of exercise (“chrono-exercise”) can influence both systemic and skeletal muscle circadian rhythms and alter a wide variety of physiological and metabolic parameters16,99. Studies in both animals and humans indicate that exercise performed at different times of day elicits distinct transcriptional, metabolic, and performance responses, reflecting the interaction between exercise stimuli and the endogenous circadian system41,96,100. Importantly, exercise timing may have clinical relevance in metabolic disease. In individuals with type 2 diabetes, afternoon high-intensity exercise has been shown to improve glycemic control more effectively than morning exercise, highlighting the potential of chrono-exercise as a therapeutic strategy for metabolic dysfunction101. Similarly, timed exercise has been proposed as an intervention to counteract the adverse metabolic consequences of circadian disruption associated with shift work, sleep restriction, and circadian misalignment. Sleep loss and circadian disturbance have been linked to impaired insulin sensitivity, altered skeletal muscle metabolism, and dysregulated expression of clock-controlled genes, factors that contribute to increased risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes102,103. Consequently, optimizing exercise timing may represent a strategy to enhance metabolic health, muscle adaptation, and circadian alignment, although optimal timing likely depends on individual chronotype, training goals, and physiological context.

More broadly, our framework may offer insights into how transient signals from a single bout of activity can become a sustained metabolic state. Once muscle contraction-induced metabolic strain shifts the phase of the skeletal muscle clock, the clock can then enact daily anticipatory preparation of muscle metabolism. A central function of this anticipatory program, as reviewed above, is the gating of skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity and substrate utilization toward the active phase32,86,104. As an autonomous oscillator, the clock can sustain this regulation across the days that follow, even in the absence of further contractile input. The durable metabolic benefit of regular physical activity, in this view, is not solely the result of reactive feedback responses of individual bouts, but also of clock-based anticipatory regulation that operates between the bouts. Understanding how metabolic strain is integrated by the clock may therefore prove useful to harnessing the metabolic benefits of physical activity, not only by changing what muscle does during a bout, but by aligning what it does in between.
