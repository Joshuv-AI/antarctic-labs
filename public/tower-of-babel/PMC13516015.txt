The Impact of Gut Flora Stabilizer on Boosting Growth and Enhancing Gut Health in Broiler Chickens
Authors: Mohamed Hussein H. Awaad, Sayed Muhammad Mahmoud Shalash, Abdelrahman Mohamed Atta, Mohamed A. Elmenawey, Eman Ibrahim Hassanen, L. Zeibich, A. Schlagheck, Mohsen Kamal, Eman Anter Morsy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Probiotic-based gut flora stabilizers are increasingly used to improve intestinal health, immune function, and production performance in broiler chickens. This study evaluated the effects of TechnoCare200, containing Bacillus coagulans DSM 32016 and Bacillus licheniformis DSM 33806, on growth performance, immunity, intestinal morphology, and gut health.Methods:A total of 500 one-day-old broiler chicks were randomly assigned to two groups (250 birds/group). The control group received a basal diet, whereas the treatment group received the same diet supplemented with TechnoCare200 at 100 g/MT throughout the trial. Growth performance, mortality, litter quality, footpad dermatitis, carcass traits, immune responses, and intestinal histomorphology were evaluated.Results:TechnoCare200 supplementation reduced mortality by 21%, improved litter condition scores, and decreased footpad dermatitis incidence compared with the control group. Treated birds showed significantly higher body weight, improved body weight gain, lower feed conversion ratio, and increased production number. Carcass evaluation revealed significant increases in carcass, breast meat, and front-part yields. Humoral immunity and delayed-type hypersensitivity responses were significantly enhanced. Histological examination demonstrated improved intestinal morphology, characterized by a higher villus-to-crypt ratio, together with enhanced villus development and normal lymphoid architecture with increased lymphoid activity in immune organs.Conclusions:Dietary supplementation with TechnoCare200 containing B. coagulans DSM 32016 and B. licheniformis DSM 33806 improved growth performance, feed efficiency, immune responses, carcass yield, and intestinal health in broiler chickens. These findings indicate that TechnoCare200 is an effective probiotic gut flora stabilizer with potential to enhance poultry productivity and welfare under commercial production conditions.

Introduction

The gastrointestinal tract (GIT) of chickens is crucial to their health and performance. Beyond its role in digesting and absorbing nutrients, it serves as a barrier against pathogens and provides a habitat for a complex microbial community that contributes to host protection.[1] Maintaining a balanced gut environment is therefore a cornerstone of successful poultry production, as disturbances in gut health often result in reduced growth, poor feed efficiency, and greater disease susceptibility.[2] Over the past few decades, the rapid industrialization of poultry farming and advances in feed formulation have placed greater pressure on producers to optimize growth while maintaining bird health. Feed additives have been widely adopted to meet these demands. Among them, antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) became the most common due to their low cost, ease of administration, and consistent benefits in improving weight gain and feed conversion.[3] However, the prolonged use of AGPs has created serious challenges. Their contribution to the development of antimicrobial resistance, together with the accumulation of residues in poultry meat and the environment, has raised urgent concerns for both veterinary and human medicine.[4] This problem has led to global restrictions on the inclusion of antibiotics in poultry feed.[56]

While the withdrawal of AGPs represents an important step for public health, it has introduced new difficulties for poultry production systems. Without their growth-promoting and disease-controlling effects, birds may experience reduced performance, higher mortality, and increased susceptibility to enteric pathogens, which in turn can result in economic losses for producers.[7] These challenges underscore the pressing need for innovative approaches that can enhance productivity while preserving flock health.

In response, researchers and industry professionals have turned their attention to natural feed additives.[8] Promising alternatives include plant-derived compounds such as essential oils,[9] yeast cell wall components,[10] probiotics,[11121314] prebiotics,[1516] and organic acids.[17] Among these, probiotics have emerged as particularly valuable due to their multiple mechanisms of action, ranging from pathogen inhibition to immune modulation.

One probiotic of special interest is Bacillus coagulans, a spore-forming bacterium that is both heat-stable and suitable for incorporation into pelleted feed. Its homofermentative metabolism results in lactic acid production,[1819] which has several beneficial effects, including stimulation of butyrate production, enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity, and modulation of the immune system.[2021] The reduction in intestinal pH created by lactic acid further suppresses the growth of pH-sensitive pathogens.[2223] In addition, B. coagulans produces digestive enzymes and supports beneficial shifts in the gut microbiota.[24] Evidence from human studies shows that it can alleviate gastrointestinal disorders such as diarrhea and dysbiosis,[24] whereas animal studies confirm improvements in feed efficiency, performance, and gut health in poultry.[2526]

Given the global restrictions on AGPs and the increasing need for natural alternatives to sustain productivity, probiotics offer a promising solution for modern poultry production. We hypothesized that dietary supplementation with TechnoCare200 would enhance growth performance, gut integrity, and immune function in broiler chickens, thereby providing a safe and sustainable alternative to AGPs.

Materials and Methods

Gut flora stabilizer

The following studies focused on a probiotic known as TechnoCare200. This gut flora stabilizer is produced by Biochem Zusatzstoffe Handels- und Produktionsgesellschaft mbH in Lohne, Germany, and has been carefully selected for its quality. The recommended inclusion rate of TechnoCare200 in the final feed is 100 g/MT, corresponding to 1.0 × 109 colony-forming unit (CFU)/kg (8 × 108 CFU/kg of Bacillus licheniformis and 2 × 108 CFU/kg of B. coagulans).

Preliminary studies related to strain characterization

Lactic acid production by B. coagulans DSM 32016 was compared with that of a typical lactic acid producer, Enterococcus faecium, and a spore-forming species, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (strains kept anonymous to protect proprietary information). The bacteria were cultured from cryocultures in de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe medium or Glucose–Yeast extract medium. The appropriate media for each strain were determined by monitoring overnight growth curves under relevant conditions (anoxic at 39°C). MRS media were used for E. faecium and B. amyloliquefaciens, whereas GluY media were used to incubate precultures and main cultures of B. coagulans. Growth curves were used to determine the optimal time for harvesting the main cultures at the beginning of the stationary phase (±15 min), at which point L-lactate production was measured. Bacillus spores’ heat and pelleting stability are well-established in livestock feed applications.[27] However, there is limited literature on heat stability testing for novel probiotic species with unique characteristics, such as B. coagulans. Therefore, heat stability testing for B. coagulans DSM 32016 was conducted using a laboratory-scale pelleting process (universal milling technology, Nr. 144/355). Conditions were set to 100°C for 8 s, 2.25 bar pressure, and a throughput of 300 kg/h, resulting in molds with diameters of 4 mm and a length of 14 mm. Recovery rates were assessed using a modified version of the CFU quantification method EN 15787. Different strains of B. licheniformis exhibit varying pathogen inhibition capabilities [Table 1]. In this preliminary study, several strains were evaluated for their ability to inhibit a pathogenic field isolate of Clostridium perfringens, a common Gram-positive, omnipresent livestock pathogen. The Agar Well Diffusion Assay was used to test pathogen suppression by measuring inhibition zones. After culturing C. perfringens under anoxic conditions for 12 h, TSYEA agar (0.7%) was overlaid. Wells were filled with a similar CFU/ml of B. licheniformis and incubated under anoxic conditions at 37°C for 18–24 h. The effectiveness of B. licheniformis was evaluated by observing the zone of inhibition, where C. perfringens did not grow. The strain with the most efficient inhibition capability was chosen for TechnoCare200 (B. licheniformis DSM 33806). In addition, the inhibition capacity of B. licheniformis DSM 33806 against C. perfringens (field isolate from chicken cecum) and Enterococcus cecorum (field isolate from chicken heart) was compared to that of other Bacillus spp. (strains kept anonymous to protect proprietary information) using the Cross Streak method, as described by Toghyani et al.[28] Specifically, Bacillus spp. was applied as a vertical line in the centre of Columbia blood agar plates (5% sheep blood) using a sterile inoculation loop and incubated under oxic conditions at 37°C overnight. Afterward, the pathogenic field isolates were streaked at a right angle (horizontal) to both sides of the Bacillus spp. cultures as close as possible but without contact. The plates were then incubated under anoxic conditions at 37°C overnight. The inhibition strength was measured by determining the area of no pathogenic growth between the Bacillus spp. and the pathogenic cultures (inhibition zone in mm).

Experimental design

This work was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Vet CU131020241055). A total of 500 1-day-old Hubbard Efficiency Plus broiler chicks (as hatched) were used. The sample size was chosen based on previous published research on probiotic interventions in broilers with a similar duration and design, in which comparable flock sizes (400–600 birds) provided sufficient statistical power to detect differences in performance, mortality, and gut health traits between treatment groups. To further ensure robustness, each group was divided into 10 replicates of 25 birds, allowing for replication at the pen level and minimization of pen-to-pen variation. The chicks were randomly allocated into two experimental groups (n = 250 per group). Group 1 (Control, C) received the basal diet without supplementation, whereas Group 2 (Treatment, T) received the basal diet supplemented [Table 2] with TechnoCare200 at 100 g/MT throughout the 35-day trial. Each group was further subdivided into 10 replicates (pens) of 25 birds. The use of replicates ensured adequate statistical analysis and reliable evaluation of treatment effects. The experimental design followed the ARRIVE guidelines (PLoS Biol 8(6), e1000412, 2010) and was conducted in accordance with the eighth edition (2011) of the rules for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Husbandry practices

The study was carried out at the experimental poultry house of the Faculty of Agriculture, Cairo University. Birds were raised on floor pens (2.0 × 1.25 m each) with a stocking density of 10 birds/m2. Fresh wood shavings (10 cm depth) were used as bedding over a concrete floor. Chicks were reared under standard broiler management conditions. The temperature was maintained at 32°C–34°C during the 1st week and then gradually reduced by 2°C–3°C per week until it reached 22°C–24°C. Continuous light was provided during the first 48 h, followed by a 23 L:1D program for the remainder of the trial. Adequate ventilation was ensured through the use of sidewall fans. Birds had constant access to fresh water via nipple drinkers. They were fed a corn-soy-based diet tailored to Hubbard poultry recommendations for broiler breeders, following National Research Council (NRC) standards expressed in grams per 1000 kcal of metabolizable energy (ME). While Hubbard provides updated breed manuals (e.g., 2021, 2023), the “NRC of Hubbard” typically refers to the comprehensive data from the 1994 revision of the Nutrient Requirements of Poultry, ensuring optimal health and performance for birds. Chickens fed ad libitum a crumbed starter, grower, and finisher diet. From d 1 to 14 of age, the birds received a starter diet (23% crude protein; 3000 kcal/Kg ME), from d 15 to 28 of age, a grower diet (21% crude protein; 3100 kcal/Kg ME), and from d 29 to 35 of age, a finisher diet (19% crude protein; 3150 kcal/Kg ME). No antibiotics were included in the diets; however, salinomycin (66 g/MT) was added as a coccidiostat. All chicks were vaccinated according to standard Egyptian broiler vaccination schedules. This included intraocular Hitchner B1 + H120 and subcutaneous inactivated multivalent (H5N1, H5N8, and H9N2) vaccines at days 7 and 10, respectively, followed by La Sota and 228E IBDV vaccines via the intraocular route at days 14 and 18. Birds were observed daily for health and behaviour, and biosecurity procedures (disinfection, limited entry, and protective clothing) were applied throughout the trial.

Clinical examination and mortality rate

The birds involved in the experiment were examined clinically at the end of the 35 days. The examination included scoring for Footpad dermatitis (FPD) and feathering. Footpad dermatitis (FPD) was assessed using a 0–4 scale, where 0 = no lesions and 4 = severe lesions with ulceration, following the method described by Brady.[29] Feathering was also scored on a 4-point scale: 1 indicated little feathers, 2 indicated average feathering with visible skin on the back of the thighs, 3 showed good feathering except on the thighs, and 4 indicated excellent welfare feathering. In addition, the mortality rate was determined at the end of the experimental period. The same trained assessor evaluated all birds to ensure consistency and minimize interobserver variability.

Zootechnical performance assay

Measurements were made using[29303132] methods for the response variables of chicken performance, such as body weight (BW), BW gain (BWG), growth rate (GR), feed consumption (g/day/bird), feed conversion ratio (FCR) (g feed/g BWG), weekly feed conversion rate (g feed per g BWG per week), coefficient of variation, and production number (PN) that equals (kilograms of growth per day × (100-mortality %)/FCR) × 100.[29] All birds were weighed individually on the 1st day and then weekly throughout the trial. The birds’ weights and feed consumption were calculated for each pen on the same days. The following values were determined as mandatory: (i) Mean starting BW per replicate. (ii) Mean BW after every feeding phase and final BW per replicate on day 35. (iii) Feed intake per phase and total per replicate. (iv) FCR per phase and total on day 35, per replicate. (v) Mortality, morbidity (daily, per replicate, with probable reasons). (vi) Medication usage (if applicable), including daily dosage and active substance. The GR was calculated according to Brady[33] using the formula (W2 − W1)/(W2 + W1)/2 × 100, where BW1: BW at the initial phase and BW2: BW at the end phase.

GR is weekly recorded as W1 = Initial weight, W2 = End weight. These values are expressed on a weekly (per period) basis rather than cumulative. Cumulative FCR values in Table 3 were calculated using the standard formula (total feed intake ÷ total BWG).

Carcass characteristics assay

Carcass characteristics included carcass weight, dressing percentage, front part weight (g) and percentage, hind part weight (g) and percentage, and intestinal measurements, including intestine length and diameter. In addition, measurements for breast meat weight and percentage, thigh drumstick, carcass meat, heart weight, gizzard weight, liver weight, and giblet weight percentages were taken. Intestinal length and diameter were also measured. These measurements were taken from randomly selected 10 birds in each group, with one bird/replicate, at the end of the experiment (35 days).

Major immune organs’ histological scoring and gut morphometry assay

At the end of the experiment (35 days of age), five birds were randomly selected from each group, and they were euthanised by exsanguination to collect samples from the thymus, spleen, bursa of Fabricius (BF), duodenum, and jejunum. Afterward, they were preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin and conventionally processed following the standard procedures outlined by.[33] Samples were dehydrated in ascending graded alcohol, cleared by xylol, impregnated in paraffin wax, and cut into 4 μm-thick sections. Finally, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined by an Olympus BX43 light microscope to inspect any histological changes within sections. Photos were captured by an Olympus DP27 camera linked with cellSens Dimension refers to the advanced version of the cellSens microscopy software suite used for image acquisition, measurement, analysis, and documentation with Olympus microscopes and cameras. Histomorphometry was performed on 15 villi/groups using Image J software to measure the villus height, width, and crypt depth in both intestinal segments. In the thymus, the thickness of the thymic cortex and the cortex/medulla area ratio were measured in 15 lobules from each group. Moreover, 15 lymphoid follicles were randomly selected from the spleen and BF sections of various groups for measuring the diameter of follicles.[34] Intestinal histomorphometry was performed on 15 villi/groups to measure the villus height, width, and crypt depth in both intestinal segments. After that, the villus/crypt ratio was calculated by dividing the villus height by the crypt depth of the corresponding villi.[35]

Litter condition scoring

The quality of the litter/bedding was evaluated using a point system ranging from 0 to 5 during the 28th day of life and at the end of the trial (day 35). Here is the grading scale used: 0 = dry, easily crumbled material throughout the pen; 1 = mostly dry material, with some crusting around drinkers and feeders; 2 = mostly acceptable litter material, with some areas of wet shavings or capped material; 3 = poor quality litter material, with a large proportion of the wet regions and capping of the litter; 4 = unacceptable litter quality, wet and capped, but with a few areas of dry material remaining; and 5 = all litter is wet and soggy, with no dry areas left.[36]

Immune status assessment

To determine the effect on humoral immunity, we estimated the anti-Newcastle disease virus (NDV) vaccine antibody titers by collecting blood samples weekly for 5 weeks from wing veins of 20 randomly selected birds in each group (2 birds/replicate). The serum was then tested using a hemagglutination inhibition test (HI) following the method described by.[37] Additionally, serum samples were collected at the end of the experiment and tested against Avian Influenza (H5N1, H5N8, and H9) using the HI test. Furthermore, serum samples against infectious bronchitis virus (IBV) vaccination were tested using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.

For assessment of the cell-mediated immune response, the delayed-type hypersensitivity (DHT) response to bovine serum albumin (BSA) was measured after.[38] The randomly chosen birds in each group were injected at the 4th week of age with 4 mg BSA in 1 ml saline in the back of the neck. After 7 days, birds were reinjected with 1 mg BSA in 1 ml saline into the flat surface of the right wattles. In contrast, the left wattles were injected with 1 ml of saline as a control. After 24 and 48 h postinjection, the thickness of both wattles was measured by a paper thickness micrometre to calculate the differences in the thickness between saline and BSA-injected wattles. The DHT response was calculated as a relative response as follows: Relative response = Thickness of the wattle (BSA response) − the thickness of the left wattle (saline response)/the thickness of the wattle before injection. Wattle thickness was measured at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h postinoculation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (Version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data were analyzed considering the pen (25 birds) as the experimental unit for performance traits, and individual birds as the experimental unit for carcass traits, immune responses, and histological measurements. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using the general linear model (GLM) procedure,[39] with treatment (Control vs. TechnoCare200) as the fixed effect. Statistical significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05.[40] The following model was used for data analysis: Yij = μ + ɑi + eij, where:

Yij: The Jth observation of the ith dose of TechnoCare200

μ: Overall mean

ɑi: Effect of the TechnoCare200 (I = 1, 2)

eij: Unexplained error.

Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean.

Results

Preliminary Strain Characterization: as shown in Table 4 and Figures 1 and 2, among the four preselected B. licheniformis strains (BC1–BC4), strain BC4 (DSM 33806) exhibited the strongest inhibition against C. perfringens. This strain produced the largest inhibition zone against field isolates of C. perfringens and E. cecorum compared with other Bacillus spp. [Figure 1]. Stability tests confirmed that B. coagulans DSM 32016 spores maintained high recovery rates after pelleting at 100°C [Figure 2a]. In addition, B. coagulans DSM 32016 produced L-lactic acid at levels comparable to those of typical homofermentative lactic acid bacteria such as E. faecium [Figure 2b].

The impact on chicken performance showed that birds receiving TechnoCare200 showed consistent improvements in growth performance compared with controls [Tables 3 and 5]. BW was significantly higher in the treated group at days 14, 21, 28, and 35, with increases ranging from +1.7% to +4.9% (P ≤ 0.05). Cumulative BWG (days 1–35) improved by + 5.0%, with the most pronounced increase observed between days 28 and 35 (+10.8%, P = 0.001).

Feed intake was slightly higher in the treated birds, with a + 2.0% increase in total feed consumption over the trial (P = 0.033). Importantly, TechnoCare200 supplementation improved feed efficiency: cumulative FCR was reduced by 2.7% compared with controls (1.426 vs. 1.466, P = 0.023). GR also improved, with the treated group showing a + 7.1% higher GR at 35 days (P = 0.043). These results demonstrate that TechnoCare200 positively influenced growth, feed utilization, and efficiency of broiler chickens. At 35 days of age, TechnoCare200 supplementation resulted in a significant increase of 9.2% in PN compared with the control group (P = 0.005). While the coefficient of variation was numerically higher in the treated group (9.1% vs. 8.3%), this difference was not statistically analysed. Birds receiving TechnoCare200 also reached a higher maximum weight (+11.8%) and maintained a greater minimum BW (+16.8%), indicating improved flock uniformity and overall performance potential.

The impact on carcass characteristics at d 35 is presented in Tables 6-8, which declared a significant increase in the weight and weight % of the front parts, weight of breast meat, weight of carcass meat, and intestinal diameter in the T group compared to the C group (P ≤ 0.05). A numerical increase in intestinal length, abdominal fat%, and proventriculus (weight and weight% %) in the T group vs. the C group was also detected. The effect on immune organs at d 35 indicated a numerical increase in BF weight and thymus gland weight, and weight% in the T group compared to the C group, as shown in Table 8.

For assessing immune status, the impact on humoral immunity revealed a significant increase in antibody levels against NDV vaccination in the T group compared to the C group at d 14 and d 21 (P ≤ 0.05) [Table 9]. In addition, there was a significant increase in antibody titers against AIV (H9) vaccination (P ≤ 0.05) with a numerical increase in antibody titers against AIV (H5N1, H5N8) and IBV vaccination in the T group compared to the C group [Table 10]. The effect on DTH response demonstrated a significant increase in the T group at 24 and 48 h after inoculation (P ≤ 0.05) [Table 11].

The effects of TechnoCare200 supplementation on intestinal histomorphology are shown in Table 12. In the duodenum, birds receiving TechnoCare200 had significantly greater villus height (1666 ± 32 μm) compared with the control group (1271 ± 25 μm; P = 0.035). Villus width was also higher in the treated group (P = 0.012), while crypt depth did not differ between groups (P = 0.852). Consequently, the villus/crypt ratio was markedly improved in the TechnoCare200 group (6.4 ± 0.30) compared with controls (4.9 ± 0.30; P = 0.008). Similarly, in the jejunum, villus height was significantly greater in the treated group (1377 ± 71 μm) than in controls (1206 ± 36 μm; P = 0.023). No significant differences were observed in villus width (P = 0.724) or crypt depth (P = 0.953). However, the villus/crypt ratio was higher in birds supplemented with TechnoCare200 (7.4 ± 0.30) compared with controls (6.7 ± 0.10; P = 0.009).

Photomicrographs shown in Figure 4 display the microscopic appearance of immune organs from the experimental groups. The C group (a-c) exhibited normal histological architecture in the thymus, spleen, and BF. Conversely, the T group (d-f) also displayed normal histology of these immune organs, but with a mild increase in the size and density of lymphoid follicles. In addition, photomicrographs in Figure 5 illustrate the microscopic appearance of various intestinal segments from the experimental groups. In the C group (a-d), the duodenum and jejunum showed normal histological architecture. Meanwhile, in the T group (e-h), the duodenum maintained normal histology (e-f), while the jejunum exhibited moderate increases in villus height, width, and crypt depth (g-h).

Discussion

Food safety is one of the most pressing challenges facing the poultry farming industry today. There is a direct relationship between consumer confidence and the safety and quality of the products they choose to purchase.[13] The poultry industry often faces stressful conditions, including disease outbreaks and deteriorating environmental conditions, which can lead to significant economic losses.[41] Probiotics are live microorganisms that help establish a beneficial microflora in the gut.[4243] They can thrive in the intestinal tract of birds, promoting a balanced microflora.[44] Probiotics can consist of either single or mixed cultures of live microorganisms that provide health benefits to the host.[45] The use of probiotic products is particularly important for newly hatched chicks, as they are highly vulnerable when they enter commercial production systems.[46]

Probiotics play a crucial role in maintaining a healthy balance of gut microflora, which helps in preventing the growth of harmful bacteria in the GIT. These beneficial microorganisms, previously referred to as ecological health control products, flourish in the intestinal tracts of birds. As a result, they create a balanced microflora that supports healthier digestive systems in chickens, leading to improved digestion and nutrient absorption.[47] This reflected by the observed decrease in FCR during the final week (28–35 days) may be attributed to the sharp increase in BWG in the TechnoCare200 group compared to the control. Such improvement in feed efficiency at later growth stages, though less commonly reported, has been documented when growth-promoting additives enhance nutrient absorption and intestinal health. Probiotics reduce inflammation in the intestinal walls, preventing tissue damage and promoting a healthy gut balance. This balance is crucial for optimal bird performance and health, as it modifies gut microbiota, reduces inflammatory responses, lowers serum cholesterol, and enhances mineral absorption.[48] Probiotics may promote growth by increasing the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and selectively regulating insulin signaling in various tissues.[49] They attain this by competing for physical attachment sites and producing antimicrobial compounds such as bacteriocins or colicins through metabolic reactions.[5051] Probiotics offer additional benefits, including increased secretion of intestinal enzymes and reduced amounts of ammonia and phenolic compounds.[52] Probiotics are known to enhance and regulate the immune system, which is a significant mechanism of action.[53545556] This improvement has been identified as a crucial area for assessing the positive effects of probiotic supplementation. The improvement in villus morphology observed in TechnoCare200-supplemented birds suggests enhanced intestinal absorptive capacity. Increased villus height is widely associated with a larger surface area for nutrient absorption, while higher villus/crypt ratios indicate more efficient intestinal function and reduced turnover of epithelial cells. Similar findings have been reported by previous studies on gut stabilizers and probiotics in broilers, where villus elongation and improved villus/crypt ratios correlated with better growth performance and feed efficiency.[5758] In the present study, the absence of differences in crypt depth between groups indicates that TechnoCare200 primarily promoted villus development without altering crypt cell proliferation. The significant improvements in both duodenal and jejunal villus height align with the observed enhancements in growth performance parameters, suggesting that improved gut morphology may be a key mechanism underlying the growth-promoting effect of TechnoCare200. These results support the hypothesis that TechnoCare200 supplementation enhances gut health by improving the structural integrity of the intestinal mucosa, which in turn facilitates better nutrient utilization and growth.[57]

Especially, lactic acid-producing bacteria are widely recognised for their broad range of positive effects on both human and livestock health.[1159] A study by Wang et al.[60] examined the impact of the lactic acid-producing probiotic Pediococcus acidilactici in broiler chicken diets and found significant improvements in various performance parameters. Similarly, lactic acid-producing B. coagulans DSM 32016 has shown positive effects on broiler growth performance, as well as on the laying performance of breeder broilers, and aqua applications.[252661] Several studies have highlighted the diverse modes of action of B. coagulans in improving intestinal health and overall well-being, including its significant role in modulating the microbiome and immune system, reducing inflammation, and alleviating the negative impacts of intestinal pathogens,[246263] suggesting that its lactic acid production contributes to these effects. Lactic acid is a key intermediate in several host-related processes with multiple benefits. It promotes barrier integrity and epithelial regeneration in the gut by supporting butyrate-producing bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae, which are common in poultry intestines.[202164] It lowers intestinal pH, inhibiting pH-sensitive pathogens and increasing mineral solubility.[222365] Lactic acid also interacts with immune cells, acting anti-inflammatory and promoting immune homeostasis.[6667] In poultry, where Lactobacillus spp. dominate the GIT, lactic acid serves as an energy source for the brain and heart and is converted into glucose in the liver and kidneys, aiding metabolic glucose homeostasis.[6869] B. licheniformis is widely recognized for producing antimicrobial substances, including bacteriocins,[70] making such bacteria a viable component of environmentally friendly alternatives to antibiotics for pathogen control in livestock.[7172] The results demonstrated the strain-dependent capabilities of B. licheniformis in inhibiting field-isolated pathogenic C. perfringens and E. cecorum. The most efficient suppression of C. perfringens was observed with the preselected B. licheniformis strain BC4 (DSM 33806), which played a key role in its inclusion in the probiotic combination in TechnoCare200 as B. licheniformis DSM 33806. This strain also showed the strongest inhibition against pathogenic field isolates of E. cecorum and C. perfringens when compared to other Bacillus spp. However, in vitro evaluations cannot fully replicate the complexity of the intestinal environment, and strain-specific capabilities may vary depending on environmental factors such as nutrient availability, host–microbe interactions, and interspecies interactions within the microbiome.[73] Therefore, in vivo studies focusing on intestinal pathogen suppression by specific strains are warranted for further investigation. Based on the aforementioned modes of action special for both strains in TechnoCare200, the positive outcome on growth performance, feed conversion, and intestinal development observed in the present broiler study was most likely driven by the interplay of their different modes of action, suggesting a synergistic effect in vivo.

In conclusion, the findings of the present study regarding reduced mortality, improved growth performance, and enhanced economic profitability are consistent with those reported in previous studies conducted by 0.[114773] These results have the potential to transform poultry health management by providing effective and sustainable solutions for the industry. The gut flora stabilizer used in this investigation is especially beneficial for promoting growth and enhancing gut health in broiler chickens. This could lead to significant economic advantages for large-scale production farms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that supplementing broiler diets with a novel gut flora stabilizer containing B. coagulans DSM 32016 and B. licheniformis DSM 33806 can enhance growth performance, immune function, and intestinal health. The supplemented birds showed improved villus development and lymphoid activity, reflecting better gut integrity and immune readiness. These findings support the value of targeted probiotic use in promoting sustainable, health-oriented broiler production. Future research should aim to optimize dosing and further elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
