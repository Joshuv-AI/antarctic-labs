Label quality as a bottleneck for YOLO detection in all-weather autonomous driving using the MUSES dataset
Authors: Dalal Z. Zreiqat, Vinh Nguyen, Nathir A. Rawashdeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autonomous-vehicle perception models perform well in clear daytime but degrade under adverse weather and low illumination. We quantify how annotation quality affects YOLO-based 2D detection using the forward-facing RGB subset of the MUSES dataset spanning clear, rain, fog, snow, and night. Focusing on lightweight detectors (YOLOv11n/YOLOv11s) to reflect embedded ADAS constraints, we observe systematic label noise (missing objects, mislocalized boxes, and class inconsistencies) that limits achievable accuracy. We therefore introduce a semi-automatic label-upgrading pipeline: a high-capacity teacher produces candidate labels, which are then verified and corrected by a human annotator. Under identical training settings, retraining on the cleaned annotations improves mAP@0.5 from approximately 0.13 to 0.56–0.62 across conditions, indicating that supervision quality is a major bottleneck for all-weather detection and that data-centric fixes can unlock large gains even for lightweight models.

Introduction

Autonomous driving perception has advanced rapidly, and modern detectors can achieve strong accuracy in clear, daytime scenes [1]. However, this progress does not transfer uniformly across operating conditions. In adverse weather and low illumination (e.g., fog, snow, heavy rain, dust, and night), visibility degradation and occlusion can cause missed detections, poor localization of distant objects, and false positives. Crucially, these errors arise in safety-critical situations, where reliable perception is most needed, making all-weather robustness a persistent barrier to dependable autonomy [2].

YOLO-style detectors and related real-time architectures remain popular in autonomous-driving stacks due to their accuracy–latency trade-off and deployability on embedded hardware [3–5]. Yet numerous studies report sharp performance drops under poor visibility, even with common countermeasures such as data augmentation or domain adaptation [6]. To benchmark these failure modes, adverse-weather datasets such as DAWN and ACDC were introduced to capture challenging conditions beyond clear-weather scenes [7, 8]. More recently, MUSES extended this line of work by providing a multi-sensor benchmark under uncertainty, covering diverse weather and lighting conditions with synchronized RGB, LiDAR, radar, event cameras, and IMU/GNSS [9].

While most prior work attributes robustness gaps primarily to model design or domain shift, an orthogonal factor is often implicitly assumed: annotation quality. In practice, adverse-weather data can exhibit systematic label noise that affects both training and evaluation. If label quality varies with weather or illumination, conclusions about model robustness can be confounded: a detector may appear to fail under fog or night partly because the supervision is incomplete or inconsistent. Motivated by this concern, we isolate and quantify the impact of supervision quality on adverse-weather detection by keeping the detector family and training protocol fixed.

Concretely, we study YOLO-based 2D detection on a forward-facing RGB subset of MUSES. Training lightweight models (YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s) on the original annotations yields low mAP@0.5, and a targeted annotation audit reveals severe issues, including unlabeled but clearly visible objects and misclassified or poorly aligned bounding boxes. To address this, we propose a semi-automatic label-upgrading pipeline: a high-capacity teacher (YOLOv12xL) generates candidate pseudo-labels for all images, followed by human verification and correction. Under identical training settings, retraining YOLOv11n/YOLOv11s on the cleaned annotations substantially improves detection accuracy, indicating that supervision quality is a major factor limiting performance in this setting, while architecture, domain shift, object scale, and weather severity may also contribute to the remaining errors.

Related work

This section reviews related work on autonomous-driving perception benchmarks and adverse-weather robustness with emphasis on datasets, evaluation protocols, and label quality. We then position our contribution in the context of label noise and relabeling for adverse weather object detection.

Adverse-weather robustness

Early advances in autonomous-driving perception were largely enabled by large-scale benchmarks collected primarily under standard visibility. The KITTI vision benchmark suite [10] established widely used tasks for road-scene understanding and object detection. Cityscapes [11] advanced urban scene perception by providing 5k finely annotated images and an additional 20k coarsely labeled images, supporting systematic evaluation of semantic and instance-level methods. To broaden domain diversity beyond a single geographic setting, Mapillary Vistas [12] released 25k high-resolution street-level images with fine-grained labels. In parallel, BDD100K [13] scaled driving data to large video collections and enabled heterogeneous multi-task learning. Despite their impact, these benchmarks remain dominated by normal conditions and provide limited coverage of severe adverse weather and nighttime scenes, motivating robustness-oriented datasets and evaluation protocols that consider not only domain shift and model capacity but also the reliability of ground-truth supervision.

To explicitly target adverse visibility, ACDC [8] covers fog, nighttime, rain, and snow, and provides uncertainty masks for regions where annotation is inherently ambiguous. WildDash [14] further emphasizes hazard-aware evaluation by curating challenging failure cases to stress-test perception systems under visual hazards. More recently, WildDash 2 [15] highlighted the importance of harmonized label policies and standardized evaluation protocols across driving datasets, showing that robustness conclusions can depend as much on annotation consistency and test design as on model architecture.

Multi-sensor datasets and the role of annotation quality

While many robustness benchmarks focus on single-camera inputs, real vehicles often rely on multi-sensor suites, and adverse conditions can make purely visual annotation inherently ambiguous. MUSES [9] is a multi-sensor driving benchmark with synchronized cameras, LiDAR, radar, event cameras, and IMU/GNSS, together with 2D panoptic annotations. Importantly, MUSES adopts a two-stage annotation process: an initial labeling stage (H1) and a refined stage (H2) supported by multi-sensor evidence and additional quality control. Their analysis shows that annotation policy strongly affects label completeness and evaluation outcomes: H2 contains roughly \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.5\times$$\end{document} more instances than H1, and they report an 11.1% drop in PQ from 58.0% (H1) to 46.9% (H2), reflecting the impact of a stricter and more complete supervision protocol. They further report that evaluation outcomes and downstream performance are sensitive to the annotation stage, indicating that supervision quality can directly limit achievable accuracy under challenging conditions [9]. Beyond driving-specific benchmarks, several studies have shown that object-detection datasets frequently contain missing, mislocalized, or inconsistent annotations, and that auditing and correcting such errors can yield substantial gains without changing the underlying architecture. To move beyond aggregate mAP and better attribute performance changes, TIDE decomposes detection errors into interpretable categories (e.g., localization, classification, missed detections, and duplicates), enabling a more precise separation of supervision-induced effects from model limitations [16–18].

Positioning of our work

In contrast to work that emphasizes new architectures or weather-specific modules, this study shows that annotation quality can explain a substantial portion of the apparent performance gap in all-weather detection, while other factors such as domain shift, object scale, class imbalance, and weather severity may also contribute. Using the same dataset (MUSES), lightweight models (YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s), and the same training configuration, we show that relabeling yields gains of tens of mAP@0.5 points across conditions. This highlights the importance of revisiting dataset supervision when benchmarking adverse-weather perception and provides a label-audited baseline for future architectural or multi-modal extensions.

Dataset and label analysis

MUSES adverse-weather subset

All experiments use a subset of the MUSES dataset, a recent multi-sensor benchmark designed for autonomous driving under uncertainty [9]. MUSES provides synchronized RGB cameras, radar, LiDAR, an event camera, and IMU/GNSS across diverse weather and lighting conditions, including clear day, clear night, fog, rain, and snow. In this study, we use only the forward-facing RGB camera and formulate the task as 2D object detection. This setting reflects common ADAS and autonomous-driving deployments, while still exposing detectors to the visibility degradations that MUSES was designed to capture.

We evaluate seven safety-relevant classes: bicycle, bus, car, motorcycle, person, train, and truck. These categories include both conventional vehicles and vulnerable road users (VRUs), which are particularly challenging to detect when objects are small, distant, or partially occluded under adverse weather and low illumination.

Original subset and effective labeled data

The provided RGB-camera subset contains approximately 2,450 images, organized into training, validation, and test splits. During inspection, some images were found to have missing or empty annotation files. These images were not automatically treated as empty-background samples, because some scenes contained visible traffic participants without corresponding labels. Treating such images as negative samples would introduce false-negative supervision and could affect both training and evaluation.

To avoid this issue, images and annotation files were matched by filename before training and evaluation. Images containing visible objects from the seven target classes were corrected when the objects could be verified, while images without visible target-class objects were retained as background samples. As shown in Table 2, the cleaned test set is not perfectly balanced across weather–illumination conditions. Therefore, condition-wise results should be interpreted with this imbalance in mind, especially for low-sample conditions such as fog night.

Compared with standard urban-driving benchmarks such as KITTI or Cityscapes, the MUSES subset considered here is noticeably more challenging [19]. Many objects occupy only a small number of pixels, appear at a long distance, or are partially occluded by other vehicles and roadside infrastructure. Adverse weather introduces additional degradations, such as rain streaks, fog, snowflakes, motion blur, headlight glare, and low contrast in night scenes. In combination, these factors make it much harder for cars, trucks, buses, and especially pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcyclists to distinguish themselves from the background.

Qualitative inspection indicates that these effects are most severe in foggy-night and snowy-night, where vehicles are barely visible even to a human observer. As shown in Sect. 5, this results in a significant performance gap between clear-weather and adverse-weather conditions, despite using modern YOLO detectors [8, 9, 19].

Methodology

Our objective is to quantify the extent to which annotation quality limits all-weather object detection on MUSES and to demonstrate that systematic relabeling can unlock substantial performance gains, even for lightweight YOLO models. Task and model setup

We formulate the problem as 2D object detection using the forward-facing RGB camera of MUSES and evaluate seven traffic-participant classes: bicycle, bus, car, motorcycle, person, train, and truck. To reflect deployment-oriented constraints, we study two lightweight detectors from the YOLOv11 family: YOLOv11n (2.6M parameters, 6.3 GFLOPs) and YOLOv11s (9.4M parameters, 21.3 GFLOPs).

The examples highlight illumination variation, ranging from clear daytime scenes to low-light nighttime scenarios. Such variation affects visibility, contrast, and object appearance, and poses additional challenges for object detection models. Including these conditions is essential for evaluating robustness under realistic driving environments.Fig. 2Representative RGB images from the MUSES dataset illustrating clear and low-illumination driving conditions

The experiments start from the original YOLO-format annotations provided with the RGB subset of MUSES, which nominally contains approximately 2,450 images split into 1,500 training, 250 validation, and 700 test samples. In practice, however, the effective supervision is weaker: a non-negligible fraction of images lack label files entirely or contain only partial annotations, particularly under adverse weather and low-light conditions. This discrepancy motivates a careful examination of annotation quality rather than model capacity alone. Step 2: baseline training on original annotations

YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s were initialized using pretrained Ultralytics weights and trained under the same configuration. Both models were trained for 200 epochs with 640 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 640 input resolution and batch size 32. The Ultralytics automatic optimizer setting was used with an initial learning rate of 0.01, final learning-rate factor of 0.01, momentum of 0.937, weight decay of 0.0005, and 3 warmup epochs. Default YOLO augmentations were used, including HSV augmentation, translation, scaling, horizontal flipping, mosaic augmentation, RandAugment, and random erasing. Validation was performed on the held-out validation split during training, and final results were reported on the held-out test split using precision, recall, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95. The same protocol was used for both original-annotation and cleaned-annotation experiments to ensure a controlled comparison.

Step 3: diagnosing annotation noise

To identify the causes of poor baseline performance, we manually inspected test images spanning all weather and illumination regimes. We observed four recurring annotation issues. First, missing-instance errors occur when visible traffic participants, especially small or distant vehicles, pedestrians, cyclists, or motorcycles, are not annotated. Second, localization errors occur when bounding boxes are shifted, too small, truncate part of the object, or include excessive background. These errors are often exacerbated by low contrast, motion blur, glare, rain, snow, or low illumination. Third, class-consistency errors occur when visually similar categories are confused, such as car versus bus or bicycle versus motorcycle. Fourth, some images contain visible objects but have missing or empty annotation files, which can incorrectly treat object-containing scenes as background images if not handled carefully. These annotation problems are more frequent under adverse weather and low illumination, where object boundaries are difficult to verify, leading to incomplete and unreliable supervision during baseline training and evaluation.

Step 4: teacher-based pseudo-labeling with human verification

To upgrade the annotations without full manual re-annotation, we applied a high-capacity teacher detector, YOLOv12xL, to all images in the selected subset to generate candidate bounding boxes and class labels. YOLOv12xL was selected because it provides higher detection capacity than the lightweight YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s student models used in the final experiments. The teacher predictions were used only as candidate annotations and were not treated as final ground truth. Because no custom confidence threshold was specified during teacher prediction, candidate annotations were generated using the default Ultralytics prediction confidence threshold and then manually verified before being used as cleaned annotations.

To reduce dependence on the teacher model alone and mitigate potential teacher bias, all pseudo-labels were reviewed by a single human annotator. Correct detections were retained, bounding-box boundaries were refined, incorrect class labels were corrected, false-positive detections were removed, and missed but clearly visible objects were added manually. Ambiguous objects with very low visibility, severe occlusion, or unclear class identity were handled conservatively and excluded unless sufficient visual evidence was available. The verification process was guided by three quality-control criteria: label completeness, localization accuracy, and class consistency. Detailed annotation timing and the exact proportion of accepted versus manually corrected boxes were not recorded during verification. However, the revised pipeline description clarifies the main types of manual intervention, including box refinement, class correction, false-positive removal, and manual addition of missed visible objects.

Step 5: cleaned subset construction and retraining

After verification, we applied basic quality checks and retained image–label pairs with valid annotations for the seven target classes. The resulting cleaned subset contains 1,281 training images, 215 validation images, and 675 test images. The cleaned annotations were used for all three splits: training, validation, and testing. Thus, the performance improvement after relabeling reflects two effects. First, the models were trained with more complete and consistent supervision. Second, evaluation was performed using cleaner ground-truth labels, which provides a more reliable estimate of detection performance.

Crucially, YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s were retrained on the cleaned subset using the same architecture, optimization strategy, and training schedule as in the baseline experiments. Therefore, the main experimental variable was annotation quality, and the performance changes observed in Sect. 5 can be largely attributed to improved supervision quality rather than changes in the model architecture or training protocol.

Experimental results

This section evaluates how annotation quality affects all-weather 2D object detection on MUSES using two lightweight YOLOv11 variants: YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s. To test whether adverse-condition performance is limited by model capacity or by noisy supervision, we compare models trained on the original annotations with models trained on the cleaned-annotation subset under identical settings. We report overall results across weather–illumination conditions, training dynamics, and per-condition performance before and after relabeling.

Two additional patterns are noteworthy. First, the results do not exhibit a stable capacity trend: YOLOv11s does not consistently outperform YOLOv11n, suggesting that architectural scaling provides limited benefit when the supervision signal is noisy. Second, apparent condition-to-condition differences should be interpreted cautiously because label noise can confound robustness comparisons. For example, foggy daytime yields slightly higher mAP than clear daytime for both models, despite lower visibility. A plausible explanation is that fog reduces long-range background detail and decreases the number of visible instances, producing simpler scenes dominated by nearer, larger objects that are more likely to be annotated consistently. In contrast, clear daytime scenes contain more objects across a wider scale range, making reported performance more sensitive to missing instances and mislocalized boxes.

Overall, Table 4 suggests that the observed degradation under the original labels reflects not only intrinsic visibility challenges but also a supervision-induced ceiling that obscures the true robustness characteristics of the detectors.

Discussion

The experiments demonstrate that annotation quality is a major factor affecting all-weather object detection performance on the MUSES subset. When training on the original labels, both YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s converge in terms of losses but achieve very low test accuracy, with mAP@0.5 close to 0.13. This mismatch indicates that the models are not only limited by capacity or optimization; rather, they are also learning from a supervision signal that is incomplete and inconsistent. In particular, missing boxes and partial labels create a training objective that may implicitly reward predicting fewer objects, especially for easier categories such as large cars, while penalizing correct detections that do not appear in the ground truth. Consequently, increasing model size does not yield a clear benefit under noisy supervision, since both models are constrained by the same label-quality limitations.

Existing noise-handling methods for object detection include robust loss functions, sample reweighting, pseudo-label filtering, and post-hoc error analysis tools. These approaches can reduce the effect of noisy annotations during training or help identify problematic labels. In this study, we focus on a data-centric annotation-cleaning strategy rather than comparing multiple noise-robust training methods. This choice was made to isolate the effect of annotation quality under a fixed detector family and training protocol. Future work could compare the proposed cleaned-annotation pipeline with noise-robust training strategies to determine whether both approaches provide complementary gains.

After applying teacher-assisted pseudo-labeling followed by human verification, the same architectures and training recipe produce substantially higher and more meaningful performance. Averaged over all eight conditions, mAP@0.5 rises from approximately 0.13 to 0.56 for YOLOv11n and to 0.62 for YOLOv11s, with similarly large gains in mAP@0.5:0.95. These improvements suggest that the cleaned annotations better reflect the true detection task by providing more complete coverage of traffic participants and tighter bounding boxes. This enables the models to generalize more effectively across weather and illumination conditions rather than overfitting to annotation artifacts. Importantly, once annotations are cleaned, the advantage of YOLOv11s becomes more visible: the small model consistently outperforms YOLOv11n, showing that additional model capacity becomes more useful when the supervision signal is reliable.

The per-condition analysis provides additional insight into the remaining failure modes. Fog-night remains the most challenging split even after annotation cleaning. The remaining errors in fog-night and snow-night conditions are likely caused by a combination of visual ambiguity, residual label noise, and model limitations. Visual ambiguity is a major factor because low illumination, reduced contrast, glare, fog, and snow obscure object boundaries and make small or distant objects difficult to distinguish from the background. Residual label noise may also remain because some objects are only partially visible or too ambiguous to annotate reliably, even during human verification. Finally, the models used in this study rely only on RGB images, which limits their ability to recover objects under severe visibility degradation. Snow-night also shows a larger performance spread between the two models, suggesting that higher-capacity backbones can better handle complex textures and background clutter, but still require stronger cues than RGB alone.

Although the results show that annotation quality is a major bottleneck in this MUSES subset, it should not be interpreted as the only factor limiting all-weather detection performance. Other factors, including domain shift, class imbalance, object scale, low illumination, and weather severity, can also contribute to performance degradation. In addition, the MUSES splits are not perfectly balanced across weather–illumination conditions, and the effective number of labeled images varies across conditions. Therefore, condition-wise results should be interpreted with this imbalance in mind. These findings suggest that improving supervision quality substantially reduces one important source of error, while further improvements in extreme conditions may require model-centric improvements, domain-specific augmentation, exposure normalization, or additional sensing modalities such as thermal or radar. Future work could also extend the proposed pipeline using large multimodal models and image-quality assessment methods. Such models could help identify visually ambiguous frames, compare image quality across weather and illumination conditions, and prioritize samples that require human annotation review. In particular, CLIP-based or magnitude-aware image-quality assessment methods may support no-reference evaluation of low-contrast, foggy, snowy, or night-time scenes where object boundaries are difficult to verify from RGB images alone.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that, in the studied adverse-weather driving subset, annotation quality can be a major factor limiting object detection performance. When trained on the original MUSES RGB subset annotations, YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s plateau at very low accuracy despite stable optimization, which is consistent with learning under incomplete and inconsistent supervision. After label upgrading through teacher-assisted pseudo-labeling followed by human verification, the same architectures achieve large and consistent gains across weather and illumination conditions. This result shows that model capacity becomes beneficial only when supervision is reliable. More broadly, robustness conclusions drawn from adverse-weather datasets can be confounded by label noise that varies systematically with visibility. Future work should therefore combine model-centric advances with standardized, auditable data-centric protocols, including error decomposition and uncertainty-aware labeling, to produce dependable all-weather perception evaluations.
