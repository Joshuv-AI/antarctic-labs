Safety evaluation of CD3×CD20 bispecific antibodies: a pharmacovigilance study using FDA adverse event reporting system

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo characterise post-marketing adverse event (AE) reporting patterns associated with CD3×CD20 bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) using multi-method disproportionality analysis and to identify potential safety signals warranting further investigation.DesignRetrospective pharmacovigilance study using disproportionality analysis with multiple complementary methods.SettingFood and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System database.ParticipantsA total of 2237 AE reports associated with CD3×CD20 BsAbs (1135 for epcoritamab, 673 for glofitamab and 429 for mosunetuzumab) were submitted from the fourth quarter of 2022 to the first quarter of 2025.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes included identification of statistically significant AE signals using reporting odds ratio (ROR) and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network with information component as the principal signal detection methods, while proportional reporting ratio was only used as a supplementary measure to enhance the robustness of signal detection. Secondary outcomes included subgroup analyses by sex and age, analysis of fatal outcomes and time-to-onset patterns using Kaplan-Meier analysis and Weibull distribution.ResultsA total of 93 AE signals were identified for epcoritamab, 65 for glofitamab and 41 for mosunetuzumab. The three disproportionality methods showed near-complete agreement (>98% concordance) in signal detection. Signals were detected for several AEs not currently listed in product labelling. For epcoritamab, unlabelled signals included progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (ROR (95% CI) 13.28 (5.51 to 31.97)), haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (ROR (95% CI) 18.19 (10.75 to 30.79)) and disseminated intravascular coagulation (ROR (95% CI) 8.76 (3.28 to 23.39)). For glofitamab, unlabelled signals included disseminated tuberculosis (ROR (95% CI) 53.30 (17.10 to 166.13)) and cerebral haemorrhage (ROR (95% CI) 6.08 (2.28 to 16.22)). For mosunetuzumab, tumour lysis syndrome (ROR (95% CI) 29.91 (11.19 to 79.97)) and uveitis (ROR (95% CI) 10.75 (3.46 to 33.41)) were detected. With respect to patient characteristics, cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome were more frequently reported in younger patients treated with epcoritamab, while pyrexia was more commonly reported among elderly patients receiving glofitamab or mosunetuzumab. Among reports with available dates, Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that most reported onset times fell within the first month following treatment initiation. In the indication-restricted sensitivity analysis, most signals remained detectable, whereas certain signals, including haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis with epcoritamab, were sensitive to comparator selection.ConclusionsThis study provides a systematic characterisation of post-marketing AE reporting patterns for CD3×CD20 BsAbs, identifying several signals for AEs not currently listed in product labelling. However, disproportionality signals do not establish causality, and clinical correlation is essential. These findings may help inform post-marketing pharmacovigilance and prioritise signals for further clinical and epidemiological validation.Keywords: CD3×CD20bispecific antibodies, real-world data, drug safety, disproportionality analyses, FAERS, pharmacovigilance

Introduction

Non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) is the most common haematological malignancy. According to global statistics, approximately 550 000 new cases and 250 000 deaths were reported in 2022.1 B-cell NHL (B-NHL) comprises 85%–90% of all NHL diagnoses, establishing this entity as the dominant disease phenotype and primary therapeutic focus.2 The integration of CD20 monoclonal antibodies with chemotherapy, along with autologous stem cell transplantation, has significantly improved survival in patients with B-NHL.3 Nevertheless, up to 50% of patients still undergo relapse or acquire resistance,4 5 underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies to enhance prognosis.

Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) have emerged as transformative cancer therapeutics in recent years. CD3×CD20 BsAbs, in particular, have provided novel therapeutic options for B-NHL. By simultaneously targeting CD20 on B cells and CD3 on T cells, these agents facilitate the formation of immunological synapses. This dual-targeting mechanism effectively activates endogenous T-cell cytotoxicity, promoting the release of cytolytic mediators and inducing tumour cell death.6

Recent clinical trial data have demonstrated that overall response rates for B-NHL patients treated with CD3×CD20 BsAbs range from 65% to 90%, with complete response rates from 40% to 60%.7 8 Given the favourable clinical efficacy and manageable safety profile, several CD3×CD20 BsAbs have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), including epcoritamab,9 glofitamab10 and mosunetuzumab.11 Table 1 summarises these agents and their corresponding indications.

Adverse events (AEs) associated with CD3×CD20 BsAbs have increasingly attracted clinical attention. These agents have been reported to be associated with severe toxicities resulting from rapid T-cell activation, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). Other commonly reported AEs include neutropenia and fever. It is important to note that certain AEs have the potential to be life-threatening in rare instances.12

As CD3×CD20 BsAbs are increasingly adopted in clinical practice, it is essential to understand the associated AEs to optimise patient care.13 Nevertheless, CD3×CD20 BsAbs represent an emerging therapeutic class with limited post-marketing surveillance. Initial safety evaluations have primarily been derived from clinical trials, which generally involve small sample sizes and limited follow-up durations. These factors underscore the imperative for comprehensive safety assessments in actual clinical practice.

The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database offers publicly accessible, timely real-world data on drugs and therapeutic biologics, which serves as a critical platform for in-depth mining of pharmacovigilance information.14 Leveraging data from FAERS, this study aimed to systematically characterise the AE reporting patterns associated with three FDA-approved CD3×CD20 BsAbs (epcoritamab, glofitamab and mosunetuzumab) through multimethod disproportionality analysis, and to examine the robustness of detected signals using an indication-restricted sensitivity analysis. The study is descriptive and hypothesis-generating in nature, aimed at identifying potential safety signals warranting further investigation and does not attempt to establish causal relationships between drug exposure and AEs.

Method

Data source

This study was a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis based on the FAERS database(dataset). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the READUS-PV guideline for reporting disproportionality analyses.15 The study period was set from fourth quarter of 2022 (Q4 2022) to first quarter of 2025 (Q1 2025). The generic name and trade name of CD3×CD20 BsAbs approved by FDA (epcoritamab, glofitamab and mosunetuzumab) were employed as search terms. Extracted information covered demographics, medication details, AEs, outcomes, report sources, duration of drug therapy and medication pointers. FAERS is a publicly available database that does not contain any personally identifiable information.

Research design

The data cleaning process was performed according to FDA-recommended protocols, and removed duplicate reports. In particular, for reports with identical case identifier (CASEID) in the demographic and administrative information (DEMO) table, only the most recent FDA_DT was retained. For reports with identical CASEID and FDA_DT, the report with the highest PRIMARYID was retained. To enhance the accuracy, analyses were restricted to reports identifying CD3×CD20 BsAbs as the primary suspected drugs. The Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (V.27.1) was employed for the standardised coding, classification and description of the AE reports, using the system organ class (SOC) and preferred terms (PT) to ensure consistency. Statistical analysis

Disproportionality analysis methods are established on the principles of 2×2 contingency tables. By comparing the proportion of a specific event associated with the target drug to that observed across all other drugs (background data), the methods enable identification of potential associations between drugs and events, which is widely applied to detect adverse drug reaction signals.16 In our study, frequentist (reporting odds ratio (ROR)) and Bayesian approaches (Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN))17 were employed as the primary disproportionality analysis methods to assess potential target drug-related AEs. The ROR method offers computational efficiency and has been shown to have advantages over the proportional reporting ratio (PRR) in certain contexts.18 Bayesian approaches, such as BCPNN, incorporate prior information and provide credible intervals that account for sparse data. The BCPNN method generates the information component (IC), a measure of disproportionality based on information theory.19 The IC is calculated as: IC=log₂P(drug, AE)P(drug) × P(AE), where probabilities are estimated using Bayesian methods with informative priors. A signal is considered significant when IC₀₂₅ (the lower bound of the 95% credible interval) exceeds 0. The PRR was only used as a supplementary metric to enhance the robustness of signal detection.20 PRR is calculated as: PRR = a/(a+b)c/(c+d), where a=reports of target drug with target AE, b=reports of target drug with other AEs, c=reports of other drugs with target AE, and d=reports of other drugs with other AEs. A signal is considered significant when PRR≥2, χ² ≥ 4 and the number of cases is ≥3. The specific algorithm and evaluation criteria are presented in the online supplemental table S1. Signals consistently identified by all three methods were considered concordant.21

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex (male, female) and age (18–64 years, ≥65 years). Patients under the age of 18 were excluded due to an insufficient number of cases for meaningful analysis. To further assess the safety profile of CD3×CD20 BsAbs, we extracted AE reports associated with fatal outcomes additionally.

An indication-restricted sensitivity analysis was conducted to explore the influence of comparator selection on the detected signals. Specifically, the background comparator was restricted to reports associated with lymphoma-related indications, allowing examination of whether signals persisted when compared against a more clinically relevant reference group. The underlying disease (B-NHL), prior treatments (including rituximab and chemotherapy), and baseline immune status were identified as potential sources of heterogeneity between the CD3×CD20 BsAb group and the full FAERS background, motivating the use of an indication-restricted comparator. This approach does not attempt to estimate causal effects or to control for confounding in a formal sense, but rather examines the robustness of signal detection under an alternative comparator definition.

The time to onset (TTO) of CD3×CD20 BsAbs-associated AEs was defined as the interval between the treatment start date (START_DT) and the reported AE onset date (EVENT_DT). Kaplan-Meier analysis was used as the primary approach to describe the cumulative distribution of reported AE onset times among reports with valid START_DT and EVENT_DT, providing a non-parametric method that does not assume a specific distribution.22 Weibull distribution analysis, incorporating scale parameter (α) and shape parameter (β), was employed as a complementary parametric approach to characterise the temporal pattern of reported AE onset times.23 The Kaplan-Meier analysis is descriptive in nature; formal between-group comparisons of time-to-onset were not performed, as such comparisons would require adjustment for multiple confounders that are not available in FAERS data. All data analyses were performed using R software (V.4.3.0).

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Descriptive analysis

From Q4 2022 to Q1 2025, a total of 2237 reports of CD3×CD20 BsAbs-associated AEs, for which the drug was identified as the primary suspect, were extracted from the FAERS database (1135 reports for epcoritamab, 673 reports for glofitamab and 429 reports for mosunetuzumab). The baseline characteristics are shown in table 2. Overall, a higher proportion of reports involved male patients compared with females, and among reports with available age information, patients aged 65 years or older accounted for the largest proportion. Among those with available weight information, individuals weighing between 50 and 100 kg accounted for the largest proportion. Most reports were submitted by healthcare professionals. A substantial proportion of reports involved serious outcomes, including death, hospitalisation, life-threatening or other medically significant conditions. The annual number of AE reports exhibited an increasing trend across most BsAbs, except for mosunetuzumab.

Distribution of AEs at SOC

At the SOC level, the proportional distribution of AEs reported with CD3×CD20 BsAbs was systematically evaluated (figure 2). The majority of reported events were concentrated in ‘infections and infestations’ and ‘immune system disorders’. Using both ROR and IC, disproportionality signals were detected for all three CD3×CD20 BsAbs within the following SOCs: ‘infections and infestations’, ‘blood and lymphatic system disorders’, ‘neoplasms benign, malignant and unspecified (including cysts and polyps)’ and ‘immune system disorders’. Drug-specific signal patterns were also observed. For epcoritamab, signals were additionally detected in the SOC category ‘investigations’ and ‘metabolism and nutrition disorders’ (online supplemental table S2). For glofitamab, signals were additionally detected in ‘investigations’, ‘general disorders and administration site conditions’ and ‘metabolism and nutrition disorders’ (online supplemental table S3). For mosunetuzumab, signals were detected in ‘cardiac disorders’ (online supplemental table S4).

Distribution of AEs at PT and subgroup analysis

For epcoritamab, the most frequently reported AEs were CRS (n=352), pyrexia (n=100), and ICANS (n=86). Statistically significant disproportionality signals were detected for CRS (ROR (95% CI) 185.00 (165.23 to 207.15)), cytomegalovirus test positive (ROR (95% CI) 167.36 (95.89 to 292.10)) and tumour flare (ROR (95% CI) 213.11 (104.40 to 435.04)). In addition, signals were detected for several AEs not prominently featured in the current product labelling, including elevated interleukin-2 receptor levels (ROR (95% CI) 459.09 (181.07 to 1163.99)), cytomegalovirus infection reactivation (ROR (95% CI) 41.81 (27.15 to 64.37)) and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (ROR (95% CI) 13.28 (5.51 to 31.97)) (online supplemental table S5). CRS and ICANS were more frequently reported in younger patients (figure 3).

For glofitamab, the most commonly reported AEs were CRS (n=165) and pyrexia (n=64). Disproportionality signals were detected for tumour flare (ROR (95% CI) 836.38 (489.09 to 1430.27)) and CRS (ROR (95% CI) 165.57 (140.71 to 194.81)). Signals were also detected for several unlabelled AEs, including disseminated tuberculosis (ROR (95% CI) 53.30 (17.10 to 166.13)), urosepsis (ROR (95% CI) 13.84 (4.45 to 43.01)) and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) (ROR (95% CI) 10.20 (3.82 to 27.23)) (online supplementaltable S5).

For mosunetuzumab, the most frequently reported AEs were CRS (n=91), pyrexia (n=25) and COVID-19 pneumonia (n=32). Disproportionality signals were detected for tumour flare (ROR (95% CI) 913.98 (464.02 to 1800.26)) and CRS (ROR (95% CI) 173.09 (139.08 to 215.40)) (online supplemental table S5). Pyrexia was more commonly reported in elderly patients receiving glofitamab or mosunetuzumab (figure 3).

Multimethod validation of signal detection

To enhance the robustness of signal detection, three complementary disproportionality methods (ROR, IC and PRR) were applied. The methods showed substantial agreement in identifying signals (online supplemental figure S1). The PRR results are presented in online supplemental table S5. The proportion of signals concordantly detected by all three methods was 98.9% for epcoritamab (92/93), 100% for glofitamab (65/65) and 100% for mosunetuzumab (41/41).

A descriptive comparison of signal patterns across the three CD3×CD20 BsAbs revealed both shared and drug-specific characteristics (onlinesupplemental figures S2S4). CRS and tumour flare were consistently detected across all three drugs. Certain signals were detected only for specific agents, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) were detected with epcoritamab, while disseminated tuberculosis was detected with glofitamab. These differences in signal patterns should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect differences in reporting rather than true pharmacological differences (see Discussion).

Indication-restricted sensitivity analysis

To explore the sensitivity of detected signals to comparator selection, the background comparator was restricted to lymphoma-related reports. This approach examines whether signals remain detectable when the reference group shares similar underlying disease characteristics, rather than using all FAERS reports as the comparator. When CD3×CD20 BsAb reports were compared with a lymphoma-restricted background, ROR estimates were generally lower than those obtained in the unrestricted analysis, but the majority of signals remained detectable. For example, CRS with epcoritamab showed ROR=6.02 in the lymphoma-restricted analysis compared with ROR=185.00 in the unrestricted analysis. Similarly, tumour flare signals remained detectable after restriction: epcoritamab ROR=6.59 (unrestricted: 213.11), glofitamab ROR=31.91 (unrestricted: 836.38) and mosunetuzumab ROR=29.36 (unrestricted: 913.98). However, some signals such as HLH were no longer detectable after lymphoma restriction (epcoritamab: unrestricted ROR=18.19, lymphoma-restricted ROR=2.01), indicating dependence on comparator selection. The forest plot comparing unrestricted and lymphoma-restricted estimates is presented in online supplemental figure S5.

Death outcome

Regarding mortality outcomes, the proportion of fatal AE reports was higher in males than females (epcoritamab: 57.3% vs 35.2%; glofitamab: 55.1% vs 35.5%; mosunetuzumab: 56.0% vs 30.0%), and among fatal-outcome reports with available age information, patients aged 65 years or older accounted for the largest proportion. Most fatal-outcome reports were submitted by MD (online supplemental table S6). Among fatal-outcome reports, CRS and ICANS were the most frequently reported AEs for epcoritamab, and disproportionality signals were detected for both. In the case of glofitamab, frequently reported AEs among fatal-outcome reports included CRS, pyrexia and pneumonia. Disproportionality signals for CRS, ICANS and septic shock were also detected. For mosunetuzumab, COVID-19 pneumonia, pneumonia and CRS were the most frequently reported AEs among fatal-outcome reports (table 3).

TTO analysis

After excluding cases with unknown or inaccurate medication data, TTO analysis was subsequently performed. As illustrated in figure 4, reported onset times for AEs associated with CD3×CD20 BsAbs were predominantly within the first month following treatment initiation. The median TTO was 16 days (IQR: 8–43 days) for epcoritamab, 25.5 days (IQR: 2–93.75 days) for glofitamab and 33 days (IQR: 9–121.5 days) for mosunetuzumab.

Time to onset of adverse events reported with CD3×CD20 BsAbs. BsAbs, bispecific antibodies.

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that the cumulative distribution of reported onset times rose steeply during the first 30 days of treatment, with approximately 61% of reported onset times falling within this period (online supplemental figures S6; S7). Descriptively, the Kaplan-Meier curves showed generally similar patterns across the three drugs, although mosunetuzumab exhibited a slightly more gradual onset pattern. The Weibull distribution analysis, conducted as a complementary parametric approach, indicated a pattern consistent with early failure (β<1) for all three agents (table 4).

Discussion

This study used FAERS database and implemented disproportionality analysis methods to comprehensively map the safety signal profile of CD3×CD20 BsAbs. An upward trend in AE reporting was observed, which may reflect both the increased clinical application of these agents and heightened post-marketing pharmacovigilance efforts. The majority of reports were submitted by healthcare professionals, who are generally considered a reliable source for AE documentation.

The detected signals can be broadly categorised into two groups. The first group comprises AEs that are consistent with the known safety profile of CD3×CD20 BsAbs as documented in product labelling, including CRS, ICANS, neutropenia and infections. The consistent detection of these labelled AEs represents a post-marketing reporting pattern consistent with clinical trial findings. The second group comprises signals for AEs not currently included in product labelling, such as HLH with epcoritamab, DIC with epcoritamab, disseminated tuberculosis with glofitamab, cerebral haemorrhage with glofitamab and uveitis with mosunetuzumab. These signals are hypothesis-generating and warrant further investigation through controlled studies.

In the indication-restricted sensitivity analysis, ROR estimates for many signals were lower when the comparator was restricted to lymphoma-related reports, indicating that the disproportionality estimates are influenced by the choice of reference group. Most signals remained detectable under the restricted comparator, although with lower estimates. However, certain signals—notably HLH with epcoritamab—were no longer detectable after restriction, suggesting that these findings are dependent on comparator selection and should be interpreted with particular caution.

The present findings are broadly consistent with the safety profiles reported in clinical trials for each agent. CRS was the most frequently reported AE across all three drugs,12 consistent with the known mechanism of T-cell engagement.24 Age-related differences in reporting patterns were observed: CRS and ICANS were more frequently reported in younger patients with epcoritamab, while pyrexia was more commonly reported in elderly patients with glofitamab or mosunetuzumab. These patterns may reflect age-related differences in immune response or reporting behaviour and warrant further investigation.25 26 The detection of a progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) signal with epcoritamab, while PML is noted as a potential risk of anti-CD20 therapies,27,29 adds post-marketing reporting data for this AE. Signals for several unlabelled AEs—including DIC,30 disseminated tuberculosis and uveitis—represent hypothesis-generating reporting signals that have not been systematically documented in the clinical trial literature and warrant further validation, although the limited number of reports for some of these events warrants cautious interpretation.

Differences in the signal profiles of the three agents were observed in this study. These differences should be interpreted cautiously, as several factors unrelated to true pharmacological differences may contribute to the observed variation. Potential contributing factors include: (1) differences in molecular structure, such as the bivalent CD20-binding design of glofitamab versus the monovalent design of epcoritamab and mosunetuzumab, which may influence the degree of T-cell activation31; (2) differences in route of administration (subcutaneous for epcoritamab vs intravenous for glofitamab and mosunetuzumab)8 32 33; (3) differences in dosing strategy and step-up protocols; (4) differences in patient selection criteria and disease characteristics across the respective clinical programmes; (5) differences in time since approval and extent of clinical use, which directly affect the number of reports available in FAERS and (6) differences in reporting patterns, including potential stimulated reporting associated with regulatory communications or safety alerts. These remain speculative and cannot be formally tested using FAERS data, and the observed differences should not be interpreted as evidence of differential risk.

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that the majority of reported AE onset dates were within the first month following treatment initiation, consistent with the known pharmacodynamic characteristics of T-cell-engaging agents. The Weibull distribution analysis indicated an early-failure pattern (β<1) for all three agents. These convergent findings suggest that early post-treatment periods may be particularly relevant for clinical awareness and for future studies of AE timing. Future studies should include extended follow-up periods to comprehensively assess the long-term safety profile.

While the safety profiles of CD3×CD20 BsAbs have been characterised in clinical trials, this pharmacovigilance study offers several incremental contributions to the existing literature. First, the identification of signals for AEs not listed in current product labelling generates hypotheses for further clinical investigation. Second, the descriptive comparison of AE reporting patterns across three approved agents using consistent methodology provides a systematic overview of both shared and drug-specific reporting characteristics. Third, the indication-restricted sensitivity analysis provides information about the robustness of detected signals to comparator selection.

It is important to emphasise that disproportionality signals identified through FAERS analysis represent statistical associations rather than established causal relationships. The detection of a signal indicates that a drug-AE pair is reported more frequently than expected based on background reporting patterns, but does not prove that the drug caused the AE.34 Multiple factors, including reporting biases, confounding by indication and stimulated reporting following regulatory communications, can influence the detection of signals. Therefore, the signals identified in this study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating findings that warrant further investigation through controlled epidemiological studies and clinical evaluation.

This study has several limitations. First, the FAERS database relies on voluntary reporting and contains incomplete information, limiting the ability to calculate the incidence of AEs. The heterogeneous nature of reporting sources may contribute to inconsistencies in event descriptions, and reporting rates may vary over time and across different drugs. Second, FAERS predominantly captures reports from the USA and Europe, which may limit the generalisability of findings to other populations. Third, FAERS data lack detailed clinical information such as disease severity, treatment history, concomitant medications and temporal relationship between drug administration and AE onset. Although we conducted an indication-restricted sensitivity analysis to explore the influence of comparator selection on detected signals, this approach does not formally control for confounding. Substantial heterogeneity in disease characteristics, prior treatments and patient status remains within the restricted comparator group, and residual confounding by unmeasured factors is possible. The observed attenuation of several signals primarily demonstrates the dependence of the estimates on the choice of comparator rather than providing evidence that confounding has been addressed. Fourthly, although multiple algorithms were employed to assess potential safety signals, each algorithm possesses inherent limitations. The choice of signal detection thresholds is based on established conventions but remains somewhat arbitrary, and different thresholds may yield different signal patterns. Fifthly, some signals were based on very small numbers of reports (fewer than five cases), and results from such analyses should be interpreted with particular caution due to the instability of the estimates. It should be emphasised that statistical correlations do not necessarily imply biological causation, which warrants further investigation in clinical practice. Given the relatively brief period since market approval, large-scale comprehensive studies are still required to identify potential AEs.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study employed an objective methodology to characterise AE reporting patterns and potential safety signals for CD3×CD20 BsAbs, partially addressing gaps in pharmacovigilance for this drug class. Signals were identified for several AEs not currently listed in product labelling, including progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, disseminated tuberculosis and disseminated intravascular coagulation. A signal for HLH was also observed in the unrestricted analysis, although it was not retained in the indication-restricted sensitivity analysis. However, this represents a preliminary, hypothesis-generating analysis, necessitating further studies to validate these findings and determine the clinical significance of the observed signals. Clinicians and pharmacovigilance researchers may consider these findings when interpreting post-marketing safety reports, while further clinical and epidemiological studies are required to validate the observed signals and clarify their clinical significance.
