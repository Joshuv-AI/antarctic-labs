Multiple mucosal Schwann cell hamartomas of the gallbladder in a patient with pancreaticobiliary maljunction and chronic pancreatitis: A case report
Authors: Omar Aljuboori, Saif Rahman, Sarag Boukhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a rare case of multiple mucosal Schwann cell hamartomas of the gallbladder in a patient with pancreaticobiliary maljunction, chronic pancreatitis, and biliary strictures. Histological examination revealed numerous Schwann cell foci within the lamina propria, highlighted by S-100 and SOX-10 immunostaining. This case underscores a potential link between chronic biliary inflammation and neural proliferation, highlighting the importance of comprehensive histopathological evaluation in patients with pancreaticobiliary maljunction. The clinical implications of this incidental finding, particularly in a high-risk inflammatory setting, and the histopathological features that distinguish mucosal Schwann cell hamartoma from other mucosal neural lesions, are discussed.

Introduction

Mucosal Schwann cell hamartoma (MSCH) is a rare, benign neural lesion of the gastrointestinal mucosa, first described by Gibson and Hornick. 1 These lesions consist of S-100-positive spindle cells within the lamina propria and are typically discovered incidentally during screening colonoscopy, most often in the distal colon.1,2 Their distinction from other neural lesions, such as neurofibromas, perineuromas, and mucosal neuromas, is important, as the latter may carry syndromic associations with significant clinical implications.

Occurrence of MSCH outside the colorectum is exceptionally rare, with only a handful of reports describing lesions at the gastroesophageal junction, gastric antrum,3,4 and, even more rarely, the gallbladder.5 –7 The pathogenesis of MSCH remains incompletely understood, but chronic inflammation has been proposed as a contributing factor in the proliferation of Schwann cells within mucosal surfaces.

Here, we report a case of multiple MSCH of the gallbladder in a 55-year-old woman with pancreaticobiliary maljunction (PBM), chronic pancreatitis, and biliary strictures. This case is notable both for the rarity of the anatomic site and for the coexisting chronic biliary pathology, which raises questions about the role of sustained inflammation in promoting neural proliferative changes within the biliary mucosa.

Case report

A 55-year-old female with a history of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and chronic pancreatitis presented with intermittent right upper quadrant abdominal pain. Her symptoms had persisted for 5 years prior to the current evaluation, during which time she had undergone a progressive workup at our institution. Physical examination findings were unremarkable, and laboratory results, including liver function tests and serum amylase/lipase, were within normal limits at the time of her presentation.

Initial imaging done during her presentation had identified biliary duct dilation and changes consistent with chronic pancreatitis. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) confirmed a distal common bile duct (CBD) stricture and demonstrated a PBM with a short common channel (Figure 1). Over the ensuing period, the patient underwent multiple ERCP procedures, which confirmed a persistent stricture and led to the placement of several biliary stents. Given her history of PBM and the associated increased risk of biliary malignancy, she was referred for elective robot-assisted laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

The surgery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged in good condition. Postoperatively, she reported doing well and was adherent to her prescribed medications. Surveillance esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) were performed for CBD stricture monitoring. The EGD was normal. EUS demonstrated the previously known distal bile duct narrowing and chronic pancreatitis, with findings stable compared to prior studies and showing no evidence of progression. Colonoscopy performed 3 years prior to the cholecystectomy had identified a single hyperplastic polyp; no neural lesions or other mucosal abnormalities were identified at that time or on subsequent review.

Gross examination of the cholecystectomy specimen revealed a 10.4 × 4.0 × 3.4 cm gallbladder with a wall thickness of 4 mm. No gallstones were identified. Microscopic examination of representative sections from the fundus and body revealed multiple foci of short bundles of spindle cell aggregates, characterized by indistinct cytoplasmic margins and wavy, tapered nuclei, within the lamina propria. Immunohistochemistry with S-100 and SOX-10 highlighted these foci (Figure 2(a)–(c)). Submission of the entire gallbladder for histologic examination identified 16 foci of MSCH in total, along with scattered intestinal metaplasia and acute and chronic (lymphoplasmacytic) inflammatory infiltrates. Immunohistochemical stains showed scattered IgG-positive plasma cells but only a few IgG4-positive plasma cells (<5/high-power field), making IgG4-related disease unlikely.

Discussion

The term “mucosal Schwann cell hamartoma” was first proposed by Gibson and Hornick for gastrointestinal lesions composed purely of S-100-positive spindle cells. 1 These lesions can develop sporadically throughout the gastrointestinal tract but are most commonly identified in the distal colon.1,2 This terminology was introduced to distinguish MSCH from true neuromas, perineuromas, schwannomas, and neurofibromas with different histologic features and, in some cases, syndromic associations. In the colon, MSCH are typically incidental polyps discovered during screening colonoscopy, with an average patient age of 60.2 years. 8 Rare cases have been reported at the gastroesophageal junction and gastric antrum.3,4

MSCH of the gallbladder is exceedingly rare, with only three prior case reports in the literature (Table 1).5 –7 These gallbladder cases tend to occur in younger patients and demonstrate a female predominance, 5 contrasting with the older age distribution seen in colonic MSCH. A critical distinction from colonic counterparts is the clinical context: while colonic MSCH are almost universally incidental and carry no significant clinical implications, gallbladder MSCH in the setting of chronic biliary disease, as in our case, warrants more careful consideration.

Histopathological differentiation from other neural lesions

Accurate diagnosis of MSCH requires careful histopathological distinction from other mucosal neural lesions, each of which carries different clinical implications. Neurofibromas are composed of a mixture of Schwann cells, perineural cells, and fibroblasts, and are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1. Unlike MSCH, neurofibromas contain a myxoid stroma and co-express S-100 and CD34. Mucosal neuromas, classically associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2B, are composed of hyperplastic nerve fibers within the lamina propria and are highlighted by S-100 as well as neurofilament markers, but lack the pure Schwann cell composition of MSCH. Perineuromas, by contrast, are composed of perineural cells and are characteristically S-100-negative but EMA and claudin-1 positive. Schwannomas are encapsulated lesions with Antoni A and B areas and peripheral lymphoid cuffing, which are not features of MSCH.

In our case, the spindle cells were strongly and diffusely positive for S-100 and SOX-10, confirming Schwann cell lineage. They were unencapsulated, lacked myxoid stroma, showed no mitotic activity or atypia, and were negative for EMA and CD34, collectively supporting a diagnosis of MSCH and excluding the above entities. SOX-10, a transcription factor expressed in neural crest-derived cells, provided additional lineage confirmation and represents a useful adjunct to S-100 in this setting. Its use is highlighted as a distinguishing feature in our case compared to the three previously reported gallbladder MSCH cases, none of which employed SOX-10 (Table 1).

Clinical relevance and implications

Although MSCH is inherently a benign lesion with no known malignant potential, its clinical significance in our patient is amplified by the underlying PBM. PBM is a congenital anomaly wherein the pancreatic and bile ducts unite outside the duodenal wall, creating a long common channel that permits reflux of pancreatic enzymes into the biliary system. This enzymatic reflux contributes to chronic mucosal inflammation, epithelial injury, and a markedly heightened risk of biliary malignancy, particularly gallbladder and bile duct carcinoma.9,10 The presence of intestinal metaplasia in our case, a recognized precursor lesion in the setting of PBM, underscores the inflammatory burden on the biliary mucosa.

In this context, the incidental identification of MSCH does not alter immediate management but reinforces the importance of thorough histopathological examination of cholecystectomy specimens in patients with PBM. The absence of dysplasia and malignancy in our case, despite the high-risk background, is reassuring. Patients with PBM who have not undergone cholecystectomy require ongoing surveillance given this malignancy risk.9,11 The finding of MSCH should be documented, but need not prompt additional intervention, given its benign nature.

Regarding surveillance of the gastrointestinal tract, our patient had undergone colonoscopy 3 years prior to her cholecystectomy (and ~2 years after symptom onset), which identified a single hyperplastic polyp, a finding of no neural significance with no evidence of synchronous neural lesions elsewhere in the gastrointestinal tract. This is consistent with the literature, as MSCH is not known to represent a manifestation of a systemic neural polyposis syndrome. Nonetheless, given the limited number of reported gallbladder cases, further data are needed to determine whether systematic GI evaluation should be routinely considered in such patients.

Inflammation and neural proliferation

The pathophysiological relationship between chronic inflammation and neural proliferative changes is supported by a growing body of literature. In the pancreas, chronic pancreatitis is associated with neural remodeling, including increased nerve density, hypertrophy, and Schwann cell activation; a phenomenon driven by inflammatory mediators such as nerve growth factor, glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor, and various cytokines. 12 Neural plasticity in the setting of pancreatic inflammation has been extensively described and is thought to contribute to the neuropathic pain characteristic of chronic pancreatitis. 13 These mechanisms provide biological plausibility for analogous neural proliferative changes occurring within the adjacent biliary mucosa under chronic inflammatory stimulation.

In the context of PBM, chronic enzymatic reflux creates a sustained inflammatory microenvironment within the gallbladder and bile ducts. Inflammation has been shown to influence the behavior of stem cells and precursor populations, potentially promoting proliferation of neural crest-derived cells such as Schwann cells. 14 The co-occurrence of MSCH with intestinal metaplasia and lymphoplasmacytic cholecystitis in our case is consistent with a diffuse mucosal response to chronic injury and may reflect shared pathogenic stimuli. Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms by which chronic biliary inflammation might promote Schwann cell hamartoma formation remain speculative, and direct causality cannot be established from a single case.

The IgG4 immunostain result (fewer than five IgG4-positive plasma cells per high-power field) and the absence of obliterative phlebitis and storiform fibrosis effectively rule out IgG4-related sclerosing cholecystitis, 15 confirming that the inflammatory infiltrate in this case is attributable to the underlying PBM-associated chronic biliary disease rather than to an autoimmune process.

Conclusion

This case adds to the growing yet still limited body of literature on MSCH occurring outside the gastrointestinal tract and underscores the gallbladder as a potential, albeit rare, site of involvement. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of multiple (16 foci) MSCH of the gallbladder with SOX-10 confirmation, occurring in the unique context of PBM and chronic pancreatitis. The coexistence of MSCH with PBM and chronic biliary inflammation raises compelling questions about the role of sustained mucosal injury in driving neural proliferative changes. While the benign nature of these lesions is well-supported by their histologic and immunohistochemical features, their occurrence in high-risk inflammatory and congenital settings warrants thorough histopathological assessment. Future studies exploring the molecular and microenvironmental mechanisms underlying MSCH formation, particularly in the context of PBM, may help clarify their pathogenesis and broader clinical significance.
