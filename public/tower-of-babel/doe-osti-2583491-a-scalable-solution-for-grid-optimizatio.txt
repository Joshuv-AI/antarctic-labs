Task 1- Perform techno-economic system study:

NREL developed distribution feeder models to create a simulation environment for time-series
power-flow evaluation of volt-VAR optimization (VVO) and smart-inverter (SI) strategies.
Additional details for all subtasks are provided in Section 0 of this report.

Subtask 1.1 Feeder Conversion and Validation for Steady-State Simulation: NREL
converted four distribution feeders fed by one substation from National Grid geographical
information system (GIS) and Synergi power-flow models to OpenDSS, using NREL’s
Distribution Transformer Tool (DiTTo) conversion. This established the power-flow model for
steady-state simulation.

Subtask 1.2 Create Black-Box Co-Simulation and Time-series Simulation: NREL created an
OpenDSS quasi-static time-series co-simulation framework for power-flow, leveraging National
Grid advanced metering infrastructure (AMI), supervisory control and data acquisition
(SCADA), DER technical information and forecasts, and voltage control setting datasets. This
enabled dispatch by Utilidata’s VVO for time-series powerflow.

Subtask 1.3 Conduct Scenario Modeling of VVO and SI: NREL conducted multiple scenarios
with and without VVO and SI controls to assess benefits and impacts, specifically examining
change in energy consumption, PV curtailment, PV integration, energy imports, and device
lifetime (i.e., potential increased wear of voltage control assets).

Subtask 1.4 Estimate Costs of Scenarios: NREL developed multiple metrics (e.g., energy
reduction from changes in supply voltage, PV curtailment, substation energy imports, etc.) that
could be used in later estimates of benefits and costs of VVO and SI integration and control.

Task 2 – Develop benefit-cost analysis (BCA) framework:

NREL developed a BCA framework for assessing the benefits of VVO, the analysis is detailed in
Section 0 of this report.

Subtask 2.1 Conduct Preliminary BCA: NREL established a BCA framework and obtained
feedback from Utilidata, National Grid, NYSERDA, and DER developers.

Subtask 2.2 Conduct Final CBA: NREL presented final and refined BCA framework presented
and benefits calculated from energy reduction and curtailment that were simulated in task 1.

Task 3 – Conduct laboratory testing:

NREL established a power hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) test set-up and evaluated VVO SI
communication strategies against two 250 kW solar inverters similar to those being used in a

and results are detailed in Section 3 of this report.

Subtask 3.1 Hardware Laboratory Set-up: NREL established OPAL-RT ePhasorSim real-
time models of National Grid distribution feeders, and configured SI communication set-up for
external controllers communication through DNP3 and RS-485 protocols, and a grid simulator,
to the two smart-inverters.

Subtask 3.2 Software Laboratory Set-up: NREL set-up the ability for VVO to dispatch

Subtask 3.3 Conduct VVO and SI PHIL Tests: NREL conducted SI tests and used time-series

Executive Summary
Utility distribution voltage control is fundamental to delivering power system stability and
ensuring the high-level of power quality required by end-user equipment. Historically, the
challenge for distribution utilities was dealing with voltage drop from the distribution substation
(i.e., where transmission voltages [e.g., 69 kV and greater] are stepped down to distribution
primary voltages [e.g., with typical voltages between 4 and –35 kV]) voltages to the customer.
As power flows along utility assets (e.g., transformers and conductors), voltage drops due to the
impedance and associated losses of these assets. Typically, utilities try to keep per unit voltages
(e.g., 1 p.u. is 100% of nominal) high at distribution substation transformers in order to account
for voltage drop as power flows down to the end user and meter. In the United States, it is typical
for the substation to have on-load tap-changing capabilities (i.e., transformers that can modify
their turns ratio, or tap position while on-load to produce voltages slightly higher or lower than 1
p.u.). Utility voltage control assets downstream of the substation (e.g., capacitor banks and line
voltage regulators) can provide enhanced control capability and coordination opportunities, and
they can allow utilities to operate voltages on the lower side of established bounds to achieve
energy efficiency. Trying to enhance voltage control by coordinating voltage control assets to
achieve some objective in terms of power quality and energy efficiency is referred to as volt-
VAR optimization (VVO), where VAR is volt-amps reactive. And utilities can try to achieve
multiple objectives with respect to VVO, including energy efficiency, power quality, and—of
interest to this study—solar integration.

VVO has the potential to reduce energy consumption by reducing voltages within utility-
specified character-of-service bounds. As voltage decreases, end-use loads consume less energy.
VVO also has the potential to increase hosting capacity by reducing the headroom for
overvoltages, as overvoltages are typically the first problem encountered with high levels of solar
integration. This was the focus of this project, funded by the New York State Energy and
Research Development Authority, Utilidata Inc. partnered with the National Renewable Energy
Laboratory (NREL), National Grid, and Standard Solar to advance VVO and smart inverter (SI)
integration.

The potential to achieve, or improve, energy efficiency and hosting capacity depends on the
initial network conditions. Networks with existing low voltages will have low potential to reduce
voltages; indeed, voltages may have to be increased, reversing the VVO benefits in favor of
improving power quality and bringing voltages into character-of-service ranges. Networks with
high voltages have higher opportunity for achieving energy reduction and increasing hosting
capacity. The number of control assets (e.g., regulators and capacitor banks) also improves the
potential opportunity for VVO. Figure ES-1 conceptually presents the conditions where VVO
will have a greater potential to reduce voltages and energy consumption and to increase hosting
capacity. Networks with existing low voltage conditions may also benefit by bringing voltages
back into bounds while still ensuring maximum energy reduction and hosting capacity benefits.

Of interest in this research was how higher levels of solar photovoltaics might impact traditional
VVO benefits. Another key element of the project research was examining the potential benefits
of integrating SI dispatch (i.e., active and reactive power dispatch) into a centralized VVO
scheme. The benefits of SI integration to VVO were examined throughout the project.

Three principal tasks were completed as part of the project:

 1. Development of a simulation model to assess the power flow benefits of VVO
 2. Assessment of how to approach cost-benefit analysis of VVO and SI integration
 3. Laboratory demonstration and analysis of centralized versus distribution control for
 SI volt-VAR control.
Overall, the key findings of this project were:

 • VVO can provide energy efficiency benefits by reducing voltages within character-of-
 service ranges and reducing energy consumption and losses. The benefits identified in
 this report that are specific to the feeders analyzed ranged from 1% to 2%.
 • VVO can help mitigate solar integration voltage control challenges by mitigating
 overvoltages. By mitigating voltage rises caused by solar, energy efficiency benefits
 can increase under VVO.
 • SI integration benefits into VVO heavily depend on the degree to which utilities have
 already deployed traditional voltage control assets (e.g., load tap changers, capacitor
 banks, and line voltage regulators). For the feeders analyzed, large-scale deployment and
 use of these assets enhance VVO benefits but leave little room for additional SI voltage
 control benefits.
 • Smart inverters can be integrated into a centralized voltage control scheme through the
 proposed communication pathways (detailed in Section 3). Centralized control was
 demonstrated through external remote control and communication for dispatching smart
 inverters in a laboratory setup at NREL’s Energy System Integration Facility.
 • When contrasting centralized control and distributed autonomous control by the inverter,
 communication lags from central dispatch and different ramp rates and response
 characteristics (linear versus first-order for centralized and autonomous respectively)
 were observed.

1 Introduction
Distribution voltage control is fundamental to delivering power system stability and ensuring the
high-level of power quality required by end-user equipment. Historically, the challenge for
distribution utilities has been ensuring voltage drop from the distribution substation—i.e., where
transmission voltages (e.g., 69 kV and greater) are stepped down to distribution primary voltages
(e.g., typically 4–35 kV)—to the customer does not bring voltages outside character-of-service
ranges. Utility character-of-service voltages are typically tied to ANSI C84.1 Range A, which
stipulates utility distribution voltages be within +/-5% of nominal (American National Standards
Institute, n.d.). Appliances have a wider band and designed to operate within +/- 10% as
specified by the National Electrical Manufacturers Association, allowing for voltage drop from
the meter to an appliance point of common coupling (e.g., a socket). As power flows along utility
assets (e.g., transformers and conductors), voltage drops due to the impedance of these electrical
assets and their associated thermal losses. Typically, utilities try to keep per unit voltages (e.g., 1
p.u. is 100% of nominal) higher at the distribution primary side of substation transformers (i.e.,
keeping these upstream voltages closer to 1.05 p.u., the upper range of ANSI C84.1 Range A).
Doing so helps account for voltage drop as power flows down to the end user and the meter,
ensuring end-user supply stays within bounds.

In the United States, a substation typically has on-load tap-changing capabilities, which allows
its transformers to increase or decrease voltage relative to nominal (a range of 16 tap positions is
not untypical, with each tap accounting for approximately a 0.625% change in voltage, enabling
a range of +/- 10% of nominal). Utility voltage control assets downstream of the substation
provide additional voltage control, such as (1) capacitor banks, which can use (volts-amps
reactive) VARs to increase voltages or (2) line regulators (autotransformers with the same
input/output nominal voltage, which can provide tap-changing capabilities). These assets offer
utilities enhanced control capability and coordination opportunities, allowing operating voltages
on the lower side of established bounds to achieve energy efficiency.

Enhanced voltage control through coordination of voltage control assets to achieve power quality
or energy efficiency objectives is referred to as volt-VAR optimization (VVO). Utilities can have
multiple concurrent objectives with respect to VVO, that include energy efficiency, power
quality, and, of interest to this study, solar integration. Utilities are exploring the integration of
additional assets to traditional voltage control, which include the use of advanced metering
infrastructure (AMI) to provide grid-edge voltage measurements, and smart inverters, which can
provide active and reactive power voltage regulation (Figure 1).

VVO can provide energy efficiency benefits by reducing energy consumption through reducing
voltages within utility-specified character-of-service bounds. This strategy is generally referred
to as conservation voltage reduction (CVR) (Kennedy & Fletcher, 1991; Wang & Wang, 2014).
As voltage decreases, end-use loads consume less energy. There are three theoretical ideal
responses, described below, for load responses to changes in voltages, which overall can be
characterized by an exponent form equation for voltage response, see (1).

 (1)

Though there are no ideal loads in reality, most loads exhibit responses that are close to those of
ideal load models (McKenna & Keane, 2016b). For an ideal load model, voltage response can
generally include the following types of response:

 • Constant Impedance (P=V2/R): Reducing voltage reduces power to the square (i.e., in
 Equation (1, np is equal to 2). Resistive heating and older style incandescent and halogen
 lighting closely followed these forms of response; most traditional CVR benefits came
 heavily from incandescent lighting before these were phased out for compact fluorescent
 and light-emitting diode lighting solutions.
 • Constant Current (P=VI): Reducing voltage reduces power directly proportional (i.e.,
 in Equation (1, np is equal to 1). Motor loads most closely follow constant current.

 12

 • Constant Power (P=VI): Reducing voltage increases current (i.e., in Equation (1, np is
 equal to 0). Modern power electronic loads more closely follow constant power type
 responses. For loads that are close to this response, as voltage decreases, current
 increases, which can increase other distribution I2R losses.

Aggregate voltage response depends on the myriad loads connected and consuming power from
the distribution system at any given time (McKenna & Keane, 2017). The power and current
response of each ideal load type with respect to voltage is presented in Figure 2. In general, with
the phaseout of incandescent and halogen lighting and their replacement with compact
fluorescent light and LEDs, and the transition to more power electronic interfaces, the voltage
response of the load has been decreasing over time. In general:

 • For np greater than 1, demand will reduce with a decrease in voltage.
 • For np less than 1, demand will increase with a decrease in voltage.

CVR strategies generally have the following effects on losses and demand:

 • Losses: As a general principle of power transmission, higher voltages result in lower per
 unit power delivered losses (P=VI: Increase V reduces I, P=I2R: Current proportional to
 the square of the voltage). CVR typically decreases overall losses but increases
 percentage losses (i.e., per unit power delivered, losses increase).
 • For voltage reduction achieving a reduction in demand:
 o CVR increases the losses per unit power delivered, and,
 o CVR decreases the overall magnitude of losses.

 13

 • However, if load does not reduce with a reduction in voltage (i.e., constant power loads):
 o CVR increases the losses per unit power delivered, and,
 o CVR increases the overall magnitude of losses.

This work focuses on how solar integration and solar hosting capacity1 impact and are impacted
by VVO. Overall, integration of solar photovoltaics (PV) causes two initial challenges: (1) the
potential of grid-edge overvoltages due to active power injections and (2) the potential for
reverse power flow resulting in overloading (in the case of very high PV integration) and
protection concerns (Seguin et al., 2016). In this work, we see that VVO can increase hosting
capacity by decreasing voltages which increases the headroom before overvoltages are
experienced.

Overall, the potential to achieve, or improve, energy efficiency and hosting capacity depends
critically on the initial network conditions. Networks with existing low voltages will have low
opportunity to further reduce voltages; indeed, voltages might have to be increased, reversing the
VVO benefits in favor of improving power quality and bringing voltages into character-of-
service ranges. Networks with high voltages have higher potential for achieving energy reduction
and increasing hosting capacity. The number of control assets (e.g., regulators and capacitor
banks) also improves the potential opportunity for VVO. Figure 3 conceptually presents the
benefits VVO may bring for networks with initial low or high (with respect to nominal and
character-of-service bounds) voltage conditions. Networks with high voltages can achieve
benefits of reducing energy consumption, reducing peak demand, and increasing solar
integration potential by lowering voltages within character-of-service ranges (McKenna &
Keane, 2016a). Networks with low voltages that introduce VVO may increase energy
consumption, peak demand, and lower solar integration potential, but they can reduce the risk
of voltage excursions.

Of interest in this research was how higher levels of solar PV might impact traditional VVO
benefits; this is dealt with in detail in later sections. An additional key interest of this research
was the potential benefits of integrating smart inverter (SI) dispatch (i.e., active and reactive
power dispatch) into a centralized VVO scheme. SI voltage control has been established in
recent standards, such as IEEE 1547-2018, and it is being required in certain states in public
utility commission rulings (California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC), 2017; Hawaiian
Public Utilities Commission (PUC), 2016; IEEE Standards Coordinating Committee 21 on Fuel
Cell, Photovoltaics, Dispersed Generation, and Energy Storage, n.d.). Previous work has
demonstrated the benefits of SI voltage control and benefits in terms of grid integration (Giraldez
et al., 2018; Giraldez Miner et al., 2017). The overall benefits of SI-VVO integration include:

 • Grid-Edge Visibility: SI technology can give VVO visibility on grid-edge voltages
 similar to AMI.
 • VAR Control: SI can provide VAR control in the form of power factor, volt-VAR, and
 volt-watt with distributed control and the potential for centralized control with
 communication.

AMI integration was also examined in this work. AMI gives VVO grid-edge voltage visibility,
which can be thought of as providing greater quantification of grid conditions and can fine-tune
VVO control (McKenna et al., 2022). Traditionally, VVO will conservatively reduce voltages in
order to allow for headroom from measurements on which VVO relies and which are typically
on the primary network. For example, if VVO has a line voltage measurement on the primary
network remote from the substation, it may regulate that voltage a few percentage points above
the lower bound of character of service to account for voltage drop to end-of-line on the
distribution secondary (e.g., regulating to 0.97 p.u. to allow for a 3% drop of the lower ANSI
C84.1 Range A limit of 0.95 p.u.). AMI may complicate VVO control and may result in VVO
taking the following actions in the following cases:
 • No Headroom or Grid-Edge Violations Seen in AMI Sata: VVO will raise voltages,
 thereby reducing energy reduction gains.
 • Additional Headroom to Reduce Grid-Edge Voltages Seen in AMI: VVO will lower
 voltages, thereby increasing energy reductions.

Also, VVO simultaneously seeing AMI grid-edge measurements above and below character-of-
service bounds could further complicate control. Overall, AMI integration should be seen as a
positive means of providing accuracy for the best control actions VVO should take.

This project examined Utilidata’s VVO algorithms performance both in isolation and in
conjunction with other technologies such as solar integration, smart inverters and AMI, and it
examined the effects on four National Grid distribution feeders. Three principal tasks were
completed as part of this project:

 4. Development of a simulation model to assess the power flow benefits of VVO
 5. Assessment of how to approach cost-benefit analysis of VVO and SI integration
 6. Laboratory demonstration and analysis of centralized versus distribution control for
 SI volt-VAR control.

2 Modeling and Cost-Benefit Analysis of VVO and
  SI Dispatch
This section details the modeling and simulation performed for the cost-benefit analysis of VVO
with SI dispatch. NREL configured and ran multiple scenarios on an OpenDSS power flow
model of four suburban distribution feeders that captured both the primary (13.2 kV) and
secondary networks (120/240 V). The OpenDSS model was developed using utility GIS and
CYME International power flow models, and it was validated against feeder SCADA
(supervisory control and data acquisition) and AMI data sets (active and reactive power, and
voltage). Synthetic distribution secondaries were added to the model, validating power flow at
the grid-edge by using AMI voltage measurements. In these simulations, VVO provided
enhanced voltage control with the objective of lowering voltages to maximize the available peak
demand and energy consumption reduction. The objective of VVO also includes minimizing
voltage excursions outside of ANSI C84.1, which stipulates voltages remain within +/-5%.

The objective of minimizing voltages also serves to aid solar integration because the typical first
constraint on accommodating solar PV are system overvoltages that result for PV active power
injection. Utilidata’s traditional VVO system controls substation load tap changers, voltage
regulators, and capacitor banks. The VVO uses a machine learning algorithm to establish
relationships between control devices, and it optimizes voltage asset dispatch. This analysis
introduces PV inverter grid support functions, such as volt-VAR and volt-Watt, in conjunction
with VVO operation, and it includes scenarios where VVO dispatches PV inverters and uses
AMI measurements to give end-of-line visibility. The overall setup of the VVO and control
assets is presented in Figure 4. The rest of this section is organized into key questions pertaining
to VVO cost-benefit framework and simulation analysis, with a focus on PV inverter grid
support function integration.

2.1 What should be considered in a benefit-cost analysis framework
 for VVO?
The benefit-cost analysis (BCA) assesses the cost and benefits of VVO to integrate and manage
distribution voltage control assets and distributed energy resources (DERs) on the distribution
network. The objectives of the BCA are to:

 1. Propose an initial framework on what should be considered in a full-fledged BCA and
 how to include stakeholder (i.e., customer, utility, solar developer) costs and benefits.
 2. Quantify benefits and costs of selected metrics (Figure 7, page 20).
 3. Include a brief discussion of the metrics that are not quantified in this study and how they
 might be examined with expanded analysis (Section 2.7, page 31).
 4. Include a brief discussion of caveats of the scope of this study, such as considerations
 when contemplating scaling or generalizing the results (Section 2.7, page 31).

The BCA considers multiple scenarios to estimate the incremental costs and benefits of using
VVO by itself or in combination with AMI, smart inverters using custom volt-VAR curves, volt-
VAR activation, and VVO direct inverter dispatch. The BCA uses results from NREL’s
simulations and information about electricity cost for different stakeholders to evaluate utility,
customer, and solar asset owner costs and benefits, including substation energy imports
(consumption), substation energy exports, network I2R losses, and PV curtailment. NREL
engaged diverse stakeholders, including utilities, developers, and other nonprofit member
organizations and advocates in developing the BCA framework.

standard-as-is voltage control asset settings (for comparison to VVO); these were in addition to
network GIS and Synergi feeder models that were used to build the OpenDSS power flow model.
For price inputs, retail prices, wholesale locational marginal prices and cost of network upgrades
(e.g. installing new voltage regulators) were used (Figure 6).

2.4 How will advanced inverter functions from smart inverters impact
 the value of VVO?
Standard SI settings (such as IEEE Category B volt-VAR, customized volt-VAR, or proposed
National Grid volt-VAR curves) have the aim to reduce the overvoltages and undervoltages. The
total energy consumption may either increase or decrease depending on whether VARs from SI
settings are required to mitigate overvoltages (in which case you would see a decrease in energy
consumption) or undervoltages (in which case you would see an increase in energy
consumption). VVO was observed to reduce energy consumption by 1.887% in the 2035
scenario for unity power factor settings. This number decreased to 1.761% for customized volt-
VAR settings where VARs were used to mitigate undervoltage, and energy consumption reduced

 25

by 1.889% for IEEE Category B and proposed National Grid volt-VAR inverter setting where
most of the VARs were used to reduce high voltages relative to customized volt-VAR setting.
The following key observations were made for advanced inverter functions:

 • VVO controls the voltage to largely within the deadband of SI volt-VAR curves, thus
 reducing the need for inverter VARs and reducing inverter throughput (Figure 9,
 page 28).
 • For the studied feeders, volt-VAR curves provided little additional benefit due to the tight
 voltage regulator from VVO and the number of voltage regulators and capacitor banks on
 the network.
 • Volt-VAR curves did not counter the objective of VVO, but they can provide support at
 the edge of ANSI C84.1 Range A for networks with particularly high or low voltages.
 That was not the case for the feeders studied here.

2.5 How can AMI enhance the value of VVO?
Advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) data provide greater visibility of how networks perform
in terms of asset loadings and voltages, which should help VVO make better decisions.
However, integrating AMI also comes with challenges.

 • Including AMI data from all customers at each time step increases complexity for VVO
 and communications may contribute to latency. Additional computing power (e.g. servers
 or cloud resources) would be required to process the AMI data.
 • AMI infrastructure may not be present for most customer premises for the utility.
 • Real-time AMI data management can pose greater challenge for utility to keep it reliable
 (in terms of keeping the system running 24-7 and making sure collected measurements
 are not erroneous, this will require real-time processing for quality control and assurance)
 and scalable.
 • The cost of integrating AMI in VVO in real-time is likely to be high, meaning stacked
 value applications may be required recoup the cost. Targeting a subset of meters (e.g.
 those at end-of-line and electrically closest and furthest from the substation) may reduce
 the cost of AMI integration and still provide high value for the VVO.

 • For the feeder examined, overvoltages are not expected with or without VVO up to their

 accommodation solar capacity.
 • Running a scenario using National Grid provided hosting capacity values for the circuit,
 overvoltages were experienced on the primary without VVO. VVO was able to bring
 these back into bounds enabling the hosting capacity limit to be reached.
 • For the feeders analyzed advanced inverter functions, such as volt-VAR and volt-Watt
 were not required as VVO tightly controlled the system voltages to within the deadband
 of these functions. For other circuits, with higher voltages and less voltage control assets,
 advanced inverter functions might provide more support than for the feeders analyzed

2.7 Considerations for Implementing a BCA for Utilities
This study was conducted for National Grid feeders, and such the following considerations are
for those specific feeders. However, some of the general lessons for implementing the BCA
could be applied to many other utilities. The results from this study are specific to the feeders
examined, and different feeders will yield different potential saving opportunities and benefits
from VVO. To expand on the BCA done as part of this project in a way that reflects the scope
outlined in the National Grid BCA Handbook version 3, future analysts could take the following
points into consideration:

 • The analysis period considered in the present study was one year. To comply more
 precisely with the Handbook, analysts would need to expand the analysis period to a
 longer span and include the time-value of money. The period should be agreed upon with
 the utility and other stakeholders.
 • Such analysis would also need to consider future effects on rates. Additionally, it would
 also need to consider effects on entire service territory with a diverse set of feeders and
 capture impacts on bulk power system (e.g. wholesale market prices, transmission losses,
 etc.). Some feeders may have greater benefits than the feeder analyzed here and some
 may have lower, in general, Figure 5 (page 18) presents the theoretical opportunity
 depending on the feeder voltage profile and number of voltage control assets.
 • Such analysis would also need to forecast future wholesale prices (i.e. for substation
 imports). The price of solar PV and how that would impact the rate of adoption, and
 potential changing price of integrating VVO and communications.
 • Figure 7 (page 24) shows metrics that were not quantified in this study. Examples include
 inverter lifetime reduction due to an increase in throughput, changes in interconnection
 study costs (performed by the utility and paid by developers), considerations about
 whether and how solar customers and independent PV producers could be compensated
 by the benefits that they may provide to the grid, and how VVO could help increase
 hosting capacity or delay the need for grid upgrades, among others. Each of these topics
 could be a research project on its own and analysts looking at these costs and benefits
 may want to spend resources quantifying them.
 • The impact of VVO on customer energy equity and justice could also be considered. The
 expectation is that all customers stand to benefit if VVO can reduce voltages and achieve
 energy consumption reductions for all. Hosting capacity benefits are likely to only benefit
 those who can afford to install solar PV, although ancillary benefits from additional
 renewable energy deployment, such as improved air quality, should benefit all. Reduced
 or deferred asset upgrades from VVO may provide uneven benefits due to how fixed
 costs are recouped in existing retail tariff structures.

3 Laboratory Integration of SI Communication
Laboratory testing set out to demonstrate remote dispatch of SI communication. Testing goals
were to establish and compare centralized and distributed SI control for voltage regulation.
Centralized distribution control of smart inverters would demonstrate how these assets could be
integrated into a utility voltage control regulation (e.g., such as VVO control scheme),
coordinating with other traditional control assets such as tap-changing transformers, capacity
banks, line voltage regulations, etc. The overall purpose was to help understand the challenges of
a centralized VVO dispatching SI active and reactive power. The research in this section was
also the subject of a conference paper (Mahmud, 2023). The testing objectives were to:

 • Demonstrate the successful operation of the proposed centralized SI communication
 pathways.
 • Compare centralized remote dispatch (open and closed-loop) versus distributed inverter-
 based dispatch (distributed autonomous) to understand response times and characteristics.
 • Demonstrate SI dispatch operating under real-time power hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL)
 testing.
The testing aimed to demonstrate and validate a communication setup from a remote SCADA
dispatch (Distribution Test Manager [DTM]) using the OrionLX and SEL-RTAC to the two 250
kW solar inverters. The inverters were demonstrated to be able to receive active and reactive
power dispatch commands.

3.1 Overview of Testing for Remote Open-Loop, Remote Closed-Loop
 Control, and Distributed Autonomous Control
NREL used Triangle MicroWorks’ Distributed Test Manager (Triangle DTM) as the centralized
controller to dispatch the inverter (two 250-kW solar inverters). The Triangle DTM was
programmed with volt-VAR curves to remotely sense inverter terminal voltage and issue
corresponding reactive power dispatch. This was contrasted with inverter-programmed volt-VAR
to compare centralized and distributed volt-VAR testing response times and characteristics.

Remote closed-loop and open-loop dispatch involves a number of steps from inverter detection,
communication to the centralized control, and issue of dispatch. Characterization of the response
time was separated into several elements:

 • Detection Time (Td): time needed for the equipment under test (EUT), in this case the
 inverter, to measure the parameter (e.g., inverter terminal voltage)
 • Uplink Communication Latency (TCU): time needed to transmit the measured
 parameter (e.g., inverter terminal voltage) from EUT terminal to the external controller
 (i.e., the Triangle DTM)
 • External Controller Computation Time (TCC): time needed for the external controller
 to compute the control signal (i.e., inverter VAR dispatch) for the EUT based on the
 measured parameter
 • Downlink Communication Latency (TCU): time needed to transmit the external
 controller generated control signal measured from external controller to EUT terminal
 • EUT Response Delay (TR): time needed by the EUT to respond to the control signal
 • Ramping time (Tramp): time needed by the EUT to change the response from one step to
 another step
 • Communication and Computation Loop Time (TL): total needed to measure the EUT
 parameter, communicate the parameter to the external controller, compute external
 control signal and transmit the control signal to EUT
 • Tracking Error: difference between the control signal and actual EUT response.

4 Conclusion
Three principal tasks were completed as part of this project, which were to: (1) develop a
simulation model to assess the power flow benefits of VVO, (2) assess how to approach cost-
benefit analysis of VVO and SI integration, and (3) laboratory demonstration and analysis of
centralized versus distribution control for SI volt-VAR control. Overall, the key findings of this
project were:

 • VVO can provide energy efficiency benefits by reducing voltages within character-of-
 service ranges and reducing energy consumption and losses; benefits identified in this
 report that are specific to the feeders analyzed ranged between 1% and 2%. The VVO
 opportunity is determined by existing voltage ranges.
 o VVO also has the potential to decrease the need for network and traditional
 upgrades by improving the voltage profile of the circuit, avoiding network under
 and over-voltages.
 • VVO has additional benefits under high solar adoption scenarios. VVO can help mitigate
 solar integration voltage control challenges, such as high voltage ramps due to net load
 changes and mitigation of overvoltages. Energy efficiency benefits can increase under
 VVO, as VVO can mitigate voltage rises from solar that would increase energy
 consumption.
 • VVO mitigating potential over-voltages from solar PV has the potential to expand the
 capability of a circuit to accommodate more solar capacity, dependent on existing voltage
 conditions.
 • The benefits of SI integration into VVO will heavily depend on the degree to which
 utility voltage control assets have already been deployed. For the scenarios studied in this
 report, a high number of voltage control assets (e.g., load tap changers, capacitor banks,
 and line voltage regulators), which enhanced VVO benefits, left little room for additional
 SI voltage control benefits. For feeders with less utility voltage control assets, smart
 inverters are likely to have greater benefits than seen in this report.
 • Integration of smart inverters into a centralized voltage control scheme was demonstrated
 through external remote control and communication for dispatching a smart inverter in a
 laboratory setup at NREL’s Energy System Integration Facility. This was contrasted to a
 distributed autonomous control by the inverter, communication lags from central dispatch
 and different ramp rates and response characteristics (linear versus first-order for
 centralized and autonomous respectively) were observed.
 o Autonomous distributed inverter control were shown to respond to voltage step
 changes in under a second. Remote external dispatch, due to communication
 delays, took on the order of 11.7 seconds with a standard deviation of 4.78
 seconds.
 o The delays in the communication and response times might be of concern for
 voltage control, and there might be advantages to using a hybrid communication
 architecture (i.e., remote control and dispatch but with the ability to receive
