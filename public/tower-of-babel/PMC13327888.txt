Comments on “Long-term outcomes of intramural rectal botulinum toxin injections for urge fecal incontinence: a salvage therapy for sacral neuromodulation nonresponders?”
Authors: Nabil Mohammad Azmi, Mohd Firdaus Mohd Hayati, Zairul Azwan Mohd Azwan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article, “Long-term outcomes of intramural rectal botulinum toxin injections for urge fecal incontinence: a salvage therapy for sacral neuromodulation nonresponders?” by Onana Ndong and Vitton [1]. The authors should be commended for addressing this therapeutically challenging subgroup, for which few minimally invasive alternatives exist after sacral neuromodulation (SNM) failure.

The study reported significant and durable improvements in both the Cleveland Clinic Fecal Incontinence Score and visual analog scale score. These improvements were maintained over a median follow-up period of 22.5 months. No adverse events were observed. The ability to repeat the procedure in an outpatient setting further supports its role as a safe and reversible option prior to considering definitive surgical diversion. The efficacy of botulinum toxin has also been demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial, which reported symptom improvement at 3 months [2].

This letter aims to raise relevant questions to clarify certain aspects of the study and strengthen the robustness of the findings. Several methodological considerations warrant further discussion. The retrospective, single-center design and small sample size limit external validity. In addition, variability may have been introduced by the sole reliance on physician discretion for reinjection decisions. Moreover, the exclusive inclusion of female patients restricts generalizability, as sex-specific differences in sphincter morphology and rectal compliance may influence treatment response profiles. From a clinical perspective, we wish to pose 2 questions to further elucidate the therapeutic applicability of this approach.

First, could baseline anorectal physiology predict treatment response? Identifying quantifiable parameters, such as rectal resting and squeeze pressures, the rectoanal inhibitory reflex, and balloon expulsion tests, could refine patient selection and optimize outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated that active (urge) and passive fecal incontinence subtypes exhibit distinct manometric characteristics and differential therapeutic responses. The inclusion of anal manometry could have provided objective pretreatment and posttreatment physiological data, thereby strengthening the overall study findings [3, 4].

Second, how does botulinum toxin compare with SNM regarding cost-effectiveness and durability? SNM is effective but resource-intensive, with reported revision rates of 20% to 30% over 10 years [5]. Evaluating botulinum toxin relative to SNM through prospective comparative studies could help determine whether it represents a cost-effective and sustainable minimally invasive alternative, particularly in healthcare systems facing fiscal constraints [6].

We congratulate the authors for their valuable contribution and look forward to further multicenter prospective research to delineate the optimal timing and patient profile for botulinum toxin injection therapy in patients with refractory urge fecal incontinence.
