How Long Does Infinite Width Last? Signal Propagation in Long-Range Linear Recurrences
Mariia Seleznova

Abstract
We study signal propagation in linear recurrent models at finite width. While existing signal propagation theory relies predominantly on the infinite-width limit, it remains unclear for how long that approximation remains accurate when recurrent depth t t grows jointly with width n n . This question is especially relevant for modern recurrent sequence models, whose natural operating regime involves long input sequences, i.e., large t t . We derive exact finite-width formulas for the hidden state signal energies in linear recurrences under complex Gaussian initialization. Using these formulas, we identify the joint depth–width scaling regimes that govern signal propagation: (i) a subcritical regime t = o ⁡ ( n ) t=o(\sqrt{n}) , in which the infinite-width approximation remains valid; (ii) a critical regime t ∼ c ​ n t\sim c\sqrt{n} , in which non-negligible deviations from infinite-width predictions appear and a nontrivial joint scaling limit emerges; and (iii) a supercritical regime t ≫ n t\gg\sqrt{n} , in which finite-width effects dominate. Thus, our results pinpoint the precise recurrent depth scale at which infinite-width theory breaks down in long-range linear recurrences. In turn, this shows when standard initialization schemes, such as Glorot, become unstable. More broadly, our results demonstrate that finite-width effects accumulate more rapidly with depth in recurrent models than in feedforward ones, leading to qualitatively different signal propagation behavior.



1
Introduction

Understanding how signals propagate through the layers of a neural network (NN) is a foundational problem in deep learning (DL) theory. When signals are repeatedly transformed by many layers of a deep network, their magnitudes may progressively grow or decay, eventually leading to numerical failure. This phenomenon is widely known as the
vanishing/exploding signals problem
, and it poses a fundamental obstacle to training deep NNs
(
5
;
30
)
. Ensuring stable signal propagation has therefore long been a central design principle in DL methods, motivating widely used initialization schemes
(
13
;
23
;
33
)
and architectural choices
(
3
;
24
;
25
)
.

Despite the foundational nature and long history of this question, the theoretical understanding of signal propagation in DL models remains limited. Much of the existing literature relies on the
infinite-width limit

(
33
;
31
;
42
;
41
)
, where the network’s width
n
(i.e, the number of neurons per layer) tends to infinity. In this regime, mean-field-type approximations yield tractable descriptions of signal statistics and lead to elegant signal propagation theories for many DL architectures
(
42
;
41
)
, including recurrent models
(
6
;
12
;
1
)
. Realistic networks, however, operate at finite width. In this setting, even small deviations from the infinite-width limit can accumulate over many layers or recurrent steps, eventually rendering infinite-width predictions inaccurate in sufficiently deep architectures or long-range recurrences
(
32
;
21
;
19
;
35
;
4
)
. This leads to the central question of this paper:

How long does the infinite-width approximation remain accurate in recurrent models?

For fully-connected feedforward networks at random initialization, the answer is relatively well understood. The deviations from infinite-width behavior become significant when depth scales proportionally with width, i.e.,
L\sim cn
, where
L
is the number of layers and
n
is the layer width
(
21
;
19
;
35
;
22
)
. That is, for fully connected feedforward networks, the critical depth scale at which finite-width effects begin to qualitatively alter signal propagation is by now known.

By contrast, much less is known for recurrent networks. Although existing work suggests that finite-width corrections behave differently in recurrent and feedforward architectures
(
34
;
14
)
, their precise effect for long-range recurrent signal propagation remains unclear. Intuitively, finite-width effects accumulate faster in recurrences, since the same weight matrix is applied repeatedly and its dominant spectral directions are therefore reinforced across recurrent steps
t
. Bar et al. (2025)
(
4
)
take a first step toward formalizing this intuition in linear recurrent sequence models with Gaussian initialization, showing that accumulated finite-width effects cause signal explosion when
t\gg\sqrt{n}
. However, their argument gives only a crude lower bound on the signal size. Thus, even for linear recurrences at random initialization, the sharp depth scale at which infinite-width predictions break down has remained unknown.

This gap is especially striking from the perspective of modern long-range sequence modeling, where recurrence-based architectures—such as state-space models (SSMs)
(
18
;
15
;
8
)
and linear recurrent units (LRUs)
(
29
)
—are actively explored as computationally efficient alternatives to transformers for long-context tasks. These models have recently achieved competitive empirical performance while retaining the computational benefits of linear recurrence. Since they are designed for long-range tasks, their natural operating regime is that of long input sequences, or equivalently large recurrent depth
t
. Yet this is precisely the regime in which signal propagation theory remains weak.

This paper helps close this gap by characterizing finite-width signal propagation in randomly-initialized linear recurrences and identifying the recurrent depth scaling regimes in which the infinite-width approximation remains accurate and those in which it fails. We first specify the considered setting in Section
1.1
, and then summarize the main contributions in Section
1.2
.

1.1
Problem Setting

Our main object of interest is the linear recurrent unit (LRU), which has recently re-emerged as a building block for long-range sequence models
(
29
)
. On the way, our analysis also yields results for the simple linear recurrent network without sequential inputs. Thus, the considered models are:

\displaystyle\text{{Linear recurrent unit (LRU):}}\qquad\mathbf{h}_{\textup{LRU}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}_{\textup{LRU}}^{(t-1)}+\mathbf{x}^{(t)}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\mathbf{W}^{k}\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)},\ t\geq 1,

(1)

\displaystyle\text{{Linear recurrent network (RNN):}}\qquad\mathbf{h}_{\textup{RNN}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}_{\textup{RNN}}^{(t-1)}=\mathbf{W}^{t}\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\ t\geq 1.

(2)

Here
\mathbf{h}^{(t)}_{\cdot}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the hidden state vector at step
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(t)})
is the input sequence, where
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
0\leq i\leq t
, and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is the weight matrix. We set
\mathbf{h}_{\cdot}^{(0)}=\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
in both models. The weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
is initialized with i.i.d. proper complex Gaussian entries of variance
1/n
, namely

\mathbf{W}_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1/n),\quad\text{i.i.d. across }i,j=1,\dots,n.

(3)

The main results in this work are formulated for complex weights. This choice is natural for two reasons. First, complex-valued linear recurrences are used in modern recurrent sequence models
29
;
16
, since complex eigenvalues provide a convenient representation of oscillatory dynamics. Second, the complex Gaussian ensemble admits a particularly clean analysis, which allows us to derive sharp finite-width results for signal propagation. At the same time, our analysis also yields a sufficient condition under which linear recurrences with real Gaussian weights deviate from the infinite-width predictions.

Our main quantitative objects are the normalized expected squared norms of the hidden states
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{LRU}}^{(t)}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{RNN}}^{(k)}
, which we call
signal energies
. They are defined as follows:

\textbf{Signal energies}:\quad S_{n,t}:=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{LRU}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr],\qquad Q_{n,k}:=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{RNN}}^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr],

(4)

where the expectation is taken jointly over the weights
\mathbf{W}
and the inputs
(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(t)})
. The same quantities are studied in classical works on signal propagation at infinite width
(
31
;
33
)
. Notably, standard Gaussian initialization schemes—such as He
(
23
)
and Glorot
(
13
)
—are designed to stabilize these signal energies across layers at infinite width, so that
Q_{\infty,k}\approx Q_{\infty,k+1}
. However, in this work we explicitly study the behavior of these quantities at
finite width
.

1.2
Contributions

In the setting described above, the main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Exact finite-width formulas for signal propagation.
We derive exact closed-form formulas for signal propagation in finite-width linear recurrences with complex Gaussian initialization. Specifically, we obtain expressions for signal energies
S_{n,t}
and
Q_{n,k}
(defined in (
4
)) for finite
n,t
and
k
. This result is given in Theorem
2.1
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first exact finite-width result for signal propagation in recurrent models, which provides a clear departure from the standard infinite-width signal propagation theory.

•

Sharp depth–width scaling laws.
Using the exact finite-width formulas, we identify the recurrent depth–width scaling regimes that govern signal propagation. In particular, we identify and characterize three regimes: (i) the
subcritical regime

k,t=o(\sqrt{n})
, in which the infinite-width approximation remains accurate; (ii) the
critical regime

k,t\sim c\sqrt{n}
, in which a nontrivial joint depth–width limit emerges; and (iii) the
supercritical regime

k,t\gg\sqrt{n}
, in which finite-width effects dominate. The corresponding behavior of the signal energies
S_{n,t}
and
Q_{n,k}
is summarized in Figure
1
. Together, these results yield a precise joint scaling signal propagation theory for recurrent depth and width in linear recurrences.

•

Limitations of the infinite-width theory for long-range recurrent models.
Our results make explicit when the infinite-width description fails in long-range recurrent models. In particular, we identify the critical depth scale
t\sim c\sqrt{n}
, at which finite-width corrections accumulate and qualitatively alter signal propagation. This makes the limits of infinite-width theory precise in the long-sequence regime relevant to modern recurrent architectures.

In addition, we validate our theoretical results with numerical experiments, which confirm the predicted signal propagation behavior.

2
Recurrent Signal Propagation at Finite Width

We now state the main theorem on finite-width signal propagation in linear recurrences. The proof of this result is given in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 2.1

(Signal propagation at finite width)
.

Suppose the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
has i.i.d. proper complex Gaussian entries with variance
1/n
, as in (
3
). Let
(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(t)})
be an input sequence independent of
\mathbf{W}
, and assume that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{(s)}\mathbf{x}^{(s)\top}]=\mathbb{I}_{n}
for all
0\leq s\leq t
.
No independence assumptions across time are required. Then the following identities hold for the hidden state signal energies of the LRU and the linear RNN, defined in (
4
):

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}Q_{n,k},\qquad Q_{n,k}=\frac{F^{+}_{k+2}(n)-F^{-}_{k+2}(n)}{n^{k+1}(k+1)(k+2)}.

(5)

Here
F_{p}^{+}(n)
and
F_{p}^{-}(n)
denote the rising and falling factorials, respectively:

F_{p}^{+}(n):=n(n+1)\cdots(n+p-1),\qquad F_{p}^{-}(n):=n(n-1)\cdots(n-p+1).

(6)

This theorem gives exact closed-form expressions for the signal energies of linear recurrences at finite width. Its main value is that it enables a precise analysis of signal propagation under arbitrary joint scaling of recurrent depth and width, for example in regimes of the form
t,k\sim n^{\alpha}
. Section
3
develops the corresponding depth–width scaling laws, summarized in Figure
1
.

Below, we make several remarks on extensions and variants of this result.

Remark 1

(General input covariances)
.

Theorem
2.1
is stated for whitened inputs for simplicity. However, the same argument extends immediately to arbitrary input covariances
\Sigma^{(0)},\dots,\Sigma^{(t)}
, where
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{(s)}\mathbf{x}^{(s)\top}]=\Sigma^{(s)}
for all
0\leq s\leq t
.
In that case, the expected LRU signal energy is

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\frac{\tr\!\bigl(\Sigma^{(t-k)}\bigr)}{n}\,\tilde{Q}_{n,k},

(7)

where
\tilde{Q}_{n,k}
denotes the RNN signal energy under whitened inputs, as given in Theorem
2.1
. In fact, the proof in Appendix
B
, and in particular Lemma
B.10
, is already formulated in this general covariance setting. Since we impose no additional assumptions on cross-time correlations or on the distribution of the input sequence
(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(t)})
, the result is fully general with respect to the inputs, apart from their independence from the recurrent weight matrix.

Remark 2

(Real weights case)
.

While our main result is stated for complex weights, which are common in practical linear recurrences, it also directly yields a lower bound for the corresponding real Gaussian models. Let
Q_{n,k}^{\mathbb{R}}
and
S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}
denote the analogues of
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
for a real Gaussian weight matrix with i.i.d. entries of variance
1/n
. Then, with an additional assumption that cross-time input correlations satisfy
\tr(\mathbb{E}\![\mathbf{x}^{(s)}\mathbf{x}^{(r)\top}])\geq 0
for all
0\leq s,r\leq t
, we have:

Q_{n,k}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq Q_{n,k},\qquad S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq S_{n,t},

(8)

for all
n,k,t
. In particular, any depth–width scaling regime that leads to exploding signals in the complex model also leads to exploding signals in the real model. We prove this result in Appendix
B.4
.

2.1
Proof Idea

The proof combines standard random matrix techniques with a reduction to a cycle counting problem for certain random permutations. The starting point is the observation that the main difficulty in computing both signal energies,
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
, is the evaluation of the finite-width
trace moment

\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\Bigr].

(9)

The reduction of
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
to this quantity is carried out in Appendix
B.3
using standard properties of Gaussian random matrices. Thus, the core of the proof is to evaluate this trace moment exactly in the given complex Gaussian setting.

To evaluate the trace moment, we use a Wick expansion argument: we first expand the trace into a sum of monomials of the form
\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{a_{0}a_{1}}\,\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{a_{1}a_{2}}\cdots\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{a_{k-1}a_{k}}\,\mathbf{W}_{a_{0}b_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{W}_{b_{k-1}a_{k}}
, and then apply Wick’s theorem for Gaussian moments
26
;
38
to compute their expectations. This is a standard way of converting random matrix moments into combinatorial counting problems, and we develop it in our setting in Appendix
B.1
. Related ideas have been used successfully in finite-width analyses of fully-connected feedforward networks
40
;
32
. In the recurrent setting, however, the resulting combinatorics are substantially more intricate because the same random matrix is reused across time. The key simplification in our setting is that, for complex Gaussian recurrences, the Wick expansion takes a particularly clean form and can be indexed by permutations
\sigma\in S_{k}
. Each such permutation contributes according to the number of matrix indices that remain unconstrained after the corresponding contractions. Under the complex Gaussian initialization with variance
1/n
, this yields the exact representation

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}n^{-k-1+F(\sigma)},

(10)

where
F(\sigma)
denotes the number of
free indices
, i.e. the number of equivalence classes of indices
(a_{0},\dots,a_{k},b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1})
left unconstrained by the contractions associated with
\sigma
.

For a permutation
\sigma
, the quantity
F(\sigma)
turns out to be exactly the number of cycles of the commutator
[\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}]
, where
\tau=(0,1,\dots,k)
is a long cycle and
\tilde{\sigma}
is the extension of
\sigma
fixing
0
. This provides the link between the Wick expansion and permutation combinatorics. As we show in Appendix
B.2
, this identification reduces the problem to counting cycles of permutations of the form
\tau^{-1}c
, where
c
is a uniformly random full cycle. This quantity can be computed exactly using known results, in particular those of Dubach (2024)
9
. While the argument in
9
and ours is formulated in a different language, it is closely related to the literature on Hultman numbers and, in particular, to the corresponding closed formulas
36
;
2
.

Putting these ingredients together yields an exact closed-form expression for the trace moment, and therefore for
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
. In this way, the proof reduces the finite-width signal propagation problem to a finite combinatorial counting problem that can be solved exactly.

3
Depth–Width Scaling Regimes for
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}

The exact finite-width formulas in Theorem
2.1
allow us to analyze signal propagation under joint scaling of recurrent depth and width. In particular, they make it possible to characterize the behavior of the signal energies in regimes such as
k\sim n^{\alpha}
and
t\sim n^{\alpha}
, and thus identify the depth scales at which the infinite-width approximation remains accurate and those at which it fails.

For this purpose, it is convenient to rewrite
Q_{n,k}
as follows:

Q_{n,k}=\dfrac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\Biggl[\prod_{j=0}^{k+1}\Bigl(1+\dfrac{j}{n}\Bigr)-\prod_{j=0}^{k+1}\Bigl(1-\dfrac{j}{n}\Bigr)\Biggr],

(11)

since this form makes the relevant asymptotic regimes more transparent. The corresponding analysis for
Q_{n,k}
is given in Appendix
C
, and for
S_{n,t}
in Appendix
D
. We now describe the asymptotic behavior of
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
across the three regimes illustrated in Figure
1
: the
subcritical
,
critical
, and
supercritical
regimes.

3.1
Subcritical (Infinite-Width) Regime:
k=o(\sqrt{n})
and
t=o(\sqrt{n})

We start with the regime in which the recurrent depth remains asymptotically below the critical
\sqrt{n}
scale. In this case, signal propagation follows the same behavior as in the infinite-width limit:

Q_{n,k}\to 1,\qquad S_{n,t}\sim t+1.

(12)

Here, and throughout this section, the notation
a\sim b
means that
a/b\to 1
in the corresponding joint scaling limit. Precise statements and proofs for this regime are given in Theorems
C.1
and
D.1
in the Appendix. Below, we make several observations about the implications of these results.

Stability of Glorot initialization.

The asymptotics in (
12
) coincide exactly with the classical infinite-width limit
n\to\infty
at fixed
k
and
t
. In particular,
Q_{n,k}
remains asymptotically equal to
1
, uniformly over recurrent depths satisfying
k\ll\sqrt{n}
. Thus, under standard Glorot initialization (
3
), signals remain stable throughout the entire subcritical regime. In this sense, our result extends the classical stability of Glorot initialization at infinite width with fixed recurrent depth
13
;
6
to the joint depth–width scaling regimes with
k=o(\sqrt{n})
.

Infinite-width theory does not distinguish recurrent and feedforward networks.

A second important observation is that, throughout the subcritical regime, recurrent and feedforward signal propagation remain asymptotically indistinguishable at the level of signal energies. More precisely, if one replaces the shared recurrent matrix
\mathbf{W}
by independent matrices
\mathbf{W}^{(0)},\dots,\mathbf{W}^{(t)}
across steps in the linear RNN (
2
) or the LRU (
1
), then the asymptotic behavior of
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
remains unchanged under the scaling
k,t=o(\sqrt{n})
. This result also holds in existing infinite-width frameworks, such as Tensor Programs
42
, and this observation is made explicit for example in
1
.

While the second observation agrees with well-established literature, it is somewhat unsatisfactory from the perspective of recurrent modeling. In practice, recurrent networks are much more prone than feedforward ones to signal instability, and mathematically one expects repeated application of the same matrix to accumulate signal growth faster than independent layer-wise multiplication. The subcritical regime therefore identifies the range in which infinite-width theory remains faithful, but it also highlights its main limitation: below the critical scale, the theory cannot distinguish recurrence from feedforward composition. One of the main contributions of this paper is to show that this distinction becomes visible precisely at the critical scaling regime, which we analyze next.

3.2
Critical Regime:
k\sim c\sqrt{n}
and
t\sim\tilde{c}\sqrt{n}

The finite-width corrections become non-negligible when recurrent depth reaches the critical
\sqrt{n}
scale. In this regime, the signal energy
Q_{n,k}
no longer follows the infinite-width prediction and instead converges to a nontrivial critical profile, illustrated in Figure
1
and given by:

Q_{n,k}\to\frac{2}{c^{2}}\sinh\Bigl(\frac{c^{2}}{2}\Bigr),\quad\text{where }k\sim c\sqrt{n},\quad c>0.

(13)

Thus, unlike in the subcritical regime, the signal energy now depends nontrivially on the ratio
k/\sqrt{n}
. In particular,
Q_{n,k}
grows strictly above its infinite-width value when
c>0
, showing systematic finite-width signal amplification over depth. This result is proven in Theorem
C.2
in the Appendix.

For the cumulative LRU signal energy
S_{n,t}
, this yields the following asymptotic law:

S_{n,t}\sim\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{\tilde{c}}\dfrac{2}{x^{2}}\sinh\Bigl(\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\,dx,\quad\text{where }t\sim\tilde{c}\sqrt{n},\quad\tilde{c}>0.

(14)

where the integrand is continuously extended to take the value
1
at
x=0
. This result is proven in Theorem
D.2
in the Appendix. For large
\tilde{c}=t/\sqrt{n}
, this integral is dominated by its upper endpoint and behaves as
\tilde{c}^{-3}\exp(\tilde{c}^{2}/2)
. Thus, in the critical regime, signal growth becomes exponential in the scaling parameter
t/\sqrt{n}
. Below, we discuss the implications of the emergence of critical regime.

Instability of Glorot initialization.

The transition to the critical regime shows that standard Glorot initialization (
3
) becomes unstable once recurrent depth reaches the critical
\sqrt{n}
scale. By Remark
2
, this conclusion applies to both complex and real weights cases. This helps explain why Glorot initialization, although natural from the infinite-width perspective, typically becomes unstable in modern long-range linear recurrences
29
;
18
. We note that Bar et al. (2025)
4
have recently shown that the scaling
t\sim\tilde{c}\sqrt{n}
already produces exponential growth in the LRU signal energy
S_{n,t}
. However, that work established only a crude lower bound, which in particular missed the
\sqrt{n}
prefactor appearing in (
14
). Our result sharpens this picture in two ways: it shows that the
\sqrt{n}
scale is exactly the threshold at which the transition to critical behavior occurs, and identifies the full asymptotic profile in this regime. In this sense, we give a precise answer to when Glorot initialization ceases to be stable in long-range linear recurrences.

Recurrent versus feedforward signal propagation.

Starting at the critical regime, signal propagation in recurrent networks begins to differ sharply from that in feedforward networks—a distinction that is invisible in the infinite-width theory. While finite-width corrections accumulate and become non-negligible once depth reaches the
\sqrt{n}
scale in recurrences, the relevant depth scale for fully-connected networks is
L\sim cn

21
;
20
;
35
. Moreover, even at this proportional regime, the signal energies remain stable in feedforward networks, while finite-width effects are primarily reflected in their fluctuations, which has important consequences for convergence to the kernel regime
19
;
35
. Thus, the effects of finite-width corrections and the depth scales at which they first become visible are fundamentally different in recurrent and feedforward architectures. This observation helps reconcile theory with practice: recurrent models are known to be substantially more prone to signal instability, but this distinction is not captured by standard infinite-width analyses.

3.3
Supercritical sublinear regime:
\sqrt{n}\ll k\ll n
and
\sqrt{n}\ll t\ll n

Beyond the critical depth scale, finite-width effects dominate, and both signal energies diverge in the joint
(n,k)
limit. In particular, when
\sqrt{n}\ll k\ll n
and
\sqrt{n}\ll t\ll n
, we have

Q_{n,k}\sim\dfrac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}e^{n\Psi(k/n)},\qquad S_{n,t}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{t^{3}}\exp\Bigl(n\Psi(t/n)\Bigr),

(15)

where
\Psi(x):=(1+x)\log(1+x)-x
. Thus, once recurrent depth exceeds the critical
\sqrt{n}
scale, signal growth becomes exponential in depth and no finite joint scaling limit exists. These results are proven in Theorems
C.3
and
D.3
in the Appendix.

A particularly tractable subregime occurs when
k,t=o(n^{2/3})
.
In that case, the exponent can be expanded to the leading order, yielding the asymptotic profiles illustrated in Figure
1
:

Q_{n,k}\sim\dfrac{n}{k^{2}}\exp\Bigl(\dfrac{k^{2}}{2n}\Bigr),\qquad S_{n,t}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{t^{3}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\Bigr).

(16)

In principle, the analysis can be extended further, beyond the proportional depth–width scaling. However, all such regimes exhibit rapid signal explosion, so we do not pursue them here.

5
Discussion

We derived exact finite-width formulas for the RNN and LRU signal energies
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
under complex Gaussian initialization, and used them to analyze joint recurrent-depth–width scaling in linear recurrences. This yields a sharp answer to the main question of the paper: the infinite-width approximation remains accurate throughout the subcritical regime
k,t=o(\sqrt{n})
, but fails once recurrent depth reaches the critical
\sqrt{n}
scale.

This transition has two main consequences. First, it shows that standard Glorot initialization, while stable in the classical infinite-width sense, ceases to be stable for long-range recurrent models from critical regime onward. Second, it reveals a qualitative difference between recurrent and feedforward architectures: in recurrent models, finite-width effects become visible already at depth
\sqrt{n}
, whereas in fully connected feedforward networks the corresponding depth scale is of order
n
. Thus, finite-width signal amplification appears much earlier in recurrent models.

Overall, this work contributes to the growing literature on finite-width corrections, joint scaling limits, and the limitations of classical infinite-width theory in modern deep learning. To the best of our knowledge, it is among the first to study these questions in the recurrent setting.

5.1
Limitations and Future Work

While this work provides a full finite-width analysis of linear recurrences under complex Gaussian initialization with general input sequences, it does not yet extend to more general recurrent models.

First, although the analysis developed here is sufficient to show that the recurrent depth scale
\sqrt{n}
leads to non-negligible deviations from infinite-width predictions in the real Gaussian weights case, obtaining a full counterpart of our main results for real weights is technically more involved and is therefore left for future work. More broadly, it would be interesting to extend the analysis to other random initialization schemes, including practical initialization strategies used in modern linear recurrent architectures such as LRUs
29
and SSMs
15
;
17
, beyond the Gaussian baseline.

A second natural direction is to move beyond the linear setting. While many modern long-range sequence models, including LRUs and SSMs, are built around linear recurrent blocks, classical recurrent architectures typically incorporate nonlinear activations
11
;
7
. Understanding finite-width signal propagation in such nonlinear recurrences remains an important open problem.

Another limitation of the present work is that it concerns only random initialization. Questions of signal propagation and stability during training are left entirely open. A natural next step would be to study when long-range linear recurrences at finite width remain close to their linearized kernel regime, which is known to describe the infinite-width limit
27
;
1
, and when recurrent models at large depth depart from that picture. Beyond this, it is also of interest to identify and characterize feature learning regimes under joint recurrent-depth–width scaling.

6
Related Work

We review related work on signal propagation theory, both at infinite width and under finite-width corrections and joint depth–width limits, and the implications for stability in recurrent models.

6.1
Signal Propagation at Infinite Width

Existing signal propagation theory primarily concerns the infinite-width limit
n\to\infty
at fixed depth
L
. In fully-connected networks, the behavior of signal energies in this regime was derived by Schoenholz et al. (2016)
(
33
)
and Poole et al. (2016)
(
31
)
. These analyses rely on the fact that preactivations in infinitely-wide networks become Gaussian, as shown rigorously e.g. in Matthews et al. (2018)
28
. The same perspective was subsequently extended to other architectures, including convolutional networks
(
39
)
, residual networks
37
, and recurrent networks
12
. More broadly, infinite-width signal propagation theory has been formulated in a unified way for general deep learning architectures within the Tensor Programs framework
42
;
43
;
44
;
41
.

Recurrent models.

Several works explicitly analyzed signal propagation in vanilla RNNs and gated recurrent units in the infinite-width, fixed-depth setting
(
6
;
12
;
1
)
. Recurrent models also fall within the Tensor Programs framework. As mentioned above, a common conclusion of these works is that, at infinite width, signal propagation in recurrent models behaves as if the weights were independent across recurrent time steps. That is, at the level of infinite-width signal propagation, recurrence is indistinguishable from feedforward composition. This observation is made explicit, for example, in Theorem 3 of Alemohammad et al. (2021)
1
and also in Segadlo et al. (2022)
34
.

Stable initialization schemes.

One of the main practical consequences of signal propagation theory is the principled design of stable initialization schemes, which prevent signals from exploding or vanishing with depth. This leads, in particular, to the familiar variance-preserving Gaussian initializations such as He initialization
23
and Glorot initialization
13
.

6.2
Finite-Width Corrections and Joint Depth–Width Limits

A growing line of work studies how finite-width networks deviate from their infinite-width limits. A central question in this literature is how such deviations accumulate with depth, which naturally leads to the study of joint depth–width scaling limits in deep learning models.

Feedforward networks.

The most extensively studied setting here is fully-connected feedforward networks. In this case, finite-width corrections become non-negligible when depth scales linearly with width, i.e., in the regime
L\sim cn
. First results in this direction were obtained by Hanin (2018)
21
, who analyzed how fluctuations of signal energies accumulate with depth. Follow-up works extended this picture to broader settings and studied implications for signal stability and convergence to the kernel regime
20
;
19
;
22
;
35
;
40
. A more general perspective on finite-width corrections was later developed in Roberts & Yaida (2022)
32
. Overall, in fully-connected feedforward networks, finite-width corrections can be analyzed layer by layer and are therefore comparatively tractable.

Recurrent models.

By contrast, the repeated reuse of the same matrix across time steps in recurrent models leads to a substantially more intricate structure of finite-width corrections and of their accumulation with depth. To the best of our knowledge, the only prior work to explicitly identify the
\sqrt{n}
scale in linear recurrences is Bar et al. (2025)
4
, where the authors used finite-width corrections to the spectral radius of Gaussian matrices to show that deviations from infinite-width behavior accumulate on the
t\sim c\sqrt{n}
scale. However, that argument yields only a crude lower bound on the signal energy and does not recover the sharp finite-width behavior derived in the present work. More broadly, several earlier works investigated fluctuations around the infinite-width limit in recurrent networks and observed that they are larger than in feedforward architectures
34
;
14
. However, these works do not identify an explicit depth scale at which infinite-width predictions break down, nor do they provide a sharp characterization of the size of finite-width corrections.

Stability of Glorot initialization in recurrent sequence models.

Because standard infinite-width signal propagation theory does not distinguish feedforward and recurrent models, it predicts that Glorot initialization
13
should remain stable in long-range linear recurrences. However, in practice, recent works on recurrent sequence models have found that Glorot is unstable and have therefore introduced additional rescaling. For example, Orvieto et al. (2023)
29
used truncated Glorot, which effectively contracts the spectrum by roughly
0.8
, while Gu et al. (2021)
18
explicitly report iteratively reducing the Gaussian variance until the model no longer explodes. More recently, Bar et al. (2025)
4
proposed a theoretically motivated approach to such rescaling. At the same time, state-of-the-art linear recurrent sequence models typically rely on structured non-Gaussian initializations, while rescaled variants of Glorot are used mainly as baselines
(
15
;
10
;
16
;
18
)
.

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\bigr]

B.1
Trace moment via Wick expansion and free-index counting

In this subsection, we carry out the standard reduction of the trace moment to a combinatorial counting problem. Namely, we expand the trace into monomials in the Gaussian entries of
\mathbf{W}
, apply Wick’s theorem, and then count how many indices remain free after imposing the Kronecker constraints associated with each Wick pairing.

We begin by expanding the trace as follows:

\displaystyle\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{0},a_{k}=1}^{n}(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}_{a_{k}a_{0}}(\mathbf{W}^{k})_{a_{0}a_{k}}

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{0},a_{k}=1}^{n}\overline{(\mathbf{W}^{k})_{a_{0}a_{k}}}\,(\mathbf{W}^{k})_{a_{0}a_{k}}

(18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{0},\dots,a_{k}=1\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\overline{\mathbf{W}_{a_{0}a_{1}}\mathbf{W}_{a_{1}a_{2}}\cdots\mathbf{W}_{a_{k-1}a_{k}}}\,\mathbf{W}_{a_{0}b_{1}}\mathbf{W}_{b_{1}b_{2}}\cdots\mathbf{W}_{b_{k-1}a_{k}}

(19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{0},\dots,a_{k}=1\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{a_{0}a_{1}}\,\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{a_{1}a_{2}}\cdots\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{a_{k-1}a_{k}}\,\mathbf{W}_{a_{0}b_{1}}\mathbf{W}_{b_{1}b_{2}}\cdots\mathbf{W}_{b_{k-1}a_{k}}.

(20)

Thus the trace moment reduces to expectations of monomials in the entries of
\mathbf{W}
, which are centered proper complex Gaussians with variance
1/n
under the complex Glorot initialization (
3
).

For such monomials, Wick’s theorem takes a particularly simple form, specified in the next lemma.

Lemma B.1

(Wick theorem for proper complex Gaussians)
.

Let
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})
be a centered proper complex Gaussian vector, meaning that

\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}Z_{j}]=0
for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.
Let
I=(I_{1},\dots,I_{k})
and
J=(J_{1},\dots,J_{r})
be arbitrary index tuples with entries in
\{1,\dots,m\}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{a=1}^{k}\overline{Z_{I_{a}}}\prod_{b=1}^{r}Z_{J_{b}}\right]=\delta_{k,r}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\prod_{a=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\overline{Z_{I_{a}}}\,Z_{J_{\sigma(a)}}\right],

(21)

where
S_{k}
denotes the set of permutations of
\{1,\dots,k\}
.

Proof.

We apply the usual Wick theorem to the family obtained by listing together the conjugated and non-conjugated Gaussian variables. Define the
doubled family

X_{1},\dots,X_{k+r}:=(\overline{Z_{I_{1}}},\dots,\overline{Z_{I_{k}}},Z_{J_{1}},\dots,Z_{J_{r}}).

(22)

Applying Wick’s theorem to this family yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{a=1}^{k}\overline{Z_{I_{a}}}\prod_{b=1}^{r}Z_{J_{b}}\right]=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(k+r)}\prod_{\{p,q\}\in\pi}\mathbb{E}[X_{p}X_{q}],

(23)

where
\mathcal{P}_{2}(k+r)
denotes the set of all pairings of
\{1,\dots,k+r\}
.

Since
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})
is proper complex Gaussian, we have

\mathbb{E}[Z_{p}Z_{q}]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\overline{Z_{p}}\,\overline{Z_{q}}]=\overline{\mathbb{E}[Z_{p}Z_{q}]}=0

(24)

for all
p,q\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Therefore every pairing containing a pair of the form

(\overline{Z_{p}},\overline{Z_{q}})
or
(Z_{p},Z_{q})
contributes zero to the right-hand side of (
23
). Hence only pairings consisting entirely of mixed pairs

(\overline{Z_{I_{a}}},Z_{J_{b}})
can survive.

Such pairings exist only if
k=r
. When
k=r
, every surviving pairing is uniquely determined by a permutation
\sigma\in S_{k}
, namely by matching

\overline{Z_{I_{a}}}\quad\text{with}\quad Z_{J_{\sigma(a)}},\qquad a=1,\dots,k.

(25)

Substituting these surviving pairings into (
23
) gives

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{a=1}^{k}\overline{Z_{I_{a}}}\prod_{b=1}^{r}Z_{J_{b}}\right]=\delta_{k,r}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\prod_{a=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\overline{Z_{I_{a}}}\,Z_{J_{\sigma(a)}}\right],

(26)

as claimed.
∎

Applying Lemma
B.1
to the monomials appearing in the trace expansion, we see that the expectation is indexed by permutations
\sigma\in S_{k}
, each of which prescribes one possible pairing between the
k
conjugated factors and the
k
non-conjugated factors. The contribution of a fixed permutation
\sigma
is therefore

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{0},\dots,a_{k}=1\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\prod_{r=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{a_{r-1}a_{r}}\,\mathbf{W}_{b_{\sigma(r)-1}b_{\sigma(r)}}\right].

(27)

Since the entries of
\mathbf{W}
are i.i.d. proper complex Gaussians with variance
1/n
, each covariance equals

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{ij}\mathbf{W}_{pq}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\,\delta_{i,p}\delta_{j,q}.

Hence the contribution of
\sigma
becomes

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{0},\dots,a_{k}=1\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\delta_{a_{r-1},\,b_{\sigma(r)-1}}\delta_{a_{r},\,b_{\sigma(r)}}.

(28)

Here we use the convention

b_{0}:=a_{0},\qquad b_{k}:=a_{k},

so that all indices appearing in the contractions are among

a_{0},\dots,a_{k},b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1}.

The key combinatorial quantity is the number of indices that remain unconstrained after all the Kronecker contractions have been imposed, as defined below.

Definition B.2

(Free-index count and defect of a permutation)
.

For
\sigma\in S_{k}
, we define
F(\sigma)
to be the number of
free indices
, namely the number of equivalence classes of indices that remain unconstrained after all
\delta
-contractions associated with
\sigma
have been imposed. The corresponding
defect
is defined by

r(\sigma):=k+1-F(\sigma).

(29)

Since each free index contributes a factor of
n
, whereas each covariance contributes a factor of
n^{-1}
, the total contribution of
\sigma
is exactly

n^{-k+F(\sigma)}=n^{1-r(\sigma)}.

(30)

After dividing by the outer factor
1/n
, we obtain the following representation of the normalized trace moment.

Lemma B.3

(Trace moment as a free-index sum)
.

For every
k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}n^{-r(\sigma)},

(31)

where
r(\sigma)=k+1-F(\sigma)
and
F(\sigma)
is the number of free indices left after the Wick contractions corresponding to
\sigma
.

Lemma
B.3
reduces the problem to understanding the free-index count
F(\sigma)
, or equivalently the defect
r(\sigma)
, associated with each permutation
\sigma\in S_{k}
. In the next subsection, we show that
F(\sigma)
can be identified with the number of cycles of a certain commutator of permutations, which allows the trace moment to be evaluated exactly.

B.2
Exact evaluation of the trace moment via commutator cycle counts

By Lemma
B.3
, the trace moment is determined by the free-index count
F(\sigma)
associated with each permutation
\sigma\in S_{k}
. We now show that this quantity admits a simple permutation-theoretic interpretation: it is exactly the number of cycles of a certain commutator. This reduces the trace computation to a cycle-counting problem, which can then be evaluated using known results on random permutations.

Lemma B.4
.

For a permutation
\sigma\in S_{k}
, the free-index count
F(\sigma)
is exactly the number of cycles of

[\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}]:=\tau^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}\tau\tilde{\sigma},

(32)

where
\tau=(0,1,\dots,k)\in S_{k+1}
and
\tilde{\sigma}\in S_{k+1}
is the extension of
\sigma
fixing
0
, i.e.

\tilde{\sigma}(0)=0,\qquad\tilde{\sigma}(i)=\sigma(i),\quad i=1,\dots,k.

(33)

Proof.

Recall from the previous subsection that a permutation
\sigma\in S_{k}
imposes the Kronecker constraints

a_{i-1}=b_{\sigma(i)-1},\qquad a_{i}=b_{\sigma(i)},\qquad i=1,\dots,k,

(34)

together with the boundary conditions

b_{0}=a_{0},\qquad b_{k}=a_{k}.

The free-index count
F(\sigma)
is the number of equivalence classes generated by these identifications.

To rewrite the constraints more symmetrically, set
j=\sigma(i)
. Then

a_{\sigma^{-1}(j)-1}=b_{j-1},\qquad a_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}=b_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,k.

(35)

Eliminating the
b
-variables yields

a_{\sigma^{-1}(j-1)}=a_{\sigma^{-1}(j)-1},\qquad j=2,\dots,k,

(36)

while the two boundary conditions become

a_{0}=a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)-1},\qquad a_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}=a_{k}.

These relations can be written uniformly by extending
\sigma
to
\tilde{\sigma}\in S_{k+1}
and introducing the full cycle
\tau=(0,1,\dots,k)
. Then for every
j\in\{0,\dots,k\}
,

a_{\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}(j)}=a_{\tau^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}\tau(j)}.

(37)

Indeed:

•

for
j=0
, this reads
a_{0}=a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)-1}
;

•

for
j=1,\dots,k-1
, this reads
a_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}=a_{\sigma^{-1}(j+1)-1}
;

•

for
j=k
, this reads
a_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}=a_{k}
.

Now let

m=\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}(j).

Since
j=\tilde{\sigma}(m)
, the previous identity becomes

a_{m}=a_{\tau^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}\tau\tilde{\sigma}(m)}=a_{[\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}](m)},\qquad m=0,\dots,k.

(38)

Hence the equivalence classes among the indices
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{k}\}
are exactly the orbits of the permutation
[\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}]
. Therefore the number of free indices is precisely the number of cycles of this commutator.
∎

Lemma
B.4
reduces the trace problem to counting cycles of the commutator
[\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}]
. The next step is to relate this commutator to a simpler random permutation model that has already been analyzed in the literature. Namely, we use the following known cycle-count formula, which is a reformulation of Theorem 2 in
[
9
]
.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
\tau=(0,\dots,M)\in S_{M+1}
, with
M\geq 0
, and let
c\in S_{M+1}
be uniformly distributed among all
(M+1)
-cycles. Then the number of cycles of
\tau^{-1}c
, denoted
\mathcal{C}(\tau^{-1}c)
, satisfies

\mathbb{E}[t^{\mathcal{C}(\tau^{-1}c)}]=\dfrac{1}{(M+2)!}\bigl(F^{+}_{M+2}(t)-F^{-}_{M+2}(t)\bigr),

(39)

where
F^{\pm}_{n}(t)
are the rising and falling factorials, defined by

F^{+}_{n}(t):=t(t+1)\dots(t+n-1),\qquad F^{-}_{n}(t):=t(t-1)\dots(t-n+1).

(40)

We note that Theorem 2 in
[
9
]
is stated in terms of the number of cycles of the commutator

[\rho,\tau]:=\rho\tau\rho^{-1}\tau^{-1},

where
\rho
is uniformly distributed over
S_{M+1}
, rather than in terms of
\tau^{-1}c
as in the formulation above. However, as also observed in
[
9
]
, the two formulations are equivalent. Indeed, one can write

[\rho,\tau]=\tau_{1}\tau_{2},\qquad\tau_{1}:=\rho\tau\rho^{-1},\qquad\tau_{2}:=\tau^{-1}.

(41)

Since
\tau_{1}
is conjugate to
\tau
, it is uniformly distributed over the conjugacy class of
\tau
, that is, over all permutations with the same cycle structure as
\tau
. In our setting,
\tau
is a single
(M+1)
-cycle, so
\tau_{1}
is uniform among all
(M+1)
-cycles. Therefore the distribution of the number of cycles of
[\rho,\tau]
is exactly the same as that of
\tau^{-1}c
, where
c
is uniform among all
(M+1)
-cycles, which yields the stated reformulation.

It remains to show that the commutator in Lemma
B.4
fits into this framework. The key observation is that conjugating the full cycle
\tau
by a permutation fixing
0
produces a uniformly random full cycle.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
\tau=(0,\dots,k)
and let
\tilde{\sigma}\in S_{k+1}
be uniform on the set

H:=\{\pi\in S_{k+1}:\pi(0)=0\}.

(42)

Then
\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}\tau\tilde{\sigma}
is uniform among all
(k+1)
-cycles in
S_{k+1}
.

Proof.

The set
H
has cardinality
k!
, and there are also exactly
k!
many
(k+1)
-cycles in
S_{k+1}
. It is therefore enough to show that the map

\phi_{\tau}:H\to\{(k+1)\text{-cycles in }S_{k+1}\},\qquad\phi_{\tau}(\pi)=\pi^{-1}\tau\pi,

(43)

is injective.

Suppose
\phi_{\tau}(\pi_{1})=\phi_{\tau}(\pi_{2})
for some
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in H
. Then

\pi_{2}\pi_{1}^{-1}\tau=\tau\pi_{2}\pi_{1}^{-1},

(44)

so
\pi_{2}\pi_{1}^{-1}
belongs to the centralizer of the full cycle
\tau
. But the centralizer of
\tau
is exactly

\{\tau^{m}:m=0,\dots,k\}.

Among these elements, only the identity fixes
0
. Since both
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
fix
0
, so does
\pi_{2}\pi_{1}^{-1}
, and therefore
\pi_{2}\pi_{1}^{-1}=\id
. Hence
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}
, proving injectivity. Thus
\phi_{\tau}
is bijective, and the image of a uniform
\tilde{\sigma}\in H
is uniform among all
(k+1)
-cycles.
∎

We can now combine the previous two lemmas to evaluate the cycle count appearing in Lemma

Lemma B.7
.

Let
\tau=(0,\dots,k)
, let
\sigma
range uniformly over
S_{k}

\tilde{\sigma}
be its extension to

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}n^{\mathcal{C}([\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}])}=\dfrac{k!}{(k+2)!}\bigl(F^{+}_{k+2}(n)-F^{-}_{k+2}(n)\bigr).

(45)

Proof.

Write

[\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}]=\tau^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}\tau\tilde{\sigma}=\tau^{-1}c.

(46)

If
\sigma
is uniform on

, then
\tilde{\sigma}
is uniform on the set
H
from (

is uniform among all
(k+1)

\frac{1}{|S_{k}|}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}n^{\mathcal{C}([\tau^{-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{-1}])}=\mathbb{E}\bigl[n^{\mathcal{C}(\tau^{-1}c)}\bigr].

(47)

Now apply Lemma
B.5
with

\mathbb{E}\bigl[n^{\mathcal{C}(\tau^{-1}c)}\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{(k+2)!}\bigl(F^{+}_{k+2}(n)-F^{-}_{k+2}(n)\bigr).

(48)

Multiplying by

gives the claim.
∎

.

For every

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\right]=\dfrac{F^{+}_{k+2}(n)-F^{-}_{k+2}(n)}{n^{k+1}(k+1)(k+2)}.

By Lemma

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}n^{-r(\sigma)}.

Using the definition of the defect and Lemma

(53)

Reduction of

We now return to the signal propagation quantities

Lemma B.9
.

Consider a linear RNN (as defined in (

\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

(as in (

Q_{n,k}=\dfrac{1}{n}\tr(\Sigma^{(0)})\cdot\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\Bigr],

(54)

where
\Sigma^{(0)}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{x}^{(0)}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})^{\top}\bigr]
is the input covariance.

Proof.

We begin by rewriting the squared norm of the hidden state as a trace:

\|\mathbf{h}^{(k)}_{\textup{RNN}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{W}^{k}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}=\tr\Bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\Bigr).

(55)

Taking expectations and using the independence between
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{W}
, we obtain

Q_{n,k}=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathbf{h}^{(k)}_{\textup{RNN}}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{n}\tr\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{x}^{(0)}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})^{\top}\bigr]\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]\Bigr).

(56)

Thus it remains to understand the matrix

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr].

We notice that this matrix is proportional to the identity, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]=\alpha\mathbb{I}_{n}

(57)

for some scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. Indeed, by unitary invariance of the complex Gaussian law, for every fixed unitary matrix
U
we have

U\mathbf{W}U^{*}\overset{d}{=}\mathbf{W}.

(58)

Raising to the
k
-th power and taking adjoints gives

(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\overset{d}{=}U(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}U^{*}.

(59)

Taking expectations, we conclude that for every unitary
U
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]=U\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]U^{*}.

(60)

Equivalently,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]\,U=U\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr],

(61)

so
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]
commutes with every unitary matrix. This is only possible if it is proportional to the identity.

Substituting this back into the trace formula yields

\displaystyle Q_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\tr\bigl(\Sigma^{(0)}\,\alpha I_{n}\bigr)=\dfrac{1}{n}\tr(\Sigma^{(0)})\,\alpha.

(62)

On the other hand,

\tr\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]\Bigr)=n\alpha,

(63)

so

\alpha=\frac{1}{n}\tr\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]\Bigr)=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr).

(64)

Combining the two identities gives

Q_{n,k}=\dfrac{1}{n}\tr(\Sigma^{(0)})\cdot\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\Bigr],

(65)

as claimed.
∎

The next lemma gives the analogous reduction for the LRU signal energy. The only new point is that, after expanding the squared norm, one must show that the mixed terms with different powers of
\mathbf{W}
vanish.

Lemma B.10
.

Consider an LRU (as defined in (
1
)) with weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, initialized with i.i.d. proper complex Gaussian entries of variance
1/n
(as in (
3
)). Then the LRU signal energy
S_{n,t}
(defined in (
4
)) satisfies

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\dfrac{1}{n}\tr\!\bigl(\Sigma^{(t-k)}\bigr)\,\tilde{Q}_{n,k},

(66)

where
\Sigma^{(k)}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{x}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})^{\top}\bigr]
are the input covariances for all time steps
k=0,\dots,t
, and
\tilde{Q}_{n,k}
is the RNN signal energy under whitened data, i.e. when
\Sigma^{(0)}=\mathbb{I}_{n}
.

Proof.

We start from the explicit representation of the LRU hidden state:

\mathbf{h}_{\textup{LRU}}^{(t)}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\mathbf{W}^{k}\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}.

(67)

Expanding its squared norm gives

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{h}_{\textup{LRU}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,r=0}^{t}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-r)})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{r})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}

(68)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,r=0}^{t}\tr\Bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-r)})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{r})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\Bigr).

(69)

Taking expectations and using the independence between the inputs and the weight matrix, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathbf{h}_{\textup{LRU}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{2}\bigr]=\sum_{k,r=0}^{t}\tr\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-r)})^{\top}\bigr]\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{r})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]\Bigr).

(70)

We now show that the mixed terms with
k\neq r
vanish. As in Lemma
B.9
, unitary invariance implies that
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{r})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]
is proportional to identity. It therefore suffices to check that all diagonal entries are zero whenever
k\neq r
.

Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

\displaystyle\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{r})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]_{ii}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{0}=1}^{n}\overline{(\mathbf{W}^{r})_{a_{0}i}}\,(\mathbf{W}^{k})_{a_{0}i}

(71)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{0},\dots,a_{r-1}=1\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\overline{\mathbf{W}_{a_{0}a_{1}}}\overline{\mathbf{W}_{a_{1}a_{2}}}\dots\overline{\mathbf{W}_{a_{r-1}i}}\mathbf{W}_{a_{0}b_{1}}\mathbf{W}_{b_{1}b_{2}}\dots\mathbf{W}_{b_{k-1}i}.

(72)

The family of Gaussian variables appearing here is proper complex Gaussian, so Lemma
B.1
applies. Since there are
r
conjugated factors and
k
non-conjugated factors, the expectation vanishes whenever
k\neq r
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{r})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]=0\qquad\text{for all }k\neq r.

(73)

Thus only the terms with
k=r
remain, and the signal energy simplifies to

S_{n,t}=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathbf{h}_{\textup{LRU}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{2}\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{t}\tr\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)})^{\top}\bigr]\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr]\Bigr).

(74)

At this point, we can apply exactly the same argument as in Lemma
B.9
to each term of the sum, obtaining

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\tr(\Sigma^{(t-k)})\cdot\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\Bigr].

(75)

By definition, the quantity

\tilde{Q}_{n,k}=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\Bigr]

(76)

is precisely the RNN signal energy corresponding to whitened inputs. Substituting this into the previous expression gives

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\tr\!\bigl(\Sigma^{(t-k)}\bigr)\,\tilde{Q}_{n,k},

(77)

as claimed.
∎

Finally, Proposition
B.8
can now be substituted into Lemmas
B.9
and
B.10
, which yields the explicit formulas for
Q_{n,k}
and
S_{n,t}
stated in Theorem
2.1
.

B.4
Real case: Proof of Remark
2

Proof.

We first treat the RNN quantity
Q_{n,k}
. Let
\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}}
denote a real Gaussian matrix with i.i.d. entries of variance
1/n
, and let

Q_{n,k}^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\left(\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}}\right)^{k}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr].

As in Lemma
B.9
, orthogonal invariance implies that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k}\bigr)^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k}\right]=\alpha_{k}^{\mathbb{R}}I_{n}

for some scalar
\alpha_{k}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq 0
, and therefore

Q_{n,k}^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1}{n}\tr(\Sigma^{(0)})\,\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tr\bigl(((\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k}\bigr)\right].

So it suffices to compare the corresponding trace moments.

Expanding the trace exactly as in Appendix
B.1
, we obtain a sum of monomials in the entries of
\mathbf{W}
. In the complex case, by Lemma
B.1
, only pairings between conjugated and non-conjugated factors contribute. In the real Gaussian case, Wick’s theorem sums instead over
all
pairings of the
2k
factors. Since

\mathbb{E}[\,\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}}_{ij}\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}}_{pq}\,]=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{i,p}\delta_{j,q}\geq 0,

every contributing pairing in the real case gives a nonnegative contribution. Moreover, the pairings that survive in the complex case form a subset of the pairings appearing in the real case. Hence

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\tr\bigl(((\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k}\bigr)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\tr\bigl((\mathbf{W}^{k})^{*}\mathbf{W}^{k}\bigr)\right],

and therefore

Q_{n,k}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq Q_{n,k}.

We now turn to the LRU quantity
S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}
. Expanding the squared norm as in Lemma
B.10
, we obtain

S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k,r=0}^{t}\tr\!\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-r)})^{\top}]\,\mathbb{E}[((\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{r})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k}]\Bigr).

Again by orthogonal invariance,

\mathbb{E}[((\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{r})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k}]=\alpha_{k,r}^{\mathbb{R}}I_{n}

for some scalar
\alpha_{k,r}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq 0
. Indeed,

\alpha_{k,r}^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tr\bigl(((\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{r})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{\mathbb{R}})^{k}\bigr)\right],

and this trace moment is again a sum of nonnegative Wick-pairing contributions. Therefore

S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}=\sum_{k,r=0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\tr\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-r)})^{\top}]\bigr)\,\alpha_{k,r}^{\mathbb{R}}.

Under the assumption

\tr\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{(s)}(\mathbf{x}^{(r)})^{\top}]\bigr)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }0\leq s,r\leq t,

each summand is nonnegative. In particular, restricting to the diagonal terms
k=r
gives

S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq\sum_{k=0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\tr\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)})^{\top}]\bigr)\,\alpha_{k,k}^{\mathbb{R}}.

By the already established inequality for the diagonal trace moments,

\alpha_{k,k}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq\tilde{Q}_{n,k},

where
\tilde{Q}_{n,k}
is the corresponding complex RNN signal energy under whitened inputs. Hence

S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq\sum_{k=0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\tr\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-k)})^{\top}]\bigr)\,\tilde{Q}_{n,k}=S_{n,t}.

This proves

Q_{n,k}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq Q_{n,k},\qquad S_{n,t}^{\mathbb{R}}\geq S_{n,t}.

The final claim follows immediately: if the complex model exhibits exploding signal energy in a given depth–width scaling regime, then so does the real model.
∎

Appendix C
Proofs for Scaling Regimes of
Q_{n,k}

This section contains the proofs of the asymptotic statements for the signal energy
Q_{n,k}
in the three joint
(k,n)
scaling regimes identified in the main text: subcritical, critical, and supercritical. Each proof is obtained by a direct asymptotic analysis of the exact finite-width formula for
Q_{n,k}
given in Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem C.1

(Subcritical regime)
.

If
k=o(\sqrt{n})
, we have

Q_{n,k}\to 1.

(78)

Proof.

Let us define
P_{\pm}=\prod_{j=0}^{k+1}(1\pm j/n)
, then we have:

Q_{n,k}=\dfrac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}[P_{+}-P_{-}].

(79)

As
k\ll n
, we can get the following by Taylor expansion of the logarithm:

\log P_{\pm}=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\log\Bigl(1\pm\dfrac{j}{n}\Bigr)=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\Bigl(\pm\dfrac{j}{n}+O\Bigl(\dfrac{j^{2}}{n^{2}}\Bigr)\Biggr).

(80)

Under
k=o(\sqrt{n})
, we have the following for the terms above:

a_{n,k}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\dfrac{j}{n}=\dfrac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2n}=O\Bigl(\dfrac{k^{2}}{n}\Bigr),\quad b_{n,k}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}O\Bigl(\dfrac{j^{2}}{n^{2}}\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\dfrac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}\Bigr)=o(a_{n,k}).

(81)

From here, we can write
P_{\pm}=e^{\pm a_{n,k}}(1+o(a_{n,k}))
. Further taking into account that
a_{n,k}=o(1)
and thus
e^{\pm a_{n,k}}=1+o(1)
, we can write:

P_{+}-P_{-}=(e^{a_{n,k}}-e^{-a_{n,k}})+o(a_{n,k})=2a_{n,k}+o(a_{n,k})=\dfrac{(k+1)(k+2)}{n}(1+o(1)).

(82)

Plugging this result into
Q_{n,k}
, we get:

Q_{n,k}=1+o(1)\to 1

(83)

for large
k,n
whenever
k=o(\sqrt{n})
.
∎

Theorem C.2

(Critical regime)
.

If
k/\sqrt{n}\to c
for some constant
c>0
, we have

Q_{n,k}\to\dfrac{2}{c^{2}}\sinh\Bigl(\dfrac{c^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

(84)

Proof.

Analogous to the proof of Theorem
C.1
, we can write:

P_{+}-P_{-}=(e^{a_{n,k}}-e^{-a_{n,k}})+o(1).

(85)

When
k/\sqrt{n}\to c
, we then have:

a_{n,k}\to\dfrac{c^{2}}{2},\quad P_{+}-P_{-}=e^{c^{2}/2}-e^{-c^{2}/2}=2\sinh\Bigl(\dfrac{c^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

(86)

Then for
Q_{n,k}
we get the following:

Q_{n,k}=\dfrac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}(P_{+}-P_{-})\to\dfrac{2}{c^{2}}\sinh\Bigl(\dfrac{c^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

(87)

∎

Theorem C.3

(Supercritical sublinear regime)
.

If
\sqrt{n}\ll k\ll n
or, in other words,
k/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
and
k/n\to 0
, then

Q_{n,k}\sim\dfrac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}e^{n\Psi(k/n)},

(88)

where
\Psi(x):=(1+x)\log(1+x)-x
. In particular, this regime holds when
k\sim n^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
.

Proof.

First, we notice that in this regime
P_{-}
is negligible in comparison with
P_{+}
. Indeed, when
k\ll n
we can write:

\log\dfrac{P_{-}}{P_{+}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\log\dfrac{1-j/n}{1+j/n}=-\dfrac{2}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}j+O\Bigl(\dfrac{1}{n^{3}}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}j^{3}\Bigr)=-\dfrac{(k+1)(k+2)}{n}+O\Bigl(\dfrac{k^{4}}{n^{3}}\Bigr)

(89)

Further, we have
O(k^{4}/n^{3})=o(k^{2}/n)
when
k=o(n)
, and therefore under
k^{2}/n\to\infty
we get:

\log\dfrac{P_{-}}{P_{+}}=-\dfrac{k^{2}}{n}(1+o(1))\to-\infty,\quad P_{+}-P_{-}=P_{+}(1+o(1)).

(90)

It remains to estimate
P_{+}
, which we will do via approximating the sum with an integral:

\log P_{+}=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\log\Bigl(1+\dfrac{j}{n}\Bigr)=n\int_{0}^{(k+1)/n}\log(1+x)dx+O(\log(1+(k+1)/n)).

(91)

Taking into account that
O(\log(1+(k+1)/n))=O(k/n)=o(1)
and computing the integral, we get:

\log P_{+}=n\Psi\Bigl(\dfrac{k+1}{n}\Bigr)+o(1)=n\Psi\Bigl(\dfrac{k}{n}\Bigr)+o(1).

(92)

Combining everything, we get:

P_{+}-P_{-}=\exp(n\Psi(k/n)+o(1)),

(93)

which results in the final expression for
Q_{n,k}
:

Q_{n,k}=\dfrac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\exp(n\Psi(k/n)+o(1)).

(94)

∎

Appendix D
Proofs for Scaling Regimes of
S_{n,t}

This section proves the asymptotic behavior of the signal energy
S_{n,t}
in the three joint
(t,n)
scaling regimes identified in the main text: subcritical, critical, and supercritical. The arguments combine the exact finite-width representation of
S_{n,t}
from Theorem
2.1
with the asymptotic behavior of the corresponding RNN signal energies
Q_{n,k}
established in Appendix
C
.

Theorem D.1

(Subcritical regime)
.

If
t=o(\sqrt{n})
, we have:

S_{n,t}\sim t+1.

(95)

Proof.

Under
t=o(\sqrt{n})
, we have

Q_{n,k}=1+o(1)

(96)

uniformly over
k\leq t
by Theorem
C.1
. This directly implies the claim:

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}Q_{n,k}=(t+1)(1+o(1)).

(97)

∎

Theorem D.2

(Critical regime)
.

If
t/\sqrt{n}\to c
for some constant
c>0
, we have

S_{n,t}\sim\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{c}q(x)\,dx,

(98)

where

q(x):=\begin{cases}1,&x=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{2}{x^{2}}\sinh\Bigl(\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}\Bigr),&x>0.\end{cases}

(99)

Proof.

We first show that, in the critical window,
Q_{n,k}
admits a uniform approximation by the profile
q(k/\sqrt{n})
for all
k\leq t
. As before, we rewrite
Q_{n,k}
via
P_{\pm}
as follows:

P_{\pm}(n,k):=\prod_{j=0}^{k+1}\Bigl(1\pm\frac{j}{n}\Bigr),\qquad Q_{n,k}=\frac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\bigl(P_{+}(n,k)-P_{-}(n,k)\bigr).

(100)

Similarly to the proof of Theorem
C.2
, we define

a_{n,k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}j=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2n},\qquad b_{n,k}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}j^{2}=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)(2k+3)}{6n^{2}}.

(101)

Under
k=O(\sqrt{n})
, we have uniformly in
k\leq t
:

a_{n,k}=O(1),\qquad b_{n,k}=O(n^{-1/2}).

(102)

Therefore, by the same logarithmic expansion as before,

\log P_{\pm}(n,k)=\pm a_{n,k}+O(b_{n,k})

(103)

uniformly over
k\leq t
, and exponentiating yields

P_{+}(n,k)-P_{-}(n,k)=2\sinh(a_{n,k})+O(b_{n,k}).

(104)

Plugging this into the formula for
Q_{n,k}
, we obtain

Q_{n,k}=\frac{2n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\sinh(a_{n,k})+O\Bigl(\frac{n\,b_{n,k}}{(k+1)(k+2)}\Bigr).

(105)

Since

\frac{n\,b_{n,k}}{(k+1)(k+2)}=\frac{2k+3}{6n}=O(n^{-1/2})

(106)

uniformly for
k\leq t
, it follows that

Q_{n,k}=\frac{2n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\sinh(a_{n,k})+O(n^{-1/2}).

(107)

Next, define

x_{n,k}:=\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}.

(108)

Then

a_{n,k}=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2n}=\frac{k^{2}}{2n}+\frac{3k+2}{2n}=\frac{x_{n,k}^{2}}{2}+O(n^{-1/2})

(109)

uniformly for
k\leq t
. Since the function

g(u):=\begin{cases}1,&u=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{\sinh(u)}{u},&u>0\end{cases}

(110)

is continuous on bounded intervals, we obtain

\frac{2n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\sinh(a_{n,k})=g(a_{n,k})=g\Bigl(\frac{x_{n,k}^{2}}{2}\Bigr)+o(1)

(111)

uniformly over
k\leq t
. In other words,

\sup_{0\leq k\leq t}\left|Q_{n,k}-q\Bigl(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)\right|\to 0,

(112)

where

q(x)=g\Bigl(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\Bigr)=\begin{cases}1,&x=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{2}{x^{2}}\sinh\Bigl(\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}\Bigr),&x>0.\end{cases}

(113)

We now sum over
k\leq t
. Since
t/\sqrt{n}\to c
, we have
t=O(\sqrt{n})
, hence

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}q\Bigl(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)+o(\sqrt{n}).

(114)

Dividing by
\sqrt{n}
gives

\frac{S_{n,t}}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{t}q\Bigl(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)+o(1).

(115)

Since
q
is continuous on
[0,c+\varepsilon]
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the sum is a Riemann sum, and therefore

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{t}q\Bigl(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)\to\int_{0}^{c}q(x)\,dx.

(116)

This proves that

\frac{S_{n,t}}{\sqrt{n}}\to\int_{0}^{c}q(x)\,dx.

(117)

∎

Theorem D.3

(Supercritical sublinear regime)
.

If
t/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
and
t/n\to 0
, then

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{Q_{n,t}}{\log(1+t/n)}.

(118)

Equivalently,

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{t^{3}}\exp\Bigl(n\Psi(t/n)\Bigr),

(119)

where
\Psi(x):=(1+x)\log(1+x)-x
.

Proof.

Recall from Theorem
2.1
that

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}Q_{n,k}.

(120)

We will show that this sum is asymptotically dominated by an endpoint window near
k=t
. Specifically, we choose an integer sequence
L_{n}
satisfying

\frac{n}{t}\ll L_{n}\ll\sqrt{n}.

(121)

Such a sequence exists because
t/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
. For
\delta_{n}:=\log(1+t/n)\sim t/n
, this choice implies

L_{n}\delta_{n}\to\infty,\qquad L_{n}=o(t).

(122)

We then split the sum into the endpoint window and its complement as follows:

S_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t-L_{n}-1}Q_{n,k}+\sum_{k=t-L_{n}}^{t}Q_{n,k}.

(123)

We first analyze the endpoint window. For
k=t-j
with
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
, we have
j=o(t)
, hence
k\sim t
. Therefore, uniformly over
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
,

\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\to\infty,\qquad\frac{k}{n}\to 0.

(124)

The proof of Theorem
C.3
, applied uniformly on this window, gives

Q_{n,t-j}=f_{n}(t-j)(1+o(1)),\qquad 0\leq j\leq L_{n},

(125)

where we defined

f_{n}(k):=\frac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\exp\Bigl(n\Psi(k/n)\Bigr).

(126)

We briefly justify this local uniformity. Writing

P_{\pm}(n,k):=\prod_{j=0}^{k+1}\left(1\pm\frac{j}{n}\right),

(127)

the exact formula for
Q_{n,k}
gives

Q_{n,k}=\frac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\bigl(P_{+}(n,k)-P_{-}(n,k)\bigr).

(128)

Uniformly for
k=t-j
,
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
, we have

\log\frac{P_{-}(n,k)}{P_{+}(n,k)}=-\frac{k^{2}}{n}(1+o(1))\to-\infty,

(129)

because
k\sim t
and
t^{2}/n\to\infty
. Thus

P_{-}(n,k)=o(P_{+}(n,k))

(130)

uniformly on the endpoint window. Moreover, by the same Riemann-sum estimate as in Theorem
C.3
,

\log P_{+}(n,k)=n\Psi(k/n)+o(1)

(131)

uniformly for
k=t-j
,
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
, since all such
k
satisfy
k\sim t
and
t/n\to 0
. This proves (
125
).

We now evaluate the endpoint contribution. For
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
,

\displaystyle\frac{f_{n}(t-j)}{f_{n}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(t+1)(t+2)}{(t-j+1)(t-j+2)}\exp\Bigl(-n\bigl[\Psi(t/n)-\Psi((t-j)/n)\bigr]\Bigr).

(132)

Since
j\leq L_{n}=o(t)
, the prefactor satisfies

\frac{(t+1)(t+2)}{(t-j+1)(t-j+2)}=1+o(1)

(133)

uniformly for
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
. Using the Taylor expansion of
\Psi((t-j)/n)
around
t/n
, we get

\Psi((t-j)/n)=\Psi(t/n)-\frac{j}{n}\Psi^{\prime}(t/n)+O\left(\frac{j^{2}}{n^{2}}\right),

(134)

where the error term is uniform over
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
, since

\Psi^{\prime\prime}(x)=1/(1+x)
is bounded on
[0,t/n]
for large
n
. Therefore,

n\bigl[\Psi(t/n)-\Psi((t-j)/n)\bigr]=j\Psi^{\prime}(t/n)+O\left(\frac{j^{2}}{n}\right).

(135)

Since
\Psi^{\prime}(x)=\log(1+x)
and
\delta_{n}:=\log(1+t/n)
, this gives

n\bigl[\Psi(t/n)-\Psi((t-j)/n)\bigr]=j\delta_{n}+O\left(\frac{j^{2}}{n}\right).

(136)

By the choice of the endpoint window,
L_{n}^{2}/n\to 0
, we then have:

n\bigl[\Psi(t/n)-\Psi((t-j)/n)\bigr]=j\delta_{n}+o(1)

(137)

uniformly over
0\leq j\leq L_{n}
. Therefore,

\frac{f_{n}(t-j)}{f_{n}(t)}=e^{-j\delta_{n}}(1+o(1))

(138)

uniformly in the endpoint window. Consequently,

\sum_{j=0}^{L_{n}}f_{n}(t-j)=f_{n}(t)(1+o(1))\sum_{j=0}^{L_{n}}e^{-j\delta_{n}}\sim f_{n}(t)\sum_{j=0}^{L_{n}}e^{-j\delta_{n}}.

(139)

Because
L_{n}\delta_{n}\to\infty
, the truncated geometric sum captures the full endpoint mass:

\sum_{j=0}^{L_{n}}e^{-j\delta_{n}}\sim\frac{1}{1-e^{-\delta_{n}}}\sim\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}.

(140)

Combining this with (
125
), we obtain

\sum_{k=t-L_{n}}^{t}Q_{n,k}\sim\frac{f_{n}(t)}{\delta_{n}}.

(141)

It remains to show that the contribution outside the endpoint window is negligible. We use the exact formula and the trivial bound

Q_{n,k}\leq\frac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}P_{+}(n,k).

(142)

The same integral estimate gives the crude bound

Q_{n,k}\leq C\frac{n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\exp\Bigl(n\Psi(k/n)\Bigr)

(143)

for all
k\leq t
, where
C
is independent of
k
and
n
.

First fix
\eta\in(0,1)
. For
k\leq\eta t
, the exponent gap satisfies

n\bigl[\Psi(t/n)-\Psi(k/n)\bigr]\geq n\bigl[\Psi(t/n)-\Psi(\eta t/n)\bigr]=\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{2}\frac{t^{2}}{n}(1+o(1)).

(144)

Since
t^{2}/n\to\infty
, the contribution of
k\leq\eta t
is exponentially smaller than the endpoint contribution:

\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\eta t\rfloor}Q_{n,k}=o\left(\frac{f_{n}(t)}{\delta_{n}}\right).

(145)

It remains to control the intermediate range
\eta t<k<t-L_{n}
. Write
k=t-j
, so now
j\geq L_{n}
. By (
143
) and the mean value theorem, uniformly for
\eta t\leq k\leq t
,

n\bigl[\Psi(t/n)-\Psi(k/n)\bigr]\geq c_{\eta}(t-k)\delta_{n}

(146)

for some constant
c_{\eta}>0
, because
\Psi^{\prime}(x)=\log(1+x)
and
k
is comparable to
t
throughout this range. Hence

Q_{n,t-j}\leq C_{\eta}f_{n}(t)e^{-c_{\eta}j\delta_{n}}

(147)

for
L_{n}\leq j\leq(1-\eta)t
. Therefore

\sum_{\eta t<k<t-L_{n}}Q_{n,k}\leq C_{\eta}f_{n}(t)\sum_{j\geq L_{n}}e^{-c_{\eta}j\delta_{n}}\leq C_{\eta}f_{n}(t)\frac{e^{-c_{\eta}L_{n}\delta_{n}}}{\delta_{n}}.

(148)

Since
L_{n}\delta_{n}\to\infty
, this gives

\sum_{\eta t<k<t-L_{n}}Q_{n,k}=o\left(\frac{f_{n}(t)}{\delta_{n}}\right).

(149)

Combining (
141
), (
145
), and (
149
), we conclude that

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{f_{n}(t)}{\delta_{n}}.

(150)

Finally, by Theorem
C.3
,

Q_{n,t}\sim f_{n}(t),

(151)

and therefore

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{Q_{n,t}}{\log(1+t/n)}.

(152)

Since
\log(1+t/n)\sim t/n
and
(t+1)(t+2)\sim t^{2}
, we also get

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{t^{3}}\exp\Bigl(n\Psi(t/n)\Bigr).

(153)

∎

Corollary D.4
.

If
t/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
and
t/n^{2/3}\to 0
, then

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{t^{3}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\Bigr).

(154)

Proof.

By Theorem
D.3
,

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{t^{3}}\exp\Bigl(n\Psi(t/n)\Bigr).

(155)

Since
t/n\to 0
, we may expand

\Psi(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+O(x^{3}),

(156)

and therefore

n\Psi(t/n)=\frac{t^{2}}{2n}+O\Bigl(\frac{t^{3}}{n^{2}}\Bigr).

(157)

Under
t=o(n^{2/3})
, we have

\frac{t^{3}}{n^{2}}=o(1),

(158)

and hence

n\Psi(t/n)=\frac{t^{2}}{2n}+o(1).

(159)

Substituting this into the previous expression gives

S_{n,t}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{t^{3}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\Bigr).

(160)

∎
