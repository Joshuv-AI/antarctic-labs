 ABSTRACT

 Griffin is a Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE)-based reactor multi-
physics analysis application jointly developed by Argonne and Idaho National Laboratories under the DOE-
NE Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program. In FY25, an online cross-
section generation capability based on the Self-Shielding Application Programming Interface (SSAPI) was
demonstrated for TRISO-fueled reactor problems under steady-state conditions. This fiscal year, that ca-
pability was extended to support depletion and transient multiphysics calculations, enabling high-fidelity
analyses that generate self-shielded cross sections on the fly from the actual evolving composition and tem-
perature states rather than from pre-tabulated libraries. For depletion, a two-way coupling was established in
which SSAPI computes compact-averaged self-shielded cross sections that the depletion solver then uses to
advance the Bateman equations, with the updated compositions returned to SSAPI at each step; the depletion
module was refactored to support both library-based and SSAPI-based cross sections, and additional logic
was added to track daughter isotopes and to exclude minor isotopes for efficiency. For transient analysis, the
SSAPI multigroup library was extended with the kinetics data required for time-dependent calculations, the
Improved Quasi-Static (IQS) scheme was coupled with SSAPI, and several supporting capabilities were im-
plemented, including a self-shielding treatment that lets control rods and drums move within a self-shielded
model, which had previously been impossible and had ruled out rod- and drum-movement transients with
on-the-fly cross sections altogether, a new mixing scheme for delayed-neutron precursor decay constants, a
checkpoint-based restart workflow, and performance improvements such as pointwise cross-section interpo-
lation and the bypassing of unnecessary Dancoff factor calculations.

 The implemented capabilities were verified against Serpent Monte Carlo solutions. For depletion,
a prismatic pin-cell problem based on a Next Generation Nuclear Plant (NGNP) Very High Temperature
Reactor benchmark showed excellent agreement, with eigenvalue differences within 200 pcm over the en-
tire burnup range (up to 140 MWD/kgU) and fission-product and actinide inventories agreeing to within
0.8% and 2.5%, respectively; a heat-pipe microreactor assembly problem with a much higher fuel loading
confirmed the same behavior and quantified the bias introduced when the multigroup equivalence effect is
neglected. For transient analysis, a pin-cell problem with a step reactivity insertion and temperature feed-
back reproduced the analytically expected asymptotic power and showed close agreement between the direct
and IQS solutions, and a two-dimensional microreactor core problem with control-drum rotation exercised
the new moving-drum self-shielding treatment and demonstrated successful coupling of the online cross-
section generation with both the direct and IQS transient methods. The capability was further exercised on
a full-core pebble-bed problem, in which Griffin was coupled with the System Analysis Module (SAM) to
simulate load-following operation of the gPBR with the Doppler feedback resolved at the TRISO fuel kernel
temperature.

 These developments in Griffin provide a convenient, high-fidelity approach to cross-section generation
for advanced thermal reactors with geometrically complex and highly heterogeneous configurations, in-
cluding TRISO-fueled prismatic and pebble-bed systems, and support steady-state, depletion, and transient
multiphysics calculations. They also enable self-shielded cross sections to be evaluated directly at the actual
coupled state of the system, thereby establishing a foundation for high-fidelity, fully coupled multiphysics
analysis of advanced reactors.

1 Introduction

 Griffin [1, 2] is a Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE)-based reactor
multiphysics analysis application, jointly developed by Argonne and Idaho National Laboratories under
the DOE-NE Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program. Griffin provides
deterministic neutron transport capabilities, including discrete finite element method–discrete ordinates
(DFEM-SN) transport, together with depletion and transient solvers that support steady-state and time-
dependent multiphysics analysis of a wide range of advanced reactor concepts.

 In FY25, an online cross-section generation capability based on the Self-shielding Application Program-
ming Interface (SSAPI) was successfully demonstrated for TRISO-fueled pebble-bed and prismatic-type
reactor problems under steady-state conditions. Verification tests using the Generic Pebble Bed Reactor
(gPBR), Generic Fluoride-cooled High-temperature Reactor (gFHR), Gas-cooled Microreactor (GCMR),
and Heat-pipe Microreactor (HPMR) benchmark problems showed good agreement with Serpent [3] Monte
Carlo solutions in terms of both eigenvalues and pin power distributions [4].

 In FY26, SSAPI was extended to support depletion and transient calculations for practical use in design
and safety analyses. For depletion, SSAPI can either generate cross sections in the ISOXML format for an
assembly-homogenized core depletion calculation or directly perform a heterogeneous whole-core depletion
calculation. The FY26 depletion work targets non-pebble-bed problems; the pebble-bed depletion capability
has been supported since FY23 [5]. For transient analysis, SSAPI performs a direct heterogeneous whole-core
transport calculation without pre-tabulating cross-section libraries. Combined with multiphysics feedback,
such a calculation captures the actual evolving composition and temperature state and therefore serves as a
high-fidelity reference.

 Depletion with SSAPI is always performed at the microscopic level. SSAPI was integrated into the
existing depletion module, which previously supported only the pre-generated cross-section path, inheriting
all of its essential logic, such as the predictor-corrector time-integration scheme and the various Bateman
solvers. For TRISO problems, a TRISO-homogenized compact is depleted rather than the fuel kernel alone,
because the existing depletion module operates on a TRISO-homogenized composition rather than on the
individual micro-regions within a compact. To this end, SSAPI passes flux-volume-weighted, compact-
averaged cross sections to the depletion module so that the reaction rates are preserved, and then translates
the compact-averaged composition updated by the depletion module back into the composition of each
micro-region, including the fuel kernel. Section 2 describes this logic and the code restructuring required to
couple SSAPI with the depletion module, together with verification results for a prismatic pin-cell problem
and a heat-pipe microreactor assembly problem.

 For transient calculations, the SSAPI multigroup library was extended to include the kinetics data
(neutron velocities, delayed neutron fractions, fission spectra, and decay constants) required for time-
dependent analysis, and Griffin’s Improved Quasi-Static (IQS) scheme [1] was coupled with SSAPI. Several
supporting capabilities were also developed, including a new mixing scheme for composition-wise delayed
neutron precursor decay constants, a restart capability that separates steady-state and transient calculations,
a control rod or drum movement support for triggering transients, and performance improvements such
as pointwise cross-section interpolation and the bypassing of unnecessary Dancoff factor recalculations.
Section 3 describes these updates and the verification results for a pin-cell transient problem driven by
a step reactivity insertion with multiphysics coupling. The transient capability is also demonstrated for

2

a two-dimensional (2D) microreactor core problem involving control drum rotation, in which the direct
transient and IQS solutions are compared. In addition, gPBR load-following transient results from a coupled
Griffin–SAM [6] simulation, carried out and funded under the NEAMS HTGR application area and reported
in detail in [7], are briefly summarized for the completeness of the feature demonstration. Section 4 concludes
the report with a summary and discussions of future work.

2 Depletion Calculation with Online Cross-Section Generation

 Coupling SSAPI with Griffin’s existing depletion capabilities required significant code restructuring in
order to deplete non-pebble-type problems, including prismatic-type problems, with SSAPI. The restructuring
was necessary because Griffin previously assumed that the cross sections used to solve the depletion equations
came from a tabulated ISOXML cross-section library file, whereas SSAPI calculates and updates these cross
sections in memory from the composition changes caused by depletion. Section 2.1 describes the coupling
methodology between SSAPI and Griffin’s depletion solvers, together with the code structure improvements
needed to support this coupling, and Section 2.2 presents the verification results.

2.1 Method and Implementation

 At a high level, SSAPI computes the local cross sections for each self-shielding region, which is a subset
of a depletion zone. For each depletion zone, SSAPI takes the compact-averaged composition updated by the
depletion module, computes the flux-volume-weighted, compact-averaged self-shielded cross sections using
the double-heterogeneity treatment [4], averages them over the depletion zone, and passes them back to the
depletion module. The depletion module then solves the Bateman equations to update the number densities
of each depletion zone over a single depletion step and passes them back to SSAPI. This two-way coupling is
repeated at every depletion step.

 This section first discusses the logic for translating the compact-averaged composition into micro-region-
wise compositions. The self-shielding calculation itself is not discussed here, as it is already described in
detail in the FY25 report [4]. The updated code structure and the calculation flow for the predictor-corrector
scheme with multiphysics coupling are then presented. Lastly, a small optimization that excludes minor
isotopes is briefly discussed.

2.1.2 Code Structure for Depletion Coupling with SSAPI

 To support both SSAPI-based and ISOXML library-based depletion calculations in Griffin, the existing
depletion user objects responsible for solving the depletion equations were significantly refactored at the end
of FY25. The existing DepletionUserObject was split into a DepletionUserObjectBase base class
and a DepletionUserObject derived class. All logic specific to solving the depletion equations and storing
depletion-related data was moved into DepletionUserObjectBase, while the logic specific to loading
multigroup cross sections from an ISOXML file was kept in DepletionUserObject. In this way, the new
user object created to support SSAPI-based cross sections can inherit from the base class without duplicating
any shared logic that is unrelated to how the cross sections are calculated or loaded.

 In FY26, two new Griffin objects were created: SelfShieldingDepletionNeutronicsMaterial
(SSDNM) and SelfShieldingDepletionUserObject (SSDUO). SSDNM is the object that the user
invokes from the input file to indicate that SSAPI is to be used with depletion. This neutronics material
automatically builds the SSDUO instance, and SSDUO is responsible for transferring data between SSAPI
and the depletion data that store the number densities of each depletion zone as a function of depletion step.
As noted above, SSDUO inherits from DepletionUserObjectBase, and all logic specific to loading SSAPI
cross sections resides directly in SSDUO.

 This work also revealed that additional improvements to SSAPI were needed to support depletion
calculations, because SSAPI was originally designed to load cross-section library data only for the initial
core composition. In a depletion calculation, daughter isotopes are produced after every depletion step, so
additional logic was added to ensure that the cross sections of all daughter isotopes are loaded into SSAPI for
the subsequent cross-section recalculations. SSDUO currently builds the decay and transmutation library and
provides SSAPI with the list of isotopes that must be loaded during initialization.

2.1.3 LE/LI Predictor-Corrector Scheme with Multiphysics Coupling

 Figure 2.1 illustrates the workflow for depletion calculations using the Linear Extrapolation / Linear
Interpolation (LE/LI) predictor–corrector scheme with multiphysics coupling. At each depletion time step,
the predictor stage begins by evaluating the cross sections and neutron fluxes with multiphysics coupling
through a Picard iteration loop. Once the cross sections and fluxes have converged, the burnup matrix is
constructed and extrapolated from the current and previous time steps using time-step-size weighting. For the
initial time step, only the burnup matrix of the initial configuration is used, without extrapolation. Using the
extrapolated burnup matrix, the Bateman equations are solved with the Chebyshev Rational Approximation
Method (CRAM) solver for each depletion zone to update the number densities (Nni∗ in Figure 2.1).

 In the corrector stage, Picard iterations are performed again to evaluate the cross sections and fluxes,
and hence the burnup matrix, at the updated configuration. The final burnup matrix is obtained by averaging
the extrapolated burnup matrix used in the predictor stage with the burnup matrix updated in the corrector
stage. The number densities for the next time step are then obtained by solving the Bateman equations once

 5

more with the final burnup matrix. The same calculation flow applies to the other time-integration options
available in Griffin, such as constant extrapolation (CE) and constant extrapolation with linear interpolation
(CE/LI); these options differ only in how the burnup matrices are extrapolated and averaged.

Figure 2.1: Calculation flow for SSAPI depletion calculations with multiphysics feedback using the LE/LI
predictor–correction scheme.

2.1.4 Exclusion of Minor Isotopes in the SSAPI Calculation

 After a single depletion step, the composition of a given depletion zone may contain hundreds of
isotopes, many of them at trace number densities. As demonstrated in [8], such minor isotopes can be omitted
from the self-shielding and scattering matrix calculations to improve the calculation speed without loss of
accuracy, and the same scheme was implemented in Griffin. Any isotope whose number density falls below a
user-specified threshold is ignored in the self-shielding and transport calculations. At present, this threshold
must be determined empirically for the specific problem being solved.

2.2 Verification

 Two verification problems were used to test the SSAPI-based depletion capabilities added to Griffin in
FY26. The first is a prismatic-type pin-cell problem based on an Next Generation Nuclear Plant (NGNP)
Very High Temperature Reactor (VHTR) benchmark [9]. The second is an assembly problem for a HPMR,
developed at Argonne National Laboratory [10], which has a much higher fuel loading and a much stronger
multigroup equivalence effect than the pin cell.

2.2.1 Pin Cell Depletion

 The VHTR problem is a unit-cell depletion problem with reflective boundary conditions, in which the
pin pitch is 2.0 cm, the compact radius is 0.8 cm, and the fuel is UCO enriched to 10.36 wt.% 235 U with a
TRISO packing fraction of 11.2%. The TRISO particles have five layers, with center-to-outer-layer radii of
175, 275, 310, 345, and 385 µm, corresponding to the fuel kernel, buffer, inner PyC, SiC, and outer PyC

6

layers. A constant linear power density of 17.1 W/cm is assumed (a specific power of ∼0.0867 MW/kgHM)
under isothermal conditions at room temperature.

 The burnup steps are 0.1, 1, 5, and 10 EFPD, followed by a constant step size of 10 EFPD up to
1800 EFPD. The Bateman equations are solved with the constant extrapolation (CE) method, without the
corrector step, using a CRAM solver. The results were found to be insensitive to the choice of predictor-
corrector scheme for this problem.

 Two sets of pin-cell problems were created in Griffin for verification purposes. The first uses a volume-
homogenized compact without TRISO particles, while the second models the TRISO particles explicitly.
The first is intended to check the accuracy of the depletion calculation itself in the absence of TRISO
particles, while the second demonstrates whether the double-heterogeneity treatment and the compact-
averaged depletion scheme for a TRISO problem, outlined in Section 2.1.1, work reasonably well. In Griffin,
both problems were solved using DFEM-SN transport with 360 elements and 72 angles, together with a
400-group library generated with NJOY [11] from a 7,178-group spectrum tallied by Serpent for the fresh
pin-cell configuration. The isotopes carried by the calculation are not chosen by the user; they follow from
the two data files. The decay and transmutation library contains 3,822 nuclides, and the 1,854 of them that
are reachable from the initial fuel composition through decay, transmutation, and fission yields—167 of
which are actinides—form the Bateman system that is solved at every depletion step. Of those 1,854 nuclides,
the 401 that also carry multigroup data in the 400-group cross-section library are self-shielded and used by
SSAPI to build the local cross sections; they comprise 83 actinides and 318 fission products, absorbers, and
structural nuclides, out of the 423 isotopes for which that library provides data. The remaining nuclides,
mostly short-lived fission products with no evaluated neutron cross sections, act as pure decay intermediates:
they neither absorb nor transmute. As a reference for the two Griffin pin-cell problems, Serpent calculations
were performed with 50 million particle histories for the active cycles and 5 million for the inactive cycles
at each depletion step. The same time-integration scheme and CRAM solver were used as in the Griffin
calculations. To ensure consistent decay and transmutation data between Serpent and Griffin, the decay
and transmutation library was converted from the Serpent data library into a Griffin-readable XML format.
Serpent therefore builds its burnup chain from the same 3,822-nuclide set, with the same decay constants
and fission yields, and applies the same rule to the nuclides that have no cross-section evaluation, so the two
codes solve the same depletion chain.

2.2.1.1 Homogenized Compact Problem

 First, three library group structures were tested: the WIMS 70-group, the WIMS 172-group [12], and
the Griffin 400-group structures. The WIMS 70-group structure is the WIMS 69-group structure with one
additional energy group that raises the uppermost boundary from 10 to 20 MeV. In every case, the weighting
spectrum used to generate the library is the fresh-fuel spectrum at zero burnup tallied from Serpent. Because
the problem spectrum evolves with burnup, an error arises from the discrepancy between this fixed weighting
spectrum and the actual spectrum. To isolate that effect, Griffin results for the three libraries were generated
with steady-state calculations using the compositions tallied from Serpent at each burnup step.

 Figure 2.2 shows the eigenvalue errors obtained with the three group structures. The errors are plotted
with symbols only, and are not connected by lines, to indicate that these are steady-state rather than depletion
results. The figure clearly shows the larger errors associated with fixed-spectrum weighting for coarser group

 7

structures: the reactivity error varies monotonically between beginning of cycle and end of cycle, spanning
roughly 150 pcm for the 172-group structure and 280 pcm for the 70-group structure. No significant bias
is observed for the 400-group structure. The mechanism is that the spectrum hardens as the fuel depletes.
If this hardening is not captured, that is, if a weighting spectrum that is too soft is used, the multigroup
downscattering cross sections are overestimated, which leads to less resonance absorption in 238 U, more
thermal fission in 235 U, and consequently a different burnup rate. The coarser the group structure, the more
pronounced this effect becomes. For this reason, the 400-group library was used throughout the burnup
calculations. Ways to mitigate the resulting computational burden without reintroducing this bias should be
investigated and implemented in the next fiscal year.

Figure 2.2: Dependence of eigenvalue errors on the number of library energy groups for the homogenized
compact problem.

 Second, the effect of the decay chain data was studied. Three decay and transmutation libraries (DTLs)
were tested: a DRAGON5 [13] DTL, a DTL converted from the Serpent data library, and the same converted
Serpent DTL supplemented with isomeric branching ratios. The third case requires explanation. Branching
ratios to isomeric states are not available in the ACE-format cross-section libraries used by Serpent, so they
must be specified by other means; Serpent uses predefined constant ratios averaged over a thermal or a fast
flux spectrum [3]. The third library therefore reuses those same predefined ratios in the conversion of the
Serpent DTL.

 Figure 2.3 isolates the effect of the DTL on the depletion eigenvalue, holding the geometry, the 400-
group cross-section library, and the depletion scheme fixed. A direct comparison of serpent dtl.xml with
serpent dtl w isomers.xml shows that the two files are identical apart from the (n, γ) branching ratios of

3 Transient Calculation with Online Cross-Section Generation

 The online cross-section generation capability of the SSAPI module has been successfully extended
to support transient simulations, including the IQS scheme [1], in multiphysics calculations. To achieve
this, (1) the SSAPI multigroup library data and the treatment of the delayed neutron data were updated,
(2) the SSAPI data structures were updated to support a restart capability, (3) supporting the existing rod
movement treatment (SelfShieldingRoddedNeutronicsMaterial) for a transient calculation, and (4)
several features of the self-shielding calculation were optimized. The capability was verified using a single
pin-cell problem involving a step reactivity insertion with temperature feedback in a coupled Griffin/SSAPI–
MOOSE heat conduction [16] calculation, and it was demonstrated on a two-dimensional microreactor
core problem involving control drum motion. It was additionally exercised in a coupled Griffin–SAM [6]
load-following transient of the gPBR, which was performed under the NEAMS HTGR application area and
is summarized here from [7].

3.1 Code Updates

 This section describes the six major code updates made to support transient calculations, including
SSAPI multigroup library data, a new mixing scheme for delayed neutron precursor data, restart calculations,
a self-shielding treatment for moving control rods and drums, faster reconstruction of pointwise cross-section
interpolation, and reduced Dancoff factor calculations.

3.1.1 SSAPI Multigroup Library Data Update

 The SSAPI multigroup library was extended to support transient simulations by adding neutron velocity
data together with the delayed neutron fractions, fission spectra, and decay constants of each of the six delayed
neutron precursor groups. The group-wise neutron velocity is obtained by averaging the pointwise neutron
speed with the inverse of the fine-group spectrum supplied to NJOY [11], which is a problem-representative
spectrum specified by the user in the SSAPI multigroup library generation code. The remaining data are
independent of the incident neutron energy and are obtained directly from NJOY. All of these additions were
implemented through updates to the library generation code.

3.1.3 Restart Capability

 Griffin supports two approaches for transient calculations: a single-run multi-app approach and a multi-
run single-app approach. In the single-run multi-app approach, a steady-state calculation is performed in a
sub-app at the INITIAL time flag of the main app, and the transient calculation is then carried out in the main
app. This method requires a single run with two input files, one for each app. However, this approach was not
pursued because the Fortran-based SSAPI implementation cannot distinguish between different apps within a
single run.

 Consequently, only the multi-run single-app approach was implemented. In this scheme, a steady-state
calculation is performed first and its results are written to a checkpoint file; the transient calculation is then
started by loading that checkpoint file. This approach requires two input files and two separate runs. It is
also the more practical option, since the steady-state solution does not need to be repeated for each transient
scenario and the workflow is consistent with conventional practice in transient analysis.

 To enable the checkpoint-based workflow, the Mixture data, which contain the macroscopic cross
sections, were made restartable so that they can be written to and recovered from a restart file for transient
calculations. Because the Mixture data are owned by the ISOXML module rather than by the MOOSE
system, they cannot be declared restartable directly. To work around this limitation, the data were copied
into the MOOSE UserObject used for self-shielding calculations (SelfShieldingUserObjectBase),
and the copied data were declared restartable. This approach introduces duplicate memory usage; a future
improvement is to make the ISOXML Mixture data directly restartable and thereby eliminate the duplication.

 One issue was identified during this update: a null transient was not obtained when cross-section-region-
wise Mixture data were used in the restart calculation. This behavior was traced to SSAPI’s treatment of

 19

the composition fission spectrum, which was previously computed on the fly using isotope-wise fission-
source weighting during the element quadrature sweep for the residual evaluation. As a result, the effective
composition fission spectrum in the converged steady-state solution was quadrature dependent, whereas the
values stored in the checkpoint file were averaged over the cross-section region. This inconsistency caused
the null transient to fail.

 To resolve the issue, SSAPI was updated to use either element-wise or cross-section-region-wise
composition fission spectra in a fully consistent manner. With this change, the null transient was successfully
confirmed.

3.1.4 Self-Shielding Treatment for Moving Control Rods and Drums

 Accurate simulation of reactor transients requires the neutronics model to follow control rods and
control drums as they move. Griffin already handled the geometric side of this well: a Legendre-projection
(“de-cusping”) treatment removes the non-physical kinks in the predicted reactivity that otherwise appear
when a rod tip comes to rest partway through a mesh cell. That capability, however, could only be used with
pre-tabulated cross-section libraries. Griffin’s on-the-fly resonance self-shielding capability SSAPI — which
generates problem-specific cross sections from the actual local composition, temperature, and flux — was
explicitly unsupported for rodded materials. In practice this meant that any model relying on self-shielding
had to treat every control rod and drum as permanently fixed in place; there was no mechanism to move them
at all, and transient rod- or drum-movement analyses with self-shielded cross sections simply could not be
performed. This was a significant gap for microreactor designs, where rotating control drums are the primary
means of reactivity control and strong absorbers sit directly adjacent to the fuel.

 The obstacle was that the self-shielding machinery had been built around static geometry: each self-
shielding zone was permanently tied to a fixed region of the mesh, whereas rod segments sweep through the
mesh as a transient progresses, and at any given instant a single mesh cell can be partly rodded and partly
unrodded. We extended the self-shielding framework so that many moving self-shielding zones can coexist
inside one mesh region; so that cells straddling a rod tip or drum boundary contribute proportionally to each
zone when temperatures, fluxes, and volumes are collected; and so that segments that temporarily move out
of the modeled domain are handled gracefully all the way down into the underlying self-shielding solver.
The result, delivered as the new SelfShieldingRoddedNeutronicsMaterial, allows control rods and
drums to move within a self-shielded model for the first time, and requires no additional setup on the user’s
part — the supporting objects are created automatically behind the scenes. It handles multiple rods or drums
within a single material definition, is covered by a new regression test suite, and has been merged into the
Griffin development branch, lifting a long-standing restriction and enabling self-shielded control rod and
drum movement transients.

3.1.5 Pointwise Cross-Section Interpolation for Faster Reconstruction

 During transient calculations with feedback, the self-shielding calculation must be repeated to update
the cross sections as the temperature changes. For the slowing-down method, pointwise cross sections are
required at the exact temperature of interest. However, reconstructing these cross sections on demand at every
temperature update is computationally expensive and impractical for transient analysis.

20

 To address this, the preparation of pointwise cross sections was changed from on-demand reconstruc-
tion to on-the-fly interpolation from existing cross-section files generated at temperatures spaced in 25 K
increments, using a log-log interpolation scheme for each energy group. This update significantly improves
computational efficiency and makes SSAPI transient calculations practical.

3.1.6 Bypass of the Dancoff Factor Calculation

 The Dancoff factor depends only on composition, density, and geometry, and not on temperature.
Therefore, in transient simulations in which only the temperature changes, recalculating the Dancoff fac-
tor is unnecessary. Because this calculation requires a DFEM-SN transport fixed-source sweep and is
computationally expensive, the code was updated to bypass it in such cases.

3.2 Verification

 This section presents the verification results for a pin-cell transient problem with a step reactivity
insertion and demonstrates the transient results for a two-dimensional microreactor problem driven by control
drum motion. A summary of the coupled Griffin–SAM gPBR load-following transient reported in [7] is then
given to complete the demonstration of the capability on a full-core problem.

3.2.1 Pin Cell Transient Problem

 To verify the SSAPI transient implementation, a single pin-cell problem was solved. The geometry
consists of a cylindrical fuel compact surrounded by a graphite moderator. The transient scenario is a step
reactivity insertion with Doppler feedback, caused by a sudden reduction in the 10 B number density in the
moderator region. Because the inserted reactivity is positive, the power initially increases. As the temperature
rises in response, negative feedback slows the rate of power increase, so the power reaches a peak and then
decreases toward a new asymptotic state whose value depends on the magnitude of the initial reactivity
insertion.

 To model this case, Griffin was used as the main app and coupled with the MOOSE heat conduction
module [16] as a sub-app. The Transient executioner was used for both applications; specifically, Griffin
used the TransientSweepUpdate executioner, which is a child class of the Transient executioner, and
the TransientMultiApp system was used for the coupling. Picard iteration was performed between the
neutronics and temperature calculations at every time step, outside the Richardson iteration loop, by setting
fixed point solve outer = true. A uniform time step size of 0.01 s was used for both the direct
transient and the IQS calculations, except for the first time step, which was set to 0.0001 s. Because IQS
supports only ramp transients, the first time step was made deliberately small so that the ramp insertion
closely approximates a step insertion.

 For the heat conduction calculation, sufficient thermal inertia was introduced to delay the feedback
and thereby make the power peak clearly visible: a thermal conductivity of 0.1 W/m·K, a specific heat of
1 J/g·K, and a density of 1 g/cc were used. A convective boundary condition was applied with a heat transfer
coefficient of 20 W/m2 ·K and a bulk temperature of 600 K.

3.2.3 gPBR Load-following Transient Problem

 The SSAPI transient capability was further exercised on a full-core pebble-bed problem, in which Griffin
was coupled with the System Analysis Module (SAM) [6] to simulate load-following operation of the gPBR.
This demonstration was carried out and funded under the NEAMS HTGR application area and is documented
in detail in [7]; only a brief summary is given here for completeness of the transient feature demonstration.

 The coupled Griffin–SAM gPBR model, established in FY23 [19] and extended to use online cross-
section generation in FY25 [20], represents the core neutronics in Griffin using an R–Z geometry with SSAPI
and the primary loop in SAM. Because SSAPI builds the cross sections directly from the local composition
and temperature of each micro-region, the TRISO fuel kernel temperature is used directly for the Doppler
feedback, whereas the earlier version of this model used the TRISO-smeared pebble temperature. Supporting
improvements were therefore made on the SAM side in FY26 as well, namely a corrected channel-wise
temperature transfer, the assignment of distinct temperatures to the fuel kernel, moderator, and coolant, and a
fuel kernel temperature calculation driven by an external power source. Two load-following scenarios were
then solved after a null transient had been confirmed: the core mass flow rate was ramped from 100% to 50%
and back to 100% of nominal (100-50-100), and from 100% to 20% and back to 100% (100-20-100).

 Figure 3.6 [7] compares the power responses obtained with the two fuel temperature feedback choices.
The power is consistently less responsive when the fuel kernel temperature is used: at the plateau it stays about
12% higher for the 100-50-100 case (115 MW versus 103 MW) and about 21% higher for the 100-20-100
case (52 MW versus 43 MW). This is a direct consequence of the Doppler coefficient becoming smaller at
the higher temperature seen by the kernel; a supporting sensitivity study, in which the kernel temperature
was perturbed uniformly by 50 to 200 K from the converged coupled state, found the coefficient to scale
approximately as T −0.7 . The choice also propagates to the thermal-hydraulic response, since the coolant
temperature rise scales as P/ṁ: at the plateau, the core ∆T reaches about 645 K (100-20-100) and 580 K
(100-50-100) with the fuel kernel temperature, compared with about 533 K and 520 K with the TRISO-

 25

smeared pebble temperature, and the reflector temperature rises roughly 70 K above its initial value instead
of about 15 K for the 100-20-100 case. These differences affect the peak coolant and structural temperatures
that set the thermal margins during load-following operation, which shows that the choice of fuel temperature
used for the Doppler feedback is consequential and not merely a refinement of bookkeeping.

 Beyond the physics findings, this exercise demonstrates that the SSAPI transient capability described in
this section operates in a coupled whole-core setting driven by a system code, complementing the pin-cell

4 Conclusions and Future Work

 In FY26, Griffin’s online cross-section generation capability based on SSAPI was successfully extended
from steady-state analysis to depletion and transient multiphysics calculations. For depletion, a compact-
averaged depletion scheme was developed and verified. Griffin’s depletion code structure was refactored to
support both ISOXML-based and SSAPI-based cross sections without duplicating shared logic, and additional
capabilities were implemented to handle the daughter isotopes produced during depletion and to accelerate
the calculation by excluding minor isotopes from the self-shielding and transport solves. For transient
analysis, the SSAPI multigroup library was extended to include the kinetics data required for time-dependent
calculations, and the IQS scheme was coupled with SSAPI. Supporting developments included a new mixing
scheme for composition-wise delayed neutron precursor decay constants, a restart capability that separates
the steady-state and transient calculations, a control rod or drum movement treatment with self-shielding,
and performance improvements such as on-the-fly pointwise cross-section interpolation and the bypassing of
unnecessary Dancoff factor recalculations.

 The implemented capabilities were verified against Serpent Monte Carlo solutions. The SSAPI-depletion
coupling was verified with a prismatic pin-cell depletion problem based on an NGNP VHTR benchmark,
in which Griffin showed excellent agreement with the reference: the eigenvalue difference remained within
200 pcm for the explicit TRISO problem over the entire burnup range, and the isotopic inventories of
representative actinides and fission products agreed to within 5% at all depletion steps. The same coupling
was then exercised on an assembly problem for a HPMR with a much higher fuel loading, where the
eigenvalue difference remained within roughly 130 pcm once the multigroup equivalence factor was applied;
that problem also showed that neglecting multigroup equivalence produces a clearly visible bias in the burnup
rate. The SSAPI-transient coupling was verified with a pin-cell problem featuring a step reactivity insertion
and temperature feedback, in which the asymptotic power agreed closely with a hand calculation and the
direct transient and IQS solutions agreed very well. It was further demonstrated on a two-dimensional
microreactor core problem with control drum rotation, which showed that the online cross-section generation
was successfully coupled with both the direct and the IQS transient approaches.

 These developments are important because they provide Griffin with an online cross-section capability
for depletion and transient analysis, which previously relied exclusively on pre-generated, tabulated cross-
section libraries. This new capability eliminates the extensive branch calculations required to generate
such libraries and enables self-shielded cross sections to be evaluated directly at the actual coupled state
of the system. This is especially valuable for geometrically complex and highly heterogeneous advanced
reactor designs, such as TRISO-fueled and microreactor concepts, where constructing pre-tabulated libraries
that adequately cover the range of possible operating states can require substantial user effort in selecting,
generating, and validating the necessary branch conditions. The verified depletion and transient capabilities
establish a foundation for high-fidelity, fully coupled multiphysics analysis of advanced reactors in Griffin.

 In summary, Griffin with online cross-section generation can now solve depletion and transient mul-
tiphysics problems for thermal reactors, including TRISO-fueled prismatic and pebble-bed systems. The
following areas have been identified for future work.

 • Memory reduction in restart calculations: As described above, the restart capability was implemented
 by copying the Mixture data from an SSAPI ISOXML object into a restartable MOOSE object. This

 27

 duplication could be eliminated by making the entire SSAPI ISOXML object restartable, although that
 approach would require custom read and write functions.

 • Performance optimization strategies: SSAPI inherently requires a large number of energy groups,
 typically more than 50. As a result, even with IQS, a transport calculation at every macro time
 step remains impractical for large problems. A specialized approach is therefore needed to reduce
 the computational cost substantially, such as the application of the on-the-fly condensation [4] to a
 heterogeneous transient calculation and on-the-fly interpolation of the macroscopic cross sections in
 place of a full self-shielding and transport solve.

 • Energy-group optimization in SSAPI libraries: As shown in Section 2.2, a 400-group library was
 required to keep the fixed-weighting-spectrum bias negligible over the full burnup range, whereas
 the 70- and 172-group structures accumulated errors of roughly 280 and 150 pcm, respectively. An
 optimized group structure, or a scheme that updates the weighting spectrum as the fuel depletes, would
 allow far fewer groups to be used at the same accuracy and would directly address the performance
 limitation described above.

 • Neutron-gamma coupled calculations with SSAPI: The current SSAPI libraries carry neutron data
 only, so gamma heating must be approximated by depositing all energy locally. Extending the library
 generation and the self-shielding treatment to coupled neutron-gamma data would allow the gamma
 transport and the resulting heat deposition to be computed directly, which is important for accurate
 temperature distributions in reflectors, moderator pins, and other non-fuel regions of microreactors.

 • Extended verification and validation tests: The verification performed this year covered pin-cell
 and single-assembly problems for depletion and transient calculations. Additional calculations were
 conducted for HTTR experiments [21], in which actual experimental conditions were used to validate
 Griffin solutions. The results will be discussed in a companion report [22] to be released this fiscal year.
 Future work should extend the verification to full-core depletion and transient problems and complete
 the validation against integral experiments for HTTR and either HTR-10 [23] or HTR-PROTEUS [24].
