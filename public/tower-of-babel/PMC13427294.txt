Exposure keratopathy and conjunctival microbial flora in mechanically ventilated patients – A hospital-based prospective study
Authors: M Bindu Malini, H M Akshay, Shreya Nataraj, Shreyanka Mane, Keerthi K. Pullukkil, T. S Vasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Patients on prolonged mechanical ventilation are at risk of exposure keratopathy and conjunctival microbial colonization. This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of exposure keratopathy and the pattern of conjunctival microbial flora in mechanically ventilated ICU patients.Material and Methods:Ocular evaluation for the presence of EK using fluorescein dye and grading was done in patients on mechanical ventilation in ICUs. Conjunctival swabs from these patients were analyzed for culture and sensitivity on day 3 and day 7. Regular follow-up was done.Results:A total of 52 patients were included with a mean age of 51.57 ± 16.65 years, with sepsis (28%) as the most common cause for admission. 32% of patients had EK, and the most common organisms found in different grades of EK were Staphylococcus haemolyticus in grade 1, Acinetobacter baumannii in grade 2, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa in grade 3. The most common commensal found in our study was Staphylococcus epidermidis (52%), and the noncommensal was Staphylococcus aureus (11%). Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Candida albicans were found to be present only on day 7 of the ICU care. Conjunctival culture yielded the same organism as in the endotracheal tube culture result in 13.5% of the cases.Conclusions:Eye care in critically ill patients on mechanical ventilation is often ignored, and it can lead to rare but potentially devastating complications such as corneal perforation and endophthalmitis, which have long-term consequences on a patient’s quality of life. Hence, it is essential to monitor eye care, assess EK, and manage at the earliest with a standard eye care protocol for mechanically ventilated ICU patients.

Introduction

Critically ill patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation are at an increased risk of developing exposure keratopathy (EK), which is characterized by damage of corneal epithelium due to inadequate eyelid closure.[1] It is a common but often overlooked complication in such patients. It is essential to recognize risk factors and manage keratopathy to prevent long-term visual impairment and quality of life. The incidence rate of EK in patients admitted in intensive care varies from 3.6% to 60% as reported from various studies.[2]

The risk of EK was found to be higher among patients with stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI), and spinal cord injury.[3] Lagophthalmos in a prolonged mechanically ventilated patient can be due to various reasons like neuromuscular causes, where prolonged use of neuromuscular blocking agents can lead to lagophthalmos.

Critical illness such as polyneuropathy characterized by muscle weakness and paralysis can affect the action of orbicularis oculi muscle.[4] Rare autoimmune disorders like Guillain–Barre (GB) syndrome are also known to affect eyelid movements. Mechanical irritation during endotracheal intubation and generalized facial edema can also result in lagophthalmos. Inappropriately placed face masks lead to retraction of eyelids which may go unnoticed. Pharmacological causes such as prolonged use of midazolam or fentanyl can cause relaxation of orbicularis oculi muscle and incomplete closure of the eyes.[5]

Because of these above causes, EK ensues with risk of superadded microbial infection. The most common microorganisms involved are Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans.[6]

This study was done to assess the frequency of EK and microbes that dictate the ocular surface in mechanically ventilated patients.

During the course of the study, complications associated with EK were documented and managed.

Material and Methods

This is a prospective study which was conducted for 6 months duration and included patients who were under mechanical ventilation and sedation in an intensive care unit (ICU) for up to 7 days. Exclusion criteria were preexisting ocular surface disorders, as verified by previous documents, and ocular and surrounding adnexal injury.

Details of patients’ demographic data, causes for ventilator support or sedation, and eyecare protocols followed by the nursing staff were documented. On day 1, a thorough ocular examination was done by a single ophthalmologist and the presence of lagophthalmos was recorded. The cornea was stained with fluorescein strip and examined using a cobalt blue filter from the direct ophthalmoscope to see for any signs of EK and graded using EK grading scale[7] on day 3 by the examiner on and on day 7 by the same examiner. On the same days, sterile cotton swabs were used to take samples from the inferior conjunctival fornix and dispatched to the microbiology lab immediately and subjected to Gram staining and then cultured on blood agar, MacConkey agar, and Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) and run on VITEK COMPACT 2 to look for sensitivity to various antibiotics.

Endotracheal tube (ET) aspirates on day 3 of intubation were sent for culture. If the ICU care was extended for more than 1 week, regular follow-up was done to see improvement.

Lab culture and sensitivity were not funded and were sent with the patient attender’s consent.

Institutional Ethical committee clearance (ECR/387/Inst/KA/2013/RR-19) and informed consent from the patients were obtained for the study from 01/06/2024 to 01/12/2024.

Results

In 6 months duration, a total of 52 patients were included, out of which 37 were males and 15 were females with a mean age of 51.57 ± 16.65 years. The most common causes for admission in ICU were sepsis (10), intracranial bleed (9), cerebrovascular accidents (CVA) (9), respiratory failure (7), and various other causes (17) such as PRESS syndrome, organophosphate poisoning, and vestibular schwannoma.

Out of 52 patients in our study, irrespective of the day of the stay, 17 (32%) patients had EK.

A total of 22 patients had an extended ICU stay of up to 7 days, among whom 14 patients (64%) had EK.

Based on EK grading scale, nine patients had grade 1, five had grade 2, and three had grade 3. The most common organisms found from the conjunctival fornix of patients with EK were Staphylococcus epidermidis (8), Acinetobacter baumannii (3), Staphylococcus hemolyticus (2), Propionibacterium acnes (2), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2).

Two out of seven patients in grade 1 EK had noncommensal Staphylococcus hemolyticus, three out of five patients with grade 2 EK had Acinetobacter baumannii as the noncommensal, and two out of three patients with grade 3 EK had Pseudomonas aeruginosa as the noncommensal.

Microbial isolate distribution in conjunctival culture of all the patients irrespective of EK showed the most common organisms found to be Staphylococcus epidermidis (27), Propionibacterium acnes (7), Staphylococcus aureus (6), Acinetobacter baumannii (4), Staphylococcus hemolyticus (3), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2), Klebsiella pneumoniae (2), and Candida albicans (1).

The Gram staining pattern of conjunctival culture isolates showed 36 Gram-positive cocci, 15 Gram-negative bacilli, and 1 Gram-positive yeast.

The most common organisms found on day 7 were Staphylococcus hemolyticus (4), Acinetobacter baumannii (3), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2), and one single culture of Candida albicans was found in a patient with a neurological cause (PRESS syndrome). The remaining patients had no growth in the culture.

It is to be noted that Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Candida albicans were found to be present only on day 7 of the ICU care.

The ET culture sent on day 3 of intubation for all 52 patients showed Acinetobacter baumannii to be the most common organism (25), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (6), Aspergillus fumigatus (2), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2), and no growth in 17.

It was compared to the conjunctival swab culture result and was found that Acinetobacter baumannii (3), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2), Klebsiella pneumoniae (1), and no growth (1) were found corresponding to the ET culture result of the same patient (13.5%).

It is to be noted that there is 100% similarity between conjunctival culture and ET culture in Pseudomonas aeruginosa samples.

One such patient diagnosed with a neurological cause had antibiotic resistance to all and was sensitive only to colistin. Hence, reconstituted colistin eyedrops was used in this patient.

The nursing staff in the ICU were using lubricants, wet gauze, and lid taping for mechanically ventilated patients but were inconsistent with frequency and its maintenance. Early signs of EK were generally missed.

Discussion

There are protocols to prevent EK in prolonged mechanically intubated patients such as mandatory daily assessment and documentation for signs of keratopathy, application of topical lubricants every 2 to 4 hours to prevent dryness, use of eye shields or goggles to prevent formation of debris and exposure,[8] and certain ventilator-associated protocols such as proper patient positioning to minimize exposure, using humidified air to prevent dryness and irritation, avoiding oversedation, and judicial use of anticholinergics which exacerbates dry eyes.

In a prospective cohort study conducted by Obaid Kousha et al.,[3] 56% of EK was noted in mechanically ventilated patients and a similar study conducted by Jammal H[9] et al. had 57% EK, where the frequency of EK was higher in patients with incomplete closure of lids. In our study, we found that 32% of mechanically ventilated patients had EK which was less compared to other studies.

An experimental study by Sama S et al.[10] divided patients into control and experimental groups, and eyecare bundle was implemented, which included hand and eye hygiene with application of HPMC (hydroxypropyl methylcellulose) gel and horizontal lid taping. The EK rate had fallen from 69% to 13% after eyecare bundle implantation.

A study conducted by E. K. Mela et al.[1] showed trauma with intracranial bleed to be the most common cause, followed by sepsis, CVA, and respiratory failure. Jammal et al.[9] found trauma to be the most common cause, and the other causes noted were cardiopulmonary, CVA, and sepsis. These findings are comparable to our study, and ultimately, it reveals that the most common causes of ICU admission are sepsis, intracranial bleed, CVA, and respiratory failure.

Normal conjunctival commensals are Staphylococcus epidermidis, Propionibacterium acnes, diphtheroids, and so on. We found that commensals resulted in opportunistic infections when the corneal protective barrier was hampered due to EK. The most common organisms found in our study irrespective of EK were Staphylococcus epidermidis (27) and Propionibacterium acnes (7), which were commensals, and Staphylococcus aureus (6), Acinetobacter bowmanii (4), Staphylococcus hemolyticus (3), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2), Klebsiella pneumoniae (1), and Candida albicans (1), which were noncommensals. A similar study by Ramani K et al.[11] had Coagulase negative staphylococcus (125) and diptheroids (33) as commensals and Staphylococcus aureus (29), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (12), Acinetobacter baumannii (10), Enterococcus (10), and Candida albicans (6) as noncommensals.

Another study by Bukke Sidda et al.[6] showed 25 patients (35 eyes) with commensals (Micrococcus) and 3 patients (6 eyes) positive for Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 2 patients (3 eyes) positive for Klebsiella pneumoniae, 1 patient (2 eyes) positive for E. coli, 1 patient (2 eyes) positive for Staphylococcus aureus, and 1 patient (2 eyes) positive for Moraxella species, which were noncommensals.

On evaluation of microbial isolates found on day 7 admission of mechanically ventilated patients, it showed Staphylococcus hemolyticus (4), Acinetobacter baumannii (3), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2), and Candida albicans (1) in our study. Bukke Sidda et al. on day 7 in their study found Pseudomonas aeruginosa (3), Klebsiella pneumoniae (2), E. coli (1), Staphylococcus aureus (1), and Moraxella (1). It is to be noted that Pseudomonas aeruginosa is found most commonly after day 7 of ICU admission and it could be due to hospital acquired infection which is comparable to a study by Eileen Hilton et al.,[12] which showed 6 out of 18 patients having Pseudomonas aeruginosa nosocomial bacterial eye infection in ICUs.

It is to our knowledge that this is one of the first studies to find if any similarity is noted between ET aspirates’ culture and conjunctival culture of the mechanically ventilated patients. The ET culture report in our study showed Acinetobacter baumannii to be the most common isolate found and other isolates such as Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were found. Some of the samples corresponded to the conjunctival samples such as Acinetobacter baumannii (3), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (1), which gives us the understanding of a possible source of conjunctival microbial infection and measures that can be taken earlier to prevent infection.

Deepti Chandra et al.[13] study was done to understand ET microbial profiles in ICU patients, which showed Klebsiella pneumoniae as the most common organism, followed by Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

In our study, antibiotic sensitivity of the cultures revealed that most organisms, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Propionibacterium acnes, Staphylococcus aureus, and Staphylococcus hemolyticus, were sensitive to gentamicin, vancomycin, and ciprofloxacin. Some Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae cultures had resistance to ciprofloxacin and ceftazidime had intermediate sensitivity to colistin.

Ramani K[11] et al. study found most of the isolates, CONS, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii, to be sensitive to gentamicin, ceftazidime, vancomycin, and ciprofloxacin and resistant to penicillin.

The limitations in this study are the limited sample size, short study duration, and absence of control groups.

Conclusions

Our study on conjunctival microbial flora demonstrates Staphylococcus epidermidis as the most common commensal in mechanically ventilated ICU patients and pathogenic isolates such as Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Staphylococcus hemolyticus to be the most common noncommensals. We noticed that Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Candida albicans were found to be present only on day 7 culture samples. 13.5% of the conjunctival culture samples in our study had similarity to the ET culture, which suggests that early identification of ET culture may help prevent conjunctival microbial infection. 32% of the study population had EK, who were managed as per their grading with medical or surgical intervention.

Clinical significance

Eye care in critically ill patients on mechanical ventilation is often ignored, and it can lead to rare but vision-debilitating complications such as corneal perforation and panophtalmitis. Hence, it is essential to monitor eye care, assess for EK, and manage at the earliest in mechanically ventilated ICU patients.
