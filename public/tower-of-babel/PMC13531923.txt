Ultrasonic elastography-guided pleural biopsy followed by as-needed semi-rigid thoracoscopic pleural biopsy versus immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopic pleural biopsy for the diagnosis of pleural effusion (UPDATE-3): a protocol of randomised controlled trial
Authors: Liwei Liao, Jiangwei Ma, Jixiang Ni, Jieru Lin, Shuai Zhao, Yang Xia, Xiaoping Liu, Mingming Deng, Ziwen Zheng, Gang Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExudative pleural effusion of an unclear aetiology remains a diagnostic challenge. Ultrasonic elastography-guided pleural biopsy (UEPB) offers a higher diagnostic yield than conventional ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy; however, its role relative to semi-rigid thoracoscopy remains unclear. This trial is designed to determine whether UEPB is non‑inferior to semi-rigid thoracoscopy in diagnostic yield when used as the initial diagnostic procedure, and to further quantify the proportion of patients in whom semi-rigid thoracoscopy could be avoided by adopting a UEPB-first diagnostic strategy.Methods and analysisIn this multicentre, open-label, parallel-group, non-inferiority, randomised controlled trial conducted at five sites in China, 420 patients with exudative pleural effusion of unknown aetiology and negative cytology will be randomly assigned 1:1 to either initial UEPB (with semi-rigid thoracoscopy reserved for non-diagnostic cases) or immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy. The primary outcome is the overall diagnostic yield of the allocated diagnostic strategy, defined as the proportion of participants in whom a specific histopathological diagnosis is stablished through the protocol-defined biopsy procedures within the allocated diagnostic pathway. A non-inferiority margin of − 5% is applied. The secondary outcomes included diagnostic sensitivity in specific disease, procedure-related adverse events, length of hospital stay, duration of chest tube drainage, thoracoscopy avoidance rate, and direct diagnostic pathway-related medical costs.DiscussionThis UPDATE-3 study will provide the evidence from a RCT directly comparing the a UEPB-first step-up diagnostic strategy with immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy in patients with undiagnosed exudative pleural effusion. Should non-inferiority be established, UEPB will be validated as a sufficiently sensitive first-line biopsy technique. Furthermore, this trial will quantify the extent to which a UEPB-first strategy can safely decrease the necessity for semi-rigid thoracoscopy and optimize healthcare utilization. These findings could facilitate a paradigm shift towards a less invasive, step-up diagnostic pathway. Conversely, should non-inferiority not be established, the findings will reinforce the status of semi-rigid thoracoscopy as the diagnostic reference standard and elucidate the appropriate role of UEPB within the diagnostic algorithm.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (Registration ID: NCT07419620; Registration Date: 2026-02-12).Keywords: Pleural disease, Pleural biopsy, Ultrasonic elastography, Semi-rigid thoracoscopy

Background

Pleural effusion is a prevalent clinical manifestation associated with a diverse range of aetiologies. Malignant pleural effusion (MPE) often signifies advanced disease and poor prognosis [1]. Histopathological examination of pleural tissue remains the diagnostic modality for determining the definitive aetiology, particularly in cases where malignancy is suspected [2]. Current guidelines advocate that patients with unknown exudative pleural effusions undergo image-guided pleural biopsy or semi-rigid thoracoscopy following initial fluid analysis [2, 3]. Nonetheless, existing diagnostic pathways are fraught with significant limitations that pose considerable challenges in clinical management.

The initial diagnostic approach, thoracic grey-scale ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy, is constrained by suboptimal and variable diagnostic sensitivities. A recent meta-analysis estimated a pooled sensitivity of 77% for detecting pleural malignancies [4]. In contrast, semi-rigid thoracoscopy has a high diagnostic yield (> 95%) and is widely regarded as the reference standard for pleural sampling [5]. However, its application is restricted by the need for specialised equipment, substantial operator expertise, procedural sedation or anaesthesia, and typically an inpatient setting, thereby limiting its accessibility [5]. Even when performed in an outpatient setting, semi-rigid thoracoscopy requires dedicated instrumentation, trained interventional pulmonologists, and anaesthesia support, restricting its widespread adoption [6]. This technological and logistical disparity results in clinical practice patterns that are heavily influenced by local resource availability, rather than by standardised, evidence-based protocols. Consequently, this may lead to the potentially unnecessary use of invasive thoracoscopy in well-resourced centres while contributing to diagnostic delays or repeated low-yield procedures in settings with limited access.

The recent incorporation of ultrasound elastography into pleural biopsy procedures represents a potential technological advance in optimising the diagnostic process. Ultrasound elastography assesses tissue stiffness, a frequently increased biomechanical property in malignant lesions owing to associated architectural changes [7]. A pivotal multicentre randomised controlled trial demonstrated that ultrasonic elastography-guided pleural biopsy (UEPB) offers significantly higher sensitivity in diagnosing MPE than conventional ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy (85.0% vs. 63.2%) while maintaining a comparable safety profile [8]. This finding is consistent with earlier prospective multicentre data that reported a diagnostic yield of 92.9% for UEPB in cases of undiagnosed pleural effusion [9]. Consequently, UEPB has been identified as a promising, minimally invasive bedside technique that may effectively bridge the diagnostic gap between conventional ultrasound-guided biopsy and semi-rigid thoracoscopy [10].

Although UEPB has demonstrated superiority over conventional ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy, its precise role within the clinical diagnostic pathway remains ambiguous owing to the absence of direct comparative trials with semi-rigid thoracoscopy. Indirect evidence suggests the value of a step-up diagnostic pathway for pleural diseases.

A previous study found that reserving thoracoscopy in cases in which less invasive biopsies were non-diagnostic achieved an overall diagnostic yield of 95.99%. This is significantly higher than the 91.20% yield achieved with semi-rigid thoracoscopy alone, and 97% of patients avoided semi-rigid thoracoscopy [11]. These findings suggest that a selective step-up algorithm can enhance diagnostic performance while substantially reducing the need for invasive procedures. Nevertheless, no randomised trial has directly compared the UEPB- first-step-up strategy with immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy. Consequently, a significant evidence gap persists, as there is a lack of randomised data to determine whether patients should proceed directly to semi-rigid thoracoscopy or if an initial UEPB-reserving thoracoscopy for cases where UEPB is non-diagnostic represents a non-inferior and more resource-efficient diagnostic approach with potentially lower diagnostic pathway-related medical costs. Clarifying this optimal sequential diagnostic algorithm is crucial for enhancing patient-centred care, optimising healthcare resource allocation, and potentially alleviating systemic economic burden.

Objectives

The primary objective of this trial is to determine whether a UEPB-first step-up diagnostic strategy is non-inferior to immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy in terms of the overall diagnostic yield for obtaining a specific histopathological diagnosis.

The secondary objectives are to compare the two diagnostic strategies across multiple domains: (1) their diagnostic performance for malignant diseases, including sensitivity; (2) their safety profiles based on procedure-related complication rates; (3) patient tolerance and procedural efficiency assessed using pain scores, procedure time, and hospital length of stay; and (4) direct diagnostic pathway-related medical costs from the healthcare system perspective.

Additionally, this trial will include exploratory analyses to further characterise the performance of the UEPB-first approach. This encompasses investigating its diagnostic efficacy in predefined clinical subgroups, such as patients with a pleural thickness of ≤ 5 mm; assessing the suitability of tissue samples obtained via UEPB for subsequent molecular profiling; and analysing the patterns and reasons for crossover to thoracoscopy within the sequential strategy arm, to inform the practical implementation of this diagnostic pathway.

Methods

Study design and setting

This trial is designed as a randomised, open-label, parallel-group, non-inferiority study. Five sites in China were included in this trial. All these centres are equipped with the necessary apparatus for conducting UEPB and semi-rigid thoracoscopic pleural biopsy. These centres have a history of involvement in clinical studies on UEPB [8, 9] and possess substantial experience in performing UEPB. The trial overview flow diagram is shown in (Fig. 1).

Patient and public involvement

This trial did not involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of the research plans.

Participant selection

This trial aimed to recruit 420 patients with exudative pleural effusion of unknown aetiology, as determined by pleural effusion examination.

Inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) patients with exudative pleural effusion of unknown origin; (3) patients with negative pleural fluid cytology results; (4) patients who had not undergone pleural biopsy before enrolment.

Exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Significantly bleeding risk, including uncorrectable coagulopathy (such as INR >1.5, platelet count <50 × 109 /L) or the need for ongoing anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy that cannot be safely paused; (2) hemodynamic instability; (3) refractory hypoxemia despite supplemental oxygen; (4) any severe concurrent illness or cardiopulmonary compromise that, in the investigator’s judgment, makes either procedural sedation (for thoracoscopy) or a bedside biopsy (for UEPB) unsafe; (5) pleural effusion conclusively identified as a transudate; (6) pregnancy or lactation; (7) any condition that, in the investigator’s judgment, would compromise protocol compliance or follow-up.

Randomisation and masking

Eligible participants will be randomly allocated to either the step-up strategy, which starts with UEPB followed by semi-rigid thoracoscopy if the UEPB results are insufficiently informative, or immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy, in a 1:1 ratio after obtaining written informed consent. An independent investigator from the primary centre, the China-Japan Friendship, will generate the randomisation sequence using an online platform (https://www.sealedenvelope.com/simple-randomiser/v1/lists) with a block size of eight. The randomisation process was stratified by participating sites, and the generated sequence is securely stored in a password-protected electronic file. The allocation sequence will remain concealed from all investigators, study coordinators, and clinicians until randomisation. Upon randomisation, the assigned intervention group will be immediately disclosed to the interventional pulmonology team via a centralised electronic system. The participants and clinicians will not be blinded to the diagnostic strategy.

Intervention procedures

Step-up strategy arm

Participants allocated to the step-up strategy first underwent UEPB. The UEPB will be performed as previously described by our team [8, 9]. Briefly, following standard B-mode thoracic ultrasonography to confirm the biopsy site and assess pleural characteristics, shear-wave elastrography (SWE) will be performed across all accessible pleural areas using a dedicated ultrasound system based on local accessibility. The shear-wave elastography mean value (SWEmean) will be calculated. The target biopsy site will be defined as an area with SWEmean >47.25 kPa or the region with the highest SWEmean in the absence of such an area. Local anaesthesia will be administered under real-time ultrasound guidance, and a minimum of five pleural biopsy specimens will be obtained from the target lesion using a coaxial biopsy needle. Additional specimens will be collected for microbiological culture analyses. The chest tube will not routinely placed after UEPB. A chest radiograph will be obtained within 2–4 h post-procedure to assess for delayed pneumothorax. The development of a significant or symptomatic pneumothorax will be managed according to clinical judgment, including chest tube insertion, if necessary, and recorded as an adverse event.

All specimens will be fixed, processed, and stained at the pathology department of each participating site, following a standardised study protocol to ensure technical consistency. Immunohistochemistry and molecular studies will be performed as clinically indicated to establish the diagnosis. The initial histopathological diagnoses will be established by two independent pathologists at each site. All the slicers will be reviewed by an independent group of expert pathologists to ensure harmony. A biopsy will be considered diagnostically sufficient if the pathological findings identify definitive features that enable a specific diagnosis, such as malignancy or granuloma. Participants whose UEPB specimens will be considered non-diagnostic when it fails to establish any specific diagnosis and will proceed to the subsequent semi-rigid thoracoscopy. This category includes inadequate or insufficient tissue, absence of representative pleural tissue or cellular material, specimens containing only blood, fibrin, or necrotic debris, and nonspecific pathological findings such as nonspecific inflammation or fibrosis without definitive diagnostic features. The initial histopathological diagnosis will be made independently by two pathologists at each participating site. All biopsy slides classified as non-diagnostic or showing nonspecific findings will be reviewed by an independent central expert pathology panel to ensure consistency of diagnostic classification. Any discrepancy between local central pathological assessments will be resolved by consensus.

Immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy arm

Participants allocated to this arm will undergo semi-rigid thoracoscopic pleural biopsy as the initial and sole diagnostic procedure. A semi-rigid thoracoscopic pleural biopsy will be performed by experienced pulmonologists following the guidelines for pleural diseases [1, 2]. All participants will receive semi-rigid thoracoscopic pleural biopsy under local anaesthesia or moderate sedation. Under direct visualisation, pleural biopsies will be obtained from macroscopically abnormal areas using flexible biopsy forceps, with care taken to sample the ribs to avoid neurovascular injury. A minimum of five biopsy specimens will be collected. A chest tube will be inserted prophylactically upon completion of the procedure. In cases with strong suspicion of malignancy during semi-rigid thoracoscopy and in which the participant has or is anticipated to develop a significant recurrent pleural effusion, the operator will perform chemical pleurodesis with povidone-iodine (a 10% solution diluted to a 1% concentration) during the same procedure. The postoperative chest image will be performed following the routine chest tube management or when clinically indicated. The chest tube will be removed according to the standard criteria, and drainage exceeding 24 h was recorded.

Follow-up

All participants will undergo a 7-day post-procedural follow-up. This follow-up will be conducted via telephone interview or outpatient clinic visit to monitor and document any procedure-related adverse events and to evaluate participants’ self-reported symptomatic recovery.

To establish a definitive final diagnosis, a long-term follow-up of 12 months is necessary only for participants who remain with non-diagnostic biopsy results after completion of the allocated diagnostic pathway. This includes participants in the immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy arm with non-diagnostic thoracoscopic biopsy results and participants in the step-up arm who remain without a specific diagnosis after UEPB followed by protocol-mandated semi-rigid thoracoscopy. For these participants, follow-up will occur at 12 months and may include a clinic visit, a telephone interview, or a review of electronic medical records, supplemented by any necessary imaging to rule out occult malignancy. Participants who remain without evidence of a specific disease after 12 months of follow-up will be classified as having ‘idiopathic pleuritis’ [2].

Reference standard

The final diagnosis will be established based on all clinical, radiological, microbiological, pathological, and follow-up data. Additionally, the independent diagnostic verification group comprised three physicians with expertise in pleural disease, one radiologist, and one pathologist.

Outcome

Primary outcome

The overall diagnostic yield of the allocated diagnostic strategy is the primary outcome of this trial, defined as the proportion of participants in whom a specific diagnosis is established through protocol-defined biopsy procedures within the allocated diagnostic pathway [12]. In the step-up arm, this includes diagnosis established by initial UEPB and, for participants with non-diagnostic UEPB, by subsequent semi-rigid thoracoscopy. In the immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy arm, this includes diagnosis established by thoracoscopic pleural biopsy.

Secondary outcome

Diagnostic sensitivity, safety, avoidance of semi-rigid thoracoscopy, and patient burden were the secondary outcomes of this study.

The diagnostic sensitivity for specific diseases was defined as the proportion of participants with a final diagnosis of specific diseases that were correctly identified by the initial biopsy method [12]. The safety profile was assessed by the incidence of each adverse event.

The proportion of participants in the step-up arm who were diagnosed with UEPB alone will be used to evaluate the avoidance of semi-rigid thoracoscopy. The rate of unexpected chest tube drainage will be calculated as any chest tube placement after UEPB or prolonged chest tube drainage (> 24 h) after semi-rigid thoracoscopy. Additionally, the total durations of chest tube drainage and in-hospital stay were recorded. Direct cost outcome will include diagnostic pathway related direct medical costs during the index admission. These data will be extracted from inpatient medical records and hospital billing records. Cost items will include procedure-related costs, biopsy-related consumables, anaesthesia or sedation, pathological and microbiological examinations, chest tube management, imaging and laboratory examinations, medications used during the diagnostic admission, hospitalization costs, and costs related to the management of procedure-related adverse-events. Costs related to disease-specific treatment after establishment of the final diagnosis will not be included in this direct cost analysis.

Statistical analysis

The diagnostic yield of UEPB alone has been reported to be 92.9% in a published study [8]. Additionally, existing literature indicates a diagnostic yield of 96.9% for semi-rigid thoracoscopic pleural biopsy [3]. These estimates supported the feasibility of a UEPB-first step-up strategy but were not used as fixed arm-specific assumptions for a direct comparison between UEPB alone and thoracoscopic pleural biopsy. The sample size was calculated for the primary non-inferiority comparison of the overall diagnostic yield between the two allocated diagnostic strategies, because participants with non-diagnostic UEPB will proceed to semi-rigid thoracoscopy according the protocol. Using an alpha level of 0.025, a statistical power of 1-β = 0.8, a 1:1 sample size ratio between the experimental and control groups, and a non-inferiority margin of 0.05, calculations conducted using PASS software determined that a minimum sample size of 189 cases is required for each group. To account for the anticipated 10% loss to follow-up and participant refusal, the minimum sample size per group is 210, yielding a total of 420 participants.

For the analysis of the primary outcome, the difference in the overall diagnostic yield between UEPB-first step-up arm and immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy strategy, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) will be calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel method. Non-inferiority is determined if the lower limit of the 95% CI for the difference exceeds − 5%. A prespecified sensitivity analysis will be performed on the population, excluding participants with a final diagnosis of idiopathic pleuritis.

The comparison of the diagnostic sensitivity between the UEPB in the step-up arm and semi-rigid thoracoscopy in the immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy arm will be conducted by Fisher’s exact or χ² test. The effect size was reported as the absolute difference with a 95% CI.

Direct diagnostic pathway-related medical costs will be summarised as mean with standard deviation and median with interquartile range. Because cost data are expected to be right-skewed, between-group comparisons will be performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The mean cost difference between the two strategies and its 95% CI will be estimated using non-parametric bootstrapping. No formal cost-effectiveness analysis will be performed, and no incremental cost-effectiveness ratio will be calculated.

For other categorical outcomes, including incidence of adverse events, unexpected chest tube drainage, and the proportion of participants avoiding thoracoscopy, the between-group comparisons will use Fisher’s exact or χ² test. The absolute risk reductions with 95% confidence intervals will be reported.

For continuous outcomes, such as procedure time, total in-hospital stay, and total chest tube duration, the groups will be compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Median differences with 95% CI (Hodges–Lehmann estimator) are presented.

All statistical tests were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05. The analysis will be performed using SPSS (version 26.0; SPSS Inc.).

Discussion

The UPDATE-3 trial aims to fill an evidence gap in the diagnostic pathway for patients with undiagnosed exudative pleural effusion. Despite advancements in pleural fluid analysis and imaging techniques, a considerable number of patients with exudative effusions do not receive a definitive diagnosis following initial investigations [13]. Current clinical guidelines advocate for image-guided pleural biopsy or semi-rigid thoracoscopy in these cases, yet the most effective initial diagnostic approach remains undetermined [2]. UEPB has been shown to provide a higher diagnostic yield compared to grey-scale ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy, especially in patients lacking visible pleural thickening or nodularity [8]. However, direct comparisons between UEPB and semi-rigid thoracoscopy, which is widely considered the diagnostic reference standard, have not been conducted in randomized trials. This study aims to furnish comparative data to ascertain whether UEPB is non-inferior to thoracoscopy as an initial diagnostic procedure.

This study will evaluate a step-up strategy in which UEPB is used as the initial biopsy method, with semi-rigid thoracoscopy reserved for patients whose UEPB yields non-diagnostic specimens. This design will be employed aims to reflect clinical decision-making, where the choice is between two management pathways rather than between biopsy techniques in isolation. The step-up approach may reduce procedure-related morbidity and lower healthcare resource utilisation. Quantification of the semi-rigid thoracoscopy avoidance rate is therefore a key secondary objective that will directly inform clinical practice and guideline development. If UEPB proved to be non-inferior, it could be positioned as an effective initial biopsy method in a minimally invasive diagnostic pathway for unknown exudative pleural effusion.

Several practical considerations necessitate careful attention. Both UEPB and semi-rigid thoracoscopy are procedures that depend on the operator’s expertise. The diagnostic performance of UEPB is potentially affected by the learning curve associated with interpretation of elastography and the selection of targets. Our previous multicentre trial revealed that standardised training and the implementation of guidance software can ensure consistent diagnostic performance across participating sites with varying levels of experience [8]. In the current trial, participating sites will undergo centralised training and protocol harmonisation prior to the commencement of the study, which may contribute to minimizing variability related to operator proficiency.

Another important consideration is the availability of equipment. The implementation of UEPB necessitates the use of ultrasound systems equipped with shear-wave elastography capability, which are not universally accessible across all clinical settings. Previous studies have identified several challenges to the routine implementation of UEPB have been noted previously, including lack of technical standardisation, potential variability between different ultrasound platforms, and operator dependency [14]. While participating centres in this trial have access to the necessary equipment and have prior experience with UEPB [2, 8], broader implementation following the trial would depend on technology dissemination and training. Real-world studies using commercially available elastography systems would be needed to evaluate generalisability.

This study inevitable has several limitations. First, the open-label design may introduce potential bias in both the primary and secondary outcomes. Although the primary outcome is based on histopathological diagnosis and is relatively objective, complete blinding is not feasible because UEPB and semi-rigid thoracoscopy differ substantially in procedural workflow, peri-procedural management, and specimen characteristics. As a result, the biopsy method may still be inferred from the obtained specimens. Standardized procedures, predefined diagnostic criteria, harmonized specimen processing, and independent pathological or diagnostic adjudication will be used to reduce variability and avoidable bias. However, these measures cannot fully eliminate the potential bias inherent to these two biopsy approaches. Second, the study is conducted in specialized centres with established expertise in both procedures. While this may limit the generalizability of the findings to less experienced settings, the inclusion of multiple sites with diverse patient populations offers some reassurance. Moreover, the centralized training protocol could facilitate broader implementation if the strategy proves effective. Third, the follow-up period may not capture late recurrences of malignancy in patients initially diagnosed with benign disease. Nonetheless, this duration aligns with previous studies in the field [8, 9], and the independent diagnosis verification group will ensure rigorous adjudication of final diagnoses. Additional questions, such as the adequacy of tissue samples obtained via UEPB for molecular profiling, will be explored in further analyses [14].

Overall, the UPDATE-3 will compare a UEPB-first step-up strategy to immediate semi-rigid thoracoscopy for diagnosing undiagnosed exudative pleural effusion. If the step-up approach is non-inferior, it could serve as a less invasive initial option, with thoracoscopy for non-diagnostic cases. If not, thoracoscopy remains the preferred method. The findings will inform clinicians and guideline developers.
