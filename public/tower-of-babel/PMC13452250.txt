Hyaluronic acid-engineered copper sulfide nanoparticles as immunomodulatory metal sulfide photothermal agents for macrophage-assisted osteosarcoma therapy
Authors: Yitian Wang, Wei Yu, Bing Liu, Gautam Sethi, Xudong Miao, Bing Xiao, Huimin Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal sulfide nanomaterials have emerged as promising photothermal agents for cancer therapy owing to their strong near-infrared absorption, favorable biocompatibility, and tunable surface chemistry. However, insufficient tumor accumulation and limited immunological activation remain major obstacles restricting their therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors. Herein, we report a macrophage-assisted delivery strategy based on hyaluronic acid-engineered copper sulfide nanoparticles (HA@CuS NPs) for enhanced photothermal-immunotherapy against osteosarcoma. In this system, adoptively transferred RAW264.7 macrophages were intravenously administered to increase macrophage enrichment within the osteosarcoma microenvironment, while HA@CuS NPs were rationally designed to target both tumor cells and tumor-associated macrophages through HA-mediated cellular recognition.The HA-coated CuS nanoparticles displayed good colloidal stability, efficient near-infrared photothermal conversion, and enhanced cellular uptake by osteosarcoma cells and macrophages. Importantly, macrophages acted as cellular reservoirs for CuS nanoparticles, promoting tumor accumulation and improving intratumoral photothermal distribution. Under 808-nm laser irradiation, the combined macrophage/HA@CuS treatment produced stronger tumor heating and more effective osteosarcoma ablation than HA@CuS nanoparticles alone. Beyond direct photothermal killing, HA@CuS nanoparticles also remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment by promoting M1-like polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, increasing IL-12p40 secretion, reducing IL-10 levels, and enhancing cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration. These immune-regulatory effects further amplified the antitumor response induced by photothermal therapy.Collectively, this study demonstrates that HA-engineered copper sulfide nanoparticles can function not only as metal sulfide photothermal agents but also as immunomodulatory nanomaterials. The integration of macrophage-assisted tumor delivery with CuS-based photothermal therapy provides a promising strategy for improving the therapeutic efficacy of metal-based nanomedicine against osteosarcoma.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a relatively uncommon type of primary bone cancer and the second most common cause of death associated with tumors in young individuals [1]. The contemporary treatment of newly diagnosed OS includes treatment with chemotherapy before performing surgery to remove the sarcoma. However, even though the chemotherapy can achieve antitumor activity, the effectiveness of the treatment is usually compromised by drug resistance and adverse effects since the therapy does not have the selectivity for specific targets [2,3]. In terms of sarcoma, high density of the osteoid also poses difficulty for systemic administered drugs to fulfill their effects [4]. Therefore, novel strategies are urgently needed.

Recently, the development of advanced nanocarriers has facilitated improvements in drug pharmacokinetics and delivery efficiency via the enhanced penetration and retention (EPR) effect, which is attributed to the size-based selectivity of tumor microvasculature [5,6]. To date, numerous studies have highlighted the likelihood of nano vehicles for the dispensing of different antitumor medications. Nevertheless, few reports showed the improved therapeutic efficacy during clinical trials due to several barriers, such as the mononuclear phagocyte screening system and the great heterogeneity of tumors in terms of genetic diversity and vascular permeation [7,8]. Despite different strategies of optimization (e.g. surface modifications) [9,10], few studies focus on the manipulation of intrinsic physiological barriers, i.e. the blood flow, the vasculature, stroma and the tumor microenvironment (TME), to improve nanosized drug delivery [11,12].

OS is characterized by cancer cells, stromal cells and immune infiltrates, forming a complex local environment [13,14]. Tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) that are recruited as monocytes from peripheral blood, play vital roles in local immunity, neovascularization and tumor invasion [15]. Studies have reported that TAMs can function as ‘cellular drug reservoirs' during cancer treatment, as they phagocytose and accumulate nanoparticles (NPs) within themselves first and then release the payloads into adjacent tumor cells, highlighting their important role in the tumor uptake of NPs [16,17]. Moreover, TAMs are believed to promote tumor angiogenesis and vascular permeability, which may potentially contribute to the EPR-based delivery system. Nevertheless, it remains unknown whether increasing the TAM content can serve as a new tactic to boost treatment efficacy by improving tumor uptake of relevant NPs.

Developing a TAM-targeting delivery system is also critical to increasing the TAM-mediated uptake of NPs in tumor. Among all nano systems, hyaluronic acid (HA)-based NPs have been widely studied for their great active and passive tumor-targeting properties [18,19]. In particular, HA NPs can accumulate in the tumor region by the EPR effect and specifically interact with cluster determinant 44 (CD44) receptors, a family of transmembrane glycoproteins expressed on tumor cells and macrophages [20,21]. Thus, they can be efficiently internalized by tumor cells and macrophages through CD44-mediated cellular uptake. HA NPs are then degraded by hyaluronidases, releasing their cargo inside the target cells. Therefore, HA is an ideal material for the development of TAM-targeting drug delivery systems (DDS).

In this research, we proposed a tactic to significantly enhance the accumulation and photothermal-immunological efficacy of nanosized drugs by combining an increase in macrophages recruitment into tumors and a TAM-targeting DDS. As shown in Scheme 1(B), RAW264.7 macrophages were intravenously administered to increase the TAM content in OS, and hyaluronic acid coated copper sulfide nanoparticles (HA@CuS NPs) were prepared to target macrophages and cancer cells in OS. Metal Sulfide Nanoparticles (CuS NPs) have been extensively employed as a photothermal therapy (PTT) agent for their low cost, low toxicity and excellent near infrared (NIR) absorbance [22]. However, their tumor-targeting capability must be further enhanced to achieve better anticancer efficacy. Here, we developed a facile method to synthesize HA-coated CuS NPs for treating OS in a spatially and temporally controllable manner. The HA-coating made the CuS NPs able to actively target tumor cells and macrophages in OS [23,24]. Once taken by the TAMs, HA-decoration could also serve as modulators to repolarize TAMs toward M1 type [25,26]. Accompanied by reeducation of TAMs, the HA@CuS NPs-induced PTT could efficiently inhibit OS growth with the elicitation of active immune responses. Experimental

Synthesis of thiolated HA (HA-SH)

HA was modified with thiol group by reacting with cysteamine hydrochloride [27]. HA solution (500 mg in 100 ml deionized water) was stirred vigorously overnight, to which EDC (766 mg, 4 mmol) and NHS (460 mg 4 mmol) were added. Then cysteamine hydrochloride (228 mg, 2 mmol) was added into the reaction solution after 2 h. The pH of the reaction was maintained between 4 and 5, and the solution was kept at room temperature under continuous stirring for 48 h. The obtained HA-SH was dialyzed (MWCO: 3500 Da) and lyophilized, and subsequently characterized by 1H NMR (400 MHz, Bruker DRX-400, Germany). 1H NMR (D2O): HA: δ 1.70–1.90, 3.00–4.02, and 4.3–4.65; cysteamine: δ 2.50–3.20. The degree of thiol substitution (DS) was determined to be 68% (denoted accordingly as HA-SH) by comparing the integrals of signals at δ2.50–2.80 (methylene on cysteamine) and 4.21–4.75 (methyl on acetyl group in HA).

Synthesis of HA@CuS NPs

The synthesis of HA@CuS NPs did not involve any adjustment of the pH value. Briefly, 20 mg of HA-SH and 8.5 mg of CuCl2·2H2O were dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water with stirring at 60 °C. Then, 50 μL Na2S·9H2O (24 mg/mL) was rapidly added into the above solution. After 2 h at 80 °C, the reaction was completed, and the obtained HA@CuS NPs were ultrafiltrated and then lyophilized under vacuum.

Synthesis of bovine serum albumin-coated CuS NPs (BSA@CuS NPs)

BSA@CuS NPs were formed following the above-reported method. Specifically, 5 mL aqueous Na2S·9H2O solution (96 mg) were added into 10 mL mixed aqueous solution (pH = 12) containing CuCl2·2H2O (8.5 mg) and BSA (250 mg). This caused the above mixture to quickly turn dark brown. Subsequently, the solution was magnetically stirred at 90 °C overnight and dialyzed (MWCO 10 kDa) against deionized water for 48 h to remove excess unbound metal ions.

Synthesis of Cy5.5-labeled HA@CuS NPs and BSA@CuS NPs

HA@CuS NPs or BSA@CuS NPs (1 mL, 10 mg/mL) were mixed with Cy5.5-NHS (0.3 mg) in a flask, and magnetic stirring was carried out without illumination for 24 h. Then the fluorescently labeled HA@CuS NPs or BSA@CuS NPs were purified through dialysis (MWCO 3.5 kDa).

Cell line and cell culture

The human OS cell line (MNNG/HOS), murine OS cell line (K7M2) and murine macrophage cell line (RAW 264.7) were obtained from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All the cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco Modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. The cells were passaged once they reached 80% confluency.

Cellular toxicity assay

The cytotoxic effect of HA@CuS NPs and BSA@CuS NPs was evaluated using RAW264.7 cells and MNNG/HOS cells. They were first seeded into 96-well plates (density of 5 × 103 cells/well) in DMEM containing 10% FBS at 37 °C. The media were replaced after 12 h, and the cells were then incubated with various concentrations of HA@CuS and BSA@CuS (with the same elemental Cu2+ concentration) for 24 h, respectively. Meanwhile, both cells were also exposed to HA@CuS NPs and BSA@CuS NPs (with the same Cu concentration of 2.7 μg/mL) for 6, 12, 24 and 48 h. Afterwards, cells were rinsed with PBS and subjected to cell viability determination via the CCK-8 method according to the instructions.

Cellular uptake and localization of NPs

RAW264.7 macrophages and MNNG/HOS cells (2 × 105 cells/mL) were incubated with either Cy5.5-conjugated HA@CuS NPs or BSA@CuS NPs (8.64 μg/mL Cu2+) for 2h at 37 °C for fluorescence visualization under microscopy. The confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1, Japan) was used for visualization of positively stained cells upon treatment with Cy5.5-conjugated NPs. Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen Molecular Probes, USA) was used to stain cell nuclei with blue color. The RAW264.7 cells incubated with HA@CuS NPs were also collected, embedded and prepared for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis.

In vitro and in vivo quantification of Cu2+ accumulation

For in vitro tests, RAW264.7 macrophages were incubated with HA@CuS NPs for 2 h. After the removal of the cell culture medium, the new medium of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the culture flask and incubated for various durations of time (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h), then cells and their corresponding supernatants were collected for ICP-MS analysis. For in vivo experiments, the mice were sacrificed and the blood was flushed out. The dissected tissue were then dried and dissolved in 2 mL of freshly prepared aqua regia with heating until the solution became transparent. Once evaporated, HCl and HNO3 were added, and dilution was made using distilled water. The Cu2+ concentrations of the samples were measured using ICP-MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP-QC).

In vitro transwell Boyden migration assay

In brief, 500 μL of target media (either DMEM or MNNG/HOS culture supernatant medium) per well were dispensed in a 24-well microplate. Suspensions of RAW264.7 macrophages in DMEM (1 × 105 cells/100 μL) were placed in the upper transwell chamber and incubated at 37 °C overnight. After incubation, cells that had not migrated to the bottom side were swept off with a cotton swab. The transwell chambers were placed in a new 24-well cell culture plate containing Hoechst33342 and incubated for 20 min, then washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, the number of migrated cells was counted under a fluorescence microscope.

Animals and tumor model

Female BALB/c mice and BALB/c athymic nude mice, 6-8 weeks of age and weighting 18-23 g, were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of the Zhejiang Chinese Medical University and maintained under SPF grade conditions while provided with sterilized food and water. Animal experiments were performed upon approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University, School of Medicine, China. Cells were transfected with luciferase reporter gene (MNNG/HOS-fLuc or K7M2-fLuc) for in vivo imaging. For the establishment of orthotopic osteosarcoma model, the procedures followed previous reports [28]. Briefly, the mice were anaesthetized using isoflurane, and a 30G needle was inserted into the proximal tibia through the cortex of the anterior tuberosity. After that, MNNG/HOS-Luc cells or K7M2-Luc cells (1 × 106 cells in 25 μL PBS) were administered into the medullary cavity using a Hamilton Syringe along with a 26G needle.

In vitro photothermal effects of HA@CuS (or BSA@CuS) on different cells

RAW264.7 macrophages or MNNG/HOS cells were incubated with PBS (as control groups), BSA@CuS NPs or HA@CuS NPs (8.64 μg/mL Cu2+) for 2 h, and then carefully rinsed three times with PBS to remove the unloaded NPs. The CuS NP-loaded cells (either RAW264.7 macrophages or MNNG/HOS) were subjected to laser irradiation using an 808 nm NIR laser with a power density of 1.5 W/cm2 for 3 min. The cells were stained with live cell solution containing calcein AM after heating.

Optical imaging

For the in vivo evaluation of the biodistribution of RAW264.7 macrophages and HA@CuS NPs, whole-animal imaging and ex vivo organ imaging were conducted using in vivo imaging systems (Lumina Series III, Caliper Life Sciences). Initially, the orthotopic OS model was established through the injection of MNNG/HOS cells into the right proximal tibia. On day 8, luciferase-expressing RAW264.7 cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse) were intravenously injected into the tumor-bearing mice. Bioluminescent images were captured at every time point. Three days after intravenous injection of RAW264.7 macrophages, Cy5.5-labeled HA@CuS NPs were intravenously injected into the tumor-bearing mice. Fluorescence images were then captured 24 h post injection, and the wavelength of excitation and emission was set to 675 nm and 745 nm, respectively. Next, tumors as well as other main organs were surgically removed for ex vivo noninvasive near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging, which allowed the identification of tissue distribution of HA@CuS NPs. Each of the organs obtained were cut with similar thickness and scanned at excitation of 675 nm and emission of 745 nm. The fluorescence intensity of tissues was measured from a region of interest (ROI) placed on the depicted tissues.

In vivo PTT for orthotopic OS-bearing mice

Mice with orthotopic OS were randomly assigned to four groups (four mice for each group). After 7 days of the initial establishment of OS models, treatment regimens were designated in the following manner: (1) PBS (100 μL); (2) RAW264.7 macrophages (5 × 106/mouse); (3) HA@CuS NPs (6.25 mg/kg); (4) RAW264.7 macrophages (5 × 106/mouse) + HA@CuS NPs (6.25 mg/kg). The mice in Group (2) and (4) were anaesthetized with isoflurane before tail vein injections of macrophages. After 3 days, mice in each group received an intravenous injection of PBS and HA@CuS NPs (6.25 mg/kg), respectively. Then the tumor region was irradiated with an 808-nm laser after 24 h. In order to avoid the burn injury of the skin surface of the tumor area as well as the surrounding healthy tissue throughout the process, the photothermal therapy process was carried out for 3 min at a power density of 1.5 w/cm2 (30 s interval after every 1 min of the exposure). Simultaneously, the temperature variations of tumor areas were recorded via using an infrared thermal imaging camera. Tumor progression was assessed based on bioluminescence intensity using an In Vivo Imaging System (Lumina Series III, Caliper Life Sciences). After the experiment, all mice were sacrificed, the tumors and major organs were harvested and stored in liquid nitrogen or fixed in formalin for further analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used for assessing the toxicity of HA@CuS NPs.

Immunofluorescence

At certain time points, tumor-bearing mice were sacrificed and tumor tissues were collected and embedded in OCT medium, then snap frozen. Tissue samples were sectioned at 6 μm and fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde solution and were subjected to staining with primary antibody. Then the primary antibody was detected by sequential incubation with secondary antibody. Slides were then viewed with the aid of a confocal microscope (Olympus, USA). The acquired images were examined using the ImageJ 7.0 software. To determine whether Cy5.5-labeled HA@CuS NPs co-localize with RAW-GFP cells, a chicken polyclonal GFP antibody (dilution 1:1000, AVES LABS INC. GFP-1020) was used as the primary antibody.

In Vivo cytokine secretion assay and flow cytometry analysis

To investigate the effects of RAW + HA@CuS NPs treatment on immune response, OS-bearing BALB/c mice were allocated to four groups as previously described. On day 3 post-irradiation, whole blood samples were collected from mice and serum was isolated for cytokine secretion assay, where TNF-α secretion was measured using an ELISA assay (eBioscience). Then mice were sacrificed and tumors were harvested for further immune response analyses. Briefly, tumor specimens were cut into small pieces and treated with 1% collagenase type IV diluted in RPMI 1640 medium with 1% BSA for 1 h at 37 °C. Collagenase activity was stopped by adding an equal volume of DMEM supplemented with 10%FBS. The sample was then subjected to centrifugation at 300g for 5 min. Finally, the supernatant from the tumor tissues were isolated and used to quantify the levels of IL-10 and IL-12p40 via ELISA assay (eBioscience). For macrophage polarization, cells were labeled with anti-CD206-APC, anti-CD80-APC and anti-F4/80-PE (Biolegend) antibodies according to the manufacturer's guidelines. For CTL evaluation, cells were stained with anti-CD3-Percp-Cy5.5 and anti-CD8-APC (Biolegend) antibodies according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Data were measured using Beckman Coulter Gallios flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) and analyzed by FlowJo software.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted from normal tumor tissues and thermally treated tumor tissues. GAPDH, Caspase 3, CD44 and HSP70 were assessed by Western Blot analysis.

Statistical analysis

The Student's t-test method was used for statistical analysis. A statistically significant difference was estimated at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P ＜ 0.0001, *****P ＜ 0.00001. Values were expressed as the mean ± SD.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of HA@CuS NPs

Scheme 1(A) illustrates the synthesis of HA@CuS NPs using thiol modified HA (HA-SH) as the template. The synthesis of HA-SH involves the conjugation of cysteamine with the carboxylic acid group of HA using the EDC/NHS coupling protocol. The reaction was performed in an aqueous buffer over a period of 48 h, at a pH around 5. The content of the thiol in HA-SH was determined using 1H NMR, showing that about 68% of the carboxylic acid groups in the HA chain were coupled with cysteamine (Fig. 1A). The HA@CuS NPs were then synthesized via the precipitation reaction between CuCl2 and Na2S with HA-SH as the dispersant. The dynamic laser scattering (DLS) measurement showed that HA@CuS had a narrow size distribution with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 61.9 nm (PDI = 0.27) and a zeta potential of −12.6 mV (Fig. 1B). The TEM image showed a spherical morphology of HA@CuS with a uniform size of ca. 30 nm. This was slightly smaller than the hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS (Fig. 1C and D), which was probably due to the hydration shell of the NPs. The obtained HA@CuS NPs demonstrated excellent solubility and colloidal stability in water, PBS, normal saline and RPMI-1640 for at least one week (Fig. 1E and Fig. S1). The HA@CuS NPs also exhibited concentration-dependent UV-Vis-NIR absorption (Fig. 1F), and their absorbance at 808 nm had a positive linear relationship with concentrations (Fig. 1G), implying their potential as a powerful photothermal agent. In vitro cellular uptake and cytotoxicity assays

To examine if the coating of HA can increase NPs uptake, the absorption of HA@CuS NPs and BSA@CuS NPs by MNNG/HOS cells and RAW264.7 macrophages was tested through immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy. HA@CuS and BSA@CuS NPs were stained with a far-red dye, Cy5.5, prior to conducting imaging tests. After incubation of both cells with HA@CuS NPs and BSA@CuS NPs separately for 2 h, bright fluorescence was observed in the cytoplasm, indicating the uptake of both NPs by the target cells. Moreover, HA@CuS NPs showed a significantly higher level of cellular uptake in both cell types compared with the BSA@CuS NPs control (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3), indicating that the HA-coating increased the cellular uptake of NPs in cancer cells and macrophages. In addition, the cellular uptake of HA@CuS NPs was much higher in RAW264.7 macrophages compared with MNNG/HOS cells, indicating that the CD44-overexpressing macrophages had better phagocytotic capability [30,31]. It was further confirmed by Western Blot analysis (Fig. S4). To further explore the binding specificity of HA@CuS NPs, a competitive binding study was performed. The CD44 receptors on RAW264.7 macrophages were first blocked by excessive amount of HA and then treated with HA@CuS NPs. After 2 h incubation, significantly reduced fluorescence in the macrophages receiving CD44 blocking was observed in this experiment (Fig. S5). The efficient cellular uptake facilitated the targeting of both intrinsic and adoptively transferred macrophages with HA@CuS NPs.

It is critical for photothermal agents to have good biocompatibility which secures the innate functions of cells (such as phagocytosis and exocytosis) [32]. Thus, the cytotoxic effects of HA@CuS NPs and BSA@CuS NPs was determined using the CCK8 assay. It is evident that both NPs had little negative impact on the viability of RAW264.7 macrophages and MNNG/HOS cells, even at the maximum concentration of CuS NPs (equivalent to 10.8 μg/mL Cu2+) or after prolonged time of co-culture for 48 h (Fig. 2B). The low cytotoxicity of HA@CuS NPs without NIR laser irradiation would prevent damage to normal cells.

In vitro photothermal property and antitumor efficacy of HA@CuS NPs

The photothermal efficacy of HA@CuS NPs was investigated by exposing the samples with an 808-nm laser beam. It is noteworthy that the temperature of the laser-irradiated HA@CuS NPs solutions elevated as the NP concentration increased at a power density of 1.5 w/cm2 (Fig. 2C). To investigate the photothermal performance of HA@CuS NPs, we performed the live/dead cell double staining assay with Calcein AM and propidium iodide (PI), respectively. No damage was found in the control groups of either types of cells treated with HA@CuS NPs or BSA@CuS NPs without laser irradiation, whereas the CuS NPs plus laser irradiation groups exhibited obvious cellular death (1.5 w/cm2, 3 min) (Fig. 2D), which demonstrated a good performance of PTT therapy. Meanwhile, much more cellular death occurred in the HA@CuS NPs group compared with the BSA@CuS NPs group, probably owing to the high efficiency of HA@CuS NPs in cellular uptake (Fig. 2A). Then, the photothermal toxicity of the nanoparticles was further quantified by the CCK8 assay (Fig. S6). Therefore, HA@CuS NPs could be considered as excellent photothermal materials that can produce hyperthermia and kill target cells in vitro.

The analysis of macrophages serving as drug depots

In the current study, as an important part of this novel strategy, macrophages have been reported to act as the ‘cellular drug reservoirs', transferring temporarily sequestered drugs into the neighboring tissues [16]. In addition, the elevated expression of efflux pump in drug-loaded macrophages has been reported, which can in part contribute to viability preservation of macrophages and drug release to the neighboring cells [33,34]. Nevertheless, there is still little evidence that macrophages can serve as drug depots. To address this issue, TEM was used to intuitively display the location of the NPs. As shown in Fig. 2E, HA@CuS NPs located in the endosomes and lysosomes, and not in the nucleus. Although HA@CuS NPs were endocytosed by RAW264.7 macrophages, a portion of HA@CuS NPs was expelled through exocytosis (Fig. 2E). This dynamic association between macrophages and NPs was further quantified by ICP-MS via respectively measuring the Cu2+ content in the RAW264.7 macrophages and the media. As shown in Fig. 2F, the Cu2+ content in the macrophages significantly increased to a high level (76.3 ± 5% of total dose) in 4 h, then maintained at ∼60% from 8 to 24 h, which was slightly higher than that in the media (∼44%). All of these findings further indicated that macrophages can release NP payloads into surrounding tissues after initial endocytosis.

The migration assay of macrophages

As a key component of the TME, macrophages have recently received increasing attention as novel therapeutic targets for antitumor treatments [35]. Instead of directly killing these cells, current efforts involve the inhibition of TAMs recruitment and reeducation of TAMs into an antitumor phenotype [36]. However, it is unknown how an increase in the number of TAMs would affect the subsequent tumor accumulation of NPs. Since macrophages can be recruited and infiltrate tumors in response to a variety of molecular signals [37], we proposed a potential antitumor strategy that an intravenous injection of macrophages during tumor progression could increase the TAMs content and thus improve the accumulation of NPs in tumors. Undoubtedly, the migration of macrophages toward orthotopic OS was crucial to the hypothesis. We thus performed the transwell Boyden assay to compare the migration of RAW264.7 macrophages under induction of the conditioned medium (prepared from MNNG/HOS cells) or the unconditioned medium DMEM (supplemented with 10% FBS) in 24-well plates. We found that the MNNG/HOS conditioned medium significantly promoted the migration of RAW264.7 macrophages compared with the DMEM group (Fig. 2G and Fig. S7). This result indicates that the macrophages in our study were capable of migrating toward OS in vitro.

The migration ability of RAW264.7 macrophages in vivo was investigated using the IVIS imaging system. The RAW264.7 macrophages were first transfected with luciferase (RAW-Luc) before being administered intravenously to the orthotopic OS-bearing nude mice (5 × 106 cells per mouse). Within the subsequent 48-72 h, the intensity and range of bioluminescence in the tumor region increased over time, and the tumor region had the highest level of bioluminescence from transfected macrophages. This can be attributed to the slow recruitment and extravasation of macrophages into the tumors [38]. The increased accumulation of NPs in tumor

To demonstrate if the adoptive transfer of macrophages increases the tumor accumulation of DDS, Cy5.5-labeled HA@CuS NPs were then administered intravenously into the OS mice at 72 h post administration of RAW264.7 macrophages. As shown in Fig. 3B, increased fluorescence intensity was observed in the RAW + HA@CuS group in the tumor regions when compared with that of the HA@CuS NPs-alone group using NIRF optical imaging. At 24 h post administration of HA@CuS NPs, tumors and major organs were excised and examined by NIRF imaging to further evaluate the tissue distribution of HA@CuS NPs. We observed relatively higher levels of HA@CuS NP fluorescence in the liver, intestine and tumor compared with other organs (Fig. 3C and D), indicating a hepatoenteral circulation and excellent tumor targeting capability of HA@CuS NPs. More importantly, the adoptive transfer of RAW264.7 macrophages further increased the accumulation of HA@CuS NPs in the tumor and liver (Fig. 3C and D), likely due to the accumulation of these macrophages in the tumor and liver. The biodistribution of HA@CuS NPs was also verified by ICP-MS for quantification of Cu2+ (Fig. 3E), which further confirmed the preferential accumulation of HA@CuS NPs in the liver and tumor and the beneficial effect of the adoptive transfer of RAW264.7 macrophages on the drug accumulation.

To further determine whether the enhanced tumor accumulation of HA@CuS NPs was attributed to the adoptive transfer of the macrophages, we transfected the RAW264.7 macrophages with GFP (RAW-GFP), and then intravenously injected Cy5.5-labeled HA@CuS NPs (red) into the OS-bearing mice 72 h after the adoptive transfer of RAW-GFP. After a subsequent 24 h, the mice were sacrificed and the tumors were sectioned. Tumor tissues were incubated with antibodies against GFP to reveal the adoptively transferred macrophages (green). The colocalization of HA@CuS NPs with cells in the tumor (pink) and the adoptively transferred macrophages (yellow) indicated that the HA@CuS NPs were taken up by the GFP-expressing macrophages, as well as by other neighboring cells (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, we analyzed the intratumoral distribution of intravenously injected Cy5.5-labeled HA@CuS NPs by flow cytometry at 24 h post drug injection (Fig. S8). Gated on PE-positive cells, it is found that 34.3% of cells in tumors were F4/80 positive macrophages, while 6.76% of them have internalized the labeled nanoparticles. Interestingly, the adoptively transferred macrophages account for 25.59% of drug in-take macrophages despite their low proportion in TAM population (9.32%). Since tumor-resident macrophages are inclined to accumulate in deep location of tumor tissues while the adoptively transferred macrophages are likely to remain in the adjacent area of blood vessels, it is reasonable for the GFP positive macrophages to take in more NPs. Together, these findings confirm that the increased tumor uptake of HA@CuS NPs resulted from the adoptive transfer of macrophages.

In vivo photothermal imaging of tumors

The in vivo evaluation of PTT effect from RAW + HA@CuS against OS was first investigated using orthotopic immunodeficient mouse model (Fig. 4A). Laser irradiation was applied in all groups 24 h after the i.v. injection of HA@CuS NPs. The in-tumor temperature during laser irradiation (1.5 w/cm2, 3 min, 808 nm) was measured by infrared thermal imaging camera. Compared with the mice injected with PBS or RAW264.7 macrophages alone, in which the temperature respectively increased to 38.0 °C and 38.4 °C upon irradiation, the one in the RAW + HA@CuS group reached 51.6 °C, which was also higher compared to that of the HA@CuS group (49.4 °C) (Fig. 4B and C). Moreover, the RAW + HA@CuS group showed a much stronger and more uniform thermal profile in the tumor region compared with the HA@CuS NPs group (Fig. 4B), which was consistent with the biodistribution study (Fig. 3). In vivo PTT efficacy of OS

We then investigated the PTT efficacy on tumor inhibition. Compared with the PBS buffer and RAW264.7 macrophages-treated groups, the area and optical density of bioluminescence in the mice treated with RAW + HA@CuS NPs or HA@CuS NPs alone significantly decreased at the end of the two-week therapy (Fig. 4D and E). This was further confirmed by the images of the tumors extracted from the mice (Fig. 4F). Of note, despite an outstanding effect of HA@CuS NPs on reducing the tumor size, the bioluminescence intensity in the mice treated with RAW + HA@CuS NPs was markedly lower compared with the HA@CuS NPs group (Fig. 4E), indicating improved PTT with the aid of the adoptively transferred macrophages. Furthermore, the body weight of mice in the RAW + HA@CuS group was higher compared with those from other groups (Fig. S9), which could also be attributed to the significant reduced tumor burden as a result of the combination of adoptively transferred macrophages and PTT with HA@CuS NPs.

For further analysis of the PTT effect mediated by RAW + HA@CuS, the tumor tissues and other organs in each group were collected after PTT treatment. H&E staining showed extensive cell death induced by the RAW + HA@CuS treatment, which was more obvious compared with the HA@CuS treated group. In contrast, no damage in the tumor was detected in the PBS or RAW treated controls (Fig. 4G). We further determined the level of cell death related proteins Hsp70 and cleaved caspase-3 using western blot and found their overexpression in the tumor of the RAW + HA@CuS treated mice (Fig. 4H), suggesting cellular stress and apoptosis in the tumor area [[41], [42], [43]]. These results demonstrated that RAW + HA@CuS induced apoptotic cell death during the PTT against OS. The main organs of mice from each group were also examined. No obvious tissue injury was found through histopathological observation (Fig. S10), which indicated that the HA@CuS NPs had high biocompatibility.

In vivo anti-tumor immune response

Through the utilization of inherent immune systems, cancer immunotherapy is able to identify and eradicate nidus efficiently. Recent studies have indicated that tumor-ablation regimens, such as PTT and photodynamic therapy, could induce robust immune responses [[44], [45], [46]]. Based on the high PTT performance of RAW + HA@CuS in vivo, the OS orthotopic models with immune-competent mice were built next to determine the possible immunological reactions caused by the PTT effect of RAW + HA@CuS (Fig. 5A). BALB/c mice bearing orthotopic OS were randomly sorted into four groups (n = 5) as stated above, followed with NIR irradiation after the i.v. injection. Tumor bioluminescence intensity and body weight changes were measured to assess the therapeutic effect. Finally, tumor weight was analyzed on day 15. As shown in Fig. 5B and C, we confirmed that HA@CuS treatment suppressed OS growth efficiently compared with the control groups. And the RAW + HA@CuS group exhibited a nearly complete inhibition of OS growth, whose increased PTT effect can probably be attributed to the enhanced uptake of HA@CuS NPs in tumors. Together with tumor weight results, the therapeutic efficacy of the DDS was further confirmed under immunocompetent conditions (Fig. 5D). Besides, the mice body weight following different treatments demonstrated insignificant changes (Fig. 5E), suggesting the acceptable safety of the strategy. It is well accepted that once recruited in the tumor tissue, macrophages are susceptible to the local immunosuppressive microenvironment, resulting in their transition into M2 phenotype and thus promoting tumor growth [47,48]. In this study, HA modification of CuS is aimed to not only facilitate cellular internalization but also offer effective immunological stimulation toward macrophages, as HA were found to switch anti-inflammatory, pro-tumoral M2 TAMs to pro-inflammatory, tumoricidal M1 macrophages [49,50]. Thus, we sought to determine whether HA@CuS or RAW + HA@CuS could resist the suppressive effect of TME. We first exploited the ratio of TAMs in TME at day 3 after NIR exposure. From Fig. 5F and G, it can be seen that flow cytometry showed significantly increased quantities of M1 macrophages (F4/80+CD80+) and decreased presence of M2 macrophages (F4/80+CD206+). Moreover, this M1 polarization was further verified by the cytokine secretion experiments. As shown in Fig. 5H, higher level of IL-12p40 (M1-type cytokine) and lower level of IL-10 (M2-type cytokine) were detected in the HA@CuS or RAW + HA@CuS group. Meanwhile, PTT is capable of generating tumor-specific antigens from ablated tumor cells, which can be presented to activate CD8+ T cells, with the possible involvement of M1 macrophages [51,52]. We therefore examined the infiltration of T cells in the tumor 3 days post-NIR irradiation. As shown in Fig. 5I and J, there were significant increases in the infiltrations of T cells (CD3+) in HA@CuS alone or RAW + HA@CuS treatment groups, while higher amounts of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) infiltration were achieved following RAW + HA@CuS treatment. The high level of tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) in the serum verified the CTL-mediated immune response following such treatment (Fig. 5K). These distinguished effects can be ascribed to the TAMs modulation and tumor ablation capacity of the DDS. Furthermore, as demonstrated in Fig. 4H, RAW + HA@CuS-mediated PTT can lead to the upregulation of heat shock proteins, further facilitating the presentation of antigens and the activation of CTL. Conclusion

In summary, while the prepared HA@CuS NPs can efficiently target TAM in OS, the adoptive transfer of macrophages can further increase their accumulation in tumors, leading to enhanced PTT and immunomodulation efficacy of HA@CuS NPs. It demonstrates that the combination therapy integrating adoptively transferred macrophages with HA-based PTT presents a promising tactic in cancer treatment.
